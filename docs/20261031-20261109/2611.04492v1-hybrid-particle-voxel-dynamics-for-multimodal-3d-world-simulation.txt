ABSTRACT
Predicting how the world evolves in response to robot actions is central to pol-
icy learning, evaluation, and planning. However, many action-conditioned world
models predict future observations in 2D image or video space without explicitly
modeling the underlying 3D scene, making spatial consistency across viewpoints
and over long-horizon interactions challenging. We introduce 3D World Simula-
tor, an action-conditioned multimodal world modeling framework that jointly pre-
dicts appearance, geometry, and tactile observations from a unified 3D representa-
tion. Our approach combines a hybrid particle-voxel autoencoder that compresses
point clouds into compact 3D voxel latents with an action-conditioned latent dy-
namics model that predicts their evolution. The shared 3D representation supports
geometrically consistent rendering from arbitrary viewpoints and flexible integra-
tion of spatially aligned tactile signals. Our experiments demonstrate interactive,
long-horizon, multimodal prediction at 50 Hz. We then show how our model en-
ables scalable data generation for policy training and evaluation using tactile and
multi-view visual observations, including wrist-mounted and novel camera views.
Furthermore, we scale our method to large datasets containing diverse objects and
demonstrate model-based planning with unseen objects and robot configurations.
Together, these results highlight the potential of hybrid particle-voxel dynamics as
a foundation for multimodal 3D world simulation.

INTRODUCTION
Predicting how the 3D world evolves in response to interaction is a fundamental capability for intel-
ligent robotic systems, with applications to data generation for policy training (Wang et al., 2026),
1
arXiv:2611.04492v1  [cs.RO]  3 Nov 2026


policy evaluation (Guo et al., 2026b), model-based planning (Huang et al., 2026b), and as back-
bones for world-action modeling (NVIDIA et al., 2025). Existing action-conditioned world models
are predominantly built using 2D video generation backbones. While able to produce photorealistic
predictions, these models do not explicitly represent the underlying 3D scene, making it difficult to
maintain spatial consistency across viewpoints and to model the precise spatial relationship between
contact and geometry. Prior work has explored using 3D representations (Huang et al., 2026b;
Zhang et al., 2024b), but these methods typically rely on strong structural assumptions, such as
object-specific state representations or explicit correspondences, that can be unreliable in complex
interactions and limit broader applicability.
In this work, we present 3D World Simulator, an action-conditioned multimodal world model-
ing framework that predicts in a unified 3D representation space, providing a common interface
capable of generating 3D particles, spatially aligned tactile signals, and multi-view consistent ren-
derings from novel viewpoints. Our model supports interactive, long horizon, multimodal 3D video
prediction at 50 Hz, and generalizes to diverse objects and environments when trained at scale.
3D World Simulator consists of two technical components. (1) Hybrid particle-voxel autoencoder.
The encoder takes point clouds obtained directly from RGB-D video and compresses them into a
compact 3D voxel latent. The decoder progressively upsamples this latent into higher-resolution
voxels and then dense 3D particles in a coarse-to-fine manner. Each particle parameterizes a 3D
Gaussian that can be rasterized from arbitrary viewpoints (Kerbl et al., 2023), while tactile signals
are represented directly as 3D particles within the same output space (Huang et al., 2024; 2025a).
(2) 3D latent dynamics model. We learn an action-conditioned dynamics model that predicts the
next 3D voxel latent directly. Because this latent encodes the scene as a whole, the dynamics model
does not rely on object-specific state representations or explicit correspondences.
A key advantage of our inherently 3D world model is that a single predicted scene state creates a
common interface for multiple concurrent capabilities. Since appearance is grounded in a shared
3D Gaussian representation, our model can (1) produce geometrically and multi-view consistent
renderings, including from novel and dynamic wrist-mounted camera viewpoints. The same spatial
representation provides a (2) flexible and principled way of incorporating multimodal signals.
Tactile predictions can be represented directly as 3D particles, remaining geometrically aligned
with contacted surfaces. The shared 3D representation also makes our predictions (3) composable
with existing 3D assets, including various static backgrounds, objects, and kinematic robot models,
enabling additional controllability over the simulated observations from our model.
The 3D World Simulator enables several important downstream applications. We demonstrate inter-
active, long-horizon, multimodal 3D video prediction across a diverse set of six tasks involving
rigid, articulated, and deformable objects, outperforming state of the art 2D video and 3D baselines.
Next, 3D World Simulator enables policy training and evaluation, generating synthetic data to
train imitation learning policies that rely on visuo-tactile observations, as well as multi-view obser-
vations including novel and wrist-mounted camera views. Our model can further be used to evaluate
policies, demonstrating strong correlation with real evaluations. Furthermore, 3D World Simulator
is scalable to diverse objects. We train 3D World Simulator on the large-scale Deform360 (Li et al.,
2026a) dataset, a large-scale multi-view visuo-tactile dataset. Our method outperforms state-of-the-
art 3D baselines and can be used for zero-shot model-based planning on unseen objects and robot
arms.
In summary, our main contributions are: (1) 3D World Simulator, a general action-conditioned
multimodal world modeling framework for interactive, long horizon 3D video prediction, (2) hybrid
particle-voxel dynamics in a unified 3D representation space, enabling view consistency and flexible
integration of multimodal signals, and (3) a unified model that enables a range of applications,
including policy training, policy evaluation, and planning.

2D Action-Conditioned Video Model.
Existing 2D action-conditioned video models can predict
photorealistic futures (Yang et al., 2023; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024b; Jiang et al., 2025b;
Agarwal et al., 2026; Liao et al., 2026). They have shown promising results in robotic manipulation
applications such as data generation (Wang et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026), policy evaluation (Guo

Jain et al. (2026), and policy improvement (Yin et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026a). However, they do
not explicitly represent the 3D world, making it difficult to enforce spatial consistency and model
complicated object interactions. Among these works, Interactive World Simulator is the most closely
related to ours Wang et al. (2026). The authors introduce a task-specific 2D action-conditioned video
model that supports interactive, stable, and physically plausible long-horizon simulation. However,
it predicts in purely pixel space, making prediction from arbitrary viewpoints and spatial consis-
tency challenging. In contrast, our 3D World Simulator explicitly represents the underlying 3D
scene, supporting spatially consistent predictions from novel viewpoint. We show that our model
can also accurately model challenging object interactions, including cloth folding and part assembly.
3D Dynamics Models.
Many prior works explore modeling object dynamics in the 3D world using
various 3D representations, including point cloud (Huang et al., 2026b;a; Bauer et al., 2024), sparse
particles (Zhang et al., 2025; Whitney et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025c; Zhang et al., 2024a; Jiang
et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2018), neural radiance fields (Driess et al., 2023), 3D Gaussians (Zhang et al.,
2024b; Xie et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2025; Abou-Chakra et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024c; Lu et al.,
2024; 2025), and 3D mesh (Pfaff et al., 2020). Some of them focus on learning dynamics of specific
types of objects, such as elastoplastic objects (Bauer et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023; 2024), cloth (Lin
et al., 2022), fluid (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020). Among these works, PointWorld and PGND are
the closest to ours (Huang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2025). Both train 3D world models in point
or particle space and show spatially consistent and physically plausible results. However, both use
point tracking to provide training supervision. In contrast, our model does not require point-tracking
supervision and is transferable across diverse scenes. In addition, they do not focus on photorealistic
2D image rendering, which is essential for visuomotor policy training and evaluation. Instead, our
model supports photorealistic 2D rendering from novel viewpoint, including fixed external cameras
and moving wrist cameras, enabling scalable data generation and visuomotor policy evaluation.
Multimodal World Models.
Researchers have explored how to incorporate tactile sensing into
world models by predicting 2D tactile images alongside 2D visual images (Higuera et al., 2026;
Zhang et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026c; Ma et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026b; Tian et al., 2019; Lou
et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025; Zang et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026a). In contrast,
our model predicts in 3D space with spatial consistency. A closely related work, RoboPack, also
models tactile sensing and object interaction dynamics in 3D space to accomplish challenging dense
packing tasks (Ai et al., 2024). However, its dynamics model mainly focuses on dense packing tasks
and predicts over relatively short horizons.

3.2
HYBRID VOXEL–PARTICLE AUTOENCODER
Sparse voxel encoder.
We first voxelize the input point cloud Pt onto a sparse 3D voxel grid. We
aggregate points by averaging the features of points that fall within each occupied voxel, producing
features only at occupied voxel locations. The encoder Eθ then applies strided sparse 3D convolu-
tions (Graham et al., 2018) to progressively downsample the voxel input into a compact latent zt,
avoiding unnecessary computation over empty space. We unit-normalize each latent voxel along the
channel dimension before passing the representation to the decoder or latent dynamics model.
Coarse-to-fine particle-voxel decoder.
As shown in part (a) of Figure 2, the decoder Dψ recon-
structs the scene from zt in two stages, upsampling coarse voxels to dense particles.
(i) Voxel Upsampling: the decoder progressively upsamples zt using transposed 3D convolutions
until reaching the desired voxel resolution. At each resolution, the decoder predicts scene occupancy,
providing progressively finer estimates of the scene structure at each layer.
(ii) Particle Upsampling: to obtain dense 3D particle prediction, we use a lightweight MLP to
predict continuous particle positions given each occupied voxel. Each resulting particle predicts
its 3D position and the parameters of a 3D Gaussian, including color, scale, rotation, and opacity,
enabling rendering from arbitrary viewpoints.
Training Objective.
We train the voxel-particle autoencoder end-to-end using the following loss:
LAE = Lvoxel + Lparticle + Lrender + Lcons.
(i) Voxel loss Lvoxel. To reconstruct the 3D occupancy, we compare predicted voxel occupancy at
each decoder resolution level with the ground truth occupancy and sum the loss across all levels.
4


(ii) Particle loss Lparticle. To reconstruct accurate particle geometry, we measure the Chamfer dis-
tance (CD) (Fan et al., 2017) between predicted particles and ground truth points. Particle color and
tactile values are additionally supervised using ℓ1 error on the matched points.
(iii) Render loss Lrender. Given the predicted 3D Gaussians, we render RGB-D images from the
training camera viewpoints and supervise them using a combination of ℓ1, SSIM (Wang et al., 2004),
and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) losses on RGB and an ℓ1 loss on valid depth pixels.
(iv) Consistency regularization Lcons. Different subsamples of the same observation can produce
inconsistent latent encodings. We therefore encode two independently subsampled point clouds and
penalize the ℓ2 difference between their latent representations.
3.3
3D LATENT DYNAMICS MODEL
After training the particle-voxel autoencoder, we learn an action-conditioned dynamics model Fϕ
over its 3D latent representation. As shown in part (b) of Figure 2, at time t, given an input latent zt,
a history with H previous latents zt−H:t−1, as well as a corresponding robot action at, the model
approximates the next latent state zt+1 by predicting ˆzt+1 = Fϕ(zt−H:t, at). The predicted latent
can then be decoded into 3D geometry, multi-view visual observations, and tactile signals.
We instantiate our dynamics model as a 3D U-Net (C¸ ic¸ek et al., 2016). We first downsample input
latent voxels with 3D convolutional layers and then apply interleaved spatial attention and causal
temporal attention (Vaswani et al., 2017; Bertasius et al., 2021). Lastly, the decoder upsamples the
predicted latent voxels back to the input latent resolution.

3.4
UNIFIED COMPOSITION OF SIMULATION ”BACKBONES”
While our model is limited to a specific workspace, to enable larger scale scene simulation, our
model can be composed with various 3D Gaussian assets. By composing the raw autoencoder output
with diverse static background made up of 3D Gaussians, we can further simulate various types of
scenes. We can further utilize kinematic 3D Gaussian robot models to allow for more complete
scene simulation, as robot arms may fall outside the specificied workspace. Although our model
does not natively depend on object-specific representations, it can be finetuned to output labels to
separate specific scene components like the robot arm, object, and table. Given this separability,
we can further insert different robot arms and kinematic models not seen during training, as well as
diverse backgrounds.
3.5
INFERENCE
At inference time, we roll out the world model autoregressively in open loop. Starting from an
initial encoded 3D latent z1, the dynamics model predicts the next 3D latent conditioned on the
robot action, and each predicted latent is fed back as input for subsequent steps. Each predicted
latent can be decoded into visual and tactile point clouds, as well as 3D Gaussians that can be
rendered from arbitrary viewpoints. To render wrist camera views during open-loop rollouts, we
fine-tune a prediction head on the decoder.

4.1
EXPERIMENT SETUP
ALOHA Task Setup. We conduct data collection and experimentation using an ALOHA Bimanual
Robot (Zhao et al., 2023), paired with FlexiTac (Huang & Li, 2026) tactile sensors on each fingertip.
We include six tasks that cover manipulation of deformable, rigid, and articulated objects: Part
Assembly, Cloth Folding, T Pushing, Box Packing, Rope Threading, and Marker Insertion. Detailed
descriptions and visualizations of each task setup can be found in Appendix C. We collect data via a
combination of kinematic devices and keyboard inputs to teleoperate the robot, resulting in around
200 episodes and 2 hours of data for each task.
Deform360 Dataset.
To investigate the scalability of 3D World Simulator, we utilize Deform
360 (Li et al., 2026a), a visuo-tactile dataset featuring 198 daily-life objects, 1,980 interaction se-
quences, and over 215 hours of observations from 41 surround-view cameras and bimanual tac-
tile grippers. Since Deform360 only provides object-centric depth information, we train our world
model purely on the object particles unlike the ALOHA setup where we reconstruct the robot and
environment surroundings.

FAITHFUL POLICY EVALUATION
To evaluate whether 3D World Simulator can serve as a faithful proxy for real-world policy evalu-
ation, we examine whether imitation policies show positive correlation when evaluated within our
simulator and the real world. We train four imitation learning policies: ACT, DP, π0.5, and Cosmos
Policy, and we utilize the same three tasks from policy training in Section 4.3, Part Assembly, Cloth
Folding, and T-Pushing. We sample 20 initial configurations for each task. Figure 7 shows that
across all three tasks and four policies, real-world policy performance is highly correlated with per-
formance in the 3D World Simulator. The x-axis denotes the real-world success rate while the y-axis
denotes the success rate within the world simulator. Each point corresponds to a policy checkpoint at
a different training step, while colors denote the policy architecture. Error bars are indicated through
Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals, obtained through 20 evaluations per policy both in simulation
and the real world.

MPPI (Williams et al., 2017) from PGND (Zhang et al., 2025). Figure 8 shows qualitatively and
quantitatively that our pre-trained 3D World Simulator can achieve zero-shot planning results.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced 3D World Simulator, an action-conditioned multimodal world modeling
framework that predicts in a unified 3D representation space, creating a common interface to gen-
erate 3D particles, multi-view consistent rendered RGB, and tactile predictions. Our unified model
enables a range of applications. 3D World Simulator enables the generation of policy training data
for visuo-tactile policies, as well as policies that rely on multi-view, novel view, and wrist-mounted
camera view observations. We can further evaluate these policies using our model, demonstrating
strong correlation between simulation and real world rollouts. When scaled to large scale datasets,
3D World Simulator enables downstream model-based planning on unseen objects and robot con-
figurations. In future work, we plan to extend 3D World Simulator to more diverse and challenging
environments, extending its general applicability. Additionally, we further aim to extend 3D World
Simulator to larger scale scenes involving multi-room environments and mobile manipulation, en-
abling increased real world applications.

Training stages.
The reference recipe first trains the voxel autoencoder for 10 epochs. During

B.2
3D LATENT DYNAMICS
Per-frame spatial encoder.
Let the observed history contain H latent grids, including the most
recent observation: zt−H+1, . . . , zt. A shared 3D convolutional encoder processes each frame in-
dependently, preserving an explicit temporal dimension. Two spatial downsampling stages produce
features at one-half and one-quarter of the latent-grid resolution.
Action conditioning.
Each observed frame is paired with its corresponding robot state or action
descriptor. A future query slot is conditioned on the action for the requested prediction. An MLP
embeds the action, relative timestamp, and history-noise level, and injects this conditioning into the
feature blocks.
For Deform360, the action descriptor contains each gripper’s 3D position, 6D orientation represen-
tation (Zhou et al., 2019), opening, and presence indicator. Positions and orientations are expressed
in the same frame as the object latent. For ALOHA, the corresponding descriptor is obtained from
the robot kinematics and gripper state.
Optionally, we concatenate four spatial action channels to each frame: a position heatmap and an
opening-modulated heatmap for each gripper. The future query receives the target action’s spatial
channels. Missing grippers produce zero-valued spatial channels.

At the coarsest resolution, the model alternates spatial
and temporal attention. Spatial attention operates over all coarse voxels within each frame:

C
ALOHA DATASET TASK SETUP
Task Descriptions
C.1
ALOHA TASK DESCRIPTIONS AND SETUP
Part Assembly: The left robot arm grasps the socket while the right arm grasps the plug in various
starting positions. The task involves reorienting the plug by the right arm pressing down on the
socket, then lifting to insert into the socket.
Cloth Folding: The left robot arm starts by grasping the top left edge of the cloth and lifting it such
that the top edge of the cloth straightens. The right robot arm slides its hand across this edge to
unfurl the cloth. Then both arms are used to fold the cloth.
T Pushing: Both robot grippers are closed and constrained to the table plane. They are used to push
the T block into various positions.
Marker Insertion: The left robot arm grasps the marker in the pencil cup. The right robot arm then
grasps the marker and performs in-hand adjustment through sliding and reorienting the marker to a
90 degree angle. The left hand then inserts the marker into the holder.
Rope Threading: The rope starts in a curled and overlapping positions. The right robot arm grasps
one end of the rope, while the left robot arm repeatedly slides along the rope until it reaches the
other end. Once the left robot arm grasps near the other end of the rope, it inserts it into a pad eye.
19


Box Packing: The lid of the box starts open and laid on the table. Both robot grippers are used to lift
the lid of the box and close it.
Camera Setup
We utilize three RealSense D455 static stereo cameras including one top-down view camera, and
two forward facing cameras on either side of the workspace. In addition, we utilize the two wrist-
mounted RealSense D405 cameras, one mounted on each arm.
Data Processing
To obtain the pointcloud observation, we utilize FoundationStereo (Wen et al., 2025) to process the
stereo observations from each RealSense camera, before back-projecting into the world frame. To
obtain tactile-particle locations from the FlexiTac tactile signals, we use forward kinematics using
the ALOHA joint positions to calculate the tactile pad positions. We then crop the point cloud using
a manually set workspace bounding box.

C.3
BASELINE DETAILS
To enable Cosmos to predict depth information and allow multi-view renders, we follow guidelines
and concatenate four views in one image, in addition to depth mapped into RGB space. For IWS, we
enable the model to predict depth in addition to RGB, and concatenate a latent for each view in ac-
cordance to training guidelines. We follow the recipe to train Robot4DGen as it is already designed
to be 3D aware and enable multi-view rendering. To train the 3D particle prediction baselines, we
utilize CoTracker (Karaev et al., 2024) to obtain point tracks for each object as well as for scene
points.
D
POLICY TRAINING AND EVALUATION DETAILS
D.1
EVALUATION CRITERIA
For evaluation, we define a success in the Assembly task to be whether the plug is successfully
inserted into the socket. For cloth folding, if the edge is not grasped, that is judged to be a failure.
If the edge is grasped and the mask IoU is higher than 0.6, we judge that to be a success. For
T-Pushing, we define a success to be if the mask IoU is above 0.60.
D.2
TASK DETAILS
Part Assembly.
The policy observes only the right wrist-mounted camera, while the socket is
heavily occluded in the left hand, making successful insertion dependent on both accurate wrist-
camera observations and tactile feedback.
T Pushing.
We utilize a novel viewpoint that was not seen during world model training. Perfor-
mance is slightly lower most probably because this novel viewpoint is harder than the top down view
for successful task performance, rather than the rendering.
Cloth Folding.
The policy is required to slide along the cloth edge which is not visible from the
front camera observation. This task shows the usage of multi-view observations.
23


D.3
POLICY TRAINING DETAILS
We train ACT, Diffusion Policy (DP), π0.5, and Cosmos on Cloth Folding, Part Assembly, and T-
Pushing. Each task contains 100 demonstrations recorded at 10 Hz, with 90 demonstrations used
for training and 10 held out. Cloth Folding uses front and wrist-mounted camera observations, Part
Assembly uses the wrist-mounted camera together with tactile observations, and T-Pushing uses a
single novel camera viewpoint. Real images are color-corrected, cropped, and resized to match the
corresponding simulated observations. ACT and DP use 128 × 128 image inputs, while π0.5 and
Cosmos use 224 × 224 inputs.
For Part Assembly, tactile observations consist of four 12 × 32 tactile pads. We apply a small
deadband before normalization and use small tactile augmentation during training; Cloth Folding
and T-Pushing do not use tactile inputs.
ACT and DP are trained with batch size 64 for 30,000 updates, while π0.5 uses batch size 8 and
is trained for 30,000 updates. ACT predicts 30-action chunks, DP predicts 16-action horizons, and
π0.5 predicts 30-action chunks. We use the optimizer and learning-rate settings listed in Table 5.
ACT uses an ImageNet-initialized ResNet-18 (He et al., 2016) visual encoder and transformer
policy.
DP uses a ResNet-18 visual encoder with a conditional temporal U-Net diffusion
model.
π0.5 is fine-tuned using LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), while Cosmos is initialized from
Cosmos-Policy-ALOHA-Predict2-2B. We use standard image augmentation techniques
like random color jitter, random crop and blur. We also add tactile augmentation to the Part As-
sembly policy by adding sparse noise, dropout, and Gaussian noise.
