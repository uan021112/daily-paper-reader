ABSTRACT
World Action Models (WAMs) improve robotic manipulation by jointly modeling
visual dynamics and robot actions. However, most existing WAMs model dense
short-horizon visual dynamics, capturing local interactions but providing limited
long-range task context. Extending prediction horizons increases uncertainty and
computation while entangling task-level evolution with fine-grained local dynam-
ics, obscuring their dependencies and distinct roles in action generation. We pro-
pose HiLo-WAM, which decouples long-horizon task evolution from local visual
dynamics while jointly modeling their interaction with robot actions. A sparse
long-horizon visual stream captures historical task context and future evolution,
while a dense local dynamics visual stream models short-horizon robot–object
interactions. The two streams operate at different temporal scales within a uni-
fied dual-DiT architecture and guide actions through structured cross-stream at-
tention. Progressive inference further reduces unnecessary computation through
stream-wise partial denoising, joint denoising, and dynamic skipping. Experi-
ments demonstrate strong long-horizon manipulation performance: HiLo-WAM
achieves a 93.70% average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 and a 17.43 progress
score on RoboDojo long-horizon tasks, with robust in-domain and OOD perfor-
mance on the Fourier GR-3 robot. Ablations confirm the complementary con-
tributions of long-horizon task evolution and local interaction dynamics, while
temporal-scale analyses highlight the importance of balancing temporal coverage
and resolution. Progressive inference further achieves a 2.93× speedup while im-
proving performance.

INTRODUCTION
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have shown strong generalization in robotic manipulation
through large-scale pretraining and diverse robot demonstrations Black et al. (2025; 2026); Kim
et al. (2025); Intelligence et al. (2026). World Action Models (WAMs) further introduce predictive
visual dynamics into action generation, either by conditioning actions on future visual states Li
et al. (2026); Hu et al. (2024); Du et al. (2023) or by jointly modeling future visual representations
and actions Yuan et al. (2026); Li et al. (2025a); Ma et al. (2026); Ye et al. (2026). Most existing
WAMs, however, focus on dense visual dynamics over relatively short temporal windows, which
effectively capture fine-grained robot–object interactions but provide limited information about how
a task evolves over a longer horizon. Long-horizon manipulation additionally requires understanding
historical task progress and future evolution beyond the current interaction window Cai et al. (2026);
Hu et al. (2026); Torne et al. (2026); Yang et al. (2026a).
To incorporate such long-range task information, recent WAMs extend the temporal scope of visual
modeling through longer observation histories Li et al. (2026); Ye et al. (2026), persistent mem-
ory Yang et al. (2026a), extended future prediction Xu et al. (2026), or long-horizon visual plan-
ning Cai et al. (2026). These approaches provide richer temporal context by exposing the policy to
task progress and future dynamics over a broader time span.
Extending the temporal horizon of visual world modeling introduces a fundamental challenge: a
single visual trajectory must capture both slowly evolving task-level progression and fine-grained
local interaction dynamics. This not only increases prediction uncertainty and redundant computa-
tion Team et al. (2026); Cai et al. (2026); Zhou et al. (2025), but, more importantly, obscures the
dependencies between different temporal dynamics and their distinct roles in action generation. This
raises a key question: how can a World Action Model explicitly model different temporal dynamics
and their dependencies on action generation?
To address this problem, we propose HiLo-WAM, which decouples the two temporal roles of visual
world modeling into complementary long-horizon and local dynamics streams while jointly learning
their interaction with robot actions. As shown in Fig. 1, We adopt a multi-scale temporal modeling
strategy to represent the two streams at different temporal resolutions. Specifically, the long-horizon
visual stream sparsely models historical task context and coarse future task evolution, capturing how
the task has progressed and where it is heading, while the local visual dynamics stream densely mod-
els short-horizon robot–object interactions required for precise control. Both streams are processed
by a shared Video DiT and interact with an Action DiT through structured cross-stream attention, al-
lowing long-horizon task information and local dynamics to jointly support action generation. This
temporal decomposition further enables progressive stream-wise inference, where visual streams
terminate and are cached at different stages to reduce unnecessary world-model computation.
We evaluate HiLo-WAM in simulation and real-robot tasks. HiLo-WAM achieves state-of-the-art
performance on RoboTwin 2.0 Chen et al. (2025) and strong performance on challenging long-
horizon manipulation tasks Chen et al. (2026). Controlled ablations and attention analyses show
that long-horizon stream with historical context, future evolution, and dense local visual dynamics
provide complementary information for action generation. Temporal-scale analyses further demon-
strate that long-horizon world modeling benefits from an appropriate temporal scale that balances
temporal coverage and resolution. Progressive inference further achieves a 2.93× speedup while
improving performance.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose HiLo-WAM, a World Action Model that decouples visual world modeling
into complementary long-horizon and local dynamics processes, and jointly exploits both
for robot action generation.
• We introduce a multi-scale temporal modeling strategy with structured cross-stream inter-
action and progressive stream-wise inference, enabling effective coordination across tem-
poral scales while reducing unnecessary world-model computation.
• Extensive simulation and real-world experiments demonstrate strong long-horizon manipu-
lation performance, with ablations and further analyses validate the benefit of complemen-
tary temporal information, the importance of balancing temporal coverage and resolution,
and the efficiency of progressive inference.

RELATED WORKS
Video Models and World Action Models. Recent advances in video generation have enabled visual
prediction to serve as a powerful dynamics prior for robot learning Black et al. (2026); Wang et al.
(2026); Hu et al. (2024). Prior works use video models to generate future observations for planning
and control Hu et al. (2024); Du et al. (2023); Li et al. (2026), or jointly model future visual states
and robot actions within a unified World Action Model (WAM) Yuan et al. (2026); Li et al. (2025a);
Ma et al. (2026); Ye et al. (2026). While early WAMs mainly focus on short-horizon interaction
dynamics, recent approaches incorporate longer context, persistent memory, or long-range future
prediction and visual planning Ye et al. (2026); Yang et al. (2026a); Cai et al. (2026); Zhou et al.
(2025); Team et al. (2026) to support more complex long-horizon manipulation. HiLo-WAM instead
decouples long-horizon manipulation into two complementary visual streams: a long-horizon stream
for long-horizon task evolution and a local stream for fine-grained interaction dynamics.
Hierarchical Policies for Long-Horizon Manipulation. A parallel line of work addresses long-
horizon manipulation through hierarchical or temporally abstract policies. These methods typically
use high-level language plans, subgoals, visual goals, or intermediate states to capture task-level
structure, and condition a downstream policy on these representations to generate fine-grained ac-
tions Shi et al. (2025); Li et al. (2025b); Long et al. (2026); Black et al. (2025); Yang et al. (2026b);
Intelligence et al. (2026); Zhao et al. (2025); Hu et al. (2026). HiLo-WAM shares the motivation of
combining long-range reasoning with precise local control, but approaches it through visual world
modeling rather than a separate planner–policy hierarchy. It decouples the visual dynamics of a
unified WAM into long-horizon and local streams and jointly learns their interaction with robot
actions.

METHOD
Overview.
We introduce HiLo-WAM, a World Action Model that decouples visual world mod-
eling into two complementary visual streams: a long-horizon visual stream which sparsely models
historical task context and coarse future evolution for task-level evolution and a dense local visual
dynamics stream for fine-grained interaction dynamics. HiLo-WAM adopts a multi-scale temporal
modeling strategy, representing the two visual streams at different temporal scales. As illustrated
in Fig. 2, the model follows a dual-DiT architecture consisting of a Video DiT for visual world
modeling and an Action DiT for action stream generation. Both visual streams are jointly modeled
by the shared Video DiT and interact with action stream through structured cross-stream attention,
allowing long-horizon task evolution and local dynamics to jointly guide action prediction. During
inference, all streams are progressively denoised: the long-horizon visual stream terminates and is
cached first, followed by the local visual stream, while the action stream is refined to completion.
3.1
PROBLEM FORMULATION
At control step t, the robot observes an image ot, a proprioceptive state st, and a language in-
struction l, and predicts an action chunk At = (at, . . . , at+H−1). Conventional World Action
Models (WAMs) augment action prediction with predictive visual dynamics by jointly model-
ing pθ(Vt, At | ot, st, l), where Vt = (ot+1, . . . , ot+T ) denotes a future visual trajectory. For
long-horizon manipulation, this formulation is extended by incorporating a longer visual history
Ot = (ot−M, . . . , ot) and a longer future trajectory Vt = (ot+1, . . . , ot+T ) to provide richer histor-
ical context and capture task evolution over a longer temporal span, yielding pθ(Vt, At | Ot, st, l),
where M and T control the historical and predictive temporal horizons, respectively.

3.5
EFFICIENT PROGRESSIVE INFERENCE
Introducing multi-scale visual streams adds denoising computation and increases inference latency.
To reduce this overhead while maintaining manipulation performance, we combine three comple-
mentary strategies: partial denoising, joint denoising, and dynamic step skipping.
Partial denoising. Useful dynamics information can emerge before visual predictions are fully
denoised Hu et al. (2024); Li et al. (2026). We assign denoising budgets nH, nL, and nA to the long-
horizon, local dynamics, and action streams, respectively, with nH ≤nL ≤nA. Each visual stream
terminates upon reaching its budget and caches its features, while the action stream completes its full
denoising schedule. The structured asymmetric attention naturally supports this scheme, allowing
downstream streams to reuse cached upstream representations.
Joint denoising. To better align representations across denoising stages and further accelerate in-
ference, we start denoising all streams simultaneously. During early denoising, action and visual
representations interact at the same denoising timestep through structured cross-stream attention.
During later denoising, the action stream uses cached visual representations that capture informative
dynamics to refine its predictions and generate precise actions.
Dynamic skipping. Following DreamZero Ye et al. (2026), we exploit temporal redundancy across
denoising steps. When consecutive predictions vt and vt−1 at denoising step t have cosine similarity

EXPERIMENTS
We conduct experiments in simulation and on a real-world Fourier GR-3 robot to investigate three
questions: (1) Does HiLo-WAM improve long-horizon manipulation performance compared with
existing VLA and World Action Models? (2) How does visual dynamics at different temporal scales
contribute to action generation? (3) How does the temporal scale affect manipulation performance?
(4) Can progressive inference improve inference efficiency while maintaining performance?
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
RoboTwin 2.0.
RoboTwin 2.0 is a large-scale bimanual manipulation benchmark containing 50
dual-arm tasks under both clean and domain-randomized evaluation (Chen et al., 2025). We follow
the multi-task protocol of Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026): the training set contains 50 clean demon-
strations and 500 randomized demonstrations per task, totaling 2,500 clean and 25,000 randomized
trajectories. We evaluate each task over 50 trials in both settings and report success rate averaged
across all 50 tasks. The randomized setting varies scene clutter, illumination, background texture,
tabletop configuration, and other visual or physical factors.
RoboDojo Benchmark.
RoboDojo Chen et al. (2026) is a general robotic manipulation bench-
mark consisting of 42 tasks spanning five evaluation dimensions. We follow the official training
and evaluation setup and train our model on 3,500 demonstration episodes. To assess long-horizon
manipulation performance, we evaluate on eight long-horizon tasks in RoboDojo, with 50 trials per
task. We report both task progress scores and success rates 1.
Real-world platform and tasks.
We deploy policies on a Fourier GR-3 humanoid robot. We con-
sider three long-horizon bimanual tasks: Build House, Disassemble House, Disassemble Heart. The
assembly tasks require the policy to maintain a target structure over multiple placements, whereas
the disassembly tasks require progress tracking while reversing the structure into its constituent
blocks. We collect 50 hours of teleoperated demonstrations in total and train each model for five
epochs on the same task-specific data.
Implementation details.
HiLo-WAM is initialized from the same pretrained video backbone
(Wan et al., 2025) used by the closest WAM baseline (Yuan et al., 2026). We use logit-normal flow-
time sampling, AdamW with learning rate 1 × 10−4 and weight decay 0.01, a cosine learning-rate
schedule, mixed-precision training, and gradient clipping at 1.0 (Yuan et al., 2026). We use 10 action
denoising steps with classifier-free guidance scale 1.0. The temporal configuration (k, m, n, T, H),
stream-specific stopping steps (nH, nL, nA), loss weights are reported in the appendix A.

action generation to account for both long-horizon task progression and local dynamics.

explicitly models the dependencies among task evolution, local dynamics, and action generation,

8 reduces performance to 92.8%, as the visual observations become too sparse to preserve sufficient
temporal information. These results indicate that long-horizon world modeling benefits from an
appropriate temporal scale that balances temporal coverage and temporal resolution, with k = 4
achieving the best trade-off in our experiments.

CONCLUSION
We introduced HiLo-WAM, a World Action Model that separates long-horizon task evolution from
fine-grained local interaction dynamics. HiLo-WAM models these two complementary processes at
different temporal scales and coordinates them through structured cross-stream interaction for action
generation. Progressive stream-wise denoising further limits the additional inference cost introduced
by long-horizon visual modeling. Experiments in simulation and on a physical robot demonstrate
strong long-horizon manipulation performance. Ablations show that long-horizon and local visual
information contribute differently to action generation, while temporal-scale analysis highlights the
importance of balancing temporal coverage and resolution. These results support decoupled multi-
scale visual world modeling as an effective direction for long-horizon robotic manipulation.
Limitations.
HiLo-WAM improves long-horizon manipulation by modeling visual dynamics at
different temporal scales within the World Action Model. However, it remains limited by the seman-
tic understanding and predictive capability of the underlying video model. Complex long-horizon
tasks that require explicit subtask decomposition, progress estimation, or higher-level reasoning
may therefore remain challenging. Future work could incorporate stronger semantic models, such
as VLMs, to enhance task understanding, reasoning, and generalization.

is assigned a progress score ranging from 0 to 10. The score is accumulated according to the com-
pletion of predefined intermediate stages, allowing us to measure partial task progress in addition to
binary task success. A score of 10 corresponds to full task completion. The detailed scoring criteria
are described below.
Build House.
The robot is instructed to arrange four orange blocks into a straight line, connect a
brown triangular block to the assembled line, lift the complete structure, and finally place it back on
the table. The total score is divided into four stages:
• Forming the block line (4 points). Up to 4 points are awarded according to the completion
and quality of the straight-line arrangement of the four orange blocks. Correctly connected
and aligned blocks receive full credit, while connected but visibly bent arrangements re-
ceive partial credit.

• Connecting the triangular block (2 points). One point is awarded when the triangular
block is moved to the designated end of the line with the correct orientation, and an addi-
tional point is awarded once a stable connection is established.
• Lifting the assembled structure (2 points). One point is awarded for establishing a con-
trolled grasp on the assembled structure. The remaining point is awarded when the com-
plete structure is lifted from the table and remains intact during the lift.
• Replacing the structure (2 points). One point is awarded when the robot places the struc-
ture back on the table and releases it in a controlled manner. An additional point is awarded
if the structure remains connected and stable for at least two seconds after release.
Disassemble House.
The robot first grasps the orange block, transfers it between the two hands,
disassembles the connected structure into individual components, and places the separated pieces
into the designated left and right regions of the table. The scoring criteria are:
• Disassembling the structure (6 points). The score is assigned proportionally to the num-
ber of required connections that are successfully separated. Full separation of all compo-
nents yields 6 points. For example, when three connections must be broken, each success-
fully separated connection contributes 2 points.
• Placement into left and right regions (2 points). One point is awarded when both sides
contain at least one separated block, and the second point is awarded when all components
are placed inside their predefined target regions.
• Final state (2 points).
Full credit is awarded when all components remain mutually
separated and stationary for at least two seconds, with no block leaving the designated
workspace.
Disassemble Heart.
The robot is required to first remove the two yellow blocks from the red
interlocking structure and place them on the table, and then disassemble the remaining red structure
into individual components. The scoring criteria are:
• Removing the yellow blocks (2 points). Each yellow block contributes 1 point when
it is successfully removed and stably placed on the table. A block that is removed but
unintentionally dropped receives partial credit of 0.5 points.
• Disassembling the red structure (6 points). The score is proportional to the number of
required connections that are successfully separated. Completely separating all red blocks
yields 6 points. For example, when three connections are present, each separated connec-
tion contributes 2 points.
• Final state (2 points). Full credit is awarded when the robot has released the objects and
all yellow and red blocks remain stable for at least two seconds without any object leaving
the workspace.
