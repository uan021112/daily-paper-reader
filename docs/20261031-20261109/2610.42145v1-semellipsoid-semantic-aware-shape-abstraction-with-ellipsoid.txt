Representing 3D shapes using a set of primitives–known as shape abstraction–
is an actively studied problem in computer graphics. with wide applications
in shape understanding, analysis, and simulation due to the compactness
and computational efficiency of the primitive-based representation. Prior
work has primarily focused on geometry-aware shape abstraction, where the
objective is to approximate the input shape with a minimal set of primitives
while preserving geometric fidelity. However, these methods often overlook
semantic consistency, inevitably leading to primitives that span across mul-
tiple semantic parts. Such inconsistency can cause undesirable artifacts in
downstream tasks such as part-level shape editing or manipulation. In this
paper, we introduce a new task - semantic-aware shape abstraction, where the
goal is to produce abstractions that are both geometrically faithful and se-
mantically consistent, ensuring that each primitive adheres strictly to a single
semantic part without crossing semantic boundaries. We propose SemEllip-
soid, a novel method to address this problem using ellipsoidal primitives. The
core idea is to inject semantic constraints into the primitive optimization
process leveraging the availability of semantic cues from large-scale founda-
tion models. We design a fully differentiable projection–blending–evaluation
scheme to measure semantic divergence against input semantic masks, and
formulate an alternating optimization strategy to iteratively solve for op-
timal semantic assignment and primitive configurations. Our method is
non-learning-based, requiring no data collection or network training, and
generalizes well to shapes from arbitrary categories. It also supports different
levels of abstraction according to user demand. Extensive experiments and
evaluations validate the effectiveness of our approach across several 3D
shape collections.

Introduction
Humans perceive real-world objects by effortlessly recognizing their
structure by parts and part semantics, serving as important cues for
many purposes, like human-object interaction and object function-
ality analysis. The structure can be represented as a composition
of simple primitives (e.g., spheres, ellipsoids, boxes) with clear se-
mantics. The decomposition and abstraction process, also known
as Shape Abstraction, has been an active research field for decades
in computer graphics. The abstract shape, its structures, and part
semantics play an essential role in downstream tasks, such as shape
geometry, structure and functionality analysis [Hu et al. 2018; Mitra
et al. 2014], shape editing [Yumer et al. 2015], shape retrieval and
manipulation [Sun et al. 2019], object grasping [Vezzani et al. 2017,
2018], and motion planning [Ruan et al. 2022].
Existing research solves this problem from different aspects -
computational optimization [Chevalier et al. 2003; Vaskevicius and
Birk 2017], deep learning [Sun et al. 2019; Tulsiani et al. 2017; Zhao
et al. 2024], to name a few. More recent work (e.g., PrimitiveAny-
thing [Ye et al. 2025], PartGen [Chen et al. 2025a]) reformulates
this task as shape-conditioned primitive generation. Their training
is performed on a human-annotated large-scale dataset, improv-
ing the generalization ability greatly. The primary focus of prior
work is geometry-aware shape abstraction, where the objective is
to approximate the input shape with a minimal set of primitives
while preserving geometric fidelity. However, these methods often
overlook semantic consistency, inevitably leading to primitives that

In this paper, we propose a new task–semantic-aware shape ab-
straction, where the goal is to produce abstractions that are both
geometrically faithful and semantically consistent, ensuring that
each primitive adheres strictly to a single semantic part without
crossing semantic boundaries (see Fig. 2(c)). Two core challenges ex-
ist to achieve this goal: i) where to obtain the semantic information,
and ii) how to inject the semantic cues during shape abstraction. We
draw inspiration from the rapid advancement of large foundation
models. For example, Segment Anything Model (SAM) [Kirillov
et al. 2023] and DINO [Zhang et al. 2022] can produce part seman-
tics robustly and accurately, making semantic information readily
accessible in a category-agnostic and open-vocabulary manner.
Based on this insight, we present a novel approach, dubbed SemEl-
lipsoid, for semantic-aware shape abstraction with ellipsoids. Our
approach falls into the category of geometrical optimization-based
shape abstraction, and we achieve semantic injection by formulating
it as a semantic penalty loss term in the optimization. Specifically,
given an input 3D shape together with multi-view semantic masks,
we first initialize a set of ellipsoid primitives to approximate the
input. In the following optimization, we aim to simultaneously find
the optimal primitive parameters and semantic labels, such that the
primitives resemble the shape with high geometric fidelity while
strictly adhering to semantic constraints.
To measure the semantic discrepancy, we choose to project the el-
lipsoid to the semantic masks. However, primitives inside the shape
volume occlude and overlap with each other. Naive projection will
simply disable the optimization for inside primitives. Inspired by
the alpha blending operator in differentiable rendering and shape
optimization [Laine et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2019; Yuan et al. 2024], we
propose an innovative differentiable projection-blending-evaluation
scheme to penalize the semantic violation. Given the non-linear
and non-convex nature of the optimization due to the integer vari-
ables of labels and continuous variables of primitives, we solve it
iteratively in an alternating way, i.e., label assignment and primitive
optimization. After the optimization, an optional post-merging step
can further group primitives possessing the same semantic label,
producing multi-level abstractions according to user intentions.
Our method significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods,
and extensive experiments, ablation studies, and discussions validate
our design choices, effectiveness, and scalability. To summarize, the
principal contributions of this work include:
• We propose semantic-aware shape abstraction, a new task
for both geometrically faithful and semantically consistent
shape abstraction.
• We propose the first approach-SemEllipsoid, which is sim-
ple, neat, and effective for solving this new problem. Our
approach can handle various shape categories without any
label-intensive data processing and network training, and
it can produce multi-level shape abstractions, facilitating
various downstream tasks.
• Our projection-blending-evaluation technical design effec-
tively injects semantic cues in a differentiable way, and might
inspire future research.

Related Works
Shape abstraction. The goal of traditional shape abstraction is
to represent a 3D shape using the smallest possible set of simple
primitives while preserving the closest possible approximation of the
original geometry. Computational approaches typically frame this as
a problem of fitting primitives to the input geometry [Chevalier et al.
2003; Gross and Boult 1988; Leonardis et al. 1997; Park et al. 2025;
Solina and Bajcsy 1990; Vaskevicius and Birk 2017] or estimating
primitive parameters using Bayesian methods [Liu et al. 2022, 2023a;
Wu et al. 2022a]. Superquadrics are widely used as primitives in
computational methods. It encodes common primitives like spheres,
cuboids, and cylinders with only five parameters, resulting in strong
expressiveness and desirable analytical properties. Another group
of work focuses on learning-based approaches. Methods from [Chen
et al. 2020; Deng et al. 2020; Fedele et al. 2025; Hao et al. 2020; Sun
et al. 2019; Tulsiani et al. 2017; Yang and Chen 2021; Yu et al. 2024;
Zhao et al. 2024] address the task in an unsupervised setting, aiming
to learn an optimal abstraction strategy for shapes within a specific
category. The core objective of both the computational and learning-
based methods is to approximate the shape in high fidelity, not
necessarily producing abstractions with human-intuitive semantics.
Part-based 3D shape generation. Part-based 3D shape generation
is an emerging topic, with concurrent works like PartGen [Chen et al.
2025a], AutoPartGen [Chen et al. 2025b], PrimitiveAnything [Ye
et al. 2025], PartCrafter [Ng et al. 2024], PartPacker [Tang et al.
2025], LLM-Primitives [Tian et al. 2025], and OmniPart [Yang et al.
2025]. These methods can directly generate semantically meaningful
parts conditioned on image or text inputs. However, on the one
hand, since they do not enforce semantic-awareness, their parts
might overlap, leading to semantic violation. On the other hand,
their generalization depends on certain datasets. In contrast, we

3D Shape part segmentation. A branch of methods has been de-
veloped to decompose a 3D shape into semantically meaningful
and geometrically coherent parts. Co-segmentation methods take
as input a family of shapes within a category and produce their
part segmentations simultaneously [Chen et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2012;
Huang et al. 2011; Paschalidou et al. 2021; Shu et al. 2016]. Our
goal is way different from co-segmentation, where geometrically
similar parts are detected from a group of object instances within
the same category. Their resulting segments are not necessarily
coincident with human-interpretable semantics, and these methods
need to work on a group of objects, limiting their applicability (see
our comparison). Unlike co-segmentation methods that are typi-
cally unsupervised, 3D semantic segmentation methods, like [Yu
et al. 2019], train deep neural networks with labelled 3D datasets for
shape segmentation. Pre-trained 2D segmentation models can also
be utilized for 3D segmentation, as demonstrated by PartSLIP [Liu
et al. 2023b], PartSLIP++ [Zhou et al. 2023], SAMPart3D [Yang et al.
2024] and SAMesh [Tang et al. 2024]. Additionally, PartField [Liu
et al. 2025] learns a continuous feature field, where the similarity
between features governs the partition of parts.

Overview
Problem definition. We introduce the task of semantic-aware shape
abstraction, formally defined as follows: Given an input watertight
3D shape S, a semantic guidance signal g, and a set of geometric
primitives {pbox, pellips, psphere, . . .}, the objective is to generate an
abstracted shape S𝑎composed of 𝑁primitives that faithfully re-
construct the structure of S while respecting semantic constraints,
defined as:
S𝑎= 𝝍(S, g, {pbox, pellips, psphere, . . .}).
(1)
Specifically, each primitive in S𝑎must be assigned to a single, unique
semantic region indicated by g, with no primitive spanning across
different semantic labels (i.e., no cross-semantic placement). See
Fig. 2(c) for an illustrative example. The abstraction may consist
of primitives of a single type or a mixture thereof. The semantic
guidance g can be derived from 2D semantic masks, or from 3D per-
vertex or per-face semantic annotations, either manually provided
or sourced from existing datasets.
Our setting and solution. In this paper, we instantiate this problem
with two modifications: i) only the ellipsoid pellips is used as the
operational primitive, due to its flexible analytical expression for
differentiability along with adequate representational ability; ii) the
semantic guidance signal g is a set of multi-view 2D semantic masks.
We assume that all views have the same level of granularity in their
segmentation with a known semantic label set of L𝑎labels (e.g., leg,
back, armrest, seat for a chair).

4
Proposed Method
Figure 3 displays an overview of our approach. In what follows, we
first introduce the pre-processing, the two optimization terms in
Eq. 3, followed by the optimization and post-processing.
Pre-processing. Given the input shape S, we begin by evenly sam-
pling 100𝑘3D points within its volume. We then apply Gaussian
Mixture Model (GMM) clustering with cluster number 𝐾to group
these points into coarse ellipsoidal clusters that respect the underly-
ing geometry. To better handle thin structures (e.g., thin table legs),
we additionally evaluate each cluster by computing the ratio be-
tween its maximum and minimum axis lengths. If the ratio exceeds
a threshold, indicating a highly elongated shape, we recursively
apply GMM with cluster number 2 to subdivide the cluster until the
ratio criterion is satisfied. We finally estimate a single ellipsoid for
each cluster. This process is efficient (∼10s) and yields the initial set
of 𝑁ellipsoids for the subsequent optimization.

In the next round, the previous primitive configuration serves

Post-merging. Similar to existing shape abstraction methods such
as Marching-Primitives, the primitive numbers in our method is
controlled implicitly through the number of initial clusters and the
compactness loss weight 𝜇, rather than constrained to a budget.
To recover the shape without much geometry sacrifice, we use 𝑁
around 40∼120. In the post-processing step, we provide a stable way
to further reduce the primitive numbers while strictly conforming
to the semantic labels obtained from the optimization. Briefly, it
contains three steps. Given the 𝑁primitives with their unique labels,
at step one, we detect connected components within each semantic
label. For example, four connected components can be found for the
four legs of a chair. At step two, within each connected component,
we recursively merge primitives into a bigger cluster if the volume
of the oriented bounding box (OBB) of their merged shape is be-
low a threshold ratio (i.e., 2.0) compared against the sum of their
volumes. At step three, we collect all the merged clusters, initial-
ize an ellipsoid for each of them, and run a few (∼500) primitive
parameter optimization steps (i.e., Step II) adhering to their unique
labels. Note that the default ratio threshold can lead to around 3x
primitive reduction (Fig. 4), and by tuning the threshold, we can
achieve multi-level abstractions.

5
Results and Evaluations
With SemEllipsoid, we produce high-quality shape abstractions
across many shape collections, as shown in Figs. 1 and 5. Pay at-
tention to Fig. 1, our method can generate multi-level abstractions
by tuning the threshold ratio in the post-merging step. End users
can tune this parameter depending on their purposes. For example,
when targeting the animation application, a bigger threshold and
a few semantic masks separating the upper and lower limbs can
be employed to facilitate the part animation. Extensive comparison
(Sec. 5.1), ablation studies (Sec. 5.2), discussions, and applications
(Sec. 5.4) validate the effectiveness and the technical choices of our
approach.
Implementation details and runtime. We implemented our method
in Python using the PyTorch [Ansel et al. 2024] framework. We
use 𝐾= 20 ∼60 for GMM clustering, leading to 40 ∼120 initial
primitives depending on the recursive GMM steps. The view number
𝑀= 9, and the views are uniformly sampled from three circles
(elevation = 0°, 45°, and -45°) at 120° intervals on the unit sphere.
The axis ratio threshold is 10.0 in the primitive initialization. For
𝜂and 𝜓in the geometric term, we adopt an exponential schedule,
growing from 40 to 600 and from 5 to 40, respectively. We sample
|Ω𝑠| = 16, 000 3D points within the input shape’s bounding box.
For the semantic loss, we sample 8000 2D points per view, the
temperature coefficient 𝛽is empirically set to 0.01, the balancing
weight 𝜆is initially set to 10.0 and then exponentially grows to
200.0, and the compactness weight 𝜇is initially set to 0.01 and then
exponentially grows to 0.1, For continuous optimization, we use
the RAdam optimizer [Liu et al. 2020]. For integer optimization, we
use SciPy [Virtanen et al. 2020], which wraps the HiGHS library
[Huangfu and Hall 2018]. Each shape is optimized for 80 alternating
steps using a learning rate of 0.005 with linear warmup and cosine
decay.
On average, the optimization of a single shape takes ∼1min on
a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX™4090 GPU and requires ∼2GB
VRAM. The same hyperparameter setting is employed for all shape
categories.

and scissors (88 shapes) from PartNet [Mo et al. 2019], human (6000
shapes) from DFAUST [Bogo et al. 2017], animal (2,500 shapes)
from Animal3D [Xu et al. 2023] and Faucet (153 shapes) from

semantic violation at the primitive, part, and shape levels, dubbed

metrics are defined in the following way. We uniformly sample
𝐾= 2, 000 points from each primitive and assign a label to every
point by finding its closest point on the shape surface. Intuitively,
a primitive is semantically consistent if all the 𝐾points share the
same label. We thus define clusters as groups of points sharing the
same label, and calculate the point percentage of the biggest cluster
out of 𝐾samples. We evaluate this metric under different levels
(Q%), if the semantically consistent points percentage is below 𝑄%,
we mark it as inconsistent under level Q. Then, SV𝑝-Q% is calculated
as the ratio of the inconsistent primitives within an abstraction, and
we report the average SV𝑝-Q% across all abstractions. At the part
level, a semantic part is marked as inconsistent if any primitive
assigned to it is inconsistent; SV𝑐-𝑄% is computed as the fraction of
inconsistent semantic parts. We report the average SV𝑐-Q% across
all abstractions. At the shape level, an abstraction is marked as in-
consistent if it contains at least one inconsistent primitive; SV𝑠-𝑄%
is computed as the fraction of inconsistent abstractions. We use
Q=80, 90, 95 in our experiments.

Comparison
Competitors. There lacks established semantic-aware methods
for shape abstraction, we thus developed a few baseline methods
and adapted existing methods to accept semantic guidance. The two
sets of competitors are:
1) Segmentation-abstraction methods. We design the baseline
methods by segmenting the input shape into parts, and applying
non-semantic-aware shape abstraction methods to the segments.
Specifically,
• We implement SA-PF, which obtains volumetric part seg-
mentation by running PartField [Liu et al. 2025] on points
uniformly sampled in space, followed by K-Means clustering.
Then we run Marching-Primitives [Liu et al. 2023a] (denoted
as MPS) directly within every segmented region.
• We implement SA-CH, which directly loads ground-truth 3D
segmentation on mesh. Then, we convert each mesh segment
into a closed surface by first using the meshing_close_-
holes filter provided by MeshLab [Cignoni et al. 2008] and
then remesh using marching cubes. Having the processed
parts, MPS is applied to each part for abstraction.
• We implement SA-EMS, which directly consumes the non-
watertight mesh segments. After loading the ground truth
3D segmentation mesh, we sample open surfaces into point
clouds and run EMS [Liu et al. 2022] for every part.
2) Part-based generation. OmniPart [Yang et al. 2025] (denoted
as OP) and AutoPartGen [Chen et al. 2025b] (denoted as APG) are
part-based generation methods that support mask inputs as ex-
plicit semantic guidance. Therefore we also include them as fully
semantic-aware methods and make ground-truth masks available
for them. Following their original setup, we provide single-view
renderings and corresponding ground-truth masks as input. Since

5.3
Discussion
Comparison to non semantic-aware methods. We also compare the
performance of our method against non-semantic-aware methods
in Tab. 4. These methods do not accept semantic annotations or
guidance by design, thus the focus of the comparison is more about
the abstraction quality.
We consider shape abstraction, co-segmentation and general part-
based generation methods:
1) Shape abstraction. Considering the nature of the method, prim-
itives, and semantics, we selected three state-of-the-art methods as
our competitors.
• [Yang and Chen 2021] is a learning-based shape abstraction
method (denoted as AVS), which uses the cuboid as the target
primitive and jointly predicts primitive parameters and co-
segmentation. For a fair comparison, we train AVS using the
training set, and conduct all comparisons both statistically
and visually on the test set.
• Marching-Primitives [Liu et al. 2023a] (denoted as MPS) uses
a non-learning-based geometric optimization to solve for
the superquadric primitive parameters without considering
semantics. We use the default hyperparameters of MPS in
the comparison.
• SweepNet [Zhao et al. 2024] (denoted as SNet) extracts
sweep-surface primitives with B-spline sweep axes and su-
perellipse profiles, using a per-shape learned network to
differentiably approximate the occupancy field.
2) Co-segmentation. The co-segmentation task implies semantic
consistency to some extent. We chose Neural Parts [Paschalidou
et al. 2021] and DAE-Net [Chen et al. 2024] (denoted as NP and DAE,
respectively). We set the number of parts to 6 for human category
and 7 for animal category, and use the number suggested by PartNet
for all other categories. Scissors and Faucet have fewer than 200
training samples. We gather extra training samples within the same
category from ShapeNet [Chang et al. 2015] and Objaverse [Deitke
et al. 2022] to avoid performance degradation due to insufficient
training data.
3) Part-based generation. Based on the availability of the code,
we chose PartCrafter [Ng et al. 2024] (denoted as PC) and Part-
Packer [Tang et al. 2025] (denoted as PP). For PartCrafter, we use
the same number of parts as in the co-segmentation setting, while
PartPacker does not accept a specific number of parts. Their evalua-
tion protocol is the same to APG and OP except that no ground-truth
masks are given.
As expected, the semantic metrics are high for all non-semantic-
aware baselines, indicating that they do not respect boundary be-
tween semantic parts. PP and PC suffer from unstable semantic gran-
ularity: while in some cases the segmentation aligns with ground-
truth labels, they more frequently produce either over-segmented or
under-segmented parts. AVS and MPS demonstrate similar seman-
tic awareness. At the primitive level, MPS obtains similar metrics
compared to AVS, while its shape-level metrics are a little bit better.
SNet exhibit poor semantic awareness on most categories while
using the least primitives. We found SNet tends to produce sweep
surfaces with significant overlap when the medial axes are not clear.
Considering the geometric fidelity, MPS wins most of the category,
while our 𝐼𝑜𝑈drops around 10% compared with it. The mild geom-
etry sacrifice, however, trades off much better semantic awareness.
Part-level generation methods PP and PC generally exhibit poor
geometric consistency caused by single-view input, which is the
same situation as in OP and APG.
Our method generally use more primitives than traditional shape
abstraction methods. However, the main purpose of putting more
primitives is to respect the semantic boundaries instead of improve
geometric fidelity, which is supported by the ablation on compact-
ness loss weight 𝜇in Tab. 2.
Robustness to noisy masks. Our method relies on 2D semantic
masks obtained through interactive annotation with pretrained
large vision models. In practice, inaccuracies may arise either from
incorrect user prompts during annotation or from imperfect mask
predictions by the vision models themselves. We therefore evaluate
the robustness of our method to mask errors by manually injecting
three types of perturbations into the ground-truth semantic masks.
The first perturbation simulates over-segmented semantic parts.
Specifically, one randomly selected part in each semantic mask is
dilated by 10 pixels, causing it to occlude neighboring parts and
increasing its area by approximately 20% ∼50%. The second per-
turbation simulates under-segmented semantic parts, where one
randomly selected part is eroded by 10 pixels, reducing its area
by approximately 20% ∼50%. The third perturbation simulates
mislabeled parts, where a smaller part (< 500px) is merged into a
neighboring part with a different semantic label.
As shown in Tab. 3, all three types of mask errors have only a
limited impact on both the geometric quality and semantic consis-
tency of the resulting abstractions. This suggests that our method
is reasonably robust to moderate inaccuracies in the input semantic
masks.
More primitives. Other than ellipsoids, our method can be adapted
to support other primitives. Specifically, our formulation relies on
the differentiable evaluation of three geometric quantities – the
3D SDF 𝑑, the 2D SDF of the silhouette 𝑑2𝑑, and the depth func-
tion 𝑧. For simple primitives such as spheres and ellipsoids, these
functions admit closed-form expressions or good approximations.
For more general primitive families, however, there are no closed-
form formulations. To overcome this limitation, we instead adopt a
support-function-based approach, in which the required quantities
are recovered implicitly by solving small optimization problems.

Failure cases. As shown in Fig. 10, two issues remain for our
method. Firstly, the thin structure is challenging for our method,
especially when the number of primitives is low (Fig. 10(a)). We
initialize primitives by clustering points sampled uniformly inside
the shape volume, leading to fewer primitives inside thin structures
due to their small volumes. Adding extra primitives can mitigate
this issue to some extent, but it is hard to determine the optimal
number. An additional structural analysis might help. Secondly, se-
mantic masks obtained from user annotations might contain noise
(e.g., around semantic boundaries) and errors. Our method is some-
what robust to semantic noise, but it cannot correct errors in se-
mantic masks. Wrong masks yield degenerated geometry (e.g., with
holes) due to the semantic conflicts (e.g., the wing in Fig. 10(b)).
5.4
Applications
Shape Editing. Our semantic-aware abstraction serves as a set
of intuitive handles for shape editing. Given a surface S and its
abstraction p, each vertex 𝑣𝑗∈S is associated with its nearest prim-
itive p𝑖. Transformations applied to p𝑖are accordingly propagated
to all vertices linked to that primitive. Directly applying the same
transformations to vertices, however, leads to discontinuities at part
boundaries. To achieve smooth transitions, we introduce a blending
scheme in which each vertex is influenced by its associated primi-
tive with a control weight defined as 𝑒−𝑑(𝑣𝑗,p𝑖), where 𝑑(·) denotes
the vertex–primitive distance. This weighting ensures gradual de-
formation across adjacent parts. Our framework supports not only
standard affine transformations, but also non-linear operations such
as tapering, bending, and twisting [Barr 1984].
In Fig. 11 (a), we compare our method with three baselines: PF,
MPS, and DAE. For MPS, which employs superquadrics, we use the
same editing pipeline as ours, since both methods support identi-
cal deformation operations. For PP and DAE, whose primitives are
represented as neural implicit surfaces, we uniformly sample 100𝑘
points from each primitive surface and apply the transformations
on the densely sampled point clouds. For bending operations, the
bending axes are estimated from each primitive’s bounding box.
We apply simple affine transformations by rotating the chair back
while simultaneously stretching the chair legs in the first exam-
ple. All four methods roughly achieve the intended edit. However,
the results from MPS and PF exhibit noticeable defects around the
joint between the chair back and seat, caused by overlapping primi-
tives in that region. In DAE, the seat is unintentionally stretched
together with the legs, since both are assigned to the same part. In
the second example, the task is to bend outward only the supporting
legs between the two table tops (highlighted in green). Our method
achieves this successfully thanks to the semantically clean abstrac-
tion, while all baselines fail. PF groups the two legs into a single
part, preventing them from bending in different directions. Both
MPS and DAE fail to distinguish between the legs connecting the
table tops and those at the bottom, leading to deformations beyond
desired region.
Shape Mixing. Beyond individual part manipulation, our abstrac-
tion enables assembling new shapes by recombining semantically
consistent parts. We implement a Part Extraction operation: the
surface is segmented according to primitive assignments, and the
resulting labels allow selective retention of desired parts. We post-
process the resulting mesh by closing small holes. The extracted
parts then work as building blocks of new mixed shapes.
In Fig. 11 (b), the task is to extract the chair back (highlighted
in blue) and place it onto a new chair base. Our method accurately
isolates the chair back, whereas all three baselines yield significantly
incomplete or fragmented extractions.

Shape Skeleton Extraction. With semantically-aligned shape ab-
straction, SemEllipsoid enables a novel task: direct skeleton extrac-
tion with geometric abstraction. Given a raw mesh as input, we
extract semantic-aware abstractions using SemEllipsoid. From the
abstracted shape, we construct a connectivity graph based on prim-
itive intersections. Specifically, we define a joint as the intersection
between two groups of primitives with different semantic labels. For
end primitives that intersect with only one other primitive, we add
a joint at the farthest point from the intersection point to represent
the endpoint. With the joints established, we apply a simple rule to
determine connectivity: for each semantic group of primitives, if
there are only two joints, we directly connect them. If more than
two joints exist, we add an additional joint at the geometric center
of all existing joints and connect each previous joint to this new
center joint. As shown in Fig. 11 (c), our method produces high-
quality skeletons for diverse shape structures, as the abstractions
align semantically with human intuition. In contrast, SegAbs fails
due to severe semantic violations in its abstracted primitives.
6
Conclusion and Future Work
We have presented a new task-semantic-aware shape abstraction,
and a novel approach-SemEllipsoid to solve it efficiently. We exploit
large foundation models to obtain the part semantic masks, and
inject them into a unified semantic and primitive optimization to
produce the desired abstract shape, approximating the geometry
faithfully and adhering to the semantic cues strictly. A further post-
merging step groups primitives within a semantic cluster, producing
multi-level abstractions. Extensive experiments demonstrate our
method’s superior performance.
Limitations and Future Work.
(1) Dynamic primitive addition or removal. In our setting, we
use a fixed number of primitives in the optimization, and
the number is set empirically. In the future, we would like to
study dynamically adding or removing primitives alongside
the optimization. A potential solution could be initializing
a large number of primitives and assigning an extra inte-
ger variable indicating their existence, and simultaneously
optimizing primitive parameters, semantics, and existence.

(2) Technically, we choose to project primitives onto semantic
masks to measure the semantic penalty. However, unpro-
jecting semantic masks onto 3D primitives and penalizing
semantic discrepancy in 3D space is potentially an promising
alternative. We would like to explore it in the future.
(3) Abstraction with interior analysis. Shape abstraction is highly
coupled with other shape analysis tasks, e.g., interior struc-
ture analysis. These tasks are mutually beneficial to each
other. In the future, we would like to combine shape abstrac-
tion with interior structure (e.g., skeleton or medial axis)
analysis.
(4) Availability of semantic masks. Our method relies on consis-
tent multi-view semantic masks as semantic guidance. Foun-
dation models for image segmentation, such as SAM [Kirillov
et al. 2023] are capable of generate high-quality semantic
masks for any single view. However, human involvement is
often required to guarantee the consistency between multi-
view mask labels in practice, which can be eliminated in the
future by using stronger foundation models.
