Abstract
High-resolution 3D indoor scene generation is crucial
for immersive applications such as virtual reality, spatial
computing, and robotics simulation. We introduce VoxLu-
tion, a coarse-to-fine transformer-based approach that gen-
erates high-fidelity indoor scenes with rich details, over-
coming the overly smooth geometry typical of existing gen-
erative models. First, we generate a low-resolution spa-
tial layout; then, based on this structural information, we
progressively refine the scene geometry locally in chunks.
Key to our approach is formulating 3D super-resolution as
a sequence modeling problem over multi-resolution sparse
voxel grids. To achieve this, we introduce a training-free,
resolution-invariant tokenization scheme that compactly en-
codes the geometric information of eight octants into a
single decimal number. We formulate the transformer se-
quence to include multiple resolutions, allowing the coarse
layout to guide the generation of fine local details. To en-

1. Introduction
Generating 3D indoor scenes that reflect the geometric com-
plexity of the real world is a fundamental problem in com-
puter graphics and vision. Many applications in virtual re-
ality, spatial computing, and robotics simulation require 3D
environments that match the dense clutter, irregular layouts,
and wide variation of object type and scale found in the
real world. For instance, immersive VR or spatial com-
puting experiences feel sterile when populated with only
tidy, sparsely furnished rooms such as those in synthetic 3D
scene datasets, and robotics policies trained in clean syn-

shelves or irregularly stacked objects. Real 3D scans [65]
capture these properties directly, yet the noise and incom-
pleteness from sensor limits and view occlusions leave them
largely untapped for generative learning.
Generating realistic 3D scenes is intrinsically challeng-
ing. Scenes vary enormously in spatial extent, with no stan-
dard canonicalization analogous to the single-object setting.
A good generator must not only capture coherent global
structure in scene layouts, but also produce fine-scale detail
across furniture and object clutter. Faithfully representing
such detail requires high spatial resolution, which can im-
pose significant demands due to the high dimensionality of
3D space, particularly for fine-grained resolutions where the
surface represents a small percentage of a full 3D volume.
As real 3D scenes amplify many of these challenges,
work on 3D scene generation focuses on training on syn-
thetic 3D scene data [14], which can simplify some of
these constraints by focusing on complete, axis-aligned
layouts with limited clutter. Building on this foundation,
recent generative approaches have demonstrated remark-
able progress [24, 29, 30, 59], producing increasingly com-
pelling and structurally coherent environments by operating
over compressed latent grids that allow higher resolution
than dense voxel representations.
In contrast, we introduce VoxLution1, an autoregressive
generative model over discrete scene geometry tokens de-
signed to match the irregular structure of scene geometry
in 3D space. VoxLution operates over a sparse scene hi-
erarchy, built on a shared, resolution-invariant voxel code-
book. Central to our approach is a training-free tokeniza-
tion that generalizes across spatial resolutions: each voxel’s
eight child octants are encoded as a single byte, yielding a
fixed 256-entry codebook shared across all resolution lev-
els.
We then serialize the multi-resolution scene hierar-
chy into a single token sequence ordered from coarsest to
finest, with coarser levels directly conditioning the genera-
tion of finer details, casting scene synthesis as a coarse-to-
fine super-resolution task. Our approach natively supports
the inherent sparsity and incompleteness of real 3D scene
scans, generating tokens only where geometry exists, adap-
tively omitting empty or unobserved regions, and thus en-
abling scalable modeling of fine-grained geometry details.
Concretely, the hierarchical generation starts from a
coarse scene structure and refines geometry chunk by
chunk, doubling resolution at each stage. Each new chunk
generation is conditioned on boundary voxels of already-
generated neighbors, ensuring coherent chunk transitions.
A mesh extractor finally lifts the scene voxels into a trian-
gle mesh by predicting per-voxel dual-contouring attributes.
Because the voxel codebook and sparse hierarchy formula-
tion are dataset-agnostic, VoxLution can be easily adapted

and trained directly on real-world scans, capturing their ir-
regular, sparse, and noisy geometry.
Experiments on 3D indoor scene datasets show that Vox-
Lution synthesizes scenes at a 41% finer voxel resolution
than existing baselines, while improving geometric and vi-
sual quality, with 59.6% lower FPD and 51.1% lower FID
than the baselines on 3D-FRONT. On real-world scans,
VoxLution generates high-fidelity scenes that capture the ir-
regularity and clutter of real indoor environments, achieving
70.5% lower FPD and 73.8% lower FID than the baselines
on Scannet++.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We propose VoxLution, a sparse autoregressive approach
that synthesizes high-fidelity 3D indoor scenes on multi-
resolution voxel octrees, exploiting scene-level sparsity to
scale hierarchical generation to real-world resolutions.
• We introduce a resolution-invariant octant code (ROC), a
training-free tokenization of voxel octants with a single
discrete codebook shared across all resolution levels.
• Building on ROC, we design sparse multi-resolution to-
ken sequences with spatially-grounded boundary condi-
tion, enabling seamless chunk-by-chunk super-resolution.
• We adapt VoxLution to real-world data, extending high-
resolution scene generation to noisy, incomplete indoor
scans.
2. Related Works
2.1. 3D Indoor Scene Generation
Approaches to 3D indoor scene generation can be broadly
grouped into three families: direct geometric synthesis,
layout-based composition with retrieved assets, and lifting
of 2D generative priors into 3D. Direct geometric methods
generate scene-scale geometry from scratch, typically using
diffusion-based approaches over compressed representa-
tions. BlockFusion [59], LT3SD [29], and WorldGrow [24]
use coarse-to-fine paradigms over per-block triplanes, latent
trees of TUDF and feature grids, and structured latents in-
herited from an object-centric foundation model [61]. Other
works explore neural-field latents [2, 9, 23], sparse-voxel
diffusion [22, 37], and compositional triplanes [62].
In
contrast, VoxLution operates directly on sparse voxel oc-
cupancy and uses an autoregressive transformer to achieve
finer resolution without learned latent compression.
Layout-based methods instead cast scene synthesis as
predicting an arrangement of object bounding boxes whose
geometry is retrieved from a CAD library [26, 35, 39, 47,
54, 56, 64, 68, 69], restricting output to the catalog and ex-
cluding novel objects and fine-scale clutter.
Finally, a complementary group of methods lifts pre-
trained 2D image and video diffusion priors to 3D using
depth estimation and iterative inpainting [11, 13, 19, 40, 66,
67], achieving photorealistic appearance but suffering from
2


geometric drift and view inconsistencies.
2.2. Autoregressive Models for 3D Geometry
Autoregressive transformers [50] have emerged as a strong
paradigm for 3D shape generation, reflecting their domi-
nance in language and image modeling. Mesh-based meth-
ods generate polygonal geometry as token sequences, from
the seminal work PolyGen [33] to modern MeshGPT [43]
and its successors [5, 6, 15]. Beyond meshes, AR has been
applied to other representations.
AutoSDF [31] autore-
gresses over a latent SDF grid, whereas other methods cover
sparse occupancy [63], point clouds [4, 44], and octree-
based representation [51, 57]. At the scene scale, AR has
been applied only at the layout abstraction – ATISS [35] and
SceneFormer [54] predict ordered or unordered sequences
of object-attribute tokens rather than scene geometry. The
recent work in the image domain, VAR [48], has introduced
multi-scale autoregressive modeling via next-scale predic-
tion over hierarchical tokens, achieving quality competi-
tive with diffusion transformers at faster inference, followed
successors [7, 71]. VoxLution extends multi-scale AR pre-
diction to 3D scene geometry using sparse voxel hierarchies
and a training-free, resolution-invariant tokenization.
2.3. Hierarchical and Sparse 3D Representations
Sparse hierarchical data structures and surface extraction
methods have undergone a long development in 3D geom-
etry processing. Octree-based representations such as Oct-
Net [38] and O-CNN [52, 53], alongside sparse 3D convo-
lution approaches [10, 16], established hierarchical sparse
voxels as efficient alternatives to dense grids. Subsequent
methods extend these structures to coarse-to-fine generative
modeling, including hierarchical neural implicit functions
in OctField [46] and NKSR [20], sparse-voxel latent diffu-
sion [37], and multi-scale generative methods learned from
single shapes [17, 25, 58]. Surface extraction from such rep-
resentations has also significantly developed, from classical
Marching Cubes [27] and Dual Contouring [21] to recent
neural variants such as FlexiCubes [42], which learn per-
voxel vertex offsets and edge attributes for sharper geome-
try [8, 41]. VoxLution operates on a sparse multi-resolution
voxel octree and extracts sharp meshes using the Flexible
Dual Grid representation [60].
3. Method
VoxLution synthesizes 3D indoor scenes as multi-resolution
voxel octrees, in an autoregressive, coarse-to-fine fashion
conditioned on scene-type labels. As 3D surfaces are sur-
rounded by empty space, and this ratio worsens dramati-
cally at finer resolutions, both our tokenization and sequenc-
ing are designed around sparsity.
To compress local geometry, our Resolution-Invariant
Octant Coding (ROC) (Sec. 3.1) maps each parent voxel’s
8-child octant occupancy to a single discrete token, from a
codebook shared across all resolutions. To compress the se-
quence itself, sparse multi-resolution sequences (Sec. 3.2)
drop empty tokens and pack hierarchical context and spa-
tial neighbor information into a 1D sequence.
A family
of autoregressive predictors {Φ(k)} is then used to synthe-
size a scene as a multi-resolution hierarchy: Φ(0) gener-
ates a coarse scene structure O(0), and Φ(k) refines it chunk
by chunk at increasing resolutions, yielding a sparse occu-
pancy O(K) at the finest voxel resolution (Sec. 3.3). Fi-
nally, a sparse VAE Φm (Sec. 3.5) lifts O(K) into an output
triangle mesh M. As our method adopts occupancy-based
generation with a final mesh extraction, it can be flexibly
applied to both synthetic and real scan data. Beyond scene-
type labels, VoxLution supports layout-controlled genera-
tion, conditioning every stage on a semantic layout of object
bounding boxes (Sec. 3.4).
3.1. Resolution-Invariant Octant Coding
To leverage the sequence-modeling capabilities of trans-
formers, 3D data needs mapping into a discrete codebook.
Rather than relying on learned codebooks [49], which typi-
cally requires resolution-specific training, we instead intro-
duce a resolution-invariant octant code (ROC), a training-
free encoding that compactly represents local 3D geometry
with a single codebook shared across all resolution levels.
Given a parent voxel V at resolution level k, we uni-
formly subdivide it into 8 equal-sized child voxels (octants)
at the next finer level k+1. Let oi ∈{0, 1} denote the bi-
nary occupancy of the i-th child under a z–y–x traversal
order (i ∈{0, . . . , 7}). The 8-child occupancy pattern of V
then takes one of 28 = 256 possible patterns, each of which
we treat as a codebook entry. The token T ∈{0, . . . , 255}
directly encodes this as

3.2. Sparse Multi-Resolution Token Sequencing
Autoregressive prediction over voxel octrees requires seri-
alizing hierarchical occupancy into a 1D token sequence.
At fine voxel resolutions a full-scene sequence becomes too
long for a single transformer pass, so we partition the scene
into non-overlapping axis-aligned chunks and serialize one
chunk at a time. The serialization orders ROC tokens within
each resolution, stitches together different resolutions of the
same chunk, and brings in context from already-generated
neighboring chunks, with a disentangled positional encod-
ing identifying the role of each token.
Intra-resolution sparse sequencing. We index resolution
levels by k, with voxel size vk = v0/2k, where v0 = 25 cm
is the voxel size of the coarse structure O(0); levels k < 0
(e.g., v−1 = 50 cm) are obtained by 2×2×2 max-pooling.
We refer to the ROC tokens of the occupied level-(k−1)
voxels as level-k tokens, as their octants are the level-k vox-
els, i.e., they encode the occupancy O(k). Level-k tokens
inherit a recursive z–y–x order from the octree: each par-
ent’s eight child tokens appear contiguously in z–y–x order,
and the parents themselves are listed in the same recursive
order at the level above. Equivalently, level-k tokens follow
the Morton (Z-order) curve along z–y–x [32]. As indoor
scenes contain mostly empty space, we omit T=0 tokens,
yielding for each chunk C (defined below) a sparse level-
k token sequence S(k)

rather than volume.
Scene chunk partitioning.
At each level k, the
scene is divided into a regular grid of non-overlapping axis-
aligned chunks {C}, each covering a fixed square footprint
at the floor plane and spanning the full scene height. This
keeps per-input sequence length tractable at the scale of 3D
scenes, where occupancy grows cubically with resolution.
A chunk C carries the occupancy of its region across all
resolution levels available so far, so C’s tokens come from
multiple resolutions of the same volume. From one cascade
level to the next, each chunk is split into 2×2 children in
the floor plane while the voxel size is halved. At a given
level, chunks are generated in raster-scan order, so when C
is processed its left, back, and corner neighbors N(C) are
already finished and available as context.

3.3. Autoregressive Scene Generation
Based on the sparse multi-resolution sequencing of Sec. 3.2,
we can now formulate autoregressive scene generation as
sampling chunk sequences and stitching them into a full
scene. A coarse predictor Φ(0) and K refinement predic-
tors {Φ(k)}K
k=1 are all decoder-only transformers sharing
the same architecture. Each predictor is trained by next-
token prediction on voxelized occupancy data, which can
come from either synthetic scenes or real-world scans with-
out changes to the architecture.
The initial generator Φ(0) consumes a single-level dense
sequence without coarser levels and is conditioned on a
prepended scene-type label, while Φ(k) for k ≥1 consumes
sparse multi-resolution sequences S(k)
C
with three coarser
conditioning levels and is conditioned on the boundary con-
text BC of already-generated neighbors.
Coarse scene generation.
Generation begins with Φ(0)
producing the coarse structure of a scene. This can be con-
sidered a degenerate case of the multi-resolution sequenc-
ing: there are no coarser levels to condition on, and the
coarse sequence is short enough to be serialized directly
as a dense sequence. The scene is partitioned into coarse
chunks at v0 = 25 cm, which are generated in raster-scan
order. Each coarse chunk is encoded by its dense level-0
tokens S(0)
C , i.e., one ROC token per 50 cm voxel with T=0
tokens kept, and we serialize all chunks into a single 1D
dense sequence, so that each chunk attends to all previously
generated chunks. We condition on a scene-type label (e.g.,
bedroom, living room, office), encoded by a frozen text en-
coder [36] and linearly projected into the transformer fea-
ture space. The generated chunks then collectively compose
the coarse occupancy grid O(0).
Cascaded chunk refinement.
Given O(0), a cas-
cade of transformers Φ(k) then synthesizes geometric de-
tails at successively finer resolutions to produce occupan-
cies O(k).
Each Φ(k) conditions on the coarser outputs
O(k−3), . . . , O(k−1) already generated. As resolution dou-
bles, we shrink the chunk footprint accordingly to maintain
manageable per-chunk sequence lengths. Note that k ≥1
cascade sequences are sparse with T=0 tokens dropped,
mirroring the inherent sparsity of 3D surface geometry.
Chunks are processed in raster-scan order at each reso-
lution level k. For each chunk, Φ(k) reads tokens at three
coarser resolutions from the prior outputs at the chunk’s
spatial extent, packs them with the target level into S(k)
C , and
autoregressively generates the chunk’s level-k tokens S(k)
C ,
which populate O(k). The cascade ends after K stages with
the finest occupancy O(K) at voxel size vK, which is passed
to the mesh decoder.
3.4. Layout-Controlled Scene Generation
A scene-type label specifies only the kind of room to gen-
erate, whereas many applications also require control over
which objects a scene contains and where they are placed,
e.g., interior design, where furniture is arranged within a
given room, and simulation environments for embodied
agents, where task-relevant objects must appear at speci-
fied locations. Following Seen2Scene [30], we provide this
control through a semantic layout, a set of object bound-
ing boxes with semantic labels. The layout specifies only
the category and placement of each object, while VoxLu-
tion generates the remaining geometry: the fine detail of
each object, the room structure such as walls and floors.

3.5. Mesh Extraction
The cascade produces a sparse occupancy grid at the finest
voxel size vK, but downstream rendering and simulation of-
ten require a mesh. Naive marching-cubes extraction [28]
from binary occupancy yields blocky, axis-aligned arti-
facts rather than sharp details that match the finest vK =
1.5625 cm voxel size we achieve. We instead extract the
mesh from this sparse occupancy with a sparse VAE Φm.
We adopt the Flexible Dual Grid representation [60],
which describes a surface by associating each occupied
voxel with a dual-vertex offset within the cell and a 3-bit
edge-intersection mask along the positive axes. A single
dual-contouring pass [21] over these attributes yields out-
put mesh M, with quad faces split into triangles using a
tessellation-time quad-lerp weight.
Generative sparse VAE.
The encoder takes the voxel
occupancy O(K) as input and progressively downsamples
it into a sparse latent. Unlike the original SC-VAE [60],
whose decoder reproduces the input voxel set of the en-
coder, our decoder predicts the child occupancy at every
upsampling level, so that it can both add and remove vox-
els. This enables Φm to fill small holes and remove stray
voxels in the generated occupancy.
Training the generative sparse VAE. We train Φm with a
per-level binary cross-entropy on child occupancy, losses on
the dual-grid attributes, a KL regularizer, and the rendering
losses of TRELLIS.2 [60], using cameras placed inside each
room. During training, we augment the input with synthetic
occupancy artifacts, such as missing voxel patches, while
supervising the decoder with the clean ground truth. We
refer the reader to Sec. F for the details.
4. Experiments
We evaluate VoxLution on 3D indoor scene generation on
both synthetic scenes from 3D-FRONT [14] and real-world
scans from ScanNet++ [65], comparing against the state of
the art on geometric fidelity and visual quality.
Datasets.
We train and evaluate VoxLution on two in-
door scene datasets: 3D-FRONT [14] for synthetic scenes
and ScanNet++ [65] for real-world scans. 3D-FRONT is
a large-scale collection of synthetic indoor scenes with di-
verse layouts and furniture arrangements. We use five room
categories, including living room, bedroom, living-dining
room, library, and kids room, yielding 19,237 rooms. From
ScanNet++, we filter out rooms whose scans contain large
unscanned regions, leaving 781 rooms. Each dataset is split
80%/5%/15% into train/validation/test, and VoxLution and
all baselines use the same splits.
Implementation details. We implement VoxLution in Py-
Torch. The coarse scene transformer Φ(0) and the K=4 cas-
cade transformers share a GPT-2 decoder backbone with 24
layers, a hidden dimension of 1024, and 16 attention heads;
each trains for ≈4 days on four NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The

Evaluation metrics.
We measure scene quality with dis-
tribution distances at two levels. At the geometric level, we
report the Fr´echet Point Distance (FPD) [70] between point
clouds sampled from generated and ground-truth meshes,
computed on the features of the pretrained point cloud en-
coder Uni3D [73]. At the visual level, we render each scene
and report the Fr´echet Inception Distance (FID) [18] and
the Kernel Inception Distance (KID) [3] against ground-
truth renderings, computed on DINOv2 ViT-B/14 fea-
tures [34] in place of Inception features. Lower is better
for all three. See Sec. B for the details of the protocol.

4.2. Ablations
Effect of ROC.
We replace ROC with a naive 2-entry
binary tokenizer that emits one occupancy bit per voxel,
keeping the chunking, multi-resolution sequence, and trans-
former backbone identical, and extending the context length
to 8,192 to fit the 8× longer stream. As shown in Tab. 4, our
ROC yields substantially lower FPD and FID at the same
target resolution, indicating that ROC’s per-token geomet-
ric context contributes substantially to quality gain beyond
multi-resolution architecture alone.
Do multi-resolution tokens help?
We formulate {Φ(k)}
at the 0.5 m chunk extent with a single-resolution sequence
containing only 3.125 cm target tokens, and restricting
cross-chunk conditioning to the same single resolution. As
shown in Tab. 4, our multi-resolution sequencing yields
substantially lower FPD and FID, indicating that progres-
sive coarse-to-fine context provides anchoring that single-
level sequencing cannot recover.
Effect of sparse formulation.
We consider a dense for-
mulation that retains every octree position (including the
T=0 tokens our sparse formulation drops) across the multi-
resolution context, the boundary prefix, and the GRU input.
As shown in Tab. 4, our sparse formulation yields lower
FPD, FID, and KID, as well as substantially faster infer-
ence, since the per-chunk sequence length scales with the
occupied geometry rather than the full octree volume. For
a comprehensive comparison of the sequence length and in-
ference speed, we refer to Sec. G.
Can one model replace the cascade?
We consider a uni-
fied transformer trained jointly on chunks at all four cas-
cade extents – enabled by ROC’s resolution-invariance and
our resolution-agnostic sequencing – in place of the four
cascaded models. As shown in Tab. 4, the unified model
underperforms the cascade because the per-chunk sequence
distribution differs substantially across resolutions, and a
single model cannot fit all four regimes simultaneously.
Limitations. VoxLution is designed for single-floor indoor
scenes; multi-story buildings would require a height-aware
positional encoding that models discrete floors. The cas-
cade comprises K+1 independently trained transformers,
so generating at a finer voxel resolution requires training an
additional stage rather than extending an existing one. On
real-world data, our model inherits the holes and noise of
the training scans; learning to complete partial geometry at
scene scale is a natural direction for future work.
5. Conclusion
We introduced VoxLution, a sparse autoregressive approach
for high-fidelity 3D indoor scene generation in a coarse-to-
fine fashion. We tokenize voxel octants with a training-free,
resolution-invariant codebook and serialize sparse multi-
resolution scene hierarchies, casting scene synthesis as
chunk-by-chunk super-resolution conditioned on neighbor
boundaries. This enables synthesizing scenes with sharp
surfaces and rich geometric details at finer voxel resolutions
than prior work, on both synthetic and real-world data. We
believe VoxLution will drive progress in realistic 3D scene
generation, opening new possibilities for embodied agents
grounded in real-world indoor geometry.

VoxLution: Evolving Voxels for High-Fidelity 3D Scene Generation
Supplementary Material
We present implementation details, additional ablations
and a detailed runtime / sparsity comparison, details of
layout-guided generation and mesh extraction, and addi-
tional 3D-FRONT comparisons, ScanNet++ results, and
layout-conditioned results.
A. Implementation Details
Data processing.
For each room, we voxelize the ge-
ometry at a leaf size of 1.5625 cm and aggregate up to 2 m
via 2×2×2 max-pooling, producing the multi-resolution in-
put to the coarse and cascade stages. Each occupied 2 m
voxel seeds a 2 m×2 m×2 m chunk for the first cascade
transformer; subsequent stages subdivide these chunks lat-
erally as described in Sec. 3.3. Rooms are bounded by a
10 m×10 m footprint and cropped at a height of 2 m, so that
every chunk spans the full scene height.
Training.
We implement VoxLution in PyTorch and train
all transformers with the AdamW optimizer at a peak learn-
ing rate of 1×10−4. The coarse scene transformer Φ(0) and
the K=4 cascade transformers {Φ(k)}4
k=1 share a GPT-2
decoder backbone with 24 layers, a hidden dimension of
1024, and 16 attention heads. The cascade context length
is 4,800 tokens for the three coarser stages (2 m, 1 m, and
0.5 m chunks) and 8,192 tokens for the finest stage (0.25 m
chunks), whose sparse sequences are longer. The per-GPU
batch size scales inversely with chunk-token length, rang-
ing from 16 to 4. Each transformer trains for ≈4 days on
four NVIDIA H100 GPUs. The layout conditioning and the
mesh VAE Φm are detailed in Secs. E and F.
B. Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate all methods with 1000 sampled scene chunks,
comparing generated and ground-truth meshes in FPD, FID,
and KID. For FPD, we sample surface points per chunk,
normalized to the unit sphere, and encode them with the
pretrained Uni3D encoder [73], ℓ2-normalizing the features
before computing the Fr´echet distance to the ground truth.
For FID and KID, we render each chunk from 8 viewpoints
uniformly spaced around the vertical axis under fixed light-
ing, i.e., 8,000 images per method, and use the CLS token
of DINOv2 ViT-B/14 [34] as the image feature. For the ab-
lations (Tab. 4), each variant refines a subset of the coarse
scenes of our full model. Since FPD, FID, and KID all de-
pend on the number of samples, we compare each variant
with our full model on the chunks at the same locations of
the same scenes, using all of them up to 1000.

C. User Study
We conducted a user study with 22 participants on three as-
pects: perceptual quality, room-type consistency, and layout
controllability. We will expand on this study in the revision.
(1) Perceptual quality: VoxLution achieved 84.6% overall
preference vs baselines (86.2% geometric fidelity, 76.7%
realism, 75.4% layout coherence). (2) Room type consis-
tency: given our generated scenes, participants correctly
identified the input room label with 90.6% accuracy. (3)
Layout controllability: under the finer-grained control sec-
tion described below, participants rated scene-layout align-
ment 4.37/5.
D. Runtime
On a single NVIDIA H100 GPU, VoxLution generates
a room in 22 minutes on average.
The cascade super-
resolution stage dominates at ∼20 minutes; the coarse
predictor and mesh extraction take ∼1 minute and ∼30
seconds, respectively.
For a 4 m × 4 m scene, VoxLu-
tion takes 16 minutes as we generate at 41% finer reso-
lution (1.5625 cm), compared to LT3SD [29] (8 minutes)
and WorldGrow [24] (6 minutes).
At an output resolu-
tion of 3.125 cm, closer to that of the baselines, VoxLution
generates in 3.5 minutes. Our implementation is not opti-
mized for speed; standard transformer-decoding optimiza-
tions (KV cache, batched decoding, speculative sampling)
could accelerate generation. Table 5 breaks down the gener-
ation time per cascade level and compares it with the dense
formulation (Sec. G).
E. Layout-guided Generation
Layout tokens. The Fourier features γ use eight frequency
bands, sin(2jπx) and cos(2jπx) for j = 0, . . . , 7, applied
to each normalized box coordinate x. The layout tokens
1


enter the transformer through a zero-initialized linear gate,
so that training starts from the unconditional model.
Semantic field.
The field of Φ(0) is defined on the
20×20×4 grid of 50 cm cells covering the 10 m×10 m×2 m
scene bound. We rasterize the boxes at 25 cm and pool them
to the coarse cells. Each cell stores the overlap-weighted
mean of the ℓ2-normalized CLIP features of the boxes cov-
ering it, together with three coverage values: the covered
fraction, the share of the dominant box, and the coverage of
its 25 cm child cells. The field is projected and added onto
the coarse tokens through a zero-initialized gate, and each
coarse token additionally receives the box of its own cell
through the same Fourier encoder.
Box selection.
Both stages use at most 96 layout to-
kens, which only affects rooms with more than 96 boxes.
Φ(0) keeps the boxes that overlap the most coarse cells,
and Φ(k) keeps the boxes whose centers are nearest to
the center of chunk C. The resulting sequence of Φ(k) is
[HC, Bglobal, Blocal, S(k)
C ], between the start and end tokens.
F. Mesh Extraction
We detail the architecture, training, and inference of the
sparse VAE Φm (Sec. 3.5).
Architecture.
Φm follows the dual-grid SC-VAE of
TRELLIS.2 [60] and is initialized from its pretrained
weights. The encoder receives only the occupied voxels
of O(K) and downsamples them in four stages of sparse
ConvNeXt blocks (64 to 1024 channels) into a 32-channel
sparse latent with 25 cm cells.
The decoder mirrors the
encoder: each of its four upsampling blocks predicts the
occupancy logits of the eight children of every voxel and
keeps the children with positive logits, and the finest level
predicts the dual-vertex offsets, edge-intersection flags, and
quad-lerp weights. In the original SC-VAE, the upsampling
blocks copy the child structure cached by the encoder, so
the output voxel set always equals the input; we remove
this cache, so that the predicted child occupancy determines
the output structure. In addition, the latent voxel set is di-
lated by its 26-neighbors before decoding, which allows the
decoder to add geometry in empty 25 cm cells next to the
input.
Training data.
The ground-truth dual-grid attributes
of each room are computed from its mesh at vK
=
1.5625 cm, keeping the geometry below 2 m. We train on
3D-FRONT [14], ScanNet++ [65], and ARKitScenes [1]
and translate each room by a random integer offset of 0–
15 voxels.
Input augmentation. For 95% of the training samples, we
corrupt the encoder input with artifacts modeled on those of
the generated occupancy, while the decoder is supervised
with the clean ground truth: (1) small surface defects at 3–8
per 1,000 occupied voxels, in clusters of 2–6 within a ra-
dius of 5 voxels, half of which remove runs of 1–3 voxels
through the surface and half of which add stray voxels or
patches of 3–8 voxels on top of it, with 60% of the clusters
placed where oblique or curved surfaces step between voxel
layers; (2) irregular holes of 20–600 voxels grown along the
surface, removing 1–5% of the voxels; (3) random removal
of up to 0.5% of the voxels; (4) spurious voxels adjacent to
the surface, up to 3% of the occupied voxels; and (5) up to
two floating blobs of 2–4 voxels.
Losses.
During training, each upsampling block contin-
ues with the union of its predicted and ground-truth chil-
dren, so that all ground-truth voxels receive supervision.
The objective combines a binary cross-entropy on the child
occupancy of every upsampling level (weight 0.2), a bi-
nary cross-entropy on the edge-intersection flags (0.1), a
mean squared error on the dual-vertex offsets (0.01), a KL
term (10−6), and the rendering losses of TRELLIS.2 on the
extracted mesh: L1 losses on the rendered mask (1) and
depth (10), and L1 (1), SSIM [55] (0.2), and LPIPS [72]
(0.2) losses on the rendered normals. Each step renders one
512 × 512 view per room from a camera placed in the free
space of the room, looking at a random occupied voxel with
a 70◦field of view.
Optimization. Starting from the pretrained weights, Φm is
trained in several stages. The final stage runs for 30,000 it-
erations with AdamW at a learning rate of 10−5 (500 warm-
up iterations, cosine decay) and a batch size of 4 on two
NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs, and we use its EMA weights
(rate 0.999).
Inference.
At inference, each upsampling block keeps
only its predicted children. We lower the decision thresh-
old of the edge-intersection flags from a logit of 0 to −3,
which reduces pinholes on flat surfaces. Finally, we close
small holes in the extracted mesh: boundary loops of at
most 6 edges are fan-filled, and holes with rims of at most
64 edges and up to 25 cm across are closed with minimum-
area patches.
G. Sparse vs. Dense Formulation
Table 5 reports per-level generation time and mean se-
quence length, comparing our sparse formulation against
the dense baseline.
The speedup grows with refinement
depth—3.5× at level 1 to 25.9× at level 4—driven by se-
quence length: a level-4 dense chunk averages 6,195 to-
kens versus 621 for sparse, and transformer decoding is
super-linear in length. End-to-end, sparse generation takes
1,321 s per room versus 27,335 s for dense, a 20.7× overall
speedup, with every one of the 33 rooms speeding up by at
least 13×. The dense baseline is in fact prohibitively slow at
level 4, completing only 38 of the 101 test rooms within the
same compute budget; the sparse formulation is therefore a
