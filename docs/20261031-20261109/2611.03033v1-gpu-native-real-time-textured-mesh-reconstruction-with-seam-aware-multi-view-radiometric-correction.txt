GPU-Native Real-Time Textured Mesh Reconstruction with
Seam-Aware Multi-View Radiometric Correction
Hiroyuki Sato
MinD in a Device Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan
h.sato@mindinadevice.com
Abstract
We present a GPU-native volumetric-video system for real-
time reconstruction of graphics-compatible textured trian-
gle meshes from multiple RGB-D sensors. Existing TSDF
systems accelerate fusion and mesh extraction, but extend-
ing them to per-frame textured meshing adds CPU mesh
processing, CPU-side texturing work, or extra CPU–GPU
transfers between stages. After input staging, our method
keeps the reconstruction and texturing stages (TSDF fu-
sion, Marching Cubes, connected-component noise re-
moval, view-based texture mapping, inpainting, and radio-
metric seam correction) resident on the GPU; no interme-
diate mesh or image data returns to the host between these
stages. A fixed-size-array representation of mesh topology
gives constant-time neighbor access on the GPU and makes
the downstream mesh-level operations data-parallel. Be-
cause we texture each face from a single camera, exposure
seams that vary smoothly across each image remain; we es-
timate a per-camera low-frequency gain field and bake it
into the atlas on the GPU. On the CMU Panoptic dataset,
our method is faster than the baselines at every tested voxel
resolution and number of sensors, achieves peak speedups
of more than an order of magnitude over CUDA-accelerated
nvblox and CPU-based Open3D, and approaches an offline
textured-meshing baseline in perceptual re-rendering qual-
ity.

Real-time texturing raises an appearance challenge.
When each face uses one camera, adjacent faces may
meet along visible exposure seams whose magnitude varies
smoothly across each camera’s image.
Texture mapping
methods reduce such seams by globally optimizing view la-
bels, colors, or texture alignment [14, 28, 45], but at offline
cost. Real-time systems instead texture the scene online,
fusing images into progressively updated texture represen-
tations [26, 27] or adjusting patch colors against a refer-
ence [17]. They scan static scenes with one moving sensor,
rather than reconstructing each dynamic frame from simul-
taneous views and correcting their seams.
We target indoor volumetric video with high-density,
high-frame-rate multi-view RGB-D input.
Our system
(Fig. 2) targets a bounded observation volume, and after one
batched input upload keeps the reconstruction and textur-
ing stages (TSDF fusion, mesh generation, noise removal,
texture mapping, and radiometric seam correction) on the
GPU. At high resolution, data movement dominates fusion
arithmetic, so GPU residency lets per-frame textured mesh-
ing, topology-aware cleanup, inpainting, and radiometric
correction fit within the 33 ms budget of 30 fps capture.
We make four contributions: (1) a GPU-native volumetric-
video system that generates graphics-compatible textured
meshes from multiple RGB-D sensors while keeping mesh-
level processing and texturing on the GPU; (2) a fixed-size-
array representation of mesh topology that enables efficient
post-MC mesh processing on the GPU; (3) GPU-resident
texture processing, including noise removal and inpainting,
built on this representation; and (4) a real-time, on-GPU ra-
diometric seam correction stage that estimates a per-camera
low-frequency gain field and bakes it into the texture at-
las, reducing inter-camera exposure seams, including mis-
matches that vary across each camera’s image.
As shown in Fig. 1, our method runs 89.7×/29.4× faster
than Open3D (CPU)/nvblox at 0.005 m voxels. At 0.02 m
it runs in real time (13.1 ms per frame including input up-
load and preprocessing, within the 33 ms budget of 30 fps
sensors), and its reconstruction quality approaches the of-
fline textured-meshing baseline in LPIPS under held-out re-
rendering. Code is available at https://github.com/
unclearness/grtmesh. Implementation details, pro-
tocols, and further results are in the appendix, and the
supplementary video is on the project page, https://
unclearness.github.io/grtmesh/.

Screened Poisson Surface Reconstruction (SPSR) [23, 24]
produces high-quality watertight meshes but requires a
large sparse solve.
Global texture mapping optimiza-
tions [3, 14, 28, 45] yield seamless atlases by leveling col-
ors along seams [28], combining global color adjustment
with local blending [45], or correcting texture misalign-
ment from global to local [14]; related work jointly opti-

ing [19, 31, 52]. These methods, like motion-graph syn-
thesis [41] and spatiotemporal atlas parameterization [42],
run offline. We cast related view selection and seam level-
ing as per-face, data-parallel GPU computations, with a per-
camera low-frequency field baked into the atlas (Sec. 3.6).
Real-time online texturing.
MeshReduce [20] produces
real-time textured meshes through distributed processing.
TextureFusion [27] and NormalFusion [16] store appear-
ance in a texture-tile voxel grid rather than a standard UV at-
las, while TextureMe [26] maintains a conventional-texture-
compatible but fragmented atlas of right-triangular patches.
All three update a static single-sensor scan online, regis-
tering each new frame to reduce geometric or photometric
misalignment. This temporal accumulation assumes a per-
sistent surface and does not fit per-frame meshes for dy-
namic volumetric video. TextureFusion and TextureMe re-
port degraded texture under severe exposure change, view-
dependent or specular lighting, and automatic white bal-
ance; NormalFusion assumes Lambertian surfaces under
static illumination. Han et al. [17] texture a globally con-
sistent reconstruction online with MRF view selection and
CPU per-patch color transfer. It is closest to ours, but ad-
justs each patch as a whole; we fit a per-camera spatial field
in fixed time on the GPU, avoiding CPU-side optimization.
3. Method
Fig. 2 shows our pipeline. We implement the host (CPU)
side in C++ and the device (GPU) side in CUDA [38]. For
each frame, we upload the raw color and depth images in
one batch; the GPU undistorts them, registers depth to color,
builds the color atlas, runs the reconstruction and texturing
stages, and writes the textured mesh back to the CPU. To
minimize synchronization and transfer overhead, we coa-
lesce data where possible and use pinned memory.
3.1. TSDF Fusion and Mesh Generation
To reconstruct a surface from a stream of depth images
we use projective TSDF-based volumetric fusion [36]. For
speed the voxel grid uses a uniform (dense) layout rather
than a hash- or tree-based sparse structure. To process the
multi-view depth input, we implement the entire fusion step
as a single CUDA kernel in which each thread owns one
voxel. The thread projects its grid point into each view’s
depth image in turn, computes the corresponding signed
distance, and integrates the per-view TSDF contributions.
Because each voxel is processed independently, the kernel
needs no atomic operations and reaches high parallel effi-
ciency.
We then extract a triangle mesh \ mat hcal {M}=(\mathcal {V},\mathcal {F}) from the
TSDF volume with MC, where \ mat h cal {V}=\{\mathbf {v}_i \in \mathbb {R}^3\} is the set
of vertex positions and \ mat h cal { F}=\ {f_j=(v_{j0}, v_{j1}, v_{j2})\} is the
set of triangular faces, each defined by three vertex indices.
Running MC in parallel on the GPU requires determining
the output size in advance and resolving vertices shared be-
tween adjacent cells; following common practice [12], we
therefore implement vertex generation and face generation
as two separate CUDA kernels in a multi-pass scheme.
3.2. Fixed-Size-Array Mesh Topology
The plain vertex/face list produced by MC carries no face
adjacency: locating the neighbors of a face requires an ad-
ditional O(|F|) search, which makes it a bottleneck for
downstream mesh processing.
We therefore introduce a
fixed-size-array adjacency data structure suited to fast con-
struction and lookup on the GPU. For every edge of a
face f we define an undirected edge key key(vi, vj) =
(min(vi, vj), max(vi, vj)) and pack it into a 64-bit integer.
For all 3|F| edges we form (key, face-ID) pairs and sort
them by key with a radix sort; faces that share an identi-
cal key are then linked, which determines the neighbor set
N(f) of each face.
Because a manifold mesh has at most three edge-
adjacent neighbors per face, we store N(f) in a fixed-size
array of length three.
Half-edge structures [5] also give
constant-time access but store several references per edge
for general polygons; our layout keeps only the three in-
dices that triangle adjacency needs. Inside a CUDA ker-
nel it yields regular, coalesced memory access and sup-
presses warp divergence. Construction consists of O(|F|)
data-parallel steps (edge generation, a radix sort over fixed-
length keys, and neighbor registration) and completes on
the GPU. Boundary slots remain invalid. For non-manifold
edge runs with more than two incident faces, the sorted-

3.3. Noise Removal
Small isolated mesh fragments, caused by depth noise, oc-
clude other surfaces during texture mapping and degrade
visual quality.
To suppress them we run a connected-
component analysis on the GPU using a label-propagation
scheme [18] built on the data structure of Sec. 3.2. Each
face is initialized with its own face index as a label; we then
propagate the minimum label over the adjacent faces (with
pointer jumping) until convergence or an iteration cap (100
in all experiments, which bounds latency). Faces sharing a
common label form a connected component; we count the
faces per component and remove components below a size
threshold in a compaction step. Casting noise removal as
an independent per-face label update lets it exploit the par-
allelism of the GPU.
3.4. Texture Mapping
Following prior work [14, 45], we adopt a view-selection
approach driven by the projected geometry of each face.
We use a fixed atlas layout that stacks the input RGB im-
ages [20, 28], giving each camera a known atlas band. The
texture mapping kernels compute UV coordinates and best-
view assignments directly against this layout on the GPU.
For each triangular face we test candidate cameras using its
three vertices. We reject a camera if a vertex has invalid
observed depth or its camera-space depth differs from the
depth at its projection by more than a tolerance. The de-
fault is 10 cm, fixed across voxel sizes. Among valid cam-
eras we select the view that minimizes the angle between
the face normal and viewing direction, excluding angles
above 80◦to avoid projective distortion. Projecting the face
into that camera’s RGB image yields local UV coordinates.
Unassigned faces go to the inpainting stage below; the scal-
ing and offset of the atlas layout then give the final mesh
UVs. We avoid MRF-style view-label optimization because
the face graph changes every frame, and a data-dependent
global solve would make latency hard to bound. Indepen-
dent per-face assignment maps directly onto the GPU; the
seam correction stage below handles the radiometric dis-
continuities it leaves.
3.5. Inpainting
The texture mapping stage of Sec. 3.4 leaves some faces
unassigned due to occlusion. We fill them with a fast in-
painting procedure that again uses the data structure of
Sec. 3.2. The procedure is iterative: whenever an unas-
signed face has an already-assigned neighbor, we propagate
the neighbor’s UV coordinates to it. To prevent artifacts,
we copy the value of a single vertex of the neighbor and fill
the entire triangle with a uniform color. The procedure ter-
minates once no face changes state in an iteration or after a
fixed cap of five iterations, which bounds the latency. Each
iteration is an independent per-face GPU operation.
3.6. Radiometric Seam Correction
View-selection texturing assigns one camera to each face.
Adjacent faces that use different cameras form a texture
seam, where exposure differences can produce a visible
brightness step (Fig. 3, left).
Our correction uses these
seams as sparse measurements of radiometric mismatch and
spreads their evidence through a coarse gain field in each
camera’s image space (Fig. 3, center). The field varies spa-
tially because a single exposure offset per camera cannot
represent mismatches that change across the image, such as
those left by lens vignetting, locally adaptive ISP process-
ing (e.g., local tone mapping), and view-dependent shad-
ing. Seams constrain only differences between cameras:
the field reduces these relative mismatches, while shading
common to all cameras is not observable.
Measure mismatch at seams.
We identify seams by
comparing camera IDs across the existing face adjacency.
At eight corresponding positions along each shared edge,
we sample the two source images after a small inward in-
set into their respective projected faces (the edge points and
inset samples in Fig. 3). Sampling a 1/8-resolution linear-
RGB proxy suppresses texture detail and sensitivity to small
misalignments. We exclude sharp folds and degenerate pro-

Solve and apply.
We use twelve matrix-free weighted Ja-
cobi iterations (ω = 0.7). Each update combines neighbor-
ing grid values with the seam constraints, clamps the up-
dated log-gain to ±0.15, and then removes the global mean
across cameras to control overall exposure drift. Nearest-
cell assignment keeps the data term local during the solve.
At bake time, we instead interpolate the solved field bilin-
early and clamp the interpolated log-gain to ±0.15. The
bake is then a single multiplication: each full-resolution
linear-RGB atlas pixel is scaled by exp(ˆgk) (Fig. 3, right)
before conversion back to sRGB. A scalar gain scales all
channels equally.
The vertically stacked camera images
give a direct mapping from each atlas pixel to its cam-
era’s grid, without changing mesh UVs. Camera exposure
changes little between frames, so an exponential moving av-
erage of the grids (α = 0.15, in all experiments) suppresses
frame-to-frame flicker of the correction (8× smaller frame-
to-frame change of the grid).
All stages run on the GPU and reuse the face adjacency,
camera assignments, and projected coordinates already pro-
duced by texturing. Persistent buffers and a fixed iteration
count keep the correction stage bounded.
4. Experiments
We evaluate the runtime and visual quality of our method on
the Panoptic Studio dataset [21, 22], in which ten Kinect v2
sensors [33] are arranged on a hemisphere and observe a
volume of roughly 6 m × 6 m and about 2 m in height. We
use two sequences: office2, in which a single performer in-
teracts with a variety of objects, and band2, in which three
performers play musical instruments. Runtimes are mea-
sured on office2, frames 400–2399; held-out re-rendering
and seam metrics use 100 frames.

Our method is faster than the baselines across the tested
settings. In particular, at a voxel size of 0.02 m the full
method runs at 13.1 ms per frame (10.9 ms excluding in-
put upload and preprocessing), within the 33 ms (30 fps)
budget of typical RGB-D sensors and thus real-time (the
texture-free “Ours (plain)” pipeline of Tab. 1 is faster still,
at 7.7 ms). At a voxel size of 0.005 m it attains a 29.4×
speedup over nvblox and an 89.7× speedup over Open3D
on the CPU (179.9× over its Uniform variant and 1.6× over
Open3D (CUDA), the closest baseline); peak GPU memory
at this setting is about 24.1 GB, of which 16.6 GB are static
grid and mesh buffers. Tab. 1 gives the per-stage break-
down; it attributes much of nvblox’s runtime to CPU–GPU
transfer and GPU-side container overhead, which our dense
GPU-resident design avoids.

5. Conclusion
We presented a GPU-native volumetric-video system for
graphics-compatible textured meshes from multiple RGB-
D sensors. A fixed-size-array adjacency keeps mesh post-
processing and texturing data-parallel on the GPU, and an
on-GPU low-frequency gain field baked into the atlas re-
duces inter-camera exposure seams, including mismatches
that vary across each image. The system reaches 13.1 ms
per frame at 0.02 m voxels and ranks second only to an of-
fline baseline in LPIPS. At 0.005 m voxels, the full method
is 89.7×/29.4× faster than Open3D (CPU, w/o nr)/nvblox.

A. Implementation Details and Hyperparame-
ters
All runtime and quality numbers were obtained on a work-
station with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 9970X, 256 GB
of RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Max-
Q. Tab. 4 lists the Panoptic defaults of Sec. 4; ablations
and the auxiliary capture specify their overrides. We sta-
bilize the seam correction gain field across frames with an
exponential moving average, gt ←α gt + (1 −α) gt−1,
with α = 0.15 and the zero-mean gauge re-applied after
the blend. Fig. 9 illustrates how the seam residuals are sam-
pled, including the direction and magnitude of the sub-pixel
inset.
Runtime protocol.
Runtimes are measured on frames
400–2399 (band2: 1397–3396) and reported as the mean
over the fastest 95% of the timed frames, which removes
start-up and scheduling outliers. Frames are decoded into
host memory before timing; our times start with the batched
upload of the raw color and depth images and include their
undistortion and depth-to-color registration on the GPU
(2.2 ms together at 0.02 m). The baselines perform these
two steps on the CPU, outside their timed region. Over all
2000 frames without the trimming, the full method takes
13.2 ms on average at 0.02 m (95th percentile 15.2 ms),
against 13.1 ms in Tab. 1.
At 0.01 m and 0.005 m the baselines are timed on ev-
ery fourth and every tenth frame of the same range (500
and 200 frames; nvblox at 0.005 m: the first 60 of these).
nvblox (nvblox torch 0.0.9) does not release GPU memory
when its mapper is cleared, so memory grows until the de-
vice is full and the following frames stall. We therefore
recreate the mapper outside the timed region whenever de-
vice memory use exceeds 50% (90% at 0.005 m). The first
frames after each recreation re-grow the memory pool and
stall. At 0.02 m and coarser, these stalls fall in the trimmed
5%. At 0.01 m we use frames 18–57 after each recreation
(291 of 500, fastest-95% mean). At 0.005 m, memory fills
within five timed frames, so we use the single regrowth-free
frame of each segment (12 of 60 timed frames, plain mean).
Excluding the stalls favors nvblox. On exactly the same
frames, our method is 23.7× and 29.4× faster than nvblox
at 0.01 m and 0.005 m, as with the reported means.
Open3D (CUDA) is the tensor VoxelBlockGrid of
Open3D 0.19 on the GPU (block resolution 16, truncation
of four voxels, mesh weight threshold 0.5; the default of 3
removes most of the surface). Its time covers the image up-
load, block allocation and integration per camera, Marching
Cubes, and the mesh download. Its 0.02 m to 0.16 m runs
were repeated back to back because the first sweep drifted
between jobs; the two sweeps differ by up to 1.9 ms per
setting.
Kernel launches per frame.
The launch counts in the
pipeline figure (Fig. 2) were measured with Nsight Systems
on office2 (ten frames, five Kinects, 0.02 m): TSDF fusion
1; Marching Cubes 12; noise removal Nnr = 100 label-
propagation launches (the iteration cap, which the room-
scale meshes reach in every frame; a convergence flag is
read back every ten iterations) plus about 28 launches for
edge sorting, adjacency construction, and compaction, and
3 for normal computation and smoothing; texture mapping
2 (view selection and UV generation) plus 13 launches
that rebuild the fixed-size-array adjacency of the compacted
mesh (edge sort); inpainting Nip = 5 (the cap, reached in
every frame); and seam correction Nsc = 55: 12 Jacobi it-
erations of four kernels (clear, splat seams, update, gauge)
plus proxy construction, seam detection, residual sampling,
the temporal EMA blend and its gauge re-application, resid-
ual statistics, and the bake (53 on the first frame, which has
no EMA history). In total, about 219 kernel launches per

Noise-removal iteration cap.
The cap of 100 label-
propagation iterations bounds the latency of noise removal,
but on our room-scale meshes propagation has not yet con-
verged when it stops. We reran 30 frames (400–690, stride
10) with a cap of 5000 and the same convergence check:
propagation converges after 111–241 iterations at 0.02 m
and 241–591 at 0.01 m (office2 and band2), and after 791
to more than 5000 at 0.005 m (office2). With the cap, a
large component can still carry several labels, and a part
with fewer than 500 faces is removed with the noise. Com-
pared with the long runs, the cap removes on average 0.02–
0.05% and at most 0.12% of the output faces per frame.
The component analysis is therefore an approximation with
a bounded number of iterations; all reported results use the
cap.
Texture atlas.
The atlas is the vertical stack of the input
color images, one 1920 × 1080 band per Kinect in input
order, i.e. 1920 × 5400 pixels for five Kinects (Fig. 8). A
face textured from camera k receives as UVs the pixel co-
ordinates of its projection into band k; an inpainted face
receives the UV of one vertex of a neighboring face and
is therefore filled with a uniform color; and the seam cor-
rection multiplies band k by exp(ˆgk) in place, so the mesh
UVs never change. Image regions that no face samples sim-
ply stay in the atlas: the layout needs no chart packing, and
the corrected atlas of the frame shown is a 1.9 MB JPEG.
The layout also suits real-time delivery: it is fixed and each
band is the camera image itself, so consecutive atlases keep
a constant resolution and are temporally coherent, and the
atlas sequence can be streamed with a standard video en-
coder (compression is not evaluated here).

Temporal filtering and bake.
Continuous capture uses
the EMA specified above, with the gauge re-applied after
blending. On office2 frames 400–699, it reduces the mean
frame-to-frame change of the (clamped) log-gain grid from
0.0079 to 0.0010, and the 99th percentile over cells and
frames from 0.057 to 0.0072; without it, single cells jump
by several percent between frames. Filtering is in camera-
grid coordinates and does not require mesh correspondences
across frames. For each atlas pixel, the band layout identi-
fies its camera and image coordinates. We interpolate that
camera’s field bilinearly, clamp the interpolated log-gain
again to [−0.15, 0.15], and multiply the original linear RGB
by exp(ˆg), where ˆg denotes this clamped value. The applied
gain therefore remains in approximately [0.86, 1.16], even if
the gauge-shifted grid exceeds the update clamp. We then
convert back to sRGB. This leaves image detail in the orig-
inal texture while modulating it with a coarse spatial cor-
rection; it does not repair geometric misalignment or sharp
local seams.

GPU reuse.
Faces, normals, camera labels, and projected
coordinates remain on the device from reconstruction and
texturing.
The correction also reuses the existing fixed-
size-array adjacency, avoiding another edge-key sort (about
1.7 ms per frame). It needs no new camera projection and
no geometry re-upload. Proxy construction, residual sam-
pling, field updates, and atlas bake execute on the GPU.
Persistent buffers avoid per-frame allocations, and the iter-
ations require no CPU synchronization.
B. Held-out Re-rendering Protocol
We reconstruct the textured mesh from one set of Kinects
and render it from a disjoint set, so the evaluation views are
never used as input. Concretely, we reconstruct from the
even Kinects {2, 4, 6, 8, 10} and evaluate on the odd Kinects
{1, 3, 5, 7, 9}, at a voxel size of 0.02 m (cmu-room). For
each held-out camera we render the reconstructed mesh
with its calibrated intrinsics/extrinsics (Blender/EEVEE,
emission shading) and compare to that camera’s real, undis-
torted RGB image.
Shared valid-pixel mask.
Each method leaves different
uncovered (black) pixels where faces are unseen or re-
moved. Scoring each method only on its own covered pixels
would compare different pixel sets and make the numbers
incomparable. We therefore evaluate every method on the
intersection of all methods’ covered pixels: a pixel is valid
only if every method renders non-black there (a light mor-
phological opening removes speckle). PSNR, SSIM, and
LPIPS are computed on this single shared mask and aver-
aged with a 5% trimmed mean. PSNR uses only the masked
pixels. SSIM and LPIPS (AlexNet) are computed on the full
frame after every pixel outside the mask is set to the same
constant in both images (black for SSIM and mid-gray for
LPIPS, whose input range is [−1, 1]). All metrics use sRGB
values in [0, 1].
Subsets.
The quality numbers of Sec. 4.2 and the
luminance-vs-RGB ablation use frames 400–697 at stride 3
(100 evaluated frames) per sequence and the same common
mask over all eight evaluated variants. The offline-texturing
comparison (Sec. D) uses frames 400–429; the voxel-size
and occlusion ablations use the 50-frame subsets specified
in their captions. Within each comparison, all methods are
evaluated on the same frames and mask. For the quality and
seam numbers of Sec. 4 and the luminance-vs-RGB abla-
tion, our method reconstructs every frame from 400 to 699
in order, and only every third frame is rendered and eval-
uated. The correction-on variants use the gain-field EMA
(α = 0.15) throughout, as do the runtimes in Tab. 1; its his-
tory starts at frame 400 (the first field is used as is) without
a separate warm-up.
Rounding.
Differences, ratios, and percentages in the text
and in the ∆columns of this appendix are computed from
unrounded metrics, so they can differ in the last digit from
values recomputed from the rounded table entries.
C. Seam Correction Variants
Our seam correction estimates a single luminance gain field
by default, which preserves chroma and solves a third as
many parameters as a per-channel (RGB) field; the RGB
field uses the same solver with three independent channels
and is meant for cameras whose white balance differs. We
compare the two variants on Panoptic, whose Kinects share
their white balance, on a synthetic capture with known per-
camera differences, and on our in-house capture, whose
cameras run their own white balance.
Panoptic.
Tab. 5 compares the two fields on the same
100-frame held-out set and common mask as Tab. 2. Cor-
recting chroma in addition to luminance changes the re-
rendering metrics negligibly (PSNR within 0.002, SSIM
within 0.0002, LPIPS within 0.0005 on both sequences in
Tab. 5): the inter-camera differences are dominated by ex-
posure, and the luminance field is sufficient.
Spatial field vs. one gain per camera.
To test whether
the spatial field is needed, we also solve a single luminance
gain per camera (a 1 × 1 field) with the same solver, gain
bound, and temporal filter (“constant” in Tab. 5). It reaches
only 72% / 35% of the PSNR gain, 54% / 30% of the SSIM
gain, and 50% / 50% of the LPIPS gain of the 24 × 16 field
over no correction (office2 / band2). Per image pair, the field
scores higher than the constant gain in SSIM and LPIPS on
499–500 of the 500 pairs of each sequence and in PSNR on
418 and 370. The seam constraint residual falls by 13.8% /
7.8% with the constant gain against 36.5% / 31.8% with the
field (Tab. 3). The variation of the gain within each camera
therefore matters beyond a per-camera exposure offset.

D. Offline Texturing on Our Geometry
The Offline baseline of Sec. 4 pairs SPSR geometry with
mvs-texturing [45], so it differs from our method in both
geometry and texturing. To isolate the texturing step, we
instead apply mvs-texturing to our own per-frame meshes
and evaluate from held-out cameras (Tab. 7). This auxiliary
comparison uses a separate mesh set (frames 400–429 of
each sequence) and a two-method coverage intersection; its
scores support comparisons within this experiment, rather
than direct comparisons with Tab. 2.
We verified the consistency of the offline texturing
pipeline: re-texturing the same SPSR meshes matches the
reference renders to 34–41 dB PSNR. mvs-texturing is run
with hole filling enabled; we also tried both settings of its
keep-unseen-faces option.
In this auxiliary comparison, offline texturing on our ge-
ometry performs worse than our texturing in all three met-
rics. Some faces unseen by the input views remain untex-
tured (rendered blank), while our inpainting supplies colors
for these faces. This result is specific to the dense, noisy
per-frame Marching-Cubes meshes tested here.

F. Occlusion-Aware View Selection
The per-face view selection (Sec. 3.4) tests all three vertices
of a face against the chosen camera’s depth image and re-
jects the camera when the depth of a vertex differs from the
observed depth at its projection by more than a threshold
τocc, in either direction. We use τocc = 10 cm and com-
pare it with a loose 1.1 m tolerance. The loose test admits
large depth discrepancies, allowing foreground texture to
be projected onto occluded background surfaces. The fixed
10 cm tolerance corresponds to five voxels at 0.02 m. Re-
jected assignments leave faces without a camera, which the
inpainting stage fills with flat color. On the common held-
out mask, the default changes PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS by
+0.67 dB, +0.028, and −0.022 on office2, and +0.41 dB,
+0.028, and −0.021 on band2 (Tab. 9). Fig. 13 shows the
effect and Tab. 9 reports the held-out metrics of both set-
tings on the same build and mask.

G. Application to SfM+MVS
Our method can also texture surfaces reconstructed from
image-based depth and camera estimates rather than RGB-
D sensors. Fig. 14 shows an example using the door se-
quence of ETH3D [43]: we take the point-cloud output of
VGGT [46] and apply our textured-meshing pipeline to the
derived geometry and camera views. This example is qual-
itative, but it illustrates that the texturing stage is not tied to
Kinect-style RGB-D input.
