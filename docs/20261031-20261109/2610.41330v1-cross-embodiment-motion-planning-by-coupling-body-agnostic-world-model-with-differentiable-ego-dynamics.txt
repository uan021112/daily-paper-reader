# Cross-Embodiment Motion Planning by Coupling Body-Agnostic World Model with Differentiable Ego-Dynamics 

Zhidong Wang ∗, Zirui Li ∗, Haoran Xu, Yujie Yan, Zitong Shan, Jingsong Liang, Han Yu, Chen Lv 

Abstract —Motion planning in clutter is embodiment-specific: the feasible free space and reachable commands depend on each robot’s geometry and dynamics. Existing cross-embodiment policies output normalized commands that disregard these differ-ences, or compensate through per-robot training and distillation. World models can in principle pool cross-embodiment data, yet command-conditioned models bind their latent dynamics to a sin-gle action space, while pose-conditioned models omit the mapping from commands to body-specific motion. On flat ground, however, embodiment affects the planner’s observations only through the camera ego-motion. This letter proposes Hourglass, an hourglass-shaped architecture that factorizes cross-embodiment motion planning at this interface: a body-agnostic world model and each robot’s differentiable ego-dynamics, which map commands to that ego-motion, are coupled only through it. The world model is trained on data pooled across embodiments and camera mounts; each robot’s ego-dynamics is a reduced-order template with iden-tified physical parameters. A generalist parameter-conditioned policy is learned once at this junction: each imagined step rolls out the template and then the world model, and imagination training through each robot’s template makes the policy body-aware. On seven simulated robots of five locomotion types, the generalist policy outperforms imitation-learning and diffusion-policy baselines on the same world model by 3.8–10.6 percentage points in success rate and matches per-robot specialists without dedicated training, while approaching its parameter-conditioned MPPI teacher within 1.4–2.8 points without an online map. After three minutes of teleoperated driving for identification, the synthetic-data-trained model transfers zero-shot to a real skid-steer robot unseen in training, navigating unseen indoor scenes. 

Index Terms —Motion and path planning, collision avoidance, vision-based navigation, integrated planning and learning.

Global planning produces a route that is largely embodiment-agnostic, whereas local planning must respect each robot’s geometry and dynamics: the geometry constrains which gaps are passable, and the dynamics govern which commands can be executed in time. A cross-embodiment local planner must therefore account for these body-specific properties without requiring a dedicated training stage or dataset for each robot. Existing approaches handle these embodiment differences at different levels, but each conflates the environment representa-tion with the embodiment in some form. Shared-interface poli-cies output normalized commands and delegate execution to each robot’s low-level controller [1, 3, 9], leaving the planner blind to the robot’s dynamics. Embodiment-aware policies re-store dynamics awareness through per-robot training, whether by distilling specialists [4, 5] or by attaching residual modules trained with reinforcement learning [5, 6], so each new robot needs its own training stage. World models move the problem into the representation: command-conditioned models need command-labelled data and retraining for each robot [5, 7], while pose-conditioned models pool data but drop the mapping from commands to motion [8, 10]. All three lines of work entangle the environment with the embodiment, yet on flat ground the embodiment affects observations only through the camera ego-motion. II. R ELATED WORK 

Cross-embodiment policies for navigation and local plan-ning take two broad forms. Shared-interface methods map all robots onto a common output space such as normalized waypoints or mid-level motion commands [1–3, 9, 11–14] and delegate execution to each robot’s low-level controller. The planner is therefore unaware of each robot’s dynamics. Embodiment-aware methods incorporate dynamics through training: randomizing embodiment parameters [15], distilling per-robot or per-embodiment-family specialists [4], attaching per-robot residual or refinement modules [5, 6, 16], or con-ditioning on footprint and velocity limits [17]. Most methods require a dedicated training stage or simulation model for ev-ery new robot, and none of them incorporates a differentiable model of each robot’s dynamics into policy training. World models for cross-embodiment navigation condi-tion either on commands or on relative pose. Command-conditioned models learn environment and dynamics jointly, so they need command-labelled data and retraining for every robot [7]; adding per-robot residual policies on top facilitates deployment but inherits this limitation [5]. Pose-conditioned models pool data across robots by conditioning on ego-motion [8, 10, 18], but omit the mapping from commands to motion, leaving the policy unaware of body-specific dynamics. How to keep the world model body-agnostic while exposing each robot’s dynamics to the policy remains open. III. M ETHODOLOGY

A. Body-Agnostic World Model 

To pool data across robots, the world model is conditioned only on camera ego-motion ∆ξt and camera parameters c,independent of which robot produced that motion. The learned model pψ ≈ pW is a recurrent state-space model [20] with state zt = (ht, st, Mt): a deterministic recurrent state, a stochastic latent variable, and an egocentric spatial memory.

2) Egocentric Spatial Memory: The spatial memory Mt is a multi-channel grid of latent features centered at the camera, rigidly transformed by each ego-motion step [21] (Fig. 2, top left). During posterior inference, depth points projected with the camera intrinsics and mount ct are fused into the memory through a convolutional gate; during imagination, only the rigid transformation is applied. The recurrent update receives a readout of Mt, the depth features are combined with an embedding of ct, and the depth decoder is conditioned on ct.

3) Training Data From Heterogeneous Robots: The world model is trained on sequences D = {(ot, ∆ξt, ct)} of depth images, camera ego-motion (from any pose source), and camera parameters. Logs from robots with different dynamics and camera mounts, whether teleoperated, autonomous, or handheld, are valid samples of pW : the relevant diversity lies in ego-motions and viewpoints, not in the number of robots. The training loss consists of depth reconstruction, an op-tional semantic target, the KL divergence, open-loop prior roll-outs with latent overshooting [20], and clearance prediction from the latent state and the spatial memory. Clearance is supervised by G on cells observed within the training window, and no loss term depends on the embodiment dynamics. 

B. Differentiable Ego-Dynamics 

For the generalist policy, each robot’s dynamics must be differentiable and appear in the policy gradient, without re-quiring a learned dynamics model or per-robot training. A reduced-order template provides this throughout the pipeline: it rolls out each robot’s dynamics in imagination, generates trajectory candidates for the planner that initializes the policy, and projects commands onto the admissible command set at deployment.

3) Policy Architecture and Training: The policy reads the recurrent state hk and the stochastic latent variable sk of zk

(the spatial memory enters through the recurrent readout), the route, goal, and velocity, as well as normalized features of the robot’s physical and camera parameters. To handle multimodal planning decisions (e.g., passing an obstacle on either side), the policy outputs Nm command modes together with a critic head. All modes are rolled out in imagination, and the mode with the lowest cost receives weight 1−η while the remaining modes share η (relaxed winner-takes-all, WTA). The critic regresses each mode’s imagined cost (Algorithm 1). The policy is initialized by imitation learning from a θ-conditioned sampling-based model predictive path integral (MPPI) planner [24] that rolls out candidates through the same template using the portion of G observed along the logged camera path. During imagination training, a trust-region reg-ularizer penalizes the winning mode’s command deviation from this initialization. The initialization planner is itself θ-conditioned, not a per-robot specialist; the regularizer does not bound the deviation, and imagination training optimizes the full policy end-to-end, including mode selection.

The experiments evaluate the generalist policy across em-bodiments, the factorization, each component, unseen robots under template error, and the transfer to a real robot. 

A. Experimental Setup and Baselines 1) Robots and Benchmarks: Seven training robots of five locomotion types are simulated in Isaac Sim with their own low-level controllers, two additional wheeled robots are unseen in training, and a real robot is used in Sec. IV-F (Table I, Fig. 3). All robots use a pinhole depth camera (RealSense D435). Each episode is a local planning task toward a point goal, with a straight reference route in place of a global planner; collision ends the episode as a failure. An episode not reaching the goal within 25 s ends as a timeout, without early termination for lack of progress. Both benchmarks use a 

39 .7 × 30 .6 m indoor scene mixing an office, a laboratory, and warehouse racks [5]. Benchmark B1 uses its fixed layout and scores all 640 episodes per robot. Benchmark B2 adds 8–16 random boxes per 100 m 2 across 80 new layouts absent from all training data, with eight episodes each; this dense clutter lowers the SR of every method (Table II). B2 scores only episodes whose goal is reachable given the footprint and the time limit, and is held out from all design and tuning choices. 

2) Training Data and Parameters: Table I lists the main parameters. No robot is simulated in the world-model data: synthetic depth sequences are rendered by a robot-free rig of virtual cameras moving through the same scene with randomized box layouts. The rig emulates the ego-motion of diverse dynamics: its trajectories are rolled out by the template over a continuous parameter range that covers and extends beyond the training robots, including each training robot’s parameter neighborhood with its ˆδ and tracking noise, with recorded body oscillations superimposed; the camera mounts span heights of 0.13–1.85 m, including cross-robot pairings of motion and mount. ∆ξ is computed from the rig poses, G

from the scene geometry, and the semantic targets from the renderer. Holding out a robot removes all of its motions and mounts; those of the unseen robots are never included. 

3) Baselines: BC-θ is the imitation initialization of Hour-glass without imagination training: a single-mode behavior-cloning policy [7] on world-model latent states, whose com-mand head is copied into the Nm modes of Hourglass with small perturbations, so that all modes start from BC-θ. DP-θ is a diffusion policy [9] trained on the same latents and labels. Both are conditioned on θ. DWA-θ is the dynamic-window planner [24] and MPPI-θ is the sampling-based planner of Sec. III-C3; both roll out each robot’s identified template with 

ˆδ, widen the collision margin by the template error within the stopping horizon (0.01–0.34 m), and plan on an occupancy map accumulated over the episode with an online geodesic cost-to-go. Privileged MPPI runs MPPI-θ on the full G as a reference. All methods use the simulator pose and one execution layer: compensation of ˆδ, projection onto Uθ , and a goal-reaching controller within 0.5 m. 

4) Metrics: The success rate (SR) is reported per robot and as a seven-robot mean. The collision rate (Coll.), success weighted by path length (SPL, referenced to the shortest collision-free path for the robot’s footprint), and mean duration of successful episodes (Time) are seven-robot means. Paired per-episode differences are tested with McNemar’s test. Three training seeds of Hourglass, evaluated with an earlier execution layer, differ by at most 0.1 points on B2.

E. Generalization to Unseen Robots and Model Mismatch 

Deploying on an unseen robot requires only the identifica-tion of its parameters (Fig. 6(a)). On Jackal, whose size but not yaw dynamics lie within the training range, Hourglass transfers without retraining and outperforms MPPI-θ, whose failures are predominantly timeouts. On iw.hub, whose body is longer than that of any training robot, Hourglass fails mostly by collisions and trails MPPI-θ, which checks the footprint explicitly; the transfer is thus bounded by the body sizes covered in training. In Fig. 6(b), MPPI-θ leads on both benchmarks on the five robots with accurate templates ( εθ ≤ 0.45 m), while on TRON1 and M20, which have the largest εθ , Hourglass leads or matches both MPPI variants. Here MPPI-θ plans with the least accurate templates and the widest margins (0.26– 0.29 m). The imitation baselines also exceed it on TRON1, but on M20 only Hourglass does, suggesting that imagination training contributes beyond the imitation of planner labels; a sample of seven robots, however, is too small to establish a correlation. On B2, the gap to MPPI-θ is driven by collisions, while Hourglass incurs fewer timeouts.

In an offline replay of the first run (Fig. 9), the world model receives no images after the start and predicts depth from the camera ego-motion of the identified template; its 2 s depth error is on average less than half that of a last-frame prediction and lower at 98% of start times. Counterfactual templates with half or twice the yaw rate produce visibly different imagined depth under the same commands. For each imagined frame, the heading of its most similar real frame correlates with the counterfactual heading ( r = 0 .88 ), confirming that the world model responds to the ego-dynamics. Identification thus suffices to deploy on an unseen robot whose size is covered in training. V. C ONCLUSION 

This letter presents Hourglass, which factorizes cross-embodiment motion planning at the camera ego-motion. A generalist policy is learned once for an embodiment distri-bution spanning seven robots of five locomotion types, and deploying a new robot whose size is covered in training requires only identifying its parameters, even from simulation to reality. The results show that the embodiment need not be modeled in the world model, and that per-robot training brings no further gain once it is factored out. The main limitations are the latent clearance precision near obstacles, the body sizes covered in training, and evaluation in a single simulated building. Future work will address these issues with near-field geometric representations and broader training data with real-world logs, and will integrate global planning for long-horizon tasks in diverse scenes with dynamic obstacles.
