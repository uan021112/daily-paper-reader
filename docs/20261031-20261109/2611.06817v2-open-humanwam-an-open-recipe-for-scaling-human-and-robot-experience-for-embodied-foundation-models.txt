Abstract
Egocentric human video offers a scalable source of manipulation priors for robot learning, yet
translating raw data scale into generalizable robot policies demands rigorous data curation and
training recipes. We introduce Open-HumanWAM, the first fully open recipe for scaling egocentric
pretraining to 50,000 hours, anchored by Open-HumanWAM-50K—a curated egocentric video corpus
densely annotated with hand motion and natural language. Leveraging this corpus, we systematically
investigate pure human pretraining via closed-loop simulation evaluations and uncover an empirical
log-linear scaling law linking pretraining data hours to downstream robot success rates. Grounded in
these findings, we develop HumanWAM-Vanilla, a world–action model pretrained exclusively on 50k
hours of egocentric human video. Upon robot post-training, HumanWAM-Vanilla reaches 92.08%
success on RoboTwin 2.0 Clean2Clean and 68.88% on Clean2Random, alongside a 14.17% mean
success rate and a 19.00 mean progress score on RoboDojo. To push the capability frontier further,
we explore joint human–robot pretraining with HumanWAM-Goal, a visual-goal-conditioned world–
action model. Hybrid data scaling unlocks broad zero-shot generalization across both simulation
and physical benchmarks, propelling post-trained RoboTwin 2.0 performance to new state-of-the-art
levels: 92.70% on Clean2Clean and a substantial leap to 84.60% on Clean2Random. On physical
robots, HumanWAM-Goal further exhibits emergent embodied in-context learning, dynamically
adapting its manipulation behavior to unseen visual goals without parameter updates. To support
reproducible scaling research in embodied AI, we open-source the Open-HumanWAM-50K corpus,
data curation and pretraining pipelines, alongside the pretrained and post-trained HumanWAM
checkpoints, inference code, and simulation evaluation suites.

Learning from human manipulation offers a scalable path toward general-purpose robot intelligence [22, 33, 73].
Robot foundation models require diverse manipulation experience to acquire skills that transfer across tasks
and environments, but collecting this experience through teleoperation is costly [22, 23]. Egocentric videos
paired with hand-motion and language annotations offer a rich record of human interactions with objects
across diverse scenes and tasks [20, 43]. However, developing effective egocentric pretraining recipes that
consistently improve downstream robot performance as data scale remains under investigation.
Previous work has demonstrated the value of human experience for robot learning. EgoScale [73] shows
that large-scale human motion pretraining improves dexterous robot manipulation, while Being-H0.8 [4]
extends egocentric pretraining to joint visual and tactile world modeling. HumanScale [33] further shows that,
after robot post-training, validation action loss on held-out real-robot trajectories decreases log-linearly with
egocentric pretraining hours. Dyna-2 [14] extends egocentric pretraining to one million hours and reports a
scaling law in zero-shot robot action prediction, with corresponding improvements in robot task performance
after post-training. These studies establish the empirical value of egocentric scaling, motivating further
analysis of data diversity and the robot supervision required for downstream adaptation.
We present Open-HumanWAM, a fully open recipe for scaling egocentric pretraining for robot learning. Within
a unified world–action model framework, we explore pretraining exclusively on 100 to 50,000 hours of human
egocentric data, using a common robot post-training protocol and closed-loop simulation for controlled
and reproducible comparisons of downstream robot task success. With curated data and a unified action
representation, we observe an empirical log-linear scaling law relating pretraining hours to out-of-distribution
success rates across two different attention configurations. Beyond duration, data diversity is a key scaling
dimension, as expanding task and scene coverage alongside duration produces steeper scaling curves than
increasing duration at fixed coverage. Larger-scale egocentric pretraining also improves the sample efficiency
of robot adaptation, yielding higher success rates at matched demonstration budgets.
Following this recipe, we develop HumanWAM-Vanilla, a vanilla world–action model pretrained exclusively
on 50,000 hours of egocentric human data. After robot post-training, HumanWAM-Vanilla achieves 92.08%
success on RoboTwin 2.0 [8] Clean2Clean and 68.88% on Clean2Random, together with a mean success rate
of 14.17% and a mean progress score of 19.00 on RoboDojo [9]. The improvements extend to physical robots
across multi-step, deformable-object, and long-horizon manipulation tasks. Under changes in tablecloth and
3


lighting, mean success across four tasks increases from 2.5% without egocentric pretraining to 67.5% with
HumanWAM-Vanilla, demonstrating stronger generalization.
To further push the model capability boundary, we extend our exploration to joint human-robot pretraining
and propose HumanWAM-Goal, a world–action model with visual-goal conditioning. Human data provide
broad manipulation experience, while robot data ground this experience in embodiment-specific perception
and control [25, 73]. Visual goals specify desired object configurations and spatial relationships, providing task
context for zero-shot execution. With the specially designed unified action representation, HumanWAM-Goal
architecture, and the hybrid training recipe, the co-trained model supports out-of-the-box execution without
parameter updates during deployment. We evaluate data scaling by expanding the robot corpus and the human
data. The performance scaling trend persists in this hybrid data setting. On physical robots, adding 45.9k
hours of human data to a fixed 14.6k-hour robot corpus increases mean zero-shot success across 20 tasks from
20.0% to 30.5%. On RoboTwin 2.0, HumanWAM-Goal achieves 13.94% mean zero-shot success rate. After
post-training, it reaches 92.70% on Clean2Clean and 84.60% on Clean2Random, yielding a state-of-the-art
average success rate of 88.65% on RoboTwin 2.0.
HumanWAM-Goal exhibits an emergent capacity for embodied in-context learning through visual goals. In
real-robot experiments, the same policy adapts its execution to different desired outcomes specified by goal
images without parameter updates. This capability is already present after joint pretraining and is retained
when the model is post-trained for more complex, multi-step manipulation. Post-trained policies also follow
goals depicted in egocentric human images, indicating that task-relevant spatial relationships can guide robot
behavior across differences in embodiment and visual appearance.
To support reproducible scaling research in embodied AI, we open-source the Open-HumanWAM-50K corpus,
data curation and pretraining pipelines, alongside the pretrained and post-trained HumanWAM checkpoints,
inference code, and simulation evaluation suites. Open-HumanWAM-50K is a curated and aligned 50,000-hour
egocentric pretraining corpus with hand-motion and language annotations, and our experiments demonstrate
consistent gains in downstream robot generalization as pretraining on this corpus scales, with out-of-distribution
success rates following an empirical log-linear scaling law.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
1. A systematic study of egocentric scaling for robot generalization. We present Open-HumanWAM,
a fully open recipe for scaling pure egocentric pretraining from 100 to 50k hours.
We identify an
empirical log-linear scaling law in downstream robot task success rates and systematically analyze the
benefits of egocentric scaling for robot generalization and adaptation. Building on this recipe, we develop
HumanWAM-Vanilla, which achieves success rates of 92.08% on RoboTwin 2.0 Clean2Clean and 68.88%
on Clean2Random after robot post-training, alongside competitive performance on RoboDojo.
2. Emerging robot capabilities through hybrid human–robot data scaling. With HumanWAM-Goal, we show

our scaling studies and HumanWAM models. The release includes Open-HumanWAM-50K, a curated and
aligned 50k-hour egocentric corpus with hand-motion and language annotations, together with training
code, model checkpoints and evaluation protocols.

Building an Open Egocentric Pretraining Corpus
To support systematic scaling studies and large-scale egocentric pretraining, we build Open-HumanWAM-50K,
an open corpus from multiple publicly available egocentric datasets (Figure 2). HumanNet [11] is the primary
source, contributing egocentric videos collected from the internet and paired with hand-motion and language
annotations. We curate manipulation sequences based on hand visibility, motion stability, and manipulation
activity. To prepare these heterogeneous sources for joint pretraining, we map action annotations into a
unified bimanual end-effector representation, aligning camera coordinates, wrist axes, and gripper openness
across sources to provide a common action interface for subsequent robot adaptation.

2.1
Pretraining Data Sources
To support systematic studies of egocentric scaling and large-scale model pretraining, we assemble a corpus of
50,000 hours of human manipulation video paired with language descriptions and hand-motion annotations.
The corpus combines HumanNet, EgoSuite-Open100K, EgoDex, Gen-HumanEgo, and EgoVerse, which provide
egocentric videos with accompanying hand-tracking annotations. Table 1 summarizes the data sources and
their scales before and after curation. The corpus contains 31,396,799 physical episodes, which yield 35,934,235
continuous clips after curation, as individual episodes may be split into multiple retained segments. The
resulting 35.9 million clips cover a broad range of manipulation settings, including household, commercial
service, and industrial environments.
HumanNet.
HumanNet [11] is a large-scale human-centric dataset combining one million hours of first- and
third-person videos with language and motion annotations. We use an egocentric subset of internet videos
covering manipulation, tool use, and multi-step activities across diverse objects and environments. These clips
are accompanied by estimated 3D wrist poses, camera trajectories, and LLM-generated captions describing
the observed activities and motions. After curation and corpus selection, we retain approximately 40,744
hours for pretraining.
EgoSuite-Open100K.
EgoSuite-Open100K [75] is a large-scale egocentric human activity dataset. It provides
head-mounted video synchronized with head-camera poses and tracked hand poses. Each sample includes
21 joint transforms per hand, episode-level task and scene labels, and time-stamped subtask descriptions.
The hand annotations support wrist-pose extraction and the estimation of hand openness from fingertip
distances, while the task and scene labels enable explicit coverage measurements. We retain 6,358 hours from
EgoStandard and 724 hours from EgoPro.

EgoVerse.
EgoVerse [43] is a continuously expanding collection of egocentric human demonstrations con-
tributed by academic and industrial partners. Its initial release comprises 1,362 hours spanning 1,965 tasks, 240
scenes, and 2,087 demonstrators, combining standardized tasks collected across laboratories with open-ended
activities recorded in diverse environments. The data include 3D hand and head poses, camera poses, and
language annotations, with fine-grained subtask descriptions for industry-contributed recordings. We use only
a small subset of the Mecka source, retaining 24.81 hours after curation for Open-HumanWAM-50K.
Gen-HumanEgo.
Gen-HumanEgo [17] contains over 1,800 hours of human demonstrations across more than
10,000 tasks in domestic, commercial, industrial, and agricultural environments. The dataset provides 21
reconstructed 3D keypoints per hand, MANO [45] parameters, hand meshes, and depth maps. Hierarchical
video-, task-, and subtask-level language annotations describe activities at different granularities, with temporal
boundaries for tasks and subtasks.
EgoDex.
EgoDex [20] contains 829 hours of egocentric human demonstrations covering 194 tabletop manipu-
lation tasks. Collected with Apple Vision Pro, it focuses on active manipulation of everyday objects, including
dexterous activities such as tying shoelaces and folding laundry. The dataset pairs 30 Hz RGB video with 3D
head, upper-body, wrist, and finger poses, camera calibration, and natural-language task descriptions.
2.2
Data Curation
Effective large-scale pretraining depends on both corpus scale and supervision quality [34, 66]. We curate
egocentric recordings to retain active manipulation with clear visual context and temporally consistent motion
annotations. These checks remove unreliable annotations and prolonged inactivity while retaining usable
manipulation segments for pretraining.
Hand visibility.
We assess hand visibility from action annotations to retain manipulation sequences with
effective visual context. We project annotated 3D EEF positions into the image using camera calibration to
determine whether each end effector lies within the field of view. When only one end effector is in view, we
retain the sequence and restrict action supervision to the corresponding hand, excluding the other hand from
both the action loss and normalization statistics. Internet videos may also contain incomplete hand views,
with only fingers or a small portion of a hand visible near image boundaries, or excessively close views that
limit the visible interaction context. We identify and filter these cases using EEF depth, projected scale, and
the location and size of detected hand regions.
Action and camera stability.
Errors in estimated hand and camera poses can introduce abrupt jumps into
manipulation trajectories. For EEF trajectories, we measure differences between original and smoothed
positions, together with acceleration, jerk, and changes in position and orientation between adjacent frames.
Camera trajectories are checked separately for abrupt pose changes and for excessive displacement or rotation
relative to the pose at the start of each chunk. We remove frames that exceed any stability threshold and
split sequences at those frames, retaining continuous segments for pretraining.
6


Idle filtering.
Long periods of inactivity introduce redundant static samples to the pretraining corpus. We
measure EEF positional variation within 32-frame sliding windows and classify a window as inactive when
this variation is at or below the motion threshold. We trim inactive portions from both ends of each chunk
and discard chunks with fewer than 70% active windows.
2.3
Unified Action Representation
Joint pretraining on heterogeneous egocentric datasets requires consistent action supervision across sources.
Although these datasets broaden the range of manipulation experience, their raw action data can encode
similar physical motions differently, complicating the learning of shared visual–action relationships. We
therefore align human trajectories and robot actions within a unified bimanual end-effector space. This
alignment gives actions consistent geometric meaning across sources, allowing diverse demonstrations to
contribute to a common action prediction objective.

3
Exploration of Pure Egocentric Data Scaling
Egocentric data offer a promising route to scaling robot foundation models through abundant and diverse human
manipulation experience. Prior work has investigated how pretraining exclusively on human egocentric data
affects performance on downstream real-robot tasks [33, 73]. However, scaling outcomes vary across pretraining
recipes, with limited or inconsistent downstream generalization gains reported in other settings [50, 56]. We
first establish empirical scaling laws for egocentric pretraining with our proposed recipe, then examine
data diversity and the sample efficiency of robot adaptation, and finally conduct controlled analyses of the
pretraining and adaptation recipe.
We organize the scaling analysis around the following questions:
• Q1. What improves in downstream robot policies as egocentric pretraining scales from 100 to 50k hours?
• Q2. How important is data diversity for effective egocentric scaling?
• Q3. How does scaling egocentric pretraining affect the sample efficiency of robot adaptation?
• Q4. Does cross-source action alignment matter for egocentric pretraining?
• Q5. Does predicting human motion during egocentric pretraining improve robot action learning?
• Q6. Can egocentric pretraining surpass robot pretraining at matched data scales?
8


• Q7. How do we extend single-view egocentric pretraining to multi-view robot policies?
3.1
Pretraining and Evaluation Protocol
To study scaling in pure egocentric pretraining, we adopt a unified world–action model backbone and a
standardized training and evaluation pipeline (Figure 3). After pretraining on human egocentric data, we
adapt each model to RoboTwin 2.0 using clean demonstrations and measure its task success rate in closed-loop
evaluation. Standardized tasks and success criteria, controlled environmental variation, and shared evaluation
seeds support reproducible comparisons across pretraining scales and training settings. Evaluations within the
demonstration distribution and under environmental randomization measure in-distribution performance and
out-of-distribution generalization, respectively. Below, we describe the model architecture and the pretraining,
robot post-training, and evaluation procedures.
3.1.1
Model.
Our experiments build on HumanWAM-Vanilla, a world–action model that jointly generates future video
and actions from a single observation and a language instruction. The model extends Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [53]
with an action expert that has the same depth as the video backbone, forming a Mixture-of-Transformers
architecture. Following prior work [35, 67, 70], we apply a widely used isolated attention mask in most
experiments, allowing action tokens to attend to the first video frame but not to future video frames. During
inference, the model can therefore omit future video generation and denoise only the action chunk, enabling
fast policy execution. We use the unified 16-D bimanual end-effector action space defined in Section 2.3 and
compute delta poses in the camera coordinate frame relative to the first-frame observation.
3.1.2
Pretraining.
We pretrain on different egocentric corpora with
the same training recipe. We use the AdamW opti-

weight decay of 0.01. The learning rate follows a co-
sine schedule over 100k steps, with a linear warm-up
during the first 5% of the steps. The training budget
is adapted to the corpus size. In preliminary runs
we observed that smaller corpora saturate quickly
and that their generalization can even degrade with
further training, so a fixed budget would either under-
train the large corpora or over-train the small ones.
We therefore adjust the global batch size according to
the corpus size, as listed in Table 2, and we post-train
and evaluate checkpoints saved every 10k pretraining

3.2
Scaling Egocentric Data from 100 to 50k Hours: What Improves?
Whether and how scaling human data improves robot policy performance is a central question for robot
foundation models. We investigate this question through a systematic study of egocentric pretraining from 100
to 50,000 hours. Under the same pretraining recipe, we find that scaling egocentric pretraining consistently
improves downstream generalization across all three settings. We validate this trend under both isolated
attention and inverse-dynamics (IDM) attention, which additionally allows action tokens to attend to the
future video.
We first examine scaling with isolated attention. Models are pretrained on 100, 500, 1,000, 5,000, and
10,000 hours of egocentric data, then post-trained and evaluated under a common downstream protocol.

policy performance on RoboTwin. On clean, success increases from 71.2% at 100 hours to 80.0% at 10,000
hours, compared with 69.5% without egocentric pretraining. These gains indicate improved in-distribution
performance under the same post-training protocol. The benefits are larger under distribution shift. From
100 to 10,000 hours, scene-ood success rises from 25.2% to 58.1%, while random success increases from 8.4%

settings closely follow the empirical log-linear scaling law observed with isolated attention, with higher R2
values of 0.969 for scene-ood and 0.955 for random. This consistency across the two model configurations
suggests that the generalization benefits of scaling egocentric pretraining extend beyond a specific attention
design and action representation, even when in-distribution performance is already strong.

yielded lower validation action loss on held-out real-robot trajectories, particularly on tasks excluded from
post-training. We extend this matched-scale comparison to closed-loop task success on RoboTwin to examine
how the relative performance of the two pretraining sources varies with data scale.

ABC-130k [2], and our in-house Cobot Magic data as shown in Table 4. Both series use only head-camera
observations during pretraining, given the effects of additional wrist-camera views discussed in Section 3.8. We
curate the robot corpora from language-annotated trajectories with verified head-camera extrinsics, excluding
data with base or head-camera motion.

4
Pure Egocentric Data Foundation Model Training
Building on the exploration in Section 3, we develop HumanWAM-Vanilla, a world–action model pretrained
exclusively on 50,000 hours of curated egocentric human data with aligned action trajectories. HumanWAM-
Vanilla achieves competitive performance on the bimanual simulation benchmarks RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo
following robot post-training, with strengths in environmental robustness, precise control, and multi-step
manipulation. Real-robot experiments further show that egocentric pretraining improves task success over a
baseline without egocentric pretraining, with particularly large gains under visual distribution shifts.
4.1
Model and Training Setup
Model configuration.
HumanWAM-Vanilla follows the world–action architecture described in Section 3.1.1
and uses inverse-dynamics attention, allowing action tokens to attend to both current and future video
frames. During training, action denoising is conditioned on ground-truth future video latents with noise
augmentation. For policy inference, the model first generates future video latents and then predicts an action
chunk conditioned on these latents.
Action representation and normalization.
We use the unified 16-D bimanual end-effector action space defined
in Section 2.3. Each end effector is represented by its three-dimensional position, quaternion orientation, and
gripper openness. EEF poses are expressed as absolute poses in the camera frame at the corresponding target
time, with camera and wrist axes aligned across sources. Actions and proprioceptive states are standardized
using dimension-wise z-score normalization after action alignment. Pretraining statistics are estimated jointly
across the curated corpus, while mid-training and robot post-training use statistics estimated from their
respective training datasets. Invalid or unsupervised hand dimensions are excluded from both normalization
statistics and the action loss.
Egocentric pretraining.
We pretrain the model exclusively on Open-HumanWAM-50K curated and aligned
as described in Section 2, comprising human videos, language annotations, and action trajectories. The action
chunk length is 32, and video frames are sampled every four action steps, yielding nine frames per sample.
We optimize the model with AdamW [30], setting the global batch size to 8,192 and the weight decay to 0.01.
18


The learning-rate schedule spans 100,000 optimizer steps, with linear warm-up to 10−4 over the first 5,000
steps followed by cosine decay to 10−6.
Mid-training.
After egocentric pretraining with a single head-camera view, mid-training adapts the model
to multi-view inputs for robot policies. We continue training on EgoPro [75], a human egocentric dataset
with synchronized video from one head-mounted camera and two wrist-mounted cameras. Under Multi-view
conditioning (Section 3.8), current observations from all three cameras condition action prediction, while
future video prediction remains restricted to the head camera.

4.2
Simulation Experiments
Since our egocentric pretraining primarily covers bimanual tabletop manipulation, we evaluate HumanWAM-
Vanilla on RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo. Comparisons with existing vision–language–action models and
world–action models assess robustness to environmental variation and capabilities such as precision, memory,
and long-horizon execution.
RoboTwin 2.0.
RoboTwin 2.0 [8] is a benchmark built on SAPIEN [60] comprising 50 bimanual tabletop
manipulation tasks on the Aloha-AgileX embodiment, including pick-and-place, stacking, object handovers,
and articulated-object manipulation. We jointly post-train a single policy on clean demonstrations from
all tasks and evaluate it in two settings. Clean2Clean evaluates performance within the demonstration
environment distribution, whereas Clean2Random randomizes background and tabletop textures, lighting,
clutter, and table height.
At inference, the policy receives RGB observations from the head camera and both wrist cameras, together
with the language instruction and proprioceptive state. It predicts 32-step action chunks and executes the
first 24 steps before replanning from updated observations. We evaluate the same checkpoint in both settings,
with 100 episodes per task, totaling 5,000 episodes per setting. We report the mean success rate across the 50
tasks in each setting and the average of these two means.
Table 7 shows that HumanWAM-Vanilla achieves the highest Clean2Clean success rate among the compared
methods, reaching 92.08% after post-training on 50 clean robot demonstrations per task. The same policy
achieves 68.88% on Clean2Random, yielding an average success rate of 80.48% that exceeds all VLA baselines
and most WAM baselines in the table. The remaining gap to the leading WAMs lies in out-of-distribution
performance, with GO-3 Nano Preview reaching 81.48% on Clean2Random. This gap may partly reflect
architectural differences, as HumanWAM-Vanilla uses a basic world–action architecture without additional
specialized modules. These results support pretraining exclusively on human egocentric data as an effective
foundation for bimanual robot manipulation, with accurate task execution and competitive generalization to
visual conditions absent from the robot demonstrations.

Pushing the Model Capability Boundary with Hybrid Data Scaling
Scaling pure egocentric pretraining to 50k hours instills strong manipulation priors, substantially improving
out-of-distribution generalization after task-specific post-training. However, purely human pretraining leaves
an inherent embodiment gap: without exposure to physical robot morphology and control dynamics, the model
cannot be deployed directly and remains confined to a finetuning paradigm that requires demonstrations
for every target task. Conversely, while large-scale robot datasets [58] provide direct, executable control
supervision, scaling teleoperated trajectories alone quickly plateaus due to collection cost and limited scene
diversity. To overcome these single-modality bottlenecks, we investigate joint co-training on human and robot
data within an extended unified action space. Through systematic scaling experiments across progressive
human and robot data budgets, we characterize how hybrid data scaling expands the boundary of model
generalization and execution capability.
23


A central question is whether scaling hybrid training experience reduces reliance on task-specific adaptation.
We therefore evaluate our co-trained models across two complementary protocols. In the zero-shot protocol,
the pretrained checkpoint is deployed directly in simulation and on physical robots without target-task
demonstrations or parameter updates, testing whether larger hybrid budgets expand the repertoire of tasks
executable out of the box. In the post-training protocol, the same checkpoints are finetuned on target-task
demonstrations, assessing whether hybrid pretraining continues to raise the performance ceiling once in-domain
data are available, particularly under out-of-distribution shifts.
Deploying a generalist policy without task-specific finetuning also requires an unambiguous interface to specify
unfamiliar tasks. Because natural language instructions often leave object configurations, spatial relations, and
terminal states underspecified, we augment our world-action model with explicit goal-image conditioning [18].
The resulting model, HumanWAM-Goal, jointly predicts future visual states and executable actions from
current observations and a visual goal. Beyond disambiguating task intent for zero-shot execution, this
interface enables embodied in-context learning [28], steering a frozen policy toward distinct manipulation
outcomes purely by varying the visual goal at inference time.
To realize this framework, we first introduce our robot pretraining corpus (Section 5.1), extend the unified
action representation (Section 5.2), and detail the HumanWAM-Goal architecture (Section 5.3) alongside its
co-training recipe (Section 5.4). We then formalize our evaluation protocols (Section 5.5), examine zero-shot
and post-trained scaling trends (Section 5.6), and finally demonstrate emergent zero-shot execution, visual
in-context learning, and long-horizon manipulation on physical robots in Section 6.

Metric gripper aperture.
Whereas Section 2.3 normalizes gripper openness per dataset, zero-shot deployment

metric scale.
Reference frames.
While absolute camera-frame EEF poses excel when post-training adapts normalization
statistics to a single target embodiment (Section 4.4), heterogeneous robot platforms exhibit substantial
variations in camera mounting geometry, arm reach, and workspace offsets that impede direct zero-shot
transfer. To eliminate these embodiment-specific spatial biases during multi-domain co-training and zero-shot
deployment, we parameterize EEF motions as chunk-relative displacements anchored to the current observation.
Specifically, given an observation at time t, EEF translation targets at each chunk step τ are expressed
as displacements from the current EEF position projected into the anchor head-camera frame at time t,
∆p(τ | t) = RH(t)⊤(p(τ)−p(t)), ensuring a stationary, embodiment-agnostic reference frame across the entire
chunk even under camera motion. Similarly, EEF orientation targets are expressed as relative quaternions
with respect to the wrist orientation at time t, and chassis targets bτ are expressed relative to the current
base pose.
Hybrid supervision and joint dropout.
Human and robot trajectories share common input and output
projections for the 16-D EEF and 3-D chassis subspaces, whereas jt uses embodiment-specific projections.
Per-source validity masks exclude unavailable dimensions (such as joints and chassis in human videos) from the
action loss. During robot training, we additionally apply stochastic dropout to the joint branch jt, preventing
the model from over-relying on embodiment-specific joint proprioception and ensuring that the shared 16-D
EEF pathway remains self-sufficient. Because at parameterizes camera-frame metric displacements and
physical gripper widths in an embodiment-agnostic coordinate frame, its global pretraining normalization
statistics transfer directly to unseen robots at test time without target-domain recalibration.

5.4
Training Recipe
Training data.
We investigate human–robot data scaling through two complementary series. In the robot-only
series, we progressively expand the robot training corpus from 2,005 to 14,618 hours (2,005 / 4,261 / 8,475 /
14,618 hours), drawn from eight real-world robot domains (Section 5.1). In the hybrid series, we hold the
robot corpus fixed at 14,618 hours and incorporate either 15,901 or 45,938 hours of egocentric human data
drawn from the curated corpus in Section 2.1, yielding total training budgets of approximately 30.5k and
60.6k hours, respectively. This design isolates the benefits of scaling robot demonstrations from the additional
contribution of human experience at a fixed robot-data budget. Both modalities are represented in the unified
action space described in Section 5.2.
Goal images.
During co-training, goal images are sampled online from future observations within the same
trajectory using an identical strategy for human and robot data. For each training example, we sample a
clean head-camera frame from four goal-conditioning options: a future observation 2–8 s ahead (40%), the
episode’s terminal observation (20%), the final observation of an upcoming annotated subtask (20%), or an
empty goal (20%). Goal timestamps are constrained to lie at or beyond the end of the supervised action
chunk. Each goal image is encoded with a learnable goal embedding and serves exclusively as a conditioning
signal without an associated reconstruction loss. When the goal is omitted, its tokens are removed entirely,
enabling a single model to support both goal-conditioned and language-only execution.
Optimization.
All configurations start from the same pretrained Cosmos3-Nano-16B initialization. We
optimize the Generator, timestep embeddings, visual and action projections, action modality embedding, and
goal embedding, while keeping the Reasoner and VAE frozen. Training uses FusedAdam with a base learning
rate of 4 × 10−5, (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.99), weight decay of 0.05, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, and a global batch
size of 1,024. The learning rate increases linearly over the first 1,000 steps and subsequently decays linearly
over a 500k-step schedule. We apply a 5× learning-rate multiplier to the action projections, action modality
embedding, and goal embedding. All evaluations use exponential moving average (EMA) weights.
RoboTwin post-training.
For simulation evaluation, we initialize from the co-trained checkpoint and adapt
the model using 50 clean demonstrations from the ALOHA-AgileX robot per RoboTwin task, totaling 2,500
trajectories. Table 12 summarizes the adaptation protocols used in our post-training scaling and leaderboard
evaluations (Section 5.6.2). Action normalization statistics are computed jointly from the clean RoboTwin
datasets and pretraining data.
For goal specification during post-training, we use a mixture of ground-truth terminal frames and synthesized
goal images. Specifically, 20% of the training episodes use the ground-truth terminal observation as the goal,
while the remaining 80% use goal images synthesized by GPT Image 2.5 [40]. Each goal image is generated
from the initial scene observation and task instruction using a fixed task-specific prompt (Appendix A). To
ensure fidelity to the intended task, every generated image undergoes an automated review using ChatGPT
Astra High [39], which verifies the target object and destination, designated manipulation arm, object count
and appearance, and background and viewpoint consistency. Images that fail any check are regenerated and
reviewed until accepted; this procedure enforces visual and instructional consistency rather than physical
feasibility. At evaluation time, each scene is assigned a single generated goal image shared across all evaluated
checkpoints to eliminate goal variation as a source of performance differences.
5.5
Evaluation Settings
Benchmark.
To ensure reproducibility and fair comparison with existing robot policies, we adopt RoboTwin
2.0 [8], a standardized simulation benchmark for bimanual manipulation. Built on the SAPIEN physics engine,
RoboTwin 2.0 provides 50 manipulation tasks with predefined success criteria and controlled environmental
variations. We evaluate all models using the ALOHA-AgileX embodiment, which is never exposed during
pretraining. During each episode, the policy receives visual observations, robot states, and task instructions,
executing actions in closed loop. Task success is determined by the benchmark’s automated, task-specific
completion checks.
27


RoboTwin 2.0 defines two evaluation conditions: Clean, which uses the default scene configuration, and
Random, which introduces variations in backgrounds, lighting, object clutter, and table height. Together,
they assess task execution under standard conditions and robustness to environmental shifts across the same
50 tasks and identical success criteria.
Evaluation protocols.
We consider two complementary protocols to disentangle the capabilities acquired
during co-training from those gained through target-task adaptation (Table 12). In the zero-shot protocol,
co-trained checkpoints are deployed directly on RoboTwin 2.0 without any target-task demonstrations or
parameter updates, testing whether scaling hybrid experience enables unseen manipulation tasks to be
executed out of the box. In the post-training protocol, each co-trained checkpoint is adapted using 50 Clean
demonstrations per task to evaluate how pretraining scale affects downstream in-domain fit and out-of-
distribution generalization. For computational efficiency, zero-shot evaluations use 50 episodes per task under
each condition (Clean and Random), whereas post-training scaling and leaderboard evaluations follow the
official protocol of 100 episodes per task and condition (10,000 episodes in total).

6
Emerging Capabilities in Real-World Robot Behavior
While recent multimodal agents exhibit strong high-level reasoning and task planning [28, 48], translating
semantic intent into physical work requires a general-purpose action executor that can reliably control real
robots. Building on the simulation scaling trends in Section 5—which show that joint human–robot co-training
with visual-goal conditioning instills transferable visuomotor priors—we transition to physical hardware to
evaluate whether HumanWAM-Goal can bridge this gap. Central to our deployment is explicit goal-image
conditioning, which decouples high-level task specification from low-level sensorimotor control. Rather than
relying solely on language instructions that often leave spatial arrangements and terminal states underspecified,
an upstream planner or user supplies a visual goal as inference-time context. This unified interface allows
the model to serve both as an “out-of-the-box” executor for unseen tasks and as a scalable foundation for
downstream post-training.
Following our experimental setup (Section 6.1), we evaluate the real-world behaviors of HumanWAM-Goal
across three core capabilities. First, we investigate zero-shot real-world manipulation (Section 6.2) by
deploying co-trained checkpoints directly across a 20-task benchmark, quantifying how hybrid human–robot
scaling expands the set of skills executable without target-task demonstrations or parameter updates. Second,
we examine embodied in-context learning (Section 6.3), testing whether a policy with strictly frozen
weights—both before and after post-training—can dynamically alter its manipulation trajectory in response to
varying robot- or human-depicted visual goals. Finally, we demonstrate long-horizon execution (Section 6.4)
on post-trained policies, showcasing temporal continuity and subgoal chaining across extended, multi-stage
tabletop and mobile-manipulation workflows.

Zero-shot task suite and evaluation metrics.
Our zero-shot benchmark comprises 20 manipulation tasks
stratified into 10 easy and 10 hard tasks. The suite spans five core manipulation families: contact and
state changes (4 tasks), grasping and transport (5), articulated opening and closing (3), spatial placement
and alignment (6), and stacking and insertion (2). We conduct 10 trials per task, totaling 200 rollouts per
evaluated checkpoint.
We report both binary task success rate and a fine-grained progress score that captures partial task

Post-training protocol.
For intricate, multi-stage, or long-horizon tasks that exceed zero-shot reliability, we
adapt the co-trained checkpoint via task-specific post-training, resetting the optimizer, learning-rate schedule,
32


and data sampler. We retain the co-training normalization statistics and recalculate only channels with
substantially shifted distributions (e.g., the articulated torso of Astribot S1). During post-training, goal
images are sampled online from the final frame of one of the next two annotated subtasks, a future frame
within 4 s, or an empty goal. To improve robustness and enforce reliance on visual goals and shared EEF
representations, we apply 20% dropout to the joint-state input branch, 10% dropout to language instructions,
and weight the action loss 10× relative to the video prediction loss. We select the EMA checkpoint that
minimizes end-effector average displacement error (ADE) on held-out validation episodes.

6.4
Long-Horizon Execution
While the paired-goal comparisons in Section 6.3 isolate immediate responsiveness to visual context, real-world
deployment often requires chaining diverse manipulation skills over extended horizons. In such settings, each
action alters the scene state for subsequent subgoals, requiring the policy to transition smoothly across subtasks
without disturbing previously completed arrangements. We evaluate HumanWAM-Goal after task-specific
post-training across two representative long-horizon workflows: multi-stage desk organization within a shared
tabletop workspace and mobile household tidying across multiple work areas.
Multi-stage tabletop organization.
Figure 27 illustrates a continuous 14-stage rollout on a cluttered office
desk. Across the trajectory, the policy coordinates distinct manipulation primitives—capping and relocating a
mug (stages 01–03), sorting small stationery into holders (04–05), opening and closing two separate drawers
to store a tape roll and tape measure (06–10), and filing notebooks into a vertical rack (11–14). Because all
operations unfold within a confined workspace, later actions must navigate around earlier placements: the
capped mug remains undisturbed near the monitor, and closed drawers stay shut while the robot manipulates
adjacent objects. This rollout demonstrates coherent skill chaining across rigid-object placement, container
insertion, and articulated-object manipulation.
Multi-area mobile household tidying.
Figure 28 extends evaluation from a stationary tabletop to room-scale
mobile manipulation on Astribot S1. Within a single uninterrupted sequence, the policy sorts toys into
bins and stacks books at the main table (keyframes 01–08), navigates to a side table to collect and discard
two plastic bottles into a waste bin (09–15), traverses to two chairs to gather deformable garments into a
laundry hamper (16–27), and finally returns to the organized tabletop (28). By coupling base navigation
with bimanual control in the unified 49-D hybrid action space (Section 5.2), the model transitions seamlessly
across spatial zones and object modalities—from rigid toys and bottles to deformable clothing. Together,
these demonstrations show that HumanWAM-Goal can sustain temporally consistent, multi-skill execution
across both dense tabletop layouts and extended mobile-manipulation workflows.

7.2
Pure Egocentric Pretraining
Pretraining on human manipulation provides visual representations and action priors for robot learning before
embodiment-specific adaptation [22, 36]. Being-H0 [31] develops human-motion pretraining for dexterous
manipulation, while EgoScale [73] scales action-labeled egocentric pretraining to over 20,000 hours and reports
a log-linear relationship between data scale and validation loss. EgoScale subsequently introduces aligned
human–robot mid-training to facilitate embodiment transfer. HumanScale [33] establishes an empirical
scaling law relating egocentric pretraining hours to validation action loss after robot post-training and
finds that human data can outperform robot data at matched pretraining durations. Dyna-2 [14] extends
human-video pretraining to one million hours, reporting scaling in zero-shot action prediction on unseen
robot data alongside improvements in post-trained robot task performance. Building on these findings,
Open-HumanWAM systematically evaluates egocentric scaling through closed-loop policy success under both
in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions. Beyond data duration, our controlled comparisons establish
diversity as an additional scaling dimension and quantify gains in the sample efficiency of robot adaptation.
7.3
Zero-Shot Generalization and In-Context Learning
Generalist robot policies increasingly extend beyond task-specific adaptation toward direct execution in
unfamiliar tasks, environments, and embodiments [21, 65]. Contextual guidance provides a complementary
mechanism for specifying unfamiliar behaviors without task-specific parameter updates. π0.7 [42] incorporates
language, behavioral metadata, and visual subgoals into multimodal prompts, enabling compositional gen-
eralization and flexible control over task execution. Demonstration-based in-context learning additionally
conditions behavior on examples and interaction history [16, 28]. RoboICL [28] combines demonstration
context with accumulated interaction memory while keeping model parameters fixed. Our HumanWAM-Goal
experiments connect these capabilities to the scale of human experience. With robot training data held fixed,
we quantify the effect of increasing egocentric data on zero-shot robot performance and qualitatively evaluate
visual-goal conditioning before and after robot post-training.
8
Conclusion
We presented Open-HumanWAM, a fully open recipe for scaling robot learning with egocentric human
experience. Our study of pure egocentric pretraining from 100 to 50,000 hours reveals an empirical log-linear
scaling law in downstream out-of-distribution success. Expanding task and scene diversity strengthens this
scaling trend, while larger-scale pretraining improves the sample efficiency of robot adaptation. Controlled
experiments further demonstrate the value of human motion prediction and cross-source action alignment.
Following this recipe, HumanWAM-Vanilla achieves competitive performance in simulation and improved
robustness to visual distribution shifts on physical robots after robot post-training. Our release of annotated
data, training code, checkpoints, and evaluation protocols provides a reproducible foundation for further
research on egocentric scaling.
HumanWAM-Goal extends this exploration to joint human–robot co-training with visual-goal conditioning,
improving zero-shot transfer in simulation and achieving state-of-the-art average success on RoboTwin 2.0.
On physical robots, hybrid scaling with robot-balanced sampling boosts zero-shot success and expands task
coverage without target-task demonstrations. Visual goals also enable embodied in-context learning with
frozen parameters, steering policies across spatial and compositional variations as well as cross-embodiment
human goal images after post-training. Finally, post-trained policies support coherent subgoal chaining across
long-horizon desk organization and room-scale mobile household tidying.

Prompts for Goal Image Synthesis and Review
RoboTwin provides no reference goal images, so we synthesize one head-camera goal image for each scene. The
only scene-specific inputs are the initial head-camera observation and the task instruction; no expert trajectory,
terminal frame, or simulator state is used. Synthesis has two stages: a grounding stage localizes the task-
relevant objects in the initial image, and an editing stage turns the initial observation into a completed-task
observation. Each newly generated evaluation goal image is then reviewed and, if necessary, repaired.
A.1
Pipeline Overview
Inputs and resolution.
The initial head image is letterboxed to 288×224 pixels (width×height) and upsampled
4× to 1152×896 for upload. GPT Image 2.5 generates a 1152×896 image at medium quality, which we
downsample to 288×224 before restoring the original black letterbox pixels. The editing request contains
three images: (1) the upsampled initial head view, (2) a task-independent robot appearance sheet, and (3) a
pixel-registered overlay of the initial view that marks the grounded objects.
Task-specific reference annotations.
For each of the 50 RoboTwin tasks, we manually wrote one reference
annotation on a reference scene. It lists the objects (identifier, description, bounding box, and whether the
object moves or stays fixed), a natural-language description of the required final state, and visual acceptance
checks. A few tasks also include optional geometric guides: hinge anchor lines for articulated objects, final-
extent outlines, relocation cut-outs, or edit masks. These 50 annotations are fixed across all experiments;
Stage 1 adapts them to each new scene.
A.2

to the current initial image and returns structured JSON with a fixed schema. Its inputs are the reference

reference instruction and annotation, the current instruction, and RoboTwin’s public task description. The
prompt template is:

A.4
Goal Review and Repair
ChatGPT Astra High reviews every newly generated evaluation goal image. For each case it sees the initial
and generated head images at native resolution, the instruction, the public task description, and the Stage-1
grounding. It treats the grounding as fallible and locates objects independently in the initial image, then
returns pass, fail, or uncertain with case-specific evidence. The review rubric is:
Check target/destination identity, exact instructed goal relation/arm when visually distinguishable, source removal/object
counts, appearance/size, fixed background/destination/camera, real grasp/contact/support, articulated hinge, and coherent
robot. Public full task only fills omissions compatibly, never overrides explicit instruction. Natural occlusion is
legitimate. Do not demand an entire trajectory in a single goal frame, invent new acceptance rules, equate image-side with
mechanical arm, or mark all cropped mounts uncertain. Pass means visible state is consistent with no clear defect; fail
means concrete visible contradiction; uncertain identifies concrete unresolved evidence.
The audit concerns whether the visible generated goal is compatible with the exact task under the fixed head camera; it
does NOT establish hidden physical state or execution history. If the requested endpoint is naturally outside the view or
inside a clipped container, all relevant visible changes and fixed geometry are consistent, and no visible contradiction
exists, use pass with an explicit visibility limitation. Retain uncertain when there is a specific visible ambiguity (which
physical object, actual contact, orientation, scale) rather than merely unverifiable hidden physics.
Repair.
Each non-passing verdict (fail or uncertain) comes with repair feedback and a repair stage. If the
grounding misidentified the source, receiver, or contact region, the reviewer supplies a corrected target from
the initial image and Stage 1 is rerun with this feedback. Otherwise, Stage 2 is rerun with the feedback inserted
before the task block, and the grounding is kept. Every replacement image is reviewed again. Regenerated
images are judged under a looser criterion than first-pass images: the reviewer accepts reasonable perspective
variation and mild size differences that can be explained by projection, but still rejects wrong targets or
receivers, duplicated or missing objects, wrong task relations, severe deformation, invented fixtures, and
disconnected or extra arms.
Review statistics.
Of the 7,032 evaluation goal images newly generated with this pipeline, 6,380 (90.7%)
passed the first review, 542 (7.7%) failed, and 110 (1.6%) were uncertain (Table 15). After repair, all 7,032
passed review; repaired images were judged under the looser criterion described above. The review checks
visual and instructional consistency only; it does not verify physical feasibility or task success in simulation.
The remaining 2,968 of the 10,000 evaluation goals are earlier images that a separate audit approved for reuse.

are averaged over trials and then equally over tasks.
