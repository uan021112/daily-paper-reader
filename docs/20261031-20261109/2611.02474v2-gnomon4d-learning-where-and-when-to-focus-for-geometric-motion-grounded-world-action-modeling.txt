WAMs add geometry as a training signal or a control inter-
face, or build native 3D models, but decide what geometry
to model rather than how the action should read it. Intu-

1. Introduction
Robot manipulation advances along complementary
routes: vision-language-action models (VLAs) map obser-
vations and instructions to actions [24, 49], world-action
models (WAMs) jointly predict future observations and
actions [18, 46, 56, 70, 93], and agentic recursive self-
improvement (RSI) refines skills or generates simulation-
ready worlds [1, 47]. All three aim to generate actions that
change object states to accomplish a task, and WAMs in
particular explicitly predict these changes to guide the action.
Most WAMs represent this future as video [26, 70, 88], inher-
iting the visual priors of large video foundation models [65].
RGB futures, however, entangle task-relevant state changes
with texture, lighting, and background, leading policies to
overfit to appearance and generalize poorly to unseen visual
conditions [42, 82]. As 2D projections of the scene, they
further lack the 3D geometry that precise manipulation such
as insertion and stacking requires [53].
Recent work brings geometry into WAMs along three
routes (Fig. 2). The first uses it as a training signal: depth or
4D trajectory fields supervise the video features [4, 17, 33,
77, 85, 90]. This supervision is dense over the whole scene,
mostly irrelevant to the task, and leaves no explicit motion
for the action to read: depth is frame-wise, and 4D fields
are discarded at inference. The second uses it as a control
interface: 3D traces, point dynamics, or object flow are
predicted and passed to a separate controller [14, 28, 29, 64],
which decouples world prediction from action generation.
The third route, native 3D WAMs, builds on 3D or geometric
backbones instead of video generators and predicts no future
video [19, 50, 57, 69], forgoing video priors and depending
on far scarcer 3D data.
More fundamentally, these designs decide what geometry
to model but not how the action should read it. Intuitively,
control-relevant cues concentrate in space at interaction-
salient regions, where an object moves differently from
the embodiment, and in time at transitions of this relation,
such as grasp and release. A plain Mixture-of-Transformers
(MoT) [35] WAM, however, leaves sparse, low-dimensional
action supervision to find these cues among dense video and
geometry tokens on its own, so the action expert tends to
memorize embodiment-centric patterns instead [37]. Prob-
ing the action attention of three released WAMs [17, 70, 83]
on RoboTwin 2.0 [11] confirms this tendency (see the sup-

To make these interactions explicit for the action, we
introduce Gnomon4D, an MoT-based WAM that jointly
generates future video, robot actions, and a video-aligned
4D geometric motion, i.e., GeoDelta (Fig. 1). For each
foreground pixel on the embodiment and the manipulated
object, GeoDelta records the image-plane displacement and
depth change (∆u, ∆v, ∆d) of the same physical point over
a short interval. Because the embodiment and the objects
share one motion field, their relative motion, the very cue
that marks an interaction, can be read off directly. Existing
geometric futures cannot provide this cue: per-frame depth
and point maps lack correspondence across frames, and 2D
optical flow misses motion along the line of sight. Defined on
the video pixel grid, GeoDelta also reuses the video VAE and
stays aligned with the predicted frames and their generative
priors. A dedicated expert generates it jointly with video and
actions and shares the denoising clock of the Action expert,
so the action reads the predicted motion as it forms.
Yet explicit 4D geometric motion representation alone
does not tell the action expert where and when to fo-
cus. Gnomon4D therefore predicts an embodiment–object
relative-motion field from GeoDelta and, conditioned on
the task, uses it to focus the action readout on interac-
tion regions and moments. Interaction-Salient Attention
(InSAttn) selects where to read by weighting tokens with
task-conditioned relative motion, and Transition-Anchored
Aggregation (TransAgg) selects when by pooling frames
around changes in this relation, such as grasp and release.
The resulting interaction tokens enter the Action expert resid-
ually, alongside its dense joint attention.
Finally, GeoDelta is embodiment-agnostic: it describes
foreground surface motion without encoding embodiment-
specific joints, poses, or control parameters, and can there-


fore serve as a shared quasi-action across human hands
and diverse robot embodiments. Existing human-video pre-
training pipelines instead treat estimated hand poses, such
as MANO parameters, as actions [15, 43], learn latent ac-
tions from human videos [76], or align human and robot
data through embodiment-specific retargeting and robot ren-
dering [45, 46]. By contrast, GeoDelta is a video-aligned
motion space of the embodiment and objects, shared di-
rectly by human and robot videos without mapping either
source into the other’s action space. We curate EgoManip-
5000H, 5,000+ hours of egocentric manipulation video fil-
tered from 37K hours of real-scene footage, and process
Robot-30H, 30+ hours of real-robot demonstrations, con-
structing GeoDelta supervision for both (see the supple-
mentary material). With GeoDelta as the common tar-
get, pretraining on EgoManip-5000H teaches the Video and
GeoDelta experts how an embodiment moves and interacts
with objects without any action labels, so mid-training on
Robot-30H can focus on grounding this motion space in
robot controls before post-training on target tasks.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Joint world–motion–action generation. Gnomon4D, a
WAM that jointly generates future video, GeoDelta, and
actions, where GeoDelta is a video-aligned 4D geometric
motion field that makes embodiment–object interaction
explicit to the action expert.
• Relation-guided interaction readout.
InSAttn and
TransAgg, which use the predicted embodiment–object
relative motion and the task to focus the action expert on
interaction regions and transition frames.
• Embodiment-agnostic pretraining.
GeoDelta as a
human–robot quasi-action that links action-free pretrain-
ing on EgoManip-5000H to robot mid-training on Robot-
30H. Gnomon4D achieves state-of-the-art results on three
simulation benchmarks and in real-robot experiments.
2. Related Work
World-action models.
WAMs use predicted futures for
control, decoding actions from video features or jointly de-
noising video and actions [26, 56, 70, 79, 86, 93], usually
with an MoT backbone [35]. Fast-WAM and GigaWorld-
Policy-0.5 train with future supervision but skip imagination
at inference [60, 83], VPP2 distills a visual planner from
event-level video pretraining [18], and InternW0-∆learns
Causal Imprint queries from 20,000+ hours of robot and hu-
man data [46]. We keep imagination at inference, yet actions
are ready halfway through sampling (Sec. 3.4).
Geometry in world-action models.
Geometry enters
WAMs along three routes (Sec. 1). As a training signal,
it supervises video features as an extra predicted target, such
as RGB-D [4, 17], pointmaps or geometric latents [23, 73],
and optical flow [12, 88], or as depth, trajectory, and 4D pri-
ors distilled only during training [33, 46, 52, 67, 77, 85, 90].
Frame-wise depth and pointmaps lack temporal correspon-
dence, 2D flow omits line-of-sight motion, and distilled
priors are discarded at inference. Control-interface methods
pass dynamic pointmaps, sparse 3D traces, or object flow to
separate decoders or controllers [14, 28, 29, 64], decoupling
prediction from action generation. Native 3D methods model
the world as 3D points, Gaussians, or 4D latents, or build on
geometric backbones [8, 19, 22, 34, 50, 57, 69, 72], forgoing
video priors and relying on scarcer 3D data. Gnomon4D
keeps the video prior and generates GeoDelta jointly with
video and actions for the action to read at inference.
Spatiotemporal readout for control.
Beyond what the
future encodes, other methods shape what the action reads
from it. Some compress the future into predictive states, joint
embeddings, latent intentions or subgoals, learned queries,
or an interaction-dynamics bottleneck [9, 16, 25, 32, 38,
46, 66, 75, 78]. Spatial selection draws on 3D grounding,
affordances, interaction-aligned pruning, active attention,
or end-effector distance [13, 41, 55, 63, 71, 80, 84]. Tem-
poral designs organize the future by latent motion chains,
subtask progress, or rolling denoising across replanning
cycles [41, 74, 92]. Interaction-aware objectives reweight
world-model supervision toward dynamic regions [58, 59]
rather than guide the readout. These cues are semantic,
embodiment-centric, or learned, and methods selecting in
both space and time use separate signals [41]. InSAttn and
TransAgg instead derive both from predicted embodiment–
object relative motion.
Human-video pretraining.
Most pipelines map human
motion into robot action spaces by treating estimated hand
poses as actions [15, 43] or through retargeting and robot
rendering [45, 46, 81, 89]. Others learn action-free 3D-aware
latent actions [76] or co-train WAMs with pixel, semantic,
or 3D-flow world targets [30]. Cross-embodiment methods
transfer 4D affordances, contact trajectories, or tool–target
motion from human demonstrations to robots [2, 20, 21].
GeoDelta is instead an explicit quasi-action that human and
robot videos share without cross-embodiment mapping.

3.3. GeoDelta: 4D Geometric Motion
GeoDelta describes the short-term 4D motion of fore-
ground surfaces between adjacent sampled frames. Let Πn
map a 3D point to its pixel coordinates and depth in the
camera of frame n. For a foreground surface point that
moves from Xn to Xn+1, GeoDelta stores at its source pixel
(un, vn) the displacement gn+1(un, vn) = Πn(Xn+1) −
Πn(Xn) = (∆u, ∆v, ∆d).
Measuring both positions in one camera makes the field
invariant to camera motion. Pixels without valid depth or
correspondence are masked out. The foreground consists
of the embodiment and the manipulated objects, so action-
driven embodiment motion and the resulting object motion
share one pixel grid and time axis and can be compared
directly. Describing surface motion rather than joints or
control signals, GeoDelta also places human hands and robot
embodiments in one supervision space (Sec. 3.6).
To reuse the frozen video VAE, we encode the three com-
ponents (∆u, ∆v, ∆d) of GeoDelta as the three channels of
an RGB video. Each component gc is soft-thresholded to
suppress label noise, normalized by a fixed scale ωc, and
mapped to an 8-bit value Ic by signed µ-law companding:

3.4. Joint World–Motion–Action Generation
Architecture.
Gnomon4D extends the Video–Action MoT
(Sec. 3.1) with a GeoDelta expert (Fig. 3a): Video imagines
the future appearance, and GeoDelta supplies its geome-
try and motion for the Action expert to turn into execution.
Each expert is a stack of DiT blocks, and all three read
the language instruction and proprioceptive state through
cross-attention. Both world experts denoise N future la-
tents, V and G, conditioned on clean tokens of the current
frame: the multi-view frame latent Vobs for Video, and the
encoded head-camera depth and foreground mask Gcond
for GeoDelta. Lacking condition tokens of its own, the Ac-
tion expert observes the scene through Vobs and Gcond and
denoises the H-step chunk A.
Denoising clock and causal mask.
For the three experts
to reinforce one another, joint attention must expose each
modality at the right time, which is set by the denoising
clock and the attention mask. Video needs many denoising
steps for its fine detail, whereas low-dimensional actions
need only a few, so a joint sampler should finish the actions
first. Sampling the noise level of each expert independently,
as in standard MoT training, spreads training over all noise
combinations, most of which inference never meets [17]. We
instead tie the noise levels σm of Eq. (1) to one sampling
progress u, which runs from 0 at pure noise to 1 at clean
outputs. GeoDelta and actions are both sparse motion signals
coupled through kinematics, so they share a clock that runs
twice as fast as that of Video:
σV(u) = 1−u,
σG(u) = σA(u) = max(1−2u, 0). (3)
Sampling thus denoises all three modalities jointly in its first
half. Once σG = σA = 0, GeoDelta and actions serve as
clean conditions while Video refines its details. Training
draws 40% of samples from this clock and the rest from any
σG = σA ≤σV, which also covers generating video from
clean actions. The attention mask follows the causal order
of execution: future Video and GeoDelta tokens attend only
to actions up to their own time step, and clean tokens never
attend to noisy ones (see the supplementary material).

3.6. Training and Inference
Training.
Because human hands and robot embodiments
share the GeoDelta space, the world experts can learn how
an embodiment interacts with objects from human video
alone. We therefore pretrain the Video and GeoDelta experts
on EgoManip-5000H without action labels. We then add
the Action expert, mid-train the full model on Robot-30H to
ground this motion space in robot controls, and post-train it
on each simulated and real-world benchmark.
Inference.
All modalities start from noise and follow the
clock of Eq. (3) over 20 solver steps, with noise levels shifted
as in Wan2.2 [65]. GeoDelta and actions become clean after
step 10, when the controller executes a prefix of the action
chunk and replans from the next observation. The remaining
steps refine Video only when a visual prediction is needed.
4. Experiments
We evaluate whether explicit 4D geometric motion im-
proves closed-loop control, where the gains emerge, and
which components of Gnomon4D produce them. The study
covers three simulation benchmarks, five real-robot tasks un-
der in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) condi-
tions, and ablations of the future representation, interaction
readout, and pretraining curriculum.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Baselines.
In simulation, we compare with representative
VLA and WAM models (Tables 1–3). On RoboTwin 2.0,
WAMs are further grouped by how they represent and read
out predicted futures: RGB futures, 3D geometry or flow
(depth, 4D geometric priors, or 2D optical flow), and struc-
tured readout that selects action-relevant information. The
real-robot baselines are Fast-WAM [83], X-WAM [17], and
FlowWAM [12], whose futures are RGB, RGB-D, and RGB
with 2D flow, respectively.
GeoDelta supervision.
GeoDelta labels require per-pixel
depth, embodiment and object masks, and cross-frame corre-
spondences, which public demonstrations do not provide in
full (details per benchmark in the supplementary material).
We replay RoboTwin 2.0 demonstrations from their seeds
in SAPIEN and regenerate VLABench demonstrations in
MuJoCo with the official scene sampler and expert skills.
Moving each back-projected pixel with the recorded rigid
motion of its robot link or object then yields exact GeoDelta
for both the embodiment and the objects. RoboDojo re-
leases only RGB videos, camera parameters, and robot states,
without the object poses or scene seeds needed to repro-
duce its scenes. Forward kinematics of the released joint
states still gives exact embodiment GeoDelta, whereas object
GeoDelta is estimated with DA3 [36] depth, SAM 3.1 [7]
masks, and RAFT [62] flow, with DA3 depth rescaled per
episode to match the rendered embodiment depth. On the

4.2. Simulation Experiments
Setup.
We train one multitask policy per benchmark and
report success rate (SR) throughout. RoboTwin 2.0 [11] eval-
uates bimanual manipulation on 50 tasks in clean and ran-
domized scenes. VLABench [87] tests language understand-
ing and commonsense reasoning with 10 task families on five
tracks: ID, unseen categories (Cat), commonsense variations
(CS), semantic instructions (Ins), and unseen textures (Tex).
RoboDojo [10] spans 42 bimanual tasks over Generalization,
Precision, Long-Horizon, Memory, and Open-Instruction,
and macro-averages SR and a partial-credit Score over them.
Results.
Gnomon4D achieves the best average result on
all three benchmarks. On the near-saturated RoboTwin 2.0
(Table 1), it ranks first in both settings and surpasses MECo-
WAM, the strongest geometry- or flow-based WAM, by 1.71
points. MECo-WAM uses 4D targets only as a training
signal, whereas Gnomon4D generates GeoDelta at inference
for the action to read. Against STARRY, which biases action
attention by distance to the predicted end effector, reading
embodiment–object relative motion adds 0.77 points.
The gains are pronounced under appearance shifts and
in precise positioning. On VLABench (Table 2), the mar-
gin over OpenWAM-Alpha grows from 0.6 points on ID to
7.4 on unseen textures, as GeoDelta encodes motion rather
than appearance. RoboDojo (Table 3) shows the second
effect. Gnomon4D doubles the SR of OpenWAM-Alpha
(23.86% vs. 11.92%), and on the Precision dimension it
reaches 28.24% against at most 20.42% for the baselines, as
these tasks demand exact positions in both the image plane
and depth, which GeoDelta encodes jointly.

5. Conclusion
We presented Gnomon4D, a WAM that jointly generates
future video, robot actions, and GeoDelta, a video-aligned
4D geometric motion field of embodiment and object points.
Because the embodiment and objects share this field, their
relative motion marks each interaction, and InSAttn and
TransAgg use it with the task to select where and when
the action reads. As a human–robot quasi-action, GeoDelta
also links action-free pretraining on EgoManip-5000H to
robot mid-training on Robot-30H. Gnomon4D leads on three
simulation benchmarks and a real robot, with the clearest
gains in precise manipulation such as insertion and under
visual shifts. Ablations show that GeoDelta helps mainly
through object motion and that the guided readout outper-


forms generic queries, suggesting that a predicted future
serves control through both what it encodes and how the
action reads it.
Limitations and future work.
Without simulator state,
robot GeoDelta is estimated from measured or monocular
depth, masks, and optical flow, whose errors on thin, reflec-
tive, or transparent surfaces mainly perturb ∆d. Human-
video GeoDelta covers only the hands and is rendered from
fitted MANO meshes, which compensate camera rotation
but not translation and omit the depth change of the hand
root. High-quality 4D annotations of real-world manipula-
tion therefore remain urgently needed. One scalable source is
agentic recursive self-improvement, which could rebuild real
scenes as simulation-ready worlds where GeoDelta is exact
or refine the estimation pipeline with embodied feedback.

Gnomon4D: Learning Where and When to Focus for Geometric
Motion-Grounded World-Action Modeling
Supplementary Material
This supplement first examines where the action reads
the predicted future (Sec. A) and how existing WAMs differ
in what they predict and how they read it (Sec. B). Section C
then details the joint attention mask and interaction readout
of Gnomon4D, and Sec. D describes the human and robot
data used for pretraining and mid-training. The last two
sections give data construction and experimental protocols
for simulation (Sec. E) and the real robot (Sec. F), including
the real-robot success counts.
A. Attention Probes
We examine whether the action readout of a WAM con-
centrates on embodiment–object interactions. The study
compares Gnomon4D with three released RoboTwin 2.0
policies, Fast-WAM, X-WAM, and OpenWAM-Alpha, with-
out updating their weights.
A.1. Probe Protocol
We use 50 clean-condition event windows, one per
RoboTwin 2.0 task, with matched observations, instructions,
and seeds. The baselines are probed through their action-to-
video attention. For Gnomon4D, we record the interaction-
token route of the benchmark checkpoint, whose 20-round
schedule completes actions after round 10. Its action-to-
token, temporal, and spatial attention weights are composed,
and the object-side and embodiment-side maps are then av-
eraged. For all models, attention is averaged over action
queries, heads, active denoising steps, and readout layers,
and then normalized within the head-camera domain.
Attention is measured in the future latent block containing
the event. Fast-WAM, whose action reads only the observed
frame, is measured on that frame instead. Simulator em-
bodiment masks and motion-associated object components
define two reference regions. The interaction region consists
of the motion-associated object and the embodiment within
3 cm of contact, and the rest of the embodiment forms the
second region. Motion requires a surface displacement of
4 mm over two steps and at least 40 moving pixels, and static
regions follow their overlap with the nearest moving frame.
Contact uses 3 cm proximity, and event selection also in-
cludes motion onset, lift-off (1.5 cm), and settling, merging
gaps of up to eight steps.
Reference masks are preprocessed to match each model’s
native input: a 384×320 three-view layout for Fast-WAM,
OpenWAM-Alpha, and Gnomon4D, and three 256×320
views with a 0.95 center crop for X-WAM. Each 8×10 fu-
ture grid pools four stride-four reference masks. Attention

C. Model Implementation
The joint attention mask determines which predictions
each expert can access, and the interaction readout selects
spatial and temporal evidence for the Action expert.
C.1. Attention Visibility
Each layer applies one masked softmax over the three
experts (Fig. 8). Its five token groups are clean current Video
Vobs, future Video V, clean GeoDelta conditions Gcond,
future GeoDelta G, and actions A. Current-observation
tokens attend only within their own modality, so noisy pre-
dictions cannot alter them. To learn the visual and geomet-
ric responses, future Video and GeoDelta tokens read all
world tokens and the actions up to the corresponding control
time, whereas actions read themselves, the clean conditions,
and all future world tokens. A zero-noise block becomes
a condition and stops attending to other modalities’ noisy
blocks. During the second half of sampling, future Video
therefore continues to read the clean GeoDelta and actions,
while these clean blocks no longer read noisy future Video
(Fig. 8b). The same visibility rule applies when training with
clean GeoDelta and actions paired with noisy Video.

D. Pretraining and Mid-Training Data
The curriculum uses EgoManip-5000H for action-free
pretraining and Robot-30H for mid-training with actions.
D.1. EgoManip-5000H for Pretraining
EgoManip-5000H is mined from a sub-pool of an
industrial-scale proprietary video library, in which egocen-
tric manipulation accounts for only about 5% of the footage.
Three stages refine the sub-pool (Fig. 10), and Table 6 reports
the clips and hours that each stage retains.
Real-scene filtering.
Production video operators split each
video into single-shot clips and keep only real-time record-
ings of real scenes. A clip is discarded if it is animated, CG,
or AI-generated, contains visual effects, or has low visual
quality. Static shots and clips whose timing or viewpoint has
been altered by fast or slow motion, time-lapse, editing, or
stylized camera moves are discarded as well. The 17.23M
surviving clips (37,132 hours) enter the next stage.
Egocentric manipulation classifier.
This stage selects
egocentric clips in which the camera wearer manipulates
objects. Running a large VLM on all 17.23M clips is pro-
hibitively expensive, so a VLM labels only the training data
of a lightweight classifier. Kimi-K2.6 [27] labels a subset
of clips from three cues: camera trajectories and 3D geom-
etry from VGGT-Ω[68] reveal how the viewpoint moves,
Qwen3-VL [3] captions and embeddings describe the con-
tent, and WiLoR [51] detections indicate whether hands are
present. A Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B [31] classifier trained
on these labels scores every clip, and the 3.42M accepted
clips (7,575 hours) form the egocentric manipulation set.
Hand reconstruction.
This stage splits the egocentric ma-
nipulation set by whether the hands remain in view. WiLoR
detects the hands in every frame, and we fit a MANO [54]
mesh to each detection by inverse kinematics. Clips whose
hands appear in at least 51% of the frames form the hand-
visible group of 1.45M clips (2,859 hours), which supervises
both the Video and GeoDelta experts. Their hand GeoDelta
is rendered from the fitted meshes during data loading, after
the camera rotation between frames is compensated with
the VGGT-Ωextrinsics. The remaining 1.96M clips (4,716
hours) supervise only the Video expert. Every clip also
carries a Qwen3-VL caption with a global intent and time-
stamped events, which prompts each training window.
Composition.
We annotate a subset of high-confidence
clips with Kimi-K2.6 scene and action labels and report their
distribution in Fig. 11. Vehicles are the most common scene
(38.5%), and showing, pressing, inspecting, and grasping
account for two thirds of the actions. The hand-visible group
contains more bimanual clips (45.4% vs. 29.4% overall), and
Fig. 12 shows examples from eight scene groups.
D.2. Robot-30H for Mid-Training
Robot platform and tasks.
We process Robot-30H from
publicly released real-robot demonstrations [91], recorded
on a dual-arm Realman platform teleoperated in VR at 30 Hz.
Each arm has seven joints and a parallel gripper, and a fixed
ZED2i camera provides the head view that our model ob-
serves. The demonstrations cover 53 tasks in household,
supermarket, restaurant, warehouse logistics, hospital, and
laboratory scenes. Each task is recorded in several scene vari-
ants that change the object layout, appearance, or category,
so the same skill appears in visually diverse settings.
Data scale and split.
The source release contains 11,220
episodes and 37.0 hours of demonstrations. We use 10,500
of these episodes (34.1 hours), which cover all 53 tasks
(Fig. 14) with a median episode length of 11.5 s. The last
two episodes of every task variant are held out for validation,
leaving 10,080 episodes (32.8 hours) for training.
Training targets.
Every episode is converted to the format
of the post-training benchmarks, with the head view resized
to 512×288 (Fig. 13). The recordings provide depth only
for the three auxiliary RealSense cameras and no camera
calibration, so DA3 [36] estimates the metric depth and
intrinsics of the ZED2i head view. Released instance masks
label the manipulated objects, and SAM 3.1 [7] segments
the robot arms. Where no masks are released, SAM 3.1
also segments the objects from prompts derived from the
task description. RAFT [62] flow between frames four steps
apart, combined with the DA3 depth at both ends, gives the
displacement of every arm and object pixel. Pixels that move
by less than 0.5 px are set to zero motion, which suppresses
depth flicker on static surfaces. Actions are the end-effector
positions, 6D rotations, and gripper states of both arms,
normalized to [−1, 1] with dataset statistics.

E. Simulation Experiments
We detail geometric supervision, post-training, and evalu-
ation on RoboTwin 2.0, VLABench, and RoboDojo.
E.1. Data and Geometric Supervision
All benchmarks label pixels as background, embodiment,
or object, and foreground GeoDelta stores the source-camera
displacement (∆u, ∆v, ∆d) to the next sampled frame.
RoboTwin 2.0.
We replay each released seed and trajec-
tory in SAPIEN, requiring joint states to match within 10−5.
Each frame records head-camera depth, actor segmentation,
and all robot-link and scene-entity poses. Robot links are
labeled embodiment, and task objects are labeled object to-
gether with any other entity whose center moves more than
2 mm. Pixels are back-projected with simulator depth, trans-
formed by the relative pose of their entity, and reprojected
into the source camera.
VLABench.
Official episodes supply depth and robot
masks but lack object segmentation and poses. We therefore
regenerate 6,575 demonstrations with the official scene sam-
pler, expert skill sequences, and success filter, changing only
the recorder to save primary-camera depth, segmentation,
and body poses per frame, and train on them together with
the official data. Franka arm and gripper bodies are labeled
embodiment, and other bodies that translate more than 2 mm
or rotate more than 1◦are labeled object. The rigid con-
struction follows RoboTwin 2.0, with a median deviation of
0.8 mm between transported and next-frame rendered depth.
RoboDojo.
Released episodes contain head- and two-
wrist-camera JPEG streams, per-frame intrinsics and ex-
trinsics, joint states, end-effector poses, and actions, but no
depth, segmentation, object poses, or scene seeds. Using the
released joint states and ARX X5 URDF, we render head-
view robot depth and link indices in SAPIEN and derive
embodiment GeoDelta from the forward-kinematic motion
of each link. Remaining pixels use DA3 depth, SAM 3.1
object masks, and RAFT flow to the next sampled frame.

Post-training.
Gnomon4D is post-trained for 5k steps
on the 250 demonstrations after pretraining on EgoManip-
5000H and mid-training on Robot-30H. We train one policy
across all five tasks, conditioned on the task instruction. Fast-
WAM, X-WAM, and FlowWAM are fine-tuned from their
released weights on the same demonstrations, with shared
RGB views, action normalization, and execution settings.
X-WAM uses the aligned sensor depth, and FlowWAM con-
structs flow supervision with its public implementation.
Inference.
Gnomon4D follows the coupled 20-step de-
noising schedule, with GeoDelta and actions ready after step
10. The controller executes the first 16 of the 32 predicted
actions, then acquires a new observation and replans. The
remaining denoising steps refine Video only when a visual
prediction is requested.
F.4. Evaluation and Results
Each method runs 40 ID and 40 OOD trials per task (400
in total), with methods alternating on shared initial configura-
tions. ID trials vary object and container placements within
the training range. OOD trials change one visual factor at a
time (Table 9), with 10 trials per factor and task. The goal
and all other conditions stay fixed, and appearance shifts pre-
serve the block–bin color correspondence in Color Sorting.
Tables 10 and 11 report complete-task success counts by
task and by visual shift, using the criteria in Table 8. These
counts underlie Fig. 5 of the main paper: Gnomon4D com-
pletes 165 of the 200 ID trials and 156 of the 200 OOD trials,
corresponding to 82.5% and 78.0% SR.
