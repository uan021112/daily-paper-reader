Abstract
Georeferenced 3D reconstruction from ground images requires recovering not
only the local scene geometry, but also localizing every camera center and 3D
point in a geographic coordinate frame. Existing cross-view geo-localization
methods are able to estimate the GPS coordinate of a single ground camera, but
do not reconstruct dense 3D geometry, while recent feed-forward multi-view
3D reconstruction models predict point maps and camera poses only in a local
coordinate frame. We introduce X-VGGT, a feed-forward cross-view geometry
transformer that unifies these tasks by jointly processing a set of ground-level
images and a georeferenced satellite image to predict ground-view camera poses,
dense 3D point maps, and a scene-level transform parameterized by gravity, scale,
yaw and translation. This transform projects the local reconstruction into the
satellite plane, resulting in the geo-registration of all ground images with the
georeferenced satellite image. We further propose an evaluation protocol for this
joint task and show that, across multiple cross-view datasets, X-VGGT produces
accurate georeferenced reconstructions and outperforms prior cross-view geo-
localization and multi-view 3D reconstruction models. Project page: https:
//dshatwell23.github.io/xvggt-page/.

Introduction
Ground-level images are easy to collect and can be used to reconstruct detailed 3D models; however,
image-based reconstruction methods typically recover geometry only up to an unknown global scale.
In recent years, high-resolution satellite images have become widely available across many regions
of the world. Since these images are often georeferenced, their image coordinates can be mapped
to real-world geographic locations, providing GPS information for each pixel. This makes them an
ideal complementary source of information for image-based 3D reconstruction, as they can provide
not only a metric reference for resolving scale ambiguity, but also a global coordinate frame for
localizing the reconstructed cameras and 3D points.
However, using satellite images as a geographic reference requires solving a difficult cross-view
registration problem: ground-level and satellite images observe the same scene from drastically
different viewpoints, with large changes in appearance, scale, and visible structure. A model that
can overcome this gap would enable practical applications in autonomous driving and robotics in
GPS-denied areas, disaster response from localized post-event imagery, and infrastructure inspection,
where observed assets and defects could be placed directly on a map.
Prior work addresses important parts of this problem, but not the full setting. Cross-view geo-
localization methods are trained to estimate ground-view camera locations within a satellite image
[35, 50, 37, 36, 54, 55, 20], but they are not designed to localize individual image pixels. This
capability is known as geo-registration, and requires additional 3D understanding about the scene
geometry. Recent feed-forward 3D reconstruction models are able recover camera poses and 3D

Georeferenced cross-view 3D reconstruction lies at the intersection of these two tasks: using both
ground and satellite images, the goal is to generate a metric 3D reconstruction where all ground-level
image pixels are localized in a geographic coordinate frame. To address this problem, we introduce
X-VGGT, a Cross(X)-View Geometry Grounded Transformer for georeferenced cross-view 3D
reconstruction. Given a set of ground images and a georeferenced satellite image, X-VGGT predicts
ground-view camera extrinsics, intrinsics, metric dense 3D point maps, and a scene-level satellite
alignment transform used to geo-localize image pixels and camera centers, as shown in Figure 1.
The satellite image is processed jointly with the ground images through a multi-view transformer,
allowing them to exchange information. Then, a dedicated Satellite Alignment Head predicts a
ground-to-satellite coordinate transform parameterized by gravity, yaw, scale, and translation, which
projects the reconstructed camera centers and dense point maps into the georeferenced satellite plane,
thus enabling dense pixel-level geo-registration.
In summary, our key contributions are as follows:
• We propose X-VGGT, a feed-forward cross-view 3D reconstruction model that jointly uses
ground images and a georeferenced satellite image to predict cameras, metric 3D point maps,
and a satellite-ground alignment transform that geo-localizes all ground image pixels.
• We enable georeferenced 3D reconstruction from flexible sets of ground-view inputs: X-
VGGT grounds camera centers and dense point maps in the satellite coordinate frame while
supporting ordered sequences or unordered sets of limited-FOV images.
• We introduce a Satellite Alignment Head and georeferencing losses that directly supervise the
ground-to-satellite transformation, projected camera centers, and pixel-wise geo-registration.
• We introduce new training and evaluation protocols for cross-view 3D reconstruction, using
ordered and unordered sets of frames from unseen locations to evaluate the generalization
of X-VGGT and competing baselines.

Related Work
Geo-localization aims to estimate where an image was captured by predicting the camera GPS
coordinates using only visual information. Existing approaches are broadly grouped into classification,
retrieval, or registration. Classification methods discretize the Earth into geographic cells and use the
centroid of the predicted geocell as the estimated GPS coordinate [53, 42, 32, 27, 30, 10]. Retrieval
methods embed the query into a high-dimensional feature space and compare it against a geotagged
gallery, formed either by encoding GPS coordinates with a location encoder [41, 19, 33, 5, 15, 4, 34],
or by using images with known locations as proxies.
2


Image-retrieval methods can be divided into same-view and cross-view settings. Same-view visual
place recognition (VPR) embeds query and gallery images with a shared image encoder and ranks the
gallery by feature similarity [39, 3, 6, 1, 2, 17, 7, 40]. Cross-view geo-localization instead matches
ground-level queries against satellite images, using either shared or view-specific encoders. Prior
work has improved this framework with ground-view warping, hard negative mining, re-ranking,
stronger backbones, and auxiliary objectives [63, 11, 22, 62, 58]. Cross-view retrieval has also been
extended to videos using multi-stage pipelines and temporal aggregation [61, 44, 29]; however, these
methods typically assume ordered image sequences and are less suited to unstructured multi-view
scenes.
A key limitation of retrieval-based geo-localization is that predictions remain tied to the gallery
discretization: the estimated GPS coordinates represent the center pixel of a query, rather than
pixel-wise GPS locations of the query image. Recent cross-view localization methods address
this by registering the ground view within a georeferenced satellite image, typically through pose
search, BEV transformations, dense correspondence fields, or fine-grained cross-view matching
[35, 54, 50, 37, 47, 20, 36, 55, 56]. However, they remain primarily 2D pose-estimation systems:
most predict a single rotation and translation within a fixed satellite area from a single ground
image or panorama, without reconstructing dense 3D structure or placing multiple cameras and point
maps in a shared georeferenced frame. This motivates moving beyond local 2D registration toward
georeferenced multi-view 3D reconstruction.
Multi-view 3D reconstruction recovers camera geometry and dense scene structure rather than
only a 2D pose. Recent feed-forward geometry models directly predict this structure from images:
DUSt3R [49] and MASt3R [21] estimate point maps and correspondences from uncalibrated image
pairs, and several methods extend this paradigm to larger or more structured inputs through multi-view
prediction, memory, recurrent state, relative-pose aggregation, or video geometry [9, 48, 12, 59]. In
parallel, learning-based SfM and general multi-view reconstruction models are increasingly adapted
to predict cameras, depth, point maps, visual appearance, or metric 3D from sparse or dense scenes
[45, 46, 24, 51, 57, 60, 18].
However, these models are not designed for georeferenced ground-satellite reconstruction. They
typically recover geometry in a local coordinate frame with arbitrary scale and rely on sufficient visual
overlap, whereas nadir satellite and street-level images differ substantially in viewpoint, appearance,
and visibility. Recent methods partially address this gap by adapting 3D reconstruction models to
drone-ground pairs [43], drone-satellite pairs [25], ground images and top-down floor plans [16],
or satellite, aerial, and ground views [52]. While promising, these approaches do not directly solve
ground-satellite registration with real orthorectified satellite imagery: they either rely on drone
viewpoints, use non-standard top-down inputs, or are trained with synthetic perspective-projection
aerial images.
While extreme-view methods make important progress, they do not fully address the setting targeted
by our method: reconstructing arbitrary ordered or unordered sets of ground views directly in the
coordinate frame of a georeferenced orthorectified satellite image, without using any auxiliary modal-
ities. In this work, our aim is to connect cross-view localization with multi-view 3D reconstruction,
using satellite images to geo-register all cameras and pixels in a geographic reference frame.
3
Method
Given a set of ground images and a georeferenced satellite image, X-VGGT predicts ground-view
camera poses, dense 3D point maps, and a satellite-to-ground transform that is used to geo-register
all cameras and image pixels. In this section, we first formulate the georeferenced 3D reconstruction
problem (Sec. 3.1), then describe the architecture (Sec. 3.2), training objectives (Sec. 3.4), and
training strategy (Sec. 3.5). Figure 2 provides an overview of our method.

Training datasets. We train our model with KITTI [14, 35] and a modified version of Aerial-
MegaDepth [43], which we call Sat-MegaDepth. KITTI provides street-level sequences with cali-

augmentations, dataset construction, and optimization are provided in Appendix C.
4
Experiments
We evaluate X-VGGT on three georeferenced reconstruction tasks: camera geo-localization, pixel-
wise geo-registration, and local 3D reconstruction. We first describe the datasets, scene construction,
baselines, and metrics used throughout the section, then report quantitative results and ablations.
4.1
Evaluation Datasets
We evaluate X-VGGT on four cross-view datasets spanning different locations, sensors, and scene
layouts. KITTI [14, 35] provides driving sequences with LiDAR-derived depth and georeferenced
6


satellite images; although KITTI is used during training, evaluation is performed on the geographically
disjoint cross-area Test-2 split. We use the augmented KITTI release of Shi and Li [35], which
supplements the original dataset with Google Maps satellite imagery at zoom level 18, corresponding
to a spatial resolution of 0.20 m/pixel. Mapillary Metropolis provides synchronized multi-directional
driving images, camera poses, MVS-derived depth, and satellite images, and is used for zero-shot
evaluation. We further evaluate camera geo-localization on WRIVA [8] and ACC-NVS1 [38], which
contain unseen locations with calibrated ground images and GPS annotations but no ground-truth
depth maps. Additional dataset and satellite-image construction details are provided in Appendix D.
4.2
Scene Generation
We construct evaluation scenes according to the specific scene layout of each dataset: for KITTI
and ACC-NVS1, we use sequences of consecutive video frames; for Mapillary Metropolis, we use
consecutive multi-directional camera groups pointing along the front, back, left, and right sides
of the vehicle frame; and for WRIVA, we create unordered image sets with similar camera poses.
Each scene consists of N ground images and a 150m × 150m satellite view sampled around the
selected ground cameras. We create satellite crops such that the centroid of the ground cameras is
randomly placed within the central 100m × 100m region of the satellite image. We sample up to
1024 scenes per dataset for the main evaluation and use larger scenes with up to 96 ground images
for the ablations. Additional scene construction details are provided in Appendix D.

4.4
Evaluation Metrics
Camera geo-localization measures whether the predicted camera centers and orientation are correctly
grounded in the satellite image. Since X-VGGT predicts camera locations in normalized satellite
coordinates ˆui, ui ∈[0, 1]2, we define the location error as ecam
i
= ∥(ˆui −ui)∥2 ρsat, where
ρsat = 150 is the satellite scale in meters. Orientation error is computed by projecting each camera’s
principal ray into the satellite plane and measuring the angle between the predicted and ground-truth
2D directions.
Pixel geo-registration measures the distance between the ground truth and predicted pixel geo-
locations. We compute this metric by projecting the image pixels into 3D space, and then into the
satellite plane using the ground truth georeferencing transform. We report the location error between
predicted and ground-truth satellite coordinates over valid pixels whose ground-truth projections lie
inside the satellite image.
3D reconstruction evaluates the local multi-view geometry independently of satellite alignment. We
align predicted camera poses to the metric ground-truth cameras with a similarity transform, then
compute pairwise relative pose errors. We report relative rotation accuracy (RRA), relative translation
accuracy (RTA), and their joint AUC at thresholds up to 30◦.

5
Conclusion
We introduced X-VGGT, a feed-forward model for georeferenced cross-view 3D reconstruction
from ground and satellite images. X-VGGT jointly predicts ground-view cameras, dense 3D point
maps, and a scene-level georeferencing transform that aligns the reconstruction with an orthorectified
satellite image, enabling both camera geo-localization and dense pixel-wise geo-registration. We also
proposed training and evaluation protocols covering ordered and unordered ground-view inputs across
multiple datasets and sensor configurations. Experiments show that X-VGGT outperforms cross-view
localization and 3D reconstruction baselines while preserving strong local 3D reconstruction quality,
suggesting that robust georeferenced reconstruction requires coupling local multi-view geometry
with explicit satellite-frame alignment.

Appendix
A
Limitations
X-VGGT assumes access to an orthorectified, georeferenced satellite image with known spatial
resolution. Performance may degrade when the satellite image is outdated, low resolution, occluded,
or visually inconsistent with the ground views, since the model relies on cross-view evidence to align
the local reconstruction to the satellite frame.
The model is also trained with satellite crops in a specific physical scale range, roughly 150–250
meters across depending on the dataset. Applying X-VGGT to substantially smaller or larger satellite
regions may change the apparent scale and context seen by the model, and would likely require
fine-tuning or adapting the crop protocol.
B
Broader Impacts
This work studies georeferenced 3D reconstruction from ground-level and satellite images, unifying
cross-view geo-localization with 3D reconstruction. We hope it can serve as an initial step toward
treating these two tasks jointly. Potential positive impacts include improved mapping from ground
images, localization in GPS-denied or GPS-degraded environments, disaster response using localized
post-event images, infrastructure inspection, and robotics or autonomous navigation systems that
need to connect local observations to a geographic map.
As with geo-localization methods more broadly, responsible use should consider privacy and data
governance when applying the model to images of real-world places. The method should be used in
accordance with dataset licenses, local regulations, and appropriate safeguards for images that may
contain sensitive locations, private property, people, or vehicles. Since georeferencing errors can
occur, predictions should be validated before use in safety-critical or decision-making settings.

resize them to 518 × 518 pixels. These crops provide different satellite contexts within the larger

C.2
Coordinate Targets and Augmentation
The satellite supervision uses normalized [0, 1]2 coordinates with a vertical axis convention in which
larger values correspond to upward displacements in the satellite plane. Each dataloader returns
normalized camera-center targets ucam
i
and the satellite image scale in meters. During training, the
satellite image is augmented with horizontal flips and random 90◦rotations. The same transform is
applied to satellite-coordinate targets, the ENU world frame, camera extrinsics, and point maps so
that the georeferencing supervision remains consistent after augmentation.
Before computing the losses, we apply the VGGT-style normalization of camera extrinsics and point
maps into the local reconstruction frame. Because this normalization changes the coordinate frame in
which the model predicts point maps and camera poses, we recompute the ground-truth georeferencing
parameters ϕ∗, dense satellite targets, and valid dense projection masks after normalization. This
recomputation keeps Gϕ∗, camera-center targets, and dense point targets in the same coordinate frame
as the model predictions.
C.3
Optimization
The model is initialized from the pretrained VGGT checkpoint. New satellite camera/register tokens
and the Satellite Alignment Head (SAH) are optimized from the start of training to learn the cross-view
alignment needed for georeferencing, while the camera and point heads retain their reconstruction
roles. The top four global attention blocks are unfrozen after 10% of training, while the camera
head, point head, and remaining default parameters are unfrozen after 40% of training. We use
separate warmup and cosine schedules for each parameter group, with the largest learning rate for
the SAH and smaller learning rates for the pretrained backbone and 3D heads. Training batches
contain 4–32 ground images per scene; all images are resized to 518 × 518 pixels, with zero-padding
for rectangular aspect ratios. We use the AdamW optimizer [26] with weight decay 0.05, bfloat16
automatic mixed precision, gradient clipping, and train for 80,000 iterations on four H100 GPUs.
Models trained for the georeferencing loss ablation in Table 5 use the same training schedule, but are
trained for 10,000 iterations on two H100 GPUs.

D
Additional Evaluation Details
D.1
Evaluation datasets.
KITTI [14] is an autonomous driving dataset collected around Karlsruhe, Germany, including
urban roads, rural areas, and highways. The capture platform is equipped with four cameras, two
grayscale and two RGB, a Velodyne LiDAR scanner, and a high-precision GPS/IMU system. We
use the augmented benchmark of Shi and Li [35], which provides satellite images around the vehicle
trajectory. For our evaluation, we use only the left RGB camera: the left and right stereo views are
highly similar, and our goal is to evaluate sequence-level ground-satellite georeferencing rather than
stereo reconstruction. We use LiDAR-derived depth maps for pixel-wise evaluation and report results
on the challenging cross-area Test-2 split.
Mapillary Metropolis is a city-scale driving dataset collected in Chicago. It provides synchronized
camera poses, georeferenced aerial imagery, panoramic street-level images, and four perspective
images at each timestamp, corresponding to the front, back, left, and right directions relative to the
vehicle. The dataset also includes multi-view-stereo reconstructions, MVS-derived depth maps for
the perspective images, and LiDAR point clouds. Although LiDAR points are available, they are
sparser than the MVS-derived depth maps for dense image evaluation, so we use the MVS depth maps
when computing pixel-wise geo-registration metrics. We use Metropolis for zero-shot evaluation,
since it differs from our training data in sensor layout, capture protocol, and scene distribution.
WRIVA is derived from the outdoor sites of the WRIVA Public Dataset [8]. Unlike KITTI and
Metropolis, which are vehicle-centric driving datasets, WRIVA contains unstructured image collec-
tions from six U.S. sites with cameras scattered around each scene. The scenes have substantially
different layouts from urban driving datasets, consisting largely of rural environments, open areas,
and training-ground-style sites relevant to first-responder and site-modeling scenarios. WRIVA
provides calibrated camera parameters and GPS annotations. Although the original dataset includes
low-resolution panchromatic and multispectral satellite imagery, these modalities are outside the
scope of our work, so we replace them with high-resolution orthorectified RGB satellite images from
Google Maps. Since WRIVA does not provide ground-truth depth maps, we use it only for camera
geo-localization and pose evaluation.
ACC-NVS1 [38] is a multi-view dataset collected for novel view synthesis across six real-world
scenes in Austin, TX and Pittsburgh, PA. It contains approximately 148K images captured from both
ground and airborne viewpoints, with calibrated camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and geolocation. In
our evaluation, we use the ground-level image sequences and supplement them with high-resolution
orthorectified RGB satellite images to support cross-view geo-localization. Since ACC-NVS1 does
not provide ground-truth depth maps, we use it only for camera geo-localization and pose evaluation.
D.2
Evaluation scene generation.
We construct evaluation scenes using dataset-specific sampling protocols that reflect the structure of
each dataset while keeping the evaluation format consistent across benchmarks.
KITTI scenes are generated from linear driving sequences. We sample contiguous or nearby frames
from each sequence to form ordered ground-view sets, using only the left RGB camera. This produces
sequence-like scenes where the model must estimate a shared satellite alignment from multiple views
along the vehicle trajectory.
Mapillary Metropolis scenes are constructed from consecutive capture groups. Each capture group
contains four perspective images aligned with the vehicle frame, corresponding to orthogonal viewing
directions. We preserve this multi-directional structure when creating the scenes, which provides
denser coverage around the vehicle than a single forward-facing driving sequence.
WRIVA contains unordered image collections rather than vehicle trajectories. We follow a VGGT-
style batch construction strategy: for each scene, we choose a seed image and sample additional
views with high pairwise pose similarity. This produces coherent local scenes while preserving the
unordered nature of the dataset.
ACC-NVS1 scenes are generated from ground-level image sequences. Similar to KITTI, we sample
ordered frame sets from each sequence to evaluate camera geo-localization under trajectory-like
motion, but on locations and capture conditions not seen during training.

centroid exactly at the crop center, we randomly position it within the central 100m × 100m region.
This prevents the evaluation from assuming a perfectly centered satellite crop while ensuring that
the selected cameras remain inside the image. For the main results, we sample up to 1024 scenes
per dataset with 16 ground images per scene. For the ablation on the number of ground images, we
construct larger scenes with up to 96 ground images.

Effect of Ground–Satellite Interaction
To study the role of joint cross-view encoding, we compare X-VGGT with a late-fusion baseline
that processes the ground views and satellite image in separate passes through a shared encoder. The
ground views retain token-level interaction with one another, but ground and satellite tokens do not
interact directly. The alignment head processes the two streams separately, pools the ground camera
tokens, and combines the ground and satellite summaries with an MLP to predict the georeferencing
transform. The camera and point heads receive only ground-view features.
Table 7 shows that late fusion increases camera localization, orientation, and dense registration
errors on both KITTI and Metropolis. For example, KITTI localization error rises from 24.62 m
to 43.58 m, while orientation error rises from 22.50◦to 95.07◦. These results support the value of
jointly encoding ground and satellite views for estimating the scene-level alignment.

Robustness to Satellite Configurations
X-VGGT uses satellite imagery to place reconstructed cameras and 3D points in a geographic
coordinate frame. We examine how this alignment is affected by the area shown in the satellite
image, the amount of visual detail it contains, and errors in its geographic coordinates. On KITTI
and Metropolis, we test these factors separately by changing the spatial dimentions, reducing the
resolution, and introducing spatial offsets, while keeping each scene’s 16 ground images fixed. To
19


compare all settings on the same scenes, we require the cameras to remain visible within the smallest
satellite crop and sufficient surrounding imagery to extract larger or shifted crops without padding.
Specifically, all camera centers must lie at least 5 m inside the smallest crop, and the available imagery
must accommodate the largest crop and shifts of up to 10 m. We select 512 scenes per dataset that
satisfy these requirements using a fixed seed of 0, before inference and independently of model
predictions.
These requirements exclude scenes with cameras spread over a large area or positioned near the
satellite boundary. Our main evaluation instead uses an independent selection of 1,024 scenes per
dataset without these restrictions. The two evaluations therefore contain different scenes, which must
be considered when interpreting their results—for example, the unmodified satellite images yield a
KITTI localization error of 12.88 m here, compared with 24.62 m in Table 2. To measure the effect
of each change, we compare it against the unmodified satellite image using exactly the same 512
scenes.
Satellite crop size. We vary the width and height of the satellite crop by ±25% around the standard
150 × 150 m region, keeping the crop center and ground images fixed. Each crop is resized to
518 × 518 pixels, and we update its geographic bounds and the corresponding evaluation targets.
Table 9 shows that larger crops increase localization and geo-registration errors on both datasets.
Smaller crops yield similar KITTI localization error and lower Metropolis errors, although KITTI
orientation error increases.

Oracle Alignment
The geographic accuracy of X-VGGT depends on both the reconstructed geometry and its alignment
with the satellite image. To examine how much error can be reduced by improving alignment, we
compare the predicted alignment with an oracle obtained from ground-truth camera locations. For
each scene, we fit a single Sim(3) transform, consisting of a rotation, translation, and uniform scale,
between X-VGGT’s 16 predicted camera centers and their ground-truth 3D locations. We apply this
transform to all predicted cameras and dense points, then project them into the satellite plane using
the ground-truth satellite transform.
Table 12 shows that oracle alignment substantially reduces camera localization and orientation errors
on both datasets. On KITTI, localization error decreases from 24.62 m to 0.09 m, while pixel-wise
geo-registration error decreases from 25.52 m to 7.65 m, indicating considerable room to improve
the predicted alignment. On Metropolis, camera localization improves from 14.35 m to 1.88 m and
orientation error decreases from 17.59◦to 3.78◦, but dense geo-registration improves only from
17.84 m to 16.84 m. Thus, accurately aligning camera centers does not by itself ensure accurate
placement of the dense reconstruction.
