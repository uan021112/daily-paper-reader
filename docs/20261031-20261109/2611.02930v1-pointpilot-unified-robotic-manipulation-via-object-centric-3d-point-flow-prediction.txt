# ABSTRACT 

Generalization remains a central challenge in robotics, calling for policies that ad-dress domain variations from multiple dimensions such as diverse embodiments, camera poses, and/or scene layouts. In order to approach this problem, we intro-duce PointPilot , a task-focused and 3D aligned manipulation framework built on a 3D space point-flow prediction. Rather than modeling the global scene, PointPilot only represents task-focused objects (robot grippers treated as objects), avoiding the prediction of potentially redundant scene parts unrelated to the manipulation tasks. Moreover, PointPilot aligns 3D spatial representation in one unified coor-dinate and supports closed-loop and fully automatic action execution across dif-ferent manipulation modes from object point flow without a separately trained action expert, while existing works typically need an extra action head or are (semi-)automatic and single-arm mode specific. Given sensor observations and language instructions, our model jointly learns subtask planning, task-relevant ob-ject grounding, and 3D point flow prediction in an end-to-end way. By processing heterogeneous data in a shared 3D point flow space, PointPilot is able to absorb demonstrations from different embodiments. Extensive simulative and real world experiments validate our method. Notably , we demonstrate the zero-shot embod-iment generalization in the PointPilot — one trained policy can be deployed onto previously unseen embodiments and successfully complete manipulation tasks, without any additional fine-tuning. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

A central promise of generalist robot learning is that manipulation policies should improve as they absorb increasingly diverse data. However, realizing this promise requires more than scaling model parameters and demonstration hours. Robot data is heterogeneous not only in visual appearance, task semantics, and environment configuration, but also in embodiments. Native actions from het-erogeneous data lack a shared representation form, preventing action prediction from directly serv-ing as a unified supervision space. The choice of prediction space therefore determines whether heterogeneous demonstrations provide compatible supervision or remain isolated by their control interfaces. Vision–language–action(VLA) models have made substantial progress toward pretraining a single policy across tasks and robot platforms (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al., 2023; Ghosh et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025) and finetuning with a specific task. Their apparent unification pri-marily occurs at the model level, for the reason that learned targets remain robot actions, whose dimensionality, coordinate convention, and control semantics depend on the embodiment and ma-nipulation setup. Existing systems accommodate these differences through data pre-processing, embodiment conditioning, or specialized action heads (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Doshi et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025a;b). Task-relevant object motion offers a natural source of shared supervision because it captures task motion without committing to a robot’s joints or visual appearance. This insight has motivated a growing family of representations, including articulation flow, general 3D object flow, object-part scene flow, dense 3D motion fields, language-conditioned point trajectories, and point-cloud world 1arXiv:2611.02930v1 [cs.RO] 2 Nov 2026 Figure 1: Overview of PointPilot. We further demonstrate real-world manipulation and promising zero-shot cross-embodiment skills transfer. models (Eisner et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2025b; Lin et al., 2026). Despite these advances in object-centric prediction for robotic manipulation, a unified and scalable framework remains challenging. To the best of our knowledge, existing approaches generally fall into three patterns: a) Without any 3D alignment, they only use object motion as an intermediate latent feature for an additional action decoder, thus hardly processing the cross-embodiment gener-alization(Xu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026); b) depend on manual manipulation or an extra controller to grasp objects at the beginning, causing a modular framework and only sup-port an open-looped or semi-automatic execution (Tang et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025); c) or specialize object-centric representations to one particular manipulation mode (Eisner et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). In particu-lar , manipulation modes such as single-arm or bimanual arm manipulation may not be a problem in VLA paradigm, but it is a problem in the object-centric prediction. Unlike VLA, different ma-nipulation modes require different numbers of object flows and different object-to-gripper motion assignments. To simplify this, most object-centric methods specify their flow to one object and only execute single-arm tasks. On the other hand, an ideal unified and scalable object-centric prediction framework should be 

manipulation-mode agnostic , allowing a single model to handle both single-arm and dual-arm manipulation by predicting motion for a variable number of objects; embodiment-agnostic , allow-ing heterogeneous robot demonstrations to be composed despite incompatible native action spaces; and directly executable , allowing the object flow prediction to control the robot in a closed loop without an additional embodiment-dependent action decoder. Existing object-centric approaches typically satisfy only a subset of these requirements, leaving object motion either mode-specific, embodiment-dependent, or disconnected from fully automatic execution. To alleviate this gap, 1) we introduce a novel manipulation formulation that predicts task-focused future states and robot actions, in a unified 3D point-flow representation of relevant objects. Notable, we treat robot gripper tools as special objects and use the proposed 3D point-flow as an action rep-resentation, which accommodates different manipulation modes (e.g., single-arm or bimanual) and robot embodiments (e.g., parallel-jaw or dexterous hand) without platform-specific action experts. Moreover, 2) we align the 3D coordinates for both multi-view input observations and predicted 3D point flows in a shared reference frame. This alignment establishes cross-view spatial correspon-2dences for visual and geometric fusion while providing a consistent prediction space across robot embodiments. Additionally, 3) we integrate task understanding, semantic grounding, 3D point flow prediction and action generation into one model. This enables end-to-end training and fully au-tonomous closed-loop rollout, compared to various related works (Duan et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024a;b; Pan et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025) that rely on external modules (e.g., sub-task orchestration or grasp planning) during the real world deployment. With these principles, 

4) we develop a comprehensive data processing pipeline, design the PointPilot model architecture, perform jointly training on heterogeneous robot data sources, and conduct various simulation and real world experiments. PointPilot demonstrates promising generalization and scalability, such as zero-shot cross embodiment transfers.

Prior works have shown that object-centric 3D flow provides a better generalization across un-seen objects, scenes, and robot embodiments (Eisner et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2025). One line directly converts predicted objects’ flow into robot trajectories, but does not provide a complete autonomous and closed-looped ma-nipulation. They either manually manipulate the gripper or use an extra controller to grasp the object. (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhi et al., 2025). Another line is also often confined to a single manipulation mode, such as the single-arm manipulation (Eisner et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhi et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2026). Cross-embodiment policies use embodiment conditioning or shared latent actions to learn from het-erogeneous data (Zheng et al., 2025b; Bu et al., 2025). Object-centric 3D flow also supports human-to-robot transfer (Yuan et al., 2025) and cross-platform deployment (Zhi et al., 2025). PointPilot separates task-focused 3D motion prediction from robot-specific execution: a non-learned geomet-ric adapter maps predicted flows to end-effector targets under local rigid coupling. We demonstrate deployment on unseen embodiments without target-robot demonstrations or fine-tuning, using each robot’s kinematics and controller. 

# 3 POINT PILOT : U NIFIED OBJECT -CENTRIC 3D P OINT FLOW PREDICTION 

Building on the definition of task-focused objects , we introduce PointPilot . In this section, we will present the model architecture, covering subtask planning, object grounding, 3D geometric alignment, cross-view fusion , and point-flow predicting, and then describe how the predicted point flow is mapped to executable end-effector trajectories through a learning-free adapter. 3.1 PRELIMINARY : T ASK -FOCUSED OBJECTS FOR 3D P OINT FLOW LEARNING 

We define a task-focused object as a physical entity whose motion characterizes the manipulation required at the current stage and can be mapped to the motion of one or both grippers. Specifically, 3Figure 2: Method of PointPilot . (a) Planning : A memory-conditioned VLM predicts current and next subtasks. (b) Grounding : Task-conditioned 3D grounding localizes task-focused objects and assigns execution roles. (c) PointFlow : Multi-view observations are geometrically aligned and used by Point DiT to jointly predict relative 3D point flow and gripper states. Predicted object motion is converted to end-effector trajectories through a learning-free geometry adapter for closed-loop execution. for each gripper, the task-focused object is either the gripper itself or a locally rigid object part currently coupled to it. To support different manipulation modes, each task-focused object is addi-tionally assigned an execution role , indicating the gripper to which its predicted point flow should be mapped. For example, when the left gripper approaches an object, the left gripper itself serves as the task-focused object with the execution role Left . Once the object is grasped and transported, the task-focused object switches from the gripper to the coupled object, whose motion then directly charac-terizes the desired manipulation. Under this formulation, PointPilot represents both gripper motion and manipulated-object motion within a shared point-flow space by casting manipulation as the motion of task-focused objects. 3.2 SUBTASK PLANNING

# 4 DATA PIPELINE FOR TASK -FOCUSED OBJECTS POINT FLOW 

We construct PointPilot’s stage-level supervision from simulated and real-robot demonstrations through interaction-stage segmentation and 3D trajectory extraction. This aligns task semantics with task-focused object motion without requiring shared low-level action encodings across data sources. 4.1 STAGE SEGMENTATION 

We primarily segment demonstrations using gripper open/close events. In simulation, gripper states and grasp relationships define interaction boundaries; for real-robot data, the same events are de-tected from the smoothed gripper-width signal. Contact and articulated-part motion cues are used when gripper events are insufficient. Bimanual interactions are aligned on a shared timeline, and offline stage-level language annotations are matched to the resulting segments. 4.2 TRAJECTORY EXTRACTION 

Simulation. We directly retrieve EEF and object pose trajectories from simulator states. For artic-ulated interactions, we track the manipulated moving part rather than the full object assembly. All trajectories are transformed into a consistent reference frame and aligned with the segmented stages. 6Real-world. EEF trajectories are obtained directly from recorded robot poses. For grasped-object stages, we recover object geometry once at a visual anchor and propagate it using the recorded EEF motion, avoiding frame-wise tracking. Following the visual-grounding pipeline of MolmoMo-tion (Zhang et al., 2026), we combine MolmoPoint localization (Clark et al., 2026) with SAM 3 masks (Carion et al., 2026), and lift the selected region to 3D using estimated depth and camera calibration. Effect of planning memory. As shown in Table 5, removing planning memory lowers average success from 85.8% to 75.2%, with the largest drop on LIBERO-Long (77.2% to 55.0%). The 22.2 percentage-point gap on Long is larger than the 5.6 –8.0 point gaps on the other suites, suggesting that planning history is especially useful for longer subtask sequences. Without history, the VLM can repeat already executed subtasks during replanning and stall execution. Retaining prior plan pre-dictions therefore complements the current visual input with temporal context for subtask selection and stage progression. 

Effect of 3D frustum and multi-view alignment. Figure 4 compares visual-geometric configura-tions under joint LIBERO and RoboTwin training. Adding main-view 3D frustum encoding reduces the final point-flow loss from 0.015 to 0.008, and auxiliary views further reduce it to 0.006. Apply-ing spatially aligned 3D frustum encoding to all views reaches 0.002. In particular, loss decreases further after adding aligned geometric encoding even when multi-view images are already available. This suggests that additional views and geometric alignment make complementary contributions. The results support expressing visual geometry in the same coordinate frame as predicted point motion, rather than relying solely on multi-view appearance. 

# 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

PointPilot represents subtask-focused objects with sparse 3D points and execution roles, providing a shared interface across supported manipulation modes and embodiments. Memory-conditioned sub-task planning and grounding connect task intent to point-flow prediction, with multi-view geometry and predicted motion aligned in the main-view coordinate. Under stage-local rigid coupling, a non-learned geometric adapter converts predicted flows into end-effector motion without an additional learned continuous-action decoder. 9Several limitations suggest directions for further research. The current formulation centers on locally rigid task-focused objects and gripper-based execution. As a scalable object-centric framework, we need to leverage more data (including human hands and dexterous hands) to prove the scalability and show the generalization. Moreover, prediction focuses on the task-focused objects selected for the current stage rather than explicitly forecasting the broader set of task-relevant entities. Jointly mod-eling manipulated objects and other entities affected by their motion could support richer reasoning about object–object interactions and indirect effects while retaining a sparse, task-focused represen-tation. Finally, reducing the burden of aligning stage semantics, object geometry, execution roles, and motion while preserving semantic–geometric consistency remains a challenge for scaling.

Gripper opening and closing events provide the primary temporal anchors for segmentation. Sim-ulation supplies recorded gripper-state changes; for our real-robot recordings, we recover the cor-responding events from the continuous gripper-width signal. We first apply median smoothing and compute the signed numerical gradient with respect to the recorded timestamps. Let si denote this gradient at sample i. The automatic event threshold is 

τs = max( τmin , Q 0.9({| si|} )) , (8) where Q0.9 denotes the 90th percentile. Contiguous intervals exceeding this magnitude threshold and satisfying a minimum duration in source frames form candidate events. Their direction is deter-mined by the median signed slope: negative for closing and positive for opening. Nearby events with the same direction are merged. A candidate is accepted only if it agrees with the current open/closed state, reaches the opposite-state plateau, and produces a sufficient change in width. Simulation additionally uses contact and moving-part cues for interactions not adequately captured by gripper events, such as drawer manipulation. Stage-level language anno-tations are obtained through a separate offline procedure and associated with the resulting intervals. A.2 TRAJECTORY EXTRACTION 

For simulated demonstrations, we associate recorded object, moving-part, and end-effector (EEF) poses with the corresponding entities and stage intervals, as described in the main paper. Each pose sequence is paired with its entity geometry in a consistent reference frame. We focus below on real-robot reconstruction, where object trajectories must be recovered from visual observations and robot motion rather than directly read from simulator states. 

Depth Calibration and Camera Geometry. For our self-collected real-robot recordings, we esti-mate depth with ViPE (Huang et al., 2025a) on sparse frames associated with grasped-object stages. These depth estimates are used for offline geometric reconstruction, not as inputs to PointPilot at inference. Reconstruction uses the supplied fixed-camera intrinsics and robot-to-camera transforms rather than ViPE’s estimated camera parameters. Estimated depth can have substantial scale and off-set errors relative to the robot reference. Even with a correct object mask, directly back-projecting this depth can misplace the object relative to the gripper and distort its reconstructed size. These errors affect the object-to-EEF transform used to propagate object motion. We therefore calibrate the estimated depth against recorded EEF positions before reconstructing the visual anchor. To construct calibration references, we use the supplied camera calibration to express recorded EEF positions in the camera coordinate frame. Their optical-axis coordinates zEi provide the robot-derived depth references. Using the camera intrinsics, we project each reference point into the corresponding depth image at (ui, v i). We then compute di, the median of finite, positive estimated depths in a 15 × 15 pixel patch centered on this projection. This gives a pair (di, z Ei ) linking an image-based depth estimate to the robot reference. Usable pairs from both arms and multiple sampled times are pooled to estimate a shared correction. We model the systematic discrepancy as zEi ≈ αd i + β, where α > 0 adjusts depth scale and 

β adjusts offset. RANSAC identifies a positive-scale relation supported by multiple observations 15 and rejects pairs with large residuals. Least-squares fitting on the current inliers then refines the parameters, with the inlier set updated from the residuals. The resulting correction is applied to the corresponding depth maps: 

Dcal (u, v ) = αD est (u, v ) + β. (9) The same fitted parameters are applied to all associated frames, rather than fitting each pixel or frame independently. Although the references are sampled near the EEF projections, the correction is applied to the depth maps used for object reconstruction, not only to those local patches. We accept a fit only when it has sufficient supporting observations, adequate depth variation and correlation, and a scale within the allowed range. If these references are insufficient for a supported fit, the designated failure path retains the supplied camera geometry and uncalibrated depth with an explicit calibration-failure status. The EEF frame origin need not lie on a visible gripper surface, so the sampled patch provides an approximate correspondence rather than an exact depth measurement of that point. The calibration therefore constrains local consistency with the robot reference without establishing scene-wide depth ground truth or an object-trajectory error bound.

MolmoPoint processes each candidate image as a short repeated-frame clip; its locations guide selection from text-conditioned SAM 3 masks. When no suitable text-conditioned mask is matched, a small box around a predicted location provides an additional segmentation prompt. These components localize candidate anchors rather than track the object throughout the stage. We evaluate up to five candidate frames and separate acceptance from ranking. Before com-bining these sources, we check that the logged EEF poses are consistent with forward-kinematic flange poses under the supplied reference-frame mapping. The resulting bounding boxes describe the gripper’s motion and opening without requiring image-based reconstruction. When object localization or geometric reconstruction fails to produce an accepted object trajectory, we fall back to the corresponding gripper trajectory and OBB derived from recorded EEF poses and robot geometry. Fallback cases are explicitly flagged and treated as gripper geometry rather than successfully reconstructed object motion. Finally, stage intervals, language annotations, and geometric outputs are associated through source-frame indices and timestamps. Reconstruction validity and fallback provenance are retained sep-arately. The stored object masks correspond to selected anchors rather than dense per-frame seg-mentation, and the sparse geometry timeline is not assumed to be uniformly sampled. These records provide pose sequences and validity information for constructing PointPilot’s point-flow supervision for task-focused objects. The prediction targets are future 3D point displacements relative to their current positions, as defined in the main paper.

C.2 REAL -W ORLD EXPERIMENTS 

Platform and tasks. The physical platform is a CobotMagic bimanual system with an external camera and two wrist cameras. The three RGB streams are aligned to a common observation times-tamp. We consider QR-code scanning and sorting, cube-to-basket transfer, and pouring glass beads between cups. Execution and assessment. Object arrangements are fixed before policy comparison and inde-pendently reset for each trial. A robot-side client converts model predictions using camera-to-base calibration and robot kinematics, with feedback checks before execution. Success requires the com-plete task: scanning all specified QR codes and placing the scanned objects in the designated tray, placing and releasing the cube in the basket, or transferring the beads into the receiving cup with-out spilling. Operator corrections are not permitted; safety interventions and policy-induced control failures count as unsuccessful trials. Offline point-flow visualizations are not counted as physical task successes. C.3 ZERO -S HOT CROSS -E MBODIMENT TRANSFER 

Frozen policy and paired scenes. We transfer an ALOHA-trained policy to ARX-X5 and dual-Piper without target-embodiment demonstrations for task fine-tuning at evaluation time. The same checkpoint, prompt, point-flow sampling, and learned gripper prediction are used for source and target robots. Calibration, robot descriptions, kinematics, and execution interfaces are embodiment-specific. Each source–target comparison uses 100 accepted scenes and equal execution budgets. Scene pairing preserves object assets and arrangements, table/background configuration, main-camera calibration, and the exact instruction. Equality is checked after natural settling and any 22 robot initialization adjustment, using tolerances of 2 mm in object position and 2◦ in orientation. Robot appearance and wrist observations remain embodiment-dependent. For Piper, initial end-effector poses are additionally aligned to the ALOHA reference within 2 mm and 2◦ per arm. ARX retains its native feasible initialization; identical initial end-effector poses are not assumed for that comparison. 

Feasibility and evaluation. Task eligibility requires the target embodiment’s task assets and a feasible expert execution. Scenes are accepted only when both source and target experts succeed and the pairing checks pass; Piper also requires successful initial-pose alignment. Experts qualify the initial conditions but do not provide actions or intermediate labels to the policy. The accepted list is frozen before policy testing and shared by the ALOHA reference and corresponding target policies.
