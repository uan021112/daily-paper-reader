## Abstract 

Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs) enable robots to perform manipulation tasks by leveraging Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to process visual observa-tions and language instructions. However, the action prediction objective itself lacks fine-grained task-specific perceptual supervision, making it prone to rely-ing on task-irrelevant visual shortcuts, leading attention away from target objects and key interaction areas, thereby limiting its operational performance and gen-eralization ability. To address this, we propose Automatic Perception-Guided Attention Alignment for Robot Manipulation (A 3-VLA), a framework that derives task-specific visual guidance from the manipulation process itself. Specifically, A3-VLA first generates heuristic candidate regions for target objects and the robot from interaction-induced visual changes, and refines them with a visual segmenta-tion model offline. These regions serve as explicit perceptual guidance through two complementary objectives: an attention allocation loss that encourages sufficient focus on task-critical regions when language or robot state tokens query the visual input, and a contrastive learning loss that aligns the representations of candidate target regions with language instructions while separating them from task-irrelevant regions. Extensive experiments on simulated and real-world manipulation tasks demonstrate that A 3-VLA consistently improves multiple VLA backbones, with an average success rate gain of 6.5% - 25.2% across benchmarks. 

## 1 Introduction 

Embodied manipulation aims to enable robots to interact with the environment through learned policies to complete manipulation tasks [9, 1]. A core challenge is enabling robots to understand both what to manipulate (from language instructions) and how to manipulate (from visual observations). Recently, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have achieved remarkable success in aligning visual and linguistic representations, demonstrating strong capabilities in tasks such as visual question answering and cross-modal reasoning [31, 2, 34, 33]. Early works on VLAs primarily focus on accurate action generation, pioneered by tokenization-based [27, 16] and flow-matching-based [4, 20] paradigms. Despite this progress in action learning, the visual perception capa-bility of these models, including their ability to attend to regions specified by language instruc-tions, remains fragile. As shown in Figure 1, these cases reveal a key failure mode that is of-ten overlooked in VLA research: the model fails because the VLM backbone’s attention mecha-nism fails to focus on the task-relevant regions specified by the language instruction, rather than being solely attributable to action generation er-rors. Thus, the VLA learns to “memorize” spe-cific action trajectories from the training data, rather than connecting the instructions with the observation of the target objects. To break this pattern, VLAs need explicit signals about where to look, raising a natural question: can we obtain such task-specific guidance from the manipulation process itself? We argue that the above limitation reveals two deeper issues with how VLAs currently handle visual perception. First, the fine-tuning process provides no explicit signal indicating which image regions are critical for the given task. The flow-matching or autoregressive objective only supervises action accuracy [40, 11, 41]; whether the model’s visual attention aligns with task-relevant regions is never directly evaluated or encouraged. As a result, the VLM backbone’s pretrained ability to discriminatively attend to semantically meaningful regions can gradually drift during fine-tuning [28, 18, 35]. Second, prior works attempt to enhance perception in different ways, such as CoT-VLA [43] with visual chain-of-thought reasoning, π0.5 [12] and Gemini Robotics [26] with open-world knowledge. The guidance they provide remains either indirect (reasoning over the entire scene) or task-agnostic (general knowledge from heterogeneous data). These motivate a different perspective: the manipulation process itself encodes rich information about what matters visually. The moment of physical interaction, the trajectory of the robot end-effector, and the target object being acted upon all carry signals about which image regions deserve focused attention. If we obtain these signals and utilize them as explicit perceptual guidance, the task-oriented visual perception capability may be preserved during fine-tuning, rather than being overwritten by the action learning objective. To address these challenges, we propose Automatic Perception-Guided Attention Alignment for Robot Manipulation (A 3-VLA), a framework that provides explicit visual perception guidance during VLA fine-tuning. The key insight of A 3-VLA is that interaction moments in the manipulation process naturally reveal the locations of task-critical regions. Based on this, A 3-VLA consists of two stages. First, a heuristic region proposal and temporal refinement stage extracts noisy candidate regions from interaction-induced visual changes and refines them with SAM2 into temporally consistent pseudo regions for target objects and the robot. Second, a perception-guided fine-tuning stage incorporates these pseudo regions via two complementary objectives: (i) an attention allocation loss that encourages the model to focus sufficient attention on the heuristic regions of target objects or the robot when language or robot state tokens query the visual input respectively, and (ii) a contrastive learning loss that aligns the representations of target object regions with language instructions while pushing away those of task-irrelevant regions. Together, these objectives help the VLA maintain task-focused visual attention throughout fine-tuning, rather than allowing it to drift toward task-irrelevant regions. Overall, our contributions are as follows: • We propose A 3-VLA, a framework that derives task-specific visual guidance from the manipulation process itself, eliminating manual annotation. • We design two auxiliary training objectives, attention allocation and contrastive representa-tion alignment, that explicitly steer VLA visual focus toward task-relevant regions. 2• Extensive experiments on both simulation and real-world settings demonstrate that A 3-VLA consistently improves multiple VLA backbones across diverse manipulation tasks. 

## 2 Related Work 

In this section, we provide previous research relevant to our work, including Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs) and multimodal perception. 

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs) 

Foundational works such as RT-1 [5] and RT-2 [45] pioneered action tokenization for end-to-end policy learning, followed by open-source VLAs including Octo [27] and OpenVLA [16], and diffusion/flow-matching approaches like Gr00t series [3] and π0 [4]. Recent extensions have explored efficient fine-tuning recipes [10, 23], and lightweight adaptation modules [29] to enhance the effi-ciency of VLAs. Across these methods, primary VLAs mainly supervise action learning yet overlook the significance of multimodal perception during fine-tuning [24], which makes language following and task understanding remain challenging for VLAs [13, 37]. 

2.2 Multimodal Perception for VLA 

VLMs have demonstrated strong multimodal perception capabilities in tasks including VQA [8, 44], reasoning [43, 38], and cross-modal generation [30, 39], etc. Some works on VLAs attempt to explore and utilize the multimodal perception capability of VLMs to enrich the scene and task understanding of embodied models. For instance, RoboCLIP [25] and Embodied CLIP [14] ground language in robotic affordances with contrastive learning on the context of egocentric videos. FPC-VLA [32] utilizes a fine-tuned supervisor to check for failure based on multimodal observations. Recently, π0.5

[12] pretrains VLAs with heterogeneous data sources to transfer open-world multimodal knowledge. Gemini Robotics [26] harnesses Gemini’s capacity for physical reasoning to advance spatial compre-hension. Unlike prior methods that rely on general multimodal knowledge or indirect scene-level supervision, A 3-VLA automatically derives task-specific interaction cues from manipulation trajec-tories and refines them with a visual segmentation model into soft pseudo regions for region-level perception guidance during fine-tuning. 

## 3 Methodology 

In this section, we introduce the framework of Automatic Perception-Guided Attention Alignment for Robot Manipulation (A 3-VLA), which automatically derives pseudo-region guidance from the manipulation process and delivers explicit guidance for multimodal perception. Its framework is illustrated in Figure 2. The key components of A3-VLA are: 1) heuristic region proposal , which extracts noisy candidate regions for target objects and the robot from demonstration trajectories and refines them with SAM2 [21]; 2) attention weight allocation , which encourages sufficient attention mass on pseudo regions; 3) contrastive learning , which aligns candidate target-region representations with language instructions; and 4) joint VLA training with the main action objective and auxiliary perceptual objectives.

3.2 Heuristic Region Proposal 

To provide explicit perceptual guidance during VLA fine-tuning, we first propose candidate regions that are likely to be task-relevant for the given manipulation task. Our key insight is that useful heuristic cues can be naturally extracted from the manipulation process itself. In many manipulation tasks, the robot physically interacts with task-relevant objects during the trajectory. These interaction moments, which are detectable from the robot states or sensors, provide direct signals for localizing both the target objects and the robot in the visual observation. In the following, we describe how interaction cues are used to generate candidate target and robot regions. Formally, consider a trajectory with a sequence of images I = {I1, ..., I T } and corresponding robot state sequence S = {s1, ..., s T }, where T is the trajectory length. The interaction timestep tk denotes a coarse moment when the robot starts interacting with the task-relevant object. In practice, such moments can be obtained from robot or task cues, e.g., gripper states, contact signals, or predefined state ranges. Once tk is determined, we partition image patches into two key region sets: the target object Ctar and the robot Crob . The two region types are identified through different procedures, reflecting their distinct roles in the manipulation process. For target object regions Ctar , to localize the target object, we leverage the fact that it remains in place before the robot makes contact but undergoes visual changes once interaction begins. We define a window of w frames, and for each patch at spatial position (i, j ), we compute the mean absolute difference between consecutive frames within the pre-interaction window [tk − 2w, t k − w] and the post-interaction window [tk, t k + w]

For tasks involving multiple interactions, the trajectory is partitioned at each interaction timestep, regions are obtained for each segment using the same method, and the final label sets are taken as the union across all segments. Notably, a key practical advantage of this heuristic region proposal approach is that it requires no manual labeling, since it operates solely on the demonstration trajectory. These heuristic proposals are not intended to be accurate object masks. Instead, they serve as high-recall pseudo regions that provide coarse task-relevant visual cues. The asymmetric criterion, i.e., low variation before interaction and high variation after interaction, helps suppress patches that are consistently dynamic or unrelated to the contact event. For multi-interaction tasks, we apply the same procedure within each interaction segment before taking the union of the resulting regions, which reduces the ambiguity caused by temporally separated object changes. Since the heuristic proposals may still be noisy or incomplete, we further propagate them across frames using SAM2 to obtain temporally consistent region tracks. This refinement serves two purposes: (i) smoothing out frame-to-frame inconsistencies of the heuristic cues, and (ii) propagating the initial proposals to frames both before and after the interaction moment, providing region coverage for the entire trajectory without requiring the heuristic to work perfectly on every frame. Therefore, the labeling process only requires coarse interaction-induced seeds, rather than precise frame-level annotations. More details are provided in Appendix A.6. 

3.3 Guidance with Attention Weight Allocation 

The heuristic regions Ctar and Crob obtained in Section 3.2 provide cues about where the VLA should look. While these heuristic regions are not guaranteed to be perfectly accurate, they provide a useful attention prior that we exploit through soft constraints rather than hard labels. Here, we adopt a soft guidance strategy: rather than hard-constraining the attention distribution, we impose only a lower bound on the total attention allocated to the pseudo regions, encouraging VLAs to preserve focus on task-critical areas while retaining the freedom for action learning and remaining visual knowledge, without disrupting the visual understanding capabilities that make the VLM backbone valuable in the first place. Here, we use the attention map from the last layer of VLMs and average over all attention heads.

3.4 Guidance with Contrastive Learning 

The guidance in Section 3.3 provides supervision at the level of attention weights, while explicitly shaping the semantic relationship between language instructions and the visual features extracted from those regions is also required. To strengthen this relationship, we turn to the representation space. Our key insight is that the regions Ctar from Section 3.2 naturally partition each pre-interaction frame into two semantically distinct sets: patches inside the refined candidate target region are treated as pseudo-positive visual evidence, while patches outside the target regions are treated as context negatives. The former should be semantically close to the language instruction, while the latter should not. Based on this, we design a contrastive learning objective that takes the language instruction representation as an anchor, pulls the visual features of Ctar toward it, and pushes the features of non-target regions away. 3.5 VLA jointly training 

With the procedures above, we have three training objectives for VLAs: LF M for action generation learning, Ll2i and Ls2i for explicit attention weight allocation guidance, and Lcl for target-oriented representation enhancement. Together, these two forms of supervision address the perception gap from complementary angles: attention allocation shapes the model’s visual focus on task-critical regions, while contrastive alignment ensures the visual features of those regions are semantically aligned with the language instruction. Finally, A 3-VLA is jointly optimized with these tasks with the following formulation:

## 4 Experiment 

In this section, we introduce details of the experiments on the evaluation of A 3-VLA. We first compare our method with state-of-the-art VLA baselines on both simulation and real-world environments. Then, the ablation study and training efficiency analysis are conducted to display the impact of the designed components. Last, we present the case study to demonstrate how perception guidance improves the performance of VLAs. 

4.1 Experiment Setup Simulation Benchmarks. For simulation, we evaluate our methods on VLABench [42] and Robotwin 2.0 [19, 7] benchmarks. VLABench provides a large scale of tasks to test the understanding of VLAs for semantic instructions and scene analysis, while Robotwin is a widely adopted benchmark with a large variety of scenes and objects for bimanual robotic simulation. For VLABench, we train and test models on five representative task suites: Chemistry Tube, Fruit, Poker, Toy and Mahjong, each containing 500 demonstrations. We test the VLAs over 50 episodes on each task suite in VLABench. For Robotwin, we compare the VLAs on four tasks: Beat Block Hammer, Click Bell, Move Playingcard Away, and Place Burger & Fries. For each task, we utilize Robotwin pipeline to generate 100 demonstrations for training, and test VLAs over 100 episodes. In each environment, tasks are all jointly trained. 

Real-World Environment. In the real-world experiments, we set up scenes of manipulation tasks in the kitchen with a Songling robotic arm. The tasks include Open Cooker, Pick & Place Bowl, Pick 2 Vegetables, Select Bottles, and Desktop Sweep, each containing 80 demonstrations. Evaluation for each task is conducted 20 times, and we further record the task progress score, which is set as 1 if the task is completed, 0.5 if the VLA fails halfway, and 0 for inconsistency with instructions. In the real-world experiments, tasks are all trained together. 

Implementation Details. We implement A 3-VLA on π0, OpenVLA-OFT, and π0.5, adopting each backbone’s original training settings. For hyperparameters, the window size w is the same as the frame rate of videos. The variance threshold τ is set as 10, while the attention weight thresholds η1

and η2 are both 0.25. The loss weights λ1 and λ2 are set as 0.1 and 0.01 respectively. All the training and experiments are conducted on a server with 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs. 

Baselines. We compare A 3-VLA with various state-of-the-art methods. Octo [27] and OpenVLA [16] are pioneering works for open-source VLAs with OpenX pretraining. RDT [17] is a VLA with unified action space for transferable physical knowledge. π0 [4] and π0-FAST [20] are flow-based VLAs that complete manipulation tasks by action chunk generation. We also evaluate the top-performing OpenVLA-OFT [15] and π0.5 [12]. π0, OpenVLA-OFT, and π0.5, A 3-VLA gains average 16.4%, 24.0%, and 25.2% success rate im-provements respectively, showing the consistent advancement of our method across different VLA frameworks. The im-provement is particularly pronounced on tasks with visually similar distractors (e.g., Poker, Mahjong), demonstrating the effectiveness of our perception guidance for fine-grained object discrimination. 

Robotwin Results. We evaluate our approach and other baselines on Robotwin 2.0, and report the quantitative results in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, A 3-VLA also improves all backbones on RoboTwin 2.0, with average gains of 9.8%, 7.0%, and 6.5% percentage points. The largest gains appear on Place Burger & Fries, suggesting that perception guidance is helpful for longer-horizon sequential manipulation. 

Real-world Results. We make real-world experiments and compare the performance of our approach with π0 and π0.5 on various kitchen-themed manipulation tasks. The results are shown in Table 3, which demonstrates the advancement of A 3-VLA to complete different real-world tasks. Overall, our method outperforms the strongest baseline π0.5 with 14% average success rates and 14% average task progress score. Among these tasks, A 3-VLA gains the most significant improvement on Select Bottles, which is a task demanding VLAs to pick up the specified bottle. This indicates that our design is helpful for VLAs’ instruction understanding in the real-world background. By contrast, without perception-guided attention alignment, π0 and π0.5 have difficulty selecting the correct bottles. However in general, π0.5 performs better than π0 in various tasks.

## 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we proposed A 3-VLA, a multi-modal perception-enhanced VLA framework that improved task-oriented visual understanding for robot manipulation. Specifically, to incorporate manipulation-progress information into language-visual understanding, A 3-VLA automatically gen-erated heuristic candidate regions for target objects and the robot from manipulation trajectories, which were then temporally refined into pseudo regions using SAM2. To guide VLAs to allocate sufficient attention to task-critical pseudo regions and learn higher-quality semantic representations, we proposed two alignment training objectives: one encouraged attention weights to concentrate on candidate target or robot regions when processing language commands and observations, while the other used contrastive learning to bring target-region representations closer to instructions. Exten-sive experiments on both simulated and real-world environments validated the effectiveness of the proposed method.

A.7 Limitations 

A3-VLA relies on interaction-induced visual changes to obtain heuristic candidate regions. Although these regions are further refined by SAM2 and used only as soft supervision, the quality of the pseudo regions may degrade when the target object is fully occluded, visually indistinguishable from the background, too small to produce reliable patch-level changes, or moves together with distractors. In such cases, the auxiliary perception losses may provide weaker or noisier guidance. Another limitation is that our current implementation assumes that a coarse interaction moment can be obtained from lightweight robot states or task-state cues, such as gripper states, contact signals, or predefined state ranges. While such cues are commonly available in manipulation demonstrations, more complex embodiments or tasks without clear interaction events may require more robust interaction discovery mechanisms. A3-VLA also introduces an offline preprocessing step using SAM2 to improve the temporal consis-tency of pseudo regions. This does not add inference-time cost, since SAM2 is not used during policy execution, but it increases data preprocessing time and introduces a dependence on the quality of the off-the-shelf segmentation model. Future work may explore uncertainty-aware weighting of pseudo regions, more robust interaction-guided region discovery, and fully end-to-end perception guidance without external refinement tools. 

A.8 Broader Impact 

Positive impacts include improving robot instruction following and reducing manual annotation for robot learning. Potential risks include failures in real-world deployment due to incorrect perception or attention alignment, which may cause unsafe robot actions.
