# ActOR: Ambient AI-Based Agentic Orchestration for Humanoid Autonomy in Operating Rooms

Abstract Purpose: Operating rooms (ORs) increasingly contain ambient sensing infras-tructure in hopes that AI-based scene understanding would enable teams to optimize workflows and utilization. We pursue an alternative path: using ambient AI to orchestrate and inform robot action to benefit usage optimization. Deploy-ing robots, such as humanoids, in the OR without ambient AI is severely limited by cumbersome de novo orientation and planning due to partial onboard observa-tions. We posit that this can be achieved by coupling an ambient AI-based agentic orchestrator with the humanoid robotic actor, providing shared perception and execution feedback for humanoid autonomy in the OR. 

Methods: ActOR fuses a wall-mounted ambient camera and the robot’s head-mounted camera into a shared object-level digital twin (DT). A large language model agent plans over the DT using pre-programmed locomotion and manip-ulation skills without task-specific training. During execution, DT updates and execution outcomes inform retries, alternative approaches, or replanning. 

Results: We evaluated ActOR in simulated and physical environments. Across three tasks in two simulated ORs reconstructed from real rooms, ambient percep-tion reduced exploration from 1.05 to 0.12 Explore calls per trial when targets were initially outside the robot’s view. Closed-loop feedback increased success from 46.7% to 83.3% under nominal execution and from 8.3% to 51.7% under injected navigation, object-relocation, and grasp perturbations. In a physical mock OR with a humanoid, ActOR completed 14 of 20 single-object disposal trials and demonstrated replanning after scene changes. 

Conclusion: Ambient AI-based observations provide scene state that improves robot action planning, increasing success and reducing exploration compared with robot-only observations. Feeding skill outcomes and DT updates back to

# 1 Introduction 

Operating rooms (ORs) face a growing shortage of perioperative nursing and support staff [1]. Room turnover, supply retrieval, and disposal of used materials consume sub-stantial staff time and directly affect OR efficiency [2]. These tasks are repetitive and physically demanding, and do not require highly trained personnel. Automating these tasks with robots rather than relying on an already strained workforce is a promis-ing avenue to improve turnover times. The postoperative phase provides a practical setting for automation: the patient has left, most staff have stepped away, and the environment is comparatively controlled [3, 4]. Humanoid robots are attractive for such tasks because they can, in principle, operate within spaces designed for humans without major modifications. At the same time, modern ORs are becoming increasingly instrumented. Ceiling-and wall-mounted cameras have been used to recognize surgical phases, localize staff and equipment, and construct digital twins (DTs) of the OR [5–8]. Yet this ambi-ent sensing infrastructure has primarily been used to inform clinical teams rather than directly support robotic autonomy. We ask whether the instrumented OR itself can instead become part of the robot’s perceptual infrastructure, providing persistent room-scale context that complements the robot’s local, egocentric view. This question is particularly relevant for humanoid autonomy in the OR, where two limitations make robot-centric operation difficult. First, autonomous task planning requires an accurate representation of the current environment, but an onboard cam-era provides only a moving and frequently occluded view of a cluttered room. Upon entering the room to complete an assigned task, a robot may therefore need to explore the environment before it can decide where and how to act. Second, humanoid exe-cution remains imperfect: navigation can terminate short of a target, and grasps can fail. A plan generated from the initial scene state therefore becomes unreliable when execution deviates from expectation. Foundation-model agents can decompose open-ended instructions into robot skills [9], but reliable physical autonomy also requires broader access to the world state and feedback about executed actions. We address these limitations with ActOR , an ambient AI-based agentic orchestra-tion framework for humanoid autonomy in the OR (Fig. 1). ActOR fuses observations from a wall-mounted ambient camera and the robot’s head-mounted camera into a shared object-level DT, combining room-scale awareness with near-field observations for manipulation. A large language model (LLM) agent plans over the DT using a library of pre-programmed skills, without task-specific training. # 2 Related Work 

Hospital robots have so far focused on logistics outside the OR, such as mobile manipu-lators that deliver supplies to nursing units [10], while humanoids in the OR have been studied mainly under teleoperation [11–13]. Outside medicine, fixed external cameras have been used to localize mobile robots and build shared semantic maps [14]. ActOR uses ambient AI-based scene understanding to support humanoid task planning in the OR and incorporates execution feedback for retry and replanning. 3LLMs can decompose open-ended instructions into skill sequences when grounded in a scene representation, such as open-vocabulary or hierarchical 3D scene graphs [15– 17]. Closed-loop variants feed back success detection or scene descriptions to recover from failures [18]. These systems typically build their representation from onboard sensing and rely on exploration to discover the scene [9]. In the OR, however, explo-ration is undesirable: the room is cluttered with sensitive equipment, and unnecessary robot motion should be minimized. ActOR therefore obtains the scene state from the ambient camera up front, and we evaluate each component through targeted ablations.

3.2 Shared Scene Representation 

The ambient camera provides persistent room-scale coverage independent of robot pose, while the robot camera provides accurate near-field geometry for manipulation. Given an RGB image from either camera, a vision-language model (VLM) [19] detects task-relevant objects with labels li; each 2D box prompts SAM2 [20] for an instance mask, whose pixels are back-projected with stereo depth and transformed into the world frame to obtain pi and Bi.The DT is kept consistent with the scene as the robot acts. It is updated after each manipulation skill and on each Observe call, and only nodes inside the observing camera’s view are modified. A detection is merged into the existing node it overlaps most when the two occupy the same volume; otherwise, a new node is created. A node is removed when consecutive depth observations find its volume unoccupied. Manipulation also changes object state: when the gripper stops before full closure, the grasped node is marked in-hand and follows the gripper until Place . Spatial relations such as in , near and on are derived from the object positions and extents and passed to the planner with the DT.

3.4 Skill Implementation 

Upper-body manipulation and lower-body locomotion are decoupled. For Navigate ,ambient depth is projected onto the floor plane to form a 2D occupancy map. A stand-off pose facing the target within arm reach is computed, and A* plans waypoints that are converted to base velocity commands and tracked by an RL-based whole-body controller [22]. For Pick , the arm closer to the target is selected, and AnyGrasp [23] predicts grasp candidates from the robot camera point cloud, guided by the target’s bounding box. We retain AnyGrasp candidates and combine them with candidates generated by a 3D bounding-box heuristic adapted from [24]. # 4 Experiments 

We assume no human presence during execution, requiring the planner to resolve ambi-guities without operator input. All task-relevant objects are placed within the robot’s reachable workspace. Controlled studies in MuJoCo [26] ablate ambient perception and closed-loop feedback, followed by physical deployment in a mock OR. 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Environments 

Controlled studies were conducted in two simulated ORs with distinct layouts recon-structed from real environments [27], with object assets generated by SAM3D [28]. Each environment included a virtual ambient camera. The simulated robot ran the same detection, segmentation, grasping, and skill stack as the physical system and executed through the whole-body controller in simulation; depth was rendered with 6simulated stereo noise. Physical experiments were conducted in a mock OR equipped with an operating table, mobile CT scanner, surgical robot, and supply tables. 

Tasks 

We evaluated three simulation tasks: single-object retrieval, requiring delivery of a specified object; multi-object collection, requiring successive transfers of two objects; and room reset, requiring identification and disposal of task-relevant objects among distractors (e.g., “put all used supplies into the biohazard bin”). Object layouts and initial robot poses were randomized across trials and kept identical across methods within each study. A trial succeeded when all target objects reached the instructed destination within 

Ttimeout . We report task success rate (SR) over 60 trials per method and condition (3 tasks × 2 environments × 10 trials) and, for the ambient perception study, the mean number of Explore calls per trial. For physical experiments, we report end-to-end success and the source of each failure. 

Implementation details 

We used a Unitree G1 EDU (29 DoF) with Dex1-1 grippers and a head-mounted ZED Mini, alongside a wall-mounted ZED X connected to a Jetson Orin. The shared world frame is defined by a checkerboard observed simultaneously by the ambient and head-mounted cameras. Hand–eye calibration registers the head-mounted cam-era to the robot frame, and the robot is localized in its map frame using onboard LiDAR SLAM. Composing these transformations at calibration time yields a fixed transformation from the SLAM map frame to the world frame. Depth estimation uses Fast-FoundationStereo [29]. GPT-6 Luna [19] and GPT-6.1 Sol [21] are accessed through API calls for detection and planning; other modules ran locally on an AMD Ryzen 9 5900HX and RTX 3080. We set dreach = 0 .85 m and Ttimeout = 1800 s. 

Baselines 

We compare ActOR against two ablated variants. All methods share the same planner, perception modules, and skill implementations. 

• Robot-only. The DT is built and updated only from the robot camera; execution remains closed-loop, and navigation uses the map built from the head camera. 

• Open-loop. Same DT, planner, and prompt, but once execution has begun, the episode ends at the first skill that reports failure, removing retry and replanning.

Closed-loop feedback under execution uncertainty 

We compared ActOR with Open-loop (Fig. 3c) on a separate set of layouts whose ini-tial robot poses were not controlled for head-camera observability; both methods used the same layouts and initial poses. We evaluated nominal execution and a perturbed condition in which each trial was assigned one of three perturbations: navigation error , stopping 1.0–2.0 m from the target; object relocation , moving the target by at least 0.5 m without updating the DT; or grasp misalignment , offsetting the first grasp attempt by 5–8 cm. Both methods were assigned the same perturbation on each trial. Under nominal execution, ActOR succeeded in 83.3% (50/60) of trials and Open-loop in 46.7% (28/60). This gap indicates that individual skills fail often enough without injected perturbations that a single plan is unreliable, especially for multi-step tasks (Open-loop succeeded in 7/20 trials for both collection and room reset). Under perturbations, success dropped to 51.7% (31/60) for ActOR and 8.3% (5/60) for Open-loop; in multi-object collection, ActOR succeeded in 12/20 trials and Open-loop in none. Since Open-loop removes retry and replanning together, this gap reflects their combined effect. By perturbation type, ActOR succeeded in 15/24 navigation-error trials, 11/18 object-relocation trials, and 5/18 grasp-misalignment trials, compared with 2/24, 3/18, and 0/18 for Open-loop (Supplementary Table S1). 4.3 Physical Mock OR Deployment 

We deployed the complete ActOR system on the Unitree G1 in the physical mock OR (Fig. 2c). We conducted 20 single-object disposal trials, each instructing the robot to find a bottle and dispose of it in the biohazard bin. Bottle and bin placements varied across trials within the robot’s reachable workspace. The robot was initialized with the bottle outside its egocentric view, so the bottle could initially be localized only through the ambient camera. A trial succeeded if the bottle reached the bin without human intervention. We also ran exploratory multi-object tests (“put all bottles into the biohazard bin”), including ones in which the scene was changed during execution by relocating the biohazard bin or introducing an additional bottle. ActOR succeeded in 14 of 20 single-object disposal trials (70%; Fig. 4a). In every successful trial, the bottle was localized from the ambient view and Explore was never invoked. Of the six failures, five originated in perception, where the VLM missed or misidentified the bottle, and one in execution, where a grasp still failed after the retry. In other trials, failed grasps were reported by the Pick skill and recovered by retrying. In the multi-object tests, ActOR completed multi-step executions and, in successful runs, replanned after scene changes toward the relocated bin or the added bottle (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Video). 95 Discussion 

Our results suggest that shared scene representation and closed-loop execution feed-back together enable a humanoid to complete simple postoperative OR support tasks. The ambient camera provides room-level scene understanding, reducing the burden on the robot’s onboard sensing and exploration. The LLM agent composes pre-programmed skills without task-specific or room-specific training. More broadly, this work explores a path from ambient AI-based scene understanding to robot action in support of OR workflow and utilization optimization. This study has several limitations. First, both detection and planning rely on pro-prietary models accessed through an API, which raises privacy concerns for clinical deployment. Locally deployable open-weight models offer a feasible alternative. For detection, however, even strong VLMs remain unreliable in cluttered scenes, and given the safety requirements of the OR, closed-vocabulary detectors trained on OR objects may be more appropriate. Second, the legged humanoid platform adds whole-body balance control on top of manipulation. This introduces additional constraints such as motor overheating and reduced manipulation accuracy, which currently limit the sys-tem to relatively large objects. A wheeled base would provide a more stable platform, at the cost of mobility in narrow spaces and over floor cables. Third, manipulation is limited to pick-and-place. More complex interactions, such as opening cabinets or pushing carts, require whole-body coordination. Learning-based policies combining imitation learning and reinforcement learning are a promising direction. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We presented ActOR, an ambient AI-based agentic orchestration framework integrat-ing shared scene representation with closed-loop robot execution. Simulation studies demonstrated reduced exploration and improved task success under execution pertur-bations. Physical deployment demonstrated task execution and adaptation to scene changes, supporting the feasibility of humanoid autonomy for OR support tasks.

14 S2 Study Conditions 

Tasks and objects. 

Targets are drawn without replacement from four bottles (alcohol, saline, hydrogen peroxide, spray). Retrieval places one target; collection and room reset place two. Every layout also contains two distractors, a glove box and a reflex hammer, on a surface without targets. Room reset reuses the collection layout of the same seed and changes only the instruction (Table S4). The planner’s site description identifies the bottles as the used disposable items (Sec. S4). 

Table S4 : Example instructions.     

> Task Instruction Retrieval “Clean up: put the [appearance] into the biohaz-ard box.” Collection “Put all the bottles into the biohazard box.” Room reset “Put all the used disposable items into the bio-hazard box.”

Observability conditions (ambient perception study). 

Layouts place all targets on one surface, at most 0.5 m apart; only the robot’s start differs between conditions. In ego-observable starts, the head camera sees every target fully and at least 30% of the bin opening, at torso leans of 0 ◦ and 5 ◦; the robot starts within 1.2 m of the targets’ centroid. In partially observable starts, at least one target is entirely outside the head camera’s view or occluded. All targets remain visible to the ambient camera in both conditions. Visibility is tested by projecting sample points on each target into the head camera and ray-casting through the room’s 2.5D height field. 

Closed-loop study. 

This study uses a separate set of layouts with one sampled start per layout, shared by both methods and both conditions. Starts are standable poses at least 0.8 m from obstacles, with random heading, and are not controlled for head-camera observability. 

Perturbations. 

Perturbation types cycle with the trial index (4, 3, and 3 trials of the three types per task and room), and both methods are assigned the same type and random seed. Because the realized perturbation depends on execution (e.g., which object is approached first), it can differ between methods. Each perturbation fires at most once: 

• Navigation error. On the first navigation toward a graspable object, the robot stops 1.0–2.0 m from it with up to 20 ◦ additional heading error, while Navigate 

reports success. 15 • Object relocation. At the start of that navigation, the target is moved at least 0.5 m to another valid placement; the DT keeps its previous position until the object is re-observed. 

• Grasp misalignment. On the first grasp, the grasp point is shifted 5–8 cm along the finger direction. Retries are unperturbed. 

# S3 Implementation Details 

Simulation. 

Both ORs were reconstructed from real rooms with TwinOR (OR-A: 5 .46 × 5.63 m; OR-B: 5 .80 × 5.42 m). The simulated G1 (29 DoF, Dex1-1 grippers) is controlled by the AMO whole-body policy in MuJoCo. The ambient camera renders 2560 × 1440 images (119 ◦ × 88 ◦ field of view, 120 mm stereo baseline). The robot camera matches the physical head-mounted ZED Mini (1280 × 720, about 85 ◦ × 54 ◦, 63 mm baseline), pitched 46.6 ◦ down. Depth is MuJoCo-rendered depth with simulated stereo noise (0.15 px disparity noise); detection, segmentation, and grasping use the same models as the physical system. 

Shared scene representation. 

• Representation. Object geometry is stored as 3 cm voxels and serialized to the planner as a footprint radius and height range. 

• Association. Two detections are merged when each voxel set covers at least 40% of the other, or when one lies at least 70% inside the other and their labels are compatible. 

• Updates. The VLM runs on each Observe call (ambient and head cameras) and on the head camera before each Pick and Place . Depth-based occupancy and free-space carving run at about 1 Hz. 

• Removal. A voxel is deleted after two consecutive carving rounds observe free space through it; a node is removed when more than half of its tested voxels (at least ten) are empty, or fewer than five voxels remain. Held objects are not carved. 

• In-hand state. After closing, the grasp is in-hand when the normalized gripper opening stops between 0.005 and 0.9; below 0.005 the fingers met and the grasp is empty. 

• Relations. on and in are derived from vertical contact and footprint overlap with a supporting object; near links objects within 1.0 m edge to edge (at most three neighbours). 

Skills and planner. 

• dreach = 0 .85 m is measured to the target’s footprint boundary (0.60 m planner clearance plus 0.25 m). The occupancy grid has 0.10 m resolution. 

• Retry is a prompt rule, not a fixed loop. After a grasp failure, the planner calls Pick 

again from the same place; after an approach failure, it approaches from the side nearest an edge and tries once more. The same failure twice from the same place leads it to change the side, the object order, or the target, or to leave the object. Open-loop ends the episode at the first skill failure after execution has begun. 16 • Ttimeout = 1800 s is a hard stop; the planner is warned at 1200 s.
