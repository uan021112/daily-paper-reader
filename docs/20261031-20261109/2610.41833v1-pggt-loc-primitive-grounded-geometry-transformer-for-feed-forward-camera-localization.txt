Abstract
Camera localization methods are mostly multi-stage pipelines relying on dedicated
and complex maps that are costly to construct, store, and maintain over time. Alter-
natively, well-structured maps that organize scenes with compact yet expressive
primitives are often readily available, providing rich structural and semantic cues
that humans can interpret. However, their potential for direct camera localiza-
tion remains largely unexplored due to the significant modality gap between map
primitives and visual observations. This motivates a new concept for localization,
which estimates camera poses by grounding query observations to map primitives.
In this paper, we instantiate the concept in the context of planar primitives with
PGGT, the Primitive-Grounded Geometry Transformer network. Given a single
query image and a set of planar map primitives, the network is trained to predict
multiple localization targets for feed-forward 6-DoF camera localization that gener-
alizes to unseen environments. With this straightforward and scalable architecture,
our method pushes the state-of-the-art localization performance on both dense
surface maps and minimal layout models. The code and models are available at
https://github.com/3dv-casia/PGGT-Loc.

Introduction
Camera localization, the task of estimating the orientation and position of a query image relative to a
pre-existing map, stands as a fundamental problem in computer vision and robotics, with wide appli-
cations in augmented reality (AR) [95], robotic navigation [42], and multi-agent collaboration [29].
One popular family of approaches, known as structure-based localization, typically establishes 2D-
3D correspondences between the query image and the scene structure, either by matching local
features [11, 34, 48, 62, 66, 72] or through direct scene coordinate regression [9, 10, 36, 69, 77]. With
these explicitly established correspondences, the camera pose is then estimated by a robust geometric
solver such as PnP-RANSAC [23, 24, 40]. Note that these approaches require a dedicated map
triangulated or trained from a large database of mapping images before a new image can be localized.
While more general map representations have been explored for the task, e.g., meshes [57] and
NeRF/3DGS [17, 33, 54, 91], these methods still depend heavily on photorealistic scene appearance
and often require an initial pose estimate for keypoint matching or iterative pose refinement.
Well-structured maps are arrangements of high-level primitives and may further be enriched with
relationships among them [3–5, 49, 50, 63, 88, 97]. Despite the significant modality gap between
the map primitives and visual observations, which hinders the straightforward adaptation of the

Among the many map primitives, planes are particularly essential in representing physical surfaces,
owing to their notable compactness and prevalence in structured environments [2, 83, 86], which
has motivated a recent work, named PlanaReLoc [90], to explore their use for camera localization.
As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the method introduces a plane-centric feature-matching pipeline that first
establishes cross-modal correspondences between planes in the query and the map, and then estimates
the pose via 2D–3D registration. While the method demonstrates the potential of planar primitives for
streamlined camera localization without map texture, pose priors, or per-scene training, it still relies
on an off-the-shelf plane recovery module to generate reliable 2D plane proposals and geometric
priors for primitive matching and pose estimation, which poses a major bottleneck in practice.
Meanwhile, the three-stage pipeline in PlanaReLoc cannot be directly applied to minimal layout
models [19, 97], whose primitives are restricted to architectural entities that do not span all physical
surfaces. For localization on such minimalist maps, the render-and-compare paradigm dominates
existing methods. As depicted in Fig. 1b, the state-of-the-art method SPVLoc [25] first renders
the layout map into a set of semantic panoramas by exhaustively sampling viewpoints across the
scene, and then regresses the relative pose between the best-retrieved panorama and the query image.
Although this paradigm neatly bridges the modality gap, reducing structure-based localization to
matching 2D renderings inevitably discards structural information that could otherwise be exploited.
In this work, motivated by the goal of fully leveraging high-level map primitives for more straightfor-
ward camera localization, we introduce primitive-grounded localization, a new concept that casts the
task as the grounding of query observations in map primitives. Driven by the concept and inspired by
the DUSt3R [82] architecture, we propose PGGT, a feed-forward network whose name stands for
Primitive-Grounded Geometry Transformer. As shown in Fig. 1c, PGGT takes a single query image
and a set of map primitives as inputs, and predicts the camera pose w.r.t. the map in a single forward
pass, eliminating the need to solve for the pose from explicit primitive correspondences or match the
query against exhaustive 2D renderings of the map. We summarize our contributions as follows:
• We introduce primitive-grounded localization, a new concept to localize cameras against
well-structured maps through the grounding of query observations in map primitives.

• The concept is concretized in the context of planar primitives by PGGT, a cross-modal
transformer network designed to ingest the query image and map primitives as direct inputs,
and predict a set of localization-specific targets for feed-forward camera localization.
• PGGT provides a shared base architecture for localization tasks on both surface maps and

localization as a promising paradigm for diverse primitive-based map representations.

Related Work
Localization with Higher-Level Maps.
Through computer vision, machines position themselves
in the real world using diverse map representations, many of which are not encoded with visual
observations but instead consist of high-level structural primitives and semantics interpretable by
humans. In large outdoor scenarios, prior works have explored 2D semantic maps for 3-DoF
localization, evolving from using only a few semantic classes [28, 31, 59, 76] to utilizing all available
map elements [1, 63, 64, 84]. CAD/LoD models of urban buildings have also been used for 6-
DoF localization [58, 99]. For indoor environments, 2D floorplan localization methods typically
employ the render-and-compare paradigm, converting maps into diverse codebooks that facilitate
intra-modal reasoning with query images [16, 32, 38, 52]. PlanaReLoc [90] and SPVLoc [25] exploit
detailed planar surface maps and minimal layout models for 6-DoF camera localization, respectively,
while adhering to conventional paradigms where primitive-encoded structural information remains
insufficiently exploited.
Multi-view Transformers.
DUSt3R [82] comes with the two-view transformer architecture which
comprises a shared ViT [22] encoder and view-specific interconnected decoders, realizing feed-
forward recovery of scene geometry and reshaping the landscape of 3D vision. Building upon this
foundation, subsequent works have expanded the paradigm along multiple dimensions, such as multi-
view inputs [43, 79], correspondence grounding [41], online streaming inference [18, 55, 80, 100],
more geometric inputs [35, 39], dynamic scene modeling [96] and novel view synthesis [13, 70]. In
particular, the paradigm also inspires several structureless localization methods [6, 21], which operate
on groups of query and mapping images. In this work, we adapt DUSt3R’s scalable architecture to
cross-modal inputs, aiming for feed-forward localization with high-level map primitives.
3
Method
We propose PGGT, a cross-modal transformer network that instantiates the concept we propose:
localizing by grounding query observations to map primitives. PGGT is designed to ingest the query
image and map primitives as direct inputs and predict multiple localization-specific targets, enabling
feed-forward camera localization on well-structured maps that generalizes to unseen environments.
3.1

represented as an inclusive collection of piecewise planar surfaces {Πm

Given a previously unseen map M and a query image Iq ∈R3×H×W , the objective is to estimate the
6-DoF camera pose g ∈S3 × R3 w.r.t. M that corresponds to the query observation. We parametrize
the camera pose as g := [q, t], with q ∈S3 and t ∈R3 denoting the rotation quaternion and
translation vector, respectively. We assume that the query image is captured within the spatial
extent of the map, with the observed region sufficiently mapped and exhibiting no major structural
discrepancies with the map. In addition, our method does not necessarily require priors on camera
intrinsics.

3.2
Model Architecture
The PGGT architecture, illustrated in Fig. 2, consists of two main components: (1) a two-branch
transformer backbone consuming two modalities of inputs, namely the query and the map; (2) a set
of output layers predicting multiple localization-specific targets.
Cross-Modal Transformer Backbone.
In light of the tremendous potential of multi-view trans-
formers in feed-forward 3D reconstruction, we inherit the architecture, adopting a backbone compris-
ing two identical stacks of N transformer decoder layers, with one stack processing query tokens and
the other processing map tokens. Here, these two inputs are simply tokenized as follows:
• The query image Iq is patchified into lq tokens through a ViT encoder, which is pre-trained
on large-scale image corpora and remains frozen throughout training.
• The map, represented as an unordered set of planar primitives {Πm
i |i = 1, . . . , lm}, is
encoded into lm tokens via a trainable projector, termed the primitive embedder. The
primitive embedder tokenizes each map primitive through the integration of: (1) a vanilla
3D encoder that consumes points sampled on the primitive surface to capture its shape and
spatial position; (2) a shallow MLP that projects the primitive parameter πm
i into a latent
space, serving as the positional encoding.
Within each layer of the transformer backbone, the cross-attention block facilitates cross-modal
interactions between the patch tokens and the primitive tokens. To predict dense outputs from the
query image, a dedicated DPT layer is attached to the query branch, which converts patch tokens into
pixel-aligned dense features. The DPT layer is instantiated with the Dual-DPT architecture proposed
in DA3 [43]: patch tokens collected from intermediate transformer layers are initially aggregated
through shared reassembly modules, and then fused via separate fusion layers to produce task-specific
feature maps.

Prediction on Primitive Parameters.
By predicting viewpoint-invariant 3D pointmaps, multi-view
transformers iteratively recover scene structures and camera poses layer by layer [71]. Departing
from their point-based representation, we further enhance our primitive grounding with 3D geometry
learning by tasking our model to predict frame-invariant primitive parameters, enabling both local
and mutual structure reasoning between the query and the map. In the query branch, one of the dense
feature maps from the DPT layer is mapped into a per-pixel plane parameter map P ∈R3×H×W
through a convolutional layer, while tokens from the map branch are decoded into plane parameters
using a 3-layer MLP. Following [47, 68, 87, 94], the prediction of a plane parameter here is a
3D vector defined as n/d. As map coordinate frames could be arbitrarily specified and may vary
significantly across different input augmentations, we anchor all predictions of plane parameters in
the query coordinate space, which provides a stable and consistent reference across different samples.
Conceptually, this design encourages the query branch to focus on perceiving local structure, while
the map branch learns to align its primitives to the query view.
Prediction on Camera Rays.
To directly target localization, a specialized prediction head is
attached to the query branch to decode the dense features into a per-pixel ray map R ∈R6×H×W .
Each camera ray r corresponds to the pixel through which it passes, and is defined as a 6D vector
[d; o] where the ray direction d ∈R3 and ray origin o ∈R3 are under the map coordinate frame.
The ray map serves as an over-parameterized camera representation, implicitly encoding both the
pose g and intrinsics. In the case of a classical perspective camera, the translation t is estimated by
averaging the ray origins, whereas the intrinsics and rotation q are derived via decomposition of an
optimal ray direction homography from an identity camera to the query camera, as detailed in [43].
Prediction from Prepended Tokens.
To implement a more straightforward localization alternative
while facilitating multi-task learning, we prepend a camera token to the query sequence and a map
token to the map sequence. At the final backbone layer, the two tokens are decoded into corresponding
poses through a shared MLP. In the map branch, the targeted pose is fixed to the identity pose with
the rotation quaternion q0 = [0, 0, 0, 1] and translation t0 = [0, 0, 0], which represents the map’s
reference frame. Accordingly, the query pose in this reference frame can be directly decoded from
the camera token.
Summary.
Towards camera localization, PGGT predicts multiple localization targets in a single
forward pass, including patch-primitive assignments, primitive parameters, camera rays, and poses.
We then describe three pathways through which the pose of a query can be inferred: (1) solve a
point-to-plane registration problem using the predicted patch-primitive assignment ˆ
A, the predicted
per-pixel parameters ˆP and the given parameters of map primitives {πm
i }, as in [90]; (2) derive the
pose from the predicted ray map ˆR; (3) directly decode the pose from the camera token. We refer to
the three alternatives above as Param.-based, Ray-based and Token-based approaches, respectively.

Training
Supervision.
PGGT is trained with a multi-task loss:
L = Lassign + Lparam + Lray + Lpose,
(2)
which combines supervision signals on all the prediction targets. Specifically, Lassign is the negative
log-likelihood loss on the soft assignment ˜
A, with supervision from the ground-truth assignment A;
Lparam combines the L1 loss and the cosine similarity loss applied to the predicted parameters; and
both the ray map loss Lray and the pose decoding loss Lpose are formulated using the L1 norm.
Training Datasets.
Following the dataset processing workflow constructed by [90], we scale our
training dataset up to approximately 400K query-map pairs from three public datasets: ScanNet [20],
ScanNet++ [92] and Matterport3D [12], covering over four thousand indoor scenes/regions in total.
Since ScanNet is also used for evaluation, scenes for training and testing are strictly separated to
ensure that all evaluation scenes are never seen during training.
Implementation Details.
The patch embedder is instantiated with the DINOv2 ViT-Large [56]
backbone. In the primitive embedder, we employ PointNet [60] as the 3D encoder, which takes 1024
5


points sampled from each primitive as input and is trained from scratch. The cross-modal transformer
backbone is configured with N = 12 layers for each modality and we feed patch tokens from the
2nd, 5th, 8th, and 11th layers into the DPT layer. The training images are resized and patchified
into lq=1200 tokens. Maps are isotropically resized and centered into a unit box, and all predictions
under the map coordinate frame should follow the same normalization. We use AdamW [51] and a
cosine LR schedule with a peak learning rate of 2 × 10−4. The model is trained for 125K iterations by
default, with bfloat16 precision and a batch size of 128 , which requires ∼2 days on 8×H20 GPUs.

Extension: Localization on 3D Layout
Having introduced PGGT in the dense surface map, where query observations are grounded to map
primitives that faithfully preserve the scene’s physical surfaces, we note that the same paradigm
naturally extends to minimalist 3D semantic models that represent only architectural layout.
Task Setting.
The scene layout follows the annotation format of Structured3D [97]. As shown in
Fig. 3, it can be translated into a 2D floorplan or a 3D minimal layout model that comprises a set of
planar primitives, each corresponding to an architectural element. Compared with primitives in the
dense surface map, those in the minimal layout model exhibit stronger regularity in terms of both
their parameters and spatial extents, and each primitive is further associated with one of five distinct
structural categories: wall, floor, door, window, and ceiling. Given such a minimal layout model, we
focus on localizing query images captured in a fully furnished scene, where the increased modality
gap poses a greater challenge to the detector-based feature matching conventions [90].

Isolation Injection.
Upon close inspection, as shown in Fig. 3, the layout primitives are organized
at the room level, i.e., no primitive is shared across rooms. Meanwhile, akin to prior works [16, 25],
we do not look into neighboring rooms through doors, treating all room connections as opaque.
Consequently, query observations are expected to be grounded only to primitives within the same
room. We enforce the isolation bias by applying attention masks to the self-attention blocks in the
map backbone, ensuring that each primitive token attends only to other tokens within the same room.
4.2
Training
The total loss in Eq. (2) is augmented with the cross-entropy loss Lseg for semantic supervision.
Following [25], our model is trained from scratch on 3250 layout models from the training split
of Structured3D [97], with query images randomly sampled from 21 835 photorealistic panoramic
images. Both empty and furnished panoramas are used for training, and query images are sampled
with FoVs ranging from 45° to 135°. Due to the shape regularity, the number of points sampled per
primitive is reduced to 256. Unless otherwise specified, the training recipe follows the settings in
Sec. 3.3.

Evaluation Metrics.
Since most prior studies on layout localization focus on estimating 3-DoF
camera pose on 2D floorplans, we follow their standard evaluation protocol [16, 52]. Specifically, we
extract the 2D translation txy ∈R2 and yaw angle qyaw ∈R from our 6-DoF pose predictions, from
which we compute recall at multiple thresholds and report median errors over samples successfully
localized within 1 m (i.e., ∆txy ≤1 m).
We first present results on S3D-official in Tab. 3, where we also highlight several methodological
differences: (1) all prior methods adopt the render-and-compare paradigm, which involves rendering
the maps into alternative representations (e.g., a set of semantic panoramas) by sweeping the entire
candidate pose space; (2) only SPVLoc [25] and our method utilize 3D layout models for 6-DoF
localization. Table 3 shows that our method achieves top performance across evaluation metrics, and
it is the only approach that (1) operates in a feed-forward pipeline without requiring exhaustive map
rendering, and (2) does not depend on FoV priors. Moreover, our method surpasses SPVLoc on the
S3D-resampled dataset, as reported in Tab. 4. In Fig. 5, we show two examples on S3D-resampled,
illustrating localization results along with additional predicted outputs.

Multi-task Learning.
We conduct ablation studies to evaluate the benefit of predicting multiple
targets in our model. To reduce computational cost and better assess convergence behavior, models in
all ablation experiments are trained for a reduced budget of 75K iterations. For the basic components
introduced in Sec. 3.2, we verify the benefit of training the network to predict multiple localization
targets that go beyond the ray map. As shown in Tab. 5, training without downstream predictions
on patch-primitive assignment and primitive parameters leads to a noticeable drop in localization
performance, whereas excluding the prepended camera and map tokens has a milder and mixed effect.

Evaluation on Multiple Rooms.
To assess generalization to larger maps, we follow the standard
Integrated Rooms protocol [8], arranging 12Scenes [75] rooms on a 2D grid with 5 m cells and
progressively merging adjacent rooms. We evaluate the original model without fine-tuning, localizing
each query within the integrated map. As shown in Fig. 6, our method consistently outperforms
PlanaReLoc [90] across evaluated scales, although recall decreases substantially as more rooms are
integrated, likely due to repetitive structures and the distribution gap from room-scale training. These
results demonstrate generalization to artificially integrated multi-room maps while highlighting the
limitations of full-map inference, motivating coarse-to-fine localization for larger environments.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we have presented PGGT, a cross-modal transformer network that estimates camera
poses from 3D planar primitive-based maps in a feed-forward manner. The design of PGGT serves as
an instantiation of the new concept that we refer to as primitive-grounded localization. By jointly
reasoning over query image patches and map primitives through two modality-specific interconnected
transformer stacks, PGGT predicts multiple localization targets, with poses derived from the densely
predicted ray map yielding the best performance. Comprehensive evaluation across hundreds of
surface maps that comprise dense planar primitives demonstrates the superior performance of our
method. Furthermore, our method naturally extends to minimal layout models, outperforming existing
layout localization methods and highlighting the potential of primitive-grounded localization as a
promising paradigm for diverse primitive-based map representations.

A.1
Datasets
Our model is trained on two independent sets of data, targeting localization on the dense surface
map and the minimal layout model, respectively. Table 8 summarizes the statistics of these training
datasets, and we provide detailed descriptions in the following.
Dense Surface Map.
For surface maps, we inherit the data processing pipeline from PlanaRe-
Loc [90], expanding the training set to three public indoor datasets. Specifically, maps are extracted
from the officially provided dense reconstructions using the sequential RANSAC [23] plane-fitting
scripts from [46, 86]; the extracted maps are further compressed with polygon simplification al-
gorithms [26]. Training images of the real-world datasets are sampled from the original RGB-D
sequences at regular intervals, and each sampled frame will be checked for adequate primitive ob-
servations and precise pose alignment. MP3D-Habitat is the only synthetic dataset curated from
Matterport3D by [37] whose training images are generated using Habitat [65]. The resolution of
training images is standardized to 640×480. Finally, per-pixel map primitive IDs are rendered onto
training images to obtain the dense pixel-primitive associations, which can further be leveraged
to calculate the ground-truth per-pixel parameter map P or downsampled into the patch-primitive
assignment matrix A for supervision.

Minimal Layout Model.
S3D-official and S3D-resampled share the same collection of layout
models, but differ in the query images used for training and evaluation. S3D-official comprises
officially provided high-resolution perspective images in a format of 16:9 with a fixed FoV of 80°,
which are resized to the resolution of 640×360 in our experiments. On the other hand, the S3D-
resampled dataset proposed by [25] starts from the original panorama images and samples training
perspective images on the fly with random camera orientations (yaw ∈[-180°, 180°], pitch ∈[-10°,
10°] and roll ∈[-10°, 10°]) as well as random FoVs from 45° to 135°. These perspective images
17


are sampled at a resolution of 320×320. The sampled images will be verified for observations of at
least three semantic classes (e.g., wall-ceiling-floor and wall-window-floor) and adequate coverage
of the room interior (i.e., doors and windows not occupying more than 70 % of the image). For
efficiency, we generate at least 100 verified candidates of yaw, pitch, roll and FoV for each panorama
when preparing the dataset, and during training, images are directly sampled from these candidates,
avoiding the redundant computation for verification. Likewise, annotations of per-pixel primitive IDs
are rendered onto the original panorama, and then can be sampled to generate perspective images for
training on the fly.
A.2
Implementation Details
Input Augmentations.
During training, we apply brightness, color and contrast jittering, and
randomly rescale the query images to heights between 384 and 600 pixels before cropping or padding
them to the final resolution of 640×480. The maps are normalized into a unit box and randomly
rescaled and rotated by a random yaw angle, with map primitives shuffled before being fed into
the model. For samples from Matterport3D, maps are assembled online from submaps of multiple
regions according to the query images. Specifically, we merge the submaps corresponding to regions
observed by the query image together with those of spatially nearby regions, resulting in dynamic
map coverage.

Plane Recovery.
Recovering piecewise planar primitives from the query image is not the main focus
of this work, since camera poses can be directly derived from the predicted ray map. Nevertheless,
plane recovery is a geometric by-product of our method, and we briefly describe how its results are
obtained for visualizations (as shown in Figs. 4 and 5) and the subsequent analysis in Sec. A.3. The
task of plane recovery requires both 2D plane segments and their corresponding plane parameters. The
2D plane segments are first obtained by partitioning the predicted parameter map ˆP q ∈R3×H×W
according to the predicted patch-primitive assignment matrix ˆ
A. Since the network does not enforce
local consistency, i.e., densely predicted per-pixel parameters within each plane segment are not
guaranteed to be consistent, the final plane parameters for each segment are robustly estimated with
RANSAC.

Impact of Primitive Richness.
In practice, camera localization becomes unreliable when query
observations are ambiguous and uninformative, e.g., when only a few map primitives are observed.
To analyze the impact of primitive richness on localization performance, we plot the localization
performance as a function of the number of actually observed primitives in Fig. 8. As shown in Fig. 8a
and b, observing more primitives generally improves localization accuracy. Notably, compared to
the feature-matching method PlanaReLoc, which depends on explicit primitive correspondences
for geometric pose estimation and can become unstable when few primitives are observed, our
method maintains robust performance, yielding reliable camera poses even under sparse observations.
Moreover, as shown in Fig. 8c, when the query observation becomes cluttered with more primitives,
there is a notable drop in the matching F1 score of PlanaReLoc due to its degraded plane recovery
performance as planes in richer observations tend to be less salient and harder to recover. In contrast,
our method performs exhaustive interaction between image patches and map primitives during the
single feed-forward pass, allowing the model to finely ground the query observations to the map
primitives, and thereby exhibiting a more stable matching performance.

A.4
Limitations and Future Work
Failure Cases.
We show representative failure cases of our method on both surface maps and layout
models in Fig. 13 and Fig. 14, respectively. In some cases (Fig. 13a, b, and Fig. 14a, b), poses derived
from the ray map are less accurate than those estimated using predicted primitive parameters. We
also observe that the failure cases mainly arise from three aspects: (1) challenging visual conditions,
such as motion blur and low lighting; (2) limited structural details or highly repetitive patterns in the
scene; and (3) uninformative query observations.
Limitations.
First, although our primitive-grounded localization paradigm naturally extends from
dense surface maps to minimal layout models, the network is trained separately for each task. Then,
our work is currently limited to maps that are assembled from planar primitives, which assume the
presence of planar surfaces and regularized layouts, and thus may not be suitable for unstructured
environments or scenes exhibiting complex or non-planar structures. Meanwhile, our model is

Future Work.
The proposed primitive-grounded localization paradigm can potentially extend to
a variety of high-level map representations in the future, such as LoD city models and structured
language commands [5]. This is enabled by the flexibility and scalability of our feed-forward
localization framework, which allows it to accommodate diverse map representations and even unify
localization across different map modalities within a single model. In addition, extending the method
to panoramic or sequential inputs offers another promising direction for practical application, as
richer visual cues and temporal context could effectively help resolve structural ambiguities.
A.5
Societal Impacts
Our work focuses on the development of a novel camera localization method that leverages high-level
map primitives under a unified and scalable framework, which is highly suitable for memory-
constrained and real-time applications such as AR/VR and robotics. However, with the rapid
growth in the popularity of cloud-based localization services, potential privacy concerns arise during
localization, as users’ scene-related information may be exposed to unauthorized access or misuse.
One possible mitigation is to anonymize sensitive scene data on-device, for instance by tokenizing
the query image and the map prior to transmission.
