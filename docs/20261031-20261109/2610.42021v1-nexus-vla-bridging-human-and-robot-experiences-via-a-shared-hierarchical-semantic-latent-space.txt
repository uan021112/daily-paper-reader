ABSTRACT
Large-scale egocentric human videos provide rich and scalable manipulation ex-
perience for robot policy learning, yet substantial embodiment gaps in visual ap-
pearance, kinematic structure, and action spaces hinder effective knowledge trans-
fer. Existing methods often decouple cross-embodiment knowledge alignment
from robot policy learning, making it difficult for human manipulation knowledge
to directly and fully contribute to robot action learning. To address this issue,
we propose Nexus-VLA, a hybrid policy learning framework that enables cross-
embodiment knowledge transfer through end-to-end human-robot joint training,
allowing human manipulation experience to directly contribute to robot policy
learning. We first introduce a hierarchical cross-embodiment semantic alignment
paradigm that progressively establishes shared manipulation semantics from task
intent to fine-grained interactions, enabling more complete use of the manipula-
tion knowledge contained in human videos. Building on this hierarchy, we design
a shared manipulation latent representation that jointly encodes multi-level shared
knowledge into an internal representation directly used for continuous action gen-
eration, improving policy learning efficiency while effectively leveraging human
knowledge. Across RoboCasa-GR1, OOD settings, and real-world robot experi-
ments, Nexus-VLA achieves an 80.5% task success rate on RoboCasa-GR1 and
demonstrates strong advantages in human-data utilization, learning efficiency, and
cross-scenario generalization.

INTRODUCTION
Because collecting robot demonstrations at scale remains costly and challenging (Khazatsky et al.,
2024), massive egocentric human videos, which capture rich real-world manipulation and interaction
knowledge, provide a scalable source of data for robot policy learning (Grauman et al., 2022). How-
ever, substantial differences between humans and robots in visual appearance, kinematic structure,
and action spaces (Chen et al., 2025a) make it challenging to extract manipulation representations
that are semantically consistent across embodiments, thereby hindering the effective transfer of hu-
man manipulation knowledge to robot policies. Despite these differences, humans and robots share
similar manipulation semantics and interaction structures when performing the same task. There-
fore, a central challenge in learning robot policies from human videos is to identify such shared
representations and transform them into a unified representation that effectively supports robot ac-
tion generation.
To better exploit human data, recent studies have begun to use embodiment-invariant manipula-
tion semantics in human demonstrations as bridges between humans and robots (Bharadhwaj et al.,
2023). However, existing approaches often establish human-robot correspondences through only
one aspect of semantic consistency, such as aligning high-level task semantics (Kim et al., 2026)
or matching human hand motion with robot end effectors (Chen et al., 2026), leaving shared cross-
embodiment knowledge only partially modeled. Manipulation semantics are not a single flat signal,
but form a hierarchy that progressively connects abstract intent to concrete physical interaction.

define the shared structure of human and robot manipulation, suggesting that cross-embodiment
correspondence should be progressively established across different semantic levels.
However, explicitly modeling manipulation semantics separately at different levels is insufficient to
capture their underlying structure. These semantics are not independent; rather, they describe the
same manipulation process at different levels of abstraction and form a progressively refined, hierar-
chically constrained structure. Modeling each level in isolation makes it difficult to preserve cross-
level dependencies and consistency, motivating a unified representation of multi-level manipulation
semantics. However, directly using explicit semantics as intermediate policy representations intro-
duces additional computation and decision latency during inference (Bai et al., 2026), while their
structured or discrete form is also ill-suited for directly driving continuous action generation (Wei
et al., 2026). The key challenge is therefore to transform hierarchical manipulation knowledge
shared across embodiments into an internal representation suitable for action generation, allowing
knowledge from human demonstrations to directly participate in robot policy decision-making. In
this way, multi-level knowledge from human demonstrations can be more completely transferred to
robot policies, enabling more effective use of human data.
As illustrated in Figure 1, we propose Nexus-VLA, an efficient mixed policy-learning framework
for jointly learning from human and robot demonstrations. Nexus-VLA encodes hierarchical manip-
ulation knowledge shared across embodiments into a Hierarchical Semantic Latent that directly par-
ticipates in action prediction, allowing shared knowledge from human demonstrations to effectively
contribute to robot policy learning while improving cross-scenario generalization. Specifically, we
first introduce a hierarchical cross-embodiment semantic alignment paradigm. With structured ma-
nipulation experts, we generate consistent supervision for human and robot demonstrations across
manipulation intent, spatial semantics, motion semantics, and interaction semantics, progressively
establishing coarse-to-fine semantic correspondences between the two embodiments. Building on
this hierarchy, we aggregate these interdependent shared semantics through joint multi-level predic-
tion objectives into a Semantic Latent, which is combined with vision-language features for action
prediction. The robot action learning objective further shapes this latent representation through end-
to-end optimization, encouraging it to preserve cross-embodiment semantic consistency while re-
maining directly useful for action generation. Nexus-VLA jointly trains on mixed human and robot
demonstrations in a single stage and, at inference time, directly predicts actions from the Semantic
Latent and vision-language features without explicitly generating intermediate semantics.
We systematically evaluate Nexus-VLA on RoboCasa-GR1, multiple out-of-distribution (OOD) sce-
narios, and real-robot platforms. Nexus-VLA achieves an 80.5% task success rate on RoboCasa-
GR1 and demonstrates strong generalization across OOD tasks and real-robot settings. These results
show that connecting human experience with robot action learning through hierarchical semantic la-
tent representations enables more effective use of large-scale human demonstrations and improves
both robot policy learning and cross-scenario generalization.

In summary, the main contributions of this paper are as follows:
1. An Efficient Single-Stage Co-Training Framework: We overcome the limitations of traditional
methods that rely on paired cross-embodiment data or multi-stage pre-training by realizing end-to-
end joint optimization over unaligned human videos and robot demonstrations, which significantly
improves both human data utilization and policy learning efficiency.
2. A Unified Hierarchical Semantic Latent Representation: We are the first to encode multi-level
manipulation semantics from abstract intents to fine-grained physical interactions into a compact
latent variable. This representation retains rich operational structures while bypassing the need for
explicit intermediate semantic inference, directly interfacing with continuous action generation.

RELATED WORK
Learning Robot Policies from Human Videos. Leveraging rich manipulation experience from
large-scale egocentric human videos to learn robot policies has been widely explored recently. One
line of work first pre-trains visual, kinematic, or action-related representations on human videos,
and subsequently adapts or fine-tunes the policy using robot demonstrations (Yang et al., 2025b;
Ma et al., 2026). Further efforts introduce explicit cross-embodiment alignment stages to facilitate
knowledge transfer from humans to robots (Zheng et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026). However, such
multi-stage pipelines allow human knowledge to act on final policy learning only indirectly. Conse-
quently, recent studies have begun to explore direct co-training on mixed human and robot demon-
strations (Kareer et al., 2025), yet straightforward data mixing still struggles to establish consistent
cross-embodiment manipulation supervision.
Cross-Embodiment Manipulation Alignment. To bridge embodiment discrepancies, constructing
cross-embodiment stable manipulation representations has emerged as a core strategy for connecting
human experience with robot control. Existing work typically establishes human-robot correspon-
dences at different levels of manipulation semantics, ranging from high-level task intents (Kim et al.,
2026; Lee et al., 2026) and mid-level spatial affordances or geometric grounding (Yang et al., 2025a;
Xu et al., 2025), to low-level end-effector trajectories and physical interactions (Chen et al., 2026).
Other methods narrow the embodiment gap by leveraging paired videos or unified pose spaces (Xie
et al., 2026; Kareer et al., 2025). However, these approaches generally focus on single-level or lo-
cal semantic alignment, isolating the inter-level dependencies across semantic granularities and thus
limiting the depth and completeness of cross-embodiment knowledge transfer.
Latent Manipulation Representations for Robot Control. Beyond extracting shared semantics,
transforming these semantics into internal representations suitable for robot control represents an-
other core challenge. One category of methods explicitly expresses the semantics as structured
intermediate information (e.g., affordance maps or predicted trajectories) (Li et al., 2025; Xu et al.,
2025; Yang et al., 2025a). While interpretable, such methods require generating explicit intermedi-
ate representations during inference, which introduces computational overhead and decision latency;
structured signals cannot directly serve as an effective interface for continuous action generation.
Another line of work encodes manipulation information into compact latent representations (Ye
et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025b; Bu et al., 2025). Although well-suited for end-to-end control, these
latent spaces lack explicit multi-level semantic supervision, making it difficult to form structured
semantic constraints and weakening their capability to support precise action generation.

3.1
HIERARCHICALLY SHAPED SHARED MANIPULATION LATENT REPRESENTATION
To transform cross-embodiment manipulation knowledge into an internal representation that can di-
rectly support action generation, Nexus-VLA constructs a Hierarchical Semantic Latent and shapes
it with multi-level manipulation semantics ranging from high-level task understanding to concrete
physical interaction.
3.1.1
HIERARCHICAL SHARED MANIPULATION SEMANTICS
Despite differences in visual appearance, kinematic structure, and execution form, humans and
robots share similar task goals, object spatial relations, motion processes, and physical interactions
when performing the same manipulation. We organize such cross-embodiment shared information
into four semantic levels (Intent, Spatial, Motion, and Interaction) according to their level of ab-
straction, progressively grounding manipulation semantics from high-level task understanding to
concrete actions and physical interactions.
Specifically, Intent represents the local task goal, i.e., “what to do”; Spatial represents the spatial
locations of the manipulated object and target region, i.e., “what to manipulate and where”; Mo-
tion represents the motion trend of the acting embodiment toward subsequent interaction, i.e., “how
to move”; and Interaction represents effective interaction locations and contact states, i.e., “how
to interact.” These four types of semantics can be defined consistently for both human and robot
demonstrations, providing shared semantic supervision for cross-embodiment representation learn-
ing and subsequently shaping the Hierarchical Semantic Latent.

4.2
OVERALL PERFORMANCE AND GENERALIZATION
As shown in Table 1, on the standard RoboCasa-GR1 test distribution, Nexus-VLA achieves an
average success rate of 80.5% across all 24 tasks, outperforming the strongest baseline, ACE-Ego-0,
at 72.8% by 7.7 percentage points. Across the five task groups, Nexus-VLA achieves the highest
success rates on Cuttingboard, Placemat, and Tray, and ranks second on both Close and Plate. These
results demonstrate stable in-distribution performance across diverse manipulation tasks.
We further construct controlled OOD tests in RoboCasa-GR1 to evaluate generalization under visual
changes, scene composition shifts, and novel objects. As shown in Table 2, Nexus-VLA achieves an
average success rate of 68.9% across the six OOD settings, compared with 48.2% for the Baseline,
corresponding to a 20.7 percentage-point gap. Under illumination changes and visual noise, Nexus-
VLA achieves 76.8% and 73.6%, exceeding the Baseline by 14.8 and 15.2 percentage points, re-
spectively. Under novel spatial configurations and object-receptacle compositions, the performance
gaps further increase to 19.8 and 21.8 percentage points. These results indicate that the manipulation
knowledge learned by Nexus-VLA transfers more robustly to new visual conditions and scene com-
positions, consistent with its hierarchical semantic modeling of task intent, object roles, and spatial
relations.

4.3
ABLATION STUDY AND LEARNING EFFICIENCY
As shown in Table 3, hierarchical semantic modeling consistently improves policy learning. Com-
pared with the Baseline, Hierarchical Alignment increases the success rate from 72.42% to 75.67%,
a gain of 3.25 percentage points, indicating that multi-level supervision from task intent to fine-
grained interaction provides useful structure for manipulation learning. Under the same hierarchical
semantic supervision, replacing independent semantic representations with a Unified Semantic La-
tent further improves the success rate from 76.92% to 78.25%. This suggests that the gains arise not
only from additional semantic supervision, but also from jointly representing manipulation knowl-
edge across different semantic levels in a form that better supports action generation.
The co-training results further reveal how human data contributes to policy learning. With matched
robot-data exposure, direct Human-Robot Co-training converges more slowly (71.75%), but reaches
76.32% at 200K steps, surpassing the Robot-only Baseline peak of 72.92%. In contrast, when
Hierarchical Alignment and the Unified Semantic Latent are kept fixed, adding Human-Robot Co-
training increases the success rate from 78.25% to 80.17%, providing an additional gain of 1.92
percentage points. This controlled comparison suggests that hierarchical semantic alignment and the
unified latent representation better organize cross-embodiment shared knowledge, allowing human
manipulation experience to contribute more effectively to robot policy learning.

CONCLUSION
We present Nexus-VLA, an end-to-end framework for joint learning from unpaired human and robot
demonstrations. By aligning cross-embodiment manipulation knowledge across semantic levels and
encoding it into a shared Hierarchical Semantic Latent for action generation, Nexus-VLA enables
human experience to directly improve robot policy learning. Experiments on RoboCasa-GR1 and
real robots show improved manipulation performance, learning efficiency, and generalization. These
results show that hierarchical semantic latents effectively transfer scalable human manipulation ex-
perience to robot control.

A.1.2
DATA QUALITY CONTROL AND LOW-QUALITY SAMPLE FILTERING
Because the structured supervision is generated automatically by offline experts, we apply hierarchi-
cal quality control and conservative filtering to reduce the effects of detection errors, tracking drift,
and temporal noise. For recoverable local errors, we first rerun object localization and segmentation;
a demonstration is discarded only when the corresponding supervision cannot be reliably recovered.
Quality control focuses primarily on visual annotation quality and temporal consistency. For visual
annotations, we examine the completeness and temporal stability of the manipuland, target, and hand
masks, including persistent missing regions, abrupt changes in position or scale, and tracking results
that are inconsistent with the observed manipulation motion. Samples that remain ambiguous after
these checks are further reviewed using a frozen vision-language model. For temporal consistency,
we verify key interaction events, active-hand tracking, and the temporal alignment among video
observations, state trajectories, and structured supervision.
Demonstrations that fail these quality checks and cannot be reliably recovered are removed at the
episode level. For otherwise valid demonstrations with locally missing supervision, the correspond-
ing invalid targets are masked out using validity masks while the remaining reliable supervision is
retained.

To further evaluate robustness under controlled distribution shifts, we construct four real-world OOD
settings while preserving the underlying task objective and success criterion. Each setting primarily
stresses a different aspect of the hierarchical manipulation semantics.
21


Object Distractor introduces additional objects that are visually or semantically confusable with
task-relevant objects, requiring the policy to maintain the correct object roles under clutter. For
example, we place an additional tape roll on the table when the instruction specifies the tape on the
plate, introduce plates with distractor colors when the target is the green plate, or add another towel
outside the sink. The intended manipuland and target remain unchanged.
Spatial Layout changes the relative positions of the manipuland, target, and surrounding objects
while preserving their identities and the task instruction. This setting evaluates whether the policy
can re-ground task-relevant entities and execute the same manipulation under novel spatial configu-
rations.
Motion Constraint introduces obstacles along the nominal motion path without changing the task
goal or task-relevant objects. The robot must therefore adapt its wrist trajectory and use an alterna-
tive feasible motion path to complete the same manipulation.
Unseen Object replaces the manipulated object with an object absent from the robot training demon-
strations while preserving the task structure. This setting evaluates whether manipulation behavior
transfers to novel object appearances and geometries rather than relying on memorized object in-
stances.
