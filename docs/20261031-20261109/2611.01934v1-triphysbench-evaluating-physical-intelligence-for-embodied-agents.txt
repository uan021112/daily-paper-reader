A B S T R A C T
Embodied agents need physical intelligence to understand their surroundings through physical perception,
physical knowledge retrieval, and physical reasoning. These capabilities must work together to interpret
events and make quantitative judgments. Existing benchmarks advance physical understanding and
general-purpose tool use, yet the coordination of these capabilities in physical scenarios remains
insufficiently characterized. Inspired by Popper’s Three Worlds, we introduce TriPhysBench, a benchmark
of 410 questions from 200 videos that brings physical perception, physical knowledge retrieval, and
physical reasoning into a shared evaluation setting. Its measurement-grounded and knowledge-grounded
subtasks impose complementary demands on obtaining observations and acquiring external specifications,
then applying them to quantitative problems. Among six representative vision-language models, the best
achieves an answer accuracy of only 50.38%, revealing limited performance on tasks that require these
capabilities to work together. We further introduce TriPhys, a framework that learns a unified policy
for physical perception, knowledge retrieval, and quantitative reasoning from complete tool-integrated
trajectories. The policy uses intermediate reasoning to guide retrieval and computation, then incorporates
tool feedback into subsequent physical inference. On 110 questions from videos excluded from training,
TriPhys improves answer and solution scores across three Qwen configurations, with a 17.7% relative gain
in answer accuracy for TriPhys-27B over its Qwen3.6-27B backbone. This work provides a benchmark
and a learning pathway toward embodied agents that move beyond recognizing events to understanding
and reasoning about the physical processes behind them.
1. Introduction
Embodied agents need to connect observations and knowl-
edge with the physical conditions of their tasks [1–6]. This
requires interpreting observations, obtaining relevant prop-
erties, and applying physical laws [7–9]. We study physical
intelligence as the coordination of physical perception, physical
knowledge retrieval, and physical reasoning. To characterize
the complementary roles of these capabilities, we draw on
Popper’s Three Worlds [10], associating perception with the
physical world (W1), retrieval with objective knowledge (W3),
and reasoning with mental activity (W2). In the schematic
example in Figure 1, physical perception estimates wrist speed
from the video, knowledge retrieval supplies a hand-mass prior,
and physical reasoning combines them to estimate kinetic
energy. Accurate physical reasoning requires the quantities re-
covered from video, the retrieved properties, and the modeling
assumptions to be consistent with the same physical situation.
Physical understanding benchmarks test plausibility and
event prediction [11–13], while PhysBench broadens coverage
across properties, relationships, scenes, and dynamics [8].
QuantiPhy advances quantitative video reasoning by estimating
kinematic quantities using a supplied physical prior [14]. Mul-
tidisciplinary benchmarks such as MMMU, OlympiadBench,
and MMVU test expert-level scientific reasoning over images
and videos [15–17]. Knowledge-based visual benchmarks
examine access to external information [18, 19], and GAIA

EmbodiedBench further tests vision-driven agents across high-

Across these settings, a central question remains insufficiently
examined: how reliably can agents acquire the required phys-
ical quantities, select knowledge applicable to the observed
conditions, and use both in quantitative reasoning?
We introduce TriPhysBench, comprising 410 questions
from 200 videos of physical events and human activities. To
construct the benchmark, we adapt event-graph-based question
generation [21] to connect video observations, external prop-
erties, and physical relations through physical event graphs
(Figure 2). The resulting questions form two complementary
subtasks. Measurement-grounded Physical Reasoning (MPR,
228 questions) uses supported video measurements and re-
quires solvers to recover designated observations from the
video when they are withheld from the question. Knowledge-
grounded Physical Reasoning (KPR, 182 questions) provides
the video context and stated observations, while requiring
the retrieval, selection, and application of external physical
specifications. Across both subtasks, withholding selected
observations or specifications controls difficulty, and questions
take four answer forms: point estimation, interval estimation,
physical judgment, and scenario comparison. Large language
model review and rule-based checks screen the questions and
their reference solutions.

in identifying relevant physical quantities and carrying them
through numerical derivations, highlighting the need to coor-

Our main contributions are:
• We introduce TriPhysBench, a benchmark with two
complementary subtasks constructed through physical
event graphs and controlled information withholding
to evaluate the coordination of physical perception,
knowledge retrieval, and reasoning.
• We propose TriPhys, a framework that combines su-
pervision of complete tool-integrated trajectories with
preference optimization for physical reasoning, improv-
ing answer and solution scores across three Qwen
configurations on videos excluded from training.
• We provide systematic evaluations and analyses of six
VLMs across both subtasks, including failure diagnosis
of three models that examines variable identification,
relation selection, numerical derivation, and conclusion
formation.
2. Related Work
2.1. Physical Intelligence Evaluation
Physical evaluation spans several task formats. PIQA
tests physical commonsense in language [32], while PHYRE
requires actions that solve simulated physics puzzles [33].
Controlled-scene benchmarks assess plausibility, interaction
prediction, and causal events [11–13]; IntPhys 2 extends
possible-versus-impossible event discrimination to more com-
plex synthetic environments [34]. PhysBench covers object
properties, relationships, scenes, and dynamics [8], whereas
PhysGame examines physical violations in gameplay videos
[35]. In real videos, CausalVQA tests causal understanding
through anticipation, planning, and hypothetical or counter-
factual questions [36], and QuantiPhy estimates object size,
velocity, and acceleration using a given physical prior [14].
Spatial reasoning benchmarks and models probe distances,
sizes, and spatial relations in images and videos [37–39],
while EgoThink and OpenEQA assess understanding from
an agent’s first-person viewpoint [40, 41]. Generative inter-
active environments and video generation models raise the
complementary question of whether learned dynamics follow
physical laws [42, 43]. These benchmarks examine distinct
aspects of physical competence, from judging event plausibility
to estimating numerical quantities.
Scientific problem-solving benchmarks such as SciBench
and OlympicArena evaluate multistep reasoning across disci-
plines [44,45], and general multimodal evaluations examine

2.2. Knowledge Retrieval and Tool Use
Knowledge-based visual question answering connects
images with information beyond their visible content. OK-VQA
and A-OKVQA require outside knowledge and commonsense
[18, 52], while InfoSeek targets entity-specific information
[19]. MuRAG retrieves from image–text memory for open
question answering [53]; ReflectiVA learns to assess the need
for retrieval and the relevance of retrieved knowledge [54].
ToolVQA evaluates multistep visual reasoning with external
tools [55], and MMSearch-R1 learns image and text search poli-
cies through reinforcement learning [56]. Agent benchmarks
such as VisualWebArena, GTA, and TravelPlanner evaluate
multimodal web interaction, real-world tool invocation, and
constrained planning [57–59]; CodeAct uses executable code
as a unified action space [60], and WebThinker equips rea-
soning models with autonomous web search for deep research
[61]. GAIA and OmniGAIA extend evaluation to reasoning
and multi-turn tool execution across information sources and
modalities [20, 21]. Our focus is the physical applicability
of acquired information: a retrieved property must match the
observed object and conditions before entering a calculation.
This connects knowledge acquisition with perception and
quantitative reasoning in the same evaluation (Table 1).
2.3. Learning Tool-Integrated Reasoning
ReAct interleaves reasoning and actions to gather infor-
mation and update plans [62]; Toolformer learns when to
call APIs and incorporate their results [63]. PAL delegates
computation to executable programs [64], while ToRA and
ToolLLM learn from tool-integrated solutions and tool-use
trajectories [23, 24]. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
learns from preferred and rejected outputs [25]. Subsequent
variants remove the reference model [65], iterate preference
optimization over reasoning chains [66], or improve a model
against its own earlier outputs [67], and ETO constructs
preference pairs from successful and failed agent trajectories
[68]. In multimodal models, RLHF-V aligns behavior using
fine-grained correctional feedback [69]; Visual CoT, composi-
tional chain-of-thought prompting, LLaVA-CoT, and Insight-V
elicit structured or long-chain visual reasoning [70–73]; and
Visual-RFT and Video-R1 apply reinforcement fine-tuning
with rule-based rewards to images and videos [74, 75]. For
multimodal tool use, VITAL combines trajectory supervision
and reinforcement learning for on-demand video perception
[76], and VTool-R1 learns Python-based visual tool use from
outcome rewards [77]. In physical reasoning, PhysGame intro-
duces instruction and preference data for training PhysVLM
[35], while IPR-1 learns through world-model rollouts in
interactive games [78]. OmniAtlas combines tool-integrated
trajectory supervision with preference optimization for omni-
modal tasks [21]. TriPhys applies these learning principles to
complete physical solutions, preserving dependencies between
observations, retrieved properties, and numerical derivations.
Intermediate reasoning guides tool calls, and tool feedback
informs subsequent inference.

3.1. Task Design and Video Sources
Each question pairs a video with a physical problem and
asks for a supported solution and final answer. The solver
receives any observations explicitly stated in the question and
can use external retrieval and numerical computation. Physical
perception supplies relevant observations, knowledge retrieval
supplies applicable properties or relations, and reasoning com-
bines them into an answer. Reference solutions and supporting
records are retained as construction and evaluation annotations.
Measurement-grounded Physical Reasoning. This sub-
task contains 228 questions from 90 EgoExo4D recordings
of skilled activities, including bicycle repair and sports [79].
Construction uses a third-person view and three-dimensional
body-pose annotations to establish supported measurements.
Questions vary how many observations are given, requiring
the solver to recover designated quantities from the video
when they are withheld. The task combines this acquisition
of observations with physical knowledge and quantitative
reasoning.
Knowledge-grounded Physical Reasoning. This sub-
task contains 182 questions from 110 recordings in BAAI-
DataCube’s Physics-aware-videos collection [80], covering
processes such as collisions, oscillations, fluid motion, and
deformation. These videos lack the calibrated pose reference
available in EgoExo4D. Questions therefore state the estimated
observations and require the solver to obtain and apply external
specifications, such as an object’s mass or dimensions. The
challenge is to select properties that match the physical
situation and use them in the derivation.
Both subtasks use four answer forms: point estimation,
interval estimation, physical judgment, and scenario compari-
son. These specify the requested output independently of the
source or difficulty. The shared forms allow both acquisition
settings to test numerical estimates as well as judgments and
comparisons grounded in physical quantities. Appendix A
gives the corresponding TYPE labels and reference-answer
conventions.
The four cases in Figure 4 illustrate how these requirements
appear in individual questions. Video frames establish the
event context, physical properties supply quantities needed
for the calculation, and the reference solution connects them to
the requested answer form. The examples cover hand motion
during bicycle repair, a marble on a stepped track, a marble on
a curved track, and heat conduction from molten sealing wax.
Video screening first matches source action descriptions
or captions against physical event categories and then checks
technical quality, visible motion, and whether an identifiable
subject supports a quantitative question. Gemini-3.6-Flash [27]
then parses accepted recordings into intervals with scene and
event descriptions that support observation extraction.
3.2. Observation and Knowledge Acquisition
For the measurement-grounded subtask, pose-based human
measurements and tracked object trajectories supply quanti-
ties tied to video intervals and available geometry. Physical
distances and speeds require compatible coordinates and
timestamps. Retained measurements include their supporting
records and construction tolerances, establishing which obser-
vations can support a metric answer. These tolerances express
the uncertainty adopted during construction. Appendix A
details measurement eligibility, extraction tools, and temporal
alignment.
For the knowledge-grounded subtask, visual estimation
targets event counts, angles, and relative quantities that do
not require a pixel-to-meter conversion. Their interpretation
still depends on the viewpoint and comparison reference. A
separate retrieval step obtains mass, dimension, or material
specifications for identifiable objects. A retrieved property must
match the object’s identity and the conditions of the question be-
fore it enters the reference derivation. Any additional absolute
value explicitly stated in a question serves as a given problem
condition. Together, the two routes provide observations and
knowledge with defined physical roles.

3.4. Quality Review
Quality review assesses question clarity, answer correct-
ness, support for physical quantities, and coherence of the
reference solution. Large language model (LLM) review is
followed by rule-based checks, and candidates that fail the
acceptance criteria are excluded. In an additional review
of all 410 questions, GPT-5.5 [26] and Gemini-3.6-Flash
independently judge question understandability and answer
correctness; together with the DeepSeek-V3.2 construction
review, the three judges agree on both criteria for 95.6% of
assessed questions. Appendix A specifies the assessment scope,
raw agreement statistics, and detailed review procedure.
3.5. Dataset Statistics
Aggregate performance on TriPhysBench is driven pri-
marily by quantitative estimation, which accounts for over
three quarters of its questions (Figure 3b). The benchmark
therefore places particular weight on converting observations
and physical knowledge into consistent numerical answers.
Work and energy form the largest shared topic, while the
knowledge-grounded subtask extends coverage to fluid, ther-
mal, electromagnetic, and optical phenomena (Figure 3d).
Overall scores therefore emphasize this shared mechanical
core, while the less represented physical domains contribute
comparatively little. Appendix A.4 gives the topic mapping.

4. Systematic Evaluation and Failure Analysis
We evaluate models before task-specific training to ex-
amine how information demands and solution failures shape
their performance. Section 6 tests whether trajectory learning
improves performance on videos excluded from training.
4.1. Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate GPT-5.5 [26], Gemini-3.6-Flash [27],
Qwen3.6-27B [28], Qwen3.5-9B [29], Qwen3.5-4B [30], and
InteractiveOmni-4B [31]. All evaluated models receive the
same instructions and solve each question in a native tool
loop with web search and Python execution for at most six
turns. Each response contains a solution followed by a final
answer. The two outputs are assessed separately using the
same criteria across models. The full-benchmark comparison
covers all 410 questions before task-specific training; the later
improvement experiments reuse this protocol on the fixed test
partition. Appendix B provides generation settings, prompts,
and parsing rules.

correctness score (ACC) averages question-level scores in
[0, 1], allowing partial credit. Point estimation is scored by
relative error; interval estimation considers overlap and center
calibration, and also accepts point predictions inside the

4.5. From Diagnosis to Training Requirements
These findings motivate learning the dependencies between
information acquisition and physical reasoning. We use super-
vised fine-tuning (SFT) on complete tool-integrated trajectories
to supervise how observations, specifications, and calcula-
tions enter a solution. Preference optimization then compares
complete accepted and rejected candidates, complementing
imitation of verified trajectories with an outcome-based signal.
Section 5 describes this training procedure, and Section 6.3
evaluates its successive stages.
5. TriPhys
TriPhys is a tool-integrated framework for improving
physical problem solving in video-capable language models.
Given a video and question, the agent identifies the target and
required observations, obtains missing physical knowledge
when needed, and combines the available quantities with an
applicable relation. Numerical tools support the calculation,
and the agent uses their returns to form an answer with the
requested units and format. We train this behavior through
supervised fine-tuning on full trajectories followed by prefer-
ence optimization. The first stage supervises the full problem-
solving sequence of self-sampled solutions that pass answer
verification; the second learns from the supervised model’s
successful and unsuccessful candidate solutions. Figure 7
shows the training procedure.

physical scenarios.

retrieval and give an estimate; only correct continuations whose
reasoning does not mention the hint are retained, and the hint
is removed from the stored trajectory. Appendix D details the
thresholds and the distinction between solver-generated content
and tool observations.

5.4. Inference
At inference time, one learned policy receives the video
and question, generates solution steps, requests retrieval or
computation when needed, and incorporates the returned
observations into subsequent steps. Tool executors provide
information or numerical outputs; there is no separate planning
model. The policy terminates with a final answer in the
requested form or at the generation budget. This preserves
the same distinction between generated decisions and returned
evidence used during training.
6. Evaluation of TriPhys Models
We evaluate TriPhys-4B, TriPhys-9B, and TriPhys-27B,
the models obtained by applying the TriPhys framework
to three Qwen backbones. We examine their performance
on videos excluded from training and the contributions of
successive training stages, using the scoring protocol in Section
4.1.
6.1. Experimental Setup
Data partition. We split each subtask independently at
the video level, keeping all questions from each recording
together. The partition contains 300 training questions and 110
test questions (Table 4). We pool the two training partitions
and sample and construct trajectories only on these questions.
Appendix C gives the stratification procedure and detailed
distributions.
Models and baselines. TriPhys-4B, TriPhys-9B, and
TriPhys-27B are initialized from Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.5-9B,
and Qwen3.6-27B, respectively, and use the same two-stage
training procedure. Each is compared with its pretrained
counterpart and two closed-source reference models on the
same test questions. These questions were included in the full-
benchmark diagnosis (Section 4) but excluded from training.
Training configuration. Base model samples four trajec-
tories per training question, and verification, answer-block
rewriting, and the two supplementary sources yield Stage-I
trajectories. We use low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [87] with
rank 32, scaling parameter 64, and dropout 0.05 on the query,
key, value, and output attention projections and the gate, up,
and down projections, keeping the pretrained weights frozen.
SFT runs for three epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4
and an effective batch size of 16. After SFT, we merge the
learned adapter into the base model and train a new adapter
for DPO, while keeping the SFT reference fixed. DPO runs for
two epochs with a learning rate of 5 × 10−6, 𝛽= 0.1, and an
effective batch size of 16 pairs.
Preference construction. We sample candidates from the
SFT checkpoint of TriPhys at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.9,
continuing each of at most two prefixes per training input
into four complete candidates. The thresholds are 𝛿+ = 0.9,
𝛿−= 0.5, and 𝑠min = 0.75, and 𝛿+ and 𝑠min also apply to
Stage-I verification and rewriting. For point estimates, ACC of
at least 0.9 corresponds to a relative error of about 3.3% or less.
We then construct at most two preference pairs per input as
described in Section 5.3. Appendix D provides the remaining
settings.
6.2. Results Across Model Sizes
All three TriPhys models improve both answer and solution
correctness over their pretrained backbones on the fixed test
partition (Table 5). Relative answer-score gains range from
16.4% to 17.7%. The improvement across model sizes and both
evaluation targets supports complete tool-trajectory learning
as a practical way to strengthen physical problem solving on
videos excluded from training.
Both subtasks benefit in all three models. The absolute
answer-score gains are similar across subtasks for TriPhys-
4B and TriPhys-9B, whereas TriPhys-27B gains more on the
knowledge-grounded subtask. For TriPhys-4B and TriPhys-9B,
the larger relative gains on the measurement-grounded subtask
also reflect its lower starting scores. The overall improvement
thus extends across both information settings, while the subtask
breakdown distinguishes the size of the gain from the difficulty
of the starting point.
Task-specific adaptation also changes the comparison with
closed-source reference models. All three TriPhys models
exceed GPT-5.5’s mean answer score of 51.14% on the same
test questions, and TriPhys-27B attains 58.18%. TriPhys-27B
also leads in solution correctness and is on par with Gemini-3.6-
Flash in knowledge-grounded answer correctness, but remains
below Gemini-3.6-Flash in overall answer correctness and the
overall score. These results establish the benefit of adaptation
within this evaluation, while the remaining answer-score deficit
shows that the tasks are still challenging.
After training, performance follows model size in all three
overall metrics. TriPhys-4B and TriPhys-9B differ by only
0.003 in answer correctness, as their backbones already differ
by only 0.005. These configurations span different releases,
so the evidence is strongest for the matched before/after
comparisons.

6.3. Training-Stage Ablation
Setup. We examine the training stages of TriPhys-4B,
TriPhys-9B, and TriPhys-27B through three checkpoints each:
the pretrained backbone (Base), the checkpoint after SFT
on verified trajectories, and the full model after subsequent
DPO. All nine checkpoints are evaluated on the same 110 test
questions with the same scoring protocol. This design compares
the full recipe with its intermediate checkpoints (Table 6).
Results. Neither training stage lowers overall answer
correctness in any configuration. For TriPhys-4B and TriPhys-
9B, SFT produces the larger initial improvement, and sub-
sequent DPO adds 0.033 and 0.012 in answer correctness.
For TriPhys-27B, SFT leaves answer correctness unchanged
while raising solution correctness, and DPO raises answer
correctness by 0.087, with gains in both subtasks. The gains
support a recipe that first learns complete verified solutions
and then refines preferences among generated alternatives.
They quantify the benefit of adding preference learning to
the supervised checkpoint; independent effects of answer-
block rewriting and loss masking remain untested. Appendix E
provides the detailed increments and their calculation.

7. Discussion and Limitations
TriPhysBench makes the acquisition and use of physical
information a shared demand of agent evaluation. Low baseline
answer scores and improvements after TriPhys training show
both the difficulty of these tasks and the value of learning from

complete physical solutions. Answer and solution scores pro-
vide complementary views, while component-level diagnosis
identifies weaknesses that an overall ranking would conceal.
Our evaluation covers six VLMs, and TriPhys training
is tested on three Qwen configurations. Broader comparisons
across model families, capacities, and tool-use strategies would
establish how the observed capability gaps and training gains
vary with model design. Repeated runs would help quantify
the stability of these gains.
The current scores assess how the three physical capa-
bilities work together. Matched questions could probe their
dependencies more directly by varying the observations or
specifications supplied for the same physical problem. Anal-
ysis of tool trajectories, supported by human validation of
intermediate quantities and of model-based ratings, which can
carry evaluator biases [88], would help distinguish failures
in information acquisition from errors in applying available
evidence. Comparing these diagnoses before and after training
could reveal which capabilities improve and where errors
continue to propagate.
8. Conclusion
We introduced TriPhysBench to evaluate physical intel-
ligence for embodied agents through the coordination of
physical perception, physical knowledge retrieval, and physical
reasoning. Its 410 questions from 200 videos span two com-
plementary subtasks with different observation and knowledge
requirements. Evaluation of six models reveals low answer
scores under these joint demands, and detailed analysis iden-
tifies weaknesses in the use of quantities and the formation
of supported conclusions. TriPhys integrates information
acquisition and computation into physical problem solving
and improves all three evaluated Qwen configurations on 110
questions from held-out videos. TriPhys-27B reaches an answer
correctness score of 0.5818 and an overall score of 0.6219,
with a 17.7% relative improvement in answer correctness. The
cumulative stage comparisons of the three configurations show
overall-score gains after supervised training and further gains
after preference optimization. Together, these contributions
provide an evaluation foundation and a learning pathway for
embodied agents to progress from recognizing physical events
to understanding and reasoning about the processes that govern
them.

A. Benchmark Specification
A.1. Records and Evidence Roles
TriPhysBench studies physical intelligence through the coordination of physical perception, physical knowledge retrieval,
and physical reasoning. It comprises two evaluation subtasks: Measurement-grounded Physical Reasoning (MPR), centered on
supported video measurements and recovery of withheld observations, and Knowledge-grounded Physical Reasoning (KPR),
emphasizing the acquisition and application of external physical specifications. MPR uses 228 questions from 90 EgoExo4D
videos [S1]; KPR uses 182 questions from 110 videos in BAAI-DataCube’s Physics-aware-videos collection [S2]. Both subtasks
combine video observations and physical knowledge in quantitative problems, and both include all four answer forms.
Each question is linked to a source recording. EgoExo4D items retain a take identifier, while Physics-aware-videos items retain
folder and video identifiers. A recording can support several physical questions. Its questions stay together in the training/test
partition, even if they concern different event intervals.

A.2. Screening and Temporal Representation
MPR recordings undergo an initial screening by physical events. Candidates are EgoExo4D takes with three-dimensional
body-pose annotations that last less than 60 s. Keyword lists for 15 physical event types, including projectile motion, collision,
rotation, oscillation, friction, deformation, pouring, and free fall, are matched against the atomic action descriptions of each take.
Takes with more detected event types are prioritized.
KPR recordings undergo an initial screening against the event categories of the physical-event library. The caption of every
Physics-aware-videos recording is matched against keyword lists for these categories, and scene types without a measurable
subject, such as ocean waves, cooking, and team sports, are excluded. The screened recordings are then inspected manually
for technical quality, visible motion, and an identifiable subject that supports a quantitative question. The benchmark retains
questions from 90 MPR takes and 110 KPR recordings, which range from 360p to 2160p and from 5.0 s to 60.0 s.
Gemini-3.6-Flash [S3] produces clip-level descriptions and visible-element lists. The temporal parsing procedure uses
intervals of at least four seconds. The output contains a global summary and entities or events visible within the interval. Local
times are associated with the original video so that a source entry can identify the temporal support for a quantity.

annotated vertical direction. Frames with an angle below 20 degrees are eligible for the upright reference; a clip without eligible
support does not provide a body-height measurement through this route.

the International System of Units (SI) using Table S4. The answer-form rule applies after normalization. The compared quantities
must also have the same physical dimension.

C. Video-Level Partition
C.1. Assignment Procedure
Partitioning is performed separately for MPR and KPR at the level of source videos, so that all questions from one recording
fall on the same side. Each video is characterized by its modal question type, difficulty, and parent physics category, and these
attributes define the strata. Sparse strata are merged with related strata, and videos are allocated toward a 70:30 training/test ratio
while keeping the question-level distributions of type, difficulty, and category close to those of the full pool.
This procedure preserves source-video groups, while stratification approximates the original distribution. Modal attributes
cannot preserve the complete mixture of question labels within every video. Sparse labels and integer video counts also prevent
exact 70:30 allocation. The realized split contains 66/24 training/test videos for MPR and 81/29 for KPR. MPR contributes 167
training and 61 test questions, and KPR contributes 133 and 49. Overall, the training/test ratios are 73.5:26.5 for videos and
approximately 73.2:26.8 for questions. The training questions from both subtasks are pooled for adaptation.
The stored partition has no overlapping video keys, and its training and test questions jointly match each complete cleaned
source collection. These checks establish separation at the source-identifier level, without establishing absence from model
pretraining or independence of related scenes under distinct identifiers.

an answer. It uses supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on full tool-integrated trajectories, followed by full-trajectory Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) [S17]. Both stages use the training partition defined in Appendix C. The resulting TriPhys-4B, TriPhys-9B,
and TriPhys-27B models are initialized from Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.5-9B, and Qwen3.6-27B, respectively.

D.1. Trajectory Sampling and Verification
The starting set comprises 300 training questions. The base model solves each question with tools, without access to the

SCS are kept.
Gemini-3.6-Flash rewrites only the final answer block of each retained trajectory into a stepwise chain, using text alone.
DeepSeek-V4-Flash scores the rewrite with the evaluation SCS rubric, and a rewrite is accepted when its SCS is at least
max(0.75, SCS0), with at most three attempts. Gemini-3.6-Flash also solves questions that the backbone never answers correctly,
without access to the reference answer. Stalled samples give no answer, call tools in at least two turns, and have no retrieval
failure; an intermediate SFT model continues their first two turns for at most three further turns, and continuations with ACC of
at least 0.9 are retained. The prefix and continuation therefore come from different models.

reasoning text, while the retrieved value and final answer remain unchanged. They broaden the expression of existing problems

D.3. Loss Mask and Adaptation Settings
SFT predicts assistant-generated reasoning, tool-call, and rewritten answer tokens throughout each complete trajectory, and
reasoning tokens are weighted equally with other assistant tokens. The system prompt, user input, padding, and external tool
responses are excluded from the prediction loss. Tool observations remain in the context of later generated segments. Thus, loss
masking does not remove the information returned by a tool from attention.
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) supplies the trainable weights [S18], with its alpha-to-rank ratio scaling the low-rank update.
The adapters contain 42.5M, 58.2M, and 159.4M trainable parameters for the 4B, 9B, and 27B backbones, or 0.93%, 0.61%,
and 0.58% of their parameters. Table S10 gives the settings used for training. SFT uses a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 for three
epochs, with 14 steps per epoch and 42 steps in total; the schedule uses a four-step linear warm-up followed by cosine decay.
Both stages use a maximum sequence length of 16,384 tokens, and videos are sampled at 1 fps and uniformly downsampled
when they exceed this budget. Seed 42 governs both data assignment and training, and each configuration is trained once.
D.4. Preference Selection and Rejected-Trajectory Labels
Each training input receives at most four sampling attempts. At most two prefixes that retrieve the reference value are kept,
and each is continued into four complete candidates, counting the original turn as one. The binary filter accepts an answer with
an ACC of at least 0.9, compared with the 2% full-credit region and continuous decay in evaluation ACC, and rejects a candidate
that gives no answer or has an ACC below 0.5. Candidates with 0.5 ≤ACC < 0.9 are excluded, so nearly correct solutions do
not serve as rejected examples, and candidates with retrieval failures are removed.
A preference pair is retained when the rejected set is nonempty and a chosen solution exists, selecting the accepted candidate
with the highest SCS or, when none exists, the Stage-I trajectory of the question with the highest SCS. The fallback lets these
questions contribute comparisons between correct and incorrect solutions. Acceptance also requires the reference value in the
