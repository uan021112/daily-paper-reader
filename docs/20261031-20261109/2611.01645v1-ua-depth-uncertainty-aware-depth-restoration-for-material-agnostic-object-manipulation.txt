I. I NTRODUCTION 

# ACCURATE 3D geometry perception is essential for robotic manipulation and supports tasks such as 6D pose estimation [1], [2], collision-free motion planning [3], and grasp synthesis [4], [5], [6]. These tasks rely on metric depth to reason about object shape, free space, and contact points. As a result, commodity RGB-D cameras are widely used on robotic platforms to provide per-pixel depth measurements [6], [7], [8]. In real-world environments, commodity depth cameras often struggle with non-Lambertian materials such as trans-parent glass and reflective metal [9], [10], [11]. On these surfaces, they can produce missing regions, structured noise, and biased measurements due to refraction, reflection, or subsurface scattering [11], [12]. Such corruptions are hard to address with simple filtering. Consequently, robotic perception and manipulation pipelines that rely on reasonably accurate depth—and are typically developed and validated on mostly diffuse objects—can degrade substantially once transparent or reflective items appear in the scene [11], [13]. Researchers have proposed many depth completion and restoration methods to mitigate these sensor failures [11], [14], [15], yet explicit, task-usable uncertainty remains underex-plored. Most approaches output a single restored depth map without explicit uncertainty estimates. For non-Lambertian objects, errors are spatially localized and view-dependent, producing holes, bias, and boundary distortions within the same observation [11], [12]. Downstream modules therefore need depth values and a per-pixel signal of where they are trustworthy. Without uncertainty, downstream components treat all pixels/points as equally reliable, so localized depth artifacts can disproportionately affect decisions and lead to failures in tasks such as path planning [16], collision checking [17], and grasp synthesis [18]. Beyond reliability, cross-dataset generalization remains a bottleneck for depth restoration [11], [14]. Many methods are designed for sparse but reliable measurements (e.g., LiDAR-style completion) [19], [20] or near-clean RGB-D inputs [21], [22]. In contrast, commodity RGB-D often yields depth that is dense in format but incomplete and biased in practice, with material-dependent holes and spatially structured distortions on non-Lambertian surfaces, so performance can degrade under domain shifts. Other approaches rely on specialized sensing (e.g., raw IR stereo) [14], [23] or camera-specific models [15]; while effective in their target setups, they introduce extra assumptions and can be sensitive to changes in camera configuration or capture conditions. To tackle these challenges, we introduce UA-Depth , an 

Uncertainty-Aware Depth restoration framework for material-agnostic object perception in robotic manipulation. Our key insight is that uncertainty should not only be predicted as an auxiliary uncertainty output, but should also be used as recurrent feedback to actively guide depth restoration. Guided by this insight, UA-Depth performs Depth-Uncertainty Co-Refinement that jointly updates depth and uncertainty. At each refinement step, the Uncertainty-aware Context Feature Calibration (UCFC) module feeds the current uncertainty back to recalibrate RGB and sensor-depth context before the next depth update, allowing unreliable context to be down-weighted during refinement rather than merely reported afterward. To strengthen zero-shot generalization, we use a DAV2 [24] monocular prior to initialize depth in missing regions, leverag-ing its relative depth with strong multi-scale structure. Since manipulation requires metric depth, we must align this relative prior to the sensor depth; however, on commodity RGB-D, non-Lambertian artifacts can bias scale estimation if alignment is computed from raw measurements. Inspired by [20], we therefore propose a robust two-stage scale normalization in log-depth space with MAD-based weighting to reduce the influence of corrupted regions, enabling stable mixed-dataset training and inference. These components play complementary roles: DAV2 with ADF provides a metrically grounded dense initialization, Robust-SN stabilizes log-depth relative-to-metric normalization under noisy measurements, and UCFC is the core feedback mechanism that changes the recurrent refinement dynamics. To evaluate zero-shot generalization, we further propose an eight-dataset benchmark spanning diverse scene types (e.g., tabletop [25], indoor [26], [27], [28], [29], and industrial [30], [31]), object materials (e.g., diffuse, transparent, reflective, and texture-less), and RGB-D sensors, providing a unified testbed for cross-dataset evaluation. The main contributions of this work are as follows:  

> •

We propose UA-Depth, an uncertainty-aware framework for metric depth restoration under non-Lambertian failures in commodity RGB-D sensing.  

> •

We introduce recurrent uncertainty-feedback refinement, where uncertainty is updated jointly with depth and fed back at every ConvGRU step through UCFC to recalibrate RGB and sensor-depth context before the next depth update.  

> •

We support zero-shot depth restoration with a frozen DAV2 prior, ADF, and Robust-SN, which provide dense metric initialization and robust log-depth alignment/normalization under noisy measurements.  

> •

We establish an eight-dataset benchmark for zero-shot evaluation across diverse scenes, objects, and sensors. Experiments on three in-domain datasets show consistent overall gains in metric-depth accuracy over strong baselines, while producing informative uncertainty estimates. On the proposed zero-shot benchmark, UA-Depth achieves the best overall performance, with particularly pronounced gains on datasets rich in non-Lambertian objects (e.g., HAMMER [26], HouseCat6D [27], and TransCG [25]). Beyond depth met-rics, we demonstrate that the predicted uncertainty is useful in the evaluated manipulation-oriented perception pipelines: uncertainty-guided sampling improves category-level 6D pose estimation, while uncertainty-guided reweighting improves mean AP in 6D grasp synthesis. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Material-Agnostic Depth Restoration 

Material-agnostic depth restoration aims to recover accurate metric depth without relying on material-specific priors, or target-material fine-tuning, remaining robust across objects with diverse optical effects such as transparency and specularity. SwinDR [11] first formalizes this setting and introduces DREDS, a large-scale RGB-D dataset with diverse transparent and reflective objects. TransCG [25] further contributes a large-scale dataset for transparent object manipulation and proposes a transformer-based depth completion baseline that leverages cross-modal attention between RGB and sensor depth. D3RoMa [14] adopts a diffusion-based framework conditioned on sensor depth or infrared (IR) stereo images to estimate metric depth, while ASGrasp [23] directly regresses dense geometry from raw IR stereo for grasp synthesis. CDM [15] instead models camera-specific noise and synthesizes realistic depth, but requires large-scale data preparation for each sensor type. DCIRNet [33] combines RGB-D feature fusion with spatial-propagation-based depth refinement for transparent and reflective objects, and evaluates its application to dexterous grasping. Overall, existing approaches improve depth perception under non-Lambertian materials, but many either assume access to low-level or multi-view cues such as raw IR stereo signals [14], [23], or require per-sensor data preparation and device-specific pretraining to match target cameras [15]. Meanwhile, systematic zero-shot evaluation of a single model across diverse datasets, sensor types, and material conditions remains limited [11], [14], [33]. 

B. Depth Completion 

Depth completion aims to infer a dense metric depth map from RGB and sparse depth inputs. Classical learning-based approaches such as SPN [34] refine initial depth with learnable affinity propagation, while NLSPN [35] employs non-local spatial propagation to exploit long-range context. More recent methods focus on generalization: Depth Prompting (DP) [36] uses a monocular depth foundation model with prompting to adapt across sensor types, OGNI-DC [19] formulates completion as differentiable optimization with neural iterations, and OMNI-DC [20] enhances cross-dataset robustness through scale-equivariant normalization in log-depth space and mixed-dataset training. Many depth completion methods are designed around sparse, relatively clean measurements and primarily model simple, Gaussian noise [20]. As a result, they may be less effective when the input exhibits more complex degradations that often appear in commodity RGB-D sensing, such as large missing regions and material-dependent biases on non-Lambertian surfaces, which motivates depth restoration tailored to robotic manipulation. 

C. Uncertainty-Aware Depth Estimation 

Modeling uncertainty in depth estimation is important for safe robotic decision-making. Kendall and Gal [37] introduce a Bayesian formulation that models depth as a heteroscedastic Gaussian and learns per-pixel aleatoric uncertainty via the loss. Poggi et al. [38] study uncertainty estimation for self-supervised monocular depth prediction and benchmark several practical uncertainty strategies. Hornauer et al. [39] demonstrate that gradient-based supervision can lead to sharper uncertainty estimates around depth discontinuities. STEPS [40] further uses uncertain pixel masking to improve self-supervised depth learning under degraded nighttime observations, while Ctrl-U [41] shows a broader use of uncertainty-aware weighting for robust conditional generation. Uncertainty and confidence have also been explored in depth completion. DDP [42] estimates dense depth posterior distributions from RGB and sparse range measurements. pNCNN [43] explicitly distinguishes propagated confidence from probabilistic uncertainty: it notes that the propagated output confidence in normalized convolution has no probabilistic interpretation and is not significantly correlated with prediction error, and then introduces a probabilistic formulation to estimate output uncertainty for completed 4

depth. SparseDC [44] supervises pseudo uncertainty and uses it to guide feed-forward feature fusion between RGB and sparse depth features. A related line of work uses confidence maps as internal reliability weights: FusionNet [45] learns confidence maps to fuse depth predictions from different branches, DeepLiDAR [46] predicts confidence masks to handle mixed LiDAR signals near foreground boundaries, and NLSPN [35] uses predicted confidence to suppress unreliable pixels during non-local spatial propagation. MUIR [47] jointly predicts matchability and disparity uncertainty during recurrent stereo refinement, using matchability to filter matching features and hidden states; TCG-Depth [48] uses predicted confidence to modulate features in a two-stage transparent-object depth completion framework. UA-Depth builds on the heteroscedastic formulation of Kendall and Gal [37] to jointly restore metric depth and estimate per-pixel uncertainty for material-agnostic RGB-D object depth restoration. Different from prior methods that estimate posterior uncertainty, propagate confidence, or use uncertainty/confidence for feed-forward fusion or spatial propagation, UCFC treats uncertainty as an evolving recurrent feedback signal: at each refinement step, depth and uncertainty are jointly updated, and the updated uncertainty calibrates both RGB and sensor-depth context before the next residual depth update. 

D. Generalizable Depth Estimation 

Generalizable depth estimation aims to develop models that remain robust under unseen domains [49]. Depth Anything V1 [50] and V2 [24] (DAV1/2) are monocular foundation models trained on large-scale, diverse datasets and provide strong zero-shot relative-depth priors. PromptDA [21] and PriorDA [22] further improve adaptability by conditioning such foundation models on learnable prompts or geometric priors. OMNI-DC [20] achieves strong cross-dataset performance for depth completion by operating in a normalized log-depth space. MOMA [51] further suggests that relative-depth priors from DAV1/2 can be leveraged for downstream robotic grasping after metric alignment. Building on DAV2 [24] as a frozen relative-depth prior, we focus on restoring metric depth from noisy RGB-D rather than monocular prediction alone. Inspired by OMNI-DC [20], we operate in a normalized log-depth space and further introduce a robust scale normalization tailored to noisy sensor depth, enabling stable mixed-dataset training across heterogeneous sensors and materials without domain-specific fine-tuning. A. Overview 

Given an RGB image I ∈ RH×W ×3 and sensor depth 

Dsen ∈ RH×W , our goal is to recover a metric depth map 

D ∈ RH×W together with a pixel-wise uncertainty map 

U ∈ RH×W . We formulate depth restoration as an iterative optimization process, where D and U are progressively refined using the RGB image and sensor depth. An overview is shown in Fig. 2. Commodity RGB-D sensors often produce missing or biased depth on non-Lambertian surfaces, making refinement directly from Dsen unstable. To provide robust guidance, we use a frozen DAV2 model to extract a relative depth Drel and RGB features Fimg , and transfer Fimg into our refinement domain (Sec. III-B). Since Drel provides dense geometric structure but lacks metric scale, we introduce Aligned Depth Filling (ADF) to obtain a metrically grounded starting point (Sec. III-C). ADF aligns Drel to sensor-valid pixels of Dsen , fills missing regions with the aligned prior, and outputs (D0, U0) for refinement. ADF is used in both in-domain and mixed-dataset settings. For mixed-dataset pretraining, heterogeneous depth ranges challenge metric-space refinement, while biased sensor measurements undermine simple scale estimates. We therefore enable Robust Scale Normalization (Robust-SN; Sec. III-F), which replaces the metric scale-and-shift alignment with robust log-bias estimation and applies the same ADF filling rule in normalized log-depth space, where subsequent refinement also operates. Starting from (D0, U0), our refinement stage aims to correct corrupted depth while learning uncertainty that stabilizes the updates under structured sensor artifacts. We iteratively co-refine (D, U) with a ConvGRU using RGB–D context features (Sec. III-D). At each iteration k, depth and uncertainty implicitly 

couple through the recurrent hidden state, and the recurrent uncertainty state Uk explicitly calibrates the context features via Uncertainty-aware Context Feature Calibration (UCFC) to suppress unreliable cues. After K iterations, (DK , UK ) are upsampled to full resolution using convex sampling [32]. We further specify the uncertainty learning objective and its error term tailored to depth restoration (Sec. III-E).

We model input-dependent aleatoric uncertainty associated with missing, noisy, or material-corrupted RGB-D observations and ambiguous depth discontinuities. Error Proxy for Uncertainty Supervision. Eq. (16) requires specifying the error proxy E(D, ˆD) used to learn uncertainty. A standard choice is the L2 error [37], but in our scenes it is often dominated by large residuals around strong depth jumps (e.g., object–background transitions and severe outliers), which can cause the learned uncertainty to be dominated by a small set of large outliers, reducing its sensitivity to fine object geometry and mild discontinuities. alone provides weak structural constraints and may blur object boundaries. To address this limitation, we define E as a combination of a pixel-wise term and a gradient-matching term:

We further assess whether the predicted uncertainty provides a meaningful ranking of object pixels using risk–coverage curves (Fig. 5). A better uncertainty estimate discards unreliable pixels first, yielding a lower-risk curve at the same coverage. As shown, an L2 proxy ranks pixels poorly, while our L1+Grad proxy yields the most informative ranking and the best overall risk–coverage behavior.

F. Robust Scale Normalization for Mixed-Dataset Training

A. Implementation Details 

We implement our framework in PyTorch and train it on NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs. The DAV2 backbone is initialized from the official pretrained ViT-L checkpoint and kept frozen throughout training to preserve its generalization ability. As DAV2 predicts a disparity-like relative depth map q, with larger values corresponding to nearer surfaces, we obtain the relative-depth prior as Drel (x, y ) = 1 /q(x, y ) for q(x, y ) > 0, and zero otherwise. We perform K=5 recurrent ConvGRU updates per sample with γ = 0 .9, operating at 1 

> r

of the input resolution, where the refine-downsample rate r is set to 2 for 320 × 180 

inputs and 4 for 518 × 518 inputs. The valid depth range is restricted to [0 .2, 5.0] meters. For ADF, the sensor-valid set is defined by Dsen > d min , with dmin = 0 .2 m. To estimate the provisional log-bias a0 in Robust-SN, we retain sensor-valid pixels with fully valid 3 × 3 neighborhoods and sensor log-depth gradients below the 80th percentile. Their log-depth differences are clipped to the 20th–80th percentile interval before taking the median; if fewer than 16 candidate pixels remain, we set a0 = 0 . In the second stage, the final log-bias 

a and anchor scale sanc are estimated using weighted medians over all sensor-valid pixels, without applying the first-stage filtering. We set κ = 1 .5 × 1.4826 = 2 .2239 and ε = 10 −6,applying the numerical floor to MAD before computing τ . If the total weight is at most ε, weighted medians fall back to ordinary medians. When no valid sensor pixels are available, we set 

a = 0 and use the clamped median of the monocular depth prior as sanc . Logarithmic inputs and relative-error denominators are clamped from below by ε for numerical stability. For uncertainty supervision (Eqs. (16) and (17)), we set 

λ1 = 0 .5, λ2 = 0 .2, and α = 1 . The error proxy is computed from the detached depth prediction, while the depth branch is supervised by Ldepth . Optimization uses the AdamW optimizer (β1=0 .9, β 2=0 .999 ) with a base learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, a minimum learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, and weight decay of 0.01 .A polynomial decay schedule is applied to the learning rate. We train for 30 epochs with batch size 4 using 518 × 518 RGB–D inputs, which takes about 4 days on 6 RTX 3090 GPUs. Data augmentation is applied only to DREDS following [11], while no augmentation is used for ClearPose and HISS. 

B. Datasets and Metrics 

Datasets. We evaluate UA-Depth in two settings: in-domain and zero-shot generalization. In-domain: we train a separate model on the official training split of each dataset (HAMMER [26], DREDS [11], and ClearPose [56]) and report results on the corresponding test split. Zero-shot: following the mixed-dataset pretraining protocol of [14], we pretrain on DREDS-CatKnown [11], ClearPose [56], and HISS [14], and evaluate

In this paper, zero-shot means that UA-Depth is not trained or adapted on the target benchmarks. This does not rule out possible upstream exposure of the frozen DAV2 prior to semantically related public images. As shown in Table I, we select these target benchmarks to cover complementary test settings, including different object categories, object materials, and sensors. We additionally build GraspNet-Trans for manipulation-centric transfer: it shares the tabletop grasping context of GraspNet-1B [57] but introduces transparent objects and challenging material effects, allowing us to probe robust-ness under non-Lambertian corruptions in a grasping-oriented setting. Appendix A-C provides additional object-overlap and sensor-depth analyses of the testing datasets. For YCB-V [28] and T-LESS [29], which do not provide per-pixel GT metric depth, we evaluate on object pixels using metric depth rendered from the provided 6D poses and CAD models. D. Zero-shot Generalization 

Table VI reports a quantitative comparison on our zero-shot benchmark. Because object-region geometry is most relevant to object-centric manipulation, we report REL and 

δ1.05 computed over object pixels. To make the comparison between DAV2-based methods fair, we use 518 ×518 (the default input resolution of DAV2) as their RGB–D input size, and use 320 ×180 for evaluation. Results on all valid pixels and different resolutions are provided in the Appendix B-B. 

Baselines. We compare against six generalized methods. SwinDR [11], D 3RoMa [14], and CDM [15] are depth restora-tion approaches tailored to robotic RGB-D sensing, with CDM additionally learning camera-specific noise models to synthesize large-scale training data. PromptDA [21] and PriorDA [22] perform prompt-based depth completion/super-resolution (e.g., using low-resolution LiDAR or generic geometric prompts), while OMNI-DC [20] addresses sparse-LiDAR depth comple-tion. Although PromptDA, PriorDA, and OMNI-DC are not explicitly designed for manipulation benchmarks, they exhibit strong zero-shot ability on completion/super-resolution tasks and, together with CDM, are all built on DAV2 [24], making them suitable baselines for our study. More implementation details are provided in the Appendix B-B. 

Results. As shown in Table VI, our method generalizes best across the eight zero-shot benchmarks, achieving a substantially lower mean rank than all other methods (1.4 vs. ≥3.1). It ranks first on HAMMER, HouseCat6D, TransCG, GraspNet-Trans, and T-LESS, improving both REL and δ1.05 (e.g., reducing REL by 21.1% on HouseCat6D and 28.8% on TransCG compared with CDM). On industrial-style benchmarks, it attains the best performance on T-LESS and reaches near-saturated accuracy on XYZ-IBD and ROBI, with δ1.05 of 98.76 and 99.05, respectively. Among baselines, SwinDR [11] is consistently strong but still trails ours on harder transparent and cluttered benchmarks such as HouseCat6D and TransCG. OMNI-DC [20] is the strongest baseline by mean rank and performs best on the industrial datasets (e.g., XYZ-IBD and ROBI), likely benefiting from its scale normalization; however, it degrades on noisier benchmarks. By contrast, our robust scale normalization is designed to reduce outlier bias under commodity RGB-D noise, improving stability under domain shifts. A more detailed analysis of OMNI-DC is provided in the Appendix B-B. CDM [15] leverages camera-specific noise modeling and can be competitive when a matching sensor model is available (e.g., D435), but it transfers less reliably across sensors. D3RoMa [14] (RGB-D) improves over raw depth on several benchmarks, yet it is less consistent than our method on harder cross-domain settings. PromptDA [21] and PriorDA [22] target completion/super-resolution from cleaner or structured inputs and are less robust to the noisy, incomplete depth typical of commodity RGB-D. More detailed comparisons are provided in the Appendix B-B. Fig. 7 shows qualitative zero-shot restoration on in-the-wild scenes with reflective and mixed-material objects. Raw depth exhibits large missing regions and structured noise on challenging surfaces. D 3RoMa and CDM partially suppress these artifacts, but can still leave oversmoothed surfaces or residual speckle and holes in the highlighted regions. Our method produces more coherent surfaces with fewer artifacts while retaining finer structures and sharper boundaries.

Ablation of UCFC Feedback. We further examine whether the performance gain comes from recurrent uncertainty feedback, rather than from extra parameters or generic feature fusion. To assess impact beyond depth metrics, we evaluate our restored depth and predicted uncertainty in three downstream tasks that are tightly coupled to robotic manipulation. We start with category-level 6D pose estimation (Sec. B. 6D Grasp Synthesis 

We further examine how our restored depth and predicted uncertainty can benefit downstream grasping. We consider 6D grasp synthesis and evaluate on GraspNet-Trans using AnyGrasp [6] as the grasp predictor. 

Metrics. GraspNet-Trans is built on the GraspNet-1Billion benchmark [57], sharing the same object set, CAD models, and grasp annotations. Following the official GraspNet protocol, the testing scenes are divided into three splits: Seen , Similar ,and Novel , depending on whether the object instances appear in the training set. Each predicted grasp is represented as 

g = [ R, t, ω, s ], where R ∈ SO(3) and t ∈ R3 denote the gripper pose, ω is the gripper width, and s is the grasp score. A grasp is counted as correct if it satisfies the official collision and force-closure checks. For each test scene and camera view, we sort predicted grasps by s and compute average precision (AP) on the top-50 predictions. We report these AP values separately for the Seen, Similar, and Novel splits, together with the mean AP over all three. 

Uncertainty-guided Reweighting. AnyGrasp [6] parameterizes each grasp hypothesis by a surface point on the object as its 

center , together with additional grasp parameters such as view rotation, in-plane rotation, offset, and gripper width. (33)) to reweight the scores of predicted grasps at inference time. Specifically, for a grasp g with original AnyGrasp score 

sg , we sample Cg = C(xg , y g ) at its center and compute the reweighted score s′ 

> g

= Cg sg . We apply this to all candidates in a scene and re-rank them by s′ 

> g

We do not use uncertainty-guided point sampling here, as it can discard informative edge points and reduce grasp accuracy; instead, we use uncertainty only at the scoring stage. First, our failure analysis of zero-shot depth restoration shows that uncertainty feedback can occasionally over-correct regions with reliable sensor observations or already accurate initial estimates, as detailed in Appendix B-E. Leveraging consistency across successive refinement steps may help mitigate such performance degradation. Second, restoring depth for scenes with stacked transparent objects remains a challenge, because multiple valid surfaces can correspond to a single RGB pixel along the same camera ray. This violates the single-depth-per-pixel assumption and can cause unstable predictions around transparent boundaries. Addressing this may require multi-layer depth representations or multi-view cues [66]. Third, inference latency is still a bottleneck. While our iterative co-refinement improves accuracy, the overall runtime 17 

is mainly limited by the frozen DAV2 backbone and context extraction (Table XII). To achieve real-time efficiency, we may need lightweight backbones, feature distillation, or reduced-resolution refinement strategies. Finally, although we exploit predicted uncertainty in down-stream tasks via point sampling or score reweighting, these strategies remain heuristic and can be brittle in some cases (e.g., off-center grasps on multi-material objects; Fig. 13). A more systematic and task-adaptive integration of uncertainty, for example through learning-based or policy-level coupling, is an important direction for future work. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduce UA-Depth, a depth restoration framework that uses predicted uncertainty as recurrent feedback to guide metric depth refinement under non-Lambertian failures in commodity RGB-D sensing. In this framework, DAV2 backbone with ADF provides a metrically grounded dense initialization, Robust-SN stabilizes log-depth relative-to-metric normalization under biased sensor measurements, and UCFC turns the evolving un-certainty estimate into an active refinement signal. Across three in-domain datasets, UA-Depth substantially improves metric depth accuracy over prior restoration/completion methods and produces uncertainty estimates that correlate with restoration errors. We also present an eight-dataset benchmark for cross-dataset transfer spanning diverse scenes, materials, sensors, and capture conditions, on which UA-Depth attains the best overall performance among strong baselines. Beyond depth metrics, the evaluated pose-estimation and grasp-synthesis pipelines show that the predicted uncertainty can support manipulation-oriented perception. Future work will explore consistency-aware refinement, multi-layer or multi-hypothesis depth representations for layered transparency, lightweight backbones or distillation for lower latency, and more principled integration of uncertainty into manipulation policies. B. In-domain Depth Estimation Datasets 

We evaluate in-domain fitting ability on three public RGB-D datasets, HAMMER [26], DREDS/STD [11], and Clear-Pose [56]. For this setting, models are trained and tested on the official splits of each dataset. 

HAMMER. HAMMER contains 13 indoor scenes (7,200 frames) with polarization-guided RGB-D and high-fidelity rendered depth [26]. We use it for both in-domain and zero-shot evaluations. 

DREDS and STD. DREDS is a domain-randomized synthetic dataset designed for non-Lambertian depth sensing, and it provides two evaluation subsets: DREDS-CatKnown (seen categories) and DREDS-CatNovel (novel categories with material perturbations) [11]. STD is the real-world counterpart following the same known and novel protocol (STD-CatKnown and STD-CatNovel). Unless otherwise stated, we train on DREDS-CatKnown and report results on all four subsets to assess both in-domain performance and category and material generalization. 

ClearPose. ClearPose provides 350k real images of transparent and translucent objects captured using a RealSense L515 across diverse scenes, backgrounds, occlusions, non-planar supports, covers, and liquids [56]. We follow the official split and evaluate on all ClearPose test settings. 

C. Zero-shot Generalization Benchmark and Dataset Prepara-tion 

To assess zero-shot generalization, we pretrain on a mixture of training splits and evaluate directly on the test sets of eight datasets: HAMMER [26], HouseCat6D [27], TransCG [25], GraspNet-Trans, XYZ-IBD [30], ROBI [31], YCB-V [28], and T-LESS [29] without any fine-tuning. For fair compari-son, the pretraining mix follows [14] and includes DREDS-CatKnown [11], ClearPose [56], and HISS [14]. Table I in the main paper summarizes the train and test composition and dataset statistics used in this benchmark. 

GraspNet-Trans. GraspNet-Trans builds on GraspNet-1Billion [57]: we reuse its CAD models and 6D object poses to re-render the official test split, producing RGB-D scenes that keep the original layout while randomizing object materials to transparent or specular. Sensor depth is synthesized by simulating an active depth sensor following [12]. Unlike DREDS-CatNovel [11], we align virtual intrinsics and extrinsics exactly to the real captures, so object–camera geometry matches one-to-one. This alignment lets GraspNet-1B grasp annotations transfer directly to GraspNet-Trans, enabling evaluation of the downstream 6D grasp synthesis task.

TransCG has the largest category coverage, 33 /46 objects (71.7%), and also rela-tively high category–material coverage, 29 /46 objects (63.0%); thus it mainly tests real transparent/specular-object sensing rather than category novelty alone. HAMMER, HouseCat6D, GraspNet-Trans, and YCB-V lie between these cases, with partial category coverage and lower strict category–material coverage. The three material regimes are globally covered by the source mixture, so the remaining material-related difficulty mainly comes from unseen category–material combinations and their interaction with new sensors. 

Sensor-Depth Transfer Audit. We also audit sensor-depth transfer on object pixels. Each target dataset is compared with the DREDS-CatKnown/ClearPose/HISS training mixture. The source reference and each target distribution are constructed using fixed weighting rules that are held constant across all comparisons. A sensor-valid object pixel has valid GT depth and Dsen > 0. For the valid-depth analysis, we use the log residual e = | log Dsen − log D∗| and compare source and target residual distributions within the same fixed GT-depth bins before averaging across nonempty bins. For hole morphology, we extract fixed-size local patches from the binary sensor-validity mask, vectorize the patches, and use the same patch extraction and random projection set for every dataset. The frame-level sensor signature concatenates missing-depth, boundary-missingness, valid-residual, and local-hole-morphology statistics; all descriptor preprocessing and RBF-kernel settings are fixed before target-domain comparison and shared across datasets. We use four distribution-shift metrics. First, object-invalid Wasserstein-1 distance (Invalid-ratio W1) compares the per-frame distribution of invalid object-depth ratios using the Wasserstein-1 distance [67]. It measures how much the overall amount of missing object depth shifts from source to target. Second, depth-conditioned residual Wasserstein-1 distance (Valid-error W1) compares the log-depth residual distribution on sensor-valid object pixels, conditioned on GT depth bins. It measures whether the valid sensor returns have a different error pattern, rather than simply more holes. Third, local-validity sliced Wasserstein distance 

(Hole SWD) compares local valid/invalid patch descriptors using sliced Wasserstein distance [68], [67]. It measures whether the spatial shape of missing-depth regions changes, e.g., isolated holes versus large connected missing regions. Fourth, sensor-signature maximum mean discrepancy (Sensor MMD) compares per-frame sensor-corruption descriptors with an RBF-kernel MMD [69]. It summarizes the overall sensor-depth corruption shift, including missing-depth ratio, boundary missingness, valid-depth residuals, and hole morphology. Robustness Analysis of Rendered Depth. To assess how sensitive this rendered-depth proxy is to pose inaccuracies, we conduct a robustness analysis on YCB-V and T-LESS by perturbing the provided 6D poses at three noise levels and re-rendering z-buffer depth. Note that we do not attempt to estimate the true pose-labeling error. This study instead serves as a sensitivity analysis that quantifies evaluation noise induced by pose perturbations and verifies that our conclusions are not driven by small rendering inaccuracies. As summarized in Table A-I, the induced discrepancy between the original and perturbed renders remains small under plausible noise (e.g., mean REL ≈1.1–1.4% at 1◦, 1mm and ≈1.9–2.2% at 

2◦, 2mm), suggesting that the pose-rendered object-region depth is reasonably stable for evaluation on these datasets. If we conservatively treat 2◦, 2mm as a representative pose-labeling error, then improvements below this REL level on YCB-V/T-LESS are within the rendering-induced error band and should be interpreted as comparable performance rather than definitive gains. Accordingly, we avoid over-interpreting small metric differences on these pose-rendered benchmarks and focus on consistent trends across datasets with sensor-provided dense GT depth. 

GraspNet-Trans. UA-Depth v.s. OMNI-DC. OMNI-DC is designed to leverage depth inputs that are either noise-free or subject to relatively low, unstructured noise. While it demonstrates robustness to mild Gaussian perturbations, it is more sensitive to the 25 

structured and material-dependent noise introduced by non-Lambertian surfaces, which commonly appears in commodity RGB-D sensing. To better understand this behavior, we conduct a pixel-regime analysis on eight datasets by separately reporting the REL error on sensor-valid pixels (pixels with depth measurements) and missing pixels (pixels where the sensor fails to return depth) within object regions. As shown in Fig. A-2, on datasets with pronounced non-Lambertian surface (HAMMER, HouseCat6D, TransCG, GN-Trans, and T-LESS), OMNI-DC exhibits limited correction on sensor-valid pixels, whereas UA-Depth consistently refines depth estimates in these regions. In contrast, on datasets with relatively low sensor noise (XYZ-IBD, ROBI, and YCB-V), UA-Depth may introduce additional refinement errors on sensor-valid pixels, while OMNI-DC preserves the original measurements more faithfully. A similar trend is observed on missing pixels. OMNI-DC’s completion quality is also affected by noisy observations, but it performs more favorably on datasets where sensor noise is limited. Taken together, these results indicate that OMNI-DC is better suited to regimes with reliable depth measurements, whereas UA-Depth is more effective when depth observations are dense but corrupted by material-dependent noise. 

C. Ablations of UCFC Feedback 

We provide additional ablations to examine whether the im-provement of UCFC comes from recurrent uncertainty feedback rather than from uncertainty supervision, additional parameters, residual-based reliability estimation, generic feature fusion, or single-step prediction. All variants use the same DAV2 backbone, ADF initialization, training data, input resolution, and evaluation protocol. For the recurrent variants, the recurrent refinement backbone is also kept fixed; for the zero-shot ablation reported in the main paper, Robust-SN is fixed across all variants. The variants differ only in whether refinement is single-step or recurrent, and in how uncertainty or reliability information is injected into the refinement process. Table A-XII summarizes the design differences. Single-step depth directly predicts a one-shot depth update without recurrent refinement or uncertainty prediction. Single-step depth+unc. additionally predicts uncertainty with the same uncertainty supervision, but still does not use recurrent refine-ment or uncertainty feedback. Plain refinement removes both uncertainty supervision and uncertainty feedback while keeping the recurrent refinement backbone. Uncertainty-only predicts uncertainty with the same supervision but does not feed it back into refinement. Static learned gate uses a learned gate that is independent of the predicted uncertainty. Validity-mask gate 

uses the raw valid-depth mask as the reliability signal. Residual gate replaces the predicted uncertainty with the normalized absolute residual between the current depth estimate Dk and the original sensor depth Dsen on valid sensor pixels, while invalid sensor pixels are set to the maximum residual value of 1 before gating. Simple conv fusion directly concatenates depth, uncertainty, RGB features, and sensor-depth features, and uses convolutional layers to predict the depth and uncertainty updates. UCFC-PM keeps the same calibration modules as UCFC but replaces the uncertainty map with a constant map, serving as a parameter-matched no-feedback control. UCFC 

uses the evolving predicted uncertainty as recurrent feedback to recalibrate RGB and sensor-depth context at each step. 

Results. Table A-XIII reports the in-domain HAMMER ablation on both object pixels and all-valid pixels. Uncertainty-only learning does not improve depth restoration over plain refinement, indicating that predicting uncertainty without feeding it back is insufficient. Static learned gating, validity-mask gating, residual gating, and simple convolutional fusion improve some metrics but remain weaker than UCFC. UCFC achieves the best REL and δ1.05 on both object pixels and all-valid pixels, and also obtains the lowest REL-AUSE among variants with uncertainty output. These results show that the predicted uncertainty is most useful when it is recurrently fed back to calibrate refinement context. The full zero-shot ablation in Table A-XV, which additionally includes the single-step depth and single-step depth+unc. baselines, shows a similar trend: single-step prediction is weaker than recurrent refinement, and UCFC achieves the best overall mean rank after uncertainty is dynamically fed back into the refinement process. 

D. Contribution Decomposition of ADF+Robust-SN and UCFC 

We further decompose the contributions of ADF+Robust-SN and UCFC in the zero-shot setting. All four variants are retrained and evaluated with the same mixed-dataset pretraining protocol, DAV2 prior, recurrent refinement backbone, uncer-tainty modeling objective, input resolution, and evaluation protocol. We only toggle two factors: whether ADF+Robust-SN is used for initialization and log-depth normalization, and whether the predicted uncertainty is fed back into refinement through UCFC. This decomposition separates the effect of robust metric initialization/normalization from the effect of recurrent uncertainty feedback. As shown in Table A-XIV, enabling UCFC improves the mean rank from 3.41 to 1.94 when ADF+Robust-SN is disabled, and from 2.78 to 1.88 when ADF+Robust-SN is enabled. Thus, UCFC does not simply inherit its gain from ADF or robust scale alignment; under the same initialization and normalization setting, feeding predicted uncertainty back into recurrent refine-ment consistently improves zero-shot performance. Conversely, ADF+Robust-SN addresses a complementary failure mode by providing a more stable metric initialization under biased sensor depth, but it does not by itself turn uncertainty into an active refinement signal.

Why not uncertainty-guided sampling for 6D grasp synthe-sis. Although uncertainty-guided sampling can benefit category-level 6D pose estimation by selecting high-confidence depth for point cloud construction, we do not adopt it as the default for 6D grasp synthesis. As summarized in Table A-XIX, Unc-Sampling consistently degrades grasp accuracy, reducing the mean AP from 23.14 to 22.26 across the three splits. To better diagnose this drop, we also report the Collision Rate (CR): CR@ K denotes the average fraction of the top-K ranked grasps that are flagged as colliding (with the object, table, or surrounding geometry) during collision checking, where lower is better. Under this metric, Unc-Sampling increases collisions in the practically relevant regime: the mean CR@10 rises from 55.98 to 57.36, and this trend holds for seen/similar/novel individually. A plausible reason is that high-uncertainty pixels concentrate near object boundaries, thin structures, and depth discontinuities; sampling them away tends to “shrink” the reconstructed shape and remove boundary support that is crucial for collision checking and contact reasoning, making top-ranked grasps more prone to penetrations or ignoring nearby obstacles. In contrast, Unc-Reweighting retains the full geometry while down-weighting unreliable points, improving mean AP to 24.02 and simultaneously reducing mean CR@10 to 53.93, indicating safer and more accurate grasp ranking.

Uncertainty-guided reweighting. We follow Sec. V-B in the main paper: for each grasp candidate, we project its center point to the image plane, sample the confidence map C(x, y ) = 1 − ˜U(x, y ), and multiply the original AnyGrasp score by this confidence before re-ranking.

An object is marked as uncleared and manually removed once it accumulates three failed grasp attempts, which need not be consecutive. In rare cases, a single successful grasp removes multiple objects simultaneously. To avoid inflating completion statistics, we count such an event as one successful execution and reduce the effective target count by the number of additional objects removed. Objects manually removed after failed attempts remain in the effective target count and are considered uncleared. 

Metrics and confidence intervals. We report Success Rate (SR) as the number of successful grasp executions divided by the total number of executed attempts. We report Completion Rate (CR) as the number of conservatively counted cleared objects divided by the effective target-object count. For scene 

i, let Ai, Si, and Mi denote the numbers of grasp attempts, successful executions, and uncleared target objects, respectively. The effective target count is Ni = Si + Mi. Accordingly, the per-scene metrics are SR i = Si/A i and CR i = Si/N i. For each method, SR and CR are first computed per scene and then averaged equally over the same 20 matched scenes. We estimate 95% confidence intervals using 10,000 scene-level percentile-bootstrap resamples with replacement (random seed 0). Each resample contains 20 scene indices. The lower and upper bounds are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap estimates. For paired comparisons, we first compute the per-scene differences between our method and each baseline, and then bootstrap these paired differences. The main manuscript reports the scene-level mean ± the confidence-interval half-width for compactness. Since percentile-bootstrap intervals need not be symmetric, the exact bounds are provided in Tables A-XX and A-XXI. For reproducibility, Table A-XXII reports the recorded per-scene attempt, success, and effective-target counts for all five methods, enabling independent reconstruction of SR, CR, and paired scene-level statistics. 31
