ABSTRACT
Coarse-to-fine 3D-native generation relies heavily on the first-stage sparse struc-
ture, which determines the global orientation and overall geometry for subsequent
refinement. However, existing coarse generators suffer from two key limitations:
their orientation is difficult to control due to ambiguous semantic canonicalization,
and their structures are often inconsistent with input images due to weak 2D-3D
grounding. We propose Coarse3D, a sparse-structure generation model that pro-
duces orientation-controllable and structurally faithful voxel geometry from arbi-
trary unposed images. Coarse3D introduces three designs: a first-frame anchored
canonical space that aligns generation with the first input view while preserving
object-centric normalization; a dual-tower DiT that couples generation with auxil-
iary feed-forward reconstruction to learn spatially grounded features; and spatial-
prior guided attention that regularizes cross-attention with pixel-to-voxel corre-
spondence. Coarse3D can directly replace the coarse stage of existing pipelines.
Experiments show that it substantially improves sparse structure quality and con-
sistently enhances final 3D generation, achieving state-of-the-art performance.

INTRODUCTION
The automatic creation of high-quality 3D assets from images is a long-standing goal in computer
graphics and vision, with applications in gaming, AR/VR, robotics, and digital manufacturing. Re-
cent 3D-native generative models, such as CraftsMan3D Li et al. (2025a), TRELLIS Xiang et al.
1
arXiv:2610.41231v1  [cs.CV]  30 Oct 2026


(2025; 2026), Sam3D Chen et al. (2026), have made substantial progress by adopting a coarse-to-
fine paradigm: a sparse geometric structure is first generated as voxels, and then refined into detailed
geometry and texture. In this pipeline, the first-stage voxel structure is particularly important: it de-
termines the global orientation and coarse geometry on which all later refinement is built, and any
error introduced here is difficult to correct in subsequent stages. Yet current coarse-stage genera-
tion still suffers from two key limitations: uncontrollable orientation and unfaithful structure. In
this work, we therefore focus on sparse-structure generation and propose Coarse3D, a model that
produces orientation-controllable and structurally faithful voxel geometry from unposed images.
Uncontrollable voxel orientation.
Much of this uncertainty stems from the object-centric canon-
ical space used by native 3D generators (Xiang et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025c), where
each object must be normalized to a shared frame with its semantic parts aligned to predefined axes.
Recovering this frame from images alone is ill-posed: for many objects a “front” is simply undefined
(e.g., the ceiling fan in Fig. 1). Even where a front is well defined, the supervision itself is incon-
sistent: large-scale datasets such as Objaverse-XL (Deitke et al., 2024) often store same-category
assets in different orientations. The model is thus asked to fit a mapping that is neither well defined
nor consistently supervised, leaving the orientation of generated assets uncontrollable.
Coarse3D instead adopts a first-frame anchored canonical space: the object is still centered at the
origin and normalized to a unit cube, but its orientation is defined by the first input view rather than
by a semantic front. This keeps generation in a compact, scale-normalized, camera-independent
domain while eliminating the ill-posed rotation inference altogether. Note that this differs from
camera-centric (Wang et al., 2025a) or pixel-aligned prediction (Li et al., 2026), where geometry is
tied to the imaging frame and its position and scale depend on focal length and camera distance. Here
only the orientation is borrowed from the view, and the representation itself remains object-centric.
Unfaithful voxel structure.
The second limitation concerns faithfulness: as shown in Fig. 1, ex-
isting coarse generators may omit parts that are clearly visible in the inputs (the missing leaves and
branches of the flower), hallucinate geometry that appears in none of the views (a spurious fifth
crossbar on the cart), or misplace structures relative to the observations (the items on the plate are
rearranged). We attribute this to weak 2D-3D grounding during image conditioning. At the feature
level, the image features used for cross-attention encode appearance and semantics but few explicit
geometric cues, leaving voxel tokens with little spatial evidence to align with and no cross-view
geometry to reconcile them. At the attention level, the 2D-3D mapping is left entirely implicit: each
voxel token attends to all image tokens with no geometric constraint, so pixel-to-voxel correspon-
dence must be learned from scratch with limited 3D supervision.
To improve faithfulness, Coarse3D strengthens grounding at both levels. At the feature level, we
introduce a dual-tower DiT that couples sparse-structure generation with an auxiliary reconstruction
tower, injecting features layer by layer and trained end-to-end. The reconstruction objective forces
the conditioning features to encode spatial and cross-view geometry and injects them into generation
at every layer, avoiding the feature mismatch and information bottleneck of staged reconstruction-
then-generation pipelines such as ReconViaGen (Chang et al., 2026) and RecGen3D (Huang et al.,
2026). At the attention level, we propose spatial-prior-guided attention, which rasterizes the ground-
truth structure into visible pixel-to-voxel associations and uses them to regularize cross-attention,
encouraging each voxel token to attend to the image regions it actually projects to. As this prior is
applied only during training, Coarse3D needs no camera poses at inference.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We present Coarse3D, a sparse-structure generator that addresses two overlooked failure modes
of the coarse stage—uncontrollable orientation and unfaithful structures. As experiments demon-
strate, it improves coarse-to-fine baselines on benchmarks by a large margin.
• We propose a first-frame anchored canonical space that anchors the generated orientation to
the first input view instead of an ill-defined semantic front, preserving an object-centric unit cube
representation while also accommodating generation and reconstruction in a single space.
• We strengthen 2D-3D grounding with a dual-tower DiT that makes conditioning features
geometry-aware via auxiliary reconstruction, and a spatial-prior guided attention that regular-
izes cross-attention with rasterized pixel-to-voxel associations.

RELATED WORK
2.1
3D-NATIVE GENERATION
Early image-to-3D methods rely on slow per-instance optimization (Mildenhall et al., 2021; Kerbl
et al., 2023; Poole et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2024). Recent 3D-native models instead generate directly
in 3D latent spaces. One family encodes shapes as unstructured vector sets (Zhang et al., 2023;
2024; Team, 2025; Ye et al., 2025) and decodes geometry in a single step, leaving no intermediate
structure to control. The other, introduced by TRELLIS (Xiang et al., 2025), attaches structured la-
tents to active voxels of a sparse grid, splitting generation into a coarse-to-fine cascade: a first stage
produces the sparse structure, a second predicts per-voxel latents, and a decoder yields the shape.
TRELLIS2 (Xiang et al., 2026) extends it with field-free O-voxels and separate shape and mate-
rial stages and several other TRELLIS-based variants have also emerged (Wu et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2025d; He et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Because the cascade is sequential, the first stage fixes
global orientation and layout and upper-bounds later stages, yet prior work mainly targets represen-
tation design and shape refinement. We instead focus on this coarse stage, identifying orientation
controllability and structural faithfulness as its two bottlenecks.
2.2
ORIENTATION IN 3D GENERATION
Most 3D-native generators place objects in an object-centric canonical space (Zhang et al., 2024;
Xiang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b): shapes are normalized to a centered, unit-scale domain, and their
semantic parts are expected to align with predefined axes. The normalization itself is convenient,
but the axis-alignment requirement forces the model to infer a semantic orientation from the input
images, e.g., which side is the front. This inference is ill-posed for objects without a clear front and
is further confounded by the inconsistent orientations of assets in large-scale datasets (Deitke et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2025). Prior work attacks this from two directions. OrientationMatters (Lu et al.,
2025) reduces dataset-level inconsistency by curating an orientation-aligned dataset with VLM-
based front-view prediction and manual calibration, which is costly and still presumes a well-defined
semantic front. Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026) instead sidesteps the inference by defining geometry in the
input camera frame, so the output aligns with the input view, but this leaves the shared canonical
domain and makes the output dependent on camera parameters and depth scale. Coarse3D keeps the
centered, unit-scale canonical domain and anchors only the orientation to the first input view, thus
removing semantic-front inference without giving up canonical normalization.
2.3
RECONSTRUCTION GROUNDING AND 2D-3D CORRESPONDENCE
Structural faithfulness hinges on how well image evidence is grounded in 3D. Most 3D-native gen-
erators condition voxel tokens on image features via cross-attention (Xiang et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2025b; Fang et al., 2026), leaving the 2D-3D relation entirely implicit. Reconstruction priors pro-
vide stronger geometric cues: ReconViaGen (Chang et al., 2026) injects multi-view stereo features
from VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a) into a TRELLIS-style generator, and RecGen3D (Huang et al.,
2026) uses canonicalized reconstructed point clouds as geometric anchors for diffusion. Both, how-
ever, keep reconstruction and generation in separate stages and never in a common frame: Re-
conViaGen leaves reconstruction in a camera-centric frame while generating in an object-centric
canonical frame, and RecGen3D bridges the two only by explicitly canonicalizing the reconstructed
point cloud. Coarse3D instead defines a single first-frame anchored space for both, and places feed-
forward reconstruction in an auxiliary tower of the same DiT, sharing features layer by layer so that
the generation tower consumes reconstruction-grounded features directly and end-to-end.
Even with geometry-aware features, cross-attention must still discover pixel-to-voxel correspon-
dence on its own, which is difficult at the scale of available 3D data. ROAR-3D (Sun et al., 2026)
routes each voxel token to the most relevant views, but the resulting alignment is view-level and
does not indicate where inside a view a token should attend. Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026) instead builds
pixel-aligned features by back-projection in camera space, which requires camera parameters at in-
ference. Coarse3D derives pixel-to-voxel associations by rasterization and uses them purely as a
training-time regularizer in spatial-prior guided attention, encouraging geometrically valid align-
ment without modifying the attention formulation or requiring camera parameters at test time.

METHODOLOGY
3.1
FIRST-FRAME ANCHORED CANONICAL SPACE
Existing coordinate systems face a dilemma (Fig. 2): a standard canonical space fixes the axes but
requires inferring an ambiguous semantic front (a teapot’s spout or its handle?), whereas a pixel-
aligned camera space removes this ambiguity by tying geometry to the imaging frame, making
position and scale camera-dependent. Our first-frame anchored canonical space bridges the two: the
object stays centered in a unit cube and we anchor only the azimuth to the first view, leaving the
canonical up-axis intact, which turns the ill-posed front inference into a well-posed alignment task.

tation that is ambiguous; pixel-aligned camera space is aligned with the input view but not object-
centric; ours is aligned with the first input view and object-centric.

3.2
RECONSTRUCTION-GROUNDED DUAL-DIT
To ground generation in visual evidence, our Dual-DiT couples the generative backbone with an
auxiliary feed-forward reconstruction branch (Fig. 3 (B)): the image tower performs reconstruction,
while the geometry tower performs iterative denoising. The two towers have matched depth, and
the geometry tower cross-attends to the image tower at every layer in the shared anchored frame, so
features shaped by the reconstruction objective ground generation hierarchically.
View-aware image conditioning. Given N unposed input views, we extract per-view patch tokens
using a frozen DINO encoder (Sim´eoni et al., 2025) and concatenate them to form a single con-
ditioning sequence C. Since plain concatenation hides which view a token comes from, we add
a learnable view embedding ei to the token set Ci of the i-th view, Ci ←Ci + ei. The ei are
initialized near zero to avoid perturbing the pretrained features early in training.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets. We train Coarse3D on about 2M 3D assets: TRELLIS-500K (Xiang et al., 2025), ∼500K
filtered assets from TexVerse (Zhang et al., 2025), and ∼1M orientation-unaligned in-house assets
covering commodities, furniture, and digital humans, all processed with the TRELLIS pipeline. We
evaluate on Toys4K (Stojanov et al., 2021) and Google Scanned Objects (GSO) (Downs et al., 2022).
Implementation details.
The sparse-structure (SS) stage is a single Dual-DiT of about 2.0B
parameters, whose geometry and image towers are initialized from the TRELLIS2 SS-DiT and
VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a). The structure resolution is set to 64. Each iteration samples 1 ∼6
unposed views. For spatial-prior-guided attention we use δ = 0.1, β = 0.2 and λattn = 0.1. We train
the base model for 200K steps at a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, then fine-tune it with the prior for
100K steps at 1 × 10−5, with batch size 256 on 64 H20 GPUs. See Appendix A.2 for full details.
Baselines and integration protocol. We compare against the multi-view (MV) variants of TREL-
LIS (Xiang et al., 2025), TRELLIS2 (Xiang et al., 2026), ReconViaGen (Chang et al., 2026), and
Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026). Coarse3D shares the coarse-stage interface of the first three, so we replace
only that stage with Coarse3D and keep their refinement models frozen, isolating the effect of the
coarse stage. In contrast, we do not apply Coarse3D to Pixal3D for two reasons: (i) it operates in
a pixel-aligned space rather than a canonical one, and (ii) its multi-view variant relies on accurate
camera poses to align cross-view features, whereas our setting assumes unposed views (detailed in
Appendix A.5). Following the evaluation protocol of ReconViaGen, all methods receive 4 arbitrary
unposed views, except that Pixal3D’s multi-view variant additionally requires camera poses. We
therefore report its native results, obtained with ground-truth poses, as an extra reference.
Evaluation metrics. We report three groups of metrics. (1) Coarse structure: IoU and PCA-
aligned IoU (PA-IoU), whose gap indicates how much error stems from orientation, and Chamfer
Distance (CD) between voxel centers. (2) Fine shape: CD, F-Score, and semantic consistency
metrics via ULIP-2 (Xue et al., 2024) and Uni3D (Zhou et al., 2024). (3) Auxiliary reconstruction:
camera RRE/RTE and point-map CD in the anchored canonical space. Note that all CD metrics
above are computed after rigid alignment. Detailed explanations of metrics are in Appendix A.3.

Structural fidelity. Coarse3D attains the best PA-IoU (0.5364 on GSO and 0.6368 on Toys4K) and
the lowest CD (0.0124 and 0.0091), i.e. about 1.45× the PA-IoU and less than half the CD of the

Qualitative results. Fig. 1 shows the same trend: the baselines often settle on an unintended global
orientation, or produce structural artifacts such as missing parts, hallucinated geometry, and con-

faithfully preserves the observed geometry.
4.2.2
FINE-GRAINED SHAPE GENERATION WITH DROP-IN REPLACEMENT
Analysis. Table 2 reports final shape quality when only the coarse stage of each baseline is replaced
by Coarse3D, denoted by + Ours. The replacement improves every metric on both datasets and
for all three pipelines: CD drops by 3 ∼6× (e.g. 0.0613 →0.0127 for TRELLIS2-MV on GSO)
and F-Score rises by 0.24 ∼0.44. Notably, the three variants become almost indistinguishable in
geometry (GSO CD within [0.0127, 0.0132]) despite their different refinement stages, indicating
that the coarse structure rather than the refinement stage dominates the final geometry metrics. The
semantic scores improve more modestly (e.g. Uni3D 0.2707→0.2836 for ReconViaGen), consistent
with their limited sensitivity at this level of shape quality. As CD and F-Score are computed after
rigid alignment, these gains reflect structure rather than pose, and Fig. 4 shows where they come
from: repeated parts that baselines fuse or duplicate (e.g., the rack’s grid shelves), thin structures
they break (e.g., the sandal’s spiral straps), and salient parts they hallucinate or omit (e.g., the ship’s
masts and sails) are all recovered after replacing the first stage with Coarse3D.
4.2.3
AUXILIARY RECONSTRUCTION TASK
Analysis. Table 3 checks whether the image tower really acquires reconstruction-grade geometry.
Although reconstruction is only an auxiliary objective, Coarse3D is the best on every metric: it cuts
the rotation error of the reconstruction foundation VGGT by about 5× (10.54→2.02 on GSO) and is
on par with the specialized RecGen3D*, showing that end-to-end training with generation objective
does not sacrifice reconstruction accuracy.

CONCLUSION
We presented Coarse3D, a coarse-stage model for 3D-native generation that targets two limitations
of current pipelines: orientation that is uncontrollable because canonicalization is semantically am-
biguous, and structures that are inaccurate because 2D-3D grounding is weak. Coarse3D anchors
the canonical frame to the first input view, grounds its conditioning features with a dual-tower DiT
trained jointly on reconstruction, and regularizes cross-attention with pixel-to-voxel correspondence.
Since it preserves the sparse-voxel interface of existing coarse stages, it can replace them without
retraining the refinement stage, raising F-Score from 0.54 to 0.86 on GSO and improving final ge-
ometry for all three pipelines we tested. Our study focuses on the coarse stage; extending our core
designs, e.g., the reconstruction-grounded Dual-DiT and spatial-prior guided attention, to fine-shape
generation as a unified framework, though architecturally natural, is left to future work.

A.1
DETAILED COMPARISON OF 3D GENERATION SPACES
In the standard preprocessing pipeline for 3D assets, objects are typically centered at the origin and
scaled within a unit cube. Subsequently, images are rendered from multiple viewpoints surrounding
the object to construct image-3D training pairs. We refer to this classical coordinate system as the
Standard Canonical Space (Fig. 2 left), which is defined independently of any viewpoint. Ideally,
the object orientation within this space should align with human intuition: the +z axis points up-
ward (opposite to gravity), the −y axis indicates the semantic front of the object, and the object
rests naturally on the “ground” formed by the xOy plane. However, methods such as TRELLIS
randomly sample rendering images from arbitrary viewpoints during training while keeping the
generated target completely unchanged. This forces the generative model to implicitly learn and
converge to a unified canonical orientation for each object category. Due to heterogeneous standards
across internet-scale datasets, actual object orientations are often highly inconsistent. Specifically,
while the gravity direction (determined by both elevation and roll angles) is mostly accurate or
can be easily corrected, the azimuth angle (determined by the object’s front-facing direction) varies
significantly due to differing interpretations among 3D creators. This leads to severe azimuth am-
biguity within the Standard Canonical Space (Fig. 2 left). OrientationMatters (Lu et al., 2025)
highlighted that such inconsistencies severely impair generative training and proposed globally cor-
recting dataset orientations. However, this process is prohibitively labor-intensive. Furthermore,
OrientAnythingV2 (Wang et al., 2025b) pointed out that real-world objects may have multiple se-
mantic fronts, making absolute orientation correction physically ill-defined.
To address this issue, Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026) introduced a Pixel-aligned Camera Space (Fig. 2
middle). During training, given a randomly sampled input image with viewing angles (θa, θe, θr)
(representing azimuth, elevation and roll), the method applies the identical rotation to the origi-
nal normalized 3D assets as the final training ground truth. Consequently, the generation result of
Pixal3D is strictly aligned with the pose of the input image, which is called “Pixel-aligned 3D Gener-
ation”. This definition forces the model to actively learn the object’s actual pose rather than fitting an
ambiguous canonical orientation, thereby improving training stability. Although the Pixel-aligned
Camera Space eliminates orientation ambiguity, its solution space covers all possible 3D rotations
(i.e., SO(3) space), substantially increasing the learning difficulty. More critically, introducing ele-
vation and roll transformations loses the gravity direction of the generated 3D assets, making them
unpredictable for downstream applications such as placing objects plausibly on a ground plane.
Given that azimuth ambiguity is the predominant issue in real-world datasets, we integrate the merits
of both above spaces and propose the first-frame anchored canonical space (Fig. 2 right). Specifi-
cally, for the first frame with viewing angles (θa, θe, θr) from multi-view inputs, we apply only the
azimuth rotation θa to the original normalized 3D asset, yielding the final training ground truth. As
a result, the azimuth of the training ground truth matches the actual azimuth of the object in the
first frame, while the elevation and roll angles maintain the original anti-gravity direction. If the
generated azimuth is undesirable, users can rotate it alone without disturbing the gravity direction.
The detailed comparison of the three spaces mentioned above is summarized in Table 5. We argue
that our proposed space definition is optimal for three reasons:
• Training robustness: It bypasses the strong assumption of a unified canonical orientation in the
Standard Canonical Space, ensuring stable training even on large-scale, inconsistently annotated
open-source datasets.
• Reduced learning complexity: Aligning solely along the azimuth dimension significantly shrinks
the full SO(3) space compared to full-view alignment in the Pixel-aligned Camera Space, thereby
facilitating the subsequent learning process and improving convergence stability.
• Controllability and intuitive usability: By selecting a desired first image, users can reliably control
the azimuth orientation of the generated object. Meanwhile, the elevation and roll angles, which
determine the anti-gravity direction, remain consistent with human intuition and world knowledge,
as well as standard 3D asset creation conventions.

A.2
MODEL AND TRAINING DETAILS
A.2.1
ARCHITECTURE AND INITIALIZATION
The sparse-structure stage of Coarse3D is a single Dual-DiT of about 2.0B parameters, consisting
of a Geometry tower and an Image tower that are coupled layer by layer and share the same depth
of 30 layers.
Geometry tower. The Geometry tower follows the SS-DiT structure of TRELLIS with 30 layers.
It takes voxel latents at 163 resolution as input, applies 3D RoPE positional encoding, and flattens
them into a sequence of 4096 tokens. Image information is injected through cross-attention at every
block, and the tower predicts the Partial View-Aligned coarse structure via flow matching.
Image tower. The Image tower adopts the VGGT architecture but, since the original VGGT has
only 24 layers, we extend it to 30 layers to match the depth of the Geometry tower for layer-by-
layer feature injection. Each input image is cropped and resized to 512 and encoded by DINOv3,
producing 1029 tokens per view. To handle a variable number of views, we introduce view-aware
learnable embeddings of shape [6, 1024], supporting up to 6 input views, where 1024 is the DINOv3
image-feature dimension. The tower uses alternating frame-wise and global self-attention, and after
each global self-attention layer, its multi-view stereo features are injected into the corresponding
layer of the Geometry tower.
Initialization. The Geometry tower is initialized from the pretrained TRELLIS2 SS-DiT which is
originally trained with a single image as the condition, and the Image tower from VGGT (Wang
et al., 2025a). Weights absent from these checkpoints (e.g., the extended VGGT blocks) are ran-
domly initialized, and the view-aware embedding is initialized to zero.
A.2.2
TRAINING
Stage 1: Base Model Pretraining. We first train the Dual-DiT jointly on the generation and aux-
iliary reconstruction objectives, with all geometric targets expressed in the first-frame anchored
canonical space and 1 ∼6 unposed views randomly sampled per iteration. Because the randomly
initialized Image tower could otherwise corrupt the pretrained Geometry tower weights, we adopt
a warmup strategy over the first 10K steps, during which the weight of the auxiliary reconstruction
loss is linearly increased from 0 to 1. After 200K steps at a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, we obtain the
base model.
Stage 2: Attention-Regularized Fine-tuning. We then enable the Spatial-Prior Guided Attention
and add its regularization loss, fine-tuning for a further 100K steps at a reduced learning rate of
1 × 10−5 with δ = 0.1, β = 0.2, and λattn = 0.1. This attention regularizer is applied only during
training and introduces no additional cost or camera dependence at inference. All experiments use
the AdamW optimizer with a global batch size of 256 on 64 H20 GPUs.

A.5
PLUG-AND-PLAY INTEGRATION WITH EXISTING PIPELINES
Direct reuse of pretrained VAE representations.
A distinctive property of Coarse3D is that it
directly reuses the pretrained Voxel VAE (Xiang et al., 2025) without any retraining, enabled by
our choice of coordinate system. Different from alternatives such as Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026) that
redefine geometry in the non-canonical frame and therefore require representations retrained for
that frame, our output stays in the same object-centric unit cube as the baseline canonical pose
and differs from it only by a rotation about the up-axis. Since the training data of these pipelines
already contains objects at different azimuths, upright and in-plane-rotated structures fall within the
distribution modeled by both their VAEs and their latent refinement stages.
A representation-agnostic interface.
Coarse3D replaces only the first stage of a cascade and
communicates with the second stage through a single minimal interface: an occupancy grid over a
normalized object-centric voxel space. It is therefore compatible with any voxel-based coarse-to-fine
pipeline, regardless of how the refinement stage represents fine geometry—for example, hierarchical
voxels (Ren et al., 2024), Flexicubes (He et al., 2025), SLAT (Xiang et al., 2025), O-Voxel (Xiang
et al., 2026), T-Voxel (Zhao et al., 2026), or VoxSets (Lai et al., 2026)—and regardless of how that
stage is trained. What matters is only that the refinement stage consumes a coarse occupancy at the
same resolution and in the same normalized object-centric space. This is precisely why Coarse3D
cannot be directly plugged into Pixal3D: although both operate on voxels, Pixal3D’s refinement
stage lives in a pixel-aligned space, so the interface simply does not match. Note also that, although
our coarse structure now lies in the anchored rather than the semantic canonical frame, the refinement
stage conditions on both the coarse structure and the input views, which remain mutually consistent
under a global change of frame. This is why the three pipelines in Sec. 4.2.2 accept our output
without any retraining.
