# GVT3D: Gesture-Vision-Text Token Enrichment for 3D Embodied Reference Understanding 

Vimarsh Shah 1, Dulanga Weerakoon 2, Ajinkya Deshpande 1, Saksham Agarwal 1,Nilesh Bhatia 1, Neel Kiran Naik 1, Parth Shah 1, Sravan Danda 1, Sanjay E. Sarma 3,Archan Misra 4, Vigneshwaran Subbaraju 5,6, Sougata Sen 1

Abstract — Natural human–robot interaction requires robots to interpret language and gestures within a 3D environment to support tasks such as pick-and-place and object retrieval. 3D Embodied Reference Understanding (3D-ERU) supports this by identifying the 3D location of a target object from a scene (point-cloud) containing a human pointing gesture and an accompanying language description. However, current 3D-ERU models lack alignment of cross-modal representations and incur high processing costs that increase with the point-cloud’s size. To address this, we propose GVT3D , which employs an Embodied Fusion module that jointly models language, gesture, and vision inputs within a shared embedding space, promoting cross-modal alignment. We additionally introduce a foveation approach that selectively processes the point cloud within a coarse region around the estimated pointing direction to reduce computations. GVT3D achieves ∼8% higher accuracy and foveation enables 2.95 × faster execution while maintaining comparable accuracy, relative to prior 3D-ERU models.

In this context, an embodied reference denotes a human instruction comprising a language expression accompanied by an optional pointing gesture that refers to a single target object within a scene [2]; such multimodal instructions are common for pick-and-place 

or object retrieval robotic tasks associated with home and warehouse logistics (e.g., [3]), as presented in Figure 1. The capability to accurately resolve such commands, through

Embodied reference understanding has been explored us-ing deep neural network-based approaches that operate on 2D visual inputs captured from ego-centric and exo-centric views [2], [4]. 3D Embodied Reference Understanding (3D-ERU) extends this problem into identifying a target object within a 3D scene , typically represented as a point cloud captured from a global, exo-centric view via LiDARs installed in the infrastructure [5], [6]. Compared to 2D settings, this intro-duces additional challenges due to spatial depth reasoning, geometric ambiguity, and articulated human pose variability, but has remained relatively underexplored; Ges3ViG [6] and ScanERU [5] being among the few approaches that address this problem in 3D settings. The limited literature on 3D-ERU leaves the following gaps unexplored:  

> •

Lack of Unified Cross-Modal Semantics: Despite substan-tial progress in grounding linguistic referring expressions, existing methods for 3D-ERU do not explicitly model the semantics of interaction between language and gesture within a common representation space. Prior work on 3D-ERU incorporates gesture only through late fusion, where the estimated pointing direction is used as the final arbiter to re-weight or select candidate regions along the pointing ray, e.g., Ges3ViG [6]. Such formulations treat gesture as an auxiliary signal operating independently of language, rather than truly exploiting the interactions between lan-guage and gesture that jointly constrain the identification and localization of the target. Previous studies have also shown that relying solely on pointing can be inaccurate due to systematic human pointing biases and variability in articulation [7], and that the relative importance of lan-guage and pointing may depend on varying environmental context [8]. Thus, gestures must be interpreted jointly arXiv:2611.04422v1 [cs.CV] 3 Nov 2026 with language throughout the reasoning process, with the relative importance adjusted based on factors like pointing fidelity, or presence of confounding distractor objects.  

> •

Lack of Computational Efficiency: Existing models for 3D-ERU [5], [6] remain untested in practical real-world scenarios involving downstream tasks/reasoning on top of 3D-ERU being executed on-device by a robot : for example, enabling a robot to rapidly and accurately navigate to the object specified by an embodied reference. Extant 3D-ERU models incur substantial computational overhead that scales with the number of points; we show in Section IV-C that this prohibitive overhead can meaningfully affect robot responsiveness on embedded edge computing platforms. Consequently, 2D ERU models [4], [8] remain widely preferred and adopted, even though 2D reasoning limits the ability to identify the 3D coordinates of the target object in the real world. Computationally efficient, edge-compatible 

3D-ERU models are thus needed to increase adoption in practical deployments. In this paper, we introduce Gesture-Vision-Text Token Enrichment for 3D Embodied Reference Understanding (GVT3D ), a new model that explicitly aligns gesture, vi-sion, and language features within a shared embedding space through a novel embodied fusion mechanism, enabling semantically-rich structured cross-modal interaction before any modality-specific score is computed. Rather than relying solely on late fusion or simple feature concatenation, GVT3D combines this joint representation learning with a final learned re-weighting stage, allowing the model to adaptively trust language, gesture, or vision depending on their relative reliability for a given instance, leading to improved accuracy compared to baseline 3D-ERU models. To further address the computational cost of processing large 3D scenes, we propose point cloud foveation , whereby candidate objects are first ranked by how well they align with the pointing direction determined via a lightweight gesture predictor, and the expensive 2D visual encoding step is executed only on the most gesture-consistent proposals. In summary, we make the following key contributions: 

1) GVT3D model for 3D-ERU: On the ImputeRefer [6] benchmark, our GVT3D outperforms Ges3ViG (prior SOTA) by achieving 8% higher accuracy, alongside a 1.75 × speedup. Our analysis further shows that GVT3D meaningfully aligns the vision, language and gesture embeddings, enabling it to adjust the importance of individual modality for a given input context. 2) Foveation for Efficient 3D-ERU with GVT3D: To address the computational overhead of processing large point clouds found in 3D-ERU data, we propose point cloud foveation-based computation. Compared to Ges3ViG, GVT3D with foveation runs 2.95 × faster and reduces energy consumption by 70.76% while improving accuracy by 2.75%. 3) Real-World Prototype of GVT3D with Foveation: We implement a robotic system that integrates GVT3D as its reasoning module and Nav2 [9] for navigation planning to support a representative object retrieval task. In real-world evaluations, foveation-enhanced GVT3D achieves a success rate of 70% across 30 scenes which the model hasn’t seen before, in comparison to 56% achieved by Ges3ViG on the same task. II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

A. 2D Visual Grounding 

Localising image regions from natural language de-scriptions, commonly known as Referring Expression Comprehension, has been a long-standing challenge in vision-language understanding. Early approaches followed a proposal-based paradigm where candidate regions were generated and matched with language features using mul-timodal embeddings [10]. More recent work has explored transformer-based architectures that align textual tokens with spatial regions through attention mechanisms [11], [12]. In parallel, large-scale vision-language pretraining has enabled strong semantic alignment between images and text [13]. Despite these advances, most 2D grounding methods assume that language uniquely specifies the referent, ignoring the use of additional cues, such as gestures and gaze, in natural human-robot communication [14]. 

B. 2D-Embodied Reference Understanding 

Recent work extends referring-expression comprehension by incorporating embodied cues such as pointing ges-tures alongside language. In the 2D setting, this integra-tion has largely focused on system-level efficiency rather than representation-level fusion. For instance, COSM2IC enhances real-time multimodal instruction understanding [4], involving 2D vision, language and pointing gestures, by introducing adaptive model selection to balance accuracy and computational efficiency, rather than jointly modeling how gesture and language features interact. 

C. 3D Visual Grounding 

3D visual grounding extends referring-expression compre-hension to 3D scene representations, typically point clouds, requiring reasoning over spatial geometry and object lay-outs. Early work, such as ScanRefer, introduced large-scale datasets and proposal-based grounding pipelines built upon ScanNet scenes [15], [16]. Subsequent work, including ReferIt3D, highlighted the challenges of spatial reasoning in cluttered environments [17]. More recent methods incorpo-rate attention mechanisms to associate language tokens with object-level geometric features [18], or adopt transformer-based architectures for end-to-end grounding [19]. Leverag-ing vision-language pretraining has further improved perfor-mance by aligning rendered multi-view images with textual embeddings [20], while approaches such as PointCLIP adapt CLIP representations for point cloud understanding through multi-view projections [21]. D. 3D-Embodied Reference Understanding 

3D embodied reference understanding studies how agents interpret multimodal instructions that combine language with physical cues like pointing gestures in 3D environments. Ges3ViG originally introduced approaches to incorporate pointing gestures into 3D visual grounding by modeling ges-ture as a geometric ray extending from the shoulder to finger-tip [6]. ScanERU further investigates interactive grounding using human pose signals to derive spatial constraints [5]. However, these methods typically represent gestures using deterministic geometric formulations and integrate gesture information through late fusion strategies. In contrast, our work treats gesture, vision, and language as tightly coupled modalities and jointly models the semantics of their interac-tions through a unified token-level fusion framework. III. GVT3D: G ESTURE -V ISION -T EXT FOR 3D-ERU We now present the proposed GVT3D approach, illus-trated in Figure 2. GVT3D differs from prior 3D-ERU meth-ods in two ways. (i) we estimate the pointing direction using a learned model instead tracing a shoulder-to-fingertip line, to capture natural arm gesture. (ii) instead of using gesture as a separate signal for refining object selection, GVT3D’s Embodied Fusion Module performs joint reasoning over gesture, language, and vision tokens, allowing each modality to inform others before the target object is chosen.

2) Language Module: We adopt the CLIP based language encoder, replicating the approach adopted in Ges3ViG [6]. The referring expression is tokenized using the CLIP tok-enizer, and both token-level and sentence-level representa-tions are extracted from the frozen CLIP text transformer. The token level features are projected through a two-layer MLP to a shared embedding dimension dt = 128 , yielding word-level features Fl,t ∈ RN tok ×128 . The sentence level representation is obtained via a learned projection head applied to the global CLIP text embedding, resulting in 

Fl,s ∈ R128 .

3) Gestural Module: In contrast to work like Ges3ViG [6], which models pointing gestures using aheuristic shoulder-to-fingertip ray, we introduce a learned pointing direction estimator. In natural human interactions, pointing gestures are rarely perfectly straight arms [7], so a fixed geometric ray does not always reflect the intended target direction. To address this, we propose a gestural module that learns the pointing direction directly from joint positions, rather than assuming a fixed arm geometry. 

a) Joint Feature Encoder: This module extracts the human point cloud segment identified by the PointGroup detector predictions. Using per-point semantic features Fs ∈

RN ×32 and semantic scores, points classified as human are selected and fed through a PointNet-style joint regressor that predicts soft assignment weights, Whum ∈ RNhum ×Nj

for Nj = 55 SMPL-X joints [23]. Joint 3D coordinates are computed as J = Softmax (WT

hum ) × Phum and initial joint features as Fj, init = Softmax (WT

hum ) × Fs, hum , similar to computation in Point2Skeleton [24]. These initial features are processed through a Joint Feature Extraction module consisting of 1D convolution layers ( 32 → 64 → 128 ) with LeakyReLU activations, producing joint-level features Fj ∈

RNj ×128 . The module also computes a global aggregate feature Fagg by average pooling intermediate representations at each layer and concatenating them. This aggregate feature captures the global pose information. The Joint Feature Extractor’s output is sequentially passed through two self-attention layers, each comprising a multi-head attention mechanism ( h = 4 , dk = dv = 32 ). This outputs a set of gesture tokens G ∈ RB×Nj ×128 that encodes pose information for the downstream Joint Fusion task. 

b) Pointing Regressor: With the computed joint fea-tures, we employ a lightweight MLP termed the Pointing Regressor, which takes as input the 3D positions of eight key joints from a set of 55 total human joints, namely the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and index fingertip for both left and right arms. For each arm, we compute shoulder-centred relative offsets: (4) δarm = [ pelbow − pshoulder , pwrist − pshoulder , pindex 

− pshoulder , pwrist − pelbow ] ∈ R12 

The offset vectors from both arms are concatenated to form a 24-dimensional input, which is then passed through a three-layer MLP ( 24 → 64 → 64 → 3) with ReLU activations. The output is L2-normalised to produce the predicted unit point-ing direction ˆdpred . This ˆdpred is used to compute proposal-wise pointing biases, which guide top-k proposal selection for foveated visual encoding and contribute to adaptive score fusion in the Cross-Modal Matching Module. The inclusion of pwrist − pelbow explicitly captures the forearm direction independently of the upper arm. This allows the regressor to learn the relationship between joint geometry and pointing direction directly from the data, rather than assuming a fixed shoulder-to-fingertip line.

D. System Implementation 

We now describe our real-world robotic system, which uses GVT3D for 3D-ERU as shown in Figure 3. Our system comprises a TurtleBot3 Burger connected to an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, which runs the grounding and navigation pipeline. An Orbbec Femto Bolt depth camera captures a 3D point cloud of the environment, including the human performing the pointing gesture. Given the point cloud, pointing gesture, and language command, GVT3D predicts a 3D bounding box for the referred object. We then select a reachable navigation goal near the predicted target and pass it to Nav2. Using a 2D occupancy map derived from the scene point cloud, together with odometry and LiDAR feedback, Nav2 guides the robot toward the selected goal, as shown in supplementary video. IV. E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS 

A. Dataset & Evaluation Metrics 

We evaluate on ImputeRefer [6], which extends Scan-Net [15] and ScanRefer [16] indoor scenes with imputed human avatars pointing at a target object, comprising 35,000 instances across 707 scenes. Every instance refers to a single object; the dataset contains no region-level or multi-object references. ImputeRefer’s referring expressions are shorter and more naturalistic than text-only benchmarks ( ∼ 10 words vs. ∼ 19 in ScanRefer), reflecting how language is under-specified when paired with a gesture. This is similar to practical instructions like “grab that box” (Figure 1). Target distance also varies across scenes. Following prior work [6], we use the same definition of unique (one object of the target class in the scene) and multiple (multiple same-class distractors), and report IoU@0.25 and IoU@0.5 as the primary metrics, along with latency and energy consumed on the Jetson Orin Nano. 

B. Accuracy Comparison with Prior Work on 3D-ERU 

We compare the performance of GVT3D against prior approaches for 3D-ERU, both with and without gestural reasoning, as shown in Table I. Methods listed without Gestures are 3D visual grounding baselines evaluated on the underlying ScanRefer scenes without gesture input; methods listed with Gestures are evaluated on the full ImputeRefer benchmark, including the imputed avatars. The results in-dicate that GVT3D consistently achieves higher accuracy compared to existing state of the art models. Among approaches that do not perform gestural reasoning, M3DRef-CLIP achieves the closest performance to GVT3D, but GVT3D outperforms it by 17.7% in IoU@0.5 on the overall ImputeRefer dataset. When compared to Ges3ViG, which supports gestural reasoning but relies primarily on ray tracing for gesture interpretation and lacks cross modal feature alignment, GVT3D achieves an improvement of 8%. The improvement is particularly pronounced in the Multi-ple setting, where several instances of the same object class are present in the scene. In this scenario, performance im-proves from 55.8% to 65.2% at IoU@0.5 (+9.4 points). This gain can be attributed to our embodied fusion module, which enables gesture tokens to interact directly with language and geometric features. Such cross modal interaction allows the model to more effectively disambiguate among multiple can-didate objects and accurately identify the intended referent. This performance gain highlights the importance of both explicit cross modal feature alignment and moving beyond ray tracing approaches for gestural reasoning. 

C. Accuracy, Latency, and Energy Trade-off of GVT3D against Prior 3D-ERU Methods 

All latency and energy measurements are taken on the Jetson Orin Nano, using 10 warmup scenes to reach a steady operating state, followed by averaging over the same 50 held-out scenes for every model and configuration. Fig-ure 4 illustrates the accuracy, latency, and energy trade-off of GVT3D at different k values, where k denotes the maximum number of object proposals retained for foveation, compared with prior 3D-ERU approaches. In the accuracy– latency comparison (Figure 4a), GVT3D is 1.75 × faster than Ges3ViG even without foveation, demonstrating its runtime efficiency. Foveation reduces accuracy as k decreases. This occurs mostly in dense scenes, where many objects lie close to the pointing direction. In such scenes, the object nearest to the pointing ray is frequently not the target, because the language instruction identifies a different object in the same region. When k is small, foveation can exclude the correct object from the retained proposals, and the model cannot recover it. However at k = 10 , GVT3D still outperforms Ges3ViG by 2.75% in IoU@0.5 while running 2.95 × faster. Similarly, in the accuracy–energy comparison (Figure 4b), GVT3D without foveation achieves higher accuracy while consuming only 67 .11% of the energy required by Ges3ViG. With foveation, GVT3D maintains higher accuracy than Ges3ViG down to k = 10 , where it consumes only 29 .24% 

of Ges3ViG’s energy. These results demonstrate that GVT3D achieves higher accuracy with lower latency and energy consumption than Ges3ViG, supporting its suitability for on-device ERU tasks in robotic deployments. D. Ablation Studies: 

Table II presents several ablation studies of GVT3D on the ImputeRefer dataset. In the w/o Fusion setting, we re-move the embodied fusion module. Under this configuration, we observe a significant degradation in performance, with IoU@0.5 on the overall dataset dropping by 15.9%. This highlights the importance of cross modal feature alignment. In the w/o Ges. setting, the human is removed from the scene environment, forcing GVT3D to rely entirely on lan-guage based reasoning. In this, we again observe a substan-tial reduction in performance, with IoU@0.5 decreasing by 38.5% on the overall dataset. This degradation is even more pronounced in the Multiple setting, where the performance drops by 43.5%. This result indicates that gestural reasoning plays a critical role in disambiguating between objects of the same class when multiple candidates are present. Finally, in the w. fixed lang. setting, where the language description for the entire dataset is replaced with a minimal expression ‘this one’, we observe that GVT3D achieves 20% higher accuracy when compared to Ges3ViG. Collectively, these ablation results demonstrate the importance of accurate cross modal fusion, as well as the complementary roles of language and gesture reasoning in 3D-ERU.

G. Success Rate Evaluation with GVT3D 

We evaluated GVT3D with our robotic prototype in real-world scenes that were not part of the training data with our system as shown in Figure 3. We placed the target between 2.75 m and 9.5 m from the robot across the trials (mean 5.89 m, SD 2.28 m). Before these trials, we fine-tuned the model on 80 samples that we collected from real persons, and we replaced the synthetic persons in the ImputeRefer dataset with these samples. Two sources of error can occur in this pipeline: (i) GVT3D grounds the wrong object, resulting in an incorrect navigation goal, and (ii) the TurtleBot fails to reach the correct goal due to navigation drift or failure. To assess (i), six participants each viewed 30 scenes showing the real-world environment and the predicted target bounding box, and judged whether the grounding was correct. Overall, GVT3D’s object local-ization was judged correct in 70% of instances, as compared to 56% of cases in Ges3ViG. To assess (ii), we evaluated navigation performance on the subset of trials where GVT3D grounded the correct object. Across 16 completed trials, the TurtleBot reached within a mean distance of 0.79 m (SD 0.36 m) from the target. Navigation was aborted in 2 trials due to odometry drift. V. C ONCLUSION 

We propose GVT3D, a novel approach for 3D-ERU that localises objects in 3D scenes given language and pointing gestures. Unlike prior methods that rely on heuristic ray tracing for gestural reasoning, GVT3D models gestures as a spatial prior jointly conditioned with language features. We introduce an embodied fusion mechanism that tightly aligns vision, language, and gesture representations within a shared embedding space. Experimental results show that (a) GVT3D achieves ∼8% higher accuracy without foveation and (b) foveation enables 2.95 × faster execution while maintaining comparable accuracy, relative to prior 3D-ERU models.
