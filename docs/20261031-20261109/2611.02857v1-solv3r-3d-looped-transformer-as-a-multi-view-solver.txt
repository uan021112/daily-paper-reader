Abstract
Classical structure-from-motion reconstruction proceeds it-
eratively, refining camera poses and scene geometry from a
current estimate. This allows maps to easily expand with-
out recomputing already computed views, or geometry esti-
mates to improve as more observations are provided. Most
geometric foundation models, however, predict entire geom-
etry in a single forward pass or update a recurrent state as
frames arrive sequentially, making this expansion and re-
finement non-trivial. We introduce Solv3R, a looped trans-
former that learns to continue solving multi-view geome-
try from its current state. Importantly, its update operator
is independent of the iteration count, allowing previously
refined and newly introduced views to be jointly updated
regardless of how “solved” they are. We train the oper-
ator to preserve accurate reconstructions under continued
iteration and correct inaccurate states, enabling refinement
of fixed image collections and integration of new observa-
tions. Representations of solved views can also be cached
once and reused as geometric context across iterations, en-
abling compact and efficient key-value caching. We analyze
the solver dynamics and compare it with the state-of-the-
art, demonstrating that a single learned solver supports of-
fline reconstruction, incremental reconstruction, relocaliza-
tion, and streaming without task-specific training or mod-
ules. The code and models will be made publicly available
at https://ubc-vision.github.io/solv3r/.

reconstruction toward predicting scene geometry directly
from multi-view images. Unlike classical Structure-from-
Motion (SfM), which relies on correspondence matching
and iterative geometric optimization [69], these models es-
timate geometry in a feed-forward manner.
They typi-
cally employ successive transformer blocks to progressively
build geometry-aware representations and predict camera
poses, depths, and point maps.
But large-scale reconstruction often extends beyond a
single forward pass. The image collection may exceed a
transformer’s input capacity, or new views may arrive after
the initial reconstruction, such as subsequent video frames
or additional images and videos of the same scene. Incor-
porating these views with a feed-forward GFM typically re-
quires processing the combined image set again [88, 95].
Streaming models [21, 45, 86, 90, 117] avoid this by updat-
ing a state frame by frame, but they are limited to views that
arrive sequentially. In contrast, classic SfM incorporates
new observations into an existing reconstruction and uses
iterative optimization to refine its estimates [69]. Hence,
a learned reconstruction model should likewise be able to
continue solving from its current state, refining existing es-
timates and integrating new views as they arrive. This re-
quires a reconstruction “state” that remains usable for fur-
ther geometric computation beyond the initial forward pass.
But conventional feed-forward GFMs like VGGT [88]
process image representations through successive trans-
former layers, each applying a different learned transforma-
tion (Fig. 2). Their intermediate states (i.e. tokens) are con-
sequently tied to particular stages of computation (i.e. layer
depth). D´ej`aView [12] moves toward iterative reconstruc-
tion by repeatedly applying a shared transformer block.
However, its architecture remains conditioned on the re-
finement step, allowing the same block to perform different
transformations at different stages. In contrast, a classical
stationary iterative solver such as Jacobi [10], applies the
same update at every iteration, and does not need to know
which iteration it is operating on. It can thus continue from
any state, including one whose variables have received dif-
ferent numbers of iterations. A key observation is that shar-
ing weights alone does not give D´ej`aView this ability.
We thus posit that a GFM should be a learning-based
multi-view solver. Given more computation, a solver should
continue refining a reconstruction until convergence, and
once converged, the solution should not change with fur-
ther iterations. A solver of this type should handle offline
reconstruction, adding views, and streaming with one uni-
fying architecture (see Fig. 1).
To this end, we introduce Solv3R, an architecture
that reformulates multi-view reconstruction as a reusable
learning-based geometric solver. Solv3R employs a station-
ary (i.e. timestep invariant) learned geometric update oper-
ator that acts on the current multi-view state. We train this
operator so that a solved reconstruction stays unchanged un-
der further iterations, and so that a reconstruction with er-
rors, for example one with newly added views, converges to
the solution (Fig. 3).
With these solver properties, Solv3R unifies different
reconstruction settings through a single learned geometric
solving process. Our solver achieves competitive accuracy
in conventional multi-view reconstruction, while allowing
existing estimates to be jointly revised as new observations
arrive. This reuse extends beyond reconstruction states to
attention context: a fixed reconstructed map can provide a
single KV cache reused across solver calls. Together, these
capabilities support incremental reconstruction, map-based
relocalization, and streaming reconstruction without task-
specific learned modules, achieving competitive streaming
performance even without streaming-specific training.
Through extensive analyses of solver dynamics and ex-
periments across reconstruction scenarios, we demonstrate
how reusable geometric computation enables flexible and
efficient multi-view reconstruction.

2. Related work
Geometric foundation models (GFMs) have advanced feed-
forward 3D reconstruction, progressing from pairwise
pointmap regression and matching [47, 93] to joint pre-
diction of camera poses and dense geometry from mul-
tiple views [15, 28, 51, 75, 88, 95, 101].
Recent meth-
ods further improve multi-view geometric prediction and
scalability [19, 20, 30–32, 63, 87, 89, 99, 114]. Related
advances extend geometric prediction to monocular in-
puts [35, 91, 92, 102], incorporate additional geometric pri-
ors [38, 42, 56, 58, 65], or produce renderable Gaussian
representations [22, 100, 105, 106].
Learned iterative refinement and solvers. Learned iter-
ative refinement has been widely applied to optical flow,
stereo, multi-view depth, and visual SLAM [8, 41, 53,
77, 78, 85, 112].
Related work on equilibrium and re-
current models investigates fixed-point behavior, stability,
and extended computation [1, 7, 9, 29].
Inspired by re-
cent advances in looped transformers for language mod-
els [5, 6, 24, 34, 39, 61, 103, 115, 116], D´ej`aView [12]
introduces shared recurrent computation for multi-view re-
construction. We extend this direction to continued geomet-
ric solving across fixed and evolving view sets, including
views at different refinement stages.
Continuous and streaming 3D reconstruction. Continu-
ous reconstruction methods maintain and update geometric
information as new observations arrive. Some approaches
employ persistent scene representations or test-time adap-
tation [21, 86, 90, 98, 113], while others preserve geo-
metric context through causal attention and caching [18,
45, 108, 117].
Window-based methods further integrate
local reconstructions through registration and optimiza-
tion [26, 50, 54, 55, 59, 62, 109, 111]. Rather than introduc-
ing a separate learned reconstruction process for streaming,
Solv3R applies the same geometric solver to both offline
and online reconstruction.
3. Method
Our key idea is to make the shared block of a looped trans-
former act as the “iteration” of a classical solver, whose up-
date logic does not depend on how many iterations have
already run. We review looped transformers for 3D recon-
struction in Sec. 3.1, and use a simple classical solver in
Sec. 3.2 to define the properties that we want from such a
solver. We then replace the classical iteration with a learned
operator in Sec. 3.3, and discuss its properties in Sec. 3.4.
3.1. Looped transformers for 3D reconstruction
D´ej`aView [12] formulates multi-view reconstruction as it-
erative refinement of a latent state.
Given input images
I={Ii}N
i=1, an image encoder initializes a multi-view state
z0, which is repeatedly updated by a shared transformer
block with frame-wise and global attention. The refined
state is decoded into depth and ray maps, from which scene
geometry and camera poses are recovered.
Unlike con-
ventional feed-forward transformers with independently pa-
rameterized layers, D´ej`aView shares the transformer block
across iterations, but conditions each update on the step:
zk+1 = Fθ(zk; τk),
(1)
where τk is the conditioning on the iteration index k, which
lets the shared block apply a different transformation at each
iteration. Because of this structure, D´ej`aView loses recon-
struction accuracy when iterated beyond its trained range,
as we demonstrate in Section 4. More fundamentally, each
state is tied to the iteration index at which it was computed.
One application of Fθ thus cannot jointly update views that
have received different numbers of iterations, because no
single τk matches all of them. Thus, sharing weights alone
does not give the block an update that is independent of the
iteration index.
3.2. An iterative solver as a running example
To make the properties we want concrete, consider a classi-
cal constraint satisfaction problem, solving a linear system
Ax=b with the Jacobi method [10]. The rows of matrix
A and corresponding observations b define the constraints,
and the state x holds the current estimate of the unknowns.
Starting from an initial state x0, each iteration applies the
same update:
xk+1 = Φ(xk; A, b),
(2)
We call one iteration, that is, one application of Φ, a solver
call. The update Φ does not depend on the iteration index k,
which makes the Jacobi stationary [10]. When the method
converges, three properties follow:

(P1) Stability.
The accurate solution x∗, which satisfies
Ax∗=b, is a fixed point with Φ(x∗; A, b)=x∗, so further
solver calls leave it unchanged.
(P2) Correction. Consider a perturbed state, that is, a solu-
tion moved off by, for example, added noise. From such a
state, the same solver calls converge back to x∗.
(P3) Continuation. When new unknowns and constraints
are added, the solver can start the old unknowns from their
solved values, and the new ones from an initial guess. It
then updates all of them together toward the solution of the
enlarged system, often in fewer solver calls than a restart
from x0. The state holds values that have received different
numbers of solver calls, and Φ still updates them in one
solver call. It can do so because it does not depend on how
a state was reached.
Multi-view reconstruction has the same structure. The
input images define the constraints, the per-view states hold
the current estimate, and the cameras and depths decoded
from the state are the current estimate of the solution. A
solved state is one whose decoded cameras and depths are
accurate, and which further solver calls change little. A
learned multi-view solver should thus leave a solved recon-
struction unchanged under further solver calls (P1), correct
a perturbed reconstruction (P2), and continue from a solved
reconstruction when views are added (P3). Meanwhile, the
update of D´ej`aView in (1) depends on τk, so it does not have
these properties by construction.

3.4. Learning solver dynamics
We train Tθ to satisfy P1–P3. When the input images do
not change, a solved state z∗should change little under fur-
ther solver calls, Tθ(z∗; z0)≈z∗. From a perturbed state,
the same operator should bring the reconstruction back to
the solution. We thus train it on states that have received
different numbers of solver calls, as well as on perturbed
states.
Training states. To keep solved states unchanged under
further solver calls (P1), we train the operator on heteroge-
neous states.
In the main training stage, starting from z0, we apply a
random number of solver calls without gradients, followed
by several differentiable calls with supervision at the fi-
nal call.
This exposes the operator to states at different
refinement stages, encouraging stable updates under con-
tinued computation. To train the operator to correct per-
turbed states (P2), we occasionally perturb the state before
the differentiable calls—we either add Gaussian noise to the
whole state or reset a subset of views to z0. Gaussian noise
mimics deviations from a solution, while partial resets sim-
ulate newly introduced views alongside refined ones (P3).
Similar in spirit to denoising-based learning [72, 83] and
Diffusion Forcing [17], these perturbations expose the op-
erator to a broader range of states, encouraging corrective
refinement beyond its usual trajectories.
Geometric supervision. We supervise geometric predic-
tions after varying numbers of solver calls, rather than after
a fixed number. At the end of each sampled rollout, we de-
code camera poses, ray maps, and depths from the current
state and apply ground-truth geometric supervision where
available. We do not supervise the states of earlier calls;
instead, varying the supervised call trains the operator to
produce accurate geometry at any call between the 24th and
the 48th.
To preserve the priors of the pretrained D´ej`aView, we
also distill its predictions after 16 solver calls into the solver.
The overall training objective is
L = Lgeo + λdistLdist,
(4)
where Lgeo is the loss against the ground truth and Ldist is
the D´ej`aView distillation loss. We provide further details on
the loss terms and training procedure in the supplementary
material.

each transformer layer. Although D´ej`aView shares trans-

5. Experiments
We initialize from the pretrained looped transformer [12]
and fine-tune using AdamW [57] with a learning rate of
2×10−5 and an exponential moving average (EMA) of the
weights. Each training iteration samples 4–64 views and
performs 16–40 solver calls without gradients, followed by
8 differentiable calls supervised at the final step. Before the
differentiable calls, we apply feature noise or reset selected
views with probability 0.15, and set λdist=0.5. We train for
approximately one week on 8×H100 GPUs. Further im-
plementation details, dataset splits, training configurations,
and evaluation protocols are provided in the supplementary
material.
5.1. Offline reconstruction
We first compare our method with state-of-the-art multi-
view reconstruction methods on standard benchmarks. As
shown in Table 1, Solv3R achieves competitive camera pose
and geometry accuracy with a compact model. Importantly,
Solv3R retains accuracy comparable to D´ej`aView at the
same 16-call budget while exhibiting the stable and cor-
rective solver dynamics demonstrated in Section 4. These
properties enable continued refinement and reuse of previ-
ously computed states beyond a predefined iteration sched-
ule. The stable solver dynamics also enable adaptive infer-
ence using (5), with γ=0.02 and a maximum of 64 solver
calls. With adaptive stopping, we can improve nearly all
reconstruction metrics over our fixed-budget inference with
only 3–4 additional solver calls on average.
5.2. Incremental reconstruction
We next evaluate whether Solv3R can extend and re-
vise an existing reconstruction as new observations arrive.
Using evaluation windows from DL3DV [52] and Scan-
Net++ [107], we first solve four views for eight calls, intro-
duce twelve additional views, and continue joint refinement
from the existing states. As shown in Figure 8, Solv3R re-
constructs the new views while improving the camera es-
timates of the original views, whereas D´ej`aView fails to
maintain a consistent reconstruction. Compared with the
no-join control using the same number of solver calls, this
improvement shows that new observations contribute to cor-
recting the existing solution rather than merely benefiting
from further computation. In Figure 9, we examine revis-
ability across different refinement stages and the efficiency
of reusing previous computation. Adding new views at dif-
ferent refinement stages, even after the initial reconstruc-
tion meets the stopping criterion, improves original-view
accuracy in most evaluation windows. When incrementally
expanding the reconstruction from 8 to 64 views, Solv3R
achieves accuracy comparable to re-solving from scratch
with fewer solver calls and lower total runtime. These re-
sults show that existing reconstructions can be revised as
new observations arrive while reusing previous computa-
tion, rather than repeatedly solving from scratch.

joint refinement when new observations reveal inconsisten-

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we introduced Solv3R, which reformulates
multi-view 3D reconstruction as an ongoing geometric solv-
ing process.
By combining a stationary update operator
with learned stable and corrective dynamics, Solv3R can
continue refining existing estimates, integrate new obser-
vations, and reuse previously computed geometric repre-
sentations. Our experiments demonstrate that these proper-
ties enable a single learned solver to handle diverse recon-
struction settings, including offline and incremental recon-
struction, map-based relocalization, and streaming, with-
out task-specific learned modules or a separate optimiza-
tion backend. These results suggest a path toward more
open-ended 3D reconstruction, where geometric estimates
can be continuously refined and revised as observations
change. More broadly, we believe this perspective could
extend to vision-language models, robotics, and world mod-
eling, where maintaining and refining evolving states is a
fundamental challenge.

ing configuration of Solv3R.
Architecture. We build on the pretrained D´ej`aView [12],
retaining its shared transformer block with frame-wise and
global attention. We replace the step-conditioned gates with
learnable constant gates, which provide the same channel-
wise scaling in each sub-block across all solver calls. We
initialize these gates by averaging the corresponding gate
values over the 16 refinement steps of D´ej`aView.
To keep the state grounded in the original observations
and stable under continued computation, we inject the ini-
tial state at every solver call and normalize the updated
state:

Here, zk is the state after k solver calls, Uθ denotes the
shared block, ¯z0 is the layer-normalized [4] initial state
without affine parameters, and W is a zero-initialized lin-
ear projection. The target RMS ρ is the RMS of the final
D´ej`aView state, measured once before training (ρ ≈0.85),
and stays fixed throughout training and inference. We scale
the entire state by a single factor, which preserves relative
feature magnitudes across views and tokens. The resulting
model has 117M parameters. It takes images whose longer
side is 504 pixels.
Training data. To cover diverse scenes, we train on a broad
mixture of indoor, object-centric, outdoor, driving, and syn-
thetic datasets, summarized in Table 5.
Training objective. The geometric loss Lgeo supervises the
ray maps, cameras, and depths decoded at the final call.
For ray maps, we apply an L1 loss to ray directions and
scale-aligned ray origins. For cameras, we use geodesic
losses on the relative rotations and translation directions.
For depth, we use a scale-aligned log-L1 loss with weight
0.5. On datasets with dense and accurate depth, we add a
multi-scale depth-gradient loss [49] with weight 0.5. These
terms align each prediction to the ground truth with its own
scale. They do not enforce a common scale across views
or between depths and camera translations, so we add two
consistency terms with weight 1.5 each. The first penalizes
the deviation of each view’s depth scale from the window
mean. The second supervises the camera centers and ray
origins after rescaling them by the window’s depth scale.
The distillation loss Ldist applies the ray, log-depth, and
camera losses against the 16-step predictions of D´ej`aView,
with λdist = 0.5. For datasets whose ground truth is sparse
or noisy, marked with ∗in Table 5, we use Ldist only.

Starting from z0, we apply a number of solver calls without
gradients, drawn uniformly from 16 to 40. We then apply
eight differentiable calls and supervise the final call. With
probability 0.15, we perturb the state before the differen-
tiable calls. We either add Gaussian noise with a standard
deviation of σ ∥z∥RMS, where σ is drawn uniformly from
[0, 0.5], or reset a random subset of up to half of the views
to z0. The noise exposes the solver to globally inaccurate
states. The reset mixes refined and unrefined views within
one window, as occurs when views are inserted into an ex-
isting reconstruction.
Optimization. We train in two stages. The first stage grad-
ually increases the window size and the number of solver
calls. It runs for about 70k iterations with a batch of 8 win-
dows. The second stage trains with the full procedure above
for about 20k iterations with a batch of 32 windows. To
cover rolled cameras, we rotate each view by 90◦in either
direction with probability 0.15 in its last 2k iterations. We
fine-tune only the shared block, the gates, and the projection
W, and freeze the image encoder and the prediction heads
of D´ej`aView. We use AdamW [57] with a weight decay of
0.01 and learning rates of 2×10−5 for the shared block and
the projection, and 2 × 10−4 for the gates. We evaluate an
exponential moving average of the weights with a decay of
0.999.

Streaming reconstruction. Streaming uses the final model
without any streaming-specific training. For each incoming
frame, the solver operates on a window of anchors, memory
frames, recent frames, and the new frame. The first a frames
serve as anchors that fix the coordinate frame and are ini-
tially solved jointly for 16 calls. The r most recent frames
form the active window, where each new frame is initialized
from its image encoding plus the solved offset of the pre-
vious frame. The solver then jointly updates the new and
recent frames for m calls, retaining their previously com-
puted states (continuation). Anchors and memory frames
remain frozen during normal solver calls, providing fixed
attention context. Since their representations remain un-
changed, their key–value (KV) pairs are computed once and
reused across solver calls (stability). When a frame leaves
the recent window, it is retained in memory if its index is a
multiple of s, and discarded otherwise. The memory holds
the latest M such frames. To detect inconsistencies with
newly observed frames, we periodically check whether the
frozen states would change under further refinement. Ev-
ery four incoming frames, one additional solver call eval-
uates the relative state update ∥zk+1 −zk∥2/∥zk∥2 on the
frozen views. If this exceeds γ = 0.02, the frozen views
rejoin joint refinement with the incoming frame, continuing
from their existing states rather than restarting from scratch
(correction). Their KV cache is then rebuilt from the up-
dated states; otherwise, the cached representations are re-
tained. The default configuration (L8) uses a = 8, r = 6,
s = 16, M = 64, and m = 8.
B. Evaluation protocols
We detail the evaluation protocols for the solver-property
analyses and the offline, incremental, map-based relocaliza-
tion, and streaming experiments.
Solver properties. The stability analysis of the main paper
uses 148 eight-view windows, 66 from DTU [40], 37 from
ETH3D [70], and 45 from ScanNet++ [107] scenes held out
from our fine-tuning. We measure rotation errors relative to
the first view of each window. To test recovery, we perturb
the state of 36 of these windows, 12 from each dataset, at
call 8 and report the rotation error at call 16. Relative noise
(rel.) is Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of one
or two times the RMS of the state of each model. Fixed-
scale noise (fixed) uses the mean state RMS of Solv3R and
D´ej`aView instead, so that both models receive the same ab-
solute noise. A reset returns every view except the first to
its initial state. The other solver-property experiments use
64-frame sequences from the DL3DV [52] validation split
and the held-out ScanNet++ scenes.
Warm start simulates a video that arrives one frame at
a time. The window holds the first frame, which fixes the
coordinate frame, and the 23 most recent frames. When a
new frame arrives, the frames that stay in the window keep
their solved states. The new frame starts from its initial state
plus the solved offset of the previous frame, as in streaming
(Sec. A). Over 16 sequences from each dataset, we count
the solver calls until the rotation error of the new frame falls
below 2◦, and compare them with solving the window from
scratch.
Pipelined reconstruction slides a window of the first
frame and the 16 most recent frames over a sequence. It
advances the window after every solver call rather than af-
ter convergence. After each call, the oldest frame other than
the first leaves the window, and the next frame enters from
its initial state. Apart from the first frame, each call thus up-
dates frames that have received between 0 and 15 previous
calls. We evaluate the pose of each frame when it leaves the
window, after its 16th call.
Random computational histories test whether the joint
update depends on how each view reached its current state.
We record the states of a window over its first 16 calls from
scratch, and build a new state by taking each view from
a randomly chosen call of this trajectory. We then run 16
joint calls from this state, and report the mean difference of
the camera rotations across 20 such draws for each of 96
windows of 16 views.
Offline reconstruction. We follow the evaluation protocol
and splits of D´ej`aView [12] on DTU [40], ETH3D [70],
7-Scenes [71], ScanNet++ [107], and nuScenes [16]. We
report the area under the curve (AUC) of relative pose errors
at 3◦and 30◦, and the relative L2 error and inlier ratio of the
world-frame point maps. For adaptive inference, we stop
when the relative update falls below γ = 0.02, with at least
4 and at most 64 solver calls. Without adaptive stopping,
Solv3R runs 16 solver calls, the budget of D´ej`aView.
Incremental reconstruction. We use 20 scenes, 10 from
the DL3DV validation split and 10 from the held-out Scan-
Net++ scenes. Starting from 8 views in capture order, we
add 8 views at a time up to 64 views, and report the pose
AUC@10 over all view pairs after each insertion.
Map-based relocalization. For each scene, we solve the
training frames once as a map and cache the key-value (KV)
pairs of every map frame. Each test frame is a query that
we solve for 16 calls against cached map frames only. The
attended frames are its 10 nearest map frames retrieved by
NetVLAD [2], the reference view of the map, and 64 evenly
spaced map frames that cover the scene. We recover the
absolute query pose from the ground-truth poses of the re-
trieved frames and their predicted poses in the map. We
report the median translation and rotation errors and the re-
call within 5 cm and 5◦for 7-Scenes [71] and within 25 cm
and 2◦for Cambridge [43]. All methods attend to the same
map frames. We time each query on one H100 GPU, from
encoding the query to reading out its pose, including copy-
17


ing the cached key-value pairs of its retrieved frames from
host memory.
Streaming reconstruction.
We evaluate on Sintel [13],
TUM dynamics [73], ScanNet [23], Bonn [64], KITTI [33],
7-Scenes [71], and NRGBD [3]. We use 1000 frames with
a stride of 3 for ScanNet, 500 frames for Bonn and KITTI,
and 200 frames with a stride of 2 for 7-Scenes and NRGBD.
We evaluate each frame with its prediction at arrival, before
any later refinement. The first eight frames are an exception,
since Solv3R solves them jointly as anchors. We report the
absolute trajectory error (ATE) after Sim(3) alignment [81],
the depth AbsRel and δ1.25 after per-sequence scale align-
ment, and the Chamfer distance and normal consistency of
the reconstructed point clouds.
C. Additional analyses
We further examine the stopping behavior of the solver, its
sensitivity to initialization, and the reuse of solved states in
streaming and incremental reconstruction. We also inspect
attention across solver calls and discuss a failure case.
C.1. Stopping and initialization
Stopping behavior. As state updates diminish, geometric
error decreases and levels off by the stopping point, re-
maining low under further refinement (Fig. 5). Adaptive
stopping matches the median accuracy of continuing to 64
calls. Of the 148 windows of the stability analysis, 144
reach γ = 0.02 within 64 calls, after 17.5 calls on average
(median 17, interquartile range 15–19). Over these win-
dows, the median rotation error is 0.44◦at the stopping call
and 0.43◦at call 64. These results indicate that our state-
based stopping criterion identifies when further refinement
yields little improvement in geometric accuracy. The other
four windows are also accurate at call 64, with rotation er-
rors below 3◦. D´ej`aView, which repeats its final step after
16 calls, does not reach the threshold in any window.
Distribution of stopping calls. The number of calls adapts
to each window (Fig. 13). All 144 windows stop between
13 and 32 calls. Solv3R runs more calls on harder win-
dows. The stopping call correlates with the rotation error at
that call, with a Spearman correlation of 0.77. The median
stopping call also differs across datasets, from 14 calls on
ScanNet++ to 18.5 calls on DTU.
Sensitivity to the threshold. The threshold γ = 0.02 bal-
ances accuracy and cost (Tab. 6). A tighter threshold needs
1.6 to 2.7 times as many calls without consistent gains. A
looser threshold saves about a third of the calls but loses 4
to 8 AUC points on DTU, ETH3D, and ScanNet++.
Other datasets. Stopping remains nearly as accurate as
continuing to 128 calls on datasets outside the stability anal-
ysis (Tab. 7). We solve 107 more eight-view windows from

Initialization of new views. The solver converges from dif-
ferent initial states of a new view. We repeat the warm-start
experiment of the main paper with three initializations of
the new frame (Fig. 14). The new frame starts from its own
initial state z0, from z0 plus the solved offset of the previous

offset gives the most accurate first call, but z0 reaches the
same error within two to four calls. Even when the new
frame starts from the solved state of the previous frame,
which describes a different camera, the solver corrects it
within about 10 calls. All three initializations converge to
the error of solving the whole window from scratch.

only a few calls, as in streaming. We run the default stream-

frames when new observations disagree with them matters
most. Without the refresh, the ATE of Solv3R grows more

essarily imply that the model preserves meaningful geomet-

global attention of the shared block across solver calls. A
head finds the correspondence of a query patch when its
strongest attention in an adjacent view falls within one patch
of the ground-truth correspondence. At call 16, the same
three heads are the most accurate in both models. They find
the correspondence for 51 to 74% of the queries (Fig. 17).
Solv3R keeps the accuracy of these heads after call 16, and
their attention maps at call 64 remain close to those at call
16, with a similarity above 0.96. The heads of D´ej`aView

C.4. Incremental reuse and insertion order
Calls per insertion.
Continuing from the current state
reaches higher accuracy than re-solving from scratch at the
same cost. With 8 calls per insertion, continuing reaches an
AUC@10 of 92.2 after 64 views, while re-solving with 8
calls reaches 84.8. Re-solving needs 16 calls per insertion

slightly, to 92.9 with 16 calls and 93.1 with 32 calls.
Insertion order. We feed the same 64 views in capture
order, in reverse order, and in two random orders (Tab. 10).
Continuing with half the calls stays within two points of re-
solving in every order.

Its views form two groups with little overlap, and the solver
places the second group at a wrong position and orientation.

aerial scenes of Mill19 [80], Rubble and Building (Tab. 11).
Every method reconstructs each scene offline from the same

D.2. Qualitative results
Arrival patterns. The same solver reconstructs a scene
whether its views arrive all at once, in batches, one at a
time, or in arbitrary order (Fig. 19). Views that join continue
the running solve instead of restarting it. All four patterns
end in nearly the same accurate reconstruction of a scene
(Fig. 20).
Offline reconstruction. Figures 21 to 23 present three large
indoor scenes, where every method sees all images at once.
Solv3R reconstructs them accurately, while some existing
methods [12, 51, 88, 89, 95] misplace or disconnect parts of
them.
Streaming reconstruction.
Figure 24 presents four
more sequences streamed with the default configuration
(L8), each frame shown with its prediction at arrival.
Solv3R gives accurate trajectories, competitive with exist-
ing streaming methods [18, 21, 26, 90].
