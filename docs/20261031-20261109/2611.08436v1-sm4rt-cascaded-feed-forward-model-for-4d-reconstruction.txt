Abstract
We introduce SM4RT, a multi-task cascade feed-forward model
for dynamic scene reconstruction from RGB videos. While
existing feed-forward approaches typically address geometry
reconstruction and motion estimation separately–and often
predict motions in a single coordinate frame that must ac-
count for camera movement–SM4RT solves these tasks sequen-
tially by first reconstructing 3D geometry and then bootstrap-
ping motion estimation from the predicted structure. By ex-
pressing each pixel’s 3D motion in its corresponding camera
frame, SM4RT reduces ambiguity arising from camera motion.
To address the sparsity of real-world motion labels, SM4RT
further incorporates a weakly supervised loss that regular-
izes unlabeled point motions using labeled point positions at
the other time step. We evaluate motion estimation on three
dynamic-scene datasets–Kubric, Waymo, and Point Odyssey–
and SM4RT achieves the best performance on most metrics
compared with prior baselines. We additionally benchmark ge-
ometry reconstruction on five dynamic scene datasets and show
that SM4RT achieves competitive performance with state-of-
the-art methods. Overall, our unified framework demonstrates
that combining geometry reconstruction and motion estima-
tion in a cascade not only avoids performance trade-offs but
also enhances motion estimation through improved geometric
reasoning.

1 Introduction
Dynamic 3D scene reconstruction is fundamental to spatial
understanding of the visual world. The goal is to recover
fine-grained scene geometry and motion from RGB videos,
enabling a wide range of applications. In robotics, it supports
precise estimation of object pose, part articulation or point
deformation; in computer vision, it facilitates simulating,
novel-view rendering or editing dynamic scenes.
Simultaneously solving geometry reconstruction and mo-
tion estimation from RGB videos remains a substantial
challenge, largely due to the strong coupling between these
two tasks. Accurate motion estimation requires reliable 3D
point locations at each time step [16], while geometry recon-
struction depends on consistent multi-view correspondences
between 3D points and their 2D projections. However, in
dynamic scenes this consistency breaks down, as dynamic
motion violates the static assumptions underlying traditional
multi-view geometry [10, 18]. Consequently, geometry recon-
struction and motion estimation are often addressed separately:
the former through Structure from Motion [1, 38] and Simul-
taneous Localization and Mapping [7], which assume static
environments and ignore modeling dynamic content; and the
latter through scene flow estimation [16, 19, 36, 45] or 3D
point tracking [55, 60], leveraging estimated or sensed depth
maps to provide 3D information for video inputs.
Feed-forward frameworks [23, 28, 48, 49, 51] have re-
cently emerged as effective solutions for static 3D geometry
reconstruction. These models bypass explicit triangulation by
directly predicting 3D point positions from multi-view images,
1


typically centered at a single camera’s coordinate frame to han-
dle varying camera poses. The same architectures have been ex-
tended to dynamic scene reconstruction [10, 17, 21, 25, 41, 57],
estimating 3D positions of every observed points across time
to jointly model geometry and motion. While conceptually
simple, such direct extensions are suboptimal: predicting fu-
ture or past 3D point locations in another camera’s coordinate
frame needs to jointly account for both global camera motion
and local object motion, which is ambiguous, and therefore
increasing the complexity of dynamic scene reconstruction
task.
To address this limitation, our key insight is to disentangle
camera motion from object motion in dynamic scenes. We
make three observations: (1) motion estimation becomes
simpler when camera motion is excluded, (2) accurately es-
timating object motion helps identify static points, and (3)
camera motion naturally emerges from the multi-view geome-
try of these static points. Building on these insights, we focus
solely on modeling object motion by predicting 3D point posi-
tions across time within each input camera’s coordinate frame.
This formulation produces motion patterns that distinctly sepa-
rate static and dynamic points, enabling robust camera motion
estimation from static-point geometry predicted by existing
feed-forward models. By defining motion in this disentangled
space, we simplify dynamic scene reconstruction and improve
the accuracy and coherence of its constituent tasks.

Dynamic scene reconstruction involves reconstructing 3D
scene geometry and estimating motion from RGB videos. In
this section, we briefly introduce the definition of these tasks
and review the seminal feed-forward model–DUSt3R [51].
2.1 3D Geometry Reconstruction
The goal of 3D geometry reconstruction is to recover scene
structure and estimate camera parameters from a set of input
images. Following [51], we represent scene structure using a
point map–the 3D positions of all image pixels. Let 𝐼∈R𝐻×𝑊
denote an RGB image with height 𝐻and width 𝑊, and 𝑃(𝜋) ∈
R𝐻×𝑊×3 denote its per-pixel 3D point map expressed in coor-
dinate frame 𝜋. This formulation allows flexible representation
of 3D points in different coordinate frames. In practice, point
maps are expressed in camera coordinate frames defined by the
corresponding camera poses, which describe transformations
between the world frame and the camera frame.
Feed-forward geometry reconstruction model.
DUSt3R is
a transformer-based [46] feed-forward model that takes as in-
put two images, 𝐼1 and 𝐼2, with corresponding camera poses 𝜋1
and 𝜋2. It predicts point maps 𝑃1(𝜋1) and 𝑃2(𝜋1)–the 3D posi-
tions of pixels in both images, expressed in 𝐼1’s camera frame.
When the input order is reversed, DUSt3R produces 𝑃2(𝜋2)
and 𝑃1(𝜋2), expressed in 𝐼2’s camera frame. This dual predic-
tion enables correspondence between the same scene points
expressed in different frames, such as 𝑃1(𝜋1) vs. 𝑃1(𝜋2) or
𝑃2(𝜋2) vs. 𝑃2(𝜋1). The relative camera pose can be directly
estimated via Procrustes alignment [32] or the Perspective-n-
Point (PnP) algorithm [26].
2.2 3D Motion Estimation
The goal of motion estimation is to measure the 3D point mo-
tion from an input RGB video. Following prior work [21, 41],
2


we define point motion as the residual between pixel-wise 3D
positions across time. Let 𝑃𝑛(𝑡, 𝜋) denote the 3D positions of
pixels from the 𝑛-th video frame, as observed at time 𝑡and
expressed in coordinate frame 𝜋. This formulation generalizes
that of static-scene reconstruction, where only the current
frame is considered (𝑛= 𝑡), to also describe future and past
point maps when 𝑛< 𝑡and 𝑛> 𝑡. Therefore, we refer to
𝑃𝑛(𝑡, 𝜋) as dynamic point maps [41].
Let 𝐼1 and 𝐼2 denote two video frames captured at times
𝑡1 and 𝑡2, with corresponding camera poses 𝜋1 and 𝜋2. The
3D motion of pixels from 𝐼1 can be defined in three ways,
depending on how camera motion is included. The first is
scene flow: 𝑃1(𝑡2, 𝜋2) −𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋1), which captures both ob-
ject and camera motion. The second is cross-frame object
flow: 𝑃1(𝑡2, 𝜋2) −𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋2), which describes object motion
expressed in the second camera’s coordinate frame, and thus
requires inferring the relative camera pose. The last is object
flow: 𝑃1(𝑡2, 𝜋1) −𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋1), which ignores camera motion
entirely and represents object motion in the first camera’s coor-
dinate frame. Among these, object flow is the least ambiguous
representation, since static points yield zero motion values by
construction. Fig. 2 illustrates the difference between object
flow and scene flow.
Feed-forward dynamic scene reconstruction model.
Exist-
ing methods [10, 17, 21, 41] extend the DUSt3R architecture
for dynamic scene reconstruction by adding independent de-
coders and dense prediction (DPT) heads [37]. Given two in-
put frames, 𝐼1 and 𝐼2, these models predict per-frame dynamic
point maps–𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋1), 𝑃1(𝑡2, 𝜋1), 𝑃2(𝑡2, 𝜋1), and 𝑃2(𝑡1, 𝜋1)–
all expressed in the first camera’s coordinate frame, resulting
in a mixture of cross-frame object flow and object flow repre-
sentation.
However, such direct extensions are suboptimal. To obtain
cross-frame object flow (𝑃2(𝑡1, 𝜋1) −𝑃2(𝑡2, 𝜋1)) for image
𝐼2, these models must implicitly infer the relative camera
pose between frames. Moreover, future and past point maps
(𝑃1(𝑡2, 𝜋1) and 𝑃2(𝑡1, 𝜋1)) are predicted independently from
the current ones, which leads to spatio-temporal inconsisten-
cies in dynamic scene reconstruction.
3 Method
SM4RT is a multi-task cascade feed-forward model for dy-
namic scene reconstruction from RGB videos. As illustrated
in Fig. 3, it adopts a geometry-first cascade: dense 3D geom-
etry is reconstructed first and then used to bootstrap motion
estimation. To reduce ambiguity from camera motion, SM4RT
represents dynamics with object flow rather than motion in a
camera-entangled coordinate frame. We further introduce a
weakly supervised loss that regularizes unlabeled point mo-
tions, enabling the model to better leverage sparsely annotated
real-world data.
3.1 Multi-task Cascade Feed-Forward Model
SM4RT addresses geometry reconstruction and motion estima-
tion within a cascaded multi-task framework. It first performs
geometry reconstruction using a DUSt3R backbone, then feeds
the predicted 3D geometry into a transformer-based decoder
followed by a DPT head to estimate motion.
For geometry reconstruction, the DUSt3R backbone takes
as input an ordered pair of video frames 𝐼1 and 𝐼2 with cam-
era pose 𝜋1 and 𝜋2, captured at time 𝑡1 and 𝑡2. It extracts visual
tokens 𝐺1 and 𝐺2 from input images with ViT encoder [6], con-
textualizes these tokens via cross attention, and projects them
to point map 𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋1) and 𝑃2(𝑡2, 𝜋1) observed at the corre-
sponding time step while expressed in the 𝐼1’s camera frame.
For motion estimation, we feed the updated tokens 𝐺1 and
𝐺2 into a transformer decoder, where they cross-attend to en-
hance spatio-temporal feature representations. Because motion
estimation depends on precise 3D localization of each pixel, we
inject 3D positional information into the decoder. Specifically,
since each token corresponds to an image patch, we compute
its 3D position by averaging the predicted 3D coordinates of
pixels within that patch. Following prior work in visual cor-
respondence [30] and robotic manipulation [22], we incorpo-
rate 3D information by modulating the attention mechanism–
encouraging tokens to attend more strongly to spatially nearby
ones in 3D. We implement this via rotary positional embed-
dings [40], encoding relative 3D positions directly in the atten-
tion layers (see [40] for details).
Finally, SM4RT predicts the object flow 𝑃1(𝑡2, 𝜋1) −
𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋1) of 𝐼1’s pixels, describing their motion from time
𝑡1 to 𝑡2, by projecting token 𝐺1 through a DPT head. The
token 𝐺1 has been progressively updated through multiple
transformer decoder layers. To obtain the reverse object flow
of 𝐼2’s pixels: 𝑃2(𝑡1, 𝜋2) −𝑃2(𝑡2, 𝜋2), we simply swap the
input frame order and perform another feed-forward pass. For
brevity, we denote the predicted object flows of 𝐼1 and 𝐼2 as
𝐹1(𝑡1, 𝑡2) and 𝐹2(𝑡2, 𝑡1), respectively.

4 Experiments
4.1 Experimental Setup
Training dataset.
We train SM4RT on a diverse mix-
ture of static (WildRGBD [54], BlendedMVS [58]) and
dynamic (Waymo [42], Kubric-F [15], Spring [33] and Point
Odyssey [62]) datasets. This collection provides a wide range
of real-world, synthetic, indoor, and outdoor scenes. We
closely follow prior work [41, 51] to prepare ground-truth
point position and motion annotations. For Point Odyssey, we
annotate 2D pixels using the corresponding 3D point trajec-
tories provided by the simulator. For Waymo and Kubric-F,
motion labels are derived from the 3D bounding box poses of
each object, while 3D position labels are obtained from Lidar
measurements in Waymo and from unprojected rendered depth
maps in Kubric-F. Spring provides ground-truth disparity and
flow in adjacent frames. We extend these labels to aggregate
for long-time difference flow with consistency filtering to
avoid mismatches. For WildRGBD and BlendedMVS, po-
sition labels are derived from unprojected depth maps, and
motion labels are set to zero.
Testing dataset.
We evaluate SM4RT under the setup of mo-
tion estimation and geometry reconstruction.
For motion estimation, we consider three dynamic scene
datasets–Waymo, Kubric-F and Point Odyssey, where mod-
els are evaluated on a held-out testing set. Following [41],
we adopt three metrics to evaluate the model performance,
including (1) end-point error (EPE) of object flow, that mea-
sures the average Euclidean distance between the predicted
and ground-truth object flow, (2) relative point cloud error
𝐿rel, that measures the relative L2 error of the predicted and
ground-truth point map, and (3) relative pose transformation,
that calculates the spatial transformation needed to align
the predicted and ground-truth point map. We calculate the
relative pose via PnP over point maps 𝑃1(𝜋1), 𝑃2(𝜋1).
For geometry reconstruction, we extensively evaluate on the
held-out testing set of 5 datasets: Sintel [4], Point Odyssey [62],
Bonn [35], Kubric-F [15], and KITTI [12]. Depth estimation is
typically considered as the proxy task for evaluating the perfor-
mance of geometry reconstruction. We adopt two established
depth metrics: absolute relative error (Abs Rel) and 𝛿< 1.25
accuracy [56]. See the Appendix for detailed formulation of
each metric.
Baseline methods.
We consider the following baseline
methods: (1) MonST3R [61], that directly extends DUSt3R
to dynamic scenes. It predicts two point maps 𝑃1(𝑡1, 𝜋1)

4.2 Evaluating Motion Estimation
We evaluate on the held-out test split of Waymo, Kubric-F and
Point Odyssey dataset. On Waymo, we sample 1000 adjacent
pairs of frames from unseen clips. On Kubric, we sample 1000
image pairs of adjacent frames from the 1470 testing scenes.
On Point Odyssey, we sample 1000 image pairs within 10
frames from 109 unseen video sections.
In this experiment, we evaluate the quality of the predicted
dynamic point maps, object flows, and pose estimation. A key
distinction is that all baseline methods express the 3D positions
and motions of pixels from both 𝐼1 and 𝐼2 in the camera frame
of 𝐼1 (𝜋1). In contrast, SM4RT expresses pixels from 𝐼1 and 𝐼2
in their respective camera frames (𝜋1 and 𝜋2). For a fair com-
parison, we transform each baseline’s predictions for pixels of
𝐼2 into its own camera frame (𝜋2) using the ground-truth rel-
ative camera pose. This transformation has no effect on their
quantitative metrics, as rigid spatial transformations preserve
Euclidean distances.
We summarize our results in Table 1. Our method SM4RT
demonstrates consistently strong performance across all three
dynamic scene datasets. While achieving competitive results
in point map estimation (𝐿𝑟𝑒𝑙) against leading methods, our
approach significantly outperforms all baselines in the more
challenging tasks of object flow and relative pose estimation.
On the Waymo dataset, SM4RT reduces object flow error by
nearly 33% improvement compared to the next-best method
(0.014 EPE vs. 0.020 EPE). Similarly, on Kubric-F, our model
achieves a competitive state-of-the-art object flow EPE and the
lowest relative pose error. This trend holds on Point Odyssey,
confirming that our model’s cascaded design and motion repre-
sentation lead to a superior estimation of dynamic motion. We
also present qualitative results in Fig. 5.

4.4 Ablation study
We perform extensive ablation studies to assess the contribu-
tion of each model component of our SM4RT. The experiments
are conducted on the Waymo dataset, where all models are

Enhancing motion estimation with geometry reconstruc-
tion.
We validate the contribution of predicted 3D point
positions to motion estimation by removing their injection
into the motion estimator’s transformer decoder. Specifically,
we replace each token’s 3D position with its corresponding
2D pixel coordinates. As in DUSt3R, RoPE is applied to
encode relative positional information in the attention layers.
This modification leads to a substantial performance drop.
The model without 3D point positions exhibits 0.072 and
0.071 higher 𝐿rel for the predicted point maps of 𝐼1 and 𝐼2,
respectively. It also shows 0.033 and 0.031 higher EPE for
the corresponding object flows. These results confirm that the
predicted 3D point positions are essential for accurate motion
estimation.
Weakly-supervised Chamfer loss.
We evaluate the effec-
tiveness of the weakly-supervised loss L𝐶𝐷under sparse
motion annotations by removing it from the total loss (Eqn. 7).
The model trained without L𝐶𝐷shows a substantial degrada-

indicate that the sparsity of real-world motion labels forms a
major performance bottleneck, and incorporating the weakly-

regularizing unlabeled points.

5 Related Work
Learning based 3D reconstruction. The field of 3D scene
reconstruction has explored diverse 3D representations, in-
cluding implicit methods, meshes [2], and 3D Gaussian
Splatting [13, 53]. Parallel to these, explicit point cloud recon-
struction has seen significant advances. Foundational models
like DUSt3R [51] have revolutionized the field by creating
a unified framework for estimating depth, camera intrinsics,
and extrinsics from stereo views. Subsequent works such as
MASt3R[28] further enable robust 3D image matching, and
several other works based on DUSt3R [47, 49, 50] extend
the model to perform global scene reconstruction from video.
Our work harnesses DUSt3R’s proven strengths in 3D scene
reconstruction and alignment as a foundation, allowing our
model to perform 4D sceneflow estimation with point cloud
reconstructions.
4D reconstruction. Beyond static 3D, 4D reconstruction aims
to model temporal dynamics. While some methods extend 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) [52], a key approach is tracking
point correspondences over time. Earlier methods such as
MonST3R[61] combined depth with optical flow trackers,
such as RAFT [44, 45], to reconstruct 3D point movement.
More recently, end-to-end networks inspired by DUSt3R [51]
directly predict point clouds and their motion.
A critical challenge in this area is the disentanglement of
object motion from camera motion. Several works have ad-
dressed this: Monst3R [61] introduced static mask prediction,
integrating RAFT to enhance static background matching.
DynaDUSt3R [21] attempts to directly predict object motion
while keeping 3D reconstruction unchanged. Dynamic Point
Maps (DPM) [41] address this by introducing time invari-
ance as well as viewpoint invariance. However, by predicting
motion into a single coordinate frame, these methods often
struggle to fully separate object motion from camera motion.
Instead, our model guarantees that object flow is predicted
from the same frame as the point map. Additionally, we lever-
age positional information from predicted point maps to aid
downstream decoder blocks in predicting displacement from
the original point cloud, simplifying the motion estimation
task.

6 Summary
This paper introduces SM4RT, a multi-task cascade feed-
forward model for dynamic scene reconstruction from RGB
videos. The model first reconstructs 3D geometry and sub-
sequently bootstraps motion estimation from the predicted
structure. By expressing 3D motion within each pixel’s cor-
responding camera frame, SM4RT effectively disentangles
object motion from the ambiguity of camera movement. The
framework also incorporates a weakly-supervised loss to
address the common challenge of sparse motion labels in
real-world data.
We evaluated SM4RT on multiple dynamic-scene datasets,
where it consistently outperformed all baselines for mo-
tion estimation tasks. Furthermore, it achieved competitive
performance with state-of-the-art methods for geometry
reconstruction. These results demonstrate that a cascaded,
geometry-informed approach enhances motion estimation
accuracy without compromising the performance of either
task, leading to improved overall geometric reasoning.
Limitation & Future work.
Our SM4RT operates on a pair
of video frames and predicts the motion between them, but ex-
tending this framework to long-horizon videos remains a sig-
nificant challenge. In addition, SM4RT currently relies on the
DUSt3R backbone for geometry reconstruction, which can de-
grade in the presence of dynamic object motion. Developing
methods that maintain robust geometry reconstruction in dy-
namic scenes is an important direction for future work. We
agree that multi-frame reconstruction is an important exten-
sion. We deliberately study the two-frame setting to isolate
the contribution of geometry priors to dynamic reconstruction.
Importantly, our cascaded design is not tied to a specific back-
bone or temporal window, and can serve as a plug-and-play dy-
namic module for multi-frame models such as VGGT [49] and
DA3 [31]. Their temporally aggregated geometry can provide
stronger correspondence priors, while our object-flow module
separates camera-induced motion from independently moving
regions. Moreover, the 3D RoPE formulation encodes geome-
try in a spatially consistent coordinate system and can be ex-
tended with temporal indexing to support correspondence rea-
soning across longer sequences.

A.2 Pose Estimation
To retrieve the relative pose between two frames, we perform
PnP RANSAC [11, 27] between the reconstructed point clouds
𝑃𝑖(𝑡𝑖, 𝜋𝑖) and 𝑃𝑖(𝑡𝑖, 𝜋𝑗). A critical factor for accurate pose esti-
mation is reference point selection, as static points yield more
robust and precise camera motion estimates. We derive a static
object mask from the confidence scores of the flow prediction
head, based on the observation that confidence scores in static
regions (where the flow head is expected to output zero) are
significantly higher than in dynamic regions. Specifically, we
generate the static mask by applying a threshold to the confi-
dence score map; in this work, we set the threshold as conf ≥7.
Finally, we apply RANSAC within this masked static region to
obtain the relative pose between the two frames.

motion and provides a structured target: static regions should
have near-zero motion, while independently moving objects
retain coherent motion. This is also consistent with robotics
works using object-centric 3D motion or articulation flow as

A.4 Voxelization for Chamfer Distance Loss
To balance accurate nearest neighbor search with computa-
tional efficiency, we discretize the coordinate space into grids
with a resolution of 1000 per axis, resulting in 𝑁= 10003
voxels. Since camera movement results in partial overlap
between input point clouds, we restrict the Chamfer distance
loss to be computer only on intersecting regions. These are
efficiently identified via voxel occupancy intersection. Finally,
we employ a sigmoid scheduler to gradually increase the
weight of the Chamfer distance loss LCD from 0 to 4.
A.5 Pretrained Weight Setup
Instead of training from scratch, SM4RT is fine-tuned from
the MASt3R [29] checkpoint. We initialize the encoder, de-
coders, and depth prediction heads with their respective pre-
trained weights. Furthermore, we initialize the flow prediction
head using the pretrained weights of the depth prediction head.
During training, the weights of the encoder are frozen, while
all other components remain trainable.
A.6 Details about Training Datasets
Obtaining ground truth object flow labels across arbitrary
frame intervals is challenging when point trajectories in world
coordinates are unavailable. For Kubric, we modify the pre-
processing pipeline in TapVid3D [24] to transform ground
truth object poses into relative poses across different times-
tamps, thereby generating pseudo ground truth data for flow
supervision. For Waymo, we derive the relative object pose by
aligning the eight vertices of the ground truth bounding boxes.
Finally, for Spring, we aggregated the optical flow on con-
secutive frames with a cycle consistency filtering on selected
clips where the cameras stay still. The estimated pose is then
applied to points within the corresponding bounding boxes to
generate object flow data. The properties of the datasets used
for training are listed in Table 4.
