Abstract
In human-robot collaboration, natural language lets people
direct a humanoid’s whole-body movements without pro-
gramming joint trajectories. A common approach generates
a kinematic reference from an instruction and relies on a
tracking controller to execute it. However, as the controller
maintains balance under contact constraints, the executed
motion can depart from the reference in ways that change
the action it conveys. Our central insight is that semantic
alignment must be evaluated directly on the complete motion
the robot physically executes. Building on this insight, we
introduce SemPhys, a framework that trains motion genera-
tors using semantic feedback from simulated executions. A
differentiable world model and a fixed tracking controller
predict a short segment of a recorded execution. SemPhys
inserts this prediction at its original position, scores the
complete trajectory against the instruction, and backprop-
agates through the predicted segment. A paired anchoring
loss stabilizes adaptation by keeping each generated refer-
ence close to its counterpart from the initial generator. On
BONES-SEED, SemPhys largely preserves instruction re-
trieval accuracy from reference to execution and achieves
the highest retrieval accuracy on executed motions among
the evaluated methods.

1. Introduction
Language lets a person direct a humanoid through a sequence
such as turning and then walking forward, leaving the robot
to coordinate its joints and maintain balance. For the turn-
and-walk instruction in Fig. 1, both parts matter: a robot that
starts walking before it finishes the turn travels in a different
direction, even if each individual step is stable.
Text-to-motion generators produce reference trajecto-
ries [10, 33, 42], which whole-body controllers track under
contact and balance constraints [21, 22, 32]. Execution-
aware methods use tracking rewards, candidate ranking, or
preferences to improve these references [3, 31, 41].
A reference can depict the requested action clearly while
leaving the controller to shorten a turn or interrupt a reach
to recover balance. Such corrections change the motion
that a person watching the robot would associate with the
instruction. On BONES-SEED [2], MotionGPT3 retrieves
the correct instruction for 74.08% of its references but only
58.18% of their executions. A local correction also depends
on the surrounding action: the heading at the end of a turn
determines where the next steps lead.
To bridge this gap between kinematic generation and
physical execution, SemPhys scores the complete execution
and backpropagates through a short segment predicted by a
differentiable world model and the fixed tracking controller.
The predicted poses replace the corresponding portion of a
recorded simulator rollout. The prefix tells the scorer how
the robot entered the movement, and the suffix shows what
followed. For a turn followed by a walk, the scorer can there-
fore interpret the turn in light of the subsequent steps, while
the world model supplies derivatives only through the se-
lected segment. Pairing each update with the initial generator
output for the same text and noise stabilizes adaptation.
Extensive evaluations on the BONES-SEED [2] and real
Unitree G1 hardware demonstrate that SemPhys achieves su-
perior executed R@1 of 79.62%—surpassing the strongest
baseline (MDM) by 19.52 percentage points—with a mini-
mal sustained failure rate of 0.25‰ on upright tasks, while
maintaining robust performance across diverse dynamic
perturbations. Real-world deployments confirm that our
simulation-trained controller successfully executes complex
sequential commands like turns and forward locomotion.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Semantic supervision on executed actions: We propose
training the generator directly on the semantic match be-
tween the instruction and the actual motion produced
by the tracking controller, outperforming traditional
generation-space scoring.
• A local world-model gradient for full actions: We in-
troduce a short closed-loop prediction embedded within
recorded trajectories to backpropagate global semantic
loss, preserving global motion context better than static
endpoint context filling.
• Paired prior anchoring for stable adaptation: By bind-
ing generator updates to initial responses under identical
instructions and noise, our method substantially reduces
training variance and physical execution failures.
2. Related work
Execution-aware motion generation.
RobotMDM pre-
dicts tracking rewards [31].
TeXedo and concurrent
physics-in-the-loop selection rank candidate motions [3, 23],
while PhysMoDPO learns preferences from tracked execu-
tions [41]. Other methods impose physical constraints during
denoising or refinement [12, 20, 31, 38]. For a compound in-
struction, the semantic consequence of a tracking correction
may emerge later in the movement: a shortened turn changes
the direction of the walk that follows. SemPhys therefore
differentiates the instruction score through a local prediction
of the tracking loop.
Differentiable dynamics and generative alignment.
Learned dynamics and short differentiable rollouts provide
gradients for policy updates [11, 15, 36]. Concurrent Su-
perMimic backpropagates tracking rewards through a world
model to train a whole-body controller [21]. We hold the
controller fixed and use execution gradients to update the
text-conditioned generator. The distinction matters at de-
ployment. The world model supplies derivatives during
training, while the tracking policy executes the resulting ref-
erences on the robot. This connects reward-based generative
alignment [1, 5, 24, 30, 34] and differentiable motion con-
trol [6, 37] to sequence-level text–motion matching [8, 29].
Kinematic text-to-motion and whole-body control.
Text-
to-motion models generate continuous poses [8, 28, 33, 40],
denoise motion latents [4], or model discrete motion to-
kens [9, 10, 19, 39, 42]. In physics-based control, AMP
learns motion priors and ASE learns reusable skills [26, 27].
Tracking policies execute reference poses [22, 25, 32]; hu-
manoid systems extend this interface to teleoperation [7, 13]
and expressive whole-body tracking on hardware [14, 17].

3.2. Executed semantic alignment
Semantic matching.
To evaluate semantic alignment,
frozen text and motion encoders Et, Em project instructions
and executed trajectories into a shared latent space [8, 29].
A differentiable conversion operator C maps raw robot poses
into the motion encoder’s input representation:
hc = Et(c),
hY = Em(C(Y )).
(5)
While the generator conditions on e(c), the training reward
is derived from the distance between hc and hY . We define
the semantic mismatch d and reward rsem as:
d(c, Y ) = ∥hY −hc∥2,
rsem(c, Y ) = −d(c, Y ). (6)
We refer to this matching distance as MM. Since both en-
coders yield unit-normalized embeddings, the squared dis-
tance simplifies to d2 = 2 −2 cos(hY , hc). This sequence-
level scoring mechanism robustly accommodates temporal
variations and pose differences that convey identical seman-
tic intent.

The mean operator aggregates across both temporal frames

A single instruction can admit several valid motions. If

and noise gives the anchor a specific reference: the motion
the initial generator would have produced for this sample.
The semantic loss rewards changes that improve the match
after execution, while the anchor penalizes how far those

From training to deployment.
At deployment, the gen-
erator outputs a motion reference from the instruction and
event plan, and the tracking controller follows it using robot
feedback. Since the world model and semantic matcher are
used exclusively during training, the robot can execute the
adapted generator’s references in real time without online
rollouts or score computations.
4. Experiments
We evaluate SemPhys on large-scale humanoid motion
benchmarks, severe dynamics perturbations, and real-world
Unitree G1 hardware to answer three key questions: (1) Does
execution-space supervision improve semantic fidelity and
physical stability over current methods? (2) How robust is
SemPhys against post-tracking semantic drift and dynamics
shifts? (3) How do individual components and surrogate
gradients contribute to learning efficacy?
4.1. Experimental setup
Dataset.
We evaluate on the processed BONES-SEED
benchmark [2], whose test split comprises 13,302 motion
clips, including 13,082 upright tasks used to measure sus-
tained physical failure.
Baselines.
To ensure fair comparison, the identical frozen
tracking controller executes every method’s generated refer-
ences on the simulated Unitree G1. ECHO, OMG, TeXedo,
and TextOp are evaluated using their official G1 check-
points [3, 16, 18, 35], while MotionGPT3, MDM, and Mo-
Mask are trained on the same processed G1 dataset as Sem-
Phys [10, 33, 42]. Each generator predicts segment durations
or applies its native stopping rule, and test clips are reassem-
bled using source segmentation metadata.
Evaluation metrics.
The frozen GloVe matcher supplies
the training reward and scores all executions. MM measures
Euclidean text–motion embedding distance while R@k mea-
sures top-k instruction retrieval over 20 shared pools of 32
candidates. FID compares executed and ground-truth mo-
tion feature distributions. Fail counts sustained failures per
thousand upright tasks.
Table 1 averages objective metrics over three generation
seeds per fixed checkpoint. In Fig. 7, each adapted variant
uses the same initial checkpoint and three training seeds.
Generation seed 42 is fixed for evaluation, including the
single evaluation of the initial checkpoint.

Stabilizing adaptation via paired prior anchoring.
Paired anchoring raises mean executed R@1 from 74.61% to
79.30% and reduces its across-seed standard deviation from

Necessity of global temporal context.
Evaluating the lo-
cal differentiable window inside its surrounding episode con-
text is essential for sequence-level semantic scoring (Fig. 8).
Under matched update counts, nominal simulator transitions,
and identical 300-frame input lengths, embedding the 32-
step predicted window within its original recorded prefix
and suffix boosts executed R@1 from 68.07% (with static
endpoint padding) to 79.31%—an 11.24-point improvement
(simultaneous 95% interval [4.52, 17.96]).
Scoring the window alone yields 64.88% R@1. Endpoint
padding restores the input length, while the ordered context
restores the movement leading into and out of the window.

Fidelity of surrogate gradients in full simulation.
We
test whether a generator update computed through the local
world model changes the score of a fresh, complete simu-
lator execution in the predicted direction. Holding instruc-
tions and noise fixed, we step along the surrogate gradient
direction (10−4 step size) and replay the full trajectory in
MuJoCo across 32 validation prompts (Fig. 9). Predicted
and replayed changes in MM agree in sign for 102 of 112
pairs above replay tolerance (91.1%). Furthermore, replayed
MM improves in 107 of 127 finite trials (84.3%), compared
to 48.8% for random perturbations of equal magnitude.
5. Conclusion
SemPhys trains a text-to-motion generator to preserve the
meaning of an instruction through humanoid execution. A
differentiable world model lets the generator update a lo-
cal motion segment using a semantic score for the full
8


trajectory. Paired anchoring keeps each update close to
the initial generator’s response to the same instruction
and noise. On BONES-SEED, the full system achieves
79.62% executed R@1, exceeding MDM by 19.52 percent-
age points, with 0.25 sustained failures per thousand upright
tasks.
