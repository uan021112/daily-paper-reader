DiffSimp: Differentiable Mesh Simplification for
Gradient-Based Low-Poly Modeling
Seonghun Oh

Abstract
Differentiable mesh extraction enables gradient-based
optimization of detailed 3D geometry across diverse
tasks.
However, compact meshes are commonly ob-
tained through post-processing simplification,
leaving
simplification-induced errors outside upstream optimiza-
tion. We introduce DIFFSIMP, a training-free, drop-in dif-
ferentiable mesh simplification layer built on QEM-based
parallel edge collapse, enabling existing geometry opti-
mization pipelines to optimize their final simplified meshes.
We formulate simplification under a fixed collapse history
as a locally differentiable collapse map, enabling gradient
propagation without relaxing discrete collapse decisions.
We combine fast, high-quality parallel simplification with a
factorized backward formulation that exploits differentiable
vertex quadrics and quadric reduction forests for efficient
gradient propagation. Experiments on low-poly mesh re-
construction and inverse rendering demonstrate improved
fidelity over post-processing simplification, with additional
gains from gradient propagation that increase under tighter
vertex budgets. DIFFSIMP supports diverse geometry op-
timization frontends, with 1.00–1.25× the end-to-end run-
time of unsimplified baselines in evaluated pipelines.
1. Introduction
Triangle meshes remain a fundamental surface represen-
tation across computer graphics, 3D vision, and robotics.
Recent advances in differentiable mesh extraction [1, 44,
46, 47, 56] have enabled gradient-based geometry opti-
mization by propagating gradients from mesh-based objec-
tives upstream, supporting inverse rendering [24, 35, 59],
scene reconstruction [10, 19], and generative 3D model-
ing [2, 13, 57].
However, these pipelines often produce
dense meshes to capture fine geometric structures, while
downstream applications may require compact meshes.
A straightforward way to obtain compact meshes is to
reduce the resolution of differentiable mesh extraction, but
this limits the recovery of fine geometric details during
optimization.
Instead, a common strategy is to recon-
struct or generate sufficiently detailed meshes, followed by
post-processing simplification to meet downstream com-
plexity requirements.
Among mesh simplification meth-
ods, quadric error metric (QEM) edge collapse [16, 17] has
become a practical off-the-shelf choice [27, 31]. It natu-
rally adapts mesh complexity to local geometry, enabling
aggressive simplification while limiting geometric approx-
imation error, without requiring separate iterative fitting as
in variational approaches [9, 58] or pretrained simplification
models [42]. These advantages have made QEM a common
post-processing step in modern 3D pipelines [7, 15, 52, 55].
However, post-processing mesh simplification decou-
ples geometry optimization from the final simplified mesh.
While differentiable mesh extraction enables gradient prop-
agation from mesh-based objectives upstream, these ob-
jectives are typically evaluated on detailed intermediate
meshes rather than the simplified meshes ultimately de-
ployed. This mismatch becomes particularly consequential
under aggressive simplification, where even QEM can in-
troduce substantial geometric distortion as fine structures
are approximated by increasingly coarse meshes.
These
simplification-induced errors provide no gradient signal to
the upstream optimizer, preventing it from directly compen-
sating for them to improve the final deployed mesh.
To address this optimization–deployment mismatch, we
seek to integrate mesh simplification as a differentiable
layer into gradient-based geometry optimization pipelines.
For practical integration, we identify three design priorities.
First, the layer should be training-free and mesh-native,
independent of learned simplification priors and specific
upstream representations, enabling its use across diverse
pipelines and downstream objectives.
Second, high for-
ward simplification quality must be maintained. At less re-
strictive budgets, simplification typically introduces smaller
geometric errors, leaving limited room for gradient-based
compensation and making forward quality particularly im-
portant. Under tighter budgets, high-quality forward sim-
plification and gradient-based compensation become com-

forward and backward execution is essential, as simplifica-

To meet these priorities, we introduce DIFFSIMP, a
training-free, drop-in differentiable mesh simplification
layer built on QEM-based parallel edge collapse, enabling
existing gradient-based geometry optimization pipelines to
optimize through their simplified meshes. Although dis-
crete edge-collapse decisions prevent global differentiabil-
ity, we exploit the recorded collapse history to express sim-
plification as a locally differentiable collapse map, enabling
gradient propagation without relaxing these decisions. To
achieve fast, high-quality forward simplification, we extend
the parallel edge-collapse scheme of PaMO [38], improving
simplification quality while retaining efficient execution.
For efficient backward propagation, we propose a factor-
ized differentiation scheme that treats vertex quadrics as dif-
ferentiable intermediates, enabling parallel collapse-local
vector–Jacobian products (VJPs), and exploits the quadric
reduction-forest structure to avoid redundant face-level dif-
ferentiation. We implement both operations in CUDA and
introduce a progressive vertex-budget schedule for integra-
tion into iterative optimization.
Experiments on low-poly mesh reconstruction and in-
verse rendering demonstrate that optimizing through DIFF-
SIMP improves the fidelity of final simplified meshes over
post-processing in both tasks. We also show that gradient
propagation provides additional gains that increase under
tighter vertex budgets, as upstream optimization compen-
sates for simplification-induced errors. We further demon-
strate compatibility with diverse existing gradient-based ge-
ometry optimization pipelines.
These improvements are
achieved with only 1.00–1.25× the end-to-end optimization
time of the corresponding unsimplified pipelines.
2. Related Work
2.1. Gradient-Based Mesh Optimization
Differentiable mesh extraction [1, 20, 21, 44, 46, 47, 56]
and differentiable mesh representations [43, 49, 50] en-
able upstream gradient propagation from mesh-based ob-
jectives, supporting diverse downstream applications [10,
13, 19, 35, 37, 57, 59].
However, extracted mesh com-
plexity remains tied to the underlying extraction structure.
Even adaptive extraction methods [1, 47] adjust spatial sam-
pling and extraction structures rather than performing ag-
gressive, geometry-adaptive mesh simplification. Gradient-
based low-poly mesh optimization has also been explored
through geometry and appearance refinement [7, 23], joint
base-mesh and displacement optimization [11], and adap-
tive remeshing or topology updates [12, 14, 39]. However,
these approaches either optimize compact meshes from
given or reconstructed geometry without propagating gra-
dients through simplification to upstream representations,
or adapt mesh connectivity within dedicated optimization
pipelines. In contrast, our reusable differentiable mesh sim-
plification layer propagates gradients from objectives on
aggressively simplified meshes to upstream representations
without redesigning existing optimization pipelines.
2.2. Mesh Simplification
Mesh simplification has been extensively studied through
vertex clustering [45], variational approximation [9, 58],
remeshing [6, 53, 54], and iterative edge collapse [25, 30].
These training-free, mesh-native methods are widely used
as post-processing in modern 3D pipelines. Among these,
quadric error metric (QEM) edge collapse [16, 17] has
become a standard approach to mesh simplification.
To
overcome the computational cost of sequential greedy edge
collapse, parallel methods [38, 40] accelerate simplifica-
tion while maintaining competitive geometric quality, albeit
with some loss relative to sequential QEM. However, these
methods do not propagate gradients through collapse oper-
ations, leaving simplification largely as post-processing in
gradient-based pipelines. Building on PaMO [38], we com-
bine the geometric adaptivity of QEM and the efficiency
of parallel execution with fixed-history differentiation, en-
abling their use in gradient-based optimization. We further
improve parallel simplification quality to better preserve ge-
ometric fidelity under aggressive simplification while re-
taining efficient execution.
2.3. Learning-Based Low-Poly Modeling
Learning-based approaches to mesh reduction and low-poly
modeling include task-driven mesh pooling [22], learned
simplification operators [42], learned geometric representa-
tions [33], progressive mesh representations [3, 5, 28], and
generative mesh priors [4, 29, 32, 48, 51]. While these ap-
proaches support mesh reduction and low-poly modeling,
they also depend on learned parameters and training dis-
tributions. Notably, Neural Mesh Simplification [42] en-
ables differentiable simplification through learned vertex
sampling and connectivity prediction, but remains depen-
dent on a pretrained network. In contrast, our training-free
differentiable layer retains the geometric adaptivity of QEM
without relying on learned simplification priors.

3.2. Parallel Edge Collapse
Parallel edge collapse contracts multiple non-conflicting
edges in a single round. PaMO [38] enables this through
independent-region selection. For each edge e = (i, j), let
N1(e) = N1(vi) ∪N1(vj) denote its 1-ring face neigh-
borhood, where N1(vi) contains the faces incident to vi.
Each edge propagates its collapse cost Ce to all faces in
N1(e), and each face selects a single minimum-cost can-
didate.
An edge e is selected if it is the minimum-cost
candidate at every face in N1(e). Since collapsing e only
modifies mesh geometry and topology within N1(e), this
neighborhood defines its independent region. As each face
selects a single candidate, the independent regions are face-
disjoint, enabling conflict-free parallel collapses. In each
round, selected edges satisfying Ce ≤τ are collapsed si-
multaneously, where τ is the cost threshold. While improv-
ing efficiency, parallel execution may compromise approxi-
mation quality relative to sequential QEM simplification.

4.1. Forward Simplification
For forward simplification, we extend the parallel edge-

5. Experiments
We evaluate DIFFSIMP on low-poly mesh reconstruction
and inverse rendering. In both tasks, we follow the progres-
sive vertex-budget schedule in Sec. 4.4, with warmup dura-
tion tw = 0.2T and budget-reduction duration tr = 0.45T,
where T is the total number of optimization iterations. All
experiments are conducted on a single RTX 5090. For de-
tailed experimental settings, see Appendix A.

Experimental Setup.
We use 79 shapes from Myles et
al. [36], excluding non-watertight and very thin shapes [47].

also compare against CWF [58], which optimizes simplified
geometry, and TriFlow [29], a learning-based method. Un-
like post-processing, we integrate DIFFSIMP into the base

5.3. Method Analysis
Effectiveness of Simplification Gradients.
We evaluate
the effect of our gradients using the base reconstruction
pipeline in Sec. 5.1 at K = 500.
We compare against
Forward-only post-processing and three in-loop variants
sharing our forward simplifier: Survivor grad propagates
each output gradient only to its surviving input vertex,
Cluster grad distributes it equally among all input vertices
merged into that output vertex, and W/o quadric grad treats
quadrics as constants while retaining direct vertex-position
gradients. Following our progressive vertex-budget sched-
ule, we measure optimization progress over three inter-
vals: Ramp (iterations 1000–3250, budget reduction), Hold
(3250–5000, fixed target budget), and Overall (1000–5000).
At iterations 1000, 3250, and 5000, we evaluate rendering
losses against ground-truth renderings using the unsimpli-
fied mesh (Raw) and its simplified version at K = 500 (De-
ployed), regardless of the current vertex budget. We report
relative changes ∆L/L = (Lend −Lstart)/Lstart.
Table 5 shows that all in-loop variants improve CD over
Forward-only, while Cluster grad and Ours also improve
F1, suggesting that optimizing through simplification can
improve geometric fidelity. Cluster grad makes substan-
tial progress during Ramp but largely stalls during Hold.
Survivor grad yields limited gains, while removing quadric
gradients worsens the deployed objective. In contrast, our
method continues to reduce the deployed objective, achiev-
ing the greatest overall improvement and best geometric fi-
delity. These results highlight the importance of differen-
tiating the geometric dependencies of simplification rather
than relying on surrogate gradient propagation. Moreover,
gradient-based optimization remains effective even as col-
lapse histories are reselected at every iteration.
The raw and deployed objectives further expose the
optimization–deployment mismatch. Forward-only reduces
the raw objective without improving the deployed objective,
whereas our method reduces the deployed objective even as

6. Conclusion
We present DIFFSIMP, integrating QEM-based mesh sim-
plification into gradient-based geometry optimization as a
differentiable layer.
By differentiating the collapse map
with the recorded collapse history fixed, our method enables
upstream optimization to compensate for simplification-
induced errors. Combining our improved forward simplifier
with a factorized backward scheme, DIFFSIMP outperforms
the evaluated post-processing simplification baselines in
low-poly reconstruction and inverse rendering, with modest
runtime and memory overhead. We further show that gra-
dient propagation provides increasing gains under tighter
vertex budgets, where simplification introduces larger ge-
ometric errors. These improvements extend across diverse
geometry optimization frontends, demonstrating the effec-
tiveness of optimizing through DIFFSIMP rather than apply-
ing simplification as post-processing.

A. Experimental Details
A.1. Low-Poly Mesh Reconstruction
For the low-poly mesh reconstruction experiments in Sec. 5.1, we build on the official FlexiCubes implementation, optimizing
a randomly initialized SDF at grid resolution n = 128 for T = 5000 iterations using multi-view silhouette, depth, and normal
supervision without ground-truth SDF. Our reconstruction objective is
L = λmaskLmask + λdepthLdepth + λnormalLnormal + λdevLdev + λsignLsign,
(S1)
where Ldev and Lsign are the FlexiCubes deformation and sign regularizers. We set λmask = 5, λdepth = 400, λnormal = 1,
and λdev = 1. Following the official FlexiCubes implementation, we linearly decay λsign from 0.2 to 0.01 over the first 0.25T
iterations. The other reconstruction pipelines in Table 3 use the same multi-view supervision scheme with frontend-specific
settings. DiffMC follows our FlexiCubes reconstruction setup without Ldev. DMTet also omits Ldev, but uses λmask = 10,
λdepth = 250, and λnormal = 1, while retaining the same sign regularization schedule. Large Steps uses the same rendering
loss weights as DMTet, without deformation or sign regularization. Within each frontend, our method and the post-processing
baselines share the same base reconstruction settings.

C.2.1. Pass Count and Quadric Reduction-Forest Efficiency
Our factorized backward substantially reduces computation within each pass by propagating quadric gradients through the
reduction forest, allowing face quadrics to be differentiated only once per pass across multiple collapse rounds. As shown

achieves 5.5–8.8× faster backward execution, demonstrating the efficiency of reusing quadrics across collapse rounds. Note
that the every-round variant still computes collapse-local VJPs in parallel, as described in Sec. 4.3.

C.2.3. Gradient Correctness
We validate the factorized CUDA backward pass of DIFFSIMP against a PyTorch autograd replay of the same recorded
collapse history. We randomly select ten of the 79 original meshes and perform a single forward and backward evaluation per
mesh at K = 1000, without optimization. For each mesh, we define a random linear loss on the simplified vertex positions.
Let G and GAD64 denote the input-vertex gradients computed by our double-precision CUDA backward and double-precision
PyTorch autograd, respectively. We measure the relative L2 error ∥G −GAD64∥2/∥GAD64∥2 over flattened vertex gradients.
The mean and maximum relative L2 errors are 4.7×10−12 and 2.5×10−11, respectively, demonstrating near-exact numerical
agreement between our CUDA backward and the autograd reference.

D.2. Scene Reconstruction with ExMesh
To evaluate the generality of DIFFSIMP across inverse-rendering pipelines, we integrate it into ExMesh and evaluate 15 scans
from the DTU dataset at K = 1000, using the authors’ released implementation and experimental settings. ExMesh jointly
optimizes explicit mesh geometry and UV textures with adaptive topology updates. We retain its initialization, loss functions,
per-scan loss weights, and 10,000-iteration optimization. We follow the same progressive vertex-budget schedule as in Sec. 5,
with tw = 0.2T and tr = 0.45T. Following ExMesh’s atlas-rebuilding schedule, we rebuild the simplified mesh’s UV atlas
19


at iterations 2000, 4000, and 6000, and once after training. Between these updates, UV correspondence is maintained through
closest-surface texture mapping. Unlike nvdiffrec, ExMesh has no separate texture-refinement stage after simplification. We
use the final dense textured mesh from the unmodified ExMesh pipeline as Reference. We therefore compare against TQEM
and ADS as texture-aware post-processing baselines, both applied to this Reference. For ADS, we follow the refinement
settings in Sec. A.2.

among the simplified methods. Fig. S5 further illustrates better preservation of planar surfaces and sharp geometric features.

E. Limitations and Future Work
Although DIFFSIMP is a training-free, drop-in differentiable mesh simplification layer, its effectiveness depends on the
quality of intermediate meshes produced during optimization. When upstream mesh generators produce fragmented geometry
or interior shell artifacts, applying simplification before these defects are resolved can exacerbate these issues and may lead
to optimization failures. Under aggressive simplification, the many-to-one nature of the collapse map can leave parts of the
intermediate geometry weakly constrained, potentially producing floating artifacts.
Future work.
Our inverse-rendering experiments demonstrate joint optimization of geometry and appearance. However,
UV correspondence is handled separately from our simplifier. Extending our geometry-based forward simplifier with UV-
aware collapse costs and seam-aware attribute propagation could reduce reliance on separate UV processing and facilitate
integration into a broader range of textured-mesh optimization pipelines.
