Abstract
Reconstructing hand–object interactions is an important
task, and achieving it with a single camera makes large-
scale capture practical but challenging due to heavy oc-
clusions. We introduce HOForce to track hands and ob-
jects, and further recover interaction forces from single-
view RGB-D video and known object properties. For two-
hand manipulation, we jointly model fingertip forces of both
hands and the contact forces between objects when two ob-
jects are presented. The reconstructed forces also improve
motion tracking by giving pose constraints in the occluded
regions. HOForce recovers hand and object motion more
accurately on DexYCB and TACO and produces less hand–

1. Introduction
Reconstructing motion and contact forces of hand–object
interactions is an important task in computer vision, graph-
ics, and embodied AI. It reveals how people grasp and
manipulate objects, providing motion for character anima-
tion and demonstrations for robot learning, and the contact
forces explain how the fingers support, turn, or push an ob-
ject to produce that motion. Achieving this using a single
1
arXiv:2611.23871v1  [cs.CV]  18 Nov 2026


RGB-D camera makes it practical to record at scale in ev-
eryday settings without installing and calibrating a multi-
camera rig.
A single view leaves an interaction underdetermined in
two major aspects. First, large occlusions caused by single-
view capture involve strong ambiguities such that several
hand poses can explain the same visible surface while plac-
ing the fingertips at different contacts. Second, the camera
does not measure the forces at those contacts. Tactile sen-
sors provide direct measurements but require instrumented
capture. Visual pressure predictors [17, 46] estimate nor-
mal loads, leaving the tangential forces that resist slip un-
resolved. Physics-based methods use simulated motion to
refine poses [14] or infer fingertip forces from object dy-
namics [13], but when two hands manipulate two objects in
contact, they cannot jointly recover the forces exerted by the
fingertips and those transmitted between the objects. Fitting
each object independently can therefore misattribute inter-
object forces to the fingers.
We introduce HOForce, an inverse dynamics method for
reconstructing hand–object interactions. Given single-view
RGB-D video and known object shape, weight, and friction,
it uses the observed motion to infer where the fingers make
contact and what forces they apply. When objects touch, the
solver also accounts for the forces they exert on each other.
The estimated forces in turn help refine the hand pose.
To recover the forces, we simulate the reconstructed
scene in MuJoCo [36], which resolves inter-object and sup-
port reactions. We fit fingertip forces to the reconstructed
trajectories by locally linearizing the motion error and eval-
uating each update in simulation. With these forces fixed,
we refine contact locations and poses, penalizing gaps more
strongly at contacts that carry larger loads.
Alternating
these updates yields forces that can be reapplied to repro-
duce the manipulation.
This inverse problem requires an accurate motion esti-
mate to anchor the solution to the video. We initialize hand
and object poses from RGB-D observations and instance
masks, compensate for fast object motion, and re-register
objects after tracking loss. We then jointly refine hand and
object trajectories across frames. Distance-based penetra-
tion penalties [1, 3] can move a finger toward the wrong
face of a thin wall. We use the finger bone axis to identify
the contact side and guide the penetration correction.
Overall, our main contributions are threefold:
• We propose a single-view RGB-D framework that re-
covers hand and object motion together with interaction
forces, including bimanual manipulation with load trans-
fer between objects.
• We couple force estimation with contact and pose refine-
ment, using the dynamics of interacting objects to con-
strain fingertip forces and improve visually ambiguous
grasps.
• We demonstrate improved motion reconstruction and in-
teraction geometry on DexYCB and TACO. We validate
the recovered fingertip forces against tactile measure-
ments and by replaying object motion in simulation.
2. Related Work
2.1. Visual Hand-Object Reconstruction
Object pose estimation and tracking.
RGB-D methods
estimate rigid poses by combining appearance and geome-
try [34, 39]. FoundationPose [41] generalizes pose estima-
tion and tracking to novel objects given a CAD model or ref-
erence images, using rendered observations for learned pose
refinement and hypothesis scoring. We initialize object mo-
tion with its model-based tracker. Beyond known meshes,
BundleSDF [40] jointly recovers shape and motion, while
Point2Pose [21] uses long-range point correspondences for
tracking and recovery after occlusion.
Hand reconstruction.
Image-based methods recover ar-
ticulation and shape through parametric models or direct
mesh regression [2, 20, 31]. HaMeR [26] improves mesh
estimation through a large transformer trained on diverse
hand images. WiLoR [29] integrates hand localization with
MANO reconstruction and refines mesh alignment using lo-
cal image features. WiLoR supplies our initial hand articu-
lation. HaWoR and Dyn-HaMR further recover global hand
motion by accounting for camera movement and temporal
continuity [42, 45].
Joint
hand–object
reconstruction.
Early
methods
jointly fit hands and objects using geometric alignment
and interaction constraints in RGB-D [10, 33, 37, 38, 44]
or RGB video [12].
HOTrack [6] combines point-cloud
hand tracking with SDF-based object tracking and in-
teraction refinement.
Learning-based approaches exploit
joint shape prediction, contact distributions, or implicit
geometric cues [9, 11, 30].
More recently, HOLD and
EasyHOI reconstruct unknown object geometry together
with hand–object configurations [8, 24], while AGILE [32]
combines generative shape priors, predicted depth, and
motion optimization. Given metric meshes and measured
depth, we focus on recovering camera-space hand and
object trajectories while maintaining consistent grasps at
thin surfaces.
2.2. Physics-Based Interaction Reconstruction and
Contact Force Estimation
Prior work uses object dynamics to infer contact forces from
observed motion [7].
Pham et al. [28] combine learned
predictions with second-order cone programming to esti-
mate finger forces. Li et al. [19] use trajectory optimization
2


to jointly recover human and object motion, contact loca-
tions, and forces. Physical Interaction [13] estimates finger-
tip forces and contact states from object dynamics and uses
kinematic confidence to regulate contact sliding, improving
hand–object interaction reconstruction.
Recent methods predict surface contact and pressure dis-
tributions from visual observations. EgoTac [46] estimates
hand-surface contact and normal pressure from egocentric
video. HOPE [17] combines video features with hand poses
to recover time-varying contact and pressure. EgoPHI [16]
uses physics simulation to generate force supervision and
predicts contact and normal force distributions on both
hands and object surfaces from a single image and object
geometry.
Our second stage combines visual confidence, contact
geometry, and hand kinematics to jointly optimize contacts,
poses, and hand–object forces for bimanual multi-object in-
teraction in one dynamics system. The simulator resolves
object–object and support reactions, allowing object motion
and interobject load transfer to jointly constrain force esti-
mation.
3. Method
3.1. Overview
Figure 2 summarizes our framework. Given a single-view
RGB-D video, its camera calibration, and the metric meshes
of the manipulated objects, HOForce recovers the hand and
object trajectories, fingertip contacts, and contact forces
over the sequence. We first initialize the hand and object
poses from RGB-D observations and jointly refine their mo-
tion over time (Sec. 3.2). Using the reconstructed motion as
a visual reference, the physical stage refines hand poses and
contact locations and estimates fingertip forces (Sec. 3.3).
3.2. RGB-D-Based Hand–Object Motion Recon-
struction
At frame t, we observe RGB It, metric depth Dt, intrin-
sics Kt, and SAM3 [4] instance masks. For each object o,
we are given its metric mesh Mo. We estimate its camera-
space pose To,t = (Ro,t, po,t) ∈SE(3) from the RGB-D
sequence. For each hand h, we use the left or right MANO
model [31] with fixed shape bh ∈R10. The hand pose Θh,t
comprises global orientation Rh,t ∈SO(3), camera-space
wrist position wh,t ∈R3, and local rotations θh,t at 15 fin-
ger joints. For this shape, Jj(Θh,t) gives the position of
joint j. For moving cameras, we use the camera-to-world
trajectory Ct estimated by DROID-W [18] to express the
refined motion in world coordinates.
3.2.1. Depth-Guided Object Motion Initialization
We use FoundationPose [41] to initialize each object trajec-
tory from the RGB-D sequence. To follow rapid motion, we
shift the previous pose by the displacement of the masked
depth centroid before refining it against the current frame.
If tracking is lost during occlusion, we re-register the object
when it becomes visible and score the candidate poses by
mask overlap and depth agreement.
3.2.2. Depth-Guided Hand Pose Initialization
We use WiLoR [29] to initialize the wrist pose and finger ro-
tations from each RGB hand crop. After transferring the ro-
tations to the corresponding left or right MANO model, we
correct wrist depth using the trimmed mean difference be-
tween observed and rendered depths within the hand mask.
Along each track, we weight the Huber data term [15] by
valid depth coverage and penalize changes in depth veloc-
ity to bridge gaps in the measurements. We back-project the
wrist pixel at the fitted depth to obtain wh,t.

tration Constraints

3.3.3. Multi-Frame Alternating Optimization
Adjacent intervals share frame states. We linearize motion
residuals with respect to cone impulses and evaluate con-
strained updates in MuJoCo. Reference motion provides
force and torque seeds for contact selection and hand-pose
fitting, followed by force re-estimation. The supplement
specifies the subproblems, weights, geometric constraints,
and acceptance criteria.
3.3.4. Force-Driven Replay and Cross-Engine Transfer
Recovered contacts and forces reproduce the MuJoCo tra-
jectory by continuous replay from the initial state. For Py-
Bullet and Isaac Sim, we adapt forces while retaining phys-
ical parameters and application-point sequences. Indepen-
dent replay uses no intermediate state resets. The supple-
ment and Sec. 4.2 provide adaptation details and evalua-
tion.

4.1. Hand–Object Reconstruction
Evaluation protocol.
We evaluate 177 DexYCB se-
quences spanning all 10 subjects and 20 object categories,
and 228 first-person TACO sequences of bimanual, two-
object manipulation.
We report ADD/ADD-S AUC and
translation and rotation errors for objects. For hands, we
report MPJPE and its per-frame (PA) and window-aligned
(WA) variants. Acceleration error measures motion fidelity,
while penetration depth (PD) and intersection volume (IV)
measure hand–object and object–object overlap.
For object tracking, we compare with FoundationPose
(FP) [41], ICG [34], and CAD-free Point2Pose [21]. For
hand reconstruction, we compare with RGB-based HaWoR
and Dyn-HaMR [42, 45]. For hand–object reconstruction,
we compare with the single-object methods HOTrack [6]
and AGILE [32]. We evaluate both only on DexYCB be-
cause TACO requires joint tracking of two objects.
FP,
ICG, HOTrack, and AGILE receive metric object meshes
and measured depth.
For each dataset, all object trackers follow the same ini-
tialization protocol. On DexYCB, we use the annotated ob-
ject pose at the start of tracking. HOTrack also requires a
hand pose, so we start scoring it after successful initializa-
tion. On TACO, we refine the first-frame object annotation
against the observed mask and depth and share the resulting
pose across trackers. For evaluation, we score all methods
directly in camera coordinates on DexYCB. On TACO, we
align each completed trajectory to the egocentric annota-
tions with a single rigid transform to account for offsets in
the projected annotations. We evaluate the full sets at 30 Hz.
For the AGILE comparison, we score both methods on the
same valid frames at 6 Hz across 100 DexYCB sequences
covering all subjects and object categories. The supplement
gives the alignment and metric definitions.
Object tracking.
HOForce recovers object poses more
accurately and with lower acceleration error than the ob-
ject trackers FP, ICG, and Point2Pose on both datasets (Ta-
bles 1 and 2). On TACO, HOForce also reduces inter-object
penetration relative to all three trackers (see supplemen-
tal ablations). HOForce outperforms HOTrack on all five
object tracking metrics, with our results averaged over the
full DexYCB set and HOTrack’s over its successful tracks.

5. Conclusion
We presented HOForce for reconstructing hand–object mo-
tion and fingertip forces from single-view RGB-D video.
Temporal joint optimization combines visual alignment
with robust nonpenetration constraints, using hand skeletal
geometry to resolve correction directions at thin surfaces.
Coupled inverse dynamics accounts for loads transmitted
between manipulated objects and guides joint refinement of
contacts, poses, and forces. In the experiments, HOForce
recovers more accurate hand and object trajectories while
reducing both hand–object and inter-object penetration. Its
fingertip force estimates match tactile measurements more
closely than those of the baselines. These forces also re-
produce the observed object motion in MuJoCo and can be
adapted for replay in other physics engines.
Limitations.
First, we require a metric-scale mesh and
mass for each object, along with contact friction co-
efficients.
RGB-D reconstruction methods such as
BundleSDF [40] can supply the mesh.
Second, HOForce currently supports only rigid objects.
To track how an object’s surface deforms under contact, fu-
ture work could incorporate a local deformation field as in
Physical Interaction [13].
Third, we model each contacting fingertip with a single
point force. This approximation omits forces exerted by the
8


palm and pressure variations across the finger pad.
Finally, object motion alone cannot uniquely determine
the forces at individual fingertips. Two opposing fingertips
can squeeze harder while producing the same net force and
torque. Our estimates therefore represent one physically
feasible distribution and may differ from the forces actually
applied. Nevertheless, their ability to reproduce object mo-
tion in simulation suggests they could serve as force targets
for robot imitation learning.

Three-dimensional force evaluation.
We evaluate the
full force vectors on all 32 measured-force sequences and
compare HOForce with a normal-only version of the same
predictions. For the unit sensor normal n, the normal-only
prediction is ˆfn = (n⊤ˆf)n; its tangential component is zero.
This is a post-processing comparison: the predicted nor-
mal component and valid sensor–interval samples are un-
changed, and no force fitting is repeated. Both predictions
are evaluated against the same measured force vectors. The
3D error is the Euclidean distance between predicted and
measured vectors; the tangential error is the Euclidean dis-
tance between their projections onto the sensor’s tangent
plane. Table S3 reports mean 3D error, mean tangential er-

the main paper, the 3D metrics evaluate the full vector in
both rows.
Comparison with the Astra agent.
The eight sequences
used for the Astra comparison were randomly selected from
the 32 measured-force sequences. The Astra agent builds
a MuJoCo simulation from RGB-D images, masks, and
meshes and refines contacts using visual observations and
simulation feedback. Both methods use the same object
masses, inertias, and friction coefficients. Each sensor cor-
responds to a particular hand, finger, and object. For Astra,
we sum all forces that finger applies to that object at each
simulation step, regardless of the distance from the contact
point to the sensor. A step with no such contact contributes
zero. We average the resulting force over each video-frame
interval, weighted by step duration. For each sensor and
frame interval valid for both methods, we compute the Eu-
clidean distance between estimated and measured force vec-
tors in a common world frame and give the eight sequence
means equal weight.

D. Additional Experiments
D.1. Penetration Ablations
We ablate the hand–object and object–object nonpenetra-
tion losses to measure their effect on mesh overlap across
228 TACO sequences and 21 rod–hole insertion segments
from five recordings. Removing nonpenetration penalties
increases both penetration depth and intersection volume on
both datasets (Tables S4 and S5).
TACO contains many thin-walled objects. When a fin-
ger penetrates a thin wall, the nearest surface can be the op-
posite face, so a standard signed-distance penalty can push
the finger farther through the wall. We therefore compare
this penalty with our skeleton-guided hand–object correc-
tion across all 228 TACO sequences, using the same surface
samples and fixed object tracks. Skeleton guidance reduces
hand–object PD from 3.38 to 1.73 mm and IV from 0.64 to
0.20 cm3 relative to the bidirectional SDF (Table S4).
