Abstract
Learning robot manipulation from egocentric human
videos requires accurate metric 3D hand motion. Recent
methods leverage pretrained video diffusion model (VDM)
priors to recover occluded hands directly from full-frame
videos, but their appearance-based depth regression gener-
alizes poorly to unseen cameras. In this work, we propose
ViDiHand2, a geometry-driven framework that reformulates
VDM-based hand depth estimation from direct regression
into fine-grained geometric constraint solving. Specifically,
we introduce Fine-Grained Geometric Lifting and Clip-Level
Ray Fitting, which recover metric depth by enforcing geo-
metric consistency between pixel-aligned 2D joints and the
metric structure of MANO hands, while leveraging temporal
observations across the clip. Since the lifting relies on these
2D joints, to improve robustness under occlusion and image
truncation, we further develop Confidence-Gated Fusion to
adaptively combine geometrically lifted joints with MANO
predictions according to localization confidence. Finally,
we enhance the VDM backbone to better capture spatial-
temporal representations and produce the per-frame 2D joint
and MANO predictions that both steps rely on. Extensive
experiments on seven public datasets demonstrate that ViDi-
Hand2 consistently outperforms 18 baselines, reducing in-
the-wild absolute joint error by 42% and detection failures
by 8.82× compared with the best baseline on each metric,
while running at 57 FPS. The project page is available at
https://vidihand2.github.io/.
1. Introduction
Human videos offer a scalable source of training data for
robot manipulation [23, 37, 72, 81] and egocentric world
models [62, 68, 76]. Accurate metric 3D hand motion is
essential for extracting high-quality supervision from these
videos. Egocentric hand motion reconstruction aims to re-
cover the 3D joint positions of both hands in camera coor-
dinates. Detect-then-reconstruct methods [46, 48, 80] first
localize hands and then estimate their 3D poses from image
1

crops. Within this paradigm, single-frame approaches lack
temporal consistency, while motion-prior-based methods
model temporal coherence but remain vulnerable to localiza-
tion and tracking failures under occlusion. The current state-
of-the-art methods ViDiHand [67] and ACE-Ego-Hand [35]
instead adapt pretrained video diffusion models (VDMs)
to reconstruct hands directly from full-frame videos, elim-
inating the need for explicit hand detection and enabling
recovery of occluded hand motion.
However, both methods exhibit substantial errors in
recovering metric 3D hand trajectories from in-the-wild
videos [12], particularly in depth estimation and spatial lo-
calization, as Fig. 1 shows. A key limitation is that directly
regressing metric depth from appearance requires learning
a complex mapping that is highly sensitive to focal length
and generalizes poorly to unseen cameras [47, 75], as illus-
trated in Fig. 2. Moreover, occlusion and hand truncation
at image boundaries weaken the visual cues for hand local-
ization, while coarse tokenization in both methods limits
the preservation of fine-grained spatial details. Addressing
these challenges further calls for richer spatial-temporal rep-
resentations from VDMs and more consistent hand trajectory
prediction over time.
In this work, we propose ViDiHand2, which reformulates
VDM-based hand depth estimation from direct regression
into geometric constraint solving. Our key insight is that
metric depth can be recovered by enforcing geometric con-
sistency between pixel-aligned 2D joint predictions and the
metric structure of MANO hands [51], both of which are
more reliable than directly regressed depth.
To realize this idea, we introduce Fine-Grained Geo-
metric Lifting (Sec. 3.2), which places each joint along its
camera ray and constrains its depth using MANO joint dis-
tances. Unlike prior methods [20, 54, 63] that recover metric
scale through bone-length fitting or 2D–3D alignment, our
approach exploits fine-grained constraints across multiple
joints. We further introduce Clip-Level Ray Fitting to jointly
estimate depth across a video clip, leveraging temporal ob-
servations to constrain frames with unreliable joints.
Accurate joint localization is essential for maintaining
reliable geometric constraints, particularly under occlusion
and truncation. In Sec. 3.3, we introduce the Joint-Heatmap
Refiner to recover fine-grained joint positions using heat
maps at eight times the resolution of the VDM token grid.
To further handle ambiguous visual evidence, Confidence-
Gated Fusion adaptively combines the refined 2D predictions
with the fitted MANO hand, assigning greater weight to
MANO estimates when joint localization is uncertain.
We further enhance the VDM-based architecture with
improved feature aggregation and temporal modeling in
Sec. 3.4. The Multi-Level Latent Encoder aggregates fea-
tures across all adapted VDM blocks to exploit multi-level
representations. The Hand Latent Decoder decodes each

Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of
ViDiHand2. We consistently outperform existing methods
on every metric. On in-the-wild data, ViDiHand2 reduces
absolute joint error by over 40% compared with the strongest
baseline. On in-domain benchmarks, it lowers the absolute
and 2D joint errors of ACE-Ego-Hand by 20% and 31%.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify a key limitation of existing VDM-based hand
reconstruction methods: direct depth regression gener-
alizes poorly to unseen cameras. To address this, we
reformulate depth estimation as fine-grained geometric
constraint solving, recovering metric depth by enforcing
geometric consistency between pixel-aligned joint predic-
tions and MANO hand structure.
• To support reliable geometric reconstruction, we introduce
fine-grained joint localization and confidence-gated fusion
for handling occlusion and truncation, along with multi-
level feature aggregation and autoregressive rollout for
stronger spatial-temporal modeling.
• We conduct extensive experiments on seven public datasets
against 18 baselines. Evaluated on all ground-truth hands,
ViDiHand2 ranks first on every metric of every dataset,
both against their officially released weights and against
the re-trained baselines, showing superior detection, 3D
pose accuracy and smoothness.

Parametric Models and Keypoints. Parametric models
such as SMPL [36] and MANO [51] accumulate rotation
errors along the kinematic chain. To avoid this, HybrIK [28],
NIKI [29] and Minimal-Hand [82] derive the rotations from
predicted 3D keypoints through inverse kinematics. Me-
TRAbs [54] uses the back-projected 2D location for joints
inside the image and the root-relative 3D prediction for
truncated ones. ViDiHand2 replaces this hard switch with
learned gates that move the placed MANO hand toward the
2D locations according to their reliability.
Diffusion Models for Perception. Recent work adapts pre-
trained diffusion models to correspondence, depth estima-
tion, tracking and 3D perception [11, 14, 17, 19, 24, 42,
58, 60, 64, 78]. ViDiHand [67] and ACE-Ego-Hand [35]
reconstruct hands from video diffusion features without a
hand detector, but both regress the hand depth. In monocular
depth estimation and human mesh recovery, a regressed
metric depth is known to depend on the training cam-
eras [30, 47, 75]. ViDiHand2 therefore replaces this re-
gression by a fit along the rays of the test camera.

3.2. Hand Depth Estimation Revisited
We begin by revisiting the mixed projection of ViDiHand
and ACE-Ego-Hand [35, 67], which regresses only the depth
and solves the in-plane shift in closed form. We pinpoint the
depth error to this regression. Regressing a metric depth re-
quires learning the metric scale of all visible objects, which is
hard for general scenes [47, 75]. The learned mapping from
appearance to depth thus depends on the focal lengths of the
training cameras, as Fig. 2 shows. We therefore seek a depth
estimate that is easier to learn and follows the test camera.

3.3. Joint Localization Revisited
The video prior of ViDiHand and ACE-Ego-Hand maintains
a 3D hand state through occlusion, but an occluded hand
offers little evidence in the current frame. The key issue is
that the joints in view cover few cells of a 16- or 32-pixel
token grid. Joints hidden by an object or the other hand have
no pixels of their own. Truncation by the image border is the
extreme case. By Eq. (3), the lifting of such a hand relies on
the few reliable 2D locations. We therefore localize every
joint inside the image on heat maps eight times finer than our
4


32-pixel grid and regress the others. We then fuse them with
the placed MANO hand, which covers the hidden joints.

3.4. VDM-Based Architecture Revisited
Multi-Level Latent Encoder. We aggregate the features of
every block of a pretrained VDM. Like ACE-Ego-Hand [35],
we repurpose this VDM into a deterministic, detector-free
encoder. We apply the first 16 blocks of the Wan2.2 trans-
former [61], adapted with LoRA [16], to noise-free VAE
latents without diffusion sampling. Each latent frame after
the first covers four video frames. The transformer pro-
cesses a clip in chunks of 21 latent frames, whereas the fit of
Sec. 3.2 spans the whole clip. The task tokens of each latent
frame are two hand tokens, 42 joint tokens and four registers.
In ACE-Ego-Hand, the same tokens enter a separate decoder
after the last adapted block and see only that block’s features.
Our task tokens instead pass through every adapted block,
where they use the block weights [65] and read the video
tokens without changing them [26]. The hand tokens predict
the MANO hand and the hand presence. The video tokens of
the last block and a ray encoding of K form the feature map.
Hand Latent Decoder. We decode the hand states and
features of each latent frame into its video frames without a
fixed interpolation. Our temporal decoders build on the Wan
VAE decoder [61] and also read latent frames of the adjacent
chunks. In contrast, ACE-Ego-Hand linearly interpolates the
hand states between latent frames. ViDiHand [67] adds a
learned residual to this interpolation.
Autoregressive Rollout Adaptation. In the last four blocks
of our transformer, each chunk also attends to the cached
keys and values of the previous chunk [6, 69, 85]. ViDi-
Hand instead processes each chunk independently, so the
hand pose can jump at chunk boundaries. On autoregressive
rollouts [18, 22], we train only a bias on this attention. The
supplement details chunked inference in Sec. D.8.
4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We train and test on six egocentric datasets,
ARCTIC [9], H2O [25], HOT3D [2], OakInk2 [79] and the
two parts of EgoDemo [31], EgoDemo Pro (Ego-Pro) and
EgoDemo Standard (Ego-Std), with 38.97 and 6.22 hours
of training and in-domain test video. To test in the wild, on
an unseen camera and scenes, we sample about one hour of
Gen-HumanEgo [12], unused in training. Rule-based checks
and visual inspection remove 3.79% of the frames of the
seven datasets, mostly with inaccurate or missing labels or
unusable images, as Sec. A of the supplement details.

the same six training splits as ViDiHand2, and Sec. B.2 of
the supplement evaluates their officially released weights.

4.2. Comparison with Existing Methods
Comparison on All Hands with Detection Failure Penalty.
Against the 18 re-trained baselines of Tab. 1 and their offi-
cially released weights in Sec. B.2 of the supplement, ViDi-
Hand2 is the best on every metric, in-domain and in the wild.
In the wild, it outperforms all baselines by a large margin.
On MPJPE-abs-p, which penalizes detection failures and
misplaced hands alike, it reduces the error of the best base-
line, WiLoR, by 42%, from 56.55 to 32.59 mm. In-domain,
it reduces that of ACE-Ego-Hand by 20%, from 24.46 to
19.62 mm. A detection failure or a false positive occurs in
only 0.07% of its in-domain and 0.11% of its in-the-wild
frames, 14.57× and 8.82× less often than for ViDiHand, the
baseline with the highest FAcc.
Comparison on Hands Detected by All Methods. Table 2
removes the penalty and compares the methods only on the
hands that all 19 detect, 59% of the hand-frames, a subset
that favors the detect-then-reconstruct baselines. ViDiHand2
remains the best on every metric, and Sec. B.4 of the supple-
ment repeats the test with the officially released weights. In
the wild, WiLoR comes close in MPJPE-abs, 30.26 against
28.09 mm, but not in MPJPE, 21.88 against 17.82 mm, or
Jitter, 11.17 against 1.46 mm/frame2, so ViDiHand2 gains
on it mainly by detecting more hands. ACE-Ego-Hand and
ViDiHand still reach 79.15 and 90.47 mm, so ViDiHand2
gains on them by better placement.
Comparison on an Unseen Camera. ViDiHand2 transfers
to an unseen camera far better than the video diffusion base-

Fitting versus Regressing the Hand Depth. As Tab. 6
shows, fitting the hand depth transfers to an unseen camera
far better than regressing it. A head trained with the features
to regress the depth reaches an in-the-wild MPJPE-abs-p
of 81.24 mm, close to ACE-Ego-Hand and ViDiHand, and
42.52 mm for the depth over the focal length, whereas every
fit stays at 38.83 mm or below. On the features trained with
the depth head, the fit still reaches 32.55 mm, so the gap
comes from how the depth is obtained, not from the features.
Importance of Clip-Level Ray Fitting and Fine-Grained
Geometric Lifting. Fitting over the clip smooths the motion,
and the bone term, which ties the joints on their rays to the
metric MANO joint-pair distances, improves the placement.
One wrist trajectory per clip lowers the jitter of the per-frame
fit from 3.39 to 1.26 mm/frame2 in-domain and from 3.65
to 1.61 mm/frame2 in the wild. Adding the bone term to the
reprojection fit of MeTRAbs [54] and HandDGP [63] lowers
its MPJPE-abs-p in the wild from 34.40 to 32.69 mm per
frame and, over the clip, on every dataset by 0.17 to 1.73 mm.
Importance of the Joint-Heatmap Refiner. In Tab. 7, the
2D locations of Sec. 3.3 have the largest in-domain effect of
all designs, as their heat maps are eight times finer than the
feature map. Using only the coarse token-attention locations,
as ACE-Ego-Hand does, is smoother but less accurate and
raises the MPJPE-abs-p from 19.62 to 32.30 mm in-domain
and from 32.59 to 39.71 mm in the wild. Keeping only those
inside the image gives 20.20 mm in-domain.
Confidence-Gated Fusion versus Either Source. In Tab. 8,
whose rows share one model and one wrist trajectory and
differ only in the output joints, the fusion is more accurate
than either of its sources on every accuracy metric in both
settings. It combines the MANO hand, which is complete but
need not align with the image, with the 2D locations, which
align with the image but are unreliable under occlusion and
beyond the border. In-domain, it thus lowers the EPE2D-p
of the MANO hand by 7%, from 5.03 to 4.67 px, and its
MPJPE-p from 9.75 to 9.50 mm. The learned gate is also
more accurate and smoother than setting the gate to the
confidence, gj = cj. Section E of the supplement defines all
variants and reports more metrics.
5. Conclusion
We propose ViDiHand2, which reformulates VDM-based
hand depth estimation from regression into geometric con-
straint solving. Fine-Grained Geometric Lifting and Clip-
Level Ray Fitting compute the depth of a clip on the rays of
the test camera, the Joint-Heatmap Refiner and Confidence-
Gated Fusion handle occlusion and truncation, and the Multi-
Level Latent Encoder, Hand Latent Decoder and Autoregres-
sive Rollout Adaptation improve accuracy and smoothness.
ViDiHand2 substantially outperforms 18 methods on every
metric, in-domain and in the wild, at 57 FPS, enabling scal-
able hand motion data collection for embodied AI.
Limitations. ViDiHand2 needs the camera intrinsics, which
egocentric devices provide in practice. It also runs offline,
which suits recorded videos but not closed-loop control.

A. Datasets and Evaluation Protocol
Table S1 lists the datasets of Sec. 4.1, and Tab. S2 lists the frames removed by label filtering. We evaluate the baselines in two
versions. For Tab. 1, we re-train all 18 baselines with their official code on the training splits of Tab. S1, the same training data
as ViDiHand2, so that every method is trained and tested on the same data. For 15 of these baselines, Tab. S10 also evaluates
their officially released weights, which are trained on other data. The released weights of ViDiHand and ACE-Ego-Hand
are themselves trained on these six datasets, and EgoHandICL has no released weights for them. Every method receives the
RGB video and the ground-truth intrinsics K, and uses K wherever its official code accepts intrinsics, for example to refit the
translation or as a network input. No method receives the ground-truth camera pose, hand shape or hand size.
A.1. Labels
We represent all labels by MANO in camera coordinates with per-frame pinhole intrinsics and the mirrored right-hand model
for left hands. For datasets that provide only 3D joints, we fit MANO to these joints, for Gen-HumanEgo to its tracked joints
with one shape per clip after undistorting the fisheye images of one camera. Rule-based checks and visual inspection remove
1,864 Gen-HumanEgo frames, most of them with inaccurate labels, as Tab. S2 lists.
A.2. Splits
ARCTIC and H2O are split by subject and the other in-domain datasets by recording. HOT3D and OakInk2 share participants
or scenes between the splits, and the two EgoDemo datasets record no subject identity.

A.3. Label Filtering
We identify the frames to remove in two steps. Rule-based checks on the labels and the video flag label jumps between
consecutive frames, abnormal hand poses, labeled hands without a visible hand, visible frames without labels and dark or
blurred frames. We then render the labels over the video and inspect the flagged events and their neighboring frames. The
196,120 removed frames of Tab. S2 fall into five groups. In 104,493 frames, the label is inaccurate, for example misplaced,
misshaped, jumping between frames or in an abnormal pose. In 1,413 frames, a hand is labeled where no hand is visible, and
in 6,570 frames, a visible hand has no label. In 25,487 frames, the dataset provides no label. In 58,157 frames, the image is
unusable: clips with privacy mosaics and dark, blurred or missing frames. We remove these frames from training and from
the evaluation of every method and record the reason for each removal. Although the public datasets have largely accurate
labels, the percentage of frames with inaccurate labels ranges from 0.07% on ARCTIC to 5.83% on HOT3D. Table S3 gives
the frames before and after filtering and the percentage of inaccurate labels of each dataset.
A.4. Joints and Matching
In each frame, a method predicts at most one left and one right hand, each as 21 joints in camera coordinates. These are the
16 MANO kinematic joints and five fingertip vertices. We compute the same joints from MANO predictions. A ground-truth
hand is visible if at least one of its joints has a depth z > 1 cm and projects inside the image, and a predicted hand likewise.
Only visible hands are evaluated. Each predicted hand is matched to the ground-truth hand with the largest intersection over
union (IoU) of the 2D boxes around all 21 projected joints, where the ground-truth box is enlarged by 10% and a hand of the
same handedness receives a bonus of 0.5. A detected hand is a visible ground-truth hand that is matched to a prediction of the
same handedness with nonzero IoU. Every other visible ground-truth hand is a detection failure. A visible prediction is a false

A.5. Metrics
The frame accuracy FAcc is the fraction of test frames with neither a false positive nor a detection failure. F1 is the harmonic
mean of the precision and the recall of the detections, with the counts pooled over all hands of a dataset. False positives
enter FAcc and F1 only. MPJPE-abs is the joint error in camera coordinates, MPJPE the joint error after subtracting the
wrist of each hand, and PA-MPJPE the joint error after aligning scale, rotation and translation. Each is the mean over the 21
joints of a hand, also of joints outside the image, and then over the hands. EPE2D is the pixel distance between the predicted
joints, projected with the ground-truth intrinsics, and the ground-truth joints, over the visible ground-truth joints, with each
distance capped at the image diagonal. The suffix -p denotes that an error is computed over all visible ground-truth hands.
Each detected hand is evaluated with the matched prediction, and each detection failure receives a penalty. For MPJPE-abs-p,
MPJPE-p and EPE2D-p, the penalty is the error of a default prediction. This default prediction places the MANO mean hand
of the same handedness with the wrist at a depth of 0.5 m on the camera ray through the image center. Since PA-MPJPE
aligns each prediction to the ground truth, the default prediction can obtain a lower error than an actual one. The penalty
of PA-p is therefore the root-mean-square distance of the ground-truth joints from their centroid, an upper bound on the
Procrustes-aligned error of any non-degenerate prediction. Jitter is the mean acceleration ∥Xt+1,j −2Xt,j + Xt−1,j∥of the
predicted joints Xt,j of a method in camera coordinates, in mm/frame2, over every three consecutive frames in which the
hand is detected. It has no penalty and no ground-truth term, so it measures smoothness and not accuracy. In the comparisons
on hands detected by all methods, a hand-frame is kept when every method detects it, the errors have no penalty, and Jitter
uses the triplets of consecutive kept frames. Every metric is computed over all test clips of a dataset, and the in-domain value
is the unweighted mean over the six in-domain datasets.

C.5. In-the-Wild 3D Accuracy with a Ruler as Ground Truth
The examples of Sec. C.3 have no reliable 3D ground truth. To measure 3D accuracy in the wild, we film a person who holds a
20 cm steel ruler between the two thumb tips, so the distance between the thumb tips stays at 20 cm while the hands move. The
video is recorded with an iPhone camera at 1920×1080 pixels and 29.97 fps. We score the 8.2 s clip of 247 frames, during
which the ruler is lifted, tilted and turned upright, and each hand in turn passes behind a cup, for 55 frames in total. All
methods use the weights of Tab. 1 and the same intrinsics. Since a monocular method recovers metric size only up to the hand
size it assumes, each method’s distance is multiplied by one factor that sets its median over a flat hold before the clip to 20 cm.
Figure S22 shows the scaled distance of every method over the clip and in four frames.
As Tab. S19 shows, ViDiHand2 is within 1 cm of 20 cm in every frame, with an MAE of 3.0 mm over the clip and 4.5 mm
while a hand is behind the cup. The closest baseline, ACE-Ego-Hand, has an MAE of 8.1 mm over the clip and 9.0 mm while a
hand is hidden, and is within 1 cm in 72% and 58% of these frames, respectively. The other baselines that detect both hands in
every frame have MAEs from 19.8 to 93.9 mm over the clip and from 38.1 to 122.4 mm while a hand is hidden, where they are
within 1 cm in at most 31% of the frames. For each of them the MAE is higher while a hand is hidden. Much of this error
is in depth. At moment 4, for example, WiLoR and HaWoR project the right thumb tip only 25 and 67 pixels from that of
ViDiHand2 but place it 12.0 and 16.8 cm deeper after scaling. HaMeR and EgoForce often lose a hand instead: HaMeR returns
both hands in only one of the 28 frames with the left hand behind the cup, and EgoForce in only 3 of the 55 frames with a
hand behind the cup. The largest errors of ViDiHand2 occur while the right hand is hidden, where its distance is 5 to 10 mm

D. Implementation Details
D.1. Multi-Level Latent Encoder
The frozen Wan2.2 VAE encodes the whole clip once as one latent stream. As in ACE-Ego-Hand [15], the first 16 of the
30 blocks of the Wan2.2-Fun-5B-Control transformer [26] process the noise-free latents of each chunk without diffusion
sampling. LoRA [9] adapts the attention and feed-forward layers of these blocks, and the pretrained weights remain frozen.
Each latent frame also carries 48 task tokens for the two hands, the 42 joints and four registers. ACE-Ego-Hand uses the same
tokens but reads them from the output of the last block with a separate decoder. In ViDiHand2, the task tokens instead pass
through every adapted block with the weights of that block. Within a block, the task tokens attend to the video tokens of that
block and to all task tokens of the chunk. The video tokens attend only to each other, over the whole chunk in space and time,
so the task tokens do not change the video features. When the task tokens attend to the video tokens, the video keys keep only
the rotary encoding of time. Table S20 lists the settings of the backbone, the temporal decoders, the hand, joint and MANO
heads and the Joint-Heatmap Refiner.
D.2. MANO Heads
In every frame, heads on the decoded hand state regress the rotations of the wrist and the 15 finger joints in the 6D
representation [34]. The same heads regress the shape and the 2D and 3D presence of each hand. Residual heat maps add the
expectation of a distribution over bins to every rotation-vector component and every shape coefficient. The shape of a hand is
the median of the per-frame shapes over the chunk in training and over the clip at inference, plus one residual. This residual
uses the presence-weighted geometric mean of the per-frame shape distributions, so each hand has one shape per clip, as in
ACE-Ego-Hand [15]. The expectation over bin centers follows AdaBins [3]. The predicted shape is supervised only by the
ground-truth shape. The MANO poses are used frame by frame without smoothing.

Initialization and solver. The fit runs in 64-bit floating point in three steps. First, every frame alone gets a closed-form start:
for every pair of joints, the depth at which the pair has its MANO length on its two rays [10], the median over the pairs as

the depth, and the median over the joints as the lateral position. Second, a fit of the depth alone over the clip, with the bone

iterations. Third, the full fit of Eq. (5) runs for at most 40 Levenberg–Marquardt steps, with banded linear systems for long
clips. The damping is 10−3 times the diagonal of the normal equations, grows tenfold after a rejected step and shrinks tenfold
after an accepted one. The fit stops when every gradient component, scaled by the square root of its diagonal entry, falls below
10−4. Every joint with a 2D location is constrained to lie in front of the camera, and a step that leaves this region is rejected. A
frame without coupled neighbors is placed when it has at least two 2D locations, and a run of coupled frames when its frames
together provide at least six constraints. A hand-frame that the fit cannot place is not reported and counts as a detection failure.
The fit has no depth prior and no bound on the depth.

with respect to the parameters ϑ of the two heads and the scalars sj as −∂ϑ⟨λ, ∇rE(ˆr; ϑ)⟩, where λ solves Hλ = ∂L/∂ˆr
with the exact Hessian H of the energy, by an undamped Cholesky factorization. A hand whose fit has not converged, or whose

Hand size as a depth cue.
Across the six training splits, the central 98% of the wrist depths of the hands in the image lie
between 0.19 and 0.64 m. This range spans 3.37× around a median of 0.34 m. The total bone length of the same hand-frames
spans 1.28×, and at most 1.19× within one dataset. The total length of the 20 bones is 0.75 m at the median training shape.
Moving any single MANO shape coefficient to either end of its central 99% training range changes this size by −5.19% to
+16.87%. If the predicted hand size deviates by some factor, the fitted depth is scaled by approximately the same factor. For
fixed joint rays, this relative depth error is therefore independent of the hand-to-camera distance. In the wild, ACE-Ego-Hand,
which regresses the depth, predicts hands 7% too small but places them 26% too far in the median, and ViDiHand predicts them
2% too small but 21% too far. A hand predicted too small should be placed too near, so this regressed depth is inconsistent
with the predicted hand size. ViDiHand2 predicts the in-the-wild hands 4% too small and places them 2% too near.
D.4. Hand Latent Decoder and Autoregressive Rollout Adaptation
Hand Latent Decoder.
The transformer and the task tokens operate at the latent frame rate, with one latent frame per four
video frames after the first. The Hand Latent Decoder contains five small decoders that bring the latent-rate quantities of
Tab. S20 to the video frame rate, so the hand states of every frame come from learned temporal decoding. Each decoder
follows the Wan2.2 VAE decoder [26] but has temporal kernels only. Two stages each double the frame rate, with separate
kernels for the two output frames of each input frame. All temporal kernels are centered and use replicate padding at both ends
of the decoded span. A linear path, initialized as Catmull–Rom interpolation between latent frames, is added to each decoder.

D.7. Training
ViDiHand2 is trained in the two stages of Tab. S23 on the six in-domain training splits of Tab. S1, starting from the released
Wan2.2 weights. The training chunks of 21 latent frames come from one causal latent stream per clip, as at inference, and every
frame contributes to the losses. Speed augmentation subsamples 60% of the chunks by 2× or 3× with equal probability. The
fit keeps the original frame rate, so these chunks show faster hand motion than the recordings. We also paste black occluders
into 25% and other hands into 20% of the chunks and low-pass filter 30% of the chunks. Clips with the smallest and the largest
hands are sampled twice as often. Both stages use AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.999) and a total batch size of eight chunks on 8
A100 GPUs, and gradients are clipped at a norm of 1.0. The first stage trains the LoRA adapters, the task tokens, the heads,
the decoders and the gates for 50k steps, with a learning rate of 2×10−4 for the heads and 10−4 for LoRA after a warm-up of
200 steps. The second stage trains the cache bias for 3k steps on autoregressive rollouts. ViDiHand2 has 3.0B parameters, of
which 107.5M are trained. The LoRA adapters and the trained patch convolution hold 86.6M of them, the task-token adapters
2.4M and the heads 18.5M, including the 3.8M of the decoders and the 0.23M of the part gates. The frozen part is blocks 0–15
of the Wan2.2 transformer with its embeddings and the encoder of the Wan2.2 VAE.

D.8. Inference
The first chunk covers 81 frames and every later chunk the next 84, so every chunk holds 21 latent frames. A clip that does not
end on a latent boundary is padded by repeating the last frame, and the padded frames are dropped from the output. The chunks
are processed autoregressively, and from block 12 on, the transformer of each chunk attends to the cached keys and values of
the previous chunk. When the cache is used, blocks 12 to 15 run twice, once with and once without the cache. The pass with the
cache provides the task-token states for the pose. The pass without the cache provides the video features and the states for the
shape and the presence. The decoders of each chunk also read the last two latent frames of the previous chunk and the first latent
frame of the next. The chunks do not overlap, but their decoded spans do, and the decoded features of the overlap are averaged.
After the last chunk, a clip-level pass takes the median of the per-frame shapes of each hand and adds the clip-wide shape resid-
ual. It then applies MANO and the Clip-Level Ray Fitting to the whole clip. Equation (7) then blends the two estimates of each
joint with the learned gate, and Eq. (S7) moves the MANO hand onto the blend to give the output joints. The hand presence confi-
dence threshold is 0.05. Hand-frames that the fit cannot place and hands with a joint behind the camera are left out of the output.
