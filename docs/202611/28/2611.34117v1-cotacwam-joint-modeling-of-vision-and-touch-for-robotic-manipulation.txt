Abstract
We present CoTacWAM, a world-action model that learns
shared contact semantics between wrist vision and touch,
achieving the best observed performance in our nominal
manipulation and missing-input evaluations. The two modal-
ities observe the same local interaction through different
signals: wrist images reveal nearby geometry, while touch
records physical contact. Our approach connects their com-
mon contact meaning while preserving the evidence unique
to each modality, giving the policy a joint description of
interaction across available input combinations. This rep-
resentation supports predictive action learning alongside
head-view scene context and conditions corrections after
an independent detector confirms a failed attempt. Exper-
iments across four real-world tasks demonstrate improved
manipulation performance over the evaluated baselines, ro-
bustness to missing local observations, and further gains
from autonomous retries.
1. Introduction
Learning-based manipulation models increasingly translate
visual observations and instructions into useful robot be-
havior [3, 41]. Generalist policies extend this capability
through diverse robot demonstrations [9] and pretrained
vision–language knowledge [2, 17, 27]. World-action mod-
els further connect action learning with prediction of future
sensory changes [37]. For contact-rich manipulation, useful
motion depends on understanding the physical interaction it
produces. Wrist vision and touch offer two observations of
this interaction: an image shows the gripper closing around
an object, while tactile measurements reveal the resulting
contact. Their signals differ, but both carry evidence about
a common event. Learning what these observations share
could help a policy relate visible local changes to physical
contact.
Recent policies integrate heterogeneous tactile sen-
sors [36] and exploit visual–tactile relationships through
cross-modal fusion and prediction [10, 38]. Visuotactile
world-action models further connect future sensory predic-
tion with control [23, 31]. However, making both streams
useful to a policy does not by itself specify a common physi-
cal interpretation of their signals. We therefore ask: how can
wrist vision and touch express common contact meaning, and
how can this shared understanding support control? Wrist
images contribute local geometry, while touch provides di-
rect physical measurements. A useful joint representation
should therefore connect their common meaning while re-
taining the evidence unique to each modality. With complete
observations, both kinds of information can inform control;
with an input missing, the remaining evidence should still
support the contact concepts it can reveal.
We introduce CoTacWAM, which organizes wrist vi-
sion and touch around shared physical concepts: whether
pad–object contact occurs and its coarse strength. Along-
side this common description, the representation preserves
the sensory detail needed for manipulation. By learning
this description from paired observations and varying their
availability, we form a joint representation that can be read
through the same interface across input combinations. Wrist
vision enters through a lightweight trainable interface, while
a separate head-view pathway supplies scene context. We
use the learned joint representation to describe current inter-
action and supervise its future evolution during predictive
action learning. When an attempt fails, an independent detec-
tor provides a failure event; the policy combines this context
with updated joint observations to generate a correction and
resume execution.
We evaluate this approach on four real-world tasks span-
ning insertion, surface interaction, liquid handling, and
fragile-object manipulation. CoTacWAM achieves 71.3%
mean success without retries, compared with 35.0% for the
strongest baseline. Representation analyses show that con-
tact semantics are readable from both modalities through a

2. Related Work
2.1. Visuotactile Representation Learning
Multimodal learning provides a foundation for extract-
ing shared representations from complementary observa-
tions [26]. Visuotactile learning applies this idea to physical
interaction, relating touch to visual observations with differ-
ent spatial coverage. Early work uses external scene views
for vision–touch prediction [19] and contact-aware multi-
modal representations [18]. Touch and Go expands paired
visual–tactile learning to human-collected interactions in
natural environments [33]. SSVTP collects spatially aligned
local surface patches [16], while UniTouch aligns tactile and
visual features across heterogeneous datasets [34]. For pol-
icy learning, FTP-1 unifies tactile inputs across sensors [36],
and FuSe connects heterogeneous modalities through lan-
guage grounding [15]. ViTacFormer integrates scene and
wrist views with tactile attention and forecasting [10].
These settings provide different visual contexts for con-
tact. On robots already equipped with scene and wrist cam-
eras, the wrist view provides local visual evidence without
additional sensing hardware or a dedicated setup for aligned
surface imaging. The wrist camera moves with the end effec-
tor and observes nearby hand–object geometry, while touch
measures the response at the contact interface. We exploit
this physical relationship to learn the contact meaning shared
by wrist vision and touch, while preserving complementary
sensory evidence. This local representation and the scene
context from a separate head-view pathway jointly inform
predictive action learning.
2.2. World-Action Models for Contact-Rich Manip-
ulation
Visuomotor policies can generate action sequences directly
through action chunking [40] or diffusion [5]. Sensory pre-
diction additionally models the environmental changes as-
sociated with those actions. Visual foresight uses predicted
images to plan robot motion [8]; UniPi generates video plans
from language [6], and UniSim learns interactive visual sim-
ulation for policy training [35]. RoboScape uses depth and
keypoint-dynamics supervision to improve the physical con-
sistency of generated manipulation videos [29]. LongScape
improves long-horizon video consistency through action-
guided chunking and context-aware expert routing [28].
World-action models bring prediction into policy learning.
GR-1 jointly predicts future images and actions after video
generative pretraining [32], while Fast-WAM co-trains video
and action prediction and omits future-video generation at
inference [37]. For contact-rich manipulation, VT-WAM
jointly predicts vision, tactile deformation, and actions [31],
while N0-TWAM uses force-based tactile representations for
predictive control [23]. TacWAM incorporates mechanics-
aware tactile prediction and temporal interaction history into
action learning [14]. These methods make the choice of sen-
sory prediction target consequential for control. CoTacWAM
focuses on how wrist vision and touch jointly describe the
physical interaction used for predictive control. We learn
their shared contact semantics before policy training, then
use the resulting representation as both current interaction
evidence and a future target.
2.3. Failure Detection and Recovery
Recovery requires connecting evidence of a failed attempt to
an executable correction. At the task level, SayCan grounds
language plans in skill affordances [13], while Inner Mono-
logue feeds execution outcomes back into planning [12].
REFLECT uses multisensory summaries for failure explana-
tion and corrective planning [22], while AHA learns failure
detection and explanation from generated trajectories [7]. To
obtain corrective behaviors, FailSafe pairs simulated failures
with recovery actions [20], and FLARE combines learned
retries with reset skills selected by an online monitor [39].
For contact-rich manipulation, corrective actions must
also account for the local interaction state following a failure.
Visuotactile regrasping illustrates this connection by predict-
ing the outcomes of candidate grasp adjustments from visual
and tactile feedback [4]. We address this through shared
wrist–tactile contact semantics, using the same interaction
representation for nominal execution and failure-conditioned
correction. An independent tactile–proprioceptive detector
confirms failure, while the policy combines the failure con-
text with continuously updated joint observations to guide
adjustment.

3.1. Overview
At each control step t, the robot receives head and wrist
images, measurements from two tactile pads, propriocep-
tive state, and a task instruction. Using these observations
and their available history, it generates an action chunk: a
sequence of arm-joint and gripper commands. The cam-
eras provide different spatial context: the head view covers
the scene, while the wrist view observes the region where
the gripper interacts with objects. We retain a separate
head-view pathway and introduce wrist vision through a
lightweight trainable interface into a local wrist–tactile en-
coder. Both pathways supply evidence to action generation
throughout execution.
Our central question is how to connect the two local ob-
servations so that they express a common interaction state
while retaining their complementary information. We first
learn this joint representation, then train action generation
together with prediction of its temporal evolution. When
an attempt fails, the same current representation and an in-
dependently confirmed failure event condition a corrective
response.
3.2. Learning Shared Wrist–Tactile Semantics
We learn a shared semantic reference for wrist vision and
touch, while preserving the complementary evidence each
modality contributes (Fig. 2). The resulting joint represen-
tation provides a consistent description of contact across
different input combinations.

Common labels alone leave freedom in how each modal-

projections using VICReg [1], encouraging agreement while
preventing feature collapse. The two modalities also provide
different evidence for these labels: touch measures a physi-
cal response, whereas wrist vision must infer contact from
appearance and geometry. Following the soft-target principle
of knowledge distillation [11], we use tactile predictions to
teach the wrist branch on trustworthy paired observations.
Together, common supervision defines the semantic refer-
ence, alignment connects the feature representations, and
tactile-to-visual distillation guides the visual interpretation
with tactile evidence. These constraints act on the seman-
tic projections, leaving the complete vectors available to
represent additional information.
Retaining complementary evidence for control.
Two in-
teractions can share contact and strength categories yet differ
in object position or force distribution, calling for different
subsequent actions. A representation organized only around
those categories could discard distinctions that matter for
manipulation. We therefore supervise preservation of physi-
cal information in the tactile vector and visual information
in the wrist vector. Physical readouts retain local force vec-
tors, per-pad force and torque, and intensity statistics; the
visual readout retains a frozen feature summary of the wrist
image. These objectives preserve detail alongside the shared
semantic connection.
The complete vectors are then fused by a multilayer per-
ceptron (MLP) into the joint interaction vector ht, which
the policy consumes. Fusion also receives an availability
mask emt: three binary entries, ordered as left pad, right
pad, and wrist view, are one for supplied inputs and zero
otherwise. Physical and visual readouts supervise the fused
vector as well, and a separate joint semantic head predicts
the same contact and strength concepts. Thus, information
is constrained both before and after fusion, while the shared
projections establish the cross-modal correspondence.

Maintaining semantic access with incomplete observa-

A common semantic reference is also useful when
only part of the local evidence is available.
Following
the principle of modality dropout [25], we withhold entire
modalities and individual pads during training so that the
remaining inputs continue to support the same contact de-
scription. For example, deliberately withholding touch can
leave a valid recorded contact label with which to supervise
a wrist-based estimate. The explicit mask distinguishes an
unavailable pad from a valid pad reporting no contact; a
single remaining pad can still contribute measured evidence
alongside wrist vision.
Valid recorded semantic labels supervise deliberately
masked inputs, while physical and visual preservation losses
use the corresponding available source inputs and valid tar-
gets. With complete inputs, both shared cues and comple-
mentary detail enter the joint vector; with incomplete inputs,
the same interface carries the evidence that remains. We
next use this representation of current interaction to learn its
evolution during manipulation.
3.3. Predictive Action Learning
Manipulation depends on how interaction changes over time.
Similar instantaneous contact readings can occur while a
grasp is being established or while contact is weakening,
making recent observations relevant to the next action. We
encode recent joint representations and proprioceptive his-
tory to condition the current interaction anchor, the feature
through which the action expert accesses local interaction
evidence. This lets the policy use the current state in the
context of its recent evolution.
Learning how interaction evolves.
To learn beyond the
current state, we train action generation alongside prediction
of future scene and interaction observations. We adopt a
three-expert predictive framework [14, 37]: Video predicts
head-view futures, Tactile predicts future joint wrist–tactile
vectors, and Action generates action chunks. The head-view
and interaction prediction tasks cover scene changes and
local interaction changes, respectively.
Future observations provide learning targets, while ac-
tion generation must rely on information available during
execution. Current observations remain clean, unnoised an-
chors, and an attention mask controls which token groups
can exchange information. Action tokens read the current
visual and interaction anchors and other action tokens. Both
the current anchors and Action are blocked from reading
future sensory tokens, preventing future observations from
4


entering action generation through either route. The current
interaction anchor and future interaction targets both encode
the joint wrist–tactile representation. This visibility struc-
ture allows future sensory supervision to shape predictive
learning while actions remain conditioned on observations
available at execution. At execution, the policy generates
action chunks using current observations and history and
replans as new evidence arrives.
Carrying physical meaning into prediction.
To preserve
contact and physical meaning in future predictions, we ap-
ply the frozen physical and semantic readouts learned with
the joint encoder to predicted interaction vectors. Their
losses compare predicted local force, force/torque, intensity
statistics, and contact/strength with the corresponding valid
targets. Gradients pass through these fixed readouts to the
predicted representation. This carries the semantic reference
established between wrist vision and touch into learning how
their joint interaction develops.

3.4. Failure-Conditioned Recovery
Failure-conditioned recovery extends the joint interaction
representation from nominal execution to corrective actions.
When insertion is blocked or effective wiping contact is lost,
recovery combines confirmation of failure with an adjust-
ment informed by the current interaction state. A lightweight
recurrent detector runs on the robot-side controller, indepen-
dently of the action policy. It uses tactile force/torque and
proprioceptive history to distinguish unsuccessful interaction
from normal contact, and initiates correction after sustained
failure evidence.
Correction uses two kinds of context: the failed attempt
and the interaction currently observed. We encode the con-
firmed event together with a snapshot of interaction statistics
as an additional condition for the Action expert, accompa-
nied by an adjustment instruction. The failure context re-
mains fixed during adjustment, while the joint wrist–tactile
representation updates with incoming observations. This
gives the learned policy information about the attempt being
corrected and the state in which each corrective action is
taken. Corrective demonstrations teach actions such as with-
drawing a blocked plug or repositioning the eraser. Correc-
tion ends when the detector confirms task-specific recovery
completion: withdrawal while retaining the plug, or restored
effective contact for wiping. The normal instruction then
resumes and the extra failure condition is disabled. Thus,
the detector determines when to enter and leave correction,
while the policy uses the joint interaction representation to
determine how to act. More details are provided in Ap-
pendix B.
3.5. Training
Training follows three stages, each providing the foundation
for the next:
• Stage 0: SAF Pretraining. Physical reconstruction trains
the adapted SAF tactile backbone to retain local contact
information.
• Stage 1: Joint Wrist–Tactile Representation Learning.
Shared semantic and preservation objectives connect wrist
vision and touch while retaining complementary evidence;
modality and individual-pad dropout expose the encoder
to varying input availability.
• Stage 2: Predictive Action Learning. We freeze the joint
encoder and video VAE to provide a fixed representation
space for current observations and future targets, then train
action generation with sensory prediction. Task-specific
post-training refines execution and adds reviewed correc-
tive demonstrations for insertion and wiping.
The failure detector is trained separately. More details are
provided in Appendix E.
4. Experiments
We first evaluate CoTacWAM on real-world manipulation,
then examine how its representation captures shared contact
semantics and preserves physical interaction detail. We fur-
ther evaluate robustness to missing local observations and the
benefits of temporal learning, connecting task performance
to the representation and its use in control.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Platform and tasks.
We use an Agilex Piper manipula-
tor with two gripper-mounted Xense G1-WS tactile sensors,
a head camera, and a wrist camera. The four real-world
tasks cover whiteboard wiping, plug insertion, tofu grasping,
and water pouring with a paper cup (Fig. 3). Insertion em-
phasizes local alignment, while wiping requires sustained
surface interaction. Tofu grasping requires holding a soft
object through transport, and pouring requires controlled re-
orientation of a held container. These tasks vary in how local
geometry and physical contact inform execution, allowing
us to examine the same learned interaction representation
across distinct manipulation requirements. We report final
task success over 20 trials per method, condition, and task;
detailed evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix A.

4.2. Real-World Manipulation
CoTacWAM achieves the highest success rate on all four
tasks (Table 1). Without retries, CoTacWAM achieves 71.3%
mean success, compared with 35.0% for the strongest base-
line, Fast-WAM, a gain of 36.3 percentage points. The gains
span sustained surface interaction, grasp-and-place, liquid
handling, and insertion, demonstrating effective manipula-
tion across different geometric and contact requirements.
This consistent advantage highlights the practical value of a
policy that combines local interaction evidence with head-
view scene context.
The strongest baseline changes across tasks: N0-TWAM
on wiping, N0-VTLA on insertion and tofu, and Fast-WAM
on pouring. CoTacWAM improves over each, showing a con-
sistent advantage across these different task specializations.
Autonomous retries further improve task completion in
insertion and wiping. Allowing retries raises insertion suc-
cess from 50% to 80% and wiping success from 80% to
100%. These final success rates cover complete trials, in-
cluding the initial attempt and subsequent corrections. The
gains demonstrate the benefit of combining failure confirma-
tion with learned corrective actions, enabling the policy to
complete tasks after unsuccessful attempts.

physical information. The encoders and trained semantic
heads remain frozen throughout these analyses. We examine
contact semantics through the existing readouts and spatial
physical information through matched reconstruction probes,
using episode-disjoint probe-fitting and evaluation subsets.

Retention beyond contact categories.
Beyond contact
categories, the joint representation preserves spatial detail
about how forces are distributed across the tactile pads. We
assess this information by fitting a linear ridge probe from
each complete vector to a coarse map of local force magni-

evaluated on the same held-out samples and target scale.
The joint-vector probe achieves 0.1768 MAE, close to
the tactile-vector probe’s 0.1557 and below task-mean and
within-task-shuffled controls (0.4850 and 0.5815). A task
mean captures a typical spatial pattern, while within-task
shuffling removes the observation–target correspondence.
The lower error relative to both controls shows that the joint
vector retains sample-specific physical information.
Physical retention also extends to force direction. The
native readout predicts signed three-component local force
vectors from the joint representation with normalized MAE
0.0451, compared with 0.0328 from the tactile representa-
tion. Together with the intensity-map probe, this readout
demonstrates that fusion preserves both directional and spa-
tial aspects of the measured interaction.
Matched reconstructions in Fig. 4 show the spatial struc-
ture retained by the joint vector. In insertion, it preserves
the distinct intensity patterns across the two pads. In wiping,
it recovers localized high-intensity regions and the broader
intensity variation around them. These spatial patterns com-

the measured interaction is distributed over the gripper. To-
gether, the semantic readouts and reconstructions show that
the policy’s joint input carries both shared contact meaning
and detailed physical evidence. Additional physical readouts

4.4. Robustness and Temporal Ablations
Missing local observations.
CoTacWAM outperforms all
three tactile baselines under both missing-input conditions
(Table 4). We remove wrist vision or touch throughout wip-
ing and tofu rollouts, retaining head vision and propriocep-
tion. All checkpoints are evaluated without additional fine-
tuning; our joint representation learning includes modality
and single-pad dropout. Without wrist vision, CoTacWAM
retains 70% and 75% success, while all three tactile baselines
achieve 0% on both tasks. Both rates are only ten percentage
points below the complete-input results, showing that the
policy continues effective manipulation using head vision,
touch, and proprioception.
Without touch, CoTacWAM achieves 60% success on
wiping and 55% on tofu grasping, exceeding the strongest
baseline under the same condition by 30 and 20 percentage
points, respectively. Restoring both local modalities further
raises success to 80% and 85%, demonstrating the value of
their complementary evidence. Together with the semantic
readouts, these results support both aims of joint representa-
tion learning: retaining useful interaction information when
an input is missing and exploiting richer evidence when both
are available.
Future supervision and history.

A. Data and Real-World Evaluation Protocol
A.1. Data Collection and Processing
Collection.
We collect manipulation demonstrations through teleoperation. Each recording contains head- and wrist-camera
observations, tactile measurements from the two gripper pads, and measured arm-joint and gripper states. The recordings
include nominal task execution and corrective behavior following unsuccessful attempts.
Alignment and quality control.
Visual, tactile, and robot-state observations are associated using recorded timestamps and
source-frame correspondences. Rectified tactile images and dense force fields are obtained through the sensor processing
pipeline. Validity masks identify missing, stale, or unreliable measurements. Sensor availability, contact-label validity, and
force-target reliability are tracked separately. Contact and strength supervision is derived from sensor observations as described
in Sec. B.6; uncertain intervals are excluded from the corresponding objectives.
Representation learning and evaluation.
Training and evaluation use disjoint source episodes. Normalization and
supervision scales are fitted only on eligible training observations and remain fixed for evaluation. Training samples are drawn
from valid episodes, segments, and temporal windows, with task and contact-state balancing. Modality and single-pad dropout
are applied to available inputs; target validity remains unchanged.
Action supervision.
Predictive action learning uses reviewed nominal windows and, for insertion and wiping, corrective
windows. Nominal windows can come from successful demonstrations or valid nominal portions of retry recordings.
Deliberately incorrect motions, invalid intervals, and unreviewed actions are excluded from action targets. History, future
sensory targets, and action sequences respect temporal ordering and supervision boundaries.

B.6. Supervision Scales and Strength Labels
Binary contact labels are automatic sensor-response proxies for tactile-pad contact with the grasped object. The labeling
rules use fixed engineering thresholds and retain uncertain intervals as unknown. At a pad, positive evidence requires a native
force-field peak of at least 10−3, RMS of at least 10−4, and an RMS/peak ratio of at least 0.03, sustained for two native frames.
Negative evidence requires a peak at most 10−4, RMS at most 10−5, and force-resultant magnitude at most 0.01 for three
frames; available recorded force evidence must also support absence. Low-response closed-gripper intervals, inconsistent
force readings, and invalid observations are marked unknown. Gaps exceeding 0.1 seconds reset confirmation. A frame is
positive if either valid pad is positive, negative only if both pads are valid and negative, and unknown otherwise. Unknown
labels are excluded from semantic supervision and evaluation.
Strength calibration.
Strength labels are generated separately from trusted contiguous force/torque sequences, using the
larger of the two pad force-resultant magnitudes. A training-fitted hysteresis gate determines the zero-strength state; positive
magnitudes are divided into light, medium, and high classes using training quantiles. This auxiliary gate does not relabel the
binary contact target.
The gate uses the 85th training percentile with an onset floor of 1.0, an offset threshold at half the onset, and a two-frame
minimum run length for on/off states. Positive strength boundaries use the one-third and two-thirds quantiles. All thresholds
remain fixed during validation. Contact and strength targets have separate validity masks.

C. Baseline and Ablation Details
C.1. Baseline Methods
The comparison covers vision-based action generation, visual world modeling, and tactile-conditioned policies with different
ways of connecting contact observations to actions. All are evaluated by complete-task success under the protocol in Sec. A.
π0.5.
This vision–language–action model combines a pretrained vision–language backbone with a flow-matching action
expert [27]. Its training recipe combines robot demonstrations with vision–language data to transfer semantic knowledge to
manipulation, while continuous action generation supports fine-grained control. Given camera observations, a task instruction,
and the robot state, the action expert generates a chunk of future controls. Our evaluation uses the instruction-conditioned
manipulation policy with head and wrist images and proprioception, without tactile input.
15


Fast-WAM.
Fast-WAM learns from future video during training while generating actions directly from current observations
at inference [37]. It combines a pretrained Wan video diffusion backbone with an action Transformer through shared attention.
Both future-video prediction and action prediction access the clean current-frame tokens, but the attention mask prevents action
tokens from accessing future-video tokens. Consequently, inference encodes the current visual context once and denoises
the action chunk without generating a future video. Our implementation supplies head and wrist RGB images together with
proprioception and the task instruction.
N0-VTLA.
N0-VTLA adds a predictive tactile pathway to a vision–language–action backbone [24]. A lightweight predictor
combines tactile features with the visual and language context to estimate latent tokens representing the contact change
expected over the upcoming action chunk. These tokens condition the flow-matching action expert directly, rather than entering
the vision–language prefix as additional image tokens. The original training recipe first grounds this predictor in future tactile
targets, then trains the action expert to use its output before joint optimization. In our wiping and tofu evaluations, the policy
receives dense tactile force fields from both pads, including current readings and episode-initial references, alongside camera
observations and proprioception.
N0-TWAM.
N0-TWAM models future vision, touch, and actions with separate video, tactile, and action experts connected
through shared attention [23]. Its causal generation schedule first predicts future scene and contact observations, then conditions
action generation on these predictions and the observation history. The published model also introduces an observed-touch
pathway that supplies current tactile feedback to the action expert. In our evaluation, the deployed model takes multi-view
RGB and two rectified optical tactile streams, with observed history used to update its streaming state between action chunks.
FTP-1.
FTP-1 learns a shared tactile policy across heterogeneous sensors using a morphology-aware tactile token space [36].
Sensor-specific encoders map image, array, or state measurements into tokens associated with functional contact regions,
allowing the tactile expert to share knowledge across sensor types. Built on π0.5, it uses separate vision–language, tactile, and
action experts: the first two process their own streams, while action queries attend to both when generating controls through
flow matching. Our implementation uses current rectified optical images from the two fingertip pads, preserving their distinct
functional-area identities, together with head and wrist images and proprioception.
For a given method and task, missing-input evaluation uses the same trained parameters as its complete-input condition,
without additional fine-tuning. CoTacWAM uses both dense force and rectified tactile appearance in its tactile pathway; the
baseline input-removal procedures are specified below.

A transient force peak or motion pause alone does not establish failure. For example, both successful seating and obstruction
against the socket panel can produce substantial resistance during insertion. Failure confirmation therefore requires the
preceding force and motion history to determine whether the interaction is making effective progress.

motion history. A single-layer, unidirectional GRU with 128 hidden units and a lightweight classification head estimates a

E.2. Correction and Resuming Execution
Once failure is confirmed, the system records an interaction snapshot and supplies it, together with an adjustment instruction,
as an additional condition for the Action expert. During correction, the failure snapshot remains fixed while the current
wrist–tactile representation continues to update. The policy combines this failure context with the evolving interaction state to
generate withdrawal, repositioning, or contact-restoration actions learned from corrective demonstrations.
Correction ends when the detector confirms recovery completion. For insertion, a separate completion head shares the
GRU state and uses changes relative to the failure snapshot to identify withdrawal and reduced obstruction load while the plug
remains held. This head is trained with binary cross-entropy while the failure branch remains fixed. Completion evidence must
persist and remain valid at an action-chunk boundary after at least 32 corrective action steps have executed. For wiping, the
detector uses contact and motion evidence to confirm that effective eraser–surface contact has been restored during wiping
motion.
After recovery confirmation, the adjustment instruction and additional failure condition are disabled, and normal task
execution resumes. A subsequent failure may trigger another correction within the trial budget.
E.3. Validation
Offline event agreement.
For insertion, causal replay against rule-derived event labels gives 95.0% failure recall. Recovery-
completion agreement is 94.7% among attempts with an eligible completion boundary. The combined failure-detection and
recovery-completion rate is 86.3% over all failed attempts.
Real-robot task completion.
Under the retry protocol in Sec. A, autonomous retries raise insertion success from 50%
to 80% and wiping success from 80% to 100%. Each condition uses 20 complete-task trials; final success includes both
initial-attempt successes and successes after correction.
