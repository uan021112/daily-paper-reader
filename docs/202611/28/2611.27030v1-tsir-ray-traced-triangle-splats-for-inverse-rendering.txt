Abstract
Inverse rendering enables realistic relighting for visual ef-
fects and augmented reality. However, overlapping Gaussian
primitives complicate surface recovery and require costly
multi-layer traversal and compositing for light transport. We
propose TSIR, a two-stage inverse rendering method that re-
constructs relightable triangle splats from multi-view images.
The first stage prepares opaque triangle splats with learnable
vertex shading normals. The second stage directly traces
these surfaces to estimate materials through Monte Carlo
integration of direct illumination and indirect reflections,
reducing reliance on multi-layer compositing. Anti-aliasing
improves rendering quality while maintaining efficiency. Ex-
periments demonstrate higher relighting quality than the
evaluated baselines and more efficient anti-aliasing than
supersampling.
1. Introduction
Inverse rendering recovers geometry and materials from
multi-view images, enabling relighting and asset editing
for visual effects, games, and augmented reality. These
tasks require scene representations that capture geometry
and appearance and support light transport under changing
illumination. Neural radiance fields (NeRF) [17] provide
continuous scene representations that have been extended
to inverse rendering [11, 16, 31]. However, repeated field
queries along rays make optimization and relighting compu-
tationally demanding.
To improve rendering efficiency, 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [12] combines explicit Gaussian primitives with
fast rasterization. GS-IR [14] extends this representation
to inverse rendering, using depth regularization for normals
and baked volumes for occlusion and indirect illumination.
IRGS [5] evaluates physically based rendering (PBR) with
Monte Carlo integration and differentiable ray tracing over
the planar primitives of 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS). How-
ever, tracing Gaussian primitives requires traversing and
compositing multiple layers, increasing the cost of relight-
ing. Hybrid methods reduce this traversal cost by combining
Gaussian rendering with mesh ray tracing. GeoSplatting [28]
grounds Gaussians on an optimizable mesh, and GHPT [1]
combines Gaussian rasterization with mesh path tracing.
These methods use Gaussian primitives for appearance ren-
dering and mesh surfaces for light transport, motivating ex-
plicit surfaces that support both operations directly.
Triangle Splatting+ (TS+) [7] provides such explicit sur-

triangle splats. These opaque primitives support direct ray

and compositing. The same triangle surfaces support both
appearance rendering and light transport. We extend this

a two-stage framework that reconstructs relightable triangle

The first stage prepares triangle geometry for subsequent
inverse rendering, with triangle splats becoming opaque
through annealing. We interpolate learnable vertex shad-
ing normals to produce a smoother normal field and sup-
press color patches under deferred shading. Hybrid visibility
blends per-triangle visibility during annealing to mitigate
abrupt changes near shadow boundaries.
The second stage performs inverse rendering on the re-
constructed geometry, evaluating PBR through Monte Carlo
sampling of direct illumination and indirect reflections. Sur-
face shading combines albedo decoded from a shared tri-
plane with visibility and incident radiance queried by Tri-
angle Splats Ray Tracing (TSRT). Our anti-aliasing method
suppresses artifacts at opaque triangle boundaries while
maintaining rendering efficiency. Our contributions are:
• We present TSIR, a two-stage PBR inverse rendering
framework that directly ray traces reconstructed triangle
splats to evaluate visibility and indirect reflections.
• We integrate a shared triplane albedo field into surface
shading and ray tracing, promoting spatially consistent
albedo recovery across triangle splats.
• We develop efficient anti-aliasing to suppress artifacts at
opaque triangle boundaries.
2. Related work
Scene representations.
NeRF [17] represents scenes as
continuous density and view-dependent radiance fields pa-
rameterized by a neural network. NeRF-Casting [23] traces
reflection rays through the radiance field to model consistent
reflections. 3DGS [12] represents scenes with anisotropic
Gaussians and uses differentiable splatting with alpha blend-
ing for real-time rendering.
3D Gaussian Ray Tracing
(3DGRT) [18] enables arbitrary ray queries. Subsequently,
3D Gaussian Unscented Transform (3DGUT) [25] handles
nonlinear projections and integrates with 3DGRT for sec-
ondary rays. Gaussian Opacity Fields [29] improves surface
extraction. 2DGS [9] models surfaces with oriented planar
Gaussian disks and uses perspective-accurate ray-splat inter-
sections for rendering. IRGS [5] introduces differentiable 2D
Gaussian Ray Tracing (2DGRT) for visibility and indirect
radiance queries. Explicit meshes represent surfaces with
connected vertices and faces and store appearance in texture
maps. ExMesh [2] directly optimizes mesh geometry and
textures with adaptive topology updates, using nvdiffrast [13]
for differentiable rasterization and texture sampling. Trian-
gle Splatting [8] optimizes independent triangle primitives
using a differentiable window function. TS+ [7] adds shared
vertices, vertex color interpolation, and an annealing sched-
ule that produces opaque triangles. UTrice [15] ray traces
primitives from the first generation of Triangle Splatting [8],
with spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients stored per triangle.
Our tracer supports vertex attribute interpolation and surface
material queries at ray intersections for inverse rendering.
Inverse rendering.
Inverse rendering recovers geometry
and material properties from images to support relight-
ing and material editing. Existing methods address this
task using neural fields, Gaussian primitives, and explicit
meshes. NeRFactor [31] initializes surface normals and visi-
bility from NeRF and estimates spatially varying reflectance
with a learned bidirectional reflectance distribution function
(BRDF) prior. TensoIR [11] improves efficiency through
tensor factorization and models visibility and indirect il-
lumination, while NeRO [16] separates glossy geometry
reconstruction from BRDF estimation. Explicit Gaussian
rendering reduces repeated neural field evaluations, motivat-
ing 3DGS methods such as GaussianShader [10], GIR [21],
and Relightable 3D Gaussians [4]. GS-IR [14] improves
normals through depth regularization and uses baked occlu-
sion to approximate light visibility for material estimation.
To improve reflective appearance, DeferredGS [26] and GS-
ROR [33] evaluate shading on accumulated surface attributes.
Building on 2DGS, Ref-Gaussian [27] combines deferred
PBR with inter-reflection modeling. IRGS [5] further queries
visibility and indirect radiance through 2DGRT and evalu-
ates the rendering equation through Monte Carlo integration.
Explicit meshes also allow recovered materials to be stored
as editable texture maps. NvDiffRec [19] optimizes mesh
geometry and material textures, and NvDiffRecMC [6] adds
Monte Carlo rendering and denoising. ExMesh++ [3] first
refines mesh geometry and topology, then estimates material
maps on the fixed mesh while tracing secondary rays for
one-bounce diffuse indirect illumination. GeoSplatting [28]
and GHPT [1] also exploit mesh light transport while retain-
ing Gaussian appearance representations. We extend TS+ to
inverse rendering by estimating materials and environment
illumination on reconstructed triangle surfaces.

Hybrid visibility.
Deferred rendering shades blended sur-
face attributes. During annealing, a single binary visibil-
ity query at the blended surface can switch abruptly near
shadow boundaries, causing sudden changes between direct
and indirect specular illumination. Specular reflection is
sensitive to normals, so the optimizer may compensate for
these photometric errors through incorrect normal rotations.
We therefore blend per-triangle binary visibility using the
normalized compositing weights from Eq. (4). This permits
a mixture of direct and indirect specular contributions as
triangle weights change, reducing the need to compensate
for visibility changes by rotating normals. Further analysis
of hybrid visibility is provided in Appendix E.

4.2. Inverse rendering with triangle ray tracing
We estimate materials and environment illumination on the
triangle surfaces reconstructed in Sec. 4.1 by matching PBR
renderings to the input images. TSRT supplies visibility and
incident radiance queries for Monte Carlo shading, while
a shared triplane supplies albedo consistently at rasterized
and ray-traced surface points. We then apply anti-aliasing
(AA) with subpixel visibility estimation to the shaded image,
suppressing triangle boundary artifacts without additional
lighting rays.

triangle splats promotes consistent albedo recovery even
without shared vertices. Roughness, metallic, and shading
normals retain vertex parameters. Architecture parameters
are provided in Appendix A.3.
Anti-aliasing with subpixel visibility estimation.
The
Monte Carlo renderer evaluates lighting at the native pixel
resolution. Repeating this evaluation at subpixel positions,
as in supersampling anti-aliasing (SSAA) [7], increases
lighting-ray cost. Native-resolution nvdiffrast [13] can miss
boundaries of small splats. Our AA estimates subpixel visi-
bility from projected geometry.
For each pixel p, we estimate coverage by resolving the
nearest triangle at each point of a uniform 4 × 4 sample set
Sp using projected support and intersection depth among
triangles passing projection, size, and opacity checks, includ-
ing triangles missed by the pixel center. For a foreground

same triangle. Otherwise, we search a 7 × 7 neighborhood
for the nearest native pixel on that triangle. If none is found,
we select a foreground pixel using spatial proximity and

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental settings
Datasets and metrics.
We evaluate TSIR on two synthetic
datasets, Synthetic4Relight [32] and TensoIR [11]. We use
PSNR, SSIM [24], and LPIPS [30] to evaluate novel view
synthesis, albedo estimation, and relighting. We evaluate
rendered normal maps with mean angular error (MAE) and
roughness with mean squared error (MSE). TSIR’s normal
maps use learned shading normals. The detailed metric
computations follow IRGS [5].
Baselines.
We compare TSIR with six inverse rendering
methods. NvDiffRec [19] optimizes mesh geometry and
material textures using differentiable rasterization. NvD-
iffRecMC [6] combines Monte Carlo rendering and denois-
ing to jointly optimize mesh geometry, materials, and light-
ing. TensoIR [11] uses tensor factorization to represent
radiance fields and estimate surface reflectance. GS-IR [14]
equips 3D Gaussians with normal regularization and cached
occlusion to model indirect illumination. IRGS [5] mod-
els inter-reflections using differentiable 2DGRT. GeoSplat-
ting [28] constructs Gaussians on a mesh, using explicit
surface geometry to guide light transport.
Implementation details.
Implementation details are pro-
vided in Appendix A of the supplementary material.
5.2. Comparison with baselines
We conduct qualitative and quantitative comparisons of novel
view synthesis, material estimation, and relighting on Syn-
thetic4Relight and TensoIR. As shown in Tab. 1, TSIR
achieves competitive results across both datasets. On Syn-

over GS-IR and relighting PSNR by 1.79 dB over IRGS. On
the TensoIR dataset, TSIR improves relighting PSNR by
0.73 dB over the TensoIR baseline. The qualitative com-
parison on Jugs in Fig. 4 shows that TSIR recovers fine
albedo details and accurate roughness, and produces relight-
ing results close to the ground truth under novel illumination.

Appendix I of the supplementary material.

Supplementary Material
This supplementary material provides additional imple-
mentation details, qualitative results on Stanford-ORB, a
comparison with TS+ in the first stage, a comparison of soft
and opaque triangle splats, an ablation of hybrid visibility, an
analysis of triplane albedo, and additional results on TensoIR
and Synthetic4Relight.
A. Additional implementation details
A.1. Data preparation and evaluation
We follow the original training and test splits of TensoIR [11]
and Synthetic4Relight [32]. Each baseline is retrained on
the corresponding training split. We obtain normal priors
using the same model as TS+ [7].
A.2. Optimization settings
All experiments use an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Geometry
reconstruction runs for 30,000 iterations: forward rendering
with direct illumination only at iterations 1 to 5,000, adding
indirect illumination at 5,001 to 10,000, then deferred ren-
dering with hybrid visibility after 10k iterations. Triangle
smoothness and the vertex opacity floor follow the anneal-
ing schedule of TS+ [7]. In Eq. (8), λ = 0.2 throughout
training. Regularization uses β1 = 1.9 × 10−6 only at it-
erations 1 to 4,999, β2 = β3 = 0.05 and β4 = 10 from
iteration 5,001 onward, and β5 = 0.05 only at iterations
5,001 to 10,000. Monocular and consistency supervision
switches from face to shading normals after 10k iterations.
For geometry reconstruction, we adopt the indirect radiance
and mesh visibility implementation of Ref-Gaussian [27]. In-
verse rendering uses Adam and deferred rendering for 20,000
iterations, with weights in Eq. (14) set to γ1 = 1, γ2 = 0.01,
γ3 = γ4 = γ6 = 0.2, γ5 = 0.05, and γ7 = 0.0005.
The learning rates are shared across all scenes. Both
stages use 0.0075 for albedo, 0.005 for vertex roughness
and shading-normal parameters, 0.01 for the environment
map, and 0.0075 and 0.000375 for the constant term and
remaining vertex SH coefficients, respectively. Albedo uses
vertex parameters in the first stage and triplane features and
the decoder in the second stage. The SH rates apply to both
appearance and indirect radiance in the first stage. The first-
stage specular strength and second-stage metallic parameters
both use 0.005. In the first stage, vertex opacity uses 0.03,
and shading-normal optimization starts at iteration 10,001.
Vertex positions use an exponential learning-rate schedule
from 0.0015 to 0.000015 over the 30,000 iterations of the
first stage, with a zero rate for the first 999 iterations.
A.3. Triplane albedo architecture
The albedo field uses XY, YZ, and ZX feature planes, each
with a 512 × 512 grid and eight channels. Positions are nor-
malized to [−1, 1] using the bounding box of the first-stage
geometry, expanded by 1% at both ends of each axis. Bilin-
ear sampling uses aligned grid corners and border padding.
The three sampled feature vectors are concatenated into a
24-dimensional input. The MLP uses fully connected layers
of dimensions 24 →32 →3, with ReLU between the layers
and a sigmoid output rescaled to [0.01, 0.99] in linear RGB.
Plane features are initialized from a zero-mean Gaussian
distribution with standard deviation 0.01. The final decoder
layer has zero weights and a bias that reproduces the inher-
ited constant albedo. The planes and decoder are optimized
jointly with the albedo learning rate in Sec. A.2.
A.4. Rendering details
Inverse rendering training uses 128 uniform hemisphere sam-
ples and 128 samples from the GGX distribution of visible
normals (VNDF) per pixel. Relighting uses 256 uniform,
256 GGX VNDF, and 256 environment map importance
samples per pixel. Both use multiple importance sampling
(MIS). During training, TSRT queries visibility and accu-
mulates vertex SH colors to approximate indirect radiance.
Relighting evaluates one indirect bounce, with secondary
surfaces shaded by unoccluded environment illumination.

G. Further analysis of anti-aliasing
We further analyze the number of subpixel samples used
for visibility estimation and the search for native pixels that
provide sample colors.
Experimental settings.
We use Hotdog from TensoIR and
fix the model after 20,000 second-stage iterations. Native
PBR images are rendered at 800 × 800 with 768 lighting
directions per pixel and indirect illumination enabled, and
are cached for all variants. We evaluate novel view synthe-
sis under the learned illumination and relighting under the
bridge environment.
Effect of subpixel sample number.
We retain the com-
plete color search and vary the uniform sampling grid from
1 × 1 to 2 × 2, 4 × 4, and 8 × 8. The single-sample con-
figuration corresponds to rendering without anti-aliasing.
Table 11(a) shows that increasing the grid from 2 × 2 to
4 × 4 improves novel view and relighting PSNR by 0.53 and
0.11 dB, respectively. Increasing it further to 8 × 8 gives
smaller gains of 0.09 and 0.04 dB, with corresponding im-
provements in SSIM and LPIPS. The quality gains therefore
diminish beyond 16 samples in this scene.

H. Limitations
TS+ annealing accelerates rendering by producing opaque tri-
angles, but reconstruction of highly specular objects remains
challenging. Despite partial vertex sharing, the topology
remains fragmented, leaving small triangles that are difficult
to optimize.
