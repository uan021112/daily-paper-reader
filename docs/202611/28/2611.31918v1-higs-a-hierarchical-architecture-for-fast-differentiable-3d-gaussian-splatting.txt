Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) makes one screen tile the
cell of partitioning, the cell of rasterization, and the unit of
parallel work. Partitioning favors large tiles and rasteriza-
tion small ones, and with one unit per tile the densest tiles
set the frame time. We present Hierarchically Tiled Gaus-
sian Splatting (HiGS), a unified architecture for rendering
and reconstruction that gives each role its own granularity:
Gaussians are partitioned over coarse macro-tiles, raster-
ized over the fine render tiles within them, and processed
in bounded depth-sorted batches. A dense region issues
more batches instead of one longer unit, so frame time fol-
lows the work in a view rather than its densest tile. The
same batches carry reconstruction: compositing supplies
the transmittance and color needed to differentiate each one
independently, leaving optimizer memory traffic as the domi-
nant training cost. We fuse the optimizer into the backward
pass, halve the parameters and moments it stores and up-
dates directly, and defer appearance updates outside the view.
HiGS renders scenes faster than every published method we
evaluate, by the widest margin on views with uneven load,
and reconstructs them faster and in less memory, at equal im-
age quality and with exact front-to-back alpha compositing.
1. Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [21] has become the leading
approach to real-time photorealistic rendering of captured
scenes, combining the fidelity of neural radiance fields [36]
with an explicit, differentiable representation that commodity
GPUs rasterize at high throughput. Reconstruction runs its
rasterizer forward and backward tens of thousands of times,
so rasterization cost governs both reconstruction and display.
Most variants of 3DGS share one pipeline: Gaussians
are projected to the screen, assigned to the tiles they over-
lap, sorted globally by tile and depth into one Gaussian list
per tile, and rasterized with one thread block per tile. The
tile serves three conflicting roles: partition cell, rasteriza-
tion cell, and unit of parallel work. Partitioning favors large
tiles, which reduce the number of (tile, Gaussian) pairs to

Prior work lowers these costs without separating all three
roles. Pairs are reduced, models compressed, and single
stages accelerated [6, 7, 9, 15, 16, 31, 33, 44, 46, 48], and
methods that leave the pipeline pay with approximate com-
positing [18, 23] or custom hardware [29, 51]. Sorting at
a coarser granularity than rasterization [3, 19, 27] shrinks
the sort but leaves each tile one unbounded Gaussian list,
as does rescheduling tiles [12], and dividing the lists of the
densest ones among thread blocks [56] shortens the tail but
keeps the global sort. Each of these remedies changes one
or two roles of the tile, and none carries the change into the
backward pass of training.

ity GPUs that gives each role its own granularity, with one
unit of work for rendering and reconstruction. Gaussians
are binned and depth-sorted over coarse macro-tiles, each a

Three consequences follow from this single decomposition.
Sorting splits into independent macro-tile segments. Every

The same units carry reconstruction, so both passes of a

batch with the transmittance in front of it and the color from
it onward, and batches are differentiated as independently

and we reduce its memory traffic until it, too, scales with
what a view sees: fused into the backward pass, it updates

renders 1.5× to 5.6× faster than recent optimized rasterizers
on standard benchmark scenes, and 6.8× to 20.0× faster on
driving views with a strongly uneven load. It reconstructs
scenes 2.3× to 3.0× faster than the fastest published method
we evaluate, with 1.9× less memory than the most memory-
efficient one, at equal image quality.
Our contributions are the architecture and its three parts:
• a unified architecture for rendering and reconstruction
that separates partitioning, rasterization, and parallel work
around one unit, a bounded depth batch of a macro-tile;
• a renderer on these units, with a segmented depth sort,
load-balanced batch rasterization with inline visibility, and
a stable half-precision (FP16) data path;
• a backward pass over the same batches, which differ-
entiates each independently from boundary state left by
compositing;
• a bandwidth-lean optimizer fused with backward pro-
jection, which updates stochastically rounded 16-bit state
directly and defers appearance updates a view does not
need.
2. Related Work
Kerbl et al. [21] established the tile-based pipeline of 3D
Gaussian Splatting, and gsplat [52] popularized it in a widely
adopted open-source library. Most of the work that fol-
lowed keeps the rendering model and lowers the cost of this
pipeline.
Pair reduction. Many pairs are false positives: a Gaus-
sian’s bounding box overlaps a tile the Gaussian does not
visibly reach. StopThePop [44] introduced opacity-aware
bounds and an exact test of which tiles an ellipse reaches.
FlashGS [9], AdR-Gaussian [48], and Speedy-Splat [16]
derive similar bounds, the first and the last with exact tests
of their own, and Faster-GS [15] adopts them. HiGS tests
ellipse overlap during binning and rasterization, but most of
its pair reduction comes from binning over macro-tiles.
Model-side reduction. Fewer Gaussians also mean fewer
pairs, and work complementary to ours shrinks the model:
by pruning in Speedy-Splat and RadSplat [39], by bud-
geted densification in Taming 3DGS [33], by importance-
weighted sampling in Mini-Splatting [8], by multi-view
consistent densification and pruning in FastGS [45], and
by the quantization and codebook schemes of LightGaus-
sian [7], EAGLES [10], Compact-3DGS [28], and Com-
pressed 3DGS [38]. Kerbl et al. [22] build a level-of-detail
hierarchy over the scene, rather than over the work of ren-
dering it, for very large captures.
Training acceleration. Several of these methods act during
training, whose time is dominated by the backward pass and
the optimizer. The backward pass of 3DGS accumulates
gradients per pixel and contends on atomic updates, which
Taming 3DGS avoids by parallelizing over splats and DIST-
WAR [6] reduces by aggregating a warp’s updates to the
same Gaussian in registers before issuing one atomic per
parameter. LiteGS [30] reduces gradients within the one
warp that owns a tile, and culls and updates Morton-ordered
Gaussians in clusters. On the optimizer side, Faster-GS pairs
the per-splat backward pass with its own Adam implemen-
tation, which it can fuse into that pass, and reports up to
5× faster training end to end. GS-Scale [26] keeps only
geometry on the GPU and defers the Adam updates of the
other parameters, held in host memory, while a Gaussian is
outside the view frustum. Other methods lower the work
of a run rather than the cost of a step: DashGaussian [2]
schedules the rendering resolution and the Gaussian count,
SAD-GS [32] shortens convergence by guiding densification
with multiscale image structure, and Grendel [57] distributes
training over many GPUs. These complement HiGS, which
redesigns the training step itself.
Rendering acceleration. In the forward pass, GPU im-
plementations have tuned the pipeline stage by stage, most
keeping its global sort and the screen tile as the unit of work.
FlashGS fuses the preprocessing kernels and pipelines the
dependent Gaussian fetches in software, and TC-GS [31]
reformulates the alpha-blending exponent as a Tensor-Core
matrix multiplication, reporting up to 2.2× speedup when in-
tegrated into 3DGS. Splatshop [46] narrows the sort keys by
ordering visible splats by 32-bit depth before tile-fragment
creation. TileGS [47] replaces the global sort by depth bins
inside each tile and sorts only selected bins exactly. Against
uneven load, StopThePop balances the per-Gaussian stages,
and AdR-Gaussian trains with a loss that evens the number of
Gaussians per pixel. In the rasterizer, Balanced 3DGS [12]
counters load imbalance by letting thread blocks claim tiles
at run time, shrinking the tile to four pixels, and spread-
ing the Gaussians of a pixel across the threads of a warp.

rendering model itself, which HiGS keeps. Exact front-to-

through per-ray ordering in StopThePop, band-limited prim-

and distorted cameras [50].
3. Rendering Architecture
The HiGS pipeline (Fig. 2, see Sec. B for implementation
details) is a hierarchy of work units, fed by fused projection
and spherical-harmonics (SH) evaluation as in recent rasteriz-
ers [9, 15], which emits each Gaussian’s screen-space center,
depth, inverse-covariance factors, color, and visibility.
3.1. Macro-tile hierarchy and work decomposition
The hierarchy is built over the standard rasterization primi-
tive, the render tile, here 8 × 8 pixels. HiGS groups M=32
adjacent render tiles into a macro-tile, arranged as 8 × 4 tiles
spanning 64 × 32 pixels. Binning and sorting produce one
depth-ordered Gaussian list per macro-tile (Sec. 3.2), while
visibility and blending operate per render tile (Sec. 3.3).
Within each macro-tile, Gaussian data is loaded into local
storage once and reused across all M member render tiles,
amortizing the fetch over fine-grained output.
Each list is split into Gaussian batches of at most
Br=1,024 Gaussians, each (macro-tile, batch) pair form-
ing an independent processing unit. The bound holds for
every list, dense or not, because it is what lets a batch re-
side in local storage. A macro-tile with n Gaussians thus
spawns ⌈n/Br⌉units. Within each processing unit, ren-
der tiles are claimed dynamically by worker groups from
a shared tile work queue Q, and a per-worker scheduling
buffer R absorbs the per-tile variability in visible-Gaussian
count so the blending pipeline runs at a fixed rate. The
schedule is therefore density-proportional rather than screen-
area-proportional: compute scales with Gaussian count, and
no single render tile can gate the frame the way the slowest
tile gates a one-thread-group-per-tile assignment (Fig. 3).
Every level of this hierarchy maps onto a level of the GPU
(Fig. 2). A processing unit is one thread block whose batch
resides in on-chip memory, our local storage; a worker group
is P=32 threads that execute in lockstep; and each thread
keeps its pixel accumulators in registers. Preallocated macro-
tile lists and batch partial buffers let units run independently,
with storage growing with the batch count.

3.2. Partitioning and segmented depth sort
Binning constructs a depth-keyed Gaussian list per macro-
tile. List lengths are data-dependent, so building the lists in
parallel requires reconciling write positions without serial-
izing through global atomics. Kerbl et al. [21] leave this to
the sort, whose composite (tile, depth) key groups unordered
records by tile and orders them by depth, which makes it
global.
Our binning produces the per-macro-tile lists during bin-
ning, leaving only in-segment ordering for the sort. Gaus-
sians are processed in partitioning batches of Bp=8,192,
each handled cooperatively by one group of threads that
keeps a private histogram in local storage, one counter per
macro-tile of the frame, and accumulates overlap counts with
local rather than global atomics. The histogram fits because
the frame’s macro-tile count is modest, roughly 1,000 entries
at 1080p and 4,000 at 4K; a render-tile histogram would
carry 30,000 to 130,000 entries, exceed local storage by an
order of magnitude, and force a global-atomic path. A hier-
archical prefix scan turns the batch-local counts into write
offsets, and a fill pass repeats the batches and writes (depth,
Gaussian-id) pairs directly into each macro-tile’s range.
Gaussians are paired with macro-tiles by a conservative
per-row scan of the opacity-threshold ellipse, following Ac-
cuTile [16] one level coarser. HiGS thus rasterizes over 8×8
tiles yet averages only 1.51M pairs at 1080p and 2.21M at
4K on Mip-NeRF 360, 2.7× and 4.3× fewer than gsplat’s
opacity-aware bounding boxes at 16×16 tiles, which already
form 2.5× and 3.1× fewer than at 8 × 8. Macro-tile binning
gives over half of our reduction, the ellipse test the rest.
Each macro-tile is then one segment of a segmented sort,
and segments are independent by construction. A segment
already identifies its macro-tile, so its key is the 32-bit depth
alone, rather than gsplat’s 64-bit composite key. Removing
tile bits shortens the radix sort and shrinks its records from
12 to 8 bytes. Splatshop [46] narrows the key within the
global sort, whereas here the tile dimension disappears from
it. As resolution grows, the render-tile pair count grows
but segments stay bounded, because more macro-tiles dis-
tribute the Gaussians into more, smaller segments. A general-
purpose segmented sort still slows down as the segments
multiply. We therefore sort with a pipeline of six stages that
run without host synchronization: a cascade of four block
radix sorts [35], one per class of segment size, and, for the
segments above the largest class, a sort of fixed-size runs
followed by their merge with merge path [11]. The sort then
stays in the same time range from 1080p to 4K (Sec. 5.3).
3.3. Batch rasterization and compositing
3DGS materializes explicit per-tile Gaussian lists before ras-
terization can begin. Our rasterizer replaces that stage with
visibility determination inline with the Gaussian load, at low
marginal cost. Each processing unit consists of W worker
groups of P threads and handles its (macro-tile, batch) pair
in two phases, followed by a compositing pass shared by all
units (Fig. 2).
Phase 1: load and inline visibility. The unit cooperatively
loads its batch into local storage in mini-batches of P Gaus-
sians, one per thread. During the load, each Gaussian’s
opacity-threshold ellipse is tested for overlap with all M ren-
der tiles, producing a per-Gaussian tile mask. GS-TG [19]
materializes these masks before rasterization, which on a
GPU serializes their generation with the sort, and SHARP-
GS [3] likewise builds one per batch of 32 Gaussians; com-
puting them during the cooperative load avoids that memory
round trip. The load identifies which tiles each Gaussian
overlaps, but blending needs the reverse, which Gaussians
each tile sees. A transpose of each mini-batch’s P×M bit
matrix converts one into the other: every render tile then
holds a P-bit mask per mini-batch, and their concatenation
is its visibility over the batch.
Phase 2: per-tile rasterization. Worker groups claim active
render tiles from Q, so no group idles while tiles remain.
For a claimed tile, the group walks its masks and extracts
only the visible Gaussians from local storage. The schedul-
ing buffer R accumulates their indices and drains them in
batches of Bd=64 through an unrolled blending core, which
decouples variable-rate mask iteration from fixed-rate blend-
ing arithmetic. After each drain, a unanimous vote tests
whether every pixel of the tile has reached transmittance
saturation; if so, the group claims the next tile.
Compositing. A macro-tile’s list may span several batches
processed by independent units, so per-tile results are partial:
batch i produces, per pixel, a local color Ci and a local
transmittance Ti, blended over its own Gaussians only. Front-
to-back alpha blending is associative over contiguous depth
ranges [43], as Balanced 3DGS and CaT-GS also use, so

The order is independent of the per-frame depth sort, and the
same coherence carries the optimizer (Sec. 4.4).
4. Reconstruction
A training step renders one view with the pipeline of Sec. 3,
differentiates the image loss through it, and updates the pa-
rameters. We retain the objective and densification policy
of 3DGS as implemented in gsplat. The independent pro-
cessing units carry the backward pass, after which optimizer
memory traffic dominates the step, and we reduce it until
appearance updates scale with what a view sees, not with the
scene size.
4.1. Differentiating independent batches
The gradient of a Gaussian’s opacity α at a pixel depends
on the transmittance in front of it and the color composited
behind it. 3DGS recovers them by walking each tile’s list
back to front and dividing transmittance by 1 −α, StopThe-
Pop [44] walks front to back and subtracts from the final
color, and Taming 3DGS [33] and Faster-GS walk front to
back from per-pixel state stored every 32 Gaussians. None
fits HiGS directly, because a tile’s list spans independently
rendered batches, and state stored per tile at that density
would forfeit the memory economy of shared loading.

pletes the boundary state of every batch. Its front-to-back
walk already forms the transmittance Fi in front of batch i;

4.2. Optimizer fused with projection
Accelerating rasterization exposes Adam’s bandwidth cost:
every step reads and writes all parameters and their two mo-
ments, a state of 708 bytes per Gaussian in FP32 with degree-
3 SH. Behind our rasterizer, PyTorch’s Adam takes longer
5


than the forward and backward passes together (Sec. 5.4),
and the three measures discussed below reduce its traffic.
The first measure removes the gradients from memory. As
in Faster-GS, the update is applied inside the backward pass:
backward projection assigns one thread per Gaussian, which
differentiates projection and SH evaluation and therefore
holds the gradient of the Gaussian’s geometry in registers,
together with both factors of every SH gradient. It applies
Adam there, the threads of a worker group updating their SH
coefficients cooperatively, and writes each parameter and
moment once. The only gradients that ever reach memory are
the rasterizer’s accumulators, 40 bytes per Gaussian, which
are needed because many tiles contribute to one Gaussian.
4.3. Low-precision parameters and moments
Fusion removes the gradient traffic but not the state. The
second measure halves it: positions stay in FP32, all other
parameters (rotation, log-scale, opacity logit, and SH co-
efficients) are stored in FP16, and both Adam moments in
bfloat16 (BF16) [20], for 360 bytes per Gaussian instead
of 708. These arrays are the optimizer’s own state, not a
rounded copy of FP32 parameters, so the renderer reads ex-
actly what the optimizer wrote. Projection, its derivative,
and Adam arithmetic remain in FP32; only the stores are
narrowed.
Narrowing a store by rounding to nearest discards every
update below half a unit in the last place, which the small
learning rates of splatting optimization make common. We
round stochastically [13], as Zamirai et al. [54] do for the
weights of BF16 training, and round the moments as well:
the stored value is one of the two representable neighbors
of the exact result, chosen with probability proportional
to proximity, so the stored value equals the exact one in
expectation. With rounding to nearest, reconstruction quality
degrades measurably; with stochastic rounding it matches
FP32 storage (Sec. 5.4).
4.4. Visibility-proportional appearance updates
What remains is mostly appearance: SH coefficients are
48 of a Gaussian’s 59 scalars, yet a training view of the
garden scene of Mip-NeRF 360 culls 66% of the Gaussians
on average, and their gradients are zero. Skipping them, as
Taming 3DGS suggests, Faster-GS evaluates, and LiteGS
does for whole clusters, is not equivalent to updating them,
since Adam’s first moment keeps moving a parameter after
its gradient vanishes, which Ding et al. [4] analyze as the
implicit update. A zero-gradient step depends only on the
stored moments and the step index, however. As sparse
online learning does for regularization [5, 25], we apply
such steps lazily: a culled coefficient stays untouched until a
view needs it and then takes all the steps it missed in closed
form.

We record the last update once per group of P=32 con-
secutive Gaussians, which one worker group of our opti-
mizer updates. A group with no visible Gaussian skips its
SH update, a worker group uniform decision that leaves
its 288 bytes per Gaussian of coefficients and moments un-
touched, and any other group updates all of its members.
Geometry is updated at every step, since it decides visibility.
A record per Gaussian would skip little more, because stor-
age is in the Morton order that already shortens rendering
(Sec. 3.4): on garden, 98% of culled Gaussians lie in fully
culled groups, even after densification ends. In the order
that gsplat’s densification leaves, with new Gaussians ap-
pended, 11% do. Faster-GS reorders Gaussians every 5,000
iterations to reduce cache misses and divergent control flow,
and LiteGS after densification steps. Here the order decides
how much appearance work is deferred, and every densi-
fication step restores it in one gather of all per-Gaussian
arrays, with each copy or pair of split children written in
its parent’s place. The optimizer then moves 375 bytes per
Gaussian and step, against 1,416 for a dense FP32 update,
which reads and writes the whole state once (see Sec. C for
detailed derivations).

6. Conclusion
HiGS starts from one observation: partitioning, rasterization,
and parallel work need not share a tile. With a granularity
for each, sorting shrinks to small independent segments, data
is loaded once and shared, and no single dense tile gates the
frame, all as consequences of one decomposition and not
as separate optimizations. Reconstruction inherits the same
units and the rule behind them, to spend work only where a
view needs it, down to the optimizer’s memory traffic. The
result renders and reconstructs faster than every published
method we evaluate, and reconstructs in less memory, with
exact compositing and equal image quality.
This parallelism is bought with memory and bandwidth.
Every batch reserves and writes its own output and is raster-
ized even where opaque foreground hides it, so in a degener-
ate scene whose Gaussians all cover the whole frame, these
costs grow with every hidden layer. Captured scenes are far
from that extreme, and in all our tests the balanced schedule
outweighs the work that compositing discards, most of all on
views with a strongly uneven load (Fig. 3). Because a unit
needs only its batch and the boundary state of its tiles, and
appearance parameters rest untouched while out of view, we
see scenes beyond one GPU’s memory, spread over several
GPUs or held partly out of core, as the natural next step.

A. Introduction
This appendix provides implementation details of the render-
ing pipeline (Sec. B) and the reconstruction pipeline (Sec. C),
including pseudocode, data layouts, and derivations. It then
reports additional results on performance and image quality
(Sec. D). We follow the notation of the main paper, which
Tab. S1 summarizes. We will release the source code of our
implementation upon publication.
B. Rendering Pipeline
B.1. Packed SH coefficients
In the 32-byte SH representation of Sec. 3.4 of the main
paper, the constant term keeps 16 bits of luma and 15 bits
per chroma channel of the YCoCg basis. Each of the 15
higher-order coefficients is quantized to 6 bits of luma and
4 bits per chroma channel after square-root companding,
with one scale per SH basis function and channel, the 99.99th
percentile of its magnitude over Gaussians of opacity 0.005
or more. Decoding inline from this representation shortens
projection and SH evaluation by 35% at 1080p and 30% at
4K relative to streaming uncompressed FP16 coefficients.
B.2. Segmented depth sort
Each stage of the sorting pipeline is one kernel, and the host
launches the six back to back.

Cascade. Each tier is launched with one thread block per
segment, and a block returns immediately unless its segment

Larger segments. The last tier does not sort a segment
above 8,192 keys: it divides the segment into runs of 8,192
keys and lays out the binary tree of pairwise merges that
joins them. The run sort then sorts every run as a tier sorts
a segment, and the merge executes the trees. Each merge
is partitioned by the GPU merge path [11] into balanced
subproblems, and a parent merge is scheduled on the device
once its children are complete. These two stages read what
the stage before them wrote and run in sequence. Each runs
a fixed number of thread blocks that claim their work on the
device, runs from a counter and merges from a queue, so
the host never reads back how many large segments a frame
holds.

C.2. Fused optimizer and stochastic rounding
The fused pass applies Adam with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999,
and εA = 10−15. A parameter is moved by the unrounded
moments. The gradient of the color with respect to the view
direction contributes to the gradient of the mean, so the
mean is updated last, after the SH coefficients. Because
the gradients do not persist, the densification statistics of
3DGS, the number of views and the norm of the screen-
space gradient of the center, are accumulated in the same
pass.
A store adds uniformly random bits to the bits it discards
and then truncates, 16 bits for a BF16 moment and 13 for
an FP16 parameter. The bits are a hash of the run seed, the
step, and the element index, so there is no generator state to
store or to carry through densification. The stored value is
thus unbiased in the normal range of the format. Widening
an FP16 parameter to FP32 is exact, so a scene trained in
this layout renders bit for bit like an FP32 scene holding the
same values.

C.4. Densification with spatial ordering
A densification step takes the Morton codes of the means,
clamped to a grid of 210 cells per axis fixed at the start of
training over the bounding box of the initial means, widened
by 10%. Its gather drops the pruned Gaussians and carries
the moments with the parameters, and the step recorded for
every group is then set to the current step. From the same
state and random stream, the resulting set of Gaussians and
optimizer state equals that of gsplat’s densification.

On the garden scene of Mip-NeRF 360 (Fig. S1), HiGS
leads every method at every budget and at both resolutions.
From the smallest model to the largest, 18 times the Gaus-
sians, its frame time grows 18.4× at 1080p and 12.8× at
4K. Its lead over gsplat narrows as the model grows, be-
cause per-Gaussian projection, which every method pays,
takes a growing share of the frame: at 1080p, 29% of the
HiGS frame at 5M and 38% at 90M. Among the per-tile
pipelines, frame time orders the methods as the pair count
does: in both rows of Fig. S1, 3DGS stands above gsplat
and StopThePop at every budget, and these above FlashGS,
Faster-GS, TC-GS, and Speedy-Splat, which form the fewest
pairs. SHARP-GS is the fastest baseline for the two smallest
models, then falls behind FlashGS, and the lead of HiGS
over it widens with the model. Its binning and rasterization
are the cause: from 5M to 90M they take 21.7× longer at
4K, against 10.2× for those of HiGS, while projection grows
alike in both.
D.2. Training performance
HiGS is the fastest in every stage of a steady-state training
step on every scene (Fig. S2). Its step takes 1.64 ms on
average, against 4.45 ms for Faster-GS, 10.20 ms for gsplat,
and 20.44 ms for 3DGS. Binning and the two rasterization
passes, whose work is the tile–Gaussian pairs, are 2.3×
to 19.5× faster than in the other methods, and in 3DGS
backward rasterization alone is more than half of the step.

The training loss (Fig. S3) follows through the run what
the image metrics of Tab. 3 of the main paper measure at its
end. With every store rounded to nearest, the loss over the
last 5,000 steps is 14.9% above that of FP32 parameters and
moments, the second row of that table, and the gap is still
widening. The drift belongs to the moments: with only the
moments rounded to nearest the gap still widens, to 5.0%
over the last 5,000 steps. A second moment decays by the
factor β2 at each step, a change of 0.1% against a spacing
of BF16 values of 0.4% to 0.8%, so the second moment of
a parameter that is updated at every step is rounded back
to its previous value and can only grow. It then records the
largest squared gradient the parameter has met rather than a
recent average, and the steps that Adam divides by it shrink
as training proceeds. With only the parameters rounded to
nearest the gap settles instead at a constant 1.7%, the cost of
the updates below half the spacing of FP16 values that such
a store discards. The two do not simply add: rounding both
to nearest costs about twice the sum of the two. With both
rounded stochastically the loss stays within 0.2% of FP32
over the whole run, a remainder we attribute to the noise that
every store adds. The excursions of the relative loss before
step 15,000 follow the opacity resets.

D.3. Image quality
Against the test set, the differences between the rasterizers
lie below the residual error of the trained model (Tab. S6).
Against the FP32 gsplat renderer they span tens of decibels,
and the lower levels trace to specific design choices. Speedy-
Splat and StopThePop reproduce the reference to numerical
precision. The fixed 3σ tile bound of 3DGS can omit con-
tributions above the 1/255 threshold, as StopThePop notes,
Faster-GS tests termination after compositing and does not
clamp opacity from above, and FlashGS writes an 8-bit pixel
buffer. At 8 bits per channel, the level of HiGS corresponds
to a root-mean-square error of about 0.1 code values.
