ABSTRACT
Existing legged-locomotion systems commonly rely on depth im-
ages or online elevation maps. However, depth-based policies suf-
fer from perceptual sim-to-real discrepancies due to imperfect depth
sensor modeling, while map-based approaches are vulnerable to drift
and misalignment. LiDAR offers direct measurements of the sur-
rounding geometry, but raw LiDAR scans lack a compact, structured
representation for high-frequency control. We propose a LiDAR per-
ception pipeline that maintains perceptual consistency across simu-
lation and reality through sensing- and representation-level align-
ment. At the sensor level, we recover frame-wise angular sampling
patterns from the physical LiDAR and replay them in simulation
with GPU ray casting. At the representation level, simulated and real
returns undergo the same spherical range image encoding, yielding
a fixed-size policy observation for end-to-end training and direct de-
ployment without teacher–student distillation, online elevation map-
ping, or adaptation. Experiments show that our range-image poli-
cies achieve stronger sim-to-real transfer than depth- and elevation-
map-based baselines, while ablations confirm the importance of the
proposed sensing components. More visualizations and real-world
videos are available at the https://lidar-img.github.io.

1. INTRODUCTION
Learning-based control has enabled legged robots to acquire increas-
ingly agile locomotion skills [1, 2]. Extending these capabilities to
challenging terrain, however, relies on a perception representation
that faithfully represents the geometry and remains consistent be-
tween simulation and real-world deployment.
Existing methods primarily use depth images [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] or
online elevation maps [9, 10, 2, 11, 12]. Many depth-based meth-
ods distill a teacher with access to a privileged local height map into
a student conditioned on onboard depth [3, 4, 5, 13], yet this su-
pervision is ambiguous when terrain configurations requiring differ-
ent actions are indistinguishable in the student’s partial view [14].
More fundamentally, simulators render clean depth, whereas real
sensors exhibit illumination- and motion-dependent artifacts, includ-
ing missing values, boundary errors, and jitter. Generic noise mod-
els cannot fully reproduce these artifacts [4, 15, 16, 17], creating a
sim-to-real mismatch that can distort terrain perception and degrade
policy performance on hardware. LiDAR provides direct range mea-
surements that are less sensitive to illumination than camera-based
depth. Although raw scans can be processed by dedicated point-
cloud encoders [18, 19, 20], many legged locomotion systems in-
stead rely on an additional online mapping pipeline that uses pose-
based registration, temporal fusion, and filtering to construct robot-
centric elevation maps [21, 22]. The accuracy of these maps, how-
ever, depends critically on the pose estimates used to register suc-
cessive measurements. Odometry or SLAM drift can therefore intro-
duce gaps, spurious steps, and spatial misalignment with the physical
terrain [22], compromising control decisions that depend on accurate
local geometry.
In this paper, we convert LiDAR signals into structured spheri-
cal range images that directly condition a locomotion policy. To im-
prove sim-to-real transfer, we address perceptual discrepancies by
aligning the sensing process and observation representation across
simulation and deployment. At the sensor level, we recover frame-
wise angular scan patterns from the physical LiDAR and replay them
in simulation through parallel ray casting.
Articulated robot ge-
ometry is included to model self-occlusion. At the representation
level, simulated and real returns undergo the same gravity-aligned
temporal aggregation and rasterization onto a fixed angular grid.
This shared encoding preserves angular organization and local range
structure while providing a compact, fixed-size policy input in both
domains. The resulting pipeline supports end-to-end policy training
in simulation and direct deployment without teacher–student distil-
lation, online elevation mapping, or post-training adaptation.
We compare our range-image policy with alternative perception
designs, such as depth images and elevation maps, on challenging
terrains. Our method outperforms these alternatives by a substantial
margin in sim-to-real traversal success. Ablations assess the pro-
posed sensing and representation design.

3. EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments address three questions: Q1: Can the proposed Li-
DAR perception pipeline support learning challenging locomotion
skills? Q2: Does the range-image policy achieve stronger sim-to-
real locomotion transfer? Q3: Do our sensor-level simulation com-
ponents: measured scan-pattern replay, self-occlusion modeling, and
LiDAR domain randomization, improve sim-to-real transfer?
3.1. Experimental Setup
We evaluate in simulation and on two Unitree Go2 robots equipped
with Unitree L1 and L2 LiDARs, respectively. To enable direct com-
parison with Extreme Parkour [5], we use its native gap, step, and
hurdle terrain generators together with the same training and test
terrain maps. The difficulty ranges are 0.1–0.9 m for gap width,
0.1–0.45 m for step height, and 0.1–0.4 m for hurdle height. For
each method and terrain, we first select in simulation the highest dif-
ficulty L at which the method achieves at least 80% success, and
then evaluate the same L on hardware. Each terrain is tested in
20 trials. We follow the Extreme Parkour implementation for the
depth baseline. For the elevation-map baseline, we use the stage-1
local height-map policy from Extreme Parkour in simulation. During
hardware deployment, the elevation map is constructed using Point-
LIO for odometry and the GridMap implementation released with
PGTT [24]. All LiDAR-based baselines and ablations use the L1

3.2. Implementation Details
We train 4096 parallel Isaac Gym environments on one RTX 4090
GPU. The terrain curriculum, rewards, action space, PPO hyperpa-
rameters, control frequency, and PD gains follow the Stage-1 teacher
of Extreme Parkour [5]. We replace its privileged scandots with
a 20 × 40 range image encoded by an MLP with hidden dimen-
sions [128, 64, 32].
The actor uses [512, 256, 128] hidden units.
We collect 1500 real LiDAR scans to recover the frame-wise an-
gular patterns, sampling N = 1000 rays per L1 scan and N =
4000 per L2 scan. The recovered patterns are replayed cyclically
in acquisition order at each LiDAR update. The simulated update
rate is randomized over 14–16 Hz, and returns within the preced-
ing ∆T = 0.1 s are aggregated into each range image. Percep-
tion randomization is sampled per episode as ϵρ, ϵβ ∼U(−4◦, 4◦),
ϵb ∼U(−0.05, 0.05) m, and ϵr ∼U(−0.05, 0.05) m. The policy
requires no distillation, mapping, or online adaptation.

locomotion skills. Table 1 examines whether the proposed range-

depth-based Stage-2 student also reaches L = 9, with only modest

We also observe less natural gaits and more frequent terrain contacts
with these two policies. We attribute this to the difficulty of ex-
tracting control-relevant geometry from irregular, unstructured point
sets. PointNet partially alleviates this issue, but still learns from an
implicitly structured set representation. Our range image instead
organizes LiDAR returns into a fixed spherical grid before policy

A2: The range-image policy achieves stronger sim-to-real loco-
motion transfer. Although the depth and elevation-map baselines
both reach L = 9 in simulation, their performance drops sharply on
hardware at the same terrain levels (0.9 m gap, 0.45 m step, and 0.4 m
hurdle). The depth policy achieves only 40/35/75% success. Across
hardware trials, depth measurements varied substantially with illu-
mination and robot motion, with unstable invalid pixels around ob-
ject boundaries. Failed step trials showed that these artifacts dis-
torted the perceived platform edge and were frequently accompanied
by premature or delayed front-leg lifting. We attribute these fail-
ures partly to this observation shift, absent from the simulated depth
used to train the distilled student. The elevation-map policy, despite
the strongest simulation performance, drops to 35/0/5%. Its Stage-
1 policy is trained with complete privileged height maps, whereas
real maps must be reconstructed from partial LiDAR observations.
Obstacle occlusion often produced incomplete local height maps on
the step and hurdle tasks, while accumulated Point-LIO drift further
misaligned the extracted height maps. The gap geometry remained
visible, consistent with its higher success rate. Our range-image
policies retain substantially more simulated performance. L1 and
L2 achieve 60/55/100% and 65/50/95% real-world success, averag-
ing 71.7% and 70.0%, versus 50.0% for depth and 13.3% for eleva-
tion maps. Their sim-to-real retentions are 76.8% and 73.7%, com-
pared with 53.6% and 13.6%. We attribute this smaller gap to sensor-
and representation-level alignment design in our pipeline. Figure 3
further visualizes this cross-domain consistency. Raw-LiDAR and
PointNet policies also exhibit unnatural motions and frequent body
or joint contacts on hardware, consistent with their weaker simula-
tion performance.
A3: Sensor-level simulation components improve transfer. We
also isolate sensing components for sim-to-real transfer while keep-
ing the range-image representation fixed. All ablations reach L = 9
with 90–100% simulated success, yet differ substantially on hard-
ware. The largest degradation occurs without scan-pattern replay:
replacing the recovered patterns with a fixed uniform pattern re-
duces mean real-world success from 71.7% to 26.7%, despite nearly
unchanged simulation performance. Thus, matching only the ray
count and field of view is insufficient, reproducing the physical sen-
sor’s frame-wise angular sampling is important for transfer. Remov-
ing LiDAR-specific domain randomization reduces mean success
to 56.7%, showing that range and preprocessing perturbations help
absorb residual sensing mismatch. Self-occlusion modeling has a
smaller but consistent effect, with mean success decreasing to 68.3%
and 5-point drops on both step and hurdle. These ablations reveal a
gap that simulation alone cannot expose: the policies remain equally
capable in simulation, while sensing fidelity determines how much
of that capability transfers to hardware.
4. CONCLUSION
We presented a LiDAR perception pipeline for end-to-end locomo-
tion that aligns sensing and representation between simulation and
reality. The proposed spherical range image provides a structured
LiDAR observation that supports learning challenging locomo-
tion skills, while scan-pattern replay, self-occlusion modeling, and
shared encoding reduce perceptual discrepancies across domains.
Experiments show stronger hardware transfer than other baselines,
and ablations identify scan-pattern replay as the most influential
component, with LiDAR-specific domain randomization and self-
occlusion modeling providing additional gains.
