Abstract
Spatial reasoning across multiple views requires infer-
ring the 3D relations between objects and the camera from
2D observations, which depends on cross-view correspon-
dence and scene geometry that vision-language models
(VLMs) do not reliably extract. Pretrained vision encoders
already capture this information in their latent features,
with CLIP linking objects to image regions, DINOv3 match-
ing semantics across views, and VGGT encoding multi-view
geometry. However, VLMs do not benefit from these features
when we present them directly in context as grayscale fea-
ture images. We therefore propose LatentParse, a training-
free framework that parses latent features into explicit spa-
tial evidence for VLMs. In LatentParse, an analyst VLM
probes the features of all three encoders with predefined
tools that locate objects, track them across views, and mea-
sure motion between views. It then distills the results into
a concise text report, which is provided together with the
images to the VLM to answer the question. We evaluate
LatentParse on MMSI-Bench, MV-RoboBench and SPAR-
Bench, where it improves all five VLMs over the photo-
only baseline by 0.7–2.4 percentage points on average. On
MMSI-Bench, it raises Qwen3.8-27B from 34.4% to 39.5%,
with the largest improvements of 19.2 percentage points on
the camera-related questions. These results suggest that la-
tent features become useful for spatial reasoning once they
are parsed into relation evidence, without training. Code is
available at https://github.com/Harvard-AI-
and-Robotics-Lab/LatentParse.

Pretrained vision encoders offer a complementary source
of this information. In particular, CLIP [29], DINOv3 [32]
and VGGT [35] are trained with image–text alignment, self-
supervision and multi-view 3D supervision, respectively.
Preliminary probing studies reveal that these features en-
code rich semantic and geometric information [2, 4, 26, 50],
making them a reliable prior for spatial reasoning. Recent
studies exploit this prior by training the VLMs to inter-
pret encoder features. For example, Spatial-MLLM [39],
VG-LLM [51] and VLM-3R [5] connect visual encoders
to the language model through learned projectors or fusion
modules and fine-tune on spatial instruction data.
Such
training must be repeated for each encoder–VLM pair,
which is computationally expensive and cannot be applied
to closed-source VLMs. Training-free methods, including
ViperGPT [33], SpatialPIN [24] and SandboxVLM [23], in-
stead support reasoning with decoded outputs such as depth
maps, reconstructions or 3D boxes. These outputs are fixed
before the question is known and may discard the cross-
view correspondence and region similarity that the latent
features encode and many spatial questions require.
A natural alternative is to use the latent features them-
selves as context, which require neither training nor de-
coding and leave the VLM free to decide what to measure
for each question. However, as Figure 2 shows, presenting
these latent features to a VLM naively as image input brings
little benefit, likely because the information they carry lies
in the relations between patches rather than in their appear-
ance.
To exploit these relations and realize these bene-
fits, we propose LatentParse, a training-free framework that
parses the relations between patches into explicit evidence
and summarizes it in a text report that an arbitrary VLM can
use in context, as illustrated in Figure 3.
LatentParse operates on the last-layer features of
CLIP [29], DINOv3 [32] and VGGT [35], which respec-
tively ground object names in image regions, compare re-
gions within and across views, and relate the views geomet-
rically. On these features, a library of tools locates named
objects, tracks them across views, measures content motion,
and rates each measurement by consensus. An analyst VLM
that never sees the photos calls these tools in a Python sand-
box [16, 37] and writes a report. The same VLM then an-
swers the question in a fresh context from the photos and
this report. Keeping the analyst blind to the photos is in-
tended to focus its report on tool measurements rather than
direct photo inspection.
We evaluate LatentParse with five open and closed
VLMs on three spatial reasoning benchmarks [6, 43, 49].
Across the benchmarks, our method raises the mean accu-
racy of every VLM over the photo-only baseline by 0.7 to
2.4 pp, whereas naive input changes accuracy on MMSI-
Bench by only −2.6 to +0.6 pp. On MMSI-Bench, Latent-
Parse improves Qwen3.8-27B from 34.4% to 39.5%, with
the largest gains on camera-related questions, which rise
from 31.4% to 50.6% as shown in Figure 1. Using mis-
matched features from a different scene lowers accuracy to
33.3%, which indicates that the gain depends on features
that match the scene.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We propose LatentParse, a training-free framework that
enables frozen VLMs to exploit the latent features of
pretrained vision encoders for spatial reasoning by pars-
ing these features into relation evidence through a photo-
blind analyst and a textual report.
• We design a library of latent-space tools for object lo-
calization, cross-view tracking, and motion estimation,

2. Related Work
2.1. Spatial Reasoning in Vision Language Models
A growing body of benchmarks evaluates whether VLMs
can infer scene geometry from a single image and relate ob-
servations across multiple views. 3DSRBench [25] focuses
on depth order, orientation and object relations within a sin-
gle image, whereas MMSI-Bench [43], MindCube [36] and
MV-RoboBench [6] require reasoning about camera mo-
tion, hypothetical movements and object correspondence
across views. SPAR-Bench [49] and VSI-Bench [42] fur-
ther extend this evaluation to indoor scenes and video. To
improve performance on these benchmarks, most existing
methods train the VLM itself. SpatialVLM [1] is trained
on synthetic spatial question-answer pairs, SpatialRGPT [3]
learns regional representations from 3D scene graphs, and
GASP [46] derives supervision from correspondence and
depth consistency without relying on 3D VQA data. An-
other line of work injects features from additional encoders
through a trained connector, in the spirit of the pretrained
projector in LLaVA-NeXT [21]. Cambrian-1 [34] aggre-
gates features from multiple encoders with a learned Spa-
tial Vision Aggregator, while Spatial-MLLM [39], VG-
LLM [51] and VLM-3R [5] incorporate geometry features
through learned connectors or fusion modules.
GeoRe-
ader [17] trains Readers that encode predicted depth and
camera poses as continuous tokens, and subsequently fine-
tunes the VLM for spatial QA with the Readers frozen.
Since such training has to be repeated for every encoder-
VLM pair, these approaches are costly to extend and cannot
be applied to closed-source VLMs. In contrast, LatentParse
requires neither a learned connector nor any VLM update.
2.2. Spatial Information in Vision Encoder Latents
Probing studies investigate how much spatial information
can be recovered from frozen vision features. Probe3D [4]
evaluates depth estimation, surface-normal prediction and
cross-view correspondence, including on DINOv2 [27] fea-
tures, and Lexicon3D [26] extends this analysis to 3D
grounding, segmentation and registration. Feat2GS [2] as-
sesses the recovered geometry through novel-view synthe-
sis, and DepthCLIP [50] shows that coarse depth can be
read from CLIP [29] even without fitting a dedicated probe.
These findings establish that rich spatial information re-
sides in encoder features, yet leave open whether a sepa-
rate VLM can exploit it to answer spatial questions. A re-
lated line of research performs reasoning directly in latent
space. Coconut [9] reasons in a continuous latent space,
LatentMAS [53] exchanges hidden states among collabo-
rating agents, and Latent Sketchpad [47] lets a model sketch
within its own latent space. These methods, however, oper-
ate on the model’s internal hidden states, which the model is
trained to produce and interpret. Our work instead draws on
the patch features of external encoders, namely CLIP, DI-
NOv3 [32] and VGGT [35], whose channels bear no corre-
spondence to the VLM’s visual tokens. We therefore com-
pare presenting these features directly as images with pars-
ing the relations among their patches into textual evidence.
2.3. Training-Free Visual Tool Use
Training-free methods equip a model with vision tools
rather than modifying its weights.
VisProg [8] and
ViperGPT [33] formulate visual reasoning as programs that
invoke perception modules, and Visual Sketchpad [10] pro-
duces intermediate drawings for the VLM to inspect. Open-
VLHarness [52] retains tool outputs in a multimodal mem-
ory across steps, while MAIA [30] executes code to inter-
pret model components rather than the scene itself. Closer
to our setting, SpatialPIN [24] supplies explicit 3D pri-
ors for spatial reasoning, and SandboxVLM [23] renders
scene abstractions from 3D bounding boxes.
Perception
Programs [11] condenses the outputs of depth, flow and cor-
respondence tools into compact text, and Coarse Correspon-
dences [20] overlays objects tracked across views directly
on the images. Such tool use is typically organized by an
agent harness, which governs the tools available to a model,
how each call is executed and what information persists be-
tween steps [38, 44]. Recent work adapts harnesses by re-
fining prompts and workflows [12, 16, 19, 28, 37, 41, 48],
updating memory during deployment [14, 40], improving
execution efficiency [22], or training the model on harness
edits and demonstrations [31, 45].
LatentParse adopts a
fixed code-execution loop of this kind, in which a photo-
blind analyst applies predefined tools to the patch features
of off-the-shelf encoders and summarizes its findings in a
report that the answering VLM reads alongside the photos.
Unlike prior methods that operate on images or on the de-
coded outputs of tools and task heads, our analyst measures
relations within each encoder’s latent features, and rates
each finding by cross-encoder consensus.

3.1. Latent Features as Context
Three complementary encoders.
We draw latent fea-
tures from three pretrained encoders whose training objec-
tives capture different aspects of a scene.
CLIP [29] is
trained with image–text alignment and links object names
to image regions.
DINOv3 [32] is trained with self-
supervision and produces features that match semantically
similar regions within and across views.
VGGT [35] is
trained with multi-view 3D supervision and encodes how
the views relate geometrically. From each encoder we take
the patch tokens of the last layer and never use the depth,
point or camera heads of VGGT. These latent features re-
tain information that decoded outputs such as depth maps
or 3D boxes [23, 24] discard, and leave the choice of what
to measure to the analyst. Table 9 compares other layers.
Aligned patch grids.
To let the tools compare encoders
patch by patch, we place the features of VGGT and DI-
NOv3 on a shared h × w grid with w = 37. Each photo
is resized to a width of 518 px with its aspect ratio pre-
served, and a portrait photo is center-cropped to a square.
VGGT-1B processes all V views jointly on 14 px patches,
and we keep its aggregator patch tokens while discarding
the camera token and the four register tokens, which yields
dk = 2048. DINOv3 ViT-L/16 encodes each photo sepa-
rately after a resize that places its 16 px patches on the same
grid, with dk = 1024. CLIP ViT-L/14 encodes the whole
photo resized to 224 px square on a coarser 16 × 16 grid
with dk = 1024, which the tools map onto the shared grid
when comparing it with the other two encoders.
Direct input as feature images.
Direct input places the
latent features F (k) next to the photos. The global min–
max image draws the patches×channels matrix as a single
grayscale image, min–max normalized over the whole ma-
trix. This rendering presents the features as an appearance,
whereas their spatial information lies in the relations be-
tween patches, which motivates the tools in Section 3.2.
3.2. Basis-Invariant Latent Tools
Basis-invariant patch comparison.
Since the channels
of an external encoder carry no meaning that the VLM
shares, we compare patches only within each encoder’s fea-
ture space and rely solely on quantities that do not depend
on the choice of channel basis. Inner products, Euclidean
distances and feature norms are unchanged under any or-
thogonal change of basis R ∈O(dk), and so is a score that
contrasts a patch with two reference regions,
(Rf(p))⊤(RµA −RµB) = f(p)⊤(µA −µB),

where f(p) ∈Rdk is the feature of patch p, and µA and µB
are the mean features of two regions. All tools are built from
such quantities, with one exception.
CLIP’s text map
compares patches with a text phrase, which works because
CLIP embeds images and text in a shared space.
Cross-encoder consensus.
The three encoders are trained
with different objectives, so their errors need not coincide.
The cross-encoder tools therefore measure with every ap-
plicable encoder and report how many of them agree, such
as the direction vote of motion or the supported cells of
locate. The analyst later uses this consensus to rate each
finding in its report as high, medium or low (Section 3.3).
Object localization by encoder consensus.
Localization
proceeds in three stages. CLIP proposes a region for each
object name, DINOv3 and VGGT verify it, and their con-
sensus sets a confidence tier. CLIP’s proposal comes from
text map, which scores each patch against the prompt
“a photo of ⟨object⟩” and subtracts its mean score for five
generic phrases. Because last-layer CLIP patch tokens are
known to score a named object below its surroundings [18],
the map is smoothed and negated. locate then z-scores
the map across all photos and selects the connected compo-
nent of the top 8% of cells that contains the highest peak.
support uses DINOv3 and VGGT to check which cells
belong to the region, and cells supported by at least two en-
coders define its extent. Encoder consensus, region area,
CLIP peak score and prominence set a confidence tier of
high, medium, low or none, and locate all applies this
procedure to every named object.

text map, DINOv3 similarity and VGGT matches find.
Cross-view motion and tracking.
Two tools match
patches across views. Given two photos, motion matches
patches with the latent features of each encoder and reports
how far the matched content shifts, whether it grows or
shrinks, and the direction on which the encoders agree, such
as “left 3/3” when all three agree. These quantities describe
how the image changes rather than the camera pose. track
instead follows a located object into the other photos using
VGGT and DINOv3 patch matches, and CLIP checks the
matched region wherever both encoders agree.
Within-view relations and layout.
Within a single photo,
relate computes the relative offset, size ratio and contact
of two located objects. layout applies k-means with k=5
to VGGT and DINOv3 features, retains the segments that
the two encoders share, and compares all three encoders
with linear CKA [15]. Other tools in Table 1 provide ba-
sic feature and geometric operations.

Tool calibration.
Several design choices in the tools
are specified manually rather than learned, including the
negated CLIP map, the top 8% of cells in locate and
k=5 in layout. The thresholds that define the confidence
tiers and decide whether an encoder counts as reliable in
motion are chosen once on 150 calibration questions (100
from 3DSRBench and 50 from MMSI-Bench) outside the
development sample s1 (Section 4) and reused in all exper-
iments. They are set so that most high-tier boxes cover the
named object, as judged by a VLM that sees the photo, and
so that Gaussian noise almost never reaches the high tier or
passes the reliability check. No answers are used in this pro-
cess, and although the tools avoid learned decoding heads,
they still involve task-specific design.
3.3. Photo-Blind Analyst and Answering
Inputs of the photo-blind analyst.
The analyst receives
the question, the answer options, the number of views, the
image dimensions and the latent features of CLIP, DINOv3
and VGGT, which we refer to as Multi-Latent features. It
never sees the photos directly: it queries the features with
tools, although its interpretation may also draw on the ques-
tion and options. On benchmarks that mark targets with col-
ored boxes or points, a rule-based color detector supplies
the color and position of each marker (Section 4).
Agent-Directed tool use.
In the Agent-Directed mode,
the analyst receives no predefined object list. It selects tools
and their arguments from Table 1 and executes them in a
sandbox over up to four steps, each of which can build on
the printed outputs of earlier steps. A heuristic rule in the
analyst’s prompt reads the image shifts from motion as
camera motion, so that content moving left indicates that
the camera turned or moved right. Image shifts alone do

4. Experiments
Implementation Details.
Each VLM is both the analyst
and the answering model. The analyst runs one round of up
to four tool steps within 600 s per question. Open VLMs
decode greedily with thinking off and at most 1,000 new to-
kens per analyst step, each on one 80 GB GPU such as the
H100. The VLMs are Qwen3.8-27B, Gemma 4 (gemma-
4-31B-it), InternVL3-38B, GPT-6 Luna (gpt-6-luna) and
Claude Haiku (claude-haiku-5-5); the two closed VLMs run
through their APIs. Tool settings follow Table 1, and the re-
port holds up to six findings. Sections A and B list every
threshold and the prompts.
Baseline Methods.
Photo only gives the VLM the pho-
tos, question and answer options. Direct input, the Direct
columns of Table 2, adds the global min–max image of one
encoder’s latent features, or of all three, next to the pho-
tos.
In the rows + CLIP, + DINOv3 and + VGGT, the
Agent columns come from an earlier single-encoder ana-
lyst, which is also photo-blind but has only the basic tools
in the lower block of Table 1 and can view the maps it
draws on the feature grid with show. In the row All, La-
tentParse parses the Multi-Latent features. For Qwen3.8-
27B on MMSI-Bench we also test whether a gain comes
from the features’ content or the extra input, by replacing
the question’s own features (Correct) with two controls.
Mismatched uses the features of another question of the
same benchmark with the same number of views, one fixed
donor per question. Random Noise is Gaussian noise with
each view’s per-channel mean and standard deviation over
patches, seeded per question.
Benchmarks.
MMSI-Bench [43] has 1,000 multi-view
questions, from camera motion to multi-step reasoning.
From MV-RoboBench [6] we take its 861 spatial ques-
tions, each with five options on synchronized views of one
robot workspace in AgiBot World or BridgeV2. They ask
which box marks the same object in another view, which
boxed object is closest to grasp, where boxed objects sit in
a 5×5×5 grid and which of five photos the wrist camera
took with a given head view. SPAR-Bench-Tiny [49] has
550 multiple-choice questions in 11 tasks of 50 on Scan-
Net frames, 350 of them with two or three views. MV-
RoboBench and SPAR-Bench mark targets with colored
boxes or points drawn into the images. Since the analyst
never sees the photos, a fixed color detector with no learned
model gives it the color and position of each marker. Every
VLM answers every question of the three sets.
Evaluation Metrics.
We report accuracy and give differ-
ences in percentage points (pp). Paired exact sign tests com-
pare two conditions on the same questions, and a differ-
ence is significant when p<0.05. All tests are on Qwen3.8-
27B; see Table 10 and Section H. Ablations change one part
of the default, LatentParse with Qwen3.8-27B on MMSI-
Bench. During development we inspected answers only on
a sample s1 of 145 questions (100 from 3DSRBench and 45
from MMSI-Bench), so we also report MMSI-Bench results
on the 955 questions outside it.
4.1. Quantitative Results and Analysis
LatentParse improves all five VLMs on average.
Across the three benchmarks in Table 2, LatentParse raises
mean accuracy over photo only for all five VLMs by 0.7–
2.4 pp, while giving the Multi-Latent features directly
changes it by −3.0 to +0.1 pp.
The gains are largest
on MMSI-Bench. On SPAR-Bench, LatentParse improves
Qwen3.8-27B by only 0.2 pp and lowers GPT-6 Luna and
7


Claude Haiku by 0.5 and 0.3 pp. Multi-Latent features are
not always the best choice, as Gemma 4 scores 1.0 pp higher
with CLIP alone on MV-RoboBench.
Mismatched features remove the gain.
On MMSI-
Bench, LatentParse raises Qwen3.8-27B by 5.1 pp over
photo only, which scores 34.4% in Table 4. Mismatched
features remove this gain and score 1.1 pp below photo only,
significantly below Correct. Random Noise keeps 2.2 pp of
the gain and is not significantly below Correct (p=0.08).
Against a paired photo-only run the gain of Correct fea-
tures is significant, and it stays at 5.8 pp on the 955 MMSI-
Bench questions outside the development sample s1. In a
separate run that varies the analyst’s input (Section H), re-
ports from the question alone or the photos without tools do
not differ significantly from photo only, whereas tools run
on RGB patches beat it (p=0.003). All inputs gain on the
68 questions with a “Cannot be determined” or “None of
the above” option, which inconclusive reports favor. With-
out them, LatentParse beats photo only (p=0.006) and RGB
patches (p=0.033) on camera questions, but not photo only
on all 932 remaining questions (p=0.48). Answered counts
differ, and the p-values are uncorrected. With one encoder,
the agent gains 4.1 pp with VGGT, 1.9 pp with DINOv3 and
1.2 pp with CLIP in Figure 2. VGGT alone is not signifi-
cantly below all three encoders (p=0.58), but the two miss
different questions, and at least one is right on 52.1%. The
single-encoder runs use the earlier analyst and its tools, and
so do not isolate the effect of adding encoders. LatentParse
is 17.5 pp better on camera motion, while VGGT alone is
7.0 pp better on multi-step reasoning.
The tested feature images do not help.
Given di-
rectly, the Correct Multi-Latent features leave Qwen3.8-
27B 1.4 pp below photo only on MMSI-Bench, and Mis-
matched features or Random Noise stay within 0.3 pp of
photo only. Each single encoder given directly also scores
below photo only there. On the development sample s1,
Correct and Mismatched features given directly differ by
0.7 pp for CLIP and for VGGT (p=1.00 for both). Figure 5
compares direct input with photo only across VLMs.

4.2. Ablation Study
A fixed tool sequence comes close.
The Fixed-Sequence
mode runs six predefined code blocks instead of letting the
analyst choose tools. On MMSI-Bench it scores 0.6 pp be-
low Agent-Directed in Table 3, and the two modes do not
differ significantly (p=0.73). With VGGT alone the two
modes tie. Fixed-Sequence also beats photo only signifi-
cantly, by 5.1 pp on the 989 questions with paired answers.
On one GPU and the same 25 questions, analysis takes
99 s per question for Agent-Directed and 79 s for Fixed-
Sequence. Feature extraction takes about 0.5 s and answer-
ing 1.5 s per question, so analysis dominates.
The two modes differ by question type.
Agent-Directed
beats Fixed-Sequence by 17.5 pp on camera motion but
trails by 7.1 pp on multi-step reasoning over the 195 paired
questions. The Agent-Directed analyst calls motion on
85% of MMSI-Bench questions and rarely uses most ba-
sic tools, as Table 7 shows. On camera-motion questions it
calls motion every time and locate all on 5%, while
on object–object questions it calls locate all on 93%
and relate on 38%.
Seeing the photo helps multi-step questions.
An analyst
that also sees the photo scores within 0.2 pp of LatentParse
in Table 5. It recovers multi-step reasoning with a signifi-
cant gain of 8.5 pp, but trails by 6.7 pp on camera motion,
not significantly (p=0.23). Different questions may need
different evidence.
5. Conclusion
We propose LatentParse, a training-free framework that en-
ables frozen VLMs to exploit the latent features of pre-
trained vision encoders for spatial reasoning. A photo-blind
analyst parses the features of CLIP, DINOv3 and VGGT
with basis-invariant tools and distills the evidence into a
text report, which the VLM reads alongside the photos.
8


While the tested grayscale feature images yield no consis-
tent benefit, this report improves the mean accuracy of all
five VLMs across three benchmarks, with the largest gains
on MMSI-Bench camera-motion questions, suggesting that
encoder latents become useful once the relations between
their patches are made explicit. The Mismatched and Ran-
dom Noise controls cover only Qwen3.8-27B on MMSI-
Bench, multi-step reasoning declines without access to the
photos, and letting the analyst choose its tools has not yet
outperformed a predetermined sequence. Future work could
combine photo and latent evidence adaptively per question.

B. Prompts
On MMSI-Bench, the analyst of LatentParse receives the
system prompt below word for word, for every question
and VLM. Its first user message gives the number of pho-
tos, which it does not see, the grid sizes, the question
and the options, and the sandbox holds the photo sizes as
photo size. On MV-RoboBench and SPAR-Bench, this
message also lists the color and position of each marker
found by the color detector, followed by one of the two
notes printed below. The printed output of each code step
returns as the next user message under its execution id.
Once four code steps or 600 s are used, the analyst is asked
at most twice to reply with findings only. The boxes it re-
turns under regions are kept for inspection, as in Fig-
ure 8, and the photos reach the answering VLM without
them.
You are the analyst. You do NOT see the photo(s). You
get a spatial question and the raw latent features of
the photo(s) from frozen vision models, loaded in a
Python sandbox:
- F["clip"][v]: CLIP ViT-L/14 patch tokens, shape (16,
16, 1024): a 16x16 grid over the whole photo (
squeezed to a square). CLIP links image regions to
words.
- F["vggt"][v]: VGGT aggregator tokens, shape (h, 37,
2048). VGGT is a 3D reconstruction model: its features
encode surfaces, depth and viewpoint, computed
jointly for all photos of a scene.
- F["dinov3"][v]: DINOv3 ViT-L/16 patch tokens, shape
(h, 37, 1024), on exactly the VGGT grid (same cells,
same vggt_valid). DINOv3 is a self-supervised image
model: features of objects, parts and surfaces,
12


computed for each photo on its own (it never sees the
other photos and has no notion of depth).
Nothing is decoded: there is no depth map, no 3D
points and no camera pose. CLIP’s grid is 16x16 over
the whole photo; VGGT and DINOv3 share a grid of 14-
pixel cells, 37 columns across the photo width (a tall
photo is centre-cropped; vggt_valid[v] is False on
padding rows). Positions are photo fractions: x from
the left, y from the top, both 0-1. Printouts number
the photos 1, 2, ...; in code and in "regions", view =
photo number - 1.
Your job is NOT to answer. Measure evidence in the
latents that bears on the question, check it across
latents, and say how reliable it is. There is no
critic and no second round; a separate model answers
from the photo(s) and your findings.
Tools that compare latents (start with these):
- locate(phrase, v=None): CLIP’s text map (already
negated, so the named object scores highest) finds the
object in the photo where it peaks (or in photo v);
VGGT and DINOv3 each check on their own which cells
belong with CLIP’s blob. Prints photo, tier, box, area
, centroid, peak, standout (peak minus the best value
elsewhere), keep (share of the core of CLIP’s blob
each latent keeps), iou_vggt_dinov3 and peaks per
photo. tier is high, medium, low or none. locate_all(
phrases) runs it for each phrase and marks two names
that land on one region (same_as).
- motion(va, vb): how the content moves from photo va
to photo vb in each latent, from mutual patch matches:
n, dx, dy (median shift of the moving matches; + =
right / down), static (share not moving), inl (share
that one shift + scale + rotation explains; noise
stays under 0.2), scale (> 1: content grows), rot (
degrees). reliable lists the latents with enough
consistent matches (an unreliable one shows only n and
inl); x, y and zoom give the direction of the
reliable latents and how many agree ("left 3/3", "left
2/3 (clip right)", "left (only vggt)", "disagree", "
mixed", "unreliable"); pt_agree is the share of cells
VGGT and DINOv3 both match whose targets lie within
one cell of each other (chance is about 0.01).
- track(region, vb): where a located region (a locate
result) lands in photo vb in each geometry latent (n,
at, spread); found yes / no / unclear; only with yes:
shift, beyond_camera (shift minus the camera motion:
the object’s own movement, or parallax) and text_at vs
text_peak (CLIP’s map at the landing place vs its
peak there).
- relate(a, b): region b relative to region a in one
photo: dx, dy between centroids, size (box area ratio)
, iou, touch.
- layout(v): k-means segments VGGT and DINOv3 share,
and cka: how alike each pair of latents’ maps are (0
unrelated, 1 the same structure).
- support(model, seed, v): the cells a latent itself
groups with a seed region (the seed is not forced in).
iou(a, b, v): overlap of two masks of photo v (either
grid).

Rules for reading the numbers:
1. Rely on a location, a motion or a relation only
when the latents agree, and set each finding’s
reliability from that agreement:
- high: locate tier high; a motion direction that
every reliable latent gives, with at least two of them
("left 3/3", "left 2/2") and pt_agree >= 0.1 when
VGGT and DINOv3 are both reliable; track found yes.
- medium: locate tier medium; a direction with a
dissenting latent ("left 2/3 (...)") or from one
latent ("only ..."); track "yes (only ...)".
- low: anything else. "disagree", "mixed", "
unreliable", tier low or none, and track no or unclear
are not evidence: say the latents are inconclusive on
that point. Never settle a disagreement by picking
the latent you prefer.
2. Agreement shows that a region is one coherent thing
in several latents, not that it is the named object;
that rests on CLIP’s peak and standout.
3. Camera motion: content moving right from photo a to
photo b means the camera turned or moved left;
content moving down means it tilted or moved up; zoom
"grows" means it moved toward the scene. A shift under
one cell (0.027) is no movement.
4. State a relation between two objects only when both
have tier high or medium; left/right and above/below
come from their centroids in one photo.
5. The latents carry no reliable signal for
orientation: which way an object faces or points, or
whether two objects are parallel or face the same way.
Do not infer these; say they are not measured.
6. PCA scores, similarities and cluster ids have
arbitrary offset and scale: compare orderings and
differences relative to the spread, never ratios,
metres or angles.
7. If nothing reaches medium reliability, say the
latents are inconclusive. Every finding must come from
the latent numbers (source "latent"); do not describe
what the scene probably looks like.

I. Further Baselines
Table 12 adds baselines that use the photos or the de-
coded outputs of an encoder, on the 986 questions of Ta-
ble 10. Neither beats photo only significantly in any column
(p≥0.064), while LatentParse does in all three columns.
Decoded camera poses do not replace the latent features.
VGGT predicts a camera pose for each photo with its cam-
era head. We convert the relative pose between the first
photo and each other photo, and between consecutive pho-
tos, into sentences that state how many degrees the camera
turns and tilts and in which direction it moves, and the an-
swering VLM receives these sentences in place of the re-
port. The decoded poses raise accuracy on the four cam-
era types from 31.4% to 35.6% (p=0.098), whereas La-
tentParse reaches 50.6%, at 89W/39L against the poses and
p<0.001. On camera motion, the poses score 51.4% and
LatentParse 71.6%, at 18W/3L and p=0.001.
More answers from the photos do not explain the gain.
Self-consistency samples five answers from the photos at
temperature 0.7 and takes the majority. It scores 33.9%
overall (p=0.94) and 32.6% on the camera types (p=0.64),
and LatentParse outperforms it by 5.6 pp at 196W/141L and
p=0.003. A report written from the question alone, which
adds the same analysis step without features, also does not
differ significantly from photo only in Table 11.
