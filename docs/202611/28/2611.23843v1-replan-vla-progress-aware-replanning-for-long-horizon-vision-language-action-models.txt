Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a
promising paradigm for general-purpose robotic manipula-
tion, yet their performance often degrades on long-horizon
tasks that require multiple interdependent manipulations.
Instead of burdening the VLA policies with long-horizon
reasoning, memory, action generation, progress tracking,
and error recovery, we argue for leveraging their short-
horizon manipulation capabilities within an execution-
aware framework.
To this end, we introduce RePlan-
VLA, a VLA-agnostic framework that translates a VLA’s
short-horizon capabilities into reliable long-horizon execu-
tion. RePlan-VLA explicitly monitors the robot’s motion for
progress evaluation, and adaptively revises the future plan
based on observed outcomes. We preserve the underlying
VLA policy entirely, requiring no additional fine-tuning. Ex-
periments on RoboCasa365 and CALVIN benchmarks show
an improvement of 21.9% and 10.4% in success rate over
the next strongest baseline. We also validate our approach
for real-world deployment on a Franka Research 3 robot.

• We introduce RePlan-VLA, a VLA-agnostic framework

tasks without requiring further VLA fine-tuning.
• We develop a fine-tuned progress-monitoring VLM that

• We demonstrate the effectiveness of RePlan-VLA on

2. Related Work
Vision-Language-Action Policies.
Early mobile manip-
ulation approaches typically trained separate policies for
individual tasks or narrowly defined sets of manipula-
tion skills, limiting their ability to generalize to new in-
structions and environments. RT-1 [2] marked a shift to-
ward generalist policies by learning multiple manipulation
skills within a unified architecture, while RT-2 [3] lever-
aged pretrained vision-language representations to improve
semantic generalization.
Building on these foundations,
Octo [28] and OpenVLA [13] scaled training across diverse
robot embodiments, enabling broader skill transfer and ef-
ficient adaptation. Subsequent approaches like π0.5 [11],
GR00T-N1.6 [23], Xiaomi-Robotics-1 [29], RLDX-1 [12],
and PRTS [35] further advanced generalist manipulation
through larger and more diverse training datasets, improved
action-generation architectures, and refined training strate-
gies. More recently, recognizing the limitations of purely
reactive action prediction, World-Action Models such as
Awomo-0.5 [27], PatchWAM [30], and ABot-M0.5 [6] have
explored coupling action generation with predictive visual
world modeling to better capture environment dynamics
and improve sequential decision-making. Despite these ad-
vances, reliable long-horizon execution remains challeng-
ing, as these approaches largely emphasize policy-level ca-
pabilities without explicitly accounting for sub-task depen-
dencies, execution-grounded progress tracking, or recovery
from intermediate failures.
Long-Horizon Planning.
Several approaches extend
VLAs beyond reactive action prediction through hierar-
chical subgoals (LoHo-VLA [33]), temporal and motion
reasoning (TRM-VLA [15], HiF-VLA [16]), and persis-
tent memory (REKI [20], Long-WAM [10]). Building on
these advances, Foreact [36], GR-1 [31], NIAF [17], and
LoHo-Manip [18] incorporated future-state prediction and
execution-aware planning. More recently, progress-aware
methods (Progress-VLA [32], Sentinel-VLA [14], SPR [7],
NeurVLA [19], R2VLM [34]) have sought to monitor ex-
ecution and guide recovery through progress estimates,
often derived from temporal advancement in demonstra-
tions or used as monotonic rewards for policy optimization.
However, elapsed-time supervision may not reflect actual
task completion, while monotonic progress signals can ob-
scure regressions caused by execution errors. Consequently,
maintaining an accurate representation of completed and
failed sub-tasks, and translating these observations into cor-
rective plans, remains challenging.
In contrast, our approach grounds progress estimation in
the observed execution of individual manipulation primi-
tives rather than elapsed time or monotonically increasing
task-level progress.
By explicitly tracking execution be-
havior, and using the diagnostic feedback to revise the re-
maining plan, our framework enables failure-aware recov-
ery across interdependent sub-tasks without retraining or
modifying the underlying VLA policy.
3. Our Approach
Overview. Our framework, illustrated in Fig. 2, consists
of three key components: (i) a few-shot planner VLM that

sub-task completion history; and (iii) a frozen VLA pol-
icy that translates the active sub-task instruction into low-
level robot control commands. To ensure compatibility with
the underlying VLA, the planner generates sub-task instruc-
tions that align with the linguistic structure of instructions
encountered during the VLA policy’s training. During ex-
ecution, the progress estimator periodically evaluates sub-

Vision-Language-Action Policy. RePlan-VLA employs a
frozen VLA policy πΦ as the low-level executor, translat-
ing short-horizon sub-task instructions into embodiment-
specific control actions. At each timestep t, the policy πΦ
receives the active sub-task instruction Sk
t and its native ob-
servations ot, generating actions Sk
t . High-level planning
and execution history are managed separately, allowing the
VLA to operate solely on the active sub-task. Upon sub-task
completion or failure-triggered re-planning, any unexecuted
actions from the previous prediction chunk are discarded,
and the policy receives the updated instruction.
RePlan-VLA is VLA-agnostic, supporting integration
with language-conditioned policies without modifying their
observation modalities, action representations, or action-
chunking strategies. Each policy is fine-tuned on the tar-
get benchmark following its recommended training proto-


col and remains frozen throughout RePlan-VLA execution,
requiring no additional training or architectural modifica-
tions. Further integration details are provided in the Ap-
pendix.
4. Experiments
4.1. Setup
Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate
RePlan-VLA on RoboCasa365 [22] and CALVIN [21]
benchmarks. For RoboCasa365, we evaluate 16 tasks each
from the target Composite-Seen and Composite-Unseen
splits, with five episodes per task. We report task success
rate (SR). For CALVIN, we follow the ABCD→harD pro-
tocol and evaluate five-task instruction sequences on a chal-
lenging subset of 100 sequences selected based on low base-
line performance to reduce saturation. All methods use the
same evaluation subset, and we report success rates at se-
quence lengths k ∈{1, . . . , 5} and the average number of
consecutively completed tasks (Avg. Len.). Further details
on CALVIN subset selection are provided in the Appendix.
Training Configuration. We train the progress estimator
for 80, 000 steps on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU using
BF16 mixed precision and AdamW optimization. We em-
ploy LoRA (r = 16, α = 32) and a 1024-dimensional
progress prediction head, with an effective batch size of
8 and gradient clipping at 1.0. The head and LoRA pa-
rameters use learning rates of 10−4 and 10−5, respectively.
Further training and data annotation details are provided in
the Appendix. All underlying VLAs are fine-tuned follow-
ing their respective benchmark protocols and remain frozen
during RePlan-VLA execution; additional VLA implemen-
tation details are provided in the Appendix.
Implementation Details. We implement RePlan-VLA us-
ing the Qwen3-VL-8B [1] models as few-shot planner VLM
and as base model for fine-tuned progress estimator VLM.
We use Awomo-0.5I [27] as our frozen VLA executor. All
inferences were performed locally. During execution, the
progress estimator evaluates the active manipulation primi-
tive every µ = 50 environment steps using a temporal his-
tory of five observations sampled at intervals of s = 10
steps. We use completion thresholds of 0.8 and 0.65 for the
go to and move to primitives, respectively. Additional
implementation details are provided in the Appendix.
Real-Robot Evaluation. We evaluate RePlan-VLA in a
real-world manipulation setting using a Franka Research
3 robotic arm equipped with a two-finger gripper.
Our
setup is illustrated in Fig. 1. Inspired by CALVIN [21], we
design long-horizon composite tasks combining language-
conditioned manipulation primitives into multi-step se-
quences. Additional details on real-robot data collection

4.3. Component and Design Ablations
We conduct ablation studies to assess five key components
of RePlan-VLA: visual grounding, instruction verification,
replanning context, planning strategies, and progress esti-
mation. Each experiment modifies a single component of
our framework while keeping the rest unchanged to iso-
late its contribution. We report success rate (SR) on Robo-
Casa365 composite tasks, with results presented in Tab. 3.
Effect of Visual Grounding Strategies.
We investigate
how different object detection and scene annotation strate-
gies affect the performance by influencing the planner’s
ability to generate scene-grounded subtasks.
As shown
in Tab. 3, SAM3 bounding boxes achieve the highest av-
erage success rate (62.5%), outperforming SAM3 segmen-
tation masks (46.9%), Grounding DINO boxes (37.5%),
object labels alone (34.4%), and unannotated observations
(37.4%). This highlights the importance of accurate object
localization and consistent visual representations for scene-
grounded planning. While providing finer object bound-
aries, segmentation masks may introduce visual ambiguity
through inconsistent mask appearances across observations.
In contrast, labeled bounding boxes offer stable spatial cues
and object identities. The limited effectiveness of object la-
bels alone further suggests that identifying relevant objects
without localizing them is insufficient for reliable planning.

Effect of Instruction Verification.
We examine the ef-
fect of validating generated subtask instructions against the
underlying VLA’s supported language forms on the perfor-
mance of our approach. As shown in Tab. 3, removing the
verification reduces average success from 62.5% to 34.5%,
highlighting the importance of aligning generated subtasks
with the underlying VLA’s learned instruction patterns. Al-
though the decomposer may generate semantically valid in-
structions, unfamiliar language formulations can lead to un-
reliable execution by the VLA policy. By identifying in-
compatible instructions and prompting their reformulation,
the verifier bridges high-level planning and the VLA’s ex-
ecution capabilities.
The consistent improvement across
both splits demonstrates that successful planning requires
not only selecting appropriate subtasks, but also expressing
them in language the policy can reliably execute.
Ablation Over Replanning Context. We investigate the
contributions of the execution history, failure explanations,
and subtask instruction towards generating contextually ap-
propriate recovery plans. As shown in Tab. 3, omitting the
failed subtask causes the largest decline in average success
(62.5% to 21.9%), highlighting the importance of identify-
ing where execution failed. Removing completed-subtask
history reduces success to 34.4%, emphasizing its role in
preserving achieved goals and avoiding redundant actions.
Notably, excluding failure explanations particularly affects
unseen tasks, reducing success from 56.3% to 18.8%. To-
gether, these findings demonstrate the complementary im-
portance of completed-subtask history, failure localization,
and diagnostic feedback for effective recovery planning.
Different Planning Strategies. We evaluate the effects of
few-shot prompting, object-specific guidance, visual scene
context, and task-conditioned object selection on plan qual-
ity and downstream execution.
As shown in Tab. 3, re-
moving few-shot demonstrations, object-specific prompts,
or scene observations substantially reduces task success.


Notably, zero-shot prompting and removing object-specific
guidance decrease unseen-task success from 56.3% to
18.8% and 18.7%, respectively, highlighting the importance
of structured examples and manipulation-specific knowl-
edge for unfamiliar task compositions. Without scene ob-
servations, success drops to 37.5% on both splits, under-
scoring the role of visual context in reasoning about ob-
ject states and prerequisites. These findings demonstrate
the complementary benefits of task-specific guidance and
visual grounding for reliable long-horizon planning.
Alternative Progress Estimation Methods. We compare
our fine-tuned progress estimator with zero-shot VLM-
based monitoring and global task-progress estimation [34]
to assess the benefits of supervised primitive-level progress
estimation.
As illustrated in Tab. 3, our fine-tuned
primitive-level progress estimator achieves 62.5% average
success, outperforming zero-shot VLM monitoring (37.6%)
and R2VLM-based global estimation [34] (18.8%). While
zero-shot reasoning lacks explicit manipulation-progress
supervision, global estimation tracks overall task advance-
ment rather than individual primitive completion, where
substantial progress toward the overall goal does not nec-
essarily imply successful completion of the current manip-
ulation primitive; such discrepancies can lead to premature
transitions or delayed corrective interventions. In contrast,
our estimator provides progress feedback aligned with the
execution granularity. These findings highlight the impor-
tance of task-specific supervision and fine-grained progress
monitoring for reliable long-horizon manipulation.
4.4. Qualitative Failure Recovery Analysis
We qualitatively investigate how RePlan-VLA handles exe-
cution failures during long-horizon manipulation tasks. We
examine representative video rollouts and their correspond-
ing reasoning traces, analyzing how the progress monitor
identifies execution discrepancies and how the decomposer
adapts subsequent subtasks.
We observe several distinct
failure-recovery behaviors. First, our approach handles in-
correct object manipulation, as shown in Figs. 2 and 3a,
where the robot grasps an unintended object, but the sys-
tem recognizes the discrepancy and revises the plan to ac-
commodate the current state. Second, it addresses missing
manipulation prerequisites as illustrated in Fig. 3b, where
repeated unsuccessful retrieval attempts lead to the intro-
duction of an additional subtask. Third, it recovers from
incomplete subtask execution as shown in Fig. 3c, where
the system identifies unfinished placements or interactions
and redirects execution toward completing them before pro-
ceeding.
Together, these examples illustrate how visual
progress monitoring and adaptive task decomposition en-
able recovery from execution errors. Further analysis of re-
covery modes, along with a complete episode rollout exam-
ple, is provided in the Appendix.

4.5. Real-World Franka Research 3 Deployment
We evaluate RePlan-VLA on a Franka Research 3 robot
performing 3-task manipulation sequences following a
CALVIN-style evaluation protocol. Using a fine-tuned and
subsequently frozen π0.5-DROID [11] policy, RePlan-VLA
successfully completes long-horizon sequences of tasks,
demonstrating its applicability to physical manipulation un-
der real-world uncertainties. Detailed results and qualitative
recovery rollouts are provided in the Appendix.
4.6. Runtime and Replanning Analysis
We evaluate RePlan-VLA’s computational efficiency and
replanning behavior on RoboCasa365, reporting component
inference latencies, replanning frequency, and planner re-
generation rates in Tab. 4. RePlan-VLA achieves an average
end-to-end execution time of approximately 0.58 s per en-
vironment step, including policy inference, simulation, and
framework overhead.
Progress estimation (∼2.35 s/call)
and object detection (∼0.53 s/call) incur additional moni-
toring costs, while replanning occurs 1.34 times per episode
on average. The primary overhead arises from planner in-
ference, particularly when instruction verification failures
trigger re-querying in 25.3% of requests, compared with
just 1.3% for malformed outputs. Although verification is
inexpensive (∼0.22 s/call), rejected instructions necessitate
additional planner calls. Overall, our results indicate that
execution-aware monitoring and adaptive replanning intro-
duce manageable overhead in terms of planning time.
5. Conclusion and Future Work
We present RePlan-VLA, a VLA-agnostic framework
that improves long-horizon manipulation through visual
progress estimation, failure-aware feedback, and adaptive
replanning while preserving the capabilities of pretrained
VLA policies.
Although its effectiveness depends on
reliable visual grounding and the underlying policy’s


short-horizon manipulation capabilities,
and recovery
may be limited by irreversible execution failures, RePlan-
VLA provides a flexible approach to execution-aware
long-horizon planning without modifying the underlying
VLA. Future work will explore skill-aware replanning that
accounts for policy capabilities, learning from successful
recoveries, and incorporating tactile sensing and other
complementary modalities to improve progress estimation.
