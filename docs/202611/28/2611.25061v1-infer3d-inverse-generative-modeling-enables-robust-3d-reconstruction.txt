Abstract
Humans can understand the 3D structure of scenes even from highly unfamiliar or extreme
viewpoints. Cognitive science suggests this generalization stems from a top-down generative
inference strategy. In contrast, state-of-the-art 3D reconstruction neural networks typically
rely on amortized, feedforward inference.
While fast, this pattern-matching approach
degrades when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) scene conditions unseen during training.
Inspired by the generative nature of human vision, we propose Infer3D, an inverse generative
modeling framework that recovers the latent variables of a generative model to best match
the monocular input. To prevent degenerate flat outputs, we couple the generative image
prior with a frozen 3D lifting network that constrains the optimization space to a valid,
multi-view-consistent 3D manifold. Across a variety of controlled synthetic and real-world
tasks, Infer3D demonstrates superior OOD reconstruction accuracy compared to feedforward
networks trained on equal or larger datasets. Furthermore, mirroring how human perception
requires more time to process confusing stimuli, we introduce an adaptive inference strategy
that expends more test-time compute on ambiguous OOD examples while efficiently routing
in-distribution inputs. Project website: amanikiruga.github.io/infer3d-site

Introduction
The human visual system exhibits a remarkable ability to parse the 3D world from 2D projections. Crucially,
this capability is not brittle: humans can readily understand the 3D structure of objects and scenes even
when viewed from highly unconventional angles (Yildirim et al., 2015), under strange lighting (Adelson,
2000), or under different compositional arrangements (Lake et al., 2017). A central challenge in perception
theory is explaining how human vision can be so robust. A rich body of literature in cognitive science
and developmental psychology suggests that this robustness arises because human vision is not merely a
passive, bottom-up filter but also includes an active, top-down generative process. By maintaining an internal
mental model of the physical world, the mind can continuously predict and match sensory inputs, allowing
it to reliably resolve ambiguities in unseen environments (Spagna et al., 2024; Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010;
Tenenbaum et al., 2011).
This cognitive perspective heavily influenced classical computational theories, which formalized perception as
“vision as inverse graphics” (Yuille & Kersten, 2006). In this paradigm, visual understanding is cast as Bayesian
inference over a generative model of image formation and scene structure (Knill & Richards, 1996; Kersten
et al., 2004). Rather than simply recognizing 2D patterns, the visual system performs analysis-by-synthesis,
actively searching for the underlying 3D scene representation that best explains the observed 2D image.
In contrast, modern monocular 3D reconstruction systems have largely abandoned this generative top-down
inference in favor of amortized, feedforward architectures (Szymanowicz et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2021; Charatan
et al., 2024; Wewer et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2023) that directly map a 2D input image to a 3D output.

While this pattern-matching approach enables very fast inference, it fundamentally lacks the robustness of
generative inference. As we demonstrate in this paper, feedforward networks degrade in unpredictable ways
when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) scene conditions unseen during training, such as unconventional
camera viewpoints (see Fig. 2).
Inspired by the generative nature of human vision and classic analysis-by-synthesis methods (Blanz & Vetter,
2023a; Yuille & Kersten, 2006), we propose Infer3D. Rather than trusting a feedforward encoder on OOD
data, Infer3D formulates perception as an inverse generative problem. We search for the scene geometry S
and rendering parameters θ that maximize the explanatory likelihood of the input observation. To ensure our
optimization remains constrained to plausible, multi-view-consistent 3D structures, we couple a 2D generative
image prior with a frozen 3D lifting network. This encourages the optimized latent variables to always decode
to a valid representation on the training manifold, effectively synthesizing an internal hypothesis that best
matches the confusing sensory input. Furthermore, mirroring how human perception requires more time
to process ambiguous stimuli (Kar et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2018; Tschantz et al., 2023), we introduce an
adaptive inference strategy. Because our generative framework leverages an encoder for initialization, we
show that the initial reconstruction error provides a very reliable and efficient signal for OOD detection.
This allows Infer3D to dynamically route standard inputs through fast feedforward passes while reserving
expensive test-time optimization exclusively for OOD images.
Overall, our work makes the following contributions: (1) we introduce Infer3D, a modular analysis-by-
synthesis framework that combines a learned 2D generative model with a feedforward 3D lifter to enable
multi-view-consistent, OOD-robust inference from single images; (2) we propose an adaptive inference and
OOD detection strategy that leverages the initial reconstruction error of the generative prior, requiring no
external classifiers; and (3) through a controlled experimental setup, we demonstrate that Infer3D successfully
generalizes to unseen viewpoints, non-linear lens distortions, and unobserved textures where state-of-the-art
feedforward methods collapse (see Fig. 1).

Prior Work
Feedforward Inference.
There has been a lot of success in models that can reconstruct 3D scenes
from monocular images. Popular choices of 3D representation are meshes (Liu et al., 2019; Tewari et al.,
2017; Chen et al., 2025), or neural radiance fields (Yu et al., 2021; Tewari et al., 2023; Szymanowicz et al.,
2023; Hong et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023b). Modern methods rely more on 3D Gaussian representations that
also use volume rendering, but are significantly more efficient than neural radiance fields (Charatan et al.,
2024; Szymanowicz et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024). In addition, point-based representations
2


are also emerging as a popular choice (Wang et al., 2024; 2025a). These approaches use neural networks
to directly transform the input image to the output 3D reconstruction and thus struggle to reason about
out-of-distribution settings.
Several papers demonstrate lack of generalization with feedforward inference in out-of-distribution set-
tings (Zhao et al., 2024; Madan et al., 2020; Alcorn et al., 2019; Abbas & Deny, 2023). Mitigating such
failures is also a topic of active research. Several works have introduced equivariant neural network models
that aim to generalize in OOD conditions. However, none of these approaches is as general as our method,
e.g., we show that our method can be used for images captured under OOD camera poses as well as under
OOD textures. In particular, equivariant approaches either assume 3D or multiview inputs (Xu et al., 2022;
Deng et al., 2021; Esteves et al., 2018), or only include equivariance in parts of their network, e.g., equivariant
intermediate representations without equivariant transformations between images and the corresponding
representations (Dupont et al., 2020; Mitchel et al., 2024). Equivariant models that take 2D image inputs
currently only guarantee equivariance under 2D or color transformations (Cohen & Welling, 2016; Yang et al.,
2024). There is also a line of work in using 3D models for data augmentation (Zhou et al., 2023; Yang et al.,
2025; Zhu et al., 2024). However, no such method has been proposed for monocular 3D reconstruction.
Analysis-by-Synthesis.
Rather than directly transforming an image into the corresponding 3D output
with feedforward inference, analysis-by-synthesis solves an optimization problem for inference that searches
for the optimal 3D solution that can explain the input observation. Because of the ambiguities in the
problem, a good prior over 3D models is necessary. Many approaches use 3D training data, or pre-captured
object geometries, to learn these priors (Blanz & Vetter, 2023b; Yuille & Kersten, 2006; Müller et al., 2023;
Gothoskar et al., 2021). Recent papers instead rely on learning priors from 2D data, e.g., using a monocular
image generative model as a prior over renderings of the 3D reconstruction (Poole et al., 2023; Xu et al.,
2023a; Melas-Kyriazi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). However, monocular priors are not sufficient to enforce
multi-view consistency in output. Other approaches use multi-view priors (Lin et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024;
Long et al., 2024; Szymanowicz et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2024); however, these methods do not generalize to
OOD images as they process input images with neural networks. The Bayesian approach of Anciukevicius
et al. (2022) is somewhat related to us, but it makes strong assumptions by assuming known scene parameters
for the OOD inputs. Recent work on inverse generative modeling for scene understanding (Wang et al.,
2025b) is also closely related in spirit: it formulates perception as inverting a learned generative model and
introduces compositional structure to handle complex 2D scenes. We are inspired by this view of inference
as solving an inverse generative problem, but focus on multi-view-consistent 3D reconstruction and explicit
control over camera parameters, rather than 2D compositional layouts.
Hybrid Inference.
Hybrid inference methods have been explored in the literature (Tewari et al., 2018;
Alldieck et al., 2019; Leung et al., 2022). However, they assume that the initial prediction is a reliable
initialization—an assumption that breaks down under OOD conditions. In contrast, we propose an adaptive
inference strategy that explicitly detects OOD inputs and selectively invokes robust optimization-based
inference only when needed.
Optimization-Based Generative Inversion.
pi-GAN (Chan et al., 2021) fits the latent conditioning of a
3D-aware GAN to an image but does not infer an unknown camera. BRFI (Pavllo et al., 2023) and 3D GAN
Inversion (Ko et al., 2023) reconstruct 3D shape by jointly optimizing a generative latent code and camera
pose. Both refine a single initialization obtained from learned pose or latent predictors, making them sensitive
to initialization errors under distribution shift. FINV (Sun et al., 2024) filters multiple hypotheses using
provided camera poses and additional observations, whereas Infer3D searches multiple joint latent and pose
hypotheses from a single unposed image. These methods couple appearance and geometry within a monolithic
3D GAN. Infer3D instead searches a powerful 2D generative prior for appearance while a separately trained
lifter supplies the 3D constraint, allowing the prior and lifter to be selected independently. We directly
compare against BRFI and 3D GAN Inversion in Tables 5 and 10.

3
Method
We provide an overview of our method in Fig. 3. We first describe the 3D scene representation and our general
analysis-by-synthesis framework in Section 3.1. We then instantiate this framework across three distinct
out-of-distribution (OOD) applications in Section 3.3: unseen camera poses, unseen sensor distortions, and
unseen appearance. Finally, we discuss efficient OOD detection in Section 3.5.
3.1
Scene Representation and Differentiable Renderer
The 3D reconstructions are represented using a scene representation S which describes the underlying
geometry and appearance of objects in the scene.
This representation can be rendered into an image
using a differentiable forward model R(S, θ), with rendering parameters θ. Depending on the scenario, θ
is parameterized to represent unknown extrinsic camera poses (viewpoint shifts) or expanded to include
uncalibrated intrinsic sensor characteristics (e.g., non-linear fisheye lens distortions).
3D Gaussians.
In practice, we use 3D Gaussians (Kerbl et al., 2023) as our 3D representation, where
the scene is described using a collection of 3D Gaussians that represent its geometry and appearance. Each
Gaussian is described with parameters that determine its shape, opacity, and color. We render the 3D
Gaussians using the differentiable splatting-based renderer from 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023), which takes
camera parameters as additional input.
3.2
Inference via Generative Decoder Inversion
Standard feedforward reconstruction networks function as amortized encoders, mapping observations directly
to 3D representations. While efficient, these encoder-based methods lack the inductive biases required for
generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) scene conditions (Brady et al., 2025). To achieve robustness, we
formulate perception as an inverse generative problem via maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation. Given
an OOD observation IOOD, we seek the scene parameters S and rendering parameters θ that maximize the
posterior:
p(S, θ | IOOD) ∝p(IOOD | S, θ)p(S)p(θ).

Optimization Dynamics.
We solve Eq. 2 via gradient descent. To avoid local minima in the non-convex
energy landscape, we employ a multi-start strategy with particle filtering. We initialize a population of
latent codes {zi} and parameters {θi}. The population is iteratively refined, with high-error particles culled
periodically, yielding a set of plausible 3D interpretations for the ambiguous OOD input. This procedure is
detailed in Algorithm 1.
The formulation in Eq. 2 ensures that the input to the lifting network Φ is always an in-distribution image
G(z), strictly adhering to the manifold where the feedforward model is valid. It is also highly flexible: we can
adapt to distinct domain shifts merely by redefining the optimized rendering parameters θ, their associated
prior p(θ), and the likelihood. We instantiate this approach for three specific OOD applications below.
3.3
Adapting to Out-of-Distribution Shifts
Out-of-Distribution Camera Poses.
For unseen camera viewpoint shifts, we jointly optimize the scene
representation alongside the extrinsic camera parameters. Specifically, we optimize the latent code z and the
camera pose θ ∈SE(3). We use a uniform prior over the camera pose parameters, continuously projecting
rotation matrices onto the SO(3) manifold via SVD (Levinson et al., 2020) to ensure stable gradient descent.
The likelihood is computed as a combination of MSE and LPIPS losses (Zhang et al., 2018) to align
low-frequency geometry and high-frequency textures.
Out-of-Distribution Sensor Shifts.
Standard feedforward models assume strict pinhole projection and
inherently fail under severe intrinsic parameter shifts, misinterpreting non-linear fisheye lens warping as
curved scene geometry. To address this, we optimize the latent code z, camera intrinsics, i.e., focal lengths
5


and principal points (fx, fy, cx, cy), and complex non-linear distortion parameters ϕ. The distortion prior
is parameterized by a Hybrid Neural Distortion Field (Deng et al., 2025), which is a learnable polynomial
fisheye model coupled with an iResNet on a sparse 2D grid. To evaluate the likelihood term, we first warp the
perspective-rendered hypothesis generated by our generative model into the fisheye space using the learned
forward distortion field (Dϕ). We then compute MSE and LPIPS losses between this distorted rendering
and the input OOD fisheye observation, effectively combining 3D Gaussian Splatting with a hybrid fisheye
forward model.
Out-of-Distribution Appearance.
When transitioning from synthetic training data to real-world cap-
tures, standard pixel-level reconstruction collapses due to large domain gaps caused by complex reflections,
unobserved lighting, and novel materials. We can bridge this synthetic-to-real domain gap by leveraging the
inherent modularity of our inverse framework. We replace the pixel-level likelihood in the previous cases with
a deep semantic feature loss, maximizing the cosine similarity of DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) patch-level
features extracted from both the OOD input and the rendered 3D hypothesis. As semantic features lack
fine-grained spatial precision, we couple this with an explicit MSE depth consistency loss to constrain the
geometric scale, all rendered via standard pinhole 3D Gaussian Splatting renderer. Similar to the OOD
camera poses setting, we search for the latent code z and camera pose θ ∈SE(3).
3.4
Lifting and Generative Networks
3D Lifting Network (Φ).
Our framework is agnostic to the specific architecture of the network Φ. In our
experiments, we instantiate Φ using either:
• Splatter Image (Szymanowicz et al., 2024), which predicts pixel-aligned 3D Gaussians using a U-Net
architecture.
• CATSplat (Roh et al., 2024), which utilizes transformer-based context aggregation for more complex
geometries.
Crucially, Φ is frozen during inference; we rely on the optimization of z to adapt the geometry.
Generative Image Priors (G).
We explore two classes of generative models to parameterize the image
manifold, see appendix Fig. 8. The choice of G dictates the structure of the latent space Z:
• GAN-based: We employ StyleGAN2 (Karras et al., 2020). The latent space is defined as z ∈W,
where W refers to the intermediate 1D representation in the StyleGAN architecture. This generator
provides a compact, smooth manifold that is amenable to gradient-based optimization.
• Diffusion-based: We also utilize Diffusion Autoencoders (DiffAE) (Preechakul et al., 2022). DiffAE
encodes an image into a semantic latent zsem and a stochastic latent xT . This offers a distinct
advantage: unlike GANs, DiffAE includes an explicit encoder E that can initialize z = E(I) for
in-distribution data, significantly accelerating convergence (see Sec. 3.5).
3.5
OOD Detection via Generative Prior
A key capability of our method is that it naturally exposes signals for out-of-distribution (OOD) detection,
without requiring the training of additional classifier models.
Inference dynamics as an OOD signal.
Given an input image, analysis-by-synthesis optimizes latent
variables and rendering parameters to minimize a reconstruction loss. For in-distribution inputs x ∈XID, the
generative encoder provides an initialization that lies close to an optimal solution. Consequently, the initial
reconstruction error of the generated hypothesis is inherently low. In contrast, OOD inputs x ∈XOOD lie
completely outside the support of the learned image distribution. The encoder fails to provide a near-optimal
initialization, resulting in a severe structural mismatch and a high initial reconstruction loss.

We leverage this asymmetry to build an efficient OOD detector. Specifically, we run one forward pass
through our method and use the initial reconstruction error (MSE and LPIPS) against the input image as
our classification score. If this initial loss exceeds a calibrated threshold, the sample is flagged as OOD and
routed to the full optimization loop (Algorithm 1); otherwise, we bypass the optimization and trust the
feedforward lifter.
Automatic Threshold Calibration.
Importantly, this detection signal requires no additional trained
classifiers. To set the decision threshold, we perform an automatic calibration step on a small held-out
validation set (5 ID and 5 OOD objects), selecting the threshold that maximizes the F1 score. As we batch
all objects and run a single encode-decode pass, this entire one-time calibration process is fast, taking a
maximum of 500 milliseconds on CO3D. At test time, we can accurately detect OOD inputs in roughly 286
milliseconds without constructing expensive backpropagation graphs. We quantitatively evaluate our fast
OOD detection and calibration on CO3D and RE10K (Zhou et al., 2018) in Section 4.3.
4
Experiments
We evaluate Infer3D’s capabilities across three core dimensions: (1) generalization to geometric and camera-
level domain shifts, (2) robustness to appearance and environmental domain gaps, and (3) efficacy of our
adaptive inference strategy. We report Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR, ↑), Structural Similarity Index
Measure (SSIM, ↑), and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS, ↓) (Zhang et al., 2018) for
novel-view fidelity, Chamfer Distance (CD, ↓) for 3D geometry error, and the Area Under the Receiver
Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC, ↑) and F1 score (↑) for OOD detection.

4.2
Appearance Shift and In-the-Wild Generalization
Unseen Texture and Lighting.
We evaluate on the RealCars dataset (Du et al., 2025) (in-the-wild
captures with complex reflections), restricting the training of our generative prior exclusively to synthetic
SRN Cars (Sitzmann et al., 2019). For the 3D lifter, we use official Splatter Image weights pretrained on
SRN Cars. Because standard pixel-level reconstruction fails under this synthetic-to-real gap, we integrate
DINOv2 semantic feature optimization into Infer3D. While Splatter Image collapses (Chamfer Distance:
0.219) and generative baselines like Large Multi-View Gaussian Model (LGM) (Tang et al., 2024), trained
on a much larger Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023) dataset, hallucinate artifacts, our analysis-by-synthesis
bypasses the texture nuisance and reconstructs the correct 3D pose and geometry, achieving a significantly
lower mean Chamfer Distance of 0.131 (Table 5, Figure 5). For fairness, we also include a finetuned Splatter
Image baseline where we provide it with the same DINO features we use as input (PCA-reduced features of
channel-32) and pseudo-ground-truth depth from Depth Anything 3 (Lin et al., 2025). This improves the
robustness of Splatter Image (Mean CD of 0.152 from 0.219) but still significantly underperforms Infer3D,
demonstrating that inverse generative modeling remains essential for generalization.
The improvement also holds against optimization-based inversion methods trained on synthetic SRN Cars.
Infer3D achieves a mean Chamfer Distance of 0.131, compared with 0.491 for BRFI (Pavllo et al., 2023) and
0.387 for 3D GAN Inversion (Ko et al., 2023), reducing error by 73% and 66%, respectively (Table 5).

Ablations on Compute Tradeoffs.
We run inference for 783 iterations, initializing 600 hypotheses (30
rotations × 20 latent codes) and pruning to 32, 10, and 5 at iterations 3, 122, and 302. Where available, an
encoder provides one warm-start hypothesis. Full optimization settings are given in Section B.3.
We find that the computational cost of Infer3D can be explicitly scaled to prioritize speed without sacrificing
robustness. By parallelizing particle optimization (B = 10), full convergence is achieved efficiently in roughly
2.8 minutes. For applications requiring even faster turnaround, early stopping the optimization loop after
just 100 steps yields a rapid 1.2-minute runtime per OOD sample. Notably, this early-stopping configuration
still achieves a PSNR of 16.98 which still outperforms the baseline feedforward model Splatter Image (15.73)
on OOD CO3D Hydrants. In practice, data streams mix standard inputs with occasional anomalies; pairing
rapid OOD detection with analysis-by-synthesis (without early stopping) allows Infer3D to process mixed
streams efficiently without bottlenecking pipelines (Table 4).
Objective and Optimization Ablations.
Finally, we validate our optimization objective and the necessity
of our constrained approach. As detailed in Table 8, relying solely on pixel-wise MSE for CO3D yields poor
geometry. Sequentially integrating perceptual penalties (LLPIPS), latent regularization (Lprior), and depth
consistency (Ldepth) is critical for recovering accurate out-of-distribution structures, improving Chamfer
Distance from 0.954 to 0.458. Furthermore, we test whether simply updating the weights of the feedforward
lifter on the OOD input (trivial test-time training) could achieve similar robustness. Table 13 demonstrates
that this naive adaptation collapses the geometry and performs worse than the frozen baseline (12.41 vs. 15.73
PSNR), indicating that optimizing over an internal generative model is more effective than naive test-time
adaptation of the lifter.
We further examine how pose and latent optimization contribute to our method. On OOD SO(3) ShapeNet
Cars, removing pose or latent updates reduces PSNR by 3.10 and 2.27 dB, respectively, while removing both
reduces it by 4.08 dB (Table 9). We do not change the initialization in these ablations, only whether the target
variable is optimized further or selected among initialization candidates. These results support jointly refining
shape and viewpoint during inference and show the gain is spread across the two optimization regimes.
The optimization also progressively improves the fit to the input image, reducing MSE by 86% and LPIPS by
67% over the reported checkpoints on OOD ShapeNet Cars (Fig. 7).
5
Limitations and Conclusion
Limitations.
While Infer3D significantly improves robustness to OOD shifts, several limitations remain.
While our method excels at OOD recovery, purely optimization-based inference can result in slightly lower
quantitative metrics on in-distribution data compared to a specialized feedforward network.
However,
our proposed adaptive inference strategy effectively neutralizes this issue by classifying inputs based on
convergence dynamics, ensuring that optimization is only triggered when necessary. We further discuss this
drop in appendix Section C.6. Additionally, the visual sharpness of our OOD reconstructions is bound by
the capabilities of the underlying 2D generative prior. In this work, we utilized lighter-weight generators
(e.g., DiffAE, StyleGAN2) which can sometimes struggle with high-frequency details. We believe that
11


integrating state-of-the-art diffusion models as the generative backbone would resolve this, albeit at a higher
computational cost.
Conclusion.
We presented a framework for robust monocular 3D reconstruction that treats inference
as an inverse generative problem.
We demonstrated that standard feedforward networks overfit to
the training distribution, failing catastrophically under viewpoint, sensor, or appearance shifts.
By
combining a learned 3D lifter with a test-time optimization loop that enforces a 2D generative prior,
we recover accurate geometry in scenarios where baselines produce collapsed artifacts.
Furthermore,
we showed that the dynamics of this optimization process serve as a strong signal for OOD detection,
enabling an adaptive system that is both efficient on familiar data and robust to the unexpected. We hope
this work encourages a shift from purely amortized inference toward hybrid methods for reliable 3D perception.

Additional Method Details
B.1
In-the-wild generalization
In order to support running our 3D reconstruction inference pipeline on in-the-wild real input images, we
first acquire pseudo-GT segmentation masks using Segment-Anything 2 (Ravi et al., 2024) by initializing
multiple point prompts, and selecting the largest mask that is close to the center. We then follow the method
of Out-of-Distribution appearance to recover the geometry S and camera extrinsics θ (See Section 3.3).

B.2
Compute Resources
To run all our experiments we use 2 80GB A100 NVIDIA GPUs. At proposal batch size = 10, our full
inference occupies a Peak VRAM of 48GB GPU memory.
B.3
Optimization Settings
Table 11 summarizes the optimization schedule. Iterations are indexed from 0 to 782. Each iteration updates
all active hypotheses, processing at most B = 10 simultaneously. Thus, B controls memory usage rather than
the total population size.
Initialization.
When an encoder E is available, we replace one random joint hypothesis with z1 = E(I)
and identity camera pose. This hypothesis undergoes the same optimization and pruning as the remaining
hypotheses. Without an encoder, all hypotheses are randomly initialized.
Prior-specific regularization.
For DiffAE, we apply a quadratic latent penalty centered on the mean of
the encoder-embedded initial latent population, with weight 0.001. For StyleGAN2, we instead use noise-map
regularization to penalize spatial correlations in the optimized noise maps.
Learning rates and batching.
The latent learning rate uses a base value of 0.01 with a cosine ramp-up
over the first 5% of iterations and a ramp-down over the final 25%. The rendering-parameter learning rate
remains 0.01. When the active population exceeds B = 10, we process it in successive batches, completing an
iteration after all active hypotheses have been updated.

We evaluate optimization variables, loss terms, and initialization counts on 25 OOD ShapeNet Cars using

C.6
In-Distribution Trade-off
Run on every input, the optimizer alone is less accurate than the feedforward lifter on ID data: by 0.93 dB on
CO3D Hydrants, 2.66 dB on ShapeNet, and 1.38 dB on RE10K (Tables 1 to 3). We perform analysis about
this drop and find that the performance gap is concentrated near the input view. On ID CO3D Hydrants,
the feedforward advantage is 5.7 dB for views close to the input but only 0.9 dB for the most occluded views.
Unlike the feedforward lifter, the lifter inside Infer3D never receives the input image directly, so it cannot
copy visible details into nearby views; for views that require inferring occluded 3D structure, Infer3D is
nearly as accurate. In practice, an image-conditioned feedforward model is preferable for narrow-baseline
rendering close to the input view, whereas Infer3D is more robust for wide-baseline views and 3D geometry.
The detector-routed system (Table 4) avoids this cost on inputs classified as ID.
