EAGLE: Entity-Aware Grounding with Layered Evidence for High-Resolution
Image Understanding
Zhongjian Zou
Quan Qian
Shanghai University
Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) still strug-
gle with fine-grained understanding of high-resolution im-
ages: attention heads vary in their reliability for ground-
ing target entities and their attributes, while localized de-
tails are often disconnected from the broader scene con-
text.
We propose EAGLE (Entity-Aware Grounding with
Layered Evidence for High-Resolution Image Understand-
ing), a plug-in framework that jointly selects grounding sig-
nals and organizes hierarchical visual evidence. EAGLE
employs a fine-grained dataset to screen visually-grounded
attention heads, followed by entity-aware grounding to ex-
tract target-specific regions. It then integrates hierarchi-
cal evidence construction and question-guided routing to
select optimal view combinations, feeding them into the
frozen MLLM to enhance fine-grained visual understand-
ing.
Experiments with Qwen2.5-VL-7B and InternVL3-
8B demonstrate that EAGLE consistently improves perfor-
mance across both backbones and achieves SOTA results on
V*, HRBench-4K, and HRBench-8K compared with existing
methods using the same backbone.
1. Introduction
Multimodal fine-grained recognition is capable of align-
ing textual entities with visual regions to uncover subtle
attributes overlooked by global models, thereby empow-
ering detail-aware decision-making in autonomous driving
and smart healthcare. Vision–language pre-training and vi-
sual instruction tuning have enabled MLLMs to answer di-
verse questions about images [2, 6, 10, 12, 13, 16]. High-
resolution images, however, still expose a gap between see-
ing an image and resolving its fine details [20, 23, 25]. A
small target may occupy only a few visual tokens, while
similar objects compete for attention. Even when a crop
contains the correct target, the model must connect its ap-
pearance to the intended entity and interpret it within the
scene.
Reliable fine-grained understanding therefore re-
quires both accurate grounding and suitable visual context.
Prior crop-based methods recover local evidence through
visual search or attention-guided region extraction [14, 18,
22, 23, 26].
However, previous token-wise localization
methods can retain responses to non-localization words and
combine regions that refer to different objects. For “woman
in black jacket”, independently localizing “black”, “jacket”,
and “woman” may preserve unrelated objects rather than
their joint referent.
EAGLE removes non-localization
words such as “in” and fuses the remaining word heatmaps
within the entity phrase before region extraction. Figure 2
contrasts this phrase-level grounding with token-wise local-
ization.
Selecting visual grounding heads is another challenge.
Prior studies show that only a subset of attention heads con-
sistently supports visual localization [4, 9]. Heads with-
out this capability often produce diffuse or unrelated im-
age responses, which act as noise when used for grounding.
The key is therefore to identify suitable visual heads be-
fore combining their heatmaps. Available data also vary
in fine-grained difficulty: in the source examples we in-
spected, some target objects occupy more than 30% of the
original image, making them less informative for screening
heads that must locate small targets. We curate and manu-
ally annotate Visual-CoT (fine-grained) to build a special-
ized visual-head pool, then use a trained ranker to select the
heads most suitable for the current entity.
Correct localization does not guarantee a correct answer.
Even a crop that clearly exposes the target can fail to pro-
vide sufficient evidence for recognition. Prior original-plus-
crop pipelines [14, 26] leave two related difficulties. A tight
crop removes context needed to identify the target, while
the large scale gap between the original image and the mag-
nified detail can hinder cross-view association. Contextual
blindness has also motivated multi-scale cropping [8]. EA-
GLE retains an intermediate view to connect target details
with their surroundings, then selects the view combination
according to the question. The examples in Figure 4 show
that adding detail alone does not consistently improve pre-
dictions.
We present EAGLE, a unified framework connecting
entity-aware grounding, layered evidence construction, and
question-guided routing (Figure 1). We curate Visual-CoT

2. Related work
Previous studies on multimodal visual understanding
and alignment.
Vision Transformers represent images as
patch sequences [7], while CLIP learns transferable visual
representations from image–text pairs [16]. Flamingo con-
nects visual encoders with language models for few-shot
multimodal learning [2], and BLIP-2 uses a lightweight
querying module to bridge frozen visual and language mod-
els [10]. LLaVA and InstructBLIP establish visual instruc-
tion tuning as a route to general-purpose image conversa-
tion [6, 12]; LLaVA-1.5 improves this recipe with a simple
connector and task-oriented data [13]. These advances es-
tablish the backbone capabilities on which EAGLE builds.
Our focus is how an existing model can access and combine
the evidence needed for a fine-grained question.
Fine-grained perception on high-resolution images.
Early MLLMs commonly encoded images at a fixed resolu-
tion, limiting access to small objects and text. LLaVA-UHD
addresses varying resolutions and aspect ratios through im-
age slicing, token compression, and spatial organization
[25].
InternVL scales visual representation learning [5],
and PS3 introduces selective local pre-training for 4K inputs
[19]. Qwen2.5-VL and InternVL3 further strengthen high-
resolution perception [3, 28]. Complementing these back-
bone advances, V* uses guided visual search [23]; ViCrop,
ZoomEye, and retrieval-augmented perception recover use-
ful regions at inference [18, 22, 26]. EAGLE also oper-
ates at inference, but jointly selects entity-specific ground-
ing signals and the views supplied for answering.
Attention-based heatmap grounding.
Attention rollout
traces information across Transformer layers [1], while re-
cent work shows that individual MLLM heads differ in
visual perception and grounding ability [4, 9].
ViCrop
and HiDe both employ attention-based heatmap localization
[14, 26]. HAVC combines offline OCR-based head screen-
ing with online heatmap selection [24]. In contrast to these
generic approaches, we construct Visual-CoT, a manually
annotated dataset, to identify a specialized head pool and
dynamically select heads tailored to the specific target.
3. Method
3.1. Overview and entity extraction
Given an image I and a question Q, EAGLE identifies
the target entities, constructs informative views, and passes
the selected views to a frozen MLLM for answering (Fig-
ure 1). Candidate-head screening and ranker learning are
performed offline. Online selection uses the current image
and extracted entity words, without access to reference an-
swers or target bounding boxes.
Entity extraction removes words that do not identify
visual content and retains the object, part, and modifiers
needed to specify the target. For “woman in black jacket”,
it removes “in” and forms “black jacket woman”. For a re-
lation between a woman with a backpack and a person with
a beanie, the two entity phrases remain separate. We fuse
the word heatmaps within each phrase before extracting re-
gions, rather than locating every word separately and merg-
ing all its boxes. This preserves a phrase as one target and
limits unrelated regions activated by individual words.
The extracted words are mapped to their positions in the
question so that their importance can be estimated consis-
tently. Regions from different entities are retained together
when constructing the intermediate image. Thus, a rela-
tional question can preserve both the queried object and its
reference object rather than enlarging one while losing the
other.

3.4. Layered evidence and question-guided routing
Constructing intermediate and detail images.
Fig-
ure 1c shows two localization rounds. Eagle uses HiDe’s
layout-preserving reorganization [14] to remove irrelevant
rows and columns while retaining the arrangement of se-
lected regions, producing the intermediate image Im. A
recorded coordinate mapping connects Im to the original
image I. The second pass localizes targets within Im, re-
moves geometrically invalid padding, and maps the refined
regions back to I. Sampling the original pixels produces the
detail image Id.
The two constructed images have complementary objec-
tives. Because the detail image depends on the interme-
diate image, the first pass favors retaining as many rele-
vant regions as possible, preserving both target evidence
and useful context. The intermediate image thus provides
the search space for refinement and bridges the original
scene and the detail image. The second pass instead favors
stronger removal of irrelevant background so that local tar-
get features become more prominent. Both views are recon-
structed from original pixels; invalid padding is removed
geometrically rather than by color. The original image is
retained when complete scene coverage or distance infor-
mation is required.
Question-guided routing.
Different questions require
different visual evidence. The router selects the original
and intermediate images for broad layout or contextual in-
terpretation, such as a sign’s meaning or an object’s func-
tion; the intermediate and detail images for clearly local-
ized attributes, text, faces, and other fine details; and all
three images when both scene-level information and local
details are needed, including full-image counting and near–
far comparisons. The decision follows the information re-
quired by the question, so the same object can use different
routes under different questions.

4.3. Effect of the ranking function
To assess whether the learned ranking function selects at-
tention heads that better cover the target region, we compare
ranked and random head selection under otherwise identical
settings. Figure 3 compares ranked and random head se-
lection on the held-out localization set. Both use the same
128-head pool, map fusion, and region extraction. Random
selection is repeated ten times for each K, with heads sam-
pled without replacement. The standard deviation measures
variation caused by random selection.
Figure 3 shows that ranked selection maintains high cov-
erage across K, whereas random selection deteriorates as
more heads are averaged. At K = 16, coverage is 81.5%
versus 57.8%, a 23.7-point gain. K = 2 and K = 4 achieve
slightly higher coverage, so this plot does not establish K
= 16 as the coverage optimum. The adopted K = 16 ag-
gregates more candidate signals while limiting each head to
1/16 of the fused map.

5. Conclusion
EAGLE combines entity-aware grounding with layered vi-
sual evidence for high-resolution understanding. Removing
non-localization words and fusing phrase-level responses
improves target specification; dynamic Top-16 selection
supplies entity-specific grounding signals. The newly in-
troduced intermediate views provide detailed context and
bridge the scale gap between global and local images, while
question-guided routing is responsible for selecting suitable
visual evidence for answering. Results on three benchmarks
with two frozen backbones support the framework’s cross-
model applicability and achieve SOTA performance among
the evaluated same-backbone methods.
Future work will evaluate EAGLE on a broader range of
multimodal large language models to assess its generaliz-
ability beyond the two backbones studied here. To address
the limited availability of high-quality, openly accessible
fine-grained datasets, we also plan to construct datasets with
more detailed category annotations and fine-grained visual
questions, supporting more comprehensive evaluation and
further research in this area.
