CoopOcc: Cooperative 3D Semantic Occupancy
Perception via Multi-Agent and Cross-Modal Fusion
Zengle Zhu, Zhen Li, Tianyi Huai, Xinhu Zheng, Member, IEEE, Liuqing Yang, Fellow, IEEE, Rongqing
Zhang, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Semantic occupancy perception has emerged as a
promising paradigm for jointly modeling 3D scene geometry
and semantic information. However, this unified representation
cannot compensate for missing information caused by occlusion
and sensor coverage. Multi-agent cooperation alleviates this limi-
tation, but information from different agents is not equally useful
across spatial regions. Meanwhile, feature representations from
each agent form the basis of reliable multi-agent fusion. Cross-
modal fusion constructs such representations by integrating
complementary semantic and geometric information. Despite this
complementarity, jointly establishing reliable 3D correspondence
across agents and preserving modality-specific structures remains
challenging. To address these limitations, we propose CoopOcc,
which performs cooperative 3D semantic occupancy perception
through multi-agent and cross-modal fusion. CoopOcc uses ego-
query cross-agent attention (EQCA) to selectively aggregate
aligned features across agents, and anchor-based bidirectional
feature interaction (ABFI) for cross-modal exchange at matched
coordinates. We further construct SemanticVVSim, a simulation
dataset, and establish a unified benchmark. For simulation-
to-real transfer, we propose confidence-aware hierarchical oc-
cupancy and relational distillation (CHORD). CoopOcc per-
forms best overall among ten evaluated methods on Seman-
ticVVSim. On Occ3D-nuScenes, CHORD improves performance
using knowledge from SemanticVVSim. The code is available at
https://github.com/LOTEAT/CoopOcc.
Index Terms—3D semantic occupancy perception, cooperative
perception, cross-modal fusion, simulation-to-real transfer.

Dense semantic occupancy perception requires estimating
occupancy throughout the entire 3D volume [5], [12]. Voxels
outside the field of view or occluded by surrounding objects
lack direct sensor observations [7], [13]. Such unobserved
voxels are especially common at intersections or on con-
gested roads. Occupancy estimates in these regions rely more
heavily on learned scene priors. However, such priors may
not provide reliable guidance for the current scene. Multi-
agent cooperation alleviates this limitation by incorporating
observations from spatially distributed agents [14]–[16]. These
agents provide complementary viewpoints [17]–[19]. Through
such cooperation, the occluded area may be directly observed
by a nearby cooperating vehicle. Occupancy estimates for the
corresponding voxels can rely more on direct sensor evidence
and less on learned scene priors. Thus, broader observation
coverage can reduce ambiguity in occupancy estimation and
support more accurate dense semantic occupancy perception.
Effective use of these complementary viewpoints for dense
semantic occupancy perception depends on how the local
observations from each cooperating vehicle represent the sur-
rounding scene. In autonomous driving, cameras capture rich
appearance and semantic cues [7], [9], [13], while LiDAR
provides direct 3D range measurements [20], [21]. However,
neither modality alone directly provides the dense, spatially
explicit representation required for semantic occupancy per-
ception. Cameras project the 3D scene onto 2D image planes,
preserving dense visual information but leaving the 3D loca-
tions of observed content ambiguous [8], [22]. This ambiguity
can affect the predicted occupancy states and semantic labels
of multiple voxels. In contrast, LiDAR measurements provide
reliable 3D support on observed scene surfaces, but sparsely
sampling the space captures limited evidence for dense se-
mantic perception [23], [24]. These sensing characteristics
make the two modalities complementary: visual observations

Combining complementary camera and LiDAR observa-
tions from spatially distributed agents broadens observation
coverage and supports dense 3D scene understanding, but
multi-modal cooperative perception still faces three central
challenges.
1) Spatial Variation in Cross-Agent Complementarity:
For regions that are already sufficiently observed by the
ego agent, features from other agents often overlap with
existing information and provide limited additional benefit,
potentially introducing redundancy [15], [17]. In contrast,
unobserved or insufficiently observed regions have a greater
need for collaborative information, yet not every cooperating
agent can provide effective complementary information due to
differences in observation coverage and information quality.
Consequently, both the demand for collaborative information
and the useful contributions from different agents vary across
spatial locations. For dense semantic occupancy perception
over the surrounding 3D space, effectively assessing these
spatially varying agent contributions is therefore crucial for
collaborative fusion.
2) Cross-Modal Representation Gap in Cross-Agent Fu-
sion: In cooperative perception, feature representations form
the basis for subsequent cross-agent alignment and information
aggregation [25], [26]. Due to the substantial differences be-
tween cameras and LiDAR, each agent needs to first integrate
local multi-modal observations before cross-agent cooperation.
However, the two modalities represent 3D scenes in different
ways. Camera features contain dense semantic information but
lack explicit 3D positions [7], [8]. LiDAR features correspond
to explicit 3D positions but remain sparse [20], [21]. This
difference makes reliable 3D correspondence across agents
difficult to establish while preserving features from both
modalities. Consequently, the construction of a unified scene
representation and subsequent cross-agent fusion become more
challenging.
3) Lack of Comprehensive Dataset and Benchmark: Com-
prehensive evaluation of multi-agent, multi-modal semantic
occupancy perception requires diverse driving scenes and
collaboration settings, including varied numbers, positions,
and viewpoints of collaborating agents. However, existing
semantic occupancy datasets mainly focus on single-agent per-
ception [11], [12], while multi-agent datasets commonly target
3D object detection or BEV semantic segmentation [28]–
[33]. This gap limits comprehensive evaluation and reliable
comparison of multi-agent, multi-modal semantic occupancy
perception across diverse scenes and collaboration condi-
tions [18], [19].
To address these challenges, we propose CoopOcc, a co-
operative 3D semantic occupancy perception framework that
integrates observations through multi-agent and cross-modal
fusion in a shared 3D space. CoopOcc uses ego-query cross-
agent attention (EQCA) to selectively aggregate spatially
corresponding features across agents. Within each agent,
CoopOcc uses anchor-based bidirectional feature interaction
(ABFI) to construct spatially consistent multi-modal features
for cross-agent cooperation. ABFI exchanges camera and
LiDAR information at coordinate-matched 3D locations. We
further construct SemanticVVSim, a multi-agent, multi-modal
simulation dataset, and establish a unified benchmark for
semantic occupancy perception. We then investigate whether
knowledge learned from SemanticVVSim can improve seman-
tic occupancy perception in real scenes. However, differences
in data distributions, sensor configurations, and semantic label
spaces make direct transfer unreliable. Therefore, we intro-
duce confidence-aware hierarchical occupancy and relational
distillation (CHORD). The main contributions are summarized
as follows.

II. RELATED WORKS
A. 3D Semantic Occupancy Perception
Perception is fundamental to autonomous driving because it
enables vehicles to understand their surroundings and supports
safe decision-making [1]. 3D object detection has long been
a dominant task in autonomous driving perception [2], [3]. It
identifies and localizes key traffic participants using 3D bound-
ing boxes. However, this object-centric sparse representation
provides limited descriptions of free space, road surfaces, and
other non-object regions [5]. To obtain a dense scene-level 3D
representation, 3D semantic occupancy perception models the
occupancy state and semantic category of each voxel in the
surrounding space [6], [11], [12].
Early methods predict 3D semantic occupancy from depth
maps or point clouds [6], [23], [24], [34], [35]. These inputs
directly provide 3D locations, so the central problem is to
recover complete voxel geometry and semantics from partial
and sparse surface observations [5]. Research along this direc-
tion focuses on encoding sparse observations, modeling spatial
context, and completing unobserved regions [6], [23], [35].
However, the available sensor information limits this recon-
struction process. Range measurements locate parts of visible
scene surfaces but do not provide continuous, semantically
rich observations throughout the voxel space [20], [24].
Therefore, camera-based methods have attracted increasing
attention [7], [13]. Images provide dense appearance and
semantic information across continuous 2D regions [9], [22].
This information enables models to use rich scene context
to distinguish semantic categories [8], [36]. Camera-based
methods map multi-view image features into 3D represen-
tations [7]–[9], [13]. The representations bring dense visual
information into semantic perception across the surrounding
voxel space [10]–[12]. Subsequent research further improves
the organization of image features, strengthening scene-level
reasoning while reducing the computational cost of the repre-
sentation [37]–[39].
LiDAR-based
methods
provide
spatially
explicit
but
sparsely sampled 3D geometry, whereas camera-based meth-
ods provide dense appearance and semantic information [7],
[21]. Therefore, research on 3D semantic occupancy percep-
tion has expanded from single-modal approaches to multi-
modal camera-LiDAR methods [25], [26]. They improve
overall perception performance by combining complementary
geometric and semantic information [4], [27], [40]. These
methods commonly align camera and LiDAR features in
a shared 3D space to establish spatial correspondence for
cross-modal fusion [4], [25], [26]. However, spatial alignment
only identifies corresponding features and does not determine
how semantic and geometric information should interact.
Camera and LiDAR features still differ in spatial structure
and observation density. Therefore, it remains challenging to
maintain fine-grained spatial correspondence while enabling
the two representations to update each other in multi-modal
3D semantic occupancy perception [27], [40].
B. Cooperative Perception
Multi-modal fusion improves single-vehicle perception
through complementary sensor cues but cannot overcome
occlusion or limited sensing range [1]. Cooperative perception
shares observations from spatially distributed agents, allow-
ing complementary viewpoints to support scene understand-
ing [14], [41]. Therefore, cooperative perception has become
an important direction for extending sensing coverage and
recovering information from occluded regions [15], [16].
A primary design choice in cooperative perception concerns
the level of information exchanged and fused across agents [1].
Raw-observation fusion shares images or point clouds and pre-
serves relatively complete sensor information, but incurs high
communication and processing costs [41]. Output-level fusion
exchanges only final outputs and reduces transmitted data,
but discards useful intermediate information [42]. Intermediate
feature fusion provides a compromise by exchanging learned
scene representations [14]–[16]. Consequently, research has in-
creasingly shifted from choosing exchange levels to designing
effective intermediate feature fusion mechanisms [17], [43].
Existing intermediate feature fusion methods use spatially
adaptive fusion, transformer-based feature interaction, and
communication-region selection to improve information ex-
change and feature aggregation across agents [14]–[17], [43],
[44]. However, most cooperative perception research focuses
on 3D object detection and BEV semantic segmentation,
which produce sparse object boxes and planar semantic maps,
respectively [28]–[33], [45], [46]. In contrast, semantic occu-
pancy perception estimates the occupancy state and semantic
category of every voxel in the surrounding 3D space [18], [19].
Therefore, this task places different requirements on cross-
agent spatial correspondence and dense 3D feature aggrega-
tion. Large-scale benchmarks such as OpenOccupancy and
Occ3D advance single-agent semantic occupancy perception,
but cannot be used to study multi-vehicle cooperation [11],
[12]. Existing data resources remain insufficient for jointly
studying multi-agent, multi-modal, dense 3D semantic occu-
pancy perception or for systematic evaluation across diverse
interaction scenarios and collaboration conditions.
III. METHODOLOGY
To fully exploit complementary multi-modal observations
captured by different agents from different viewpoints, we
propose CoopOcc for cooperative 3D semantic occupancy per-
ception. The remainder of this section describes the framework
design and implementation in detail.

A. Framework Overview
As illustrated in Figure 1, CoopOcc is a unified frame-
work for multi-agent, multi-modal 3D semantic occupancy
perception. Given multi-view images and LiDAR point clouds
collected by the ego agent and multiple cooperating agents, the
framework constructs a multi-modal feature representation for
each agent and establishes cross-agent spatial correspondence.
The relevance of agent features to ego perception varies across
spatial locations. Therefore, CoopOcc introduces ego-query
cross-agent attention (EQCA). EQCA uses the ego feature as a
query to assess spatially corresponding features from different
agents. It adaptively adjusts the contribution of each agent at
each location and selectively fuses complementary information
into an ego-centered cooperative representation.
To support reliable cross-agent cooperation, modality-
specific encoders first extract image and LiDAR features.
Anchor-based bidirectional feature interaction (ABFI) then
uses nonempty LiDAR voxels as shared 3D anchors to in-
tegrate camera semantics and LiDAR geometry at spatially
corresponding locations. ABFI provides EQCA with spatially
consistent multi-modal feature representations. Finally, the
occupancy-aware hierarchical decoder converts the coopera-
tive representation into a dense voxel grid and progressively
predicts occupancy states and semantic classes.

encoders, respectively. The same camera and LiDAR encoders
are applied to all agents.

C. Anchor-Based Bidirectional Feature Interaction
Camera-plane features and LiDAR voxel features describe
the same 3D region through complementary representations.
Camera-plane features encode dense visual information on
three orthogonal planes. However, each plane aggregates 3D
information along one spatial axis. This aggregation loses
precise positional distinctions along that axis and prevents an
explicit feature representation for every 3D voxel. In contrast,
the LiDAR encoder preserves features at explicit 3D voxel
locations, but only at spatially sparse sites supported by
LiDAR measurements. Directly converting either representa-
tion into the other requires dense 3D lifting or discard their
complementary structural properties.
To bridge this representational gap, we propose anchor-
based bidirectional feature interaction (ABFI), as shown in
Figure 2. Rather than converting either modality into a com-
mon dense volume, ABFI uses sparse LiDAR-supported 3D
anchors as an interface between the two representations. This
interface injects plane context into explicit 3D locations and
returns geometry-conditioned updates to the compact plane
representation through coordinate-aligned bidirectional path-
ways.

IV. SIMULATION-TO-REAL TRANSFER
Evaluation confined to simulation cannot establish whether
knowledge learned from synthetic data remains effective in
real-world scenes. To examine whether dense voxel super-
vision from SemanticVVSim benefits real-world semantic
occupancy perception, we study simulation-to-real transfer.
Conventional source-domain pretraining followed by target-
domain fine-tuning does not explicitly separate transferable
occupancy structure from source-specific observation patterns.
Differences in scene appearance and sensor configurations
between the simulation and real-world domains lead to a
distribution shift in sensor observations. Therefore, direct
observation-level alignment is unreliable.
Knowledge distillation transfers knowledge across domains
without requiring aligned raw observations [50]–[52]. How-
ever, direct distillation can propagate unreliable teacher out-
puts, while semantic-logit transfer requires compatible output
spaces. Therefore, existing distillation approaches do not di-
rectly address spatially varying teacher reliability and incom-
patible semantic taxonomies in this setting.
To address these cross-domain transfer challenges, we in-
troduce CHORD, which maps target-domain inputs to spa-
tially aligned egocentric occupancy representations instead of
aligning raw observations. A frozen CoopOcc teacher trained
on SemanticVVSim and a target-domain student process each
target sample. At the representation level, CHORD distills nor-
malized plane features and spatial token relations, while target-
domain supervision adapts the student to real observations. At
the output level, confidence-guided distillation transfers only
binary occupancy and compatible coarse semantic groups to
accommodate differences between the source and target label
spaces.

covers both foreground traffic participants and static scene

For foreground traffic participants, CoopOcc obtains the
highest IoU for car, pedestrian, truck, and van. These cate-
gories cover road users with different spatial scales and shapes.
The consistent results indicate strong semantic occupancy per-
ception for safety-relevant foreground objects. CoopOcc also
ranks first for drivable surface, terrain, sidewalk, manmade,
and vegetation. These classes describe large static surfaces
and scene structures. The broad class-wise advantage shows
that CoopOcc recognizes foreground traffic participants while
retaining accurate estimates of the surrounding scene layout.

D. Robustness Analysis
We evaluate CoopOcc under two practical perturbations in
cooperative perception: communication delay and pose error.
The zero-perturbation result provides a common reference.
Communication delay leads to a gradual change in perfor-
mance. As the delay increases from 0 to 1000 ms, geometric
IoU remains between 76.46% and 76.57%. Over the same
interval, mIoU decreases from 62.75% to 60.29%. The sta-
ble geometric IoU shows that CoopOcc maintains consistent
occupied-space estimates under delayed collaborative obser-
vations. The limited decrease in mIoU further indicates that
semantic occupancy perception remains stable as the delay
increases.
CoopOcc also shows smooth performance changes under
pose perturbations. As the combined translation and yaw
perturbations increase, geometric IoU changes from 76.57%
to 75.13%, and mIoU changes from 62.75% to 58.48%. No
perturbation level causes an abrupt performance drop. Under
the largest pose perturbation, CoopOcc still retains 75.13%
geometric IoU and 58.48% mIoU, showing tolerance to spatial
misalignment.
Overall, CoopOcc preserves most of the zero-perturbation
performance under both communication delay and pose error.
The gradual performance changes across both tests show that
the cooperative fusion framework maintains stable semantic
occupancy estimates under imperfect temporal and spatial
alignment.
E. Ablation Studies
We compare the complete CoopOcc model with four vari-
ants under both single-agent and multi-agent settings. The
variants examine the two modality branches, ABFI, and hi-
erarchical occupancy decoding. The single-modality variants
keep only the camera or LiDAR branch. ABFI is disabled
in these variants because ABFI requires both modalities. The
ABFI ablation keeps both branches but uses simple feature
fusion. Simple fusion projects sparse LiDAR voxel features
onto three orthogonal camera planes and averages voxels at the
same location. A plane-specific linear layer fuses the features,
followed by residual addition and LayerNorm. The decoder
ablation also keeps both branches and replaces the hierarchical
decoder with a flat classifier.
Using either modality alone reduces performance under both
the single-agent and multi-agent settings, while the complete
model consistently achieves the best results. This pattern
demonstrates the complementary value of camera and LiDAR
features. The larger performance drop caused by removing
the camera branch indicates that camera features provide rich
semantic information. LiDAR features provide complementary
geometric and spatial information. Therefore, the fusion of the
two modalities improves overall performance.

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper studies cooperative 3D semantic occupancy per-
ception through multi-agent and cross-modal fusion. To sup-
port research in this direction, we construct SemanticVVSim,
a multi-agent, multi-modal dataset for semantic occupancy
perception, and establish a unified benchmark. CoopOcc com-
bines camera semantics and LiDAR geometry through anchor-
based bidirectional feature interaction, while ego-query cross-
agent attention aggregates spatially aligned information from
collaborating agents. To address domain and label-space dif-
ferences between simulation and real-world data, CHORD
transfers knowledge through confidence-aware hierarchical
and relational distillation. Experimental results show that
