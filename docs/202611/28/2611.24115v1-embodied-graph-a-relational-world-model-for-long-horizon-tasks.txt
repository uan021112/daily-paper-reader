Embodied Graph: A Relational World Model for Long-Horizon
Tasks
Zongyuan Wu, Xin Wang∗, Tongtong Feng, Yuan Meng, Zeyang Zhang, Ling Feng, Wenwu Zhu∗
Tsinghua University
Abstract
Long-horizon tasks in embodied and web environments involve ex-
tended decision sequences and delayed feedback, making accurate
planning crucial for success. World models tackle this by learning
latent dynamics for imagination-based planning. However, existing
approaches merely rely on dense pixel-level observations, which
forces the model to absorb redundant information while overlook-
ing relations among task-relevant entities. Consequently, small
errors may accumulate over long imagined rollouts and gradually
corrupt the predictions. In this paper, we explore world models
that imagine over sparse state abstractions, which poses two key
challenges: 1) how to remove redundant information and form
a compact state abstraction; 2) how to incorporate the relational
structure to learn reliable long-horizon dynamics. To address these
challenges, we propose Embodied Graph, a relational state abstrac-
tion that supports structured dynamics modeling and long-horizon
imagination, and the Relational World Model (RWM), which per-
forms imagination directly over graph states. RWM consists of (i)
a Task-Aware Embodied Graph Constructor that filters redundant
details and maintains graph memory through online updates, and
(ii) an Embodied Graph Recurrent State Space Model that learns node-
wise latent dynamics while capturing inter-node dependencies. The
imagined graph states provide predictions of action consequences
to guide decision-making. Experiments on Minecraft and Crafter
demonstrate the effectiveness of RWM in embodied environments.
Notably, in Minecraft, RWM is the only method that obtains dia-
monds within the given budget. Experiments on web interaction
further illustrate the applicability of relational state prediction to
the decision making process of web agents.
Keywords
Embodied Graph, World Model, Long-Horizon Task, Web Agents

Introduction
Embodied long-horizon tasks [12, 14, 18, 47] are interactive tasks
in which agents must maintain coherent planning and decision-
making over an extended interaction horizon to achieve the fi-
nal goal. Despite recent progress, these tasks remain challeng-
ing in open dynamic environments [15], ranging from manipu-
lation [38, 55], navigation [27, 35] to task planning [11, 51], largely
due to sparse rewards and delayed credit assignment over long time
spans [40]. World models have emerged as a promising approach
for such tasks by learning internal representations and predicting
future states of the environment for imagination-based planning
and control [13, 22, 34, 42, 46].

Nevertheless, existing world models are typically trained with
dense supervision from high-dimensional observations, which in-
jects massive redundant details [5, 10, 30] and obscures the spatio-
temporal relations that matter for long-horizon prediction and
planning [31]. The amount of visual change does not necessarily re-
flect its importance for decision-making. A visual change can alter
many pixels while leaving the underlying object states unchanged,
whereas acquiring a needed item may produce only a small visual
difference. Dense prediction objectives therefore devote capacity
to appearance changes while overlooking state transitions that de-
termine subsequent actions, which can lead to errors in predicting
task-relevant state changes. Small errors may then accumulate over
long rollouts [49], forming imagination drift to corrupt imagined
trajectories and shortening the effective imagination horizon. In
Fig. 1, a smoothed sandbar compounds into a hallucinated oasis,
and a policy trained on such rollouts is steered toward states that
the real environment never offers.
For this reason, we seek to use sparse relational graphs as state
abstractions, alleviating error accumulation through a compact
state representation. Unlike dense representations, graphs offer a
structured and compact abstraction of objects and their relations,
which can naturally filter out irrelevant details and focus on crit-
ical interactions. For instance, on interactive webpages, such an
abstraction can capture interactive page elements and their rela-
tions, supporting predictions of changes in field values and control
states that inform subsequent action choices. However, leveraging
graph representations for long-horizon tasks introduces several
challenges: (1) since environments are open and dynamic, objects
and relations may appear, disappear, or alter their attributes, it is
challenging to distill informative structure and update the graph
online while maintaining consistency and stability. (2) It is also
challenging to learn stable graph dynamics whose rollouts incur

less compounding error, thereby extending the effective imagina-
tion horizon, i.e., how far the model can be rolled out before its

To tackle these challenges, we introduce Embodied Graph, a
sparse relational state abstraction for open dynamic environments.
In Embodied Graph, nodes represent the agent and environment
entities, edges encode the relations along which the effects of inter-
actions propagate, and an interaction is an agent action applied to
a target node. On top of it, we build the Relational World Model
(RWM), a graph world model with two learned components: (i) a
Task-Aware Embodied Graph Constructor (TA-EGC) that maintains
the embodied graph as a bounded global memory updated from
online observations, and (ii) an Embodied Graph Recurrent State
Space Model (EG-RSSM) that learns node-wise latent dynamics with
relational propagation and imagines over graph states. Experiments
in Embodied environments (Minecraft [26] and Crafter [20]) show
that RWM outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, with the largest
gains on long-horizon targets, and its open-loop prediction error
grows much more slowly than that of DreamerV3. And further
experiments on web agents [61] illustrate the effectiveness of RWM
on web agents. Our contributions are:
• We propose Embodied Graph, a graph-based paradigm
for modeling environment dynamics in long-horizon tasks.
• We propose Relational World Model (RWM), a graph-
based world model framework consists of a Task-Aware Em-
bodied Graph Constructor for maintaining a sparse global
embodied graph, and an Embodied Graph Recurrent State
Space Model that performs node-wise latent dynamics learn-
ing while capturing inter-node dependencies.
• Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that the
proposed RWM achieves superior performance over strong
baselines in challenging long-horizon tasks on both of the
embodied control and web agents.

3.3
Decision Making with RWM
In embodied control, imagined graph rollouts train a policy over
graph-level macro-actions. While in Web agents, the imagination
helps a verifier compare candidate browser actions. The graph
construction in each setting is given in Appendices A.1 and B.
3.3.1
Embodied Control. In embodied control, the embodied graph
is built from the segmented objects of the current observation,
and an interaction is one of two types, moving to or acting on
a target object (Appendix A.1). Given the learned EG-RSSM and
the imagination horizon 𝐾, we roll out graph latents {ℎ𝑡,𝑧𝑡}𝑡𝑠+𝐾

3.3.2
Web Agents. On webpages, the embodied graph is built from
the DOM elements of the current page, and an interaction is a
native browser action (click, fill, press, scroll, or select) on a target
element (Appendix B). At decision time, a frozen web agent, WebRL-
Llama-3.1-8B [41], proposes several candidate actions. From the
common posterior, RWM starts the corresponding independent
imagined branch with each candidate action. A verifier, Qwen2.5-
VL-32B-Instruct [3], receives the imagined rollouts to make the
final decision.
4
Experiments
4.1
Experiments on Embodied Control
4.1.1
Setup. We evaluate on two long-horizon embodied bench-
marks: Minecraft [26] and Crafter [20].
Minecraft is a procedurally generated open-world 3D sandbox
where the agent must navigate large biomes and cave systems,
manage survival signals (e.g., health and hunger), and interact with
the environment by breaking blocks and fighting hostile mobs.
We use diamond acquisition as the target task, where agents need
to explore, mine, and obtain diamonds under sparse and delayed
rewards, and report the intermediate targets stone, iron and gold.
The agent starts with the required tools so that it can focus on
discovering and mining the targets. Following prior work [22], we
increase the block breaking speed to accelerate training and form
the observation and action space. Additionally, RWM obtains its
graph candidates from the observed images with a learned object-
segmentation front-end (Appendix A.2).
Crafter [20] distills similar mechanics into a compact 2D set-
ting in which the agent must gather materials, craft increasingly
advanced tools, and handle hazards to unlock 22 achievements. Its
setup is given in Appendix A.7.
Baselines. We compare with DreamerV3 [22], a state-of-the-
art model-based agent that imagines trajectories in a latent space
learned from high-dimensional observations and was the first to
obtain diamonds in Minecraft without human data. LS-Imagine

Implementation details. A macro-action is selected every
𝐻= 50 environment steps. RWM has 4M parameters, and the
executor is a 200M-parameter DreamerV3 [22]. The complete hyper-

and measured computational cost are reported in Appendix A.6.
For Minecraft, all agents are trained for 5M environment steps
with a maximum episode length of 36,000 steps, and we report
results after 1M, 2M, 3M, 4M and 5M steps. For Crafter, we follow
the default setting in [20], training agents for 1M steps with a
maximum episode length of 10,000. Unless otherwise stated, we
apply the same environment configuration to all baselines. Results
are the mean ± standard deviation over 3 training seeds.
Metrics. For Minecraft, we report the Success Rate (SR), the
fraction of episodes in which the agent obtains the target item, and
the Success Length (SL), the earliest time step at which the target is
obtained, averaged over successful episodes (“–” when no episode
succeeds). For Crafter, we follow the standard evaluation protocol
and report reward and the aggregated Crafter score, defined as the
geometric mean over achievement success rates to emphasize broad
and compositional skill acquisition.
4.1.2
Results. Table 1 shows that RWM outperforms the baselines,
with the largest gains on long-horizon targets.
For relatively simple tasks, such as collecting stone and iron,
their performances remain similar since these tasks can be solved
through simple random exploration without requiring a deep under-
standing of environment dynamics. The performance gap becomes
more evident on challenging tasks, such as collecting gold and di-
amonds. These materials are buried deeper and associated with
sparse rewards. Among the strong baselines, RWM outperforms
the others by capturing abstract object relations and leveraging
sparse feedback more effectively, resulting in continued gains and
shorter average success lengths. Notably, it is the only method that
finds diamonds within the given 5M training budget.
To further assess the generality of our approach beyond 3D sce-
narios, we evaluate RWM on Crafter. The results are provided in
Table 3, which exhibit a similar trend to those on Minecraft. Com-
pared with the baselines, RWM achieves the best performance in
terms of both Crafter score and episodic reward. Since the Crafter
score is defined as the geometric mean over achievement success
rates [20], the improvement in both reward and score suggests that
RWM not only improves the overall episode performance, but also
helps the agent make broader progress across diverse achievements.
A further analysis of the learned agent skills is provided in Appen-
dix A.7, where RWM achieves comparable performance on simple
early-stage achievements and obtains clear gains on harder long-
horizon achievements that require multi-step resource collection
and crafting.

Imagination Drift Analysis. To directly examine whether
RWM mitigates compounding prediction errors during long-horizon
imagination, we evaluate the accumulating imagination drift in Fig.
3. Starting from the same posterior state, each model performs open-
loop imagination for up to 500 environment steps, and we measure
the discrepancy between the imagined state and the corresponding

ground-truth future state through the decoded predictions. Specifi-
cally, since the decoders and prediction targets vary across methods,
the raw MSE values are not directly comparable. For a fair compar-
ison, we normalize the MSE at each imagination step by the MSE
at the first imagination step (Normalized MSE), so that the metric
reflects the relative growth of prediction error, i.e., the magnitude
of imagination drift. As shown in Fig. 3, the drift increases as the
imagination horizon becomes longer, confirming that long-horizon
rollout is inherently vulnerable to error accumulation. However,

baseline (DreamerV3). This indicates that the proposed embodied
graph abstraction and relation-aware EG-RSSM provide a more
stable predictive structure, thereby extending the effective imagina-

In addition, the smaller variance of RWM further suggests that our
method is more stable across rollout samples.
This is consistent with where RWM helps in Table 1: short roll-

quires coordinating many interdependent interactions, where small
rollout errors lead to wrong macro-action choices.

4.2
Evaluation on Interactive Webpages
Beyond embodied control, we test whether the same relational
world model carries over to Web agents, where page elements and
their DOM relations form the graph.
4.2.1
Benchmark. We evaluate 35 content-management tasks (CMS35)
of WebArena-Lite [36, 61], which are performed on the administra-
tion back end of an e-commerce website (Magento). Each episode
starts from a freshly deployed site and is limited to 30 steps. We use
the official observation and action spaces and the official evaluators.
4.2.2
Training data. RWM is trained offline on 94 recorded browser
trajectories collected by a frozen A3-Qwen3.5-9B Web agent [37]
on 12 WebArena CMS tasks. None of these tasks, nor their task
templates appears in CMS35.
4.2.3
Baselines. We compare RWM with a pixel-based world model
under the decision procedure of Section 3.3.2: WebRL-Llama-3.1-
8B [41] proposes the candidate actions, a world model forecasts the
consequence of each candidate, and another verifier (Qwen2.5-VL-
32B-Instruct [3]) selects one of them. The three variants differ only
in what the verifier receives for each candidate. Verifier-only branch
receives the candidates without any forecast. Verifier + image RSSM
additionally receives the next screen predicted by a DreamerV3-
style pixel world model on 288×512 screenshots, trained on the
same trajectories with the same number of updates and history
length. Verifier + RWM receives the element-level effects predicted
by RWM. Each task is run once per variant from a freshly deployed
site. For reference, we also report the published WebArena-Lite
results of WebRL and other agents.
4.2.4
Decision-making on CMS35. Table 4 reports task success on
CMS35. Without forecasts, the verifier-only branch solves 45.7%
of the tasks, even worse than the pure WebRL-Llama-3.1-8B. Pixel
forecasts raise this to 51.4%, and RWM further improves it to 60.0%.
This indicates that without proper evidence on the consequences
of the candidate actions, a general LLM struggles to tell which
action is correct. The predicted screen conveys the consequence of
an action only implicitly. Changes in the state of a single element
are hard to read from it. Whereas RWM explicitly indicates which
elements change and how, which the verifier can relate directly to
the task.

cent methods scale capacity and generalization. PWM [16] lever-
ages large pre-trained world models for policy learning across
continuous-control tasks, STORM [59] combines stochastic latent
dynamics with transformers for strong multi-step prediction on

Imagine [32] further extends imagination in open-world settings
via long-horizon “jumpy” transitions. Object-centric world models,
such as C-SWM [31], OP3 [50] and SlotFormer [54], factorize the
state into object slots discovered from pixels, mostly in small syn-
thetic scenes with a fixed number of objects. Most world models still
rely on dense pixel-level or entangled latent representations and
lack explicit relational structure, which can limit rollout stability
and the effective imagination horizon. Open-world tasks further
require the set of modeled objects to change online as the agent
explores.
Graphs in Embodied AI. Graphs provide structured repre-
sentations of entities and relations for embodied perception and
reasoning. Scene-graph methods such as SG-Nav [56], ESCA [28],
and GraphEQA [44] support navigation, grounding, and question
answering with 3D semantic context. EmbodiedVSR [60] updates a
dynamic scene graph after each action to guide the chain-of-thought
reasoning of a multimodal language model, but the graph serves
as input for reasoning rather than as the state of a learned dynam-
ics model. Graphs are also used as supervision, e.g., Scene Graph
Contrastive loss [48], and as knowledge memories for instruction
following, e.g., Optimus-1 [33] and ExRAP [57]. In model-based
and hierarchical RL, graph abstractions have been used for plan-
ning with skills, including Deep Skill Graphs [2] and intrinsically
motivated temporally abstract graph-based world models [1]. In
these methods, nodes represent subgoals or abstract states, and
edges represent skills that move the agent between them through
sequences of primitive actions. The graph records which subgoals
can be reached through skill execution, allowing the planner to com-
bine several skills into a longer plan. Despite these advances, these
methods primarily use graphs for perception, reasoning, or skill-
level planning, rather than modeling interaction-driven changes in
entity states over long imagined rollouts.
World Models for Web Agents. Web agents act on realistic
websites through browser actions such as clicking, typing and select-
ing. WebArena [61] provides self-hosted websites for e-commerce,
content management, forums, code hosting and maps, and WebArena-
Lite [36] is a human-verified subset of its tasks. WebRL [41] trains
LLM agents on WebArena-Lite with online reinforcement learning,
using a self-evolving curriculum and an outcome-supervised reward
model. To anticipate the consequences of actions before commit-
ting to them, recent work equips Web agents with world models.
WMA [6] trains an LLM to predict the change of the observation
caused by an action in natural language and uses these predictions
to select actions at inference time, and WebDreamer [17] prompts
an LLM to simulate the outcomes of candidate actions and plans
by scoring the simulated outcomes. However, these world models
describe the next state as free-form text and do not explicitly repre-
sent page elements and their relations, which makes it difficult to
tell which elements an action changes and to verify a prediction
against the actual page.
6
Limitations
RWM relies on an entity-level front-end: object-segmentation mod-
els trained with object labels in Minecraft and Crafter, and the DOM
on the Web. The result may be affected by the quality of the front-
end, and transfer to real-world settings requires a detector for the
relevant objects. Moreover, predefined relations (𝐾𝑛𝑛spatial neigh-
bors, DOM links) are adopted as edges, which may be unavailable
in some cases. Learning the edges is left for future works.
7
Conclusion
Existing world models often struggle with long-horizon tasks be-
cause prediction errors accumulate during imagination, leading to
drift in long rollouts. We proposed the Relational World Model
(RWM), a graph world model that imagines over Embodied Graph,
a sparse relational state abstraction. RWM combines a Task-Aware
Embodied Graph Constructor with an Embodied Graph Recurrent
State Space Model, and is instantiated for embodied control and
Web agents. In Minecraft, RWM’s open-loop prediction error grows
much more slowly than that of a pixel world model, and the result-
ing agent is the only one in our comparison that obtains diamonds
within the given budget. On webpages, RWM forecasts element-
level action consequences. Given these forecasts, a verifier selects
better actions, and solves more tasks on WebArena-Lite. These find-
ings suggest that sparse relational abstractions are a useful way to
extend the effective horizon of world models, and their value grows
with the difficulty of the task.

Implementation Details and More Results on
Embodied Control
A.1
Graph Construction
In Minecraft and Crafter, candidates are produced by an object-
segmentation front-end applied to the image observation (Appen-
dix A.2). An interaction is one of two types, moving to or acting on
a target object. The graph has a fixed capacity of 𝑁nodes, where
node zero is the agent. Each object node carries its position 𝑝∈R3,
type 𝜏, size 𝑚and last-seen step 𝑙, and the agent node carries its
position and inventory. A candidate matches an existing node if
both have the same type and their squared distance is below 𝛿2,
and the closest such candidate is selected. Edges connect each node
to its 𝐾𝑛𝑛nearest neighbors in space. The context 𝑐𝑡
𝑗of EG-RSSM
is computed by an MPNN over these edges with the same number
of layers and hidden size as the dynamics model. All scorer labels
are computed from the front-end’s detections and the agent’s own
decisions. A candidate receives a positive task label if it lies within a
distance threshold of the target of a macro-action chosen within the
next𝑊steps, and the task loss is weighted by the policy advantage.
Novelty and coverage labels use type rarity and distance from the
agent. The prediction heads of EG-RSSM decode node position, type
and size, and the agent’s position and inventory. The structural
outcome in L𝑡𝑜𝑝𝑜is node birth, a binary indicator of whether the
interaction creates new nodes.
A.2
Perception Front-End and Node Candidates
Minecraft. The front-end has two branches applied to the same
frame. An instance branch, a YOLOv8m segmentation model [29],
detects and segments objects in nine categories: log, tree, craft-
ing table, furnace, gold ore, diamond ore, iron ore, coal ore and
zombie. A semantic branch, DeepLabV3+ [8] with a ResNet-50 en-
coder [24], labels every pixel as background, water, grass, sand,
lava or stone. Both branches are trained on frames collected from
Minecraft episodes with random exploration, which we annotated
manually with the category labels above, and are frozen during
RL. Instance masks take precedence over semantic labels where
they overlap. Each instance yields a candidate with its type, mask
centroid, bounding box and area. The 2D outputs are lifted to 3D
with a monocular depth model (Video Depth Anything [9]), which
predicts depth. The 3D position is used to match candidates across
steps.
Figure 5 shows the front-end output on the agent’s 64 × 64
observations in three scenes. Large regions such as grass, water
and sand are segmented as contiguous areas, and small objects such
as single ore blocks are kept as separate instances. The areas and
instantces form the node candidates.
Crafter. As the observation image in Crafter is a grid of different
objects, segmentation is naturally provided. Node candidates are
produced by an object semantic classifier MLP head trained on
Crafter observations.
A.3
Observation and Action space
For Minecraft and Crafter, we follow the observation and action
spaces of DreamerV3 [22].
A.4
Goal-Conditioned Executor and
Macro-Action
The executor is a 200M-parameter DreamerV3. It takes dense ob-
servations together with the macro-action and outputs primitive
actions. The macro-action consists of an interaction type and a
target node. The executor receives the interaction type and the
target’s position as its goal, which is embedded and concatenated
with the policy inputs, without modifying the observation or action
spaces. A maximum of 𝐻= 50 environment steps are given to the
executor to achieve the goal.

A.5
Optimization
RWM is trained with the total loss of Eq. (12) (weights in Table 5)
using DreamerV3’s optimizer settings unless otherwise stated. For
policy learning in embodied control, we summarize the graph by a
readout 𝑔𝑡that concatenates the latent state of the agent node with
pooled latent states of the object nodes. Reward and continuation
heads predict ˆ𝑟𝑡and ˆ𝑐𝑡from 𝑔𝑡. The graph policy assigns a score to
each admissible interaction based on the latent state of its target
node and selects an interaction by a softmax over these scores. A
value function on 𝑔𝑡is trained on 𝜆-return targets, as in DreamerV3.
A.6
Online Update Cost and Graph Size
The graph has fixed capacity, so the per-step cost and memory of
RWM do not grow with episode length. Only the current graph 𝐺𝑡
is kept online (at most 𝑁nodes and 𝐾𝑛𝑛𝑁directed edges. Each step
of TA-EGC has four parts. First, three small MLP heads score at most
𝑀candidates, which costs 𝑂(𝑀). Second, the Top-𝑃candidates are
selected in 𝑂(𝑀log 𝑃). Third, each of the 𝑃admitted candidates
is compared with the 𝑁existing nodes of the same type, which
costs 𝑂(𝑃𝑁), and the nodes unseen for the longest time are evicted
when the graph is full. Finally, the 𝐾𝑛𝑛-nearest-neighbor edges are
rebuilt. Our implementation computes all pairwise distances for
this, which costs 𝑂(𝑁2) and is cheap for 𝑁= 128. Additionally,
EG-RSSM’s message passing costs 𝑂((𝑁+ 𝐾𝑛𝑛𝑁) 𝑑) per step for
hidden size 𝑑. In Minecraft (𝑁= 128, 𝑀= 256, 𝐾𝑛𝑛= 4) the full
agent runs at 63.28±0.88 policy steps per second, compared with
70.76±1.53 for DreamerV3 with the same hyperparameters (AMD
EPYC 7543 CPU, single NVIDIA A100 GPU).

B
Implementation Details on the Web
B.1
Web embodied graph.
We follow the official observation and action spaces [36, 61]. Candi-
date nodes are the DOM elements of the current page, with their tag,
ARIA role, input type, text, value and instruction strings, normal-
ized bounding box, and corresponding flags. A virtual page node
replaces the agent node. Edges connect parent and child elements in
both directions and link native labels to the controls they activate.
An interaction 𝐴= (𝑖, 𝑣𝑡𝑎𝑟) is one of click, fill, press, scroll, or select
applied to a target node, together with its payload (typed text, key,
option, or scroll amount).
B.2
Construction and Learning
TA-EGC scores the DOM candidates with the same task, novelty
and coverage heads as in embodied control and admits the Top-𝑃
into the graph. Instead of spatial proximity, a candidate is matched
to an existing node by its document, element reference and tag. The
task label marks the targets of the next𝑊=16 recorded interactions.
Novelty and coverage use the rarity of the node type and the rarity
of its position on the screen, respectively. All labels are weighted
uniformly, since the Web policy is fixed and provides no advan-
tage. The prediction heads decode the state flags, input values and
bounding boxes of the elements, and TA-EGC and EG-RSSM are
trained jointly.
B.3
Navigation Head
On the Web, the structural outcome in L𝑡𝑜𝑝𝑜is the page that an
interaction leads to: a click either stays on the current page, where
changes of elements are captured by the predicted flags, or loads
a new page. We predict this outcome with a navigation head, a
softmax classifier on the frozen EG-RSSM latents. Its classes are
stay or the site routes of the pages seen in training.
B.4
Decision procedure.
At each step, WebRL proposes 3 candidate actions from the official
prompt. The world model forecasts each candidate from the shared
posterior. The verifier (Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct) receives the task,
the current screenshot, the observed action history, the candidates
and their forecasts, and returns one candidate. Unparsable replies
fall back to WebRL’s top candidate. The verifier is greedily decoded
