Abstract
3D Gaussian splats enable real-time rendering but require substantial storage. Existing compression methods
either encode the primitives of a given splat or optimize a compact representation independently for each scene.
Yet a Gaussian splat is only one realization of a scene: different arrangements of primitives can produce nearly
identical rendered views. We exploit this freedom on the decoding side with SplatTok, a learned scene-resynthesis
codec. Trained across thousands of real-world scenes, a transformer encodes an existing splat into compact,
spatially anchored latent tokens, and a shared decoder synthesizes a new set of standard Gaussian primitives
without preserving correspondence to the input. The representation is learned through rendering supervision
rather than primitive reconstruction. At compression time, a quantization-aware latent search refines only
the scene code, keeping the networks frozen, and closes most of the encoder’s remaining error. On standard
benchmarks, using only renders of the input splat for the latent search, without the original training images,
SplatTok matches the PSNR of the feed-forward codec FCGS with 4 to 6.5 times smaller files, compressing input
splats about 75 to 110 times. With the training images, it exceeds the PSNR of the per-scene method HAC++
with 13 to 41% smaller files. A single model trained on DL3DV generalizes to unseen scenes with millions of
Gaussians and supports multiple target rates without retraining.

1 Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting [21] has quickly become a standard way to capture and share photorealistic 3D scenes. A scene
is reconstructed once from multi-view images captured from many camera poses and is then rendered in real time on
ordinary hardware. Rendering is fast because the scene is stored directly as millions of Gaussian primitives, each with
dozens of attributes, and that is also what makes it large: a typical scene occupies hundreds of megabytes to over a
gigabyte. That is too large to stream to a headset or phone, to store by the thousands in an asset library, or to send as
easily as an image or a video. In this work we compress existing splats. We want a file one to two orders of magnitude

in renderers built for the standard format.
Existing compression methods each meet some of these goals but not all of them. The most compact results come from
per-scene methods, which build a new, structured representation for every scene and optimize it against the scene’s
training images, often with learned context models for entropy coding [8, 10, 28, 42]; see [2] for a survey. The strongest
of these reach compression ratios near 100-fold, but they need the training images, take tens of minutes per scene, and
usually must be retrained for every target size. Their output is also not a standard splat: anchor-based methods store
learned features from which small networks generate the Gaussians for each view at render time, so they need their own
renderer. Post-training methods prune and quantize a splat without building a new representation [15, 24, 33, 38, 43]
and keep the standard format, but reach more modest ratios or need minutes of fine-tuning to recover quality. The
feed-forward codec FCGS [9] removes per-scene optimization altogether: a network trained on many scenes compresses
a splat in seconds, without images, and decodes to a standard splat. It does so primitive by primitive, coding each
Gaussian’s attributes with limited context from its neighbors and keeping the original set of Gaussians, and reaches
about 10 to 30-fold compression. No existing method reaches per-scene compression ratios without the training images
and without per-scene representation training, while decoding to a standard splat.
We take a different view: rather than learning a new representation for each scene, or a codec for individual Gaussians,
we learn a model of whole scenes and compress within it. Our method, SplatTok, encodes an entire splat into a compact
set of latent tokens anchored at 3D locations across the scene, and decodes them into a new set of Gaussians placed
around those anchors. The encoder attends across the whole scene, so each token summarizes its region in the context
of everything else; the decoder never sees the input Gaussians and is free to synthesize whatever arrangement renders
the scene best. We train the model on a large dataset of real-world scenes, supervised only by rendering, and quantize
and entropy code the tokens to form the bitstream. This differs from FCGS in what is learned and what is stored:
FCGS learns to code the attributes of given primitives, whereas SplatTok learns how scenes are structured and stores a
description from which primitives are regenerated.
Because the decoder maps any code in its latent space to a renderable splat, compressing a new scene reduces to
finding its code. The encoder’s output is already a good latent code, and a short search over the tokens alone, with the
network frozen and quantization applied during the search, brings it close to the best code the decoder can express.
The search needs only renders of the input splat from its cameras as its target, so it works without the training images;
when they are available, they can serve as the target instead. This mirrors encode-time refinement in learned image
compression [7, 46], here applied to whole 3D scenes. Our analysis shows why it is effective: the search changes the
code by only 4 to 10% yet improves fidelity to the input splat by about 8 dB, indicating that refinement closes at least
84% of the initial optimality gap within the fixed decoder and admissible code space. The rate is set by the precision of
the tokens and by the density of the anchors, so a single trained model serves many target sizes, and the same weights
are used for every dataset in our experiments.
The resulting codec outperforms the best published per-scene methods in PSNR at smaller sizes. On Mip-NeRF 360 [5],
SplatTok reaches 27.93 dB at 7.46 MB per scene, 0.46 dB above HAC++ [10] at 8.61 MB, and 28.09 dB at 10.89 MB,
0.49 dB above HAC++ at 18.48 MB, with similar gains on Tanks and Temples [23] and Deep Blending [18], using one
model for all sizes instead of one training run per size. With coarser anchors, the same model still exceeds HAC++ in
PSNR with files 53 to 60% smaller. Without the training images, SplatTok matches the PSNR of FCGS on the same
input splats with 4 to 6.5 times smaller files on DL3DV, Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks and Temples and Deep Blending. The
same model, trained only on DL3DV splats, compresses scenes of several million Gaussians from these other datasets
without retraining.

• A scene-resynthesis codec for Gaussian splats that encodes a whole scene into a compact set of 3D-anchored
tokens and synthesizes a new set of standard 3DGS primitives from them, trained across thousands of real scenes
with rendering supervision alone.
• Compression as a short search in the decoder’s latent space, which reaches per-scene compression quality without
the training images, without per-scene representation training, and with one model for all target sizes. We show
that it works because most of the encoder’s remaining error is an amortization gap rather than a limit of the

2 Related Work
Compact Gaussian splats trained per scene. A first line of work makes the splat itself smaller while it is fit
to the training images. These methods learn masks that prune redundant Gaussians [24, 34], replace attributes with
codebook indices or quantized latents [17, 24, 32], arrange attributes into smooth 2D grids that standard image codecs
compress well [30], or add an explicit rate term to the training objective [40]. SpeedyGS [52] compresses ordinary
3DGS primitives with a content-aware two-stage optimization. These methods keep the output close to a standard splat
and reach roughly 15 to 45-fold compression on Mip-NeRF 360, but each scene still needs a full optimization from its
training images, and changing the target size means optimizing again.
Anchor-based methods with learned entropy models. The smallest files come from methods that replace
the unstructured splat with a structured one. Scaffold-GS [28] stores sparse anchors with learned features, from
which small networks generate nearby Gaussians for each view. HAC [8] and HAC++ [10] model the distribution
of these anchor features with a hash-grid context and, in HAC++, context within each anchor, while ContextGS [42]
decodes anchors coarse to fine with each level conditioned on the previous ones, and CompGS [26] predicts primitives
as residuals from anchors. Recent methods refine the context model further, with context-adaptive triplanes [50],
hierarchical sparse transform coding [44], local geometry-aware hierarchical context [12], or anchor-wise causal context
with integer-only inference [48]. Together they reach close to 100-fold compression, the best reported. The price is a
per-scene optimization of tens of minutes against the training images, repeated for every target rate except in progressive
coding [11], which trains one representation whose bitstream can be decoded at several rates, and an output that is not a
standard splat but needs the method’s own networks at render time. SplatTok instead learns the structure of scenes once,
across thousands of them, so that a new scene needs no representation of its own, and its latents decode to ordinary
Gaussians.
Post-training compression. Another family compresses an already trained splat. LightGaussian [15] prunes
Gaussians by their contribution to the training views, distills spherical harmonics to a lower degree and vector-quantizes
the rest; Compressed3D [33] clusters attributes by their sensitivity to rendering error; MesonGS [43] combines
importance-based pruning with octree coding of positions and a transform coder for attributes. Each then fine-tunes
the compressed splat against the training images for several minutes. FlexGaussian [38] removes the fine-tuning and
chooses pruning and mixed-precision quantization without training. KISS-GS [31] combines strong compaction with an
image-based attribute codec and encoding-aware fine-tuning, and reaches the highest ratios of this family while keeping
a standard splat. These methods keep the standard format, but reach more modest ratios, especially without fine-tuning.
SplatTok’s latent search instead optimizes the latent code itself, with quantization in the loop, while the frozen decoder,
trained across thousands of scenes, keeps the code within the space of plausible scenes. This reaches several times
higher compression ratios than pruning and quantizing primitives, and since SplatTok accepts any standard splat, it is
complementary to these methods and can further compress a splat they have already compacted (Sec. 5.5).
Feed-forward compression. FCGS [9] is, to our knowledge, the first codec that compresses a splat in a single
forward pass of a network trained on many scenes. It needs no images and no per-scene optimization, decodes to a
standard splat, and runs in seconds. It codes the given Gaussians themselves: each primitive’s attributes are entropy
coded with context from already decoded neighbors, and the decoded splat has the same set of Gaussians as the input,
which limits compression to about 10 to 30-fold. LocoMoco [27] extends FCGS with long-context entropy modeling
over Morton-ordered windows of Gaussians, and still codes the given primitives; without public code or models, we do
not include it in our comparisons. Feed-forward reconstruction methods have also been combined with compression of
their predicted Gaussians [19, 49]; these compress the output of reconstruction from images rather than an existing

Learned compression and encode-time refinement. Learned image codecs pair analysis and synthesis
transforms with an entropy model [3, 4, 29]. Since an amortized encoder does not find the best code for every input,
refining each latent at encode time with the decoder fixed recovers part of this gap [7, 46]. Implicit neural representations
take the opposite extreme and overfit a network to each instance [14], which is also what per-scene splat compression
methods do. SplatTok sits between the two: a codec amortized over thousands of scenes and refined per scene at encode
time with the network frozen, and we show that this refinement closes most of the gap to the best code the decoder can
express.
Set latents and latent models of Gaussian splats. Our architecture builds on set latents for 3D shapes, which
encode a point cloud into a fixed-size set of latent vectors through cross-attention [51] and now underlie many 3D
generative models [53]. Latent models of Gaussian splats [36, 45] are built to support a generative prior, mostly at
object level, rather than to reconstruct a given scene faithfully, and are not evaluated in terms of size and rendering
quality. At scene level, Can3Tok [16] is a variational autoencoder for 3D Gaussians, designed to provide a latent space
for diffusion-based scene generation. A fixed set of learned queries cross-attends to the input Gaussians, so every scene,
whatever its extent or complexity, is mapped to a latent of the same size, and the decoder always outputs the same
number of Gaussians. To fit this budget, each input splat is first filtered and subsampled to a fixed number of primitives
(40K in their experiments), which discards most of a real capture. The model is trained with an L2 loss on the attributes
of these input Gaussians rather than on renderings. Since many different sets of Gaussians render the same scene, this
loss asks the decoder to reproduce one particular arrangement of primitives rather than the scene’s appearance, and
reconstructions are measured by attribute error rather than rendering fidelity. Its checkpoints are not publicly available,
which prevents a direct comparison. GaussianGPT [39] generates scenes autoregressively over the discrete tokens of a
sparse 3D convolutional autoencoder with lookup-free quantization. Its encoder takes a voxel grid with at most one
Gaussian per voxel, stored as an offset from the voxel center; when a voxel contains several Gaussians, all but one
are randomly dropped. Its training scenes are therefore built to match this structure, by fitting Scaffold-GS with one
Gaussian per anchor aligned to the voxel centers, and view-dependent color is not modeled for scenes. A splat trained
with standard 3DGS, with many overlapping primitives of different sizes per voxel, falls outside this input domain.
SplatTok instead grows the number of anchors, and hence the code and the output, with the input splat, accepts any
standard splat, and is supervised only through rendering.
Scene tokens from images. TokenGS [35] and SceneTok [1] also describe a scene with a set of learned tokens,
but are built for feed-forward reconstruction from posed images rather than for compressing a splat. In TokenGS,
learnable 3DGS tokens cross-attend to features of a few input views and each regresses a fixed number of Gaussians,
trained with a rendering loss. SceneTok encodes many context views into a small set of unstructured tokens, from
which a rectified-flow decoder generates novel views as images rather than an explicit 3D representation. Neither takes
an existing splat as input or produces a bitstream evaluated in size and rendering fidelity; their architectures, losses and
pipelines target the fast construction of renderable scenes from images. SplatTok instead starts from a finished splat,
anchors its tokens in 3D across the whole scene, and is trained and evaluated as a codec.

Finally, a latent search refines the quantized code at encode time with both networks frozen, using renders of the input
splat or, when available, the training images as the target (Sec. 4.7).
Input and normalization. SplatTok takes a splat exactly as it is stored: an unordered set of Gaussians in the
parametrization of Sec. 3, of any size N, without the training images. Before encoding, the splat and its cameras are
brought to a normalized frame by a similarity transform computed from the cameras, consistent with the normalization
of the gsplat library [47], so that splats of different scenes share a common position and scale. The decoded splat lives
in the same frame and is rendered with the transformed cameras.
4.2 Scene partition into anchors
We partition the scene into A anchors, chosen proportional to N for a fixed target number of Gaussians per anchor, so
that the number of anchors follows the size of the scene. The anchor positions {ak}A
k=1 are the centers of a k-means
clustering of all Gaussian positions {µ i}. Clusters with more than K members are split. Each Gaussian joins its nearest
anchor and, as long as that anchor has fewer than K members, also its second-nearest one. The resulting member sets
Nk, with |Nk| ≤K, overlap near cluster boundaries, so that neighboring anchors see the Gaussians between them.
Each anchor also stores a size σk, the median distance of its members from ak, capped at a multiple of the scene’s
median anchor size so that isolated anchors on sparse outliers do not span large regions. Positions inside an anchor are
expressed as normalized offsets (µ i −ak)/σk, so that every anchor is processed at a common scale, whether it covers a
dense surface or a sparse background region. The anchor positions and sizes are the only geometric side information of
the code.

Because attention normalizes over however many members an anchor has, the output is always T tokens per anchor.
This is the central structural choice of SplatTok. The code length of an anchor is fixed, independent of how many
Gaussians fall into it, how they are arranged, and what their individual attributes are. The bitstream therefore grows
with the number of anchors and the token precision, and the decoder is free to represent an anchor with a different set of
Gaussians than the input had. FCGS [9], by contrast, codes each given Gaussian, so its code length is tied to the input
primitives. It also makes the anchor density a rate control: placing fewer anchors per scene shortens the code without
retraining (Sec. 5.5).
The tokens of all anchors are then processed jointly by a transformer, so that each token summarizes its region in the
context of the rest of the scene. Before the transformer, a learned embedding of the token index t is added to each token.
The first few layers apply self-attention among the T tokens of each anchor, and the remaining layers alternate between
this local attention and global self-attention over all AT tokens of the scene, similar to VGGT [41]. In the global layers,
queries and keys carry a 3D rotary position encoding [37] of their anchor position ak, shared by the T tokens of the
anchor, so that attention between tokens depends on the relative position of their anchors. Global attention gives each
token context beyond its own anchor, which a per-anchor or per-primitive encoder cannot use; restricting the encoder to
attention within anchors lowers the quality of the reconstructed scene (Sec. 5.7). A final layer normalization and linear

5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental setup
Evaluation datasets. On DL3DV [25], we evaluate on 173 held-out test scenes, which do not overlap with our
training scenes. All methods, including FCGS, compress the same input splats. Some test scenes may appear in the
training set of FCGS, which can only favor FCGS, although our input splats are retrained and differ from those it saw.
To measure generalization to other capture setups and scene sizes, we further evaluate on Mip-NeRF 360 [5], Tanks and
Temples [23] and Deep Blending [18], whose splats contain up to 10× more primitives than the DL3DV training splats.
For these datasets we follow the evaluation protocol of 3DGS [21] as specified in [2, 31]. The held-out views are used
neither to fit the input splat nor as targets of the latent search, so all reported metrics are on views that SplatTok’s search
has never seen.

rates, the training-free FlexGaussian [38], and LightGaussian [15] and HAC++ [10], which we fine-tune or retrain
on renders of the input splat instead of the training images. All of them except FCGS use the cameras of the input
splat, as SplatTok’s latent search does: FlexGaussian to score the importance of Gaussians, and LightGaussian and
HAC++ to render their targets. With training images (Tab. 2), we compare with the strongest per-scene methods,
ContextGS [42] and HAC++ [10], which we rerun three times per scene because of its run-to-run variance; with
KISS-GS [31], the strongest post-training method, which also decodes to a standard splat; and with representative
compact and post-training methods [34, 43]. SplatTok, the methods in Tab. 1 and our HAC++ reruns use the same
metric implementation and resolution; the remaining numbers in Tab. 2 are taken from the respective papers.
Metrics. We report PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS (VGG) against the GT images of the held-out test views, the size of the
compressed file in MB (220 bytes), and the compression time per scene. Unless stated otherwise, times exclude fitting
the input splat and are measured on a single GH200 GPU. SplatTok’s low and high rates are b=6 and b=8 bits at one
anchor per 128 input Gaussians. All SplatTok results in this report come from one trained model with the same weights,
for every dataset, bit depth, anchor density and search target; the network is never retrained or fine-tuned per dataset,
rate or scene.
Training data. We train on 9,101 DL3DV scenes and hold out 173 for testing, every 50th scene of the sorted list,
after excluding scenes whose splats fit their training images poorly or whose images have a non-standard resolution.
Each scene’s input splat is fit for 10k steps on images at 480×270 with gsplat. Each training step renders 32 random
views of a scene.
Architecture and training. Each anchor holds T=16 tokens of C=32 channels, the anchors are placed at one per
128 input Gaussians, each anchor takes at most K=640 members, and the decoder generates M=160 Gaussians per
anchor. The encoder has width 512 and 12 layers with 8 heads of dimension 64; the first four layers attend within
anchors, and the remaining eight alternate between scene-level and within-anchor attention. The decoder uses one
cross-attention layer at the token width, followed by six residual feed-forward layers of width 128 and a linear output
head. Colors use spherical harmonics up to degree ℓ=3 for luma and the DC term only for the two chroma channels.
The model has 57.3M parameters: 56.0M in the encoder (50.4M in its 12 transformer layers, 5.0M in the cross-attention
from the learned anchor queries to the Gaussians, and 0.6M in the input projection, query and position embeddings, and
the output projection to the tokens) and 1.25M in the decoder. We train with Muon [20] for the 86 weight matrices
(56.6M parameters, learning rate scaled by 3 and matched to the AdamW update size) and AdamW for the remaining
parameters, with weight decay 10−4, gradient clipping at 1.0, λ=0.2 in Eq. (3), bf16 mixed precision, and a batch of
16 scenes (including gradient accumulation of 4). Training runs for 300 epochs at a learning rate of 5×10−5 after a
10-epoch linear warm-up, with gradient checkpointing and FlashAttention-2, and takes about 6 days on 4 NVIDIA
A100 40GB GPUs.
Latent search. We first search the 8-bit code and then derive the 6-bit code from it. The 8-bit search starts from
the encoder’s code and runs 500 steps of Adam on 32 random training views per step, with a peak learning rate of 0.2
decayed to 0.02 with a cosine schedule (on DL3DV, 300 steps with a peak learning rate of 0.1). The stored code is an
exponential moving average of the iterates with a time constant of 5% of the steps. For the 6-bit code, the 8-bit result is
rounded to 6 bits and refined for 100 further steps at a peak learning rate of 0.03, again with a moving average. This is
faster than a separate 6-bit search and also gives a better code (Sec. 5.6).
Quantization and entropy coding. The per-channel steps ∆c are set from the encoder’s code at each bit depth
and stay fixed during the search. The rANS coder uses 16-bit probabilities and byte-wise renormalization. Anchor
positions are quantized to 10 bits per axis for the Morton order, and the context neighbor π(k) is the nearest of the 32
nearest anchors that precedes k in this order. The neighbor’s symbol is binned into at most 64 bins, with one extra

5.2 Compression without training images
Tab. 1 compares methods that compress a splat without its training images; all are evaluated against the GT images of
the held-out views, not against renders of the input splat. Without the latent search, the SplatTok encoder is far below

and Temples with Deep Blending, and 0.14 dB higher on DL3DV) with 4 to 6.5 times smaller files, and exceeds FCGS

5.5 Rate control
Since every anchor is coded with the same number of tokens, the size of a code is set by two quantities: the bit depth of
the tokens and the number of anchors. The same trained model serves every setting of both, and since the number of
anchors follows the number of input Gaussians, compacting the input splat beforehand acts as a third control.
Bit depth. Tab. 3 varies the precision of the tokens on Truck. Lowering the precision from 8 to 6 and 5 bits costs only
0.15 and 0.35 dB, while 4 bits costs 1.1 dB; the same trained model and latent search cover this whole range.
Anchor density. Tab. 4 uses the model trained with one anchor per 128 input Gaussians, without retraining, at half
the anchor density, so that each anchor summarizes about twice as many Gaussians and the decoder emits about 0.6
times as many Gaussians as the input. The model handles the coarser anchors well: at 8 bits, one anchor per 256
Gaussians matches or nearly matches the PSNR of one anchor per 128 Gaussians at 6 bits (within 0.02 dB, and 0.08 dB
higher on Tanks and Temples) with 19 to 23% smaller files. At low rates, fewer anchors at a higher precision are
therefore a better use of the bits than more anchors at a lower precision. At 6 bits, the coarser anchors reach 1.2 to
4.0 MB per scene and still exceed HAC++ at its low rate (Tab. 2) in PSNR, by 0.29 dB on Mip-NeRF 360, 0.41 dB
on Tanks and Temples and 0.07 dB on Deep Blending, with 53 to 60% smaller files. With half as many anchors, each
step of the latent search decodes half as many Gaussians, which roughly halves the search time. Fine detail suffers

5.7 Ablations
Global attention. To measure the encoder itself, we compare codes without the latent search, which could com-
pensate for a weaker encoder. Replacing the global attention layers with attention within each anchor lowers the
encoder-only PSNR on the DL3DV test scenes by 0.82 dB at the same size, confirming that scene-level context improves
the code beyond what each anchor’s own Gaussians provide. The latent search narrows this gap, but 0.26 dB remain
after the same number of search steps. A local-only encoder has a cheaper forward pass, but it does not speed up the
latent search, the slowest stage, which uses only the frozen decoder.

faster, the encoding quadratically because of the global attention; on the largest scenes the latent search still accounts
for about 80% of the time, and GPU-accelerated clustering could shorten the remainder further. Decoding a stored code
takes well under a second even for the largest scenes. The decoder emits 160 Gaussians per anchor, and anchors are
placed at one per 128 input Gaussians, so the decoded splat holds about 1.25 times as many Gaussians as the input; its
rendering cost is therefore comparable to that of the input splat, at 171 against 184 frames per second on the Garden
scene of Mip-NeRF 360 (4.4 against 3.5 million Gaussians, one 1600-pixel-wide frame at a time).

Among methods that reach comparable compression ratios, SplatTok’s compression time is similar or lower. On
the same input splats and GPU, HAC++ retrained on renders of the input splat takes about 33 minutes per scene on
Mip-NeRF 360, against 10 to 13 minutes for SplatTok (Tab. 1). KISS-GS [31] reports timings for a single scene, Bicycle
from Mip-NeRF 360, on one A100 GPU: at its higher rate, compaction, encoding and encoding-aware fine-tuning take
about 950 seconds, excluding the reconstruction of the input splat, against roughly 820 seconds for SplatTok on a splat
of 3.5 million Gaussians (Tab. 7). Since the KISS-GS number is read from the runtime plot in their paper and measured
on different hardware, this comparison is approximate. Feed-forward and training-free methods such as FCGS and
FlexGaussian compress a scene in seconds (Tab. 1), but produce files 3 to 6.5 times larger.
6 Discussion
Limitations. The latent search is the slowest stage: it takes close to three minutes on scenes of one million Gaussians,
far longer than a single forward pass of the encoder. The encoder also needs substantial GPU memory on large
scenes, about 30 GB for four million Gaussians, because its global attention spans all tokens of the scene; a chunked
implementation could reduce this.
Accelerating the latent search. Decoding all anchors is by far the most expensive part of each search step on
scenes with many Gaussians. Since each step renders only a few cameras, decoding only the anchors visible from these
cameras would reduce this cost. Fewer anchors also shorten the search, as Sec. 5.5 shows.
Adaptive anchor density. The anchor-density experiment (Sec. 5.5) shows that the same model handles coarser
anchors well, but that fine detail suffers more than overall fidelity when every region receives fewer tokens. Allocating
anchors according to local complexity, rather than uniformly per input Gaussian, would spend the bits where they matter
most.
Learned entropy models. The counted probability tables capture little of the dependence between neighboring
anchors (Sec. 5.7). A learned entropy model conditioned on already decoded tokens, as in learned image compression [4,
29], could reduce the rate further, and a rate term in the latent search would let it trade size and quality per scene.

Scaling with data. As a learned codec, SplatTok should further benefit from more training data: without the latent
search, the encoder scores 1.8 dB higher on training scenes than on unseen ones, a sign that 9,101 scenes do not yet
cover the diversity of real captures, while the latent search closes this gap on unseen scenes. Better data augmentation
for training the autoencoder, for example over scene scale, density and capture style, is a complementary way to reduce
this gap. Since the latent search primarily compensates for errors in the amortized code, a better encoder trained on
larger and more diverse data may also reduce the number of search steps needed to reach a given quality.
Scene tokens beyond compression. Beyond compression, the tokens form a compact, 3D-anchored description
of a whole scene, a few thousand anchors instead of millions of primitives, which makes them a natural interface for
downstream tasks such as scene generation, editing, retrieval and semantic understanding.
7 Conclusion
Instead of coding the primitives of a given splat or optimizing a new representation per scene from scratch, SplatTok
resynthesizes the scene: it learns a latent space of spatially anchored tokens from thousands of splats and compresses a
splat by searching for its code in this space with the networks frozen. A single model compresses splats from different
datasets at a wide range of rates without their training images, exceeds per-scene methods in PSNR at smaller sizes
when these images are available, and decodes to a standard splat. Because it compresses whatever splat it is given, it
also inherits future improvements in splat reconstruction.
