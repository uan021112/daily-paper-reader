Abstract
Image-to-code methods for 3D scene reconstruction pro-
duce executable code that generates editable digital content
and supports visual simulation. However, existing meth-
ods primarily rely on appearance similarity, which pro-
vides limited guidance for distinguishing errors in geom-
etry, materials, and illumination. Light shapes scene ap-
pearance through its interactions with geometry and ma-
terials, influencing shading, cast shadows, and reflections.
We therefore present LightHarness, a VLM-based agen-
tic framework that incorporates explicit lighting reason-
ing into iterative scene reconstruction. To be specific, we
leverage complementary geometric, intrinsic, and illumi-
nation cues to jointly refine scene structure and appear-
ance, and propose Lighting Reasoning Memory to main-
tain light-source hypotheses, which supports evidence, and
revision history throughout iterations. Together, they sup-
port reasoning about partially visible and unseen sources,
with evidence-guided verification directing subsequent revi-
sions. Our framework reconstructs explicit scenes through
coherent reasoning about geometry, materials, and illumi-
nation without additional training or differentiable render-
ing. Extensive experiments demonstrate superior image-to-
code reconstruction accuracy under challenging shadows
and complex illumination, further supporting downstream
scene editing, object insertion, and versatile relighting.
1. Introduction
Reconstructing an executable, editable 3D scene from a sin-
gle image connects visual understanding with digital scene
creation. Image-to-code reconstruction offers a promising
direction: a scene program (e.g., Blender, Three.js) explic-
itly represents geometry, spatial layout, materials, cameras,
and light sources, enabling novel-view rendering and subse-
quent scene editing [10, 29, 35]. Except for spatial ground-
ing that concerns object poses or location, lighting is also
central to this process. Shading, cast shadows, highlights
and reflections arise from the complex interactions between
illumination, geometry, and materials. Accurate image-to-
code reconstruction requires recovering object structure and
layout together with the illumination that explains their ob-
served appearance.
Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) [9,
24] have enabled scene construction through executable
programs, connecting visual and semantic reasoning with
explicit control over scene components.
This capabil-
ity supports a common iterative paradigm: an agent pro-
poses or edits a scene program, inspects the rendered re-
sult, and revises its proposal based on visual feedback.
SceneCraft [11] and BlenderAlchemy [12] demonstrate this

Recent

Across these approaches, rendered-reference compar-
isons provide a central feedback signal for scene revi-
sion. However, such a discrepancy on appearance does not
uniquely identify its underlying cause, because similar im-
age differences can arise from geometry, reflectance, or il-
lumination.
For example, a dark region may arise from
low surface reflectance, a surface facing away from light
sources, or insufficient illumination from an unseen source.
Simply brightening the material can reduce a local image
discrepancy, but leave the underlying illumination error un-
resolved, leading to an incorrect material estimation. The
challenge is therefore to connect observed lighting effects
to their plausible geometric, material, and illumination ex-
planations. This motivates our central question: How can
we explicitly reason about scene geometry, materials,
and light sources for more accurate image-to-code re-
construction beyond appearance?
To mitigate this, we propose LightHarness, a VLM-
based agent framework that incorporates explicit lighting
modeling into iterative scene generation and verification
with complementary evidence and reasoning. We first
associate light-source hypotheses with evidence of their in-
teractions with scene geometry and materials. Specifically,
we combine estimated HDR illumination [3], depth and sur-
face orientation [13, 27], intrinsic albedo and shading [13].
These complementary sources of evidence relate appear-
ance discrepancies to candidate revisions of the underlying
scene factors, helping the generator agent determine which
geometry, material, or light parameters to update during re-
construction, and finally output an executable Blender pro-
gram. As for the verifier agent, we equip it with a rubric
scorer and a VLM. The rubric scorer assesses rendered-
reference agreement based on predefined criteria, while the
VLM uses these assessments, reference-image history revi-
sions, and decomposed rendering evidence to diagnose and
propose refinement updates.
To preserve and refine illumination reasoning iteratively
in the proposed agentic system, we design Lighting Rea-
soning Memory (LRM), shared by the generator and veri-
fier agents. LRM associates each hypothesized light source
with a group of stable parameters, including identity, vis-
ibility, location, color, etc., establishing a clear correspon-
dence among observations, editable Blender elements and
the revision history. Notably, we explicitly consider par-
tially visible, occluded, and off-frame light sources as hy-
potheses, enabling a more complete model of scene illu-
mination that accounts for both visible and inferred light
sources. In each iteration, the generator uses this persis-
tent lighting proxy to build scene programs and refine pa-
rameters, while the verifier examines it based on rendered
evidence and recommends corrections. By incorporating
source-specific observations, decisions, and their support-
ing evidence in a synergistic framework, LRM provides
persistent illumination-centric context for LightHarness,
enabling subsequent geometry, material, and lighting revi-
sions to build coherently on historical judgments.
Extensive experiments demonstrate our competitive 3D
image-to-code reconstruction accuracy. We further verify
our effectiveness on hard cases under complex lights and
shadows, and showcase that our method can generalize to
rich downstream applications, including image relighting,
material/texture editing, and harmonious object insertion.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We propose a VLM-based 3D image-to-code reconstruc-
tion framework that incorporates explicit lighting model-
ing to guide the recovery of geometry, materials, and light
sources in an executable scene program.
• We introduce Lighting Reasoning Memory to preserve
source identities, supporting evidence, and revision his-
tory across generation and verification, combining geo-
metric and intrinsic priors with light-transport evidence
to guide iterative scene refinement.
• We demonstrate competitive 3D image-to-code recon-
struction accuracy and further illustrate the utility of the
reconstructed scenes through downstream applications,
including image relighting, enhancement and complex
light understanding.


2. Related Work
Lighting Estimation. Inverse rendering [14] aims to esti-
mate scene geometry, reflectance, and illumination to ex-
plain observed image appearance and enable editing [15].
PhotoScene [33] optimizes materials and lighting on ap-
proximately aligned CAD geometry, while Materialist [28]
combines intrinsic prediction with differentiable material
and environment-light refinement. Multi-view methods fur-
ther constrain material and illumination recovery through
shared observations [18, 32]. LUMEN [3] instead predicts
local HDR illumination, providing a lighting prior rather
than a complete scene program. Generative relighting also
incorporates physical guidance and local control through
light-transport consistency [36], intrinsic conditioning and
scribbles [6], or estimated illumination priors [34]. These
methods provide complementary forms of lighting estima-
tion and control, with different assumptions about avail-
able geometry and observations. Our focus is to use such
evidence to guide geometry, material, and light revisions
during executable scene reconstruction, with relighting as a
downstream application.
Executable Scene Representations and Agentic Recon-
struction. SceneCraft [11] translates scene descriptions and
spatial constraints into Blender programs, while Blender-
Alchemy [12] searches scene edits through rendered feed-
back. For image-to-code reconstruction, VIGA [35] com-
bines generation and verification with multimodal memory,
and SEIG [10] refines geometry, materials, composition,
and lighting in stages. HARMONY [26] and Lumera [4]
incorporate geometric priors and explicit illumination, with
Lumera retaining shared refinement history.
VETR [21]
additionally uses execution observations to distinguish pro-
posed edits from their realized effects. Notably, Code Video
Model [17] combines executable codes with frontier gener-
ative models, achieving versatile controllable video gener-
ation. Although these systems already incorporate multiple
forms of scene evidence and iterative feedback, our focus
is to maintain source-specific lighting hypotheses and con-
nect memorable evolution to geometric, intrinsic, and de-
composed rendering evidence throughout reconstruction.
3. Method
3.1. Motivation and Overview
Image-to-code reconstruction represents geometry, materi-
als, and lighting in an executable program, providing an
interpretable proxy for rendering, scene manipulation, and
downstream applications. Existing methods primarily ex-
ploit this problem through scene composition, spatial con-
straints, and interactive environments [8, 11, 19]. However,
faithful reconstruction requires understanding how scene
components interact with light to produce the observed ap-
pearance. Inferring lighting from incomplete observations
is ill-posed because different scene configurations can pro-
duce similar images.
A dark wall may indicate low re-
flectance, occlusion, or a surface facing away from illumi-
nation. Misinterpreting these effects can lead to incorrect
materials or geometry, hampering reconstruction fidelity
and subsequent editing. We therefore treat lighting reason-
ing as a crucial part of image-to-code reconstruction.
As shown in Fig. 3, LightHarness formulates a T-round
loop which follows Perceive →Generator →Execute &
Render →Verifier and combines visual evidence E ex-
tracted from image I with Lighting Reasoning Memory
(LRM) Mt.
Perception forms the scene context, which
guides the Generator in producing code Ct and light-source
hypotheses. Following rendering, rubric scoring and VLM
diagnosis assess the reconstruction and guide subsequent
revisions.
Generated scene states and verification find-
ings update LRM, preserving evidence and reasoning across
rounds. The best valid candidate is selected by its rubric
score, yielding jointly refined geometry, materials, and
lighting for a wide range of downstream applications.
3.2. Enriched Evidence for Lighting Reasoning
Lighting reasoning requires both the spatial structure gov-
erning light transport and evidence of its effects on surfaces.
We extract complementary geometric, intrinsic, and light-
ing cues to support these judgments.
Geometric evidence. SAM 3 [2] provides fine-grained se-
mantic object masks, while VGGT [27] estimates relative
depth and cameras to establish a common reconstruction
coordinate system. For planar structures, we back-project
reliable masked depth pixels and fit planes through robust
estimation using least-squares refinement. Each plane pro-
vides a surface point and normal for reasoning about posi-
tion, orientation, and illumination. Non-planar objects re-
tain masks and depth cues without planar fitting.
Intrinsic and lighting evidence. Marigold [13] estimates
albedo and diffuse shading to help distinguish reflectance
from light effects. LUMEN [3] offers an HDR image sug-
gesting light-source directions and chromatic properties.
Evidence is extracted once and remains fixed, while its in-
terpretation and reasoning evolve with rendering feedback.

the evidence underlying previous decisions. Therefore, we

3.4. Evidence-Guided Verification
The Verifier consists a deterministic rubric scorer and a
VLM diagnosis to assess reconstruction quality and guide
scene refinement. The scorer measures agreement with the
reference image and enriched evidence, while the VLM in-
terprets these measurements alongside rendered outputs and
LRM to identify plausible causes of discrepancies.
Rubric-based assessment. The rubric is six-fold as below.
(1) Object-mask error measures projected-region disagree-
ment. (2) Relative-depth error compares reliable overlap-
ping regions after positive scale alignment and penalizes
missing coverage. (3) Orientation error compares depth-
derived plane normals while accounting for sign ambigu-
ity. (4-5) Albedo and diffuse-shading errors are measured
in trusted diffuse regions in linear color space. Shading uses
one positive alignment scale shared across channels to pre-
serve chromatic differences. (6) RGB error evaluates over-
all appearance. We aggregate these errors into scores qt:

Evidence-guided diagnosis.
Beyond scalar assessment,
the VLM combines component errors, rendered passes,
source contributions, and LRM to recommend geometric,
material, or lighting revisions. For an underlit wall, it ex-
amines orientation, occlusion, albedo, and relevant source
contributions. Geometric discrepancies motivate structural
revisions, whereas consistent geometry and albedo direct at-
tention to lighting. Each recommendation specifies the dis-
crepancy, proposed revision, and supporting evidence, pro-
viding guidance for the subsequent reconstruction round.

execution/rendering, and verification. At round t, the per-

tural cues, lighting hypotheses, and unresolved discrepan-

4. Experiments
Our experiments compare reconstruction quality with re-
cently blooming image-to-code methods, examine the roles
of visual evidence and Lighting Reasoning Memory, and as-
sess generalizable downstream application and hard cases.
4.1. Implementation Details
Framework development.
We load external modules
(i.e., SAM3, VGGT, Marigold, LUMEN) on two NVIDIA
4090-48G GPUs, and use GPT-6-Astra [24] as the Genera-
tor/Verifier VLM via API. All reconstructions are done with
10 rounds. We also examine our framework under different
VLMs in the Supplementary materials.
Data.
We evaluate on 32 images drawn from diverse
benchmarks from robotics,
relighting and image en-
hancement, comprising 10 from Hypersim [25], 4 from
LuxRemix [16], 10 from BAID [20], 7 from MIT-Adobe
FiveK [1], and 1 from RoboDojo [5] with complex light-
ing conditions, diverse geometry and materials. The whole
testing set contains 17 real photographs and 15 synthetic
images, covering 22 indoor and 10 outdoor scenes.
Metrics.
We evaluate source-view consistency using
PSNR [30], SSIM [31], LPIPS [37], and DreamSim [7].
Following SceneRig [19], we assess 3D reconstruction
quality from the source view and nearby views at ±15◦. By
orbiting the camera around the world vertical axis through
a pivot located four scene units along the original camera’s
viewing direction, we use GPT-6-Astra as a judge to com-
pute the mean-centered Bradley-Terry scores among these
rendered pairs, with higher scores indicating stronger rela-
tive preference.

reconstruction of scene structure and illumination.

4.4. Generalizable Applications
Building upon the precise executable scenes we reconstruct,
our framework supports diverse downstream applications,
including scene texture editing, harmonious object inser-
tion, versatile relighting, and degraded illumination restora-
tion. We also demonstrate that our rendered edits can com-
bine with the frontier generative model, GPT-Image [23] to
produce more physically realistic results. Note that simply
applying generative models is hard to achieve precise phys-
ical modeling like lighting interaction with complex shad-
ows and textures. Our integration with GPT-Image enables
advanced editing ability.

5. Conclusion, Limitation and Future Work
We propose LightHarness, a VLM-based agentic frame-

nation evidence guides reconstruction, while rubric-based
assessment and VLM diagnosis support iterative refine-
ment. Lighting reasoning memory preserves source iden-
tities, evidence, semantic relationships, and revision his-

Limitation.
