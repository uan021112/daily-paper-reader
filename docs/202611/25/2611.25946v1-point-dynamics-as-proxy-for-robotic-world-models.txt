Overview
Robotic world models predict future video frames from the current observation and a sequence
of robot actions. While recent models follow the given actions closely, manipulated objects often
collapse or move inconsistently with the action. We attribute this to the conditioning: an action,
or a rendering of the robot, specifies where the robot goes but not how the scene responds, leaving
a single video model to infer the interaction dynamics implicitly while also rendering appearance.
We therefore separate dynamics prediction from visual rendering, with 3D point dynamics as the
proxy between them. Specifically, we lift the first frame into 3D points, simulate how they respond
to the robot’s motion with a learned point dynamics model, and render the simulated points back
into pixel-aligned videos that any video model can read. In controlled experiments on the DROID
dataset with two video renderers of different architectures, this point dynamics proxy reduces object
motion error by 8.6% and 6.2% without degrading image quality, and by 8.1% on a real bimanual
robot. Moreover, the video follows the simulation: more accurate dynamics yield more accurate
object motion, and editing the points steers the video. This suggests that advances in dynamics
modeling can translate into more faithful robotic world models.
1
Introduction
Given the current observation and a sequence of robot actions, a robotic world model predicts the future
video frames. Such models serve as learned surrogates of the real world in robot manipulation, where they
are used to evaluate policies, to plan actions and to synthesize training data [22, 41, 84, 96]. These uses
impose two requirements on the predicted video. The first is action following: the robot must execute the
given actions. The second is interaction fidelity: the objects in contact with the robot must respond as
they would in reality, whether they are carried, pushed, dragged, deformed or released, while preserving
their shape and identity.
Early robotic world models encode the action as a numeric vector, such as end-effector poses or joint
angles [22, 84, 96], and leave the video model to infer from pixels both how the robot appears and how
the scene responds. Recent work instead renders the robot into pixel-aligned conditions, such as masks,
URDF renders or surface tracks [1, 2, 6, 35, 39]. This specifies the robot’s appearance in every frame and
makes action following far more reliable, but interaction fidelity improves much less. As shown in Fig. 1b,
objects may collapse during contact, as when a slice of bread and the spatula dragging it come apart, or
respond inconsistently with the action, as when a released cup rises with the open gripper. We attribute
these failures to the conditioning (Fig. 1a): an action, numeric or rendered, specifies where the robot goes
but not how the scene responds, so a single video model must infer the interaction dynamics implicitly

We therefore separate dynamics prediction from visual rendering. This separation is inspired by code
world models [12, 14], which evolve an explicit world state with a program, render it into a coarse proxy
video, and let a video model synthesize the final frames; physics-grounded video generation does the
same with simulated physics [46, 69]. For robot manipulation, the central design choice is the proxy: it
must represent arbitrary objects, including deformable and articulated ones, be obtainable from a single
real observation, and admit dynamics that can be predicted under robot actions. Physics simulators
require physical parameters, object assets and object-specific solvers that are difficult to obtain in real
scenes [47, 81], and hand-written rules cannot express pushing, dragging or deformation. We instead use
3D points as the proxy. Points represent any object without segmentation or a model, can be lifted from
a single RGB-D frame, describe the robot and the scene in the same space, and their dynamics can be
learned directly from real robot interaction data.
With points as the proxy, the world model first simulates how the points move and then renders them
into video (Fig. 2). We lift the first frame into 3D scene points and convert the actions into robot points
via forward kinematics. A learned point dynamics model, the simulator, predicts how every scene point
responds to the robot’s motion; we instantiate it with PointWorld [26]. The simulated points are rendered
back, together with the robot, into pixel-aligned proxy videos, which a video model, the renderer, reads
through its own encoder and turns into realistic frames. The simulator thus decides what happens and
the renderer decides how it looks, and each can be replaced independently. We study this design in a
controlled setting on the DROID dataset [34] with two renderers of different architectures, Wan2.2 [67]
(DiT) and Ctrl-World [22] (UNet), comparing four conditions, each giving the video model more explicit
information than the last: a numeric action, a robot render, a robot render with depth, and the point
dynamics proxy. We measure interaction fidelity by object motion error, computed from object points
tracked in the generated and the real video rather than from image metrics dominated by the background,
and repeat the study on a real bimanual robot.
Our experiments show that:
• Interaction fidelity improves. Against the robot render, the proxy reduces object motion error by
8.6% and 6.2% on DROID, without degrading image quality, and by 8.1% on the real robot. The
gain concentrates on pushed, dragged and deformed objects (Sec. 4.2), and on the real robot on
deformable ones (Sec. 4.5).
• The video follows the simulation. Object motion error decreases as the simulator improves, from
points held static through a hand-written rule to learned simulators, and editing the simulated
motion steers the video (Sec. 4.3).
2


• Point dynamics help more as input than as a matched auxiliary loss. With the same data and
training budget, point forcing, an auxiliary point-tracking loss following PhysisForcing [90], lowers
object motion error by 3.4%, and the simulated point dynamics as input by 8.6% (Sec. 4.4).

Related Work
Robotic world models for manipulation.
Robotic world models are increasingly built on large video
generation models [17, 22, 27, 29, 44, 51–53, 58, 76, 84, 96]. What such a model has to infer depends
on how the action is given. A numeric action leaves it to infer both where the robot goes and how the
scene responds. Rendering the robot into the frame as masks, meshes, skeletons, point maps, tracks or
flow [1, 2, 6, 16, 35, 36, 39, 65, 75, 83] settles the first question and makes action following far more reliable,
but not the second. A few models also receive a signal about the scene. Some take it as given, such as
simulated depth [24] or occupancy from reconstruction or simulation [85]; others predict it together with the
frames, as next-frame scene flow learned mostly in simulation [21] or as a low-resolution preview video [62].
Most work, however, keeps the scene’s response inside the video model. The model is post-trained on
counterfactual actions [40], off-expert actions [10] or its own rollouts with reinforcement learning [3].
Others make it generate depth, point maps or flow along with the frames [48, 68, 93, 94], or add auxiliary
losses on motion and geometry, first for general video generation [7, 31, 79, 92] and recently for robotic
world models [9, 30, 43, 74, 90, 95]. We compare one such loss with an explicit input in Sec. 4.4.
Simulate, then render.
Several lines of video generation keep how a world evolves apart from how it looks.
Code world models [12, 14] and game generators [28, 45] let a program evolve the state and a video model
paint it. Physics-grounded generation does the same with a physics simulator [8, 15, 42, 46, 47, 82, 91],
a learned physics model [69] or an evolving 4D scene state [70], whose renders, flow or tracks reach the
video model as the signals already used to control motion and camera [18–20, 61, 87]. Both stages can also
be learned: a language-model backbone evolves a latent world state under actions, and a video diffusion
decoder renders it [54]. Three concurrent works are closest to ours, as they predict dynamics in 3D before
rendering. DeXplicit models hand–object interaction and needs object labels and canonical shapes [37]. 3D
World Simulator fits particle–voxel dynamics and a Gaussian renderer to each multi-camera workspace [71],
and VPTwin simulates a digital twin that a VLM builds for each task [81]. Our intermediate is instead an
explicit 3D point state, predicted from the actions by a separately trained point model learned from real
robot data, without object models, physical parameters or per-scene capture.
Learned 3D dynamics.
Learned dynamics models predict how particles, graphs or Gaussians move
and deform under robot action, but are mostly fitted to one object or task [25, 32, 49, 88, 89], and
object-centric variants assume segmented rigid or articulated parts [37, 77]. Scene-level point models drop
these assumptions and scale to in-the-wild robot data [4, 13, 26, 38, 55, 56, 73, 86], and generative particle
models are trained on large simulated corpora [11], including concurrent work [60]. These models predict
the evolving 3D state rather than video. Because the proxy only needs per-point displacements, they could
serve as our simulator; we use PointWorld [26], which is public and trained on real robot data, and vary
its output at test time in Sec. 4.3.

Forward kinematics turns the actions into robot surface points Q0:T , the simulator S predicts how the

a rendering of the robot points Q0:T alone, and a depth condition [24] adds the scene’s geometry, here a
depth rendering of Q0:T over the dense scene points held in place, as if S were the identity (Sec. 4.1).
3.2
3D state and simulator
Each camera’s depth map is back-projected into the robot base frame, without the pixels covered by the
robot at t=0. Since the cost of simulation grows with the number of points and the quality of rendering
with their density, the simulator receives sparse voxelized points P0 = {pj} merged over cameras, while
the proxy uses dense points ¯P v = {¯pi}, one per pixel of camera v with its color. Robot points are sampled
once on the URDF surface, proportionally to area, and placed at every frame by forward kinematics. The
simulator predicts a displacement ∆pj,t for every scene point and step, and any model with this interface
can serve as S (Sec. 4.3). We instantiate S with PointWorld-large [26], a scene-level point dynamics model
trained on DROID that reads the gripper points; on the real robot, we fine-tune it on our own recordings
(Sec. 4.5). PointWorld describes each point, voxelized at 1.5 cm, by DINOv3 features [63] and predicts
its displacement for 10 steps, one every two video frames, which we interpolate linearly in time. On real
data such as DROID, it is trained on label point flow, pseudo-labels made of point tracks estimated in the
video [33] and lifted to 3D with depth.
3.3
Point dynamics proxy
Rendering the points into pixel-aligned videos lets any video model read them through its own VAE. The
proxy sees the simulator only through the simulated points. It contains the robot render and adds the
scene’s geometry and its simulated response. For each camera, Π renders three videos by z-buffered point
splatting [78, 97], where each pixel shows the nearest projected point: the robot render crob, a warp cwarp

The moved dense points and the robot points share one z-buffer, so the arm, the objects it moves and the

Tracks.
The track video draws a fixed subset of 4,000 simulated scene points and 1,500 robot points
without spatial interpolation, each colored by its initial 3D position in the robot base frame [20]. A dot
keeps its color as it moves, so the renderer can follow each point across frames and views, and objects
separate from their surroundings by moving differently, without segmentation. The warp carries appearance
but inherits interpolation errors at object boundaries, whereas the tracks show the simulated points directly
but sparsely; Sec. 4.2 compares the two paths.
3.4
Renderer
The renderer is a pretrained video model, Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [67] or Ctrl-World [22] initialized from SVD [5],
that receives the first frame of each camera and the three proxy videos, but no text; Ctrl-World, which
natively reads numeric actions, keeps this input in every variant. Each proxy video is encoded by the
renderer’s frozen VAE, concatenated with the noisy latents along channels and read by a widened first layer
whose new weights start at zero, so training starts from the pretrained model. Each token thus carries the
proxy at its own space-time location, and the backbone’s attention relates tokens across space, time and
views. The clean first frame supplies appearance, the warp shows where it goes and the tracks how the
points move; the renderer fills the black regions and synthesizes the real arm, lighting and contact. With
E the VAE encoder and zτ the noisy latents of the future frames at noise level τ, the denoiser computes

4
Experiments
On DROID and under one controlled protocol, we ask whether point dynamics improve interaction fidelity
(Sec. 4.2), whether the generated video follows the simulator (Sec. 4.3), and whether point dynamics help
more as input or as an auxiliary training loss (Sec. 4.4). We then repeat the main comparison on a real
robot (Sec. 4.5).
4.1
Setup
Data.
All models are trained on 326K clips from 41.5K DROID [34] episodes and tested on 1,515 held-out
clips from 900 episodes across 13 labs; 300 validation clips are used only to monitor training. The test clips
come from PointWorld’s test split, so the simulator is not trained on them either. A clip is 21 frames at
15 Hz from the two external cameras, stacked along height at 640×352 per view, with depth and calibration
from PointWorld [26]. No wrist camera and no text are used.
5


Renderers and conditioning variants.
We use two renderers and train each with four conditions, each
giving it more explicit information than the last: the robot’s appearance, then the scene’s geometry, then
the scene’s simulated motion. Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [67] (Wan for short) is a DiT conditioned on the first
frame; we train it with a numeric action (8-D end-effector pose and gripper), a robot render, a robot render
plus depth (depth for short), and the point dynamics proxy. The depth condition is a second pixel-aligned
video that composites the rendered depth of the robot over the depth of the first-frame scene, so it adds
the scene’s geometry but not its motion (Appendix B). Ctrl-World [22] is an SVD [5] UNet that reads
numeric actions through frame-level cross-attention; we retrain it from its SVD initialization in our setting
with the numeric action alone and with the same three pixel-aligned conditions added. The effect of point
dynamics is always measured between the robot render and the proxy of the same renderer, which differ
only in the proxy’s warp and track videos.
Training and inference.
Every renderer variant uses the same data, the same global sample order,
global batch 32 on 8 A100 GPUs, and 30K steps of full fine-tuning from the renderer’s pretrained weights
(AdamW, learning rate 10−5, 200 warm-up steps, bfloat16 autocast, no EMA). Each renderer keeps its own
noise schedule, first-frame conditioning and VAE. Sampling uses 50 steps without guidance. We report
the 30K checkpoint: on the 300 validation clips, every variant with a robot render has nearly converged
by 20K steps, and the proxy leads the robot render at every 5K checkpoint (Appendix E). On one A100
(batch 1, two views), PointWorld and proxy rendering take 0.93 s per clip, next to 17.2 s for generating the
video with Wan and 30.9 s with Ctrl-World; the depth maps are given and not timed. We do not compare
against released checkpoints, because most DROID world models require the wrist view and published
numbers use different protocols.
Metrics.
We measure action following by robot error and interaction fidelity by object motion error, our
main metric. Both track first-frame query points with CoTracker3 [33] in the real and in the generated
video and report the mean pixel distance between the two tracks in the frames where the real track is
visible: robot error for up to 150 points inside the robot mask, object motion error for up to 200 points of
a 10-pixel grid outside the dilated robot mask that move by at least 5 px in the real video. The latter is
defined on the test clips with at least 10 such points (1,017 and 1,029 of the 1,515 clips in the two views);
“large” denotes the third with the largest object motion (details in Appendix C). We also report PSNR,
LPIPS and FVD, but they are dominated by the background and the robot, and visual realism is known to
be a poor indicator of physical correctness [50]. Every metric is computed per view and averaged over the
two views, and changes are relative differences of the mean error between two variants, computed before
rounding. All tables report one training seed per variant; the robot render and the proxy are trained with
two more seeds (Sec. 4.2).
4.2
Do point dynamics improve interaction fidelity?
Main comparison.
Simulating point dynamics makes object interaction more faithful on both renderers
(Tab. 1). Going from the numeric action to the robot render mostly fixes the robot: robot error drops by
62% on Wan and 58% on Ctrl-World, while object motion error drops by only 13.0% and 9.0%. The point
dynamics proxy lowers object motion error by another 8.6% and 6.2% against the robot render (11.2%
and 8.3% on large motions), more than half of the robot render’s own gain in pixels (0.44 vs. 0.76 and
0.33 vs. 0.52 px). Since CoTracker3 also produces the label point flow, we re-measure with AllTracker [23],
which uses no pseudo-labels: the proxy then lowers object motion error by 11.5% and 7.1%, and SAM 2
object masks [59] agree (Appendix D). On the whole test set, image metrics barely move (PSNR +0.06
and +0.08 dB), and the track error of points that are static in the real video does not rise (0.46 px with
and without the proxy on Wan). In Fig. 3, the models without point dynamics move the arm correctly
but get the object wrong; the proxy gets both right.

Where the gain comes from.
Geometry alone does not explain the gain, which lies mostly in non-rigid
responses. The proxy does use the scene’s depth, but the depth condition lowers object motion error by
only 3.0% on Wan and 1.7% on Ctrl-World against the robot render, and the proxy remains 5.7% and
4.6% below depth. We further sort the test clips by their label point flow. A clip has no labeled motion if
fewer than 20 label points move by more than 2 cm within 15 cm of the gripper’s path, although other
points may still move in the video. A clip with labeled motion has a non-rigid response if fewer than
80% of these moving label points follow the gripper rigidly, as in pushing, dragging, opening, folding and
releasing. The gain concentrates on the last group (Tab. 2), where object motion error drops by 12.3% on
Wan and 8.2% on Ctrl-World, as much as the robot render gains over the numeric action on the same
clips (11.9% and 8.3%), while clips without labeled motion change little. This is the division one would
expect: the robot render shows where the gripper goes, and with it any object it holds firmly, but not
whether an object is held (Fig. 3, top), nor how a cloth falls or a slice of bread slides.

Proxy paths.
The proxy draws the same point dynamics twice, and either path alone carries most of the
gain (Tab. 3): the warp alone reduces object motion error by 7.0% and the tracks alone by 6.5%, against
8.6% for both, so the gain comes from the simulated motion rather than from one way of drawing it. The
two are complementary (Sec. 3.3): the warp moves the first frame’s appearance densely but blurs motion
at object boundaries, the tracks show the simulated points exactly but sparsely, and together they are
best in every column, by up to about 3 percentage points.
Robustness.
The gain holds across training seeds and labs. Pooled over three seeds it is 7.7% on Wan
and 6.7% on Ctrl-World, with every seed in the same direction, and on each renderer the error decreases
in 12 of the 13 DROID labs (Appendix E).

PointWorld-large nearly matches label point flow (4.69 vs. 4.66 px on Wan, 4.97 vs. 4.86 px on Ctrl-World).
Label point flow is itself estimated from 2D tracks and stereo depth [26], so it is a reference, not an upper
bound.
Perturbations and edits.
The renderer also follows changes to the simulator’s output, whether errors or
deliberate edits. On Wan, coherent errors cost far less than incoherent ones (Fig. 4b). Scaling all simulated
motion by 0.5× or 1.5×, or delaying it by two frames, costs about 5–10%, whereas independent 2 cm noise
on each point costs 33%, because the renderer paints incoherent motion as given.
Edits steer the video (Fig. 5): we edit the predicted motion of one object near the gripper in 200 test clips
with non-rigid responses, leaving everything else unchanged, and measure the fraction of tracked object
points that end closer to the edited than to the original trajectory, on the 55–76 clips per edit and view in
which the edit moves at least five tracked points. The 200 clips are the non-rigid ones with the largest
label motion, and the edited object is the largest connected group of scene points, at 1.5 cm voxels, that
PointWorld-large moves by more than 2 cm and that start within 15 cm of the gripper’s path. When the
object is frozen, 61% of its points follow the edit, against 29% for the robot-render model, which does not
see the edit, and the proxy model’s error with respect to the edited trajectory drops from 25.2 px without
the edit to 12.8 px. Reversing the horizontal motion or doubling it is followed less often (28% vs. 13% and
19% vs. 13%), as the renderer pulls large, unusual motions back toward what it saw in training. The edits
leave the background unchanged and barely affect the robot.
4.4
Point dynamics as input or as an auxiliary loss?
Point dynamics can also reach a video model through an auxiliary training loss on motion [7, 31, 74, 90],
which needs no extra input at inference. We train another Wan variant on the robot render with point
forcing: an auxiliary head on block 15 regresses point tracks from intermediate features during training
and is discarded at inference, following PhysisForcing [90] in its loss weight, head learning rate and
moving-point targets. Its targets are the label point flow, the strongest teacher available. Data, sample
order and budget match the other variants.
Both help; here, input helps more.
Against the robot render, point forcing lowers object motion error
by 3.4% and raises PSNR by 0.06 dB (Tab. 4); with loss weight 1 and the backbone’s learning rate for the
head, it does not help (Appendix G). Giving the simulated dynamics as input lowers object motion error
by 8.6%, to 4.69 px against 4.95 px with point forcing. A likely reason is what the renderer is asked to do:
with forcing it must still infer the scene’s response from the robot render, whereas the proxy hands it a
response computed in 3D.

4.5
Real robot
Setup.
We collect 300 episodes of 15 tasks on a bimanual setup of two WidowX AI arms, recorded at
30 Hz by a single static RGB-D camera (RealSense D405, 640×480) that looks down on the table. The
tasks cover pick-and-place, stacking, pushing, folding a shirt or a bag, unfolding a bag, and handing a
bag or a pillow from one arm to the other. We calibrate the camera by fitting the rendered URDF to the
robot’s silhouettes and depth, and refine the noisy sensor depth with LingBot-Depth [66]. Label point
flow follows PointWorld [26], with CoTracker3 [33] tracks lifted to 3D with this depth (Appendix H). The
renderer is trained on 270 episodes and tested on 431 clips of 30 held-out episodes (two per task); the
simulator never sees the test clips. We fine-tune PointWorld-large on the label point flow for 6K steps,
which lowers its error on moving test points from 4.05 cm zero-shot to 2.24 cm. Each Wan variant starts
from its DROID model and is fine-tuned for 6K steps with the same recipe on the single view (640×352,
21 frames at 15 Hz). Object motion error is defined on the 265 test clips with moving objects, 89 of them
large.
Results.
The ordering found on DROID carries over to the real robot (Tab. 5, Fig. 6). Against the
numeric action, the robot render lowers robot error by 65% and object motion error by 25%. Against the
robot render, the proxy lowers object motion error by another 8.1%, and by 13.4% on large motions, close
to the 8.6% and 11.2% on DROID. Against robot render + depth, it lowers object motion error by 4.3%,
and by 8.0% on large motions. The gain comes mostly from deformable objects. On the 112 clips of the
five bag, cloth and pillow tasks, the proxy lowers object motion error by 12.7% against the robot render
10


and by 7.6% against depth, close to the 12.3% and 8.5% for non-rigid responses on DROID (Tab. 2); on
the 153 clips of the other ten tasks it changes object motion error by at most 2.1%. PSNR and LPIPS
barely move, while FVD rises from 14.5 to 15.2.
5
Conclusion
Robotic world models that render the robot follow the given action, but still have to guess how the objects
respond. We decouple such a model into a simulator that predicts how 3D scene points respond to the
robot and a renderer that turns the prediction into video, connected by a pixel-aligned point dynamics
proxy. On DROID and on a real robot, explicit point dynamics make object interaction more faithful,
and the generated video follows the simulator and responds to its edits. This suggests that progress in
3D point dynamics, whether learned or written as code, can carry over to robotic world models without
changing how they render.
Limitations.
The video is only as faithful as the simulation, since the renderer follows plausible simulated
motion, errors included. The simulator, in turn, learns from label point flow, which is itself estimated
from tracking and depth. Errors thus add up from depth and tracking to the labels, the simulator and
the video, and each step leaves room for improvement. The simulator and the renderer are also trained
separately, which keeps each replaceable but prevents the renderer from correcting the simulation; training
them jointly is a promising direction.

Appendix
This appendix gives the details needed to reproduce the experiments and the results that did not fit in the
main text. Sections A–C describe the DROID setup, with every setting in Tab. 6. Sec. D measures object
motion error again with a second point tracker and with object masks. Sec. E breaks the results down by
view, training seed, checkpoint and lab. Sections F–H detail the edit experiment, point forcing and the
real robot.
A
Simulator and proxy
The simulator is PointWorld-large [26], a Point Transformer V3 [80] with about 1B parameters, used
frozen from its released DROID checkpoint. PointWorld-small, used in Sec. 4.3, is the smaller released
DROID checkpoint. The simulator reads the scene points, voxelized at 1.5 cm and described by frozen
DINOv3 features [63], together with the gripper points. In a single forward pass, it predicts displacements
for 10 steps, one every two video frames. It was trained on DROID label point flow, but not on our test
clips, which come from the test split of its release. The renderers never see a test episode either. Stereo
depth and camera calibration also come from PointWorld’s DROID release, for training and test alike.
For the proxy, the depth map is upsampled to the video resolution of 640×352, and every pixel with a
depth between 0.1 and 3.5 m becomes a dense scene point. Robot pixels at t=0 are removed with the
rendered robot mask, dilated by 7×7 pixels. The simulator receives only the gripper points, while the
proxy renders the full arm from 1M points sampled on its URDF surface. Simulated displacements are
interpolated linearly in time to the 15 Hz frame rate. Track points are colored by their initial position,
through a fixed box of the robot base frame mapped to RGB.
B
Renderers
Wan2.2-TI2V-5B [67] reads the proxy through a widened patch embedding. Its first latent frame is
the clean first frame at timestep zero. The latents of the two views are stacked along height into one
token sequence, so tokens attend across views. Ctrl-World [22] reads the proxy through a widened input
convolution and otherwise keeps its released design, including the first-frame latent concatenation and the
cross-attention to numeric actions.
Numeric action.
On DROID, the numeric action has 8 dimensions: the end-effector position, the sine
and cosine of its roll, its pitch, its yaw and the gripper. Each dimension is normalized by the 1st and 99th
percentiles of the training set. Ctrl-World reads it in every variant, through its action cross-attention.
Wan reads it only in the numeric-action variant. There, the actions of the four video frames in each
latent frame are concatenated, and a two-layer MLP maps them to an offset of that latent frame’s time
embedding. The last layer of this MLP starts at zero. In every Wan variant, the text cross-attention
receives the embedding of an empty prompt.
Depth variant.
The robot render + depth variant (depth for short) adds one video per camera to the
robot render, encoded like the proxy videos. Its first two channels hold the z-buffer depth of the rendered
robot in each frame and the depth of the dense first-frame scene points. The third holds their per-pixel
minimum, i.e. the robot composited over the scene. Depth is mapped linearly from [0.3, 2.0] m to [1, 0],
so near surfaces are bright and pixels without geometry are black. The video is rendered from the same
depth maps, calibration and points as the proxy, but its scene points stay in place.

C
Metrics
Object motion error asks whether scene points that move in the real video move the same way in the
generated one. Its query points lie on a 10-pixel grid in the first real frame, outside the robot mask dilated
by 15×15 pixels, and CoTracker3 [33] tracks them through the 21 real frames. A point counts as moving
if it moves by at least 5 px. It must also be visible in at least half of the frames and lie on the dilated
robot mask in fewer than 30% of them. Clips with at least 10 moving points enter the metric (1,017 and
1,029 of the 1,515 clips per view). In each of them, up to 200 moving points are sampled with a fixed
seed and tracked in the generated video as well. The error is their mean distance to the real track, over
the frames 1–20 in which the real track is visible. “Large” denotes the third of the clips with the largest
mean peak displacement of their moving points, about 20 px and above. Robot error uses up to 150 points
sampled in the first-frame robot mask eroded by 5×5 pixels. The static-point check of Sec. 4.2 uses up to
150 grid points that move by less than 1 px and never touch the dilated robot mask. FVD uses the I3D
features of StyleGAN-V [64] on the 21 frames at 224×224.
The label-motion groups of Tab. 2 use the label points that start within 15 cm of the gripper’s path and
end more than 2 cm from where they start. Points whose displacement lies along either camera ray are left
out. A clip has no labeled motion if fewer than 20 such points remain. This holds for 335 and 338 of the
1,017 and 1,029 clips with object motion error (ext1, ext2). The gripper’s rigid motion is fitted to the
points of its base with the Kabsch algorithm. A moving label point follows the gripper rigidly if, from the
step before it first moves by 1 cm, its label trajectory stays within 2 cm of this motion. A clip is rigid if at
least 80% of its moving label points follow the gripper rigidly (29 and 33 clips) and non-rigid otherwise
19


(653 and 658 clips).

F
Editing the simulated motion
The edit experiment of Sec. 4.3 uses the 200 non-rigid test clips whose moving label points have the largest
95th percentile of final displacement. In each clip, we take the PointWorld-large points that move by more
than 2 cm and start within 15 cm of the gripper’s path. The edited object is their largest 26-connected
22


group at 1.5 cm voxels. A group needs at least 20 points; otherwise the next clip in the ranking is taken.
The edits change the object’s predicted displacements in the robot base frame. Freezing keeps the object
points at their initial positions, reversing negates their horizontal displacement and keeps the vertical one,
and doubling multiplies their displacement by two. The edited points are rendered into the proxy exactly
like the prediction, and the robot render and the rest of the scene are left unchanged.
For scoring, we take the query points of object motion error that lie within 6 pixels of a visible object point
in the first frame. CoTracker3 tracks them in each video. Each query point has two targets: its position
moved by the original and by the edited simulated motion, interpolated as in the warp and projected into
the view. A point counts if it is visible in at least 5 of frames 1–20 and its two targets are at least 2 px
apart on average over these frames. It follows the edit if its track is closer on average to the edited target
than to the original one. A clip and view enters the follow rate if at least 5 of its points count, which
leaves 55–76 clips per edit, view and video. The follow rate is the fraction of counted points that follow
the edit, averaged over these clips. The follow error is the mean distance of their tracks to the edited
target. Against the unedited proxy video, the edits change background error by less than 1% and PSNR
by at most 0.23 dB. They change robot error by at most 0.25 px (reverse, ext1, from 2.12 px) and by at
most 0.15 px otherwise.
G
Point forcing
The forcing head reads block 15 of Wan and regresses the label point flow of moving points. The variant
in Tab. 4 follows PhysisForcing [90] in its loss weight (0.1), the learning rate of the head (10−4, with the
same warm-up as the backbone) and its use of moving points only. Unlike PhysisForcing, it learns from
label point flow rather than CoTracker3 tracks and uses a Huber rather than an RMSE loss. Its batch
size and training length follow our protocol (32 and 30K steps, against 128 and 20K). Our first forcing
variant used loss weight 1, the backbone’s learning rate 10−5 for the head and static points weighted by
0.25. It reached 5.21 / 7.95 px object motion error (all / large) and 26.53 dB PSNR, on par with no point
dynamics (+1.6%). The recipe of Tab. 4 lowers object motion error by 4.9% against this first variant.
Sec. E reports point forcing for three training seeds.
H
Real-robot details
Data.
Five of the 15 tasks put objects in place: all food on a plate, a cake on a yellow plate, an eggplant
into a white box, a lemon into a blue bowl and a red cuboid on a green plate. Two push a green bowl to a
purple bowl and a green cube to a blue cube. Three stack a purple bowl in a blue bowl, a red cube on a
green cube and three bowls. The other five fold a black bag and a red shirt in half, unfold a black bag,
and hand a black bag or a U-shaped pillow from one arm to the other. Each task has 20 episodes, 176K
frames in total. The robot state is the 14-D joint state of the two arms (six joints and a gripper each).
Calibration.
No camera calibration was recorded, so we estimate it. RGB and depth share the optical
center, since a free translation between them converges to 4 mm. The principal point of the depth image,
however, is about 11 pixels lower, so we reproject depth onto the RGB grid. In 360 frames, we render the
arm from the URDF at the logged joint state. The focal length, principal point and camera pose are fitted
jointly to the arm’s silhouettes and to the depth on its pixels. The fitted focal length is about 380 pixels
and varies by 0.7% across three disjoint subsets of frames. On held-out frames, the depth error on robot
pixels is 17–18 mm and the silhouette IoU is 0.76–0.77.
Depth.
At about 1 m, the raw sensor depth on the dark tablecloth fluctuates by centimeters. We compared
raw and filtered sensor depth with MoGe-2 [72] aligned to the sensor and with LingBot-Depth [66], which
takes RGB and raw depth, on 232 held-out windows of training episodes. Three measures score them: the
23


median depth error on robot pixels against the rendered robot, the frame-to-frame jitter of static points,
and the change of pairwise distances between points on carried rigid objects. LingBot-Depth gives 17.9,
12.1 and 4.0 mm against 17.8, 27.7 and 8.1 mm for raw depth, while MoGe-2 places the arm 85 mm off.
We therefore use LingBot-Depth for the labels, the simulator’s input points and the depth condition.
Label point flow.
We follow PointWorld’s labeling [26]. CoTracker3 [33] is queried on a dense 3-pixel
grid in the first frame, excluding robot pixels. Each track is lifted to 3D with the median depth of its 5×5
neighborhood on the same surface. In every frame, points hidden by the arm, found with the rendered
robot mask, are marked invisible and not supervised in that frame. Outliers are removed with PointWorld’s
DBSCAN filter. Two changes account for the single overhead camera. First, the depth jitter of static points
(12 mm) exceeds the 5 mm per-step scale of PointWorld’s motion weighting. Static points are therefore
pinned to their first-frame position and supervised only in frames where they are visible. Second, a held
object is often hidden by the gripper. Points whose observed motion follows the gripper are attached to it.
The other points of the same object move with them, by the object’s rigid motion fitted per frame with
the Kabsch algorithm. In frames where these points are occluded, they are filled in with that rigid motion.
Points with contradictory evidence, such as a depth change without 2D motion, are left unsupervised after
the first frame.
Simulator.
We start from the released PointWorld-large checkpoint used on DROID and feed it the
points of the single camera. The state normalization is that of the DROID release, with the gripper
statistics applied to both grippers. It is fine-tuned on 8 GPUs with global batch 32 and learning rate 10−5
for 6K steps, and all other settings follow the release. Fine-tuning lowers the error on moving points from
4.05 to 2.24 cm (Tab. 11).

Renderer.
Each Wan variant is initialized from its 30K DROID model and trained on one view of
640×352, the lower 352 rows of the 640×480 frame. Training follows the DROID recipe of Tab. 6 on 8
GPUs for 6K steps, and sampling uses 50 steps without guidance. The numeric action is the position, roll,
pitch and yaw of each end effector, obtained by forward kinematics from the measured joints, and the
gripper of each arm. It is normalized by the 1st and 99th percentiles of the training set, and its encoder
is trained from scratch because the dimension differs from DROID. The depth condition is built as on
DROID, from the first-frame LingBot-Depth. The proxy comes from the fine-tuned PointWorld-large. As
on DROID, the training proxies are its predictions on clips it was trained on.
