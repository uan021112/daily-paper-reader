Abstract
World models facilitate planning and control in robotic
manipulation by enabling agents to anticipate the conse-
quences of their actions. However, they typically rely on
action-labelled interaction data that is costly to acquire and
difficult to scale. These models are also often tied to the spe-
cific embodiment they were trained on, limiting transferabil-
ity to previously unseen robot morphologies. We introduce
a 3D latent-action world model that learns continuous ac-
tion representations directly from point-cloud observations
of retargeted human manipulation demonstrations. A con-
trastive objective is used to encourage consistent represen-
tations of motion across embodiments. The resulting unified
latent action space serves directly as the search space for
model-predictive control, where sampled latent actions con-
dition particle-based future predictions. Executable robot
commands are then recovered from the predicted geomet-
ric motion without requiring an embodiment-specific action
decoder. Through extensive evaluations, we demonstrate
that the learned latent actions exhibit controllable and com-
positional motion, transfer to unseen robot embodiments,
and support zero-shot manipulation with unseen gripper ge-
ometries. Following task-agnostic adaptation, our model
achieves 90%, 92%, and 60% success on reaching, grasp-
ing, and placing, respectively, compared with DexWM’s
reported 72%, 58%, and 28%.
Finally, we demonstrate
zero-shot real-world manipulation with an unseen dexter-
ous hand, achieving an 86% success rate across 50 tri-
als on five diverse tasks involving pushing, grasping, and
lifting everyday objects. These results highlight the poten-
tial of geometry-grounded latent actions as a unified, trans-

1. Introduction
Developing world models that generalise across diverse
robot embodiments remains a central challenge in robotic
manipulation.
Embodiments differ substantially in their
morphologies, kinematics, and action spaces, making it dif-
ficult to learn predictive dynamics that transfer across plat-
forms. This motivates learning a unified action represen-
tation that enables a world model to capture reusable in-
teraction dynamics across different embodiments, including
those unseen during training.
A promising direction is to learn action representations
directly from observed manipulation, without relying on
embodiment-specific action labels [31]. Human manipu-
lation videos provide a rich source of such observations,
but representations learned from human motion alone may
not generalise across robot morphologies. Retargeting these
demonstrations to different robot embodiments produces
corresponding manipulation trajectories that preserve the
underlying interaction despite differences in morphology
[33].
These correspondences provide a natural basis for
learning a shared latent action space that captures motion
rather than embodiment-specific variation.
Manipulation involves physical interactions that evolve
over space and time. Explicit 3D geometry provides a nat-
ural way to represent this evolution, capturing the spatial
relationships and contact geometry between interacting en-
tities [1, 14]. Representing scene evolution through parti-
cle displacements provides direct supervision of physical
motion, allowing predictive dynamics to focus on how the
scene evolves rather than its visual appearance. We there-
fore seek to ground both latent action inference and predic-
tive dynamics in a common geometric representation, en-
abling actions to be learned and their consequences to be
predicted in the same physical space.
In this work, we introduce DexLatentWorld, a unified 3D
latent-action world model for prediction and control, learn-
ing continuous action representations directly from action-
unlabelled manipulation observations (Fig.
1).
We rep-
resent these observations as temporally corresponding 3D
scene particles derived from tracked hand–object trajecto-
ries, with human manipulation trajectories retargeted to dif-
ferent robot embodiments. A cross-embodiment contrastive
objective aligns corresponding human and robot transitions
within a shared latent action space, while a forward dynam-
ics model uses these representations to predict scene evo-
lution in 3D particle space. At deployment, we perform
closed-loop model-predictive control (MPC) directly in the
learned latent action space, evaluating candidate latent-
action sequences through their predicted geometric conse-
quences. We then recover executable robot commands from
predicted motion using known robot geometry and kinemat-
ics, without additional action-labelled demonstrations for
training an embodiment-specific latent-to-action decoder.
The learned latent actions exhibit controllable and com-
positional motion, enabling consistent geometric predic-
tions across unseen robot embodiments. To evaluate the
effectiveness of these capabilities in robotic manipulation,
we employ MPC in both simulation and real-world de-
ployment.
We further demonstrate zero-shot manipula-
tion with unseen gripper geometries without embodiment-
specific adaptation. Extending to dexterous manipulation,
our framework achieves 90%, 92%, and 60% success on
reaching, grasping, and placing following task-agnostic do-
main adaptation, compared with DexWM [9]’s 72%, 58%,
and 28%. In real-world deployment, the original pretrained
model achieves 86% success on five manipulation tasks
with an unseen dexterous hand, without any fine-tuning.
Our contributions include:
• A unified 3D latent-action world model. We introduce
a self-supervised framework that combines particle-based
scene dynamics with a shared continuous latent action
representation, enabling cross-embodiment world mod-
elling and direct latent-space planning and control.
• A geometry-based prediction-to-control framework.
We plan directly in the learned latent action space and
recover executable robot targets from predicted 3D mo-
tion, avoiding the need for a learned embodiment-specific
latent-to-action decoder.
• Cross-embodiment generalisation and dexterous ma-
nipulation. We demonstrate zero-shot transfer to held-
out end effectors, including real-world dexterous manip-
ulation without world-model adaptation, and task gener-
alisation following task-agnostic domain adaptation.
2. Related Work
Latent Action Models. Learning latent actions from ob-
servations has emerged as a promising approach to lever-
aging large-scale, unlabelled interaction data.
Typically,
an inverse dynamics model encodes changes between ob-
servations into a latent action, which is then used to con-
dition a forward dynamics model to predict the resulting
scene evolution [24, 31]. However, the learned represen-
tation does not necessarily isolate the underlying physi-
cal motion.
When inferred from images, latent actions
may become entangled with viewpoint changes, camera
motion, background appearance, and other visual varia-
tions.
Reconstruction-based objectives can also encour-
age shortcut learning, allowing latent representations to
encode information about future observations rather than
meaningful action-like transformations. To address these
challenges, prior work has explored approaches including


action-category-guided contrastive learning [5], early tem-
poral differencing and temporal contrastive learning [28],
cross-viewpoint reconstruction [19], and contrastive align-
ment of video-derived latent actions with executable ac-
tion tokens learned from robot trajectories [32]. We pursue
a complementary direction by inferring latent actions di-
rectly from 3D point representations of the scene and align-
ing matched manipulation transitions across embodiments,
encouraging shared motion representations across different
morphologies.
World Models for Robotic Manipulation.
Learning
predictive dynamics has become an important direction
in robotic manipulation, supporting model-based planning
and control [11].
Much of this research has explored
video-based world models, with more recent approaches
investigating explicit 3D representations to model spa-
tial interactions and scene evolution.
PointWorld [14]
scales particle-based world modelling to diverse manip-
ulation settings, predicting full-scene 3D point displace-
ments through a shared geometric state and action rep-
resentation. 3DPWM [22] addresses partial observability
through point-cloud completion, enabling dynamics predic-
tion over temporally corresponding 3D geometry. Explor-
ing this direction in dexterous manipulation, He et al. [10]
train a graph-based particle dynamics model across differ-
ent hand embodiments, demonstrating generalisation to un-
seen morphologies. These works demonstrate the effective-
ness of explicit 3D representations for predictive modelling
in robotic manipulation.
Latent Action World Models. Early work demonstrated
that latent actions inferred from unlabelled videos could
provide a conditioning interface for interactive world mod-
elling [3, 7]. These approaches typically learn the latent
action representation separately from the predictive model,
limiting their ability to adapt jointly. This motivates joint
training to better align action representations with predic-
tive dynamics [2, 8, 25]. More recently, LAC-WM [13]
extends latent-action world modelling across human and
robot embodiments, learning a unified action representa-
tion using action-labelled motion supervision during train-
ing. Adapting the model to unseen robots additionally re-
quires labelled robot interactions to train an action-to-latent
projector. LAWM-3D [29] introduces geometric constraints
and multi-view consistency to learn 3D-aware latent ac-
tions; however, its world model remains video-based. De-
spite these advances, planning for dexterous manipulation
remains challenging, with existing approaches employing
large sampling budgets and task-specific initialisation [9],
manually designed motion primitives [10], or learned mo-
tion priors [27, 30] to facilitate planning and control. In
contrast, we optimise directly in the latent action space,
recovering executable robot targets from predicted particle
motion through known geometry and kinematics.
3. Method
3.1. Problem Formulation
We formulate 3D world modelling as latent-action-
conditioned prediction of temporally corresponding scene
particles. Each state Xt ∈RN×3 represents the human
or robot hands, manipulated object, distractors, and back-
ground geometry.
During training, an inverse dynamics
model infers a latent action from each consecutive pair
of observed states, zt = qϕ(Xt, Xt+∆).
Given the ini-
tial scene Xt and a sequence of K latent actions, the for-
ward dynamics model jointly predicts the next K states in
a single forward pass. Prediction at horizon k is condi-
tioned only on the initial scene and the first k latent actions,
ˆXt+k∆= fθ(Xt, zt, . . . , zt+(k−1)∆), using causal chunked
decoding. We use K = 5 and sample states every six frames
from H2O’s 30 Hz data, giving ∆= 0.2 s and a prediction
horizon of 1 s [18]. This interval is chosen to capture appre-
ciable scene motion while retaining fine-grained motion.
3.2. Latent Action World Model
Shared Scene Encoding. We jointly learn inverse and for-
ward dynamics models using a shared Point Transformer
V3 (PTv3) backbone [26]. We adopt Utonia’s PTv3 imple-
mentation [34] and train the backbone from scratch. The en-
coder produces a four-stage hierarchy of geometric features,
combining local surface detail with progressively broader
spatial context. The inverse dynamics model uses attention
between the coarsest PTv3 features extracted from consecu-
tive observations to infer the transition, whereas the forward
dynamics model leverages multi-scale features from the ini-
tial scene to predict particle motion.
Latent Action Inference.
For each consecutive pair of
observations (Xt, Xt+∆), we augment their coarsest PTv3
features with temporal embeddings encoding their observa-
tion order. We then concatenate the two feature sequences
with a separator token and four learnable query tokens, al-
lowing a self-attention transformer to capture the underly-
ing motion between observations. Each query output is pro-
jected to eight dimensions, yielding a continuous latent ac-
tion zt ∈R4×8. This 32-dimensional bottleneck encour-
ages a compact representation of the observed transition,
while retaining multiple tokens allows individual particles
to attend selectively to different aspects of the inferred ac-
tion. The latent representation is learned jointly through
prediction and cross-embodiment alignment objectives.
Causal Multi-Horizon Prediction. Rather than directly
predicting future particle positions, we formulate the for-
ward dynamics model to predict displacements relative to
the initial scene. This encourages the model to focus on
temporal changes in particle positions rather than learn-
ing to reproduce static scene information. Given the ini-

3.4. Latent-Space Planning and Control
At deployment, we use the learned dynamics model
for goal-directed planning by optimising latent-action se-
quences directly. Given the current scene Xt, we search
for a sequence Zt = (zt, . . . , zt+(K−1)∆) whose predicted
scene evolution minimises a task-specific geometric cost:

We solve this optimisation using the Cross-Entropy Method
(CEM) [6], initialised with a diagonal Gaussian fitted to
latent actions inferred from training transitions. For fine-
tuned models, these statistics are estimated from their task-
agnostic adaptation sequences.
CEM samples candidate
latent-action sequences, evaluates their predicted particle
trajectories against the task objective, and iteratively refits
the sampling distribution to the elite candidates. We adopt a
receding-horizon strategy [20], repeatedly replanning from
the latest observation and executing the initial portion of
each optimised trajectory.
To execute the predicted motions, we recover robot con-
figurations from the corresponding particles using known
geometry and kinematics.
For grippers, predicted parti-
cle centroids provide Cartesian position targets, while rigid
alignment can additionally recover target orientations. For
dexterous hands, we estimate the palm pose through Kabsch
alignment [15], exploiting the known particle correspon-
dences, and subsequently refine finger joint targets through
bounded nonlinear least-squares inverse kinematics. The re-
sulting targets are passed to embodiment-specific low-level
controllers, enabling robot control without a learned latent-
to-action decoder.
4. Dataset Curation & Experimental Protocol
We construct training sequences from a subset of H2O [18]
bimanual manipulation data, using fitted MANO [23] hand
meshes, object meshes, and tracked object poses. Train-
ing and validation sets are partitioned at the sequence level
to prevent temporal overlap.
We first refine the human
hand trajectories to reduce spurious hand–object separation
and excessive interpenetration while preserving the demon-
strated motion and keeping object trajectories fixed. Refine-
ment reduces contact-region Chamfer distance by 15.1%
and the median 95th-percentile penetration depth across
hand trajectories by 22.6%. We then retarget the human
hand motion to eight robotic hands using UniDex-style
wrist alignment and fingertip inverse kinematics, yielding
matched transitions across one human and eight robot em-
bodiments [33]. We sample 1,024 particles per entity, in-
cluding each hand, manipulated object, table, and static dis-
tractors, using area-weighted barycentric sampling where
mesh geometry is available. We additionally reconstruct
the table by fitting a plane to unprojected multi-frame depth
observations using RANSAC. Static distractor objects are
placed once per sequence outside an expanded bounding
box enclosing the hand and manipulated object over the full
trajectory, varying scene clutter across embodiments while
preserving the underlying interaction.
We evaluate geometric prediction accuracy using mover
endpoint error (Mover EPE), defined as the mean per-point
Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth 3D
positions, measured in millimetres. Hand particles outnum-
ber object particles 2:1 in each scene. Since a larger propor-
tion of hand particles move more than 5 mm, they account
for 74.0% of moving particles, compared to 26.0% for ob-
ject particles, pooled across all five prediction horizons. We
therefore additionally report hand- and object-specific EPE,
alongside EPE for static scene points (table and distractors).
We focus on moving particles to prevent stationary scene
geometry from obscuring prediction errors in regions un-
dergoing manipulation. Moving particles are identified by a
ground-truth displacement exceeding 5 mm at each predic-
tion horizon. We evaluate predictions over five steps span-
ning 1 s, with errors reported at the horizons specified in
each experiment. This dense geometric metric captures sys-
tematic differences in rollout fidelity that task-level success
rates may not reveal. Since predictions span only one sec-
ond, absolute differences in EPE can appear modest despite
meaningful differences in predicted motion. We therefore
assess geometric fidelity alongside qualitative rollouts and
downstream task success rates.

5. Experiments
We investigate whether latent actions learned from action-
unlabelled 3D observations support transferable prediction
and control across robot end effectors. We examine latent
conditioning, composition, and cross-embodiment transfer
(Sections 5.1–5.3).
We then evaluate zero-shot gripper
manipulation (Section 5.4), planning-space and execution-
strategy ablations (Section 5.5), and dexterous task gener-
alisation following task-agnostic adaptation (Section 5.6).
Finally, we assess real-world deployment on an unseen dex-
terous hand without fine-tuning (Section 5.7).
5.1. Latent Conditioning Ablations
Given an observed manipulation window, we infer its la-
tent action sequence and condition the forward dynamics
model on the initial scene to predict subsequent states. To
assess whether the model relies on latent conditioning, we
compare predictions using the inferred latents against three
interventions:
zero conditioning, latent sequences shuf-
fled across unrelated trajectories, and a fixed latent se-
quence shared across evaluation windows.
As shown in
Table 1, correct conditioning achieves mean hand and ob-
ject EPEs of 7.94 mm and 13.59 mm, respectively, sub-
stantially outperforming zero (53.17/43.43 mm), shuffled
(75.01/51.96 mm), and fixed (60.29/46.78 mm) condition-
ing. On average, static-scene errors remain below 1 mm
across all latent conditions. These results demonstrate that
trajectory-aligned latent actions are essential for accurately
predicting hand and object motion.

5.5. Planning & Execution Ablations
World-Model Fine-Tuning. We evaluate closed-loop ma-
nipulation in RoboCasa [21] under partial observability, us-
ing scene geometry reconstructed from two RGB-D views
and forward-kinematics-derived hand geometry.
Unlike
the fully observed pretraining scenes, this setting intro-
duces noisy, partial observations.
To improve perfor-
mance under this domain shift, we fine-tune the world
model using rank-2 LoRA [12] on 84 task-agnostic ex-
ploratory play sequences from 7 non-evaluation kitchens.
Since these observations lack temporal particle correspon-
dences, we use CoTracker3 [16] to estimate them for dis-
placement supervision. To handle reconstruction noise, we
extend the movement-weighted Huber loss with learned
aleatoric uncertainty, replacing each weighted residual by
wt,k,i(e−st,k,iℓδ + λst,k,i), where ℓδ is the Huber residual,
st,k,i the predicted log-uncertainty, and λ = 1.
Planning and Execution.
We compare four configura-
tions using the same fine-tuned world model, evaluation

5.6. Task Generalisation after Domain Adaptation
Following DexWM [9], we evaluate zero-shot task gen-
eralisation after task-agnostic fine-tuning on their Robo-
Casa benchmark, comprising 50 held-out episodes each of
Reach, Grasp, and Place with a Franka arm and Allegro
hand. To ensure a fair comparison, we restrict both fine-
tuning and evaluation to the same single camera view used
by DexWM. Our method reconstructs 3D scene geome-
try from single-view observations and estimates complete
goal-hand geometry through kinematic fitting to SAM 3-
segmented hand masks [4]. We fine-tune the model using
rank-2 LoRA on task-agnostic exploratory play sequences
from kitchens excluded from the evaluation set, without
task-specific demonstrations. At deployment, we optimise
latent actions using CEM and execute predicted hand mo-
tion through kinematic fitting and closed-loop replanning.
As shown in Table 4, our method achieves 90%, 92%, and
60% success on Reach, Grasp, and Place, compared with
DexWM’s reported 72%, 58%, and 28%, respectively. No-
tably, these improvements are achieved with 20× fewer
candidate evaluations per planning step (256 vs. 5,120),
using a single H100 GPU compared with DexWM’s eight
H100s. These results demonstrate dexterous generalisation
to unseen tasks following task-agnostic adaptation, with
substantially lower planning compute requirements.

6. Limitations & Conclusion
Several limitations remain, including the learning of corre-
lations rather than explicit causal interaction mechanisms,
contact inconsistencies introduced by retargeting, and lim-
ited grasp stability without explicit tactile or force feed-
back. Addressing these challenges through improved in-
teraction modelling, contact-aware retargeting, and richer
physical feedback presents promising directions for future
work. Nevertheless, our results demonstrate that a unified
latent action space learned from action-unlabelled obser-
vations can condition shared predictive dynamics and en-
able zero-shot control across diverse robot embodiments.
DexLatentWorld enables zero-shot manipulation with un-
seen grippers, outperforms DexWM on dexterous tasks with
20× fewer candidate evaluations, and achieves 86% success
across 50 real-world trials with an unseen dexterous hand,
without fine-tuning.

ually arranging the robot and recording calibrated depth and
joint states. Target hand points are reconstructed by FK
in the robot base frame, requiring consistent object place-
ment. CEM searches five-step latent-action sequences from
the H2O-derived Gaussian prior, selecting the lowest-cost
sampled trajectory across iterations or a zero-displacement
alternative. The cost measures horizon-weighted distances
to corresponding goal-hand points, weighting finger-surface
points twice relative to palm points.
Execution and evaluation.
Predicted hand points are fit-
ted to the Sharpa kinematics to recover wrist and 22 fin-
ger targets, executed through Cartesian arm motions and
interpolated finger commands. We execute the first three
targets sequentially using UR Cartesian linear motions
and minimum-jerk finger interpolation through the Sharpa
SDK. Each target is checked against fresh joint feedback,
controller IK, and motion limits. The system alternates ob-
servation, planning, motion, and settling, rather than run-
ning at a fixed planning rate. Table 16 lists the key settings
and physical success criteria. Each task is evaluated over
ten trials.
