Physics-Informed Tactile-based Shape Estimation
of Deformable Linear Objects
Rotem Atari, Stav Ashur and Avishai Sintov
Abstract— Accurate shape estimation of Deformable Linear
Objects (DLOs) is a fundamental challenge in robotic ma-
nipulation, often hindered by visual occlusions and the high-
dimensional configuration space of flexible materials. While
Force/Torque (F/T) and vision-centric methods have been pro-
posed, they frequently struggle with grasp-site ambiguities and
environmental interference. In this letter, we investigate the
hypothesis that global shape estimation can be achieved through
tactile sensing and proprioception, without any visual percep-
tion. We contend that dense, high-resolution tactile imprints
capture critical boundary conditions and contact gradients that
are sufficient to resolve the high-dimensional configuration of
the DLO. To prove this, we introduce a Physics-Informed Vision
Transformer (ViT) framework that maps tactile images to a
3D vertex chain. By leveraging self-attention mechanisms and
a physics-weighted loss function, our model enforces structural
priors to ensure that the predicted geometry adheres to the
object’s physical manifold. Experimental results demonstrate
that our tactile-based approach achieves superior reconstruc-
tion accuracy across Euclidean and Chamfer metrics. Further-
more, we demonstrate the efficiency of this modality, achieving
model transfer to novel DLO materials and geometries. Our
findings suggest that tactile feedback provides a robust and
computationally efficient alternative for DLO state estimation
in visually degraded or occluded environments.
I. INTRODUCTION
Deformable Linear Objects (DLOs), such as cables, wires
and tubes, are ubiquitous across industrial, medical and do-
mestic environments [1]. The ability of robots to manipulate
these objects effectively is contingent upon accurate 3D
shape estimation, which enables complex tasks including
shape control, cable insertion and knot tying [2], [3]. Shape
control is particularly critical in specialized applications like
automotive cable routing and assembly. These tasks require
deforming a DLO into a specific target configuration, a
process that relies heavily on a tightly coupled loop of
perception and manipulation planning [4].
To achieve this, the shape of a DLO must be perceived
with high fidelity to provide the necessary feedback for
control algorithms. Currently, the predominant approach to
DLO state estimation relies on external vision sensors,
typically RGB-D cameras. Early methods utilized point
cloud registration paired with physics-based simulations to
enforce physical consistency [5], while more recent data-
driven architectures leverage deep learning and diffusion
models to reconstruct 3D geometries from partial visual ob-
servations [6]–[8]. However, vision-based perception remains

fundamentally constrained by line-of-sight requirements and
visual clarity. In practical tasks, external cameras frequently
suffer from severe hand-object occlusions caused by the
robot’s own grippers, workspace fixtures, or physical barriers
[1]. Furthermore, optical tracking degrades significantly in
complex environments with background clutter or dense
cable harnesses, where disentangling identical-looking target
DLOs poses an persistent computer vision bottleneck [9].
While physics-based models can temporarily bridge mo-
mentary visual gaps, they fail under complete, sustained
loss of sight or heavy clutter. In contrast, tactile-based
shape estimation is indispensable in scenarios where visual
feedback is entirely unavailable or rendered unreliable, such
as routing cables within an automotive chassis during vehicle
manufacturing, maneuvering wire harnesses inside dense
electronic enclosures, navigating unlit industrial conduits, or
when severe hand-object occlusions completely obstruct the
view of contact sites and the curve path.
An alternative to vision is the use of force-based sensing
for shape estimation. In this domain, significant theoretical
groundwork has been laid by Bretl and McCarthy [10], who
explored the relationship between contact forces and the
equilibrium configurations of elastic rods. Specifically, they
have shown that the configuration space of the wire, i.e., the
set of all equilibrium configurations, is a six-dimensional
smooth manifold. Based on this, later work has proposed a
full framework for real-time shape estimation and control of
a wire solely using a Force/Torque (F/T) sensor and without
any visual feedback [11]. However, translating these force-
based approaches into practical robotic applications may
be infeasible. These methods are fundamentally based on
analytical models that cannot account for the inherent uncer-

tainties in the DLO, such as non-uniform material properties
or manufacturing defects. In addition, these models typically
assume a perfectly straight shape in the relaxed state and rely
on fixed mounting to the robotic arm [12]. This rigid setup
prevents the system from performing spontaneous pick-and-
manipulate tasks, limiting its utility in the unstructured envi-
ronments where robotic DLO manipulation is most needed.
Furthermore, both F/T-based and vision-centric approaches
struggle to account for the specific orientation of the DLO
tip within the grasp. While F/T methods rely on point-
contact assumptions that lack local geometric resolution,
visual systems often suffer from severe occlusions at the
gripper interface. Consequently, these methods are frequently
inapplicable in practical scenarios where the grasp-site ori-
entation varies significantly across trials, as they cannot
perceive the critical boundary conditions at the contact level
that dictate the global DLO configuration.
Recent advancements in optical-based tactile sensing, such
as GelSight [13], DIGIT [14] and AllSight [15], offer high-
resolution contact images by embedding miniature cameras
behind compliant elastomers [16]. These sensors have been
successfully used with cables for local reactive tasks, such
as detecting grasp stability [17], guiding motion primitives
for weaving and insertion [18], and performing in-hand
DLO following [19]. Some research has even utilized tactile
data to reconstruct the grasped portion of DLOs to enable
contour-following [20]. However, to the best of the authors’
knowledge, optical-based tactile sensing has not yet been
utilized to estimate the full 3D global shape of a DLO beyond
the immediate contact region. Building upon prior F/T work,
we hypothesize that optical-based tactile sensors can capture
the same fundamental F/T information while simultaneously
encoding the local grasp direction. In this work, we argue that
these combined high-resolution features enable the model
to resolve the global state of the DLO, effectively moving
beyond local reactive control to full-curve shape estimation.
We propose a novel data-based framework for high-
fidelity, vision-free DLO shape estimation. Our primary con-
tributions are as follows. First, we introduce the first frame-
work capable of reconstructing the complete 3D geometry of
a DLO using exclusively tactile sensing and proprioception,
operating entirely without external visual perception. Sec-
ond, we introduce a tactile-proprioceptive architecture that
leverages the Sparsh tactile foundation model [21] through a
multi-scale attentive fusion mechanism. We employ a cross-
attention decoder conditioned on proprioceptive queries, in-
tegrated with a differentiable B-spline layer for geometric
regularization. This approach demonstrates that the latent
representations of Sparsh, originally designed for discrete
tasks, can effectively generalize to the structured geometric
regression of deformable objects. Consequently, the proposed
framework achieves end-to-end, real-time DLO shape es-
timation directly from raw inputs, entirely bypassing the
need for explicit calibration. Third, we formulate a physics-
informed weighted loss grounded in the Discrete Elastic
Rods formulation [22], enforcing structural priors on tan-
gency, length and curvature to ensure predicted shapes adhere
to the physical manifold of the object. Fourth, we validate
our framework through extensive experimentation, including
baseline comparisons, ablations of sensor configurations,
and transfer learning. Our findings confirm that the model
generalizes robustly to DLOs with distinct mechanical and
geometric properties using minimal additional data. Lastly,
the trained model and labeled dataset is made available open-
source for the benefit of the community1.
While existing DLO manipulation frameworks rely heavily
on vision or force tracking, these modalities are vulnerable
to hand-object occlusions and grasp ambiguities. Rather
than replacing them, this letter investigates whether high-
resolution tactile imprints and proprioception can serve as
a standalone, vision-free alternative for global shape estima-
tion. We establish a robust baseline for visually degraded en-
vironments, ultimately serving as a complementary modality
to fuse with vision pipelines for fail-safe estimation under
severe occlusions.
II. METHOD
A. Problem Formulation
We consider the problem of a DLO grasped by two parallel
grippers: one gripper (G1) remains stationary, serving as a
fixed base, while the second (G2) is mounted to a robotic arm
to facilitate manipulation (Figure 1). To describe the system’s
configuration, we define O as the global coordinate frame,
which is rigidly fixed to G1. Each finger of both grippers
is equipped with an optical-based tactile sensor that serves
as the finger pad. Consequently, let Iij,t ∈I denote the
tactile image acquired from sensor j ∈{1, 2} of gripper
Gi at time t. This configuration yields a comprehensive set
of four tactile images at each timestep, defined as It =
{I11,t, I12,t, I21,t, I22,t}. The pose of G2 with respect to G1
is denoted by At ∈SE(3), and can easily be computed
through the forward kinematics of the arm.
A DLO is grasped between the two finger pads of each
gripper. It is important to emphasize that the DLO tips are
oriented arbitrarily with respect to the grippers. This implies
that the local contact geometry is not pre-aligned to some
axis. The DLO is represented as a discrete ordered set of n
vertices X = {x1, x2, . . . , xn}, where xi ∈R3 is expressed
with respect to coordinate frame O. We explore the learning
of a discrete map
Ψt : Jt × SE(3) →Xt,

given a tactile measurement Jt and the pose At, the map
will output a set of points Xt = Ψ(Jt, A) along the DLO.

C. State Estimation Model
To train Ψ, we propose a multi-modal encoder–decoder
architecture that fuses tactile image features with robot
proprioception to regress the spatial vertex chain X. The
architecture, illustrated in Figure 2, comprises four stages:
(i) a tactile encoder that extracts spatial tokens from the four
sensors; (ii) a proprioceptive encoder that maps a G2 pose
to a set of marker queries; (iii) a cross-attention decoder
that fuses both modalities and predicts per-vertex coordi-
nates; and (iv) a differentiable B-spline layer that enforces
geometric smoothness on the output curve.
1) Tactile Encoder: To enable temporal tactile reasoning,
we formulate Jt to include temporal tactile images. For each
sensor, the three-channel RGB tactile frame Iij,t is con-
catenated with the previous one Iij,t−1. Hence, each tactile
sensor provides a six-channel input, encoding local temporal
deformation cues. A Sparsh-based Vision Transformer (ViT-
S) with a 16×16 patch size serves as the tactile encoder. This
encoder is initialized from the Sparsh foundation model [21],
which provides a family of general-purpose touch represen-
tations. Sparsh was pretrained via DINO self-distillation on
a large-scale dataset of over 460,000 tactile images, encom-
passing a diverse range of vision-based sensors to ensure ro-
bust feature extraction. ViT-S remains predominantly frozen
to preserve the integrity of the pretrained representations.
Task-specific adaptation is facilitated by training only the
final two transformer blocks and the concluding LayerNorm
at a reduced learning rate, specifically ×0.05 the base rate.
Rather than using only the final-layer output, we extract
intermediate representations from a set of the transformer
layers S = {3, 6, 9, 12}. They are then combined by a
learned weighted sum (attentive probing)

III. EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we evaluate the proposed approach and
provide a comprehensive performance analysis across several
key metrics. To ensure computational efficiency, all models
were trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU with
mixed-precision training. Videos of 4experiments can be seen
in the supplementary material.
A. Hardware Platform
We base our experiments on four DIGIT tactile sensors
[14], mounted on the fingers of two Robotiq 2F-85 parallel
grippers. One gripper (G1) is fixed to the workspace while
the other (G2) is mounted to a Kinova Gen3 7-DOF manipu-
lator. The grippers hold the two opposite ends of a DLO. The
system is seen in Figure 1. Each DIGIT sensor provides a
320×240 RGB tactile image at a frequency of approximately
15 Hz. For data collection and to acquire the ground-truth
shape of the DLO, an OptiTrack motion capture system
tracks m = 14 reflective markers affixed along the DLO
at roughly equal arc-length intervals. The pose At of G2
with respect to G1 is acquired through forward kinematics
of the robotic arm. Robot motion and data acquisition are
controlled using ROS 2.
B. Dataset
We begin by collecting data from a primary DLO, a
braided steel wire with a diameter of 1.5 mm and a length
of 550 mm. The collection was performed over 36 episodes,
where in each episode, the wire was grasped in a new
pose somewhat covering the orientation range [−45◦, 45◦]
with respect to the gripper axis to ensure grasp diversity.
This range represents the physical limit of the setup, as
wider angles cause structural collisions or slippage. Then,
the robotic arm executed quasi-static gripper motions while
deforming the wire under gravity. The m = 14 unordered
tracked markers are sorted via a momentum-based greedy
algorithm and resampled to K = 16 evenly-spaced vertices
through B-spline fitting. The value for K was empirically
optimized through an ablation study, with the estimation
error showing minimal variation across different values. The
collection yielded a total of N = 196, 678 labeled training
samples. An additional 21, 853 samples were collected over
four independent episodes and used as the test set.
C. Model Training
All models are trained using the AdamW optimizer (β1 =
0.9, β2 = 0.999) and a OneCycleLR schedule with a 10%
linear warmup. The base learning rate and weight decay
are both set to 10−4. To facilitate task-specific adaptation
while preserving the Sparsh foundation model’s features, the
backbone is kept mostly frozen; only the last two transformer
blocks are unfrozen and trained at a reduced learning rate of
10−5. Training is conducted with a batch size of 32 over
10 epochs. To improve feature extraction, a reference tactile
image (captured under no-contact conditions) is subtracted
from the observed tactile frames. This subtraction ensures
the model remains agnostic to the sensor’s static background
and focuses exclusively on the color gradients generated by
physical contact. Both the input poses and output marker
positions are standardized to zero mean and unit variance
to ensure numerical stability and balanced gradient updates.
The training objective is the physics-informed loss L defined
in Eq. (6), with the loss hyperparameters set to λw = 1.0,
λc = 0.25, wn = 1.0, we = 0.05, wℓ= 0.5 and wκ = 0.01.
These weights were determined empirically during training
to properly balance spatial localization accuracy with phys-
ical smoothness constraints, ensuring that the predicted 3D
vertex chain adheres closely to the DLO’s material continuity
without causing optimization instability.
D. Model Evaluation
Standalone analytical estimators (e.g., DER) are not viable
baselines here because our arbitrary grasp orientations leave
the required boundary conditions unobservable. Furthermore,
prior learning-based DLO trackers rely on base F/T sensing
rather than distributed tactile arrays, precluding direct de-
ployment. We therefore validate our proposed DLO shape
estimation model against five baseline architectures designed
to isolate the contributions of different sensing modalities
and architectures. The first, a Pose-Only (MLP) baseline,
serves as a lower bound by mapping the 7-D gripper pose At
directly to the predicted vertex chain, attempting to capture
only robot proprioception. To investigate the specific tactile
features learned by our model, we introduce a pose and
DLO tip direction baseline; this model removes complex
tactile features in favor of a geometric cue available at
the contact site. For each sensor, the 2D tip direction is
extracted with respect to the gripper. The direction is derived
via a non-differentiable pipeline of adaptive thresholding,
morphological cleanup, and robust line fitting to the contact

to a notable degradation in reconstruction fidelity. This
suggests that the Lweighted term effectively bridges the gap
between raw data-driven estimation and the underlying phys-
ical manifold of the DLO, ensuring that the model adheres
to essential geometric and structural priors during inference.
Similarly, removing the B-spline layer eliminates the inher-
ent spatial coupling between neighboring nodes, resulting
in high-frequency, physically implausible oscillations and
decreased accuracy. Consequently, our proposed ViT-based
model achieved superior performance, yielding the lowest
estimation error across both Euclidean and Chamfer metrics.
This success also suggests that the attention mechanisms in
the ViT architecture are particularly well-suited for capturing
long-range spatial correlations between local tactile contact
gradients and the global DLO configuration. By leveraging
these high-dimensional features, the model effectively re-
solves the inherent ambiguities of proprioception-only ap-
proaches, providing a robust and high-fidelity reconstruction
of the entire vertex chain. Figure 3a shows a shape estimation

To assess the computational efficiency and suitability for
real-time robotic control, Table I also reports the mean
inference time for each architecture over 1,000 trials. We
note that the tip direction computation was not optimized and
was utilized solely for accuracy estimation. Consequently, its
computational time is not reported, as it does not reflect the
potential real-time performance of a production-ready imple-
mentation. The results indicate that our proposed ViT-based
model exhibits the highest inference time, which is attributed
to its larger number of trainable parameters compared to the
baseline architectures. Nevertheless, the model achieves a
mean inference rate of 16 Hz (61.5 ms latency), matching
or exceeding state-of-the-art DLO state estimation pipelines
(10-15 Hz) [6], [23]. Crucially, this execution speed easily
surpasses typical closed-loop DLO control frequencies (5-
15 Hz) [19], ensuring real-time feedback aligned with both
sensor throughput and control demands.
E. Data Requirement Analysis
We subsequently analyze the data requirements for train-
ing the proposed ViT-based architecture. To quantify how
the size of training data influences performance, we evaluate
the model’s accuracy across a varying number of collec-
tion episodes. This evaluation is conducted via a cross-
validation approach: for each specified episode count, the
model is trained 10 independent times using randomly sam-
pled episodes from the full dataset. Each episode contains
approximately 5, 460 training samples, allowing us to charac-
terize the learning curve and identify the point of diminishing
returns for data collection.
Figure 4 illustrates the evolution of the RMSE and Cham-
fer distance as a function of the number of training episodes.
Both metrics exhibit a sharp initial improvement after the
first five episodes, with the RMSE decreasing by 35% and
the Chamfer distance by 28%. This steep trajectory suggests
that the model rapidly internalizes the behavior of DLO
deformation, while the subsequent leveling of the curves
indicates the onset of diminishing returns as the dataset
grows beyond a critical threshold. Performance continues
to improve gradually as more data is introduced, though at
a significantly reduced rate compared to the initial phase.
Notably, the inter-trial variance increases with more than 25
episodes, suggesting that in higher data regimes, the specific
composition of episodes becomes more influential. This
phenomenon is likely due to the inherent variation in DLO
configurations across different recording episodes, where
certain rare geometries may disproportionately affect model
convergence. Overall, these results indicate that reliable state
estimation is achievable with as few as 15 episodes (∼42% of
the full dataset). This has significant practical implications, as
it demonstrates that data collection effort can be substantially
reduced in new deployment scenarios without compromising
the system’s operational integrity. Hence, we next investigate
the ability to transfer the model to unseen DLOs.
F. Transfer Learning
To evaluate for cross-DLO transfer, we investigate the
effort required to fine-tune the model, previously trained on
the primary DLO (braided steel wire of 1.5 mm diameter and
550 mm length), to estimate shapes of new DLO types. We
consider two new cables: a USB cable of diameter 4.5 mm
and length 550 mm, and a braided steel wire of diameter
2 mm and length 750 mm. The latter DLO has the same
material and structural properties as the primary DLO, but
with slightly larger diameter and length. For each of the two
cables, 50, 000 new data samples were collected along with
a test set of 14,000 samples. Then, the model trained for the
primary DLO was fine-tuned with a lower learning rate by
an order of magnitude compared to the original training.
Figures 5a and 5b illustrate the scaling of RMSE and
Chamfer errors relative to the size of the fine-tuning dataset,
contrasted against models trained from scratch on the same
novel data. In the zero-shot regime, the model exhibits signif-
icantly diminished performance, confirming that a network
trained on a single DLO cannot inherently generalize to
the distinct mechanical variations of novel DLOs without
adaptation. However, the subsequent inclusion of limited data

G. Robustness to Partial Loss of Tactile Information
To evaluate the framework’s resilience to partial data
loss or hardware failures, we benchmarked performance
under two paradigms where {1, 2, 3} sensors are randomly
removed: retraining the model on a reduced number of
sensor, and 2) evaluating the accuracy of the model, with-
out retraining, while masking faulty channels with blank
inputs to simulate sudden runtime failure. As summarized
in Table II, the retrained model exhibits excellent structural
adaptation, maintaining a low RMSE even when relying on
just half of the original tactile array. When subjected to
sensor faults without retraining, performance degrades more
rapidly due to the stark distribution shift at the input layer.
This performance gap suggests a valuable future direction:
incorporating stochastic sensor dropout during training to ex-
plicitly condition the network against runtime sensor faults.
IV. CONCLUSIONS
This paper demonstrates that high-fidelity global shape es-
timation of DLOs can be achieved using only tactile sensing
and proprioception, bypassing visual limitations. By integrat-
ing high-resolution tactile contact gradients with global pro-
prioceptive data through a physics-informed ViT framework,
our approach accurately resolves high-dimensional DLO
configurations. The framework effectively determines DLO-
gripper orientation, maintains high performance in data-
sparse regimes, and generalizes well to novel DLOs via
sample-efficient transfer learning.
The performance degradation observed during sudden
sensor failure and out-of-distribution states, such as near-
buckling or rapid object slippage, motivates several direc-
tions for future work. To address these limitations, we
intend to utilize high-fidelity simulation environments to
generate diverse datasets across a wide range of mechanical
properties and extreme deformation regimes, enabling a
more generalized model that can handle sensor faults via
dropout, as well as varying stiffness and friction coefficients.
Additionally, we will explore incorporating the total wire
length, measured directly by the robotic system, as a dynamic
network input to enhance the model’s adaptability to different
DLO geometries without sacrificing tracking accuracy.
