Abstract
Even in multi-camera setups, occlusions can leave individ-
ual body joints with only a single usable 2D observation.
Such an observation defines a viewing ray but leaves the
joint’s depth undetermined. We introduce Body of Evidence
(BoE), a multi-view 3D human pose estimator that predicts
per-camera joint depths alongside 2D joint locations. To
reduce monocular depth ambiguity, we propose geometry-
conditioned depth prediction, which combines image fea-
tures with previously reconstructed 3D body geometry as
contextual depth cues.
We further introduce learnable
depth-aware triangulation (LDAT), which augments differ-
entiable algebraic triangulation with predicted depth con-
straints, enabling reconstruction even from a single con-
tributing camera while preserving the benefits of geometric
triangulation when multiple views are available. The re-
constructed 3D poses subsequently guide depth prediction
in the next iteration, allowing depth estimates and 3D ge-
ometry to progressively refine each other. Experiments on
CMU Panoptic demonstrate state-of-the-art in-domain per-
formance and superior average performance across eval-
uated camera subsets and unseen camera arrangements.
BoE is particularly effective under sparse camera coverage,
outperforming the previous state of the art by 10.6 AP25
points in the challenging three-camera setting.

relies on observations from multiple views. When a joint is
observed in only one camera, its 2D location constrains it to
a 3D viewing ray, leaving its position along that ray undeter-
mined. Consequently, reconstruction remains challenging
under severe occlusion or limited camera overlap.
We address this limitation with Body of Evidence
(BoE), an end-to-end multi-view 3D human pose estima-
tor that predicts per-camera 2D joint locations and depths.
We propose learnable depth-aware triangulation (LDAT),
which incorporates these predictions as complementary
constraints within a differentiable algebraic triangulation
system.
By combining predicted depths with calibrated
multi-view geometry, LDAT enables 3D reconstruction
even when a joint is observed in only one camera, while
preserving the geometric inductive bias of triangulation.
A central challenge, however, is the inherent ambiguity
of monocular depth prediction. While the depth of an indi-
vidual joint may be ambiguous from a single view, the es-
timated 3D configuration of the body provides metric con-
text that can help constrain its depth. To exploit this re-
lationship, we propose geometry-conditioned depth predic-
tion, which conditions individual joint-depth estimates on
previously reconstructed body geometry. These depth esti-
mates are incorporated into LDAT to update the 3D poses,
which in turn provide refined geometric context for sub-
sequent depth predictions. This iterative feedback allows
multi-view geometry to inform monocular depth estimation,
while learned depth provides additional constraints for 3D
reconstruction.
We summarize our contributions as:
• We propose two complementary components: geometry-
conditioned depth prediction, which leverages previously
reconstructed metric body geometry to reduce monocu-
lar depth ambiguity, and LDAT, which incorporates pre-
dicted depths into geometric reconstruction, enabling re-
construction even from a single contributing camera.
• We introduce BoE, an end-to-end multi-view 3D human
pose estimator that iteratively couples depth prediction
and triangulation, allowing depth estimates and 3D poses
to progressively refine each other.
• Experiments on CMU Panoptic demonstrate state-of-the-
art in-domain performance and the highest average AP25
and mAP across evaluated camera subsets and unseen
camera arrangements.
2. Related Work
Multi-view 3D human pose estimation.
Classical multi-
view methods detect 2D poses independently, associate
them across cameras, and reconstruct 3D joints through tri-
angulation [5, 7, 10, 11, 35]. While explicit geometry facil-
itates generalization across camera configurations, errors in
2D estimation and cross-view association propagate to the
final reconstruction, particularly under occlusion. Learn-
able Triangulation [16] introduces differentiable algebraic
triangulation into end-to-end learning, enabling 2D predic-
tions to be optimized for 3D reconstruction. Other meth-
ods address reconstruction ambiguities through 3D picto-
rial structures and graphical models
[2, 3, 12], stochas-
tic triangulation [1], or explicit anatomical constraints and
holistic pose priors [8, 28]. While structural constraints fa-
vor anatomically plausible reconstructions, they generally
cannot uniquely determine the depth of a joint observed
in only one camera.
Alternatively, volumetric methods
fuse multi-view features in a common 3D representation
[16, 27, 34, 36], but introduce computational and discretiza-
tion costs and may generalize poorly to unseen camera con-
figurations.
Recent transformer-based approaches enable end-to-end
multi-view reasoning without dense 3D volumes. MvP [29]
uses hierarchical queries and attention to directly estimate
3D poses from multi-view features, but its learned mapping
can depend on the training camera configuration. MVG-
Former [19] instead alternates learned per-view 2D refine-
ment with explicit geometric triangulation. MV-SSM [9]
extends this paradigm by modeling spatial joint dependen-
cies through state-space models. Both demonstrate strong
generalization to unseen camera configurations, highlight-
ing the benefits of explicit geometry. However, since con-
ventional triangulation cannot determine the depth of a joint
observed in only one camera, these methods struggle in
sparse camera setups and crowded scenes where occlusions
are frequent. BoE addresses this limitation by predicting
per-view 2.5D observations and incorporating learned depth
constraints into triangulation.
Monocular 3D human pose estimation.
Monocular 3D
pose estimation is inherently ambiguous, as variations in
body scale, articulation, and camera geometry can pro-
duce similar 2D projections. Many methods focus on root-
relative 3D pose estimation, predicting joint locations rela-
tive to a reference joint [6, 22, 25]. Alternatively, 2.5D pose
representations combine image-plane joint locations with
estimated depths, separating 2D localization from depth
inference [14, 15].
Nie et al. [23] combine local image
evidence with global skeleton context for joint-depth pre-
diction, while Jin et al. [17] estimate absolute root and
root-relative joint depths using pose-guided depth querying.
Iqbal et al. [15] predict 2.5D poses from monocular images
and exploit multi-view consistency for weakly supervised
learning, without fusing per-view predictions during 3D re-
construction. BoE instead estimates per-view 2.5D observa-
tions within an iterative multi-view framework, where pre-
viously triangulated joints provide metric 3D context for
depth prediction.

tary constraints within a differentiable algebraic triangula-
tion system.
To the best of our knowledge, BoE is the first multi-view
3D human pose estimation framework to iteratively cou-
ple monocular depth prediction with depth-aware triangu-
lation, enabling bidirectional refinement through geometry-
conditioned depth estimation and reconstruction.

Pose scoring (PS).
To assess the quality of each recon-
structed pose, we predict a confidence score from the joint
appearance tokens of the complete pose query. Specifically,
we prepend a learned classification token to the sequence of
J joint appearance tokens and process the sequence using
multi-head self-attention. The classification-token output is
mapped to a scalar pose confidence, allowing the model to
assess the joint evidence of the complete pose. Low-scoring
hypotheses are filtered between decoder layers and during
final post-processing.
3.5. Training Objective
We adopt the fixed many-to-one anchor-based assignment
of [19] for pose regression, but replace its anchor-based
classification targets with labels determined by reconstruc-
tion accuracy. Specifically, a predicted pose is labeled posi-
tive if its MPJPE to the closest ground-truth pose falls below
a threshold. These labels are recomputed at each decoder
layer, encouraging pose scores to reflect reconstruction ac-
curacy rather than initial anchor proximity.
At each decoder layer, we supervise the regression-
matched queries using 3D pose, 2D joint-location, and
depth losses, while the classification loss supervises all
queries using their layer-specific labels. The per-layer train-
ing objective is
L = λclsLcls + λ3DL3D + λ2DL2D + λDLdepth.
(11)
The depth loss measures the ℓ1 error between predicted and
ground-truth absolute camera-frame joint depths, excluding
joints whose ground-truth projections fall outside the im-
age bounds. All decoder layers are supervised; further loss
details are provided in the supplementary material.
DropView augmentation (DV).
To improve robustness
under sparse-view conditions, we randomly reduce the
number of available cameras during training. For each mini-
batch, we uniformly sample the number of retained cam-
eras between a configured minimum and the total number
of available cameras. This exposes the model to varying
observation conditions and encourages it to combine multi-
view geometry with monocular depth cues when only lim-
ited camera evidence is available.
4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We follow the evaluation protocol of [9, 19],
training exclusively on CMU Panoptic [18] with the stan-
dard five-camera CMU0 configuration and train–test split.
To evaluate generalization to different numbers of cameras,
we denote by CMU0(K) the CMU0 configuration evalu-
ated with K cameras, removing or adding cameras at in-
ference relative to the training configuration.
For cross-
arrangement evaluation, we test on CMU1–CMU4, which
use camera arrangements unseen during training. We addi-
tionally evaluate cross-scene generalization on Campus [2]
and Shelf [2]. We use CMU3 alongside CMU0 for ablation
studies and CMU3 for qualitative comparisons, as it has the
fewest cameras among CMU1–CMU4 and provides a chal-
lenging setting for evaluating unseen camera arrangements
under limited camera coverage.
Evaluation metrics.
On CMU Panoptic, we report Av-
erage Precision at a 25mm threshold (AP25), mean Av-
erage Precision (mAP), and Mean Per Joint Position Er-
ror (MPJPE), following prior work [9, 19].
mAP is
computed by averaging AP over MPJPE thresholds of
25, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150.
MPJPE is reported in millime-
ters. For Campus and Shelf, we report Percentage of Cor-
rect Parts (PCP) following the standard evaluation protocol.
Implementation details.
Following prior works [9, 19,
27, 29], we use a ResNet-50 [13] backbone pretrained for
2D pose estimation [31] on COCO. We use four decoder
layers and follow [19] for query initialization, hidden di-
mension, confidence filtering, and non-maximum suppres-
sion. We train for 40 epochs with a learning rate of 4×10−4
and early stopping.

4.2. In-domain results
Table 1 compares our method with prior work on the stan-
dard five-camera CMU Panoptic configuration, CMU0(5),
using the conventional train–test split. BoE achieves the
highest AP25 (93.7) and lowest MPJPE (15.4 mm), improv-
ing over MVGFormer by 1.4 AP25 points and 0.6 mm, re-
spectively. Compared with MV-SSM, BoE improves AP25
by 0.2 points and reduces MPJPE by 0.3 mm. These re-
sults indicate that incorporating monocular depth cues into
geometric reconstruction can improve pose accuracy even
under the five-camera training configuration.
4.3. Cross-camera and cross-arrangement general-
ization
We first evaluate generalization to different numbers of
cameras using CMU0(K). Following prior work [9, 19],
we compute averages over K = 3 to 7, including the in-
domain configuration CMU0(5). As shown in Table 2, BoE
achieves the highest average AP25 (87.5) and mAP (97.3),
outperforming MV-SSM by 2.7 and 0.6 points, respectively.
The largest improvements occur with fewer cameras. On
CMU0(3), BoE achieves 66.0 AP25 and 92.9 mAP, exceed-
ing MV-SSM by 10.6 and 2.6 points. On CMU0(4), the cor-
responding improvements are 2.8 and 0.5 points. With six
or seven cameras, performance remains competitive with
the strongest baselines. These results suggest that the ad-
ditional monocular depth constraints are particularly bene-
ficial when multi-view geometric observations are limited.
This is further supported by the analysis in Sec. 8 of the sup-
plementary material, which shows that LDAT provides the
largest benefits for joints with fewer contributing cameras.
We further evaluate generalization to unseen camera ar-
rangements using CMU1–CMU4. As shown in Table 2,
BoE achieves the highest average AP25 (82.0) and mAP
(95.7), surpassing MV-SSM by 3.4 and 2.2 points, respec-
tively. The largest AP25 improvement occurs on CMU2,
where BoE reaches 82.0 compared with 74.6 for MV-SSM.
On CMU3, BoE improves mAP from 84.8 to 91.6, while
AP25 increases from 59.5 to 62.0. BoE also achieves the
highest AP25 on CMU1 and CMU4, although MV-SSM
retains a slightly higher mAP on CMU4. Overall, these
results indicate strong generalization to camera configura-
tions not observed during training. Qualitative comparisons
on CMU3 are shown in Figs. 3 and 4, with additional visu-
alizations provided in the supplementary material.
4.4. Cross-scene generalization
Table 3 evaluates cross-scene transfer from CMU Panop-
tic to Campus and Shelf.
On Shelf, BoE achieves 87.7
average PCP, comparable to MVGFormer (87.9) and MV-
SSM (88.0).
However, performance drops substantially
on Campus, where BoE achieves 26.6 average PCP, com-
pared with 58.1 for MVGFormer and 67.3 for MV-SSM.
Further analysis in Sec. 7 of the supplementary material
reveals a substantial increase in mean depth-prediction er-
ror, from 27.5 mm on CMU0 and 34.1 mm on CMU3 to
61.7 mm on Shelf and 6,583.4 mm on Campus. This coin-
cides with a shift in typical subject depths from 2–5 m on
CMU Panoptic to 5–15 m on Campus, suggesting that the
learned depth predictions are sensitive to substantial depth-
distribution shifts.
The modular pipeline of [10] outperforms all evaluated
end-to-end methods [9, 19, 27, 29] on both datasets. These
results highlight the distinction between generalization to
unseen camera configurations and transfer across scenes,
where changes in appearance and environmental conditions
introduce additional challenges.

5. Conclusion and Future Work
We presented BoE, a multi-view 3D human pose estima-
tor that augments geometric reconstruction with learned
monocular depth cues. BoE reduces monocular depth am-
biguity by conditioning depth predictions on previously re-
constructed 3D body geometry. LDAT incorporates pre-
dicted camera-frame depths as additional constraints in dif-
ferentiable algebraic triangulation, enabling reconstruction
even when only a single camera contributes an observa-
tion. Through iterative refinement, predicted depths and re-
constructed body geometry inform and improve each other.
Our experiments demonstrate the benefits of conditioning
monocular depth prediction on reconstructed body geome-
try and of incorporating predicted depths into triangulation
when multi-view evidence is limited. On CMU Panoptic,
BoE achieves state-of-the-art in-domain performance and
superior average performance across evaluated camera sub-
sets and unseen camera arrangements, with a 10.6 AP25-
point improvement over the previous state of the art in the
three-camera setting.
Nevertheless, the weaker transfer to Campus highlights
an opportunity to improve the cross-scene generalization of
depth prediction. Moreover, while predicted depths enable
reconstruction from a single-view observation, BoE can-
not recover joints that are occluded in all cameras. Future
work could address this limitation by incorporating kine-
matic models or learned 3D pose priors to enable inference
of completely unobserved joints.

6. Implementation details
Input images are resized to 960 × 512 and processed by
a ResNet-50 backbone pretrained for 2D pose estimation
on COCO. Following [19], we use 256-dimensional joint
tokens and initialize 1024 person queries on a regular 3D
grid over the capture volume. Queries with confidence be-
low ϵ = 0.1 are filtered between decoder layers, followed
by non-maximum suppression to remove redundant predic-
tions. Training takes approximately 60 hours on a single
node equipped with four NVIDIA GH200 GPUs.
Training objective.
We supervise the outputs of all L = 4
decoder layers. For regression, we follow MVGFormer [19]
and assign the K = 5 nearest initial query anchors to each
ground-truth pose, keeping these assignments fixed across
decoder layers. For classification, each predicted pose re-
ceives a positive label if its MPJPE to the closest ground-
truth pose is below 150 mm, and a negative label otherwise.
Classification labels are recomputed independently at each
decoder layer.

8. Depth-aware triangulation error
Figure 5 compares the distributions of 3D joint errors ob-
tained using depth-aware triangulation and conventional
2D-only DLT on the same CMU3 predictions. Results are
grouped by decoder layer (rows) and number of contribut-
ing cameras (columns): one, two, or three to four cameras.
For this comparison, the depth constraints are removed from
the triangulation system while keeping the predicted 2D ob-
servations fixed.
The largest improvements from depth-aware triangula-
tion occur when only one camera contributes to recon-
struction. The benefit remains evident with two cameras,
while LDAT and conventional triangulation yield increas-
ingly similar results with three or four contributing cam-
eras. This is expected, as additional 2D observations pro-
vide stronger geometric constraints, reducing the reliance
on monocular depth estimates. These observations support
the value of monocular depth constraints when multi-view
observations are limited.
