Abstract
World action models (WAMs) predict how the world re-
sponds to actions and offer a promising path toward gen-
eral embodied intelligence, but they are large and run the
network many times per plan, so they are hard to deploy
on robots. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) compute with
sparse binary spikes and use additions instead of multi-
plications, which saves power. However, existing WAMs
use dense videos as the world state, so a spiking version
would spend most of its computation on the background.
We therefore introduce SpikingWAM, the first spiking WAM,
which uses a sparse task skeleton as the world state. In-
spired by biological motor control, which attends only to
the key positions of a task, the Task Skeleton State (TSS)
keeps the keypoints of the arm and the task objects, and a
small spiking front end estimates the object keypoints from
images. Body Frame Spike Coding (BFSC) and Grid Coin-
cidence Attention (GCA) keep millimetre precision, and the
Persistent Single Step Readout (PSR) runs the network once
per plan. On the simulated benchmarks LIBERO, LIBERO-
Plus and RoboTwin 2.0, SpikingWAM achieves competitive
success rates with far less energy than ANN methods and
surpasses all compared methods on LIBERO-Plus.
Real
robot experiments further confirm its effectiveness. These
results show that a task skeleton lets spiking networks im-
plement WAMs at very low energy, offering a new solu-
tion for low power embodied intelligence. Project page:
https://wxqnl.github.io/spikingwam
1. Introduction
World action models (WAMs) [15, 18, 29, 48, 49] let a robot
learn in one model how to act and how the world changes
in response. They offer a promising path toward general
embodied intelligence. However, most existing WAMs use
videos or visual latents as the world state [15, 18, 48, 49].
These models are large and hard to run on robots with lim-
ited resources. To this end, recent work skips future gen-

These models still rely on dense multiply and accumulate
operations, and each plan runs the network several times
for multistep sampling. A robot in closed loop control plans
again and again, so this cost adds up. Lowering the energy
of each plan is therefore key to deploying WAMs on board.
Neurons in spiking neural networks (SNNs) communi-
cate with discrete spikes and fire only when the membrane
potential crosses a threshold. On neuromorphic hardware,
this event-driven computation replaces multiply and accu-
mulate with accumulate, so its energy grows with the num-
ber of events [28]. SNNs have recently moved from vi-
sual perception [46] to embodied control. Spiking vision-
language-action models (VLAs) [31, 38] and spiking diffu-
sion policies [10, 35] generate actions, and a spiking world
model [33] predicts the next state after an action. How-
ever, WAMs are hard to implement directly with SNNs.
Mainstream WAMs are built on large video generation mod-
els [15, 18, 48, 49], and there is still no mature way to turn
such models into SNNs. Even if this were done, dense video
representations would still spend most events on a back-

ground unrelated to the task. Small jitter in the image would
also keep triggering new events. Running the network sev-
eral times per plan further cancels the savings from sparsity.
Existing approaches therefore cannot exploit the sparse and
event-driven nature of SNNs. Integer spikes also struggle to
keep the millimetre precision of fine manipulation.
In contrast, biological motor control attends to the key
positions of a task and represents space relative to the body.
When people manipulate objects, their gaze falls almost
only on grasp points and targets [12]. The premotor and
posterior parietal cortices represent targets relative to the
arm or the hand [3, 7]. Grid cells encode position with pe-
riodic firing [8], and cortical neurons can detect spikes that
arrive at the same time [16]. This suggests that the world
state only needs the key positions of the arm and the task
objects. Once coordinates are written into spikes relative to
the body, the positions of hand and object can be compared
by spike coincidence. Following the event-driven principle,
only changes pass between consecutive plans.
Based on these ideas, we propose a sparse and event-
driven paradigm for spiking WAMs and build SpikingWAM
on it. To our knowledge, SpikingWAM is the first spiking
world action model. A single spiking network trained from
scratch learns future skeletons and action chunks together,
and it generates only the action chunks at inference (Fig. 2).
First, we propose the Task Skeleton State (TSS), which uses
only the keypoints of the arm and the task objects as the
world state. A small spiking perception front end estimates
the object keypoints from images. Next, we propose Body
Frame Spike Coding (BFSC) and Grid Coincidence Atten-
tion (GCA). BFSC encodes coordinates relative to the body
and GCA compares positions by spike coincidence, so the
geometry keeps millimetre precision. Finally, we propose
the Persistent Single Step Readout (PSR). It runs the net-
work once per plan and passes only the changes between
consecutive plans. On LIBERO [21] and LIBERO-Plus [5],
SpikingWAM reaches success rates comparable to state-of-
the-art (SOTA) artificial neural network (ANN) methods.
On bimanual manipulation in RoboTwin 2.0 [4], it reaches
a mean success rate of 80.20% without pretraining on robot
data. On LIBERO, it needs only 1.467 mJ per plan includ-
ing the perception front end, which is more than an order
of magnitude below every compared method (Fig. 1). On
a real robot arm, SpikingWAM completes a pick and place
task given by a language instruction, with a success rate
comparable to ANN VLAs and only 3.07 mJ per plan. Our
main contributions are as follows.
• We design TSS and a small spiking perception front end
that estimates its object keypoints from images. They fo-
cus the events and attention of the policy on the parts that
matter for the task, which lowers the energy of each plan.
• We propose BFSC and GCA to recover the millimetre
precision that integer spikes lose. We propose PSR and
an offline check that tells when it applies, so the network
runs only once per plan.
• We propose a sparse and event-driven paradigm for spik-
ing WAMs and build on it SpikingWAM, the first spik-
ing world action model. It reaches 98.25%, 88.76% and
80.20% on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0.
On LIBERO-Plus, it exceeds all compared methods.
2. Related Work
2.1. World Action Models and Fast Sampling
VLA models generate actions directly from camera images
and language instructions [2, 11, 14], and compact designs
further reduce the inference cost [42]. World action models
build on this by adding future prediction, so that the model
also learns how the scene evolves. Mainstream approaches
build on pretrained video models and generate future frames
together with actions [15, 18, 48], which makes them large.
Recent work steadily reduces the cost of the world state.
VLA-JEPA [32] replaces pixel reconstruction with visual
latents. Fast-WAM [49] shows that the benefit of future
prediction comes mainly from joint training, so future gen-
eration can be skipped at inference. SkeleWAM [29] rep-
resents the world state as a 3D skeleton and compresses
the scene into a few keypoints. In parallel, policy distil-
lation [26, 40] and mean flows [6, 30] compress action gen-
eration into one or a few steps. However, while these works
shrink the model and the sampling steps, the computation
itself still consists of dense multiply and accumulate oper-
ations. Repeated planning in closed loop control thus still
costs too much energy on board.
2.2. SNNs and Spiking Embodied Models
Spiking Transformers allow SNNs to handle vision tasks at
a large scale. Spiking self-attention [52], spike-driven at-
tention [44, 45] and integer training [46] have steadily im-
proved their scale and accuracy. This progress has carried
SNNs into embodied control along two lines. One line gen-
erates actions. Spiking VLAs [31, 38] and spiking diffu-
sion policies [10, 35] have been applied to navigation and
manipulation.
The other line predicts states.
A spiking
world model [33] learns the next state after a given action
for model-based reinforcement learning. A separate net-
work produces the actions. However, the two lines have
developed separately. Action generation and scene predic-
tion have not been unified in one spiking network, so world
action models do not yet use event-driven computation.

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and energy.
LIBERO [21] tests fine manip-
ulation with 40 tasks in four suites, and each task is evalu-
ated for 50 episodes. LIBERO-Plus [5] applies seven kinds
of perturbation to LIBERO in 10,030 variants and tests ro-
bustness under perturbations. RoboTwin 2.0 [4] tests bi-
manual manipulation with 50 tasks. Its Easy setting uses
5


clean scenes, and its Hard setting adds scene randomiza-
tion. The evaluation protocols are in Sec. F. We estimate the
mean energy per plan for a 45 nm process [9], and the per-
ception front end is counted as well. Each accumulate in a
layer that reads spikes costs ESOP = 0.9 pJ, and each mul-
tiply and accumulate in a layer that reads continuous values
costs EMAC = 4.6 pJ (Sec. E). For other methods, we es-
timate one forward pass that generates one action chunk,
image encoding included. Their FLOPs come from Spike-
driven VLA [38] or from their public architectures.
Model.
The perception front end and the policy are
trained separately on keypoints from the simulator state,
and are connected directly at evaluation (Sec. B). The train-
ing images of the front end are randomized procedurally
and use no LIBERO-Plus assets. LIBERO and LIBERO-
Plus share one model trained on LIBERO demonstrations,
which reads out with PSR. Like the compared methods, the
RoboTwin 2.0 model uses 50 clean and about 500 random-
ized demonstrations per task. It reads out with the medoid
chunk of N = 10 steps and k = 3 samples (Sec. 5.3). The
architecture and training are in Sec. C.
Compared methods.
We divide the compared methods
into three groups.
The first group contains ANN VLAs
and WAMs with more than 1B parameters, such as π0 [2],
π0.5 [11], Fast-WAM [49] and Cosmos-Policy [15]. The
second group contains lightweight ANN VLAs with fewer
than 1B parameters, such as TurboVLA [42]. The third
group is the spiking VLA Spike-driven VLA [38]. Their re-
sults and parameter counts are taken from the cited papers.
The main results of SkeleWAM use keypoints from the sim-
ulator state as input. The comparison with SpikingWAM in
this setting is in Sec. G, and the energy of SkeleWAM is
computed on our reproduction (Sec. E).

state, extends to bimanual manipulation and keeps compet-
itive robustness under scene randomization.

Coordinate precision.
Encoding coordinates with integer
spikes loses precision, and the loss happens in the input
layer where coordinates are written into spikes (Sec. A). We
read the coordinates back linearly from the spikes of this
layer. With BFSC, the error of absolute coordinates comes
close to that of an ANN model with the same architecture
and continuous input, and the error of relative coordinates
is only 3.7 to 4.8 mm. Without BFSC, the error of abso-
lute coordinates grows to 17 to 24 mm. This shows that
BFSC recovers the loss from encoding coordinates with in-
teger spikes, so the SNN keeps the millimetre precision that
fine manipulation needs.
When the one-step readout applies.
The one-step read-
out gives the conditional mean of the action chunk. This
mean is an executable action only when it lies among the
model’s own samples. We check this condition offline with
a ratio. It divides the distance from the mean to its nearest
sample by the nearest neighbour distance among the sam-
ples (Sec. D). The ratio is at most 1.00 on every LIBERO
task and at least 2.00 on every RoboTwin 2.0 task, so
RoboTwin 2.0 reads out with the medoid chunk of 10 steps.
Closed loop results agree with the check.

5.4. Real Robot Experiments
To test whether SpikingWAM also works in real scenes, we
run a pick and place task on an SO-101 arm. The instruc-
tion is “Put the green cube into the left cup.” The object
keypoints are estimated from camera images by spiking net-
works. SpikingWAM, X-VLA [50] and π0.5 [11] are trained
on the same demonstrations, and each model runs 10 tri-
als (Sec. I). The success rate of SpikingWAM lies between
those of the two models, and its energy, inference time and
memory are far lower (Tab. 3). SpikingWAM thus performs
pick and place efficiently on a real robot (Fig. 6).

6. Conclusion
We proposed a sparse and event-driven paradigm for
spiking world action models and built on it SpikingWAM,
the first spiking world action model. TSS uses only the
keypoints of the arm and the task objects as the world
state, and BFSC and GCA keep its millimetre precision
in integer spikes.
PSR runs the network once per plan
and passes only the changes between plans. On simulated
benchmarks, SpikingWAM achieves competitive success
rates with far less energy than ANN methods, and it also
works on a real robot arm. Our results show that the choice
of world state is key to spiking WAMs. A task skeleton
lets the network spend its events on the arm and the task
8


objects, so its sparsity translates into low energy.
We
hope this paradigm helps bring WAMs to neuromorphic
hardware and advances low power embodied intelligence.

With images as

spacing. GCA obtains relative geometry directly from the
coincidence of their spike codes.
Readout.
In Eq. (A.1), the factor kN multiplies the whole
energy. With N = 10 integration steps and the medoid of
k = 3 samples, each plan runs the network 30 times, and
95% of the energy is in the action branch (Sec. E). Incre-
mental firing can reuse what stays the same between consec-
utive integration steps of one sample. However, each plan
draws new noise, so the whole input of the action branch
changes, and nothing can be reused between consecutive
plans. PSR reduces the readout to one step and one sample
and then fixes the starting noise, so only changes pass be-
tween consecutive plans. Table 5 gives the energy of each

B. Perception Front End
The perception front end Φ of LIBERO (Eq. (4)) estimates
the positions of the task object nodes from the third-person
and wrist RGB images. The arm nodes still come from for-
ward kinematics.
Architecture.
Both images are resized to 160 × 160 and
encoded by one shared spiking convolutional backbone.
Convolutions and depthwise separable residual blocks bring
the resolution down to 1/16 step by step. Two spiking self-
attention layers [52] run on top of it. Their output is upsam-
pled and merged with the 1/8 features, which gives a 20×20
feature map for each view. All layers use D = 4 and fire
only non-negative spikes. The first convolution reads pix-
els, and all other layers read only spikes. The front end has
12


3.46M parameters.
Queries and readout.
Each object node has a query vec-
tor. It is the sum of the embeddings of the descriptor ιj and
of the object class, plus a linear map of the sentence vector
zℓ, the gripper position and opening, and the camera matrix
of the view. The object class comes from the task defini-
tion, and the camera matrix holds the known intrinsics and
extrinsics. The query takes an inner product with every cell
of the feature map. Two spiking fully connected layers then
read out three distributions over one axis each. They cover
the horizontal pixel coordinate, the vertical pixel coordinate
and the height of the node, which is coordinate classifica-
tion [17]. We take the expectation of each distribution and
project the pixel along its camera ray onto the horizontal
plane at this height. This gives the position in the robot
frame. The positions from the two views are fused with
weights given by the softmax of their confidences.
Training.
The training images come from rendering all
LIBERO demonstrations again with the renderer of the
closed loop evaluation. The node positions in the simulator
state serve as labels. Besides the original scenes, three more
groups of images replace the textures of the table, floor and
walls, change the lights and turn the third-person camera.
They also move the objects on the table that are not near
the gripper at random, which follows domain randomiza-
tion [34]. All textures are generated procedurally, and no
LIBERO-Plus assets are used. During training, half of the
third-person images get blur, fog or sensor noise, and in-
structions are rewritten from a word list. The last 10% of
the demonstrations of each task are held out for validation,
and the training set has 627k frames. The loss sums three
terms. They are the cross entropy of the three distributions
against Gaussian soft labels, the L1 errors of the positions
from each view and after fusion, and the binary cross en-
tropy of the confidence. We train with AdamW, learning
rate 10−3 with cosine decay and batch size 128 for 200k
steps. The front end is trained alone, and the policy is not
fine-tuned on estimated skeletons.

C. Model Details
Nodes.
On LIBERO, the arm has 11 nodes, namely the
origins of Panda links 1 to 7 and of the hand, the grip site
and the two fingertips. On RoboTwin 2.0, each of the two
arms has 10 nodes, namely the origins of the base link and
13


of links 1 to 6, the grip site and the two fingertips. Each
task object adds a group of nodes. They are its centre, the
centres of its top face and four side faces, which rotate with
the object, its functional points, and the centres of moving
parts such as drawers, doors and knobs. RoboTwin 2.0 also
has contact points, target points and orientation points. Co-
ordinates are in the robot frame. On RoboTwin 2.0 they are
in the world frame that holds the bases of both arms. The
descriptor ιj is the sum of three embeddings, for the node
index, the role and the slot of the object. The same index
means the same part in every episode. The future skeleton
has one token per frame.
Actions.
On LIBERO, the action is the change of the end
effector pose plus the gripper command. On RoboTwin 2.0,
the action holds the joint angles and grippers of both arms.
The joint angles are written as offsets from the current joint
angles qt, and qt is added back outside the network. On
RoboTwin 2.0, each link node of the arms has one more
channel with the measured angle of its joint. The network
thus sees the posture to which the action chunk is relative.
Hyperparameters.
Spiking neurons use D = 4, and the
coordinate input layer uses Din = 16. The initial thresh-
old of SN Din is scaled by D/Din, so the range stays the
same while the number of levels grows. The length scale
of relative coordinates is ρ = 0.2 m, and in bimanual tasks
each hand gives its own relative coordinates. The grid spike
code has 6 directions and 12 scales, which start at 1 cm and
grow by a factor of 1.5. Each ring has 8 phase neurons, so
the code has 72 rings. Rings with small scales resolve mil-
limetres, and rings with large scales tell near from far. The
offset δh of each GCA head starts from a normal distribu-
tion with a standard deviation of 3 cm, and the gain κ starts
at 0. In bimanual tasks, each hand takes half of the GCA
heads. Shiftmax [37] rounds attention scores to multiples
of 1/4 and rounds the denominator to a power of 2.

E. Energy Counting
Energy model and counting rules.
Each accumulate in
a layer that reads spikes costs ESOP = 0.9 pJ, and each
MAC in a layer that reads continuous values costs EMAC =
4.6 pJ, for a 45 nm process [9]. Weight layers that read only
spikes are counted by the accumulates that unit spikes trig-
ger. Layers that read continuous values, layer normaliza-
tion and adaLN modulation are counted as MACs. The pol-
icy and the perception front end both run with incremental
firing (Eq. (12)), so a layer accumulates only the weight
columns of the spikes that changed. The first convolution
of the front end reads pixels and is counted as MACs. GCA
follows the same rule. Each query spike performs one AND
with each key, and each coincidence adds one gain in each
layer. Each of these counts as one accumulate, and together
they cost 2.18 µJ per plan. Energy is the mean per plan. On
LIBERO it is averaged over 446 plans on the demonstration
trajectories of the 40 tasks, with object keypoints from the
perception front end. On RoboTwin 2.0, the policy is av-
eraged over 708 plans on the demonstration trajectories of
the 50 tasks, and the perception front end over 2,150 plans
of closed-loop episodes. The instruction is encoded only
once per episode. The spiking MiniLM needs 0.578 mJ, and
the text keys and values of the action expert need another
21.7 µJ. This part is not counted in the energy per plan.

F. Evaluation Protocol
Each of the four LIBERO suites has 10 tasks. Each task runs
one episode on each of its 50 official initial states, which
gives 2,000 episodes. The seven perturbations of LIBERO-
Plus are camera, robot initial state, language, lighting, back-
ground, sensor noise and object layout, and each variant
runs one episode. RoboTwin 2.0 runs 100 seeds per task
on both Easy and Hard, and none of the evaluation instruc-
tions appear in training. The policy uses the EMA weights
at the end of training.
Paired episodes.
Two models are compared episode by
episode on the same initial states or seeds. We write the
result as a : b, where a counts the episodes in which only the
first model succeeds and b those in which only the second
succeeds.
G. Comparison with SkeleWAM
The main results of SkeleWAM [29] take object keypoints
from the simulator state (Sec. 5.1). We therefore compare
it with SpikingWAM §, whose object keypoints also come
from the simulator state (Tab. G.1). The two have similar
parameter counts. SpikingWAM § is 2.71 points higher in
total. The gain comes mainly from robot initial state and
layout, by 13.3 and 17.8 points, and SpikingWAM § is 11.4
points lower on language. With the perception front end,
SpikingWAM reaches 88.76% on LIBERO-Plus (Tab. 1),
still above SkeleWAM §.

I. Real Robot Experiments
Platform and task.
The experiments run on an SO-101
arm. The table holds four cubes of different colours and two
transparent cups, and the instruction is “Put the green cube
into the left cup.” All three models use the same 324 teleop-
erated demonstrations. X-VLA [50] and π0.5 [11] are fine-
tuned from their public weights. SpikingWAM is trained
from scratch with the same policy architecture as in simu-
lation, and it reads out with the medoid chunk of 5 steps
(k = 3).
Keypoints.
The perception front end (0.81M parameters)
estimates the cube nodes at every plan. A fine-tuned SDT-
V3 [46] estimates the cup nodes once at the start of the
episode. The label of a cup node is the gripper position
at release in the demonstrations. On 18 held out demonstra-
tions, the median error of the cube nodes at the grasp time
is 0.32 cm.
Measuring cost.
The energy of SpikingWAM is counted
with the rules of Sec. E over all plans of the 10 trials, in-
cluding the perception front end. The energy of locating
the cups and of reading the scene at the start of an episode
is spread over the plans. The energy of X-VLA and π0.5
is estimated with the rules of Sec. 5.1, and image encod-
ing counts the image streams that each model actually pro-
cesses. All three models run on the same H100 in BF16
with batch size 1, and X-VLA and π0.5 use their official
inference code. Inference time is the mean time of one in-
ference over 18 validation demonstrations, including pre-
processing and excluding network transfer. Peak memory
covers loading and all inference.
