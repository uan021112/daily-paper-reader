Figure 1: Teaser. We propose a Multi-Rate Flow Matching (MRFM) for point-cloud upsampling. Given the same
sparse input, MRFM adaptively allocates more integration steps to difficult local regions, leading to lower global and
local reconstruction errors than uniform-step inference while better preserving fine geometric details.
Abstract
Flow-matching point-cloud upsamplers refine sparse inputs into dense point clouds by integrating a learned
velocity field with a fixed temporal discretization. However, local reconstruction requirements vary substantially
across a shape, while a shared solver budget applies the same temporal resolution everywhere. We propose
Multi-Rate Flow Matching (MRFM), a patch-adaptive framework that selects an integration budget for each
local patch at inference time while keeping the learned velocity field frozen.
MRFM relaxes the discrete
budget into a continuous variable, optimizes it online through differentiable soft rollouts using an input-
derived geometric fidelity objective, and projects the result back to a discrete budget for the final solver rollout.
Experiments on PU1K and PUGAN show strong reconstruction quality, while matched-budget permutation
and multi-factor analyses demonstrate that the benefit depends on patch-specific allocation rather than on the
step distribution alone. MRFM is accuracy-oriented and intentionally uses additional test-time optimization
to obtain more suitable local temporal resolutions.

Introduction
Point-cloud upsampling aims to recover dense, uni-
form, and geometrically faithful point sets from sparse
observations.
Sparse, noisy, and non-uniform point
clouds are the default output of most 3D sensors,
yet downstream tasks assume denser, more regular
geometry: robotic mapping and SLAM need locally
complete surfaces for reliable registration, LiDAR-
based 3D detection for autonomous driving degrades
on sparse or occluded returns, and AR/VR rendering
needs smooth, hole-free surfaces [5], [14], [10].
The
same sparse-to-dense problem also arises beyond 3D
vision, for instance in sparse-view medical surface re-
construction or physical-field reconstruction from a
limited sensor array.
The problem is therefore not
only to add points, but to infer plausible local geom-
etry while preserving the structure the sparse obser-
vation already supports, particularly around bound-
aries, thin parts, and unevenly sampled regions where
small local errors noticeably affect the reconstructed
surface.
Early learned upsamplers such as PU-Net and its
successors [19] expand sparse points in a learned fea-
ture space and substantially improve point distribu-
tion and geometric fidelity, but do so through one-shot
or staged expansion rather than an explicit refinement
trajectory. More recent methods instead treat upsam-
pling as iterative or generative refinement – optimizing
point coordinates, denoising, or transporting a learned
flow – with PUFM [10] and PUFM++ [9] represent-
ing the current state of the art for flow-matching-based
upsampling. These flow-based methods make the tem-
poral discretization of the learned trajectory an ex-
plicit design choice, which motivates asking whether
the same discretization is equally appropriate for ev-
ery local region.
Local reconstruction difficulty, however, varies sub-
stantially within a single shape – smooth,
well-
observed regions may need only a coarse trajectory,
while boundaries, thin structures, and sparsely sam-
pled neighborhoods require finer refinement – yet a
shared solver budget applies the same temporal reso-
lution everywhere. The closest adaptive mechanisms
address a different axis of this problem: PUFM++
redistributes a shared global time grid [9], Grad-PU
applies gradients to point coordinates rather than the
solver budget [4], PatchScaler groups image patches
by diffusion difficulty [8], and TG-MSFM gates multi-
scale velocity components over time [16], but none
of them selects a patch-specific flow-matching budget
from the response of a frozen point-cloud upsampler.
Even a spatially varying budget is not simply recov-
erable from the input geometry alone: curvature or
density are informative descriptors, but they do not
measure how the learned flow actually behaves on a
particular patch, or whether that patch would benefit
from additional refinement.
To address this gap, we propose Multi-Rate Flow
Matching (MRFM), which assigns an individual in-
tegration budget to each local region.
Inspired by
gradient-guided generative optimization [2], MRFM
relaxes the discrete budget into a continuous rate and
optimizes it through differentiable soft rollouts using
an input-derived geometric fidelity objective.
The optimized rate is projected to an admissible dis-
crete budget, and the final dense patch is recomputed
with the standard flow solver. The velocity field re-
mains fixed throughout. MRFM therefore uses addi-
tional test-time optimization to obtain patch-specific
temporal resolutions and is designed for reconstruc-
tion accuracy rather than inference acceleration. The
overall pipeline and the inference-time optimization
loop are summarized in Figure 2.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
1. We introduce a patch-wise multi-rate formulation
for flow-matching point-cloud upsampling, allow-
ing local regions to use different integration bud-
gets while sharing the same velocity model.
2. We develop a gradient-guided test-time rate opti-
mization strategy that selects patch-specific bud-
gets from the response of the frozen flow model,
without a separately trained router or a hand-
crafted allocation rule.
3. We
evaluate
MRFM
on
PU1K
and
PU-
GAN against state-of-the-art point-cloud upsam-
pling methods, assessing reconstruction quality
and computational cost.
Controlled budget-
permutation experiments and patch-level analy-
ses further examine the benefit of spatial budget
allocation and its relationship to reconstruction
difficulty.

Related work
2.1
Point cloud upsampling
Early learned upsamplers expand sparse points in
a learned feature space and then generate new co-
ordinates.
PU-Net established this paradigm [19],
PU-GAN improved point uniformity with adversar-
ial training [5], 3PU progressively upsamples local
patches [18], PUGeo-Net emphasizes geometry-centric
reconstruction [12], and PU-Transformer introduces
self-attention [13]. Although their architectures dif-
fer, these methods primarily learn how to represent
local geometry and how to expand sparse features
into denser coordinates. Their computation is there-
fore largely determined by the network design rather
than by a patch-specific numerical refinement pro-
cess.
More recent methods increasingly use itera-
tive generative refinement: Grad-PU follows gradients
of a learned distance field [4], PUDM and PDANS
employ conditional diffusion [14], [20], and PUFM /
PUFM++ use flow matching [10], [9]. In these meth-
ods, reconstruction is expressed as a sequence of up-
dates, making the number and placement of updates

2.2
Flow matching and adaptive inte-
gration
Flow matching and rectified flow learn continuous ve-
locity fields along simple probability paths [6], [7].
In point-cloud upsampling, PUFM uses EMD pre-
alignment to obtain a short transport trajectory [10],
while PUFM++ adapts the placement of evaluations
on a shared inference time grid [9]. These approaches
improve the global transport path or its temporal sam-
pling, but the same schedule remains shared by the full
shape. Classical adaptive ODE solvers instead control
numerical truncation error [3]; this criterion is differ-
ent from selecting computation according to recon-
struction behavior, because local numerical residual
and patch-level reconstruction quality need not coin-
cide. Gradient-guided diffusion optimization is closer
in spirit because it modifies an inference process using
an external objective without retraining the generator
[2]. MRFM applies this optimization view to the tem-
poral resolution of each patch while keeping vθ frozen,
so the learned transport itself is unchanged and only
its local discretization is adapted.
2.3
Multi-scale
and
patch-adaptive
generative models
Adaptive or multi-scale computation has also ap-
peared outside point-cloud upsampling.
TG-MSFM
blends multi-scale velocity components with a time-
dependent gate for time-series imputation, but the
gating
is
global
rather
than
patch-specific
[16].
MLPCM organizes 3D generation across point- and
super-point latent resolutions and uses consistency
distillation for fast sampling [1], while LaS-Comp cou-
ples latent and spatial consistency for zero-shot 3D
completion [17].
PatchScaler is the closest compu-
tational analogue:
it groups image patches by es-
timated reconstruction difficulty and assigns differ-
ent diffusion sampling configurations [8]. These ap-
proaches motivate adaptive computation at different
scales, whereas MRFM specifically performs test-time,
patch-wise solver-budget selection for flow-matching
point-cloud upsampling.
3
Method
The overall framework is illustrated in Figure 2. Given
a sparse point cloud, we first decompose it into over-
lapping local patches and expand each patch to the
target cardinality using a deterministic interpolation
operator. The expanded patches are then refined by a
shared rate-conditioned flow model. Instead of using
one uniform integration budget for all regions, MRFM
optimizes a patch-specific continuous rate through
gradient guidance at inference time. The optimized
rates are projected to discrete budgets, after which the
corresponding flow trajectories are recomputed and
merged into the final dense point cloud.

3.2
Patch-level rate selection
Local reconstruction requirements are heterogeneous:
smooth, well-observed regions may need coarse tra-
jectories, whereas boundaries, thin structures, sparse
neighborhoods, or ambiguous geometry may benefit
from finer resolution.
We therefore define difficulty
operationally through the response of the frozen flow
model rather than a single predefined geometric statis-
tic. Curvature or density can describe the input ge-
ometry, but neither directly measures whether the
learned flow would benefit from additional refinement.
MRFM instead adjusts each test-patch rate according
to consistency between its generated dense patch and
observed sparse geometry, coupling allocation to both
input geometry and model-realized reconstruction be-
havior.
This distinction is important because the selected
budget is not treated as a direct geometric label. Two
patches with similar curvature or density can still re-
ceive different budgets when the frozen model pro-
duces different reconstruction responses. Conversely,
a geometrically complex patch need not receive the
maximum budget if its current trajectory is already
consistent with the observed support. The rate there-
fore reflects the interaction between local geometry
and the behavior of the trained flow model.

4
Experiments
4.1
Experiment Setup
Dataset. We evaluate on the PUGAN [5] and PU1K
[11] benchmarks, which are widely used for evaluating
learned point-cloud upsampling methods. We use the
official training splits and sparse test inputs contain-
ing 256 points, and report 4× and 8× upsampling,
with 16× included as an additional evaluation setting
in Table 1. Evaluating on both datasets allows us to
examine the same rate-selection mechanism under dif-
ferent shape collections and output cardinalities with-
out changing the underlying experimental protocol.
Evaluation Metrics.
Reconstruction quality is
measured by Chamfer Distance (CD), Hausdorff Dis-
tance (HD), Point-to-Surface Distance (P2F), and
Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD), following standard
point-cloud upsampling protocols [5], [11], [10]. CD
measures average set-level proximity, HD emphasizes
the largest geometric discrepancy, P2F evaluates con-
sistency with the underlying surface, and JSD reflects
distributional agreement. We report the same metric
definitions and scaling for all compared methods.
Implementation Details. The model is trained
for 40 epochs with AdamW, batch size 64, and an
initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4. All experiments are
conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti
GPU with 11 GB memory.

Multi-Factor Allocation Analysis. To examine
what is associated with MRFM’s patch-wise alloca-
tion, we select 500 patches with diverse local struc-

pute Spearman ρ between the predicted steps and sev-

under a common 8-step budget. The fixed-budget er-
rors provide a common reference because every patch
is evaluated with the same solver budget before corre-
lation is measured.
Random permutations preserve
the same step set, mean budget, and total Patch-
NFE, providing a control for correlations that could
arise from the marginal step distribution alone. As
shown in Table 2, CD@8 has the strongest association
with MRFM steps (ρ = 0.4812), followed by curva-
ture (ρ = 0.3161) and sparsity (ρ = 0.2575). P2F@8 is
weaker (ρ = 0.1412), while surface non-uniformity has
only a limited association (ρ = 0.0602). In contrast,
the random-permutation correlations remain near zero
across all factors. The different correlation strengths
indicate that the allocation cannot be reduced to a sin-
gle geometric rule: static properties such as curvature
and sparsity are informative, but the strongest associ-
ation is observed for the reconstruction error realized
by the frozen flow under a common budget. Together,
these results support a multi-factor interpretation of
the predicted rates, in which geometry and model be-
havior are both reflected in the patch-specific alloca-
tion.
Taken together, the matched-budget permutation
and multi-factor analyses provide two complementary
views of the same behavior.
The permutation ex-
periment shows that preserving the step distribution
while destroying the patch-to-budget correspondence
reduces reconstruction quality, whereas the correla-
tion analysis shows that the original correspondence is
structured rather than random. MRFM therefore ben-
efits from the assignment itself, and that assignment
reflects several complementary patch properties rather
than a single geometric heuristic. Together with the
permutation control, they support interpreting the se-
lected rate through the response of the trained model.

Conclusion
We presented Multi-Rate Flow Matching (MRFM),
a patch-adaptive framework for point-cloud upsam-
pling that selects local integration budgets through
gradient-guided optimization of a frozen flow model.
Experiments on PU1K and PUGAN show strong
reconstruction
quality,
while
controlled
ablations
demonstrate that gains arise from patch-specific allo-
cation rather than the step distribution alone. The se-
lected rates reflect both geometric and model-realized
difficulty. MRFM uses additional test-time optimiza-
tion and is therefore accuracy-oriented rather than
an acceleration method.
Its sparse-coverage, local-
surface, and spacing objectives guide budget selection
without modifying the frozen velocity model, while fi-
nal predictions are recomputed using discrete budgets.
The central distinction is between adapting a shared
temporal schedule and adapting the amount of refine-
ment assigned to individual spatial regions. MRFM
uses the response of the frozen model to select the
latter, rather than treating curvature or density as a
direct prescription for computation. The continuous
relaxation makes this selection differentiable, while
projection and a restarted discrete rollout preserve a
conventional final integration procedure. The result-
ing mechanism changes the local numerical resolution
without requiring a separately trained allocation net-
work or a gradient update to the final point coordi-
nates.
The matched-budget permutation experiment is
particularly informative in this setting.
Preserving
the same collection of step counts while changing
their spatial assignment reduces reconstruction qual-
ity, showing that heterogeneous budgets alone do not
explain the improvement.
The patch-level analysis
complements this result: geometric factors are asso-
ciated with the selected rates, but their correlations
differ, and fixed-budget reconstruction error provides
the strongest association among the evaluated fac-
tors. These observations support interpreting alloca-
tion through the interaction between observed geom-
etry and the behavior of the learned flow.
Soft rollouts and online optimization add cost, and
integer projection remains approximate. Local planes
can be unreliable near sharp edges or degenerate
neighborhoods. Future work should reduce guidance
overhead and improve geometric reliability.

B.3
Downstream Application
We also evaluate the upsampling performance of our method on real-world data. We test MRFM together with
two strong flow-matching baselines, PUFM and PUFM++, on ScanNet. We report Chamfer Distance (CD,
10−3) as the quantitative metric. PUFM obtains a CD of 1.4490, PUFM++ improves slightly to 1.2305, while
MRFM achieves the best result of 1.2112. The qualitative comparison is shown in Figure 6.
Figure 6: Qualitative comparison on ScanNet.
ScanNet presents a substantially harder upsampling setting than the synthetic benchmarks because the sparse
observations are affected by missing regions, non-uniform density, measurement noise, and complex indoor
geometry. Although the CD differences among the three methods are relatively modest, the qualitative results
reveal a clearer distinction. PUFM and PUFM++ recover the overall scene geometry but tend to lose or smooth
fine local structures in difficult regions. In contrast, MRFM reconstructs more complete and visually detailed
geometry, indicating that its patch-adaptive refinement remains beneficial under real-world sparsity and noise.
These results suggest that the advantage of MRFM is not limited to synthetic test shapes and can transfer to
more challenging real-world point-cloud observations.
