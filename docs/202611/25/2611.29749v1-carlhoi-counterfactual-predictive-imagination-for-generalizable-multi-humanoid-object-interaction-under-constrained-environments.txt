Abstract
Recent advances in physics-based humanoid control have
enabled teams of varying sizes to coordinate their move-
ments and cooperatively transport shared objects. How-
ever, adapting learned cooperative behaviors to unseen en-
vironmental constraints remains challenging, particularly
when obstacles require coordinated detours and changes in
team configuration. To address this challenge, we propose
CarlHOI, a counterfactual predictive imagination frame-
work built upon a counterfactual-anchored relational la-
tent world model (CarlWM) for human-object interaction
(HOI), enabling multi-humanoid teams to adapt to unseen
environmental constraints without retraining their underly-
ing cooperative control policies. CarlHOI explicitly rep-
resents the shared object, individual humanoids, and envi-
ronmental obstacles as interacting entities. Trained offline
using counterfactual simulator rollouts, the model imag-

ines alternative collective futures under different shared
waypoint commands, predicting how the coupled humanoid
team and object would respond. By evaluating these imag-
ined outcomes through predicted progress, failure, and con-
tact risks, and long-horizon value estimates, CarlHOI se-
lects adaptive guidance commands while preserving the
pretrained cooperative controller. We introduce a bench-
mark comprising three challenging obstacle-constrained
transport tasks to evaluate adaptation and generalization.
Despite being trained exclusively with four humanoids, our
world model generalizes without retraining to teams of two
to ten humanoids, unseen obstacle geometries and place-
ments, unseen objects, and novel combinations of environ-
mental constraints. Extensive experiments demonstrate im-
proved cooperative transport success over evaluated base-
lines across diverse configurations. These results highlight
the potential of counterfactual predictive imagination to ex-
tend pretrained multi-humanoid cooperative skills beyond
their original training conditions.

1. Introduction
Physics-based human-object interaction (HOI) requires co-
ordinating body motion, contact, and object dynamics [8,
15, 28, 29]. In cooperative transport, the shared object fur-
ther couples the humanoids’ movements: each agent must
respond to both the load and its teammates. Recent ap-
proaches have enabled such coordination across different
objects and team sizes [3, 5, 11, 14]. However, constrained
environments require the team to adapt both its route and
its configuration around the object [1, 16, 19]. Narrow pas-
sages may require humanoids to reposition, while blocking
walls demand coordinated detours. These decisions must
account for the entire team. For example, a collision-free
path for the object may still cause a humanoid to collide
with obstacles. We therefore study whether pretrained co-
operative transport skills can meet these demands through
shared temporary waypoints, avoiding repeated policy re-
training for new layouts and team sizes. The central chal-
lenge is to predict how each waypoint affects the coupled
team and object.
Hierarchical skill reuse [13, 17, 18, 21, 23, 25] and pre-
dictive planning with world models [6, 7, 33] provide a ba-
sis for this adaptation. However, adapting cooperative trans-
port to constrained environments remains challenging, as
alternative waypoint commands can affect not only object
motion but also the collective behavior of the humanoid
team.
Effective guidance must therefore consider object
progress alongside team-level obstacle contacts and fail-
ure risks.
To address this challenge, we introduce Carl-
HOI, a counterfactual predictive imagination framework
built on a relational world model, CarlWM. CarlWM rep-
resents humanoids, the carried object, and obstacles as in-
teracting entities and learns from counterfactual simulator
rollouts to imagine how alternative waypoint commands af-
fect the coupled team and object. Counterfactual anchor-
ing expresses command effects relative to unchanged guid-
ance. CarlHOI then selects shared waypoints using pre-
dicted progress, failure and contact risks, and long-horizon
value estimates, while the frozen transport policy coordi-
nates individual movements.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Counterfactual predictive guidance for cooperative
transport. We propose CarlHOI and its relational world
model, CarlWM, to predict and compare collective re-
sponses to alternative shared waypoints.
Counterfac-
tual anchoring, branched-rollout supervision, and long-
horizon value estimation enable adaptive guidance of
frozen transport policies.
• A benchmark for constrained multi-humanoid trans-
port.
We introduce narrow-passage, wall-detour, and
multi-obstacle tasks that require coordinated transport un-
der environmental constraints, with matched initial states
and a common arrival criterion for systematic evaluation.
• Zero-shot generalization to unseen teams, objects, and
controllers. Trained with four humanoids, one object,
and one controller, CarlWM transfers without retraining
to teams of 2-10, unseen objects, controllers, and obsta-
cle configurations, improving transport success over eval-
uated baselines.
2. Related Work
Physics-based HOI and cooperative transport.
Motion
imitation and adversarial motion priors underpin realistic
humanoid control [20, 22, 27, 31].
PhysHOI and Inter-
Mimic extend imitation to whole-body interactions [28, 30],
while Catch & Carry demonstrates reusable manipulation
controllers [17]. For cooperative transport, CooHOI learns
coordination through shared object dynamics [5], while


TeamHOI uses teammate tokens and a masked motion prior
to support varying team sizes [14].
The decMHT fur-
ther addresses decentralized pickup and transport through
object-centric control [18]. In mobile manipulation, kin-
odynamic planners jointly account for robot motion, ob-
ject transport, and obstacle constraints [19].
However,
adapting learned skills to environments that require coor-
dinated changes in both route and formation remains chal-
lenging without retraining the transport policy. CarlHOI ad-
dresses this challenge by guiding pretrained transport poli-
cies through shared waypoints without retraining.
World models and relational prediction.
TD-MPC and
TD-MPC2 combine latent dynamics with learned termi-
nal values for predictive control [6, 7].
SkiMo extends
planning to skill-level dynamics [26], while Freed et al.
learn skills and temporally abstract world models from of-
fline data [4]. For multi-agent cooperation, SeqWM uses
sequential agent-wise predictions [33], and COMBO pre-
dicts joint action outcomes with compositional generative
models [32]. Relational models represent physical systems
through entities and their interactions, supporting structured
dynamics prediction [2, 9, 10, 12, 24].
For cooperative
transport, a key challenge is to evaluate how alternative
shared waypoints affect the coupled motion of humanoids
and their carried object. CarlWM addresses this challenge
through entity-based encoding that captures their collective
response to waypoint commands. It learns from counter-
factual rollouts starting at matched simulator states and an-
chors command effects to unchanged guidance, enabling
predictions that support the comparison of candidate way-
points.
3. Problem Formulation
A team of n humanoids must lift an object and carry it to a
goal g ∈R2 through an obstacle-constrained environment.
An episode succeeds if the object’s center pt comes within
a tolerance of g before the time limit, without a humanoid
falling or the object being dropped.
Each humanoid executes a shared pretrained transport
policy π, conditioned on a shared goal, to generate its
joint actions.
We keep π frozen and influence the team
only through the goal it observes. At decision times k =
0, 1, . . . , a planner replaces g with a temporary aim point

(θk, σk) ∈A,

where θk and σk specify the rotation and rescaling of the
goal direction, respectively. The selected command is held
fixed until the next decision.
The planner thus decides
where the team goes, and the frozen policy decides how it
carries the object there.
The planner’s objective is to maximize the expected re-
turn of the per-step reward

where dt = ∥pt −g∥is the distance to the true goal, ft
and ct indicate a failure and a wall contact, and wf, wc >
0. The predictive model is trained under limited conditions
and must generalize without retraining to unseen team sizes,
obstacles, objects, and transport policies.
4. The CarlHOI Framework
CarlHOI instantiates the planner of Sec. 3 with a discrete
command library and a learned world model.
Command library.
CarlHOI restricts the admissible set
A = [−45◦, 45◦] × [0.5, 1.5] to a library U of nine com-
mands u = (θ, σ): the unchanged goal u0 = (0◦, 1), rota-
tions of ±15◦, ±30◦and ±45◦, and rescalings by 0.5 and
1.5. CarlHOI selects a command every ∆= 0.5 s; the aim
distance (1) of every command except u0 is clipped to 0.5–
9 m, while u0 passes the true goal unchanged. CarlWM is
activated after object pickup; before that, the policy sees the
true goal. The model operates at 1/6 s intervals. We write
rτ for the reward (2) accumulated over model step τ, with
wf = 3 and wc = 0.3/H. A command is executed for
three model steps but imagined over a horizon of H = 9
steps (1.5 s).
Overview.

CarlHOI executes the command with the highest estimated

obstacles, objects and policies, as required in Sec. 3, is es-
tablished empirically (Sec. 6).

5. Experiments
We seek to answer three questions through our experiments:
• Performance comparison. In our CarlHOI framework,
does steering a frozen transport policy with CarlWM
improve its success, and how does CarlWM compare
with existing state-of-the-art world-model planners under
matched simulation budgets and model sizes?
• Generalization ability.
CarlWM is trained only with
four humanoids, one frozen policy (TeamHOI-FT), the
table object, 2.1 m gates and 1.25–1.75 m walls. Does it
transfer, without retraining, to other unseen team sizes,
obstacle geometries and placements, objects, transport
policies, and to obstacles chained into a course?
• Analysis. Which components of CarlWM matter, and
how does the team adapt its behavior to overcome ob-
stacles?
Benchmark.
We create and evaluate three cooperative
transport tasks with challenging obstacles in Isaac Gym, as
illustrated in Fig. 1. In Obstacle Wall, a single wall with a
span of 1.0–3.0 m stands across the route, and the team must
carry the object around it. In Narrow Passage, the route
leads through a gate whose gap of 2.1 or 2.0 m leaves 10 cm
or no clearance for a 2 m round table. In the 13 m Multi-
Obstacle Course, two offset walls are followed by a 2.0 m
gate, a combination never seen in training. Each task is run
with three objects: round table (2 m diameter and 78 kg)
used for training, and unseen bed and sofa (1.8 × 1.4 m and
1.8×1.2 m, both 50 kg). An episode succeeds when the ob-
ject’s center comes within 3 cm of the target. We make fair
comparisons with matched episodes.
Transport policies.
Planners act through the policy’s goal
input and never change its weights. We use frozen poli-
cies: (1) TeamHOI [14]; (2) TeamHOI-FT, fine-tuned from
TeamHOI for target arrival with four humanoids in the
2.1 m Narrow Passage and subsequently used to collect all
CarlWM training data; and (3) CooHOI [5], a separately
trained cooperative-transport policy. Planning on CooHOI
is therefore zero-shot transfer to a policy that CarlWM has
never seen.

6.3. Ablation Study
As shown in Tab. 2, we ablate four components of CarlWM:
the relational encoder, long-horizon value, counterfactual
anchoring, and ranking loss. Every component contributes.
On the passage task, removing the long-horizon value
causes the largest drop (8.8 pp), followed by removing the
ranking loss (7.3 pp), the relational encoder (4.3 pp), and
counterfactual anchoring (3.4 pp). On the course task, re-
moving counterfactual anchoring or the long-horizon value
lowers success from 55.1% to 34.8%. Removing the rela-
tional encoder and ranking loss reduces success by 5.1 and
3.1 pp, respectively.

Guidance quality and its limits.
With ten humanoids,
CarlWM raises TeamHOI-FT’s table-course success from
7.0% to 43.4% without policy updates (Tab. 1), whereas
TD-MPC2 and SeqWM reduce average success. Effective
waypoint selection can therefore recover existing coopera-
tive capabilities, but shared guidance alone does not ensure
improvement. Gains diminish or reverse for larger teams
carrying unseen objects around walls, and multi-obstacle
transport remains difficult (Fig. 3). Arrival curves still rising
at the deadline also suggest time-budget constraints (Fig. 4).
These limits motivate jointly adapting the planner and trans-
port policy to improve guidance and execution.
7. Conclusion
We presented CarlHOI, a counterfactual predictive imagi-
nation framework for guiding frozen multi-humanoid trans-
port policies through environmental constraints. Its rela-
tional world model, CarlWM, learns from counterfactual
rollouts to predict collective responses to alternative way-
point commands, enabling long-horizon guidance for co-
ordinated detours and team reformation. Across our bench-
mark of obstacle walls, narrow passages, and multi-obstacle
courses, CarlHOI improves average success over the eval-
uated policies and world-model planners.
Trained with
four humanoids, a table, and one policy, it transfers with-
out retraining to teams of two to ten, unseen objects, other
policies, and unseen obstacle configurations. These results
demonstrate the potential of predictive shared guidance to
extend pretrained cooperative transport skills, supporting
generalizable multi-humanoid object interaction.

Figure 7 shows the same episode of each task under the four

Figure 8 extends the main-text reformation figure to every

8.4. Other metrics
Table 3 reports the rates of support loss and non-
arrival, together with the final object-to-target distance.
With TeamHOI-FT, most of CarlWM’s gain comes from
episodes that would otherwise not arrive within the time
limit: on the Obstacle Wall, these drop from 40.4% to 2.8%,
and on the course from 61.0% to 32.1%, while the final dis-
tance to the target shrinks from 2.25 to 0.55 m and from 4.41
to 2.37 m. With TeamHOI-FT, loss of support accounts for
approximately 30–36% of episodes in the Narrow Passage
across all planners. The baseline planners mainly increase
the share of episodes that do not arrive.
9. Implementation
• Checkpoints: all models and evaluations use the same
simulator settings and frozen skill checkpoints; the check-
point hash is recorded with every run.
• Hardware: all training and evaluation run on 8 NVIDIA
H100 GPUs and 1 NVIDIA A100 GPU.
• No method uses hand-written rules, privileged informa-
tion or outcome-based tuning on test conditions.
10. Benchmark and evaluation protocol
Task.
• Setup: n simulated humanoids pick up an object and
carry it to a target 7.5 m away (13 m on the multi-obstacle
course task), in Isaac Gym at 30 Hz.
• Objects: the round table used for training (diameter 2.0 m,
height 0.85 m, 78 kg), and the unseen bed (1.8 × 1.4 m)
and sofa (1.8 × 1.2 m), both 50 kg.
• Start: the humanoids start dispersed around the object and
must first approach and lift it.
• Success: the object center comes within 0.03 m of the tar-
get (arrival).
• End: arrival, a failure (a humanoid falls, the object is
dropped, or the simulator’s own termination fires), or the
time limit: 30 s for the table (52 s on the course task) and
60 s for the bed and sofa.
• Physics, termination and the success rule are those of the
released TeamHOI task [14], unchanged everywhere.

11. Planners
Shared interface.
• Aim point: every 0.5 s a planner may replace the target
that the skill observes with a = p + s R(θ)(g −p). Here
p is the object position, g the true target, |θ| ≤45◦and
s ∈[0.5, 1.5]. The aim distance is clipped to 0.5–9 m.
• Unchanged: the skill, its reward, the success rule and the
true target.
• Inputs, the same for all planners: recent object and hu-
manoid states, the target, the obstacle pieces as boxes,
and the episode time.
CarlWM.
• Commands: nine aim commands — the unchanged tar-
get, rotations of ±15◦, ±30◦and ±45◦, and scales of 0.5
and 1.5.
• Planning: each command is rolled out in latent space for
1.5 s (nine steps of 1/6 s) and scored by Eq. (10); the high-
est score is executed. The reference bonus ω = 0.02
favours unchanged guidance when scores are close. The
planner engages once the object has been picked up.
• Observation: the last six states at 1/6 s intervals. For the
object: position, height, heading, velocity and tilt. For
each humanoid, relative to the object: position, height,
heading, velocity, hand-to-rim distance. Also the previ-
ous commands, the target in the route frame, the obsta-
cle pieces as boxes relative to the object, and the episode
time.
• Architecture: a 3-layer transformer (d = 128) over to-
kens for the object, each humanoid (shared weights), each
obstacle piece and the context. Absent humanoids and
pieces are masked.
The output is pooled into a 128-
dimensional SimNorm latent.
• Anchoring: the dynamics and the progress, failure, con-
tact and arrival heads are anchored on the unchanged
command, z′ = SimNorm

Baseline principles.
• Shared: the aim-point interface, the training conditions
used in the 43 collection runs, the simulator budget that
produced CarlWM’s data (21,364,224 environment steps,
branch rollouts included), and a model size matched
within 0.4%.
• Training: online, from scratch, with each method’s own
exploration, on the reward that CarlWM’s score encodes.
Per decision: progress toward the true target, −3 for a
failure, −0.3/9 per wall-contact step, in units of 5 cm.
Arrival and failure are terminal; the time limit is not.
• Timing:
decisions every 0.5 s from the start of the
episode.
• Evaluation: the final agent is evaluated, without explo-
ration noise or checkpoint selection.
TD-MPC2 [6].
• Implementation: the official implementation, ported with
identical update and planning math to run inside our sim-
ulator process. The episodic variant, with a termination
head.
• Action: one continuous command (θ, s) for the team.
• Network: the 1M preset (latent 128, encoder width 256,
two Q-functions), with MLP width 375 to match the pa-
rameter count (1,522,234).
• Planner: MPPI with 512 samples, 6 iterations, 64 elites,
24 policy-prior trajectories, horizon 3 decisions (1.5 s)
and temperature 0.5. The mean is warm-started from the
previous plan.
• Training: discount 0.95 (the official heuristic); 1,000 seed
transitions with uniform actions; then one update per
collected transition, batch 256, Adam with learning rate
3 × 10−4 and the encoder at 0.3×.

12. CarlWM training details
• Optimizer: AdamW. The encoder uses a 0.3× learning
rate; with the full learning rate the transformer encoder
did not train stably. Training runs for at most 40 epochs.
The model is selected by the ranking loss (17) on the five-
command validation branch points, with early stopping


after 10 epochs without improvement.
• Team size: the model is trained with four humanoid slots.
After training, its humanoid token capacity is expanded
to ten; no humanoid parameters are added, since all hu-
manoid tokens share weights. Slots of absent humanoids
are masked out of the attention, and active humanoids are
never masked, so the output for four humanoids is un-
changed and teams of 2–10 use the same weights. The
expanded model also has a team-size input to the context
token whose weights are initialized to zero, so it has no
effect.
• Ranking-loss branch points: the first collection rounds
branched the five commands u0, ±15◦, ×0.5 and ×1.5;
later rounds branched all nine. Equation (17) is computed
on two sets, all branch points restricted to these five com-
mands and the nine-command branch points (16,535 in
training) over all nine, each sampled at 64 branch points
per minibatch; the two terms are added with equal weight.
• Event loss: the binary cross-entropy of each event uses
a positive-class weight equal to its ratio of negative to
positive training steps, clipped to [1, 50], since failures,
contacts and arrivals are rare.
• Target of u0: the unchanged command is simulated twice
at each branch point, and its realized return in Eq. (17) is
the mean of the two.

14. Low-level policies
• TeamHOI: the released multi-agent transformer policy,
trained on the open carrying task with adversarial motion
priors. It has never seen gates or walls.
• TeamHOI-FT: initialised from TeamHOI (policy, value
function and discriminator) and fine-tuned with PPO for
4,096,000 transitions on the 2.1 m gate with four hu-
manoids. The task reward is replaced by target-arrival
shaping, and a geometry encoder of the passage is added
as a zero-initialised residual.
Settings:
128 environ-
ments, horizon 32, minibatch 512, 6 epochs, learning rate
4 × 10−6, γ = 0.995.
• CooHOI: re-implemented following the specification
in [14].
– Network: TeamHOI’s transformer with cross-attention
replaced by self-attention and no teammate tokens.
– Contact points: fixed designated points, evenly spaced
on the rim, with the designated point listed first in the
observation.
– Reward: TeamHOI’s, with the formation terms re-
placed by the approach-angle reward.
– Stage 1: a single agent with halved table mass and fric-
tionless table legs, for 3 paper-days (2.00 × 109 transi-
tions).
– Stage 2: CooHOI-4, for 6 paper-days (1.32 × 109 tran-
sitions), validated four times per paper-day. It stops at
the paper’s four-agent success of 94.5%.
– Team sizes: one model, trained with four agents, is
used for every team size, as for TeamHOI.
– Multi-GPU training, when used, is data-parallel with
the totals of environments and minibatch samples un-
changed, so each update equals the single-GPU update.
