Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) demonstrate
promising capabilities in 2D visual understanding, yet remain
limited in 3D spatial reasoning. Recent studies explore in-
tegrating 3D reconstruction models into MLLMs. However,
these approaches either use a single geometric granularity
across all tasks or adopt a fixed multi-level fusion scheme,
regardless of task-specific reasoning requirements. Through
systematic experiments on multi-level geometric fusion, we
find that spatial tasks at different cognitive levels (e.g., dis-
tance estimation vs. route planning) require geometric repre-
sentations of different granularities, revealing that fixed fusion
cannot accommodate diverse spatial reasoning requirements.
To bridge this gap, we propose SpatialMind, a task-adaptive
framework for dynamically integrating multi-level geometric
representations. SpatialMind employs GeoFus, a multi-layer
geometric fusion module built around a gating network jointly
conditioned on textual instructions, visual semantics, and ge-
ometric representations. This network adaptively modulates
the contributions of shallow, intermediate, and deep geomet-
ric features across the layers of the LLM decoder. Further-
more, we introduce a negative-entropy regularization loss to
mitigate gate collapse. Gate visualizations indicate that Ge-
oFus adapts geometric granularity to task requirements. Ex-
periments demonstrate that SpatialMind achieves competitive
performance across spatial benchmarks, attaining a state-of-
the-art score of 72.3 on VSI-Bench. The source code is pub-
licly available at https://github.com/zhaosb-star/SpatialMind.

Introduction
In recent years, Multimodal Large Language Models
(MLLMs) demonstrate remarkable capabilities in open-
world visual understanding and reasoning (Alayrac et al.
2022; Li et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Bai et al. 2023). With
the rapid development of embodied and spatial intelligence,
MLLMs increasingly need capabilities beyond general 2D vi-
sual semantics. These capabilities, ranging from the percep-
tion of object locations, geometric distances, and topological
structures to navigation and interaction, are fundamental to
embodied intelligence. However, existing MLLM architec-
arXiv:2611.22415v1  [cs.CV]  17 Nov 2026


tures are primarily designed for 2D visual-semantic align-
ment, which constrains their ability to perceive and reason
about 3D spatial structures (Yang et al. 2025a; Stogiannidis,
McDonagh, and Tsaftaris 2025; Fu et al. 2024). Therefore,
spatial intelligence requires the effective integration of 3D
geometric priors into vision-language representations with-
out compromising the semantic capabilities of MLLMs. This
challenge has motivated growing interest in more effective
multimodal fusion paradigms.
To enhance the 3D spatial understanding of MLLMs, some
approaches incorporate auxiliary geometric modalities as ad-
ditional inputs (Hong et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2025; Zhu et al.
2024). As illustrated in Fig. 1(b), other methods employ ge-
ometry encoders and fuse their representations with visual
features or inject them into multiple layers of the LLM de-
coder (Zheng et al. 2026; Fan et al. 2026; Wu et al. 2026;
Zhao et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026b; Li et al. 2026). How-
ever, these methods either rely on a single level of geometric
representation or adopt a fixed multi-level fusion scheme
across all spatial tasks. Such task-agnostic strategies over-
look the varying requirements for geometric representations
of different spatial tasks and cannot fully exploit multi-level
geometric cues. This raises a critical question: how can a
fusion architecture adaptively match the granularity and in-
tegration scheme required by each spatial task?
We conduct systematic experiments to answer this ques-
tion, injecting multi-level geometric features into different
layers of the LLM decoder. For evaluation, we follow the spa-
tial cognitive hierarchy defined in SPAR (Zhang et al. 2026a)
and divide VSI-Bench (Yang et al. 2025a) into low-level per-
ception and high-level reasoning tasks. As shown in Fig. 3,
shallow features better support low-level tasks, while deeper
features better support high-level tasks. However, previous
fixed fusion methods cannot adaptively adjust the relative
contributions of these features based on task requirements,
as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). This task-dependent behavior is
consistent with findings in cognitive neuroscience, where
the brain dynamically reallocates activation between low-
and high-level neural pathways according to task complexity
(Goodale and Milner 1992). Therefore, we argue that each
LLM injection layer should adaptively aggregate multi-level
geometric representations to match the spatial granularity
required by the task.
We propose SpatialMind, a task-adaptive framework for
spatial reasoning that employs GeoFus, a multi-layer geomet-
ric feature fusion module. GeoFus employs a gating network
that jointly models textual instructions, visual semantics, and
spatial information. This network dynamically modulates the
contributions of shallow, intermediate, and deep geometric
features across different LLM injection layers, enabling the
model to better adapt feature granularity to task-specific re-
quirements. To mitigate gate collapse, we further design a
negative-entropy regularization loss. Visualizations of the
learned gating weights show that GeoFus selects geomet-
ric representations at task-appropriate granularities. Spatial-
Mind achieves competitive results across spatial reasoning
benchmarks, with its 5B and 10B variants setting state-of-
the-art scores of 71.5 and 72.3 in their respective model-scale
groups on VSI-Bench. The main contributions of this work
are summarized as follows:
• We analyze the limitations of fixed geometry-to-reasoning
pairings and reveal that spatial tasks at different cognitive
levels require geometric representations of varying gran-
ularities and integration patterns.
• We propose SpatialMind, a task-adaptive framework for
spatial reasoning with GeoFus, which modulates the con-
tributions of multi-level geometric representations across
LLM injection layers and adopts a negative-entropy reg-
ularization objective to mitigate gate collapse.
• SpatialMind achieves competitive performance across
multiple spatial reasoning benchmarks and establishes a
new state of the art on VSI-Bench with a score of 72.3.

Related Work
2.1
Multimodal Large Language Models
MLLMs extend the capabilities of LLMs to visual under-
standing by aligning visual and textual representations in a
shared multimodal space. Early frameworks like CLIP (Rad-
ford et al. 2021) and ALIGN (Jia et al. 2021) employed
contrastive learning to support zero-shot transfer. Subse-
quently, paradigms such as Flamingo (Alayrac et al. 2022)
and BLIP-2 (Li et al. 2023) introduced lightweight inter-
faces, utilizing Q-Formers or cross-attention mechanisms to
connect pre-trained visual encoders with LLMs. Driven by
the advancement of visual instruction tuning, models like In-
structBLIP (Dai et al. 2023), LLaVA (Liu et al. 2023, 2024),
and Qwen-VL (Bai et al. 2023) employed projection layers
to bridge pre-trained visual encoders and LLMs, while ex-
ploring more efficient representation and fusion paradigms.
Recently, video-based MLLMs (Lin et al. 2024; Zhang et al.
2024; Li et al. 2024) have extended this paradigm to the
temporal domain through spatiotemporal token compression,
temporal attention, and long-context modeling, achieving
substantial progress in general video understanding. Despite
these advances, existing MLLMs remain limited in spatial
reasoning. Most rely on final-layer semantic features and
frame-wise 2D tokens, overlooking fine-grained geometry
and explicit modeling of depth, camera motion, and cross-
view consistency (Meng et al. 2024; Oquab et al. 2023).
Consequently, existing MLLMs lack robust 3D spatial repre-
sentations, limiting their ability to generalize across diverse
geometric perception and spatial reasoning tasks.
2.2
MLLMs for Spatial Intelligence
To enhance the spatial intelligence of MLLMs, prior stud-
ies have explored various ways of incorporating geometric
information (Schonberger and Frahm 2016; Yao et al. 2018;
Ranftl, Bochkovskiy, and Koltun 2021; Wang et al. 2024;
Leroy, Cabon, and Revaud 2024; Wang et al. 2025b,a). Early
approaches incorporated auxiliary modalities to enhance 3D
spatial understanding. For instance, LLaVA-3D (Zhu et al.
2024) and Video-3D LLM (Zheng, Huang, and Wang 2025)
leverage lightweight RGB-D encoders to capture explicit ge-
ometric cues. However, their dependence on depth sensors or
pre-scanned environments limits their applicability to stan-
dard RGB videos. To alleviate this dependence, recent meth-
ods integrate geometric representations directly into MLLM

architectures. VG-LLM (Zheng et al. 2026) augments visual
tokens with patch-level geometric features, whereas Spatial-
Stack (Zhang et al. 2026b) injects multi-level geometric rep-
resentations into selected LLM decoder layers. Nevertheless,
these methods typically rely on fixed and task-agnostic fu-
sion schemes, which may introduce conflicts between seman-
tic and geometric representations. Another line of research
transfers geometric knowledge through feature distillation
or representation alignment. Frameworks like 3DRS (Huang
et al. 2026) and 3DThinker (Chen et al. 2026) distill the spa-
tial priors of 3D representation models into the visual rep-
resentations of MLLMs. Although such alignment improves
geometric awareness, it does not explicitly support sample-
dependent selection of geometric information during infer-
ence. Overall, existing methods apply the same predefined
geometric feature levels and injection pathways across tasks.
To resolve these limitations, this paper proposes an adaptive
feature fusion strategy to achieve task-adaptive integration of
multi-level geometric features.
3
Hierarchical Roles of Multi-Level
Geometric Features
To investigate how the geometric granularity required by
spatial tasks varies with cognitive complexity, we conduct
controlled ablation experiments on VSI-Bench (Yang et al.
2025a) using SpatialStack as the baseline. With the model ar-
chitecture, training settings, and injection routes unchanged,
we separately remove the shallow injection (VGGT-11 to
LLM-0), intermediate injection (VGGT-17 to LLM-1), and
deep injection (VGGT-23 to LLM-2), and evaluate the perfor-
mance of these configurations across various spatial tasks.
Following the spatial cognitive difficulty spectrum defined
in SPAR (Zhang et al. 2026a), we divide VSI-Bench into
low-level tasks, including relative distance, absolute distance,
relative direction, object size, and room size, and high-level
Figure 3: Effects of Multi-Level Geometric Features. Shallow
features contribute more to low-level tasks, while deep features
are more important for high-level tasks, demonstrating hierarchical
specialization and cross-level complementarity.
tasks, including route planning, object appearance order, and
object counting. This analysis examines two hypotheses: (i)
Cross-level Complementarity, whether the complete multi-
level configuration outperforms variants with any geometric
layer removed, indicating that features from different levels
provide complementary information; and (ii) Relative Func-
tional Specialization, whether features at different levels
contribute differently to distinct task hierarchies, suggesting
that multi-level geometric representations serve specialized
functional roles.
Qualitative Analysis. To examine the hierarchical dif-
ferences among multi-level geometric features, we visual-
ize their attention patterns on scene0030_00 from ScanNet
(Dai et al. 2017). As shown in Fig. 6, shallow features re-
spond primarily to object boundaries, local contours, and
depth discontinuities, preserving fine-grained geometric de-
tails. Meanwhile, intermediate features exhibit an expanded
focus that covers more holistic object regions, beginning to
consolidate object shapes and local spatial configurations.
Ultimately, deep features exhibit more globally distributed at-
tention patterns, emphasizing inter-object relationships and
the overall scene layout. These observations indicate that
multi-level geometric features form a progressive represen-
tation hierarchy, evolving from fine-grained perception to
local spatial configurations and ultimately to global spatial
understanding.
Quantitative Results. The quantitative results presented
in Fig. 3 further corroborate the aforementioned qualitative
insights. The complete multi-level configuration achieves the
best performance on both low- and high-level tasks, demon-
strating that features at different levels provide highly com-
plementary information. Removing the shallow injection re-
duces low-level task performance by 1.43 percentage points,
while causing only a 0.17 point drop on high-level tasks.
This suggests that shallow features contribute more strongly
to fundamental spatial attributes, such as distance, direction,
and scale. In contrast, removing the deep injection decreases
high-level task performance by 2.07 percentage points, com-
pared with a smaller drop of 0.66 points on low-level tasks.
This result indicates that deep features play a more important
role in modeling global scene context and higher-level spatial


relationships. Removing the intermediate injection leads to
comparable drops of 1.33 and 1.58 percentage points on low-
and high-level tasks, respectively. These results suggest that
intermediate features provide stable support across both task
hierarchies, bridging local geometric perception, object-level
relations, and global spatial reasoning.
The visualization and quantitative results reveal hierarchi-
cal specialization and cross-level complementarity, motivat-
ing each LLM injection layer to adaptively fuse the geometric
granularity most relevant to the current spatial task.

Sec. 4.3 presents the negative-entropy regularization loss.

5.1
Training
Training Data Construction.
To cover diverse spatial rea-
soning tasks, we construct the training set through balanced
sampling across multiple spatial reasoning datasets. Specif-
ically, the training corpus combines VG-LLM (Zheng et al.
2026), including the SPAR and LLaVA-Hound subsets, the
ScanNet subset from VLM-3R (Fan et al. 2026), a curated
subset of VSI-590K (Yang et al. 2025b), ViCA-322K (Feng
2025), MindCube (Yin et al. 2025), and Spatial-MLLM-
203K (Wu et al. 2026). By combining these complementary
data sources, we enhance the model’s spatial perception and
reasoning capabilities in complex 3D environments.
Two-Stage Training Strategy.
To improve optimization
stability, we adopt a two-stage training strategy. We use
Qwen3.5-4B (Team 2026) as the multimodal backbone and
a frozen VGGT-1B (Wang et al. 2025a) to provide geomet-
ric priors. The native visual encoder of Qwen3.5-4B and
VGGT remain frozen throughout both training stages. In
Stage 1, we adopt the fixed layer-wise mapping of Spatial-
Stack (Zhang et al. 2026b) and jointly optimize the LLM and
geometric projectors for one epoch using the standard au-
toregressive language modeling objective. We use AdamW
with a learning rate of 1 × 10−5, a warmup ratio of 0.03,
a cosine learning-rate schedule, and a global batch size of
64. In Stage 2, GeoFus is activated for adaptive multi-level

size of 64, and a cosine learning-rate schedule for 1 epoch.
All experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs.
Training SpatialMind-5B takes approximately 30 hours in
Stage 1 and 15 hours in Stage 2.
5.2
Main Results
Evaluation on VSI-Bench.
VSI-Bench (Yang et al. 2025a)
comprises over 5,000 carefully curated questions derived
from the ARKitScenes (Baruch et al. 2021), ScanNet (Dai
et al. 2017), and ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al. 2023). It in-
cludes two answer formats, Numerical Answers (NA) and
Multiple-Choice Answers (MCA). Following the official
evaluation protocol, we use relative accuracy for NA ques-
tions and mean accuracy for MCA questions. We compare
SpatialMind with proprietary models, open-source MLLMs,
and specialized spatial reasoning models in the 3–5B and
8–10B parameter groups. As shown in Table 1, SpatialMind-
5B achieves a state-of-the-art overall score of 71.5 among
models in the 3–5B parameter range, while SpatialMind-
10B reaches 72.3 and establishes a new state of the art among
8–10B models. The consistent gains across both parameter
ranges indicate that the improvements are not limited to a
specific backbone scale, but arise from the effective adaptive
fusion of multi-level geometric representations.
Evaluation on Other Spatial Understanding Bench-
marks.
To assess the overall spatial understanding capa-
bility of SpatialMind, we further evaluate it on five ad-
ditional spatial understanding benchmarks: SPAR-Bench
(Zhang et al. 2026a), SPBench (Stogiannidis, McDonagh,
and Tsaftaris 2025), BLINK (Fu et al. 2024), MindCube (Yin
et al. 2025), and SQA3D (Ma et al. 2022). These benchmarks
cover diverse task formats and spatial capabilities, ranging
from multi-view and single-image spatial reasoning to 3D
question answering. As shown in Table 2, SpatialMind-5B
achieves the highest average score of 70.7 in the 3–5B group,
outperforming the same-data SpatialStack-5B by 2.1 points
and ranking first on five of the six benchmarks. SpatialMind-
10B achieves the highest average score of 71.6 in the 8–10B
group and ranks first on four of the six benchmarks. These
results demonstrate that SpatialMind delivers strong perfor-
mance across diverse spatial intelligence benchmarks.

6
Conclusion
In this work, we introduced SpatialMind, a task-adaptive
framework for multi-level geometric fusion in MLLMs. Our
analysis reveals that geometric features exhibit hierarchical
specialization and cross-level complementarity: shallow fea-
tures preserve fine-grained spatial details, while deeper fea-
tures capture global scene structures and high-level relations.
This finding also explains why fixed fusion strategies are in-


adequate, as diverse spatial tasks require geometric cues at
different levels of granularity. To address this limitation, we
developed GeoFus, which jointly leverages textual, visual,
and geometric cues to adaptively fuse multi-level geometric
features across LLM layers. Negative-entropy regularization
is further introduced to mitigate gate collapse. Visualizations
of the learned gating weights show that GeoFus selects geo-
metric representations at task-appropriate granularities. Our
experiments demonstrate the strong performance of Spatial-
Mind across diverse spatial reasoning benchmarks. On VSI-
Bench, SpatialMind-5B and SpatialMind-10B achieve state-
of-the-art scores of 71.5 and 72.3 within the 3–5B and 8–10B
parameter ranges, respectively, validating task-adaptive geo-
metric fusion across model scales.

A.2
Two-Stage Training Strategy
We adopt a two-stage training strategy to stabilize the opti-
mization of adaptive geometric fusion. In Stage 1, we follow
a fixed layer-wise mapping: VGGT layer 11 is injected into
LLM layer 0, VGGT layer 17 into LLM layer 1, and VGGT
layer 23 into LLM layer 2. The LLM and geometric projec-
tors are jointly optimized using the standard autoregressive
language-modeling objective, while VGGT, the native vision
encoder, and the visual MLP projector remain frozen. This
stage establishes a stable correspondence between multi-
level geometric representations and the early LLM layers. In
Stage 2, training is initialized from the Stage-1 checkpoint
and GeoFus is activated. At each injection position, GeoFus
adaptively combines geometric representations from VGGT
layers 11, 17, and 23. The LLM backbone, language mod-
eling head, VGGT, native vision encoder, and visual MLP
projector are frozen. Only the position- and layer-specific
geometric projectors Ps,k, gating parameters (Ws, bs), and
scaling factors αv,s and αg,s are optimized. The Stage-2 ob-

local geometric structures, such as sofa cushion boundaries,

bookshelf–table configuration, and table–chair arrangement.

tially coherent responses, emphasizing object-level relation-

and the holistic organization of the reconstructed point cloud.
These visualization results suggest that shallow representa-
tions primarily encode local geometric details, intermediate
representations capture object-level configurations, and deep
representations progressively aggregate global scene context.
Quantitative Results. The task-level ablation results in

observed in Fig. 6. The complete multi-level configuration
achieves average scores of 68.85% and 65.48% on low-level

D.1
VSI-Bench Task Taxonomy
As illustrated in Fig. 7, we follow the general hierarchy of
spatial capabilities in SPAR (Zhang et al. 2026a) and divide
the eight VSI-Bench subtasks into low-level perception and
high-level reasoning according to the dominant operation
required to obtain the answer. The low-level group includes
absolute distance, relative distance, relative direction, object
size, and room size. These tasks primarily require the direct
extraction of metric, directional, or scale-related cues from
visual observations.
The high-level group includes route planning, appearance
order, and object counting. These tasks require cross-view
information integration, global scene modeling, or multi-
step reasoning over spatial relations. Although object count-
ing also relies on perceptual evidence, we categorize it as
a high-level task because counting in VSI-Bench commonly
requires cross-view aggregation and global object-identity
consistency rather than isolated object recognition. The up-
ward arrow in Fig. 7 represents the increasing reasoning
complexity from local spatial perception to global spatial
reasoning. This taxonomy is used only for analysis, while all
eight subtasks retain their original evaluation protocols.

Per-Task Adaptive Gating Analysis
Visualization Protocol. To examine whether the sample-
wise gating decisions learned by GeoFus produce task-
dependent geometric selection patterns, we randomly sample
50 examples from each of the eight VSI-Bench subtasks. For
every example, we record the softmax-normalized weights
assigned to the shallow, intermediate, and deep geometric
representations at the three LLM injection positions L0, L1,
and L2. We then average the weights over the sampled ex-
amples within each subtask. For the group-level comparison,
we further compute macro averages over the five low-level
perception tasks and the three high-level reasoning tasks, en-
suring that each subtask contributes equally regardless of its
original dataset size.
Low-Level Perception Tasks. As shown in the upper part
of Fig. 8, low-level perception tasks generally assign greater
weight to shallow geometric features at the early injection po-
sition. At L0, object size and absolute distance assign 37.4%
and 36.2% of their weights to shallow features, respectively.
These tasks depend strongly on local object boundaries,
fine-grained depth variations, and metric cues. Relative dis-
tance and relative direction exhibit more balanced shallow–
intermediate distributions, assigning 34.9% and 33.7% to
shallow features and 35.6% and 36.4% to intermediate fea-
tures, respectively.
Room-size estimation shows a relatively stronger depen-
dence on deep representations than the other low-level tasks.
Its deep-feature weight rises from 32.4% at L0 to 42.3% at
L2, reflecting the need to aggregate scene-scale geometric
information despite being categorized as a perceptual task.
Nevertheless, averaged across all five low-level tasks, shallow
features receive 35.0% of the gating weight at L0, confirming
their importance for fine-grained spatial perception.
High-Level Reasoning Tasks. The lower part of Fig. 8 re-
veals a progressively stronger preference for deep geometric
representations as reasoning complexity increases. At L2,
object counting, appearance order, and route planning assign
42.9%, 46.8%, and 50.7% of their weights to deep features,
respectively. Object counting requires the model to aggregate
objects across views while maintaining identity consistency.
Appearance-order reasoning additionally requires temporal
and cross-view scene integration, leading to a stronger pref-
erence for global geometric representations. Route planning
exhibits the largest deep-feature weight because it requires
holistic scene understanding and multi-step reasoning over
navigable spatial structures.
The distinction is already visible at L0. Object counting
remains relatively balanced, with weights of 30.8%, 35.7%,
and 33.5% for shallow, intermediate, and deep representa-
tions, respectively. In contrast, appearance order and route
planning assign their largest L0 weights to deep features,

Layer-Wise Selection Pattern. As shown in Fig. 8, Ge-
oFus exhibits a consistent local-to-global selection pattern
across the three LLM injection positions. From L0 to L2,
the weight assigned to shallow geometric features decreases
across all eight tasks, whereas the contribution of deep fea-
tures progressively increases. Intermediate features remain
comparatively stable, receiving approximately 33%–43% of
the total weight across tasks and injection positions. This
pattern suggests that intermediate representations provide a
persistent bridge between fine-grained geometric details and
global scene structure.
The gating distributions also vary systematically across
task groups. At L0, low-level perception tasks assign an av-
erage weight of 35.0% to shallow features, compared with
28.8% for high-level reasoning tasks. Conversely,at L2, high-
level tasks allocate 46.8% of their weights to deep features,
whereas low-level tasks allocate 39.3%. These results in-
dicate that GeoFus does not employ a fixed geometric fu-
sion pattern. Instead, it adjusts the relative contributions of
shallow, intermediate, and deep representations according to
both the injection position and the spatial requirements of
each task.

D.4
Qualitative Results across VSI-Bench Tasks
Figures 10 and 11 present six representative comparisons
on VSI-Bench. Figure 10 shows three low-level perception
tasks: absolute-distance estimation, relative-direction rea-
soning, and room-size estimation. SpatialMind accurately
estimates the distance between the door and the ceiling light,
correctly determines the relative direction of the table, and
predicts a room size close to the ground truth, whereas
the baseline produces substantial metric and directional er-
rors. Figure 11 presents three high-level reasoning tasks:
appearance-order reasoning, cross-view object counting, and
route planning. SpatialMind correctly integrates information
across frames to recover the appearance order, avoids dupli-
cate counting across views, and selects the correct navigation
action, while the baseline fails in all three cases. These qual-
itative results demonstrate that GeoFus effectively adapts the
granularity of geometric representations to different task re-
quirements, supporting both fine-grained spatial perception
and global, multi-step spatial reasoning.
