Looking Right Is Not Being Right:
Evaluating the Geometry of Agent-Built 3D Scenes
Ziyang Long1, Yen-Liang Lin2, Shifan Zhu3, Allen Tu4, Meher Gitika Karumuri2, Chenqi Li5, Srikumar
Sastry6, Sarosij Bose7, Mellon M. Zhang8, Hsin-Jung Yang9,1
1University of California, Los Angeles, 2Amazon, 3Department of Computer Science, University of
Massachusetts Amherst, 4Department of Computer Science, University of Maryland, 5University of
Oxford, 6Washington University in St. Louis, 7University of California, Riverside, 8Georgia Institute
of Technology, 9Cedars-Sinai Medical Center
Vision-language agents can reconstruct rooms from video as editable 3D scenes, but convincing renders
do not establish geometric accuracy. We introduce a scan-referenced evaluation and use controlled
experiments to study reconstruction accuracy, the role of geometric inputs, and agents’ ability to
verify and correct their scenes. Our geometric score measures surface, depth, and furniture placement
agreement with laser scans of 30 real rooms on a 0–100 scale. The strongest tested agent, GPT-6
Astra, scores 51.4 from video alone, while a dense reconstruction from the same video scores 75.9.
Given the point cloud used for that reconstruction, Astra produces an editable scene that scores within
three points of it. Agents also inherit input errors: rescaling the supplied geometry changes scene scale
almost proportionally. During revision, viewing renders improves appearance more consistently than
geometry. Logs of their tool calls show that agents repeatedly measure their inputs but rarely compare
them with the scenes they build. To provide this missing check, we introduce a geometric error
report that compares each saved scene with the agent’s input points and lists the largest discrepancies.
Without new geometric evidence, the report raises the revision gains of GPT-6.1 Terra and Sol by 3.9
and 5.5 points over concurrent controls.

Introduction
Reconstructing rooms in 3D for furniture placement or robot navigation requires accurate geometry, not
just convincing renders. Suppose we ask an agent to rebuild a living room from a video. The sofa, rug, and
window may look right in its renders, but a cabinet that fits between the reconstructed sofa and wall may not
fit in the real room.
Vision-language agents build these scenes by writing Blender programs that define named, editable objects [6,
15, 16]. They can render, inspect, and revise their scenes. However, evaluation focuses largely on the renders:
the BVB benchmark, for example, scores reconstructions by question-answer retention and similarity between
rendered and reference videos [16]. Looking right is not being right. In Figure 1a, two reconstructions of one
room have nearly the same render score, while their agreement with the room’s laser scan differs by more
than a factor of two.
A render score also cannot tell whether an agent misjudges geometry from the video, loses supplied geometry
when creating editable objects, or fails to check the result. These failures need different remedies, so we study
each.
We therefore measure geometry directly and vary the agent’s geometric evidence and feedback one at a time,
keeping the harness, prompt, and budget fixed. Replays of saved scenes and logs of tool calls show what agents
revise and verify. Our contributions answer three questions, each raised by the answer to the one before:

Q1. How accurately do agents reconstruct rooms from video alone? We introduce a geometric score that
compares surfaces, per-view depths, and furniture placement with laser scans of 30 real rooms from ARK-
itScenes [3]. Controlled edits show that it detects errors render similarity misses. On its 0–100 scale, the
strongest tested agent scores 51.4 versus 75.9 for a dense reconstruction of that video.
Q2. Where does geometric accuracy come from? Our controlled study shows that accuracy depends largely on
supplied geometry. Given estimated poses and points, the strongest agent comes within three points of a
dense surface built from the same points while producing an editable scene. Agents also inherit the supplied
scale, even when it is wrong.
Q3. Can agents verify and correct what they build? Viewing renders improves appearance more consistently
than geometry, and logs of their tool calls show that agents measure their inputs but rarely compare them
with their scenes. We introduce a geometric error report that compares scenes with the agent’s own points. It
raises the revision gains of GPT-6.1 Terra and Sol by 3.9 and 5.5 points over concurrent controls.
Together, these results show that building a scene and verifying it are separate abilities. The strongest tested
agent converts supplied geometry into an editable scene with little loss, so its accuracy is bounded by the
errors of that geometry. Other agents revise without checking their scenes against that geometry. Our error
report makes the check for them and improves their accuracy.

Feedback and verification.
Visual feedback supports iterative refinement of scene programs [7, 8]. LEGO-
Anything documents regressive edits and unreliable self-evaluation, and uses image-derived residuals and
rollback to refine single-image reconstructions [11]. Programmatic evaluation also covers scene-state repair
and physical validity [13, 20]. We isolate image viewing in matched runs and test feedback that reports
discrepancies between the scene and the agent’s existing points.
Geometric evaluation.
Laser-referenced precision, recall and F-score are standard measures for multi-view
reconstruction [10, 21]. Feed-forward methods estimate cameras and points from images or video [12, 17, 18],
providing the geometric evidence our agents receive. We adapt these measurements to executable scene
programs, whose frame and scale are not fixed, adding an object-layout term and registrations that distinguish
scale and placement errors.
3
Evaluation and Study Design
Agents reconstruct rooms from walkthrough videos by writing Blender programs. We export the scene
geometry, register it to a laser scan of the same room, and then measure surface agreement S, per-view depth
agreement V , and furniture placement L. Figure 2 illustrates the components and their sensitivity to known
errors. Our experiments vary the geometric evidence and feedback agents receive to evaluate their effects on
reconstruction accuracy.
3.1
Geometric Scoring
All three components are computed over the region where the laser scan has valid depth inside the room.
Missing scene surfaces count as failures. For surface agreement S, we voxelize the reconstructed surfaces and
reference scan at 2 cm resolution, then average F-scores over 5, 10, and 20 cm thresholds to penalize missing
or excess geometry. View agreement V combines per-view depth accuracy at 10 cm with a depth-boundary
F-score that excludes specular regions detected by SAM3 [4] to avoid artifacts from reflections. Layout
3


agreement L compares the scanned surfaces inside annotated furniture boxes with the scene’s non-structural
surfaces using an F-score at 10 cm. Scoring furniture separately prevents the larger surfaces of walls and floors
from overshadowing placement errors.
Each component is normalized by its corresponding real-scan score to give each room a reference level of one.
We define the overall geometric score as G = 100

To compare visual similarity with geometric accuracy, we also report BVB’s latent similarity (LS) as a 0–1
measure of agreement between reference and rendered videos in learned feature space [16].
Registration.
The scene and scan may use different coordinate frames. We fit a single gravity-aligned rigid
transform to align the scene’s visible surfaces with the scan, preserving relative placement, metric scale, and
handedness. Unless stated otherwise, G denotes the score after this rigid fit. Allowing a uniform scale gives
Gs. Both fits minimize a truncated symmetric Chamfer distance rather than G, so Gs −G is a scale-error
diagnostic, not an exact decomposition. When camera poses are supplied, Gdirect scores the scene in their
coordinate frame without geometric fitting.
Known-error controls.
We apply controlled geometric edits to three scan-derived scenes to compare the
sensitivity of G and render similarity. Scaling a scene and its camera together by 1.25 leaves the renders,
and therefore their similarity score, unchanged, yet lowers G by 58.5 points. Figure 2b shows that mirroring
the scene with its camera, or rotating, shifting, or removing furniture, also lowers G sharply while render
similarity barely moves. The components respond to different errors: removing furniture, for example, lowers
L far more than S.
3.2
Study Design
Our study uses 30 rooms from ARKitScenes with RGB walkthrough videos, laser depth scans, camera
trajectories, and annotated 3D boxes [3, 19]. Agents run in our own harness, a minimal tool loop: a Blender
sandbox with a shell and frame extraction, plus image inspection in viewing conditions. They build scenes
from primitives. We evaluate GPT-6 Luna and Astra and GPT-6.1 Terra and Sol [14]. Terra, Sol, and Astra
completed every condition and are our main models. Terra and Sol receive $3 per scene and Astra $39, which
at Astra’s prices buys the same number of tokens as Terra’s $3. The budgets do not bind, as every run ends
when the agent declares the scene done. Most runs leave over half their budget unspent.
We run each model once per room and condition, keeping its harness, prompt, and budget fixed; unless noted,
results cover all 30 rooms. The conditions in Table 1 vary the geometric evidence supplied and whether agents
can inspect their own renders. DA3 [12] provides estimated camera poses and 3D points, while oracle points
are derived from ground-truth laser depth. Measured camera poses come from ARKit tracking and typically
agree with video frames to about 1◦. A separate experiment in Section 6.3 tests feedback on discrepancies
between the scene and its supplied points. We estimate intervention effects from paired comparisons within
rooms and obtain 95% confidence intervals by bootstrapping rooms.

How Accurate Are the Reconstructions?
From video alone, Terra, Sol, and Astra reach G of 41.1 to 51.4. For reference, a dense Poisson surface [9]
built from points estimated from the same video scores 75.9; the laser scan’s walls, floor, and ceiling alone
score 36.3. Table 2 shows that furniture placement is the weakest component for all three models.
Scale errors explain part of this shortfall. Median absolute scale errors are 10.4%, 5.8%, and 4.6% for Terra,
Sol, and Astra. Fitting scale at evaluation raises Terra’s G from 41.1 to 48.5, while Sol and Astra gain only
about two points each. Thus, most of the shortfall remains after scale fitting.

Metric Evidence Improves Accuracy
Table 4 shows that supplying measured camera poses raises G over video alone by 17.0–24.5 points. Estimated
poses also raise mean G, and adding estimated points increases it by a further 18.0 and 18.2 points for Terra
and Sol. Astra reaches 66.3 with estimated poses alone and gains another 6.9 points with the point cloud. The
same agents build substantially more accurate scenes when given poses and points, indicating that geometric
inference from video is a major source of reconstruction error.

5.3
Scenes Inherit the Scale of Their Evidence
To measure how agents respond to scale errors, we jointly rescale supplied points and camera positions
by factors of 0.75–1.25 in 10 rooms per model. Image projections and the RGB video remain unchanged.
Figure 3c shows that scene scale tracks supplied scale, with log–log slopes of 0.93–0.96 across Terra, Sol, and
Astra. A slope of one indicates proportionality. By comparison, their video-only scenes have median scale
errors of 4.6–10.4%, well below the maximum 25% perturbation.
Scenes built from unmodified estimated points also have median absolute scale errors of 2.9–3.3%. Mean
scale-fitting gains are 8.6–12.1 points in G for the agents in Table 4 and 8.8 points for the Poisson reference.
For Astra, the scale-fitting gain exceeds the gap to Poisson, suggesting that inherited scale error is a greater
limitation than geometric loss during scene construction.
Scale fitting also removes most of the gain from replacing estimated points and poses with oracle input.
Figure 3b shows that laser depth points and measured poses raise G by 8.8–14.0 points. After scale fitting in
both conditions, these gains shrink to 1.5–4.2 points. Because oracle input changes points, poses, and coverage
together, we do not attribute the remaining gain to any single factor. The scenes therefore gain accuracy
from supplied geometry while inheriting its scale errors.

and execution traces, the logs of each agent’s tool calls, show what agents revise and verify. An error-report
intervention tests whether feedback on scene-to-input discrepancies improves geometry correction.

6.1
Visual Checks Mostly Improve Appearance
We assess visual verification by allowing agents to inspect their own renders. Figure 4 contrasts the effects on
appearance and geometry, and Table 5 reports paired changes in G.
With a measured camera, viewing produces no detectable gain in Gdirect for any of the three models. All three
intervals include zero, and their upper bounds are small compared with the gains from the camera itself.
Without a camera, G rises by 2.6 points on average across five models, although only Sol’s interval excludes
zero. The gain is small and partly depends on how left–right mirrored reconstructions are scored.
Viewing affects appearance more consistently. With a measured camera, all five appearance measures move
toward the video in all three models, and 8 of the 15 intervals exclude zero. Astra’s mean turn count rises
from 63.9 to 112.1 per scene, without a corresponding improvement in geometric accuracy.
6.2

revising a scene. Geometric checks explicitly compare the saved scene with input geometry; visual inspection
alone does not count as a geometric check. Figure 5 summarizes the reconstruction loop, changes in accuracy
during revision, and checks against the supplied geometry.

Agents analyze supplied geometry but rarely use it to check their scenes. Before the first save, they summarize
points with histograms and quantiles. Sol and Astra also fit planes, estimate local surface normals, and cluster
points. Placements traced to printed input measurements differ from those measurements by a median of
2.5 cm. Figure 5c shows that after the first save, each main model re-measures inputs in most estimated-point
runs but explicitly compares the scene with them in at most two of 30 runs. The broader audit finds explicit
scene-to-input comparisons in only 11 of 380 point-cloud runs, based on recorded operations rather than visual
judgments. Geometric analysis therefore guides construction but rarely serves as a check on the resulting
scene.
9


6.3
An Error Report Improves Revision
We test whether reporting scene-to-input discrepancies improves revision in the estimated-point condition, for
Terra, Sol, and Astra. After each save, the harness casts rays from the supplied cameras into the scene to
compare its visible surfaces with the estimated points. It reports the fraction of visible surface within 10 cm
of these points and lists up to five objects with the largest discrepancies, giving a direction and distance for
each. The agent already has everything needed to compute this comparison, so the report adds no geometric
evidence. It checks agreement with the supplied geometry, not whether that geometry is itself accurate.
As a control, we run each agent again on the same rooms at the same time. The harness performs the same
check but does not show the result. Table 6 shows that the report increases the first-to-final gain in G by
3.9 points for Terra and 5.5 points for Sol at equal spend, with 95% confidence intervals that exclude zero.
For Terra, the gain is 4.3 points under our standard rigid registration, about a third of the 12.1-point gap
between the control and the dense reference.
Figure 6 shows reconstructions with and without the error report in one room. For Astra, the interval includes
zero. Its revision already improves G when the report is hidden, and its final scenes with and without the
report differ by only 0.2 points; the larger difference in gains reflects lower first saves in the runs with the
report. Sol and Astra each corrected a grossly misplaced first scene in one room after receiving the report.
Excluding these rooms reduces the differences from 5.5 to 3.7 points for Sol and from 2.8 to 0.5 points for
Astra.
All three agents act on the report. Among report lines followed by another save, the named object is edited in
39%, 68%, and 57% of cases for Terra, Sol, and Astra when the report is shown, and in 10%, 14%, and 19%
when it is hidden. The report experiment shows that geometric evidence can support correction as well as
construction when it is used to check the scene, for agents that do not make this check effective on their own.
7
Limitations
Our study covers 30 indoor rooms from one dataset, three main models from one developer, and one harness,
with a single run per room and condition. The error report was designed on the rooms on which it was tested,
so its transfer to other rooms and to agents from other developers is untested. It checks agreement with
the supplied points, not their accuracy; whether independent metric cues let agents correct inherited scale
errors remains open. We could not confirm that our rooms were held out of DA3’s training. G covers visible
geometry only and does not measure object semantics or how editable the programs are.
8
Conclusion
We introduced scan-referenced evaluation and geometric error reporting to measure and improve scene
geometry. Video-only reconstructions fall well below dense references from the same video, and render
similarity offers little guidance on geometric accuracy in individual rooms. Given estimated poses and points,
the strongest tested agent nearly matches the dense reference’s geometric accuracy in an editable scene, but
inherits the supplied scale errors. Viewing renders improves appearance more consistently than geometry, and
execution traces show that agents measure their inputs but rarely check their scenes against them. Our error
report makes this comparison explicit, improving geometric accuracy during revision for GPT-6.1 Terra and
GPT-6.1 Sol without adding geometric evidence. Geometric verification should therefore be an explicit part
of the reconstruction loop, allowing the same evidence to guide correction as well as construction.
