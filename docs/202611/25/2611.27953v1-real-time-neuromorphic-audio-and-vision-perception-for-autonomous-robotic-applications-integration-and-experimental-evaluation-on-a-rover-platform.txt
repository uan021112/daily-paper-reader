Real-time neuromorphic audio and vision perception for
autonomous robotic applications: Integration and experimental
evaluation on a rover platform
Guillem Fustier-Castillo, Ruben Ribes-Serrano, Javier Naranjo-Alcazar, Vicent
Ortiz-Castello and Pedro Zuccarello
Instituto Tecnologico de Informatica (ITI), Valencia, Spain
Abstract
Neuromorphic sensing and computing offer a promising alternative to conventional deep
learning pipelines for mobile robots, where perception must run continuously under tight
latency and energy constraints. However, most neuromorphic solutions are still evaluated of-
fline or on isolated benchmarks, and their integration into complete robotic systems remains
largely unexplored. In this technical report, a four-wheel rover platform is presented that
combines three real-time perception pipelines: a classical audio tagging pipeline based on
Pretrained Audio Neural Networks (PANNs) running on an NVIDIA Jetson GPU, a neuro-
morphic audio pipeline in which a spiking neural network (SNN) is deployed on the SynSense
Xylo Audio 2 chip, and a neuromorphic vision pipeline in which events from an event-based
camera are processed by the SpikeYOLO spiking detector for traffic cone detection.
On
the Xylo chip, the whole processing chain—from the microphone signal to the classification
of low- and high-pitched sounds—is performed on-chip, while the host only configures the
device and reads the output neurons. All models are deployed as containerised services and
integrated through ROS 2, and a configurable action dispatcher maps the detected classes to
robot behaviours, closing the loop between perception and actuation. The platform is demon-
strated in a proof-of-concept scenario in which the robot reacts to sound classes detected by
both audio pipelines and detects traffic cones with the event camera, showing the feasibility
of integrating neuromorphic hardware into a multimodal robotic platform operating in real
time.
Keywords: Neuromorphic Computing, Spiking Neural Networks, Xylo, Event-Based Vision,
Audio Tagging, Robotics, ROS 2

Introduction
Autonomous mobile robots must perceive their environment continuously and react to it within
short time frames, while relying on embedded computing platforms with limited energy budgets.
Deep learning has become the standard approach for robotic perception, and edge devices such
as the NVIDIA Jetson family make it possible to run convolutional neural networks (CNNs) close
to the sensors, reducing the dependence on network connectivity, the latency of the response
and the transmission of raw data [1]. In the audio domain, recent studies have shown that
audio tagging models can be deployed on resource-constrained devices, although their inference
time and thermal behaviour strongly depend on the selected architecture and the deployment
conditions [2, 3]. These constraints are even more critical in battery powered robots, where
perception competes for energy with locomotion and the remaining onboard systems.
Neuromorphic computing addresses these limitations by taking inspiration from the way bio-
logical neural systems process information. Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) process information

through discrete spike events instead of continuous-valued activations, which enables sparse and
event-driven computation with high energy efficiency [4, 5]. This paradigm is complemented
by neuromorphic sensors, such as event-based cameras, which generate spike-like outputs di-
rectly [6], and by dedicated neuromorphic processors that execute SNNs at very low power.
These properties make neuromorphic systems particularly attractive for always-on perception
in mobile robotics. Nevertheless, most neuromorphic audio and vision solutions are evaluated
offline or on isolated benchmarks [7, 8], and their integration into complete robotic platforms
operating in closed loop remains largely unexplored.
In this technical report, the integration of neuromorphic audio and vision perception into a
four-wheel rover platform is presented. The robot combines three real-time perception pipelines:
(i) a classical audio tagging pipeline based on PANNs [9] running on the GPU of an NVIDIA Jet-
son device, (ii) a neuromorphic audio pipeline based on an SNN deployed on the SynSense Xylo
Audio 2 chip, in which the audio signal is processed entirely on-chip, and (iii) a neuromorphic
vision pipeline in which the output of an event-based camera is processed by the SpikeYOLO
spiking detector [10] to detect traffic cones. Running classical and neuromorphic pipelines on
the same platform allows both approaches to be compared under the same operating conditions,
while providing the robot with complementary perception capabilities.
The main contributions of this work are the following:
• A multimodal rover platform that integrates conventional and neuromorphic sensors and
processors, including an NVIDIA Jetson device, a SynSense Xylo Audio 2 development kit
and an event-based camera.
• The closed-loop integration of the Xylo Audio 2 neuromorphic chip into the robot, from the
deployment of a trained SNN on the hardware to the execution of robot actions triggered
by its predictions.
• An event-based vision pipeline that feeds the SpikeYOLO spiking detector with event
representations built in real time from the camera stream.
• A modular and containerised software architecture, in which each model is exposed as
an independent service and integrated through ROS 2, so that models can be replaced
without modifying the robot software.
• A proof-of-concept demonstration in which the robot detects sound classes with both the
classical and the neuromorphic audio pipelines and translates them into actions, while
detecting traffic cones with the event camera.

the robot. Sections 5, 6 and 7 detail the classical audio, neuromorphic audio and neuromorphic

Background and related work
2.1
Spiking neural networks and the LIF neuron
Unlike traditional artificial neural networks (ANNs), which rely on continuous-valued activa-
tions, SNNs process information through discrete spike events, mimicking the behaviour of
biological neurons [11, 5]. Each neuron integrates its input over time and emits a spike only
when its internal state exceeds a threshold, so that computation and communication only take
2


place when relevant information is present. This sparse and event-driven behaviour is the basis
of the energy efficiency of SNNs when they are executed on dedicated hardware [4].
The most widely used neuron model in SNNs, and the one used by the neuromorphic audio
pipeline of this work, is the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron. A LIF neuron accumulates
the incoming spikes, weighted by the synaptic weights, into its membrane potential, which
decays over time with a characteristic time constant. When the membrane potential reaches a
threshold, the neuron emits a spike and its potential is reset [5].
2.2
Neuromorphic hardware and sensors
To harness SNNs in real-world applications, sensor data need to be represented as spike
trains [12]. While conventional sensors require an additional encoding step, which strongly in-
fluences the performance and efficiency of the network [8], neuromorphic sensors are specifically
designed to generate spike-based outputs directly [6].
Event-based cameras are the most mature example of neuromorphic sensors.
Instead of
capturing frames at a fixed rate, each pixel independently and asynchronously reports changes
in log-intensity as events, each one composed of the pixel coordinates, a timestamp and a polarity
indicating whether the brightness has increased or decreased [6, 13]. As a result, event cameras
offer microsecond temporal resolution, high dynamic range, low power consumption and no
motion blur, which makes them well suited for mobile robots.
On the processing side, several neuromorphic processors have been proposed for the efficient
execution of SNNs, such as TrueNorth [14], SpiNNaker [15] or Loihi [16]. In the audio domain,
the SynSense Xylo family offers low-power SNN inference processors for edge applications [17].
In particular, Xylo Audio 2 combines an analog audio front-end, which converts the microphone
signal into spike trains through a bank of band-pass filters, with a digital SNN core, enabling
end-to-end audio processing on a single chip. Xylo devices are programmed through the open-
source Rockpool library [18], which allows SNNs to be trained in PyTorch and mapped to the
hardware. The Xylo Audio 2 platform is described in detail in Section 6.1.
2.3
Related work
Edge computing for audio tagging has received increasing attention in recent years, as processing
audio close to the sensor reduces the transmission of raw data, the latency of the response and
the dependence on network connectivity [1]. Lamrini et al. [19] evaluated pre-trained CNNs
for anomaly detection in smart cities on embedded platforms, and Bibbo et al. [2] studied the
deployment of a single CNN-based audio tagging architecture on a Raspberry Pi, highlighting
challenges related to thermal management and inference latency over extended periods. These
works were extended in [3], where all the PANNs architectures, together with other CNN-based
audio tagging models, were evaluated on a Raspberry Pi during continuous 24-hour inference ses-
sions, showing that the choice of architecture and the deployment conditions determine whether
real-time operation can be sustained. Edge inference nodes of this kind have been integrated
into complete monitoring pipelines with remote supervision through IoT platforms [20], and
their deployment in real industrial environments has shown that AudioSet-trained models may
assign high confidence to wrong classes under real-world conditions [21]. All these works rely
on conventional deep learning models executed on CPUs or GPUs, and none of them considers
a mobile robotic platform.
In the neuromorphic domain, the application of SNNs to sound processing has been reviewed
in [7], which identifies the need for specialised SNN architectures tailored to audio tasks. Since
conventional microphones do not produce spikes, a large part of the research has focused on spike
encoding techniques [12]: Speech2Spikes [22] proposed an efficient audio encoding pipeline for
real-time neuromorphic systems, mainly in the context of speech, while [8] benchmarked several
spike encoders for environmental sound, showing that the choice of the encoder affects both
3


the classification performance and the spike firing rate, and hence the energy efficiency, of the
network. Dedicated hardware removes this step from the host: the Xylo family integrates the
encoding and the SNN inference on the same chip, enabling audio processing applications with
sub-mW power consumption [17]. However, these works evaluate neuromorphic audio processing
on datasets or as standalone applications, without integrating it into a system that acts on its
environment.
Regarding vision, event-based cameras have been widely studied for robotics and computer
vision due to their high temporal resolution, high dynamic range and low power consump-
tion [6, 13].
The release of large-scale event-based detection datasets, such as Gen1 in the
automotive domain [23], has fostered the development of object detectors for event data, and
SNN-based detectors have recently narrowed the performance gap with ANNs. SpikeYOLO [10]
combines a simplified YOLOv8 architecture with integer-valued training and spike-driven infer-
ence, achieving state-of-the-art results among SNNs on COCO and Gen1, while SpikeDet [24],
the evolution of SpikSSD, improves the firing patterns of spiking neurons to obtain accurate and
energy-efficient detection. As in the audio case, these detectors are mostly evaluated offline on
benchmark datasets.
Finally, the use of SNNs for robot control has been reviewed in [25], and neuromorphic
processors such as Loihi [16] have been proposed as low-power computing platforms for embedded
and robotic applications. Most of these works, however, focus on a single sensing modality or
on simulated environments.
In contrast to the works above, this report presents a robotic platform in which neuromorphic
audio and vision perception run in real time on the same robot, together with a classical audio
pipeline that serves as a reference. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that
integrates the Xylo Audio 2 chip into a mobile robot in closed loop, so that the predictions
computed on-chip directly trigger robot actions, and that combines it with an event-based
spiking detector in a single modular architecture.
3
Robotic platform
This section describes the rover used in this work, referred to as LISA. First, the mechani-
cal design and the kinematic model are presented. Next, the hardware components and their
connections are detailed. Finally, the software stack that runs on the robot is described.
3.1
Mechanical design and kinematics
LISA is a four-wheel skid-steer rover, in which the two wheels on each side are driven together
and the robot turns by commanding different velocities to the left and right sides (see Fig. 2).
The wheels have an effective radius of 0.077 m (154 mm diameter), and each wheel is equipped
with an encoder of 980 pulses per revolution.
Wheel odometry is computed from the encoder readings using a differential-drive model, in
which the velocities of the left and right wheels are averaged on each side. Due to the lateral
slippage that occurs in skid-steer platforms when turning, the effective track width differs from
the geometric one. The effective track width was therefore calibrated empirically: during a full
turn in place, the encoders reported approximately 803◦instead of 360◦, which corresponds to
a scrub factor of 2.23. Consequently, the geometric half-track of 0.281 m is scaled to an effective
track width of 0.627 m. The kinematic model of the robot is shown in Fig. 1.

3.3
Software stack
The robot software is built on ROS 2 [26]. The robot nodes are organised in several packages: a
serial bridge that communicates with the Arduino, converts the velocity commands published on
/cmd_vel and the light commands published on /led_robot into serial frames, and integrates
the wheel odometry published on /odom; a robot description package that provides the URDF
model and the transforms between the robot frames; the camera drivers, which publish the
event stream on /event_camera/events and the RGB images on /image_raw; and the action
dispatcher described in Section 4.
All software components run inside Docker containers, with one image per target platform
(JetPack 5, 6 and 7, and Raspberry Pi 4), so that the same software can be deployed on different
devices without modifying the host system. Communication between machines, for instance
between the robot and a laptop used for teleoperation, relies on the CycloneDDS middleware.
In addition, a web dashboard served from the robot, based on rosbridge, allows any device on
the robot’s network to display the audio detections and the actions they trigger, and to drive the

4
System architecture
The software architecture that integrates the perception pipelines with the robot is shown in
Fig. 5. The design follows a modular approach, in which each perception model runs in its own
container and is exposed as an independent HTTP service, so that models can be developed,
replaced or updated without modifying the rest of the system. Three model services are used in
this work: SpikeYOLO for event-based object detection, PANNs for classical audio tagging and
Xylo for neuromorphic audio classification. SpikSSD [24], a spiking detector based on the Single
Shot Detector (SSD) architecture, was also studied as an alternative for the vision pipeline
and integrated as a service with the same interface, but SpikeYOLO was finally selected for
the experiments presented in this report, as it provided the lowest inference time of the two
detectors.
A central orchestrator connects the sensors, the model services and the robot. It runs two
independent pipelines. The vision pipeline is event-driven: events are read from the ROS 2 event
topic, accumulated into fixed time windows, converted into the representation expected by the
detector and sent to the model service by a dedicated inference worker. The audio pipeline is
time-driven: the audio service is polled at a fixed interval (250 ms by default) to retrieve its
latest predictions.
Since the audio models capture the audio themselves, the audio pipeline
does not depend on the event camera and can run with the vision pipeline disabled. Modules
communicate through queues and shared buffers protected by locks, so that the acquisition of
events is never blocked by the inference requests.

classes to robot behaviours. The dispatcher only implements two primitives: move, which pub-

time, and led, which publishes a light command on /led_robot. The mapping between classes
and actions is not hardcoded but defined in a YAML file, one per audio model, together with
a minimum confidence threshold (60% by default).
For each message, the dispatcher walks
through the detections from the most likely to the least likely and executes the action associated
with the first class that is present in the mapping and whose confidence exceeds the threshold.
As a result, a new audio model can be added to the robot simply by providing its action file,
without code changes.

Classical audio perception
The classical audio pipeline is used as a reference for the neuromorphic one and is based on the
Pretrained Audio Neural Networks (PANNs) framework [9]. PANNs have become a cornerstone
in the field of computational audition, offering robust and transferable representations across
a wide range of audio-related tasks. These models are trained on AudioSet [27], a large-scale
dataset of weakly labelled 10-second clips covering more than 500 sound classes, and have been
widely deployed on edge devices for real-time audio tagging [3, 20]. In this work, the CNN14
model trained on AudioSet is used. CNN14 is the PANNs architecture with the best performance
on AudioSet and is widely used in state-of-the-art applications [9, 3]. Although its computational
cost makes it demanding for CPU-only devices such as the Raspberry Pi [3], the GPU of the
Jetson platforms allows it to run in real time with a wide margin, as shown below.

The inference pipeline follows the real-time application presented in [3], adapted to the Jetson
platform. The model is converted from its original PyTorch checkpoint to the Open Neural
Network Exchange (ONNX) format and executed with ONNX Runtime using the TensorRT
execution provider on the Jetson GPU. The application is composed of independent modules
that communicate through asynchronous queues: an audio recording module captures audio
from the USB microphone, an inference module computes the predictions, and an API module
publishes the latest results so that the orchestrator can retrieve them. The model used in the
experiments expects 10-second inputs sampled at 32 kHz, so that each prediction is computed
on a window of 320,000 samples. This choice aligns with the structure of AudioSet, which is
composed of 10-second clips, providing the model with a sufficient temporal context to capture
the relevant acoustic events. To ensure temporal continuity and reduce the response time, a
sliding window with an 80% overlap is used, so that a new prediction is obtained every 2 s, each
one based on the last 8 s of the previous window and 2 s of new audio. In addition, versions
of the model with shorter analysis windows, of 4 s and 2 s, are available and can be selected
depending on the response time required by the application. The three classes with the highest
scores are returned together with their confidence values. The complete audio-based control
pipeline, from the microphone to the robot actions, is shown in Fig. 6.
These configurations are feasible because the inference time of the model is short compared
with the time between predictions. Table 2 reports the mean inference time of CNN14 on the two
Jetson platforms considered in this work, with the board in its maximum performance power
mode, when the model is executed on the CPU, on the GPU with CUDA and on the GPU
with TensorRT. The CUDA and TensorRT times were averaged over 24 hours of continuous
inference, while the CPU times were averaged over 100 consecutive windows. With TensorRT,
the mean inference time is 8.4 ms on the AGX Orin and 25.0 ms on the Orin Nano, two orders
of magnitude below the 2 s interval between predictions of the 10-second configuration.

6
Neuromorphic audio perception on Xylo
This section describes the neuromorphic audio pipeline of the robot. First, the Xylo Audio 2
platform is introduced. Next, the classification task and the SNN architecture are presented,
followed by the training procedure. Then, the deployment of the trained network on the chip
and the decoding of its outputs are detailed, together with the integration of the pipeline in the
robot.
6.1
The Xylo Audio 2 platform
Xylo Audio 2 (SYNS61201) is a neuromorphic processor developed by SynSense for low-power
audio processing at the edge [17]. The chip integrates two main blocks. The first one is an analog
audio front-end (AFE), which amplifies the microphone signal and splits it into 16 frequency
channels by means of a bank of band-pass filters. The output of each filter is rectified and
converted into spike trains, so that the audio signal is encoded into 16 spiking input channels
without any processing on the host.
The second block is a digital SNN core that executes
networks of LIF neurons.
In the digital core, the synaptic weights are represented as integers and the exponential
decays are implemented as bit-shift operations, so that the time constants are quantised to the
values that can be represented by the hardware.
Xylo Audio 2 is used through its hardware development kit, which connects to the host
via USB and is controlled with the Rockpool [18] and Samna libraries. Rockpool provides a
complete workflow to train SNNs in PyTorch, simulate the AFE and the digital core, map the
trained networks to the hardware resources, and deploy and run them on the chip.
6.2
Task definition
The neuromorphic audio pipeline addresses the classification of sounds into two classes, low-pitch
and high-pitch, according to their dominant frequency content. In addition, an implicit silence
class is considered when none of the output neurons is active, so that the robot does not react in
the absence of sound. This task was chosen because it can be solved by a very small network, its
classes can be easily produced during a demonstration (for instance, by whistling or humming
at different pitches) and it is naturally suited to the frequency decomposition performed by the
AFE.

6.3
Network architecture
The SNN deployed on the chip is based on the SynNet architecture provided by Rockpool [18, 17],
a feed-forward network of LIF neurons specifically designed for audio classification on Xylo. The
network is shown in Fig. 7 and its hyperparameters are summarised in Table 4. The input layer
has 16 channels, one per AFE filter. The network has a single hidden layer of only two LIF
neurons with a single synaptic time constant, followed by an output layer of two LIF neurons,
one per class. All neurons use a synaptic and a membrane time constant of 20 ms, a threshold
of 1.0 and a time step of 10 ms, which matches the rate at which the AFE delivers spikes to the
digital core. The time constants and thresholds are kept fixed during training, and the number
of spikes that a neuron can emit per time step is limited to 15 in the hidden layer, which is the
maximum supported by the hardware, and to one in the output layer.
The resulting network has only four neurons and
trainable parameters, which uses a negligible fraction of the resources of the chip. Such
a small network is sufficient for this task because the AFE already performs a frequency de-
composition of the signal: distinguishing between low- and high-pitched sounds mainly requires
weighting the activity of the low- and high-frequency channels, which can be achieved with very
few neurons.
6.4
Deployment on the chip
The deployment of the trained network on Xylo Audio 2 follows the workflow provided by Rock-
pool and is illustrated in Fig. 8. First, the trained PyTorch network is loaded and converted into
a computational graph that describes its neurons, connections and parameters independently
of the training framework. Next, this graph is mapped to the hardware resources of the chip,
assigning each neuron of the network to a physical neuron of the digital core. The floating-
point weights, thresholds and time constants are then quantised to the integer representation
supported by the hardware, using a per-channel quantisation scheme. From the quantised speci-
fication, a hardware configuration is generated and validated, ensuring that the network satisfies
all the constraints of the chip.
The configuration is finally written to the chip through the development kit. At this stage,
the AFE is configured with a high amplification level and its auto-calibration is enabled, a process

Once the network is deployed, the audio signal never leaves the chip during inference: the
microphone signal is converted into spikes by the AFE and processed by the SNN core, while the
host only triggers the evolution of the chip and reads the state of the output neurons. This is a
key difference with respect to the classical pipeline, in which the raw audio has to be captured,
transferred and processed by the Jetson.
6.5
Output decoding and integration in the robot
The end-to-end neuromorphic audio pipeline is shown in Fig. 9. During inference, the chip is
evolved in blocks of 10 time steps, that is, 100 ms of audio, and the membrane potentials Vmem of
the two output neurons are read at each block. For each output neuron, the maximum membrane
potential within the block is computed. If both values are zero, the block is classified as silence;
otherwise, the class associated with the output neuron with the highest value is selected. As a
result, the neuromorphic pipeline produces a new prediction every 100 ms, compared to the 2 s
of the classical pipeline and its 10-second analysis window.
The inference loop runs in a dedicated thread inside the Xylo container, which stores each
new prediction in a queue.

an HTTP API, built with FastAPI, that exposes the latest prediction. The orchestrator polls
this API as described in Section 4 and publishes the result on /audio_detections, where it

7
Neuromorphic vision perception
7.1
Event-based sensing and representation
The event camera is managed by the robot through its ROS 2 driver, which publishes the event
stream on /event_camera/events. The orchestrator subscribes to this topic, decodes the events
and accumulates them into consecutive time windows of 250 ms.
Since the detector operates on dense tensors, the events of each window are converted into
a frame-like representation. The representation is initialised to a neutral grey value of 127, and
each pixel that receives an event is set to 255 or 0 according to the polarity of its most recent
event, so that positive and negative brightness changes appear as bright and dark pixels over a
grey background. The representation is built at the resolution of the sensor and replicated in
three channels to match the input format of the detector. All the events of each 250 ms window
are collapsed into a single frame.
7.2
SpikeYOLO detector
Object detection is performed with SpikeYOLO [10], a spiking object detector that simplifies the
YOLOv8 architecture and incorporates meta SNN blocks. SpikeYOLO introduces the Integer
LIF (I-LIF) neuron, which is trained with integer-valued activations to reduce quantisation errors
and is converted into binary spikes at inference time, preserving the spike-driven nature of the
computation. SpikeYOLO achieves state-of-the-art performance among SNNs on the COCO
and Gen1 [23] datasets, the latter being an automotive detection dataset recorded with an event
camera.
In this work, only the SpikeYOLO architecture is reused: the network was adapted to the
HD resolution of the event camera of the robot (1280 × 720 pixels) and trained from scratch for
cone detection on event data recorded by the research group with the same Prophesee EVK3 HD
camera, so that the training data matches the sensor and the representation used at inference

7.3
Integration in the robot
The detector runs in its own container on the Jetson GPU and exposes an HTTP endpoint
that receives a voxel grid and returns the detected bounding boxes. In the orchestrator, the
construction of the voxel grids and the inference requests are decoupled by a queue, so that
a dedicated inference worker sends each voxel grid to the service while new events continue
to be accumulated.
The detections whose confidence exceeds a threshold are published on
/detections and can be visualised in RViz together with the robot model.
In the current
version of the system, the vision pipeline is used for perception only: the detected cones are
published and visualised, but they are not yet mapped to robot actions.
8
Proof-of-concept demonstration
The aim of this report is not to provide a quantitative benchmark of the perception models,
but to demonstrate that classical and neuromorphic perception pipelines can be integrated and
run together on the same robot, and that their outputs can drive the robot in real time. This
section describes the proof-of-concept demonstration carried out with the platform and discusses,
qualitatively, the differences between the two audio pipelines.
Each perception capability was demonstrated separately, in an environment chosen to suit
the characteristics of the corresponding sensor and task.
The three scenarios are described
below.
8.1
Neuromorphic audio
The neuromorphic audio pipeline was tested in a semi-anechoic room, in the absence of back-
ground noise. This controlled environment ensured that the activity of the output neurons of
the chip was caused only by the sounds produced during the test, which is particularly relevant
given the simplicity of the task and of the deployed network. The operators produced low- and
high-pitched sounds in front of the robot, and the robot executed the corresponding action:
high-pitched sounds made it move forward and low-pitched sounds made it move backward,
while it remained still in the absence of sound. During the test, the whole audio processing took

8.2
Classical audio
The classical audio pipeline was tested in a similar way, but without the need for a semi-anechoic
room, since CNN14 is trained on AudioSet, which covers a wide variety of real-world recording
conditions [27]. The operators produced the sound of the class associated with the desired action,

respectively. The detections and the executed actions were monitored in real time through the
web dashboard (Fig. 4).
8.3
Event-based cone detection
For the vision pipeline, a circuit with traffic cones was set up for the robot to detect along its way,
and the robot was teleoperated through the circuit with a gamepad. Since event cameras respond
to changes in brightness, the ambient lighting can generate a large number of background events
that are not related to the objects of interest. To reduce this event noise, the test was carried
out with the lights of the room switched off, using only the LEDs of the robot to illuminate the
scene. Under these conditions, SpikeYOLO detected the cones of the circuit, and the detections
were published on /detections and visualised in RViz together with the robot model.
An
example of a cone detected during the test is shown in Fig. 10.
8.4
Classical vs. neuromorphic audio pipelines
Table 5 summarises the main differences between the two audio pipelines, as derived from their
design. The classical pipeline offers a rich vocabulary of sound classes, inherited from AudioSet,
at the cost of capturing and processing the raw audio on the Jetson GPU with a large CNN and
long analysis windows. The neuromorphic pipeline solves a much simpler task with a network of
only four neurons, but it does so entirely on the chip, producing a new prediction every 100 ms
without any audio processing on the host. Both approaches are therefore complementary: the
former is suited to recognising a wide range of sound events, while the latter is suited to simple,
always-on audio triggers with a minimal load on the main computer. A quantitative comparison
in terms of latency, energy consumption and accuracy is left for future work.
9
Limitations and future work
The current system presents several limitations. First, the neuromorphic audio task is restricted
to a binary low- versus high-pitch classification, which demonstrates the integration of the Xylo
chip in the robot but is far from the variety of sound classes handled by the classical pipeline.
15


Second, the Xylo service reports the winning class without a calibrated confidence value. Third,
the detections of the vision pipeline are published and visualised but do not yet trigger robot
behaviours. Finally, the system has been validated through a proof-of-concept demonstration,
and a systematic quantitative evaluation has not yet been carried out.
As future work, the neuromorphic audio pipeline will be extended to richer sets of sound
classes, taking advantage of the remaining resources of the chip and of efficient spike encoding
schemes [8]. In addition, vision-driven behaviours, such as stopping or avoiding obstacles when
cones are detected, will be added to the action dispatcher, and the fusion of audio and visual
information will be explored. Finally, a systematic experimental evaluation of the platform will
be conducted, including the end-to-end latency of each pipeline, the accuracy of the models in
the robot’s operating conditions and the energy consumption of the classical and neuromorphic
pipelines, in order to quantify the benefits of neuromorphic processing at system level.
