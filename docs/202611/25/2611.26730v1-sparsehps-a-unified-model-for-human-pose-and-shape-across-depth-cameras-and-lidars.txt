SparseHPS: A Unified Model for Human Pose and Shape
across Depth Cameras and LiDARs
Ilya Zaychuk
linkedin.com/in/ilya-zaychuk
Abstract
Recovering 3D human pose and shape from point clouds
is central to motion capture, robotics and self-driving ve-
hicles, because depth cameras and LiDARs sense a person
directly in metric 3D and work under any lighting. However,
current approaches are each tailored to one sensor type:
point density differs by more than ten times between devices,
public datasets are labeled with incompatible body models,
and LiDAR approaches need long input sequences to offset
sparse scans. In this work, we present SparseHPS, a novel
sensor-agnostic network that recovers SMPL-X pose, shape
and global translation from a human point cloud with one set
of weights, for single frames and streams alike. Specifically,
SparseHPS encodes the cloud with a LitePT backbone over
sparse voxels, represents every joint by a learnable query
decoded with one common head, localizes the body by vot-
ing over voxels, and draws temporal context from a causal
memory of tokens from the seven preceding frames. A single
model is trained on ten datasets that mix rendered clouds
with real depth-camera and LiDAR captures, all converted to
one body model. Experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art
results on the SLOPER4D and FreeMotion LiDAR bench-
marks, where SparseHPS lowers the root-relative joint error
by 22% and 11%, respectively, compared with the strongest
prior method, which needs 32 frames. On real Azure Kinect
captures of held-out HuMMan subjects, SparseHPS attains
a joint error of 34.7 mm. Moreover, the current frame alone
already yields the bulk of this accuracy.

Estimating the 3D pose and shape of the human body is a
long-standing task in computer vision that underpins mo-

images, depth cameras and LiDARs deliver metric 3D mea-
surements of the body: they remove the ambiguity of depth
and scale, are unaffected by illumination, and place the per-
son in world coordinates.
However, existing point-based methods are designed
around one kind of sensor. Depth-camera approaches [10,

novel network that estimates human pose and shape from
point clouds, frame by frame or over a stream. Its encoder is
LitePT [61], a point transformer over sparse voxels that uses
convolutions at the fine levels and attention at the coarse
ones. Specifically, 24 learnable query tokens, one per body
joint plus one for shape and one for translation, cross-attend
to voxels from the two coarsest stages of the encoder, and
all joint tokens share one linear head. Instead of predicting
the global position directly from a token, each voxel votes
for the pelvis location, weighted by the translation token.
In addition, a causal memory lets each token attend to its
own states in the seven previous frames; for a frame with no
history, the computation reduces exactly to the single-frame
model. We refer to the single-frame variant as SparseHPS-S
and to the variant with memory as SparseHPS-T.
To train one model for all sensors, we bring ten sources
into a common label space, the neutral SMPL-X model with
16 shape coefficients: three LiDAR datasets (SLOPER4D,
FreeMotion, RELI11D), four depth-camera datasets (HuM-
Man, BEHAVE, EgoBody, FORM-HOI), and renderings
of synthetic and scanned humans (AMASS, BEDLAM2,
CAPE). Per-source quotas keep large datasets with many
views from dominating training, and a rotation augmentation
that respects gravity retains the vertical direction on which
sparse scans depend.
Extensive experiments on the full mixture support ev-
ery design decision. Over seven consecutive single-frame
models, each changing a single component, the vertex error
averaged over nine test sets falls from 43.3 mm to 36.0 mm
(Tab. 4); a data ladder further shows that every sensor family
needs data of its own, whereas adding the other families
comes at almost no cost (Tab. 5). The memory leaves the
mean single-frame accuracy intact and cuts acceleration error
by 5% (Tab. 6). On the LiDAR benchmarks, SparseHPS sets
a new state of the art: with a history of seven frames, its root-
relative joint error is 28.6 mm (SLOPER4D) and 41.8 mm
(FreeMotion), whereas the strongest published method ob-
tains 36.5 mm and 47.2 mm from 32 frames (Fig. 1). Notably,
a single frame already reaches 28.9 and 42.9 mm, since the
encoder sees every point of the scan.
In summary, our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce SparseHPS, a novel one-stage model that
recovers SMPL-X pose, shape and global position from
single-frame or sequential point clouds, using per-joint
query tokens with a shared decoding head, a voting scheme
over voxels and a causal memory of tokens.
• We develop a training recipe that spans range sensors:
ten sources mapped to one label space, per-source quotas
and a gravity-preserving augmentation, yielding shared
weights across depth cameras and LiDARs.
• We provide controlled ablations on the complete train-
ing mixture and reach state-of-the-art accuracy on LiDAR
benchmarks with one quarter of the frames that sequence-
based methods take.
2. Related Work
Human mesh recovery from images.
Parametric body
models such as SMPL [33] and SMPL-X [39] are re-
gressed from an image with a pooled feature and an it-
erative head [23, 26, 63], or with query tokens that at-
tend to image features, per joint and vertex [12, 30] or
per person [3, 15, 19], following detection queries [11].
Recent progress is largely driven by data: large synthetic
datasets [7, 52] and joint training on many datasets [9, 43],
also with heterogeneous annotations [47]. Video-based meth-
ods further exploit temporal context [24, 25], and gravity-
aligned coordinates facilitate global motion recovery [48].
Depth cameras and scans.
Early depth-based approaches
label pixels with body parts [49], an idea that we adopt as
an auxiliary loss on voxels. Subsequent methods regress
SMPL parameters from PointNet++ features of partial point
clouds [21, 55]; VoteHMR [31] lets points vote for joints,
inspired by Hough voting for object detection [42], and
PointHPS [10] refines point features in a cascaded architec-
ture. In contrast, we employ voting only to localize the body
and estimate the pose with query tokens. Another line of
work fits a body model to complete scans, through predicted
correspondences and optimization [4, 5, 27, 29, 56] or equiv-
ariant regression [17], whereas we target single-view sensor
data and a single forward pass.
LiDAR-based motion capture.
LiDARCap [28] pio-
neered long-range motion capture from LiDAR point se-
quences, and LiDAR-HMR [16] reconstructs the mesh from
a single scan. LiveHPS [45] and LiveHPS++ [44] estimate
pose, shape and translation from windows of 32 frames, each
subsampled to 256 points, in free environments with noise
and occlusion. Sparkle [46] proposes a representation that is
evaluated on LiDAR and depth-camera benchmarks with a
separate model for each, and BMLiCap [1] models the degra-
dation of sparse scans. In autonomous driving, 3D keypoints
of pedestrians are estimated from LiDAR alone [60] or to-
gether with images [62]. In contrast to these sensor-specific
and mostly sequence-based methods, we show that a single
model trained across sensor families is more accurate on
their benchmarks already from a single frame.
Point cloud backbones.
PointNet++ [40, 41], the encoder
of most methods above, operates on a few hundred to a
few thousand sampled points. 3D scene understanding has
moved to sparse voxel convolutions [13, 20] and point trans-
formers [57, 58, 65], which scale to all points of a scan; we
adopt the encoder of LitePT [61], which applies convolutions
at fine resolutions and attention at coarse ones.

Problem setting.
The input is the point cloud P

translate P to the centre c of its bounding box and voxelize
it at 2 cm, keeping one point per voxel; the point coordi-

the SMPL-X model [39] in the neutral locked-head variant
of BEDLAM2 [52]: 16 shape coefficients β, the rotations
R0, . . . , R21 ∈SO(3) of the root and of the 21 body joints,
each relative to its parent, and the position t of the pelvis
joint in the centred frame, so that the pelvis in the sensor
frame is t + c.
3.1. Sparse-voxel encoder
We adopt the encoder of LitePT [61], a five-stage point trans-
former on sparse voxels, and discard its U-Net decoder, since
a body model has no per-point output. Each stage halves
the resolution by grid max-pooling, which yields cells of 2,
4, 8, 16 and 32 cm with 36, 72, 144, 252 and 504 channels.
Stages 1–3 consist of two submanifold sparse convolution
blocks [20] each, whereas stages 4 and 5 consist of 6 and 2
pre-norm transformer blocks, which attend within patches of
up to 1024 voxels serialized along space-filling curves [58]
and use rotary embeddings [51] of the grid positions.
This design accommodates the density gap between sen-
sors: at 2 cm, the median cloud contains 2.0–2.7k voxels for
the depth-camera datasets, 0.8k for SLOPER4D and 0.26k
for FreeMotion, whereas at stage 4 every sensor is reduced
to 45–75 voxels of 16 cm, few enough for a single attention
patch to cover the whole person and fine enough to separate
the limbs.
3.2. Per-joint query decoder
Instead of regressing all parameters from one pooled fea-
ture, we introduce T = 24 learned query tokens Q =
[qβ, qt, q0, . . . , q21] ∈RT ×252, shared across inputs: one
for the shape, one for the translation and one for each body
joint. The tokens pass through D4 = 4 decoder blocks after
the encoder blocks of stage 4, are projected to 504 channels,
and pass through D5 = 8 further blocks after the encoder
blocks of stage 5. Given the voxel features F of a cloud at
the respective stage, a decoder block updates the tokens of
this cloud as

Prediction heads.
The shape is predicted from the shape
token as β = ¯β + Wβqβ, where ¯β is the mean training
shape. All joint tokens are decoded by the same linear layer

mean rotation of joint j. With a shared WR, the head has
no joint-specific parameters, and the identity of a joint is
encoded by its query alone.

Voxel part segmentation.
As auxiliary supervision, a lin-
ear layer on the stage-4 voxel features predicts one of 23
classes: 22 body parts, defined by the dominant skinning
weight of each vertex (fingers are assigned to the wrists and
the face to the head), or background. The label of a voxel is
the part of the nearest of 2,000 ground-truth vertices selected
by farthest point sampling, or background if this vertex is
farther than 5 cm, and LS is the cross-entropy over the vox-
els. This task encourages every voxel to encode the body
part it lies on, which is the information that the joint tokens
query; the head is discarded at test time.
4. Unified Training Across Sensors
The architecture of Sec. 3 contains no sensor-specific com-
ponent, so generalization across sensors has to come from
the data. We unify ten sources that differ in sensor, clothing,
subjects and annotation (Tab. 1), balance them, and train in
two stages.

The sources are annotated with
SMPL [33] (HuMMan, FreeMotion, SLOPER4D, RELI11D,
CAPE), SMPL-H (BEHAVE), SMPL-X (EgoBody) or
MHR [18] (FORM-HOI), often with gendered models and
10 shape coefficients, whereas BEDLAM2 and AMASS al-
ready use the neutral locked-head SMPL-X model with 16
coefficients. We refit all other sources to this model by opti-
mizing pose, shape and translation against the source mesh

Sources and splits.
BEDLAM2 provides depth render-
ings of clothed people captured by moving cameras; as it
replays the same motions on many bodies, we exclude from
training every track whose poses are within 2◦of a BED-
LAM2 validation frame or of the AMASS evaluation sets.
AMASS contributes pose diversity: we ray-cast its body
meshes from a virtual Azure Kinect and, for 75% of the
clouds, replace the body shape by a random one, which
decorrelates pose and shape. CAPE contributes real clothing
in motion; its registered 4D scans are ray-cast in the same
way. We split HuMMan by subject (295 for training, 42 for
testing), since a per-cloud split would place the 10 views
of a labelled frame on both sides, and hold out 9 subjects
of FORM-HOI. EgoBody, BEHAVE, RELI11D and CAPE
keep their standard splits, and FreeMotion and SLOPER4D
follow LiveHPS [45]. All clouds with at least 50 points are
used for training.
Balanced sampling.
The raw union is highly imbalanced:
FORM-HOI accounts for 48% of all clouds and BEHAVE
for 1%. We assign each source other than BEDLAM2 and
AMASS a fixed number of draws per epoch, 1.25–2 per
labelled frame (0.4 for FORM-HOI, whose 410k frames
show only 13 people), and let these two sources share the
remaining draws in proportion to the square root of their
size [2], which amounts to three quarters of every epoch.
Gravity-aware augmentation.
With probability 0.85, we
rotate the cloud about the vertical axis by a uniform angle and
then tilt the vertical axis by up to 15◦in a random direction.
The tilt covers calibration errors and sloped ground, while
the direction of gravity, which disambiguates sparse scans, is
preserved; uniform rotations over SO(3) discard it (Sec. 5.3).
Before the rotation, we apply point dropout, Gaussian jitter
and outlier points and, for depth clouds with probability
0.05, subsampling to 250–2000 points, with strengths set per
sensor group.
Optimization.
We train with Muon [22] in the variant of
Liu et al. [32] for the weight matrices and with AdamW [34]
for the remaining parameters. The peak learning rate is
4.4 · 10−4 for the encoder and decoder blocks and 4.4 · 10−3
for all other parameters, with weight decay 0.05, a one-cycle
schedule [50], batch size 128 and mixed precision [37].
Two-stage training.
In the first stage, the network without
memory is trained on single frames for 12 epochs from ran-
dom initialization. In the second stage, the memory is added
and training continues for 4 epochs at a quarter of the learn-
ing rate, on batches in which 70% of the clouds form win-
dows of 8 consecutive frames, 0.1–0.3 s apart, and 30% are
independent frames; every frame of a window is supervised
with its own causal history. The two stages take 5.8 and 2.2
hours on one RTX 4070 Ti SUPER and yield SparseHPS-T.
All ablations in Sec. 5 are single-frame models trained as
in the first stage; their final model, SparseHPS-S, is trained
without CAPE.
5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental setup
Datasets.
We evaluate on nine test sets, one per source
of the ablations, grouped by sensor: synthetic (the official
AMASS test subsets, ray-cast from fixed virtual cameras,
and the BEDLAM2 validation subjects), depth cameras (the
official test splits of EgoBody and BEHAVE and the held-
out subjects of HuMMan and FORM-HOI) and LiDAR (the
LiveHPS test splits of SLOPER4D and FreeMotion and the
official test split of RELI11D). All clouds with at least 10
points are evaluated; SparseHPS-T is additionally evaluated
on 8-frame windows of the same sets.
Metrics.
On our test sets, we report SMPL-X errors: the
mean vertex error without alignment (MVE, mm), which in-
cludes the error of the global position, its Procrustes-aligned
counterpart (PA-MVE), and the mean geodesic error of the
22 predicted rotations (JAE, degrees). For the compari-
son with published methods, we follow the LiveHPS pro-
tocol [1, 44–46] on SMPL [33], with the original SMPL
annotations as ground truth and the predicted SMPL-X mesh
converted to the SMPL topology by the standard deforma-
tion transfer. J-Err(L) and V-Err(L) denote the mean joint
and vertex errors after subtracting the root joint, without

5.3. Ablation on network design
We start from a plain regressor and add one component at a
time, training every variant on the nine-source mixture (no
CAPE, no memory) for 12 epochs (Tab. 4). The baseline
(row 1) applies the same encoder and decoder blocks to a
single token, with linear heads, ℓ1 losses and uniform SO(3)
rotation augmentation.
Query tokens (row 2). Separate tokens for the shape,
the translation and every joint, decoded by a shared joint
head, reduce MVE by 1.5 mm and PA-MVE by 0.4 mm at
a nearly unchanged number of parameters, with the largest
gain on the synthetic sets (−3.0 mm). Voxel voting (row 3)
reduces MVE by a further 1.1 mm (−1.6 mm on the LiDAR
sets) while PA-MVE remains unchanged, which confirms
that it improves the localization of the body rather than its
pose. Auxiliary supervision (row 4). The global-rotation
loss and the voxel part segmentation yield the largest single
improvement, −2.3 mm MVE and −0.8 mm PA-MVE, con-
sistently across sensor groups; as both were added in one
run, their individual contributions cannot be separated. Loss
form (rows 5–6). Replacing the element-wise ℓ1 rotation
losses with the Frobenius form changes the means by only
0.1 mm per step, which is within run-to-run variation; we
claim no gain and adopt this form because it depends on
the error angle alone. Gravity-aware augmentation (row 7).
Replacing uniform SO(3) rotations with the yaw–tilt aug-
mentation reduces MVE by 2.2 mm and benefits every group,
most notably the sparse FreeMotion clouds (−8.0 mm) and
the synthetic sets (−2.4 mm).
Overall, the chain reduces MVE by 7.3 mm (17%) and
PA-MVE by 1.9 mm without adding a block.

5.5. Effect of the token memory
Tab. 6 compares SparseHPS-T with the single-frame net-
work obtained after the first training stage. With its history
disabled, SparseHPS-T matches this network on the win-
dows and on the mean of the nine single-frame test sets
(36.0 mm); the second stage thus preserves the single-frame
accuracy on average, although the error on the AMASS test
set increases from 11.7 to 13.0 mm. With the history, MVE,
JAE and the acceleration error [24] decrease by 1.4%, 2.1%
and 5.3% with respect to the first-stage network, on 8, 9 and
9 of the nine sets. The gain depends on the quality of the
current frame: 0.7% for clouds of more than 1000 points,
3–5% for clouds of fewer than 200 points, and 11% when the
current frame is thinned to a tenth of its points. On top of an
encoder that processes all points, temporal context therefore
mainly improves smoothness and robustness to degraded
frames rather than accuracy.
5.6. Robustness and efficiency
Sparse clouds.
On FreeMotion, SparseHPS-T stays be-
low 36 mm J-Err(L) for clouds of at least 200 points and
reaches 63 mm at 50–100 points. Keeping sparse scans in
training is essential: a single-frame model trained without
them matches SparseHPS-S above 200 points but reaches
only 96 mm at 50–100 points and flips the body (J-Err(L)
above 300 mm) in 3.1% of them, against 0.7%. Below 50
points (2.4% of FreeMotion), SparseHPS-T obtains 173 mm
and flips 7% of the clouds (13% without its history).
Efficiency.
SparseHPS-T has 50.0M parameters, about
half the size of the released LiveHPS model (105.7M) and be-
low the 82.3M reported for Sparkle [46]; PointNet++-based
single-frame models are smaller (PointHPS: 4.7M [10]).
On an RTX 4090, a single cloud takes 10 ms including the
SMPL-X layer, for dense and sparse clouds alike, and the
memory adds 6–29% for an 8-frame window.
6. Conclusion
We have presented SparseHPS, a unified network for human
pose and shape estimation from range data. A LitePT en-
coder with per-joint query tokens, a shared joint head, voxel
voting and a causal token memory, trained on ten sources
in one label space, serves depth cameras and LiDAR with
a single set of weights. A single frame already surpasses
published sequence-based methods on LiDAR benchmarks,
while the memory adds smoothness and robustness.
Limitations.
SparseHPS estimates one person per cloud
and requires this person to be segmented from the scene, as
do all compared methods; on several datasets the crop relies
on released masks or labels. It requires the vertical axis to
within about 15◦, does not estimate hands or the face, and
often fails below 50 points. The comparison with published
methods is not fully controlled: we train on more sources,
