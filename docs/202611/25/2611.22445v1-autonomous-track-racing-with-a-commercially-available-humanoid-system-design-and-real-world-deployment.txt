Abstract—Learning-based methods now let humanoids run
robustly, and they have begun to compete in athletic events. Yet
few reports describe a complete autonomous humanoid running
system in an actual race, where the robot must follow a lane at a
new venue, pace itself within hardware limits, and finish without
intervention. We report such a system deployed on the Unitree
G1, a commercially available general-purpose humanoid, in the
100 m, 400 m, and 1500 m events of the World Humanoid Robot
Games 2026. The system integrates vision-based lane perception,
path tracking, and a running policy trained by reinforcement
learning with a human motion prior. To address the challenges of
humanoid running, we stabilize head-camera images against body
motion and use IMU-assisted leg odometry to bridge flight phases.
To manage motor heating, we penalize hip-pitch torque during
training, add heat-dissipation plates, and apply temperature-
based speed limiting with event-specific pacing. The robot placed
13th of 74 in the 100 m, 10th of 44 in the 400 m first round before
falling in the semifinal, and seventh of 35 in the 1500 m. In this
deployment, thermal margin set the pace in every event, and the
semifinal fall followed a lane-detection error.

Because the races allowed no restart and a fall could damage

Instead, we focus on system integration, design decisions, race
results, and observed failures.
Lane detection is well established in autonomous driv-
ing [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], while onboard vision has supported
terrain traversal and navigation in legged robots [6], [7].
Learning-based locomotion has advanced from quadrupeds to
humanoid running [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. Robot
competitions have also produced system-level reports [15],
[16], [17], [18], and a magazine report describes the hard-
ware of a half-marathon-winning humanoid but not its auton-
omy [19]. Reports of fully autonomous humanoid track racing
under real-world perception and thermal constraints remain
limited.
Without developing a race-specific body, we entered a
commercially available Unitree G1 in the 100 m, 400 m, and
1500 m events at the World Humanoid Robot Games (WHRG)
2026 in Beijing. The games were held on 22–26 August with
666 teams across all events. Our G1 placed 13th of 74 entrants
in the 100 m at 24.3 s, was 10th of 44 in the 400 m first round
at 1:55.93 before falling in the semifinal, and placed seventh
of 35 in the 1500 m at 7:43.89.

tracking, and learned running control on a commercially
available humanoid (Sections III to V); (2) image stabilization
and ground-plane lane tracking against head-camera shake,

II. COMPETITION SETTING
The WHRG 2026 running events took place on an athletics
track laid over an indoor speed-skating oval in Beijing, so
its curves were tighter than those of standard athletics tracks,
which constrained the lookahead and turning radius (Sec-
tions IV and V). We entered the 100 m and 400 m small-class
races (height up to 1.4 m) and the open 1500 m race (Table I).
The rules required the robot to run autonomously, with
no external control after the start, making onboard sensing
the source of all steering commands (Section IV). The only
operator input was the start command, issued just after the
official start, so the official times include this delay.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
We used a commercially available 29-DoF Unitree G1;
Fig. 1 numbers the components we added or programmed.
An added Jetson AGX Orin (marker 1), which we call PC3,
processes images from a head-mounted Intel RealSense D555
(marker 2) and generates the forward-speed and yaw-rate
command (v, ω) (Section IV); the name PC3 follows the G1’s
built-in PC1 and PC2. The D555 replaces the stock camera
for its wider field of view and supplies RGB and depth
images at 1280×720 and 15 Hz. The command is transmitted
over Ethernet using ROS 2 [20] to the built-in Jetson Orin
NX (PC2, marker 3), which executes the running policy and
thermal speed limiter (Sections V and VI). As (v, ω) is the
sole interface between perception and locomotion, we tuned
the running policy with manually issued commands before
integrating autonomy. We also added heat-dissipation plates to
the actuators. The modifications increased the manufacturer’s
stated mass of 35 kg [21] by 1.5 kg.
IV. LANE PERCEPTION AND PATH TRACKING
We adapted autonomous-driving techniques to humanoid
running, where two assumptions failed: moderate camera
motion (Section IV-A) and continuous ground contact for
odometry (Section IV-B). Given limited on-site tuning time,
we selected RGB-based lane detection rather than developing
a LiDAR-based pipeline. The head-mounted D555 provides
RGB images for lane detection, Ethernet connectivity to
avoid vibration-induced USB disconnections, stereo depth for
ground-plane estimation, and an IMU for image stabilization
and leveling. To rehearse the venue before arrival, we built a
replica of its track in MuJoCo [22] from the venue dimensions
and ran the same ROS 2 stack, from detection to the running
policy, in closed loop on rendered head-camera images.
A. Lane perception
We qualitatively compared GANet [3], BezierLaneNet [23],
UFLDv2 [24], and CLRerNet [5] on stadium images. CLRer-
Net produced the most stable lane detections for the ground-
plane tracking described below and was therefore selected. We
attribute this to its use of global image context: track lanes are
spaced more narrowly than road lanes, and keypoint-based
detectors [3] tended to merge them in their low-resolution
heatmaps. We initialized its ResNet-34 backbone [25] from
CULane [1] weights and fine-tuned it on 3000 real and 300
synthetic frames. The real frames were recorded at 30 outdoor
stadiums by a person running with an action camera, not by the
robot. The synthetic indoor-venue frames were rendered from
the replica and made photorealistic with an image-generation
model, because a detector trained on real frames alone made
many errors on them. A further 500 real and 200 synthetic
frames were reserved for validation. Augmentation varied
brightness, color, scale, and rotation while preserving the
annotated lanes’ bottom-to-top direction, which the detector
assumes. The detector runs in TensorRT FP16 at 17.6 ms per
frame, supporting 15 Hz operation.
The first failed assumption was that image motion is mod-
erate, which running violated in two ways. First, the head
camera’s roll oscillated around its intended orientation by 2.0–
3.3◦root mean square (RMS) during the races, causing the
apparent lane orientations to fluctuate. Second, a downward-
tilted camera shifted the vanishing point upward relative to its
position in the training images. To compensate for both, we
stabilize and level each frame before lane detection. Following
gyroscope-based video stabilization [26], we estimate the in-
tended orientation using a Madgwick filter [27] and a low-pass
filter with a 0.5 s time constant. A pure-rotation homography
then removes the deviation from this intended orientation.
We apply the same smoothing to the camera pose used for
ground-plane projection, preventing lane oscillations caused by
small phase mismatches between the stabilized image and the
estimated pose. Training and testing without leveling visibly
degraded detection, although we did not quantify this effect.
Stabilization and roll leveling were used in all races, while
camera-pitch correction for the second issue was introduced
before the 100 m race (Section VII).
Tracking lanes directly in the image proved unreliable
because lane detections were occasionally missed or occluded
by other robots. In the image, camera motion shifts each lane
from frame to frame, so earlier detections cannot directly fill
these gaps. We therefore project detections onto the ground
plane and accumulate them in the odometry frame (Sec-
tion IV-B), where lanes stay nearly fixed because robot motion
is compensated, so detections from successive frames can be
combined. The ground plane is estimated robustly from depth
pixels with upward-facing surface normals. When insufficient
valid depth data are available, such as in overexposed regions,
we instead estimate the plane using the camera IMU and
known camera height. The accumulation uses a 0.20 m voxel
grid. Voxel weights decay over time without new observations.
For each lane line, the accumulated points are then fitted with
a cubic polynomial extending up to 18 m ahead. Across the
three logged races, the detector identified at least four lane
lines in every frame, so no frame lacked detections entirely;
the accumulation only filled in lane lines that were missed or
occluded.

B. Path tracking
The second failed assumption was that ground contact is
continuous for odometry. The velocity estimate provided with
the G1 reverses sign during part of every step, so its integrated
position advances at only about 1 m/s on average during
running. We infer that its lagging contact detection keeps
trusting the foot that has just lifted off. Our own leg-kinematic
odometry therefore assumes a non-slipping stance foot [28],
[29], detects stance from foot height, and bridges flight phases
with the IMU. Over a 400 m lap of a practice track, the
odometry measured 377 m (a ratio of 0.94), which we judged
accurate enough for tracking the path relative to the detected
lanes. At the venue, an overall ratio that also includes path-
tracking offset and heading drift was about 0.90 (Appendix).
Shortly after the start, the nearest tracked lane line to
the right of the camera is selected, and the path is that
line shifted toward the lane center. Vector pursuit [30], an
extension of pure pursuit [31], tracks the path at 100 Hz with a
6.0 m lookahead and a 16.5 m minimum turning radius. These
settings kept the yaw-rate command within the ±0.4 rad/s
command range of the controller, without requiring the robot
to slow in curves.
C. Venue adaptation
The venue appeared in the training set only as synthetic
frames rendered from a replica built to its dimensions. The
venue was darker than the outdoor stadiums in the training
set, and reflections from the ceiling lights made the brightness
uneven along the track. We therefore recorded footage at the
venue, fixed the camera exposure and gain manually, and
confirmed on the footage that the detector found the lanes
correctly before using it in the races. We did not retrain the
detector on site, to avoid regressions.
V. RUNNING CONTROL
The running policy maps commanded speed and yaw rate
to joint position targets and feedforward torques.
A. Training
We trained the running policy in Isaac Lab [32], building
on Unitree’s open-source training code [33], in three stages:
PPO [34], PPO with an adversarial motion prior (AMP) [35],
[36], and final PPO fine-tuning. In simulation, the hip-pitch
torque saturated when policies were trained without AMP.
The AMP stage, which uses reference motions captured from
human runners and retargeted as in [37], [38], [39] (Appendix),
reduced this saturation. The reward included speed tracking
and a penalty on squared hip-pitch torque as a proxy for
resistive heating [40]. We targeted the hip pitch because it
was the hottest joint in practice runs, as it also was in
the 100 m race. Temperature was excluded from observations
and rewards because actuator thermal behavior is difficult to
simulate. The policy runs at 50 Hz using five steps of pro-
prioceptive, command, and action history. It outputs position
targets for 29 joints and feedforward torques for the hip-pitch
and knee joints. PC2 interpolates the position targets to 1 kHz
for MIT control [41]. We trained two policies, endurance
and sprint, with forward-speed commands from 0 up to the
curriculum levels reached by the deployed checkpoints, 5.46
and 6.20 m/s, respectively (Table I); the endurance policy’s
curve commands used ω = v/R for a sampled turning radius
R, whereas the 100 m sprint policy, run on a straight, used
none in its final training stage. Additional training settings are
provided in the Appendix.
B. Hardware-aware policy design
• Low stiffness with feedforward torque on the propulsive
joints. Prior work reports that the gains and action space
shape what a policy learns [42], [43], [44]. These studies
examine the effects of action spaces and controller gains,
but do not evaluate the combination of position targets
and feedforward torque used here. With position targets
alone, the PD stiffness must generate the propulsive
torque as well as track the targets, so we expected feedfor-
ward torque to drive these joints more efficiently. Across
the two policies, the hip-pitch and knee stiffness values
were 10.0–10.7 and 15.0–15.3 N · m/rad, respectively,
compared with 100 N · m/rad at hip roll and yaw.
• Thermal degradation in simulation. To account for a
possible reduction of the torque constant with heating,
we added to the dynamics randomization [45] a per-joint
first-order thermal model that lowers the torque constant
within an episode (Appendix). The policy cannot observe
this loss directly and must continue running despite it;
this randomization targets robustness, not heat reduction.
VI. THERMAL MANAGEMENT AND PACING
A. Heat as the key speed constraint
Sustained speed was limited primarily by motor heating.
Before mitigation, the firmware’s over-temperature protection
stopped the motors after approximately 50 m of running, caus-
ing a fall. We therefore treated thermal margin as a primary
constraint on race pacing. A half-marathon humanoid faced
a similar limitation but addressed it through running-specific
gearing and liquid cooling [19].
B. Three mitigations
• Policy-level. The reward penalizes torque as a proxy for
heat (Section V-A).
• Runtime limiter. We monitor the maximum motor-driver
MOSFET temperature across all joints (the motor-driver
temperature). Above 120 ◦C, the thermal cap decreases
toward 2.0 m/s over 4 s; at 127 ◦C, which is below the
observed 130 ◦C firmware cutoff, the robot is commanded
to stop. A race-director node on PC2 sends the speed cap,
the lower of the thermal and event-specific caps, to PC3,
where path tracking uses it as the target forward speed
v and recomputes ω = v/R to keep the path curvature;
PC2 also clips the policy’s forward-speed command.
• Hardware. Heat-dissipation plates on the hip-pitch, knee,
and ankle actuators conduct motor heat to the housing
(Section III).

C. Per-event operating points
Because heat accumulates over a race, we deliberately
operated the same robot at different points on the speed-
versus-completion-margin curve: the 100 m spent its thermal
margin on speed, the 400 m raised the event-specific cap only
for the last straight, and the 1500 m held one constant cap.
Apart from the camera-pitch correction added before the 100 m
(Section VII), two settings changed between events (Table I):
the policy and the event-specific cap, which was scheduled by
distance, time, and detected curve state.
VII. RESULTS AT WHRG 2026
The robot finished every race it started except the 400 m
semifinal, in which it fell (Table I); the motor-driver tempera-
ture remained a limiting factor (Fig. 2). The 400 m and 1500 m
races were held on 23 August and the 100 m on 25 August.
A. 400 m
The robot qualified in 10th place in the first round and did
not finish the semifinal. In the first round, 90.4 s after the start
command, the event-specific cap rose from 4.0 to 5.0 m/s for
the final sprint on the last straight. At system start-up before
the semifinal, lane detection appeared abnormal, and there was
no time to correct it during setup; in the race, the robot moved
diagonally to the right across its lane and fell approximately
5 s after the start. No log was recorded for this run because
of a technical problem, so the cause could not be identified.
The semifinal was run in lane 1, where detection and running
had worked in practice; one unverified hypothesis is that the
camera mount was bent in a fall during morning practice,
tilting the camera downward and shifting the vanishing point
relative to the training images. For the 100 m race, held on a
later day, we warped each image by the camera pitch estimated
from the camera IMU, keeping the vanishing point near the
center.
B. 1500 m
The motor-driver temperature rose from 53 ◦C at the start
command and peaked 460.6 s later, near the end of the plotted
interval, at 119 ◦C: 1 ◦C below the limiter trigger and 11 ◦C
below the observed 130 ◦C firmware cutoff. From 10 to 460 s
after the start command, the logged speed command stayed at
4.0 m/s, whereas the leg-odometry speed averaged 2.98 m/s,
1.02 m/s lower. The official time gives a mean speed of
3.23 m/s over the race distance, including the start, so the
robot ran below the command; the lower odometry speed
is consistent with the odometry scale error at the venue
(Section IV-B). Seven of the 35 entrants finished the race.
C. 100 m
The robot did not advance beyond the first round. Its
high-speed setting reached the limiter trigger. The motor-
driver temperature rose from 52 ◦C at the start command and
crossed the 120 ◦C limiter trigger 22.1 s after the command;
the limiter began lowering the speed cap from 5.6 m/s, and
the temperature peaked at 123 ◦C, 4 ◦C below the 127 ◦C stop
command. Because the logs are not aligned with the official
timing, the time lost to the slowdown is unknown.
VIII. CONCLUSION
At WHRG 2026, a commercially available Unitree G1
completed three of four autonomous races; it fell in the 400 m
semifinal after a lane-detection error whose cause could not
be identified. This experience provides four practical lessons
for real-world deployment. First, perception must withstand
body motion and occlusion: head-camera roll of 2.0–3.3◦
RMS motivated image stabilization and ground-plane lane
tracking, and lanes were detected in every logged race frame
(Section IV). Second, state estimation must bridge the flight
phases of running, which leg odometry with IMU bridging pro-
vided (Section IV). Third, adapting perception to a new venue
need not require retraining on site: in the three logged races,
fixing camera exposure and gain sufficed for a detector already
trained on synthetic frames of the venue (Section IV). Finally,
reliable running benefits from hardware-aware policy training
and thermal management (Sections V and VI). Together, these
findings identify perception robustness, state estimation, venue
adaptation, and thermal management as key barriers to reliable
humanoid running in real-world environments.

LEG ODOMETRY IMPLEMENTATION AND ACCURACY
Our odometry detects stance when a foot is within 2 cm of
its lowest height over the preceding 0.8 s. During stance, foot-
kinematic velocity and IMU-based prediction are combined
with weights of 0.8 and 0.2, respectively; during flight, the
estimate relies on the IMU alone. On a standard 400 m practice
track, the odometry measured 377 m per lap, corresponding
to a distance scale of 0.94. At the competition venue, we
further assessed its consistency using the recorded trajectories.
Because heading drift rotated the estimated oval between
laps, we segmented laps by accumulated heading change and
fitted circles to individual curve passes. In the 1500 m race,
three complete laps measured 352.3, 345.1, and 349.3 m, with
a standard deviation of 1.0 %. The estimated curve radius
averaged 24.20 m across seven passes. The corresponding
lane-center dimensions were 387.3 m per lap and 26.49 m in
radius, giving an overall scale ratio of approximately 0.90.
This ratio includes not only odometry error but also path-
tracking offset, heading drift, and circle-fitting approximation.
LANE DETECTOR TRAINING AND TRACKING
CLRerNet uses a ResNet-34 backbone initialized from
CULane weights, with an input resolution of 960 × 960.
The training and validation sets contained 3000 and 500
real stadium frames, respectively, and 300 and 200 synthetic
frames. The synthetic frames reproduced indoor-venue features
absent from the outdoor training images, including ceiling
lights, advertising boards, and spectators. They were ren-
dered using a replica of the competition track and made
photorealistic with Gemini (gemini-3.1-flash-image,
Google). We did not quantify the contribution of synthetic
images to detection performance at the venue. For ground-
plane lane tracking, voxel weights decay by a factor of 0.95
per processed frame, and each lane line is fitted once at least
eight voxels are available. Voxels are keyed by their position
in the odometry frame and are not moved afterward; voxels
more than 5 m behind the camera, 18 m ahead of it, or 8 m
to either side are discarded. If the camera position jumps by
2 m or more between frames, which we treat as an odometry
reset, all accumulated voxels are discarded. Lane IDs are fixed
approximately 2 s after the start. When no lanes are detected,
the last estimated path is retained.

Rewards and commands
In the final stage, only the sprint policy, used on the straight
100 m, received no curve commands (Table S2). The reward
penalized squared hip-pitch torque as a proxy for heating,
without using temperature. Squared knee, hip-yaw, and ankle
torques were also penalized, so that the load did not simply
shift from the hip pitch to other joints. Table S2 gives these
torque weights for each policy. An energy penalty based on

Dynamics randomization
Table S3 lists the randomized parameters and the con-
stants of the thermal model. The thermal model described in
Section V reduced the torque constant according to a first-
order model driven by the squared ratio of joint torque to its
limit. The per-joint factors in Table S3 were drawn uniformly.
The model scaled each joint’s total output torque. The 28 %
reduction and both time constants were assumed rather than
measured. Episodes terminated when the base height fell
below 0.2 m or the tilt exceeded 0.8 rad.
SPEED LIMITS AT DEPLOYMENT
The thermal cap (Section VI) recovers over 3 s once the
motor-driver temperature falls back to 90 ◦C, which never
occurred during a race. Path tracking changes the target for-
ward speed v at no more than 2.0 m/s2 upward and 1.0 m/s2
downward.
