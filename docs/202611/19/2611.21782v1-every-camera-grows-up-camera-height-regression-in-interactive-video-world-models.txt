## Abstract 

Interactive video world models serve as simulators for em-bodied agents: from a starting image and an action tra-jectory, they generate the views the agent would see. The starting image fixes how high the camera is, for example 0.15 m for a floor robot or 2.2 m for an overhead camera, and a faithful simulator must keep this height as the camera moves. We show that six recent open-weight models fail to do so: as motion replaces the starting view with generated content, they move the camera toward human eye level, a failure we call camera-height regression . Our benchmark, 

EYE HEIGHT , pairs six rendered elevations (0.15 m to 2.2 m) and four calibrated real-world rig heights with scale-free multi-view height estimation. All six models lose their initial height during a 180° turn-and-walk rollout: initialized at 0.15 m, only 0–20% of clips end within 1.3× of the starting height as inferred viewpoints converge to human heights of 1.1 m to 1.5 m, whereas ground-truth renders remain within a few percent. Regression grows with rota-tion angle, begins while the start frame remains in context, and largely reverses in SANA-WM when a 360° turn restores the initial view. ## 1. Introduction 

Action-conditioned video generators that synthesize future frames from a starting image and a camera trajectory now serve as interactive world models, promoted as simulators 1arXiv:2611.21782v1 [cs.CV] 16 Nov 2026 in which embodied agents preview the visual consequences of their actions [3, 12, 33, 50]. The trajectory determines how the camera moves, while the starting image determines where the camera is, including its height above the floor and its tilt. A robot vacuum at 0.15 m and a standing adult at 1.6 m see the same room differently, and visuomotor policies act on the view they receive. To simulate a specific body, a world model must therefore keep the camera height of its starting frame. This paper shows that six recent open-weight world mod-els do not meet this requirement. As motion replaces the starting view with generated content, they move the camera toward human eye level, so that low cameras rise and high cameras sink (Fig. 1a). We call this failure camera-height regression , measure it across the six models, trace it to its causes, and test remedies. To measure it, we introduce E YE HEIGHT , which drives the models along constant-height, constant-pitch trajectories from rendered indoor starts (0.15 m to 2.2 m) and calibrated multi-height real photographs (Fig. 1b), and recovers camera height from the generated frames with scale-free multi-view 3D reconstruction. After a 180° turn in place and a forward walk, all six models lose their starting height and drift to-ward 1.1 m to 1.5 m (Fig. 1c). From 0.15 m, only 0–20% of rollouts end within 1.3× of the starting height, whereas ground-truth renders remain within a few percent. Overhead cameras (2.2 m) sink toward the same band ( ×0.63 –×0.85 

in five models), and on real photographs all four models rotating past 100° regress as well. Comparing 3D recon-struction with floor homographies shows that Matrix-Game turns are geometrically split: local 2D floor motion stays consistent with constant elevation, while newly outpainted walls and furniture imply a higher viewpoint. Controlled experiments trace the regression to scene pri-ors learned in pretraining and strengthened during sampling (Fig. 2). Public internet and game videos cluster near 1.6 m with floor-level views nearly absent, which pulls both low and high starts toward this mode. The drift grows with rota-tion angle and begins while the start frame is still in context, so keeping that frame does not prevent it. In SANA-WM, the drift largely reverses when a 360° turn brings the starting view back. Supplying metric height to SANA-WM does not stop the drift either. Textual height cues have no effect, and a fine-tuned floor-disparity branch responds to counterfactual height scaling with a slope of only 0.03 , where faithful use would give 1. Stronger classifier-free text guidance deepens the regression, and most of this effect comes from the first half of the denoising steps. The drift matters for embodied control (Fig. 1d): coun-terfactually re-rendering ground-truth scenes at each roll-out’s drifted height and pitch shifts the path endpoints pre-dicted by seven navigation policies by 0.12 m to 1.18 m, more than mild blur does on five of them. Among remedies for SANA-WM, unguided sampling lifts real-start retention from β = 0 .36 to 0.56 at unchanged MUSIQ and slightly weaker caption alignment. Adding height-balanced LoRA fine-tuning reaches β = 0 .68 , although rollouts still drift toward a lower default height. It costs 5 MUSIQ points, a loss that arises in the same early steps that set height, and post-training RL adds little. Our contributions are as follows. • We introduce E YE HEIGHT , a benchmark for camera-height preservation with rendered starts from 0.15 m to 2.2 m and calibrated real starts, together with a scale-free height measurement validated on constant and drifting ren-ders and cross-checked with VGGT, monocular depth, and floor homographies. • We show that all six open world models regress toward human eye level from both low and high starts, that Matrix-Game turns separate floor motion from scene scale, and that the drift alone shifts the predictions of seven naviga-tion policies. • We link the regression to human-height video priors, show that neither keeping the start frame in context nor supply-ing height inputs prevents it, and identify early-step text guidance as a factor that strengthens it. • We evaluate fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and guid-ance schedules on SANA-WM and find that height re-tention and perceptual quality are coupled in the early denoising steps. We will publicly release E YE HEIGHT and the code. 

## 2. Related Work 

Interactive video world models. Action-conditioned video generators have evolved from closed-domain games [1, 3, 35] to open-domain scenes synthesized from a single im-age [7, 12, 25, 33, 41, 50], alongside hybrid pipelines that render explicit 3D caches along camera paths [15, 28]. Ex-isting benchmarks score visual quality, action adherence, and 3D consistency [6, 16, 45], test generalization to unseen robot bodies visible in the frame [5], or fix one non-human vantage point such as UAV video [8]. E YE HEIGHT comple-ments them by sweeping the starting camera height to test whether a model keeps the sensor elevation of the body that carries the camera. 

Camera control and viewpoint priors. Pose-conditioned video diffusion typically encodes trajectories as relative Pl¨ ucker rays [2, 9, 40], which fix inter-frame motion but leave floor elevation and gravity tilt implicit in perspec-tive. Canonical-camera biases also affect object novel-view synthesis [24] under non-standard elevations or focal lengths [21], and errors under out-of-distribution scene mo-tion have motivated explicit geometric conditioning [46], unified lens encodings [17], and gravity-grounded world states [23]. ## 3. Benchmarking Camera-Height Preservation 

An interactive world model takes an initial image I0, an optional prompt, and relative camera poses or actions to synthesize future frames. Geometrically, I0 fixes the agent’s embodiment: its height h0 above the floor and its pitch. Ev-ery trajectory in E YE HEIGHT restricts motion to horizontal translation and in-place yaw, holding true height and pitch constant, and E YE HEIGHT measures whether rollouts pre-serve h0 or drift. 

Camera trajectories. Each script spans 160 frames be-yond I0 with zero vertical motion. Forward translates ahead at 1.5 cm/frame (2.4 m total). Turn rotates rightward in place at 2.8°/frame for 64 frames (180°) before walking forward for 96 frames (1.44 m): the rotation forces outpainting of the unseen room behind the camera, while the walk adds parallax to probe 3D consistency with h0. To study rotation angle and context, we add turns of 45°, 90°, 135°, and 360° at 2.8°/frame (each followed by the same walk), a half-rate slow turn (180° over 128 frames, then 32 forward), and a static script. 

Synthetic and real starting views. With Habitat-Sim [29, 34], we render synthetic starts across seven indoor scenes: five ReplicaCAD apartments with baked lighting [34], a Matterport3D house [4], and a Habitat castle hall. At three open spots per scene, we mount cameras at six embodiment heights: 0.15 m (robot vacuum), 0.3 m (pet/small robot), 0.6 m, 1.0 m (child/wheeled robot), 1.6 m (standing adult), and 2.2 m (mounted/aerial sensor). Two spots lack clear-ance at 2.2 m, leaving 124 pinhole starts ( 1280 × 704 , 70° horizontal FoV, 10° downward pitch, zero roll), paired with ground-truth renders for all 124 forward starts and 59 turn starts (ten spots). To evaluate photographs free of rendering gaps, we curate 52 real starts from Eyeful Tower [44], taking 13 spots across five scenes at four vertical rig tiers (0.36 m to 0.46 m, 0.63 m to 0.92 m, 1.01 m to 1.32 m, and 1.64 m to 1.72 m) and reprojecting each photograph via dataset calibra-tion into our synthetic pinhole camera so that starts at a spot differ only in height. 

Prompts and models. Prompted models share one elevation-neutral room caption per scene across all heights. We prepend “A first-person view” for LingBot-World and Matrix-Game 3.0, SANA-WM’s standard prefix “A first-person view from a strictly stationary observation point,” and “First-person view” plus a one-sentence motion sum-mary for Wan2.2. Scale-free multi-view height measurement and metrics. 

Because monocular metric depth priors pull extreme views toward human height, we measure within-clip height ratios 

from a unified multi-view reconstruction: Pi3 [39] recon-structs every fourth frame in one pass under a shared global scale while reading executed yaw. Segmenting floor and rug pixels via ADE20K-trained SegFormer-B5 [43, 48] and fitting a plane to the lowest dense horizontal ground layer gives the reconstruction-inferred height h(t). The terminal ratio r = h(T )/h (0) (h(T ): median over the final 20% of frames) cancels scene scale, giving hend = h0 r. Clips lack-ing a reconstructable scene (no initial floor) count as failing to keep height, while continuous fits use measurable clips. We validate this measurement on ground-truth renders and against three other estimators (supplementary material). On constant-height renders, Pi3 recovers β = 0 .95 on the turn script and 0.99 on the forward script, and it follows synthetic 

2× and 0.6× height ramps during the turn to within 12%. VGGT [37] reads the same constant renders at β = 1 .03 and 

1.01 and confirms the regression of every model ( β = 0 .15 –

0.51 , against 0.18 –0.67 with Pi3). MoGe-2 [38] estimates the calibrated heights of real starts with median errors of 3 cm to 9 cm. Tracked floor homographies, which measure how much height change the floor motion alone supports, match constant renders within 3% and drifting renders within 9%. Per clip we report | log r| and whether the clip keeps its height ( | log r| < log 1 .3). Per model and script we fit 

log hend = β log h0 + (1 − β) log h∗, (1) or hend = h∗(h0/h ∗)β , by least squares, which gives the 

retention β ∈ [0 , 1] (1: ideal, 0: collapse) and the default height h∗ (undefined near β = 1 ). For example, with β =0.5 and h∗ = 1 .3 m , a 0.15 m start would end at 0.44 m and a 2.2 m start at 1.69 m. Tables report 95% intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples over start spots. Viewpoint drift biases visual navigation. To isolate the camera pose, we re-render the true 3D scene along each roll-out’s executed yaw twice, at the start height and pitch and at the height and pitch that Pi3 measures in the rollout (Fig. 1d). On both renders we run seven navigation policies every 8 walk frames of both scripts: GNM [30], ViNT [31], No-MaD [32] in exploration and image-goal modes, MBRA [13], S2E [11], and FlowPilot [10]. The pose drift alone moves the predicted path endpoint by 0.12 m to 1.18 m, depending on policy and world model. This is 1.6 to 6.2 times our measurement-noise floor and 29–64% of the shift caused by the generated video itself. For GNM, ViNT, both NoMaD modes, and MBRA, the shift exceeds that of a mild blur (half resolution, Gaussian σ = 1 .5 px, supplementary material). 

## 5. Why Cameras Regress 

Public video concentrates near human eye level. We measure camera heights in two public corpora (Fig. 2, mid-dle): with MoGe-2 on 339 of 1,500 random OpenVid-1M [26] clips that show the floor, and with ground-truth depth on 161 of 196 OmniWorld-Game [49] scenes. On these scenes, MoGe-2 heights match the ground truth at a median ratio of 1.01. Both corpora concentrate near standing adult eye level, with medians of 1.56 m and 1.72 m. Views near our lowest starts are almost absent (0–1% of clips within 20% of 0.15 m or 0.3 m), whereas 20–34% of OpenVid-1M clips lie within 20% of the six default heights h∗ (Tab. 1). Elevated views are common (17% and 26% of clips within 20% of 2.2 m), yet 2.2 m starts still sink toward h∗. The regression therefore reflects a pull toward the most frequent training heights, since it also affects viewpoints that are common in the training data. 

Context retention does not anchor height. If the models drifted because they forget I0, the drift would start only after I0 leaves their context, but it starts while I0 is still there. LingBot-World keeps its first six latent frames as an attention sink, yet from 0.15 m it rises by ×1.58 by the end of a commanded 135° turn (Fig. 5), and SANA-WM, which attends to all frames, reaches ×4.49 after 90°. Halving the turn speed of Matrix-Game 2.0/3.0 keeps I0 in view twice as long, but the cameras still rise by ×1.26 and ×1.23 by the end of the turn.

## 6. Mitigations and Quality Trade-offs 

Section 5 points to two causes that can be acted on: low viewpoints are nearly absent from training videos, and early text guidance strengthens the pull toward eye level. We there-fore evaluate three remedies on SANA-WM (Tab. 2, Fig. 8): height-balanced fine-tuning to supply missing low-elevation views, online reinforcement learning rewarding multi-view height retention, and early-step guidance scheduling. Com-bining fine-tuning with unguided sampling preserves the most height but lowers perceptual quality, a loss that arises in the first half of denoising, where camera height is also set. 

With guidance, fine-tuning helps mainly on synthetic starts and RL adds little. We render 81-frame ground-truth turns from 258 new starts in our seven synthetic scenes (42–44 per height, at least 1.5 m from benchmark spots, two per height held out for validation). On these clips we train a rank-32 LoRA on SANA-WM’s self-attention, cross-attention, and camera layers with its unguided flow-matching loss and keep epoch 1 (64 steps). On synthetic starts, fine-tuning doubles retention from β = 0 .18 to 0.35 . It also lowers the default height from 1.32 m to 0.83 m, so 2.2 m starts sink further, to ×0.50 . Fine-tuning on eye-level (1.6 m) views alone gives no gain at the same number of steps. On real Eyeful Tower photos, default guidance brings the prior back: retention reaches only β = 0 .40 , and forward retention drops from 0.85 to 0.73 . We then continue training with DiffusionNFT [47] on a Pi3 reward that penalizes errors in height, pitch, and yaw as well as low-confidence pixels (supplementary material). ## 7. Conclusion 

The six open-weight interactive video world models we eval-uate do not keep the camera height of their starting frame. On E YE HEIGHT , all six pull both floor-level and overhead viewpoints toward human eye level during rotational out-painting, and this drift alone shifts the trajectories predicted by visual navigation policies. In Matrix-Game, the result-ing rollouts are geometrically split: local floor motion stays consistent with constant elevation while newly outpainted room structure implies a higher camera. We trace this pull to human-eye-level priors in large-scale video corpora: in SANA-WM, neither retaining the start frame in context nor supplying explicit textual or dense geometric height inputs prevents it, whereas early-step classifier-free text guidance strengthens it. 

Limitations and outlook. Because generated rollouts lack ground-truth 3D geometry, our primary metric relies on multi-view floor reconstruction and requires visible ground surfaces, which we cross-validate against VGGT, monocular metric depth, and learning-free floor homographies. The navigation analysis measures shifts in predicted paths in open loop, and closed-loop effects remain untested. Our training and guidance interventions also center on SANA-WM, and we leave remedies for causal streaming models to future work. Finally, when we combine fine-tuning with unguided sampling, the quality loss arises in the same early 8denoising steps that set camera height, so this post-hoc fix trades one for the other. Height-diverse pretraining data and guidance that does not strengthen the eye-level prior may avoid this trade-off, and world-model benchmarks should report camera-height retention alongside trajectory adher-ence.
