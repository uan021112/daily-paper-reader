## Abstract 

Advancing from individual embodied intelligence to swarm embodied intelli-gence is a key step toward scalable, general-purpose embodied intelligence. Com-plex tasks often require multiple agents to divide responsibilities, coordinate han-dovers, and manipulate shared objects collaboratively. However, existing robot learning methods rely primarily on individual demonstrations, which provide limited supervision for modeling dependencies among agent actions, while col-lecting multi-robot demonstrations at scale remains difficult. Human egocentric demonstrations offer a scalable alternative, yet distributed observations from mul-tiple participants cannot directly supervise decentralized policies. We introduce EgoSwarm, the first robot learning framework for learning decentralized multi-robot collaboration from human swarm demonstrations. EgoSwarm collects more than 50 hours of collaborative experience using a robot-free multi-person egocen-tric system. We introduce the Swarm Interaction Packet (SIP) and Swarm Inter-action Token (SIT) to recover cross-agent interaction relations from distributed observations and transform this experience into local supervision consistent with deployment-time information constraints. We further propose SwarmVLA, which transfers knowledge of swarm behavior to decentralized policies conditioned on local observations, local history, and limited neighbor messages. SwarmVLA achieves a mean success rate of 72.2% across three categories of multi-robot collaborative tasks. Project page: https://xitong-c.github.io/EgoSwarm_ Page/ .

## 1 Introduction 

Robotics has long pursued general-purpose embodied intelligence to autonomously perform com-plex tasks. Many real-world tasks, however, inherently require collaboration among multiple robots. Collaborative transport, multi-participant handovers, and collaborative assembly require robots to divide responsibilities around shared objects and adapt behavior to partner states and task progress. Although vision-language-action (VLA) models have substantially improved generalization in in-dividual manipulation [1, 2], existing robot learning methods still focus primarily on individual demonstrations and provide limited supervision for inter-agent action dependencies. Multi-robot learning therefore requires more than parallel individual policies: robots must learn coordinated behavior from interaction experience and make decentralized decisions based on locally available information [3–5]. Learning coordinated behavior requires interaction experience at scale. Robot demonstration datasets provide state–action supervision for learning manipulation policies [6, 7]. Humans naturally perform complex collaborative tasks, and egocen-tric recording offers a more scalable way to capture such real-world experience. Recent large-scale egocentric datasets [8–10] and human-to-robot transfer methods [11–15] have demonstrated that human experience can effectively support robot skill learning. However, existing work primarily focuses on individual behavior. A general learning paradigm for extracting cross-agent interaction relations from multi-person egocentric experience and using them to supervise decentralized multi-robot policies remains underexplored. Multi-person egocentric experience cannot directly provide supervision for decentralized swarm policies. Individual demonstrations establish a clear correspondence between observations and ac-tions, whereas the information required for collaborative decision-making is often distributed across the local views of different participants. An agent action may depend on partner states, shared objects, and interaction progress, and the relations underlying waiting, handovers, and joint manip-ulation cannot be recovered by simply combining independent trajectories. Moreover, offline fusion of observations from multiple participants can reconstruct a global view of the swarm state, whereas each deployed robot has access only to local observations, local history, and limited neighbor mes-sages. This creates an information mismatch between training and deployment. Collecting more collaborative data alone does not resolve this mismatch. The central challenge is therefore to re-cover cross-agent interaction relations from distributed human experience and transform them into local supervision consistent with deployment-time information constraints. To address this challenge, we propose EgoSwarm, the first robot learning framework for learn-ing decentralized multi-robot collaboration from human swarm demonstrations. EgoSwarm syn-chronously records natural human collaboration from multiple egocentric views, enabling collabo-rative experience to be collected at scale without requiring robots during data collection. Using this system, we collect more than 50 hours of human collaborative experience. From these distributed observations, EgoSwarm integrates temporal information, relative geometry, shared-object informa-tion, and contact relations into Swarm Interaction Packets (SIPs) with associated quality annota-tions. It then encodes the interaction information available at deployment from local observations and neighbor messages into Swarm Interaction Tokens (SITs). Building on these representations, we introduce SwarmVLA, which leverages the rich interaction information contained in human swarm demonstrations to learn coordinated behavior and transfer this knowledge to decentralized local policies. ## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Egocentric Experience and Human-to-Robot Transfer 

Egocentric research has expanded from modeling individual daily activities to understanding skills across viewpoints and three-dimensional hand–object interactions. Ego4D, Ego-Exo4D, and HOT3D provide large-scale data on egocentric activities, cross-view behavior, and three-dimensional hand–object interactions, respectively [8–10]. In human-to-robot learning, UMI and DexUMI explore robot-free collection of human manipulation demonstrations and cross-embodiment skill transfer [16, 17]. EgoMimic, HumanEgo, Ego2Robot, DexWild, and EgoZero fur-ther leverage human egocentric experience to learn robot representations and manipulation policies [11–15], while Open X-Embodiment advances robot learning across diverse tasks and embodiments [6]. However, existing research still primarily focuses on individual skills and policies, with lim-ited modeling of cross-agent behavioral relations in multi-person egocentric experience. EgoSwarm extends egocentric data collection to synchronized multi-person collaboration, providing a scalable source of interaction experience for learning coordinated multi-robot behavior.

## 3 EgoSwarm Method 

EgoSwarm learns decentralized multi-robot coordination from human collaborative experience. We introduce a robot-free multi-person egocentric data collection system (Sec. 3.1), reconstruct swarm interactions into structured SIP and SIT representations (Sec. 3.2), and develop SwarmVLA to trans-fer this experience into decentralized policies using only local information (Sec. 3.3). 

3.1 Hardware Setup 

EgoSwarm uses a shared sensing interface for humans and robots to learn multi-robot collaboration policies from human swarm experience. This design enables collaborative demonstrations to be col-lected without robot hardware while preserving consistent observation and interaction information across data collection and deployment. 

EgoSwarm Capture. EgoSwarm Capture is a robot-free multi-person egocentric data collection system that enables participants to directly manipulate real objects and collaborate naturally with-out deploying or teleoperating robots. As shown in Fig. 2, each participant wears a SwarmEgo sensing head and performs tasks within the coverage area of the Swarm UWB base stations. Each 4sensing head integrates six cameras, an inertial measurement unit (IMU), and an ultra-wideband (UWB) tag. Two infrared cameras operate together with distributed infrared markers to provide short-range relative observations between participants. The remaining four cameras synchronously record hands, partners, and shared objects while supporting visual-inertial motion estimation. UWB provides inter-agent ranging measurements and link-quality information to help identify candidate interaction windows. All sensor data and timestamps are logged independently on each device, pro-viding raw observations for subsequent cross-device temporal alignment and reconstruction of local interaction relations. 

Multi-robot platform. To reduce perceptual differences between human demonstrations and robot execution, we build a multi-robot platform that reuses the SwarmEgo sensing head. A shared mount-ing interface allows the robots to retain the camera, IMU, UWB, and time-synchronization config-urations used during human data collection. Humans and robots therefore share a consistent sens-ing interface and interaction fields. The robots additionally record proprioceptive states and control commands to establish correspondences between interaction states and the robot action space. Robot adaptation data and action mapping account for human–robot kinematic differences, while relative geometry, shared-object information, contact relations, and message quality are represented in a common form. Each robot carries an NVIDIA AGX Thor and independently performs local percep-tion, online Packetizer processing, SwarmVLA inference, and neighbor communication, enabling decentralized closed-loop execution without a central server. 

3.2 Swarm Interaction Data Processing 

EgoSwarm converts synchronized multi-person egocentric recordings into structured swarm interac-tion supervision for SwarmVLA. As shown in Fig. 3(a), distributed RGB, infrared, IMU, and UWB observations are processed through temporal synchronization, interaction reconstruction, quality es-timation, and action mapping. The resulting information is then organized into Swarm Interaction Packets (SIPs) with validity and quality annotations. 

Temporal Synchronization. SwarmEgo devices operate on independent clocks, and fixed clock offsets and frequency drift can disrupt temporal correspondence between events observed by differ-ent participants. EgoSwarm adopts TICSync-style time mapping [28] to map continuous recordings from each device onto a common session timeline, providing a shared temporal reference for asso-ciating observations across participants. 

Interaction Window Reconstruction. Visual-inertial simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) first constructs a local map and estimates a motion trajectory for each participant [29]. UWB-based proximity, interaction duration, and link quality are used to identify candidate interac-tion windows, which are then validated using overlapping views, shared objects, or contact evidence. Because the local maps have different origins and orientations, the system selects the SLAM map of one participant as a temporary reference frame within each interaction window and jointly refines the local-map transformations and residual time offsets:

Interaction Annotation and Quality Estimation. EgoSwarm assigns validity, uncertainty, and ev-idence levels to reconstructed interaction relations based on the observations that support them. Fields with insufficient measurement constraints are downgraded or masked. Using the recon-structed interaction graph, the system identifies interaction events such as waiting, handovers, joint manipulation, failures, and recovery behaviors from relative motion, changes in object possession, and contact transitions. A frozen annotation model provides labels for roles, task stages, shared 5objects, and contact states, which are further verified through geometric consistency checks and manual spot checks. 

Cross-Embodiment Action Retargeting. Human actions are derived from hand and shared-object motion. Hand keypoints define a virtual gripper pose and aperture, which are mapped to robot end-effector targets through embodiment-specific action mapping. 

SIP Construction and Causal Sampling. Each valid interaction window is organized into an SIP. To strictly separate information available during policy execution from supervision used only during offline training, SIP fields are divided into three categories: 

1) Observable Interaction Fields. These fields include relative geometry supported by currently available observations, inter-agent distances, shared objects, contact states, and the validity and quality of interaction relations. They correspond to the online sensing interface available during robot deployment and are further encoded into SITs as policy inputs. 

2) Offline Supervision Fields. These fields include action targets, roles, task stages, interaction events, and quality information associated with the complete interaction window. They are used only for teacher learning, training-target construction, and sample selection, and are not provided as policy inputs during deployment. 

3) Causal Training Prefix. Each training sample is bounded by a decision time. Policy inputs are constructed using only local history, neighbor messages, and relation quality available before that time. The complete interaction window is used only to generate supervision labels and assess sample quality, thereby preventing future information leakage. Segments without valid evidence of swarm interaction are retained as Human Individual Operation and used to adapt the model to individual manipulation. SIPs thus convert distributed multi-person egocentric recordings into learning samples that are tem-porally and spatially aligned, explicitly encode interaction relations, and satisfy causal input con-straints. They provide a common interface for subsequent SIT construction and decentralized policy learning with SwarmVLA. 

3.3 SwarmVLA: Decentralized Swarm Policy 

SwarmVLA learns a shared-parameter decentralized policy from SIP supervision. As shown in Fig. 3(c), each robot encodes local visual history, proprioception, language, and online SITs, then aggregates neighbor information into an Action Context for future action prediction. Training uses offline-reconstructed swarm information to learn collaborative relations, while deployment relies only on local observations and neighbor messages.

Swarm-to-Local Distillation: SwarmVLA distills offline-reconstructed swarm information into decentralized policies using only local information. Training combines human swarm recordings for collaborative behaviors, robot adaptation data for perception-action alignment, and task-specific joint teleoperation for additional action supervision. Dataset details are provided in the experimental setup. Training follows three stages. Stage I adapts the model to individual manipulation using robot adaptation data and Human Individual Operation. Stage II trains a swarm teacher on human swarm interactions and robot demonstrations. Stage III freezes the teacher and distills its coordination representations and action flow fields into a shared student policy. Message delays, packet loss, and missing fields are simulated during training for communication robustness. At deployment, the swarm teacher and offline SIP reconstructor are removed. Each robot runs the shared SwarmVLA policy with a local Packetizer and acts independently from local visual history, proprioception, task instructions, and limited neighbor messages, enabling decentralized closed-loop execution.

4.1 Dataset and Experimental Setup 

As shown in Fig. 4, EgoSwarm Capture has collected approximately 50 hours of synchronized human collaboration data across 30 real-world scenario categories, covering diverse objects, par-ticipants, roles, and collaboration patterns. The dataset also captures natural interaction events, including waiting, interrupted handovers, role adjustments, and failure recovery, demonstrating the scalability of EgoSwarm Capture. We evaluate EgoSwarm on three representative multi-robot tasks requiring continuous adaptation to partners, shared objects, and task progress. 1) Multi-Robot Flexible Handover (MRFH): Three robots sort laundry through coordinated handovers and basket loading, testing conditional handovers and temporal coordination. 2) Dynamic Collaborative Transport (DCT): Two robots load a box onto a cart before a third takes over transport, testing joint support and role transitions. 3) Large-Scale Deformable Object Manipulation (LSDOM): Multiple robots jointly manipulate a wire harness, testing sustained coordination through a shared deformable object. 

4.2 Multi-Robot Deployment Experiments

We compare EgoSwarm with four baselines: the rule-based Local-Rule, Independent VLA without communication, Dec-VLA-Comm with standard aggregation of neighbor messages, and Centralized Joint VLA with global observations. The centralized method serves only as a reference with access to global information. All learning methods use the same data splits, robot-data budget, and hard-ware configuration. 4.3 Ablation and Robustness Tests 

We evaluate the contributions and robustness of EgoSwarm on the same three tasks as the main experiments. Specifically, we ablate human swarm experience, SIT, and quality-aware aggregation, and further test sensitivity to reduced human data and unreliable neighbor communication. The following variants are considered: • Robot-only: Removes human swarm data and trains only on robot adaptation and joint teleoperation data. • 50% human swarm data: Uses half of the human swarm demonstrations while retaining the full robot-data budget and original data splits. • w/o SIT: Removes SIT features from the local vision-language model and neighbor aggre-gation while retaining visual observations and basic messages. • w/o quality gate: Replaces continuous weighting based on uncertainty, message age, and link quality with binary validity masks. • 30% loss + 100 ms delay: Evaluates the fully trained SwarmVLA policy under 0.3 in-dependent message loss and an additional 100 ms one-way delay, while retaining original timestamps and validity thresholds. Except for the factor specified by each variant, the comparisons retain the same model initialization, training budget, parameter sharing, causal inputs, and message format and transmission rate. All ablation and robustness conditions follow the same 30-trial-per-task evaluation protocol as the main experiments. The communication stress test uses the same full-model checkpoint and task evaluation protocol as the nominal setting.

Interaction representation and aggregation. Removing SIT reduces the mean success rate from 72.2% to 38.9%, a decrease of 33.3 percentage points, while replacing quality-aware weighting with binary validity masks reduces it to 45.6%, a decrease of 26.6 points. 4.4 SIP Accuracy and Runtime Performance Temporal synchronization accuracy. We use 100 hardware-triggered events jointly observed by three head-worn sensing units and an independent recorder as reference events. These events are excluded from estimation of the time-mapping parameters. After synchronization, the mean absolute timestamp error relative to the reference timeline is 0.21 ms, the 95th-percentile (P95) error is 0.47 ms, and the maximum error is 1.08 ms. These results demonstrate stable submillisecond temporal alignment across devices, providing a reliable basis for associating visual observations, motion, and contact events across participants. 

Relative geometry accuracy. We evaluate local relation reconstruction on 30 independent multi-person interaction segments using a 120 Hz optical motion-capture system as reference ground truth. The test segments cover representative interactions, including mutual approach, relative motion, han-dovers, and joint manipulation. Full SIP reconstruction achieves a translation root mean square error (RMSE) of 4.8 mm, a rotation RMSE of 2.6 ◦, and a valid-output ratio of 92.7%. Compared with a baseline that directly fuses visual-inertial odometry (VIO) and UWB measurements, the transla-tion and rotation errors are reduced by approximately 63.6% and 67.9%, respectively, indicating that multimodal constraints within interaction windows substantially improve the stability of local interaction geometry. 10 Online runtime performance. During collaborative execution with three robots, the local Packe-tizer requires an average of 2.2 ms per update, while SwarmVLA requires an average of 14.1 ms per policy inference. The system achieves an end-to-end P95 latency of 38.6 ms and consistently maintains closed-loop control at 30 Hz. The mean communication bandwidth of neighbor messages is approximately 11.8 KB/s per robot. Overall, EgoSwarm reliably reconstructs local interaction relations with submillisecond temporal error and millimeter-scale spatial error while satisfying the computational and communication re-quirements for decentralized closed-loop execution at 30 Hz. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented EgoSwarm, a framework that converts synchronized multi-person egocentric experi-ence into local supervision for decentralized multi-robot collaboration. With EgoSwarm Capture, SIP/SIT representations, and SwarmVLA, it learns cross-agent relations from human swarm experi-ence and executes using only local observations and limited neighbor messages. Across three real-world multi-robot task categories, EgoSwarm achieves a mean success rate of 72.2%, supporting the contributions of human swarm experience, structured interaction representations, and quality-aware aggregation. 

Limitations and Future Work. The current system mainly relies on visual, inertial, and UWB signals, reconstructs interactions only within sufficiently observed local windows, and is evaluated primarily with three robots. Future work will incorporate tactile and force sensing to model contact transitions, force transmission, and dynamic object states, and extend to larger teams, dynamic membership, and degraded communication for improved robustness and open-world generalization.

A.1 EgoSwarm Capture Hardware 

The SwarmEgo sensing head is a wearable multi-camera system designed to capture natural human collaboration from egocentric perspectives. As illustrated in Fig. A1, each device integrates four RGB cameras, two infrared cameras, an inertial measurement unit (IMU), and an ultra-wideband (UWB) tag. The RGB cameras capture hand movements, shared objects, and interactions with neighboring participants, while the infrared cameras operate with distributed infrared emitters to support short-range relative observations. The IMU provides inertial measurements for motion esti-mation, and the UWB module supplies inter-agent ranging and link-quality information. All participants use an identical sensing configuration, providing a consistent multimodal observa-tion interface across human demonstrations. The integrated design enables robot-free collection of multi-person interaction experience without restricting natural collaborative movements. 

A.2 Multi-Person Data Collection and Synchronization 

As shown in Fig. A1, the multi-person data collection system consists of multiple participants equipped with SwarmEgo sensing heads and a set of spatially distributed UWB anchors. Partic-ipants perform collaborative manipulation tasks naturally, including coordinated transport, object handovers, and shared-object manipulation, without requiring robot hardware or teleoperation in-terfaces. The UWB infrastructure provides ranging measurements that support the identification of spatial proximity and potential interaction between participants. Each SwarmEgo device independently records RGB, infrared, IMU, and UWB observations with associated timestamps. Since the devices operate on independent clocks, their recordings may exhibit temporal offsets and clock drift. To establish a consistent temporal reference, we adopt TICSync-style time mapping to align individual sensor streams onto a shared session timeline. The synchronized recordings are subsequently used for interaction-window reconstruction and Swarm Interaction Packet (SIP) construction, as described in Sec. 3.2.

## B Training Details 

B.1 Training Configuration Optimization. We initialize SwarmVLA from the pretrained π0.5 weights and train it on a com-pute node equipped with four NVIDIA A100 GPUs, each with 80 GB of memory, a 64-core CPU, and 256 GB of system memory. We use AdamW with (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.95) , weight decay of 0.01 ,and ϵ = 10 −8. Each training stage begins with 1,000 steps of linear warmup to a peak learning rate of 2.5 × 10 −5, followed by cosine decay to 2.5 × 10 −6. A per-GPU micro-batch size of 2 and eight gradient accumulation steps yield an effective global batch size of 64. Training uses bfloat16 mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and parameter sharding, with the gradient norm clipped at 1.0. Each of the three stages runs for 30,000 steps. The vision-language backbone, action head, and interaction modules are jointly optimized; the swarm teacher is frozen during Stage III. The student is trained with action flow matching, coordination representation distillation, and action flow-field distillation. The overall distillation weight is λKD = 1 .0, and the relative weight of representation distillation is λh = 0 .1. At inference time, action chunks are generated using 10 Euler integration steps. Inputs and model architecture. All input images are resized to 224 ×224 pixels. For each camera view, the model uses the current frame and the immediately preceding frame. The vision-language backbone and action expert retain hidden dimensions of 2,048 and 1,024, respectively. The SIT en-coder maps structured interaction fields and their validity masks to a 256-dimensional representation. The Coordination Token is further compressed into a 128-dimensional message for communication with neighboring agents. At the receiver, each message is concatenated with its corresponding SIT representation, projected, and processed by a two-layer Quality-Gated Set Attention module to pro-duce a 256-dimensional Neighbor Token. This token is fused with local vision-language features, the Coordination Token, proprioceptive state, and the embodiment description to form the conditioning context for the action expert. The action head predicts chunks of 50 time steps through a unified 32-dimensional output interface. An embodiment-specific mask selects the valid action channels. The interaction modules use GELU activations, with a dropout rate of 0.1 in both the attention and feed-forward layers. 

Baseline configurations. All learning-based baselines use the same data splits, robot-data budget, and hardware configuration. Dec-VLA-Comm matches SwarmVLA in message dimensionality and transmission frequency, but aggregates neighbor information using standard attention. Independent VLA uses local observations and task instructions, whereas Centralized Joint VLA receives global observations from multiple robots and serves as a centralized reference. 

B.2 Data Composition and Training Stages Data composition. Training draws on three complementary data sources: human swarm demon-strations, robot adaptation data, and joint robot teleoperation data. Human swarm demonstrations provide cross-agent interaction experience, including waiting, handovers, joint manipulation, role transitions, and recovery from failures. Robot adaptation data support alignment between obser-vations and embodiment-specific actions, while joint robot teleoperation data provide task-specific supervision for coordinated robot actions. Human recording segments without valid evidence of swarm interaction are retained as Human Individual Operation data for individual manipulation adaptation. 17 Human swarm recordings are reconstructed into Swarm Interaction Packets (SIPs) with validity and quality annotations. For each training sample, policy inputs are restricted to the local observation history, neighbor messages, and interaction information available at the decision time. Information from the complete interaction window is used only to construct offline supervision and assess sample quality. This separation preserves the causal information constraints of decentralized deployment. 

Stage I: individual adaptation. We first adapt the pretrained model to individual manipulation us-ing robot adaptation data and Human Individual Operation data. This stage establishes perception– action alignment before learning cross-agent coordination. 

Stage II: swarm teacher learning. We train a swarm teacher using human swarm interactions and robot demonstrations. The teacher uses offline-reconstructed swarm information to learn coordina-tion representations and action flow fields under the centralized-training, decentralized-execution framework. 

Stage III: shared student distillation. We freeze the teacher and distill its coordination represen-tations and action flow fields into a shared-parameter student policy. The student combines action flow matching with the two distillation objectives described in Section B.1, while conditioning on locally available information. During training, we simulate message delays, packet loss, and missing interaction fields to improve robustness to unreliable communication and incomplete observations. At deployment, the swarm teacher and offline SIP reconstructor are removed. Each robot executes the shared student policy with a local Packetizer, using local observations, local history, and limited neighbor messages.

## C Baseline Failure Analysis 

To better understand the challenges of multi-robot collaboration, we analyze representative failure modes associated with temporal coordination, joint support, and shared-object deformation. We further discuss how these failures relate to the information available to different baseline policies. Successful collaboration requires robots to account for partner readiness, object possession, and task progress when deciding when to wait, release, or take over an action. 

C.1 Temporal Misalignment and Failed Handovers 

Multi-Robot Flexible Handover. Garment handover requires the transferring and receiving robots to coordinate a transition in object possession. After bringing a garment into the handover region, the transferring robot must maintain support until the receiving robot establishes a stable grasp. The receiving robot, in turn, must approach and grasp the garment while it remains supported. The temporal relationship between these actions is critical to a successful handover. Premature release can cause the garment to lose support before the transfer of possession is com-plete. If the transferring robot treats arrival at the handover position as task-stage completion, the garment may shift or deform before the receiving robot has secured it. Subsequent gripper closure may then miss the intended grasp location, resulting in a dropped garment or an interrupted han-dover. Conversely, the interaction may stall when both robots wait for the partner to initiate the next action or repeatedly adjust their local grasp configurations. Garment deformation, arm occlusion, and viewpoint differences further complicate the estimation of partner readiness. Independent VLA relies on local visual observations and history to infer partner states, which may be insufficient when the interaction region is occluded. Dec-VLA-Comm incorporates neighbor messages through standard aggregation but does not explicitly structure object-possession relations or information reliability. Local-Rule advances actions according to predefined conditions, making its effectiveness dependent on how well the rules cover approach, contact, and waiting states. Across these baselines, the central challenge is to relate partner readiness to the completion conditions of each robot’s own actions. 19 On MRFH, EgoSwarm achieves an 80.0% success rate, compared with 43.3% for Independent VLA and 63.3% for Dec-VLA-Comm. EgoSwarm represents partner relations and contact states through the Swarm Interaction Token (SIT), incorporates quality-aware neighbor aggregation, and transfers cross-agent action dependencies through swarm-to-local distillation. These components provide a structured basis for coordinating reception, waiting, and release during collaborative handovers. 

C.2 Coordination Failures in Shared-Object Manipulation 

Dynamic Collaborative Transport. Box loading requires two robots to maintain joint support during grasping, lifting, and transfer, followed by a handover of transportation responsibility to a third robot. If one robot initiates lifting or translation while the other is still establishing its grasp, the resulting support imbalance may cause the box to tilt, slip, or require repositioning. Similarly, releasing support before the box is stably placed on the cart can interrupt the loading process. Inconsistent task-stage transitions introduce an additional source of failure. Premature cart move-ment by the transporting robot may shift the placement target before loading is complete, whereas prolonged waiting after successful loading can delay subsequent transport. DCT therefore requires both continuous coordination during joint manipulation and appropriate transitions between load-ing and transportation roles. EgoSwarm achieves a 73.3% success rate on this task, compared with 46.7% for Independent VLA and 56.7% for Dec-VLA-Comm. Explicit representations of shared-object interactions and task progress provide information relevant to these coordination require-ments. 

Large-Scale Deformable Object Manipulation. The wire harness physically couples multiple grasp-ing points throughout manipulation. Motion at one location changes the configuration and available manipulation space at other locations, requiring each robot to adapt its actions to both object de-formation and partner motion. If one robot moves ahead while the remaining grasping points fail to follow, the harness may become excessively stretched, potentially causing local slippage or loss of grasp. Incompatible motion directions can introduce crossings or entanglement, while repeated local corrections may prevent the team from making progress toward the target configuration. Such tasks require sustained awareness of multi-point relations throughout execution. Indepen-dent VLA primarily generates actions from local observations, whereas standard neighbor-message aggregation must implicitly recover these relations from exchanged representations. SwarmVLA instead organizes relative geometry, shared-object information, contact states, and validity annota-tions into SITs. Quality-aware aggregation further adjusts the contribution of neighbor information according to its reliability and message age, allowing action generation to incorporate interaction context over time. On LSDOM, EgoSwarm achieves a 63.3% success rate. Removing SIT reduces this rate to 23.3%, while removing quality-aware aggregation reduces it to 43.3%. These ablations support the contri-butions of structured interaction representations and reliability-aware neighbor aggregation to suc-cessful deformable-object manipulation. Initial Configuration. Three robots collaborate to load a box onto a mobile cart and subsequently transport it. Two robots are initially responsible for manipulating the box, while the third robot takes over the transportation stage. The task involves both simultaneous physical cooperation and sequential role transitions, requiring the robots to coordinate their actions according to the progress of the shared manipulation. 

Step 1: Coordinated Approach. Two robots approach the box from their respective positions and establish suitable configurations for collaborative manipulation. Their movements must be coordi-21 nated to avoid interference and prepare for joint grasping. Evaluation Criteria: Both robots must reach appropriate manipulation positions without disrupting the box or obstructing one another. 

Step 2: Collaborative Grasping. The two robots establish grasps on the box, preparing to support its weight during lifting. Since both robots interact with the same rigid object, the grasping actions must provide compatible support before the lifting motion begins. Evaluation Criteria: Both robots must establish stable grasps that allow the box to be lifted collaboratively. 

Step 3: Collaborative Lifting. The robots lift the box together while maintaining coordinated motion. Differences in lifting timing or motion direction can cause the box to tilt or become unstable, making this stage dependent on the actions of both robots. Evaluation Criteria: The box must be lifted and maintained in a stable configuration without being dropped. 

Step 4: Placement and Moving. After lifting, the robots coordinate the movement and placement of the box onto the cart. Both robots must adjust their actions as the shared object approaches its destination and release it only after stable placement. Evaluation Criteria: The box must be successfully placed on the cart without falling or becoming unstable during the transfer. 

Step 5: Cart Transport. Once the box has been loaded, the third robot takes over the transporta-tion stage and moves the cart toward the designated destination. This transition requires the task to progress from simultaneous box manipulation to a different collaborative role configuration. Eval-uation Criteria: The task is considered successful when the box is securely loaded and the cart completes the required transportation without losing the load. 

D.3 Large-Scale Deformable Object Manipulation (LSDOM) 

Initial Configuration. Multiple robots collaboratively manipulate a large flexible wire harness to-ward a target configuration. Unlike rigid-object transport, the wire harness can deform continuously during manipulation, and the motion of one robot influences the object configuration experienced by the others. This task therefore requires sustained coordination throughout the manipulation process rather than synchronization at isolated handover events. 

Step 1: Coordinated Approach. The robots approach the wire harness from different positions and prepare to manipulate the shared deformable object. Their initial configurations must allow coordinated access to the harness while avoiding interference between manipulators. Evaluation Criteria: The robots must reach suitable grasping positions without disturbing the object in a way that prevents subsequent manipulation. 

Step 2: Cooperative Grasping. The robots establish grasps at different locations along the wire harness. Because the object is deformable, grasping at one location may change the positions of other segments and affect the remaining robots. Evaluation Criteria: The robots must establish and maintain stable grasps that support subsequent coordinated manipulation. 

Step 3: Cooperative Lifting. The robots lift the wire harness together while adapting to its changing shape. Uncoordinated lifting can introduce excessive deformation or uneven motion, making syn-chronization essential for maintaining control of the shared object. Evaluation Criteria: The wire harness must be lifted while maintaining stable grasping and coordinated support among the robots. 

Step 4: Coordinated Transfer. The robots transport and adjust the wire harness toward the target region. During this stage, each robot must account for the motion of neighboring robots and the changing configuration of the object, since independently generated movements may produce con-flicting displacements. Evaluation Criteria: The wire harness must be transferred toward the target configuration without losing grasp or causing a task-disrupting deformation. 

Step 5: Placement and Moving. Finally, the robots coordinate the placement of the wire harness into the target configuration. The robots must adjust their relative movements and release the object in a coordinated manner to avoid disturbing previously positioned segments. Evaluation Criteria: The task is considered successful when the wire harness reaches the required target configuration and the robots complete the placement without losing control of the shared object. 22
