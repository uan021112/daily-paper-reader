Abstract
Dynamic spatial reasoning from monocular video enables
vision-language models (VLMs) to understand how 3D en-
vironments evolve over time, supporting physical world
understanding and embodied intelligence.
Existing ef-
forts primarily address observed-dynamics understand-
ing, whereas agents in dynamic environments also require
future-dynamics prediction beyond the observation bound-
ary. Moreover, external geometric processing and textual
descriptions can increase inference complexity or lose in-
formation when representing continuous dynamics. These
limitations motivate a shared internal state that preserves
observed dynamics and remains predictive beyond the in-
put video.
Accordingly, we present Pros4D, a frame-
work for modeling evolving 4D worlds in latent space that
unifies retrospective understanding of observed-dynamics
with prospective reasoning for future-dynamics prediction.
Specifically, Pros4D-Engine derives geometry-grounded
supervision and constructs paired examples for both tasks,
while Pros4D-Bench evaluates them across eight spatial
task categories spanning dynamic cameras, objects, and
scenes. At the model level, Pros4D compresses observed
dynamics into latent observed states and autoregressively
rolls them forward into latent future states.
4D Latent
Alignment (4DLA) grounds these states through comple-
mentary visual-semantic and geometric supervision, while
joint language supervision connects them to task reasoning.
Experiments across benchmarks show consistent improve-
ments over strong baselines in both capabilities.

1. Introduction
Dynamic spatial reasoning aims to understand how cam-
eras, objects, and their spatial relations evolve over time in
a 3D environment [23, 59, 75]. From monocular video, this
requires an agent to disentangle observer and object motion,
preserve spatial relations across time, and reason about how
these dynamics may continue beyond the observed segment
[10, 39]. Such capability is essential for connecting visual
perception with embodied decision making, where an agent
must not only understand what has happened but also pre-
dict what may happen next [4, 18, 20, 24]. Although vision-
language models (VLMs) have made substantial progress
in spatial understanding [16, 38, 57, 61, 66], extending this
ability from observed dynamics to subsequent 4D evolution
remains an open challenge for video-based agents.
Existing 4D reasoning methods primarily focus on
observed-dynamics understanding.
Explicit approaches
augment VLMs with geometric modules or tool-mediated
computation [9, 11–13], or express intermediate dynamics
through text [21, 47, 66]. Geometric augmentation adds
inference-time components and perception dependencies,
while verbalizing dynamics can lose fine-grained motion
and geometric information. More importantly, supervision
centered on observed evidence does not require a state that
continues evolving once observation ends. Dynamic spa-
tial reasoning therefore calls for an internal representation
that preserves observed dynamics while remaining predic-
tive beyond the input video in dynamic environments.
An implicit latent state provides a natural basis for such
a representation. As an intuitive analogy, people integrate
successive observations into an internal mental model to in-
terpret ongoing motion and imagine what may happen next.
Latent representations can similarly preserve visual infor-
mation and support state updates without verbalizing each
change [4, 44]. Prior work explores latent visual computa-
tion for fine-grained perception [27, 45] and persistent spa-
tial memory [36, 51]. Video world models further combine
latent dynamics forecasting with semantic guidance, link-
ing motion cues to context useful for prediction [69]. These
advances motivate shared states that preserve observed 4D
dynamics and support geometrically grounded evolution be-
yond the observation boundary in dynamic scenes.
Following this principle, we present Pros4D, a frame-
work for modeling evolving 4D worlds in latent space that
unifies retrospective understanding of observed-dynamics
with prospective reasoning for future-dynamics prediction.
To support both capabilities within this shared modeling
framework, we develop Pros4D-Engine, an automated data
engine that derives motion and spatial supervision from es-
timated 3D geometry and constructs paired examples for
the two tasks. We further introduce Pros4D-Bench, which
evaluates these tasks across eight spatial categories span-
ning dynamic cameras, objects, and scenes.
Within this framework, Pros4D performs 4D Latent
State Reasoning (4D-LSR) by compressing observed dy-
namics into continuous latent observed states and autore-
gressively rolling them forward into latent future states
for future-dynamics prediction.
4D Latent Alignment
(4DLA) grounds both types of state through complemen-
tary visual-semantic and geometric supervision from ob-
served and held-out frames, while joint language super-
vision connects these states to task reasoning.
At infer-
ence, 4D-LSR operates entirely within the VLM using
only the observed video prefix, without external geomet-
ric tool calls during question answering. Extensive exper-
iments across multiple dynamic spatial reasoning bench-
marks demonstrate consistent improvements over strong
baselines in both retrospective understanding and prospec-
tive reasoning across 3B and 8B model scales.
Our contributions are summarized as follows.
• We propose Pros4D, a unified latent reasoning frame-
work that supports retrospective understanding and
prospective reasoning through a shared evolving 4D state.
• We develop Pros4D-Engine, an automated data en-
gine for paired supervision of observed-dynamics under-
standing and future-dynamics prediction, together with
Pros4D-Bench for evaluating both tasks across eight spa-
tial categories under a shared observation boundary.
• We introduce 4D Latent State Reasoning (4D-LSR),
which reasons through latent observed and future states,
and 4D Latent Alignment (4DLA), which grounds these
evolving states with complementary visual-semantic, ge-
ometric, and language supervision.
2. Related Work
2.1. Latent Space Reasoning
Latent space reasoning studies how intermediate computa-
tion can proceed without verbalizing every step. Textual
chains make reasoning explicit but bind it to discrete lan-
guage [56]. Work on latent reasoning relaxes this constraint
through pause tokens, progressive internalization of reason-
ing steps, and continuous hidden states [14, 17, 19, 46, 72].
These approaches separate computation from its verbal ex-
pression. For multimodal problems, intermediate represen-
tations must also retain task-relevant visual evidence. This
shifts the focus from how many reasoning steps to generate
toward what information those steps preserve across modal-
ities and make available to subsequent scene reasoning.
Visual grounding supervises latent tokens with visual
features or aligns intermediate states with perceptual tar-
gets [26, 27, 64]. Predictive embedding objectives distill
expert multimodal trajectories without explicit tool calls at
inference [1]. Latent imagery also supports dynamic spa-
tial understanding through visual-semantic supervision of
camera and object motion [10].

2.2. Visual-Spatial Understanding
Spatial reasoning requires consistent object locations and
relations across viewpoints. Existing work improves this
capability through geometric supervision, structured coor-
dinates, and reconstructed 3D features [6, 15, 34]. Region
grounding further links answers to queried objects [11].
Complementary approaches retain spatial evidence in in-
ternal representations or persistent memory [36, 65], while
benchmark evaluations test whether that information sup-
ports relational and quantitative reasoning [37, 62]. The
common requirement is to preserve scene structure beyond
appearance. When cameras and objects move, this require-
ment extends across time.
A useful representation must
maintain entity identity across frames, preserve relations in
a consistent spatial reference, and connect successive obser-
vations to changes in the underlying scene.
Dynamic spatial benchmarks expose challenges of view-
point change, object motion, and evolving relations [23, 73,
76]. Geometry-guided reasoning and perceptual distillation
strengthen representations of observed dynamics [61, 75].
DynTrace additionally maintains continuous object evi-
dence through geometry-informed trajectories and struc-
tured temporal traces that preserve dynamic cues across
successive video frames [16], helping separate object mo-
tion from viewpoint changes.
Meanwhile, training on
synthesized future views transfers motion-conditioned spa-
tial knowledge into VLMs [70]. These methods motivate
learning temporally consistent spatial evolution within the
model. Our focus is a shared sequence of geometrically
aligned latent states for understanding observed dynamics
and predicting their continuation from the same prefix.
3. Method
Reasoning about a dynamic scene requires a representa-
tion that captures observed motion and remains predictive
beyond the observation boundary.
We propose Pros4D
to meet this need by unifying retrospective understand-
ing and prospective reasoning through evolving 4D latent
states, as illustrated in Figure 1.
The framework cou-
ples Pros4D-Engine, which constructs geometry-grounded
questions and traces for 4D Latent State Reasoning (4D-
LSR) in Section 3.1, with 4D Latent Alignment (4DLA),
which grounds latent observed and future states through
visual-semantic and geometric supervision in Section 3.2.

4. Experiments
Experimental setup.
We evaluate Pros4D on seven
benchmarks to assess its reasoning capabilities and cross-
task transfer. Pros4D-Bench evaluates observed-dynamics
understanding and future-dynamics prediction, while Dyn-
Bench [23], VLM4D [76], R4D-Bench [61], and STI-
Bench [31] test dynamic spatial generalization.
OpenX-
VQA [7] and STRIDE-QA-Bench [25] extend evaluation to
manipulation videos and estimation and prediction in driv-
ing scenes. We report accuracy for multiple-choice QA and
prescribed success rates for STRIDE-QA-Bench.
Baselines and implementation.
We compare spatial un-
derstanding models, video understanding models, and
training-free models that use external tools. All baselines
follow the same evaluation protocol within each bench-
mark. For both backbones, Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen3-
VL-8B, we train on Pros4D-40K for one epoch with frozen
visual encoders and K = 16 tokens per state. We optimize
the decoder and alignment head with 4DLA.
4.1. Retrospective and Prospective Reasoning
We first evaluate whether shared latent states support both
observed-dynamics understanding and future-dynamics
prediction.
On these tasks, Pros4D-8B outperforms its
backbone by 15.2 and 18.8 points, respectively (Table 1).
Pros4D-3B follows the same pattern, reaching 62.0 overall
accuracy compared with 62.9 for Pros4D-8B. Both tempo-
ral tasks receive the same observed prefix, so the improve-
ment on future questions reflects the ability to infer sub-
sequent scene changes from the available visual and motion
evidence. These gains across the observation boundary sup-
port our design because the shared representation both in-
terprets observed dynamics and predicts how they continue.
In 4D-LSR, autoregressive rollout carries evidence of scene
motion from latent observed states into latent future states.

4.2. Dynamic and Embodied Generalization
We next test transfer beyond Pros4D-Engine data. Table 2
reports gains of 4.6 and 9.2 points over the 8B backbone
on Dyn-Bench and VLM4D, with overall improvements at
the 3B scale as well. Dyn-Bench includes questions about
relations between the camera and objects, which require
interpreting motion relative to a changing viewpoint. On
VLM4D, Pros4D-8B gains 7.9 points on real videos and
13.4 on synthetic videos, indicating transfer across video
sources, scene configurations, and question formulations.
R4D-Bench marks the target with a region prompt, so
a question about motion requires tracking the same object
across frames. STI-Bench asks how far or how fast an ob-
ject moves, requiring the model to relate its positions over
time. Compared with Qwen3-VL-8B, Pros4D-8B raises ac-
curacy from 40.3 to 43.5 on R4D-Bench and from 40.5 to
43.6 on STI-Bench (Tables 3 and 4). These gains support
the use of evolving latent states that preserve the identity of
the queried entity together with the geometry of its motion.
OpenX-VQA distinguishes the current phase of a robot
manipulation task from the action that follows (Table 5).
Pros4D-3B improves average accuracy by 20.91 points over
Qwen2.5-VL-3B. The gain grows from 6.71 points for rec-
ognizing the current action phase to 52.60 for anticipating
the next action, with a 45.80-point gain for identifying the
intended goal. This pattern suggests that evolving latent
states help relate the observed robot–object configuration
to the goal of manipulation and the action likely to follow.

Complementarity of the alignment objectives.
Fig-
ure 5(a) asks whether latent future states capture how a par-
ticular scene continues after observation ends. Copying the
last observed state already yields substantial visual similar-
ity, but it barely distinguishes the true continuation from an-
other video’s future in the same task category. With 4DLA,
this similarity margin grows from 0.04 to 0.27. Of the two
ablations, removing visual-semantic alignment causes the
larger drop in visual similarity, while removing geometric
alignment causes the larger drop in geometric similarity.
Either removal also lowers question-answering accuracy in
Figure 5(b), where CE-only retains the latent pathway and
scores lowest. These ablations suggest that predicting how
a scene continues benefits from a state that retains the visual
features of the queried entity alongside the spatial structure
needed to reason about its motion.
Effect of latent states at inference.
With model weights
fixed, we alter generated states to test their role in predic-
tion (Figure 5(c)). Zero Future Latents removes future-state
content, lowering future accuracy by 5.4 points. Keeping
content but shuffling state order (Shuffle Temporal) causes
a 6.7-point drop, whereas Cross-Video Swap inserts another
video’s states and causes a 10.2-point drop. Both exceed the
4.6-point drop from Bypass All Latents, which skips the la-
tent pathway. Prediction therefore relies on ordered, video-
specific state evolution beyond additional computation. All
drops are percentage points relative to unmodified future ac-
curacy, with intervention details in Appendix D.

5. Conclusion
We propose Pros4D to unify observed-dynamics under-
standing and future-dynamics prediction through evolving
4D latent states. The framework integrates Pros4D-Engine
for geometry-grounded data construction, 4D-LSR for in-
terleaved textual and latent state reasoning, and 4DLA
for joint language, visual-semantic, and geometric super-
vision.
Experiments on Pros4D-Bench and six exter-
nal benchmarks demonstrate improvements across model
scales, while ablations support the value of aligned latent
states beyond textual reasoning alone. Future work will im-
prove geometric estimation quality and extend the frame-
work to longer prediction horizons, with an emphasis on
preserving spatial structure and temporal consistency dur-
ing latent state rollout across complex dynamic scenes.

A. Data Preparation and Model Configuration
Video and state targets.
For the pipeline in Section 3.1,
we sample 20,000 raw videos from SpatialVID without us-
ing its annotations. Each clip contains 16 observed frames
and four future frames sampled at 4 FPS. Observed tar-
gets use zero-based frame indices {0, 4, 8, 12}, and fu-
ture targets use {16, 17, 18, 19}, with tracked entities high-
lighted in each target overlay.
SAM3 supplies segmen-
tation masks, while Depth Anything 3 provides monocu-
lar geometry; invalid depth and sky regions are excluded
from object-centroid estimation.
Native visual features
are pooled into 16 ordered token groups per state to form
visual-semantic targets. For geometric targets, frozen Pi3X
encodes 224 × 392 overlays into 16 × 28 grids with 1,024
feature channels at every position in the spatial grid.
Alignment head and optimization.
Both backbones
share the same alignment-head design. To match the ge-
ometry teacher, the head reshapes each 16-position latent
block into a 4 × 4 grid, upsamples it to 16 × 28, and applies
normalization and a two-layer GELU projection. Learned
spatial embeddings and local residual convolutions refine
the output. The cosine, feature-value Smooth L1, and log-
norm Smooth L1 terms have weights 1.0, 0.1, and 0.1, with
equal weighting of observed and future targets. Alignment
is applied at decoder layer 17 for Qwen2.5-VL-3B and layer
15 for Qwen3-VL-8B, using zero-based indices. For both
models, visual-semantic alignment supervises the preceding
final-layer hidden states. The language decoder, token em-
beddings, and alignment head are trainable, while the native
vision tower and geometry teacher remain frozen. The joint
loss uses (λ1, λ2, λ3) = (0.1, 1.0, 0.5). Evaluation uses au-
toregressive greedy decoding from the observed prefix and
question, without access to future targets. Tables 11 and 12
summarize the respective configurations.
