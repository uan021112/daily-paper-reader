# Sim-to-Real Room-Mediated Object-Goal Navigation with ROS 2 and Nav2

# Abstract 

Although object-goal navigation (ObjectNav) approaches are usually evaluated in simula-tion, which offers scale and repeatable envi-ronments, real-world deployments are rarely examined beyond feasibility demonstrations. This paper presents the deployment of Room-Mediated Co-occurrence, an ObjectNav pol-icy that reasons over Room Probability Vec-tors (RPVs), on two robotic platforms (Boston Dynamics Spot and Clearpath Robotics Turtle-Bot 4) through a robot-agnostic ROS 2 bridge. We study how these semantic priors gener-alise to physical environments and describe the adaptations required to transfer RPV from sim-ulation to hardware, covering sensing, localisa-tion, and the action interface. Across 66 tri-als on Spot, searching for 11 object categories from two starting positions in a university post-graduate research facility, RPV achieves a raw Success Rate (SR) of 0.455. Our analysis iden-tifies undetected glass walls, entry into off-limits areas, limited usable depth range, target size, and mismatch between the room lexicon and physical layout as plausible contributors to the 8.4-percentage-point gap between the real-world SR and the reported simulated SR of 0.539. Code is available at https://github. com/Matthew-K-Chua/rpv-ros2-bridge .

Zero-shot Object-Goal Navigation (ObjectNav) [Ye et al. , 2021] is a foundational problem for general-purpose robotics, requiring an agent to find an instance of a spec-ified object in an initially unseen environment without domain-specific fine-tuning. Rather than coupling the semantic policy directly to a robot-specific SDK, we communicate through the open-source Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) [Macenski et al. , 2022] and standard navigation interfaces. This design lowers the effort re-quired to evaluate embodied-AI ObjectNav pipelines on different physical robots. Figure 1 illustrates one suc-cessful Spot trial. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 

• A modular ROS 2 bridge implementing RPV on Boston Dynamics Spot and Clearpath Robotics TurtleBot 4. 

• An analysis of the adaptations required to transfer the simulation pipeline to physical control. 

• Real-world evaluation and analysis of RPV over 66 trials on Boston Dynamics Spot. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Sim-to-Real Reality Gap and Deployment 

Simplified sensing, actuation, and physical interaction in simulation create a divergence from reality such that policies evaluated in simulation may generalise poorly to real-world deployment. Traditional approaches to bridg-ing this “reality gap” make an attempt to mimic real life by introducing random sensor noise [Tobin et al. ,2017], designing high-fidelity physics simulations [Gao 

et al. , 2026], or using photorealistic datasets such as HM3D [Ramakrishnan et al. , 2021]. However, poli-cies benchmarked in simulation can overfit to simulator-specific artefacts, such as taking shortcuts through oth-erwise non-navigable space [Kadian et al. , 2020]. Truong et al. [Truong et al. , 2022] counter-intuitively suggest that lower-fidelity simulation can improve generalisation to real-world navigation. Despite advances in closing the reality gap, it remains important to evaluate new robotics methods on phys-ical platforms. ROS 2-compatible simulators such as NVIDIA Isaac Sim [Gao et al. , 2026] allow engineers to deploy simulated programs onto robots without sig-nificant code duplication. Habitat [Savva et al. , 2019], one of the most widely used simulators in ObjectNav re-search, can interface with physical robots through the Habitat-PyRobot Bridge [Kadian et al. , 2020] and the open-source PyRobot framework. 

2.2 Zero-Shot ObjectNav Evaluation on Physical Robotic Platforms 

Recent ObjectNav methods demonstrate strong simu-lation performance, but their real-world robustness re-mains largely unverified. Several works evaluate their methods only in simulation [Scicluna et al. , 2026; Gadre 

et al. , 2023; Zhou et al. , 2023]. Others report successful deployment but do not analyse failure modes or the sim-to-real gap [Yokoyama et al. , 2024; Ginting et al. , 2024; Busch et al. , 2025]. VLFM [Yokoyama et al. , 2024] is deployed through the proprietary Boston Dynamics (BD) SDK, which limits reproducibility and portabil-ity to other platforms; SEEK [Ginting et al. , 2024] and OneMap [Busch et al. , 2025] do not report their deploy-ment frameworks. OpenFrontier [Padilla et al. , 2026] deploys ROS on Spot, reporting an SR of 70% over 10 trials, but confines its detailed failure analysis to simu-lation. TopoNav [Liu et al. , 2025] deploys on a Unitree Go2 using an Intel RealSense D435i and HESAI XT-6 LiDAR. Of the deployments that use ROS, L3MVN [Yu et al. ,2023] offers the most detailed account, highlighting the noise that real RGB-D, LiDAR, and odometry data feed into its pipeline. However, it does not examine individual trials or identify which properties of the physical system cause drops in performance. LOAT [Lin et al. , 2025] also reports a successful ROS deployment, but does not provide a per-trial analysis or describe the required sim-to-real adaptations.

3.2 Room Probability Vectors 

Room-Mediated Co-occurrence [Scicluna et al. ,2026] is an ObjectNav framework that extends VLFM [Yokoyama et al. , 2024] by assigning each detected object spatial context through room labels. An open-vocabulary perception stack segments and localises objects from RGB-D observations and maps each detection to a Room Probability Vector (RPV). The RPV is compared with that of the target, and the resulting semantic signal is written into a value map through geodesic flood fill. Frontiers are ranked on this map, and the highest-valued frontier becomes the next navigation goal. Several design choices make RPV a practical candi-date for physical deployment. The pipeline is modular, so the perception and frontier-scoring process can be detached from agent control. To enable real-time per-ception, RPV uses a dual perception funnel featuring YOLOE [Wang et al. , 2025] for lightweight label propos-als, which it then passes to SAM 3 [Carion et al. , 2025] to remove low-likelihood segmentations. The RPV authors advocate robotic deployment and recommend offloading the perception stack to an edge server. We adopt that recommendation directly. 

3.3 Robot-Agnostic Democratisation and Modularity 

Physical demonstrations of zero-shot ObjectNav policies using Boston Dynamics Spot have been programmed directly against its proprietary SDK [Yokoyama et al. ,2024; Busch et al. , 2025]. We instead rely on ROS 2 and standard navigation interfaces (laser scans, Nav2, SLAM Toolbox, and velocity commands), confining robot-specific software to the vendor’s ROS 2 driver, a Nav2 parameter file, and 126 lines of integration and con-figuration code for Spot and 45 lines for the TurtleBot 4. The goal is to enable physical evaluation on a wider range of affordable and attainable hardware. For the same rea-son, we split the computation between an off-board GPU host and a low-cost on-board sensing module that can be moved between platforms, enabling repeatable test-ing with an identical sensing setup. In exchange for this portability, the system depends on an additional network layer between the GPU host and sensing module. 

3.4 Software Architecture 

Figure 2 shows a high-level overview of our pipeline. At runtime, the on-board sensing module runs the robot’s ROS 2 driver and camera pipeline, projecting a raw 2.5-D depth stream into a 2-D laser scan for mapping and forwarding the depth-aligned RGB-D frames required by the RPV policy. These topics reach the GPU host through a VPN tunnel. On the host, SLAM and Nav2 build an occupancy map and plan motion, and a bridge node sends a snapshot of the robot’s occupancy map, pose, and RGB-D observation to the RPV policy over HTTP in the same manner as VLFM. The policy’s se-lected frontier is sent back to Nav2 as the next navigation goal, and the resulting velocity commands are relayed to the robot through the on-board sensing module. 

3.5 Differences Between Simulation and Physical Embodiment 

Deploying RPV on physical robots required adapting the simulation-oriented pipeline in ways that simula-tion avoids. Table 1 summarises differences in em-bodiment, sensing, perception, mapping, navigation, and evaluation. Two key design changes are removing Mask2Former (M2F) and lowering confidence thresholds for the YOLOE and SAM 3 components of the percep-tion stack. Originally, Mask2Former was used to mask uninfor-mative regions in the RGB image (e.g., floors, ceilings, walls, and other architectural structures). Its computa-tional cost prevented real-time operation on the deployed system, so we removed this stage and instead excluded uninformative labels from the perception vocabulary. We lowered the YOLOE and SAM 3 confidence thresh-olds after initial tests produced many false negatives. Motion blur from robot movement and distortion from the D435’s rolling-shutter RGB sensor caused prominent objects such as wheelie bins to be missed, leaving too few detections to guide exploration before timeout. 

# 4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Hardware 

Our off-board GPU host is a Dell Precision 5820 equipped with an RTX 3090 and 24 GB of VRAM. It communicates with a low-cost on-board sensing module comprising a RealSense D435 RGB-D camera connected to a battery-powered Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (Figure 3). 

Figure 3: Annotated hardware for Spot and TurtleBot 4. The on-board sensing module comprises a Raspberry Pi 4, RealSense D435, portable battery, and an Ether-net connection to the robot. It communicates with the off-board GPU host over Wi-Fi. The module is mounted on Spot and secured with a ten-sioned cord to reduce camera vibration. Experiments are conducted in part of a university floor containing of-fices, research laboratories, kitchens, an elevator room, and a printing station. We test RPV in an unmodified, uncontrolled environment to evaluate its generalisation to a realistic deployment scenario. 

4.2 Episode Initialisation 

We choose two points in the scene as the robot’s starting locations for experimentation. These were deliberately chosen such that during the initialisation phase of each episode, multiple frontiers leading to regions of interest are generated. P1 has three prominent frontiers in the form of hallways that lead to: (a) a printing station, a kitchenette and an elevator room, (b) a robotic research laboratory, and (c) a hallway that leads directly to P2.

P2 offers 4 prominent frontiers: (a) the rear entrance to the aforementioned robotic research laboratory, (b) offices, (c) an office meeting space, and (d) a kitchen and an elevator room. 

4.3 Metrics 

To evaluate our physical deployment against simulation benchmarks, we report Success Rate (SR) as a binary metric indicating whether the target was correctly de-tected and the robot navigated to it. We also measure Figure 4: Distribution of policy steps to first detection and stopping over successful physical RPV trials. The vertical axis is logarithmic. the policy steps to first target detection, to, and to the stopping action, ts. To capture failures arising from sim-to-real deployment, we define four categories: timeout (the robot did not detect the target within 250 steps), false positive (the target label was assigned to a non-target object, as verified against ground truth), emer-gency stop (the operator terminated a run for safety), and banned area (the robot entered a predesignated off-limits area containing none of the selected targets). 

4.4 RPV Variables 

We choose a room lexicon specific to the university en-vironment. The list includes nine terms: meeting area, kitchen, office, elevator room, printing station, labora-tory, exit, tooling, and lecture hall. We split our tar-get objects into three size categories (small, medium, and large) to analyse how object size affects the over-all search pipeline. Large objects are fridge, printer, garbage bin, and cabinet. Medium objects are projec-tor, microwave, backpack, and desktop monitor. Small objects are laptop, whiteboard marker, and paper tow-els. For each object, we run six trials: three from P1

and three from P2, giving 66 tests. For consistency with prior Spot deployments [Yokoyama et al. , 2024; Busch et al. , 2025], we perform the controlled experi-ments exclusively with Spot and use the TurtleBot to demonstrate the portability of the bridge.

5.1 Qualitative Behaviour Analysis 

Trial 36 (target object: “fridge”), shown in the top row of Figure 6, demonstrates RPV’s intended behaviour. At t = 19, the robot has begun the “explore” phase and sees only generic office objects (locker, television, lamp, and chair), with room co-occurrence values between 0.19 and 0.27. This results in a nearly uniform map, with the best frontier being a hallway leading to the kitchen, worth only 16.1%. As t grows, the laboratory frontier rises to 22.5%, and the robot switches headings. After further exploration, at t = 56, the frontier value shrinks to 15.1%, and the best frontier switches to the kitchen at 16.5%. By t = 100, a chair, laptop, and second locker lift the kitchen-side frontier from 16.9% to 22.9% by t = 109. The fridge is segmented at t = 117, and Spot stops at 

t = 264 after successfully navigating to the target (recall an episode will only end if Tmax is reached without target object detection so this trial is still valid). This trial highlights how room co-occurrence scores change as an environment is explored. By contrast, trial 62 (target object: “whiteboard marker”), shown in the bottom row of Figure 6, shows the consequences of objects with low room associa-tion. Every segmented object receives a low room co-occurrence score, with the highest scores at 0.30 for a storage locker and 0.29 for a filing cabinet, while high-entropy furniture contributes little (0.04 for desks and 0.03 for chairs). The best frontier is 9.2% when the pol-icy enters “explore” at t = 12, and the frontier values decline as t grows. With little contrast, frontier choice is driven by small fluctuations, causing the robot to re-peatedly cross the central junction as it switches between distant frontiers at t = 70, t = 79, t = 99. Despite the trial reaching Tmax and being recorded as a timeout, we note that at step 264, a pen scores 0.03 and does not redirect the search. Two limitations follow from this trial. First, the re-sult is consistent with RPV’s hypothesis that room co-occurrence is uninformative for high-entropy objects that often move between spaces and are not room-specific. Second, flat value maps can degrade to near-random frontier selection, yielding little information gain per unit distance travelled. In environments with long hall-ways, such as hospitals or universities, this would be especially costly. 

5.2 Robot-Agnostic Deployment 

We demonstrate robot-agnostic deployment by imple-menting the same stack used for our Spot experiments on a TurtleBot 4 with limited code changes. We attached our low-cost on-board sensing module to the TurtleBot’s top plate. Starting from P1, the robot found an instance of “paper towels” at t = 32 and finished navigating at 

t = 48. The TurtleBot’s smaller footprint suits the vinyl-floored building, whereas Spot’s rectangular body strug-gled in tight passages between desks in the laboratory. Paths requiring a 180 ◦ turn with as little as 20 mm of physical clearance caused Nav2 to refuse to plan, con-tributing to timeouts. Nevertheless, Spot remains the better platform for this feasibility assessment because it handles carpet and small changes in floor elevation that stop the TurtleBot. Using the original confidence thresholds of 0.50, the policy sometimes failed to segment large objects such as garbage bins in blurred or distorted frames. Lowering the thresholds improved these detections qualitatively. We did not isolate the effects of lighting, sensor noise, or network delay experimentally; after threshold tuning, however, no failure was assigned solely to one of these factors. 

# 6 Discussion 

Deploying RPV on real hardware exposes strengths and weaknesses that simulation does not. The raw real-world SR is 0.455, or 0.508 after excluding the seven trials ter-minated by an emergency stop or entry into an off-limits area. We recognise the gap compared with the simulated SR of 0.539 [Scicluna et al. , 2026] and identify three plau-sible contributing factors in addition to undetected glass walls and entry into off-limits areas. First, the univer-sity layout differs from the simulated scenes: it contains only three semantically clear rooms (kitchen, printing station, and office), while the laboratory, which makes up roughly half of the navigable space, is dominated by high-entropy objects such as chairs and tables, which Sci-cluna et al. [Scicluna et al. , 2026] identify as weak cues. Second, long hallways separate rooms in the test envi-ronment, causing costly policy steps to traverse known ground when the best frontier switches to a different hall-way and contributing to timeouts for small and medium objects. Finally, the D435’s usable depth of about 3 m results in an occupancy map with a 6 m diameter during initialisation, compared with a 10 m diameter in simu-lation, so frontiers appear closer to the robot and are exhausted quickly, encouraging frontier switching. Safety interventions play a crucial role in real deploy-ment. Although failed trials arising from entry into off-limits areas reflect experiment-specific conditions rather than unsafe behaviour, glass exposes a significant safety limitation of this sim-to-real deployment. In trials 41 and 52, because the camera did not register an office’s hallway-facing glass wall, the policy selected a frontier inside the office and Nav2 planned a path through the glass. During navigation, Spot’s internal collision de-tection also failed to react, and an operator ended each episode before the robot reached the wall. Until trans-parent surfaces are handled, the system requires close supervision. VLFM’s real-world Spot deployment integrated the monocular depth estimation model ZoeDepth [Bhat et al. , 2023], suggesting that learned depth estimation is feasible in physical ObjectNav scenarios. However, met-ric monocular models still struggle with glass [Zheng et al. , 2026]. Zheng et al. [Zheng et al. , 2026] instead propose a training-free method that aligns an affine-invariant monocular depth prior to raw sensor depth through local RANSAC-based scale-and-shift estima-tion, recovering glass geometry from the prior. Since the method consumes the existing RGB-D stream, it could run on the GPU host, combining the D435’s met-ric stereo depth with the prior’s glass geometry, provided the depth-to-scan projection also moves to the host. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We deployed Room-Mediated Co-occurrence (RPV) on two ROS 2-compatible platforms, Boston Dynamics Spot and Clearpath Robotics TurtleBot 4, using a robot-agnostic ROS 2-based pipeline for physical ObjectNav evaluation. We analyse how RPV generalises to real-world deployment on an individual-episode basis over 66 Spot trials covering 11 objects and two starting loca-tions. RPV achieved a raw SR of 0.455 compared with 0.539 in simulation, or an adjusted SR of 0.508 after ex-cluding seven safety- or boundary-related terminations. Per-episode analysis shows RPV behaving as intended when the target object has a clear semantic association with a room. The observed gap may be associated with undetected glass walls, off-limits entries, the D435’s 3 m usable range, long hallways with few semantically dis-tinct rooms, and detection thresholds tuned coarsely to reduce false negatives. Two limitations of RPV emerged: high-entropy targets such as a whiteboard marker pro-duced a flat value map, and the frontier hysteresis in-herited from VLFM was too weak to prevent repeated retargeting across the building. The D435 also failed to observe glass walls reliably in our environment, creating a safety limitation. These findings suggest four directions. First, fron-tier commitment should account for travel cost through distance-aware hysteresis or the information-gain term proposed by the original authors, since repeated switch-ing between hallway ends contributed to timeouts. Sec-ond, the effective depth range and 6 m propagation cap should be evaluated together to determine whether they can be increased without reducing reliability. Third, RPV should be benchmarked in an office or household layout containing rooms that match its lexicon, because our laboratory has little room-level structure. Finally, we do not report Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) because no ground-truth map is available. A geodesic shortest path computed on the final SLAM occupancy grid could provide an estimate for comparison with prior physical deployments [Ginting et al. , 2024], provided its limitations are reported.
