## Abstract 

Deformation-based 4D Gaussian splatting (4DGS) enables dynamic scene rendering. However, evaluating deforma-tion networks for all Gaussians at every frame is compute-intensive on edge devices. We find that Gaussian trajectories are often locally predictable, yet prediction reliability varies across Gaussians and time. Thus, we propose CUE SPLAT , a post-training framework that exploits local temporal regular-ity while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen and retain-ing its Gaussian set. Specifically, CUE SPLAT predicts Gaus-sian attributes from recent history and uses a lightweight learned router to select exact deformation updates within a scene-specific budget calibrated offline. Selection accounts for prediction reliability and rendering contribution. Se-lected indices are reused across nearby frames to reduce routing overhead, while selected Gaussians still receive ex-act updates each frame. To control accumulated prediction error, we use drift signals to schedule three consecutive full-deformation frames that rebuild the prediction history. Eval-uated across four mainstream 4DGS algorithms, CUE SPLAT 

achieves up to 6.74 × and 8.15 × speedups on Jetson AGX Orin and Orin NX, respectively, with a negligible quality drop (at most 0.16 dB in dataset-average PSNR). 

## 1. Introduction 

4D Gaussian splatting (4DGS) [25, 29, 43, 48] enables pho-torealistic dynamic scene reconstruction by representing dynamic scenes with time-varying Gaussian attributes and rendering them through efficient rasterization [19]. These reconstructed scenes can be interacted with on edge devices and allow people to relive their memories [23, 40]. In main-stream 4DGS, a deformation network transforms Gaussians into their states at a given timestamp. Then, the resulting Gaussians are projected and rendered to a targeted viewpoint. 

Bottleneck. However, evaluating the deformation net-work for all Gaussians at every frame is costly on edge de-vices. For E-D3DGS [1] on Jetson AGX Orin, deformation accounts for about 85% of GPU pipeline time on N3DV [24]. Existing methods reduce computation through Gaussian pruning [39], static–dynamic decomposition [4, 22], or shared motion representations [17, 20, 21]. Nevertheless, reducing the Gaussian set or restricting motion expressive-ness can compromise reconstruction quality. Static–dynamic partitions also remain fixed during rendering, and misclas-sifying dynamic Gaussians as static can degrade motion re-construction. Our approach retains the pretrained Gaussian set and deformation model, combining temporal prediction with adaptive selection of deformation updates. 1arXiv:2611.22095v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 Insight. There is a long history of leveraging short-term temporal regularity in various video-based applica-tions [2, 27]. All these approaches use recent history states to predict future states. Our key insight here is that many Gaus-sian trajectories exhibit local temporal regularity (Fig. 2). This motivates us to leverage temporal extrapolation from recent history. However, prediction reliability varies across Gaussians and over time, and the impact of a state error on the rendered image depends on visibility and rendering contribution. These observations motivate selecting Gaus-sians for exact updates based on prediction reliability and rendering contribution. 

Method. Based on our insight, we propose C UE SPLAT ,a post-training acceleration framework (Sec. 4) that enables sparse Gaussian deformation while keeping the pretrained 4DGS model intact without pruning. During inference, a lightweight router periodically selects a subset of Gaussians for exact deformation updates. For the remaining Gaussians, we predict their states from the three most recent accepted states. We merge the exact deformation outputs for the se-lected Gaussians with the predicted states of the remaining Gaussians for rendering. In addition, to improve the render-ing efficiency, we further reuse the indices of the Gaussian subset across nearby frames to reduce routing overhead, while deformation is recomputed for the selected Gaussians at each frame. To train the router, we first calibrate a scene-specific up-date budget using prediction errors collected offline over the calibration frames (Sec. 4.4). We then train the router to estimate the update priority of each Gaussian using super-vised learning. Lastly, we validate the budget through offline closed-loop rendering checks and increase it if the quality criteria are not met. To control accumulated prediction error, C UE SPLAT mon-itors online drift signals and schedules three consecutive full-deformation frames to rebuild the prediction history (Sec. 4.5). To mitigate the latency spikes caused by these full refreshes, we use refresh precomputation to distribute part of the deformation workload across preceding frames. 

Results. We evaluate C UE SPLAT over three datasets [24, 36, 47] using four 4DGS algorithms, E-D3DGS [1], 4DGaus-sians [43], GaGS [28], and SpeeDe3DGS [39]. Compared with the corresponding GPU baselines, C UE SPLAT achieves up to 6.7× speedups on Jetson AGX Orin and up to 8.2×

on Orin NX, with dataset-average PSNR decreases of at most 0.16 dB across the twelve backbone–dataset pairs. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • A sparse deformation framework that combines histori-cal state prediction with selective updates from frozen deformation models. • Scene-specific budget calibration and teacher-supervised routing to allocate updates according to prediction reliabil-ity and rendering contribution. • Drift-aware history refresh and refresh precomputation to control accumulated error and distribute refresh work across frames. 

## 2. Related Work 

Dynamic Gaussian Representations. Canonical defor-mation fields used in dynamic NeRFs [33–35] also model Gaussian motion [6], while other representations explic-itly model spacetime evolution [7, 41, 49]. Deformable 3D Gaussians [48] maps canonical primitives to time-varying states, while 4DGaussians [43] combines factor-ized 4D features [3, 12] with a lightweight deformation decoder. GaGS [28] incorporates geometry features, and E-D3DGS [1] combines per-Gaussian and temporal embed-dings with coarse and fine deformations. Explicit temporal approaches [18, 26, 32] include Spacetime Gaussian Feature Splatting [25], which combines temporal opacity, parametric motion and rotation, and feature rendering. For deformation-based models, repeated network evaluations remain costly. CUE SPLAT learns to allocate evaluations for sequential ren-dering with frozen pretrained parameters. 

Reducing Dynamic Computation. Compact motion models [20, 54] reduce computation, while pruning and com-pact encoding improve efficiency for static [10, 11, 14] and dynamic scenes [53]. SC-GS [17] interpolates motion from sparse control points, while MoDec-GS [21] uses global and local scaffolds. DASH [4] combines static–dynamic de-composition with 4D hash encoding, and SpeeDe3DGS [39] combines temporal pruning and sampling with groupwise rigid motion. However, pruning or motion constraints can impair fidelity, while fixed static–dynamic partitions do not adapt update allocation over time. C UE SPLAT retains the pretrained Gaussian set and deformation model, periodically selecting Gaussians for exact updates using learned priorities and predicting remaining states from history. 

Temporal Reuse and Selective Computation. Temporal coherence supports streaming reconstruction [13, 15, 16, 46] and long sequences [44, 45]. 3DGStream [38] uses a com-pact neural transformation cache, while SWinGS [37] fits consistent representations over sliding windows. Both ex-ploit temporal structure during fitting. At inference, CaT-GS [52] reuses culling and sorting results and schedules tiles to balance rasterization. Video methods reuse intermedi-ate states through sparse updates [8, 30, 31], and Eventful Transformers [9] selectively recomputes tokens. C UE SPLAT 

applies selective computation to frozen Gaussian deforma-tion models, predicting states from accepted history and learning which Gaussians require exact updates. ## 3. Motivation 

In this section, we first show that Gaussian center trajectories often exhibit local temporal regularity. Fig. 2 analyzes five-frame E-D3DGS [1] trajectories on two datasets: N3DV [24] and Technicolor [36]. We classify each Gaussian trajectory into: static, linear, quadratic, and other moves. On aver-age, static, linear, and quadratic fits jointly cover 66.2% of Gaussian-window pairs on N3DV and 68.8% on Technicolor. Additional linear and quadratic fits cover 26.5% of all pairs. These results motivate exploiting local temporal regularity for inexpensive history-based prediction. However, this local regularity varies across both Gaus-sians and time. Fig. 3 shows locally straight and nonlinear projected trajectories co-existing in a sear steak scene. E.g., a red trajectory may be locally straight before a turn, but the change in direction can make extrapolation from recent motion less reliable. Across N3DV sequences, 54.4% of sampled Gaussians satisfy the five-frame fitting criterion in some windows but violate it in others. These observa-tions motivate adapting the selection of Gaussians for exact updates over time. Choosing which Gaussians to update also requires con-sidering how prediction errors in position, rotation, scale, and appearance affect the rendered image. We therefore combine history-based prediction with learned update prior-ities based on each Gaussian’s state history and rendering contribution. To limit error accumulation, we also rebuild the prediction history with full-deformation refreshes. We evaluate prediction and history refresh in Sec. 5.4.

5.1. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate C UE SPLAT on Technicolor [36], N3DV [24], and NeRF-DS [47], comprising 5, 9, and 7 sequences, respec-tively, using E-D3DGS [1], 4DGaussians [43], GaGS [28], and SpeeDe3DGS [39]. Each backbone and its C UE S- 

> PLAT

variant share the same pretrained checkpoint and gsplat/AccuTile renderer [50]. All SpeeDe3DGS experi-ments use TSP and TSS, without GroupFlow. Unless other-wise stated, we use a selection-reuse period of N = 5 . For Technicolor’s 50-frame sequences, we disable refreshes after initialization. Throughput is measured on a Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB Developer Kit, an Orin NX 16 GB, and an A6000, with both Jetsons operating in MAXN mode. We evaluate quality over full sequences using PSNR, SSIM [42], AlexNet LPIPS [51], and the temporal metrics tOF and tLP × 100 [5]. FPS is the number of rendered frames divided by synchronized wall-clock time, including prediction, selection, deformation, rendering, and refresh computation. Backbone timing and instruction profiling exclude the three initialization frames. We time each experi-ment independently over three runs per scene and average scenes equally. Speedup is the ratio of dataset-average FPS. FPS P5 is the fifth percentile of instantaneous frame rates, computed per run and then averaged across runs and scenes. Higher values indicate better slow-frame performance. We also report executed GPU warp instructions per frame. Scene-specific preparation uses training views and times-tamps and takes 1–2 minutes on an RTX A6000, plus about one minute for budget escalation after failed validation. Routers have about 2.0k–2.6k parameters. Implementation and measurements are shown in Supplementary Sec. D.1– Sec. D.3 and Sec. E. 

5.2. Main Results 

Tbl. 1 compares image quality, temporal consistency, and rendering efficiency across twelve backbone–dataset pairs. CUE SPLAT achieves 1.75–6.74 × speedups on Jetson AGX Orin, with dataset-average PSNR decreases of at most 0.16 dB. SSIM, LPIPS, and the temporal metrics remain close to their full-deformation baselines, indicating that the speedups largely preserve image and temporal quality. 

Slow-frame Performance. FPS P5 improves across all twelve pairs. For GaGS on Technicolor, it increases from 10.63 to 52.58 FPS. C UE SPLAT therefore improves both average throughput and the frame rates experienced during slower parts of rendering. 

Executed Computation. Separate server-side profiling shows a 29.9%–90.7% reduction in executed GPU warp instructions per frame. GaGS on Technicolor decreases from 8206.42 to 760.01 million instructions per frame. These measurements confirm that sparse deformation reduces the computational work of the rendering pipeline. 

Results on Other Devices. Fig. 5 shows throughput gains across all twelve pairs on both A6000 and Orin NX, reach-ing 5.04 × on A6000 and 1.81–8.15 × on Orin NX. GaGS on Technicolor achieves the largest gain on both devices, increasing from 45.23 to 228.05 FPS on A6000 and from 3.79 to 30.91 FPS on Orin NX. 

Camera Motion. With unchanged routers and budgets, E-D3DGS + C UE SPLAT achieves 48.54 dB PSNR against full-deformation renderings along moving-camera trajectories across all nine N3DV scenes (Supplementary Sec. D.10). 

5.3. Comparison with DASH 

DASH [4] fixes its static–dynamic partition for the entire sequence, while C UE SPLAT periodically reselects Gaussians for exact updates. On Technicolor, we compare DASH-style controls on pretrained backbones and a separately trained reproduction of the official DASH implementation. Against DASH-style, C UE SPLAT improves mean PSNR for all four backbones (Tbl. 2). E-D3DGS and 4DGaus-sians gain 0.37 and 0.26 dB, respectively, with less than 0.5% lower FPS. GaGS and SpeeDe3DGS gain 0.22 and 1.19 dB, with 81.3% and 26.3% higher FPS, respectively. At matched scene budgets, C UE SPLAT improves mean PSNR over a fixed-partition variant using the same history-based prediction by 0.26–1.69 dB across the four backbones (Sup-plementary Tbl. S4).

## 6. Conclusion 

We presented C UE SPLAT , a post-training framework that combines history-based prediction with learned update selec-tion to accelerate dynamic Gaussian rendering. A calibrated scene budget determines how many Gaussians receive exact updates, and the router adapts their selection over time. His-tory refreshes control accumulated prediction error, while precomputation improves slow-frame performance with lit-tle effect on average throughput. Across four backbones and three datasets, C UE SPLAT achieves 1.75 –6.74 × speedups on Jetson AGX Orin, with dataset-average PSNR decreases of at most 0.16 dB. These results show that adapting de-formation computation to local predictability can improve efficiency while preserving the pretrained representation. The current implementation assumes sequential render-ing at uniform time steps.
