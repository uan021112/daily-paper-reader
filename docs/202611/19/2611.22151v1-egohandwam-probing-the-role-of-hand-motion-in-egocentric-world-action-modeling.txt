Abstract —How much does hand motion tell us about what a first-person camera will see next? EgoHandWAM forecasts three-dimensional wrist and fingertip trajectories and a future visual feature from the current image and recent hand states. We train on 751,065 EgoDex windows using a frozen DINOv2 encoder. On held-out development sessions, the action-only model reduces two-second wrist endpoint error from 60.26 to 56.48 mm relative to a stationary forecast. It still underestimates long reaches. Adding future-feature supervision gives nearly identical trajectory accuracy. With the recorded hand history, the visual head reduces smooth L1 error by 1.32% relative to copying the current feature. Shuffling histories across sessions erases this gain. In a separate four-task probe, adding recorded future displacements to observed context and future tracking-validity masks reduces visual-feature error by 3.93%. Masks alone give little improvement. A trajectory predicted from the same observations adds little. On two simulated RoboTwin tasks, substituting aligned human examples for an auxiliary batch of robot examples gives no consistent increase in task success across three training seeds. 

Keywords —Egocentric learning, world–action models, hand trajectory forecasting, visual feature prediction, imitation learn-ing. 

I. Introduction 

When someone reaches for a lid, a first-person camera records the hand, the object and the motion of the camera itself. A wrist trajectory captures part of this interaction. How much can a model anticipate before the hand moves, and how useful is that information for predicting what comes into view? Ego4D and EgoDex make it possible to study these questions in recorded human activity [1], [2]. We examine hand motion in three roles. First, motion is a 

prediction target . Given the current image and eight observed hand states, EgoHandWAM predicts the next two seconds of wrist and fingertip positions. A second head predicts the visual feature at the last time step. We vary the weight of the visual loss while holding the trajectories, inputs and training budget fixed. Second, motion is an input to a visual forecast . A separate regression model receives either a recorded future trajectory with its tracking mask or a trajectory predicted from the observed context. Comparing these inputs distinguishes infor-mation available after the movement from information a model can supply at prediction time. Third, human recordings provide auxiliary training data for a robot policy . A shared Transformer learns from human and robot observations, while simulated demonstrations supervise the robot commands. We measure task completion in simulated bottle grasping and bimanual block handover, and test whether the correspondence between a human hand history and its video matters. Only robot demonstrations supply executable action labels. Together, these experiments trace how far a hand signal that helps forecast a visual feature carries over to trajectory accuracy and robot task success. 

II. Related Work 

A. Egocentric data and hand forecasting 

Ego4D establishes a broad setting for understanding and forecasting first-person activity [1]. EgoDex provides manip-ulation video with 3D hand tracking and an official two-second forecasting comparison for the same wrist and fingertip landmarks [2]. Its benchmark includes task language and a fixed test split. We use session-held-out development data to compare supervision conditions. Some recordings lack confidence fields [3]. EPIC-KITCHENS-100 expands fine-grained action recog-nition and anticipation in everyday environments [4], while Ego-Exo4D links first- and third-person observations of skilled activity [5]. Geometric datasets provide complementary su-pervision: H2O annotates both hands and manipulated ob-jects [6]; AssemblyHands supplies multi-view-derived hand pose annotations [7]; HOT3D supports egocentric hand-object tracking [8]; and ARCTIC captures bimanual manipulation of articulated objects [9]. These resources differ in capture setting, geometry and task definition. We use EgoDex for the human forecasting experiments and specify how we select windows with usable tracking labels. Hand pose recovery and future trajectory prediction also have different input-output requirements. WildHands stud-ies pose estimation in everyday egocentric images, includ-ing the effects of perspective and camera information [10]. HaMeR reconstructs hand meshes from monocular images with a transformer-based model [11]. The Object-Centric Transformer jointly predicts hand motion and interaction hotspots from egocentric video [12], connecting future motion arXiv:2611.22151v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 2

with likely interaction locations. Our target is instead a fixed set of future three-dimensional wrist and fingertip positions. Prior trajectory forecasting methods model temporal de-pendencies and uncertainty. The uncertainty-aware state space transformer studies three-dimensional hand forecasting from observed egocentric RGB video [13]. EgoMAN considers three-dimensional hand trajectory prediction from human in-teraction video [14]. EgoH4 distinguishes global wrist mo-tion from articulated hand prediction and evaluates motion baselines [15]. These works motivate geometric displacement and position errors. Our observed hand-state inputs, landmark set, horizon and coordinate protocol differ, so their published scores are not treated as directly comparable baselines. 

B. Visual representations for world-action learning 

Visual pretraining objectives offer different forms of infor-mation. DINO learns by self-distillation without labels [16], masked autoencoders reconstruct missing image patches [17], and CLIP aligns images with natural-language descriptions [18]. We use the pretrained image representation as one input to the hand forecast. Robotics studies further examine how pretrained visual features transfer to control. R3M learns a representation from human video for downstream manipulation [19]; VIP derives visual representations and rewards through value-based pretraining [20]. VC-1 and CortexBench evaluate represen-tations across diverse embodied tasks [21], while Voltron combines language-conditioned visual reconstruction with vi-sually grounded language prediction [22]. We examine both geometric forecast accuracy on EgoDex and closed-loop task success in simulation. DINOv2 learns transferable visual features through self-supervision [23]. Register tokens address artifacts in visual transformer feature maps [24]. We use the released register-equipped Base encoder without updating its parameters. All matched runs reuse the same cached visual features. 

C. World-action prediction and visual reconstruction 

EgoWAM compares pixel, feature and motion targets for human–robot co-training [25]. We hold the compact visual target fixed and vary the role of hand motion. In the human forecaster, two heads use the same observed context to predict future hand coordinates and a visual feature. Only the separate visual probe receives a future trajectory as input. Representation autoencoders couple a representation en-coder to an image decoder [26]. Latent diffusion models [27] and diffusion transformers [28] can generate images through a learned latent representation. Our visual objective operates directly on a pooled DINOv2 feature. We therefore evaluate it in feature space. Auxiliary objectives also require a choice of relative loss scale. Uncertainty-based weighting learns task weights [29], while GradNorm adapts them using gradient magnitudes [30]. We compare a zero visual-loss weight with a fixed positive weight under matched training conditions.

B. Coverage and compatibility 

Throughout this paper, full-data training means using ev-ery eligible training window in the fixed available EgoDex in-ventory after session separation and geometric validity checks. Of 777,244 candidate windows, 762,777 remain after preserv-ing the held-out sessions; 11,712 of these fail the validity criteria. The full training set includes 689,806 confidence-filtered windows and 61,259 additional windows admitted by the compatibility rule, for a total of 751,065. The compatibility rule applies only to sources missing all 12 required confidence fields. For these sources, geometric validity is determined from finite, valid rigid transforms and valid parent wrists, without applying a confidence threshold. Missing confidence is never imputed as 1. Supplied confidence values must be finite and within [0 .5, 1] ; partially missing fields remain masked. Existing camera, temporal and anchor-validity checks are preserved.

IV. Joint world-action formulation 

Figure 1 connects recorded EgoDex observations to an action head and a world-feature head through a shared predic-tor. In the current implementation, action means a sequence of human wrist and fingertip positions. The visual target is a normalized 64-dimensional feature two seconds after the anchor. This is an observation-conditioned joint forecast. The predicted action is not fed into the world head, and the 64-dimensional output does not encode explicit object dynamics or support RGB decoding. 

A. Egocentric visual interface and shared state 

The DINO branch uses frozen DINOv2 Base weights with registers through the RAE encoder interface. This interface removes the final LayerNorm affine parameters and applies the released RAE latent statistics before pooling. In the evaluated cache pipeline, a 128-pixel preprocessing stage precedes 224-pixel encoder input. The spatial feature grid 

Ft ∈ R768 ×16 ×16 is reduced by fixed 3 × 5 pooling and a fixed seeded projection to 64 dimensions. Let ft denote this 64-dimensional projected feature and vt = ( ft − μf )/σ f its normalized value. Subtraction and division act componentwise using training-set statistics. Let ¯Ht denote the observed hand history normalized using its own training statistics. For each query time τ ∈ { τ1, . . . , τ 60 }, the predictor concatenates the history, image feature and time encoding: 

x(τ ) = [ ¯Ht, v t, q (τ )] ∈ R548 ,q(τ ) = [ τ, τ 2, sin( πτ ), cos( πτ )] . (1) The three inputs contribute 480, 64 and four values, respec-tively. Two linear layers of widths 32 and 32, each followed by GELU, produce the shared state h(τ ). All 60 queries use the same observed context and can be evaluated in parallel.

A. Metrics and aggregation 

For each valid landmark and frame, the Euclidean error in millimeters is dkj = 1000 ∥ekj ∥2 when ekj is expressed in meters. Wrist average displacement error (ADE) averages dkj 

over the two wrists and all valid future frames. Wrist final displacement error (FDE) uses the wrists at k = 60 . Mean per-joint position error (MPJPE-12) averages dkj over the 12 wrist and fingertip landmarks and all valid future frames. Metrics are averaged within windows, then hierarchically across episodes, sessions and tasks with equal weight at each level. Missing entries are excluded, not set to zero. Endpoint metrics never substitute the last valid earlier frame. The combined cohort has 36,596 valid windows for trajectory errors and 36,469 for exact wrist endpoints. Predictions are deterministic ( K = 1 ); no best-of-K selection is used. The sample SD across four seeds measures training variation, not a confidence interval over independent sessions. For se-lected paired contrasts we also report 95% Bayesian bootstrap intervals over sessions. Each of 10,000 draws assigns one independent exponential weight of mean one to each session, shared across conditions and tasks. We renormalize session weights within each task and average the task estimates equally. Intervals use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles with tasks, folds and fitted models fixed. 

B. Matched full-data comparison 

The stationary baseline repeats the observed anchor posi-tion. Constant velocity extrapolates each landmark using the velocity between its most recent valid historical observation and the anchor; it uses zero velocity when no valid pair is available. The two learned models use identical data and architecture. Action-only uses the pose loss; Joint adds the future-feature loss. Both models receive the current image and hand history. The action-only model improves all three trajectory metrics over the stationary baseline (Table I). At two seconds, wrist endpoint error falls by 3.78 mm. The joint model improves on stationary prediction by 3.80 mm (session interval 3.39 to 4.21 mm). Extrapolating the last observed velocity gives the largest endpoint error. Adding the future-feature loss changes wrist endpoint error by just 0.02 mm at the tested loss weight. For the action-only model, training on the expanded in-ventory lowered MPJPE-12 on development windows without confidence fields (73.74 to 71.29 mm) and slightly raised it on confidence-filtered windows (40.96 to 41.09 mm). Both training runs covered two epochs with normalization fitted to their respective training data.

VI. Can a hand trajectory inform a visual forecast? 

The main experiment varies the supervision used to train the action predictor. Here we ask a different question. For the same observed context, how much does knowing the subsequent hand motion improve a visual forecast? We compare recorded and predicted trajectories as inputs to a separate regression model. 

A. Inputs and separation of sessions 

We use 927 eligible windows from 183 recording sessions covering four tasks: adding or removing a lid, assembling or disassembling blocks, cleaning a surface, and coloring. These windows came from the training partition of the smaller cached experiment. Five outer folds hold out complete ses-sions. Each window is scored on its outer held-out fold. Forecast-conditioned predictions are evaluated for each pose seed. The target is the change in a 64-dimensional pooled full-image feature from the anchor to the exact two-second endpoint. A zero change corresponds to copying the current feature. The context includes the current image features and the observed hand history with validity indicators. The trajectory condition adds the displacement of each of the 12 landmarks relative to the anchor at four horizons: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 s. We fit ridge regressions with a fixed unit penalty. Feature scaling and target normalization use only the training sessions of the corresponding outer fold. Window-level mean squared errors are averaged within sessions and then across sessions and tasks. The resulting scores measure squared error in normalized feature space.

Figure 2 shows three examples selected from the four-task probe. In A, the forecast closely matches the magnitude of the two-second displacement (40 mm predicted, 41 mm recorded). In B and C, it predicts about 68 mm for reaches of 221 and 331 mm, respectively. The recorded condition supplies future landmark positions and validity masks. For the forecast condition, four inner folds generate out-of-fold trajectories to fit the visual regression. For each outer fold we fit a separate compact action predictor using the four-task training sessions. Its held-out trajectories use only anchor-time inputs. VII. Does human hand history help a robot policy? 

We evaluate policies on two simulated RoboTwin 2.0 tasks: grasping two bottles and handing over a block between arms [32]. We measure full-task success on 100 held-out scenes per task and training seed. The bottle comparisons share the same 48 ALOHA demonstrations and evaluation scenes. One spatial policy also uses demonstrations from three additional robots. We inspected the bottle suite during policy design. 

A. Training and execution 

Figure 3 shows the connection between embodiments. A human example contains eight observed hand states and the current image feature. Its target is the DINO feature two seconds later in the same recording. A robot example contains eight robot observations. Its visual target is 16 demonstration steps ahead. During human–robot training, the robot action loss and both domains’ feature losses update the shared Transformer. At deployment, the action head predicts 16 steps of end-effector and gripper commands. The robot executes the first eight steps before the policy receives a new observation. 

B. Hand-history supervision and task success 

The robot-only policy learns robot actions and future visual features. For the comparison with human supervision, we re-place an auxiliary robot feature batch with EgoDex examples. VIII. Discussion 

A. What the forecasts capture 

The displacement penalty in the pose loss favors short forecasts. Consider one target at distance D, with the di-rection known. Ignoring the 1 mm smoothing term, pre-dicting distance d costs |D − d|/s p + d2/(3 s2

> p

), minimized at d = min( D, 1.5sp). With sp = 0.1 m this idealized optimum saturates at 150 mm. In the four-task probe, removing the penalty increased mean predicted landmark displacement from 11.28 to 18.72 mm over four horizons, still well below the recorded 69.62 mm. This ablation concerns the compact predictor. The contribution of the penalty to underreach in the full-data model remains unmeasured. The probe provides a complementary result. Recorded dis-placements add predictive information beyond the observed context and future tracking masks. The target covers the entire frame. Hand appearance, viewpoint and object state can all 7

change it; the lid experiment measures no separate object-state outcome. A deterministic trajectory forecast can re-express the observations for the linear probe, but it supplies no indepen-dent observation of the future. Its negligible incremental gain therefore measures its value to this probe. It does not by itself establish that trajectory error caused the gap. 

B. Scope of the robot results 

Human hand history enters the robot policy through an auxiliary feature-prediction objective. Across the two simu-lated tasks, preserving its alignment with the video gave no consistent improvement over shuffling it. All policies within a task share the initial robot core: a trained bottle checkpoint for grasping and a common random initialization for handover. Comparisons therefore pair policies within each task. Success varied most across seeds on handover. The hand-history comparison uses three training seeds per task. The bottle spatial-feature and cross-embodiment compar-isons each use a single training seed. For EgoDex, the four paired seeds measure training variation on fixed development sessions. Geometric validity extends coverage to recordings without confidence fields, whose tracking quality remains unmeasured. 

IX. Conclusion 

EgoHandWAM reduced wrist endpoint error on EgoDex but underestimated long reaches. The visual head forecast two-second features more accurately than copying the current feature when the hand history matched the image. In aseparate probe, recorded displacements improved the whole-image feature forecast after accounting for future tracking validity. Trajectories from the probe’s compact action predictor added little. On simulated bottle grasping and block handover, training with aligned human histories gave no consistent increase in robot task success.
