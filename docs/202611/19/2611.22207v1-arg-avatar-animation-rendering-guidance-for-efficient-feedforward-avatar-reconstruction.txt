## Abstract 

Reconstructing animatable 3D human avatars from im-ages is important for applications such as gaming and aug-mented reality. Feedforward methods make avatar recon-struction accessible by reducing inference time to a few sec-onds, but recent approaches rely heavily on scaling model capacity, training data, and computation. We ask whether such scaling is necessary. To this end, we introduce ARG-Avatar, an efficient feedforward framework with only 68M trainable parameters that achieves SOTA reconstruction quality. Our key insight is that 3D animation and render-ing already provide strong geometric correspondences that existing methods largely attempt to learn from data. In light of this, we design the Foreground Avatar Coordinate RoPE attention (FACRoPE) and Intermediate Token Rendering (ITR) pipeline to guide avatar tokens to attend to their cor-responding patches. Experiments across multiple datasets show that ARG-Avatar consistently outperforms prior meth-ods when using two or more input images. Compared with LHM++, ARG-Avatar uses approximately 9 × fewer train-able parameters, 4.8 × fewer training sequences, and 14 ×

less training compute, while running 6 × faster and achiev-ing a 2.7 dB PSNR improvement on the out-of-distribution NeuMan benchmark. 

## 1. Introduction 

3D avatars serve as our embodied representations in virtual environments, enabling users to appear, move, and inter-act in 3D while preserving their identity and motion. They are already widely used as controllable characters in games, virtual actors in film production, and digital humans for livestreaming. Their adoption could expand substantially if non-expert users could create high-quality, animatable avatars at low cost through simple capture procedures. Ul-timately, such technology could enable virtual spaces in which anyone can participate using a personalized 3D re-construction of themselves. arXiv:2611.22207v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 Animatable avatars can be obtained through either gen-eration or reconstruction. Generative methods learn a dis-tribution of human avatars conditioned on inputs such as a single image or a text description [6, 7, 106]. In contrast, re-construction methods estimate an avatar that explains a set of observed images or video frames [22, 28, 89]. This work focuses on reconstruction, with the goal of making high-quality avatar creation accessible with casual captures and modest computational resources. Existing avatar reconstruction methods broadly fall into two categories: optimization-based and feedforward-based. Optimization-based approaches fit a 3D representation sep-arately to each subject using input images. Recent meth-ods [39, 55, 89] can reconstruct avatars from monocular videos captured with ordinary smartphones, but their per-subject optimization may still require tens of minutes or even hours. Feedforward methods [67, 68, 118], by con-trast, predict an avatar in a single network forward pass from one or a few images, reducing reconstruction time to only a few seconds. These methods typically predict an avatar an-chored to a parametric human template, allowing the result-ing representation to be animated to target poses using Lin-ear Blend Skinning (LBS). Despite their efficient inference, recent feedforward approaches have followed a scaling-first training paradigm, relying on increasingly large transformer architectures and increasingly large proprietary datasets. For example, IDOL [118] is trained on 102K sequences, while the LHM series [67, 68] uses approximately 300K in-ternal videos. This raises a fundamental question: is large-scale model and data growth necessary for high-quality feedforward avatar reconstruction? 

We challenge this scaling-first philosophy. First, increas-ing model capacity cannot overcome the representational constraints imposed by a template-anchored avatar. Sec-ond, existing architectures do not fully exploit the geomet-ric structure already provided by the template. Most meth-ods rely on stacks of generic attention layers, forcing the network to learn the correspondence between avatar ele-ments and image regions almost entirely from data. How-ever, because each avatar element is attached to a known template and transformed by LBS, much of this correspon-dence can be computed geometrically rather than learned from scratch. Ignoring this prior leads to an unnecessarily data- and compute-intensive learning problem. Motivated by this observation, we introduce Animation and Rendering Guidance for Avatar Reconstruction , or 

ARG-Avatar , an efficient feedforward framework that ex-plicitly incorporates template, animation, and rendering ge-ometry into the reconstruction network. An overview of ARG-Avatar is shown in Fig. 1. The central idea is to guide each avatar token toward the image region in which the cor-responding 3D element would appear after being animated and rendered under the observed pose. Related Work 

Optimization-based avatar fitting. Animatable human reconstruction is usually organized around articulated body templates such as SMPL-X [14, 18, 50, 60, 61]. Per-subject methods optimize avatar attributes so that the ren-dered subject explains the captured images. Neural-field methods model the canonical body with NeRF, SDF, oc-cupancy, or learned blend-weight fields [1, 8, 16, 27– 29, 53, 59, 62, 63, 90, 105, 116]; they are expressive but costly to optimize and must resolve difficult posed-to-canonical correspondences. Explicit Gaussian and mesh variants improve rendering speed and make LBS-style ani-mation more direct [9, 21, 22, 30, 34, 37, 39, 43, 45, 47, 56, 58, 65, 71, 73, 80, 89, 98, 103, 119]. ExAvatar [55] uses SMPL-aware regularization and triplane-parameterized [6] Gaussian attributes, but still requires subject-specific train-ing that ranges from tens of minutes to hours. 

Generative and native 3D avatar methods. Generative avatar methods reduce capture requirements by hallucinat-ing missing views. Some optimize a representation with SDS [64] or related generative priors [4, 24, 46, 81, 91, 108]. Others use multiview or video diffusion for con-sistency or refinement [2, 5, 17, 25, 48, 49, 52, 66, 74, 75, 79, 82, 83, 117]. These systems can operate from a single image, but they consume even more compute than optimization-based fitting methods. Native 3D and large re-construction models [36, 41, 84, 95, 99, 106, 109, 113] learn 3D distributions over massive 3D datasets [11, 12]. Most of these methods require massive 3D assets and have difficulty scaling to the human domain due to the lack of large-scale, high-quality 3D data. SIGMAN [102] is the closest native 3D human baseline, and we compare against it. 

Feedforward avatar reconstruction. Earlier feedfor-ward human methods [3, 31, 33, 35, 42, 107] made avatar reconstruction efficient but typically produced static meshes or volumetric implicits rather than real-time animatable avatars [20, 26, 44, 69, 70, 76, 96, 97, 111, 112, 114, 115]. Generalizable human renderers and earlier animatable mod-els introduced canonical body spaces and learned view synthesis [15, 23, 86, 101], setting the stage for recent scaled feedforward systems. IDOL [118] regresses UV-space Gaussians with a Sapiens-scale encoder; LHM [67] uses a large transformer trained on 300K+ private videos; LHM++ [68] uses Point Transformer [93] and extends it to multiple images. LCA [38] pushes scaled-up training to an extreme with massive internal data. ARG-Avatar belongs to this feedforward avatar category, but instead of scaling up model and data, it revisits the geometry of template-based avatar reconstruction, resulting in a strong model with sub-stantially fewer trainable parameters. 

Geometry-aware attention. More recent literature ex-plores injecting geometric guidance into attention with positional encodings and rotary position embeddings (RoPE) [77]. GTA [54] generalizes rotary encodings from 1D sequences to relative camera transforms on multiview tokens, and PRoPE [40] and RayRoPE [94] further use projective frusta or ray segments to guide RoPE atten-tion, an idea carried into stereo and multiview video dif-fusion [13, 78]. We propose FACRoPE with two distinct novelties: first, Foreground Avatar Coordinates (FAC) link tokens in 2D animated and rendered space with 3D canoni-cal tokens through an articulated human template; second, a rendering-in-the-loop pipeline guides attention by decoding and rendering an avatar from intermediate tokens.

Template-based feedforward avatar reconstruction requires extracting appearance and geometry information from the input images and transferring it to the corresponding loca-tions on a predefined avatar template. Coordinate-based guidance. We use Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) [77] as a framework for injecting geo-metric guidance. For queries and keys with associated posi-tions, RoPE makes attention scores depend on their relative coordinate offsets. Given queries Q and keys K carrying coordinates cq and ck, RoPE rotates each token according to its coordinates before computing the attention product: 

RoPEAttn( Q, K, V) = Attn( R(cq )Q, R(ck)K, V),

(5) where R(·) applies a per-token block-diagonal rotation built from that token’s coordinates. Consequently, the similar-ity between query token i and key token j depends on the relative coordinate offset through the relative rotation 

R(ckj − cqi ). In our cross-attention, UV tokens serve as queries and image tokens as keys. Aligning their coordi-nates helps each UV token match its corresponding image token.

u and v are both in [0 , 1] . These coordinates serve as ck in RoPE attention. We then assign each UV token the coor-dinates of its projected image location after animation and rendering. The algorithm is described in Algorithm 1. The resulting coordinate is referred to as FAC and serves as cq

in Eq. (5). Given a candidate avatar Ccand , we animate it to the pose observed in image v, project it into the camera view, and render its depth. The candidate avatar can be a neutral mesh or a coarse Gaussian avatar obtained through Intermediate Token Rendering (ITR, Sec. 3.3). As shown in Row 1 of Fig. 3, FAC makes the attention precise and sharp. The last column of Fig. 3 visualizes the coordinates relative to the image plane: the coordinates of visible tokens lie on the plane, whereas those of occluded tokens are pushed away from it. 

3.3. ARG-Avatar Network

M = 2 multiview ARGBlocks. The output UV tokens are decoded into a canonical avatar C and pose-dependent de-formations for each target driving pose θ.

Feature Extraction. Each input image is processed through a frozen SAM3DBody [100] model and a train-able ConvNeXt [92] model. SAM3DBody estimates a per-image body pose θv and supplies dense ViT backbone fea-tures [57] that are added to the ConvNeXt features, forming per-view image tokens Fv ∈ RHf ×Wf ×Df .

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We train ARG-Avatar using only publicly avail-able datasets: DNARendering [10], 2K2K [19], 4D-Dress [85], Human4DiT [72], THuman2.1 [104], and Hu-manVid [88]. Together, these datasets contain 6.8K subjects and 64K sequences. We evaluate the model on NeuMan [29], a standard benchmark for evaluating animatable avatars, and on vali-dation splits of the training datasets. We construct the val-idation splits by randomly selecting 10% of the subjects in each dataset and excluding them from training. 

Training. ARG-Avatar is trained for 100K steps with a global batch size of 96 using AdamW [51]. We use a learn-ing rate of 10 −4 with cosine scheduling. Training takes around 200 hours on four A6000 GPUs. For the ablation study, we train all variants at 512 2 UV resolution for 50K steps and observe that their training losses have converged. Full training details are available in Appendix E. 

Baselines and metrics. We compare ARG-Avatar with four feedforward baselines trained on large-scale datasets: SIGMAN [102], IDOL [118], LHM [67], and LHM++ [68]. We evaluate all methods on the novel-pose synthesis (NPS) task. Given one or more input views, each method renders the reconstructed avatar in novel target poses, and the resulting images are compared with the corresponding ground-truth images. Following standard practice, we re-port PSNR, SSIM [87], and LPIPS [110]. For both in-put and target poses on all evaluation datasets, we use MHR poses estimated by SAM3DBody for ARG-Avatar and SMPL-X poses estimated by LHM-Track [67] for the baselines. On NeuMan, our evaluation of LHM++ yields 27.97 dB and 28.03 dB with two and four views, respec-tively, closely matching the 27.83 dB and 27.94 dB reported in the original LHM++ paper [68]. This close agreement supports the reliability of our tracking results for evaluating baseline performance. 

4.2. Results 

Quantitative Results. We report the quantitative results for all methods in Tab. 1. Two main findings emerge. First, ARG-Avatar outperforms all baselines on every dataset with two or more input views. With six views (V = 6 ), it beats LHM++, the strongest baseline, by 2.7 dB in PSNR on NeuMan, with similar gains on multiview videos (3.2 dB), 3D scans (4.3 dB), and HumanVid (3.4 dB). Second, ARG-Avatar makes more effective use of ad-ditional input views. In the “input poses” evaluation, it reaches 33.30 dB with six views, outperforming LHM++ by 4.8 dB. On NeuMan, increasing the number of input views from one to six improves ARG-Avatar by 3.8 dB, whereas LHM++ improves by only 0.4 dB. This indicates that ARG-Avatar integrates information from multiple ob-servations more effectively. We note that ARG-Avatar is not trained to inpaint invis-ible regions, so its weaker performance at V = 1 is ex-pected. In real-world reconstruction scenarios, users can easily capture multiple input images by recording a video with a smartphone. The detailed per-dataset quantitative re-sults are presented in Appendix H. 

Qualitative Results. We present a qualitative comparison in Fig. 6. ARG-Avatar preserves both input texture and ge-ometry more faithfully than the baselines. In the DNARendering example, the red hair band is missing in LHM and only partially reconstructed in LHM++, while ARG-Avatar pro-duces the most complete reconstruction. Second, the blue boxes show the geometric differences. All three examples show clear shadows around the body in the overlays of the baseline re-sults, while ARG-Avatar has the smallest shadow area. Model Efficiency. With only 68M trainable parameters, ARG-Avatar has 6% of LHM’s and 11% of LHM++’s train-able parameter count. It is trained exclusively on public datasets, using ∼ 21% as much training data as LHM. Training requires around 125 H100-equivalent GPU-hours, 7% of LHM++’s compute (Appendix F). Six-view feedfor-ward reconstruction, including the SAM3DBody forward pass, takes 2.3 seconds: 6.2× faster than LHM++ ( 14 .2 sec-onds) with comparable GPU memory usage. Appendix G provides detailed model profiles.

## B. Adaptive UV Resolution 

ARG-Avatar uses connected components of the valid UV domain to adapt the sampling resolution before rasteriza-tion. Specifically, we identify the disconnected UV is-lands, associate each island with its corresponding anatom-ical region, and rasterize each component independently at a component-specific scale. Starting from a base resolution of 512 , we use 1.0× resolution for the face, 0.5× for the hands, and 2.0× for the body, limbs, and feet. We patchify the components separately, producing NT UV tokens in to-tal, and apply the CNN decoder independently to each com-ponent’s feature map; this prevents convolution and patch formation from mixing unrelated UV islands across empty atlas space. The valid samples from all decoded compo-nents form the L dense Gaussian primitives; each sample retains its face index and barycentric coordinates, so the adaptive sampling does not alter its surface anchor. This component-wise construction increases token density where clothing and articulation induce larger geometric variation, while avoiding unnecessary samples in compact regions.

## E. Training Details 

Each training batch samples six input observations and six target supervision views from the same subject. The model is trained for 100K steps with AdamW, cosine learning-rate scheduling, and a global batch size of 96. Train-ing uses bf16 mixed precision. The SAM3DBody back-bone is frozen. The final model is trained on four RTX A6000 GPUs for approximately 200 hours, corresponding to 125 H100-equivalent GPU-hours under the conversion used in Sec. F. For the ablation study, every variant uses 

512 2 UV resolution without adaptive UV resolution and is trained for 50K steps. 

Gaussian Decoders. Both CDec and PDec take 1024-dimensional tokens arranged in UV space. A linear layer and a 1 × 1 convolution project the token map to 256 chan-nels, followed by three residual blocks with 256, 128, and 64 output channels. Each block uses GroupNorm and SiLU and is followed by 2× upsampling and a convolution, giv-ing 8× upsampling in total. A final GroupNorm, SiLU, and 1 × 1 convolution predict the Gaussian attributes or pose-dependent residuals. The two decoders have separate weights and operate independently on each UV component. 

Training Dataset. Our training set is composed entirely of public human datasets and contains 6,821 subjects and 64,142 sequences in total. Each sequence corresponds to ei-ther a subject in a 3D scan dataset or a clip in a monocular or multiview video dataset. DNARendering [10] contributes 313 subjects and 53,100 motion and camera clips, providing the largest source of calibrated multiview dynamic supervi-sion. We use only Parts 1–4 because of storage limitations. 4D-Dress [85] adds 27 subjects and 972 sequences with challenging garment deformation. HumanVid [88] provides 2,537 human video clips. We further include scan data from 2K2K [19] and THuman2.1 [104], which contain 1,732 and 2,135 subjects, respectively, and generate synthetic views from these scans. Human4DiT [72] consists of 77 subjects in different outfits, totaling 3,666 scans. For every training dataset, we reserve 10% of subjects as a held-out validation split and exclude them from training. NeuMan [29] is never used in training or validation.
