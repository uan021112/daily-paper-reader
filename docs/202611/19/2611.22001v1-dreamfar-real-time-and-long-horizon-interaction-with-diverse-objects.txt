ABSTRACT
World models have shown great potential in serving as a simulator for robot manipulation. However,
existing models are either fit to a single environment or lack ability to simulate long-horizon contact-
rich events. We present DreamFAR, a world model whose dynamics model is trained from scratch
on a large robot-native teleoperation dataset, with no internet-video pretraining. We present an
architecture and learning recipe designed for training on robot-native data. DreamFAR is significantly
smaller than the high-performance video generation models, yet shows better understanding of contact
and causality, and runs 10 FPS on a commercial GPU. Experimentally, DreamFAR significantly
outperforms existing models in predicting the long-horizon future. We further demonstrate various
applications where DreamFAR can be used, including policy evaluation and mixed-data co-training.

Introduction
A world model (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018) predicts the future conditioned on an action input. The major appeal of
learning such model is that it’s a powerful abstraction of the world for dynamics and control, allowing robots to plan
many steps into the future, simulate the outcomes of their behavior and like humans (Flanagan and Wing, 1997), search
for the optimal actions in order to achieve their goals. Given these strengths, what prevents world models from being the
prominent tool for solving contact-rich manipulation tasks?
One of the major shortcomings of today’s world models is their inability to do long-horizon prediction (Gao et al., 2026;
Ye et al., 2026; Ali et al., 2025). Rolling out future predictions for long horizons, even for tens of seconds, often leads
to catastrophic error accumulation. And even when some world models do achieve long-horizon prediction (Wang
et al., 2026), they often struggle with generalization, as they are unable to properly model the physics of the diverse
contact-rich manipulation scenarios that robots commonly interact with.
We propose DreamFAR, a world model for real-time, long-horizon, contact-rich interaction across diverse objects
and scenes. Our key contribution is a contact-based memory context, motivated by the need to remember how earlier
interactions changed the scene even after objects left view. We combine a short window of recent frames with a set of
long-term keyframes, capturing ongoing motion and past interactions without expanding the attention context. Inspired
by human haptic memory (Hutmacher and Kuhbandner, 2018), we select keyframes at contact-making or breaking
moments, when robot–object interactions change the scene structure. The model predicts these events to update its
memory during rollouts without external contact annotations, and retrieves keyframes by end-effector-pose similarity to
recall relevant object states.
Together with other architectual choices decisions detailed in the method section, we demonstrate that DreamFAR
generalizes to diverse scenes and tasks, sustains minutes of open-loop rollouts under recorded actions, and supports
live teleoperation at 10 Hz. Compared with state-of-the-art world models trained on the same robot dataset, DreamFAR
produces more accurate predictions and accumulates less visual error over long rollouts. We evaluate this on 1,000
held-out scenes, where it achieves higher average prediction quality over one-minute rollouts (Fig. 5). We also evaluate
DreamFAR by the correlation between policy rollouts inside our model and the real world: across two policy checkpoints
and three tasks, simulated and real-robot mean task progress have a Pearson correlation of r = 0.71 (Fig. 6). Finally,
we demonstrate that data collected inside the simulator can improve real-robot policy performance. Experimentally,
training a policy on mixed real-world and simulator demonstrations increases the task progress by two-fold compared
to a policy trained on real-world demonstrations alone.

Related Work
Video-generator world models.
Most recent robot world models pretrain a large video generator on internet-scale
videos, followed by post-training on robot specific data (Agarwal et al., 2025; Ali et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Ye et al.,
2026; Gao et al., 2026). These models typically contain billions of parameters, ranging from 2B to 14B (Agarwal et al.,
2025; Ali et al., 2025). While large-scale video pretraining provides useful physical priors, accurately reproducing
physical dynamics remains challenging (Kang et al., 2024; Motamed et al., 2025), and extending generation beyond the
training horizon can lead to accumulating errors (Song et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). DreamFAR takes the opposite
route: its dynamics are trained causally from scratch on robot-native data and stream frame by frame.
Causal world models on robot or agent data.
A parallel line of work train on robot-specific data from the start.
Termed by Ha and Schmidhuber (2018), World Models jointly learn action-conditioned future observation prediction by
factorizing the problem into representation learning (mapping high dimensional observations into latent state vectors),
dynamics prediction (future state prediction conditioned on current state and actions) and future image generation
(mapping a low dimensional future state into a high dimensional observation). Follow up works like DREAMER
(Hafner et al., 2019) and many others use this framework for planning, RL and control (Hafner et al., 2025; Alonso
et al., 2024; Bruce et al., 2024). Model-based RL (Silver et al., 2017; Deisenroth and Rasmussen, 2011; Levine and
Koltun, 2013) works predate this recent interest in world modeling. On robots, action-conditioned video prediction from
robot-only data dates to Finn et al. (2016) and continues with autoregressive, diffusion and feature-space predictors
(Wu et al., 2024a; Zhu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026). Closest to ours are WEAVER (Jain et al., 2026), a
causal world model trained on robot-native manipulation data, and the Interactive World Simulator (IWS) (Wang et al.,
2026), which adds real-time, long-horizon interactive generation.
Long-horizon and memory mechanisms.
Two fundamental mechanisms allow modern video generators to model
worlds; first, generation which controls how errors compound, e.g., per-frame noise levels, KV-cached or rolling-
window denoising, training on the model’s own rollouts (Chen et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; Liu
et al., 2026a), and a frame-level attention sink on the initial generated frame(s) (Yang et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a).

Policy evaluation and data generation.
Physics simulators with real-to-sim assets rank policies in agreement with
real trials (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a; Badithela et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2025), but each scene is built by hand.
Learned world models skip the assets: policies are rolled out and scored inside the model (Quevedo et al., 2025; Li
et al., 2025; Tseng et al., 2026) or trained on generated data (Jang et al., 2025), and IWS reports real-to-simulated
correlation of policy outcomes (Wang et al., 2026). DreamFAR targets the same two uses as a streaming simulator in
which a policy, or a human through leader arms, acts closed-loop at 10 Hz.

We describe how unit-sphere normalization constrains latent scale (Sec. 3.3), how predicted contact transitions and
pose-based retrieval select long-term memories to complement recent context (Sec. 3.1), and how hybrid attention,
pipelined denoising over a rolling KV cache, and a persistent attention sink enable streaming prediction (Sec. 3.2).
3.1
Contact-Based Memory
A short context window can forget earlier interactions, such as where an object was placed after it leaves view. We
therefore represent the visual history in ht using both a window of T recent latents zt−T +1:t and a long-term memory
set Mt. The recent window captures ongoing motion, while memory retains selected earlier observations without
expanding the transformer’s context with rollout length.
We select memory keyframes based on predicted contact transitions, which mark the onset or end of robot–object
interactions. An auxiliary head on the dynamics transformer predicts two contact bits ˆct ∈{0, 1}2, one per arm; a
change in either predicted contact adds the corresponding frame to the keyframe bank. The head is trained with binary
cross-entropy loss against label predictions from a fine-tuned vision-language model (Clark et al., 2026), with gradients
detached from the dynamics backbone. During rollout, the model updates the bank using its own contact predictions,
requiring no external contact labels.
To construct Mt, we retain an initial anchor frame and retrieve keyframes preceding the recent window, ranked by
end-effector-pose similarity to that window and recency. Poses are computed from predicted joint states via forward
kinematics. Retrieved frames carry relative time offsets, and unused slots are masked, giving a fixed memory budget of
eight frames. Labeling, retrieval, and training details are provided in App. A.
3.2
Streaming Dynamics Transformer
Our causal dynamics transformer predicts zt+1 conditioned on action at, recent latents zt−T +1:t, and memory Mt,
while jointly predicting the robot’s joint state, as well as contact. Hybrid attention combines spatial attention within
frames and causal temporal attention across frames, with selected layers using joint spatio-temporal attention to capture
motion across spatial positions. A learned sink frame (Xiao et al., 2024), never noised or evicted, provides a persistent
attention target even when memory slots are empty. We train with latent flow matching and independently sampled
noise levels per frame, following diffusion forcing (Chen et al., 2024).
For streaming inference, we maintain P frames at staggered flow times and denoise them in parallel using a rolling KV
cache (Liu et al., 2026b; Jain et al., 2026). Each model call advances all in-flight frames, commits the oldest to the
recent context, evicts the oldest context frame, and admits a new noise frame conditioned on the next action. Caching
the committed context, memory, and sink keeps the dynamics computation per step constant in rollout length, while
pipelining introduces a delay of P steps between an action and its rendered frame.

the encoded tokens and re-normalize predicted tokens before decoding and reuse as context to limit scale drift.

4.1
Experimental Setup
Dataset. DreamFAR is trained on an internal bimanual teleoperation dataset collected on YAM robots: two 6-DoF arms
with parallel grippers, a 14-D state and action space, and three camera views. We process all episodes to 10 Hz and
crop each of the three views to a square that is resized to 256 × 256. Actions are the 14-D joint targets at the same
rate. For the baseline comparison, we instead train every model on the public ABC dataset (Allshire et al., 2026) for
reproducibility: about 131k episodes over 201 tasks (roughly 3,500 hours). We hold out a fixed set of 1,000 ABC
episodes for evaluation.
Implementation details. Our tokenizer follows the architecture of SSDD-M (Vallaeys et al., 2026), with a 46 M-
parameter convolutional encoder and a 50 M-parameter decoder. Each 256 × 256 view becomes a 16 × 16 grid of
16-dimensional latents, with each position projected onto the unit sphere. Both are initialized from a pretrained SSDD
checkpoint and fine-tuned jointly on our dataset. During fine-tuning, we add a uniformity (Wang and Isola, 2020) term
to the loss function as a regularization. The dynamics model of DreamFAR is a 1.9 B-parameter transformer with 28
blocks of hidden size 2048 and 16 attention heads; every block applies spatial attention within a frame followed by

4.3
Baseline Comparison
We compare DreamFAR with three baselines: IWS (Wang et al., 2026), WEAVER (Jain et al., 2026), and Cosmos-
Predict 2.5 (Ali et al., 2025). For a fair comparison, we implement WEAVER and DreamFAR at a matched scale of
about 0.8 B parameters. We size IWS to about 0.4 B to match the streaming step time. All models are trained on the
same ABC split and evaluated on the 1,000 held-out episodes: each model is seeded with 15 s of real frames and then
rolled out autoregressively for 60 s. We report PSNR, LPIPS, and FID in 10-s bins, averaged over episodes.
Fig. 4 shows one held-out episode rollout over 60 s. DreamFAR produces plausible frames throughout: the table layout,
the arm pose and the manipulated object remain consistent with the real frames after 60 s of rollout. Cosmos-Predict 2.5
progressively changes the arm geometry and the scene texture; WEAVER keeps the top view roughly coherent while
the wrist views fill with hallucinated texture; and IWS collapses into a blurry image within the first 10 s.
Fig. 5 confirms that DreamFAR drifts much more slowly than every baseline on all three metrics. Over the 60-s rollout,
DreamFAR averages 16.4 dB PSNR against 15.8 for Cosmos-Predict 2.5, 14.5 for WEAVER and 11.0 for IWS. The gap
widens further on the perceptual and distributional metrics: DreamFAR has the lowest LPIPS in every bin and its FID
rises from 15 to 32 over the minute, whereas Cosmos-Predict 2.5 rises from 30 to 73, WEAVER from 46 to 87 and IWS
stays above 180 throughout.
4.4
Policy Evaluation
We evaluate whether DreamFAR can serve as a faithful proxy for real-robot policy evaluation. We define three tasks:
placing plates into a bin, throwing plastic bottles into a bin, and folding a t-shirt. The policy is π0 (Black et al., 2024)
fine-tuned on teleoperation demonstrations of our tasks, and we compare its late checkpoint (15k steps) against an early
checkpoint (2.5k steps). For every policy–task pair we run 32 episodes on both real robot and inside DreamFAR, with
the same prompt; each episode is hand-labelled with a task progress in [0, 1], the fraction of the task completed within
the episode. Fig. 6 summarizes the results. Per-task mean progress in the simulator correlates with the real robot at
Pearson r = 0.71 over the six policy–task points. The simulator is optimistic in absolute terms: it tends to report higher
progress score than the real robot, and in the episode of Fig. 6(a) the simulated rollout reaches every stage of the fold
5–10 s before the real one.

4.5
Co-Training with Generated Demonstrations
Since DreamFAR runs in real time from teleoperation input (Fig. 1), an operator can collect demonstrations inside the
simulator without a real robot: the GELLO leader arms (Wu et al., 2024b) drive the simulated robot at 10 Hz, and each
episode is recorded in the training-data format, with the rendered views, the predicted joint state and the commanded
actions. We test whether such simulated demonstrations are useful for policy learning on a single task, placing three
bags of chips into a basket. We fine-tune the π0 policy (Black et al., 2024) on three datasets: 75 real demonstrations, 75
simulated demonstrations, or their union (150 episodes). Each policy is evaluated in 10 real-robot trials of 60 s. Fig. 7
reports the results. Combining the two nearly doubles the real-only result to 63%. This study shows that demonstrations
collected inside DreamFAR carry useful signal for the real robot, rather than isolating the value of a simulated episode
against a real one.
4.6
Ablation on Contact-Based Memory
We ablate the memory design of Sec. 3.1 on a task with 4.6k episodes where objects leave and re-enter view: “organize
condiment bottles”. Three models differ only in memory: no memory, sparse memory with keyframes taken at a fixed
1-s stride, and our contact-based memory. To isolate the effect, all three use a short 1-s recent context and two memory
slots. Fig. 8 shows the three rollouts and the real observation when the left wrist camera leaves and looks into the bin
again. Without memory, the bin is rendered empty: the bottles placed earlier are no longer in the context and the model
produces an unoccupied bin. With sparse memory, the bin contains bottles, but the wrong ones. With contact-based
memory, the bin contents match the real observation. This comes from the mechanism: a predicted contact transition,
here the release of a bottle, writes exactly the frame that shows the new scene state into the keyframe bank, and when
the end-effector returns to a similar pose the frame is retrieved as clean context. The memory therefore makes placed
objects persistent across the rollout at a fixed cost in context length, enabling long rollouts of Secs. 4.2 and 4.3. Tab. 1

5
Discussion and Limitations
We presented DreamFAR, a world model for real-time, long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation across diverse ob-
jects and tasks. By combining long–short-term memory, keyframe selection based on predicted contact transitions,
and streaming dynamics with a persistent attention sink, our framework supports extended interactive rollouts and
applications in policy evaluation and data generation.
Scene diversity. Our model is limited to interactions within a YAM station. Future work can extend this framework to
open-world environments with more diverse backgrounds, layouts, and scenes.
Robot embodiment. Our model captures a single robot embodiment. Learning a world model across multiple
embodiments is a next step toward simulating interactions across robots with different kinematics and action spaces.
Manipulation dexterity. The YAM station’s grippers offer less dexterity than multi-fingered hands, limiting the range
of manipulation behaviors studied here. Future work can extend our model to more dexterous robots and fine-grained
manipulation tasks, such as in-hand object reorientation.

Ethics Statement
DreamFAR is trained on recordings of robot teleoperation: camera views of a bimanual robot workspace together with
the robot’s joint states and commanded actions. The recordings contain no personal data, no human subjects were
studied, and no identity, biometric or demographic information is collected or used; the teleoperators are not identified
and appear in no released artefact. The public ABC dataset is used under its Apache-2.0 licence and cited by its paper.
The simulator is trained on predominantly successful demonstrations and may over-estimate the success of a policy; it
must not be the sole gate for deploying a policy, and policies evaluated inside it must still be validated on hardware.
Training the model required a substantial multi-GPU compute budget; releasing the weights avoids repeating it.
Reproducibility Statement
The architecture, training recipe and inference procedure are described in Sec. 3; the evaluation protocols, baseline
adaptations and inference settings are described in Sec. 4. We release the code for training, inference and the interactive
demo, and the model weights of the full DreamFAR and of the ABC-trained DreamFAR. With the release, the equal-data
comparison and the speed measurements can be reproduced: we release our ABC split (training tasks, held-out episodes
and the 10 held-out tasks), the benchmark episode list, the square-crop preprocessing and the evaluation scripts.

Architecture and Training Details
Tokenizer recipe.
Each observation xt contains three 256 × 256 RGB views. A shared convolutional encoder with
stride 16 (46.1 M parameters) maps each view to a 16 × 16 grid of 16-dimensional vectors; zt collects the latent tokens
from all three views. The decoder is a U-ViT flow-matching decoder (50.3 M parameters) (Bao et al., 2023). The
encoder output is L2-normalised per spatial position; there is no KL term, and one learned scalar inside the decoder
rescales the latent, so the served latent is exactly unit-norm (measured mean norm 1.000, standard deviation 10−4).
The decoder reconstructs each view from noise conditioned on its latent tokens. Its loss is the flow-matching error
(Lipman et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022) plus LPIPS (weight 0.5) (Zhang et al., 2018), representation alignment (0.1)
against a frozen vision-transformer teacher with a warm-up (Yu et al., 2025b; Fu et al., 2026), and a uniformity term
(0.1), a hinged squared-MMD reformulation of the loss of Wang and Isola (2020). Decoding takes two Euler steps,
about 14 ms per view-frame; for video, one noise tensor is drawn per episode and reused for every frame. Tab. 2
reports reconstruction on held-out episodes; on held-out tasks PSNR is 26.51 / 30.06 / 30.65 dB for the top / left-wrist /
right-wrist views.

Sequence layout and positions.
The three 16 × 16 × 16 view latents are stacked into a 48 × 16 × 16 tensor per
frame and patchified with patch size 2 (192 tokens) plus a state token carrying the 14-D joint positions, 193 tokens per
frame. The backbone has 28 blocks with hidden size 2048 and 16 heads of width 128. Spatial attention uses 2-D rotary
positions and temporal attention rotary positions on the frame index; the sink frame (193 learned vectors) receives no
rotation, memory frames are rotated by a log-compressed time offset ∆t to the current frame, never by a slot index, and
unused slots are masked. The five joint-attention blocks use a frame-causal block-sparse mask. Every attention applies
qk-RMSNorm (Dehghani et al., 2023); the flow time enters through adaptive layer normalisation with zero initialisation
(Peebles and Xie, 2023), and each frame’s action conditioning is added to that frame’s modulation through a small MLP.
Dynamics training.
The flow loss is applied on the context positions with independent logit-normal flow times per
frame (Esser et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024); the state token carries a flow loss with weight 0.1 and the contact readout a
BCE loss with weight 0.1. Memory slots are dropped with probability 0.2. We use AdamW with learning rate 10−3, 1k
warm-up steps and then a constant schedule, β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at 0.5 and an EMA
of the weights with decay 0.999; the EMA weights are used at inference. The effective batch size is 2048 windows. The
model was initialised, weights only, from an earlier training run of the same architecture taken at 14.5k of its steps.
Contact labeler.
Each call of the fine-tuned vision-language model (Clark et al., 2026) receives, for one arm, an
8-frame clip at 5 Hz of composites of that arm’s wrist view and the base view, and returns per-frame booleans with a
soft probability from the token logits; two passes offset by one frame are interleaved to 10 Hz and smoothed with a
3-frame box filter, a hysteresis (enter above 0.6, leave below 0.4) and a minimum dwell. The model was fine-tuned on
human-corrected labels with task-disjoint splits. In a visual audit, contact onsets lag gripper closing by 0.03–0.38 s,
releases coincide with re-opening, and sustained holds stay at high probability with no gripper motion, so the detector
is not a gripper proxy. A re-run reproduces the reference predictions at 99.2% per-frame agreement, a determinism
check rather than a validity measure. Rejected design choices: a gripper open/close hysteresis as labeler (blind to
non-prehensile contact; misses about 60% of grasps on the achieved gripper state), field-of-view-overlap retrieval keys
(degenerate under a static top camera), adaptive contact thresholds (they leak future statistics), and co-denoising contact
as a flow modality (the detached readout was better calibrated).
