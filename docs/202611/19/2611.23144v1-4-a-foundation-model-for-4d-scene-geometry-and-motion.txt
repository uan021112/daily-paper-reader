Abstract
π4 is a unified foundation model for 4D scene perception. From diverse, uncal-
ibrated RGB observations in the wild—a single image, an unordered collection,
or a video sequence—it recovers dense geometry, camera poses, and metric scale,
together with 2D/3D point tracks and moving-object masks for multi-image in-
puts. It couples a geometry branch with a geometry-conditioned motion branch,
which predicts image-space correspondences that are lifted into 3D using recon-
structed target-view geometry. Across diverse benchmarks, π4 achieves the best
overall average rank in geometry evaluation, alongside competitive performance
in single- and multiview 2D/3D point tracking and moving-object segmentation.
With a shared backbone, the full geometry-and-motion system contains approxi-
mately 0.5B parameters—less than half the size of most baselines. It produces joint
reconstructions and tracks in one forward pass, including large-scale scenes with
thousands of input images despite training with contexts of at most 64 views. We
further present the data and training recipe underlying these capabilities, together
with empirical studies of key design choices. Code and models are available at Pi4.
1
Introduction
Feed-forward models have established a powerful paradigm for reconstructing scenes directly
from RGB observations [1, 2, 3]. More recently, this paradigm has been extended beyond scene
reconstruction to encompass motion recovery [4, 5]. A key question, however, is how well these
capabilities generalize to in-the-wild observations, where scene content, camera configurations,
temporal continuity, and the number of observations are unconstrained. Maintaining high-quality
geometric reconstruction while robustly recovering motion from such diverse inputs remains a
significant practical challenge.
To address this challenge, we present π4, a unified foundation model for 4D scene perception from
RGB observations. It handles single images, unordered image collections, and videos, recovering
dense geometry, camera poses, and metric scale, together with 2D and 3D point tracks and moving-
object masks for multi-image inputs. Across the geometry benchmarks in Table 2, π4 achieves the
best overall average rank among the compared methods, while delivering competitive performance
in single- and multiview 2D/3D tracking and moving-object segmentation (Figure 2). Despite this
broad functionality, the model employs a shared backbone across these capabilities and contains
approximately 0.5B parameters in total—less than half the size of most baselines. Beyond bench-
mark results, Figure 1 showcases joint geometry and motion reconstruction across diverse in-the-
wild observations, including aerial footage, casual handheld recordings, driving and sports scenes,
and stylized content. These reconstruction and tracking results are all produced in a single forward
pass per scene. This combination of accuracy, efficiency, and generality is enabled by coordinated
choices in model architecture, training, and data.
At the architectural level, the geometry network of π4 retains the permutation-equivariant formu-
lation of π3 [3], treating all input views symmetrically without designating a reference view. Both
the geometry and motion branches employ looped Transformer decoding [6], which we adopt for
its strong cross-benchmark performance and parameter efficiency. We choose loop weight shar-
ing partly to reduce memory usage, freeing capacity for larger within-sequence contexts. Training
stochastically samples view counts from single images, 2–24-view sets, and 32/48/64-view sets,
with short contexts dominating the mixture. A sparse 3D refiner [7] further enhances local surface
structure and geometric detail.
To incorporate motion estimation without compromising reconstruction, we freeze the geometry
network and train a separate geometry-conditioned motion decoder. Direct 3D tracking couples
correspondence estimation with geometry, camera placement, and scene scale in the regression

Related Work
2.1
Visual Geometry Reconstruction
Classical structure-from-motion (SfM) estimates cameras and sparse structure through image
matching and bundle adjustment, while multi-view stereo (MVS) reconstructs dense surfaces from
calibrated views. COLMAP exemplifies this modular pipeline [11, 12]. Learned MVS methods such
as MVSNet [13] learn cost-volume regularization while retaining camera calibration as input. More
recently, DUSt3R [1] predicts pairwise pointmaps without supplied cameras, and MASt3R [14]
combines this 3D representation with learned matching descriptors, bridging reconstruction and
correspondence estimation.
Feed-forward multi-view models increasingly estimate geometry and cameras jointly. VGGT [2]
predicts cameras, depth, pointmaps, and tracks from image sets, while π3 and its released Pi3X
variant [3] use permutation-equivariant modeling without a privileged input reference. MapAny-
thing [15] targets metric reconstruction with flexible geometric inputs, whereas Depth Anything
3


3 [16] uses depth and ray prediction across arbitrary views. VGGT-Ω[17] incorporates matching
supervision, and DVLT [6] reuses Transformer blocks through looped decoding. Monocular models
provide complementary components: MoGe2 [18] separates metric-scale prediction from relative
geometry, and MoGe3 [7] refines surface detail with sparse volumetric processing. Together, these
methods provide complementary designs for joint camera–geometry estimation, reference-free re-
construction, metric prediction, and iterative decoding.
2.2
Image Correspondence and Point Tracking
Classical image tracking estimates motion through local appearance alignment, as in Lucas–
Kanade [19]. Modern optical-flow models such as RAFT [20] learn dense two-frame correspon-
dence, while TAPIR [21] and CoTracker3 [22] follow queried points over videos and estimate visi-
bility. CoWTracker [23] replaces correlation-volume lookup with iterative feature warping. Optical
flow and query-based tracking thus provide dense or sparse image-space correspondences, typi-
cally within ordered video sequences.
2.3
Dynamic Scene Reconstruction and 4D Perception
Dynamic scene reconstruction extends geometry estimation to scenes with moving and deform-
ing content. Shape of Motion [24] reconstructs 4D scenes from monocular videos using compact
SE(3) motion bases and data-driven geometric and tracking priors. MonST3R [25] instead adapts
pointmap reconstruction to dynamic videos by estimating per-timestep geometry without an ex-
plicit motion representation. These approaches connect scene geometry with temporal variation,
but differ in how motion is represented and how reconstruction and tracking are coupled.
Recent methods further extend tracking to 3D and connect it to scene reconstruction. D4RT [26] and
ARROW [5] use query-based decoding to recover point positions across time and reference cam-
eras; ARROW additionally supports unordered and multiview observations. MoSE3 [27] models
dense world-space motion through per-pixel SE(3) transformations, while Track4World [4] com-
bines dense correspondences, geometry, and camera estimation for world-centric tracking.
Moving-object segmentation provides a complementary object-level characterization of scene dy-
namics by identifying regions that undergo motion [28, 29]. Across these directions, methods differ
in their supported input configurations, output representations, and the degree to which geometry
and motion are modeled jointly.
3
Method
π4 combines geometric reconstruction and motion estimation within a single feed-forward model
(Figure 3). A shared image encoder provides features to two task-specific looped Transformer
decoders. The geometry branch predicts dense pointmaps, camera poses, and metric scale; a sparse
3D refiner then improves the predicted pointmaps. Conditioned on geometry features, the motion
branch predicts image-space point tracks and moving-object masks. The predicted tracks are lifted
to 3D using the reconstructed target-view geometry.

3.2
Shared Encoding and Looped Decoding
Image encoder.
A shared DINOv3 encoder [30] extracts per-image patch features for both
branches. We combine intermediate and late-layer encoder features, project them into the decoder
feature space, and append shared learnable register tokens. We examine the choice of encoder
layers in Section 6.3.
Looped decoders.
Both the geometry and motion decoders alternate frame and cross-view at-
tention [2, 3]. Following Déjà View [6], each decoder repeatedly applies the same pair of blocks,
sharing their weights across its loop steps. The two decoders have independent parameters; weight
sharing occurs within each branch, not between branches. Starting from encoded tokens Z0, the ge-
ometry decoder applies

Geometry-conditioned motion decoding.
The motion decoder starts from image tokens and
adopts the same frame- and cross-view-attention block design as the geometry decoder, but with

try conditioning: spatial adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) conditions its attention and feed-

progress. A final within-image readout supplies the tracking and moving-object mask heads. The
motion decoder uses no temporal positional embeddings.

3.4
Motion Estimation and 3D Tracking
Image-space tracking.
The tracking head predicts correspondences from the geometry-
conditioned motion features. We adapt CoWTracker’s iterative warping head [23] for arbitrary-
view tracking by removing its temporal positional embeddings. The head takes upsampled mo-

and retains zero-displacement initialization and visibility/confidence outputs, without assuming a
chronological ordering of the input views. Outputs satisfy the query anchor Uq→q(u) = u. Sparse
query tracks are obtained by sampling the dense correspondence maps.

Rather than directly regressing 3D motion vectors or endpoint coordinates, we

and metric prediction are optimized jointly once metric-scale estimation is introduced. For motion
training, the pretrained geometry model remains frozen, and we optimize the motion decoder and

mixtures and the progressive training schedule are described in Section 4.6.

Geometry confidence.
Following Pi3X [3], we freeze the geometry model and train the confidence
branch with continuous targets that reflect reconstruction quality, rather than binary valid/invalid
labels. The branch estimates per-pixel reliability of the final refined geometry.
Motion supervision.
We follow CoWTracker [23] for tracking supervision, combining iterative
coordinate regression with binary cross-entropy for visibility and tracking confidence. We addition-
ally supervise static-region correspondences using ground-truth geometry and cameras. Moving-
object masks are supervised with binary cross-entropy on annotated moving/static pixels, ignor-
ing unknown labels. We optimize the motion decoder and prediction heads with geometry and the
SAM image encoder frozen, applying each loss only where annotations are available.

Training Data and Curation
4.1
Data Sources and Mixture
We combine public datasets with scene-level synthetic renderings, curated Internet videos, and
game-based supervision to broaden scene and camera coverage and provide complementary su-
pervision for dynamic scenes. Motion training uses point trajectories, static geometric correspon-
dences, and real-video masks. Table 1 summarizes the data sources and sampling probabilities in
the final geometry and motion training stages.
4.2
Scene-Level Synthetic Data
Scene assets.
We curate approximately 10,000 scene-level 3D assets from Funes [8] and Sketch-
fab [9] for synthetic multi-view rendering. These assets include architectural environments and
other large-scale scenes, rather than only isolated objects. They support exterior observations and,
where the geometry permits, interior views. Our goal is to broaden the diversity of scene layouts
and camera configurations represented during training, rather than simply increase the number of
rendered images.
Candidate views and validation.
We generate candidate cameras across positions, elevations,
viewing directions, and distances using surrounding-space and surface-guided proposals. Exterior
views capture both overall structure and local detail; interior views require sufficient free space and
renderable surfaces. Visibility and RGB-D quality checks reject invalid cameras and degenerate
observations, including views dominated by open mesh backsides.
Coverage-aware views and connected chains.
We select views by their incremental coverage of
observable surfaces, balancing surface area with spatial and orientation diversity so that large pla-
nar regions do not dominate. Depth-consistent reprojections then define a view-overlap graph,

4.3
Sequence-Consistent Projection Augmentation
Geometry training data are dominated by pinhole or rectified imagery, limiting the camera projec-
tions encountered during training. To counter this bias, we apply sequence-consistent lens and
crop-and-resize augmentation.
Each sequence receives an identity, barrel, blended equidistant
(fisheye-like), or pincushion mapping with probability 40%, 35%, 20%, or 5%, respectively. Lens
parameters and the final crop and resize are shared across frames.
The augmentation transforms RGB and prepared geometric supervision together. For each out-
put pixel, an inverse warp identifies a source location whose calibrated ray and camera-axis depth
define the new point target, without approximating the distorted view with a new pinhole cam-
era. Correspondence targets, visibility, and cross-view overlap are recomputed under the same
mapping, while camera poses and scene scale remain unchanged. We crop to valid source-image
support and resize, reducing distortion strength when necessary to satisfy a zoom limit.
These transforms diversify image-to-ray mappings within the observed field of view, complement-
ing genuine camera diversity.

4.5
GameRealm: Game-Based Geometry and Motion
To broaden supervision for joint geometry and motion learning in dynamic environments, we use
GameRealm [10], a game-based dataset developed jointly with collaborators. It provides engine-
native geometric and motion annotations, including posed multi-view RGB-D observations for
reconstruction and persistent 2D/3D point trajectories for tracking. Covering diverse scenes, view-
points, characters, and interactions, these data complement our static scene renderings and real-
video pseudo-labels with supervision for both spatial reconstruction and temporal correspondence.
4.6
Training Protocol
π4 is trained progressively on 64 NVIDIA A800 GPUs, with approximately 300,000 optimizer up-
dates across geometry-training stages. Starting from a pretrained DINOv3 encoder, early stages
train the image encoder, looped decoder, and point, camera, and matching heads. Later stages in-
troduce sparse 3D refinement and metric-scale estimation while expanding the data mixture with
curated Internet videos. Successive stages inherit the preceding model weights.
Separately, motion training spans approximately 100,000 updates, including joint optimization of
the motion decoder, tracking head, and moving-object mask head, with geometry frozen through-
out. The SAM image encoder remains frozen during mask training, and the confidence head is
trained separately with geometry fixed.
Optimization uses AdamW and BF16 mixed precision. In the final geometry stage, images have a
long side of 512 pixels and varying aspect ratios, with at most 64 images per GPU and no gradient
accumulation. For sets of K views (Section 6.2), each GPU packs ⌊64/K⌋independent examples
per batch. The base and encoder learning rates are 1.5 × 10−5 and 2 × 10−6, respectively, with
cosine decay. All update counts denote optimizer steps under weighted data sampling, rather than
dataset epochs.

Design Choices and Empirical Observations
We study the architectural and training choices underlying π4 through targeted ablations and qual-
itative comparisons, highlighting the empirical findings and practical trade-offs that inform our
final design. For loss-based ablations, we use matched model-development configurations, and
percentage changes refer to relative differences in validation loss unless otherwise stated.
6.1
Looped Transformers: Training Dynamics and Step Conditioning
Following DVLT [6], we use a looped Transformer decoder with explicit loop-step conditioning.
The looped and non-looped reconstruction models follow similar early training trajectories, but
the looped model’s advantage emerges later in training and is more pronounced with step condi-
tioning. Figure 8 shows the training and validation objectives of these decoder variants. A separate
comparison within the looped architecture isolates the effect of conditioning: adding the step signal
reduces local point loss by 2.8% and camera loss by 10.2% relative to the unconditioned baseline.
These results support the use of step conditioning in our decoder.

6.3
Encoder Layer Selection and Fine-Tuning
Layer selection.
UFM [102, Appendix E] shows that intermediate features from a frozen encoder
can outperform its final-layer features for dense prediction. Our geometry ablations using DI-
NOv3 [30] show a consistent trend. Jointly using layers 11 and 17 gives the best local point re-
construction and camera estimation among the combinations evaluated. This pair, also used in
RoMa v2 [103], supplies the input to our multi-view decoder.
Freezing and learning rate.
Fine-tuning the encoder at 10−4 initially reduces the loss faster than
freezing the encoder, but performs worse later in training. By contrast, fine-tuning at 10−5 reduces
local point loss by 8.1% and camera loss by 4.5% relative to freezing. The comparison therefore
depends on both learning rate and training duration. Our encoder remains trainable, with a lower
learning rate than the decoder and prediction heads.
6.4
Output Factorization and Geometric Consistency
Local geometry and camera placement.
Following π3 [3], we predict camera-local pointmaps and
camera poses rather than directly regressing a global pointmap. Global point errors mix errors in
16


local surface geometry with errors in its cross-view placement. Image evidence may constrain local
surface structure even when the relative camera configuration remains uncertain. Separate local-
point and camera supervision preserves this distinction, allowing local geometry to be learned
without requiring global placement to be equally accurate.
Correspondence and 3D tracking.
Factorizing 3D tracking into image-space correspondence pre-
diction and geometric lifting avoids asking the motion head to independently reproduce target-
view geometry and camera placement when predicting 3D endpoints or displacements. For visible
tracks, lifting enforces agreement with the target geometry at the predicted correspondence by con-
struction; independently predicted 3D positions need not satisfy this constraint.

6.5
Geometry-Conditioned Motion
Our geometry-conditioned motion branch is designed to reuse the spatial correspondence cues
integrated by the geometry branch across views. Rather than independently learning cross-view
spatial relationships, the motion branch conditions on this representation through Spatial AdaLN.
In our ablation, geometry-feature conditioning reduces EPE by 7.17 px overall and 8.10 px on
large-camera-baseline pairs compared with an otherwise identical model without this condition-
ing. These improvements show that geometry features provide useful spatial context for motion
estimation, particularly for image pairs with large camera baselines.
6.6
Camera Supervision and Gradient Routing
Camera targets.
Our camera ablations favor the reference-free formulation of π3 with relative
pose supervision [3] over regression tied to an explicitly designated reference frame, with more
efficient convergence. We therefore use relative pose supervision, together with patch-matching
supervision [17] to support learning at initialization, when relative-pose optimization is difficult [3,
Appendix A.4].
Camera readout.
In an early parameter-matched comparison, patch-token readout gives lower
local point and camera losses than readout from a dedicated register token. We therefore retain
patch-token readout for the camera decoder.

6.7
Relative-Point Supervision and Loss Weighting
Supervising geometry constructed from multiple predicted quantities is established practice:
VGGT-Ω[17] forms point maps from depth and camera predictions, while DA3 [16] supervises
points constructed from depth and rays. We use relative-point supervision alongside local-point
and camera objectives. In the loss-weight ablation, increasing the relative-point weight by an order
of magnitude reduces local point loss by 5.4% and camera loss by 2.3%. Removing this term and
compensating with a stronger local-point objective does not recover the same camera quality; fur-
ther increasing the local-point weight does not consistently help. These comparisons guide the loss
weighting in our model.
6.8
Dense Prediction Heads: Practical Trade-offs
Motivated by PXDepth’s monocular-depth results, we tested a PXDepth/PixelDiT-inspired pixel-
level head [104, 105] in place of the standard convolutional head. Patch-aligned artifacts persisted
in local point predictions, particularly on high-uncertainty inputs. VGGT-Ωreports related blocky
artifacts for all-MLP depth decoding in uncertain regions [17, Section 3.1.3].
We therefore retain the standard convolutional head for local point prediction. It reads the final
decoder representation, without additional multi-layer feature fusion, to keep the design simple.
6.9
Surface Structure Refinement with 3D Sparse Convolution
Following MoGe3 [7], we include a 3D sparse-convolutional refinement stage that aggregates over
geometric neighborhoods, reducing mixing between image-adjacent but depth-separated surfaces.
In qualitative zero-shot tests, we apply the pretrained MoGe3 refiner to point clouds predicted by
the convolutional head, replacing its image-derived conditioning features with zeros or random
noise. In the examples shown in Figure 11, both settings yield more coherent surfaces and reduce
distortions in thin structures near depth discontinuities.
Despite these structural improvements, zero and random image conditioning increase mean nor-
malized XYZ L1 error by 8.77% and 7.47%, respectively, relative to the coarse prediction in our
diagnostic. We therefore condition our refiner on projected geometry-decoder features to guide
structural corrections toward image-supported surface locations.

Limitations
Camera–object motion ambiguity.
Although foreground-suppressed pseudo-labeling (Sec-
tion 4.4) reduces reliance on dynamic foreground cues, π4 can still interpret a moving foreground as
stationary when the static background is weakly textured or the foreground is visually dominant.
In these cases, the model confounds camera motion with object motion, yielding a reconstruction
that is inconsistent with the static background. Resolving this ambiguity requires scene and motion
understanding beyond local texture correspondences.
Sensitivity to distance from the coordinate origin.
We observe larger camera-position errors for
cameras farther from the reconstruction origin than for those near it. This suggests that pose accu-
racy may be sensitive to the coordinate representation and the numerical scale of absolute transla-
tions. Understanding this dependence and reducing sensitivity to the choice of coordinate origin
and translation magnitude remain open problems.
Accuracy and long-sequence generalization.
π4 does not consistently match the accuracy of tra-
ditional geometric optimization methods, particularly when inference sequences are longer than
those seen during training. Maintaining globally consistent camera trajectories and reconstructions
under such sequence-length extrapolation remains challenging. Improving long-sequence gener-
alization and closing the accuracy gap to optimization-based methods are important directions for
future work.
8
Conclusion
We have presented π4, a unified foundation model for 4D scene perception from diverse, uncali-
brated RGB observations. With a shared backbone and approximately 0.5B parameters, it combines
dense geometry, camera pose, and metric-scale estimation with 2D/3D point tracking and moving-
object segmentation. By predicting image-space correspondences conditioned on geometry and
lifting them into the reconstructed 3D scene, π4 unifies geometry and motion in a single forward
pass.
Across diverse benchmarks, π4 achieves the best overall average rank in geometry evaluation and
competitive performance in single- and multiview 2D/3D tracking and moving-object segmenta-
tion. It generalizes to diverse in-the-wild observations, producing coherent joint reconstructions
and tracks from thousands of input images in large-scale scenes. Together with our data and train-
ing recipe and empirical studies of key design choices, these results mark a step towards unified
4D scene perception.
