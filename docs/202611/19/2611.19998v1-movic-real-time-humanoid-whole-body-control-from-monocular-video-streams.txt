Abstract—Real-time whole-body imitation from monocular
video requires both a robust universal tracker and high-quality
real-time reconstruction. We present MoViC, a framework that
uses live RGB video as a control signal for humanoid robots
without wearable tracking devices. The framework combines
monocular human motion reconstruction, robot retargeting, and
a transformer-based behavior foundation model. The policy
receives proprioceptive history, a single explicit next-step ref-
erence, and a compact frequency-domain motion representation
that supplies temporal context without explicitly stacking future
reference frames, which reduces the input dimension and network
size. During deployment, our extrapolation method bridges
the 10-Hz visual estimation with 50-Hz controller, enabling
asynchronous visual processing and policy execution. Hardware
experiments further demonstrate highly dynamic motion tracking
and device-free teleoperation of bimanual and single-arm ma-
nipulation tasks from live monocular RGB input. Project page:
https://movicproject.github.io/ .

I. INTRODUCTION
As humanoid robots increasingly enter everyday human
environments, their behaviors are expected to become not
only functional but also natural and human-like. Learning
from large-scale human motion data provides a promising way
to acquire such behavioral characteristics. However, achiev-
ing human-like whole-body control (WBC) on full-sized hu-
manoid robots remains challenging. Traditional reinforcement
learning (RL) approaches typically train task-specific policies
with carefully designed rewards, but these policies transfer
poorly across skills. In contrast, extracting motion patterns
from human motion data and learning a unified behavior foun-
dation model (BFM) through motion tracking has emerged as
an effective approach for humanoid WBC, enabling humanoid
robots to acquire diverse whole-body behaviors directly from
reference human motions.
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) for
natural language processing and vision-language-action (VLA)
models suggest that data scale-up has become a key driver
behind the emergence of generalist models with strong zero-
shot generalization ability. Humanoid whole-body control
can also benefit from the same principle. Imitation learning
framework provide a promising way to leverage large-scale
datasets for humanoid WBC [1]–[4]. However, some existing
approaches, such as ASAP [5] and BeyondMimic [1], have

limited generalization capacity across various motion modali-
ties due to their model architectures or the limited scale and
quality of their training data. These methods can reproduce
highly dynamic in-domain motions, but fail to transfer zero-
shot to unseen movements. In contrast, some methods, such
as the TWIST series [6], [7] and UniTracker [8], demonstrate
stronger generalization but tend to underfit highly dynamic
behaviors. To solve this trade-off, recent studies have begun
to adopt models that accommodate large-scale, diverse motion
corpora and to scale up the datasets used for training, such
as OmniH2O [9], and SONIC [10], which achieve substan-
tial progress in improving the learned policies’ robustness
in tracking diverse motions. Scaling the reference-motion
dataset, however, raises a deeper question of how its statistics
should be exploited. A large motion dataset—typically in-
cluding motion capture (MoCap) data and video-reconstructed
motions—defines a highly complex, multi-modal distribution
over whole-body motions. Most prevailing methods imitate
reference trajectories only implicitly through RL and treat the
motion data as an unstructured collection of samples. Such an
imitation framework clearly benefits from growing scale, but
the rich structure of the underlying distribution, such as its
behavioral modes and inter-motion relations, remains largely
unexploited. Beyond enlarging the motion scale, therefore,
modeling the mathematical structure of the motion distribution
offers a meaningful complement to building stronger behavior
foundation models. Grouping similar motions provides the
tracker with additional information for distinguishing different
types of motion.
Moreover, existing popular humanoid real-time whole-body
tracking systems can be roughly categorized into two types
according to the teleoperation devices they use: MoCap-
based [6], [11], [12] and VR-based [7], [13], as well as
combinations of these two [9], [10]. Both of these solu-
tions require expensive external hardware or operator-sensitive
calibration, which restricts their deployment. MoCap-based
methods typically depend on expensive, non-portable motion
capture setups. VR-based methods require prior calibration
that makes instant deployment impossible, and prolonged use
of head-mounted displays (HMDs) can cause physiological
burdens, such as visually induced motion sickness (VIMS).
Vision-based solutions provide a practical alternative that pre-
serves precise tracking ability while removing expensive and
cumbersome wearable devices. They require nothing beyond
a low-cost RGB camera. As humanoid robots increasingly
move into public life, daily interaction between humans and
humanoids should not heavily rely on dedicated or specialized
instruments. The visual interface should be an appropriate
choice that lets whole-body control blend seamlessly into
everyday human activities.
Video-driven WBC have been used, but mostly offline [14],
[15]. The resulting policies do not follow a live operator.
Video-based teleoperation instead requires online tracking of
the operator’s motion as it is estimated from the video stream.
Curretnly, existing systems show that this is feasible with a
single RGB camera [9], [10], [16], [17]. However, they either
track only sparse targets, which discards whole-body detail,
or rely on additional sensing that complicates deployment.
Besides, their monocular pose estimation is noisy and de-
grades under occlusion. High-quality motion reconstruction
from video is typically too slow to keep pace with real-time
control. SAM 3D Body [18] targets robust full-body mesh
recovery across diverse poses, but it runs at about 10 Hz,
well below the control rate. To resolve this trade-off between
reconstruction quality and speed, we propose an extrapolation
method that bridges low-rate visual references and high-rate
whole-body control, without requiring per-video training.
In summary, we propose MoViC framework. MoViC ad-
dresses the interface between visual motion estimation and
whole-body control through the following three contributions:
1) We develop MoViC, a monocular-video interface for
real-time humanoid whole-body control. Human motion
reconstruction and retargeting convert live RGB input
into motion commands for a pretrained tracker, without
wearable tracking devices or per-video policy training.
2) We formulate a compact frequency-domain conditioning
interface for a transformer-based behavior foundation
model. A θ goal window supplies temporal motion con-
text alongside proprioceptive history and a single explicit
next-step reference, reducing the need for stacked future
reference poses.
3) We construct an online reference generator that combines
extrapolation method with motion increments anchored
to the latest measurement. It supplies 50-Hz references
between approximately 10-Hz visual updates and com-
pensates for reconstruction offsets shared by the current
and predicted decoded states.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Whole-Body Humanoid Teleoperation
Motion imitation enables physically simulated characters to
acquire coordinated skills. PHC [19] progressively expands a
control policy for diverse motion tracking and fall recovery.
Neural Categorical Priors [20] learn reusable vector-quantized
skills. MaskedMimic [21] distills a transformer tracker into a
conditional variational controller for partial motion constraints,
while CALM [22] and PULSE [23] learn motion-conditioned
latent policies through adversarial imitation. But the physically
simulated characters have different joint configurations and
degree of freedom, compared with real humanoid robots.
These methods have not yet been demonstrated feasible on
real humanoid robots.
For physical humanoids, ASAP [5] incorporates a residual
action model learned from real-world data into simulation to
reduce dynamics mismatch during policy refinement. However,
the high cost of collecting real-world data makes such dynam-
ics adaptation difficult to scale across diverse motion trajecto-
ries, potentially restricting the robot to a limited repertoire of
learned motions. To enable a policy to track a broader range of
reference motions, several works adopt similar teacher–student
design. OmniH2O [9], HOVER [24], and TWIST [6] transfer
privileged tracking skills to students operating with sparse
targets, masked command modes, or restricted future refer-
ences. UniTracker [8] follows this paradigm while using a
conditional variational student to model motion ambiguity


3
under partial observations. However, these methods still fall
short of achieving universal motion tracking ability.
To enable a single policy to track a broader range of
reference motions, architectural improvements and tempo-
ral modeling of the input can enhance its representational
capacity. Recent studies have made some progress toward
universal motion tracking along these lines. HumanPlus [17]
and TWIST2 [7] directly train history-conditioned tracking
policies through reinforcement learning, using a decoder-
only transformer and a convolutional encoder with an MLP,
respectively. GMT [25] and HoloMotion-1 [26] increase repre-
sentational capacity through mixtures of experts, implemented
as softly combined expert policies and sparsely routed trans-
former layers, respectively. However, their performance is still
bounded by how well the training data covers the space of
motions. The out-of-distribution (OOD) motions are tracked
poorly. Learned latent interfaces and generative models further
broaden controller functionality. Exbody2 [27] extends whole-
body tracking to expressive humanoid motion. SONIC [10]
conditions control through shared latent representations and
quantized codes, which unify heterogeneous motion com-
mands. BeyondMimic [1] and Heracles [28] combine tracking
with motion synthesis, using guided latent diffusion for skill
composition and state-conditioned trajectory generation for
disturbance recovery, respectively. However, many of them
rely on multi-stage training pipelines, such as teacher–student
distillation, or latent-space pretraining. These make training
costly, and hard to reproduce.
Moreover, fully exploiting the representational capacity of
these high-capacity models with large parameters amount
demands vast high-quality motion data. Training data combine
public motion-capture datasets with video-reconstructed mo-
tions and self-captured data. Dataset scales reach hundreds or
thousands of hours [10], [26]. As motion libraries grow, their
structure can guide learning beyond providing reference targets
and imitation rewards. However, few work mentioned above
have considered the dataset structure. Some recent works,
Humanoid-GPT [11] and BumbleBee [29] group motions for
expert training and transformer distillation using Harmonic
Motion Embedding and learned motion–text representations,
respectively. But these methods both assign a single embed-
ding to an entire sequence, so sequences containing multiple
behaviors collapse into a mixed descriptor. Moreover, neither
of them can be reused by the policy at deployment. A
complementary use of motion structure is to encode local
temporal context directly for policy conditioning.
Frequency-domain representations offer a structured way to
encode this context. DeepPhase [30] learns phase manifolds
with a periodic autoencoder. FLD [31] extends this formulation
with latent dynamics and multi-step consistency for periodic
and quasi-periodic motion, enabling phase-based extrapola-
tion. DFM [32] emphasizes local reconstruction and updated
encodings to retain details in less periodic motions. This allows
motions to be organized at the segment level rather than per
sequence, and the same representation to be reused online for
expert routing or continuous, adaptive sampling.
Video-driven deployment additionally requires handling re-
construction errors and the timing of reference updates. Sev-
eral works learn humanoid behaviors from videos offline.
Humanoid-X [14] builds over 20 million humanoid poses from
internet videos, HDMI [15] trains humanoid–object interaction
policies from monocular videos, and GenMimic [33] tracks
motions reconstructed from generated videos. These methods
process the videos before execution rather than following a
live operator. Teleoperation instead requires online tracking,
in which the robot imitates the operator’s whole-body motion
while it is being estimated from the video stream. Several
systems achieve this visual interface with a single RGB
camera. Although H2O [16] teleoperates a full-sized humanoid
from a webcam, the robot’s root velocity is still measured by
a MoCap system during deployment. OmniH2O also supports
an RGB camera, but tracks only the head and hand targets
used for its VR interface [9]. The sparse reference specifies
only part of the operator’s posture. VisualMimic [34] focuses
on task-specific egocentric command generation, and requires
a separate high-level policy, with its own reward design and
privileged teacher, for each task. This task-dependent visual
control is therefore unsuitable for vision-based teleoperation,
which requires following arbitrary operator motions rather
than executing a predefined task. HumanPlus [17] shadows
body and hand motions on a humanoid with WHAM [35] and
HaMeR [36]. SONIC [10] accepts human video as a real-time
command source. These systems show that video-based whole-
body teleoperation is feasible, but they also expose some
limitations. Firstly. the tracking ability is poor on the OOD
highly dynamic motion references. Besides, the monocular
pose estimation fails under large occlusions [16], [17], and
the WBC tracker has to tolerate the delayed reconstruction
input [10]. The difficulties of pose estimations remain with
the reconstruction models.
B. Video Reconstruction
Monocular human reconstruction offers a way to ob-
tain motion references without body-worn tracking devices.
VIBE [37] regresses pose and shape over time with an ad-
versarial motion prior learned from motion-capture sequences.
HMR 2.0 [38] estimates meshes with a transformer for human
tracking in video. HybrIK [39] solves joint swings from 3D
keypoints and regresses only 1-DoF twists, yielding pixel-
aligned and kinematically valid meshes. But it is sensitive
to keypoint errors. WHAM [35] and GVHMR [40] further
recover world-grounded motion, using camera angular velocity
with contact-aware refinement and gravity-view coordinates,
respectively. However, such models are largely trained on
laboratory captures or pseudo-labeled data and often fail on
challenging poses and under severe occlusion. Moreover, they
rely on temporal context rather than per-frame inference.
GVHMR in particular reconstructs entire sequences offline,
which incurs latency and suffers leads to severe degradation
under strong camera jitter. SAM 3D Body [18] targets these
failure cases. It is trained on annotations from a data engine
that selects rare poses and imaging conditions, and represents
the full body with the Momentum Human Rig, which de-
couples skeletal pose and body shape. It outperforms prior
single-image methods and is competitive with video-based


4
approaches. Because it requires only a single image, it can
process a live stream frame by frame, which suits real-time
teleoperation.
Our proposed MoViC combines temporal reference condi-
tioning and online reference generation for whole-body control
from live RGB video. MoViC implements SAM 3D Body for
per-frame human reconstruction, and extrapolation for gener-
ating 50-Hz references from available visual estimates to align
the reference trajectory sampling rate with the control rate. We
combine existing reconstruction capabilities with structured
motion conditioning, where compact motion representations
are fed to a task-agnostic WBC tracker.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
As illustrated in Fig. 1, MoViC drives humanoid whole-
body control from live monocular RGB video through an
online video-to-motion module, SEG, which reconstructs hu-
man motion with SAM 3D Body [18] at approximately
10 Hz, and then retargets and extrapolates it to the robot
into reference motions at 50 Hz. A transformer-based behavior
foundation model then tracks these references by combining
proprioceptive history with an explicit next-step reference and
a frequency-domain θ goal.
A. Data Curation
We use a combination of AMASS [41], LAFAN1 [42],
Motion-X++ [43], OMOMO [44], and self-captured motion
data to train a BFM as our robust tracking policy. Because
these datasets contain human motion sequences, we retarget
them to the robot to form the reference-motion library for
training a motion tracker.

B. Universal Motion Tracker
1) Overview: In this section, a scalable training frame-
work for a universal motion tracker is proposed. Unlike
other trackers that use the explicitly stacking future reference
frames, we innovatively integrate the future information into
frequency-domain motion representations, and design the actor
architecture accordingly. The proposed framework, MoViC,
integrates two coupled components: (i) a pretrained motion
encoder, built on latent dynamics, compresses the reference
motions into a compact periodic parameterization θ and (ii) a
tracking policy incorporates θ into a Transformer actor. The
actor receives ten frames of proprioceptive history, an explicit
next-step reference, and a θ goal window. During training,
this θ window conveys information about ten future reference
frames. The explicit target specifies the immediate tracking
objective, while the frequency-domain representation supplies
additional motion context.
Most motions are relatively simple and can be learned with
the next reference frame only, while more challenging motions
may benefit from a longer future context. We therefore aug-
ment the explicit next-frame reference with a compact θ token
that summarizes the next ten reference frames. Compared
with explicitly providing ten or more future reference frames,
this formulation reduces the input dimensionality, and thus
supports learning with a smaller network, enabling the policy
to acquire a broad repertoire of motions spanning different
levels of difficulty.

2) Reference Motions Encoding and Frequency-Domain
Representation: Reference motions contain temporal structure
that can inspire WBC tracking. Most existing trackers treat
the motion library as an unstructured collection of samples,
so clips containing multiple behaviors collapse into a mixed
descriptor. In contrast, we encode every short motion window,
rather than an entire sequence, into per-channel sinusoidal
latent parameters, namely a frequency, amplitude, and offset
that describe the high-level features of the motion and a phase
that indexes local time within it. Assuming that the frequency,
amplitude, and offset remain quasi-constant over a short hori-
zon, an encoder is trained with a multi-step forward-prediction
loss, so that future states are predicted by advancing only the
phase at the encoded frequency and decoding the result. These
latent dynamics yield a compact, and temporally coherent rep-
resentation. To reduce the impact of the local periodicity of this
method and improve the tracking performance of less periodic
motions such as dance, we remove the forward-prediction
terms in order to train on local reconstruction alone. Its
parameters are re-estimated from each new reference window
rather than constrained to remain constant across a prediction
horizon, so the frequency, amplitude, and offset can follow
expressive, non-periodic changes in the motion. These two
formulations thus serve complementary roles in MoViC. The
local-reconstruction encoder provides an accurate, and time-
varying description of the reference motion, which is used
to construct the θ for policy conditioning and to characterize
the reference motion library. Thanks to its motion prediction

and genreation capacity, the predictive latent dynamics of the
periodic representation are used during real-world deployment
to generate reference motions that compensate the low-rate
visual updates.

D. Transformer Actor Architecture
Existing universal trackers typically rely on teacher–student
distillation or mixtures of experts to gain capacity, and on
explicitly stacked future reference frames to obtain temporal
context that inflates the policy input. Our actor is a single
transformer conditioned on a compact θ goal window, which
summarizes the upcoming reference motion in the frequency
domain. The actor receives an 810-dimensional proprioceptive
history, a 52-dimensional next-step reference, and a 320-
dimensional θ goal window, for a total of 1182 input features.

for much of the evaluated motion set. Additional context

explicit ten-frame preview incurs a larger input at every control

motivates encoding extended reference context compactly. The
results show that θ reaches 99% success with one explicit
target, exceeding the 95% obtained from ten explicit frames.

they do not identify which individual trajectories account for
the improvement.

frames are retained. This configuration does not outperform θ
with 1 frame on any reported metric: both achieve 99% suc-

and velocity errors. Thus, providing nine additional explicit
reference frames yields no observed improvement once the
actor receives the frequency-domain representation, supporting
the use of a single explicit next-step target alongside θ.

input. Its input dimension is 1182, compared with 1330 for
10 future frames and 1650 for θ with 10 frames, corre-
sponding to reductions of 11.1% and 28.4%, respectively.

the 320-dimensional θ code and the 52-dimensional next-step
target, compared with 520 features for ten explicit frames.
Relative to θ with 10 frames, reducing the explicit reference
input from ten frames to one also decreases the number of
weights in the reference embedding’s input layer when its
hidden widths are held fixed. For a first-layer width of d,
this removes 468d input weights. Smaller observation vectors
also reduce rollout-storage requirements. The reduced input
dimensionality therefore, accompanies equal or higher tracking
success and lower tracking errors in these comparisons.

the lower body must stabilize the robot against object loads.
Their completion suggests that the θ-conditioned tracker and
the reference generation are not limited to rhythmic motions.
Neither the visual reconstruction nor the policy observes the
object, and monocular reconstruction provides limited depth
cues for such fine alignment. Incorporating object perception
or haptic feedback is therefore a natural extension of this
interface.
V. CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
We presented MoViC, a framework for humanoid whole-
body control from live monocular RGB video. The frequency-
domain motion representations supply temporal context with
fewer policy input features than normal design, while a single
next-step target retains direct tracking guidance. This separa-
tion gives the transformer complementary information about
the immediate tracking objective and the surrounding motion,
providing a structured way to use temporal information in
the reference library. Our extrapolation method enables 50-Hz
reference generation between slower visual updates, and mea-
surement anchoring compensates for shared reconstruction off-
sets. The asynchronous pipeline allows visual reconstruction
and policy execution to operate at different rates. This avoids
forcing the controller to wait for every visual update, while
retaining an input interface based on a single RGB camera.
Hardware deployment demonstrates that the framework can
track highly dynamic dance motions from live video. The same
tracking policy also accomplishes device-free teleoperation
tasks, including bimanual and single-arm object manipulation,
without task-specific training
These advantages come with trade-offs. Reference quality
still depends on visual reconstruction and retargeting, and
latent phase propagation cannot guarantee accurate predictions
during prolonged occlusion or abrupt motion changes. Future
work will focus on improving the accuracy and process-
ing speed of video-based human motion reconstruction. We


15
will also extend the current fixed-camera setup to support
egocentric video from a camera mounted on the robot’s
head, accounting for camera motion and viewpoint changes
during robot movement. More motion data should also be
incorporated to discover the scaling law of the frequency-
domain representations. The frequency-domain representation
summarizes local temporal structure in a shared feature space,
providing a compact conditioning interface across diverse mo-
tion clips. This structure may facilitate the reuse of temporal
features across clips. We hypothesize that such conditioning
can help the tracking policy benefit more effectively from
increases in the size and diversity of the motion dataset.
