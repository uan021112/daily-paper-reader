Abstract 

Successful manipulation requires the manipulated objects to progress toward a desired task out-come. Generative policies can produce plausible robot motions without ensuring this progress, allowing successive action chunks to stall or undo earlier changes to the scene. We introduce a task-centric inference-time refinement framework for flow matching policies that evaluates can-didate action chunks through parallel physics-based rollouts and searches for motions satisfying sufficient conditions for task convergence. Task-level performance funnels bound temporary re-gressions while requiring net progress over each chunk. We establish sufficient conditions under which repeated funnel satisfaction yields convergence throughout execution, and use interval-wise Lipschitz bounds to verify continuous-time funnel satisfaction from sampled rollouts. To accom-modate contact-rich hybrid dynamics, we refine the flow’s source seeds using derivative-free Co-variance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES), constrained to radial quantile bands of the source distribution to help retain the learned motion prior. This formulation supports both Eu-clidean and pose-valued Riemannian Flow Matching (RMFM) policies. On PushT and three-cube stacking, refinement raises success from 31.3% to 88.5% and from 77.0% to 86.0%. On PushT, whose coverage score responds continuously to the motion of the block, a learned funnel lowers the average maximum loss of achieved progress by 19% relative to constraining the endpoint of each chunk alone, at a similar success rate. With the radial band, 11.3% of the executed motion windows are out of distribution, a rate within the range of held-out demonstrations, compared with 34.1% for an unconstrained search. 

Keywords: Generative robotics, flow matching, task-level convergence, motion planning, inference-time optimization 

1. Introduction 

Diffusion and flow matching policies learn multimodal distributions over action chunks, enabling complex manipulation behaviors from demonstrations (Chi et al., 2025; Rouxel et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025). Yet plausible motions do not by themselves ensure task completion: successive chunks can stall or undo earlier progress. For example, a robot folding a shirt may generate plausible grasping and sweeping motions while repeatedly disturbing an earlier fold. Progress must therefore be assessed through the evolution of the manipulated objects, whose desired arrangement defines completion even when many robot configurations are compatible with it.

Existing methods refine generative plans at inference time to satisfy safety and feasibility con-straints, using barrier functions or constrained sampling (Xiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Stability-oriented methods also guide generation or constrain learned motion dynamics through conditions that encourage or require a Lyapunov function to decrease at each step (Mizuta and Le-ung, 2024; Pérez-Dattari et al., 2026). These approaches address collision avoidance, admissible trajectories, and convergence of robot motion. Such requirements do not by themselves ensure that successive action chunks advance the manipulated objects toward task completion. A recent line of work instead evaluates and refines generated actions through their consequences predicted by learned world models (Qi et al., 2026; Du and Song, 2025). Task-oriented objectives include predicted progress or distance to a goal at the end of a chunk or planning horizon (Yan et al., 2026; Hegde et al., 2026), as well as accumulated rewards and terminal value estimates (Hamel, 2026). These objectives improve predicted outcomes, but do not specify how much intermediate regression is acceptable or provide sufficient conditions for convergence under repeated execution. In particular, a favorable endpoint can conceal substantial loss of progress within the chunk. What remains unresolved is how to formulate task-progress conditions for successive action chunks of generative manipulation policies that accommodate temporary setbacks while ensuring convergence, and how to use these conditions for inference-time refinement through physics-based simulation. We introduce a framework for flow matching policies that evaluates candidate action chunks through parallel simulated world rollouts and searches for motions satisfying sufficient con-ditions for task convergence. The pretrained policy supplies candidate motions, while simulation predicts their effects on the manipulated task before execution. This framework is supported by three contributions: task-level performance funnels, verification from sampled rollouts, and con-strained optimization of the flow’s source seed. First, manipulation can require temporary setbacks, such as repositioning fabric before com-pleting a fold (Mao et al., 2026). We introduce task-level performance funnels that bound these setbacks while requiring net progress over each action chunk, and establish sufficient conditions under which repeated funnel satisfaction yields convergence throughout execution. These funnels build on finite-step Lyapunov conditions, which permit intermediate increases while requiring net decrease (Noroozi et al., 2020), and on funnel Model Predictive Control (MPC), which constrains transient error (Berger et al., 2022), applying both ideas to task error over action chunks.

Second, sampled rollouts can miss funnel violations between prediction times. We use interval-wise Lipschitz bounds (Lera and Sergeyev, 2013) to verify satisfaction throughout continuous exe-cution, and introduce a practical variant based on locally estimated rates of change. Third, contact-rich hybrid dynamics motivate sampling-based optimization. To exploit parallel candidate evaluation while seeking effective refinement within few sequential iterations, we use the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) (Hansen, 2023). We constrain the search to a radial band of the source distribution’s typical set to help retain the learned motion prior, extending earlier approaches based on noise standardization and typical-radius projection (Stölzle et al., 2026; Hegde et al., 2026). In summary, our contributions are 1. Inference-time refinement for task convergence. A framework for flow matching policies that evaluates candidate action chunks through parallel physics-simulated world rollouts and searches for motions satisfying sufficient conditions for task convergence. 2. Task-level performance funnels. Bounds on task error that allow temporary setbacks while requiring net progress over each action chunk, with sufficient conditions for task convergence throughout execution. 3. Verification from sampled rollouts. A procedure for checking continuous-time task-funnel satisfaction using interval-wise Lipschitz bounds, with a practical variant based on locally estimated residual rates. 4. Seed refinement with radial quantile bands. An extension of prior seed-refinement meth-ods (Stölzle et al., 2026; Hegde et al., 2026) that replaces fixed-radius normalization with radial quantile bands and applies CMA-ES in the Gaussian source coordinates of both Eu-clidean and pose-valued Riemannian Flow Matching (RMFM) policies. An extended discussion of related work is provided in Appendix A.

3. Task-Centric Refinement 

World dynamics, task state, and task potential. Let x(t) denote the state of the robot and its environment, u(t) the applied input, and w a waypoint action chunk, which the controller κ tracks according to its execution timing. Under the controlled dynamics of (1), at iteration k the plan induces a rollout xkw(t) = Rκ(xk, w ; t) from the current world state over t ∈ [0 , H ], including tracking and contact dynamics (Appendix F.2 states the regularity assumptions). We formalize task completion through the manipulated objects rather than a desired robot con-figuration. In stacking, for instance, the relative cube placement describes the target, while the arm can take many configurations compatible with it. We introduce a task state y = T (x) ∈ Y and a goal set G ⊆ Y .1 The task rollout is ykw(t) = T (xkw(t)) . Progress is measured by a task-level Lyapunov candidate V : Y → R≥0, which we call the task potential , with goal set G = {y : V (y) ≤ vgoal },

vgoal ≥ 0. An exact goal has vgoal = 0 , with V positive outside G, and a positive threshold describes completion within tolerance. The potential encodes the goal and a useful ordering of intermediate task states, but it need not decrease under any prescribed controller. 

Performance funnels and task convergence. Although decreasing V expresses progress toward the goal, requiring it to decrease at every instant would exclude useful preparatory motions. We therefore describe acceptable task evolution through a nonnegative envelope ρk : [0 , H ] → R≥0.Starting from the current value Vk = V (yk), this envelope allows temporary increases while requir-ing a smaller terminal value, so that those maneuvers produce net progress:

4. Generation and Refinement 

Flow-matching prior. Given demonstrations D = {(oi, w i)} of observation histories and action chunks, a conditional flow-matching policy learns a vector field vθ(z, τ | o) (Lipman et al., 2023, 2024). Integrating dψ θ/dτ = vθ(ψθ, τ | o) from a source seed, ψθ(0 | z0, o ) = z0, to τ = 1 decodes the seed into an action chunk w = ψθ(1 | z0, o ). Generative time τ parameterizes the construction of the entire chunk, and physical time t describes the execution of the resulting motion. The framework supports Euclidean actions, trained with the Optimal Transport (OT) path, and pose-valued actions, trained with RMFM (Chen and Lipman, 2024; Braun et al., 2024) from a data-centered wrapped-normal rotational source (Appendix B). With the policy, the observation and the sampler fixed, decoding is a deterministic map from seed to chunk, and refinement searches over its input. 

Search objective. The search ranks candidate plans by a smooth maximum of their sampled resid-uals, the unnormalized log-sum-exp 

Jk(w) = β−1 log Pni=1 eβg i . (8) It accounts for the worst residual and for the values close to it, and the temperature β > 0 con-trols how closely it approximates the largest sampled residual Mk (Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004, §3.1.5). This smoothing acts across the samples of one rollout and serves the ranking of candidates. The funnel verdict of a plan comes from estimated verification, which uses the regularity of the residual in physical time (Section 3; Appendix F.5). Each planning call starts from the nominal proposal of the policy, keeps the lowest-Jk plan among all evaluated candidates (Algorithm 1), and executes it together with its verification status. The results of Section 3 apply to the executed chunks that satisfy their funnel. Appendix E.1 gives the stopping rules. 

Radial quantile band. Seeds drawn from a high-dimensional standard Gaussian concentrate on a thin shell around a typical radius, and the flow is trained to transport seeds from this shell. Prior work fixes the radius of the seed to its typical value (Stölzle et al., 2026; Hegde et al., 2026). We extend this projection to a radial band, so that the radius of the seed varies within a prescribed quantile interval of the shell. 5. Experiments 

We compare nominal execution, the flow-matching policy without refinement, with refined execu-tion on planar PushT and three-cube stacking in robosuite (Zhu et al., 2020; Todorov et al., 2012), with stacking demonstrations from MimicGen StackThree_D1 (Mandlekar et al., 2023). Appendix E.4 gives the environment and execution details, and Appendix G reports further ablations and two-nut assembly. 

Task progress and evaluation. Refinement is guided by the task potential V , where lower is better: a weighted pose error on the single-stage PushT (Appendix C.4) and a phased potential on stacking, which also orders the intermediate task states (Appendix C.5). We measure the physical outcome with each environment’s own task score r ∈ [0 , 1] , where higher is better, and its success predicate. On PushT, r is the coverage of the goal by the block, with success at r > 0.95 . Coverage changes continuously with the motion of the block, so the score registers progress within a chunk. On stacking, r = 12 aRG (1 + aBR ), where aXY is the footprint overlap of cube X on cube Y times their contact, and success requires the tower to stand after the gripper releases it and retreats. The score also measures partial progress in episodes that do not succeed. 

Policies. On PushT we follow Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2025) and use its one-dimensional U-Net conditioned by Feature-Wise Linear Modulation (FiLM), trained with the Euclidean OT path. On stacking we use a transformer based on the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture (Peebles and Xie, 2023) trained with RMFM (Chen and Lipman, 2024; Braun et al., 2024) along screw-motion paths on End Effector (EE) poses (Appendix B). Architectures and training settings are in Appendix E.3.

The remaining funnels are fitted to demonstrated traces of V and share one shape, which descends to (1 − γk)Vk and adds an allowance that vanishes by the end of the chunk (Appendix D). Calibrated funnels use constant parameters fitted over the whole task (global ). Learned funnels predict the parameters from the observation with a small network, with allowances in units of V (additive ) or as fractions of Vk (multiplicative ). Fitting uses demonstrations only, never refined rollouts, so a fitted funnel describes the regressions and progress of demonstrated behavior. A funnel is more lenient when it requests less terminal progress or allows V to rise further within the chunk. The leniency α of a learned funnel is a quantile level: given the observation, a fraction α of the demonstrated chunks reaches the requested progress and a fraction α stays within the allowance, so a larger α gives a more lenient funnel (Appendix D.2). 

Protocol and metrics. An episode runs until the success predicate holds or its step budget is spent, and every chunk is executed in full. All configurations share 400 initial conditions and are paired: in each episode they use the same initial state, random seeds and initial source draw, from which refinement starts. The policy is trained on all demonstrations, and the fitted funnels on a separate split (Appendix D.2). We report success and the CVaR 10 of the final score, the mean over the 10% of episodes with the lowest score (Rockafellar and Uryasev, 2000). Progress retention is measured by the maximum drawdown max t(max s≤t rs − rt), the largest fall of the score below its running maximum, in points ( ×100 ), through its mean and its CVaR 90 , the mean over the 10% of episodes with the largest drawdown. For funnels with an interior bound, we report the share of executed chunks that pass estimated verification. A difference is significant when its paired-bootstrap 95% interval excludes zero (Appendix G.1).

The band thus trades some success for motion that stays close to the demon-strations. Appendix G.8 reports the complete comparison, including Adam, Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), the fixed-radius sphere and a sweep of the band width. 

6. Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work 

We presented a task-centric inference-time refinement framework for flow matching policies, which evaluates candidate action chunks through parallel physics-based rollouts and searches the flow’s source seeds for motions that satisfy sufficient conditions for task convergence. These conditions take the form of task-level performance funnels, which accommodate the temporary regressions that manipulation often requires while asking for net progress over each chunk, so that their repeated satisfaction drives task error down throughout execution and can be verified from sampled rollouts through interval-wise Lipschitz bounds. On PushT and three-cube stacking, refinement raises suc-cess far above nominal execution, as the funnel’s terminal contraction steers each chunk toward net progress, and its transient bound helps retain the progress already achieved on PushT, where the task score registers it. Radial quantile bands on the source keep the refined motion close to the demonstrations. 

Limitations and future work. The quality of refinement follows the quality of the task potential 

V , which we design by hand from privileged world state. Learned progress measures are a natural route to perceptual and long-horizon tasks. Refinement also uses the simulator as its world model, so it requires the state of the scene and a simulator whose rollouts predict the executed motion. Learned world models could take this role on physical systems. The convergence results apply to executed chunks that satisfy their funnel, the search can execute a plan that does not, and estimated verification uses rates derived from the samples in place of certified bounds. Finally, planning takes seconds per chunk (Appendix H), during which the scene has to stay still, so refinement does not yet run in real time. Appendix I discusses these points further.

Appendix A. Related Work 

Generative policies for robotics. Generative policies learn expressive models of multimodal robot behavior from demonstrations, representing multiple valid ways to perform a task (Chi et al., 2025; Rouxel et al., 2024). Diffusion and flow matching support conditioning on robot state, im-ages, point clouds, and language instructions (Chi et al., 2025; Chisari et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025), allowing motion generation to respond to both the scene and the requested task. These poli-cies commonly generate action chunks: sequences of future commands whose initial segment is executed before replanning from updated observations in a receding-horizon loop (Chi et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025). Actions often specify desired joint configurations or end-effector poses over time, so a chunk describes a robot motion sequence tracked by a lower-level controller (Chi et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025). Manifold-aware formulations additionally account for the geometry of pose-valued actions (Braun et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2025). We use a pretrained flow matching policy as a fixed prior over waypoint chunks. 

Inference-time refinement of generative policies. Inference-time refinement can act on gener-ated motions, intermediate sampling states, or source noise. Direct motion refinement uses gener-ated action sequences to initialize trajectory optimization (Qi et al., 2026; Hamel, 2026), but subse-quent updates can drift from the learned prior. Gradient guidance steers diffusion or flow updates using differentiable objectives (Carvalho et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025). Projection methods en-force constraints on intermediate samples or their estimated clean outputs (Christopher et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2023; Watanabe et al., 2026), requiring feasibility evaluation before generation is com-plete. Source-noise optimization evaluates objectives on fully generated motions: gradient-based updates differentiate through both the generator and the objective (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024; Karun-ratanakul et al., 2024), while sampling-based updates use only objective evaluations (Stölzle et al., 2026; Hegde et al., 2026). We use sampling-based source optimization with radial quantile bands to restrict the search to typical source-noise radii. 

World models for task progress evaluation. World models support action selection and refine-ment by predicting the task outcomes used to evaluate candidate actions (Ebert et al., 2018; Suh and Tedrake, 2021). Distances between predicted and goal representations guide planning in learned visual spaces (Zhou et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025; Hegde et al., 2026). Generative policy propos-als can also be ranked or refined using predicted rewards and task objectives (Qi et al., 2026; Du and Song, 2025; Hamel, 2026), or guided by learned progress estimates (Yan et al., 2026). We use physics-based world rollouts to evaluate task evolution under tracking and contact dynamics. 

Task progress measures. Quantifying this progress is difficult when tasks involve intermedi-ate milestones, regressions, and recovery. Beyond geometric task potentials, visual and language-conditioned representations provide learned goal proximity and reward signals (Ma et al., 2023b,a). Reward models learn completion and partial-success assessments from robot videos, incorporat-ing failures and trajectory comparisons (Lee et al., 2026; Liang et al., 2026). Finer assessments model task stages and progress within them (Chen et al., 2026a,b), or process-level advancement, regression, and recovery (Mao et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026). Reward machines encode sequential milestones explicitly (Camacho et al., 2021; Baert et al., 2025). We express task progress through a scalar measure of deviation from desired object relationships, combining subtask measures to account for sequential goals.

Safety and convergence of robot motion. One line of work incorporates Lyapunov or contrac-tion conditions into training objectives or model architectures to obtain stable robot motion dy-namics (Pérez-Dattari and Kober, 2023; Beik-Mohammadi et al., 2024; Pérez-Dattari et al., 2026). Another establishes closed-loop stability through online predictive optimization, using predictive control to select inputs or predictive filters to correct proposals from an existing policy (Mayne et al., 2000; Didier et al., 2024). Stability conditions also differ in how they treat transients. Finite-step Lyapunov conditions allow intermediate increases by requiring net decrease over sev-eral steps (Noroozi et al., 2020). To also regulate the transient error, prescribed-performance and funnel control specify admissible envelopes (Ilchmann et al., 2002; Berger et al., 2025), which can be incorporated into predictive control (Grandia et al., 2020; Berger et al., 2022). We apply these principles to progress of the manipulated objects, combining bounded transient regression with ter-minal contraction of a task potential over generated action chunks. Convergence remains conditional on finding satisfying chunks and agreement between evaluated and executed dynamics. Recursive feasibility is not established. 

Continuous-time verification from sampled rollouts. Extending discrete trajectory checks to continuous-time satisfaction requires regularity assumptions and margins (Fainekos and Pappas, 2009). Lipschitz optimization bounds function values between samples and uses local rate estimates to reduce conservatism (Lera and Sergeyev, 2013). Our verification procedure uses bounds on how quickly the task-funnel residual can change to rule out violations between consecutive rollout samples. With valid upper bounds on this rate of change, passing the check guarantees that the task error stays within the prescribed envelope throughout the chunk. In our experiments, we estimate these rates from the sampled rollouts, and call the resulting check estimated verification.

Appendix C. Task Progress Measures and Task Potentials 

This appendix constructs the task potential V of Section 3, a task-level Lyapunov candidate, so that 

V does not increase while the task progresses as intended: the subtasks are completed in order, each by first meeting a prerequisite, such as lifting a cube, and then completing it, such as placing the cube on its support. Executed motions can leave this progression, through robot-only motions that leave V constant, contacts that push, drop or topple objects, and detours away from the goal, so V

remains a candidate, and the funnel imposes its decrease on each executed chunk. All constructions are stated on a bounded operating domain Ω of task states, set by the task definition and the training

data: the physical limits of the scene, such as the table extent, together with the range of task states covered by the demonstrations, with a margin. This appendix has two parts. The first is a construction procedure that can be reused for other tasks with identifiable subtasks: the requirements that a potential should meet, a way to blend the costs of one subtask into a smooth residual without ridges (Appendix C.1), and a way to compose the residuals of successive subtasks so that completing one never increases the potential (Appendix C.2, Proposition 3). The second part consists of the potentials of our three tasks (Appendices C.4–C.6). Their coordinates, costs, gates and constants are designed by hand for each task. They are instances of the procedure and make no claim of generality. 

Requirements. The method places four requirements on the potential over Ω.• Zero set. V is nonnegative and vanishes exactly on the completed task, so that the terminal contraction of Theorem 1 drives execution toward completion. • Regularity. V is Lipschitz along rollouts, as the verification bound requires (Lemma 5). We construct it C1, which gives a Lipschitz constant on the bounded domain Ω and keeps kinks out of the trace that the funnel evaluates. • No ridges. A motion that advances the current subtask never increases V , so the funnel does not penalize progress on that subtask. • Ordering. Completing a subtask never increases V , and V does not depend on a later subtask until the earlier ones approach completion. For a single-stage task, a weighted distance to the desired state meets these requirements, as in PushT (Appendix C.4). For tasks with identifiable subtasks and prerequisites, we give a construction procedure. A subtask residual ϕ is a nonnegative scalar function of the task state that vanishes exactly when its subtask is complete. The procedure blends the costs of a subtask into a smooth residual (Appendix C.1) and composes the residuals of successive subtasks, so that activating the next subtask preserves the desired ordering of task states (Appendix C.2). In stacking, for example, the first subtask places the red cube on the green one and the second places the blue cube on the red one. The prerequisite of each placement is lifting the cube from the table, and its completion is the cube centred and resting on its support. Each task supplies its own geometric subtask costs and completion gates, with a few constants calibrated on demonstrations. We apply the full procedure to stacking (Appendix C.5) and to two-nut assembly (Appendix C.6). Learning the potential from demonstrated progress, in place of the hand-designed instances, is left to future work.

The funnel is built for a fixed task potential V (Appendix C) and is frozen at the start of each planning call. The results of Section 3 place four requirements on it. • Anchoring. It starts at the current value Vk, possibly raised by a start allowance that fades over the chunk and makes the first samples verifiable (Appendix F.2). • Terminal contraction. It ends at the requested value (1 − γk)Vk, which drives the convergence of Theorem 1. • Bounded transient. It stays below (1+ dk)Vk, which bounds the task error between replanning times.

D.2. Fitting Funnels to Demonstrations 

The learned and calibrated funnels take their parameters from how the task potential evolves along demonstrated chunks. The labels come from demonstrations only, measured with the fixed task potential, and not from refined rollouts or their outcomes. A fitted funnel therefore describes how far the potential rises within a chunk and how much progress it makes along demonstrated behavior, and refinement requires proposed chunks to stay within that envelope. The fitted parameters are fixed before refinement, and every evaluated leniency level is reported in Appendix G.3. 

Demonstration labels. The policy is trained on the full demonstration dataset. Funnel fitting uses a separate three-way split of the same demonstrations into training, validation and test groups (70/15/15%), made at the level of complete episodes before windows are extracted. Task outcomes are evaluated in simulator episodes from the sampled initial conditions. Each window of horizon 

H starting at boundary k gives the initial value Vk, the clipped relative endpoint progress pk =clip(1 − Vk+1 /V k, 0, 1) , and the interior trace V (ti), 1 < i < n . We use the demonstrated prefix up to first success and, for sequential tasks, exclude windows that undo a completed prerequisite according to the task’s completion gates. 

Calibrated funnels. Calibrated funnels use the additive family of (30) with constant parameters, fitted directly to the demonstration labels. The training and calibration episodes are disjoint. The bump time and onset are fixed to τ = 0 .5 and σ = 0 , and the progress γ is the 10% quantile of the progress labels pk of the training chunks. The two allowances share one scale, δ = η = c. Since the envelope is affine in (δ, η ) at fixed γ, each calibration chunk has a smallest scale ck for which the envelope contains its samples before the endpoint, the largest ratio between the room it needs above the descent and the room a unit allowance gives. The calibration score of an episode is the largest 

ck over its chunks, so an episode is contained at scale c when all its included interior samples lie inside the funnel. With n calibration episodes, we set c to the ⌈(n + 1) · 0.9⌉-th smallest episode score, the finite-sample split-conformal upper quantile at coverage 0.9 (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023). Optimizers. Both searches start from the nominal seed. Adam uses a learning rate of 0.05 and default moments, so that each seed coordinate moves by at most about 0.05 per iteration. The search returns the iterate with the lowest loss on the model, the nominal seed unless some iterate is strictly better. 

Planning and execution models. All searches optimize the same funnel objective. The popula-tion searches evaluate it on rollouts of the native simulator. The gradient searches evaluate it on rollouts of the smoothed differentiable reimplementation, starting from the observed start state of the chunk, and differentiate through that rollout and the flow decoder. Every loss, gradient, line-search trial and stopping decision of a gradient search comes from this model, which agrees with the native simulator to within 0.16 px in median on recorded chunks. The selected plans of all searches are executed in the native environment.

We train on dense, stride-one windows of the demonstrations, padding at episode boundaries by repeating the first or last sample, and normalize features to [−1, 1] with min–max statistics from all policy-training demonstrations. Two choices are specific to our setting. For stacking, the gripper target is the signed command at each control step, while the measured aperture and the previous command are observations. Decoding executes the command through its sign. Rotation blocks are left unnormalized, since they already lie in [−1, 1] ,before conversion to the RMFM carrier. The policy is trained on all demonstrations. The funnels are fitted on a separate three-way split of the same demonstrations, described in Appendix D.2.

G.1. Paired Bootstrap Intervals 

Each comparison uses matched episodes. For a given episode index, all compared configurations share the environment initial condition, the random seeds of the environment and the policy, and the initial flow-matching source draw. The funnel ablation uses N = 400 episodes and the search comparison N = 200 .For each of 4000 bootstrap replicates, we sample N episode indices with replacement and use the same indices for both configurations. A draw such as [2 , 2, 5, 1, . . . ] selects those episodes, duplicates included, from both configurations. We recompute the metric for each configuration on the sampled episodes and take the difference from the reference configuration, which is endpoint-only in the funnel ablation and our search in the search comparison. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of these differences give the 95% percentile-bootstrap interval. We report it together with the point estimate, the difference of the metric between the two configurations on the original paired episodes, and call a difference significant when its interval excludes zero. Pairing retains the correspondence between starting conditions, so the variation in episode difficulty is shared by the two configurations. The intervals describe the uncertainty across the evaluated initial conditions for the reported policy and execution setup. Figure 6 and Table 14 report these intervals, and the shading of Tables 2 and 13 follows them, except for the OOD column, which is shaded when the rate lies above the 95% interval of the held-out demonstrations (Appendix G.8). For tail metrics, each replicate identifies the lowest-scoring or largest-drawdown fraction of the resampled episodes separately for each configuration and averages that fraction before taking the difference. An episode can therefore belong to the tail of one configuration and to the bulk of the other, while both resampled datasets are built from the same episode indices.

Why terminal contraction suffices here. Progress on two-nut assembly comes in two discrete steps, one per seated nut, and the phased potential decreases along the motions that achieve them. Requiring V to fall from one chunk boundary to the next therefore already selects the plans that advance toward the next seat or keep what was achieved. A nut dropped within a chunk ends it at a higher V , which terminal contraction rejects, as a toppled tower does on stacking. The V-score is consistent with this. The drawdown under the interior constraints equals that under endpoint-only within the confidence intervals, and its largest values, 40 to 48 points, have the size of the progress of a whole nut. Two-nut assembly is the opposite case to PushT. On PushT the task score registers progress inside a chunk, and the interior bound improves retention (Section 5). On two-nut assembly the interior bound of the funnel contributes estimated verification of the transient at the success level of endpoint-only.

G.7. Verification Details 

A funnel with an interior envelope gives a condition on the transient that can be checked on each chunk. Verification applies to the configurations with an interior envelope, and endpoint-only is covered by its endpoint condition. For these configurations, Table 12 reports the share of complete executed chunks that pass sampled verification and estimated verification. As a reference, it also reports the share of planning calls whose nominal plan passes estimated verification on its predicted trace. Estimated verification uses the window wL = 0 .02 s on the physics-step grid of the rollout. Estimated verification is stricter than sampled verification by up to 16 points on PushT and 27 points on stacking, which shows how much the bound between samples contributes to the verdict, and the difference grows with the leniency of the learned funnels. For the plateaus the two shares agree to within a point, since their envelope lies far above V inside the chunk and the endpoint is the binding condition. Under the learned funnels, the executed chunks pass estimated verification more often than the nominal plans of the same calls, by 28 to 38 points on PushT and by 11 to 21 points on stacking. Under the linear funnel, few candidates meet the constraint, and the executed share is 0.5% on PushT and 3.3% on stacking. The nominal share counts every planning call on its predicted trace, including the last chunk of each episode, and the executed share counts complete executed chunks. Two-nut assembly follows the pattern of stacking. The difference between sampled and esti-mated verification reaches 53 points for the calibrated global funnel, and under the learned funnels the executed chunks pass estimated verification more often than the nominal plans, by 5 to 17 points. The sampled and estimated shares of the plateaus coincide, as on the other tasks.

Every iterative search runs on a time budget of 1.5 s per chunk, with patience and the generation cap disabled. The clock is checked after every generation or iteration, so a search exceeds it by at most one. A gradient search can still stop earlier, on a stationary gradient, a failed line search, or a plan that never reaches the block.

Appendix I. Extended Limitations 

The task potential. Refinement depends on the task potential. Our potentials are designed by hand from task geometry, with constants calibrated on demonstrations, and they read privileged simulator state. The search optimizes V , and the native task outcome improves when V follows the task through its intermediate stages, as with the phased potentials of Appendix G.6. Future work should replace the hand-designed potential with a learned progress measure that relates to completion and is regular enough for verification. The learned representations and reward models discussed in Appendix A could be starting points. 

World model. Refinement rolls out candidates in the simulator that also executes them, so the evaluated rollout matches execution, and it reads the full state of the scene. On a physical system, the rollouts would come from a calibrated simulator or from a learned world model, and the mismatch between predicted and executed motion would enter the convergence result as the endpoint error of Corollary 6. Learned simulators of contact-rich rigid-body dynamics (Allen et al., 2023) and latent

world models (Zhou et al., 2025; Assran et al., 2025) are candidates for this role. How model error affects funnel satisfaction is an open question. 

Search and guarantees. Refinement reaches useful motions through the prior within a finite search budget and executes its lowest-objective plan together with its estimated-verification sta-tus. The convergence results hold under funnel satisfaction of the executed chunks and agreement between simulated rollouts and execution. Two directions would strengthen them: conditions under which a satisfying chunk exists at every planning step, which would give recursive feasibility, and certified bounds on the residual rate, such as the one of Lemma 5, in place of the estimated rates. 

Optimization freedom and fidelity to the prior. Any refinement of a generative prior trades optimization freedom against fidelity to the prior. The more the optimizer can change a motion, the lower it can drive the objective and the constraint violation, and the further the motion can move from the demonstrated distribution, where the policy, the tracking controller and the rollouts are less reliable. The radial band is one way to set this tradeoff, through its width ζ. On PushT, the band-constrained search reaches 86.5% success with 11.3% of its motion windows out of distribution, and the unconstrained search reaches 96.0% with 34.1% (Table 2). The band constrains the radius and the mean of the seed and leaves its direction free, so it limits the shift of each chunk. The states that a refined chunk leads to still determine the next observation, which can differ from the demonstrated observations, the covariate shift familiar from imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011). A band width that adapts to the state, wider where the nominal plan is far from satisfying its funnel, is a possible extension. 

Computation and deployment. The candidate rollouts within each generation run in parallel, and the generations run in sequence, which gives a planning delay of 0.8 to 8.3 s per chunk in our experiments (Appendix H). Physical deployment would need a scene that stays still during planning, or a prediction of its evolution over the computation interval. Learned rollout models would shorten each generation. The refined seeds could also be amortized into a network that predicts them from the observation, as in learned noise policies for diffusion models (Wagenmaker et al., 2025; Eyring et al., 2025).
