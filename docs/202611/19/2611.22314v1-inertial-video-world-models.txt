ABSTRACT
The physical world has inertia: it evolves from what already exists rather than
being recreated at every instant. However, many existing video world models lack
an explicit mechanism for inertial state evolution, leaving the continuity of motion
and structure implicit in video prediction. During autoregressive rollouts, repeated
reconstruction can accumulate errors and destabilize the predicted dynamics. To
address this limitation, we introduce INERTIALWORLD, an Inertial Video World
Model that makes inertia a structural prior for autoregressive prediction: existing
scene evidence is carried across time rather than reconstructed at every step. Central
to this formulation is Inertial Memory, which turns memory from passive predictive
context into an evolving video state with two complementary views: a dense
Eulerian grid preserves scene-aligned evidence, while persistent Lagrangian carriers
bind local content to learned canonical coordinates. At each step, the model
transports the carrier content according to action-conditioned motion, predicts the
complete next video state from the transported carriers and dense grid, and uses a
learned gate to combine transported and newly predicted content into the memory
for the next step. Extensive experiments across multiple domains, including robot
manipulation, 3D game simulation, and open-world navigation, demonstrate that
INERTIALWORLD achieves competitive autoregressive prediction performance
against strong video world model baselines. Codes link: https://github.
com/YuanGao-YG/InertialWorld.

INTRODUCTION
Learned world models provide predictive environments for planning and control by modeling action-
conditioned dynamics (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019; 2025). Within this broader
family, video world models instantiate learned dynamics through action-conditioned video prediction,
enabling interactive simulation in games, robotic manipulation, and embodied navigation (Micheli
et al., 2022; Bruce et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023; Bar et al., 2025). Autoregressive and diffusion-based
formulations have substantially improved generation fidelity and controllability (Wu et al., 2024;
Alonso et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026), while post-training further refines predicted
rollouts (Wu et al., 2026b;a). Despite these advances, physical evolution is inertial: the world evolves
from what already exists rather than being recreated at every instant. Most video world models,
however, still formulate temporal evolution as repeated conditional generation, predicting persistent
scene content again at every step rather than explicitly carrying it forward. Since each generated
state conditions the next transition, small reconstruction errors can repeatedly re-enter the rollout and
destabilize predicted motion and structure (Wang et al., 2017; Babaeizadeh et al., 2021).
A prominent line of research mitigates this limitation by augmenting video prediction with memory.
Recurrent video predictors retain temporal features across prediction steps (Wang et al., 2017; 2018),
while transformer world models expose longer histories to the transition function (Chen et al., 2022;
Wu et al., 2024). This additional context is particularly valuable when occlusion or accumulated
generation noise makes the current frame ambiguous. Earlier features can retain identity and layout
cues that are no longer directly visible but remain relevant to the next transition. Spatial and persistent

memories preserve scene-aligned evidence across recurrent prediction steps (Wu et al., 2026c; Zhou
et al., 2026). Object-centric representations provide a complementary form of persistence by binding
content to structured entities (Locatello et al., 2020; Kipf et al., 2021), and their video extensions
model the dynamics of these entities through time (Wu et al., 2022). Collectively, these mechanisms
expose historical evidence to subsequent state transitions and improve temporal consistency, but
retention alone does not specify how the retained content should evolve. When memory serves
as context for a generic transition, the transition remains free to synthesize the next state without
explicitly advancing the stored content. Transformation-based predictors demonstrate the value of
warping observations or features (Jaderberg et al., 2015; Finn et al., 2016), yet warping does not
by itself make transport the recurrent evolution rule of persistent memory across model-predicted
steps. Thus, memory determines which past evidence remains available, whereas inertia additionally
specifies what persists and where it moves. This distinction motivates the central question of this
work: How can a video world model evolve the world it already remembers, rather than repeatedly
reconstructing its persistent content?
To address this question, we introduce INERTIALWORLD, an Inertial Video World Model that makes
inertia a structural prior for autoregressive prediction: existing scene evidence is carried across time
rather than reconstructed at every step. As illustrated in Figure 1, its central component, Inertial
Memory, turns retained predictive context into an evolving video state with two complementary views:
a dense Eulerian grid preserves scene-aligned evidence, while persistent Lagrangian carriers bind
local content to learned canonical coordinates. At each step, the model transports the carrier content
according to action-conditioned motion, predicts the complete next video state from the transported
carriers and dense grid, and uses a learned gate to combine transported and newly predicted content
into the memory for the next step. This carry-before-write transition establishes persistence as
the default path of state evolution while preserving the capacity to model changes that transport
alone cannot explain, without requiring object annotations or calibrated physical parameters. We
evaluate INERTIALWORLD on RT-1 robot manipulation (Brohan et al., 2022), Counter-Strike 3D
game simulation (Huang et al., 2025), and RECON open-world navigation (Shah et al., 2021). Across
all three interactive domains, INERTIALWORLD produces competitive autoregressive predictions
against strong video world model baselines. Our contributions are threefold:
• We formulate inertial video world modeling, a state-evolution view of video world models
in which existing scene evidence is inherited by default rather than repeatedly reconstructed.
• We introduce Inertial Memory, a hybrid Eulerian–Lagrangian video state that preserves dense
scene evidence while transporting persistent regional content through action-conditioned
coordinate motion before a learned gate updates the memory.
• We conduct extensive autoregressive prediction evaluations across robot manipulation, 3D
game simulation, and open-world navigation, demonstrating competitive autoregressive
prediction across all three domains against strong video world model baselines.

RELATED WORK
Video World Models. Action-conditioned video prediction learns how future observations evolve
under control (Oh et al., 2015; Finn et al., 2016). Early approaches use stochastic, recurrent, and

physics-inspired dynamics to model uncertainty and temporal dependence (Denton & Fergus, 2018;
Wang et al., 2017; Guen & Thome, 2020; Babaeizadeh et al., 2021). Learned discrete representations
(Van Den Oord et al., 2017; Esser et al., 2021) enable scalable autoregressive and masked video
prediction (Yan et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023; 2024; Wang et al., 2024). These formulations support
interactive simulation across games and robotics (Bruce et al., 2024; Valevski et al., 2025; Wu et al.,
2024; Yang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024a;b), while diffusion-based models provide an alternative
generative backbone (Alonso et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). Reinforcement learning and alignment
objectives further refine predicted rollouts (Wu et al., 2026b;a). In parallel, latent world models learn
compact predictive states for planning and imagined policy learning (Hafner et al., 2019; 2020; 2025),
sometimes prioritizing task-relevant dynamics over frame reconstruction (Schrittwieser et al., 2020;
Hansen et al., 2024). Rather than changing the video generator or its training objective, our work
changes how the recurrent video state evolves by introducing transport-structured persistence that is
compatible with different generative backbones.
Memory and Structured Video State. Existing memory mechanisms primarily determine what a
video world model retains. Recurrent states and token histories preserve temporal context (Wang
et al., 2017; 2018); spatial and persistent memories preserve scene-aligned content (Wu et al., 2026c;
Zhou et al., 2026); and object-centric representations bind content to persistent entities (Burgess et al.,
2019; Greff et al., 2019; Locatello et al., 2020), with temporal extensions supporting tracking and
prediction (Kipf et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022; Kipf et al., 2019). Relational models expose interactions
between state elements (Battaglia et al., 2016; Kipf et al., 2018; Battaglia et al., 2018), while
Hamiltonian and graph-based simulators impose additional structure on state transitions (Greydanus
et al., 2019; Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020). These approaches enrich what persists, but they do not
by themselves specify how retained content should evolve. A generic recurrent update can encode
motion without requiring that motion to relocate content already in memory. Transformation-based
predictors demonstrate the value of warping observations or features (Finn et al., 2016; Jaderberg
et al., 2015), but warping alone does not make transport the recurrent evolution rule of a persistent
state. Inertial Memory instead treats persistent canonical evidence as the transported quantity: it
maintains a dense scene field, binds selected local content to learned coordinate frames, and uses
their predicted evolution to transport the bound content before the memory is revised. Its defining
property is therefore the explicit coupling of coordinate evolution and content transport within a
hybrid dense–regional video state, rather than recurrence, slots, or warping in isolation.

METHOD
3.1
PROBLEM DEFINITION
Figure 2 summarizes the complete prediction pipeline. At time t, a video world model receives the
current frame ot and action at and predicts bot+1. A multi-step rollout observes ot only once; all
subsequent frames are generated from the model’s own state and the action sequence. We use a frozen
codec that represents the observed scene with a rollout-level Context code C and each predicted
frame with a Dynamic code D. We encode C = EncC(ot) and initialize the recurrent video state as
St = Init(C). Each causal prediction step is then
( bDτ+1, Sτ+1) = Fθ(Sτ, aτ; C),
boτ+1 = Dec(C, bDτ+1).
(1)
The Context code remains fixed and anchors the observed scene throughout the rollout. The predicted
Dynamic code is a complete code for the next frame, not a residual correction to a ground-truth frame.
At the first transition, there is no previous Dynamic code; all observed information enters through
C and the initialized state. Future frames define training targets D⋆
τ+1, but neither those targets nor
their encoded features are fed back into the recurrence. This causal interface defines the prediction
problem but does not specify how the state should evolve. A generic recurrent state can retain past
information while the transition still rewrites its content at every step. We instead make persistence
structural: predicted motion first acts on stored content, and prediction updates the state only after
this content has been carried forward.

rτ,k = (Aτ,k, ατ,k, ξτ,k) is a persistent Lagrangian carrier. The grid remains aligned with scene
coordinates and preserves evidence across the full spatial domain. Each carrier instead moves with a
local region: Aτ,k stores canonical content, ατ,k describes its support, and ξτ,k specifies where that
content is placed. The soft binding map Bτ assigns evidence between the carriers and a dense fallback.
The carriers are learned regions rather than supervised semantic objects, so this representation does
not require object annotations or a complete object decomposition.
Carrying persistent content.
Let ξτ collect all carrier coordinates. The first part of a transition
predicts their action-conditioned motion and applies it to the stored state:
ξτ+1 = Uθ(Sτ, aτ),
eSτ+1 = Carry(Sτ; ξτ →ξτ+1).
(2)
The coordinate update Uθ predicts bounded motion from current memory and action. Carry re-renders
each carrier’s canonical content and support at its new coordinates, so local evidence moves with
the carrier rather than being synthesized again at the destination. For the dense grid, the binding
map combines carrier-induced motion with a learned fallback flow; normalized forward splatting
propagates evidence while accounting for ownership and visibility. The resulting eSτ+1 is therefore the
state inherited from the past. When the predicted motion is zero, this operation reduces to an identity
carry. The coordinates are learned representational variables, not calibrated physical parameters.
Predicting and updating.
Transport alone cannot explain newly visible content, deformation, or
other changes not represented by the current memory. Let f
Mτ+1 denote the writable content in the
carried state. A predictor reads that state and jointly produces the complete next Dynamic code, a
candidate memory update, and a learned write gate. Suppressing time subscripts within this transition
and using + for time τ + 1, this is
( bD+, Mnew, g) = Pθ( eS, a; C),
M+ = (1 −g) ⊙f
M + g ⊙Mnew.
(3)
The first output is decoded into the complete next frame, allowing the model to generate changes that
cannot be obtained by transport alone. The second output proposes new writable memory content,
while the gate determines which carried values should persist and which should be replaced. Together
with the transported carrier coordinates, the updated content forms Sτ+1. Thus, persistence is the
default path, but the memory can still adapt when the newly predicted state provides evidence for
change. Because the proposal is generated from the model prediction rather than the target Dynamic
code, repeated application of Eqs. 2–3 remains strictly predicted-only. This ordered transition—carry,
predict, then update—is our operational definition of representational inertia; it does not impose a
hard-coded Newtonian law.

3.3
LEARNING AND AUTOREGRESSIVE ROLLOUT
We train on the same predicted states used at inference and backpropagate through the complete
rollout. Discrete internal decisions remain hard on the forward path and use a straight-through
estimator for optimization. The recurrence is never refreshed with a target state or detached between
autoregressive steps. We progressively increase the prediction horizon from 8 to 16 and then 32 steps
so that the model learns to preserve and update its own state under repeated application.
The training objective combines prediction, memory-consistency, and regularization terms:
L = Lpred + λmemLmem + λregLreg.
(4)
Here, Lpred supervises the complete Dynamic token IDs, their finite scalar quantization dig-
its (Mentzer et al., 2024), and the decoded frame’s appearance and temporal change. Lmem provides
consistency signals for transporting, binding, and updating memory from adjacent frozen-codec
representations without object or contact annotations. Lreg regularizes carrier diversity and dense
motion.
We use λmem = λreg = 1 in all experiments; each grouped loss is a fixed weighted sum of its
constituent terms. Appendix B.2 gives the complete constituent list and coefficients together with the
full staging schedule and gate initialization.
Ground truth is used only to compute these losses; it never replaces Sτ in the rollout. Training
therefore matches the causal, predicted-only recurrence used at inference.

EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments address four questions that follow directly from the proposed inertial principle: (i)
does the complete video world model improve the accuracy and stability of autoregressive prediction
across interaction domains, (ii) are its gains retained when evaluation focuses on action-induced
motion, (iii) do its generated rollouts preserve behavioral differences between policies, and (iv) does
performance degrade when Inertial Memory is removed? We answer these questions in the same
order below.
4.1
STABLE AUTOREGRESSIVE VIDEO PREDICTION
Evaluation Protocols. We first evaluate autoregressive video prediction on RT-1 (Brohan et al.,
2022). Every method is evaluated on the same 1,024 episodes and predicts 32 future frames using its
native observation interface. All outputs are compared with the same 256×320 ground-truth frames.
We evaluate the released checkpoints of RLVR-World (Wu et al., 2026b), Vid2World (Huang et al.,
2025), WorldGym (Quevedo et al., 2026), and DriftWorld (Lu et al., 2026) without retraining. The
frozen protocol is disjoint from our training and development episodes; because released methods use
different native RT-1 partitions, it is not necessarily held out for every baseline. Under the released
RLVR-World partition, 1,015 of the 1,024 evaluation episodes belong to its training subset. We
therefore treat this as a descriptive released-checkpoint comparison, rather than a controlled estimate
of held-out generalization. For Counter-Strike 3D game simulation, we follow the CSGO benchmark
and data partition used by Vid2World (Huang et al., 2025). From its 500 held-out episodes, we
reserve the first 128 for validation and evaluate a fixed 70-episode subset of the remaining split.
Each selected episode contributes one fixed anchor with at least 32 preceding frames, and every
method predicts the same 32 adjacent targets at 256×320 resolution. We compare against the released
checkpoints of Vid2World and DIAMOND (Alonso et al., 2024); each method retains its native
conditioning interface, while conditioning frames are never scored. Appendix C specifies the frozen
selection rule and the complete frame–action alignment. We additionally evaluate video prediction
for embodied navigation on RECON (Shah et al., 2021). We freeze 128 distinct test episodes from
the official 2,367-episode held-out split after excluding the 17 trajectories used for validation. The
common anchor in each episode provides the last observed frame ot, and every method predicts the
same 32 adjacent targets ot+1:t+32 at 240×320 resolution; baselines retain their native four-frame
context when required. We compare the released checkpoints of Vid2World, NWM (Bar et al.,
2025), and RAE-NWM (Zhang et al., 2026). For all three dataset blocks in Table 1, we first average
each frame-level metric over the 32 predicted frames of an episode. We then report the mean and

4.2
MOTION FIDELITY UNDER ACTION
Motion-Focused Diagnostics. Aggregate appearance metrics can be dominated by static regions, so
we add three fixed diagnostics targeted at action-induced change. Because RT-1 lacks optical-flow
annotations, the same frozen RAFT (Teed & Deng, 2020) estimator measures flow in every prediction
and reference rollout. Flow EPE averages their endpoint discrepancy over all pixels and 32 transitions;
it is not an error against measured flow ground truth. Flow Cosine measures directional agreement on
reference-dynamic pixels with flow magnitude of at least one pixel. Dynamic-region LPIPS applies
spatial LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) only within the same reference-motion mask. Each quantity is first
aggregated within an episode and then across episodes.

ABLATION STUDY
We train four 0.8B-scale models from scratch for 30k updates under a matched protocol. The Full
model combines dense Eulerian state with persistent Lagrangian carriers; Dense-only removes the
carriers, canonical content, and transport while retaining the Eulerian state; Recurrent Memory
replaces this structured state with a conventional GRU; and Plain AR uses no recurrent memory. All
variants share the tokenizer, data, action representation, optimizer, AR8→AR16→AR32 curriculum,
global batch size, and ordered 1,024-episode H32 protocol. We evaluate content persistence on frozen
DINO tracks defined solely from ground truth: long-lived error measures displaced Context content
over the fixed late half of the rollout (Steps 17–32), post-reappearance error evaluates content after a
ground-truth gap of at least four steps anywhere in H32, and late-track error covers all reliable tracks
in the same late-half window. The main evaluation covers all 32 steps; this shared midpoint window is
used only to emphasize accumulated state persistence rather than short-range prediction quality. For
all three prediction-to-reference feature errors, lower is better; they target persistence failures that can
be obscured by static regions in full-frame metrics. On all three measures in Table 3, the Full model
ranks first, followed by Dense-only, Recurrent Memory, and Plain AR. The Full model also improves
over Dense-only in every case; all three pointwise paired 95% percentile-bootstrap intervals exclude
zero and are reported in Appendix Table 13. The larger improvements over Recurrent Memory
and Plain AR further favor structured inertial state over generic recurrence or direct autoregression,
and the Full–Plain advantage repeats under an independent RT-1 seed and on CSGO. Together with
Figure 5, these results demonstrate that our proposed Inertial Memory preserves evolving content
more effectively than dense recurrence, generic recurrence, or direct autoregression. Appendix E
gives the complete protocol and paired statistics.
5
CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduce INERTIALWORLD, an Inertial Video World Model that treats prediction
as the evolution of an existing video state rather than its repeated reconstruction. Inertial Memory
realizes this principle through two complementary views: a dense Eulerian grid preserves scene-
aligned evidence, while persistent Lagrangian carriers bind and transport regional content under
action-conditioned motion. The model then predicts the complete next state and uses learned gates to
incorporate changes that transport alone cannot explain. This carry-before-write transition makes
persistence the default while retaining the flexibility required for newly visible content and non-rigid
change. Across paired 32-step evaluations on RT-1, CSGO, and RECON, INERTIALWORLD achieves
the strongest aggregate prediction results and improves diagnostics focused on action-induced motion.
Controlled ablations and mechanism diagnostics further support the roles of structured transport and
state-dependent memory updates. Together, these results identify inertial state evolution as a useful
design principle for interactive video world models. Extending the current deterministic, learned-
coordinate formulation to multimodal futures and explicit three-dimensional scene representations
remains an important direction.

COMPLETE FORMULATION OF INERTIAL MEMORY
This section expands the compact formulation in Section 3.2. We separate the state that implements
the proposed inertial prior from auxiliary predictor context, and then describe a complete causal
transition. Batch dimensions are omitted. Every recurrent variable is a function only of the initial
observation, the action prefix, and previous model predictions.
A.1
FROZEN VIDEO REPRESENTATION
The frozen video tokenizer decomposes a video into a rollout-level Context code C and frame-level
Dynamic codes Dτ. The Context code contains two 32×40 lanes and is computed once from ot. A
Dynamic code contains four 8×10 lanes that represent base and detail components of motion and
appearance. It is a complete discrete representation of a frame, rather than a residual relative to an
observed image. During a rollout, C remains fixed and every future bDτ is predicted by the model.
Let τ index recurrent rollout steps. The recurrent video state is
Sτ = (C, Iτ, Hτ, Qτ),
Iτ = (Gτ, {Rτ,k}K

In Eq. 5, Iτ is Inertial Memory and Rτ,k is the complete recurrent state of carrier k. Its Eulerian
component Gτ is a dense 16×20 grid aligned with scene coordinates. Its Lagrangian component
contains K = 16 persistent regional carriers. Carrier k maintains a descriptor, a canonical feature
map and support, a translation–rotation–scale coordinate, persistent velocity, visibility and existence
estimates, and a bounded local deformation. The soft binding map Bτ assigns Eulerian evidence
between the carriers and a dense background branch. The carriers are learned regions, not supervised
semantic objects, and the background branch avoids imposing a complete object decomposition.
The implementation additionally keeps the four most recent predicted Dynamic states Hτ and 64
generic interaction slots Qτ. They provide predictor context inherited from the video backbone; the
proposed persistent video state and its carry operation are defined by Iτ. This distinction is important:
the method is not equivalent to retaining a longer image history as passive attention context.
A.2
INITIALIZATION AND DUAL-VIEW BINDING
Learned projections of C initialize the dense grid and interaction slots. The first K slots initialize the
carrier descriptors. Carrier coordinates begin on a normalized 4×4 lattice with zero velocity; their
canonical content is sampled from the initialized dense state. This constructs S0 = Init(C) from the
single observed frame, without an object detector or calibrated geometry.
At each recurrent step, a carrier is rendered into scene coordinates using its canonical content,
support, coordinate, rigidity, and deformation. Its binding score at an Eulerian cell combines content
compatibility, rendered support, visibility, and existence. A softmax over the K carriers and the
background branch gives Bτ. The dense view consequently retains globally available scene evidence,
whereas the carrier view gives local content a persistent coordinate system in which it can move.

learned reveal proposal fills newly uncovered locations. This prevents a feature from being duplicated
at both its old and new positions. Zero predicted motion reduces to identity carry. We denote the
complete inherited proposal by eSτ+1.
A.4
COMPLETE PREDICTION AND GATED COMMIT
The predictor reads the fixed Context code, carried dense state, rendered carriers, predicted-state
history, interaction slots, and current action. It predicts all four lanes of bDτ+1. For each lane and
cell, the readout assigns probability to three sources: newly generated content, stationary content
from the previous predicted state, and content transported from a previous predicted location. Invalid
persistence routes are masked. At evaluation time, the hard route is sampled deterministically from
the learned posterior using an immutable key derived from the Context code, recurrent step, lane, and
cell. No route has access to a future target.

B.1
FROZEN VIDEO TOKENIZER
We train a separate tokenizer for each domain while keeping its learnable architecture, objective, and
optimization recipe identical. The copies differ only in their data readers and image preprocessing.
Each tokenizer contains 120.386M parameters, uses 384 hidden and 256 latent channels, and maps
a 256×320 internal canvas to the shared interface in Table 4. Every token is represented by five
finite-scalar-quantization digits with levels (7, 5, 5, 5, 5), giving 4,375 joint states (Mentzer et al.,
2024).
Tokenizer training uses 33-frame clips: one Context frame and 32 future frames. An update supervises
the Context frame and eight deterministically distributed future frames, always including the first and
last targets and covering all four temporal quarters; validation decodes all 32 targets. The objective
combines pixel reconstruction, edge and high-frequency fidelity, two-level wavelet detail, SSIM,
LPIPS-VGG (Zhang et al., 2018), quantization balance, and lane-specialization losses. Motion-region
and late-frame terms are introduced at fixed stages after the initial reconstruction phase. We use no
adversarial loss. This objective is identical across the three domains.

All protocols were materialized as ordered episode lists before metric aggregation. A protocol
records the source episode, anchor, action slice, conditioning frames, and 32 reference targets. Every
method receives the same anchor and the same actions for the 32 scored transitions. A baseline may
additionally use frames and aligned actions strictly before the anchor when required by its released
interface, but no method observes any scored target. Table 7 summarizes the resulting contracts.

C.1
RT-1
We construct a fixed 1,024-episode protocol from RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2022). Episodes used by our
development validation protocol are first excluded. Of the remaining episodes, 1,142 admit both a
16-frame observation prefix and 32 adjacent future targets. We sample 1,024 distinct episodes without
replacement using seed 0 and then sort the selected episode identifiers. The anchor within an episode
is generated deterministically from the episode identifier and constrained to retain the preceding 15
frames and all 32 future frames. The common current observation is ot; the raw 13-D action at+j is
paired with target ot+j+1. Our model observes only ot, whereas a released baseline may use frames
from the reserved prefix according to its native interface.
This protocol is disjoint from the data used to train and validate INERTIALWORLD. The released
baselines were trained with different RT-1 partitions, so it cannot simultaneously be a held-out
split for every public checkpoint. In particular, 1,015 of the 1,024 episodes occur in the released
RLVR-World training subset. We therefore use this block as a paired released-checkpoint comparison
under a common rollout contract, not as a claim that every baseline is evaluated on its own held-out
distribution.
C.2
CSGO
We follow the 5,500-episode CSGO corpus and the 500-episode held-out partition used by
Vid2World (Huang et al., 2025). The first 128 held-out episodes are reserved for validation and the
remaining 372 define the test pool. For each test episode, a deterministic per-episode key selects an
anchor uniformly from indices 32 through 967. Thus, every episode retains at least 32 earlier frames
for native baseline context and exactly 32 future targets. All models use the same anchor and raw
51-D action interval.
The CSGO block reported in Table 1 is a fixed 70-episode slice of this 372-episode protocol: zero-
based canonical request indices 0, 5, . . . , 345. We state this rule explicitly so that the subset can be
reconstructed exactly.
The source frames are 150×280 BGR images. We convert them to RGB and apply one antialiased
bilinear resize to 256×320. The same spatial transform is applied independently to every frame and
does not alter the temporal pairing between at and ot+1.
C.3
RECON
We use the official Vid2World RECON split over 11,835 trajectories (Shah et al., 2021; Huang

deterministic key selects one valid anchor per episode. Every method receives the same anchor and
sequence of normalized local x–y displacement and relative-yaw actions.
RECON images are decoded at 480×640, resized with antialiased bilinear interpolation to 240×320,
and reflection-padded by eight pixels above and below to form the 256×320 internal training canvas.
The padding is removed before serialization and scoring, so all reported RECON metrics use the
undistorted 240×320 field of view. Predictions from baselines with a different native image size are
transformed once to this common scoring resolution.

D.4
PREDICTION BEYOND THE TRAINING HORIZON
Long-horizon protocol and findings. To evaluate INERTIALWORLD at temporal scales beyond
those used for training, we extend the RT-1 rollout from the maximum training horizon of AR32
to an uninterrupted 64-frame prediction in Table 10. Constructing this H64 benchmark without
padding requires 64 aligned future RGB targets and the corresponding action sequence after the
common conditioning anchor. Exactly 35 of the 1,024 episodes in the frozen test protocol satisfy
this requirement, and we evaluate all 35 rather than sampling a subset. Eligibility is fixed solely by
sequence length before inference and is independent of any model output or metric. All five methods
are therefore evaluated on exactly the same ordered episodes, common anchors, action sequences,
and future targets. Each method retains its native conditioning interface and continues a single rollout
without being restarted at step 32; every predicted frame in ot+1:t+64 is scored using the main-table
metrics and the same episode-first aggregation. For INERTIALWORLD, the recurrent state, action
sequence, and logical rollout step continue through all 64 predictions, while steps 33–64 reuse the
zero-based terminal trained time-conditioning index (t=31) rather than querying embedding rows
outside the AR32 training horizon. INERTIALWORLD ranks first on all four metrics. Relative to the
strongest baseline in each column, it improves PSNR by 2.36 dB and SSIM by 0.060, while reducing
LPIPS by 0.0243 and MSE by 0.00534. Thus, its aggregate prediction-quality advantage persists at
twice the maximum training horizon. This evaluation establishes long-horizon predictive quality;
direct carrier correspondence, reappearance, transport-route, and write-gate analyses are provided
over all 35 episodes in Table 11 and visualized for an occlusion–reappearance example in Figure 5.

ABLATION PROTOCOL AND PAIRED ANALYSIS
Controlled training protocol. All variants in Table 3 are independently trained from scratch rather
than obtained by disabling pathways in a trained checkpoint. The four RT-1 seed-0 models are all
0.8B-scale and use the same tokenizer, training episodes, action representation, AdamW schedule,
BF16 precision, full BPTT without teacher forcing, AR8→AR16→AR32 curriculum, global batch of
32 on the same 4×8-GPU topology, 30k-update budget, and ordered 1,024-episode H32 evaluation
protocol. Their active parameter counts are 824.450M (Plain AR), 819.397M (Recurrent Memory),
808.397M (Dense-only), and 818.746M (Full). Dense-only removes the carrier-specific machinery
without introducing an auxiliary capacity-matching module, thereby preserving a clean structural
ablation rather than adding a new architectural component. The independent-seed RT-1 pair and the
CSGO pair retain the matched recipe within their respective settings.
State and objective matching. Full and Dense-only share the 320-token dense Eulerian scene, 64
generic interaction slots, and four-frame hard-prediction history. Dense-only retains the applicable
innovate/stationary route, slot-diversity, and dense-flow terms, but structurally removes carriers,
canonical content, source pointers, transported routing, and all losses defined on them. Recurrent
Memory instead uses the latest hard prediction and an 80×1280 action-conditioned GRU state,
while Plain AR retains only the latest hard prediction; neither has a scene, slot, route, or carrier
variable. All four retain the same applicable joint-code, generation-code, digit, ordered-value, and
frozen-decoder targets, with identical coefficients and schedules. Terms whose state variable does not
exist are omitted rather than replaced by a variant-specific auxiliary objective. Thus the histories are
intentionally architecture-specific, while the data, prediction targets, optimization, sample/update
budget, and 0.8B model scale remain controlled. Table 12 summarizes these state interventions, and
Table 5 gives the exact Full and Plain-AR loss correspondence.
Track and reappearance selection. Track and reappearance selection is fixed by ground-truth DINO
ViT-B/16 features; predictions never determine which locations are scored. A Context patch is eligible
when its cosine separation from the closest other Context patch is at least 0.02. At a later frame,
a reliable correspondence must be a mutual nearest neighbor with cosine at least 0.60, separation
margin at least 0.02, and at least eight reliable observations accumulated along that track. For each
scored destination patch, let bz and z⋆be the ℓ2-normalized DINO features from the predicted frame
and its corresponding ground-truth frame. The content error is
