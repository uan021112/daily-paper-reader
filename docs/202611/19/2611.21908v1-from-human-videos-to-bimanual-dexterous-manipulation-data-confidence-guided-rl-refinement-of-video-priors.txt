Abstract— Collecting demonstrations for bimanual dexterous
manipulation is costly, motivating the use of human videos as
a source of robot training data. However, uncertainty in video-
derived motion estimates and differences between human and
robot embodiments make direct motion transfer challenging. In
this work, we introduce PriDex, a framework that transforms
human-video Priors into executable robot Dexterous manipula-
tion demonstrations through reinforcement learning in simula-
tion. PriDex preserves task-relevant interaction structure while
allowing embodiment-specific behavior to be regenerated for
the robot. Human–object interactions provide affordance cues
for selecting task-relevant, robot-specific grasps from geometry-
based candidates. For motion refinement, object trajectories
provide a task-level reference, while relative bimanual wrist
trajectories offer complementary guidance for coordinated
motion. Since object-motion estimates can become unreliable
under occlusion or visual ambiguity, PriDex uses object-motion
confidence to adaptively balance these two priors, emphasizing
object-level guidance when confidence is high and increasing
the contribution of wrist-level guidance as confidence decreases.
Reinforcement learning refines these video-derived priors under
the robot’s kinematic and physical constraints, and the resulting
policies are rolled out to generate robot demonstrations for
training a vision-based diffusion policy. We evaluate PriDex on
four bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks, including pouring,
sweeping into a dustpan, bottle-cap unscrewing, and plate
cleaning, assessing both simulation-based robot data generation
and downstream visuomotor policy learning.

I. INTRODUCTION
Learning bimanual dexterous manipulation requires large
amounts of high-quality robot interaction data. Collecting
such data directly on robots is particularly demanding for
high-dimensional dexterous systems, where demonstrations
must coordinate two arms, multiple fingers, and contact-
rich object interactions over extended horizons. Human ma-
nipulation videos provide an appealing alternative: they are
abundant, naturally capture coordinated bimanual behavior,
and contain rich cues about where and how objects are
manipulated [1], [2]. However, these videos do not directly
provide supervision that can be readily transferred to robots.
Human and robot hands differ substantially in morphology
and kinematics, while object and hand motions recovered
from video can be inaccurate due to occlusion, partial
observability, and reconstruction uncertainty.
To extract more actionable supervision from human
videos, recent approaches reconstruct 3D hand and object
motions and use the resulting trajectories for retargeting,
reinforcement learning, or simulation-based skill acquisi-
tion [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. However, reconstructed motions
remain only approximate proxies for robot behavior. Their
quality can vary due to partial observability and recon-
struction uncertainty, while even accurately recovered human
motions may not transfer directly to a robot because of differ-
ences in kinematics and collision constraints. Consequently,

video-derived motion should not be treated as uniformly
reliable or directly executable robot supervision. The key
challenge is therefore not simply how faithfully to reproduce
reconstructed human motion, but which cues from the video
should guide robot learning and when.
Our key insight is to separate transferable interaction
structure from embodiment-specific motion when transfer-
ring human demonstrations to robots. Human videos provide
useful cues about where interaction occurs and how the
manipulated object evolves, whereas exact human grasp
configurations and hand motions are strongly embodiment-
dependent and therefore need not be reproduced by the target
robot. We therefore preserve interaction cues that transfer
more naturally across embodiments, while allowing grasping
and motion details to be regenerated and refined for the robot.
This reframes human-video transfer from reproducing human
motion to preserving the interaction structure most relevant
to successful execution.

through reinforcement learning in simulation. PriDex first
infers affordance regions from human-object interactions
and uses them to sample task-relevant robot grasps from
geometry-based candidates [9], [10], avoiding direct retar-
geting of human grasp configurations that may be sensitive
to pose-estimation uncertainty and embodiment differences.
For motion learning, object trajectories provide the primary
reference for the desired evolution of the manipulated object.
Since the reliability of object-motion estimates can vary over
time, PriDex complements this object-level reference with
wrist-motion cues that capture how each hand moves from
the onset of interaction. These wrist trajectories provide a
coarser motion cue that is less dependent on detailed hand
morphology. PriDex then uses object-motion confidence to
adaptively balance the two guidance signals, emphasizing
object-level supervision when confidence is high and placing
greater weight on wrist-level guidance as confidence de-
creases. Reinforcement learning refines these video-derived
priors under the robot’s kinematic and physical constraints,
producing coordinated behaviors without requiring exact
reproduction of the demonstrated human motion. Finally,
the learned policies are rolled out in simulation to generate
bimanual dexterous robot demonstrations, which are subse-
quently distilled into a vision-based diffusion policy [11] for
downstream visuomotor control.
We evaluate PriDex on four bimanual dexterous ma-
nipulation tasks from the EgoDex dataset [1], including
sweeping, pouring, bottle-cap unscrewing, and plate clean-
ing. Starting from only three human videos per task, PriDex
performs reinforcement learning in Isaac Sim [12] to expand
these demonstrations into hundreds of robot trajectories for
downstream policy learning. We evaluate the resulting data-
generation pipeline through simulation RL and downstream
visuomotor policy learning, examining both skill acquisition
and the utility of the generated demonstrations.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We introduce a human-video-to-robot framework that
converts video-derived priors into executable bimanual
dexterous robot demonstrations through reinforcement
learning in simulation, allowing robot behavior to be
refined beyond direct human-motion retargeting.
• We develop a structured prior refinement strategy that
preserves transferable interaction cues while adapting
embodiment-specific behavior, combining affordance-
aware grasp selection with confidence-guided motion
refinement.
• We evaluate PriDex on four bimanual dexterous tasks
from EgoDex, generating thousands of robot trajectories
from only 12 human manipulation videos for simulation
and downstream visuomotor policy learning.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Learning Robot Manipulation from Human Videos
Human videos provide a scalable source of manipulation
supervision without requiring extensive robot demonstra-
tions. Early approaches mainly use them as high-level or
embodiment-agnostic guidance. MimicPlay [13] learns latent
plans from human play to guide low-level policies trained
with robot demonstrations, while Im2Flow2Act [14] uses 2D
object flow as a cross-embodiment interface between human
videos and simulated robot experience. More recent methods
recover explicit 3D motion to provide denser supervision.
X-Sim [5] uses object trajectories extracted from RGB-D
demonstrations to guide gripper-based manipulation, whereas
DexMV [15], ViViDex [3], and DemoBot [16] rely on
retargeting reconstructed human hand motion to dexterous
robot hands. Recent work has begun to reduce reliance
on full hand-motion imitation. Human2Sim2Robot [4] uses
human hand poses primarily to initialize manipulation and
object trajectories as task-level guidance, while DexImit [17]
synthesizes feasible robot motion from reconstructed human
demonstrations rather than directly replaying human hand
trajectories. These methods progressively move beyond di-
rect human-motion retargeting toward more selective use of
video-derived supervision. PriDex instead transfers video-
derived cues according to their cross-embodiment trans-
ferability and dynamically balances complementary motion
priors during RL-based exploration and refinement.
B. Dexterous Manipulation
Dexterous manipulation remains challenging due to its
high-dimensional action space and contact-rich dynamics,
making demonstration collection considerably more demand-
ing than for parallel-gripper manipulation. Existing systems
acquire dexterous supervision through instrumented human-
motion capture systems [18], direct robot teleoperation [19],
or human-to-robot motion retargeting [15]. The challenge
becomes more pronounced for bimanual dexterous manip-
ulation, which requires coordinated control of two arms and
multi-fingered hands. To improve data scalability, DexMim-
icGen [20] expands a small set of source teleoperated demon-
strations into large bimanual datasets through automated sim-
ulation generation. ManipTrans [21] pre-trains a dexterous

A. Problem Formulation and Overview

than treating reconstructed human motion as directly exe-
cutable supervision, PriDex recovers structured interaction

As illustrated in Fig. 2, PriDex first extracts object and bi-

cues guide the selection of task-relevant robot grasps from
geometry-based candidates, avoiding direct transfer of hu-
man grasp configurations (Sec. III-C). These grasps initialize
confidence-guided RL, with object trajectories providing
task-level references and relative wrist motion offering com-
plementary coordination guidance (Sec. III-D). Confidence
modulates these priors while RL adapts motion to robot
kinematics and contact dynamics. Finally, successful simu-
lation rollouts train a vision-based Diffusion Policy [11] for
downstream visuomotor control (Sec. III-E).

C. Affordance-Guided Robot Grasp Initialization
Human videos provide useful evidence about where an
object should be contacted, but the corresponding human
hand configuration need not be suitable for the robot em-
bodiment. We therefore preserve this contact structure while
regenerating the grasp for the robot. From the aligned hand–
object reconstruction, we extract contact intervals and aggre-
gate surface contact evidence in the object frame, yielding
a task-conditioned affordance region A that captures the
demonstrated interaction area.
We use A to guide robot-specific grasp synthesis with
Dexonomy [9]. Rather than transferring the demonstrated
finger configuration, Dexonomy proposes geometry-based
robot grasp candidates, which we rank according to how
well their intended contacts align with the video-derived
affordance region. In this way, the grasp preserves the
task-relevant interaction location while allowing the detailed
finger configuration to adapt to the robot hand. We further
refine and filter the candidates in simulation to remove
configurations that violate kinematic constraints, introduce
invalid collisions, or fail to establish stable contact.
Finally, a suitable hand–object grasp must also be exe-
cutable by the full robot in the demonstrated scene. We
therefore instantiate each retained candidate at the initial
object configuration and solve for a reachable wrist pose,
with cuRobo [29] used to obtain the corresponding arm
configuration and approach motion. After the grasp closure,
a brief hold test removes candidates that cannot maintain
the intended contact. The validated grasp then defines the
robot–object initialization for RL, allowing the subsequent
stage to focus on refining the manipulation motion rather
than discovering a viable grasp from scratch.
D. Confidence-Guided RL Refinement
The grasp initialization in Sec. III-C establishes a robot-
feasible contact configuration, but does not yet provide an
executable manipulation trajectory. The reconstructed object
motion remains only an approximate task-level prior: em-
bodiment differences and contact dynamics may make exact
tracking infeasible, while its reliability can vary over time.
We therefore refine this prior with reinforcement learning
in simulation. We first convert the object trajectory into
a robot-specific motion reference, and then learn residual
corrections whose flexibility is modulated by object-motion
confidence. When confidence decreases, object tracking is
relaxed and relative bimanual wrist motion provides com-
plementary coordination guidance, allowing the robot to
preserve task-relevant motion while adapting to its own
physical constraints.

RL Objective and Training. The confidence-conditioned
object and wrist objectives above provide the primary guid-
ance for refining the video-derived motion. We additionally
provide a terminal task-success reward upon successful task
completion, together with grasp-stability and safety penalties
that discourage grasp loss and joint-limit violations; for fair
comparison, the same auxiliary rewards are applied to all RL
baselines in our experiments.
We train PPO policies in simulation, each operating at
20 Hz. The policy receives state-based observations com-
prising robot proprioception, the current object pose and its
associated confidence, and a short-horizon segment of the
future reference trajectory. Since the RL policy is used only
to generate robot demonstrations, it can leverage privileged
simulator information that is not required by the final visuo-
motor policy at deployment.
E. Policy Distillation
We distill the privileged RL teachers into a vision-based
Diffusion Policy [11] through supervised imitation. Deter-
ministic teacher rollouts are collected under randomized
initial conditions, and only successful trajectories are re-
tained to form Dsim. A student observation ot consists of
multi-view images together with the proprioceptive state qt,
comprising all joint positions and both wrist poses; privi-
leged simulator states and reference-trajectory information
are excluded. Each action at ∈R58 specifies absolute joint
position targets for the full 58-DoF bimanual system—two
7-DoF arms and two 22-DoF hands—commanded at 20 Hz.
Whereas the teacher emits bounded residuals about a nominal
reference, we record the composed targets actually sent to
the controller; the student therefore predicts joint commands
directly and requires no reference trajectory at inference.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
We conduct simulation experiments in Isaac Sim 5.1
using a Dexmate bimanual robot equipped with two Sharpa
dexterous hands. We evaluate four tasks shown in Fig. 3:
pouring (Pour), sweeping into a dustpan (Sweep), bottle-cap
unscrewing (Unscrew), and plate cleaning (Clean). Each task
uses three source videos, and each evaluated RL configura-
tion receives a training budget of 20 million environment
steps per source video.
For both RL and Diffusion Policy evaluation, we perturb
task-relevant initial positions in the tabletop plane, sampling
each coordinate offset from [−a,a]. We set a = 3 cm for
Pour, a = 1 cm for Clean, and a = 5 mm for Sweep and Un-
screw. In Pour, Unscrew, and Clean, the manipulated objects
undergo a common translation that preserves their relative
configuration. In Sweep, we perturb the debris position.
For policy distillation, we collect separate datasets of
successful rollouts from full PriDex and its variant without
human-motion guidance, using the same position perturba-
tions. Each dataset contains 200 demonstrations per source
video, totaling 600 per task. We train one task-specific Diffu-
sion Policy [11] per data source with matched demonstration

B. Performance and Analysis of PriDex
1) Baselines:
We compare PriDex with task-adapted
implementations of Human2Sim2Robot (H2S2R) [4] and
ViViDex [3]. H2S2R uses reconstructed object trajectories
as dense tracking objectives and initializes exploration from
a retargeted pre-manipulation pose. We adapt its single-
arm policy interface by duplicating and concatenating the
observation and action spaces for bimanual control. Unlike
PriDex, H2S2R relies on a fixed object-tracking objective
rather than confidence-conditioned object- and wrist-motion
guidance.
ViViDex trains state-based PPO teachers using recon-
structed human hand–object trajectories. We extend its
single-hand control and trajectory targets to paired left-
and right-hand channels for bimanual execution. Unlike
PriDex, ViViDex directly treats the reconstructed trajectories
as policy guidance without confidence-based adaptation or
affordance-guided robot grasp generation.
All methods use the same RL interaction budget.
2) Ablations: We design two cumulative ablations to
examine the roles of confidence in RL refinement: auxiliary
motion guidance and adaptive exploration. Object trajecto-
ries describe the intended task behavior, whereas human
hand trajectories provide one possible means of achiev-
ing it. Relative wrist motion therefore serves as auxiliary
guidance when object-motion references are unreliable. At
low confidence, our method strengthens wrist guidance and
expands exploration through two independent mechanisms:
increasing per-step and cumulative residual-action bounds,
and relaxing positional and rotational tolerances in reference-
tracking penalties.

4) Performance Analysis: Table I shows that PriDex out-
performs the adapted H2S2R and ViViDex baselines on all
four tasks. The ablations further indicate task-dependent ben-
efits from human-motion guidance and confidence adapta-
tion, reflecting different trade-offs between action exploration
and contact stability.
Human-motion guidance complements object references
with additional cues about action direction. Object trajec-
tories describe the desired manipulation outcome and its
evolution, but the policy must still discover robot actions that
realize these motions. Pouring requires a large tilting motion
while maintaining a grasp. Bottle-cap unscrewing requires
one hand to stabilize the bottle while the other rotates the
cap relative to it. Our auxiliary reward uses arm-motion
directions induced by wrist references to guide exploration
of these coordinated actions, without requiring exact repro-
duction of full human-hand poses. The success-rate drops
on Pour and Unscrew after removing HP are consistent with
directional guidance facilitating the exploration of effective
coordinated actions.
In contrast, Clean and Sweep achieve higher success rates
without HP. Rollout inspection provides a task-specific expla-
nation for Clean. The source demonstration includes wiping
along the plate rim, and wrist-motion guidance encourages
PriDex to retain this behavior. Such motions can place greater
demands on contact stability and grasp maintenance. Without
HP, the policy tends to reduce rim-wiping motions and adopts
a simpler strategy that is less prone to failure while still
satisfying the cleaning success criterion. These observations
suggest that the improvement after removing HP partly re-
flects greater freedom to simplify the demonstrated behavior
under the current evaluation criterion.

For Sweep, the improvement without HP may reflect
greater flexibility to adapt motion to the current contact

directional guidance can facilitate coordinated exploration,

Confidence adaptation adjusts object-tracking tolerances
and arm-residual bounds according to reference confidence.
High-confidence segments constrain tracking more tightly,
while low-confidence segments allow larger motion correc-
tions. With wrist guidance disabled, removing this adaptation
reduces mean success from 55.5% to 43.0%. Success de-
creases on Pour, Sweep, and Unscrew, while Clean remains
nearly unchanged. This pattern supports adapting tracking
constraints and correction freedom to reference confidence
during motion refinement.
C. From RL Rollouts to Visuomotor Policies
We further evaluate whether PriDex-generated rollouts
provide useful supervision for visuomotor policy learning.
For each task, we train Diffusion Policies using successful
rollouts from either full PriDex or the w/o HP variant. Both
datasets contain 200 demonstrations per source video (600
per task), and all other training settings are matched. Each
policy is evaluated over 150 closed-loop trials per task.

improve

the mean success rate from 24.2% to 31.3%. On Unscrew,
success increases from 0.7% to 5.3%, indicating that this
task remains challenging for visual-policy distillation under
the current training budget.
D. Real-Robot Deployment Experiments
We demonstrate real-world deployment on water pouring,
sweeping into a dustpan, and plate cleaning using a Dexmate
bimanual robot equipped with two Sharpa dexterous hands.
Each policy receives RGB images from one head-mounted
and two wrist-mounted cameras, together with proprioceptive
observations comprising joint angles and wrist poses, and
outputs absolute joint-position targets.
Training Protocol. We compare two training conditions
for each task. Real-only trains a task-specific Diffusion
Policy [11] using 20 successful real-world demonstrations
collected through teleoperation. Sim+Real first pretrains the
policy on 120 successful PriDex simulation demonstrations
per task and then fine-tunes it on exactly the same 20 real
demonstrations. Fine-tuning uses only real-world data.
During deployment, the policy conditions on the two most
recent observation steps and uses a prediction horizon of 16


steps. It executes eight control steps before replanning from
updated observations.
Evaluation Protocol. Both training conditions are evalu-
ated using the same objects and initial placements, without
randomized perturbations. Pour succeeds when the bottle
opening is aligned with the cup opening while the bottle is
tilted beyond 90◦. Sweep succeeds when the parts are swept
into the dustpan. Clean succeeds when the cloth wipes back
and forth over the plate four times. Returning the objects
to their initial positions is not required. Real-robot trials
were monitored by an operator without a fixed timeout. The
operator terminated each trial upon task success, an object
drop, or when the motion was judged unsafe or had deviated
substantially such that successful completion was considered
infeasible. Trials terminated before meeting the task-success
criterion were recorded as failures.
Real-Robot Results. We evaluated each training condition
over 10 trials per task. As shown in Table III, simulation
pretraining increased mean success from 30.0% to 63.3%
using the same real demonstrations, with improvements on
all three tasks. These results show that PriDex provides
useful pretraining data for bimanual dexterous manipulation
with limited real demonstrations. Representative executions
are shown in the supplementary video.

V. CONCLUSION
We presented PriDex, a framework that converts human-
video priors into bimanual dexterous robot demonstra-
tions through affordance-guided grasp initialization and
confidence-guided RL refinement. PriDex achieves a 57.2%
mean simulation success rate and generates demonstrations
that improve distilled visuomotor-policy performance on all
four tasks under matched training budgets. The ablations
highlight the task-dependent role of wrist-motion guidance
and the value of adapting motion refinement to reconstruc-
tion confidence. Real-robot experiments further show that
simulation pretraining improves policy success with the same
limited real demonstrations. Future work will improve visual-
policy distillation for challenging contact-rich tasks.
