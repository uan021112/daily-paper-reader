Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) jointly generate future video
and robot actions through iterative denoising. Few-step dis-
tillation reduces the number of model evaluations, but its
coarse updates can move the student away from the teacher’s
trajectory. We propose Anchored Path Matching (APM),
which supervises endpoint and short-transition predictions
from states reached by the student. From each state, we
continue the teacher to zero noise and use its endpoint and
first intermediate state as targets. Modality-routed adapters
learn to match both targets from the same starting state.
When the two gradients conflict, anchor-priority updates
preserve the endpoint gradient and remove the opposing
component of the local gradient within each modality. One
checkpoint supports 1-, 2-, and 4-step joint generation. The
sampling budget can be chosen at deployment without re-
training or teacher queries. On RoboTwin 2.0, we train
only on Clean demonstrations and evaluate directly on the
domain-randomized Random setting. At one step, APM im-
proves closed-loop success over continued base distillation
at matched inference budgets by 2.0 percentage points on
Clean and 1.4 on Random. Video peak signal-to-noise ra-
tio (PSNR) rises from 30.169 to 32.317 dB, with gains in
structural similarity (SSIM) and learned perceptual image
patch similarity (LPIPS). On two real-robot tasks, APM im-
proves success over the one-step teacher and reduces control-
cycle latency by 21.3× relative to the eight-step teacher.

1. Introduction
World Action Models (WAMs) [17, 36, 46] jointly predict
what a robot will see and do. Given the current observa-
tion, a language instruction, and the robot state, they extend
vision-language-action policies [15, 47] by generating future
video and actions. However, iterative generation requires
repeated model evaluations, leading to substantial inference
costs. Few-step distillation addresses this limitation by train-
ing a student to approximate a multi-step teacher with fewer
sampling steps [1, 11, 28, 30]. Supporting multiple sampling
budgets with a single student is particularly desirable, as it

allows the trade-off between inference efficiency and predic-
tion quality to be adjusted at deployment without retraining.
Recent efforts improve few-step WAM distillation
through teacher-guided transitions or endpoint supervision.
AnyStep-WAM restarts the teacher from the first recycled
preview state to supervise subsequent sampling-budget in-
tervals, while its separate endpoint loss is computed from
states along the teacher trajectory [32]. RealtimeWAM in-
corporates teacher endpoint targets into action-consistency
training under frozen video conditioning [25]. These ap-
proaches provide complementary forms of supervision, but
their effectiveness across different sampling budgets remains
an important challenge.
Specifically, coarse student updates can drive generation
away from the teacher trajectory, causing the student to
encounter states that the teacher would not normally visit.
Consequently, local-transition supervision and endpoint su-
pervision may be applied to different state distributions: a
teacher transition provides guidance on local prediction dy-
namics, whereas an endpoint target constrains the completed
video and actions. Supervision based on teacher-trajectory
states may therefore be misaligned with the states encoun-
tered by the student, while local-transition guidance alone
does not directly constrain the final output. Pairing these
targets at the same student-reached state could address both
limitations, but introduces an additional optimization chal-
lenge: their gradients may conflict when updating shared
parameters, such that improving local transition matching
can undermine endpoint predictions. Conventional weighted-
loss objectives do not explicitly resolve this conflict, moti-
vating a supervision framework that jointly aligns the two
targets and prioritizes endpoint consistency.
To address these challenges, we propose Anchored Path
Matching (APM), which pairs endpoint and local-transition
supervision at each student-reached state (Figure 1). A sin-
gle teacher continuation from this state provides both targets:
its endpoint anchors the completed video and actions, while
its first intermediate state guides the local transition. Both
student queries thus share the same starting state, without
requiring an additional teacher rollout. To coordinate their
optimization, we introduce anchor-priority correction, which
preserves the endpoint gradient while removing conflict-
ing components of the local-transition gradient within each
modality. We adopt a two-stage training strategy, first distill-
ing LingBot-VA [17] using AnyFlow [11], and then freez-
ing the resulting backbone while training modality-routed
adapters with APM and retaining the base distillation objec-
tive. The resulting single checkpoint supports joint video–
action generation with 1, 2, or 4 sampling steps, without
teacher access at inference.
On RoboTwin 2.0 [6], APM achieves average success
rates of 80.9% and 91.4% at 1 and 4 steps, respectively,
outperforming continued base distillation by 1.7 and 1.6 per-
centage points under matched trainable parameter counts
and training budgets (Table 1). At one step, video PSNR
improves from 30.169 to 32.317 dB, with consistent gains in
SSIM and LPIPS. We further evaluate the effects of anchor
selection, local-transition supervision, and gradient coordi-
nation through ablations, including fixed-state prediction
evaluations. On two real-robot tasks, single-step APM also
outperforms the step-matched undistilled teacher, reducing
control-cycle latency from 4323.6 to 203.1 ms relative to the
full teacher, a 21.3× speedup.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose APM, which pairs endpoint and intermediate
teacher targets from a single continuation to supervise
video–action predictions at the same student-reached state
(Section 3.2).
• We introduce anchor-priority correction, which priori-
tizes endpoint supervision by removing conflicting local-
transition gradient components while preserving compati-
ble guidance within each modality (Section 3.3).
• We demonstrate that a single APM checkpoint improves
closed-loop success over continued base distillation at 1,
2, and 4 steps on RoboTwin 2.0, and outperforms the step-
matched undistilled teacher on real-robot tasks (Section 4).
2. Related Work
Efficient World Action Models.
World Action Models
(WAMs) [17, 36, 46] jointly generate video and actions, ex-
tending vision-language-action policies [3, 8, 15, 47] with
visual prediction capabilities at additional inference cost.
Existing approaches improve efficiency by removing video
generation at deployment [40], leveraging image-editing fea-
tures [42] or latent subgoals [5], and adopting lightweight
backbones [16, 19]. Alternatively, few-step distillation re-
duces the number of denoising updates. Flash-WAM [1]
adapts consistency learning to different video and action
noise regimes, while AnyStep-WAM [32] learns budget-
aligned transitions and preview recovery for flexible infer-
ence. RealtimeWAM [25] incorporates teacher endpoint
targets into action-consistency training. Unlike these ap-
proaches, APM pairs endpoint and finite-interval transition
targets from a single teacher continuation at each student-
reached state, with explicit gradient coordination.
Distillation and State Correction.
Diffusion distilla-
tion accelerates sampling through progressive compres-
sion [2, 28], trajectory rectification [22, 23, 34], consistency
learning [14, 24, 30], distribution matching [37, 38, 44],
and adversarial training [29]. Flow-map methods [4, 27]
learn finite-interval transitions; MeanFlow [10] parameter-
izes them through average velocity, while AnyFlow [11]
enables arbitrary-step video distillation. Shortcut Models [9]
further learn step-conditioned predictions without requiring

Multi-Objective Optimization.
Endpoint and local losses
can produce conflicting gradients. Loss weighting changes
the relative strength of objectives [7, 13]; gradient meth-
ods modify their joint update, with PCGrad [39] removing
conflicting components and CAGrad [20] balancing average
descent with local task improvement. APM prioritizes the
endpoint because it supervises the final video and actions.
A one-sided projection preserves its gradient and removes
the opposing component of the local gradient within each
modality.
3. Method
APM first distills LingBot-VA into a finite-interval video–
action transition map, then corrects predictions at student-
reached states (Figure 2). With the base frozen, modality-
routed adapters learn endpoint and local-transition targets
from one teacher continuation. Anchor-priority correction
coordinates their gradients alongside the base objective. One
checkpoint supports 1-, 2-, and 4-step generation without
teacher access.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Tasks and Data.
We evaluate language-conditioned ma-
nipulation tasks on RoboTwin 2.0 [6], covering object inser-
tion, placement, and assembly. Closed-loop performance is
measured by task success rate under two test configurations:
Clean and Random, where the latter applies domain random-
ization. All models are trained using Clean data and eval-
uated directly under Random conditions, without domain-
randomized training data or test-time adaptation. For locally
evaluated methods, we use identical tasks, evaluation seeds,
episode counts, and inference budgets within each setting.
Specifically, we evaluate 50 tasks with 50 episodes per task
in each condition, resulting in 2,500 episodes per condition.
Each episode uses a fixed environment initialization seed,
giving 50 evaluation seeds per task and condition.
Training.
Stage I is trained for 4,000 optimization steps us-
ing AnyFlow-based flow-map distillation [11], with KT = 8
teacher steps and Ktrain ∈{2, 4} student steps. We evaluate
the Stage II checkpoint after 5,000 additional optimization
steps, giving 9,000 steps in total. In Stage II, we freeze the
Stage I backbone and introduce modality-routed adapters,
with an FFN rank of 64 and a temporal rank of 128. The
batch size is 32.
Correction schedule.
At each optimization step, we uni-
formly sample a correction budget from Ktrain ∈{1, 2, 4}.
The one-step branch uses the initial state, while the two-step
branch uses its sole intermediate state. For the four-step
branch, we draw b ∼Beta(5, 2) and select the state index i
and grid time r as
i = min(1 + ⌊3b⌋, 3),
r = 1 −i/4.
This rule favors later states with lower noise.
Starting from the selected state, the teacher follows the
remaining steps of its eight-step Euler grid. We use the
resulting endpoint as the Restart anchor and cache the first
teacher transition as the local target. Both correction losses
use weights of 1.0 for video and 0.1 for action.
Inference.
We evaluate matched grids Keval ∈{1, 2, 4}
(denoted 1/1, 2/2, 4/4). Each student variant uses one
checkpoint for all three budgets, matching the Stage II cor-
rection settings. The LingBot-VA teacher and APM use the
official LingBot-VA classifier-free guidance (CFG) parame-
ters [17]. The model-specific video evaluation settings are
given in Section 4.4.
Baselines.
We compare APM against LingBot-VA [17]
evaluated with 1, 2, and 4 sampling steps to assess per-
formance at matched inference budgets. We also include
Flash-WAM [1]. Its single-step result is reported by Direct-
WAM [43], while its two- and four-step results are obtained
from our local evaluations. Shared denotes the Stage I stu-
dent before correction. Continued starts from the same
Stage I checkpoint and continues training with the base dis-
tillation objective only, using the same trainable parameter
count, optimization settings, and total training steps as APM.

4.5. Real-Robot Deployment
Robot platform. We conduct physical experiments using
the Astribot S1, equipped with an onboard camera, two 7-
DoF arms, and dual grippers (Figure 6). Inference runs on
an NVIDIA A800 GPU with 80 GB of memory. Without
task-specific retraining, we compare the full teacher, the
undistilled teacher evaluated on a coarse 1/1 sampling grid,
and APM at 1/1. The evaluation covers two tasks, PEN-
HOLDER and FOLDTOWEL, with 24 trials per method for
each task. Initial states are matched across methods.
Quantitative results. Table 3 reports task success rates
and control-cycle latency. Compared with the coarse-grid
teacher, APM substantially improves success on both tasks,
from 29.2% to 62.5% on PENHOLDER and from 25.0% to
75.0% on FOLDTOWEL, with an additional 15.6 ms of la-
tency. Compared with the full teacher, APM reduces control-
cycle latency from 4323.6 to 203.1 ms, achieving a 21.3×
speedup. Its success rate is lower on PENHOLDER (62.5%
vs. 83.3%) but higher on FOLDTOWEL (75.0% vs. 70.8%).
These results demonstrate that APM recovers substantial
8


task performance under a one-step inference budget while
retaining the latency advantage of coarse generation.
Qualitative observations. Figure 6 illustrates represen-
tative stages of physical task execution. Observed failure
modes include grasp misalignment, cloth slippage, and in-
sertion errors caused by pose drift. The coarse-grid teacher
tends to fail during reaching and initial grasping, whereas
APM failures occur more frequently in later manipulation
stages.
5. Conclusion and Discussion
In this work, we introduced Anchored Path Matching
(APM) for few-step World Action Model distillation. By
pairing endpoint and local-transition supervision at the same
student-reached state and prioritizing endpoint gradients,
APM addresses both trajectory mismatch and conflicting
supervision. A single checkpoint supports multiple sampling
budgets without retraining or teacher access at inference. Ex-
periments on RoboTwin 2.0 and physical robots demonstrate
improved closed-loop manipulation and video prediction
quality, together with substantially reduced inference latency
while maintaining competitive task performance.
The current real-robot evaluation is limited to two ma-
nipulation tasks on a single platform.
Both endpoint
and local-transition targets are derived from the same
teacher continuation, avoiding an additional teacher roll-
out for local supervision.
Future work could adapt the
correction according to teacher reliability and investi-
gate longer prediction horizons and additional robot plat-
forms.
