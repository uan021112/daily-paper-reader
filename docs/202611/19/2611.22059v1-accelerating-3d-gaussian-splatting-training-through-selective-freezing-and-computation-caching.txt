Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) becomes a key primitive for immer-
sive 3D experiences. However, its applicability is limited by its long
training overhead. Although prior work has shown that gradient
accumulation is the dominant bottleneck in training, existing works
either have limited speedup or degrade reconstruction quality.
To address this issue, we present ElsaGS, an algorithm–hardware
co-designed framework that accelerates 3DGS training with neg-
ligible accuracy degradation. Our key observation is that most
Gaussians converge early, while later iterations only refine a small
subset of Gaussians. Thus, we propose a technique, progressive
Gaussian freezing, which dynamically freezes well-converged Gaus-
sians and caches their cumulative contributions, thereby reducing
computations in both forward rasterization and backward gradient
accumulation. To make caching efficient, we further propose an
architecture, ElsaGS core, which integrates a pixel-parallel rasteri-
zation array with a set-associative frozen-Gaussian cache, FGcache.
Across three 3DGS datasets, ElsaGS freezes up to 90% of Gaussians
with negligible quality loss and achieves up to 20.6× end-to-end
speedup and 99.4% energy reduction over GPUs.
Keywords
3DGS Training, Algorithm-Hardware Co-Design.

Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [6, 7, 23, 31, 68] has emerged as a
next-generation primitive for spatial computing and immersive
3D social experiences. Recent VR platforms, e.g., Meta Quest 3,
already integrate 3DGS with applications, e.g., Arrival Space [59],
and enable users to relive scenes interactively. Huawei and Kiri
recently introduced Remy [47] into HarmonyOS, allowing everyday
users to reconstruct high-fidelity spatial content directly using
their smartphones. These applications fundamentally change how
users record and share their memories, transforming photos into
interactive 3D experiences rather than flat images.
Despite its promising user experience, 3DGS is still limited by its
training time. E.g., reconstructing even a small-scale scene, Room,
requires around 60 minutes on a mobile Nvidia Ampere GPU [52].
This means that users must wait tens of minutes before viewing or
sharing captured scenes, breaking the immediacy expected from
mobile and social applications. Therefore, accelerating the 3DGS
training process is critical for interactive spatial memory capturing.
To accelerate 3DGS training, prior studies target backward gradi-
ent accumulation, where numerous pixels update the same Gaussian
parameters through costly atomic operations [5, 18, 21, 46, 66, 67].
Existing solutions attempt to alleviate this overhead either by re-
designing computational dataflows [5, 21, 46, 66] or by pruning in-
significant gradients [18, 67]. However, those approaches typically
have limited speedup or introduce noticeable quality degradation.
Motivation. Our goal is to speed up training by orders of mag-
nitude while preserving accuracy. We notice that a large portion of
Gaussians stabilize early with low gradients, while subsequent itera-
tions predominantly refine a small active subset of Gaussians (Sec. 3).
This creates an opportunity to stop their updates and reuse their

per-view contributions across iterations to reduce computation in

Framework. We thus propose ElsaGS, a progressive Gaussian-
freezing framework in Sec. 4 that “freezes” low-gradient Gaussians
dynamically while continuing to optimize the remaining active
ones. The framework combines two optimizations. First, it caches
their per-view contributions to avoid repeated computation in both
the forward and backward passes over iterations. Second, it skips
backpropagation for frozen Gaussians to eliminate their gradient
accumulations. Sec. 7.1 shows that ElsaGS achieves better recon-
struction quality than prior gradient pruning techniques [18].
Caching. While ElsaGS avoids redundant computations, caching
individual frozen-Gaussian contributions incurs substantial memory-
access overhead. We therefore introduce FGcache in Sec. 4.2, which
stores cumulative contributions for consecutive frozen Gaussians.
To further make these group-level summaries usable by canonical
backward rasterization, which only accepts individual contribu-
tions, we redesign gradient evaluation to consume the summaries
directly. Together, these designs reduce the data transmission over-
head and enable cache reuse in both rasterization passes.
Architecture. To support Gaussian lookups and updates, we pro-
pose a streaming architecture that pipelines different 3DGS stages
while supporting sparse Gaussian updates in Sec. 5. The key inno-
vation is the ElsaGS core, which integrates the rasterization array
with our dedicated cache, FGcache, to enable efficient Gaussian
reuse. The rasterization array performs pixel-wise processing with
on-chip partial gradient reduction to minimize off-chip memory
traffic. Meanwhile, FGcache is designed to share the cached results
across both forward and backward passes, further eliminating re-
dundant storage overhead. These optimizations allow ElsaGS to
reduce both rasterization and backpropagation overheads.
Results. We evaluate ElsaGS on three widely used datasets [3,
19, 26]. To demonstrate the effectiveness, we evaluate our approach
on both canonical and pruned 3DGS algorithms [23, 46]. Our re-
sults show that ElsaGS can freeze up to 90% of Gaussians during
training with negligible quality loss. We further compare against off-
the-shelf GPUs and dedicated 3DGS accelerators [18, 66]. ElsaGS
delivers up to 20.6× speedup and 99.4% energy savings.
The contributions of this paper are as follows:
• We propose ElsaGS, a framework that accelerates training
by progressive and selective Gaussian freezing.
• We develop FGcache and a cache-compatible backward ras-
terization to reuse contributions of frozen Gaussians.
• We introduce an algorithm–hardware co-design that ad-
dresses the irregular memory access patterns introduced
by sparse Gaussian updates via our cache architecture.
• ElsaGS achieves up to 20.6× speedup and 99.4% energy sav-
ings against GPUs and prior accelerators.

Background
2.1
3DGS Training Pipeline.
The goal of the 3DGS training process is to optimize the Gaussian
primitives, {𝐺𝑖}, that model the 3D scene. Each Gaussian encodes
scene attributes such as position, opacity, and color. These Gaussian
primitives are initially obtained by photogrammetry tools, e.g.,
COLMAP [60]. Then, the training process refines these Gaussians
via iterations of the forward pass and the backward pass (Fig. 1).
During the forward pass, the pipeline renders an image from a
camera pose. The backward pass then computes the loss between
the rendered image and its corresponding ground-truth image, and
back-propagates the loss to Gaussian parameters for parameter
updates. We next describe the key stages.
Forward Pass. This pass contains projection, sorting, and forward
rasterization. This pass renders images tile-by-tile.
Projection. The projection stage filters out Gaussians that are
outside the current view frustum. In this tile-based pipeline, it
determines the intersection between Gaussians and rendering tiles,
and stores the results in a tile-Gaussian intersection list.
Sorting. Gaussians within each tile are depth-sorted from nearest
to farthest to ensure correct rendering order.
Forward Rasterization. The sorted Gaussians are then rendered
pixel-by-pixel. Each pixel in a tile iterates over the Gaussians and
performs 𝛼-checking to determine whether a Gaussian contributes
to that pixel. A Gaussian is considered intersecting if its trans-
parency 𝛼𝑖at the pixel exceeds a threshold 𝛼∗. The pixel color is
then accumulated from its contributing Gaussians based on,

2.2
3DGS Training Procedure.
With the training pipeline in Sec. 2.1, 3DGS adopts a progressive
optimization procedure to produce photorealistic representations.
Fig. 2 shows the workflow. It begins with a 3D point cloud recon-
structed by a photogrammetry pipeline, e.g., COLMAP, which is
converted into initial Gaussian primitives.
Procedure. Training proceeds iteratively. The initial model is
first optimized for 𝐼𝐾iterations, during which Gaussian parame-
ters are updated through repeated forward and backward passes.
After this stage, the algorithm checks whether the total number of
training iterations, 𝐼total, exceeds a threshold, 𝐼th.
If the criterion is not satisfied, the system performs a densifica-
tion step [23, 46]. During densification, the system clones or splits
Gaussians with high gradients to better capture scene details, and
removes those with low opacity. After densification, the model con-
tinues training for an additional 𝐼𝑁iterations to refine the scene.
This process repeats until the threshold, 𝐼th, is reached.
Once the iteration threshold is satisfied, the training stops gen-
erating more new Gaussians and enters a final finetuning stage
with 𝐼𝑀training iterations. This step refines Gaussian parameters
to stabilize rendering quality until the training finishes.
Characteristics. During training, the initial stage 𝐼𝐾is relatively
short, 500-1000 iterations. The following densification phase often

Motivation
With an understanding of the 3DGS training, we next give two key
observations that drive our algorithm-hardware co-design.
Observation 1: Forward and backward rasterization dominate
training, and the dominance persists as the model scales.

Fig. 4 shows the runtime breakdown of the forward and backward
passes. Specifically, we profile three widely used datasets [3, 19, 26],
at 10k, 20k, and 30k training iterations on canonical 3DGS and
variants with atomic operation reduction [5]. Across all methods
and training iterations, forward rasterization and backward ras-
terization dominate the total execution time. Though method [5]
reduces atomic operations, the forward and backward rasterization
are time-consuming, taking up over 80% of the execution time. Both
rasterization stages are computationally expensive since every pixel
must traverse thousands of Gaussians during each iteration [10].
Observation 2: A majority of Gaussians converge initially during
training, and only a small fraction of Gaussians require further
training in subsequent iterations.
Fig. 5 presents six violin plots of the gradient magnitude distribu-
tion across all Gaussians during training. The results are measured
on three datasets [3, 19, 26]. To reduce data noise, gradients are
averaged over 100 iterations, and we plot every 2k iterations. We
consider a Gaussian to be well-trained once its gradient magnitude
stabilizes and no longer decreases.
As shown in Fig. 5, the distributions shift toward low magnitudes
within the first 6k iterations, while their upper tails continue to de-
crease later in training. This trend yields two key observations. First,
the majority of Gaussians converge early in training and no longer
require further training after the initial training stage. Second, a
small subset of Gaussians still requires additional optimization to
refine reconstruction details. Meanwhile, the densification process
would introduce new Gaussians that require further optimization.
Existing methods exploit early Gaussian convergence but ei-
ther leave forward passes to be recomputed [1] or rely on order-
independent rendering for contribution reuse [69]. In contrast, El-
saGS reuses frozen Gaussian contributions in both forward and
backward passes while preserving depth-ordered alpha blending.
4
Progressive Gaussian-Freezing Framework
With these two key observations in Sec. 3, we present our training
framework that “freezes” the majority of Gaussians during training,
i.e., stops updating their parameters. We first give an overview
of the training workflow in Sec. 4.1, and then describe our cache
design that enables efficient execution in Sec. 4.2. Lastly, we explain
how we modify backward rasterization in Sec. 4.3 to enable sharing
a single cache between forward and backward passes.
4.1
Overview
We first introduce our training pipeline that leverages frozen Gaus-
sians to reduce computations, and then describe how these Gaus-
sians are identified dynamically during training.
Pipeline. Fig. 6 shows our pipeline. The orange blocks denote
the stages in the original pipeline, while the blue blocks highlight
our augmentations. Specifically, our pipeline accelerates two bot-
tlenecks, forward and backward rasterization.
During forward rasterization, the original pipeline computes the
transparency of every Gaussian and blends its color contribution
into individual pixels as defined in Eqn. 1. In contrast, our pipeline
first determines whether a Gaussian is frozen, i.e., its parameters are
held fixed and no longer updated. For frozen Gaussians, we avoid
their computations and directly query the Frozen Gaussian Cache,
FGcache, to retrieve their precomputed contributions, e.g., trans-
parency 𝛼𝑖and color c𝑖. For active Gaussians, the pipeline falls back
to the canonical rasterization. To further reduce caching overhead,
we group consecutive frozen Gaussians and cache their cumula-
tive contributions rather than individual Gaussian contributions.
Sec. 4.2 further describes the detailed cache design.
Similarly, during backward rasterization, the pipeline needs to
compute partial gradients of transparency𝛼𝑖and color c𝑖(Eqn. 2 and
Eqn. 3). In particular, canonical backward pipeline requires travers-
ing the tile-Gaussian sorted list in reverse order and reconstructing
the cumulative color and transmittance contribution through iter-
ation of per Gaussian. Here, we avoid the computation of frozen
Gaussians by first checking whether a Gaussian is frozen. If not, we
directly compute the gradients for the active Gaussians; otherwise,
we query FGcache. Note that, FGcache stores cumulative con-
tributions of consecutive frozen Gaussians rather than individual
Gaussian contributions. We thus redesign backward rasterization
in Sec. 4.3 to enable FGcache sharing for backward rasterization.

sists of an initial training, progressive freezing during densification,
and final finetuning. For the first 6k iterations, we follow the origi-
nal training and densification schedule. After this, our framework
begins to gradually freeze Gaussians.

candidates for freezing after removing low-opacity ones. Similar
to the densification process, we rank Gaussians using the same
gradient statistic in densification and freeze those with the smallest
scores. All attributes of a frozen Gaussian are excluded from further
optimization. The freezing ratio 𝜌𝑡is based on:

Cache Policy. In the following paragraphs, we describe the
cache management policy of FGcache. Specifically, we explain
four operations: read, update, insert, and evict.
Read. We define a read operation as querying results from
FGcache. During rasterization, FGcache maintains a temporary
queue buffer filled with Gaussians from the sorted Gaussian list.
The rasterizer processes either a single unfrozen Gaussian or a con-
secutive frozen group at the top of the queue. It queries FGcache
when the frozen group reaches the maximum group length, is fol-
lowed by an unfrozen Gaussian, or reaches the end of the list. Then,
each consecutive frozen group is queried using ⟨𝑇ID,𝐺ID, L⟩. Next,
the length of the frozen Gaussians in the current temporary buffer
is checked against the metadata stored in the cache entry. A match
means a cache hit. Thus, we can directly reuse the cached result.
On a cache miss, we recompute all the Gaussians in the current
temporary buffer, and follow the update step described below to
update the new cumulative contribution to FGcache.
Update. However, during training, the positions of the sorted
Gaussian list and frozen groups can be changed by the updates to
active Gaussians and newly frozen Gaussians. Cache entries must
therefore be updated accordingly. Fig. 9 shows three scenarios in
which cache entries need to be updated. In these examples, we
assume the maximum frozen group length is 3. For simplicity, some
operations in the original rasterization are omitted.
Fig. 9a shows the first case, where a newly frozen Gaussian, 8,
follows the unchanged cached prefix of Gaussians 4 and 5. Accord-
ing to the policy, we retrieve the entry using the key ⟨𝑇ID, 4⟩and
find its stored length of 2 is shorter than the current group length
of 3. Since the cached group remains an unchanged prefix of the
current group, we reuse its cumulative contribution. Meanwhile,
the opacity and color contribution of Gaussian 8 are computed on
the fly. Lastly, we combine the computed contribution with the
cached prefix in rendering order, and update the entry in place with
the combined result and the new frozen group length of 3.
Fig. 9b shows the second scenario, where an existing frozen
Gaussian 4 is led by two newly frozen Gaussians, 8 and 9. Since
Gaussian 8 is newly frozen and not stored in cache, querying the
new key ⟨𝑇ID, 8⟩finds no valid entry, causing a cache miss. We
therefore recompute all three Gaussians and insert their cumulative
contribution with a group length of 3. The old subgroup starting at
Gaussian 4 does not need explicit invalidation. Since it is no longer
accessed, it will eventually be evicted after rasterization.
In last scenario (Fig. 9c), a frozen Gaussian, 8, moves between
frozen Gaussians 4 and 5 and splits their cached group. The cur-
rent frozen group starting at Gaussian 4 ends before Gaussian 8,
so querying ⟨𝑇ID, 4⟩finds an entry with a longer stored length.
Thus, we recompute the contributions of all frozen Gaussians in
the temporary buffer and update the cache with new results.
Insert&Eviction. In FGcache, insertion occurs when new cumu-
lative results need to be stored. Unlike conventional caches that
rely on least recently used (LRU) or similar replacement policies,
we propose a new shortest Gaussian length (SGL) replacement pol-
icy. The policy is based on two observations. First, during per-tile
rasterization, the pipeline queries each frozen group exactly once.
Thus, the temporary locality across entries is similar, and the pri-
mary difference is the amount of computation each entry can save.
Second, an entry that is not queried indicates that its cached group
no longer corresponds to any current query group. These entries
are expired and should be removed to keep consistency.
Therefore, after each rasterization, any entries not queried are
invalidated for that view and tile. Upon insertion, FGcache will
first replace any invalid cache entry. If FGcache is full, SGL policy
selects the entry with the smallest group of Gaussians for eviction.
This way, it retains entries that yield higher computational savings.

With FGcache, we can eliminate a large portion of redundant com-

In this section, we first describe the overall architecture. We then
describe the designs of two key components in the ElsaGS core:
the rasterization unit array and FGcache.

Overview. Fig. 11 shows the overall hardware design of ElsaGS.
We implement ElsaGS as a streaming architecture consisting of
five major components: an ElsaGS core, a projection unit, a sorting
unit, an aggregation unit, and a postprocessing unit. The ElsaGS
core is our key contribution, which will be further explained in the
following paragraphs. The projection and sorting units execute the
projection and sorting stages in the forward pass, similar to prior
work [30, 40]. The aggregation unit collects partial gradients for
each Gaussian and writes the accumulated results back to memory,
like GSArch [18]. The postprocessing unit updates parameters and
executes densification or freezing according to gradient statistics.
To rank Gaussians by gradients, we reuse the existing sorting
unit, so ElsaGS does not require an additional dedicated hard-
ware unit for Gaussian selection. ElsaGS further adopts tiled SSIM,
like [66], so that the image loss can be computed in a streaming
manner. This enables our architecture to pipeline sorting, forward
rasterization, and backward rasterization. To do this, we implement
double buffering between consecutive stages, allowing overlap be-
tween computation and data communication.
ElsaGS Core. The key component of our architecture is the
ElsaGS core, which supports training with our technique, Gauss-
ian freezing, while accelerating both forward and backward ras-
terization. As shown in Fig. 11, the ElsaGS core consists of five
components: a sorted Gaussian buffer, an output buffer, a FGcache,
a rasterization unit array, and a reduction unit. Both the Gauss-
ian buffer and the output buffer are double-buffered to support
our streaming processing and hide off-chip data traffic latency. The
rasterization unit array performs both forward and backward raster-
ization computations, while the on-chip reduction unit aggregates
partial Gaussian gradients to reduce off-chip memory traffic.
Rasterization Array. Our rasterization array comprises a set
of rasterization units executing in parallel. Each rasterization unit
adopts a pixel-wise processing paradigm to execute forward or
backward rasterization. The right side of Fig. 11 shows a single-
unit design. It is organized into two separate stages: a frontend
that accumulates Gaussian contributions (light blue) and a back-
end that computes the image loss or gradients (dark blue). Each
array maintains a temporary buffer, which fetches data from the
sorted Gaussian buffer and detects consecutive frozen Gaussians for
FGcache queries. This buffer is set to twice the maximum Gaussian-
group length used for FGcache queries, ensuring sufficient capacity
for grouping consecutive frozen Gaussians.
In forward rasterization, the frontend continuously consumes
consecutive frozen Gaussians or individual active Gaussians at the
top of the temporary buffer, then pops the processed entries and
refills the buffer from the sorted Gaussian list. When the frozen
Gaussian group reaches the maximum length, encounters an un-
frozen Gaussian, or reaches the end of the list, it queries the FG-
cache. On a cache hit, the returned cached contribution is directly
accumulated into the rendered pixel. Otherwise, the frontend per-
forms recomputation and updates FGcache based on the policy in
Sec. 4.2. Meanwhile, transitional or active Gaussians still go through
the canonical forward rasterization pipeline and accumulate their
contributions to the pixel. After the frontend completes the color
accumulation over all Gaussians in the sorted Gaussian list, the
backend then calculates the image loss and saves it in the buffer.

During backward rasterization, the backend traverses the list in
the same order and reconstructs the cumulative color and transmit-
tance. The backend uses these values and losses in the image buffer
to compute partial gradients for active Gaussians. Our architecture
adopts the redesigned backward rasterization in Sec. 4.3, so that
the FGcache can be shared in both rasterization stages. Lastly, the
partial gradients from all rasterization units are locally aggregated
by an on-chip gradient reduction unit to reduce off-chip traffic.
FGcache. Fig. 12 shows FGcache design, which is implemented
as a 𝑁-way associative cache to store precomputed frozen-Gaussian
attributes. For simplicity, we show the cache design for a single
view, where the key consists of the tile ID and leading Gaussian ID.
During a cache lookup, we first index the target set through the
least significant bits of the group key. The cache then compares
its tags in parallel. A valid tag match identifies a candidate entry.
The rasterization array directly reuses the cached transmittance
and color contributions if the stored group length |{𝐺}| of the
entry matches the current group length. Length mismatches are
handled by the cache update policy. This way, we can reduce the
computation of these consecutive frozen Gaussians.
To further regularize off-chip data access, we design FGcache
with double buffering. Each FGcache buffer is shared across the
rendering of eight image tiles, where each tile has a size of 16×16.
While the current eight tiles are being rendered, the cache contents
for the next eight tiles are prefetched into the fill buffer. In this way,
off-chip data movement for FGcache becomes streaming.

Experimental Setup
Hardware Setup. Our overall architecture consists of 4 projection
units, 4 sorting units, 4 gradient reduction units, and a ElsaGS core.
We implement ElsaGS core with 2 rasterization arrays clocked at
1 GHz. Each has 8 × 8 rasterization units. Both the sorted Gaussian
buffer and the output buffer are shared across all rasterization
arrays. They have a total size of 64 KB and 4 KB, respectively.
The FGcache is shared across the rasterization arrays. This
512 KB cache has 32 sets with 8 entries per set. The tag of each
cache entry is constructed by the leading Gaussian ID, tile ID, and
Image ID. Each entry stores a tile (16 × 16) of the accumulated color
and transparency values. Each cache access requires 8 cycles to
return 512 B of cached data (an 8 × 8 tile accumulation result). Two
8 × 8 tiles can be accessed in parallel. FGcache is shared across
8 image tiles with a size of 16 × 16. We cap each frozen group at
64 Gaussians. Each rasterization array has a temporary buffer that
holds up to 128 Gaussians, twice the maximum group size.

ward passes, ElsaGS-hw consumes over 40% less dynamic energy
per frame than CamGS and over 85% less than GSArch at finetun-
ing stage.
Memory Traffic. In Fig. 18, we report the off-chip memory
traffic of ElsaGS under a 90% freezing ratio. We break down the
traffic into four categories. The first category is the memory traffic
in forward and backward rasterization. The second one is the data
transmission during aggregation, where the pipeline accumulates
gradients through atomic operations. The third one is the data
transmission caused by the FGcache, which only occurs in ElsaGS.
The last one includes the remaining memory traffic.
As the results show, ElsaGS reduces most memory traffic in the
aggregation stage. This is because ElsaGS leverages Gaussian-wise
sparsity compared to other methods, naturally reducing most gra-
dient accumulation. Moreover, ElsaGS reduces over 50% memory
traffic in rasterization by caching the cumulative contributions.
Meanwhile, our overall cache design effectively reduces the cache
traffic and further reduces the overall memory traffic.
Cache Miss Rate. Fig. 19 gives a case study of cache miss
rates on 3DGS with 90% Gaussians frozen. We break down misses
by forward and backward rasterization and further classify these
misses into four categories. The first one is the conflict misses due to
new cache entries. This comes from the original grouped Gaussians
being split into two. The second one is the conflict misses due to
cache entry mismatches. This is from the grouped Gaussians having
been expanded or shortened. The third one is the canonical conflict
misses. These are misses similar to the conventional cache conflict
misses due to cache associativity. The last one is capacity misses.
Most misses occur in the forward pass. Among them, 64.8% are
classified as conflict misses. However, most of these conflicts come
from the first two categories, i.e., new entries and entry mismatches,
rather than conventional cache conflicts. These misses are mainly
caused by the dynamic nature of 3DGS training. As Gaussian pa-
rameters are updated over iterations, active Gaussians can alter the
ordering of existing Gaussian groups. In contrast, entry-mismatch
and new-entry misses do not appear in the backward pass, because
Gaussian parameters remain unchanged in the same iteration and
backward rasterization can fully reuse cached results.

7.3
Ablation Study
Fig. 20 further dissects the contribution of ElsaGS-hw. Specifically,
we gradually include more components in ElsaGS, +Freezing and
+Fwd. FGcache. Base-hw runs with all Gaussians active and FG-
cache disabled, which improves performance over GPU by 3.1×.
+Freezing skips gradient computation and accumulation for frozen
Gaussians, yielding a 2.4× speedup and reducing energy by 51.9%
relative to Base-hw. +Fwd. FGcache only supports FGcache for
forward rasterization and further reduces energy by 20.8% relative
to +Freezing, with negligible change in speedup because backward
rasterization still recomputes the contributions of frozen Gaussians.
Finally, ElsaGS adds the cache-compatible backward rasterization
in Sec. 4.3, achieving a further 2.5× speedup and reducing energy
by 40.3% relative to +Fwd. FGcache.

Shared Tiles per FGcache. Fig. 23 shows the sensitivity of per-
formance to the number of shared tiles per FGcache, normalized
to the 8-tile configuration. As the number of shared tiles increases,
overall speedup decreases due to higher cache conflict rates. Mean-
while, energy efficiency is also improved as tile sharing increases
from 2 to 8. However, further increasing the number of shared tiles
from 8 to 16 decreases both speedup and energy efficiency due to
increasing cache contention.
Cache Configuration. Fig. 21 shows the sensitivity of speedup
to cache configuration with 90% of Gaussians frozen. All numbers
are normalized to the configuration with 32 sets and 8 ways. The re-
sult shows that increasing either the number of sets or associativity
generally improves speedup by reducing cache conflicts. However,
the overall performance gains diminish beyond our default con-
figuration, i.e., 32 sets and 8 ways, where further increases yield
marginal gains. This shows that our default configuration achieves
a good balance between performance and hardware cost.

that ElsaGS can be applied to other 3DGS-based algorithms by
leveraging similar freezing and caching techniques.

8
Related Work
Neural Rendering Accelerators. In recent years, a wide range of
studies have focused on accelerating neural rendering [5, 10, 11, 13,
18, 27–30, 33, 34, 37, 38, 40, 44, 49, 54, 55, 61, 66, 70, 72, 73]. Some
studies [10, 29, 40, 41, 53, 78] propose dedicated accelerators for ren-
dering only. Meanwhile, others [18, 21, 67] focus on optimizing the
training procedure. REACT3D [63], RTGS [36], and Splatonic [21],
propose dedicated accelerators for SLAM-based 3DGS.
3DGS Acceleration Techniques. In addition to hardware sup-
port, numerous software optimizations have been proposed to ac-
celerate 3DGS. To reduce rendering workload, studies [6–8, 17, 46]
propose to prune insignificant Gaussians. Others [24, 32, 50, 51, 56]
introduce alternative data representations to alleviate memory
pressure. Another line of research targets optimizing the 3DGS
pipeline itself, e.g., online filtering techniques [14, 16, 25, 30, 64]
or rasterization optimization [9, 15, 39, 77]. Recently, GSta [1] and
SparseOIT [69] attempt to accelerate training by fixing the parame-
ters of a subset of Gaussians. However, they either only optimize the
backward pass or rely on order-independent transparency render-
ing assumptions. In contrast, ElsaGS exploits the unique training
characteristics to accelerate both forward and backward passes,
and is applicable to different 3DGS-based algorithms.
Memoization Techniques. There is a long history in the archi-
tecture community of leveraging memoization to reduce computa-
tion. Recent work [2, 4, 20, 57, 65] has explored computation reuse
for vision DNNs at multiple granularities. Other works [42, 43]
target resource-constrained platforms by clustering similar inputs
to avoid redundant convolution operations. Meanwhile, another
line of research exploits temporal correlations in video streams to
bypass unnecessary computation [12, 45, 71, 75, 76, 79].
9
Conclusion
We propose ElsaGS, accelerates 3DGS training by progressively
freezing low-gradient Gaussians and reusing their cached contribu-
tions with negligible quality loss. To improve the reuse efficiency,
we develop FGcache and cache-compatible backward rasterization.
We further introduce a streaming architecture, ElsaGS core, with
our dedicated frozen-Gaussian cache. Our results show that El-
saGS freezes up to 90% of Gaussians with higher speedup against
off-the-shelf GPUs and prior accelerators.
