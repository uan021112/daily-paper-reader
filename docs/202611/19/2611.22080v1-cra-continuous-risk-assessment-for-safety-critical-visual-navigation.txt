## Abstract 

Traditional safety-critical control methods often rely on predefined constraints that separate states into safe and un-safe regions. While recent studies have extended geometric safety toward semantic safety, real-world safety is rarely purely binary. The risk associated with an object or hu-man can vary continuously with its physical properties, like motion, and interaction context; for example, a person car-rying a heavy object or a hot object moving at high speed may require a different safety response from an ordinary static obstacle. In this paper, we propose a vision-based continuous risk assessment framework for safety-critical visual navigation. Our method uses RGB and LiDAR ob-servations to estimate four risk factors (kinetic, potential, contact-severity, and thermal) and combines them into a con-tinuous risk score for each assessed object or person. These estimates are integrated into a Poisson safety function with a Laplace guidance field as a safety filter, enabling online mon-itoring and intervention in open-world robot navigation. We demonstrate the proposed method in a simulated restaurant scene. Demonstration videos are available on our project webpage. 

## 1. Introduction 

Navigation is one of the most important capabilities of au-tonomous systems and has been studied for decades [19, 42]. In navigation studies, vision plays an important role in lo-calization, map construction, environment monitoring, and risk detection. With the development of camera-based per-ception, visual navigation has been widely studied in appli-cations such as mobile robots [13], autonomous driving [12], and aerial robots [46]. Beyond geometric obstacle avoidance, recent research has shown that navigation agents should per-ceive objects, humans, and scene context for better decision-making [17, 39, 41, 58]. Despite significant progress in visual navigation across a wide range of applications, navigation systems typically operate by producing a nominal trajectory or control com-mand. However, when deployed in complex real-world en-vironments, this nominal motion may become unsafe due to perception errors, occlusions, model mismatch, dynamic obstacles, or imperfect trajectory tracking [36]. Therefore, safety should not only be considered inside the motion plan-ner, but also enforced by an additional real-time safety layer before the robot executes its action. Safety filters provide such a mechanism by monitoring the proposed control input at runtime and intervening when necessary to ensure safety during execution [38]. Representative safety-filtering methods include control barrier functions (CBFs) [5], model-predictive control (MPC) [71], Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability [8], and data-driven safety filters [26]. These methods provide rig-orous guarantees by maintaining the system within a pre-scribed safe set. The safe sets of these methods are often designed through predefined constraints [4, 63], learned from data [51], or constructed from occupancy maps and geomet-1arXiv:2611.22080v1 [cs.RO] 17 Nov 2026 ric primitives [48]. As a result, classical safety-filtering approaches are often “semantically blind” [67], focusing on geometric rather than contextual notions of safety: they can reason about where an obstacle is, but not necessarily what the obstacle is or why it may impose different levels of risk. Recent work on semantic safety addresses this limita-tion across several robotic settings, including robot ma-nipulation [15], open-world robot navigation and embod-ied AI [60], and autonomous driving [34]. Context-aware robotic safety is especially important in open-world environ-ments [56], where the same action may be safe or unsafe depending on surrounding humans, objects, and physical context. Although semantic reasoning has improved safety-critical robot decision-making, many existing approaches operate primarily at the planning or task-reasoning level, where LLMs and vision-language models (VLMs) are used to ground language instructions and generate semantic safety rules [3, 60, 69]. However, these methods are often de-signed for high-level planning rather than real-time safety filtering. Directly using LLMs or VLMs inside the safety filter loop can introduce high latency, while the safety filter and controller require low-latency monitoring and interven-tion [13, 55]. Moreover, existing semantic safety methods often convert context into discrete rules or constraints, but real-world risk is continuous and context-dependent, varying with object properties, motion, and interaction context. This continuous and context-dependent nature of risk sug-gests that safety cannot be completely captured by fixed discrete rules. Prior work on fuzzy safety analysis has simi-larly argued that safety is difficult to define exactly and may involve different degrees of hazard [31]. In open-world vi-sual navigation, such ambiguity naturally arises: a person walking freely and a person carrying a heavy object may not be strictly classifiable as simply safe or unsafe, even if they are at the same distance from the robot; instead, the robot should assess how safe the interaction is under the current context. Similarly, a static object and a sharp or fast-moving object may involve different levels of risk despite similar geometry. Therefore, rather than relying only on predefined semantic rules, a robot should assess how safe the current scene is and use this continuous risk assessment to guide real-time safety filtering. Motivated by this observation, we propose a vision-based continuous risk assessment framework for safety-critical visual navigation. Instead of assigning predefined safe or unsafe labels to semantic objects, our method analyzes scene risk through four physical factors derived from visual reason-ing, including object properties, motion, and interaction con-text. We integrate continuous risk assessment into a safety filter based on Safe-SAGE [67]. The risk scores set the nor-mal boundary flux of the Laplace guidance field (LGF) [7], which shapes the Poisson safety function (PSF) [6]. The filter uses both fields to adjust the robot’s commands online through MPC and CBF filtering [11]. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Geometric Safety Filters 

Geometric safety filters constrain nominal control inputs to keep the system within a prescribed safe set [5, 8, 63]. In robot navigation, these safe sets are often constructed from geometric representations, such as pairwise safety dis-tances [64], point-cloud-based obstacle representations [24, 27], and occupancy-grid maps [52]. These methods pro-vide rigorous runtime safety guarantees, but they usually do not distinguish objects by their semantic or physical risk. Our work extends geometric safety filtering by using vi-sual reasoning to construct continuous, context-aware risk assessments for the safety layer. 

2.2. Semantic Safety 

Semantic safety utilizes language, vision-language mod-els, and scene understanding for robotic safety. Language-conditioned planning grounds semantic instructions to robotic affordances [3]. Other works study semantic safety benchmarks for evaluating unsafe robot behaviors [60]. With the advancement of LLMs and VLMs, recent safety-filter methods further connect semantic information to control-level safety through CBFs [15, 55] or HJ reachability [57]. Other works use latent-space safe sets to address hard-to-model safety constraints and out-of-distribution (OOD) fail-ures [49, 59]. However, foundation-model reasoning can introduce latency for real-time control [44]. Safe-SAGE [67] provides a semantic-aware framework for real-time safety filtering by modulating PSF/LGF-based safety fields with visual semantics. However, these methods often represent semantic context as rules, object classes, or constraints. Our work builds on this framework but estimates continuous risk from visual reasoning for real-time safety filtering. 

2.3. Risk-aware and Robust Safety 

Risk-aware and robust safety methods address uncertainty in safety-critical control, including measurement and per-ception uncertainty [22, 28], model uncertainty and distur-bances [18, 25], stochastic uncertainty [20, 23], and CVaR-based risk-aware formulations [2]. These methods improve safety when the robot state, dynamics, measurements, or disturbances are uncertain. However, they usually assume a predefined safety specification, such as a geometric distance constraint. Our work represents safety on a continuous scale by estimating how the risk posed by an object or interaction varies with object properties, relative motion, and the robot’s planned route. 

2.4. Fuzzy Safety 

Fuzzy views of safety have been explored more in other safety-critical domains than in autonomous robotic systems. 2Prior work on inherent safety argues that safety is difficult to define exactly and should be evaluated by degrees rather than only by strict safe/unsafe categories [31]. Fuzzy logic has also been used for qualitative safety modeling in ma-rine systems [61] and for traffic safety to avoid crisp safety thresholds [47]. These works show that safety can be better represented as a gradual concept, such as “not very safe” or “highly unsafe,” rather than only as a strict safe/unsafe category. However, this perspective has not been widely ex-plored in semantic safety for autonomous visual navigation. We apply this view to visual navigation by representing risk on a continuous scale. 

2.5. Visual Physical Reasoning 

Visual physical reasoning studies how to infer object prop-erties, dynamics, and interactions from images and videos. Prior work examines object motion and interaction dynam-ics [65], collision events [68], physical dynamics such as rolling, sliding, and falling [9], and block-tower stability [43]. Other work recognizes materials [10] and affordances [30]. In our work, we combine RGB and LiDAR observations to estimate kinetic, potential, contact-severity, and thermal risk for visual navigation.

4.2. RGB–LiDAR Association 

At each update, we process an RGB image ItC and LiDAR returns PtL = {pj }, retaining their source timestamps. LiDAR clustering and tracking organize the observed re-turns into geometric fragments. We use pretrained SAM 2.1 Tiny [54] to propose class-agnostic instance masks {Mi}

and COCO-pretrained YOLO11n-seg [40, 45] to obtain in-stance masks and class cues for humans. We associate visual instances with observed 3D returns through calibrated projection, as used in projection-based camera–LiDAR fusion [62]. Using sensor calibration and the ego poses at the corresponding observation times, we project each LiDAR return as 

zj = Π C (RCL pj + tCL ) , (13) where (RCL , tCL ) transforms the point from the LiDAR frame at tL to the camera frame at tC , and ΠC is the cal-ibrated fisheye projection. The resulting zj is the image coordinate of return pj .For each visible LiDAR fragment, we use a projected return as a point prompt for SAM to obtain a candidate mask [66]. LiDAR may sometimes split a single static ob-ject into several fragments. When multiple fragments con-sistently align with the same SAM mask, we group their verified returns into one object assessment. For each accepted match, we pass the SAM mask, the associated LiDAR returns, their original observation times, and the SAM-derived mask confidence to the physical-factor estimators. When the YOLO human mask agrees with the as-sociated SAM mask in the same RGB image, we use human-specific priors for mass and body contact. LiDAR returns without an accepted image match remain in the occupancy map.

5.1. Experimental Setup 

We conduct the navigation experiments in NVIDIA Isaac Sim 6.0.1 [50] on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Ti. We adapt the Omniverse Restaurant Demo Assets Pack for the restaurant demonstration. 2

5.2. Continuous Risk Assessment Analysis 

We evaluate contact and thermal risk on RGB images and videos (Fig. 3(a) and (b)). In Isaac Sim , we vary the requested speed past a static obstacle to test kinetic risk (Fig. 3(c)). For potential risk, CRA uses the estimated depth of a shallow hole to guide the robot around it (Fig. 3(d)). We also evaluate CRA in a combined-risk scenario involv-ing a human carrying a steaming pot. CRA jointly considers the human’s motion, the potential risk of the elevated pot, and the thermal risk associated with the visible steam (Fig. 4). 

5.3. Restaurant Navigation in Simulation 

We further evaluate CRA in a simulated restaurant with live performance. The robot starts with a meal on its tray, delivers it to the left dining area, returns to the service bar for a second meal, and delivers it to the right dining area (Fig. 5). The complete service sequence is shown in the video available on our project webpage. 

## 6. Conclusion 

Motivated by the idea of fuzzy safety, we presented Con-tinuous Risk Assessment (CRA) for safety-critical visual navigation. CRA uses RGB and LiDAR observations to es-timate kinetic, potential, contact-severity, and thermal risk. During navigation, we aggregate these factors into a continu-ous risk score for each object or human and use the resulting scores to shape the guidance and safety fields for the MPC and analytical CBF layers. We evaluated CRA in Isaac Sim 

on a restaurant delivery mission.
