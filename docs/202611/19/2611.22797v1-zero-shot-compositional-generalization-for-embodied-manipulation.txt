Abstract
Robot demonstrations are collected on different embodi-
ments, leaving many robot–skill combinations unobserved.
Pooling these data shares visual knowledge, while joint
labels remain tied to the demonstrating kinematic chain.
We propose a geometry-supervised training framework for
zero-shot composition of familiar robots and skills.
The
central insight is that a demonstrated command provides
a tool-space target for every robot that can realize it. Cal-
ibrated forward kinematics supplies this supervision to for-
eign native action interfaces without their joint labels for
that skill. Native reconstruction retains demonstrated con-
figurations, and positive reachability checks select targets
for cross-robot supervision. A visual policy learns to pre-
dict shared actions and executes through the target robot’s
native decoder. During policy learning, one prediction re-
ceives geometric supervision through both source and for-
eign native realizations. Matched supervision controls, re-
peated policy training and evaluations across three back-
bones and two simulators demonstrate improved transfer to
missing robot–task combinations.

1. Introduction
Suppose one robot learns to place a cup on a shelf and an-
other learns to place containers in a drawer. Each has prac-
ticed grasping, transporting and releasing objects. A shared
policy has also seen both tasks. Yet asking the second robot
to place the cup introduces a missing combination of a fa-
miliar robot and a familiar skill. This gap is common when
robot datasets are assembled from separate platforms, en-
vironments and collection efforts. Collecting every task on
every robot scales with the product of the two sets. Reusing
demonstrations requires a way for supervision from an ob-
served combination to inform a missing one.
The action labels make this difficult. A joint vector de-
scribes how the demonstrating robot moves. Its entries de-
pend on joint count, link geometry, joint conventions and
installation. Even when two arms have the same number of
joints, equal numerical commands can move their tools dif-
ferently. A shared model can learn useful transfer from im-
ages, language and repeated task structure, but native labels
provide no explicit correspondence between these different
motions. Padding labels to a common width makes them
compatible with a network input or output. The remaining
learning problem is to connect their physical meanings.
Known robot geometry supplies a physical correspon-
dence. A demonstrated joint command determines the cal-
ibrated tool’s position, orientation and opening in a shared
task frame. For another robot, this target constrains the ef-
fect of its action while leaving its joint solution to its own
kinematic chain. One source demonstration can therefore
supervise several native action interfaces, including those
without demonstrations of the source task. The required in-
formation is the robots’ geometry and calibration, together
with evidence that a foreign chain can reach the target. Fig-
ure 1 illustrates how this supervision can connect familiar
robots and skills across missing combinations.
Our approach learns native action interfaces around this
supervision path. A source action is encoded into shared
coordinates and decoded by both its own interface and a
foreign robot’s interface.
Native reconstruction anchors
each robot to its demonstrated commands. Cross-robot ge-
ometric supervision trains the foreign decoder to realize the
source tool target, using a positive reachability check to se-
lect admissible targets. A visual–language policy subse-
quently learns the shared action coordinates and executes
through the appropriate native decoder. Geometry estab-
lishes a relation among action realizations.
The policy
must still learn from observations and language when to ap-
proach, grasp, transport and release, and closed-loop execu-
tion determines whether those decisions complete the task.

Our contributions are twofold. First, we learn hetero-

3. Problem Formulation
3.1. Missing Combinations of Known Robots and
Tasks
Let B be a set of robots and K a set of tasks. Demonstra-
tions define edges in a bipartite robot–task graph. Action-
interface pretraining observes edges Ocodec, and visual pol-
icy learning observes Opolicy. We evaluate held-out combi-
nations H satisfying
H ∩(Ocodec ∪Opolicy) = ∅.
(1)
Every tested robot and task individually appears in pol-
icy training. Each robot therefore has native demonstra-
tions, and each tested skill has been demonstrated by an-
other robot. Zero-shot transfer means executing their miss-
ing combination without its expert demonstrations in either
training stage. Evaluation episodes are also held out. Kine-
matic descriptions, joint limits, installation transforms and
tool calibration are known for every robot.
A source demonstration provides images o, language ℓ,
current native state qs, and future native commands q∗
s,1:H.
The policy maps current observations and a task instruction
to native commands for the executing robot. Cross-robot
supervision may use tool targets derived from the source
demonstrations. It supplies no target-robot images or expert
joint trajectory for the missing combination. This distinc-
tion defines the permitted information transfer across the
graph. Source-derived geometric targets can constrain tar-
get action realization while visual task behavior is learned
from the observed edges.

Whole-arm realization introduces two constraints. First,
reachable tool poses depend on the target chain, installation
and joint limits. We supervise a foreign arm only when a
numerical IK procedure finds a finite, joint-limited solution
within the specified pose tolerances. This positive witness
supports the particular target, while an unsuccessful search
leaves it unsupervised. Second, redundant arms can realize
one pose with multiple joint configurations. Each robot’s
reconstruction supervision anchors its decoder to demon-
strated native behavior, and foreign geometry constrains the
tool effect of decoding a source action. The resulting target
is a pose-level constraint. Trajectory continuity, collision
avoidance and successful contact remain properties to as-
sess in execution.

4.2. What Transfers Between Robots?
A source robot’s cup-grasp command specifies its shoul-
der, elbow and wrist configuration. Native reconstruction
anchors its own interface to that command. A foreign de-
coder receives supervision from the calibrated grasp pose
and opening and can select its own native configuration. In-
stallation transforms place both kinematic roots in the task
frame, and each FK function carries its chain’s link geom-
etry and joint conventions. Tool calibration identifies the
point and orientation being compared. The experiments use
fixed calibrated installations for each known robot. A differ-
ent installation would require its corresponding transform in
Eq. 2.
The task-frame target is unchanged by camera placement
when the demonstrated motion is fixed. The visual policy

must still accommodate deployment viewpoints. Camera-
frame targets would require the corresponding extrinsics.
Shared tool targets support both learned native decod-
ing and direct pose prediction with online IK. Native recon-
struction teaches a decoder to retain configurations repre-
sented in its robot’s demonstrations. Direct tool prediction
delegates joint realization to an online solver. We evaluate
task success and command-generation latency for these two
routes. The Source/Cross comparison holds the learned-
interface route fixed and isolates its foreign geometric su-
pervision.
Foreign realization measures whether source actions can
be decoded into the intended tool motion. Visual closed-
loop execution then tests whether a policy can use the tar-
get robot’s decoder on its own observations to complete the
missing combination.
4.3. Learning When to Use the Shared Actions
After interface training, we freeze the encoder and all na-
tive decoders. The policy’s supervised target is the posterior
mean of each demonstrated future action, transformed by a
shared TRAIN-fitted diagonal affine normalization A,
z0 = A(µs(q∗
s,1:H)).
(7)
The interface supplies shared action targets and their native
realization through frozen robot-specific decoders. It leaves
observation-dependent skill prediction to the policy back-
bone. A predictor trained in these coordinates can execute
through the corresponding decoder.
The shared coordinates retain command-level granular-
ity. Temporal dependencies are modeled by the policy’s se-
quence predictor, which learns a horizon of shared actions.
At each chunk position, the executing robot’s decoder ap-
plies the same framewise map to native commands. Inter-
face training can therefore pool command instants across
demonstrations with different motion durations.
Our three evaluated backbones instantiate flow learning.
Gaussian noise ϵ and noise level σ define zσ = (1 −σ)z0 +
σϵ. The policy predicts the velocity target ϵ −z0 with its
native weighted flow objective,
Lflow = w(σ)∥vθ(zσ, σ, c) −(ϵ −z0)∥2,
(8)
where c contains the observation conditioning. At deploy-
ment, a predicted chunk is transformed by A−1 and decoded
through the executing robot’s Db. The robot executes a pre-
fix and observes again. Decoded commands specify abso-
lute joint-position targets and gripper opening.
The visual backbone supplies object localization, contact
timing and observation-dependent correction. Cross-robot
interface training makes source actions meaningful to an-
other robot’s native decoder. Our hypothesis is that these
two learned components compose on a missing robot–task
pairing. Closed-loop task success measures that composi-
tion.
4.4. An Additional Geometric Loss During Policy
Learning
A policy exposing a differentiable clean-action estimate can
also receive geometric supervision through the frozen inter-
faces. For flow predictors, the estimate is recovered from
the noisy input and predicted velocity,
bz0 = zσ −σvθ(zσ, σ, c),
bqb = Db(A−1(bz0)).
(9)
Applying calibrated FK to bqb gives predicted tool targets for
the source and sampled feasible foreign robots. Their dis-
crepancy from the source demonstration defines LFK, us-
ing the same pose and opening terms. The action objective
becomes Lflow + λFKLFK. Frozen decoder and FK param-
eters retain derivatives with respect to their inputs, so this
loss updates the policy through the clean prediction. Tar-
gets and feasibility masks are detached and used only dur-
ing training. The supplementary implementation specifies
source-group averaging and valid-target normalization.
The policy-stage loss compares predicted tool poses and
openings with demonstration-derived targets through frozen
native decoders and calibrated FK. Equal-sized latent de-
viations can produce different tool displacements because
the decoded tool map has direction-dependent sensitivity.
This path supplies physical weighting of coordinate-space
prediction errors. Its target remains the source demonstra-
tion, so interface reconstruction residuals also contribute to
the geometric discrepancy. Cross/Full tests this supervision
with the learned action correspondence held fixed.
We separate these two opportunities for geometric su-
pervision experimentally. Source learns the interface with
γ = 0 and trains the policy with λFK = 0. Cross enables
foreign-interface supervision and keeps λFK = 0.
Full
uses the same cross-trained interface and enables the policy-
stage loss. Source and Cross match action-pretraining data,
initialization and exposure. Cross and Full share the frozen
interface. These comparisons distinguish learning a trans-
ferable action interface from geometrically correcting a pol-
icy’s clean predictions. Direct EEF matches Cross in visual,
language and state conditioning and in its flow objective. It
predicts task-frame tool poses and openings, then uses joint-
limited numerical IK to obtain native commands. The sup-
plementary implementation gives its representation, solver
and fallback settings.

5.2. How Does Geometric Supervision Help?
Table 2 separates action realization from the two stages of
geometric supervision. Cross raises held-out success over
Source by 9.5 points with OpenWAM and 8.6 points with
FastWAM, while retaining strong seen performance. Source
already learns a native action interface. The extra corre-
spondence supplied by foreign tool targets makes that inter-
face more useful for missing combinations.
Independent paired studies examine the correspondence
at scene level (Figure 4a–c). Three matched policy runs iso-
late foreign-interface supervision. Cross improves held-out
success over Source by 19, 16 and 11 points, averaging 15.3
points. The positive effect recurs under different policy ini-
tializations. Paired outcomes reveal both failures resolved
and regressions after adding each supervision stage. These
additional trials form a separate panel from the table-level
aggregates. The supplementary analysis gives the complete
paired outcomes and removes interface-selection pairings
while retaining the held-out advantage.
The interface diagnostics isolate geometry before visual
control. On certified held-out actions, Source/Cross for-
eign TCP errors are 302.13/4.37 mm, while own-body re-
construction remains accurate.
Figure 4d measures for-
eign realization, Figure 5 tests expert-command execution,
and Figure 3 tests visual-policy task completion from each
robot’s own observations.
Policy-stage FK further raises held-out success by 3.6
points with OpenWAM and 3.5 points with FastWAM (Ta-
ble 2). Cross remains slightly stronger on the seen entries.
The clean-prediction loss can correct task-space errors that
are weakly penalized in action coordinates, improving the
missing combinations in this comparison. Additional learn-
ing curves show that policy-stage gains vary across check-
points. The supplementary results retain the complete fresh-
scene and checkpoint comparisons.
5.3. Native Realization and Further Transfer
Direct EEF + IK provides a task-space alternative. Full,
combining interface and policy-stage supervision, exceeds
its held-out success by 5.0 points with OpenWAM and 4.6
points with FastWAM (Table 2). Native reconstruction an-
chors the decoder to demonstrated joint configurations, and
native decoding generates commands directly.
An inde-
pendent paired observation-to-command benchmark gives
Cross a 1.71× median model-side speedup over Direct EEF
+ IK. This measurement includes image encoding, flow pre-
diction and native command generation. The supplemen-
tary setup gives the timing protocol and solver configura-
tion.
ManiSkill3 [16] tests portability in a second simulator.
Across the three backbones, Full achieves 79.7–81.3% seen
success and 28.6–43.5% held-out success, while native-
joint policies reach 3.1–11.6% on the held-out combina-
tions (Table 1). This recurring contrast connects the frame-
work’s gains to the missing robot–task edges across dif-
ferent policy architectures. Figure 6 illustrates transfer in
recorded executions.

6. Discussion and Conclusion
Calibrated geometry lets a source demonstration supervise
native action realizations across robots.
The same rela-
tion trains action interfaces and guides policy predictions
through their frozen decoders. Matched comparisons across
backbones and simulators demonstrate improved transfer to
missing robot–task combinations.
