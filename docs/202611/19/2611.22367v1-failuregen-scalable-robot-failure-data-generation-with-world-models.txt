Abstract: Robot failures are difﬁcult to collect at scale, limiting the range of
failure scenarios represented in robot learning datasets. We present FailureGen, a
method for generating synthetic videos with temporal event annotations by reusing
action trajectories from recorded failures. The method comprises four stages:
ﬁne-tuning an action-conditioned Cosmos world model on successful and failed
real executions; annotating source recordings to identify failure-associated donor
segments and compatible successful hosts; transferring donor motion into host
contexts, with optional spatial offsets, to generate continuous video rollouts; and
annotating and ﬁltering the generated samples according to observed events and
visual quality. We study two implementations with different tasks and robot con-
ﬁgurations, with visual failure detection serving as one downstream application.
In a preliminary comparison, a detector trained on real and generated data achieves
an average precision of 0.51, compared with 0.41 using real data alone, and de-
tects 33 of 36 failure intervals, compared with 28 of 36. By reusing observed
failures in additional contexts, FailureGen expands the data available for studying
robot failures and developing downstream learning methods.
Keywords: Synthetic data generation, Robot failure generation, World models,
Failure detection

Introduction
Collecting diverse examples of robot failures at scale is difﬁcult. Failures can depend on speciﬁc
combinations of robot motion, object conﬁguration, and contact conditions. Capturing this variation
requires repeated physical executions, and less frequent failure modes may still remain underrepre-
sented.
We present FailureGen, a method for expanding failure datasets by reusing action trajectories from
recorded failures. A failure-associated donor supplies the motion, while a successful host supplies
the target scene and nominal trajectory. An adapted Cosmos world model [1] generates video roll-
outs conditioned on the host scene and transferred motion. Different hosts and optional spatial
offsets vary the context and location of the intervention, producing additional examples without
requiring a new physical execution for each video.
Prior work has explored synthetic failure construction through procedural perturbations in simu-
lation, as in AHA [2], and through action-conditioned world models with multiple veriﬁers, as in
Dream2Fix [3]. FailureGen offers a complementary approach by using observed failures as the
source of motion interventions and expanding the contexts in which these behaviors are represented.
This approach makes further use of failures that have already been captured, including events that
would be difﬁcult to collect repeatedly.

Related Work
Simulation and procedural failure generation.
Simulation provides controlled environments for
generating robot failures and studying their consequences. AHA constructs failure examples through
procedural perturbations for detection and reasoning [2]. FailSafe pairs generated failures with exe-
cutable recovery actions, using simulator execution to validate proposed corrections [4]. DenseRe-
ward synthesizes failures such as missed grasps, collisions, and object drops to learn dense rewards
for downstream planning and policy learning [5]. Guardian combines simulated action perturba-
tions with supervision constructed from real recordings for planning and execution veriﬁcation [6].
These methods demonstrate how targeted changes to robot execution can produce useful examples
of unsuccessful behavior.
World-model-based data generation.
Action-conditioned world models generate visual contin-
uations from scene observations and robot motion. Ctrl-World and Cosmos provide models and
adaptation tools for this form of generation [7, 8, 1], while RoboDream composes robot motion with
scene conditioning for data synthesis [9]. DreamGen, VLAW, and WorldSample use generated expe-
rience to support policy learning and improvement [10, 11, 12]. For failure generation, Dream2Fix
perturbs actions, generates candidate outcomes with a world model, and applies multiple veriﬁers to
construct recovery supervision [3]. StressDream steers generated futures toward speciﬁed outcomes,
including failures, by optimizing the initial diffusion noise [13]. FailureGen builds on conditional
video generation by supplying motion derived from recorded failures and transferring it into com-
patible host contexts.
The reliability of these generated interactions is also an active research topic. MiraBench evaluates
physical consistency, action following, and optimism bias [14]. WorldSimProbe examines action
realization and interaction dynamics, including a donor–receiver setup that replays actions from one
task in another scene [15]. This provides a related use of motion–scene composition for evaluating
world models. Together, these studies motivate assessing generated motion and interaction outcomes
alongside visual quality.
Trajectory reuse and data augmentation.
Recorded demonstrations provide another basis for
expanding robot datasets.
MimicGen transforms demonstrated motion for new object conﬁgu-
rations [16], while DemoGen adapts trajectories and synthesizes corresponding point-cloud ob-
servations through spatial transformations [17].
Real2Render2Real reconstructs object motion
and appearance from recordings to render robot demonstrations without object-dynamics simula-
tion [18]. GenAug and ROSIE expand visual diversity through generative edits to recorded observa-

These lines of work offer complementary ways to expand robot experience. Capturing the diversity
of failures, particularly the long tail of events across changing scenes and interaction conditions,
remains challenging. FailureGen contributes to this effort by reusing observed failure motion in ad-
ditional compatible contexts. Such motion provides an additional source of interventions that could
be combined with procedural perturbations, visual augmentation, and guided world-model genera-
tion to broaden failure coverage. The resulting data may support several learning objectives: prior
work studies failure detection [21], diagnosis and corrective guidance [22], and reward learning [5].
This paper evaluates visual failure detection as one downstream application.
3
Method
Problem setup.
We are given a source dataset Dsrc of successful and failed robot executions. Each
episode contains synchronized visual observations o0:N and measured robot states s0:N, where t
indexes sampling instants and N is the number of transitions. The subscript range includes both
endpoints. Our goal is to construct an additional dataset Dgen of generated video–motion pairs with
temporal event annotations, expanding the contexts in which rare robot failures are represented.
We call a recording that supplies failure-associated motion a donor, and a successful recording
that supplies a target scene and nominal motion a host. The generated data can support multiple
downstream learning tasks; in this paper, we evaluate its utility for visual failure detection.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, FailureGen comprises four components: world-model adaptation, source
annotation, motion-conditioned generation, and annotation and ﬁltering of generated data. We adapt
the model using successful and failed executions, identify compatible donor–host segments, and
transfer failure-associated motion into host scenes, optionally varying the intervention location. Fi-
nally, we label the generated events and ﬁlter unreliable examples.

We adapt a pretrained Cosmos world model [1] using aligned video–motion windows from success-
ful and failed executions. We combine forward- and inverse-dynamics supervision with the aim of
improving spatial dynamics: the visual objective supervises scene evolution under robot motion,
while the action objective supervises motion associated with observed visual changes.
In forward-dynamics examples, the model generates a visual continuation conditioned on initial
observations, the recorded motion sequence, and a text description. In inverse-dynamics examples,
it predicts motion conditioned on the observed video. Each training sample is randomly assigned
to one of these two directions, using the model’s rectiﬁed-ﬂow objective in video-latent or action
space, respectively. Text descriptions provide task or execution context; each experimental study
speciﬁes whether task, phase, or event-speciﬁc captions are used.
Motion enters the model through domain-speciﬁc input and output projections, with a dedicated
domain for each embodiment and motion representation. Fine-tuning updates the generation expert
and its associated video, action, and conditioning modules, while keeping the remaining parameters
frozen. Implementation details and study-speciﬁc adaptation settings are provided in Appendix A.
Motion encoding, coordinate conventions, visual preprocessing, and normalization remain consis-
tent between adaptation and generation. We denote the adapted parameters by θ∗and the forward
model’s conditional distribution over visual continuations by pθ∗.
3.2
Temporal annotation of source data
We annotate the source recordings to locate motion segments that can be transferred. An agentic
labeling pipeline using vision-capable LLMs (Appendix D) identiﬁes task steps, failures, and recov-
eries, assigning each event a category and start and end times. We distinguish the failure event itself
from its visible aftermath, and label these intervals independently of whether the episode eventually
succeeds. Proposed labels are checked for consistent categories and valid timestamps, and can be
corrected during review.
These annotations guide donor and host selection. We match failure-associated donor segments
with successful host segments at corresponding task steps, checking compatibility of their robot and
object conﬁgurations. Recordings are partitioned by episode before selection, with only training
recordings serving as donors or hosts. Evaluation recordings are also excluded from world-model
adaptation and normalization.

generated video determines the observed event and its timing.
3.4
Annotation and ﬁltering
We annotate generated videos using the same labeling pipeline as the source data. Labels describe
the events visible in each video, which may differ from the intended intervention. Because world
models can produce visual artifacts, implausible interactions, or unclear outcomes, a cleanup ﬁlter
can exclude clips that cannot provide reliable training examples. The annotation and ﬁltering policy
used for each study is speciﬁed in the experiments.
Each retained example contains the generated video, its associated motion sequence, temporal event
labels, and source identiﬁers. These video–motion pairs form Dgen and provide data for downstream
learning tasks.
4
Experimental Studies
Our experiments examine two aspects of FailureGen: its ability to expand the available failure data
across host contexts, and the usefulness of these generated examples for downstream learning. We
study an in-house industrial bimanual manipulation task and a focused three-task ViFailback follow-
up. The industrial setting emphasizes multistage execution and interactions with surrounding equip-
ment, while ViFailback examines additional manipulation scenarios and downstream model sizes.
Both settings use an adapted Cosmos3-Nano world model with forward- and inverse-dynamics su-
pervision. Downstream evaluation compares ﬁne-tuning on real data alone with ﬁne-tuning on the
same real data augmented with FailureGen examples. The industrial experiment additionally in-
cludes a pretrained reference without task-speciﬁc ﬁne-tuning.
Visual veriﬁcation and ﬁltering.
Generated videos are assessed using the agentic annotation
pipeline and visually checked and veriﬁed by human reviewers. Review considers visual coherence,
preservation of the host scene, and the events depicted in each generation. Unsuitable examples are
removed before the data are considered for downstream use.
Across the accumulated generation experiments, ﬁltering removes 9.8% of the industrial data and
11.2% of the ViFailback data, leaving 90.2% and 88.8%, respectively, usable. These cumulative
usability rates are reported separately from the experiment-speciﬁc selection of failure clips for
downstream training. Review procedures and ﬁltering details are provided in the appendix.

4.1
Industrial bimanual manipulation
Task and data.
Our in-house dataset captures a bimanual YAM robot performing an industrial
material-handling task: picking up an SMT reel, transferring it to the other arm, and placing it onto
a stack. Successful execution requires coordinated grasping, handover, and placement. Failures
include pickup misses, pickup slips, and handover drops, which can produce further interactions
with the table, tote, and surrounding objects.
The source collection contains 80 real episodes: 45 clean executions, 12 pickup misses, 13 pickup
slips, and 10 handover drops. World-model adaptation and downstream detector training use the
same 66 training episodes.
The remaining 14 recordings provide downstream validation data.
Generator-speciﬁc validation and test allocations are detailed in Appendix B.
World-model conditioning uses measured end-effector motion and gripper state for both arms. Each
arm contributes translation, a six-dimensional rotation representation, and gripper state, yielding a
20-dimensional conditioning representation.
Failure-data expansion.
FailureGen transfers failure-associated motion into successful host
recordings, reusing their scene appearance and initial robot–object conﬁgurations. This allows a
failure mechanism to unfold in additional contexts: for example, the consequences of releasing a
reel depend on its position relative to the table and tote.
For the downstream experiment reported here, the annotation pipeline identiﬁes 75 synthetic failure
clips from 108 donor-transfer candidates. These clips span 26 distinct host recordings and 23
failure donors. Of the selected clips, 69 reproduce the intended failure mechanism and six exhibit
another observed failure, which determines their assigned label.
The resulting clips expand the available examples of pickup misses, pickup slips, and handover
drops. Source–generation comparisons examine how the host scene is preserved while the trans-
ferred motion produces different object trajectories and interactions with surrounding surfaces.
Figure 2 illustrates a donor–host transfer in the industrial task. The donor shows a reel falling
outside the bin, while the host maintains its grasp during transport. The generated continuation
preserves the host’s blue reel and scene arrangement while depicting a drop onto the surrounding
table, transferring the failure behavior to a different object and initial conﬁguration.

Downstream evaluation.
We evaluate Cosmos3-Edge-based failure recognition under three con-
ditions: no task-speciﬁc ﬁne-tuning, real-only ﬁne-tuning, and ﬁne-tuning with FailureGen augmen-
tation. Both ﬁne-tuning conditions use the same 66 real training episodes; the augmented condition
adds the 75 generated failure clips. Inputs contain video without robot telemetry, and the prediction
target is active failure during execution.
Checkpoints and decision thresholds are selected using real validation recordings. Evaluation uses
a separate real recording session containing 74 eligible episodes: 41 clean episodes and 33 episodes
containing 36 annotated failure events. An event is detected if an alert falls within its annotated
interval. We also measure average precision and the number of clean episodes containing an alert.
Model implementation, training budgets, and evaluation-label review are detailed in Appendix B.
Results.
The model trained with FailureGen augmentation detects 33 of 36 failure events
(91.67%), compared with 28 of 36 (77.78%) for the strongest real-only conﬁguration and 25 of
36 (69.44%) without ﬁne-tuning (Table 1). This corresponds to a 13.9-percentage-point increase
in event recall over the strongest real-only baseline.
The augmented model detects all 12 pickup-slip events and all ten handover-drop events, together
with 11 of 14 pickup misses. It achieves the highest average precision, 0.51, and raises no alarms
in the 41 clean episodes. The augmented conﬁguration therefore provides greater failure-event
coverage while maintaining reliable behavior on clean executions.
4.2
Focused ViFailback evaluation
Tasks and setup.
Following an initial task-level screening, we conduct a dedicated follow-up on
three ViFailback tasks [22]: placing an object into a black box, placing a vial and test tube into
rack slots, and opening a drawer, inserting a can, and closing the drawer. These tasks cover object
placement, precise rack placement, and articulated-object manipulation. Additional conditioning
and evaluation details are provided in Appendix C.
The available host recordings vary in object identity, arrangement, tabletop appearance, clutter, and
background conﬁguration. FailureGen uses joint-motion and gripper-state conditioning to produce
additional examples within these recorded scene contexts. The generation mixture and source-
selection details are provided in the appendix.
For downstream evaluation, Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-8B are ﬁne-tuned on the focused three-
task dataset. For each model, the initial condition uses real training recordings, while the augmented
condition uses the same real recordings together with task-matched FailureGen examples. Both
conditions are evaluated on the same real evaluation episodes, which are excluded from training and
donor–host selection.
We report episode-level accuracy and failure recall. Accuracy measures correct success/failure clas-
siﬁcation across all evaluation episodes; failure recall measures the fraction of failure episodes cor-
rectly identiﬁed.

Results.
FailureGen augmentation improves both accuracy and failure recall in ﬁve of the six
model–task combinations, with the remaining combination unchanged (Table 2).
The largest improvement occurs in drawer manipulation. Qwen3-VL-2B accuracy increases from
84.4% to 97.0%, while failure recall increases from 82.5% to 96.7%. Qwen3-VL-8B also im-
proves, reaching 97.0% accuracy and 96.7% failure recall.
For object-into-black-box placement, both models reach 100% accuracy and failure recall with
augmentation. Qwen3-VL-2B improves from 94.1% accuracy and 93.3% recall, while Qwen3-VL-
8B improves from 98.5% accuracy and 98.3% recall.
For vial/test-tube rack placement, Qwen3-VL-8B improves from 49.6% to 54.1% accuracy and from
43.3% to 48.3% failure recall. Qwen3-VL-2B remains unchanged. These results extend the ob-
served utility of FailureGen-augmented training to additional manipulation tasks and two down-
stream model sizes.
5
Conclusion
We presented FailureGen, a method for expanding long-tail robot failure data through motion-
conditioned world-model generation. By transferring failure-associated motion into compatible host
contexts, FailureGen produces additional video–motion examples with varied visual contexts and in-
teractions, annotated according to their observed outcomes.
Experiments on industrial bimanual manipulation and a focused ViFailback subset show that these
examples can support improved failure recognition on real recordings. Together, the results support
FailureGen as a way to extend the learning value of limited failure demonstrations and motivate its
evaluation in broader downstream robotics tasks.

A
Implementation Details
World-model adaptation.
We adapt Cosmos3-Nano through forward- and inverse-dynamics su-
pervision. Fine-tuning updates the generation expert, time embedding, video and action projec-
tions, and action-modality embedding; the remaining parameters are frozen.
The correspond-
ing implementation modules are moe_gen, time_embedder, vae2llm, llm2vae, action2llm,
llm2action, and action_modality_embed.
Generation inputs.
Both studies use windows containing 17 observations at 5 Hz and 16 motion
transitions, spanning 3.2 s between the ﬁrst and last observations. Motion is derived from measured
robot states at the corresponding sampling instants. Training-derived normalization is reused during
generation. Neutral task descriptions provide context without specifying a future failure.
Motion variation.
Donor motion is aligned with the selected host segment, with transitions con-
necting the intervention to the host motion. For ViFailback, variation scales joint-motion excursions
around the host anchor by factors {0.95, 0.975, 1.025, 1.05} while preserving gripper values and
timing. Each generated example retains its donor and host identiﬁers, conditioning sequence, trans-
fer settings, and inference seed.
B
Industrial Experiment Details
Data and generator.
The 80 source recordings are partitioned into 66 generator-training, seven
validation, and seven test episodes. The training partition contains 37 clean executions, ten pickup
misses, eleven pickup slips, and eight handover drops. Downstream detectors use the same 66
training episodes and combine the remaining 14 recordings for validation. All synthetic donors and
hosts come from training.
Generator observations are subsampled from 30 to 5 Hz and assembled into a 960 × 960 canvas,
with the overhead view above the two wrist views. The 20-dimensional conditioning contains rela-
tive end-effector translation, six-dimensional rotation, and absolute gripper state for each arm. Poses
are obtained through forward kinematics, with relative motion computed as T −1
t
Tt+1. Normaliza-
tion uses training-only ﬁrst and 99th percentiles. Equal-probability forward- and inverse-dynamics
training uses batch size 16 for 1,243 updates.
Synthetic supervision.
The generation cohort pairs each of 27 failure donors with two hosts and
renders each pairing with seeds 0 and 1. The 75 selected failure clips contain 18 pickup misses, 27
pickup slips, and 30 handover drops. They contribute 128 positive decision targets. Only active-
failure timestamps in synthetic clips receive supervision; other frames remain available as temporal
context.
Detector implementation.
Fine-tuned detectors initialize independently from Cosmos3-Edge re-
vision 2a00e87e and train its visual tower, projector, and causal binary prediction heads. Each
prediction receives seven RGB observations at 5 Hz, spanning 1.2 s, on a 640 × 320 three-view
canvas. The target indicates active failure at the ﬁnal timestamp; unknown labels are excluded. The
pretrained reference retains the language decoder and scores yes/no responses under a ﬁxed prompt.
Training and model selection.
Table 3 summarizes the ﬁne-tuned conﬁgurations. The stronger
real-only baseline in the main paper uses a 5:1 negative-to-positive target ratio and positive-loss
weight 5.0. Each run is conﬁgured for ﬁve epochs; validation average precision selects the epoch-4
checkpoints. Decision thresholds maximize validation frame F1 and remain ﬁxed during evaluation.
The conﬁgurations share their real source episodes but differ in sampled negatives and optimization
budgets.

C
ViFailback Experiment Details
Tasks and conditioning.
The focused evaluation covers object-into-black-box placement (task
49), vial/test-tube rack placement (task 55), and drawer/can manipulation (task 80). Condition-
ing contains twelve measured joint-motion channels and two absolute gripper-position channels.
Overhead and wrist observations are subsampled from 25 to 5 Hz. Generation uses the adapted
Cosmos3-Nano exponential-moving-average checkpoint at update 1,500, selected using validation
replay ﬁdelity.
Host variation and generation.
Hosts provide variation in object identity for black-box place-
ment, tabletop appearance and setup for rack placement, and background objects and cabinet pose
for drawer manipulation. Generated examples include donor-motion transfers, host-motion controls,
and original-context failure replays, with labels assigned from their observed outcomes. Repeated
inference seeds provide additional renders of a source pairing rather than additional donor–host
contexts.
Downstream comparison.
Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-8B are ﬁne-tuned on the three selected
tasks. Initial models use real training recordings; augmented models use the same real recordings
with task-matched synthetic examples. Both conditions are evaluated on the same real recordings,
excluded from training and donor–host selection.
Accuracy measures correct episode-level success/failure classiﬁcation. Failure recall measures the
fraction of failure episodes identiﬁed correctly. These episode-level metrics are reported separately
from the industrial study’s active-event detection metrics.

D
Agentic temporal annotation
The labeling pipeline coordinates vision-capable LLM workers through evidence preparation, label
proposal, and review. Given episode videos and a task-speciﬁc label vocabulary, it prepares chrono-
logical frame grids with synchronized camera views and source-frame indices. Workers inspect
overlapping windows to propose task phases, failure events, and recovery intervals. Independent
review passes check these proposals and look for missed events.
Candidate events and temporal boundaries receive closer inspection using denser frame sequences.
Disagreements trigger additional visual adjudication, while unresolved cases are ﬂagged for human
review. When available, robot telemetry can nominate intervals for inspection; event labels are
determined from visual evidence.
Structural validation checks label categories, timestamp bounds, interval overlaps, and required
timeline coverage. The output records event categories, temporal boundaries, source references,
and review status. A review interface lets human annotators inspect the videos and correct labels
or boundaries. Episode processing runs in parallel, with intermediate results retained for resuming
interrupted annotation.
