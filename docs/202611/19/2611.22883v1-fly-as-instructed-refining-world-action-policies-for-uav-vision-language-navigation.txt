Abstract 

Natural language-guided UAV navigation enables intuitive human–robot interaction and flexible autonomous flight in complex environments. However, reliable navigation requires accurate instruction following, continuous task progress tracking, and safe obstacle avoidance, which re-main challenging for existing world–action models that pri-marily focus on action prediction. To address these limita-tions, we propose a world–action learning framework that integrates three complementary mechanisms: a language-conditioned action interface with targeted post-training for instruction-responsive motion, recurrent instruction-consumption prediction to track task progress and determine termination, and Gaussian-scene post-training to enhance obstacle avoidance through trajectory imitation and differ-entiable geometric feedback. Closed-loop evaluations and ablation studies demonstrate improvements in sequential task completion, safe goal reaching, and timely termination. Furthermore, direct one-step denoising reduces inference overhead, and real-world indoor and outdoor flights validate the framework’s effectiveness. 

1. Introduction 

Aerial vision–language navigation (VLN) enables unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) to follow natural-language instruc-tions using visual observations [8, 32]. By expressing goals through objects, spatial relations and motion com-mands, users can specify flight tasks without constructing detailed waypoint sequences. This capability supports in-tuitive human–robot interaction and flexible autonomous navigation in complex environments. Reliable aerial VLN requires the vehicle to ground language in continuous three-dimensional motion, track the progress of multi-stage tasks, and avoid obstacles throughout execution. Recent world–action models couple visual representa-tions with action generation [35], providing a foundation for aerial policies such as WorldVLN and WorldFly [37, 38]. Turning these predictions into complete flights brings three learning challenges. First, action policies can rely on visual shortcuts when the requested motion changes [9], making language-responsive control essential. Second, multi-stage instructions require persistent knowledge of completed re-quirements to guide subsequent actions and determine when to stop [14, 21]. Third, obstacle avoidance requires feedback on the path induced by predicted actions and the vehicle’s motion response [36]. These challenges connect instruction grounding, execution history and physical motion within the same control loop. In this paper, we propose a world–action UAV frame-work that connects language response, task progress and obstacle-aware motion (Fig. 1). The action expert reads the instruction through text cross-attention and undergoes tar-geted post-training for instruction-responsive motion while preserving pretrained conditioning modules. A recurrent instruction-consumption head predicts completed require-ments, continuously reweights the complete instruction in-side the frozen executor, and supplies task-level stopping. Safety post-training in reconstructed Gaussian scenes com-bines successful-detour imitation with geometric feedback differentiated through vehicle tracking dynamics. Con-strained adapter updates retain general-task behavior, and direct one-step denoising reduces deployment cost. A unified closed-loop evaluation and targeted controls assess navigation, progress and safety. Equal-budget data controls test language-conditioned response, while six indoor and outdoor flights demonstrate the integrated system. Our contributions are: • A language-conditioned action interface and targeted post-training design for instruction-responsive UAV motion, evaluated with matched training and paired text interven-tions. • A recurrent instruction-consumption interface that predicts completed requirements from visual and execution history, continuously reweights the complete instruction inside a 1arXiv:2611.22883v1 [cs.RO] 17 Nov 2026 frozen executor, and supplies task-level stopping. • Gaussian-scene obstacle-avoidance post-training that learns from successful detours and differentiable pathwise geometric feedback, with constrained updates that retain general-task behavior. 

2. Related Work 

Aerial VLN draws on three lines of research: language-guided navigation, task progress estimation, and learning to avoid obstacles in flight. 

Language-guided UAV navigation. VLN has developed from panoramic navigation graphs [1] to continuous envi-ronments [19] and long-horizon instruction execution [28]. Aerial VLN extends this setting to three-dimensional motion: HTNav organizes aerial decisions hierarchically [8], UAV-Flow provides fine-grained flight demonstrations and sim-ulation evaluation [32], and FLIGHT supplies multi-stage demonstrations with state-reasoning text [39]. HUGE-Bench emphasizes process correctness and safety [11]. Generative visual models support trajectory planning and language grounding through visual imagination [2, 23]. With pretrained video representations such as Wan [31], world– action models connect visual prediction and action gener-ation [35], with aerial examples including WorldVLN and WorldFly [37, 38]. UAV-Flow++ further introduces online post-training for FlowVLA [33]. CAST shows how counter-factual labels expand instruction–action diversity [10], and LIBERO-CF shows how visual shortcuts can persist when instructions change [9]. We combine language conditioning with targeted action-expert post-training and use matched training and paired instructions to assess the resulting lan-guage response. Action policies generate commands through diffusion, flow matching, or discrete tokens [7, 18, 20]. Prior effi-ciency work uses visual compression and latent subgoals with consistency flow matching [6, 34]. Our action expert supports direct one-step denoising, reducing deployment cost within the same policy architecture. 

Task progress and instruction execution. Self-Monitoring learns navigation progress [21], and sub-instruction-aware navigation uses fine-grained path annotations to shift attention across instruction seg-ments [12]. VLN-AIEP suppresses completed content using progress learned from navigation labels [14], while Route2Step uses a step-level interface to connect semantic progress with local actions [15]. Coverage attention provides a related mechanism for tracking consumed content in sequence generation [30]. Our recurrent head learns token satisfaction from clause completion. The same completion state continuously reweights the full instruction inside a frozen action expert and determines when the UAV should stop. 

Learning obstacle avoidance for UAVs. Gaussian splat-ting supports scene rendering [17] as well as online plan-ning and localization in Splat-Nav [4] and action filtering in SAFER-Splat [5]. Differentiable flight training shapes the policy itself by propagating feedback through simulated vehicle motion [36]. We use reconstructed Gaussian scenes to post-train a language-conditioned flight policy. The ac-tion expert learns from successful detours and geometric feedback differentiated through vehicle tracking dynamics. Our world–action policy brings these lines of work to-gether to execute complete flight tasks from natural language, using learned progress to guide execution and stopping and safety post-training to shape flight behavior. 

3. Method 

The Action Policy uses direct one-step denoising after post-training and supports two execution modules: a consumption head that reweights the complete instruction and triggers stopping, and safety adapters that shape obstacle-aware mo-tion. The Full System enables both. Figure 1 summarizes their backbone and learning signals. 

3.1. Language-conditioned action learning 

At decision time t, the policy receives four RGB history frames Ht, proprioceptive state st and instruction features c.It generates a four-action chunk of body-frame XYZ and yaw increments with one denoising step, using cached instruction features. A Wan-based video branch [31] supplies visual conditioning to a 12-layer action expert; inference operates on the observed history without generating an explicit future video. The action expert retains shared attention and text cross-attention, followed by an action mixer (DiM) with separate temporal and channel MLPs [29]. Text cross-attention sup-plies language throughout action generation; the progress interface in Sec. 3.2 modulates its logits as requirements are completed. Implementation details are given in the supple-ment. Starting from a UAV-pretrained policy, we post-train the action expert for instruction-responsive motion while freez-ing the visual, VAE and proprioceptive modules. The result-ing policy directly supports one-step denoising for deploy-ment; a UAV-only continuation provides an equal-budget data control. Training schedules, data composition and matched evaluation settings are detailed in the supplement.

3.3. Gaussian-scene safety adaptation 

We train low-rank action-expert adapters [13] in recon-structed Gaussian scenes [17], freezing the remaining policy. A bounded tracking response executes body-frame displace-ment and yaw commands with velocity, acceleration and delay. The same map F advances safety rollouts and sup-plies geometric gradients with observations fixed. Gaussian rendering supplies observations outside the gradient path. The supplement gives the dynamics and objectives. 

Persistent exploration and outcome filtering. A sampled lateral detour is held over an initial window and released at a displacement cap. Paired rollouts share the scene, start pose and action noise, while mirrored detours explore both sides [24, 26]. The full instruction remains available after the detour; progress updates change its attention bias. Successful contact-free flights with positive group-relative advantage enter replay. Self-imitation [22] regresses predicted actions toward detached successful executed actions, with replay balanced across scenes and directions.

4. Experimental Setup 

Data and tasks. We evaluate 521 closed-loop episodes: 181 general-task, 170 safety, and 170 progress episodes, cov-ering UAV-Flow subtasks, composite instructions and com-plex scenes. Test instances or layouts differ from training configurations. Main comparisons use the complete suite; module ablations use the corresponding subsets. 

Comparison methods. We compare reference systems from OpenVLA [18], π0.5 [3], UAV-Flow++ [33], HUGE-Bench [11], WorldVLN [37], and Fast-WAM [35]. Methods share evaluation tasks, initial states, instructions, budgets and scoring rules while retaining native model interfaces. Internal controls cover equal-budget post-training and the progress and safety modules. 

Implementation. Post-training updates the action expert with frozen conditioning modules. Latency is measured on an RTX 5880 Ada at batch one using four 256 × 256 

history frames, four output actions and cached text. Full-model timing covers observation encoding, action generation and output transfer; HTTP timing is reported separately. Six real-world tasks each have one recorded flight using the final teacher with direct one-step denoising, progress consumption and safety adaptation on an RTX 5070, with 10 Hz trajectory updates and 50 Hz control. 

Metrics. We report goal reach ( SR reach ), correct stop-ping ( SR stop ), path fidelity (nDTW [16]), ordered comple-tion (OC), safe-flight success (SFR), and collision-free rate (CFR). Correct stopping requires ordered completion and policy-issued termination. Failed and uncertain outcomes remain in the denominators. Task retention measures reach success on the 181 general tasks. 5. Results 

We evaluate complete flights, the contribution of each learn-ing mechanism, and efficient physical deployment. 

5.1. Overall results on the unified test suite 

The full system improves arrival, ordered execution, and termination together. Table 1 reports aggregate performance on the unified 521-episode test suite. Ours (Full System) achieves 83.7% goal-reach success, 77.0% stop success, 0.648 nDTW, 79.8% OC, and 76.6% SFR, with an 86.8% collision-free rate. WorldVLN is the strongest external ref-erence on reach, stop, nDTW, OC and SFR, scoring 45.3%, 31.5%, 0.448, 34.7% and 30.3%, respectively. The full system improves reach by 38.4 percentage points and stop success by 45.5 points over this reference. Relative to our action policy, adding safety adaptation and instruction consumption raises reach from 54.5% to 83.7%, OC from 43.6% to 79.8%, and SFR from 40.3% to 76.6%. 

Complementary module effects. Table 2 separates the module effects. Safety adaptation raises safety-subset SFR from 30.6% to 78.2%, with progress OC changing from 24.1% to 24.7%. Consumption yields 81.2% progress OC and 77.1% stop success. Together, the modules retain 78.2% safety SFR, 81.2% progress OC, and 77.1% progress stop success, while general-task reach rises from 76.8% to 84.0%. The combination retains the reported gains in safe arrival, ordered execution, and stopping across these subsets.

5.4. Targeted safety-navigation ablation 

Safety-adaptation ablation. Table 5 evaluates the safety adapter on 170 safety episodes and measures task retention on 181 general-task episodes. The full method achieves 78.2% SFR with a 17.1% collision rate, compared with 30.6% and 64.1% for the base policy. Replacing persis-tent exploration with per-step noise reduces SFR to 46.5%; removing outcome filtering reduces it to 40.0%. Removing auxiliary signals yields 59.4% SFR. Removing constrained updates retains 72.9% SFR but lowers general-task reach to 53.6%, compared with 76.2% for the full method and 76.8% for the base policy. The auxiliary ablation measures geometric and side feedback jointly; the update ablation mea-sures the norm cap and KL constraint jointly. These bundles improve safe arrival and preserve general-task behavior. Supplementary first-person sequences show successful detours and a wrong-way failure of a fixed safety candidate.

6. Discussion and Limitations 

High-speed flight leaves less time to react to new observa-tions, motivating further reductions in end-to-end policy la-tency. The recurrent head tracks progress through an instruc-tion, while much longer missions may also require recalling landmarks or decisions from earlier in the flight. Extend-ing the policy with longer-term memory is a direction for these tasks. Generalization can also improve in environments whose layouts or visual conditions differ substantially from those seen during training. 

7. Conclusion 

We present a world–action policy for UAV vision–language navigation that combines targeted action post-training, recur-rent task progress prediction, and obstacle avoidance learned in reconstructed Gaussian scenes. In simulation, the method leads the evaluated baselines in complete instruction exe-cution and in joint success on contrastive and paraphrased instruction pairs. Recurrent progress estimates guide atten-tion to unfinished requirements and support timely stopping. Safety post-training combines successful detour imitation with geometric feedback through a differentiable tracking model, improving safe goal reaching while preserving gen-eral navigation performance.
