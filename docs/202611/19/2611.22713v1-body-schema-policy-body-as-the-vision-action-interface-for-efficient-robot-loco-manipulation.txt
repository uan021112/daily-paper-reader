Abstract
Currently, robots can learn whole-body loco-manipulation
through scale: the strongest policies have billions of param-
eters and still need task-specific fine-tuning. Humans have
limited processing capacity, yet learn and act efficiently
through a body schema that organizes what they see and
how they move. We introduce Body Schema Policy (BSP), an
imitation learning policy that builds such a schema into the
vision–action interface. Its latent body schema places joint
and link tokens on the robot’s kinematic tree, where learned
screw motions carry features between links. Through body-
queried vision, each body part retrieves its own visual ev-
idence, which conditions the denoising of its actions. With
this inductive bias, BSP returns an action chunk faster than
any policy we compare.
Compact, body-specific visual
readouts also emerge without spatial supervision: manipu-
lation commands depend strongly on task objects, while lo-
comotion commands respond more broadly to the scene. On
nine simulated Unitree G1 whole-body tasks, BSP matches
the Ψ0 humanoid foundation model with 75× fewer param-
eters and nearly doubles Diffusion Policy’s success. The
same holds on a real G1 and a Fetch mobile manipulator.
1. Introduction
We study visuomotor policy learning for whole-body loco-
manipulation, where a robot must coordinate locomotion
and precise manipulation from camera images and proprio-
ception. Recent progress has combined large-scale pretrain-
ing with increasingly capable vision–language–action mod-
els (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs) [4, 5, 40, 44].
These models bring progress from manipulation to loco-
manipulation, but with billions of parameters, each must
still be fine-tuned on demonstrations for every task before
deployment. Humans, in contrast, have limited processing
capacity and attention [27], yet learn and carry out hard
whole-body skills efficiently. This raises our central ques-
tion: Can a visuomotor policy learn whole-body skills as
efficiently as humans do?
Human motor control suggests an answer: perception
and action are organized around the body. A body schema
represents the body’s structure, posture, and movement [13,
26]. Vision follows the body: visual receptive fields move
with the arm [11], people look toward task-relevant lo-
cations before acting on them [16, 18], and focal vision
examines detail in the small area where the hands work,
while ambient vision guides locomotion through the space

at large [37]. Movement is refined on the same schema:
an internal model predicts how the body will move under a
motor command and combines that prediction with sensory
feedback to estimate the body’s state [42]. These observa-
tions motivate a robot representation of the body in which
an action hypothesis decides what each body part looks at,
and what it sees refines the action.
Common visuomotor policy architectures, however, are
not designed around such structure: they represent the ac-
tion as a flat vector of coordinates and the image as features
shared by the whole action. For example, Diffusion Policy
conditions the action chunk on a pooled visual represen-
tation shared across coordinates [8]. As a result, how the
joints move together, and which part of the image matters
to which part of the body, stay hidden from the network and
must be learned from demonstrations [47]. Our forward-
kinematics experiments illustrate the first point: generic ar-
chitectures such as MLPs and Transformers, even with mor-
phology masks, struggle to extrapolate beyond their training
range (Section 4.4). Building this structure into the vision–
action interface can make today’s policies more efficient.
To address the problem, we bring the structure of hu-
man motor control to robot policies with Body Schema Pol-
icy (BSP), a visuomotor policy for loco-manipulation with
three components (Figures 1 and 2). First, a latent body
schema places joint and link tokens on the robot’s body
graph, together with a global token for whole-body features.
A Neural Screw Operator propagates link features through
learned latent screw motions using the graph’s parent–child
relations. Second, body-queried vision lets body tokens in-
dependently retrieve visual evidence from a pretrained en-
coder’s patch tokens. Third, body-aware action denoising
uses the resulting schema to condition a shared temporal
decoder for each action coordinate. The current noisy ac-
tion chunk initializes the schema, so its representation and
visual queries can change as denoising refines the action.
We evaluate BSP on two embodiments: a Unitree G1
humanoid, in the SIMPLE benchmark [41] and in the real
world, and a wheeled mobile manipulator in ManiSkill-
HAB [35]. The main findings are:
1. Action denoising on the latent body schema is fast
and accurate. After five of 100 iterations, the median
error of BSP’s next action on training frames is below
2◦, against 7–12◦for a Diffusion Policy denoiser of the
same size, and BSP returns action chunks faster than any
other policy we compare (Section 4.4, Figure 5).
2. Body-specific visual readouts. Without spatial supervi-
sion, BSP develops distinct visual sensitivities for ma-
nipulation and locomotion: manipulation commands de-
pend strongly on task objects, while base commands re-
spond more broadly to the scene (Section 4.4, Figure 6).
BSP also keeps up with foundation models many times
its size under distractors, lighting and material changes,
and leads every method when poses change (Table 1).
3. Foundation-model-level task performance from a
compact policy. On simulated G1 whole-body tasks,
the 35M-parameter BSP matches Ψ0’s success rate with
75× fewer parameters and nearly doubles Diffusion Pol-
icy’s success rate (Figure 1, Table 1). On the real G1
whole-body tasks, it also ties with the Ψ0 foundation
model, far ahead of Diffusion Policy (Table 3). On the
Fetch mobile manipulator, it ties with the π0.5 founda-
tion model and beats both policies of its own size on all
three subtasks (Table 2).
2. Related work
Visuomotor policies at scale
Visuomotor imitation-
learning (IL) methods such as Diffusion Policy and ACT
learn action chunks from camera observations [8, 48].
Vision–language–action models build on pretrained vision–
language representations [4–6, 17, 40], while world-action
models such as DreamZero jointly model future video and
actions [30, 44, 45]. These policies solve many manipula-
tion tasks, and the largest have billions of parameters. In
loco-manipulation, the same policies command a wheeled
base directly or, on humanoids, a large-scale pretrained
whole-body controller [7, 20, 21] that executes their com-
mands while maintaining balance. While scaling data, com-
pute and model size drove most of this progress, we study a
complementary axis, efficiency: how explicit body structure
can organize perception and action generation.
Body structure in robot policies
Robot body-schema
learning studies representations of a robot’s own struc-
ture and state [15]. Graph policies [33, 39], Body Trans-
former [34], MetaMorph [12] and a morphology-aware
VLA expert [36] shape their computation by the body’s
graph, but none models how the joints’ motions compose.
Implicit kinematic policies [10], HDP [24] and KStar Dif-
fuser [22] are instead given this composition as exact for-
ward kinematics, which ties them to an idealized robot
model. The Rodrigues Network [47] embeds rotation-based
kinematic composition in learned operators, but its pub-
lished policy takes no images. BSP, in contrast, propagates
latent features with full screw transforms, and its body to-
kens query the image.
Vision–action interfaces for robot policies
Visual pre-
training has expanded robot perception from compact em-
beddings [25, 29] to dense patch features from vision trans-
formers [1, 9, 31, 46] and vision–language backbones [2, 3].
The vision–action interface determines how these features
are selected and used to generate actions. Patch Policy [49]
lets transformer policies consume pretrained patch tokens
directly. Robot-centric Pooling [50] uses proprioception to

4. Experiments
We compare BSP with state-of-the-art policies on task suc-
cess and inference speed, ablate its parts, and analyze its
operator, denoising, and visual representation.
4.1. Experimental setup
SIMPLE
We evaluate nine tasks from six categories
of the SIMPLE benchmark [41] (Figure 3, right), cov-
ering rigid-object grasping (Tabletop Grasp, TTG; Bend
& Pick, BPMP), bimanual handover (Handover, HAND;
Bend & Handover, BHT), mobile picking (Move & Pick,
XMP; Move, Bend & Pick, XMBP), whole-body loco-
manipulation (Pick, Loco. & Place, LPBT), non-prehensile
pushing (Push Chair, POC), and articulated-object opening
(Move & Open, OTC). Each task has about 100 demon-
strations. Predicted actions are executed by the decoupled
whole-body controller shipped with GR00T N1.6 [20]. Ev-
ery policy runs in a closed loop in the benchmark’s evalua-
tor on 30 fixed episodes per task, 10 in each of its three eval-
uation levels: distractors (L0), materials and lighting (L1),
and object and robot poses (L2). Success is the benchmark’s
own criterion for each task.
ManiSkill-HAB
In ManiSkill-HAB [35], we evaluate
BSP on a second embodiment: the Fetch mobile manip-
ulator (Figure 3, bottom left). Its base and torso move by
translation, which the screw operator models directly. We
train BSP, the Rodrigues Network and Diffusion Policy at a
matched size of about 25M trainable parameters, from head
and hand RGB images, joint state and a language instruc-
tion, on three SetTable subtasks: opening the fridge, clos-
ing it, and placing an object at a goal marker that we render
in the camera images. We also fine-tune π0.5 on each sub-
task. Each model is trained with three seeds, and each seed
is evaluated on 1,008 episodes.
Real-world G1
On a real G1,
we test two loco-
manipulation tasks (Figure 3, top left): pushing a trolley
and picking up an item, opening a trash bin with a foot,
and throwing the item in. The G1 has 29 body joints and a
7-DoF Dex3-1 hand on each arm; the policies see through
its head-mounted RealSense D435i camera. On each task,
Diffusion Policy (DP), Ψ0, and BSP learn from the same 15
demonstrations, run on the same RTX 5090 host, and start
from the same 10 initial placements of robot and objects.
They command all 43 joints, from legs to fingers, through
the joint-space control policy HoloMotion v1.4 [7].

Role of the DINOv2 encoder
With one pooled vector
as the image input, replacing DINOv2 with a ResNet-18
trained from scratch halves the total: BSP benefits largely
from strong DINOv2 features. The reliance is mutual: with-
out the latent body schema, the policy gains little from DI-
NOv2. The latent body schema thus unlocks the policy’s
potential to benefit from state-of-the-art visual encoders.
4.4. Analysis
Operator expressivity
We first test the operator’s expres-
sivity alone, on forward kinematics beyond the training
range. Each model maps a root pose and joint configura-
tion to every link’s metric pose; it trains on the middle half
of each joint’s range and is tested on all of it (ten robots,
five seeds; supplementary). The error is the mean squared
error (MSE) between predicted and true link poses, at met-
ric scale.
Generic networks fit inside the training range
and fail beyond it: on every robot, the MLP, Transformer,
Body Transformer and graph networks average at least 11×
our error, and their predicted bodies come apart (Figure 4).
Against the rotation-only Rodrigues operator, the screw op-
erator has the lower error on the G1 and on all four robots
with prismatic joints; on Fetch, whose base translates, the
gap is seven orders of magnitude. On the five other robots,
which have only revolute joints, the Rodrigues operator is
ahead by 2–6×, while ours is the only network with error
below 10−4 on all ten robots.
Denoising efficiency
Conditioned on the latent body
schema, BSP’s action estimate comes close to the demon-
stration within the first few denoising iterations. Figure 5
shows this on one Handover frame, next to DP and a Ro-
drigues Network of the same size that read the same patch
tokens. The gap is not particular to this frame: over 256
training frames per task, BSP’s median error after five it-
erations is below 2◦on each of three tasks, against 7–12◦
for DP (supplementary). This pays off at deployment: ten
DDPM steps are sufficient for the results in Table 1, where
BSP is also the fastest policy we compare.

5. Conclusion
We introduced Body Schema Policy, a human-inspired vi-
suomotor policy that coordinates visual perception and ac-
tion generation through a latent body schema. Its Neural
Screw Operator carries features along the robot’s kinematic
tree with learned screw motions, and each body part re-
trieves its own visual evidence through the schema. With
the schema, the denoiser reaches the action within the
first few iterations: BSP is the fastest policy we compare,
matches the Ψ0 foundation model with 75× fewer parame-
ters, and nearly doubles Diffusion Policy’s success on nine
G1 whole-body tasks. On the Fetch mobile manipulator, it
leads policies of matched size on all three subtasks, and on
a real G1 it ties with Ψ0. This gives one answer to our open-
ing question: organized around its own body, a visuomotor
policy learns whole-body skills with a small fraction of the
parameters, and none of the robot pretraining that founda-
tion models use.
6. Limitations and future work
Due to limited compute, we could not scale the training
and evaluation on SIMPLE to more seeds. BSP relies on
the kinematic meaning of joint commands. Whole-body
controllers that are commanded in a latent space, such as
SONIC [21] and BFM-Zero [19], replace the joint com-
mands with latent coordinates that belong to no body part,
and the latent body schema is less effective there than in
joint space (supplementary). Natural next steps are: (1)
cross-embodiment policies that condition on the latent body
schema, (2) integration into other robot learning policies
such as WAMs and VLAs, and (3) compact libraries of
atomic skills for agentic systems: a hundred BSP policies
have fewer parameters than one π0.5.
