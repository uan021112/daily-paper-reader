Abstract
Robot manipulation videos provide rich visual experience, yet recovering robot action and
gripper-state annotations from RGB observations remains challenging. Using the embodiment-
agnostic tool-center-point (TCP) pose as the action space, we present TCP4D, a unified model
in which a shared 4D scene representation both tracks observed TCP motion and generates
future TCP trajectories. Given a monocular video and initial 2D TCP queries, the tracker
directly recovers metric TCP poses in SE(3) and gripper states alongside scene geometry and
camera parameters. The generator samples local features from the same 4D representation
along the recovered TCP tracks, then combines them with robot observations and language
instructions to predict future TCP states. Our new cross-embodiment augmentation pipeline
converts existing RGB videos into demonstrations for alternative embodiments by retargeting
the recovered motion and synthesizing the corresponding observations, without recorded
source-robot states. Experiments show that the tracker achieves absolute trajectory errors of
7.60 and 16.07 mm on DROID and RoboLab and adapts to a new embodiment with only ten
demonstrations. The generator achieves 69.19% mean success on RoboTwin and outperforms
well-established policy models on two real-world tasks, while joint training on source and
augmented demonstrations improves target-robot success by 41.5% over augmented-only
training.

Introduction
Large-scale video data of robot manipulation (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration et al., 2023;
Contributors et al., 2026) offer a rich source of experience for learning diverse skills. These data provide
an important foundation for modern vision-language-action (VLA) models (Pan et al., 2026; Intelligence
et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026c) and world action models (WAMs) (Ye et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026b;
Su et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026), whose training relies on action supervision. However, not all videos
include synchronized action annotations, and recovering these labels from RGB video alone remains
a nontrivial challenge.
Recovering the end effector’s motion offers a practical route to obtaining such supervision from videos.
This motion can be represented by the tool-center-point (TCP) pose (i.e., SE(3)), which specifies the
position and orientation of the end effector’s tool frame. With a common reference frame, TCP provides
a unified action representation: the same pose encodes the same task-space goal across robot platforms,
even when their joint configurations differ, since each robot can reach the pose through its own inverse
kinematics (IK) (Xiaomi Embodied Intelligence Team et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026) if within its work range.
Recent 4D point tracking methods (Luo et al., 2026; Karhade et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Sucar
et al., 2026) recover scene geometry and track 3D surface points from RGB video. The TCP, however,
is the origin of a virtual tool frame and can lie in free space between the gripper fingers. Its image
projection can therefore fall on a background pixel. Moreover, point trackers predict only 3D positions
not tool orientation, so even an accurate trajectory of the TCP origin does not determine the full TCP pose.
Recovering full TCP poses from these trackers therefore relies on additional robot-specific priors and
multi-stage post-processing, along which errors accumulate and may lead to inaccurate poses or tracking
failures.
To address this challenge, we propose TCP4D, a unified model that tracks observed TCP motion and
generates future TCP trajectories from a shared 4D scene representation. Given a monocular RGB video
and an initial 2D TCP query for each arm, the Tracker directly estimates TCP poses in SE(3) and gripper
states throughout the observed sequence, together with metric scene geometry and camera parameters,
without additional robot priors or post-processing. The Tracker further adapts to new embodiments in a
few-shot manner. TCP poses provide an action interface for robot control, allowing predicted future TCP
trajectories to be executed as robot actions. The Generator extracts local visual features along the tracked
motion. A Diffusion Transformer (DiT) combines these features with the shared scene representation to
generate task-conditioned future TCP and gripper states, serving as a manipulation policy.
Since the recovered TCP motion is embodiment-agnostic, TCP4D further enables a novel cross-
embodiment data augmentation pipeline that converts existing RGB videos of a source robot into demon-
strations for target embodiments. Unlike existing methods that require recorded source-robot states (Chen
et al., 2024b; Ji et al., 2025), our pipeline recovers TCP trajectories and gripper states directly from video,
removes the source robot via segmentation and inpainting, and renders the target embodiment driven by
the recovered trajectory through IK. A single video thus yields demonstrations for any target embodiment
that can reach the recovered motion, without additional data collection.
Extensive experiments validate TCP4D on TCP tracking, manipulation, and cross-embodiment data
augmentation. For tracking, TCP4D achieves absolute trajectory errors of 7.60 and 16.07 mm on DROID
and RoboLab, respectively, and 8.85 mm on the UR5 using just ten demonstrations. As a manipulation
policy, it achieves 69.19% average success on RoboTwin and 78.33% across two real-world tasks. Joint
training on source and augmented demonstrations improves success rates on both source and target robots.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We introduce TCP4D, a unified model that directly recovers TCP poses and gripper states from RGB
videos and generates future TCP trajectories conditioned on observations and instructions. TCP4D
adapts effectively to a new robot embodiment with only a few demonstrations.
• We develop a novel cross-embodiment augmentation pipeline that transforms RGB demonstrations
into target-robot training data through recovered TCP motion, without requiring recorded source-
robot state trajectories.

• Extensive experiments across diverse tasks in simulation benchmarks and real-world settings demon-

Related Work
4D Reconstruction and Point Tracking. Recent feed-forward approaches to 4D reconstruction include
dense geometric prediction (Wang et al., 2026b; Peng et al., 2026; Sucar et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Jiang
et al., 2026) and query-based frameworks (Zhang et al., 2026a; Luo et al., 2026) that provide flexible
access to scene geometry and motion across space and time. Beyond tracking observed motion, recent
methods (Zhang et al., 2026b; Duisterhof et al., 2026) predict future point-cloud dynamics through 3D
trajectory forecasting and completion. These approaches primarily characterize scene geometry and point
positions, leaving local orientation implicit. Concurrent work MoSE3 (Cheng et al., 2026) extends motion
estimation to dense per-pixel SE(3) transforms by fitting rigid motions within soft clusters of tracked
scene points. In contrast, TCP4D directly tracks the SE(3) pose of a virtual TCP, without requiring a
corresponding physical surface point or rigid-body grouping.
Point-based Manipulation Policy. Prior work uses predicted 2D point tracks to guide manipulation
policies (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024a; Cao et al., 2026; Wen et al., 2024). In 3D, world models predict
geometric motion to support planning and action learning (Zhi et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026; Huang et al.,
2026a; Wang et al., 2026d; Hu et al., 2026). Other approaches infer waypoints or poses from visual
predictions (Huang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2025), or incorporate motion cues into VLA policies (Wang
et al., 2026a; Yuan et al., 2026a). Point-based policies also use depth-derived scene geometry to ground
action prediction. DP3 (Ze et al., 2024) conditions action diffusion on compact features from sparse point
clouds, while Act3D (Gervet et al., 2023) searches a 3D feature field for the next end-effector position.
3D Diffuser Actor (Ke et al., 2024) denoises pose trajectories conditioned on 3D scene features. TCP4D
uses TCP pose and gripper state as a shared interface between motion tracking and action generation,
tracking them across observed frames in a 4D scene representation reconstructed from RGB video.
Cross-Embodiment Augmentation Pipeline. Existing approaches to robot data augmentation include
simulation-based demonstration synthesis (Mandlekar et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2024) and image-space
semantic augmentation (Yu et al., 2023; Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b), which expand motion and scene
diversity without additional real-world collection. For embodiment transfer, Mirage (Chen et al., 2024a)
uses test-time cross-painting to present a target robot as the source robot expected by a pretrained policy.
RoVi-Aug (Chen et al., 2024b) uses diffusion-based image generation to replace source-robot appearances
with target embodiments while retaining the associated task-space motion. OXE-AugE (Ji et al., 2025)
further scales embodiment augmentation through simulation-based trajectory retargeting, background
inpainting, and target-robot rendering. However, RoVi-Aug and OXE-AugE assume access to recorded
source end-effector poses and actions. In contrast, TCP4D recovers TCP trajectories in SE(3) and gripper
states from RGB videos and obtains target joint configurations through IK, enabling augmentation without
recorded source-robot states.

introduce the TCP4D Tracker and Generator in Sec. 3.2 and 3.3, and describe two-stage training in
Sec. 3.4. Finally, Sec. 3.5 introduces a novel downstream data augmentation pipeline using TCP4D.

4
Experiments
We evaluate TCP4D across four dimensions: (1) TCP tracking performance, (2) the effectiveness of
cross-embodiment data augmentation, (3) robot manipulation performance of the TCP generator, and (4)
real-world manipulation performance on a physical robot platform.
4.1
Training Setup
Datasets and Benchmark. We use the subset of DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) annotated by Point-
World (Huang et al., 2026a), comprising 33,966 episodes (67,932 camera sequences) of Franka Panda
7DOF Arm. We use 90% of the episodes for training and randomly sample 100 episodes from the
remaining 10% as the evaluation set. For in-the-wild evaluation, we additionally collect 100 episodes
spanning 100 distinct tasks in RoboLab (Yang et al., 2026b). To evaluate robot manipulation, we use
RoboTwin (Chen et al., 2025) and collect 50 clean demonstrations per task for 48 tasks, using the third-
person view. We train on these demonstrations and evaluate under both clean2clean and clean2random
settings.
Implementation Details. In Stage 1, we train two separate TCP trackers on DROID and RoboTwin,
respectively, initializing their visual backbones and geometry modules from pretrained 4RC (Luo et al.,
2026) weights. RGB image resolutions are 320 × 180 for DROID and 320 × 240 for RoboTwin. We
sample Stage 1 clips with random starting frames, a per-clip temporal stride randomly chosen from 1–5,
and temporal reversal. In Stage 2, we use only RoboTwin data to train the TCP generator. Each sample
contains L = 4 consecutive observation frames paired with the next h = 32 TCP states, preserving
chronological order. The Stage 1 tracker and the full TCP4D model contain approximately 1.522B and
2.272B parameters, respectively, including frozen modules and retained heads. Further implementation
details are provided in Appx. C.

Evaluation Metrics. We report AbsRel, MAE, and δ1 for depth within 3 m; Average Percentage of Points
(APD) and Mean Position Error (MPE) for 3D TCP tracking; Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE), Relative
Rotation Error (RRE), and Relative Translation Error (RTE) for SE(3) tracking; and tracking Success
Rate (SR). Gripper Accuracy (Acc.) uses a 0.5 threshold. Metric definitions and evaluation protocols are
provided in Appx. D.
Results. As shown in Table 1, TCP4D achieves state-of-the-art performance on most evaluated metrics
across both benchmarks. On DROID and RoboLab, it achieves APD scores of 74.78% and 43.71%,
respectively, more than four times those of the strongest baselines, and reduces ATE to 7.60 and 16.07 mm.
TCP4D also achieves 100% SR on both datasets, successfully processing all evaluated tracking windows,
compared with the best baseline SRs of 89.64% and 96.33%, respectively. Competing point-tracking
methods rely on tracking rigid point groups and fitting rigid transformations in post-processing to recover
TCP poses; inaccurate or inconsistent point tracks can yield unreliable pose estimates or prevent successful
recovery. By directly predicting TCP poses and gripper states within a unified feed-forward framework,
TCP4D avoids this recovery step, delivering accurate and reliable tracking even in unseen manipulation
scenarios. Fig. 4 visually compares TCP4D with other baselines. More results can be found in Appx. D.8.

4.4
TCP4D Manipulation Performance
Results. We evaluate TCP4D on 48 RoboTwin tasks with 100 trials per task and report the success rates
in Table 3. TCP4D achieves the highest reported average success rate of 69.19% under clean2clean.
It surpasses established manipulation policies ACT, RDT, and DP3 by 39.94%, 35.27%, and 14.92%,
respectively, and also outperforms several VLA models, including EventVLA (65.71%), GalaxeaVLA
(62.73%), and π0 (45.21%). These results support the effectiveness of predicting future TCP motion from
4D scene representations and highlight the potential of this paradigm for robotic manipulation. Complete
results for all 48 tasks are provided in Appx. F.1.
The average success rate drops to 10.94% under clean2random, where environmental randomization
introduces a distribution shift. Our TCP generator is trained solely on clean demonstrations. Incorporating
large embodiment data pretraining or 3D geometric data from a broader mix of sources could improve

5
Conclusion
We presented TCP4D, a unified model using a shared 4D scene representation to track TCP motion from
RGB video and generate future actions conditioned on the recovered tracks. Our cross-embodiment
pipeline converts videos into target-robot demonstrations. Experiments demonstrate accurate tracking,
few-shot adaptation, effective manipulation, and improved policy learning with augmented data. TCP4D
has the potential to scale up with more diverse data and robot embodiments.

B
Cross-Embodiment Augmentation
B.1
Augmentation Pipeline
TCP4D enables cross-embodiment augmentation by recovering task-space motion directly from RGB
demonstrations. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the pipeline comprises motion recovery, embodiment retargeting,
and visual composition. Given a source video and initial TCP queries, TCP4D predicts TCP trajectories,
gripper states, and camera geometry. The recovered poses are aligned in a common coordinate frame
19


and transferred to the target robot through inverse kinematics using its kinematic model, producing joint
configurations that follow the demonstrated task-space motion.
We segment the source robot using SAM3 (Carion et al., 2026) or, when available, its geometric
model and recorded states. ProPainter (Zhou et al., 2023) completes the masked regions to reconstruct
the background. We then render the target robot at the recovered configurations and composite it
into the completed frames using the aligned camera geometry. The resulting images are paired with
target configurations to form augmented demonstrations. Depth from TCP4D can optionally improve
segmentation quality or distinguish foreground and background, but the pipeline does not depend on
depth.
During Retarget, if IK fails at the target robot’s initial base placement, the pipeline automatically
searches nearby robot-base translations and retries IK over the full trajectory (auto_base is enabled by
default). The base orientation remains fixed during this search, and the target arm is accepted only when
every frame passes IK. For further details on robot-base adjustment in cross-embodiment augmentation,
refer to the OXE-AugE (Ji et al., 2025) paper and project page.
B.2
Motion Recovery from RGB
The key advantage of TCP4D is that motion information for retargeting can be inferred from video rather
than supplied as recorded source-robot states. Table 4 compares the required resources and the sources
of motion and camera information. Predicted TCP trajectories and gripper states provide the motion

C.2
Frame Sampling Strategy
Stage 1 Sampling. Each minibatch contains a fixed total of 18 frames per device. For each minibatch,
we randomly choose the number of clips S ∈{1, 2, 3, 6, 9} and sample 18/S frames per clip, yielding
the respective clip lengths 18, 9, 6, 3, and 2. Each clip comes from a single camera stream, with a
temporal stride sampled uniformly from the feasible values in {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} and a randomly chosen
starting frame. The sampled stride is constant within a clip and can differ across clips. Each clip is
reversed with probability 0.5; images and all associated labels are reordered together. The first frame in
the resulting sequence serves as the TCP query frame, so queries can originate at different points within
an episode and support tracking in either temporal direction. Temporal supervision uses the signed time
intervals between sampled frames, accounting for both subsampling and reversal. Clips remain within
valid trajectory segments and do not cross detected discontinuities.
Stage 2 Sampling. For each sample, we randomly choose a current frame t and use the four consecutive
observations ending at t as history (L = 4), with the following 32 TCP states as targets (h = 32).
Sampling is strictly forward with unit temporal stride and no reversal. If fewer than four historical frames
are available within the current valid segment, we repeat its earliest observed frame. If fewer than 32
future states remain, we repeat the last valid state for storage and mask the padded positions in attention
and loss computation. The current robot state and wrist images are taken at t, and a language instruction
is sampled from those associated with the episode.
C.3
Sliding Window Inference
As illustrated in Fig. 10, TCP4D extends TCP tracking to long videos by propagating visual queries across
overlapping windows. Only the initial frame requires 2D TCP queries for the available arms; subsequent
windows use queries derived from the model’s recovered TCP positions.
TCP Tracking. For example, the tracker processes n −1 frame windows by default, with a configurable
length of 2–18 frames. Consecutive windows share exactly one boundary frame, so a full n −1 frame
window advances the sequence by n −2 frames; the final window can be shorter. After processing a
window, we project its final-frame 3D TCP positions into the shared boundary image using the camera
intrinsics decoded from the ray map. These projected positions become the reference-frame queries for
the next window, as shown in Fig. 10.

D
Tracking Evaluation
This section describes the datasets, protocol, and metrics used for the tracking evaluation in Table 1
and reports additional results. All TCP poses are expressed in the camera coordinate system of the
corresponding frame.
D.1
Datasets
DROID. The PointWorld-annotated DROID subset contains 33,966 episodes. We use a fixed episode-level
split with seed 42, assigning approximately 90% of the episodes to training. From the remaining 10%, we
randomly sample 100 episodes for evaluation. Each DROID episode has two third-person view cameras
and is sampled at 15 Hz.
RoboLab. The additional RoboLab set contains one episode for each of 100 distinct tasks and is used
exclusively for in-the-wild evaluation. We use π0.5 (Intelligence et al., 2025) as the policy to generate
trajectories, including both successful and failed trajectories. During data collection, we randomize the
backgrounds and camera positions. RoboLab is sampled at 15 Hz.
D.2
Evaluation Protocol
Baseline Evaluation Windows.
The 4RC (Luo et al., 2026), Any4D (Karhade et al., 2026),
Track4World (Lu et al., 2026), VDPM (Sucar et al., 2026), MoSE3 (Cheng et al., 2026), and Co-
Tracker3 (Karaev et al., 2025) baselines use independent, non-overlapping nine-frame windows, with a
potentially shorter final window. Each window’s query frame is excluded from tracking metrics.
TCP Pose Recovery from Point Tracks. For 4RC, Any4D, Track4World, VDPM, and CoTracker3, we
recover TCP poses by rigid fitting of point tracks. As shown in Fig. 11, each window is initialized with
23


ground-truth-projected points on the fixed Robotiq gripper body. Each query is associated with a known
surface point on the gripper URDF model, and this query-to-model correspondence is retained throughout
the window. CoTracker3 uses VGGT-Ωdepth predicted for every frame, together with the calibrated
camera intrinsics, to lift its 2D tracks into 3D. For all these baselines, including CoTracker3, we calibrate
the predicted 3D point group’s scale using the known metric dimensions of the corresponding URDF
points at window initialization and keep this scale fixed throughout the window.
At each tracked frame, we robustly fit a rigid transformation from the corresponding gripper model
points to their scale-calibrated 3D predictions. The fitted rotation and translation describe the gripper
body’s pose in that frame’s camera coordinates. We apply the known fixed body-to-TCP offset to recover
the TCP position and use the fitted body rotation as the TCP orientation, consistent with the dataset’s TCP
convention. Repeating this fit across frames converts the point-group tracks into a TCP pose trajectory.
MoSE3 Native SE(3) Motion Estimation. For MoSE3, we use its official rigidity-weighted per-point
SE(3) estimation procedure. At the start of each window, we perform the same URDF-based scale
calibration and rigid fitting to initialize the TCP pose from the predicted 3D point group. We then
propagate this pose using the SE(3) motion associated with a fixed gripper-surface query, selected as the
query nearest the image-space mean of the group, and transform it into each frame’s camera coordinates
using the predicted camera poses. The initialization scale is applied to both motion and camera translations.
No additional CAD-to-track rigid fitting is performed at subsequent frames. The same evaluation windows,
query-frame exclusions, and metric definitions apply.
Metrics and Alignment. We evaluate the recovered TCP positions with APD and MPE, and the recovered
pose trajectory with ATE, RRE, and RTE in Table 1. APD uses one GT-based global-median scale per
episode–camera trajectory, computed by Eq. 25. MPE is computed without evaluation-time alignment;
each method retains the scale of its recovered or directly predicted TCP trajectory. ATE, RRE, and RTE
share one position-based Sim(3) alignment per episode trajectory: its scale, rotation, and translation
transform positions, and its rotation transforms orientations. 3D Tracking and SE(3) for those baselines
are computed only over each method’s successful windows. SR reports the percentage of successfully
processed windows.
TCP4D uses nine-frame windows with one shared boundary frame and propagates predicted queries
after the initial ground-truth query, following the Sliding Window Inference strategy in Appx. C.3. Only
its initial query frame is excluded, and overlapping frames are scored once using the preceding window’s
prediction.

E.2
Downstream Policy Training
We use Diffusion Policy with a randomly initialized ResNet18 visual encoder and a conditional 1D U-Net
action denoiser. The policy receives three consecutive observations, each comprising a third-person
RGB image and a 14-dimensional robot state. The state contains six joint positions and one continuous
gripper value for each arm. Recorded source states are unnecessary for the RGB-based augmentation
pipeline; downstream policies use the evaluated robot’s joint and gripper states as proprioceptive inputs.

For each task and evaluated embodiment, policy evaluation uses 50 episodes following a common
environment-seed schedule across training settings. Success is determined by RoboTwin’s task-specific
criteria, and the success rate is the fraction of evaluation episodes completed successfully. Results are
obtained from one training seed per setting. Downstream results are reported in Fig. 6 and discussed in
Sec. 4.3.

G
Limitations and Future Work
Embodiment Coverage. While TCP4D demonstrates strong performance in DROID setting, its training
data currently covers only embodiments with Franka and Robotiq grippers, and other embodiment data
remains limited. After pretraining on DOIRD data, the TCP4D adapts effectively to UR5-WSG with only
a few demonstrations (Table 2). These results support the use of a shared SE(3) TCP representation for
cross-embodiment transfer. With sufficient training data from a diverse range of embodiments, we expect
TCP4D to generalize well to new robot platforms and gripper designs.
Environmental Generalization. The TCP4D Generator shows strong performance under RoboTwin
clean2clean, but its performance under clean2random remains limited. The Generator is trained on a
relatively small set of clean demonstrations without large-scale policy pretraining, providing little exposure
to the environmental variations encountered during randomized evaluation. This limited training diversity
may partly explain the performance gap. Large-scale pretraining on diverse manipulation data, together
with domain randomization and targeted data augmentation, offers practical directions for addressing this
gap and realizing the broader potential of 4D-conditioned TCP generation.
