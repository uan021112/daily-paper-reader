# ReLAP: Latent Action Pretraining through Recurrent Video Prediction in JEPA Space 

R. Gnana Praveen, Ali Rouzbayani, Zhiying Jiang, Bidhan Roy Bagel Labs 

Abstract — Videos provide a rich source of physical interaction for learning robot behavior without action annotations, yet effective latent actions must capture not only local transitions but also how those transitions evolve over time. We introduce ReLAP, a latent-action pretraining framework that couples local transition learning with recurrent multi-step prediction in a frozen JEPA representation space. A shared inverse dynamics model infers compact continuous latent actions from adjacent observations, while a latent world model recurrently predicts the future JEPA features. Because each predicted feature becomes the context for the next transition, errors at later steps backpropagate through the rollout to earlier latent actions, encouraging temporally coherent dynamics beyond pairwise reconstruction. We transfer this representation to a vision-language model using two complementary objectives: latent distillation from the frozen inverse teacher and recurrent future-feature prediction through the frozen latent world model. The resulting policy is then adapted to robot demonstrations with action flow matching. At deployment, the explicit latent-action and world-model branches are removed, and the fine-tuned VLM predicts physical actions directly, reducing inference complexity. With only 11M pretraining samples and 1.06B trainable parameters, ReLAP achieves 97 .9% on standard LIBERO and 70 .0% over zero-shot LIBERO-Plus perturbations. On the LARY latent-action benchmark, ReLAP ranks second in both mean regression error and mean classification accuracy among the compared methods. These results demonstrate that recurrent action-free latent action pretraining can learn compact, temporally structured, and transferable representations for downstream robot control. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Videos of human and robot interaction provide abundant supervision for robot learning without requiring physical action annotations. Latent action models exploit such action-free data by inferring compact pseudo-actions from visual transitions. A typical latent action model combines an inverse dynamics model, which infers a latent action from two consecutive observations, with a forward dynamics model that reconstructs the resulting transition. These inferred actions can then serve as supervision for pretraining vision-language-action policies before adaptation to action-labeled robot demonstrations. While effective, pairwise transition reconstruction does not explicitly require consecutive latent actions to remain compatible over time. Each transition is typically reconstructed from its observed starting state, so accurate one-step predictions do not necessarily yield an accurate multi-step trajectory when predicted states become the context for later transitions. This distinction is particularly important for manipulation: a reach changes the state from which a grasp begins, and the grasp changes the state from which a lift is executed. A. Latent Action Learning from Action-Free Video 

Latent action models (LAMs) provide a scalable way to learn action-like representations from videos without physical action annotations. Early approaches such as LAPA learn discrete latent actions through inverse–forward reconstruction and use them as pseudo-action targets for VLA pretraining [5]– [7]. Subsequent work explored richer representations by exploring task-centric discrete actions in DINO feature space [8], while CoMo [9] and Garrido et al. [10] show the benefits of continuous latent actions for capturing fine-grained motion and diverse in-the-wild dynamics. JALA [11] instead avoids explicit observation reconstruction and aligns predictive action embeddings with inverse dynamics and physical actions. Most of these approaches primarily optimize the quality of individual transitions or the predictability of latent actions. ReLAP instead focuses on whether a sequence of video-inferred actions remains predictive when its own earlier consequences become the context for later transitions. This introduces recurrent supervision directly into latent-action discovery rather than only into the downstream policy. 

B. Temporal Structure and Future Prediction 

Recent works highlight that independently reconstructed latent actions need not form a coherent sequence. Moto [12] models latent motion tokens autoregressively, while ViPRA jointly predicts future visual observations and fine-grained latent-action sequences and regularizes the underlying motion representation with perceptual and optical-flow objectives [13]. Other approaches impose explicit structure directly in the latent space. AC-LAM [14] enforces additive composition and inverse consistency; RotVLA [15] represents actions on a rotational manifold and uses frame triplets to capture temporal relations; and ALAM [16] and DLAM [17] impose composition and reversal constraints on deterministic and distributional latent transitions, respectively. These approaches establish the importance of temporal structure, but largely encode it either through explicit algebraic constraints. ReLAP takes a complementary view: temporal compatibility is defined by the predicted consequences of the latent sequence. Each action is applied to the feature state produced by the preceding action, and losses at later horizons backpropagate through these predicted states to earlier inferred actions. Thus, temporal structure emerges through learned state-dependent dynamics without adding additional complexities of additive, rotational, or other fixed composition rule in latent space. Future prediction provides another route for strengthening latent-action learning. LAWM [18] uses world modeling as supervision for latent-action pretraining, while CoLA-World [19] and CLAW [20] jointly optimize latent actions with expressive world models, allowing predictive losses to shape the learned action interface. ViPRA [13] similarly combines latent-action prediction with future visual prediction. ReLAP is closest in spirit to this line of work, but differs in where the temporal constraint is applied: rather than evaluating future prediction only from observed context, it recurrently feeds predicted intermediate states back into the forward model, explicitly coupling earlier latent actions to the accuracy of later transitions. 

C. Feature-Space Dynamics for Robot Learning 

Predicting in pretrained visual feature spaces avoids the cost and ambiguity of reconstructing every pixel. DINO-WM [21] predicts future DINO features conditioned on physical actions for test-time planning, while V-JEPA 2-AC [22] learns action-conditioned dynamics in V-JEPA representations from robot interaction data. In latent-action learning, UniVLA [8] moves reconstruction from pixels to DINO features. Most closely related to ReLAP, Garrido et al. [10] jointly learn a continuous inverse model and world model in frozen V-JEPA 2-L features from action-free video. Their forward model, however, is trained with teacher forcing, so each next-state prediction is conditioned on observed history rather than the model’s recursively predicted intermediate states. JEPA representations are also increasingly used to provide predictive supervision directly to robot policies. VLA-JEPA [23] predicts future latent states without pixel generation, while JEPA-WAM [24] couples transition prediction and continuous action generation in pretrained V-JEPA space. FLARE [25] similarly aligns policy representations with future latent observations. These methods demonstrate the value of predictive feature-space supervision, but do not address the same problem as ReLAP: learning a sequence of latent actions from action-free video by allowing future rollout errors to shape the inverse model that inferred the earlier actions. ReLAP lies at the intersection of three emerging directions: label-free latent-action discovery, temporally structured latent actions, and predictive world modeling in pretrained feature space. Prior work has explored each component separately— including future prediction, explicit latent composition, and JEPA-space dynamics—but their intersection remains less studied. ReLAP combines them through a simple recurrent principle: local transition reconstruction grounds each latent action, while multi-step rollout through predicted JEPA states couples the sequence through its decoded visual effects. This provides temporal supervision without physical action labels, pixel reconstruction, or an explicit algebraic composition rule. III. P ROPOSED APPROACH 

ReLAP learns latent actions from action-free video by requiring their predicted latent actions to remain coherent over a sequence. The central mechanism is a shared latent world model that consumes its own previous feature prediction at each step. Figure 2 summarizes the three stages: learning this representation from video, predicting it from the current observation and instruction, and adapting it to robot control. Physical action labels are used only in the third stage. 

A. Stage 1: Learning Latent Actions through Recurrent Prediction in JEPA Space 

Stage 1 learns compact latent actions that explain local visual transitions while remaining consistent over a multi-step rollout. Given a sequence of H+1 observations o0: H , we extract patch features ft = E(ot) ∈ RN ×df using a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder [1]. We use H = 4 transitions sampled at 0.4 s intervals and perform all prediction directly in the JEPA representation space. For each adjacent feature pair, the inverse model Iϕ infers a dz = 16 -dimensional continuous latent action. Each latent is inferred independently from its corresponding transition. A low-dimensional bottleneck together with KL regularization toward N (0 , I ) encourages a compact representation of the observed visual change. The forward latent world model Fψ predicts how a latent action transforms the current feature map. We first optimize a local transition loss , which reconstructs each observed transition independently by minimizing the Smooth-L1 distance between 

Fψ (ft, z t) and ft+1 , with zt sampled from the posterior. This anchors each latent action to its corresponding local transition. Local reconstruction alone, however, does not require consecutive latent actions to remain compatible when applied sequentially. We therefore introduce recurrent prediction. Starting from the first observed feature, the model recursively applies the inferred latent sequence,

Because the inverse model observes both sides of each transition, Stage 1 learns a representation of observed visual dynamics rather than predicting future actions from the current observation.

These two objectives play com-plementary roles. Latent distillation preserves the semantic coordinates learned by the inverse model, whereas future-feature prediction constrains the predicted latent sequence through its cumulative visual effects. Although Fψ remains frozen, gradients propagate through the full recursive rollout to the Q-Former and VLM, encouraging latent actions that are individually consistent with the teacher and jointly predictive of future visual dynamics. The rollout loss is introduced gradually after initial latent distillation to stabilize optimization. Since the inverse teacher observes future frames, its posterior latents provide training references rather than unique action targets: multiple latent trajectories may be compatible with the same current observation and instruction. Stage 2 therefore learns a future-free latent prior that predicts a coherent transition sequence from the current visual-language context alone, without using physical-action supervision. 

C. Stage 3: Adapting Latent Representations to Robot Control 

Stage 3 adapts the pretrained representation to physical robot control using action-labeled demonstrations. The policy receives the current main-camera image, wrist image, and language instruction. The latent queries are positioned before the wrist-view tokens in the causal VLM sequence, so latent prediction depends only on the main view and instruction, while the action pathway can additionally exploit wrist-view information. The Stage 2 Q-Former is retained, with bidirectional attention among the four latent queries to preserve sequence-level consistency. During training, the predicted latent sequence is recurrently decoded by the Stage 1 forward model to obtain its anticipated future JEPA representation. A flow-matching action head then predicts an eight-step, seven-dimensional action chunk from the VLM context. We additionally expose the action head to the predicted latent sequence and current/future JEPA features during training, providing complementary semantic and visual-dynamics context. Importantly, the latent-action and JEPA world-model branches serve as training-time supervision rather than mandatory deployment modules. At inference, we retain only the fine-tuned VLM direct-action pathway and discard the latent decoder and visual-dynamics attention branches. Datasets. Stages 1 and 2 train on a mixture of seven large-scale video datasets: Bridge-v2 [26], DROID [27], AgiBot [28], EgoDex [29], Airoa-MOMA [30], Intern-Data [31], and Ego4D [32]. These datasets provide diverse demonstrations of physical interactions in both human and robot environments. In stage 1, we use only the raw video streams without accessing any robot action annotations, proprioceptive signals, or language instructions, ensuring that the latent actions learned are derived exclusively from visual dynamics. We follow the preprocessing strategy of OSCAR [33] to process raw videos, standardizing frame rates and resolutions while preserving the temporal integrity of the interactions. Specifically, videos are sampled at 20 Hz, and each training clip consists of 5 frames spaced 0.4 seconds apart, yielding 11,127,635 training windows as shown in Fig 3. In stage 3, we use the LIBERO benchmark suite [2], which includes 40 tasks across four suites: LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-10, resulting in 2K demonstrations and perform downstream evaluation of LIBERO and LIBERO plus benchmarks. 

Implementation Details. ReLAP learns latent actions in the V-JEPA representation space. We use a frozen V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-B [1] encoder on (256 × 256) RGB frames, producing 256 visual tokens per frame. In Stage 1, the inverse dynamics model consists of 24 transformer layers and the forward latent world model uses six transformer layers, both with hidden width 1024. Each transition is represented by a 16-dimensional continuous latent action, and four consecutive transitions span a 1.6 s clip. Stage 1 is trained for 100k steps using AdamW with a learning rate of 3×10 −4 and an effective batch size of 512. The recurrent prediction loss weight λr is gradually increased to 2, while the KL coefficient β is ramped to 5×10 −5 early in training for stable optimization. In Stage 2, we initialize from Qwen3-VL-2B and retain its first 16 text Transformer blocks. Eight learned latent-query tokens interact with the backbone through a single-layer Q-Former. Stage 2 is trained with an effective batch size of 256, with the recurrent prediction objective gradually introduced from 5k steps. For Stage 3, we initialize the latent-action model from the final Stage 1 checkpoint and the vision-language backbone from the 80k-step Stage 2 checkpoint, while initializing the action expert from scratch. The action expert is a 16-block flow-matching Transformer with hidden width 1024. The policy receives the current main-camera image, wrist-camera image, and language instruction; no proprioceptive state is used. In the causal input sequence, latent queries are placed before the wrist-view tokens, such that latent actions are predicted only from the main view and language instruction, while the action expert can additionally access wrist-view information through the full backbone hidden sequence. Stage 3 is optimized using AdamW with a learning rate of 10 −4, batch size 256, 1,500 warmup steps, and cosine decay to 5 × 10 −7. Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of 1. The loss weights are 1.0 for action flow matching, 0.1 for terminal future-feature prediction, and 0.1 for latent-action distillation. Throughout Stage 3, the visual encoder and inverse dynamics model remain frozen, while the forward latent world model, latent prior, action expert, and VLM backbone are optimized. 

Evaluation Benchmarks We evaluate on LIBERO [10] and LIBERO-Plus [11]. LIBERO has four suites, Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long (the libero 10 suite), each with ten tasks. Latent dependency and sample specificity. We first test whether the recurrent world model genuinely depends on transition-specific latent information. We replace the inferred sequence with three controls: global mean , which uses the dataset-average latent; and zero latent , which removes latent conditioning entirely. These interventions increase latent world model prediction error by 34 .80% , and 77 .70% 

on Bridge, and by 28 .65% , and 99 .67% on LIBERO-40, respectively. Thus, accurate future prediction requires not merely a latent input, but the transition-specific information carried by the inferred sequence. We further test sample specificity by substituting a latent action from a different transition, and found that the latent world predictions degrade with latents from different transitions (0.439/0.074) on Bridge and (0.471/0.211) on LIBERO-40., highlighting the sample specific latent actions of ReLAP. 

Temporal Coherence We first test whether the four latent slots form a temporally coherent sequence. Reversing the latent order while preserving the same four latent vectors increases rollout error by 4.03% on Bridge and 2.50% on LIBERO-40. Corrupting a single latent slot causes a larger degradation of 6.03 –13 .52% and 5.73 –6.70% , respectively. These results indicate that the representation is temporally aligned and slot-specific: each latent captures transition information associated with a particular temporal interval, while global ordering has a smaller but measurable effect. 

Physical-action correspondence We next evaluate whether the temporal structure of the latent actions aligns with robot actions. For each latent slot zi, we linearly predict each of the four future action segments. Temporally matched latent–action pairs explain 36 .0% of the action variance on Bridge, compared with 22 .5% for mismatched pairs, and 

53 .0% versus 31 .0% on LIBERO-40. Thus, each latent is preferentially predictive of the physical action occurring during the same temporal interval, providing direct evidence that the learned latent sequence is aligned with the temporal structure of robot behavior. 

C. Qualitative Analysis of Results 

Across the examples, the policy successfully executes diverse multi-stage manipulation behaviors. In Fig. 4(a), the robot first interacts with the stove and subsequently transports the moka pot to the target location, requiring sequential interaction with two distinct objects. Fig. 4(b) demonstrates object-specific grasping and spatial placement of the book into the designated compartment of the caddy. Fig. 4(c) further illustrates a longer compositional task in which two different objects must be sequentially manipulated and placed into the basket. These rollouts qualitatively demonstrate that the final VLM policy can ground language instructions into temporally coordinated, object-centric manipulation behaviors, including grasping, transport, placement, and multi-object sequencing. Notably, these behaviors are produced through direct VLM-to-action prediction at deployment, suggesting that the transition structure learned during latent-action pretraining can be transferred into the downstream policy without requiring the explicit latent-action pathway at inference. VI. D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

Our results also reveal two important limitations of the current ReLAP design. First, recurrent latent rollout intro-duces an inherent temporal asymmetry across the latent sequence. Earlier latent actions influence not only their immediate transition, but also all subsequent predictions through the recurrent rollout, and therefore receive stronger effective supervision than later latent actions. This bias can favor accurate short-horizon dynamics while making errors increasingly difficult to correct toward the end of the 1.6 s prediction horizon. Second, the current pretraining pipeline does not explicitly enforce multi-view invariance. In Stage 2, latent prediction is learned primarily from the main camera view, and no objective aligns representations across different camera viewpoints. Although the Stage 3 action policy can access the wrist image, the pretrained visual representation is therefore only weakly constrained to remain consistent under viewpoint changes. Extending the recurrent latent representation to substantially longer horizons and incorporating robustness to multiple views remain important future directions. VII. C ONCLUSION 

ReLAP achieves superior performance on standard LIBERO with only 11M pretraining samples and 1.06B trainable parameters, remains competitive under zero-shot LIBERO-Plus perturbations, and learns latent representations that transfer strongly on the LARY benchmark. Our analyses show that these latents are sample-specific, temporally seg-mented, and aligned with future physical actions, including on datasets unseen during pretraining. These results suggest that action-free visual pretraining can provide compact and transferable supervision for downstream robot control without requiring an explicit latent-action bottleneck at deployment. The remaining gaps are concentrated in long-horizon manipulation and robustness to camera and sensor shifts, motivating more balanced long-horizon supervision and multi-view pretraining.
