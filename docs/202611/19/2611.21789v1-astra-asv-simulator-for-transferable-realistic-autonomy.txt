# ASTRA: ASV Simulator for Transferable Realistic Autonomy 

Tsimafei Lazouski 1, Cody Sheltraw 2, Alan Papalia 1, and Maani Ghaffari 1,2 

Abstract —Autonomy development for marine autonomous sur-face vehicles (ASVs) remains challenging because field testing is costly and difficult to reproduce. Existing marine simulation tools provide mature capabilities in vessel dynamics, robotics integration, and visual rendering, but these strengths are empha-sized differently across platforms. This paper presents ASTRA, an ASV simulation framework built in NVIDIA Isaac Sim that combines marine vehicle dynamics, photorealistic perception, and Robot Operating System (ROS 2) autonomy interfaces within a common environment. ASTRA incorporates a Fossen-style ma-rine dynamics model together with geometry-based buoyancy and programmable spectral wave disturbances, enabling non-planar hydrostatic and wave-induced motion. The simulator reproduces the sensor configuration of a physical BlueBoat testbed, including GPS, IMU, LiDAR, and RGB-D sensing exposed through ROS 2. This allows localization, perception, planning, and control software to be developed against interfaces consistent with the physical platform. The BlueBoat’s existing ROS 2 localization and perception modules were ported directly into ASTRA, while targeted zero-propulsion experiments were used to verify the implemented wave-body coupling. The simulated roll response was consistent with the expected non-oscillatory hydrostatic behavior, and heave remained highly coherent with the generated wave field, with approximately unity gain at the dominant wave frequency in the tested sea states. ASTRA is intended as an open-source testbed for marine autonomy development, simulation-based benchmarking, and sim-to-real experimentation. 

Index Terms —Autonomous Surface Vehicles, Isaac Sim Simu-lation, Sim-to-Real Transfer. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous surface vehicles (ASVs) have progressed to-ward practical deployment across marine science and engineer-ing [1], with applications including environmental monitoring, hydrographic surveying, and offshore structure inspection [2]– [4]. Yet, developing reliable autonomy for these platforms remains difficult. Field trials are physically and logistically demanding, often exposing the vehicle only to narrow cases of the operational environment. The challenging test domain also limits rapid iteration, refinement of algorithms, and sensor integration. In robotics, this is often solved through the use of high-fidelity simulations. For many years, AirSim [5] was a strong substrate for aerial vehicles and CARLA [6] for autonomous driving. In

recent years, however, NVIDIA’s Isaac Sim has emerged as a promising core environment for high-fidelity simulation and research. Isaac Sim provides photorealistic rendering, a strong physics engine, sensor models, and established Robot Operating System (ROS 2) development practices. It also naturally opens a path toward reinforcement-learning (RL) applications through Isaac Lab. These strengths have been demonstrated through the successful transfer to hardware of legged-locomotion policies trained in NVIDIA’s physics stack [7], and Isaac Lab has consolidated this workflow into a general robot-learning framework [8]. Despite this, the use of Isaac Sim remains limited for marine applications. While several strong marine simulation solutions are avail-able, there remains a gap in open-source Isaac Sim environ-ments that jointly support surface-vehicle dynamics, wave-body interaction, high-fidelity visual rendering, and ROS 2 autonomy development. Such a simulation environment would allow for joint iteration on the full autonomy stack in a scal-able, photorealistic marine surface-vehicle setting. As such, in this work we present ASTRA (ASV Simulator for Trans-ferable Realistic Autonomy) , a simulation framework that couples Isaac Sim’s photorealistic rendering and ROS 2 bridge with a Fossen-style 6-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) dynamics model, a real-time geometry-based buoyancy solver, and an ASV that matches a physical BlueBoat testbed. Packaged as an extension, it is easy to modify for use with a specific arXiv:2611.21789v1 [cs.RO] 16 Nov 2026 platform or test environment. Importantly, Section II positions ASTRA against other marine simulators in more detail. The key contributions of this work are: 1) An open-source Isaac Sim framework for full-stack ASV autonomy development, with ROS 2 sensing and a physical BlueBoat counterpart accessible through the 

ASTRA GitHub repository .2) A coupled marine simulation model combining Fossen-style 6-DOF dynamics, geometry-based buoyancy, and a programmable spectral wave disturbance, synchronized with the ROS 2 sensor stack. 3) Field-informed planar dynamics identification and simu-lator verification, extracted from 29 field-test bags from the physical BlueBoat together with wave-body and hydrostatic verification experiments in Isaac Sim. II. B ACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

Marine robotics simulators span a range of design pri-orities, including vessel dynamics, perception, middleware integration, and robot-learning workflows. For surface-vehicle autonomy development, we focus on four capabilities that are particularly relevant to ASTRA’s design: surface-vehicle support, wave-body interaction, visual realism, and ROS 2 integration. Several mature simulation platforms address these capabilities with different emphases [9]–[14]. Notably, all of these simulators have some form of ROS 2 support. Table I summarizes these distinctions, while the following discussion positions ASTRA within this existing simulator landscape. 

A. Simulation Platform 

There is no single simulator platform that is the best fit for all robotics and marine research. Stonefish [12] stands out as a mature, custom-built simulator specifically for marine robotics research. VRX [13] is built on Gazebo, which provides a strong physics engine and years of hydrodynamic plugin support alongside ROS 2 compatibility. Yet, it lacks the photo-realism that Unreal Engine or NVIDIA platforms can provide. Unreal Engine [10], [11] also provides a strong physics engine coupled with high rendering quality. Yet, being a game engine, it lacks a native ROS 2 workflow and the tightly integrated, GPU-parallel robot-learning tooling that NVIDIA’s Isaac platforms provide. Isaac Sim targets interactive robotics simulation and provides native sensor and ROS 2 interfaces, while Isaac Lab extends this environment with GPU-parallel tooling for robot-learning applications. This combination of robotics integration and a direct learning workflow motivated ASTRA’s use of the NVIDIA simulation stack. 

B. Surface Vehicle Support 

MarineGym [9] is built exclusively for the underwater domain. Stonefish and HoloOcean were primarily built for underwater robotics research, and while they support surface vehicles, that support remains limited. VRX, ASVSim [10], and RANS-ASV [14] were explicitly built for surface vehicles. Among the simulators designed specifically for surface vehi-cles, VRX provides explicit 6-DOF wave-driven vessel motion, whereas ASVSim and RANS-ASV primarily model planar surface-vehicle dynamics. We believe a simulation supporting full 6-DOF is important for studying problems involving wave-induced motion, such as navigation through waves. 

C. Waves 

Marine surface autonomy couples perception and control because wave-induced motion perturbs both vehicle dynamics and sensor orientation. Existing simulators address different portions of this problem. VRX provides surface-vessel dy-namics driven by a spectral sea state, but its Gazebo-based rendering limits visual realism [13]. HoloOcean provides high-quality Unreal Engine rendering and a broad marine sensing and autonomy framework, but it is built around underwater robotic applications [11]. Stonefish provides geometry-aware hydrodynamics and FFT-based ocean waves, but the emphasis remains on underwater dynamics and rendering [12]. ASTRA targets the combination of above-water visual realism, surface-vehicle wave-body coupling, and ROS 2 autonomy interfaces within Isaac Sim. The “Waves” column in Table I denotes explicit wave-field support and does not imply equivalent wave-body fidelity across simulators. 

D. Photorealism 

Visual mismatch between simulation and reality is a known contributor to the sim-to-real gap. Prior work has shown that variation in textures, lighting, object appearance, and camera parameters can substantially affect transfer from synthetic to real imagery [15]. In marine robotics, recent simulation work similarly emphasizes high-fidelity, physics-based rendering as a means of reducing perception-domain mismatch [16]. These considerations, together with our prior development experience in VRX, motivated ASTRA’s emphasis on perceptual variation in lighting, shoreline texture, and water appearance. Between Stonefish’s custom rendering, Unreal Engine, and NVIDIA’s Isaac Sim, we do not identify a single simulation platform that outperforms the others in terms of photorealism, however. We therefore do not treat rendering quality alone as the primary distinction between these platforms. Choosing NVIDIA’s Isaac Sim as the development medium was moti-vated by: 1) The possibility of extension to Isaac Lab for RL. 2) A strong underlying physics engine. 3) ROS 2 and built-in sensor model support. 4) Successful usage in the broader robotics community.

III. T ECHNICAL APPROACH 

The ASTRA extension is built around a small set of cooperating components: the ASV and buoyancy solver, the ocean-surface model, a GNSS sensor node, and a manager for floating environmental debris. All of them advance on the same physics-step callback, so vehicle dynamics, water anima-tion, sensing, and environment interactions stay synchronized within a single step. LiDAR, RGB-D, and IMU sensing are provided directly by Isaac Sim’s own ROS 2 sensor prims. The following sections describe each of these pieces. 

A. 6-DOF Dynamics 

The motions in our simulator are based on Fossen-style surface-vessel dynamics [17]. This choice provides a phys-ically meaningful representation of low-speed ASV motion while maintaining a structure compatible with guidance and control methods commonly used for ASVs, where Eq. These approximations are intended to strike a practical balance between realism and real-time per-formance, retaining computational efficiency and scalability.

Each BlueBoat pontoon is represented by a lower-resolution proxy mesh that preserves the overall hull shape and waterline profile while reducing the number of vertices queried at each physics step. E. Autonomy Stack 1) Sensors: Autonomy stack compatibility is essential to this work. Isaac Sim contains robotics interfaces providing LiDAR, RGB-D, and IMU outputs, making it well-suited for this goal. Since Isaac Sim does not natively supply a GNSS sensor, collected real-world data were used to develop a custom configuration that mimics RTK-GPS capabilities of the Septentrio mosaic-X5 sensor. 

2) Simulation Environment: The developed simulator in-cludes a diverse inland water scene intended to expose the autonomy stack to variation in lighting, visual environmen-tal features, and shoreline structure. Floating algae patches, branches, buoys, shoreline vegetation, and underwater rocks are added from the Isaac Sim asset library, enriching the scene overall. Water transparency can be toggled on or off, as shown in Figure 1, and scene lighting can be adjusted. These features allow for flexible simulation of test environments in varying perceptual conditions. IV. D EMONSTRATION 

To demonstrate the functionality of our simulator, we aim to answer the following questions: 

(Q1): Do the simulated waves induce meaningful motions on the ASV? 

(Q2): Can existing autonomy modules from the physical testbed be used directly in simulation? 

(Q3): Is ASTRA fast enough for interactive development? 

(Q1): Wave and Buoyancy Verification. 

To verify the wave-body interactions described earlier, we ran zero-propulsion tests directly in ASTRA at varying wave and ASV starting conditions, checking that the wave-buoyancy coupling chain (surface elevation → submerged volume →

buoyancy force → vessel motion) behaves as intended and that the buoyancy model’s roll dynamics are consistent with a hydrostatics-only prediction from the same hull geometry. These experiments do not claim the dynamics are faithful to the real world and are intended only to demonstrate that the water mesh and buoyancy estimator meaningfully drive the boat’s motion. 

1) Roll response: We tested free roll decay in still water as a consistency check. Since no measured roll field data exist, we use several test-specific assumptions. A static analysis of the hull mesh gives a roll stiffness of Kϕ = 220 .5 N m/rad. For the test, a roll inertia of Ixx = 1 .0 kg m 2 and total roll damping c = Kp + cbuoy + cP hysX = 30 .0 N m s/rad were used. Here, Kp denotes the linear roll-damping coefficient in D(ν), cbuoy is the damping contribution introduced by the buoyancy solver, and cPhysX is the additional rigid-body angular damping applied by the physics engine. Because all three act simultaneously in simulation, all three are included in the effective damping used for this consistency check. This gives ζ ≈ 1.01 , corresponding to a slightly overdamped, non-oscillatory response. Two trials released from ±8◦ returned upright without overshoot, consistent with this qualitative pre-diction. Because the roll inertia and non-planar damping terms have not been calibrated from field data, we treat this result as an internal consistency check rather than a quantitative validation of the physical BlueBoat’s roll dynamics.

(Q2): Autonomy-Stack Portability 

To evaluate ASTRA’s sensor and autonomy interfaces, we ported existing BlueBoat modules without changing their ROS 2 interfaces. GPS-DRIFT [22] is an Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF) localization algorithm that fuses IMU and GPS data and provides pose, twist, and path ROS 2 messages. ConvBKI [23], a probabilistic semantic mapping module, tests the usability of the LiDAR and camera while fusing the perception algorithms with localization. Both mod-ules are directly ported from the physical BlueBoat, where they have already demonstrated general functionality. The Lie-algebraic Model Predictive Controller [24] used on the real vehicle is architecturally compatible with these same interfaces. Figure 3 summarizes the simulated autonomy stack pipeline and its evaluation on recorded real-world data. 

A. Localization 

GPS-DRIFT [22] fuses proprioceptive IMU propagation with GPS position corrections in a symmetry-preserving In-EKF and runs directly against ASTRA’s GNSS and IMU interfaces, publishing the same pose, twist, and path messages used on the physical BlueBoat at 61.8 Hz. Against Isaac Sim ground truth over a 189 m trajectory, it achieves a position RMSE of less than 3 cm. Fig. 3. On the left are the 3rd-person and RGB views of the Isaac Sim stage and the semantic map in RViz built from its localization, LiDAR, and RGB-D inputs. On the right is the same ConvBKI pipeline, with the same weights and configuration, run offline on real-world data from the physical BlueBoat. 

B. Semantic Mapping 

We use ConvBKI [23], a convolutional Bayesian kernel in-ference network for real-time probabilistic semantic mapping, as a stress test for the combined use of LiDAR, RGB-D, and pose (Figure 3, left and center panels). It consumes point clouds, images, and pose at the sensor rate and accumulates them into a persistent map over a run. Map updates were published at 2.2 Hz, producing a spatially coherent map of the stage. This demonstrates that ASTRA’s LiDAR and RGB-D outputs can drive ConvBKI while the mapping pipeline consumes pose estimates from GPS-DRIFT. To probe transfer, this same ConvBKI pipeline, with the same weights and configuration, was then run offline on a recorded ROS 2 bag of real-world sensor data collected by the physical BlueBoat on the pond that ASTRA models. The resulting real-world semantic map is qualitatively consistent with the scene structure recovered in the simulated run.

C. Limitations 

ASTRA currently provides field-informed planar (surge, yaw) damping but has not been calibrated against physical heave, roll, and pitch response. As such, the roll-decay check above is a consistency result against a placeholder inertia, not a real-vehicle validation. Semantic-mapping transfer is similarly qualitative, since no labeled ground truth exists yet for the real pond. Finally, the wave model here is verified for repeatable, physically consistent disturbance generation rather than field-validated seakeeping prediction or a calibrated sea state. V. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 

ASTRA provides an Isaac Sim-based ASV environment combining ROS 2 sensing, field-informed marine dynamics, geometry-based buoyancy, and programmable wave distur-bances. Field data improved the simulator’s planar damping model, while hydrostatic and wave-response experiments veri-fied the intended vessel-water coupling. The existing BlueBoat autonomy modules, running directly against ASTRA’s ROS 2 interfaces, demonstrated the usability of the platform at interactive rates. Future work will focus on calibrating the remaining 6-DOF dynamics and propulsion model, along with extension to Isaac Lab for reinforcement learning research applications. ACKNOWLEDGMENT
