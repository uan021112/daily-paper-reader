Beyond Semantic Classes: Online Physical Parameter
Estimation via Embedding-Conditioned Gaussian Mixtures

Abstract: Enabling robots to infer interaction-relevant physical properties from
vision before contact would improve planning and control in unstructured environ-
ments. Vision provides useful pre-contact information, but appearance alone is
often ambiguous: visually similar regions can induce different friction, stiffness, or
sinkage responses upon contact. Existing approaches often collapse this ambiguity
to a point estimate or aggregate experience through fixed semantic classes, limiting
their ability to preserve distinct physical regimes. Instead, we propose a real-
time estimator that replaces semantic class indexing with an interaction-grounded
Gaussian-mixture posterior over latent physical parameters, conditioned directly on
continuous visual embeddings. Our approach maintains interaction-derived Gaus-
sian parameter estimates, typically from inverse modeling, as mixture components
while using embedding similarity to retrieve and weight relevant past interactions
for each new query region. A support-aware fusion mechanism incorporates a
conditional generative prior model when interaction history is sparse, while a
learned embedding refinement improves retrieval relevance without discretizing the
representation. Our experiments show that the estimator recovers multimodal phys-
ical beliefs, rejects unrelated interaction history, avoids spatial map discretization,
and evaluates at controller rates. In a high-fidelity autonomous racing simulation
with unknown low- and high-friction surface regions, the posterior enables the con-
troller to adapt over repeated laps as interaction evidence accumulates, improving
performance while maintaining friction-aware behavior.
Keywords: Parameter Estimation, Multimodal Beliefs, Vision-Based Control

Introduction
Autonomous systems operating in unstructured environments select control actions whose outcomes
depend on physical parameters of the surface ahead that cannot be measured at range. Model-based
controllers that reason over uncertainty in these parameters can bound risk against adverse outcomes,
but doing so requires a distributional estimate rather than a point prediction. Vision provides pre-
contact evidence, yet visual appearance does not uniquely determine physical parameters: dry asphalt
and black ice can appear similar while differing in friction by an order of magnitude. A pre-contact
estimate should therefore preserve the set of plausible parameter values rather than collapse them to a
single prediction. Since vision cannot resolve this ambiguity on its own, interaction measurements
provide the missing physical evidence: they reveal which parameter regime was realized and should
reshape the belief assigned to visually similar regions encountered later. This paper investigates
how to maintain a real-time, interaction-based multimodal belief over latent physical parameters by
combining visual embeddings with post-contact measurements, producing a posterior that can inform
downstream planning and control under physical-parameter uncertainty.
Existing vision-based physical prediction methods [1, 2, 3, 4], use appearance to estimate terrain
parameters before contact, but they typically return a single nominal value. This discards information
when the same visual evidence is consistent with multiple physical regimes. Methods that incorporate
interaction measurements address a different part of the problem: they can revise parameter estimates
after contact and propagate those updates to future predictions. However, existing formulations
typically organize this experience through a fixed set of semantic terrain or material classes [5, 6].

In these methods, the semantic label becomes the unit of generalization: measurements from one
region update a class-level Gaussian that is reused for other regions with the same label. This creates
a representation bottleneck. Physically distinct surfaces that share a label are averaged into a single
unimodal estimate, while materials outside the vocabulary must be forced into an existing class,
shifting that class’s estimate toward an unrelated parameter regime. The resulting belief is constrained
by the semantic vocabulary rather than the continuous structure of visual observations.
We propose to replace semantic class indexing with a posterior conditioned on continuous visual
embeddings. Each interaction produces a Gaussian estimate of the latent physical parameter through
inverse modeling, which we store together with the visual embedding of the corresponding region.
For a new query, a similarity kernel over embeddings assigns weights to estimates from visually
related past interactions, forming a Gaussian mixture posterior. Because the interaction-derived
estimates remain separate mixture components, the posterior can represent multiple physical regimes
for visually similar terrain rather than averaging them into a single class-level Gaussian. When the
query is weakly supported by prior interactions, we blend the history-based mixture with a conditional
prior over Gaussian parameter estimates; when instantiated as a diffusion model, samples from this
prior contribute additional Gaussian components while keeping the final posterior explicit, efficient
to evaluate or sample on the GPU, and directly usable inside a real-time model-based controller.
In practice, our formulation depends on the geometry of the visual embedding space. Similarity in
that space should reflect relevance for physical-parameter prediction, but raw pretrained embeddings
are also sensitive to nuisance factors such as lighting, viewpoint, camera pose, and residual positional
structure. These effects can separate observations that should share interaction evidence, or make
irrelevant observations appear close under the kernel. We therefore learn an input-dependent refine-
ment of the visual embeddings before computing similarity. The refinement improves retrieval of
relevant interaction measurements while avoiding a fixed semantic vocabulary.
Together, these pieces turn visual prediction into an interaction-based posterior that remains multi-
modal, tractable, and fast enough for model-based control. Our contributions are as follows:
• An interaction-grounded Gaussian-mixture posterior over latent physical parameters, con-
ditioned on continuous visual embeddings, that preserves multimodal variation without
semantic class indexing.
• A support-aware fusion mechanism that combines interaction history with a generative prior;
when the prior is defined to output Gaussian belief parameters, the posterior remains an
explicit mixture that is efficient to evaluate and sample.
• A retrieval-space refinement that improves embedding similarity without discretizing the
visual representation, together with a real-time evaluation in a closed-loop, high-fidelity
autonomous racing simulation with unknown low- and high-friction surface regions.

Related Work
Visual observations are widely used to infer terrain properties before contact. Early work used
appearance and material recognition to estimate terrain friction, demonstrating that pre-contact visual
evidence can inform physical interaction [7]. Subsequent methods predict traction, traversability,
speed, cost, or dynamics from visual input for off-road planning and control [3, 8, 9, 10], including
recent approaches that use simulation-derived labels [11], cross-modal supervision [4], or physics
decoders [12] to predict quantities such as friction and stiffness. These methods establish vision as
a useful source of pre-contact physical information, but most do not return a distribution over the
latent physical parameters. Some other approaches explicitly represent uncertainty in the predicted
quantity. For example, learned speed-distribution maps estimate distributions over achievable speed
for risk-aware planning [13]. However, such methods do not update the physical-parameter belief
using interaction measurements collected after contact.
Prior work has also used tactile, proprioceptive, or other measurements collected through interaction
to revise physical-parameter estimates after contact. Semantic-property mapping methods associate
terrain or material classes with probabilistic estimates of physical quantities, allowing measurements
collected at one location to inform predictions elsewhere through the shared class representation. [14]
maintain probabilistic terrain-property estimates indexed by semantic class, and [5] jointly update
semantic predictions and physical-property estimates from visual and tactile measurements. Related
filtering approaches model evolving associations between semantic classes and inferred dynamical
parameters, allowing the estimated physical properties of a class to change as new measurements are
observed [6]. These methods provide a principled way to combine visual prediction with physical
evidence collected through interaction, though the fixed semantic representation can cause class-level
beliefs to average distinct physical regimes within a label and be corrupted when out-of-vocabulary
materials are absorbed into an existing class.
Large-scale visual pretraining has produced continuous embeddings that transfer across robotic
perception, mapping, navigation, and prediction tasks. Vision foundation models [15, 16] such
as CLIP and DINO provide high-dimensional features used for traversability learning, off-road
navigation, open-dictionary mapping, and dynamics prediction [8, 9, 17, 10]. These works show
that continuous representations can support robotic prediction without reducing every observation
to a fixed semantic label. However, pretrained embeddings are not guaranteed to provide the right
similarity geometry for physical-parameter prediction: nuisance factors such as lighting, distance,
viewpoint, occlusion, or residual positional structure can affect nearest-neighbor relationships in
feature space. We therefore refine the embedding space so that similarity better reflects relevance for
physical interaction, rather than generic visual or semantic similarity. In contrast to using continuous
embeddings only as features for prediction, we use them as the conditioning space for an interaction-
grounded posterior over latent physical parameters. For probabilistic prediction from these continuous
representations, Gaussian-process regression offers another approach, with sparse approximations
that can support real-time prediction [18, 19]. However, standard Gaussian-likelihood formulations
produce a single Gaussian predictive belief at each query, which cannot preserve distinct physical
modes associated with similar visual observations. Their Gaussian priors also restrict the distributional
form available when interaction evidence is limited. Sparse inducing-point methods additionally
compress accumulated evidence into a finite representation, introducing an approximation whose
quality depends on the number and placement of inducing points.

Method
We consider the problem of estimating a latent physical parameter zq ∈Rd associated with a query
region observed before contact. The parameter may encode friction, stiffness, sinkage, or other local
quantities that govern the environment’s interaction response. The estimator receives a continuous
visual embedding eq ∈Rm for the query region and an interaction history DN = {(ei, ϕi)}N
i=1,
where ei is the embedding associated with a previously traversed region and ϕi parameterizes an
uncertain estimate of the corresponding physical parameter. Our goal is to construct an interaction-
based predictive belief ˆp(zq|eq; DN) given DN, without reducing the visual observation to a discrete
semantic class. In what follows, we use p(·|eq) instead of p(·|eq; DN) when clear from context.

Retrieval-space refinement.
The posterior in Eq. (6) relies on the kernel Kh(eq, ei) to identify
which interaction-derived beliefs are relevant to a query. This requires an embedding geometry in
which similarity reflects physical-parameter relevance, not only generic visual similarity. In practice,
raw pretrained embeddings can be sensitive to nuisance variation such as lighting, viewpoint, camera
pose, texture bias, or residual positional structure. These effects can distort retrieval: regions with
relevant interaction evidence may be separated in embedding space, while physically irrelevant
regions may receive high similarity. Appendix A provides an empirical illustration of this behavior
on our simulated images. We therefore learn a residual refinement of the base visual embedding
before computing kernel similarities.

Experiments
We evaluate our approach through four simulation experiments and a real-vehicle parameter-prediction
experiment. First, we test whether conditioning on continuous embeddings avoids the class averaging
and out-of-vocabulary contamination that arise in baseline approaches with fixed semantic classes.
Second, we show that the same posterior construction can use kernels beyond visual similarity by
composing the embedding similarity kernel with a spatial proximity kernel. Third, we benchmark
history-query latency as the interaction database grows. Fourth, we evaluate the estimator when used
for control in a high-fidelity autonomous racing simulation. Finally, we evaluate real-vehicle parame-
ter prediction using recorded trajectories and controls (Section 4.4). The simulation experiments are
run in BeamNG [24] with a forward-facing camera; images are encoded with DINOv3 [16], refined
as in Sec. 3, and stored with camera poses in a FIFO buffer. Contact points along the trajectory
generate uncertain local parameter estimates ϕi = (µi, σ2
i ) from a Normal-Inverse-Gamma model,
which are linked to vision by projecting each contact into the buffered frames and adding the closest
valid embedding-estimate pair (ei, ϕi) to the interaction history. Further details of the simulation
setup are given in Appendix B.

Semantic Class Bottlenecks: Multimodality and Out-of-Vocabulary Contamination
We compare our estimator with a semantic-property baseline in the style of [5]. The environment
contains grass, dirt, and concrete. We test the estimators in two scenarios: E1 and E2. Grass and
dirt have the same unimodal friction distributions in both scenarios. In E1, concrete is included in
the semantic vocabulary, and we assign it a bimodal friction distribution representing high-friction
dry areas and low-friction areas covered by black ice. This creates two physical regimes within one
semantic class, providing a controlled test of whether the predictive belief preserves both modes. In
E2, concrete has a unimodal distribution but removed from the semantic vocabulary, so concrete
observations are represented by ambiguous semantic evidence over the remaining grass and dirt
classes.

Closed-loop adaptation on mixed-friction surfaces. We test this stack on an oval track containing
high-friction asphalt and an unknown low-friction icy segment. A generative prior-only controller,
which does not update tire parameters from interaction, plans aggressively into the low-friction
region, typically reaches the lateral-error bounds, and exhibits peak entry errors near 2 m with
sideslip increasing to about 0.18 rad. In contrast, the proposed estimator updates the posterior as
interaction evidence accumulates, allowing the optimizer to generate progressively more surface-
aware references. As shown in Fig. 5, online adaptation reduces the low-friction entry error to about
0.5 m and keeps sideslip below 0.1 rad, significantly improving closed-loop racing performance
relative to baseline.
Across nine complete traversals of the same s = 200–300 m low-friction segment, the proposed
estimator reduces traversal time from 9.304±0.316 to 8.135±0.007 s (12.6%) and increases mean
speed from 10.51 to 11.78 m/s. The prior-only baseline overshoots at ice entry and slows to 3.0–
4.8 m/s during MPC correction, whereas the interaction-based controller remains above 7.84 m/s
with 16× lower across-traversal speed-profile variance. Lateral-error, heading-error, and sideslip
root-mean-square errors decrease from 1.637 to 0.121 m, 0.173 to 0.017 rad, and 0.089 to 0.027 rad,
respectively. These results show that a more conservative planned entry speed can preserve momentum
and reduce traversal time. Additional plots and implementation details are provided in Appendix C.

4.4
Real-vehicle parameter prediction
We evaluate vehicle-motion prediction on recorded grass trajectories from the autonomous off-road
vehicle shown in Appendix D. Local Gaussian parameter beliefs are inferred from measured states
and controls through trajectory-based nonlinear log-likelihood inversion, with covariances obtained
from a temperature-scaled Gauss–Newton inverse-Hessian approximation. These beliefs are stored
with the corresponding terrain embeddings. Embedding–measurement pairs from a separate concrete
run are included as distractors, testing retrieval of relevant grass evidence.
Retrieval uses a cosine-similarity cutoff of 0.90, kernel scale 10, and support parameter κ = 2. We
set α = 0. At selected locations, look-ahead images supply query embeddings. We sample physical
parameters from the prior and retrieved history and simulate 2.5 s trajectories from the same measured
state under the recorded controls. This evaluates prediction against the recorded trajectory rather than
closed-loop adaptation.
Figure 6 compares the resulting rollouts. As interaction evidence accumulates across 18 measure-
ments, mean per-particle average displacement error over the prediction horizon decreases from 0.737
to 0.628 m (14.8%), while final displacement error decreases from 2.096 to 1.750 m (16.5%).
5
Conclusion
We propose an interaction-grounded estimator for online physical-parameter prediction from visual
observations. By combining continuous visual embeddings with Gaussian beliefs inferred from
prior interactions, the method produces an explicit multimodal posterior without semantic class
indexing. Through high-fidelity simulation, we show that the estimator avoids class-level averaging
and out-of-vocabulary contamination, scales to large interaction histories at real-time control rates,
and enables closed-loop adaptation in autonomous racing across low- and high-friction surfaces.
Limitations and future work. Closed-loop adaptation has so far been evaluated in simulation; the
real-vehicle experiment evaluates parameter prediction under recorded controls. In addition, although
the estimator represents a multimodal posterior over physical parameters, the current controller uses
posterior summaries rather than explicitly optimizing over the full distribution. Risk-sensitive or
chance-constrained control could further improve robustness and guide active data collection in
poorly supported regions. Finally, our formulation assumes access to local Gaussian beliefs from
interaction; improving the robustness of trajectory-based inverse modeling across broader operating
conditions remains an important direction for future work.

Simulation and Experimental Setup
This appendix details the simulation environment and the runtime pipeline shared across all simulation
experiments, followed by the hyperparameters used in each. The controller and generative-prior
details specific to the closed-loop racing experiment are deferred to Appendix C.
B.1
Environment
All simulation experiments run in the BeamNG.tech driving simulator [24], which provides high-
fidelity vehicle dynamics and rendered camera imagery. A forward-facing pinhole camera rigidly
mounted to the vehicle produces 512 × 512 RGB frames; its calibrated intrinsics K, mounting offset,
and per-frame pose T (f)
wc ∈SE(3) are recorded with each frame so that world points can later be
projected into image space. The simulator reports the vehicle’s world-frame position at every step,
and the terrain layout is known as a per-pixel map, so the surface a contact lands on is recovered by
indexing the map at the vehicle’s position; this terrain map is used only to assign the physical surface
at a contact, never as an input to the estimator. Fig. 8 shows the live system during a run.
B.2
Runtime pipeline
We describe the pipeline in the order events occur during a run: frames are encoded as they arrive,
buffered with their poses, and later associated with contact events to populate the interaction history
DN.
Encoding.
Each incoming camera frame is passed through a single perception pipeline that is
reused identically at training, deployment, and offline replay. The pipeline (i) encodes the frame
with a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 backbone [16], producing a 32 × 32 grid of 768-dimensional patch
embeddings (ii) removes a fixed low-dimensional positional component from each patch embedding
(iii) applies the learned residual refinement rη of Sec. 3 and (iv) renormalizes each patch embedding
to the unit sphere. The first two stages are deterministic and shared with the embedding refinement
training described in Appendix A.
Frame buffering.
Encoded frames are placed in a rolling buffer that retains the most recent 256
frames together with their camera poses. Buffering is necessary because contact evidence is not
available at the instant a region is first seen: the vehicle observes a region from a distance, then
contacts it some frames later. The buffer therefore preserves a short window of recent viewpoints
so that, when a contact occurs, the system can recover a frame in which the contacted location was
actually visible.

C.5
Generative prior
The conditional prior p0(· | eq) used in the racing experiment is a flow model over the 21-dimensional
tire-parameter vector conditioned on the embedding eq. The prior is trained with flow matching on
synthetically constructed embedding–parameter pairs. Embeddings assigned to ice receive noisy
targets centered on the ground-truth concrete tire-parameter vector θconcrete; embeddings assigned
to snow receive noisy targets centered on 0.9θconcrete + 0.1θsnow, where θsnow is the ground-truth
snow vector. These deliberately biased targets provide an imperfect prior for evaluating correction
through interaction evidence; the simulated surface parameters retain their ground-truth values.
The trunk has two hidden layers of width 256 with 64-dimensional time embeddings; we train with
AdamW at learning rate 3 × 10−4, weight EMA (decay 0.999), for ∼12k iterations, and use a single
integration step at deployment.

E
Effect of Multimodality on Collision-Risk Estimation
The importance of preserving multimodality becomes apparent when evaluating collision risk under
uncertain physical parameters. Consider a surface that may correspond to either high-friction concrete
or low-friction black ice, yielding a bimodal friction distribution. We compare this bimodal belief
with a moment-matched Gaussian having the same mean and variance.
To evaluate the effect of these different belief representations, we sample friction parameters from
each distribution and propagate the resulting trajectories through the nonlinear vehicle dynamics
described in Appendix C.1. All rollouts begin from the same initial state and follow the same fixed
control sequence near an obstacle. Figure 12 shows the resulting trajectories.
Despite having identical means and variances, the two beliefs produce substantially different collision
rates. More than 40% of rollouts sampled from the bimodal belief collide with the obstacle, compared
with fewer than 1% of those sampled from the moment-matched Gaussian. This illustrates how
collapsing distinct physical modes into a single Gaussian can substantially underestimate collision
risk. While this experiment isolates the effect of belief representation under fixed controls, it motivates
risk-aware controllers that explicitly reason over the full mixture posterior rather than relying on
posterior summaries.
