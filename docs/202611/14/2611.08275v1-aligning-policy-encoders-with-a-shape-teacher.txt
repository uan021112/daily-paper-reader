Abstract 

3D-aware representations can improve robotic manipulation by providing geometric information that is robust to changes in viewpoint, object position, and occlusion. However, using 3D inputs at deployment requires additional sensing and processing, making image-based policies more practical. We ask whether an image-based policy can acquire 3D shape information during training while retaining a purely image-based interface at deployment. We propose shape-teacher alignment , a training-time mechanism that uses a pretrained 3D shape representation as an alignment target for an image-based policy encoder. Specifically, we use Point-MAE features from individual objects as targets for corresponding image patches, transferring geometric structure to the policy without changing its deployment inputs or architecture. On LIBERO, shape-teacher alignment produces image representations that are more structured around object shape and corresponding parts, while substantially accelerating policy convergence and improving success rates. For pretrained policies, convergence is accelerated by 4.7 × and 5.6 × on the Object and Spatial suites, respectively, without requiring the shape teacher or depth input at deployment. The project is available at: https://miepjemadelon. github.io/aligningpolicieswithashapeteacher/ .

1 Introduction 

Geometric information is important for robotic manipulation, with 3D-aware policies demonstrating improved data efficiency and robustness to variations such as viewpoint changes, object displacement, and occlusion [Zhen et al., 2024, Sun et al., 2025, Li et al., 2026a, Qu et al., 2025, Ze et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2023]. However, incorporating 3D observations into a robotic policy requires additional sensing and processing, including depth cameras, calibration, and depth estimation. These costs make purely image-based policies attractive in practice, but such policies can lack the geometric information needed for robust manipulation [Sun et al., 2025, Garcia et al., 2025]. We therefore ask: can a 2D robotic policy acquire shape-aware representations during training without requiring 3D inputs at deployment? Recent work on diffusion models has shown that pretrained representations can guide a target model through Representation Alignment (REPA). REPA [Yu et al., 2025] aligns intermediate representations with features from a frozen pretrained encoder, improving convergence and generation quality, and has since been applied to robotic policies using pretrained vision encoders as alignment targets [Li et al., 2025, Qiu et al., 2026]. However, these approaches do not specifically target 3D geometric structure, leaving open whether alignment with a representation that explicitly captures 3D shape can introduce geometric structure into an image-based policy. We use a pretrained 3D shape teacher, Point-MAE [Pang et al., 2022], to align the image encoder of a 2D diffusion policy during training. Point-MAE features from individual objects are aligned with their corresponding image patches, encouraging the policy encoder to incorporate geometric structure while retaining the original image-based policy interface. 2 Method 

Baseline Diffusion Policy We use the diffusion policy of Chi et al. [2025] as our behavioral cloning baseline. The policy conditions on two consecutive observations, t − 1 and t, and predicts a 10-step action trajectory, of which 8 steps are executed. We keep the action diffusion process unchanged, while modifying the observation encoder and transformer decoder. The observation encoder is a ViT/S-14 with the same architectural modifications as DINOv2: the CLS token is discarded, and the remaining patch tokens are used as observation features. The language token is produced by a frozen CLIP language model [Radford et al., 2021], and the diffusion timestep is encoded using a sinusoidal embedding. The transformer decoder follows the cross-attention architecture of Chi et al. [2025] and consists of a 12-layer decoder and a 2-layer encoder for the conditioning tokens. Inspired by Dasari et al. [2025], the output projections of the cross-attention blocks are zero-initialized to improve stability. 

Shape-Teacher Alignment We align image-patch representations with features from a frozen pretrained 3D shape encoder. We use Point-MAE [Pang et al., 2022] as the shape teacher, which encodes individual 3D objects into local shape features. For each object, we construct a point cloud from its mask and depth map, resample it to 1024 points, and partition the points into local groups of 

M = 32 points. Point-MAE then produces a feature for each group. The key challenge is to associate these 3D features with the corresponding image patches. We there-fore back-project each 3D point to its corresponding pixel in the image and use these correspondences to determine how strongly each local 3D group contributes to each image patch (Figure 6). 3 Experiments 

We evaluate whether shape-teacher alignment changes the learned image representations and improves policy learning, while preserving the image-only deployment interface. We consider two common regimes for robotic policy learning: training a task-specific policy from scratch on the target tasks, and fine-tuning a policy from a pretrained checkpoint. This allows us to evaluate shape-teacher alignment both when the policy learns its visual representation from scratch and when it starts from an existing pretrained representation. 3.1 Shape- and Object-centric Representation 

We examine whether shape-teacher alignment transfers geometric structure into the image encoder, using patch-level similarity to assess correspondence across object parts and object-level clustering to assess object structure. 

Shape Grouping We first examine whether image patches representing similar object parts and shapes become more similar after alignment. For each selected patch, we identify the most similar patch among the image features and record whether it corresponds to the same object, another object with similar shape, or an unrelated region. Table 1 shows the resulting distribution on LIBERO-Object. For pretrained policies, alignment with the shape teacher increases the fraction of matches to another similar-shape object from 8.2% to 35.1%, while the fraction of matches falling into the “unrelated” category decreases from 81.6% to 56.0%. The same trend is observed for policies trained from scratch, where similar-shape matches increase from 10.7% to 36.4%. Object Grouping We next examine whether alignment also organizes representations at the object level. We average the patch features corresponding to each object and visualize the resulting representations with t-SNE in Figure 2. Compared with the baseline, the aligned representations form clearer clusters of objects, indicating that alignment also produces stronger object-level structure. Together with the patch-level analysis above, this suggests that shape-teacher alignment organizes the image representation both across corresponding object parts and at the level of whole objects. 

3.2 Improved Convergence 

We next examine whether the representational changes induced by shape-teacher alignment translate into faster policy learning. Figure 3 compares the success rate throughout training for the baseline and Point-MAE-aligned policies in both training regimes. For pretrained policies, alignment reaches the same success rate as the baseline 4.7 × faster on LIBERO-Object and 5.6 × faster on LIBERO-Spatial. The effect is also present when training from scratch, with convergence accelerated by 2.3 × and 3.9 × on the Object and Spatial suites, respectively. The consistent acceleration across both training regimes shows that the shape-teacher objective improves the optimization trajectory of the policy rather than only changing its final representation. The larger gains for pretrained policies suggest that shape-teacher alignment is particularly effective when adapting an existing visual representation to the target manipulation tasks. 

3.3 Robustness 

We next examine whether the representational changes induced by shape-teacher alignment improve robustness to changes in the deployment environment. We evaluate performance under target displacement and object occlusion by measuring the change in success rate relative to the standard evaluation setting. Figure 4 shows that the effect of alignment depends on both the type of distribution shift and the task suite. Under occlusion, the aligned policy has a similar performance drop to the baseline on LIBERO-Object but a smaller drop on LIBERO-Spatial. 3.4 Alignment Target Ablation 

We finally examine whether the structure learned by the policy encoder depends on the choice of alignment target. We replace the Point-MAE shape teacher with DINOv2 [Oquab et al., 2024], a pretrained semantic vision encoder, while keeping the alignment procedure and policy architecture unchanged. DINOv2-based runs use an alignment weight of λ = 1 . Table 2 compares the resulting policy performance and convergence speed. DINOv2 provides a strong alignment target and achieves higher success rates in several settings, while Point-MAE remains competitive on pretrained policies. More importantly, the two alignment targets produce qualitatively different representation structures. Figure 5 shows patch-level similarity for the two aligned encoders. DINOv2 produces more semantic-oriented similarity patterns, whereas Point-MAE produces greater similarity across similarly shaped objects and corresponding object parts. Thus, the effect of alignment is not determined only by the alignment procedure itself: the choice of teacher influences the structural bias learned by the policy encoder. 64 Conclusion 

We introduced shape-teacher alignment as a training-time mechanism for transferring 3D shape structure into an image-based robotic policy without requiring 3D inputs at deployment. On LIBERO, alignment produces image representations that are structured around both object shape and object identity, and substantially accelerates policy convergence, with the largest gains observed for policies initialized from pretrained checkpoints. Robustness to distribution shifts is more conditional, varying across perturbation types and task suites. Comparisons with a semantic teacher further show that the choice of alignment target determines the structure induced in the policy representation. These results suggest that representation alignment can be used not only to improve policy optimization, but also to deliberately shape the geometric structure learned by an image-based robotic policy while preserving its deployment interface. 

Limitations and Future Work Our evaluation is limited to a single policy architecture and the LIBERO benchmark, and uses Point-MAE as a single 3D shape teacher. The effect of shape-teacher alignment across other policy architectures, robotic manipulation benchmarks, and pretrained 3D representations therefore remains to be established. The robustness experiments also show that alignment does not uniformly improve performance under all distribution shifts. Future work will investigate whether stronger or more diverse 3D teachers, including representations that combine geometric and semantic information, can transfer complementary structure to the policy encoder, and whether alignment can improve robustness under more challenging forms of occlusion and distribution shift. Extending the evaluation to additional architectures and real-robot settings will further clarify the generality of training-time geometric alignment.

B Shape grouping Experimental Details 

For the shape grouping experiment, similar patches are grouped based on the object mask they land on. See Table 3 for the clustering groups. To get the final result, per encoded image, we select all patches that overlap by at least 50% with object masks in Table 3. For each of these patches in the object, the most similar patch in terms of cosine similarity is selected. Examples of this can be found in Figure 1 and in Figure 5. We record whether this patch is inside the same object instance, inside an object within the same group (see Table 3), or somewhere else in the image. We average these results over 1000 images to get the final results shown in Table 1. 

C Iterations over Lambda 

In addition to testing the effect of alignment for a fixed alignment weight, we also examined the effect of different alignment weights λ on the model’s convergence speed. The results for the from-scratch and pre-trained models can be found in Figure 7. These results suggest that the alignment weight λ

has a sweet spot, and that exceeding it can hurt performance, especially when training from scratch, as shown on the LIBERO-Object set. However, the convergence results differ greatly per configuration. For instance, on the pre-trained models, a higher alignment weight does not seem to strongly affect convergence, nor does it clearly degrade convergence. Still, more configurations and more seeds are needed before we can draw a clear conclusion from these results.

All experiments were run on the <redacted for anonymity> compute cluster. All experiments were run on an A100 GPU. Each training run took about 5 hours, and each checkpoint validation took about 15 minutes, for both standard and out-of-distribution validation. Experiments that only used the image encoder, such as patch similarities and t-SNE clustering, were negligible in compute, with each task taking less than 10 minutes.
