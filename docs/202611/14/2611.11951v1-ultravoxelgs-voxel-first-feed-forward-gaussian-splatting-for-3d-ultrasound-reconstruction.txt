Splatting for 3D Ultrasound Reconstruction

Abstract
We present UltraVoxelGS, a 3D ultrasound reconstruction method that achieves
both fast reconstruction and physically faithful rendering for the first time, bring-
ing live spatial feedback during 2D ultrasound scanning within reach. This is made
possible by a feed-forward design that predicts a complete Gaussian scene repre-
sentation from posed ultrasound images directly, entirely bypassing the per-scene
optimization bottleneck that has confined prior methods to offline use. Realising
such feed-forward prediction in this setting is non-trivial: unlike natural images,
ultrasound slices share at most one-dimensional intersections and exhibit view-
dependent appearance due to acoustic propagation and attenuation, violating the
dense overlap and photometric consistency assumed by existing feed-forward Gaus-
sian methods. Hence, to achieve fast reconstruction, we introduce a feed-forward
voxel-to-Gaussian pass that decouples representation size from input count by
lifting all posed slices into a fixed-size voxel support and predicting Gaussian
parameters directly. For faithful rendering, we propose ultrasound-aware appear-
ance rendering strategy factorize reflection from cumulative attenuation during
training and exploit appearance continuity among spatially adjacent slices for ef-
ficient Render-time Appearance Adaptation. On both real-world and simulated
datasets, UltraVoxelGS achieves best PSNR/SSIM compared with per-scene opti-
mization baselines while reducing inference from 10-20 minutes to approximately
10 seconds—over 35× faster—and scales to sequences exceeding 1,000 frames.
Code is available at https://github.com/YuebyYue/UltraVoxelGS-camera-ready.

Introduction
Despite being the most widely used diagnostic imaging modality [1, 2], ultrasound remains funda-
mentally limited by its 2D acquisition: sonographers scan a sequence of slices and must mentally
reconstruct the underlying 3D anatomy to determine which regions remain unseen. Incomplete
coverage is therefore common, especially among less experienced sonographers, and often goes
unnoticed during scanning. While dedicated 3D probes exist, their limited field of view makes
them inadequate for large-anatomy or extended-field-of-view examinations, leaving 2D freehand
scanning the dominant acquisition mode in practice. These limitations motivate the need for real-time
automatic 3D reconstruction from 2D ultrasound sequences[3–5]: during acquisition, a live 3D
view can provide immediate spatial feedback to guide operators toward uncovered regions. After
acquisition, a compact 3D representation also facilitates subsequent diagnosis and efficient archival.
Realising this vision (Fig. 1) requires both fast reconstruction—consolidating posed 2D slices into a
compact 3D representation in seconds, not minutes—and faithful rendering—synthesizing novel
ultrasound views that respect the directional, cumulative-attenuation physics of acoustic imaging.

No existing approach satisfies both requirements. Classical interpolation-based methods [6, 7] are
computationally cheap but treat ultrasound frames as generic intensity images, ignoring the directional
propagation and cumulative attenuation of acoustic waves; they reproduce plausible intensities along
scanned trajectories yet produce artifacts at novel viewpoints. Neural-field methods [8–11] achieve
higher fidelity by learning a continuous scene representation, yet they rely on iterative per-scene
optimization coupled with expensive volume rendering, making real-time reconstruction impractical.
More recently, Gaussian splatting (GS)[12] has emerged as a compelling alternative: its explicit
primitives support efficient rasterization-based rendering, and recent adaptations [13] show that the
formulation can accommodate ultrasound imaging assumptions. However, these 3D GS methods still
reconstruct each scene through iterative optimization, meaning that they enable fast rendering but not
fast reconstruction. The missing piece is not simply a faster renderer, but a feed-forward pathway
from input slice sequences to a high-fidelity Gaussian representation.
Feed-forward Gaussian prediction has been extensively explored in natural imaging [14–18], but
those methods assume dense view overlap and photometric consistency across viewpoints (Fig. 2
(a)). Freehand ultrasound violates both assumptions: each frame captures only a thin 2D slice of the
anatomy, so any two frames share at most a one-dimensional intersection (highlighted in Fig. 2 (c)),
providing far too little overlap for dense cross-view matching; and acoustic anisotropy makes the
appearance of the same tissue view-dependent, undermining photometric consistency even along
that shared line. The central challenge is therefore clear: each ultrasound slice carries only a thin
cross-section of the anatomy, so the information from any single frame or even a small group of
frames is far too sparse to support reliable Gaussian prediction. Existing multi-view feed-forward
methods partially address this by jointly processing several frames (Fig. 2 (b)), but their computational
cost scales with the number of input views, making them impractical for the thousands of slices
typical in freehand ultrasound. A scalable solution must therefore decouple representation size from
input count (shown in Fig. 2 (d)): first consolidate all posed slices into a fixed-size voxel support,
then predict Gaussian parameters from this unified spatial context.
We instantiate this idea in UltraVoxelGS, a feed-forward Gaussian splatting framework that, for the
first time, enables both real-time reconstruction and physically faithful rendering for 3D ultrasound.
Unlike prior feed-forward GS methods that generate pixel-aligned Gaussians from individual views
and aggregate them afterward, we use probe poses to lift 2D ultrasound slices into 3D space and
interpolate them into a unified voxel support, consolidating sparse observations into a coherent voxel
support. We then predict Gaussian parameters directly from this shared voxel support, turning recon-
struction into a feed-forward voxel-to-Gaussian pass, entirely avoiding long per-scene optimization.
2


For rendering, a separate challenge arises from the physics of ultrasound image formation: ultrasound
pixel intensity couples local reflection/scattering with cumulative attenuation, making appearance
strongly direction-dependent, yet this coupling also means that slices acquired under nearby probe
poses can share similar local appearance. We exploit both aspects through the proposed ultrasound-
aware appearance rendering strategy, which decouples reflection/scattering from attenuation during
training and leverages the local appearance similarity among neighboring slices for lightweight
Render-time Appearance Adaptation. We demonstrate for the first time that feed-forward Gaussian
splatting can match the reconstruction quality of per-scene optimization methods in ultrasound while
reducing inference time from 10-20 minutes to 10 seconds, bringing 3D ultrasound reconstruction
within reach of real-time clinical use.
In summary, our contributions are fourfold:
• We propose UltraVoxelGS, the first feed-forward Gaussian splatting framework for 3D ultrasound,
enabling both real-time reconstruction and physically faithful rendering.
• We design a feed-forward voxel-to-Gaussian pass that lifts posed slices into a unified voxel support
and predicts Gaussian parameters directly, achieving real-time 3D reconstruction.
• We introduce ultrasound-aware appearance rendering that decouples reflection/scattering from
attenuation and exploits local appearance continuity for lightweight Render-time Appearance
Adaptation, providing high-fidelity rendering at sub-second latency for operator inspection.
• We validate on two datasets spanning diverse anatomies, matching per-scene optimization baselines
in quality with over 35× speedup and scaling to sequences exceeding 1,000 frames.

Related Works
3D Ultrasound Reconstruction Traditional methods depend on interpolation to aggregate images,
such as kernel-based interpolation [7] to create an isotropic 3D volume. ImplicitVol [8, 19, 20]
and RapidVol [9] extend this interpolation from discrete representation to continuous representation.
However, all of these construct an isotropic volume, violating the anisotropic property of ultrasound.
The emergence of NeRF-based methods, such as Ultra-NeRF [10, 21], formulate ultrasound parame-
ters as continuous parameter field, then rendering images according to ultrasound imaging, following
anisotropic property. These methods achieve high-quality synthesis, but suffer from low rendering
speed coupled with dense neural field querying. As an alternative, GS methods model the scene as
a set of Gaussian primitives, achieving fast rendering by rasterization. Recent methods focus on
ultrasound imaging assumptions, assuming the imaging plane as a slice of the scene [13, 22–24] or
adopting a variable frustum projection [25]. These methods render high-quality images efficiently in
specific settings, but still belong to per-scene optimization.
Feed-Forward 3D Reconstruction Feed-forward methods [14] have emerged in natural imaging.
These methods predict Gaussians in a single feed-forward pass. Most existing works focus on a
pixel-aligned mechanism, directly predicting per-pixel Gaussian primitives and then aggregating
them. More recent extensions improve scalability through structured aggregation or voxel-aligned
prediction [16, 26]. Similar feed-forward ideas have also been explored in projection-based medical
imaging such as sparse-view CT [27]. These methods rely on stronger cross-view correspondence
but each observation in ultrasound is only a thin probe-aligned slice, cross-view overlap is extremely
limited, and appearance is strongly view-dependent due to acoustic anisotropy. Therefore, existing
feed-forward pipelines cannot be directly applied to freehand ultrasound.

paradigm and freehand ultrasound reconstruction. We first review the pixel-aligned feed-forward
GS formulation used in natural imaging, then characterize ultrasound frames as sparse posed slice

support before predicting Gaussian primitives.

However, for dense ultrasound sequences, the voxel support can remain high-resolution, making it
computationally prohibitive to predict Gaussian primitives over the entire volume. Fortunately, doing
so is also unnecessary: each frame only observes a thin imaging slice and its nearby acoustic slab,
while voxels far from this slab contribute little to the rendering of that frame. We refer to this property
as thin-slab locality, and use it to restrict voxel-to-Gaussian prediction to local sub-volumes. For each
target frame, we extract a local block Bm from the consolidated voxel support that covers the imaging
slice and its nearby acoustic slab, and predict Gaussian primitives only within this block. Because
this block is carved from the full voxel support—which has already fused posed observations across
the entire input sequence—prediction operates on dense, multi-view spatial context rather than sparse
single-frame information.

4.1
Datasets and implementation details
Dataset A: Real-world Robotic Ultrasound Video Sequences. This dataset consists of robot-
assisted lumbar spine ultrasound scans with synchronized probe pose tracking [29], acquired from
63 healthy volunteers (used under CC BY 4.0). Each scan contains over 3K densely sampled frames.
In the default setting, we uniformly sample every 5-th frame as input and supervision, and evaluate
on frames with index 3 mod 5 to avoid near-duplicate testing.
Dataset B: Simulated Liver Ultrasound Sequences. This dataset contains 100 simulated liver
ultrasound sequences rendered from abdominal CT volumes from 3D-IRCADb [30] and CRLM-
CT [31] based on LOTUS [32] (used under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 and CRLM-CT under CC BY 4.0).
We hold out every 7-th frame as novel views and use the remaining frames as input and supervision.
All datasets are used for non-commercial research purposes in accordance with their respective terms.
Additional implementation details, baseline settings, and dataset/tool license information are provided
in Appendix A.

4.2
Main results on real-world and simulated data
Quantitative comparison. Tab. 1 compares UltraVoxelGS with interpolation-based and per-scene
optimization baselines on Datasets A and B. UltraVoxelGS achieves the best overall performance on
real tracked scans and remains competitive under controlled simulation, while using a feed-forward
reconstruction pipeline for sub-second rendering rather than per-scene optimization. These results
support the effectiveness of the voxel-first formulation and ultrasound-aware appearance rendering
for held-out slice reconstruction. The contribution of RAA is further analyzed in Appendix B.5,
highlighting its role in compensating for fine details lost during feed-forward reconstruction.
Qualitative comparison. Fig. 5 shows representative qualitative comparisons on Dataset A and B.
Interpolation-based reconstruction produces visible interpolation artifacts and loses anatomical detail.
ImplicitVol yields overly smoothed responses, while UltraNeRF exhibits noisy and unstable texture
patterns. 3DGS preserves some high-frequency appearance but introduces unstable speckle-like
artifacts and local structural distortions. UltraGauss recovers part of the anatomical structure, but
still shows degraded contrast and less coherent local organization. In comparison, UltraVoxelGS
reconstructs clearer layered structures and more faithful local appearance.
Segmentation-based anatomical consistency. To assess 3D anatomical consistency, we evaluate on
Dataset B using CT-derived liver segmentation as the reference. For each method, we propagate an

5
Conclusion
We have presented UltraVoxelGS, a feed-forward framework that, for the first time, achieves both
fast reconstruction and physically faithful rendering for ultrasound imaging. The core insight is
that when views are geometrically thin and photometrically inconsistent, the right strategy is to
consolidate all observations into a shared volumetric representation first and predict locally. Paired
with an ultrasound-aware appearance rendering strategy grounded in acoustic image formation, our
design delivers reconstruction quality surpassing per-scene optimization baselines while reducing
inference time from minutes to seconds and scaling to over 1,000 frames. Looking ahead, we
hope UltraVoxelGS can enable real-time spatial feedback during ultrasound acquisition, guiding
sonographers toward uncovered regions. Limitations are discussed in Appendix F.

A.1
Implementation details.
UltraVoxelGS first constructs a unified voxel support from posed ultrasound slices and then predicts
voxel-aligned Gaussian primitives from view-interacted local regions. Unless otherwise specified, the
input image resolution is 176×88, the voxel grid spacing is 0.00075 m, and each voxel patch has size
8×8×8. For voxel support construction, each voxel uses up to k = 3 nearest lifted observations. The
Gaussian primitive is parameterized as (cg, Σg, ρg, βg), where ρg ∈R9 corresponds to second-order
spherical-harmonic coefficients for anisotropic reflection and βg denotes the attenuation coefficient.
We train UltraVoxelGS using Adam with learning rate 1 × 10−5 for 300k iterations on one NVIDIA
H20 GPU. The batch size is varied between 2 and 12 according to the number of selected view-
interacted local regions and GPU memory. The overlap consistency weight is set to λ = 0.01.
Render-time Appearance Adaptation (RAA) is disabled during training and is applied only during
target-view rendering.

B.4
Comparison with Per-scene Optimization
RAA differs from short per-scene optimization in two aspects (shown in Tab. 9). First, they differ
in what is optimized: RAA adapts transient appearance parameters while keeping the reconstructed
geometry frozen, whereas per-scene optimization updates the full scene representation itself. Second,
they differ in how the cost scales: RAA is a query-time refinement with a fixed per-query cost that is
independent of the number of source views, whereas per-scene optimization scales with the length
of the input sequence, making it ill-suited to long ultrasound acquisitions. Overall, RAA is not a
shortened per-scene reconstruction but a query-time appearance adaptation, offering a more practical
refinement strategy for interactive ultrasound inspection.
B.5
Role of RAA Across Different Acquisition Densities
The feed-forward pass and RAA are complementary: whenever interpolation smooths out detail,
RAA restores it—decisively when the effect is severe (Dataset A) and as a consistent refinement
when it is mild (Dataset B). RAA is thus an integral component, not an optional add-on, keeping
UltraVoxelGS robust across acquisition densities.

C.4
Comparison with Generic Feed-Forward GS
To evaluate the effectiveness of our ultrasound-specific feed-forward reconstruction paradigm, we
introduce VolSplat[26], a generic feed-forward GS baseline, for comparison. However, directly
applying VolSplat to long ultrasound sequences is computationally impractical because its native
formulation jointly processes a limited number of input views before lifting their features into a 3D
feature space and decoding the scene.
We therefore adapt VolSplat to our long-sequence setting while preserving its original training
paradigm. During training, randomly sampled source views are used as input, their features are lifted
into a 3D feature space, and the complete scene is decoded. During inference, we divide all source
views into groups, process each group independently to obtain lifted 3D features, fuse the features
from all groups into a common global feature space, and decode the scene representation once. This
allows VolSplat to use the full source sequence.
The results on Dataset A are shown in Tab. 13. UltraVoxelGS w/o RAA substantially outperforms the
adapted VolSplat baseline. This gap reflects the distinct reconstruction paradigms. VolSplat must
process limited view subsets independently, losing the global scene context and making it difficult
to aggregate information distributed across different subset views. In contrast, UltraVoxelGS first
consolidates the complete sequence into a unified 3D support and then predicts Gaussians only from
the view-interacted local regions. Owing to ultrasound thin-slab locality, each such local region
contains the relevant context from the globally constructed support without requiring repeated whole-
scene decoding. The result therefore supports the contribution of the ultrasound-specific voxel-first
formulation and local-to-global reconstruction strategy.

E.2
Comparison with Commercial Reconstruction Systems
The key difference lies in the underlying scene representation. UltraVoxelGS uses a direction-aware
Gaussian representation, which allows it to model ultrasound-specific appearance factors such as
anisotropic reflection and cumulative attenuation. This representation better accommodates flexible
scanning trajectories, including local revisits and changes in scanning direction.
In contrast, many conventional or commercial reconstruction systems form an isotropic voxel vol-
ume by lifting registered 2D slices into 3D space and compounding their intensities, producing a
tomographic-style representation similar to CT or MRI volumes. Such reconstruction commonly
favors smooth, approximately uniform sweeps with limited dwelling or repeated sampling of the same
region, because redundant observations are often averaged during voxel compounding, which may
smooth local details. This can constrain the acquisition trajectory and, consequently, the applicable
scanning scenarios.
This difference is particularly relevant near interfaces with large acoustic-impedance differences,
where strong specular reflection or acoustic shadowing may hide structures from a single insonifica-
tion direction. Additional observations from different probe directions can therefore be important.
21


UltraVoxelGS is designed to preserve and render such direction-dependent observations within a
unified representation rather than collapsing them into a single isotropic intensity value.
E.3
Visual Feedback and Scan Guidance
Real-time scan guidance requires two capabilities: (i) fast 3D reconstruction and rendering that keeps
pace with the ongoing scan, and (ii) a mechanism to identify under-reconstructed regions and translate
them into actionable probe-pose suggestions. This work addresses capability (i). UltraVoxelGS
enables a live reconstruction workflow: as the operator scans, the system simultaneously streams
acquired frames into a voxel support and, at fixed intervals, generates local GS representations and
fuses them into the global scene. This construction proceeds in the background, overlapping with the
scan rather than waiting for it. When inspection is needed, the operator can render corresponding
views in real time.
Regarding capability (ii), reconstruction incompleteness is already visually observable in the rendered
views (see Fig. 11), but it has not yet been converted into explicit guidance. One natural extension is
to associate uncertainty with the Gaussian representation and compute a pose-dependent cumulative
uncertainty score for candidate poses, directing the operator toward under-observed regions. Beyond
visual inspection, the reconstructed scene also supports downstream analysis such as 3D segmentation
, which can further assist the operator in assessing anatomical coverage. These extensions are enabled
by, and fundamentally depend on, the fast reconstruction and rendering prerequisite addressed in this
work.
F
Limitations and Broader Impact
Limitations. UltraVoxelGS relies on sufficiently accurate probe tracking to lift posed 2D ultrasound
slices into a unified voxel support. The method may degrade when the observed coverage is extremely
sparse, when severe acoustic shadowing removes local structural evidence, or when probe poses
contain noticeable errors. In addition, our evaluation focuses on held-out slice reconstruction and
representation quality, which does not directly establish clinical diagnostic performance. Bridging
this gap would likely require explicit uncertainty estimation to flag unreliable regions and probe-pose
guidance to direct operators toward uncovered anatomy, both of which we leave to future work.
UltraVoxelGS also adopts a simplified ultrasound-aware appearance rendering model. Specifically,
Decoupled Reflection and Attenuation Rendering focuses on anisotropic reflection and cumulative
attenuation, which capture the main direction- and path-dependent factors in B-mode ultrasound
intensity formation: local reflection determines the generated echo response, while cumulative attenu-
ation controls signal decay along the acoustic path. This formulation is compatible with Gaussian
scene representation, where Gaussian appearance parameters model local reflection/scattering and
accumulated transmittance models attenuation. Our results on real-world and simulated datasets
suggest that this approximation is effective for the reconstruction tasks studied in this work, but it
is not a complete acoustic simulation. Other effects, such as speed-of-sound variation, refraction,
speckle decorrelation, beamforming artifacts, and device-specific post-processing, are not explicitly
modeled and may become important under large view changes, strong specular interfaces, severe
shadowing, or cross-device settings. Future work may incorporate richer acoustic effects while
preserving the efficiency of the feed-forward voxel-to-Gaussian pass.
Broader impact. Fast 3D ultrasound reconstruction can support low-latency scan guidance, spatial
feedback, and more efficient review of ultrasound acquisitions. By reducing reconstruction time and
hardware requirements, feed-forward 3D ultrasound reconstruction may also help extend volumetric
imaging capabilities to resource-limited clinical settings where dedicated 3D probes are unavailable.
However, the current method should be viewed as a reconstruction and visualization tool rather
than a standalone clinical decision system. Future work should further study uncertainty estimation,
pose-noise robustness, broader freehand ultrasound validation, and prospective clinical evaluation.
