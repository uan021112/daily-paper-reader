## ABSTRACT 

Creative tool use is a fundamental human capability and an important step toward general-purpose embodied intelligence. It requires agents to reason about object affordances, physical constraints, and manipulation strategies, especially when standard tools are unavailable. However, progress in this area is limited by the lack of real-world datasets that capture human tool-use behavior with sufficient scale, fidelity, and diversity. Existing benchmarks are often simulation-based, focus on simplified interactions, or lack detailed observations and annotations. We introduce T OOL DEX , a large-scale egocentric-exocentric (ego-exo) human demonstration dataset for creative physical tool use. TOOL DEX captures sce-narios in which humans select, adapt, and repurpose available objects as tools. Using synchronized egocentric cameras and eight exocentric cameras, the dataset provides high-fidelity multi-view observations and rich annotations for hands, tools, target objects, and their deformable interactions. Beyond data collection, we establish a benchmark suite characterizing three core capabilities: physical tool-use reasoning, sparse-view 3D and 4D reconstruction, and robot policy learn-ing. T OOL DEX provides a foundation for studying human tool-use behavior and developing embodied agents that can adaptively use tools in complex real-world environments. Dataset visualizations and benchmarking results are available at 

https://tooluse-dataset.github.io/ .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Tool use is a fundamental human capability and a key step toward general-purpose embodied intelligence (Goodall, 1964; Hunt, 1996; Shumaker et al., 2024; Fitzgerald et al., 2021). In everyday environments, humans do not rely solely on a fixed set of predefined tools; they also flexibly repurpose available objects based on their physical properties and functional affordances. For example, when a knife is unavailable, a person may use a spoon, chopsticks, or another nearby object to divide a cake. This form of creative tool use requires more than recognizing object categories. It demands physical reasoning about contact, geometry and task constraints. As such, physical tool use provides a natural testbed for evaluating whether embodied agents can generalize beyond memorized object-action associations and adapt to novel physical scenarios. Despite recent progress in robotic manipulation (Black et al., 2024; 2025; Kim et al., 2026), learning generalizable tool-use behavior remains difficult (Shi et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Task and motion planning methods can solve structured tool-use problems when symbolic task descriptions are carefully specified, but often struggle in open-ended real-world settings (Garrett et al., 2021). Recent large language models and vision-language models have shown promising reasoning ability for selecting or describing tools, yet they remain limited in grounding such reasoning into physically executable manipulation (Fang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026). This gap is especially pronounced in tasks involving deformable target objects (such as sand or cake), non-rigid contacts, and long-horizon interactions, where success depends on fine-grained hand-object-tool dynamics rather than high-level semantic reasoning alone.

A major obstacle is the lack of datasets and benchmarks that capture human physical tool-use capabilities at sufficient scale, fidelity, and diversity . Existing robot tool-use benchmarks are often simulation-based or focus on simplified object interactions, making it difficult to evaluate the com-plexity of real-world manipulation (Xu et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2026; Bakhtin et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2025). Meanwhile, recent egocentric human manipulation datasets have greatly expanded the coverage of hand-object interactions (Hoque et al., 2026; Punamiya et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2024). Among them, TACO explicitly captures tool-action-object compositions for hand-object manipulation (Liu et al., 2024). However, it does not explicitly model the target objects manipulated by the tools, such as cakes to be cut or water to be stirred. As a result, existing datasets either contain limited tool-use instances or lack the full hand–tool–target-object interactions and annotations needed to study physical tool use. In this paper, we introduce T OOL DEX , a large-scale ego-exo human demonstration dataset for learning and benchmarking creative tool-based manipulation (Figure 1). Our dataset is designed around everyday tool-use scenarios in which humans creatively solve manipulation tasks by selecting, adapting, and repurposing available objects. A key feature of our dataset is its explicit coverage of deformable target objects , such as dough, fluids, and sand, where success depends on fine-grained contact and material interactions. To capture these behaviors with high fidelity, we use a synchronized multi-view capture system consisting of two egocentric cameras and eight exocentric cameras. Egocentric views capture local interaction context, while exocentric views enable accurate multi-view tracking and reconstruction. The resulting dataset provides rich annotations for studying both high-level tool-use reasoning and low-level physical manipulation. Beyond data collection, we use T OOL DEX to establish a benchmark suite for evaluating tool-use capabilities across three levels. First, we evaluate physical tool-use reasoning , testing whether vision-language models can identify appropriate tools, infer object positions, and provide plausible manipulation strategies. Second, we evaluate 3D and 4D reconstruction , focusing on the recovering hand, tool, and deformable scene states from multi-view observations. Third, we evaluate robot policy learning , where human demonstrations are used to train robot policies for tool-based manipulation. Together, these benchmarks characterize semantic reasoning, spatial perception, and embodied control, offering a comprehensive evaluation protocol for physical tool-use. Our contributions are threefold. First, we present a real-world ego-exo dataset centered on creative physical tool use spanning diverse tools, deformable target objects, tasks, and interaction strategies. Second, we provide rich multi-view annotations that support hand tracking, tool tracking, object interaction analysis, and scene reconstruction. Third, we introduce a benchmark suite that evaluates embodied agents from reasoning to reconstruction to policy execution. We hope this dataset will serve as a foundation for studying human tool-use behavior and for developing robots that can creatively use tools in complex, open-world environments.

Beyond datasets, several benchmarks study physical reasoning and tool use from complementary perspectives. ## 3 THE TOOL DEX DATASET 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Target-centric tool manipulation. Tool manipulation, following work on tool use in human and animal cognition Beck (1980); St Amant & Horton (2008); Shumaker et al. (2011) , is the indirect alteration of a target object ’s physical state by an agent acting through a tool object , in a scene that may also contain support objects , such as containers or work surfaces, that constrain the interaction without being directly actuated. The agent does not act on the target object (e.g., a cake on a plate) directly: it actuates the tool, and the tool’s contact with the target produces the state change. We adopt this three-role decomposition—hand, tool, and target object—because it is the interaction between tool and target, shaped by tool geometry, target physical properties, and environmental support, that determines whether a manipulation succeeds. T OOL DEX is designed to capture precisely these interactions. 

Tool manipulation as a goal-conditioned MDP. We formalize a tool-manipulation trial as a goal-conditioned Markov decision process (S, A, T, γ, G). The state st ∈ S is the configuration of all participants of the scene—tool, target, support, and agent—at time t. The action at ∈ A is decomposed into two levels: a tool action that determines how the tool moves and, through contact, how the target’s state changes; and an agent action that determines how the agent’s manipulator moves and thereby controls the tool. The transition T captures the physics of the interaction. The goal

Diversity of tools, target objects, and actions. TOOL DEX contains 10,853 trials spanning 60 high-level (tool, action, target) task combinations, and 168 total variants that capture changes in tool instances and task attributes (e.g., different tool materials or shapes, and variations in target configurations). In total, the dataset includes 45 tool objects, 23 support objects, 16 deformable object types, and 16 action types. As shown in Figure 3, the tools span a range of geometries, including flat blades (knife, spatula), volumetric scoopers (spoon, ladle, ice-cream scooper, open scooper, measuring scooper), elongated rods (rolling pin, rolling rod, chopstick, straw), and bulkier creative-repurposing items (book, cup, pan), each affording a different contact profile with the target. Target objects span a wide range of physical properties: rigid (toy, box, banana), granular (nuts, sand, kinetic sand, coffee), deformable (dough, naan, towel, tomato sauce). For each (tool, action, target) combination, we collect trials for 60 minutes in which we vary the initial pose of the tool, target, and support, and additionally place 4–5 distractor tools on the work surface to produce realistic clutter and to require visual grounding of the active tool when conditioned on language. 

Zero-shot and compositional generalization. The combinatorial structure of our dataset enables the study of both zero-shot and compositional generalization. As shown in Figure 2, each tool participates in 2.3 actions on average, and each action is realized by 3.6 tools on average across targets. From these (tool, action, target) triples, we construct two families of evaluation splits. Zero-shot generalization encompasses three cases, testing the model on a withheld tool, target, or action. 

Compositional generalization holds out a co-occurrence rather than an element, specifically a (tool, action, target) combination in which each individual component has been seen but their joint has not. For example, a held-out triple may evaluate a known tool performing a known action on a target it has not been paired with at training, even though that target has been manipulated by other tools. 

Multi-view capture of 3D interaction. We capture each interaction with high fidelity to recover the 3D state of the tool, target, and hand, which together provide the inputs required to learn the interaction function T defined in Section 3.1. Each trial records eight calibrated exocentric Intel RealSense RGB-D streams at 1280 × 720 resolution and 30 Hz, together with a stereo egocentric stream from a ZED Mini stereo camera mounted on an Apple Vision Pro headset worn by the demonstrator. Depth observations from each exocentric viewpoint enable 3D reconstruction of state changes, while synchronized multi-view videos capture non-rigid phenomena such as dough deformation and granular flow. We provide per-frame multi-view segmentation masks for the target object , and tool and hand. Each trial lasts an average of 18.92 seconds and contains 567.58 frames.

## 4 DATA COLLECTION 

Following the task taxonomy introduced in Section 3, we collect each demonstration in a single calibrated multi-camera studio rig and run an automated annotation pipeline offline after each capture session. The capture rig and annotation pipeline are designed jointly so that each recorded trial yields synchronized RGB-D video, per-frame 6-DoF tool pose, MANO hand poses for both hands, and multi-view segmentation masks for the target object. 4.1 TASK AND OBJECT CURATION 

Each task combination is a ( tool, action, target ) triplet specifying a set of demonstrations. We curated the task combinations in two stages. We first enumerated 16 everyday tabletop tool-use scenarios drawn from cooking, food preparation, child play, and household activities, each defined by an action verb and a default tool–target pair. We then expanded each scenario along the action ×tool axis. For each action, we explicitly enumerated the alternative tools a human might creatively substitute when the canonical tool is unavailable. We select tool, target, and support objects to span diverse geometries, material properties, covering rigid, granular, and deformable objects. Within each task combination, we vary the initial pose of the tool and target object on the work surface, and additionally place 4-5 tool objects as distractor objects in the scene to match the cluttered tabletop conditions of real deployments and require visual grounding of the correct tool when conditioned on language. Each task combination is paired with a natural-language instruction that specifies both the tool motion (e.g., “swing the spatula laterally under the egg, then lift”) and the intended effect on the target (“flip the egg so its underside faces up”). 4.2 HARDWARE SETUP 

Our capture rig (Figure 3) consists of 8 Intel RealSense 435 RGB-D cameras and an Apple Vision Pro headset fitted with a Stereolabs ZED Mini stereo camera. The 8 RGB-D cameras are mounted on a Vention extruded-aluminum frame surrounding the work surface, in positions designed to maximize joint coverage of the work volume while preserving sufficient view overlap for multi-view triangulation, 6-DoF object pose estimation, and 4D scene reconstruction. RGB and depth streams are recorded at 1280 × 720 resolution and 30 Hz; streams across the 8 cameras are synchronized in software. We calibrate the cameras using an April Tag followed by multi-view bundle adjustment 5to obtain rig-relative camera parameters. The ZED Mini, mounted on the Vision Pro, provides two additional 1280 × 720 stereo RGB egocentric streams. To support 6-DoF tool pose estimation, we scan each tool with a Shining3D scanner to obtain a high-resolution textured mesh. The sparse 8 camera setup overcomes occlusion and enables photo-realistic 3D and 4D scene reconstruction. 4.3 DATA COLLECTION AND ANNOTATION 

Data is collected under a scripted protocol with a predetermined execution procedure. We collect one hour of data per task. We also vary the positions of the 4–5 distractor tools across recordings to introduce greater spatial diversity. All 8 exocentric RGB-D streams and the ZED Mini stereo stream are recorded synchronously and stored together. We then run an annotation pipeline that produces three synchronized outputs for each trial: 6-DoF tool pose, MANO hand pose for both hands, and per-frame multi-view segmentation masks for the target object. All annotations are manually verified for each trajectory. When automatic segmentation or tracking fails, annotators provide additional initialization prompts to correct the annotations. 

Target-object segmentation. We segment target objects across 8 exocentric views using SAM2 (Ravi et al., 2025) with DINOV2 (Oquab et al., 2024) seed initialization using the cosine similarity of the scanned tool meshes; SAM2’s built-in video tracker propagates the mask across the rest of the trial. 

Tool pose. We obtain per-frame 6-DoF tool pose by running FoundationPose (Wen et al., 2024) independently on each exocentric stream, conditioned on the scanned textured mesh of the tool used in that trial and seeded with a SAM2 (Ravi et al., 2025) first-frame mask. To consolidate per-view estimates into a single trajectory in the rig frame, we perform a confidence-weighted multi-view fusion using FoundationPose’s per-view score, followed by a sliding-window bundle adjustment that minimizes per-view reprojection error together with a temporal-smoothness term on the 6-DoF pose. 

Hand pose. We estimate per-view MANO (Romero et al., 2017) parameters with WiLoR (Potamias et al., 2025), which produces hand pose at unknown scale from a single RGB image. We recover metric MANO predictions by solving for a per-trial global scale and rigid alignment of the WiLoR-predicted 3D hand keypoints to the multi-view RGB-D point cloud and the multi-view-triangulated 2D hand keypoints. We then apply the same multi-view fusion and temporal smoothing as for tool pose to obtain a single MANO trajectory per hand in the rig frame. 

Joint hand–tool refinement. Following HO-Cap (Wang et al., 2025), we perform a joint hand–tool pose optimization that minimizes a weighted sum of (i) multi-view 2D hand-keypoint reprojection error, (ii) hand-keypoint-to-RGB-D-depth distance, (iii) tool 6-DoF reprojection error against the multi-view masks, and (iv) a signed-distance-field-based contact prior between the MANO mesh and the tool mesh that penalizes interpenetration while encouraging contact during active manipulation phases. Full pipeline details, hyperparameters, and per-component ablations are in the appendix.

Experiment setup. We evaluate three levels of reasoning capabilities. For Tool Selection , models are asked to identify all tools that could plausibly complete the task, and a prediction is counted as correct if any predicted tool matches the demonstrated ground-truth tool. For Spatial Grounding ,models localize the selected tool using bounding boxes, evaluated using IoU only on semantically matched tool predictions. For Behavioral Planning , models are provided with a natural-language instruction, e.g., “stir the coffee.” and must generate a high-level manipulation trajectory based on the first frame from a top-view camera. The evaluation subset contains 180 manually verified trajectories spanning 32 task variants. We evaluate a tool-center projection ground-truth trajectory and a correspondence-aware transported-point trajectory, using trajectory distance metrics including Mean L2 and Chamfer-L2. Details are in Appendix A.2.

5.3 ROBOT PHYSICAL TOOL -U SE FROM HUMAN DEMONSTRATIONS 

We benchmark representative paradigms for transferring human tool-use demonstrations to dexterous robot policies, ranging from direct motion transfer to interaction-aware reinforcement learning. 

Kinematic Retarget directly executes retargeted human motion, while BC (Retarget) learns from the retargeted trajectories. ManipTrans Li et al. (2025) uses residual reinforcement learning to track demonstrated interactions, and Dex-X (RL) Chen et al. (2026) jointly adapts hand and arm motion through physical interaction. 

Results. Table 5 summarizes the task success rates. Direct motion transfer performs poorly under physical interaction: Kinematic Retarget achieves 14.0% average success, while BC reaches 4.6%. In contrast, reinforcement-learning-based methods perform substantially better, with ManipTrans achieving 40.5% and Dex-X RL 55.1%. The gap is especially pronounced on contact-intensive tasks: pestle crushing improves from 16.8% to 46.7%, and squeegee collecting from 12.6% to 57.5%. Performance also varies considerably across tools for the same action, indicating that tool geometry and contact structure strongly affect transfer difficulty. 

Findings. The benchmark highlights three main challenges in human-to-robot tool-use transfer: contact-rich interactions remain difficult, tool geometry can substantially affect performance even within the same action category, and embodiment mismatch limits direct transfer across human and robot morphologies. These results motivate further study of cross-tool generalization, embodiment-aware policy learning, and robust transfer from human demonstrations to physical robot interaction. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced T OOL DEX , a large-scale ego-exo human demonstration dataset and benchmark for creative physical tool use. T OOL DEX explicitly captures the full hand–tool–target interaction in real-world manipulation scenarios, including challenging deformable target objects. With multi-view observations, the dataset provides rich annotations for studying embodied physical tool-use. Beyond the dataset itself, we establish a unified benchmark spanning physical tool-use reasoning, 3D and 4D reconstruction, and robot policy learning. Meanwhile, the dataset may raise concerns related to the misuse of human behavior data or unsafe downstream robotic deployment. To mitigate these, all data are collected with consent and released for research purposes under a non-commercial license. We hope T OOL DEX will serve as a foundation for future research on developing robots capable of adaptive tool use in open-world environments. A.4 GEMINI ROBOTICS EVALUATION AND RESULT DETAILS 

Trajectory Representation For behavioral planning evaluation, models predict a sequence of normalized 2D image-space trajectory points conditioned on the task instruction and the initial scene image. The first predicted point is constrained to lie on the selected tool, while the remaining points describe the predicted manipulation trajectory. To simplify evaluation across models with different output styles, trajectories are represented as sparse waypoint sequences rather than dense per-frame motion predictions. Predicted trajectories are converted back to pixel coordinates using the image resolution before evaluation. We prompted the model to generate 32 points, enough to create a dense table-top action task trajectory. 

Ground-Truth Trajectory Construction Ground-truth trajectories are derived from tracked tool pose sequences recovered from the object pose solver. For each frame, the tool translation component is projected into image space using calibrated camera intrinsics, producing a dense 2D tool trajectory. The projected trajectory is then arc-length resampled to match the number of predicted points generated by the model. The first ground truth trajectory is generated by taking the center point of the tool mesh and applying transforms to that throughout. We additionally evaluate a correspondence-aware trajectory variant that anchors evaluation to the predicted interaction point. Specifically, we identify the tool mesh vertex whose first-frame projection is nearest to the predicted starting point, and transport this vertex through 19 (a) Scooper scooping nuts. Qualitative Results It can be seen in Figure 11 that tasks with relatively well-defined mo-tions—typically those involving short actions, clear end states, or trajectories that start directly on the target object—tend to achieve stronger results. For example, the banana slicing trajectory(b) closely matches the ground-truth cutting motion, despite only requiring the model to infer the cup position and cutting direction from the scene context. However, for more complex tasks, such as shaping kinetic sand into a circular structure (e), the model often oversimplifies the interaction dynamics and fails to fully capture the physical difficulty of the task. In these scenarios, successful manipulation requires more than simply tracing a circular trajectory; the tool must repeatedly reposition, push material forward, and gradually accumulate shape over multiple passes. The generated trajectories instead tend to approximate only the high-level geometric pattern of the motion. Nevertheless, these results still demonstrate the strong capability of the model to capture the overall structure and intent of tool-use trajectories across a wide range of diverse manipulation settings. A.5 POLICY LEARNING PIPELINE DETAILS 

This section provides additional implementation details for the policy learning baselines described in the main text, including retargeting, training, and evaluation. A.5.1 RETARGETING 

The evaluated policies use retargeted robot trajectories derived from the human hand and tool motion annotations. Different retargeting procedures are used according to the robot embodiment and policy formulation. 20 ManipTrans + Inspire. For ManipTrans Li et al. (2025), we follow its hand retargeting procedure and optimize Inspire hand joint angles to match the MANO reference, with emphasis on fingertip alignment. Since this setting controls only the dexterous hand, the resulting joint-space trajectory is used as the reference for residual policy learning. 

Dex-X. For Dex-X Chen et al. (2026), the human demonstration is retargeted to the Franka FR3 + Sharpa hand embodiment. The retargeting jointly maps wrist motion and hand articulation into the robot arm–hand configuration space while respecting robot joint limits and reachable configurations. We follow the retargeting and simulation pipeline described in Dex-X and use the resulting trajectories as references for interaction-aware reinforcement learning. 

Figure 12: Visualization of the tool retargeting. A.5.2 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

We evaluate six task signatures spanning four action categories: stir, scoop, crush, and collect. Demonstrations contain 254–600 frames ( ≈4–10,s at 60,Hz). Each method is evaluated over 1,000 episodes per task with randomized initial states and reference start frames. Following the reference-tracking evaluation protocol, an episode is considered successful if the policy reaches the end of the demonstrated interaction without violating the task-specific tracking and stability constraints. The same success definition is used consistently across methods whenever applicable. A.5.3 TRAINING CONFIGURATION 

For ManipTrans(RL), we use the training configuration of ManipTrans Li et al. (2025). A residual policy is trained with PPO in IsaacGym for each task using the same hyperparameter configuration across the evaluated task set. For Dex-X (RL), we follow the released training pipeline of Dex-X Chen et al. (2026). Policies are trained with reinforcement learning in IsaacLab using the retargeted human demonstrations as interaction references. We use the same training pipeline across all evaluated tasks without task-specific policy design. A.6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS .Despite its scale and diversity, T OOL DEX has several limitations. First, the dataset focuses primarily on tabletop setting in indoor environments, and therefore does not cover the full range of human tool-use behaviors found in large-scale outdoor or mobile settings. Second, while we include rigid, granular, and deformable target objects, the physical coverage of materials and interaction dynamics remains incomplete. Third, although the ego-exo capture setup enables high-fidelity reconstruction and tracking, some annotations may still contain noise due to occlusion or challenging contact 21 configurations. In addition, the VLM benchmarking pipeline is intentionally permissive to account for multiple plausible tool choices. While this better reflects real-world tool use, future work could incorporate human-validated sets of acceptable tools. 22
