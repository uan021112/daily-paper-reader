## ABSTRACT 

Feed-forward 3D models use Transformers to predict camera poses, depth, and structure in one pass, avoiding iterative multi-view optimization. Long sequences and deep attention incur high compute and memory costs, motivating Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) for deployment. Existing PTQ methods mainly sup-press channel-wise outliers by smoothing or orthogonal rotation. Geometry Trans-formers introduce a different challenge: heterogeneous token types share one ac-tivation quantization grid. A few special tokens may exert high leverage over global outputs and be updated by only a subset of blocks. Token-blind transforms, which apply the same transformation to every token, reduce overall quantization error but cannot remove differentiated degradation across token types. We pro-

We cast floating-point-preserving rotation and smoothing as a token-blind reparameterization group, and prove that they yield common-mode gains while leaving relative token SNR un-changed. We introduce a statistic Γ that combines a special token’s quantization disadvantage and output leverage to predict whether differentiated bias correc-tion is needed beyond rotation. Experiments on geometric benchmarks show that W4A8 closely matches full-precision performance. Under W8A8, peak memory falls by 2.2–3.1× versus FP32, with up to 3.1× speedup on real hardware. These results provide a principled and efficient approach to deploying feed-forward 3D reconstruction models in resource-constrained edges. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Classical multi-view reconstruction combines geometric priors with iterative optimization (Mur-Artal & Tard´ os, 2017; Sch¨ onberger & Frahm, 2016; Sch¨ onberger et al., 2016). Learning-based systems first moved dense matching and point-map prediction into neural networks (Wang et al., 2024; Leroy et al., 2024), and recent feed-forward Transformers now recover camera pose, depth, and 3D structure jointly in a single forward pass (Wang et al., 2025; Keetha et al., 2026). This paradigm offers strong generalization without per-scene optimization, but its long token sequences and deep attention stacks impose substantial memory and computation costs, making post-training quantization (PTQ) attractive for deployment. Recent PTQ methods suppress outliers through scaling, smoothing, or orthogonal rotation (Xiao et al., 2023; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Tseng et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Related recipes have been extended to visual geometry Transformers (Feng et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These methods act along the channel axis, whereas feed-forward geometry models can be asymmetric along the token axis. We identify four properties that characterize this mismatch: (S1) 

heterogeneous token types share one activation grid. (S2) A single token has multiplicative, output-wide leverage. (S3) Channel energy is heavy-tailed. (S4) The special token is updated by only a subset of blocks. Rotation addresses S3, but its token-blind action cannot remove the differentiated error created by S1–S2. Consequently, metric outputs may fail even when scale-aligned geometry remains plausible, and a recipe successful on an up-to-scale model need not transfer to a metric one. We present QMapAnything, a structure-aware PTQ framework that uses these properties to select mechanisms. We first characterize all floating-point-preserving rotation and smoothing transforma-tions as a token-blind reparameterization group. Orthogonal transformations provide a common-mode signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) gain but leave relative token SNR unchanged. More general trans-formations face a conditioning ceiling and a potential weight-side quantization trade-off. We then introduce Γ, a screening statistic that combines a token’s grid disadvantage with its output lever-age. It requires only one forward and backward pass per scene and screens for the need for token-selective treatment. A residual statistic measured during calibration determines affine correctability. Guided by this analysis, QMapAnything pairs a rotation matrix for S3 with standard bias correction and, where warranted, a row-selective affine correction for S1–S2, inserted only where S4 makes it necessary. MapAnything provides the primary stress test, while Visual Geometry Grounded Trans-former (VGGT) and Depth Anything 3 (DA3) provide complementary transfer cases. VGGT has high gradient leverage on its special token without a grid disadvantage, whereas DA3 has a grid disadvantage without effective output leverage. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 1. We identify four structural properties (S1–S4) that explain when channel-oriented PTQ transfers to a geometry Transformer and when it does not, separating metric predictors from up-to-scale ones. 2. We prove that floating-point-preserving rotation and smoothing form a token-blind group. They act identically on every token, so they have common-mode strength but no row-indexed freedom. QMapAnything keeps a rotation inside this group and adds a row-selective affine correction outside it, inserted only where S4 indicates. 3. We introduce a screening statistic for that need and a residual statistic to test affine cor-rectability. The former distinguishes MapAnything from VGGT and DA3, and QMap-Anything attains near-lossless low-bit accuracy with 3.1× lower peak memory. 2Preprint Scale Token Patch Token

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Learning-based 3D reconstruction. Classical pipelines separate structure-from-motion and multi-view stereo (MVS) (Mur-Artal & Tard´ os, 2017; Sch¨ onberger & Frahm, 2016; Sch¨ onberger et al., 2016). Learned MVS (Yao et al., 2018) and cross-view pretraining (Weinzaepfel et al., 2022) move geometry into networks. DUSt3R and MASt3R predict pairwise geometry (Wang et al., 2024; Leroy et al., 2024), VGGT jointly predicts pose and dense geometry (Wang et al., 2025), and Map-Anything factors metric geometry into an up-to-scale representation and one global scale (Keetha et al., 2026). Beyond their differing prediction targets, these models also differ in whether a special token is routed through every block and whether it multiplies rather than merely summarizes the output. Those differences drive our analysis. 

Quantization, viewed as reparameterization. Second-order compensation (Frantar et al., 2023) is one PTQ line. Rounding and reconstruction (Nagel et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021; Hubara et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2022), Transformer scaling (Yao et al., 2022; Dettmers et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024), rotation or structured codebooks (Chee et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Egiazarian et al., 2024), and Vision Transformer (ViT) calibration with nonlinear operators (Liu et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022; Ding et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025a;b) are others. For geometry Transformers, QuantVGGT combines rotation, smoothing, and fine-grained activations (Feng et al., 2026). QVGGT uses mixed precision, token filtering, and principal component analysis (PCA) compensation (Pan et al., 2026). FGQ instead uses diagonal Fisher information for affine calibration (Zhang et al., 2026). Diagonal (Xiao et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2024) and orthogonal (Tseng et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026) reparameterizations reduce common-mode error but provide no criterion for when a token-blind recipe suffices. We add a pre-quantization criterion Γ that keeps in-group rotation and inserts a special-row term only when needed, so the same rule transfers. Standard bias correction (Banner et al., 2019; Nagel et al., 2019) is token-uniform and misses that residual. 3Preprint

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Evaluation settings. We evaluate on ETH3D (Sch¨ ops et al., 2017), ScanNet++v2 (SN++v2) (Yesh-wanth et al., 2023), and TartanAirV2-WB (TAV2) (Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2025), covering indoor, outdoor, and synthetic geometry. Results are macro-averaged across the three datasets under 

N = 2 and N ∈ { 8, 16 } with the official MapAnything sampling (Keetha et al., 2026). Main tables use images only. Because S2 predicts differentiated degradation, we report a metric family (scale rel. error, absolute trajectory error (ATE)) and an up-to-scale family (point and depth rel. error and 

τ , area under the pose-accuracy curve (AUC@ 30 ◦), ray error), so a collapse of m is not masked by scale-aligned scores. Bit-widths are W4A8 and W4A4. The component ablation and the checks on VGGT and DA3 use W4A4, where the gap between mechanisms is largest. We measure accuracy, latency, and peak memory consumption on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU (96 GB). Calibration uses 64 samples at 518 ×518 from the MegaTrain mixture (Keetha et al., 2026) and contains no ETH3D data, so that set also probes cross-dataset generalization. We conduct camera pose esti-mation experiments on ETH3D, SN++v2, and TAV2 using 2, 8, and 16 input views, and report AUC@30 ◦ macro-averaged over the three datasets. This metric jointly measures relative rotation and translation accuracy. Notably, ETH3D is excluded from calibra-tion and therefore also evaluates cross-dataset generalization. Un-der the relatively moderate W4A8 setting, QMapAnything preserves camera pose accuracy with near-lossless performance. Under the more aggressive W4A4 setting, its gap to full precision grows progres-sively with the number of input views. This trend agrees with S1. As the view count increases, more patch tokens compete to determine the shared absmax, further reducing the effective quantization resolution of the distinguished pathway. The ordering among baselines further validates our mech-anism analysis. RTN and SmoothQuant do not adequately address the heavy-tailed channel distri-bution in S3 and therefore fail to recover the dominant common-mode loss. QuaRot substantially reduces the overall quantization error through orthogonal rotation, but its token-blind transforma-tion cannot alter the relative SNR between special and patch tokens. All three models share the same signed quantizer, weight and activation granularity, rotation construction, calibration data source, and W4A4 evaluation setting. MapAnything additionally uses a staged 40-step refinement, whereas VGGT and DA3 use closed-form correction without gradient refinement. No accuracy-driven tuning is performed on either transfer model. Although the audit prescribes omitting bs on VGGT and DA3, we deliberately instantiate the full B as a conservative falsification test. All three models satisfy S1 and S3. They use per-tensor activation scales, and their within-token max /99 th-percentile ( P99 ) ratios at the final residual are substantially above the refer-ence value for a Gaussian of the same width. Rotation is therefore expected to benefit all three. Both transfer models fail S2 and S4. VGGT is an up-to-scale predictor with no multiplicative scalar con-trolling the global output. Its camera token is read by a dedicated head from the first layer of every block, so it is neither multiplicatively load-bearing nor sparsely routed. DA3 introduces a camera token, but its intermediate-layer interface removes that token before the dense head. The evaluated pose pathway instead regresses from dense rays, leaving the token with no effective output leverage. VGGT’s camera token is output-critical but not quantization-disadvantaged . Although its dedicated head concentrates the pose gradient on that token, 1/κ s = 0 .94 shows that its norm matches the strongest patch token, so the shared grid does not disadvantage it. DA3 is the mirror image. Its camera token is disadvantaged but not critical ( 1/κ s = 2 .68 , λs = 0 ). Consequently, both yield Γ = 0 .This distinction shows that gradient concentration induced by dedicated-head routing exists in both floating-point and quantized models and is not itself a quantization pathology. Rotation restores VGGT and DA3 to 85 .9% and 85 .4% of full-precision AUC@30, as S3 predicts. Standard correction β adds 3.11 and 4.09 points, whereas bs is negligible ( −0.02 and +0 .04 ), versus 

+3 .2 on MapAnything. Thus, Γ predicts the materiality of row-selective correction rather than generic bias correction. The models separate the two axes. VGGT has high gradient leverage but no grid disadvantage, DA3 has a grid disadvantage but no effective output leverage, and MapAnything exhibits both, supporting S1–S4 as family-level properties.

We identify four structural properties that determine when rotation-based PTQ needs token-selective treatment beyond common-mode rotation, and prove that FP-preserving reparameterizations lack row-indexed freedom. Guided by this analysis, we propose Γ to screen this need and ηs|β to test affine correctability. The resulting framework, QMapAnything, achieves near-lossless W4A8 quan-tization with a substantially reduced memory footprint. ## C CLASSICAL ROTATIONS AS CONTROLS 

For a data-independent, non-learned rotation, we isolate three factors. (A) Orthogonality . Exact cancellation by R⊤, so the substitution is lossless in FP. (B) Block-wise flatness . |Rij | = 1 /√p for all non-zero entries of a block of size p. (C) Random de-alignment . Random signs break alignment between a fixed basis and the model’s persistent outlier channels. Randomness is not cosmetic. Software and backend. Our accuracy environment uses Python 3.10.12, PyTorch 2.6.0+cu126, CUDA 12.6 (nvcc 12.6.85), cuDNN 9.5.1, NVIDIA driver 550.127.05, and

No standalone CUTLASS or fast hadamard transform package is used. 

Quantization and rotation. We use symmetric signed absmax quantization with qmax = 2 b−1 −1

(INT4: [−7, 7] , INT8: [−127 , 127] ). Weight scales are static per-output-channel and activation scales are static per-tensor. Main accuracy tables use W4A8 and W4A4. The quantized scope comprises the four linear maps in each Transformer block. There are 64 modules for MapAnything, 192 for VGGT, and 160 for DA3. We instantiate the rotation as an independent per-layer Walsh– Hadamard matrix preceded by a random sign diagonal D, with seed 0. The signs are applied first.
