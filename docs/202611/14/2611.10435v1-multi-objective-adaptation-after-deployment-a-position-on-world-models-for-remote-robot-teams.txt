# Multi-Objective Adaptation After Deployment: A Position on World Models for Remote Robot Teams 

Jamie Santos and Kagan Tumer 

Collaborative Robotics and Intelligent Systems (CoRIS) Institute Oregon State University 

{santjami,Kagan.Tumer }@oregonstate.edu 

Abstract: Autonomous robot teams deployed in remote, unstructured environ-ments may face changes in the environment, hardware, and team composition that degrade previously effective policies. Shifting mission objectives can be handled by selecting a different solution from an existing Pareto set, but shifting deploy-ment conditions can reorder the set itself, making previously dominated policies competitive or new policies necessary. However, evaluating these policies in new contexts while minimizing risk to the hardware remains an open problem. We argue that, although largely untested in this domain, world models merit investi-gation for post-deployment multi-objective adaptation. By predicting policy out-comes under updated estimates of current conditions, world models could enable teams to re-evaluate existing policies without physically executing each candi-date, provided predicted performance changes reflect those realized on deployed systems. We identify research priorities for model-based policy re-evaluation, validation of predicted against realized performance, and incorporating shared in-formation into world models to support coordinated team adaptation. 

Keywords: World Models, Long-Term Autonomy, Multi-Objective Reinforce-ment Learning, Post-Deployment Adaptation, Multi-Robot Systems 

## 1 Introduction 

Autonomous robot teams are increasingly important to sustaining long-term missions in which hu-man intervention is difficult, such as space and marine environments. Multi-robot coordination can improve efficiency and fault tolerance in such missions [1], and efforts such as NASA’s CADRE are investigating how teams of rovers can coordinate with minimal human intervention [2]. Single-robot systems such as the Curiosity Mars rover or MBARI’s Tethys Autonomous Underwater Vehi-cle [3, 4] have also already demonstrated autonomous navigation and task-planning, and autonomy stacks such as NASA’s NeBula aim to integrate these capabilities [5]. However, sustaining autonomy over extended deployments remains challenging. Environmental conditions, hardware performance, team composition, and mission objectives can change in ways that undermine previously effective policies and affect team coordination and overall mission performance [6]. These challenges are particularly acute in unstructured environments, where conditions may shift unpredictably. Sandstorms may coat solar panels with dust, hardware components may degrade or become damaged, and team members may become incapacitated [3, 7]. Such changes can invali-date assumptions made during training and shift the performance dominance ranking of available policies. A policy that was previously dominated may become competitive under new conditions, while previously effective policies may no longer achieve their expected performance. For example, when the front right drive wheel actuator of the Spirit Mars rover failed, operators began driving the rover backwards from then on [8, 9]. The locked wheel also became an unexpected scientific tool; its trenching effect produced the discovery of opaline silica deposits, which may indicate past locally habitable conditions [8]. Whereas changing mission preferences may require selecting a different solution from an existing Pareto set, changes in deployment conditions may alter the relative per-formance of stored policies, making previously dominated solutions competitive or requiring new policies when existing alternatives are insufficient. Yet, adapting policies in situ is difficult because physical experimentation can be costly or risky, particularly when robots operate far from human operators or have limited computational and energy resources. World models are a candidate for post-deployment adaptation because they allow a robot to learn from deployment experience and use imagined rollouts to re-evaluate existing policies [10] under recent conditions [11], without physical trial and error. Recent work by Domberg et al. [11] has demonstrated the ability to adapt world models by tracking model prediction residuals and finetuning the models when the context is out-of-distribution. This could help teams identify suitable existing policies, including alternatives that were previously dominated, and determine when new policies are needed, reducing reliance on physical experimentation. However, the approach depends on whether decentralized policy adaptations can preserve effective team coordination. We argue that world models merit further investigation as tools for continually re-evaluating and adapting multi-objective team policies during deployment, with particular attention to the reliability of predicted improvements and their effects on team-level behavior. 

## 2 Background: World Models and Existing Adaptation Approaches 

2.1 World Models for Post-Deployment Learning 

Preparing robots for deployment can improve their ability to handle uncertainty, but cannot elim-inate the need to adapt to unforeseen conditions. Domain randomization can improve sim-to-real transfer by exposing policies to a distribution of environmental conditions during training [12] while simulation during pretraining and methods that combine offline demonstrations with limited online adaptation can reduce reliance on real-world experience [13, 14, 15]. However, these approaches cannot guarantee effective adaptation to deployment conditions that are inadequately represented during training or modeling. This challenge is especially pertinent to long-term deployments in unstructured environments such as Mars or Antarctica. Post-deployment adaptation is particularly challenging for remote robots, where opportunities for safe physical experimentation may be limited. Current systems in high-stakes regions such as the Moon rely on heavy human intervention to maintain mission safety, but this approach becomes less manageable as the number of agents increases and robots are used to take on more diverse roles. Testing unverified policies in environments where human intervention is difficult can have serious consequences; unexpected behavior may render a robot inoperable. This constraint in particular 2motivates approaches that can evaluate candidate behaviors using recent deployment experience while reducing reliance on costly or risky physical experimentation. World models offer a potential means of supporting this process. By learning a compact predic-tive representation of environmental dynamics from onboard data, they allow agents to evaluate candidate behaviors through imagined interactions rather than relying exclusively on physical ex-perimentation [10]. DayDreamer, for example, demonstrated that world-model-based reinforcement learning can train and adapt policies on physical robots with substantially less interaction than con-ventional approaches [16]. These results suggest that learned models could provide a useful basis for evaluating and adapting robot behaviors after deployment. A key challenge, however, is maintaining model accuracy as deployment conditions change. Domberg et al. [11], as aforementioned, demon-strate one approach to addressing this challenge by monitoring prediction residuals and triggering online model updates when residuals deviate substantially from their expected values. However, extending such approaches to multi-objective robot teams requires accounting for changes not only in environmental conditions but also in team capabilities and mission requirements. 

2.2 Approaches to Maintaining and Adapting Policy Repertoires 

Several approaches address the challenge of maintaining effective behaviors across different tasks and conditions. Multi-task learning trains policies across distributions of tasks or objectives [17], while quality-diversity methods such as MAP-Elites generate repertoires of diverse policies [18], including policies optimized across multiple objectives [19]. These approaches can provide a range of behaviors from which a robot or team can select as conditions change. However, they do not guarantee that available policies will remain effective under unforeseen deployment conditions. Meta-learning methods such as Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML) instead aim to enable rapid adaptation from limited experience [20, 21], with extensions such as Decentralized Multiagent Meta Learning (Dif-MAML) addressing adaptation in multiagent settings without requiring central-ized coordination [22]. However, these methods still depend on informative experience, which may be costly or risky to obtain during deployment. World models offer a complementary approach to these methods. Rather than relying exclusively on updating individual policies from new experience, a learned dynamics model can be updated using deployment data collected under different policies and then used to re-evaluate multiple existing policies. In a multi-objective setting, this could allow a team to assess its policy repertoire across competing objectives as conditions change, potentially identifying suitable existing policies before committing resources to training new ones. Whether model-based evaluations can reliably support these decisions under changing deployment conditions remains an open question. 

2.3 Reliability and Coordination Challenges 

For multiagent systems, maintaining reliable world models introduces additional challenges. Par-tial observability and communication constraints may limit the information available to individual agents, making it difficult to construct accurate representations of joint team dynamics. Changes in team composition or individual robot capabilities may further complicate predictions of team behav-ior. Even when individual agents identify promising policy updates, independently adapting agents may not produce improved team-level performance. In contrast, early work in multiagent world models demonstrates the efficacy of maintaining both individual and team-level world models on each agent in scenarios with high-latency communication [23]. Recent work has begun to investi-gate world models for multiagent task learning and planning [24, 25], but it remains unclear whether these models can support reliable policy improvements under unforeseen deployment changes while preserving effective team coordination and acceptable tradeoffs across objectives. 

## 3 World Models as Tools for Adaptation 

Over long-term deployments, changes in terrain, hardware, team composition, or mission objectives may exceed the adaptability of existing methods [6]. A robot team originally deployed to conduct 3scientific experiments, for instance, may eventually be reassigned to perform logistical or mainte-nance tasks, so adaptation must cover not only low-level behaviors but also higher-level decisions about which policies to deploy. Post-deployment adaptation must therefore account for both chang-ing mission priorities and changes in the behaviors available to meet them. Furthermore, determining when adaptation is necessary is itself a challenge. A change in observed performance may indicate that a policy has become less effective under current conditions, but be-coming less effective for one objective may also correspond to increased performance in another; for example, a rover that begins to cover less ground due to hardware degradation may also make more scientific discoveries. Environmental changes and hardware failures may also alter the tradeoffs the team as a whole can achieve, potentially rendering previously discovered Pareto-optimal policies now suboptimal. Distinguishing between a degraded policy, a shifted trade-off, and a change in priorities requires evaluating policy performance in the context of both current conditions and com-peting objectives. Whereas changes in deployment conditions may require updating the model and re-evaluating the available policy repertoire, changes in mission priorities may only require selecting a different policy from the existing Pareto set. World models could support this process by enabling ongoing re-evaluation of policy repertoires during deployment. Figure 1 demonstrates a possible pipeline to accomplish this in a multiagent system. Distributed agents can collect local observations that can be used to update a shared world model (either updated via a distributed or centralized manner depending on the system architecture) [23]. By combining experiences collected across agents, a shared model could capture team-level interactions and changes that may not be observable from any individual agent’s local experience alone. Differences between model predictions and observed outcomes can indicate that the model is no longer accurately representing current deployment conditions. Such discrepancies could trigger model updates, followed by re-evaluation of existing policies using the updated model. Policy up-dates or the generation of new policies would then be considered if the existing repertoire no longer provides suitable alternatives [11]. By updating a learned dynamics model with newly collected experience, potentially while giving greater weight to recent observations, a robot team could main-tain predictions that better reflect its current operating conditions. The model could then be used to re-evaluate existing policies, identify which remain suitable, and determine when further policy adaptation may be necessary. This approach could reduce reliance on physical experimentation, but its effectiveness depends on whether sufficiently accurate models can be learned and updated from limited deployment experience. A reliable framework must therefore assess not only predicted policy performance, but also whether predicted improvements transfer to the deployed system. 

## 4 Research Priorities and Conclusion 

Realizing the potential of world models for multi-objective post-deployment adaptation requires evaluating both the reliability of model predictions and the effectiveness of the resulting policy updates. First, research should investigate how deployed systems can detect when adaptation is necessary, distinguishing changes in environmental conditions from changes in mission priorities. Second, model-based adaptation should be evaluated by comparing predicted and realized improve-ments across multiple objectives and under changes in hardware, environment, and team composi-tion. Finally, evaluations should consider whether local policy updates preserve team-level coordi-nation and acceptable tradeoffs amongst objectives. Together, these directions would help establish when world models can support reliable post-deployment adaptation. Conversely, if world-model predictions of policy performance after a change consistently fail to track realized performance, or if other approaches such as meta-learned or fault-randomized policies recover comparable perfor-mance under similar data and compute budgets, research effort should shift to those approaches. In summary, current world models have not yet demonstrated that they are reliable enough for deployed adaptation in unstructured environments for multiagent systems. However, given their potential, we argue that these limitations warrant further investigation into their use for multi-objective adaptation in remote robot teams rather than ruling them out. 4Acknowledgments
