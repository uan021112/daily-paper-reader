Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models typically generate actions in chunks, organizing con-tinuous robot execution into separately predicted segments and thus introducing potential discontinuities at action-chunk transitions. We propose ODEAction, a chunk-free action generation paradigm that replaces action-chunk prediction with Continuous Action Evolution. Given visual observations, language instructions, and robot proprioceptive information, ODEAction learns an action transition field defined over action space, where a transition vector characterizes the evolution rate of the current action with respect to evolution time. During execution, the model takes the current action as its initial condition and integrates the learned field using an ordinary differential equation (ODE), recursively generating subsequent actions with explicit dependencies. ODEAction is implemented as an action head and does not depend on a specific vision-language backbone architecture. Experiments on LIBERO, EBench, RoboTwin, and real robots show that ODEAction improves both task performance and trajectory quality over matched action-chunking baselines, with particularly pronounced gains on fine manipulation tasks. 

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models [22, 24–26, 38, 41, 53] widely adopt action-chunk generation (Action Chunking Generation): they jointly predict a sequence of future actions in a single inference pass, organizing continuous execution into successively generated action chunks [6–8, 23, 39, 40, 46]. However, adjacent chunks predicted independently typically lack explicit temporal consistency constraints across chunk boundaries, potentially causing abrupt action changes and trajectory discontinuities at transitions [9, 31] (Figure 1(a)). Existing methods alleviate this issue by imposing constraints on continuity across chunks: RTC [9] constrains new action chunks during inference, whereas Training-time RTC [10] and Legato [32] introduce continuity constraints during training. Nevertheless, these methods still treat action chunks as the fundamental unit of generation, improving the connections between chunks rather than changing the action generation paradigm. This leads us to ask a further question: can we move beyond chunk-wise prediction and directly model the continuous evolution of actions over time?

Robot execution itself progresses incrementally over time rather than occurring in action chunks. We therefore characterize continuous action evolution using an ordinary differential equation (ODE): the current action provides the starting point, a learned action evolution rate determines subsequent changes, and integration progressively generates an action sequence without jointly predicting an entire future action chunk. Action generation thus shifts from predicting future segments to learning the dynamics that drive actions to evolve continuously. From this perspective, we propose ODEAction , an action generation paradigm centered on Continuous Action Evolution . Given visual observations, language instructions, and proprioceptive information, ODEAction learns an Action Transition Field defined over action space. At each action and evolution time, the field outputs a transition vector representing the evolution rate of the current action with respect to evolution time. During training, we construct a target field around demonstration trajectories that combines progression along the demonstrations with deviation correction, and learn this field through direct supervision. During inference, starting from the current action, we integrate the learned action transition field using an ODE, so that each generated action becomes the starting point for subsequent evolution, instead of independently predicting and concatenating action chunks (Figure 1(a)). ODEAction is implemented as an action head that can be integrated with different vision-language backbones. We evaluate ODEAction on simulation benchmarks, including LIBERO [29], RoboTwin [12], and EBench [16], as well as on real-robot tasks. Compared with matched action-chunking baselines, ODEAction improves both task performance and action trajectory quality, achieving a 61.8% relative improvement in average success rate on EBench. Further analysis shows that evaluation units exhibiting larger improvements in boundary continuity also tend to achieve greater success-rate gains. In a representative execution of the real-world Place Cup task, ODEAction smoothly transports a water-filled cup without spilling, while producing a more continuous end-effector trajectory (Figure 1(a)). Our contributions are threefold: • We propose ODEAction, a chunk-free generation paradigm based on continuous action evolution, transforming action generation from future action-chunk prediction into a learnable continuous evolution process.

2.1 Continuous Action Evolution 

Unlike methods that jointly predict a future action chunk [7, 13, 23, 35, 51], ODEAction uses an ordinary differential equation to describe the continuous evolution of the current action over execution time. Let c denote the conditioning information provided by visual observations, language instructions, and robot proprioceptive information, and let normalized evolution time τ ∈ [0 , 1] parameterize this process. 2.2 Demonstration-Induced Action Transition Field 

Training demonstrations consist of discrete action sequences. We interpolate between adjacent demonstrated actions along evolution time to obtain a continuous demonstration trajectory γ(τ ).Its derivative ˙γ(τ ) = d γ(τ )/dτ gives the action evolution rate of the demonstration at evolution time τ ,providing supervision for the action transition field in Equation (1) along the trajectory. However, prediction errors or execution deviations during inference may cause the current action to drift away from the demonstration trajectory during recursive evolution. Supervising only the evolution rate on the trajectory cannot provide a corresponding evolution direction at off-trajectory positions. We therefore sample query actions a = γ(τ ) + σϵ around γ(τ ), where ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ) and σ controls the extent of neighborhood coverage. To enable off-trajectory actions both to progress along the demonstration and to return toward its path, we define the target action transition field as 

F ∗(a, τ ) = ˙ γ(τ ) + k γ(τ ) − a, (2) where the first term specifies the direction of progression along the demonstration, and the second drives deviating actions back toward the corresponding demonstration path; k controls the correction strength [19]. When a = γ(τ ), the correction term vanishes, and the target transition vector equals the demonstration’s evolution rate. When the action deviates, the two terms jointly determine the direction of subsequent evolution. In this way, discrete demonstrations induce a target action transition field in the neighborhoods of their trajectories (Figure 2(a)). Supervision samples from different demonstrations, conditions, and evolution times are jointly used to learn the conditional action transition field Fθ . ODEAction uses a fixed neighborhood scale σ and correction gain k throughout the entire evolution interval.

## 3 Experiments 

This section addresses the following questions: • Can ODEAction improve task performance and trajectory quality simultaneously? Are improvements in trajectory continuity associated with success-rate gains? (Section 3.2) • Does ODEAction remain effective across different base policies and tasks? (Section 3.3) • Can ODEAction outperform methods designed to maintain continuity across action chunks? (Section 3.4) • Which design choices are necessary for ODEAction’s performance? (Section 3.5) • Can ODEAction improve both task performance and trajectory quality on real-robot tasks? (Sec-tion 3.6) 

3.1 Experimental Setup 

Policies and Protocols. We consider three base-policy configurations. With π0.5 [8] as the base policy, we compare action-chunk generation and ODEAction on LIBERO and EBench, and additionally include RTC and Legato in the real-robot experiments. With StarVLA-OFT [14, 23] as the base policy, we evaluate ODEAction on all 50 RoboTwin tasks. With StarVLA-π as the base policy, we compare the original action-chunking method, RTC, Legato, and ODEAction on five representative RoboTwin tasks. LIBERO [29], RoboTwin [12], and EBench [16] cover tabletop manipulation, bimanual manipulation, and long-horizon fine manipulation, respectively. Real-robot experiments use AgileX Cobot Magic 1.0, and each benchmark follows its corresponding evaluation protocol. Within each comparison, we keep the base backbone, task-training data, control frequency, and visual-observation update times identical; details of evolution time and the execution mechanism are provided in Supplementary Materials A and B. Except for ablation experiments, ODEAction consistently uses σ = 0 .1 and k = 5 across all benchmarks, tasks, and real-robot experiments, without task- or platform-specific adjustments; parameter selection is discussed in Supplementary Material A. 

Metrics. We measure task performance using the success rate (SR) across all evaluation episodes. For trajectory quality, we use four metrics, all of which are better when lower. NLDLJ, NSPARC [2, 5], and EE-MAJ measure time-domain smoothness, frequency-domain smoothness, and end-effector mean absolute jerk, respectively, and are collectively referred to as Motion Smoothness metrics. Boundary Jump measures the magnitude of changes between adjacent control commands at chunk boundaries, characterizing Trajectory Continuity. For ODEAction, Boundary Jump is computed at the chunk-boundary times of the corresponding Base Policy. Trajectory metrics are calculated from model-output control commands in the successful episodes of each method; the separate evaluation protocol for offline recursive error is described in Supplementary Material C.

3.5 Ablation Studies 

Ablation Setup. Two key design choices jointly shape ODEAction’s target action transition field: the action neighborhood scale σ determines the extent of supervision coverage around demonstration trajectories, while the correction gain k determines how strongly deviating actions are driven back toward the demonstration evolution path. To validate the roles of these two choices, we conduct ablations using the base policy and five tasks from Section 3.4. We compare the full ODEAction with two variants: w/o neighborhood sets σ = 0 , providing supervision only on the demonstration trajectory; w/o correction sets k = 0 , retaining neighborhood sampling but removing the correction term. Because the correction term naturally vanishes when σ = 0 , these three groups cover all effective ablation combinations of the two designs. We additionally include Direct Next-Action, which likewise removes action chunks, to examine whether ODEAction’s performance advantage stems solely from action-by-action recursion.

Both the Action Neighborhood and Correction Term Are Necessary. As shown in Table 4, removing the neighborhood or correction term reduces the success rate from 84.4% to 47.4% or 56.2%, respectively, while increasing offline error from 4.25 to 8.84 or 13.17. Distinction from Ordinary Action-by-Action Recursion. Direct Next-Action recursively generates actions by directly predicting the next action. Under the same five-task setting, it achieves an average success rate of 78.4%, below ODEAction’s 84.4%, and performs worse on all four trajectory-quality metrics (Supplementary Material F). This result indicates that ODEAction’s benefits do not arise solely from removing action chunks, but from the combined effects of modeling continuous action evolution rates and supervising the demonstration-neighborhood target field.

## 4 Related Work 

4.1 Action Chunking and Cross-Chunk Continuation 

Action chunking [13, 51] has become a common approach to action generation in large robot foundation models [7, 11, 23, 24, 30, 37, 42, 43, 45, 50], with models such as GR00T N1 [6] and π0.5 [8] jointly generating a sequence of future actions in a single pass. However, independently predicting adjacent action chunks can lead to action discontinuities at chunk transitions. To improve continuity across chunks, RTC [9] constrains new chunk generation using the overlapping portion of the previous action chunk during inference, while other methods enhance cross-chunk consistency through candidate selection [31] or generation guidance [2, 34]. Training-time RTC [10] and Legato [32] incorporate known actions as conditioning information during training, enabling policies to learn chunk continuation; related studies also combine masked action learning with prefix-preserved sampling to improve action consistency during asynchronous execution [44]. These methods retain action chunks as the unit of generation, whereas ODEAction recursively generates subsequent actions through continuous action evolution. 

4.2 Dynamical Systems and Continuous Action Generation 

Continuous dynamical systems have a long history in modeling robot motion. Prior work uses movement primitives and stable motion fields to reproduce demonstrated behaviors [17, 21], and explores constructing motion fields that combine progression along reference trajectories with recovery from deviations [20, 27, 28]. This perspective has also been incorporated into visuomotor policies: NDP [3] and related approaches embed movement primitives into neural policies [4], while SFP [19], SSIP [18], and other methods enable streaming action generation through continuous dynamics in action space [33]. Under vision-language conditioning, studies have further explored the continuous evolution of proprioceptive representations [36] and the integration of vision-language reasoning with movement primitive generation [1, 15]. ODEAction models the action generation process using an ordinary differential equation, recursively generating subsequent actions from the current action to improve task success rates and trajectory quality. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We propose ODEAction, a chunk-free action generation paradigm that replaces future action-chunk prediction with Continuous Action Evolution. Conditioned on visual, language, and proprioceptive information, ODEAction learns an action transition field and recursively generates subsequent actions through ODE integration, using the current action as the initial condition. Experiments on LIBERO, EBench, RoboTwin, and real robots show that ODEAction improves both task performance and trajectory quality over action-chunking baselines, with particularly pronounced benefits on fine manipulation tasks. We further find a strong positive correlation between boundary discontinuity reduction and success-rate gains: evaluation units with larger improvements in trajectory continuity also tend to achieve greater success-rate gains. Overall, ODEAction offers a new modeling paradigm for action generation in VLAs and robot control.

## F Direct Next-Action Prediction and Continuous Action Evolution 

ODEAction transforms action generation from future action-chunk prediction into continuous evolution starting from the current action. However, simply removing action chunks and establishing recursive dependencies between adjacent actions does not imply that stable action evolution dynamics have been learned. To examine whether ODEAction’s benefits arise solely from this chunk-free recursive formulation, we compare it with Direct Next-Action prediction (Direct Next-Action). Both methods generate subsequent actions step by step, but use different learning objectives and generation mechanisms. 

Direct Next-Action Prediction (Direct Next-Action). Given the current action, evolution time, and vision-language conditioning, Direct Next-Action directly regresses the next action value, with the recursion 

ˆan+1 = Gψ (ˆ an, τ n | c), (S12) where ψ denotes the parameters of the prediction network Gψ . Supervised by adjacent action pairs from demonstrations, this network learns a direct mapping from the current action to the next action and uses the predicted action as the input to the next step during inference. In contrast, ODEAction learns an action evolution rate jointly defined by the demonstration tangent and the deviation correction term, and advances actions through ODE integration. Its supervision also covers demonstration neighborhoods, providing corresponding evolution directions for off-trajectory actions. We use the StarVLA-π base policy and five representative RoboTwin tasks from Sec. 3.4 of the main paper, keeping the vision-language backbone, training data, control frequency, and visual-observation update schedule identical. Task performance is measured by success rate, and control-command trajectory quality is measured by NLDLJ, NSPARC, EE-MAJ, and Boundary Jump, following the same statistical protocol as Sec. 3.1 of the main paper.

As shown in Table S3, Direct Next-Action achieves an average success rate of 78.4%, indicating that direct action-by-action prediction can complete a substantial portion of manipulation tasks, but the trajectory quality of its generated actions is markedly worse than that of ODEAction. ODEAction raises the average success rate to 84.4%, an improvement of 6.0 percentage points, while improving all four trajectory metrics on all five tasks: average NLDLJ and NSPARC decrease from 34.88 and 4.141 to 22.96 and 2.096, respectively; EE-MAJ decreases from 7.151 to 0.533; and Boundary Jump decreases from 0.3243 to 0.0847. These results indicate that, although direct next-action prediction establishes generative dependencies between adjacent actions, its recursive process remains prone to pronounced command jitter and abrupt action changes. This comparison further clarifies ODEAction’s modeling advantage: chunk-free action-by-action recur-sion alone is insufficient to achieve high-quality continuous action generation. Direct Next-Action 

> 20

directly learns a mapping between adjacent demonstrated actions, whereas ODEAction formulates action generation as a continuous evolution process, characterizes evolution rates by learning an action transition field, and combines neighborhood supervision with deviation correction to provide evolution directions after deviations from demonstrations. Together with the component ablation results in Sec. 3.5 of the main paper, these findings show that ODEAction’s gains in task performance and trajectory quality arise not only from removing action chunks, but are also closely related to continuous action evolution modeling and its corresponding field-supervision design. 

## G Neighborhood Supervision and Recursive Behavior of the Action Transition Field 

ODEAction models action generation as continuous evolution starting from the current action, using an action transition field to jointly characterize motion progression along demonstration trajectories and directional correction after deviations. During training, we sample demonstration neighborhoods at the same scale across different evolution times, allowing the action transition field to learn evolution directions for deviations from demonstrations throughout the entire evolution interval. Given a demonstration action curve γ(τ ), ODEAction’s training query distribution is

Time-Uniformity here means that the neighborhood scale of the training query distribution does not vary with evolution time, rather than requiring the variance of the action distribution to remain constant during integration. Training can thus continuously cover demonstration neighborhoods of the same scale at different stages of evolution. Jointly incorporating motion progression and recovery from deviations into dynamical systems has a long history in robot control. Dynamical Movement Primitives (DMP) generate goal-directed motion through attractor dynamical systems and learnable motion-shaping terms [17]; Stable Estimator of Dynamical Systems (SEDS) learns nonlinear motion fields that converge to a goal under stability constraints [21]. These studies embody the basic idea of using dynamical systems to organize motion generation and recovery from deviations. ODEAction applies this idea to action transition field learning conditioned on visual, language, and proprioceptive information. Through demonstration-induced local supervision, action generation can proceed recursively along the learned evolution rates. Streaming Flow Policy (SFP) [19] also constructs a stabilized velocity field using the demonstration tangent and proportional deviation correction, in the form

This method has primarily been studied in visuomotor imitation learning settings such as Push-T, RoboMimic, and single-arm robots [19]. ODEAction, in contrast, addresses action-chunk generation in vision-language-action models, replacing joint prediction of future action chunks with continuous action evolution and examining its effects on task performance, action-trajectory quality, and recursive error. The two methods share the same local corrective field form, but differ in their research questions, base policies, and the temporal distribution of training neighborhoods. Both constructions use the same target action transition field and the StarVLA-π base policy and five representative RoboTwin tasks from Sec. 3.4 of the main paper, differing in how the neighborhood scale of the training query distribution varies with evolution time.
