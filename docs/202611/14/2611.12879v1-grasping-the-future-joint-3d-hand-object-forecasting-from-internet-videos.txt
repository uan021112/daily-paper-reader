ABSTRACT
To interact with an object, we need to anticipate both how it will move and
how our hands should move with it. Human videos capture these relationships
across everyday activities, offering a natural source for learning to predict fu-
ture interactions.
Learning from them requires recovering motion from com-
pleted interactions and using it to anticipate what could happen next. We present
Grasping-the-Future, a unified generative model of joint 3D hand-object motion,
including wrist motion, finger articulation, and object position and orientation.
Through masked spatiotemporal completion and flow matching, we train a sin-
gle model for several prediction tasks: forecasting future interactions, predict-
ing hand motion given object motion or vice versa, generating both trajectories
toward a desired final object pose, and completing partially observed interac-
tions. We explicitly model hand-object relations and incorporate contact con-
straints to help preserve grasp relationships during motion. To obtain 3D super-
vision from internet videos, we develop a scalable pipeline that combines pre-
trained perception models with joint geometric optimization to reconstruct com-
pleted interactions. We combine these recovered trajectories with annotated inter-
action datasets as training data for all tasks. We demonstrate strong performance
across all tasks, outperforming released forecasters on datasets with 3D annota-
tions. Moreover, we show geometric and physical plausibility of the predicted
trajectories by retargeting them to a robotic embodiment. Videos are available at
https://grasping-the-future.github.io.

INTRODUCTION
“Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards.”
— Søren Kierkegaard
To interact with an object, we need to anticipate how our actions will affect it. Placing a mug on a
shelf is a simple everyday action, but predicting how it will unfold requires understanding how the

hand and mug move together. The fingers must hold the mug as it is lifted and release it only once
it rests on the shelf. The way the mug is grasped affects how it can be carried, and the position of
the shelf affects where the hand needs to move. From the eyes of the human manipulating, parts
of the hand or the object may be occluded, and several plausible future motions may be possible.
Anticipating the interaction therefore requires predicting the motion of both the hand and the object,
while preserving firm hand-object contact as the object moves through the 3D world.
Human videos are a natural source for learning these predictions, showing how people handle ob-
jects across many everyday situations. Learning from them involves two steps: first, extracting hand
and object motion from completed interactions, and then using these examples to learn how an in-
teraction might continue from a partial observation. These steps echo Kierkegaard’s distinction: a
recorded video allows us to look back and understand what happened, while acting requires antici-
pating what comes next. Image-based predictions, such as generated videos, can leave relative depth
and contact ambiguous. Predicting in 3D makes the spatial relationships between the hands, objects,
and scene explicit: where the fingers touch the mug, how it is oriented as it is carried, and where it
comes to rest on the shelf. This geometric description also provides an unambiguous reference for
later retargeting the predicted motion to a different embodiment like a robot.
Our key insight is that a single model can learn these different forms of prediction: predicting
an object’s trajectory given the hand’s motion, predicting the hand’s trajectory given the object’s
motion, generating both trajectories given a desired final object pose, and forecasting or completing
an interaction from partial 3D observations. People reason this way intuitively: watching a hand
move suggests where an object will go, while imagining where an object should end up suggests
how the hand needs to move. We present Grasping-the-Future, a unified generative model of joint
3D hand-object motion. The same model forecasts an interaction from a short observed context,
predicts hand trajectories given object motion or object trajectories given hand motion, generates
motion toward a desired object pose, and fills gaps between observed poses (Figure 1). Across these
tasks, we must handle observations of different entities and times. We bring these tasks together
through masked spatiotemporal completion: we reveal different parts of a hand-object trajectory to
the prediction model, and a flow-matching objective learns to generate the missing pieces of the
trajectory. The generated trajectories include wrist motion, finger articulation, and object position
and orientation. Since plausible hand and object trajectories can still drift apart, we use explicit
hand-object relations and contact constraints to keep them moving together during a grasp.
Learning this predictive model requires 3D hand-object supervision that large-scale internet videos
do not directly provide. We therefore develop a scalable pipeline that combines pretrained per-
ception models with joint geometric optimization to extract hand-object trajectories from complete
monocular videos. The pipeline separates camera motion from object motion, refines the alignment
between the hands and object using contact and temporal constraints, and filters unreliable recon-
structions. We extract over 62,000 trajectories from internet videos and combine them with existing
annotated hand-object datasets in a common 3D representation, accounting for differences in anno-
tation quality during training. On egocentric video, our reconstructions are on par with or slightly
better than those of HOPformer (Bansal et al., 2026), a recent model for egocentric images. From
third-person viewpoints, our hand error is about nine times lower and our object pose error about a
quarter lower (Appendix B.3). The reconstructed trajectories serve as pseudo-ground truth for all
tasks.
We evaluate Grasping-the-Future across forecasting and conditional generation tasks on datasets
with 3D annotations, including OakInk2 (Zhan et al., 2024) and HOT3D (Banerjee et al., 2025),
and on held-out internet videos using our extracted trajectories as pseudo-ground truth. On the
annotated datasets, a single forecast from Grasping-the-Future has 17% lower final wrist error and
10% lower final object error than the best released methods. The same model also predicts object
motion from hand motion, which none of these methods supports, with 63% lower final error than
keeping the object rigidly attached to the hand. We also demonstrate that these predictions can
guide robot manipulation: by retargeting the predicted human motion to simulated robot hands with
SPIDER (Pan et al., 2026), we enable the hands to manipulate objects along the forecast trajectories
for different types of manipulation tasks.
In summary, our contributions are threefold. We develop a unified generative model of 3D hand-
object motion that supports forecasting, cross-entity prediction, goal-conditioned generation, and
temporal completion through a common masking formulation. We propose a scalable pipeline for

RELATED WORK
Reconstructing hand-object interactions from video. Recent methods recover 3D hand-object
geometry and motion from monocular videos by combining visual observations with geometric
and learned priors (Hasson et al., 2021). Joint priors over hand configurations and object shape
support reconstruction and grasp synthesis (Ye et al., 2024), while temporal attention helps recover
coherent hand trajectories (Ye et al., 2026a). Other methods reconstruct bimanual interactions using
Gaussian splatting (On et al., 2025) or use novel-view synthesis priors to recover object geometry
that is not visible in short clips (Wang et al., 2025b). Hand priors also support joint hand-object pose
estimation from a single egocentric image (Bansal et al., 2026). Recent work recovers world-space
hand and object motion from egocentric videos with known object templates and supports hand-
guided interaction synthesis (Ye et al., 2026b). We build on these advances to extract supervision
from completed human interactions. Our focus is on using the recovered trajectories to learn how
an interaction unfolds over time from a partial observation.
Interaction forecasting and robot manipulation. Prior work predicts hand trajectories and inter-
action hotspots from egocentric video (Liu et al., 2022). Recent methods account for uncertainty in
future 3D hand motion (Bao et al., 2023), use language queries to guide trajectory prediction (Bao
et al., 2025), and combine interaction reasoning with 3D hand motion prediction (Chen et al., 2026b).
Forecasting object poses (Soraki et al., 2026) and 3D point trajectories (Bharadhwaj & Jangir, 2026;
Zhang et al., 2026) further describes how objects move during an interaction. Some methods model
the relationship between human and object motion by conditioning object forecasts on hand mo-
tion (Thakkar et al., 2025) or combining diffusion with physics-informed correction for full-body
human-object forecasting (Xu et al., 2023). Other work forecasts motion in unconstrained videos
through dense 2D point trajectories (Thakkar et al., 2026). These predictions can also guide robot
manipulation. HOPMan translates predicted human interaction plans into robot actions (Bharadhwaj
et al., 2024a), while Track2Act combines predicted point tracks with geometric transfer and a resid-
ual policy (Bharadhwaj et al., 2024b). Gen2Act learns policies conditioned on generated human
videos (Bharadhwaj et al., 2025). We learn to predict articulated hand motion together with object
pose, capturing how the fingers, wrist, and object move through an interaction. We then retarget
these forecasts to simulated robot hands to test their use for manipulation.
Controllable motion synthesis. A complementary line of work, often motivated by graphics and
animation, generates plausible motion from text, object geometry, reference grasps, or goal poses.
These methods typically emphasize generation plausibility without necessarily requiring grounding
the predictions in a real scene. Prior work synthesizes dynamic grasps from a grasp reference and
a desired object pose using physics-based control (Christen et al., 2022). Bimanual manipulation
datasets also provide models for completing task primitives (Zhan et al., 2024). Text-conditioned
methods generate hand-object motion through contact-guided diffusion (Cha et al., 2024) or masked
latent flow matching (Chen et al., 2026c), while other work separates global motion from local
hand articulation using latent diffusion and a grasp VAE (Li et al., 2025a). Some methods ground
generated motion through initial human-object poses and sparse object waypoints (Li et al., 2024).
Masks over frames and joints also support sparse or partial motion constraints (Cohan et al., 2024).
We ground our predictions in partial observations of a hand-object interaction and the surrounding
scene geometry. In Grasping-the-Future, one model forecasts its continuation, predicts either entity
from the other’s motion, generates both trajectories toward a desired object pose, and completes
missing portions of an interaction while modeling hand-object contact.

Window coordinates. We call the last frame with an observed cell the present frame. For forecast-

entity is always observed, it is the last frame of the window. We re-express all poses in the camera

the 3D point halfway between the observed wrists and object at that frame from every position so
that each window is centered on the interaction.

We train the masked tokens with flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023). With x1 their clean en-

Architecture and sampling. Each hand, object, or relation token is embedded into the feature
space using a layer for its type. Both hands share a layer, as do both relation tokens, so a learned
embedding identifies each of the five tokens. We also add a learned embedding for whether the
token is observed or masked, a sinusoidal encoding of elapsed time, and a learned projection of
its confidence. The confidence distinguishes annotated data from trajectories extracted from video
(Section 3.4), so the model can rely less on noisy labels. The transformer repeats a group of four
blocks. The first three attend to the five tokens over time and to each of the five tokens for each
frame. The fourth attends over all tokens of all frames at once. Every attention layer also reads the
context c as extra key-value tokens, with the object mesh encoded by Uni3D (Zhou et al., 2024)
and the scene point cloud, carrying DINOv3 features (Sim´eoni et al., 2025), by LitePT (Yue et al.,
2026). At inference, the masked tokens start as Gaussian noise and are denoised by integrating the
predicted velocity over 10 Euler steps.

Per-frame estimates. We use Molmo 2 (Clark et al., 2026) to identify manipulation clips from
Action100M (Chen et al., 2026a) and locate the manipulated object, then track its mask with
SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2025). We estimate MANO hand poses with WiLoR (Potamias et al., 2025),
object meshes and poses with SAM 3D (Chen et al., 2025), metric depth with MoGe (Wang et al.,
2025a), and camera motion with MegaSaM (Li et al., 2025b).
Trajectory refinement. Each per-frame estimate is in its own frame’s camera coordinates, so we
use the MegaSaM camera poses to bring them into one world coordinate system. We lift Co-
Tracker3 (Karaev et al., 2025) object tracks into 3D with depth and fit a rigid object trajectory
by robust bundle adjustment (Huber, 1964). We then jointly refine hand and object poses to match
the 2D masks and keep contact, penalizing interpenetration, abrupt motion, and implausible finger
poses, with hand scale fixed. When a held object is hidden for a long stretch, its rotation follows the
hand’s. A transformer trained on corrupted motion-capture trajectories estimates each clip’s depth
bias, which we correct by shifting the object and the grasping hand along the viewing direction.
Finally, we smooth the remaining hand jitter (Appendix B.2).
Verification. Finally, we check image agreement, contact, interpenetration, temporal consistency,
and object size. To make sure we tracked the correct object, we also ask a VLM to point to the
object in the hand through the clip. Of the total candidate clips, about 12% yield valid trajectories
after final verification. For each verified trajectory, we also compute an interaction-fidelity score. A
higher score means the reconstructed hand(s)-object contact fits the video better, the object motion
is smoother, and the hands and object align better with their 2D detections. This score goes into
the model as part of the confidence input. If a supporting surface is visible, we also estimate the
direction of gravity from it.

the benefit of training on trajectories extracted from internet videos (Section 4.2). We then evaluate

4.1
SETUP
Training. We train on eight annotated hand-object datasets (Chao et al., 2021; Banerjee et al.,
2025; Liu et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2024; Rim et al., 2026; Hampali et al., 2020; Kwon et al., 2021;
Wang et al., 2024) and trajectories extracted from internet videos. Together, they provide 74,312
trajectories and 134,661 five-second windows, including 62,562 trajectories and 109,346 windows
from internet videos. Each training window receives a task observation pattern sampled uniformly
across the five task families, or a random block mask. Training details are provided in Appendix A.
Benchmark. We evaluate every task on the same 331 five-second windows from diverse scenarios
unseen during training, 256 from the annotated datasets and 75 from internet videos, with one win-
dow per video. For forecasting, the model observes the first 0.5 seconds and predicts the remaining
4.5 seconds. For internet videos, the references are our extracted trajectories, which are less accurate
than the annotations (Appendix C). We will release the code and data publicly.
Metrics. We compare each prediction with the recorded trajectory of its window, which we call
the reference, and measure position error in millimeters at the wrists and the object center. For a
predicted trajectory, the average displacement error (ADE) is this error averaged over its generated
frames, while the final displacement error (FDE) is the error at its last frame. When the final pose
is observed, the final error is zero by definition, so we report the mid-gap error, calculated at the
midpoint of the generated frames. Since a single observation can lead to multiple plausible futures,
we generate 20 samples per input and evaluate the results in three different ways..
• Single sample. We score the first of the 20 samples on its own, as when the model is asked to
make a single prediction.
• Energy score. The energy score (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007; Shahroudi et al., 2024) of the 20
samples is their average error minus half their average distance from each other. The second term
rewards spread, while the first penalizes samples far from the reference. If we view the reference
as one draw from all the futures that could follow the observation, the expected score is lowest
when the samples come from the same distribution as those futures. A method that makes only
one prediction has no spread, so its score equals its error and can be compared directly.
• Best of 20. The error of the sample with the lowest ADE shows how close the nearest of the 20
samples comes to the reference. This sample is selected using the ground-truth reference, which
is unavailable to the model at inference time.
The energy score rewards both accuracy and spread, so we also check the spread on its own. Let
x1, . . . , x20 be the sample positions and x21 = y the reference position at the frame scored by
FDE or mid-gap error, and let di be the distance from xi to the mean of the other 20 positions.
The reference lies inside the samples if d21 is not the largest of d1, . . . , d21. If the samples and
the reference come from the same distribution, each di is equally likely to be the largest, so the
reference lies inside the samples in 20/21 ≈95% of windows. A rate below 95% means that the
samples are too concentrated or centered away from the reference, and a rate above 95% means that
they are too dispersed. For all of these metrics, each annotated dataset counts equally, and annotated
data and internet videos each count for half. Appendix C gives full definitions, eligibility rules, and
additional interaction metrics.
Baselines. Stay-put holds the last observed pose, while constant velocity extrapolates observed

4.3
TESTING PHYSICAL PLAUSIBILITY OF FORECASTING THROUGH RETARGETING
We assess the physical plausibility of our forecasts by retargeting them to simulated XHands. We
test whether the robot hands can manipulate the object and reproduce its predicted motion under the
simulator’s dynamics.
Kinematic retargeting matches the robot’s wrist and fingertips to the predicted hand poses. Dynamic
retargeting with SPIDER (Pan et al., 2026) further optimizes robot controls in simulation to follow
the predicted object motion. We also test kinematic retargeting with SPIDER’s grasp preparation
but without trajectory optimization. Using only the observed frames, grasp preparation adjusts the
robot hand’s starting pose and grip in simulation until the hand holds the object steadily. While the
forecast hand holds the object, it then moves the robot wrist together with the predicted object and
keeps the grip unchanged. All three methods use the same forecast for each observation.
We evaluate 103 examples whose reference hand-object trajectories retarget successfully with SPI-
DER, and we run SPIDER with three optimizer seeds per example. We consider a retarget to be
successful if the robot hand moves the object through the whole prediction interval and the object’s
position and rotation errors relative to the forecast, averaged over the predicted frames, are below
100 mm and 0.5 rad.
We see that kinematic retargeting succeeds in only 14.6% of examples, showing that these manipu-
lations are difficult to carry out under physics by matching wrist and fingertip poses alone (Figure 5).
Grasp preparation raises success to 43.7%, so many of these failures come from a grasp that does not
hold the object. When the robot controls are also optimized with SPIDER, 73.8% of our forecasts are
reproduced in simulation. Once adapted to the robot hand, most of our forecast hand-object motions
can therefore be carried out through physical interaction, which indicates that they are physically
plausible.

CONCLUSION
We presented Grasping-the-Future, a unified model for forecasting and completing joint 3D hand-
object motion. We combined masked spatiotemporal completion with explicit hand-object relations
and contact supervision to predict coordinated motion under different observations and goals. We re-
constructed over 62,000 trajectories from internet videos and showed that this supervision improved
forecasting beyond training on annotated datasets alone. Under the energy score, our forecasts out-
performed all released hand and object forecasting methods by 16–37%. We also demonstrated that
our forecasts could guide object manipulation after retargeting to simulated robot hands, connecting
prediction from human videos to robot execution.

MODEL AND TRAINING DETAILS
In this section we provide additional details on the model, losses and training from Sections 3.2,
3.3, and 4.1. Also, Tables 4 and 5 provide information on the settings and loss weights.
Representation. We divide each trajectory into five-second windows with a 2.5-second stride, with
the last window ending at the trajectory’s endpoint. Frames keep their timestamps and cover 5
seconds for each window, regardless of frame rate. So, a window can hold at most 150 frames
for 30 fps recordings or about 50 frames for 10 fps extracted videos. We pad shorter windows
(and windows from trajectories less than 5 seconds) and exclude padding and absent entities from
attention and losses.
The coordinates are as described in Section 3.1, with two additional details. If the gravity direction
is known, we also rotate them so that this direction points downward. When the object is unobserved
at the present frame, centering uses the mean wrist position alone, and when no hand is observed
there, the object alone.
∆-codec. We compute rotation changes by subtracting consecutive 6-D rotation vectors, including
those for finger joints. Relation tokens skip this encoding and describe each frame directly. The
absolute observed states of Section 3.2 are standardized and paired with a flag, 1 when the state is
observed and 0 otherwise. Both are zero at unobserved frames. They are mapped to a vector added
to the state embedding by a linear layer initialized to zero, so they have no effect at the start of
training.
Flow-matching loss. The flow-matching loss LFM is split into six components, for hand translation,
wrist rotation, finger articulation, object translation, object rotation, and hand-object relations. Each
component is the squared velocity error averaged over its channels and masked frames. Hand and
relation components are computed for each side and averaged over the sides with masked frames.
The weights in Table 5 combine the components into a weighted mean for each window, and the
loss averages these means across windows. A component with no masked frames in a window is left
out of that window’s mean, and a window with no masked frames is left out entirely. This averaging
prevents over-weighting windows with many masked frames and components with many channels.
For losses on decoded trajectories, we estimate clean tokens as ˆx1 = xt + (1 −t)vθ and decode
them into poses.
Architecture. We use a 12-block transformer with 512-dimensional embeddings and eight attention
heads, with the attention pattern and token embeddings of Section 3.2. Each token type has a two-
layer MLP that embeds it and a linear layer that predicts its flow velocity. Both hands use the same
layers, and so do both relation tokens. AdaLN-Zero (Peebles & Xie, 2023) gives every attention and
feed-forward layer the flow time and the average time between frames. Another MLP takes the three
state tokens and predicts fingertip contact, defined as a fingertip-to-object distance below 5 mm, and
is trained with binary cross-entropy. Other settings are given in Table 4.
Each entity’s confidence features describe motion smoothness, stability of fingertip-to-object dis-
tances, presence in adjacent frames, and source quality. The relation token receives the confidence
features of both its hand and the object. Smoothness and fingertip-distance stability are computed
from neighboring frames, so close to a masked frame they can reveal the hidden pose. We therefore
set them to 0.5 within 0.1 seconds of the entity’s masked frames. Fingertip distances depend on both
the hand and the object, so for this feature we use the masked frames of both.
Conditioning. The scene point cloud is derived from the final frame in which all entities are ob-
served. This is because using partially observed frames might reveal a masked entity’s pose. We
then remove the hand and object pixels using their masks. We then prune background points farther
than 0.5 m from the hand-object midpoint, widening this to 1 or 2 m when too few points remain.
We then downsample the point cloud to 4,096 points with farthest-point sampling and express it in
the present frame’s centered coordinates.
Each scene point has 12 channels to pack the point’s position, surface normal, distances to the hands
and object, height above the support plane, normal tilt from vertical, curvature, and log density. It
also has 32 channels of PCA-reduced DINOv3 image features (Sim´eoni et al., 2025). We then use
LitePT-S (Yue et al., 2026) (pretrained on ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017)) to encode the point cloud.

limit violations and the MANO pose prior when they exceed the reference’s. Table 4 lists the joint
limits.
The fingertip-distance loss is the absolute error in fingertip-to-object distance, counted only for

where the hand or the object is predicted and on up to 15 other such frames chosen at random. The
attachment loss of Equation 3 applies to periods of at least two consecutive frames in which the
hand touches the object in the reference. For each hand, we take up to 48 frames spread across these
periods, longest first. Its velocity term also covers steps between observed and predicted frames
within a contact period.

B.1
PIPELINE IMPLEMENTATION
We describe the exported trajectories, processing steps, and quality checks for the video extraction
pipeline introduced in Section 3.4.
Exported trajectories. Each exported trajectory contains frames sampled at approximately 10 Hz,
with their original video timestamps. It includes MANO parameters (Romero et al., 2017) for up to
two hands, per-frame validity flags for each hand, and the rotation and translation of a reconstructed
object mesh. The export also includes an estimated gravity direction when available.
Clip selection and validation. We take candidate clips from Action100M’s timestamped action
captions (Chen et al., 2026a). Molmo 2 (Clark et al., 2026) names the manipulated object from each
caption, with no predefined object list, and clips where it names none are discarded. The same model
is used again, this time on the video, to determine whether a hand is interacting with any objects. In
clips that pass, it checks one frame per second to find when the interaction occurs, and these times
decide which frames we use to locate the object. Clips are split at detected shot boundaries, and
each shot of at least 4 seconds is processed independently.
Hand and object estimation. WiLoR (Potamias et al., 2025) gives MANO hand estimates in each
frame. We discard low-confidence detections and short-lived tracks, and reject the clip if no hand
track remains. To find the object, we ask Molmo 2 (Clark et al., 2026) to point to it in a frame using
the name given in the clip’s caption. We remove outlier points and require the remaining points to
lie near a hand whenever one is tracked in that frame. If we can’t find the object with enough points
in up to four frames, we discard the candidate clip. SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2025) then tracks the
object mask forward and backward through the clip, starting from the accepted points.
SAM 3D (Chen et al., 2025) then reconstructs the object mesh from one frame, chosen to have a
large object mask. Masks much larger than the adjacent frames or multi-part or oddly shaped are
given lower priority. At other sampled frames, SAM 3D reuses this mesh to estimate the object’s
pose and scale. We run the full reconstruction only when this fails for a frame.
Object motion estimation. CoTracker3 (Karaev et al., 2025) tracks points in the object mask, and
depth from MoGe (Wang et al., 2025a) lifts them into 3D. RANSAC PnP (Fischler & Bolles, 1981;
Lepetit et al., 2009) gives initial object poses, which we refine before removing tracks with high
reprojection error. Bundle adjustment then refines the poses and the points’ positions within the
object together, with fixed camera poses. The Huber loss (Huber, 1964) assesses agreement with
image tracks, while additional penalties promote smooth rotations and maintain the estimated 3D
shape. Rotations come from this fit and object centers from SAM 3D’s estimates. If no fit is found
or if the median per-frame reprojection error is > 12 pixels, we fall back on the rotation estimates
from SAM 3D.
Joint optimization. We then fix the hand shape and size and jointly refine the hand and object
poses. Although the object shape is fixed, we refine the object size estimated from MoGe depth with
a single scale factor per clip. The objective penalizes interpenetration, fits the reprojected object
to its mask, and maintains hand contact with the object. Other terms discourage abrupt motion,
unnatural finger poses, and large deviations from the initial estimates. We also penalize changes in
the hand’s position relative to the object and, during grasps, changes in its relative rotation.
We optimize with Adam and a cosine learning-rate schedule in stages. In the initial frames, we align
the wrists and fix those poses. We then refine the wrist and object motions, followed by the finger
poses, and finally all of them together. A final stage again refines the wrists and fingers in frames
with low tracking confidence and poor hand-object alignment by fixing the object and pulling each
finger toward its position relative to the palm in nearby reliable frames. After optimization, we
correct object rotation during occlusions, remove a learned depth bias, and smooth the remaining
hand jitter (Appendix B.2).
Object scale and depth correction. An object’s reconstruction can change size and depth across
frames and still match the image mask, since a larger object farther away casts the same silhouette.
We correct this when the 95th-percentile object size is at least 1.35 times the 5th-percentile size.
Each frame’s mesh is first rescaled to the clip’s median object size. Comparing its projected area

Interaction intervals. We call a frame an interaction frame when the object is visible and a tracked
hand is within 5 cm of its surface. Consecutive interaction frames form intervals, and we merge
intervals separated by at most 2 seconds. We then extend each interval by up to 0.75 seconds on
both sides to include the frames where the hand approaches the object or releases it. Frames where
the estimated object pose jumps abruptly are likely reconstruction errors. We leave them out, and no
interval is merged or extended across them. We export each interval as a trajectory if it lasts at least
2.5 seconds and includes at least eight frames. Trajectories from the same clip do not overlap.
Final pose refinement. We adjust each trajectory’s object position so that the projected silhouette
more closely resembles the observed mask. Each modification is restricted to 0.30 m of translation
and 35◦of rotation. We consider a hand to be grasping an object if it remains within 4 cm of it
for multiple consecutive frames. To maintain their relative stance throughout a grip, we adjust each
frame and move the hand with the object. Next, without altering finger articulation, we smooth the
hand’s rotation in relation to the object during grasps. Lastly, we relocate each hand by up to 8 mm
to minimize residual hand-object penetration.
Gravity estimation. We estimate gravity from a supporting surface, such as a table or the floor,
in the MoGe depth maps of several trajectory frames. The surface must appear consistently across
these frames and lie below the hands, the object, and most of the scene. We favor large surfaces
near the interaction, especially one the object rests on. A wall can meet these conditions too, since
the hands and the object are in front of it. But for a wall to be the floor, the camera would usually
have to be rolled far to the side or pointed sharply upward. People rarely film their hands like that,
so we reject these surfaces. There are two more cases that we skip, because in both of them we
cannot tell which way is up. A surface the camera sees at a grazing angle could face up or down.
A surface facing the camera squarely could be a wall in front of the camera or a table filmed from
straight above. Gravity is the normal of the selected surface, pointing away from the interaction; if
no surface qualifies, we leave it undetermined.
Quality filtering. We keep only trajectories that agree with the video. The projected object must
match the outline and size of its tracked mask, and we reject trajectories in which the object changes
shape or size or moves abruptly. A hand must also stay within 5 cm of the object in at least 25%
of the frames. Further checks catch shot boundaries, excessive hand-object penetration, and object
masks that follow the hand rather than the object. We also drop trajectories where the hand touches
the object in 3D but, in the image, almost never touches its mask. Lastly, we ask Molmo 2 to point
to “the object held in the hand” in several frames, and we reject the trajectory if it often returns no
point or points far from our tracked mask (i.e., we are likely tracking the wrong object).

REFINEMENT DETAILS
Object rotation. When the object is occluded for at least three consecutive frames, the grasping
hand’s motion guides its rotation only when contact is stable and the camera rotates slowly. During
the occlusion, the object rotates more closely with the hand, with the correction growing gradually
as the occlusion begins and shrinking as it ends. We reject corrections that introduce large rotation
jumps.
Bias denoiser. A separate denoiser learns to recover clean trajectories from simulated extraction
errors. It trains on 11,568 windows of 50 frames from DexYCB and HOT3D, sampled at 10 Hz,
excluding the 129 segments from the sequences we use to evaluate refinement. The simulated errors
are designed to reproduce the observed errors in the extraction pipeline. For instance, hands and
objects are positioned too near or too far from the camera, their positions are disturbed over several
frames, and the object’s orientation changes gradually.
The denoiser receives the hand and object poses at each frame, including finger articulation, and
predicts corrections that we add to the standardized input states. It is an eight-layer bidirectional
transformer with width 384, six attention heads, and feedforward width 1024. We train it for 15,000
steps with batch size 256, learning rate 2 × 10−4, and 500 warmup steps. The loss is squared recon-

B.3
EXTRACTION QUALITY
We evaluate the extraction pipeline of Section 3.4 on the EPIC-Contact test set (Bansal et al., 2026)
with the authors’ released evaluator and fitted reference poses (Table 7). Unlike HOPformer, our
pipeline is not trained on EPIC-Contact. It produces trajectories for 215 of the 237 test clips, and
we score both methods on these clips. Since our trajectories are sampled at about 10 Hz, we sam-
ple HOPformer’s predictions at the same frames for metrics that depend on frame rate. Our hand
joint error is lower than HOPformer’s (16.0 vs. 19.7 mm), and so is our motion deviation (10.6 vs.
13.8 mm). Our contact deviation (34.6 vs. 20.8 mm) and hand-object relative position error (71.8
vs. 64.7 mm) are higher, reflecting the main limitation of the extracted trajectories, namely absolute
depth and hand placement relative to the object.
Table 8 repeats the comparison when every input comes from the video, as for an internet clip.
HOPformer then receives hand crops from the WiLoR detector and the SAM 3D reconstruction
of the object, aligned to the template of the class it predicts. On EPIC-Contact, its hand error
rises from 19.7 to 23.9 mm and its object error from 20.6 to 24.0 mm, both above ours (16.0 and
18.3 mm). Its object head was trained to pose its own templates, and it predicts the right class for
only 60% of clips and labels almost every DexYCB object a bottle. HOPformer also depends on the
egocentric viewpoint. On egocentric HOT3D, its hand error is close to ours (52.2 vs. 55.0 mm), but
on third-person DexYCB it rises to 96.6 mm, against 10.3 mm for ours. The failure depends on the
camera. For the camera that views the table from the side, its hand error is 117.8 mm, compared
with 41.4 mm for a camera that looks down at about 50◦, while ours stays between 9 and 16 mm.
Our object error is also lower on both datasets (25.5 vs. 38.5 mm on HOT3D and 20.7 vs. 28.0 mm
on DexYCB).

Benchmark construction.
The 256 annotated windows come from 168 recordings: 36 from
DexYCB; 35 each from HO3D, HOT3D, OakInk2, SHOW3D, and TACO; 24 from HO-Cap; and 21
from H2O. The 75 internet windows come from 75 different Action100M videos. We further refine
the extracted poses to better match the video and add a new AI-assisted review using six sampled
frames to increase reference accuracy. The selection process never uses model predictions or errors,
and no window is hand-selected.
How extraction error affects internet-video scores. We evaluate predictions on internet videos
against our own extracted trajectories, so extraction errors can affect the results. Much of what
Appendix B.2 reports, however, is a constant offset per clip, mostly in depth (45–62% of the squared
position error on DexYCB, 77% on HOT3D). An offset like this largely cancels. If a clip sits 10 cm
too far from the camera, the observed frames the model receives sit 10 cm too far as well, and so
does the reference. What remains drifts slowly over the clip, is also mostly in depth, and this part
does affect the scores. On clips that pass our quality checks, a true motion of 50–150 mm comes
out 10–15% too short, and the error in that motion is smaller across the image than in depth, with
medians of 28 versus 49 mm for objects and 41 versus 55 mm for wrists.

FDE uses only windows in which the reference moves at least 50 mm from its last observed position.

reports every metric for that sample. For samples x1, . . . , xN and a reference y, the energy score

Physical metrics. Finger error (Table 9) is the angle between predicted and reference rotations,
averaged over the 15 finger joints of each hand. For closed object meshes, we measure how deeply
hand vertices penetrate the object and the percentage of vertices inside it. Joint limits follow Table 4
and are strict, since 14.8% of joint poses in the annotated references exceed them. A violation
therefore counts only when the predicted joint exceeds a limit and the reference joint does not, and
we report violations as a percentage of all evaluated joint poses.
Interaction metrics. The hand-object metrics of Table 10 use frames where the hand and object are
both present and at least one of them is generated. A fingertip touches the object when it is within
10 mm of its surface. Tip error is the fingertip position error relative to the object on frames where
the reference hand touches the object, and contact F1 accepts predicted contact up to two frames
earlier or later than the reference. A reference grasp is a run of at least three frames in which at least
two reference fingertips touch the object. On each grasp frame, the movement error is the difference
between how the predicted and reference fingertips have moved relative to the object since the grasp
began, averaged over the five fingertips. The frame is held when at least two predicted fingertips
touch the object and this error is at most 10 mm, slipping when a predicted fingertip touches the
object but the frame is not held, and detached when every predicted fingertip is at least 20 mm away.
Other frames are unclassified, and we report the percentage of grasp frames in each class.
Each window’s interaction score averages the 20 samples, and the two hands when both are present.
We then average windows within each task, separately for annotated data and internet videos, and
unlike for the other metrics, every annotated window counts equally regardless of its dataset. Tasks
count equally, and so do annotated data and internet videos. For differences between models, we
estimate 95% confidence intervals by resampling video segments 5,000 times, using the same re-
sampled segments for both models.
Learned baselines. For Table 2, we use the USST ResNet-18, ObjectForesight EPIC-DiT, and Uni-
Hand SAM-3D-Body/DINOv2 checkpoints, the variant of each that scores best on this benchmark.
ObjectForesight takes one step per video frame, which scores better than one step per 1/55 s, the
frame interval of its training videos. The ForeHand4D checkpoint was trained on DexYCB, HOT3D,
and H2O, and the Uni-Hand checkpoints on robot demonstrations. Methods that use depth get
monocular depth from Depth Anything 3 for MotionForesight and from MoGe-2 for the others,
including our model. General Flow and MolmoMotion get action text for each clip, from dataset
labels where available and a generic description otherwise. All predictions start from the reference

Accuracy and spread of the samples.
By energy score, the main model has the lowest ADE
and FDE, yielding 1–7% lower values than the other configurations for hands and 3–6% lower for
objects. At the final position, the main model’s samples are on average 2–6% closer to the reference
for hands and 3–5% closer for objects, while the distance between samples is about the same or
smaller. Across both data sources, the reference lies inside the samples about equally often for all
configurations: 80–84% of windows for hands and 76–79% for objects, versus an ideal of 95%. On
annotated data, the samples are too close together, and the reference lies inside the main model’s
samples more often than for the ablations without physical losses, relation tokens, or augmentation
(69% versus 64–66% for hands and 69% versus 60–63% for objects). On internet video, where every
configuration comes close to the ideal, the main model’s samples are closer together and closer to
the reference.
Losses and relation tokens. Two parts of the model are there for hand-object contact, the physi-
cal losses and the relation tokens (Section 3.2). The physical losses are the attachment, fingertip-
distance, joint-limit, MANO pose prior, and support penalty terms, which supervise contact and
plausibility on decoded trajectories (Section 3.3). We train one ablation without them and one with-

out the relation tokens. The latter still has the attachment and fingertip-distance losses, so its contact
is supervised. Removing either part weakens the grasp. In the main model, fingertip error during
contact is 7–9% lower, contact F1 is 68.2% (versus 65.3% and 66.3%), and 24.9% of grasp frames
are detached (versus 29.1% and 28.1%; Table 10). The closer grasp costs a little. Slipping, i.e.,

same depth. The main model’s hand ADE and FDE are also 2–8% lower in every column of Table 9.
Scene conditioning. Nearby surfaces and obstacles limit where an object can go, and the scene
encoder tells the model about them. We also train without it, i.e., without scene geometry or DINOv3
features (the object shape is still given). The hands are barely affected, within 2% on ADE and FDE,
and detach about as often (26.0% versus 24.9% of grasp frames). The objects are not. The main
model’s object FDE is 4–6% lower in every column of Table 9, and 7% lower for a single sample on
annotated data, where the references are most accurate.
Augmentation. We rotate the hands, object, and scene together around the vertical axis and resam-
ple windows at lower frame rates (Appendix A) so that the model does not depend on the usual
orientation and frame rate of each training dataset. Without this augmentation, every ADE and FDE
in Table 9 gets worse. The main model’s are 3–9% lower, and the gap is largest for best-of-20 hand
FDE. The ablation’s hand also detaches from the object more often (27.8% versus 24.9% of grasp
frames). It has the most joint-limit violations in the table (10.8% versus 9.9%) and the largest finger
error (13.3◦versus 12.9◦).
Interpreting the interaction scores. Each sample is scored against a single reference, frame by
frame, so a sample that follows a plausible but different hand path is penalized. The scores therefore
depend on how much of the hand’s path the model has to generate. Fingertip error during contact is
26 mm in temporal infilling and 46 mm when the hand motion is given, but 60–93 mm in the five
tasks where the model generates most of the hand’s path. Table 10 averages all seven tasks. For
scale, stay-put on the same frames has a fingertip error of 92 mm and a contact F1 of 55%. The
main model beats it on both in every task, and its overall fingertip error is 28% lower.

LIMITATIONS
We extract the internet-video trajectories with pretrained models, and their mistakes end up in our
training data. MoGe estimates depth from a single image, where a large object far away casts the
same silhouette as a small one nearby, so the hands and object can sit at the wrong distance from
the camera. Refinement fixes much of this, though not all, and extracted motions come out a bit
short. WiLoR loses the fingers when the object covers them and occasionally takes a hand-shaped
object for a hand. SAM 3D can flip an object’s orientation between frames, and we often fail to
pose thin objects such as spoons or cables. On tripod shots, MegaSaM sometimes reports a small
camera motion, which then shows up as motion of an object resting on the table. We also reject
most candidate clips, many only after all these models and the per-clip optimization have run, so
extracting more videos takes a lot of compute.
