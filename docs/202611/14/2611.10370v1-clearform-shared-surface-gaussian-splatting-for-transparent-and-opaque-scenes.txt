Abstract
Transparent-interface reconstruction must recover glass sur-
faces even when most observed color comes from the scene
behind them.
We present ClearForm, a shared-planar-
Gaussian method for jointly reconstructing transparent in-
terfaces and the appearance seen through them. We iden-
tify and analyze the Geometry–Appearance Compositing
Dilemma (GACD): when the same transparency-dependent
alpha controls both color and geometry, revealing the back-
ground can weaken a transparent surface’s contribution to
geometric reconstruction. We derive its effect on plane-
based depth estimation, identify a mechanism that can
weaken geometric learning, and quantify the loss of in-
terface support through controlled comparisons.
To ad-
dress this conflict, we develop geometry-preserving dual-
opacity surface reconstruction. Shared planar Gaussians
retain interface contributions in geometry compositing while
modeling transmission and sparse reflection in RGB, sup-
ported by transparency-aware geometric constraints. To man-
age the different primitive-visibility requirements of these
compositors, we introduce Dual-Evidence Lifecycle (DEL),
which retains Gaussians supported by multi-view geome-
try or RGB reconstruction evidence.
On 3D-FRONT-T,
ClearForm achieves 27.05 mm Chamfer distance (CD) and
0.928 F1, compared with 59.18 mm CD for our PGSR re-
production and the reported 34 mm CD and 0.836 F1 for
GLINT. On TransLab, it achieves 1.65 mm CD, 0.963 F1,
and 40.14 dB PSNR. In matched ablations, dual-opacity com-
positing reduces CD from 59.25 to 27.09 mm, while DEL
improves PSNR by 2.06 dB. Our project page is available at
https://hajeongx.github.io/clearform/.

Introduction
Glass transmits light while introducing a physical boundary
between materials. We call this boundary a transparent inter-
face. Along a camera ray, the near-side interface lies before
the scene surfaces visible through the glass, which we call
background geometry. The appearance of these background
surfaces as seen through the interface constitutes transmitted
appearance. Reconstruction must therefore recover interface
geometry while preserving the observed appearance.
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] renders color through
front-to-back alpha compositing, where each Gaussian is
weighted by its opacity and the transmittance remaining af-
ter nearer Gaussians. Surface-oriented methods improve
geometry through surface alignment, planar primitives, or
opacity-field extraction [2–5]. Planar-based Gaussian Splat-
ting (PGSR) [5] also uses these compositing weights to blend
plane normals and distances for depth estimation. When
the same alpha is reduced to reveal background appearance
through a transparent interface, its geometric contribution is
reduced as well.
We call this conflict the Geometry–Appearance Composit-
ing Dilemma (GACD). For a single plane, its alpha weight
cancels out in PGSR’s depth calculation. However, when
transparent interfaces and background surfaces contribute to
the same pixel, transparency changes their relative weights
and can shift the estimated depth. It can also weaken a
geometric gradient pathway and cause small interface con-
tributions to be skipped during rendering. We derive these
effects and measure how shared alpha reduces support for
the ground-truth first surface. Separate training ablations
assess whether geometry-preserving compositing improves
reconstruction accuracy.
Guided by this analysis, we develop geometry-preserving
dual-opacity surface reconstruction over shared planar Gaus-
sians. The geometry path blends plane attributes without

constraints then supervise the interface without requiring it
to block transmitted color in RGB. Together, these compo-

Dual-opacity compositing also separates the visibility con-
ditions for geometry and RGB. A transparent interface can
contribute strongly to geometric rendering while allowing
background color to pass through. It may therefore occlude
background Gaussians in the geometry pass even though
they remain useful for transmitted RGB. If their learned
opacity becomes small during optimization, conventional
opacity-based pruning can remove them; a geometry-only
visibility test likewise misses their appearance contribution.
We introduce Dual-Evidence Lifecycle (DEL), which retains
Gaussians supported by geometry-pass visibility or positive
RGB-loss contribution across distinct camera views, and
maintains evidence across cloning and splitting.
At a fixed Gaussian state, changing only the geometry
alpha rule raises ground-truth first-hit coverage from 7.08%
to 14.70%. In the cumulative 3D-FRONT-T ablation, dual-
opacity compositing reduces Chamfer distance (CD) from
59.25 to 27.09 mm; DEL then raises PSNR from 32.05 to
34.11 dB while retaining similar mesh accuracy. The com-
plete model achieves 27.05 mm CD and 0.928 F1 on 3D-
FRONT-T, and 1.65 mm CD and 0.963 F1 on TransLab.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• Geometry–Appearance Compositing Dilemma. We an-
alyze how transparency-dependent alpha can weaken the
geometric contributions of transparent interfaces while re-
vealing the background, including its effects when different
surfaces overlap along a ray. We derive the consequences
for depth rendering and geometric optimization and quan-
tify the loss of first-surface support.
• Geometry-preserving dual-opacity surface reconstruc-
tion. We formulate separate geometry and appearance
compositing over shared planar Gaussians so transparent
interfaces can contribute to surface reconstruction without
obscuring transmitted color. Transparency-aware PGSR
regularization constrains interface geometry, while learned
transparency and sparse reflection reproduce the observed
appearance.
• Dual-Evidence Lifecycle. We introduce a multi-view
Gaussian survival criterion that addresses the geometry–
RGB visibility mismatch in dual-opacity reconstruction.
By retaining primitives supported by geometry-pass vis-
ibility or positive RGB-loss contribution across distinct
cameras, DEL helps preserve transparent interfaces and
appearance-useful background Gaussians with different
rendering roles.

Related Work
Surface reconstruction with neural and Gaussian rep-
resentations.
Neural surface methods including VolSDF,
NeuS, and Neuralangelo [11–13] extend radiance-field op-
timization [14] with surface structure or geometric regu-
larization.
Gaussian methods offer efficient alternatives:
SuGaR aligns Gaussians to surfaces [2], 2DGS uses ori-
ented disks [3], Gaussian Opacity Fields extracts surfaces
from opacity [4], and PGSR estimates depth from alpha-
blended plane normals and distances while enforcing single-
and multi-view constraints [5]. GS-2DGS adds learned ge-
ometric guidance for reflective objects [15]. In ClearForm,
PGSR’s plane representation provides the surface attributes
for a distinct geometry-compositing path.
Reflective and refractive appearance.
Ref-NeRF, NeR-
FReN, and NeRO address non-Lambertian appearance
through directional representations, radiance decomposition,
and geometry/material estimation [16–18]. GlassGaussian
models glass reflectance [19]; Ref-GS and EnvGS capture
view-dependent reflections [20, 21], and GUS-IR combines
forward and deferred shading [22]. Other methods model
refractive transport: Eikonal Fields traces curved rays [23],
NU-NeRF reconstructs nested transparent objects [24], and
TransparentGS uses deferred refraction [25]. ClearForm in-
stead uses straight-ray splatting and learned view-dependent
appearance without explicitly modeling refracted light paths
or estimating refractive indices.
Transparent surfaces and depth extraction.
TSGS [9]
separates geometry learning from transparency-aware appear-
ance refinement using normal and de-lighting priors and a
first-surface depth rule. TSPE-GS [26] and UniGS [27] rep-
resent multiple depths per ray; G-GSTR [28] and TSTR [29]
add geometric or specular constraints. ClearForm focuses on
how transparency changes the interface’s geometric contribu-
tion during plane-based rendering and optimization.
Geometry–appearance factorization and scene decom-
position.
RT-Splatting [8] factorizes geometric occupancy
and optical opacity for surface-attribute aggregation in de-
ferred reflection and volumetric transmission. GLINT [10]
represents interface, transmission, and reflection using sepa-
rate Gaussian components and secondary-ray transport. Al-
phaSurf and Car-GS also distinguish geometric from op-
tical roles [6, 7]; related appearance methods include TR-
Gaussians, RT-GS, and TransmissiveGS [30–32]. Clear-
Form investigates the geometric consequences of sharing
transparency-dependent alpha, including the depth shift
caused by overlapping interface and background planes. We
derive the associated gradient and rasterization effects and
quantify interface support using fixed-Gaussian comparisons.
Our geometry-preserving dual-opacity formulation combines
transparency-independent plane compositing, transmitted ap-
pearance, and sparse reflection on shared planar Gaussians
with transparency-aware PGSR constraints.
Gaussian pruning.
LightGaussian and PUP 3D-GS prune
by significance or reconstruction sensitivity [33, 34],
while MaskGaussian preserves gradients for masked prim-
itives [35]. FastGS and birth–death structural learning use
multi-view reconstruction signals [36, 37]. RT-Splatting [8]
prunes by geometric occupancy to preserve transparent sur-
faces. In dual-opacity reconstruction, a geometry-visible
transparent interface can occlude background Gaussians in
the geometry pass even though they contribute to transmitted

RGB. Opacity-based pruning may discard such Gaussians
when their learned opacity is low, while geometry-only vis-
ibility cannot capture their RGB usefulness. ClearForm’s
Dual-Evidence Lifecycle (DEL) instead retains primitives
supported by geometry-pass visibility or positive first-order
RGB-loss contribution across distinct cameras. Lineage ag-
gregation and temporary evidence inheritance extend this
criterion to clone/split children.

Geometry–Appearance Composit-
ing Dilemma
GACD arises when the same transparency-dependent alpha
is used for both appearance rendering and geometric com-
positing. Reducing a transparent interface’s alpha reveals
background appearance but also weakens its contribution
to geometric rendering. We analyze this conflict in PGSR,
which estimates depth from alpha-blended plane normals and
distances. Specifically, we examine how shared alpha affects
blended-plane depth, a local geometric gradient pathway,
and the inclusion of interface contributions during rasteri-
zation. Figure 2 illustrates the corresponding interface and
background contributions.

4
ClearForm
To prevent transmission from suppressing interface plane
contributions as analyzed in Section 3, ClearForm separates
geometry and appearance compositing over shared planar
Gaussians (Section 4.1). Transparency-aware PGSR con-
straints supervise the interface geometry (Section 4.2), while
RGB rendering preserves transmitted color and sparse reflec-
tion. DEL addresses the complementary survival require-
ments of interface and background Gaussians (Section 4.3).
Figure 3 summarizes the pipeline.

4.3
Dual-Evidence Lifecycle

weights for geometry and RGB rendering. A transparent
interface can contribute strongly to geometry compositing,
reducing the accumulated transmittance to background Gaus-

geometry-pass weights while remaining useful for transmit-
ted RGB, where interface alpha is attenuated by transparency.

their appearance contribution, while conventional opacity-
based pruning can remove them when their learned opacity
is low. We introduce Dual-Evidence Lifecycle (DEL), which
determines Gaussian survival using geometry-pass visibility
or positive RGB-loss contribution across distinct cameras.

5.3
Ablation study
Table 3 and Figure 6 trace the cumulative changes from
PGSR to the full method in geometry and rendering.
Normal guidance and shared-opacity appearance.
Nor-
mal guidance lowers MAE from 26.01◦to 21.84◦, but mesh
CD remains approximately 59.2 mm. Adding transparency
and reflection improves PSNR to 33.05 dB while CD remains
59.25 mm and AbsRel increases to 0.2164. Improved image
fitting alone therefore does not resolve interface geometry in
this shared-opacity configuration.
Dual-opacity compositing.
Separating geometry and base
RGB opacity reduces AbsRel from 0.2164 to 0.0263, normal
MAE from 21.20◦to 7.43◦, and CD from 59.25 to 27.09 mm.
F1 increases from 0.794 to 0.925. This is the largest re-
duction in CD and normal MAE in the cumulative ablation,
linking the geometry-specific alpha rule to substantially im-
proved surface reconstruction under matched supervision.
PSNR falls to 32.05 dB, so geometry improvement alone
does not guarantee better RGB. As this is a training abla-
tion, it includes both the compositing rule and its effects
on the learned representation. The matched view shows a
more coherent transparent interface in depth and normals
(Figure 6).
Dual-Evidence Lifecycle.
Adding DEL raises PSNR by
2.06 dB and reduces LPIPS from 0.0787 to 0.0529, while
CD changes only from 27.09 to 27.15 mm and F1 remains
approximately 0.925. This gain is consistent with DEL pre-
serving RGB-useful Gaussians behind geometrically visible
interfaces under dual-opacity rendering, while maintaining
similar aggregate surface accuracy. The cumulative ablation
evaluates the complete lifecycle; it does not directly identify
individual pruning events or isolate the effects of its evidence
and lineage components.
Boundary RGB loss.
The boundary term yields small
changes in PSNR (34.11 to 34.14 dB) and CD (27.15 to
27.05 mm). Appendix E separately examines the transparent-
region geometry terms by leaving one out at a time.
5.4
Effect of shared alpha on interface support
The preceding ablation shows that training with decoupled
geometry compositing substantially improves surface recon-
struction. To examine the compositing effect separately from
changes in the learned representation, we evaluate the shared-
and decoupled-alpha geometry rules on the same Gaussian
checkpoint.
9


Using ground-truth first-surface depth, we identify Gaus-
sians whose planes intersect near the interface and measure
their geometric contribution weights and pixel coverage.
Decoupled geometry compositing increases mean first-hit
weight from 0.0179 to 0.0401 and coverage from 7.08%
to 14.70%. Under shared alpha, 44.95% of first-hit pixel–
Gaussian pairs active under decoupled alpha fall below the
per-pixel alpha threshold. These results directly quantify the
loss of interface support caused by shared alpha at a fixed
Gaussian representation.
Appendix D defines these measurements and reports a
supplementary cross-evaluation of shared- and decoupled-
trained checkpoints. This comparison distinguishes immedi-
ate compositing-rule effects from differences between trained
representations, without isolating individual gradient mecha-
nisms.
6
Conclusion and Discussion
ClearForm analyzes and addresses the loss of transparent-
interface geometry when the same transparency-dependent al-
pha controls appearance and geometric compositing. GACD
explains why alpha affects mixed-plane depth despite can-
celling for an isolated plane, identifies a local geometric gra-
dient pathway and per-pixel threshold effects, and quantifies
interface support with ground-truth first-hit measurements.
Geometry-preserving dual-opacity reconstruction then re-
tains plane contributions from transparent interfaces while
representing transmitted appearance and sparse reflection on
shared planar Gaussians under transparency-aware PGSR
constraints.
Dual-opacity compositing also exposes different survival
requirements: transparent interfaces can occlude background
Gaussians in the geometry pass while allowing their RGB
contributions to pass through. DEL addresses this mismatch
using geometry-pass visibility or predicted positive RGB-loss
contribution across distinct recent views. The matched ab-
lation shows that dual-opacity compositing gives the largest
geometric gain, while DEL gives the largest subsequent ren-
dering gain. These findings establish complementary roles
for the reconstruction and lifecycle components, not a causal
attribution to any single gradient path. Strong reflections
and missing transparency guidance can degrade interface re-
construction, and single-depth mesh fusion can spuriously
cap open containers. Across 3D-FRONT-T and TransLab,
ClearForm improves transparent-surface reconstruction while
preserving transmitted appearance and view-dependent re-
flection.

We ablate transparent-region single-view (SV) and multi-

interface-normal accuracy; omitting MV has a smaller effect
and slightly improves CD and F1, so a consistent MV benefit
