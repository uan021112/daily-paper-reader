Abstract
Hybrid Mesh–Gaussian rendering combines compact tex-
tured surfaces with flexible 3D Gaussian primitives, offer-
ing an efficient alternative to purely Gaussian scene rep-
resentations for structured environments. Existing scene-
reconstruction hybrids commonly follow an additive for-
mulation, where Gaussian residuals compensate for errors
left by the mesh. However, not every Mesh–Gaussian con-
flict is caused by missing content: residual geometry errors,
occlusion mismatch, texture-projection artifacts, and view-
dependent appearance can make the same mesh content con-
sistent in one view but inconsistent in another. We therefore
formulate hybrid rendering as a multi-view inconsistency
problem and present Gaussolve, a subtract-and-refill frame-
work that selectively dissolves inconsistent mesh content
before residual Gaussians refill the revealed regions. We
introduce a lightweight Neural Dissolve Field, parameter-
ized by scalar spherical-harmonic Gaussians, to learn where
and from which viewing directions mesh content should be
dissolved. A continuous transmittance fusion rule progres-
sively exposes residual Gaussians behind the dissolved mesh,
preserving their reconstruction gradients and avoiding hard
visibility discontinuities. Experiments on structured and
semi-structured scenes demonstrate higher visual fidelity
with fewer Gaussian primitives and faster rendering. Project

1. Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become a strong represen-
tation for real-time novel-view synthesis due to explicit prim-
itives and efficient differentiable rasterization [17]. How-
ever, practical environments contain broad regular structures,
such as road surfaces with lane markings and large architec-
tural surfaces [15]. Representing these large regular regions
purely with Gaussian primitives can require a large primitive
budget, increasing storage and rasterization cost [8, 15, 20].
Textured meshes provide a complementary representation:
they compactly anchor broad regular surfaces, while Gaus-
sians remain effective for thin structures, local residual geom-
etry, and view-dependent appearance [6, 15, 17]. However,
existing hybrid scene-reconstruction pipelines still inherit
limitations in both how the mesh is constructed and how the
two representations are combined.
First, we revisit how the textured mesh is constructed. Ex-
isting methods typically extract the mesh from a previously
optimized NeRF or Gaussian representation and then bake
the reconstructed appearance onto the mesh [6, 15], making
the resulting texture inherit the constraints of such recon-
struction. In particular, enforcing cleaner and more regular
geometry can reduce the flexibility of these representations
to fit the observed appearance, leading to a trade-off between
geometric alignment and rendering quality [6, 14, 45]. In-
stead, we reconstruct the geometry independently and project
the training images directly onto the mesh, thereby preserv-
ing the observed appearance when the geometry is reliable.
More importantly, we revisit how the mesh and Gaussians
are combined. Even with such a textured mesh, the funda-
mental Mesh–Gaussian conflict remains. Conceptually, exist-
ing hybrids largely follow an additive formulation: the mesh
accounts for the regular scene content, while Gaussians com-
pensate for what the mesh fails to represent. But not every
Mesh–Gaussian conflict is caused by missing content. Such
conflicts may arise from residual geometry errors, occlusion
mismatch, texture-projection artifacts, or view-dependent ap-
pearance, and importantly, the same mesh content can agree
with one view while becoming inconsistent with another. We
therefore reinterpret the remaining Mesh–Gaussian conflict
as a multi-view inconsistency problem, which requires se-
lectively suppressing inconsistent mesh content rather than
only adding Gaussian residuals.
This reformulation exposes a fundamental mismatch be-
tween the problem and existing conflict-handling strategies.
Existing mesh–Gaussian hybrids often resolve representa-
tion conflicts through hard visibility or assignment decisions.
For example, prior methods remove inaccurate mesh regions
to make room for Gaussians [15], enforce hard assignments
between mesh and Gaussian representations [19], or discard
Gaussians occluded by an opaque mesh surface [6, 19]. Al-
though these designs differ in implementation, they reduce
the conflict to fixed removal, hard assignment, or binary

When the mesh conflicts with the current observation, this

We propose Gaussolve, a hybrid Mesh–Gaussian frame-
work that resolves these inconsistencies through a subtract-
and-refill process (Fig. 1). Instead of treating the mesh
as a fixed explanation and only adding Gaussian residuals
around it, Gaussolve allows inconsistent mesh content to be
selectively dissolved before residual Gaussians refill the re-
vealed regions. To this end, we introduce a Neural Dissolve
Field parameterized by scalar spherical-harmonic Gaussians,
which learns where and from which viewing directions the
mesh should be attenuated under the visual reconstruction
objective. We further integrate mesh dissolution and Gaus-
sian accumulation through continuous transmittance-based
fusion, so that suppressing the mesh progressively exposes
the residual Gaussians behind it rather than abruptly switch-
ing visibility. This preserves their reconstruction gradients
and allows them to correct the inconsistencies exposed by
mesh dissolution, while mesh regions that remain consistent
with the current view are preserved.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We reformulate hybrid Mesh–Gaussian rendering as a
multi-view inconsistency problem, moving beyond the
purely additive formulation by selectively subtracting in-
consistent mesh content before Gaussian refill.
• We introduce a view-dependent Neural Dissolve Field that
learns where and from which viewing directions mesh
content should be dissolved, while preserving it in views
where it remains consistent.
• We propose a continuous transmittance fusion rule that
avoids hard visibility discontinuities, preserves reconstruc-
tion gradients for residual Gaussians, and enables them to
refill the content revealed by mesh dissolution.
• We introduce a progressive optimization curriculum with
loss-driven dual densification, further improving optimiza-
tion stability and performance.
2. Related Work
2.1. 3D Gaussian Splatting
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [17] represents scenes using
explicit 3D Gaussian primitives and achieves efficient opti-
mization and rendering via differentiable rasterization, offer-
ing a strong quality–speed trade-off for novel-view synthe-
sis. Subsequent works improve the Gaussian-only pipeline
through densification, pruning, sorting, compression, or re-
duced memory footprints (e.g., StopThePop [26], Revising
Densification [29], Taming 3DGS [23], LightGaussian [8],
Mini-Splatting [9], Scaffold-GS [22], Octree-GS [27], and
Reduced 3DGS [24]), and recent surveys summarize these
branches [2].
2.2. Mesh Priors and Surface Reconstruction
SfM–MVS pipelines such as COLMAP [31, 32], as well as
LiDAR and photogrammetry workflows, are widely used to
produce point clouds, depth maps, and mesh priors for down-
stream rendering, editing, and interaction. Surface recon-
struction from these inputs (e.g., Poisson reconstruction [16])

2.3. Hybrid Mesh–Gaussian Rendering
Hybrid representations typically employ a surface mesh to
anchor large-scale structures and use Gaussians or surfels
to capture thin geometry and appearance residuals. Such
designs have been explored for general scene rendering
(e.g., MeshGS [6], CityGo [21], HybridMeshGS [15], Mesh-
Splats [35], OMeGa [4], UniMGS [40], and When Gaus-
sian Meets Surfel [42]) and for controllable avatars and
heads (e.g., SplattingAvatar [33], GaussianAvatars [25],
HAHA [34], MeGA [37], HERA [3], Gaussian Pixel Codec
Avatars [12], and relightable Gaussian avatars [30, 38, 43]).
Related works such as SuGaR [10], MILo [11], and
GaMeS [36] further demonstrate the benefits of explicit
surfaces for geometric stability and mesh-in-the-loop op-
timization. However, many existing scene-reconstruction
hybrids rely on two-pass rendering, hard depth-based split-
ting [21], or heuristically pruned hard occluders [15], which
can suppress residual Gaussians, block their reconstruction
gradients, or abruptly switch the mesh–Gaussian visibility
state. Gaussolve instead formulates visibility fusion as a
single continuous transmittance accumulation with a volu-
metric Neural Dissolve Field, enabling continuous fusion
and selective suppression of inconsistent mesh content.
3. Method
As shown in Fig. 2, given posed multi-view images and a
textured mesh prior, Gaussolve constructs a hybrid represen-
tation with three branches: a textured mesh branch, residual
RGB Gaussians, and a Neural Dissolve Field (NDF). The
mesh branch provides compact structural and texture evi-
dence for broad regular regions, while residual RGB Gaus-
sians model content that cannot be consistently represented
by the mesh, including fine details and view-dependent ap-
pearance. Instead of treating the mesh as a hard occluder,
Gaussolve uses the NDF to selectively dissolve inconsistent
mesh content and continuously exposes residual Gaussians
through transmittance-based fusion.

3.4. Optimization Curriculum and Loss-Driven
Dual Densification
We progressively introduce the three components instead of
optimizing the full hybrid representation from the beginning.
1) We first optimize the residual Gaussian branch alone using
the standard 3DGS objective, which provides a stable initial-
ization for the subsequent hybrid optimization. 2) We then
introduce the projection-textured mesh and NDF and enable
continuous transmittance fusion. During hybrid optimiza-
tion, we freeze the mesh texture and employ loss-driven dual
densification: First, we sample high-error pixels from the
current fused rendering. These pixels are back-projected into
3D, with mesh depth serving as a coarse geometric anchor,
to add candidates to both the dissolve branch and the residual
RGB branch. The former increases the capacity to dissolve
inconsistent mesh content, while the latter increases the ca-
pacity to refill the revealed regions. All inserted candidates
remain fully trainable and are not constrained to the mesh
surface. 3) Finally, after Gaussian densification terminates,
we continue optimizing the Gaussian representation for a
short stabilization period before unfreezing the mesh texture
and jointly fine-tuning all components.
The main Gaussian branch follows the same global den-
sification window as standard 3DGS. For plug-in variants,
each Gaussian backbone retains its update strategy. Dissolve
Gaussians are initialized according to multi-view mesh-texel
errors. Additional details of the initialization and dual densi-
fication are provided in the supplementary material.

4.2. Quantitative and Qualitative Comparisons
Table 1 summarizes the results on structured and semi-
structured scenes. Across all three Gaussian backbones,
integrating Gaussolve consistently improves reconstruction
quality, showing that the proposed fusion is not tied to a
specific Gaussian optimization strategy. The 3DGS-MCMC
variant reaches the highest PSNR on both datasets, while
the Reduced 3DGS variant provides a stronger quality–
compactness trade-off with substantially fewer Gaussians.
The MCMC variants show slightly negative ∆GS because
3DGS-MCMC removes the opacity reset and opacity-based
pruning used by vanilla 3DGS. We retain its original up-
date strategy as our goal here is to evaluate whether the
fusion framework improves the backbone without changing
its native optimization behavior. Compared with the Hard-
Z Proxy under the same textured-mesh setting, Gaussolve
consistently improves reconstruction quality, confirming the
benefit of continuous subtract-and-refill fusion over hard
visibility. Table 2 further compares against MeshGS and
Hybrid Mesh-Gaussian on the two commonly reported Deep
Blending scenes. Gaussolve achieves higher average PSNR
and substantially lower LPIPS while reducing the Gaussian
population by 59.5%, compared with approximately 30% for
MeshGS and 36% for Hybrid Mesh-Gaussian.

5. Conclusion
We presented Gaussolve, a hybrid Mesh–Gaussian renderer
that reformulates representation conflicts as multi-view in-
consistencies. Gaussolve selectively dissolves inconsistent
mesh content with a Neural Dissolve Field and lets resid-
ual Gaussians refill the revealed regions. Experiments on
structured and semi-structured scenes demonstrate improved
visual quality across different Gaussian backbones, together
with substantial Gaussian reduction and real-time rendering.

S1. Additional Method Details
S1.1. Fixed Mesh Depth in Continuous Fusion
A possible extension of dissolution is to recompute a new
depth map after the mesh has been partially dissolved, and
then use this updated depth to separate front and back Gaus-
sian contributions. We do not adopt this design. Once dis-
solution becomes soft, a unique post-dissolution depth is no
longer naturally defined. Any such depth would require an
additional heuristic, such as thresholding a soft visibility pro-
file or defining an expected depth along the ray. This would
introduce extra hyperparameters and make the front/back
partition itself depend on the dissolve prediction.
More importantly, such a design creates an undesirable
feedback loop. If dissolution changes the depth, then the
updated depth changes which Gaussians are assigned to the
front or back segment. Those changed Gaussian contribu-
tions in turn modify the gradients that supervise the dissolve
field. As a result, the partition boundary itself becomes
unstable near occlusion transitions.
For this reason, Gaussolve always uses the rasterized
mesh depth Zm(u) as a fixed depth for the Gaussian-center
front/back partition. This depth is used only to compute the
front-segment statistics (Cf, Tf) required by the decompo-
sition in the main paper. The dissolve field does not alter
Zm(u). Instead, it only modulates the effective mesh opacity
αeff
m (u) = 1 −D(u).
(S1)
Thus the mesh depth remains fixed for the front/back decom-
position, while dissolution only changes the final blending
weight.

S2.2. Plug-in Gaussian Backbones
3DGS.
The default variant uses a 3DGS checkpoint ob-
tained after the Gaussian-only warm-up. At the hybrid hand-
off, we retain the Gaussian positions, scales, and rotations,
while resetting their color coefficients and opacities. The
residual field then continues with the standard 3DGS clone,
split, and opacity-based pruning operations. The NDF uses
an independent scalar-Gaussian optimizer and densification
process.
3DGS-MCMC.
For the MCMC variant, the Gaussian-only
warm-up is performed using the official 3DGS-MCMC up-
date strategy. Unlike the default 3DGS handoff, the MCMC
Gaussian colors and opacities are retained because they de-
termine the current particle distribution and relocation prob-
abilities.
During hybrid optimization, the MCMC operations are
applied only to the residual Gaussian field. Low-opacity
residual Gaussians are relocated to samples drawn from
the alive population, their opacity and scale are adjusted
using the original MCMC relocation rule, and new residual
Gaussians are added until the prescribed scene capacity is
reached. The original opacity-dependent SGLD perturbation
is also retained.
The NDF, the textured mesh and continuous fusion re-
main unchanged.
Because 3DGS-MCMC does not use the opacity-based
pruning mechanism of standard 3DGS, we retain its original
update strategy, as our goal is to evaluate whether the fusion
framework improves the backbone without altering its native
optimization behavior. Consequently, this variant can yield
slightly negative ∆GS, as reported in the main paper.
Reduced 3DGS.
For the Reduced 3DGS variant, the
Gaussian-only warm-up is performed using the official Re-
duced 3DGS update strategy. Because the Gaussolve ren-
derer expects a standard SH tensor layout, the grouped Re-
duced 3DGS checkpoint is converted to an equivalent full-
SH representation at the hybrid handoff. This conversion
changes only the storage layout and preserves the Gaussian
geometry and appearance.
During hybrid optimization, the redundancy-aware mercy
pruning and dead-point removal of Reduced 3DGS are ap-
plied only to the residual Gaussian field. Moreover, we retain


its non-DC SH sparsity regularization. At the end of the den-
sification window, multi-view SH-band culling determines
the lowest sufficient SH degree for each residual Gaussian
and removes the unused higher-order coefficients.
The NDF, the textured mesh and continuous fusion re-
main unchanged.
Compression codebooks and entropy coding are offline
storage mechanisms and are not required for the plug-in
training comparison. We therefore transfer the backbone’s
Gaussian initialization and population-reduction strategy,
while storing the final hybrid fields in the common represen-
tation used by our renderer.
S3. Surface-Based Dissolve Alternatives
A natural simplification of the Neural Dissolve Field is to
directly optimize opacity on the mesh surface. We study two
such alternatives: per-vertex alpha and texture-map alpha.
Per-vertex alpha is constrained by mesh tessellation and has
difficulty isolating thin or localized failure regions. Texture-
map alpha is less constrained by vertex count, but the learned
pattern is often diffuse and fragmented because texture-space
errors do not correspond cleanly to view-space photometric
conflicts. By contrast, the volumetric Neural Dissolve Field
is localized in 3D and can be view-dependent, allowing the
residual Gaussian branch to take over after unreliable mesh
evidence is removed.

S5. Limitations on Irregular Scenes
Gaussolve is designed for scenes where a lightweight tex-
tured mesh can replace broad structured regions, while resid-
ual Gaussians handle complex geometry and appearance
details. In scenes dominated by foliage, thin branches, and
severe geometric ambiguity, the extracted mesh may be too
inaccurate to provide a reliable surface prior. The mesh and
residual branches may then compete to explain the same im-
age evidence, limiting the reduction in Gaussian primitives.
This defines the intended operating regime of our method:
its largest compactness gains arise when broad regular re-
gions can be represented efficiently by the textured mesh.
Geometry-aware routing for highly irregular regions is an
interesting direction for future work.
