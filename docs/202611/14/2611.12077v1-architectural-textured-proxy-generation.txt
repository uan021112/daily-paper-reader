Textured proxy models are a fundamental representation for architectural
scenes, enabling efficient rendering while preserving visual fidelity. Existing
approaches construct such proxies through a sequential pipeline in which
geometry is first abstracted from a dense reconstruction and textures are
later projected from input images. This geometry-only formulation neglects
image evidence during proxy generation, leading to misalignment of struc-
tural features such as facades, edges, and their intersections across views.
As a result, inconsistencies are deferred to a downstream correction stage
during texturing, where the underlying geometric structure can no longer
be reliably recovered. In this work, we introduce an image-guided proxy
formulation in which proxy generation is jointly constrained by both the
dense reconstruction and the input aerial images. By incorporating image
derived structural cues directly into the construction process, the proxy
is optimized to align with observed features across views from the outset,

Introduction
High-quality 3D reconstruction from images has become widely
accessible, producing dense geometric models with detailed appear-
ance [Musialski et al. 2013; Sinha et al. 2008]. However, such models
remain impractical for downstream applications due to their com-
plexity. Proxy representations address this limitation by abstract-
ing geometry into compact, structured models that enable efficient

rendering and manipulation. In architectural scenes, proxies are
typically composed of piecewise planar elements, capturing the
dominant structure of buildings while serving as the support for
texture mapping [Gao et al. 2022; Garland and Heckbert 1997; Guo
et al. 2024; Salinas et al. 2015].
Despite their utility, existing pipelines suffer from a fundamen-
tal limitation: geometry abstraction and texturing are treated as
separate stages. A proxy is first constructed from a dense model,
and textures are later projected from the input images [Xiong et al.
2025a, 2023]. This sequential design leads to inconsistencies in what
we term structural features—planar facades, edges, and their intersec-
tions that define the geometry of buildings. These features should
coincide across the proxy and all input views, yet in practice they
drift. Errors from camera estimation, geometric abstraction, and
projection accumulate, causing edges to misalign, planar regions to
fragment, and textures from different views to disagree (Fig. 2). As
a result, texturing becomes a corrective step that attempts to com-
pensate for misaligned geometry, often with limited success [Xiong
et al. 2025a, 2023]. Prior approaches either optimize textures with-
out modifying geometry, rely on manual adjustments [Sinha et al.
2008], or employ learning-based generation that may deviate from
the observed scene [Chen et al. 2025].
We replace this pipeline with a coupled formulation in which
proxy geometry is directly constrained by image-derived structural
features. Rather than constructing a proxy and then fitting textures,
we build the proxy so that structural features are already aligned
across views, prior to texturing. By enforcing this alignment at
construction time, the proxy becomes a geometrically consistent
support for all image observations, eliminating the need for down-
stream correction. To realize this formulation, we extract priors
from the input images and use them to jointly guide camera refine-
ment and proxy generation. Camera poses are optimized to align
projected geometry with image-derived normal cues, while proxy
geometry is iteratively adjusted under a guidance field that enforces
piecewise planar structure consistent with these priors. This shared
structural signal couples geometry and appearance throughout the
pipeline. Given the resulting aligned proxy and refined cameras,
texture mapping reduces to aggregating consistent observations
rather than resolving inconsistencies.
Our approach produces textured proxies with significantly im-
proved alignment of structural features, reducing artifacts such as
ghosting, edge duplication, and texture drift. We demonstrate consis-
tent gains in both geometric accuracy and visual fidelity over prior
methods that decouple geometry and texturing. More broadly, our
results suggest that enforcing structural alignment at the level of
representation, rather than repairing it afterward, is key to reliable
proxy reconstruction. Our contributions are summarized as:
• An automatic framework for generating texture-geometry-
aligned proxy models given aerial images. Our novel frame-
work can achieve not only high-fidelity textures but also
high-quality proxy geometries;
• A building-oriented camera pose refinement method leverag-
ing normal consistency between the input images and recon-
structed dense geometries;
• A planar structure segmentation benchmark targeting urban
aerial images and a fine-tuned segmentation model. We hope
the benchmark can benefit the community;
• The first proxy generation method targeting texture-geometry
alignment, from a textureless dense building reconstruction.

Related Works
Texture Remapping. Existing texture remapping methods for re-
constructed dense geometry can be classified into two categories: i)
Image-only remapping methods associate each face or vertex with
one or multiple appropriate input images, then create textures via
seam/weights optimization to avoid visible seams between adjacent
patches [Bernardini et al. 2001; Bi et al. 2017; Fu et al. 2018; Lee et al.
2020; Waechter et al. 2014; Wang et al. 2018], ii) Joint optimization
methods jointly rectify the camera poses and geometric errors, to
avoid texture misalignment caused by geometric error and camera
drifting [Fu et al. 2020; Gal et al. 2010; Lempitsky and Ivanov 2007;
Zhou and Koltun 2014].
In recent years, texturing architectural proxy models has emerged
as a topic of growing interest. Xiong et al. [2025a, 2023] generate a
texture map for each planar polygon of an architectural proxy, min-
imizing the intra-misalignment via image warping or weights opti-
mization. However, these methods focus only on texture improve-
ment, which subjectively decouples the inter-relationship between
camera pose, geometry and texture. These image-only methods still
struggle in tackling inter-misalignment and structure misalignment.
In this paper, we focus on generating a texture-geometry aligned
proxy model given aerial images. To the best of our knowledge, we
are the first textured proxy generation method that jointly optimizes
camera poses, proxy model, and texture maps.
Camera Calibration. Camera calibration methods can be cate-
gorized by optimization granularity. Pixel-based methods [Schon-
berger and Frahm 2016; Triggs et al. 1999] minimize per-pixel re-
projection errors and achieve high accuracy, but are sensitive to
textureless regions and image noise. Primitive-based methods [Hu
et al. 2024; Shu et al. 2023] exploit geometric primitives such as
feature points, lines, and planes to formulate more robust align-
ment objectives, yet their effectiveness depends on reliable prim-
itive detection and association. In large-scale urban reconstruc-
tion, building facades are dominated by weak/repetitive textures
and convex/concave structures varying with viewing angles, which
yield sparse and unreliable feature correspondences. Furthermore,
current methods minimize average error over all matched feature

Proxy Model Generation. Early methods used geometric error met-
rics or structure-aware decimation techniques [Cohen-Steiner et al.
2004; Gao et al. 2022; Garland and Heckbert 1997; Khan et al. 2020;
Yan et al. 2006]. Appearance-preserving simplification approaches
seek to approximate the rendered appearance of an existing textured
model [Bo et al. 2026; Choi et al. 2024; Cohen et al. 1998; Garland and
Heckbert 1998; Liu et al. 2025b]. Another important family follows
a detect-then-assemble paradigm, where planar primitives are first
extracted and subsequently assembled into polygonal or polyhedral
meshes through optimization [Bauchet and Lafarge 2020; Chauve
et al. 2010; Fang and Lafarge 2020; Fang et al. 2018; Kelly et al. 2017;
Nan and Wonka 2017]. Representative methods such as PolyFit [Nan
and Wonka 2017] and KSR [Bauchet and Lafarge 2020] produce com-
pact and structured models, but their performance heavily depends
on the completeness and accuracy of primitive detection. Missing
or noisy planar hypotheses often lead to structural misalignment.
More recently, BLOD [Pan et al. 2025] extends this paradigm to
multi-scale LOD generation by introducing an Inside/Outside-view
analysis and organizing reconstructed structures into a hierarchical
LOD-Tree representation. Despite their effectiveness in geometric
abstraction, these approaches largely rely on geometry-only for-
mulations and do not explicitly enforce consistency with image
observations during proxy construction.
In contrast to prior geometry-only pipelines, our method incor-
porates image-derived structural cues directly into proxy optimiza-
tion. By jointly enforcing geometric abstraction and cross-view
image consistency during proxy generation, our approach produces
texture-geometry aligned architectural proxies.
Monocular-Plane Extraction. Estimating planar structures from
images has been studied extensively. Existing monocular plane ex-
traction methods have achieved notable progress in recovering local
planar regions from single images. CNN-based methods [Liu et al.
2018; Yang and Zhou 2018] formulate plane extraction as pixel-
wise prediction with a predefined set of planar hypotheses, while
instance-level approaches [Liu et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019] identify
multiple plane instances in a scene. More recent Transformer-based
and foundation-model-based methods [Deng et al. 2025; Liu et al.
2025a; Tan et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2024b,a] further improve ro-
bustness through stronger global reasoning, multiscale context, and
large-scale data. However, these methods are mainly developed
for indoor scenes or street-level imagery, where planes are typi-
cally observed as local surfaces from close-range viewpoints. In
contrast, our goal is building-plane extraction from low-altitude
UAV images, which requires globally coherent reasoning over large
facade and roof structures under oblique aerial views. The scarcity
of fine-grained aerial facade annotations further limits the direct
applicability of existing supervised plane extraction pipelines.

Overview
Given a set of RGB aerial images {I𝑘}, we aim at generating an
architectural proxy model M capturing dominant and salient planar
structures, along with high-quality texture maps U aligned with
the proxy geometry.
Our framework is illustrated in Fig. 3. Firstly, we calibrate the
images and generate a dense reconstruction. To reduce the geometry-
image misalignment, the generated camera poses are then refined
to minimize the differences between the normal priors estimated
from input photos and the normal maps rendered via a differential
renderer. Next, we construct local/global guidance of dense mesh
from planar priors estimated from the input photos via our planar
structure segmentation model, called ProxySAM. Guided by such
priors, we optimize the mesh vertices to generate a piece-wise planar
building proxy M, via balancing the geometric planarity, preser-
vation of distinct structures, and mesh quality. Finally, given the
refined camera poses and image-guided proxy, we perform texture
remapping to generate the texture maps, U.

4.2
Proxy Generation
After obtaining accurate camera poses and dense geometry, our next
goal is to transform the dense model D = (F, V) into a structured
proxy M that aligns with the images.
Previous approaches derive proxy models solely from the interme-
diate dense representation (i.e., dense mesh, point clouds) [Bauchet
and Lafarge 2020; Cohen-Steiner et al. 2004; Nan and Wonka 2017;
Pan et al. 2025]. These methods struggle to distinguish structural
planes from the noisy dense model, generating inaccurate proxy ge-
ometry that heavily deviates from the building shape (see Fig. 2). We
propose to generate proxy models by jointly leveraging the dense re-
construction and the aerial images. This new approach enforces the
alignment of structural features (planar facades, etc) across views
during proxy generation, leading to more accurate and consistent
structural features.
Our proxy generation method consists mainly of three stages.
Firstly, we extract 2D planar priors and then compute 3D aerial
planar priors accordingly (Sec. 4.2.1). Next, we compute local and
global normal guidance based on planar priors (Sec. 4.2.2). The mesh

4.2.1
Planar Priors Extraction. Conventional geometry-only meth-
ods are sensitive to local noise, whereas the rich structural cues in
the images can compensate for this limitation. Hence, we extract
2D planar structure from images as priors, then map them onto D
to generate 3D planar priors.
2D Planar Priors. Existing planar structure segmentation methods
and benchmarks target indoor scenes[Deng et al. 2025; Liu et al.
2018; Zhang et al. 2024a], or street view images[Li et al. 2022; Zhou
et al. 2020], which failed in aerial images of buildings. Successful
cases demonstrate the strong semantic understanding ability of Mod-
ern fundamental models given sufficient and high-quality training
data [Carion et al. 2025]. Hence, we constructed PlaneSet, a building
planar structure benchmark leveraging the Tokyo Dataset [Ministry
of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT), Japan 2023]
(covering approximately 3.62 km2). We render approximately 1000
aerial views of the Tokyo Dataset using the provided plain proxies.
Next, we apply the region-growing algorithm [Rabbani et al. 2006]
to aggregate faces with similar face normals into the same planar
structure. After that, we employ Stable Diffusion 3.5 [Esser et al.
2024] conditioned on the rendered plain geometry views via dual
ControlNets (Depth and Canny) [Zhang et al. 2023], to yield realistic
rendering. Thus, each view has a planar structure mask (see Fig. 6).
We then fine-tuned SAM 3 [Carion et al. 2025] using PlaneSet via
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [KMUTT 2025], to obtain a variant
called ProxySAM (see supplementary material for details).
Formally, given an aerial image I𝑘and a text prompt “facade
plane", ProxySAM predicts corresponding masks of all the distinct
planar structures (see Fig. 6). These masks remain fixed in the sub-
sequent stages.

4.3
Texture Remapping
Given the refined camera poses and the optimized proxy mesh
P = {𝑝}, we adopt the plane-based texture remapping method,
DiffTex [Xiong et al. 2025a], to generate a texture map for each
proxy polygon 𝑝. With our framework, the calibrated photos and
proxy model are aligned within the same coordinate system. This
allows users to generate a high-quality textured building proxy in
a single workflow, without manual alignment between different
coordinate systems (such as BLOD [Pan et al. 2025]).
5
Experimental Results
Implementation. We calibrate the input aerial images with the
commercial software, called ContextCapture (CC). The target build-
ing is reconstructed as a mesh manifold D (no self-intersection,
etc.). Our camera refinement is implemented with Nvdiffrast [Laine
et al. 2020], and proxy generation is implemented with Nvdiffrast
and CGAL. All the presented experimental results are obtained on a
desktop computer equipped with an Intel i9-14900k processor with
3.0 GHz, 128 GB RAM and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.
Dataset. We evaluate the quality of textured proxies using syn-
thetic models and real-world buildings. We construct a synthetic
dataset, containing six models with various layouts and complex-
ity [Xiong et al. 2025b]. The real-world buildings come from public
sources [Xiong et al. 2023].
5.1
Stage Results
In this section, we provide the stage results of the Hall example in
Fig. 10. Our input is a set of aerial RGB images. We first obtain the
initial poses (visualized as a sphere and a cylinder) of aerial images
with ContexCapture, reconstruct a dense model, and estimate nor-
mal maps by MoGe. We then refine the initial camera pose through
translation and rotation. In Fig. 10, we visualize the refinement re-
spectively: for translation, the camera spheres are colored by the
magnitude of the translation update, while the viewing-direction
cylinders are shown in blue; for rotation, the cylinders are colored by
the relative rotation angle in degrees between the initial and refined
poses, and the camera spheres are shown in blue. Each color map
spans the range from zero to the maximum update over all camera
poses. Next, the aerial images are segmented by our ProxySAM to
obtain 2D planar segmentation. The 3D planar segmentation is then
obtained by lifting the 2D predictions through multi-view voting.
Finally, we generate a proxy using our image-guided method and
obtain the textured proxy based on refined camera parameters.

5.2
Camera Refinement
Firstly, we evaluate the textured proxy models generated by PolyFit,
KSR and our method, given input aerial images with the initial cam-
era poses and refined camera poses of a real scene called Hisense.
As shown in Fig. 11, the textured proxy given initial poses exhibits
distinct texture misalignment. After refinement, such misalignment
is substantially reduced for the textured proxy of all methods, vali-
dating the effectiveness of our camera refinement.
For the quantitative evaluation of the textured proxy, we adopt
the evaluation scheme in [Waechter et al. 2014] to compare ren-
dered images of the reconstructed scenes against the corresponding
real images following a specific view from the input cameras. The
statistics are provided in Table 1, across all three proxy generation
methods, camera pose refinement consistently achieves superior
SSIM and LPIPS scores compared to the initial camera baselines. See
supplementary material for more comparisons.
5.3
Proxy Generation
Next, we estimate the effect of local and global guidance in mesh
optimization, as shown in Fig. 9. If we construct the guidance field
with only local guidance, the result is overall smooth while pre-
serving sharp edges. However, the local guidance considers only
local faces, which cannot distinguish planar structures. As a result,
it blends small-scale planar structures and fails to encourage large-
scale planarity (second column, Fig. 9). If we construct the guidance
field with only global guidance, dominant planar structures and lo-
cal structures are preserved, yet small-scale noise is retained (third
column, Fig. 9). After incorporating local and global guidance into
the guidance field, the generated mesh model is structurally faithful
and piecewise-planar (fourth column, Fig. 9).
5.4
Comparison of Proxy Model
We compare our method on two challenging synthetic buildings
against three proxy generation approaches: VSA [Cohen-Steiner
et al. 2004] and BLOD [Pan et al. 2025], which take a dense triangle
mesh as input; PolyFit [Nan and Wonka 2017], which takes a point
cloud as input. For a fair comparison, we tune the parameters of
each method to obtain proxies with similar vertex counts. Due to
the nature of different algorithms, we cannot guarantee an identical
number of vertices.
Following Smith et al. [2018] and Xiong et al. [2025b], we compute
Error, which quantifies how closely P approximates the ground-
truth model. The results are visualized in Fig. 12. VSA produces
compact approximations through variational surface clustering;
however, its region boundaries often exhibit noticeable zigzag arti-
facts. PolyFit and BLOD recover more regular planar surfaces, but as
geometry-only approaches, they rely solely on geometric evidence
to determine the planar decomposition. Consequently, stepped or
recessed structures with weak geometric cues may be oversimpli-
fied, with reconstructed planes spanning across distinct structural
levels. Our method achieves the lowest Error among all methods,
demonstrating the effectiveness of our image-guided method in
preserving faithful structural details.
5.5
Comparison of Textured Proxy
Serving as geometric support, an accurate proxy model consistent
with the input images is critical for the generation of coherent
texture mapping. Finally, we compare our method with five proxy
generation approaches on the textured proxy models: QEM [Garland
and Heckbert 1997], VSA and BLOD, which take a dense triangle
mesh as input; KSR [Bauchet and Lafarge 2020] and PolyFit, which
take a point cloud as input. Here, we aim at comparing the visual
quality of textured proxies. Instead of simply encouraging similar
face/vertex count, for a fair comparison given such noisy dense
models as input, we tune the parameters of each method to bal-
ance good visual quality with a small face count. The generated
proxies are textured following the identical pipeline given the input
aerial images. Statistics are provided in Table 2. See supplementary
material for comparison based on similar face/vertex count.
Qualitative Evaluation. The qualitative results are visualized in
Fig. 13. The reconstructed dense geometry and vertex colors (as-
signed by CC) are provided as the reference for ground truth struc-
ture and colors. The QEM progressively simplifies the mesh geome-
try without considering architectural regularity or planar coherence.
The generated models exhibit zigzag planar boundaries and irregu-
lar structures in all examples. Meanwhile, VSA approximates the
input surface through variational clustering and produces piecewise-
smooth regions, but it does not explicitly enforce architectural pla-
narity, leading to locally uneven surfaces on building structures.
In contrast, PolyFit, KSR, and BLOD approximate the dense model
using planar structures, generating models consisting of smooth
surfaces and sharp edges. However, without considering image evi-
dence, they generate fragmented planar patches or spurious planes
that span multiple distinct structural surfaces. These geometry-only
proxy generation methods fail to accurately preserve structural de-
tails that align with the ground truth shape (first, fourth, sixth and
twelfth rows in Fig. 13). Among all the results, our image-guided
proxies achieve the best balance between simplicity and structural
accuracy.

Quantitative Evaluation. Following Smith et al. [2018] and Xiong
et al. [2025b], we compute two metrics to assess the quality of the
reconstructed geometry P: i) Error, which quantifies how closely
P approximates the dense model D; and ii) Completeness, which
measures the coverage of P relative to D. These metrics are com-
puted for all mesh vertices. We further analyze the distributions
of these values and report the percentage of vertices with values
smaller than 95% in Table 2. QEM and our method achieve the best
or second-best Completeness in all scenes. While VSA, PolyFit and
BLOD produce a compact proxy, they deliver noticeably higher Er-
ror and lower Completeness. KSR achieves reasonable Error and
Completeness, but with a comparable or larger number of faces.
Following Shen et al. [2023] and Chen et al. [2022], we use Edge
Chamfer Distance (ECD) and Edge F1-score (EF1) to evaluate the
preservation of sharp features. In most cases, our proxies achieve
the best ECD and EF1 scores, demonstrating superior preservation
of sharp geometric features, such as facade boundaries.
For the evaluation of textured proxies, we compare the rendered
images of the reconstructed scenes against the corresponding real
images following a specific view from the input cameras [Waechter
et al. 2014]. The comparison results are reported in Table 2. The
rendered views of our textured models achieve the best SSIM and
LPIPS scores all the time.
6
Conclusion
Current textured proxy generation methods treat geometry process-
ing and texturing as independent steps, which struggle with texture
and geometry misalignment. In this paper, we propose: i) a frame-
work for generating texture-geometry aligned proxy models given
aerial images; ii) A planar structure segmentation benchmark, Plane-
Set, targeting urban aerial images, and a fine-tuned segmentation
model, ProxySAM. We hope the benchmark can benefit the commu-
nity; iii) A building-oriented camera pose refinement method lever-
aging monocular normal consistency; iv) The first image-guided
proxy generation method, targeting texture-geometry alignment.
With our novel framework, we can achieve not only high-fidelity
textures but also high-quality geometries.
Limitations and Future Work. First, our algorithm will preserve
unrecognized objects, such as foliage. We plan to incorporate more
semantic information to allow the removal of user-specific objects.
Second, we regard the creation of hierarchical planar structures that
enable the generation of LODs as future work. Moreover, unlike
performing sequential optimization on geometry and images in our
proposed framework, a challenging direction is to jointly optimize
the geometry and images for the generation of textured proxy in a
single step.
