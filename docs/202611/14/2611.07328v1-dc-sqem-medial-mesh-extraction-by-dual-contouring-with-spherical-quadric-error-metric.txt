The medial axis, as a compact representation, describes not only the boundary
but also the interior of the shape, and has been widely used in analyzing and
simplifying shapes. However, approximating feature-preserving medial axis
from unstructured data, such as oriented point clouds, remains challenging
due to the lack of explicit connectivity and the presence of noise in the
source. We propose DC-SQEM, a dual-contouring-based framework that

directly from oriented point clouds without relying on watertight manifold
surfaces or volumetric structures. With the help of an adaptive octree, our
method fits local medial spheres for each cell based on the Spherical Quadric
Error Metric (SQEM). We address several long-standing robustness issues
in dual-contouring and mesh extraction, including out-of-cell solutions,
unstable feature classification, and redundant topological connections. To

INTRODUCTION
The medial axis was first introduced by Blum [Blum 1967] as a
skeleton-like representation of shape defined by the centers of inte-
rior maximal balls. Unlike purely boundary-based descriptions, the
medial axis simultaneously encodes local thickness, global topol-
ogy, and salient geometric features. Consequently, it has become
a fundamental tool for tasks such as shape editing [Tam and Hei-
drich 2003], recognition [Hu et al. 2019], deformation [Lan et al.
2020], segmentation [Lin et al. 2020], animation [Yang et al. 2018],

and hexahedral meshing [Zhang et al. 2024]. However, computing
high-quality Medial Axis Transforms (MATs) from discrete inputs
remains a significant challenge. Its extreme sensitivity to noise and
intrinsically non-manifold structure make it difficult to balance
geometric accuracy with topological reliability, structural clarity,
and sharp-feature preservation. For CAD models, sharp edges, cor-
ners, and internal structures such as seams and junctions further
complicate medial mesh reconstruction.
Existing methods usually make trade-offs among input require-
ments, topological quality, geometric accuracy, and structural ex-
pressiveness. Traditional Voronoi-, scale-axis-, or voxel-based meth-
ods [Amenta et al. 2001; Miklos et al. 2010; Yan et al. 2018] can
approximate the medial axis under certain sampling or resolution
conditions, but they may produce redundant sheets, spikes, or topo-
logical errors in complex CAD features, thin structures, or local
tubular regions. Point-cloud-based or neural methods [Clemot and
Digne 2023; Huang et al. 2024] can process point cloud inputs, but
their outputs are often biased toward 1D skeletons or sparse medial
approximations, making it difficult to stably recover complete 2D
medial sheets. Recently, Restricted Power Diagram (RPD)-based
methods [Wang et al. 2024b, 2022, 2025] have made significant
progress in feature preservation, topology control, and structure
optimization for CAD models. However, they typically rely on high-
quality watertight surface meshes or tetrahedral volume meshes,
which limits their direct applicability to oriented point cloud inputs.

In this paper, we focus on the following problem: how to di-
rectly extract geometrically accurate, feature-preserving 3D medial
meshes with low topological error rates from oriented point clouds.
A high-quality 3D medial representation should not only contain 2D
sheets, but also reasonably represent curves, seams, and junctions.
Meanwhile, it should preserve sharp external features, avoid exces-
sive cross-sheet connections and redundant volumetric cavities, and
remain stable under different sampling densities and complex local
geometries.
To this end, we propose DC-SQEM, a framework for extracting
medial meshes directly from oriented point clouds. The key obser-
vation behind our method is that the medial axis is the singular
structure of the distance field induced by the surface. Away from
the medial axis, a spatial point is locally associated with a single clos-
est surface region, and the distance-field gradient varies smoothly.
Near the medial axis, multiple surface regions compete as closest
projections, and the gradient structure becomes locally ambiguous
or discontinuous. This suggests that medial-axis extraction can be
formulated in a Dual Contouring spirit: instead of detecting sign
changes of an implicit function, we build a local spherical quadratic
error metric (SQEM) [Thiery et al. 2013] from projection constraints,
use its singular-value structure to infer whether the local solution
behaves as a point, a line, or a plane, and extract representative dual
points from valid cells.
However, directly applying the standard Dual Contouring frame-
work to medial-axis extraction is not sufficient. First, a valid medial
cell does not necessarily provide a stable dual point inside the cur-
rent cell: the SQEM minimizer may lie outside the cell, and line-
or plane-type solution spaces may only intersect neighboring cells.
Second, local type classification based only on singular-value thresh-
olding is sensitive to noise and sampling density, which may lead to
an overly complex or overly restrictive local type. Third, unlike sur-
face extraction, medial-axis extraction lacks a simple zero-crossing
criterion on grid edges, so active cells and their connectivity must
be determined from local projection structure rather than from sign
changes. These differences make it necessary to combine local SQEM
fitting with additional geometric and topological mechanisms.
DC-SQEM addresses these issues with three main designs. First,
we introduce the concept of virtual nodes into the dual contouring
process. When an SQEM solution lies outside the current cell but
can be consistently represented by a neighboring real node, the
current cell can keep a virtual reference to that solution, completing
otherwise incomplete four-cell stencils and reducing local gaps. Sec-
ond, we select candidate non-empty cells using projection clustering
on the surface graph, and use the number of projection groups as
an upper-bound constraint during SQEM-based type determination.
This combines geometric evidence from surface projections with
algebraic evidence from singular values, improving the robustness
of local node classification. Third, we apply topology-aware and
geometry-guided cleanup to the initial quadrilateral medial mesh.
By detecting solid-cell complexes, preserving feature-supporting
faces, and using a medial-face error metric to guide face removal
and triangulation, the method removes redundant cross-sheet or off-
medial faces while retaining thin and geometrically faithful medial
sheets.
Extensive experiments on the ABC CAD dataset [Koch et al. 2019]
and various organic shapes demonstrate that DC-SQEM performs
comparably to state-of-the-art methods across multiple evaluation
metrics, including Medial Structural Error Rate (MSER), Topological
Error Rate (TER), and Triangle Quality (TQ), despite relying solely
on point cloud inputs. Furthermore, DC-SQEM exhibits computa-
tional efficiency while maintaining surface reconstruction quality
comparable to existing methods. These results establish our frame-
work as a practical and robust tool for high-fidelity medial surface
extraction from unstructured point clouds. The technical contribu-
tions of this paper are summarized as follows:
• We propose DC-SQEM, a medial surface extraction frame-
work that operates directly on oriented point clouds. The
method combines adaptive octree subdivision, local SQEM
fitting, Dual Contouring, and post-processing to generate
medial meshes from point-based inputs.
• We introduce virtual nodes into the dual contouring frame-
work to handle SQEM solutions that lie outside their current
cells. These virtual references complete otherwise incom-
plete four-cell stencils and reduce local connectivity gaps in
complex non-manifold medial structures.
• We propose a non-empty node determination and SQEM-
based node type update strategy. By combining surface-
graph projection clustering, singular-value analysis, and
projection-group constraints, the method improves the ro-
bustness of local node classification and reduces missing
faces or inaccurate internal feature lines caused by missed
non-empty nodes.

RELATED WORKS
In this section, we review the research most relevant to our proposed
framework and to the baseline methods used in our experiments.
We begin with a brief overview of classical medial approximations
based on Voronoi diagrams, scale-axis filtering, and voxel repre-
sentations, followed by a discussion of Restricted Power Diagram
(RPD) methods tailored for feature-preserving and topology-aware
medial surfaces on CAD meshes. Subsequently, we examine ap-
proaches that construct medial or skeletal structures directly from
point clouds or neural implicit fields. For a more comprehensive
background on the medial axis beyond the scope of this paper, we
refer interested readers to the survey by Tagliasacchi et al. [2016].
2.1
Voronoi- and Voxel-based Medial Approximations
Early 3D medial axis approximations primarily relied on Voronoi and
power diagrams. A foundational approach, Power Crust [Amenta
et al. 2001], constructs poles and polar balls from the Voronoi di-
agram of sampled surface points. By utilizing a power diagram
for both surface reconstruction and medial approximation, it pro-
vides topological and geometric convergence guarantees under strict
sampling conditions. In practice, however, when the geometry is
complex or the sampling is imperfect, the resulting medial structure
may become dense or unstable.
To address the medial axis’s inherent sensitivity to noise, the
Scale Axis Transform (SAT) [Miklos et al. 2010] introduces a scale-
dependent skeleton by scaling medial balls and filtering unstable
branches. While SAT is a seminal framework for scale-space rep-
resentations, its main goal is stable simplification rather than the
preservation of clean medial sheet structures or sharp features in
CAD models.
In volumetric settings, Voxel Cores [Yan et al. 2018] utilize bound-
ary voxels as generators to construct an internal Voronoi structure.
This method proves that the resulting core becomes increasingly
thin and homotopy-equivalent to the true medial axis as resolution
increases. While Voxel Cores provide strong theoretical guaran-
tees, they typically require voxelization or closed surface inputs,
and high-resolution results may contain dense redundant sheets or
spikes. Therefore, VC is more suitable when a reliable voxelization
is available, but less direct for our setting of extracting compact,
feature-preserving medial meshes from oriented point clouds.
2.2
RPD-based Feature-preserving Medial Surfaces
For CAD models characterized by sharp edges and corners, methods
based on Restricted Power Diagrams (RPDs) have significantly im-
proved feature preservation and medial mesh quality. MATFP [Wang
et al. 2022] utilizes a surface RPD to classify medial spheres and de-
fines an energy functional to jointly manage external sharp features
and internal medial structures. It can preserve medial features and
reconstruct sharp CAD features well, but it does not provide a global
topological guarantee. MATTopo [Wang et al. 2024b] extends this
direction by introducing a volumetric RPD and local Euler charac-
teristic checks to pursue homotopy equivalence between the medial
mesh and the input shape. More recently, MATStruct [Wang et al.
2025] integrates structure-aware optimization and SQEM-based pro-
jections to further enhance sheet clarity, seam/junction consistency,
and triangle quality. These methods achieve strong results on high-
quality mesh inputs, but they require watertight surface meshes or
tetrahedral volume meshes and involve complex RPD computations.
Beyond the RPD family, energy- and flow-based methods min-
imize distance- or curvature-related energies to extract stable in-
ternal skeletons [Chu et al. 2019; Li et al. 2015; Pan et al. 2019].
However, these are generally optimized for smooth geometries and
lack the explicit modeling required for sharp CAD features and strict
medial feature preservation.
While RPD-based methods are the current state-of-the-art for
manifold mesh or volumetric mesh inputs, their reliance on high-
quality watertight surfaces or tetrahedral meshes limits their direct
applicability to oriented point clouds.
2.3
Medial and Skeletal Extraction from Point Clouds
When only point clouds are available, one research trajectory fo-
cuses on extracting skeletal structures directly from these repre-
sentations. Early methods like the L1-medial skeleton [Huang et al.
2013] treat skeletonization as a clustering and geometric optimiza-
tion problem. By minimizing the 𝐿1 distance between points and 1D
prototypes, these methods robustly recover curve skeletons from
noisy data. Such methods are mainly designed for compact curve
skeleton extraction, which is different from our goal of constructing
explicit two-dimensional medial sheets.
VMAS [Huang et al. 2024] approaches the problem via variational
clustering, alternating between Gauss-Newton updates for sphere
positions and point-to-sphere assignments. VMAS provides inter-
active control over the number and distribution of medial spheres,
making it suitable for controllable skeleton or medial sphere sam-
pling. In contrast, our method focuses on constructing a sheet-based
medial mesh with explicit cell connectivity from oriented point
clouds.
Recently, deep learning methods have gained traction. P2MAT-
Net [Yang et al. 2020] learns the pattern of sparse point clouds and
transform them into spheres approximating MAT. Point2Skeleton [Lin
et al. 2021] and Point2MM [Ge et al. 2023] use neural encoders to
predict skeletal points or medial mesh elements and infer their con-
nectivity through graph networks. Neural Skeleton [Clemot and
Digne 2023] combines implicit neural representations with cover-
age formulations to sample skeletal points inside the shape. These
methods provide flexible point-cloud or neural-field skeletonization
pipelines. Our method instead follows a purely geometric route and
explicitly constructs medial sheets through the octree dual complex.
A related direction embeds medial data into neural implicit fields.
Deep Medial Fields [Rebain et al. 2021] and Q-MDF [Kong et al. 2026]
learn quasi-medial distance fields to recover medial membranes from
isosurfaces. Notably, Q-MDF employs a double-covering technique

Finally, the Coverage Axis family [Dou et al. 2022; Wang et al.

projection clustering and SQEM fitting. It operates directly on ori-

octree, and generates sheet-like medial meshes through Dual Con-
touring and topology-aware cleanup.
2.4
Property Comparison
The methods discussed above have different input assumptions and
output goals. Some methods primarily target compact curve skele-
tons, some focus on voxel- or Voronoi-based medial approximations,
while RPD-based methods are designed for feature-preserving me-
dial meshes on high-quality mesh inputs. Our DC-SQEM follows
a different setting: it directly takes oriented point clouds as input,
without relying on watertight surface meshes, tetrahedral meshes,
or RPD computation, while aiming to generate explicit medial sheet
structures rather than only one-dimensional skeletons. Since our
experiments compare representative methods from all these cate-
gories, we further summarize them using a unified set of properties
in Tab. 1.
We adopt the properties defined in Tab. 1 of MATTopo [Wang
et al. 2024b], namely homotopy preservation, medial feature preser-
vation, thinness, centeredness, reconstructibility, and scalability,
while introducing the input type as an additional criterion. We do
not repeat their detailed definitions here, but briefly explain the
rationale behind the entries. The theoretical properties of PC and
SAT generally depend on sampling conditions or scale parame-
ters, so several properties are only conditionally satisfied. VC has
stronger theoretical support for homotopy equivalence under suf-
ficiently high voxel resolution, but its scalability is limited by the
required voxel resolution. Neural Skeleton and VMAS are flexible
point-based methods suitable for skeletonization or medial sphere
sampling, but their goal is not to explicitly construct sheet-manifold
medial meshes; therefore, they do not fully satisfy properties such
as thinness and medial feature preservation in the sense of medial
mesh computation. MATFP preserves medial features and has good
reconstructibility on surface mesh inputs, but it does not provide
a guarantee on homotopy preservation. MATTopo and MATStruct
further improve topology and structural quality using volumetric
RPDs, but require volumetric mesh inputs and introduce substan-
tial computational cost. DC-SQEM does not provide a guarantee
on homotopy preservation. However, through non-empty node de-
termination, local SQEM solving, virtual nodes, Dual Contouring,
and topology-aware cleanup, it preserves medial features, thinness,
centeredness, and reconstructibility in practice. Since it directly
operates on oriented point clouds and avoids voxelization, tetrahe-
dralization, and RPD computation, it also provides good scalability.

point cloud. For CAD-like inputs where sharp-feature preservation
is desired, we optionally assume a feature label for each input point,
indicating whether the point lies on a sharp feature; such labels
can be obtained by preprocessing methods such as RFEPS [Xu et al.
2022].

Fig. 2 illustrates the overall pipeline of DC-SQEM and highlights
its main technical components. Given an oriented point cloud with
optional feature-point labels (Fig. 2 (a)), we first construct an octree
over the shape domain and retain candidate cells for medial recon-
struction (Fig. 2 (b)). On these cells, our SQEM formulation serves as
the local fitting primitive: it estimates a medial sphere for each cell,
classifies the local solution type, and extracts a representative me-
dial vertex for dual construction (Fig. 2 (c)). To handle cases where
the SQEM solution does not yield a point within the current cell, we
introduce virtual nodes that allow adjacent-cell-supported points to
preserve dual connectivity. To improve robustness against unstable
local type classification, we further combine projection clustering
constraints with a feature-downgrading strategy, which prevents
overly aggressive point/line/plane assignments from producing in-
valid local structures. The resulting real and virtual nodes are then
connected through the grid’s dual structure to form an initial quadri-
lateral medial mesh (Fig. 2 (d)). Finally, because the initial dual mesh
may contain redundant connections, solid-cell cavities, and cross-
sheet bridges, we apply a geometry-driven, topology-aware cleanup
procedure to remove these artifacts while preserving the intended
medial topology. The cleaned quadrilateral mesh is then triangu-
lated, yielding an explicit medial triangle mesh that preserves sharp
features and maintains the non-manifold structure of the medial
axis (Fig. 2 (e)).
In this section, we will introduce the SQEM formulation [Thiery
et al. 2013] and the virtual-node extension for handling out-of-cell
solutions (Sec. 3.2); the candidate-cell selection stage, which avoids
unnecessary SQEM solves (Sec. 3.3); the type-downgrading strategy
for improving the robustness of local SQEM classification (Sec. 3.4);
and the topology- and geometry-aware cleanup procedure that
converts the initial dual mesh into a clean medial mesh (Sec. 3.5).
3.1
Dual Contouring
Dual Contouring [Ju et al. 2002] is a cell-centered method for ex-
tracting an implicit surface from a spatial grid, typically an octree.
Unlike Marching Cubes, which places vertices on grid edges, Dual
Contouring constructs a dual representation of the grid cells. Each
active cell, usually identified by a sign change of the implicit func-
tion on its boundary, contributes one representative vertex. This
vertex is commonly computed by solving a local quadratic error
function (QEF) from Hermite data, such as intersection points and
normals. The final mesh is then assembled by connecting the rep-
resentative vertices of neighboring active cells around grid edges.
This one-vertex-per-cell formulation naturally supports adaptive
octrees and can better preserve sharp features through the local
QEF solve.

robustness, as described in Sec. 3.3.
The number of degenerate singular values and the corresponding
singular directions determine the type of the current cell 𝐶:
Case 1: Corner Type: no degenerate singular direction. In this case,
the SQEM’s solution is an isolated point (see Fig. 3(c)), which cor-
responds to a 𝑇4-type medial sphere, and the solution space is an
isolated point. If the center c∗lies inside the current cell 𝐶, we tenta-
tively mark 𝐶’s dual as a real node. Otherwise, if the point is inside
the face- or edge- adjacent cell (its neighbor cell), we treat 𝐶’s dual
as a candidate virtual node. If c∗does not fall inside 𝐶or any of
its neighbors, 𝐶’s dual is treated as an empty node.
Case 2: Line Type: one degenerate singular direction (𝜎3 ≈0). The
solution space is approximately constrained to a one-dimensional
line ℓ(see Fig. 3(b)), corresponding to a 𝑇3- or 𝑇∞-type sphere lying
on a medial seam or a medial curve. We obtain the singular vector
associated with 𝜎3 and take the least-squares solution c∗as a point
on the line ℓ. We intersect ℓwith the 6 faces of cell 𝐶. If they inter-
sect, we take the center of the intersected segment as a candidate
center c∗and tentatively mark 𝐶’s dual as a real node. If ℓdoes not
intersect 𝐶but intersects some of its neighboring cells, we record
the intersection centers in those neighboring cells, and treat 𝐶’s
dual as a candidate virtual node. If ℓintersects neither 𝐶nor any of
its neighbors, 𝐶’s dual is treated as an empty node.
Case 3: Plane Type: two degenerate singular directions (𝜎2, 𝜎3 ≈0).
The solution space is approximately a plane Π that corresponds
to a 𝑇2-type sphere lying on a medial sheet. We derive the plane
normal n from the associated singular vectors and enforce c∗to
lie on the plane Π (see Fig. 3(a)). We intersect Π with the current
cell 𝐶. If Π intersects 𝐶, we compute the intersected polygon, take
its centroid as a candidate center c∗, and tentatively mark 𝐶’s dual
as a real node. If Π does not intersect 𝐶, but only intersects some
of its neighboring cells, we record the intersected centers in those
neighboring cells, and mark 𝐶’s dual as a candidate virtual node. If
there is no intersection with 𝐶or any of its neighbors, 𝐶’s dual is
treated as an empty node.

3.3
Candidate-cell Selection
With the per-cell computations established, we now outline how
candidate cells are selected to avoid unnecessary SQEM evaluations.
We build an adaptive octree over the bounding box of the in-
put point cloud and increase resolution only in potential medial
regions. We recursively subdivide cells from coarse to fine: a cell
is not further subdivided if it is classified as outside the shape,
“obviously-empty”, or has already reached the maximum depth; af-
ter subdivision, only non-empty leaf cells are used for SQEM-based
medial sphere fitting.
Projection grouping. Unlike traditional criteria that rely on whether
a zero level set intersects the cell, we use clustering of local sam-
ple projections to decide whether a cell may contain the medial
axis. Given an octree cell 𝐶, we uniformly sample a set of points

3.4
Cell Type Downgrading
Now we have two sources of cell types: one coming from the singular
values of SQEM – Corner (𝑇4), Line (𝑇3 or 𝑇∞), Plane (𝑇2), and Null
(empty cell); another one coming from the projection grouping –
four clusters (𝑇4), three clusters (𝑇3), two clusters (𝑇2), and one cluster
(empty cell). In this subsection, we discuss how the conservative
grouping result mentioned in the above Sec. 3.3 could be used to
constrain the SQEM classification, thereby improving the robustness
of cell type determination.
In practice, if a cell contains four or more projection groups, then
a corner-type solution is allowed. If it contains three groups, then
we restrict the solution to a simpler type, such as edge or plane. If
only two groups, then we restrict the solution to the plane type only.
We allow the cell types initially determined by projection grouping
to be downgradable, in the order of Corner →Line →Plane, based
on the SQEM solution space.
In this way, the projection grouping serves two purposes: it avoids
unnecessary SQEM solves on those empty cells, and it prevents the
cell type computed by SQEM from being more complex than what
the projection grouping supports.
A candidate cell may pass the grouping test and still fail to accept a
dual vertex through SQEM solution (Sec. 3.2). This happens precisely
when the dual vertex lies far outside the cell. In the medial setting,
such cases are common enough that they must be treated as part of
the method rather than as numerical exceptions. We therefore place
the local SQEM computation inside an outer loop. For a non-empty
cell𝐶, we first determine the cell type under the projection grouping
in Sec. 3.3, and then compute the corresponding dual vertex through
SQEM as in Sec. 3.2.
If the cell type computed by SQEM fails on the current cell (the
cell is neither marked as a real or virtual node), we downgrade the
type in the order of Corner →Line →Plane, and repeat the dual
vertex solution with SQEM. If none of these types yields an accepted
dual vertex, the cell is finally labeled as empty.
This outer loop is a key part of the method. It makes the one-
vertex-per-cell representation reliable in the medial setting: a valid
cell is not rejected simply because its SQEM-based cell type does
not provide a legal dual vertex. At the same time, the downgrading
rule avoids forcing an overly restrictive local type when a simpler
type already explains the cell. A detailed algorithm about the type
downgrading process is provided in the Supplementary Document.

3.5
Dual Mesh Construction and Cleanup
3.5.1
Dual mesh construction. After the outer loop, each maximum-
depth cell is either active or empty. Similar to DualMesh-UDF [Zhang
et al. 2023], the mesh connection rule is designed as follows. Each
edge in the grid is shared by four adjacent cells. If all four adjacent
cells are non-empty, we generate one quad connecting the dual
points (the medial sphere centers obtained from the solution in
Sec. 3.2) of these four cells.
This step is where the above-mentioned design choices come
together. The one-vertex-per-cell representation keeps the dual
construction simple, while virtual nodes prevent valid local configu-
rations from leaving holes in the mesh merely because one cell did
not provide its own inside-cell dual vertex.
3.5.2
Topology- and geometry-aware cleanup. The initial dual mesh
captures the main medial structure, but it is still not the final result.
Because the dual mesh is built from dual vertices and local connec-
tivity, it may contain artifacts that are topologically or geometrically
inconsistent with the intended medial axis. We remove them by a
topology-aware, geometry-guided cleanup procedure.
Topology-preserving solid-cell deletion. A solid-cell complex is a
2 × 2 × 2 block of adjacent active cells. Such a block creates a volu-
metric region in the dual mesh due to the edge-based quad mesh
construction. The solid-cell complex should not be retained in the
final medial mesh. We perform the cleanup of solid-cell complexes
by removing simple pairs of (solid, face) or (face, edge), inspired by
the simple removal process of Ju et al. [2007]. We first break every
such complex by deleting at least one of its boundary faces together
with the solid cell, i.e., the (solid, face)-simple pair. After all solid-cell
complexes are broken, we continue deleting remaining pocket faces
by removing those (face, edge)-simple pairs. To monitor topology,
we use the Euler characteristic:

4.1
Baselines
We compare our method with eight state-of-the-art (SOTA) ap-
proaches: PC [Amenta et al. 2001], SAT [Miklos et al. 2010], VC [Yan
et al. 2018], Neural Skeleton (NSK) [Clemot and Digne 2023], VMAS
[Huang et al. 2024], MATFP [Wang et al. 2022], MATTopo [Wang
et al. 2024b], and MATStruct [Wang et al. 2025]. Among them, Neu-
ral Skeleton and VMAS are skeletonization methods that primarily
aim to produce sparse medial representations (typically with only a
few hundred balls), while the others are medial-axis approximation
methods.
We include VMAS in our comparison because its input is also
point clouds. However, when the target number of balls increases
to several thousands, VMAS becomes unstable: the optimization
exhibits oscillatory behavior, repeatedly inserting and deleting balls
without converging. For a fair comparison, we run VMAS on dense
surface points (each with more than 600K points) and gradually
increase the target number of spheres. If the number of spheres does
not change for 100 consecutive iterations, we terminate the opti-
mization and use this configuration as the maximal stable capacity
of VMAS for evaluation.
4.2
Evaluation Metrics
To evaluate the quality of the generated medial meshes, we adopt
four metrics: medial structural error rate (MSER) [Wang et al.
2025], topological error rate (TER) [Wang et al. 2025], self-
intersecting triangle ratio (SITR), and triangle quality (TQ) [Frey
and Borouchaki 1999]. We also use the Hausdorff distance (HD)
to assess the geometric fidelity between reconstructed surfaces and
the original inputs.
Medial Structure Error Ratio (MSER). MSER [Wang et al. 2025]
quantifies the percentage of misclassified seam and junction spheres
on medial mesh. Fundamentally, a regular medial sphere on a sheet
contacts the surface at exactly two points, while seam and junction
spheres must possess three or more distinct tangential points to
represent structural transitions. We employ a robust iterative search
strategy to recover these contact points. A detected seam or junction
sphere is considered misclassified if the number of its distinct tan-
gential points does not exceed that of its neighboring sheet spheres.
MSER is defined as the ratio of these misclassified singularities to
the total number of seam and junction spheres. We restrict this
evaluation to CAD models, where such medial structures like seam
and junctions are unambiguously defined.
Topology Error Ratio (TER). TER [Wang et al. 2025] denotes the
percentage of medial meshes that fail to preserve the ground-truth
topology. A medial mesh is considered topologically correct if and
only if both the number of connected components (i.e., the 0th
Betti number 𝑏0) and the Euler characteristic 𝜒match those of the
ground-truth. Given that there is no cavities in any of our dataset’s
3D shapes (i.e., the 2nd Betti number 𝑏2 = 0), achieving consistency
in both 𝑏0 and 𝜒inherently ensures consistency in the 1st Betti
number 𝑏1, according to the Euler-Poincaré formula 𝜒= 𝑏0 −𝑏1 +𝑏2.
TER is then defined as the fraction of computed medial meshes that
do not satisfy the topological correctness.
Triangle Quality (TQ). TQ [Frey and Borouchaki 1999] evaluates
mesh regularity by quantifying the deviation of each triangle from a
perfect equilateral one, where higher values indicate better element
quality.
Hausdorff Distance (HD). HD evaluates the reconstruction error of
the computed medial mesh, as a percentage relative to the diagonal
length of the input shape’s bounding box. Specifically, we report
two one-sided Hausdorff distances. 𝜖1 represents the distance from
the ground truth surface to the MAT-reconstructed surface, while
𝜖2 represents the distance in reverse side.
Self-Intersecting Triangle Ratio (SITR). SITR measures the per-
centage of triangles involved in at least one self-intersection with
a non-adjacent triangle. We detect all intersecting triangle pairs,
discard pairs sharing mesh vertices, and collect the unique triangle
indices appearing in the remaining pairs. SITR is defined as the
number of such self-intersecting triangles divided by the total num-
ber of triangles in the medial mesh. Lower values indicate fewer
geometric self-intersections.

method achieves a score of 0.6831, which is competitive with the
best-performing baselines. While MATStruct and VMAS obtain
slightly higher TQ scores of 0.7124 and 0.6953, respectively, VMAS
exhibits much worse structural and geometric accuracy, with an
MSER of 95.6633% and an HD of 1.9718%. This suggests that VMAS
prioritizes element regularity at the expense of accurate medial
structure and geometric fidelity. In contrast, our method maintains
high element quality while preserving both the underlying geometry
and the medial structure, providing a more balanced triangulation
suitable for downstream applications.
In terms of TER, our method achieves a low error rate of 2.15%.
This result is close to the zero TER reported by MATTopo and
MATStruct, while VC obtains a TER of 3.0928% on this dataset.
Other baselines, including PC, SAT, NSK, and VMAS, show higher
topological error rates under this evaluation. These results suggest
that our method performs well in this empirical topological evalua-
tion, although unlike theoretically guaranteed methods, our current
framework does not provide a formal topological guarantee.
Regarding self-intersections, our method achieves an SITR of
4.0711%. This value is substantially lower than SAT-0.03 (20.3429%),
SAT-0.04 (16.8332%), and VMAS (10.4908%), although it is higher
than those of the Voronoi/RPD-based methods and Neural Skeleton.
PC, MATFP, and MATTopo contain no self-intersecting triangles in
this evaluation, while VC and MATStruct achieve near-zero SITRs of
0.0009% and 0.0064%, respectively. These low values are consistent
with the fact that the connectivity of Voronoi/RPD-based methods
is largely inherited from globally embedded Voronoi/Delaunay-type
complexes. Neural Skeleton also achieves an SITR of 0: after select-
ing skeletal points through its coverage formulation, it constructs a
regular triangulation of the selected skeletal points together with
the surface samples and extracts the skeletal edges and triangles as
a subcomplex of this triangulation. In contrast, SAT simplifies the
medial structure through multiplicative scaling of medial balls and
does not explicitly impose a non-self-intersection constraint on the
resulting discrete medial mesh, which is consistent with its higher
SITR in our evaluation.
For our method, the observed self-intersections mainly arise from
the virtual-node mechanism used to recover local medial connec-
tivity when an SQEM solution lies outside its current octree cell. If
the reconstructed position lies in the current cell or a face-adjacent
cell, the corresponding local Dual Contouring configuration pre-
serves the usual face-based neighborhood relation, and we do not
observe this type of self-intersection in our experiments. However,
the virtual-node construction additionally allows a node to adopt
an SQEM solution located in an edge-adjacent cell in order to re-
cover difficult local configurations that would otherwise produce
missing connections. This more permissive relocation weakens the
strict geometric locality of the standard Dual Contouring construc-
tion: in some configurations, dual faces generated from neighboring
cells can fold across one another, producing local self-intersections.
Therefore, the current virtual-node strategy introduces a practical
trade-off between recovering local medial connectivity and preserv-
ing a fully intersection-free embedding. Nevertheless, the measured
SITR remains substantially lower than those of SAT and VMAS.
Overall, existing methods often optimize for a specific aspect,
such as topology, element regularity, structural correctness, or geo-
metric fidelity, while underperforming in others. Our method pro-
vides a balanced solution: it achieves the best Hausdorff distance,
competitive MSER and triangle quality, and near-zero topological
error. The SITR evaluation further shows that the method maintains
a relatively low level of geometric self-intersection despite using
the more permissive virtual-node construction to recover difficult
local medial connections. These results demonstrate that our ap-
proach can generate clean and accurate medial meshes with robust
performance across structural, geometric, and topological criteria.

4.6
Implementation Details and Parameter Sensitivity
KNN Graph Construction. To cluster projected points and deter-
mine connectivity in the medial axis extraction, we build a 𝐾-nearest
neighbor (KNN) graph. We evaluate 𝐾∈{26, 64, 128} to analyze
parameter sensitivity. As shown in Fig. 11, even with a large value
such as 𝐾= 128, the resulting medial sheets remain correct, indi-
cating that our method is not sensitive to this parameter within a
reasonable range. The main reason is that the KNN graph is not
used directly: during graph construction, we remove edges between
two non-feature points when their normal angle exceeds 45◦, which
corresponds to the complement of our dihedral-angle threshold for
feature-edge detection. For CAD models, the most common prob-
lematic KNN edges are those that cross a sharp feature and connect
two surface regions that should be disconnected. Our normal-based
filtering effectively suppresses such cross-feature edges, making
the subsequent projection grouping stable even when a larger 𝐾
introduces many additional candidate edges.

nections in our experiments, the resulting graph still provides only
a discrete approximation of the underlying surface connectivity and

We further examine the relation between the local voxel size ℎ

This difference is reflected by the corresponding octree-node
configurations. For 𝑤= 2ℎ, the non-empty nodes associated with
the two nearby layers remain continuously adjacent across the gap,
resulting in an erroneous connection. For the larger gaps tested
here, empty nodes appear between the two groups of non-empty
nodes and separate the corresponding medial structures. These
observations indicate that the minimum LFS of the shape is not
by itself a resolution criterion for DC-SQEM; rather, for this type
of failure, the more relevant scale is the LFS associated with the
separation between distinct nearby surface sheets.
Robustness to Noise. We evaluate the impact of noise on medial
axis computation. Starting from the same CAD model, we generate
three oriented point clouds with labeled feature points and add

shown in Fig. 15, higher noise levels lead to increasingly irregu-

Hausdorff Distance increases accordingly; the topology of the com-

increased to 4%.

4.8
Runtime Comparison
To evaluate the efficiency of our method, we compare the medial
axis extraction runtime on a selection of ABC models with existing
baseline methods. Tab. 5 reports the single-run processing time, in
seconds, for several representative models. Our method is evalu-
ated using the optimized C++ implementation with an octree of
maximum depth 7, a default input size of 𝑛samples = 500,000 ori-
ented points, and KNN parameter 𝐾= 26. All runtime experiments
are conducted on Ubuntu to ensure consistent multithreading be-
havior and reproducible performance under OpenMP. We report
MATTopo as a representative RPD-based method to indicate the
typical runtime scale of this family of approaches.
As shown in Tab. 5, the optimized implementation of our method
achieves competitive runtime across the tested models. Compared
with MATTopo and NSK, our method is substantially faster on all
listed examples. It is also generally faster than the Power Crust
variants, especially on models where dense point samples lead to
expensive reconstruction in PC_160 and PC_200. Compared with
SAT, VC, and VMAS, our method is not uniformly the fastest, since
these methods may use simpler or more aggressively simplified
extraction procedures. However, our runtime remains in the same
order of magnitude on most models while producing clean medial
meshes with explicit sheet structure.
The remaining computational cost mainly comes from two stages.
The first is the construction and processing of the KNN graph over
approximately 500,000 oriented samples with 𝐾= 26, which is
required for robust local grouping and feature-aware medial clas-
sification. The second is the solid-cell deletion stage, where each
deletion requires updating edge–face adjacency tables and face-
priority queues. These updates are partly sequential and therefore
less amenable to parallelization. Nevertheless, after porting the ma-
jor components to C++, the total runtime is reduced to a few seconds
to about twenty seconds on most tested models, making the method
practical for large-scale CAD inputs.
4.9
Limitations
Although our method achieves high-quality medial axis reconstruc-
tion on oriented point clouds sampled from CAD-style or organic
shapes, it still has several limitations:
Point Cloud Sampling. We extract oriented point clouds from
input meshes by detecting sharp features and uniformly sampling
surface points. We perform a convergence analysis using 50,000,
15,000, and 7000 points to investigate the influence of sampling
density on medial mesh extraction. Fig. 21 shows the resulting
medial meshes: lower sampling densities produce incomplete sheets
and locally inaccurate features, while higher densities stabilize the
extraction.

Our method may produce local breaks in connectivity when pro-
cessing tubular regions. This occurs in both certain organic shapes
and some CAD models. T∞nodes are used to connect 1D medial
curves. Both rely on heuristic strategies, which can occasionally
cause incorrect connections or missed links. Note that the detection
of T∞nodes is not robust; the detection method is detailed in the
Supplementary Document. Fig. 22 illustrates a typical failure case:
the red-marked medial segment is not properly connected via T∞,
resulting in a local break of medial mesh.
Parameter sensitivity. The method is sensitive to octree resolution
and sampling density. With too few sample points, some critical
nodes may contain only a single projection direction due to sparse
sampling, causing them to be misclassified as empty. If the octree
resolution is too low, medial structures with holes may fail to recon-
struct (Fig. 14), affecting overall topological quality.

Conclusion and Future Work
We have presented DC-SQEM, a framework for extracting medial
meshes directly from oriented point clouds. By combining SQEM-
based sphere fitting, virtual nodes, cell type downgrading, and
topology- and geometry-aware thinning, DC-SQEM produces me-
dial meshes that preserve sheets, curves, junctions, and seam struc-
tures with high geometric fidelity and low topological error rates.
Extensive evaluations on CAD and organic shapes show that
