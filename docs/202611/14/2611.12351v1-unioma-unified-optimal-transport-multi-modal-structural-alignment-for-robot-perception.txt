UniOMA: Unified Optimal-Transport Multi-Modal Structural
Alignment for Robot Perception
Xinrui Zu, Mark Hoogendoorn, Kevin Sebastian Luck, Shujian Yu
Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, the Netherlands
Abstract
Achieving generalizable and well-aligned multimodal representations remains a core challenge
in robot learning. While recent approaches have attempted to align modalities by modeling
conditional or higher-order statistical dependencies, they do not explicitly consider the structural
coherence across modalities. In this work, we propose a novel multimodal alignment method
that augments existing contrastive losses with a geometry-aware Gromov–Wasserstein (GW)
distance-based regularization. To this end, we encode intra-modal geometry for each modality
and reduce cross-modal structural discrepancies through a dynamically learned GW barycenter,
thereby encouraging structural consistency in addition to instance-level contrastive alignment.
We apply this optimal-transport-based alignment strategy to robot perception tasks involving
underexplored modalities such as force and tactile signals, where modality data often exhibit
varying sample densities. Extensive experiments on five robotic benchmarks show that our method
improves structural alignment and downstream performance over state-of-the-art multimodal
contrastive baselines across a wide range of downstream tasks, including robot and environment
state prediction. Moreover, our GW-based regularization can be seamlessly integrated into most
InfoNCE-like objectives.
Key Words— Robot perception, Multimodal alignment, Gromov–Wasserstein distance.
I
Introduction
The integration of information from diverse sources or modalities has received increasing attention
across a wide range of AI applications, including image/video/text generation [1,2], healthcare [3],
autonomous systems [4], scientific discovery [5], and robotics, particularly robot learning and
perception [6,7]. Recent advances in contrastive self-supervised learning (CSSL) [8–11], particularly
those leveraging InfoNCE losses [12], have shown strong performance in aligning heterogeneous
modalities into a shared representation space [13]. Such alignment has enabled zero-shot cross-modal
retrieval, transfer, generation, and completion [13–17], while also providing a foundation for learning
multimodal representations in robotic systems. In this setting, heterogeneous observations, such
as visual, tactile, and proprioceptive modalities, are aligned for tasks like manipulation, imitation
learning, reward learning, and perception [6, 18, 19]. By maximizing agreement between paired
modalities of the same instance while minimizing similarity between distinct instances, CSSL
encourages the learning of invariant and semantically meaningful features.
Despite their empirical success, existing CSSL methods fundamentally cast multimodal alignment
as a binary classification problem [20]: paired samples are encouraged to be close, while unpaired
samples are pushed apart, without explicitly modeling the continuous intra-modal distance geometry.
As a result, multimodal representations may appear statistically aligned at the population level, yet

Crucially, the structural alignment gap is not an implementation artifact but a theoretical
limitation of InfoNCE-style objectives. InfoNCE and related contrastive losses can be interpreted as
variational lower bounds on mutual information [22,23]. However, mutual information is invariant
under certain invertible reparameterizations of the variables [24,25], and is therefore underdetermined
with respect to modality-specific geometry. Consequently, while effective at capturing population-
level statistical dependence, they do not by themselves constrain the intra-modal geometry to be
consistent across modalities.
Fig. 1 illustrates this gap: the cross-modal InfoNCE similarities remain compatible, while the
intra-modal kernels Kx and Ky (Sec. III-C) differ. Thus, instance-level correspondence is preserved,
yet the intra-modal relational geometry is inconsistent, which InfoNCE-like objectives alone do not
penalize.
This structural alignment gap is particularly critical in robotics, where multimodal sensor
streams are neither i.i.d.
nor structureless: trajectories form subclusters [26], contact events
induce discontinuities [27], and proprioceptive signals follow physical constraints [28,29]. These
robotic settings motivate us to test whether explicit relational alignment complements instance-level
correspondence. To address the identified structural alignment gap, we introduce a Unified Optimal-
transport Multi-modal structural Alignment (UniOMA) framework that supports alignment
across three or more modalities. UniOMA augments contrastive learning with a structure-aware
regularization based on Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) distances and barycenters [30, 31]. In our
formulation, observations from each modality are represented as a metric space through intra-modal
similarity matrices. A dynamic GW barycenter is then computed as the structural consensus
across modalities, and each modality is softly aligned to this consensus by minimizing weighted
GW distances. To our knowledge, UniOMA is the first method to employ a GW barycenter as a
structural-consensus regularizer for contrastive representation learning. The modality weights are
optimized end-to-end alongside encoder parameters, enabling adaptive contributions of different
modalities to the structural consensus. By aligning each modality to a shared structural consensus
rather than every other modality, this barycentric formulation reduces structural couplings from
O(M2) to O(M), where M is the number of modalities, and thus is well suited to settings with

three or more modalities.
In summary, our main contributions are:
(C1) We identify and formalize the structural alignment gap, demonstrating why InfoNCE-style
objectives do not explicitly enforce intra-modal structural consistency, supported by synthetic
analysis.
(C2) We propose UniOMA, a structure-aware multimodal alignment framework based on Gromov–
Wasserstein distance and barycenters, which is well suited to settings with 3+ modalities.
(C3) We conduct extensive evaluations on five robotic benchmarks (including a new real-world
robot-centric dataset with a 6-DoF manipulator), spanning heterogeneous modalities, 3+
modality settings, and complementary downstream tasks, showing that UniOMA improves
downstream performance in most evaluated settings and achieves better structural alignment.

These methods make progress on multimodal alignment but still remain limited to instance-level
dependencies, overlooking intra-modal structure. More advanced objectives (e.g., CoMM [48] and
TRIANGLE [49]) strengthen cross-modal matching, yet still do not explicitly align intra-modal
relational geometry. [50] introduces a standard optimal transport (OT) regularizer for cross-modal
consistency; however, the matching is performed at the distributional level of the embeddings z,
thereby ignoring intra-modal structure in the observation space. In contrast, we align structures by
encoding each modality by its intra-modal similarity (kernel) and enforcing a GW-based consensus
in the input space X, preserving centroid geometry rather than embedding-level OT matching.
B
Multimodal Representation Learning in Robotics
Robotics is inherently multimodal: vision, force–torque, tactile sensing, and proprioception provide
complementary views of the robot–environment system. While multimodal representation learning
has been extensively studied in vision–language settings, its exploration in robotics remains limited.
Existing work, including the recent Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models, has primarily focused
on modality fusion or transfer [51–56].
By comparison, alignment of robotic perception modalities into a shared representation space
remains underexplored. Recent efforts [57,58] demonstrate cross-modal retrieval and perception,
while [26,59] show that cross-modal or cross-temporal alignment can yield transferable representations.
These developments highlight that robotic perception data is inherently structured, which is shaped
by trajectories, contact events, and physical constraints, motivating alignment methods that preserve
intra-modal geometry across modalities rather than relying solely on feature fusion. Importantly,
our work addresses a complementary setting to recent VLA models. Rather than focusing on
language-conditioned policy learning, we study multimodal alignment for robotic perception using
physically grounded sensor modalities, such as force, tactile sensing, and proprioception, with the
goal of learning structure-preserving multimodal representations.
III
UniOMA: Unified Optimal-Transport Multi-Modal Structural
Alignment
Our proposed UniOMA aligns three or more heterogeneous modalities by combining instance-level
statistical correspondence with structure-level geometric consistency.
As illustrated in Fig. 2,
UniOMA operates in two stages. Given a mini-batch of multimodal observations, it first constructs
modality-specific input-space kernels and estimates a GW barycenter as the batch-wise structural
consensus. It then updates the modality-specific encoders by aligning their embedding-space kernels
to this consensus while optimizing a contrastive objective.

Gromov–Wasserstein Distance
The Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) distance [30,31] extends optimal transport (OT) [60], which seeks
the minimum cost of transporting one probability measure into another within the same metric
space, to measures supported on different metric spaces. Since a cross-modal cost c(x, y) is generally
difficult to define, GW replaces it with a relational cost that only requires the two intra-modal
similarity matrices.

Results: Comparisons on Downstream Tasks
We compare UniOMA against state-of-the-art multimodal alignment methods. First, we consider
a family of flexible InfoNCE-style frameworks: (i) Pairwise (CMC) [35], which sums pairwise
contrastive losses; (ii) Symile [46], extending InfoNCE to triple-wise contrastive objectives; and (iii)
GRAM [47], which replaces cosine similarity with Gramian volume similarity for M ≥3 modalities.
These methods share a contrastive backbone, allowing our GW regularizer to be incorporated in a
plug-and-play manner; we therefore also report “ +GW” variants for (i), (ii), and (iii).
In addition, we compare against three recent, more specialized multimodal objectives: (iv)
OTLC [50], which augments pairwise InfoNCE with a standard OT regularizer; (v) TRIAN-
GLE [49], which aligns three modalities via triangle area similarity; and (vi) CoMM [48], a strong
fusion-based baseline with a dedicated multimodal architecture. All methods are trained with
matched optimizer, batch size, temperature, and learning budget (see Supplementary Material,
Sec. I).

Results: Modality Weights
UniOMA learns modality weights {λm} that determine each modality’s contribution to the consensus
(Appendix A). Fig. 5 shows the modality weights on each dataset; these values are an empirical
indicator of modality-wise agreement with the learned structural consensus rather than a measure
of the importance of a modality for the task.
Weight sensitivity and kernel bandwidth. To better understand the learned modality weights,
we tested their sensitivity to cross-modal disagreement and kernel bandwidth on VFD and VFP.
First, we destroyed cross-modal pairing by replacing images in 75% of the training samples with
images from other samples. The vision weight decreased from 0.46 to 0.23 on VFD and from 0.63
to 0.38 on VFP. Second, we varied the kernel bandwidth of one modality at a time by factors of
0.25, 0.5, 2, and 4, while keeping the data unchanged. The corresponding weight changes remained
below 0.05.
These results suggest that the weights reflect the tested cross-modal corruption and show limited
sensitivity to kernel bandwidth. We further compared learned weights with fixed uniform weights
(λm = 1/M) under the same GW objective. Learned weights showed higher cross-modal transfer R2
under corruption (−0.26 vs. −0.58 on VFD; −1.44 vs. −2.17 on VFP). Thus, the weights serve as
an empirical indicator of modality-wise agreement with the learned structural consensus.

Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work
We revisit multimodal alignment through structural consistency: while pointwise correspondences are
statistically strong in existing methods, intra-modal geometries can still disagree across modalities.
UniOMA reduces this gap by combining contrastive learning with a GW-barycenter regularizer
that aligns 3+ modalities to a shared structural consensus. Concretely, UniOMA represents each
modality as an intra-modal similarity (kernel) space, computes a dynamic GW barycenter as the
structural consensus, and aligns each modality to this consensus with learned modality weights.
This barycentric formulation avoids pairwise couplings across modalities, and is well suited to
scaling to more modalities. Across VFP, VFD, MuJoCo Push, VAT, VIP, and 4–7 modality settings,
UniOMA improves regression, classification, and cross-modal retrieval in most paired comparisons,
brings the similarity structure of each modality closer to a fixed input-kernel reference, and learns
dataset-specific modality weights that serve as an empirical indicator of modality-wise agreement
with the learned structural consensus.
Limitations. UniOMA introduces additional computation due to the barycentric GW updates, but
we explicitly quantify this overhead in Table II. Importantly, the runtime scales linearly with the
number of modalities, rather than superlinearly, which makes UniOMA practical for 3+ modalities.
A second consideration is the choice of intra-modal similarity kernels used to form K(m)
x
. While
kernel mismatch can affect the learned consensus geometry, we partially address this sensitivity
through the kernel-bandwidth test of Sec. IV-F and the feature-extractor swap of Supplementary
Material, Sec. III-H, which empirically validate the robustness of UniOMA under reasonable kernel
choices.
The shared structural consensus is most appropriate when common physical factors induce
related relational patterns across modalities. If the modalities capture largely distinct information,
alignment may ignore useful modality-specific structure. In addition, the learned weights are an
empirical indicator of modality-wise agreement with the learned structural consensus and do not
directly measure modality importance. The entropy term discourages but does not prevent the
concentration of the weights on one modality. Better structural alignment does not necessarily yield
better downstream performance: the gains in Table I are small on tasks close to saturation, and one
16


comparison shows a decrease. Simpler relational losses that use the same input-space kernels can
be comparable to or better than the GW term on the structural measures (Table V). Finally, the
GW term increases the training time per epoch by 1.6–1.8× relative to Pairwise (Table II), which
may not be justified when the modalities share little structure or when the contrastive baseline is
already close to saturation.
Future work. Building on these findings, a promising direction is large-scale real-robot alignment
under heterogeneous sampling rates, missing segments, and asynchronous sensor streams, which
calls for more robust couplings and barycenter estimation in the presence of temporal misalignment.
Another direction is extending the framework beyond symmetric similarity kernels to asymmetric or
directed similarities (e.g., temporal precedence or causal structure), enabling the consensus geometry
to capture directional relations that arise in contact-rich robotics data.

Datasets and Preprocessing
We detail the exact splitting, windowing, and per-modality preprocessing used in our experiments.
Unless specified, all randomization uses a fixed seed (seed=42), and splits are performed at the
file/trajectory level to avoid leakage.
VFD / VFP (Vision–Force–Depth / Vision–Force–Proprioception). The data contain
3,000 trajectories; with 48 samples per trajectory, this gives 144,000 samples. We split the data
at the trajectory level with seed = 42 into training, validation, and test trajectories at a ratio of
80/10/10 (48 samples per trajectory). Validation data are used for early stopping and checkpoint
selection, and test data only for the final evaluation; the TCK model is fitted on the training
trajectories only. Each episode has length 48. For time step t, we form a fixed history window
of length L for force (default L=32) and read targets at t+1. RGB images are center-cropped to
128×128, normalized by ImageNet statistics (mean [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]).
Depth is stored as (128, 128, 1), normalized by mean 0.5/std 0.5, and used as single-channel tensors.
Force–torque histories are truncated to the last L steps. The resulting tensor has shape [b×L×6].
Proprioception is parsed from the first 7 pose components (end effector position/orientation) in the
loader and returned as [b×7] at the current step.
Tasks. For VFD, we follow the main text: (1) next-step end-effector orientation regression (4D)
and (2) trajectory-pair discrimination (binary classification of whether two samples come from the
same trajectory), using (RGBt, F/Tt−L+1:t, Deptht) as inputs. For VFP, we perform next-step
contact prediction (binary) using (RGBt, F/Tt−L+1:t, Propriot); class balance is enforced by uniform
sampling across trajectories.

VIP (Vision–IMU–Proprioception). To complement benchmark and simulation-based eval-

6-DoF robotic manipulator. This dataset is designed to evaluate whether the learned multimodal
structural alignment generalizes to noisy and embodiment-specific sensing commonly encountered
in real robotic systems.
Data collection. The robot is a commercial 6-DoF serial manipulator with a reach of 900 mm,
operated without payload and without contact. Each trajectory is a reaching motion toward

a sampled end-effector target, executed by a trained reaching policy that commands joint in-
crements of at most 10◦per step.
The initial joint configuration is sampled uniformly from
[±180◦, ±30◦, ±90◦, ±180◦, ±180◦, ±180◦], so that the trajectories cover varied initial configurations
and reaching targets. All data were collected in a single workspace under constant indoor lighting.
Scale and split. The dataset contains 1,000 trajectories of 100 steps recorded at 5 Hz (20 s each),
i.e., 100,000 synchronized samples. We split it at the trajectory level into training, validation, and
test trajectories at a ratio of 80/10/10 (80,000/10,000/10,000 samples); training and test trajectories
are drawn from the same distribution, and there are no held-out conditions.
Modalities and preprocessing. Each sample consists of three synchronized modalities: (1) a
third-person RGB image captured from a fixed external camera on a tripod about 2 m from the
robot at 320×240, resized to 64×64 and normalized using ImageNet statistics (the end-effector is
not visible in every frame); (2) proprioceptive signals given by the current joint angles of the 6-DoF
arm, represented as a [b×6] vector; and (3) inertial measurements (IMU) from the six joint modules,
each reporting three linear and three angular accelerations, forming tensors of shape [b×6×6]. Each
of the 36 inertial channels is standardized with the mean and variance of the training trajectories.
The three modalities are hardware-triggered and recorded as single-step readings at the 5 Hz control
frequency, without temporal windowing.
Task: end-effector position regression. Given the multimodal input (RGBt, Propriot, IMUt), the
model is trained to predict the current Cartesian position of the end-effector (regression, 3D). The
ground-truth end-effector position is measured by an optical motion-capture system from markers
rigidly attached to the end-effector and is expressed in a table-fixed frame; it is not computed from
the joint encoders. We report the mean Euclidean distance between the predicted and measured
end-effector positions (×10−2 m) on the held-out test set, as summarized in Table III of the main
paper.

Choice of the Pre-trained Feature Extractor
The input-space kernel of the image modality is built from frozen ViT-B/16 features (Sec. I-B).
To test whether the structural gains depend on this choice, we replaced ViT-B/16 by DINOv2
ViT-B/14 [9] or an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 [10], retrained our method on VFD and VFP
(30 epochs, five seeds), and evaluated the structural measures of Sec. III-A against the same ViT-
based input-kernel reference as in Table V of the main paper. Table V shows that the gains over
Pairwise are preserved with both feature extractors, and ResNet-18 features give slightly higher
rank correlation and lower GW distance than the default. The structural alignment therefore does
not rely on a particular pre-trained encoder.
