XM4D: 4D Reconstruction through Certifiable
Geometric Optimization
Haoyu Han1, Jack Benarroch Jedlicki1, Jiahui Lei2, Heng Yang1
1Harvard University, 2UC Berkeley
Accurate 4D reconstruction remains challenging: jointly estimating camera motion, scene geometry, and
non-rigid deformation entails highly nonconvex optimization, making global optimality difficult to guarantee.
We present XM4D, a framework for accurate and efficient 4D reconstruction built upon certifiably globally
optimal geometric optimization. By separating the estimation of camera motion from non-rigid deformation,
we formulate both stages as structured quadratically constrained quadratic programs (QCQP) that admit
semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations and verifiable global optimality certificates. For camera motion
estimation, we lift static point tracks into 3D using foundational depth prediction models and formulate a scaled
bundle adjustment problem that jointly estimates camera poses, depth scales, and static landmarks. By
analytically eliminating translations and landmarks, we reduce the problem to a QCQP over scaled rotations.
For non-rigid reconstruction, we represent dynamic motion using an embedded deformation graph (EDG) and
show that its tracking, rigidity, and temporal regularization objectives similarly reduce to a rotation-only QCQP.
Both problems admit SDP relaxations that we solve efficiently with a GPU-accelerated Burer–Monteiro solver
based on Riemannian optimization. The recovered geometry further initializes dynamic Gaussian splatting
for photometric refinement. Extensive experiments across seven benchmarks demonstrate accurate camera
motion estimation on both static and dynamic scenes, efficient optimization that scales to thousands of frames,
and state-of-the-art novel-view rendering quality.

1 Introduction
4D reconstruction aims to recover the time-varying 3D structure of a scene from visual observations. It supports
applications including dynamic novel-view rendering [36, 42], deformable and articulated object reconstruction [24, 48,
74], digital humans and digital twins [25, 53, 70], and motion retargeting for humanoid control [3, 21, 40]. Despite its
broad utility, reconstructing a dynamic scene from monocular images or videos remains highly challenging [17, 18].
Camera and object motion are entangled in the visual observations [15], while dynamic geometry itself is weakly
constrained and may undergo complex non-rigid deformation. Long sequences introduce further difficulties, including
fragmented point tracks, noisy and scale-inconsistent monocular depth, and temporally incomplete observations of the
dynamic scene [35, 68].
Recent 4D reconstruction methods commonly rely on coupled nonconvex optimization over camera poses, scene
geometry, deformation, and appearance [35, 68]. Such formulations have achieved impressive results, but their strong
coupling makes the optimization difficult: the objective is highly nonconvex and local optimization may therefore be
sensitive to initialization and converge to undesired local minima. As illustrated in Fig. 3, local optimization of the
motion of an embedded deformation graph (EDG) can converge to an inferior solution whereas the certifiable solver
presented in this work achieves an accurate reconstruction. Similar failures of local optimization in static reconstruction
were demonstrated in XM [20] (Fig. 10). This raises a natural question:
Can we achieve accurate and efficient 4D reconstruction through globally certifiable geometric optimization?
In this work, we present XM4D, a framework for accurate and efficient 4D reconstruction based on globally certifiable
geometric optimization. Our approach separates camera motion estimation from non-rigid deformation, allowing both
stages to be formulated as structured (nonconvex) quadratically constrained quadratic programs (QCQPs) and solved
through (convex) semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations. Fig. 2(a) illustrates this decomposition as a view graph
and Fig. 2(b) instantiates it on a real scene: static and dynamic point tracks share the same cameras and predicted
depth, but static tracks constrain time-invariant landmarks and camera poses, while dynamic tracks constrain the
motion of an EDG. In the following, we overview the problem formulation and solution approach for both stages.

We optimize the EDG node transformations with tracking loss, as-rigid-as-possible (ARAP) and temporal smoothness

express all node transformations relative to a fixed rest frame. With fixed EDG structure and skinning weights,

In summary, XM4D makes the following contributions:
• For static reconstruction, we build on the QCQP–SDP–BM framework of our conference version, XM [20],
retaining its efficient optimization and global optimality certificates. We introduce a uniform sphere constraint
that resolves the scale ambiguity without anchoring any individual frame.
• For dynamic reconstruction, we design an EDG-based non-rigid reconstruction formulation that incorporates
tracking, rigidity, and temporal smoothness into a structured QCQP. This formulation admits the same globally
certifiable optimization framework.
• We build XM4D, an accurate and efficient 4D reconstruction pipeline that combines certifiable geometric
optimization with dynamic Gaussian splatting. Extensive experiments across seven benchmarks demonstrate
leading camera accuracy, fast and scalable optimization, and the best average novel-view rendering performance
on all three dynamic benchmarks (DyCheck, NVIDIA, and Syn4D).

constraint. Section 4 extends this machinery to the EDG-based dynamic reconstruction and shows that it admits the
same certifiable solver. Section 5 describes the complete pipeline. Section 6 evaluates camera accuracy, runtime,
and novel-view rendering on seven benchmarks. Proofs and implementation details are given in the appendices.

2 Related Work
2.1 Dynamic Scene Reconstruction
Existing dynamic scene reconstruction methods can be grouped by how they constrain the reconstruction. Rendering-
based methods fit time-varying scene representations to images, while geometry-driven methods use measurements
such as depth and point tracks to estimate structure and motion. Rendering-based methods represent dynamics
using time-varying (volumetric) fields [36, 42], deformable canonical radiance fields and their higher-dimensional
extensions [51, 52, 57], or moving Gaussians [44, 62, 72]. Many of them incorporate geometric regularization, and
some jointly estimate camera motion [41]. However, recovering object or part level SE(3) motion for downstream scene
understanding generally requires additional motion estimation. Moreover, many methods rely on accurate camera
poses, carefully designed initialization, and heavy per-scene optimization.
Geometry-based methods use geometric measurements and motion priors to constrain dynamic reconstruction. Earlier
non-rigid structure-from-motion methods recover 3D shape from monocular point tracks using low-rank or subspace
priors [19, 88], while RGB-D methods use depth observations with deformation graphs or volumetric models to track
motion and reconstruct geometry [9, 24, 48]. More recently, MoSca [35] lifts learned depth and point-track priors into a
motion scaffold and anchors Gaussians to it for photometric reconstruction. Shape of Motion [68] represents scene
motion using shared SE(3) motion bases, with per-point weights combining the bases to recover persistent trajectories
from monocular depth and long-range 2D tracks. XM4D also uses explicit local SE(3) transformations as an EDG.
Unlike them, XM4D estimates camera poses and temporally coupled graph transformations from depth and tracks
with a certifiable geometric solver, making local rigid motions explicit without involving Gaussians.
2.2 Learning-Assisted Geometric Reconstruction
Learned geometric priors have become important components of reconstruction pipelines. Monocular depth models [5,
23, 38, 55, 79] predict dense scene depth, while point trackers [16, 30, 32, 73, 85] establish correspondences
across frames. Together, depth and correspondence estimates provide complementary constraints for geometric
reconstruction: depth informs scene structure, while tracks connect observations over time.
Recent feed-forward methods predict richer 3D scene representations directly from images. DUSt3R [69] predicts
pointmaps for image pairs and aligns them in a shared coordinate frame, and MUSt3R [13] extends it to many views,
and VGGT [67] jointly predicts camera parameters, depth maps, pointmaps, and 3D point tracks. In XM4D, learned
depth and tracks are measurements for an explicit geometric optimization.
2.3 Certifiable Geometric Optimization
Certifiable geometric optimization solves nonconvex estimation problems, such as bundle adjustment, while verifying
global optimality [33, 78, 84]. Semidefinite relaxations provide such certificates for pose synchronization [60, 82],
outlier-robust registration [77], and general polynomial formulations of robust estimation [76].
Our prior conference work, XM [20], introduced depth-assisted scaled bundle adjustment with a scalable solver based
on BM factorization [11] and Riemannian optimization [8], which XM4D extends to dynamic scenes.

Summary
The static stage lifts 2D point tracks to 3D with learned depth, eliminates translations and landmarks to ob-
tain (SRO), and relaxes it through (QCQP) to the convex program (SDP), solved by the Riemannian staircase
with a certificate of global optimality and a suboptimality gap (15). Unlike XM, a sphere constraint fixes the global
scale without anchoring any frame. The recovered camera poses (Ct, ct, st) define the world frame in which
dynamic tracks are lifted for the non-rigid stage.

4.1 EDG Model and the Dynamic Objective
Dynamic motion is represented by an EDG [65], GEDG = (VEDG, EEDG), consisting of J sparse control nodes (Eq. (1)).
Each node j has a rest position gj ∈R3 and a rigid transformation (Rj,t, pj,t) ∈SO(3) × R3 describing its motion at
frame t. Dynamic points are attached to nearby nodes and deformed using linear blend skinning. In this section, the
graph (its nodes, edges, rest positions, and skinning weights) is taken as given; Section 5.3 describes how it is built
from the lifted dynamic tracks.

Summary
The dynamic stage models non-rigid motion within each temporal segment with an EDG whose transformations
are expressed relative to a single rest frame, which makes the tracking, ARAP, and temporal smoothness
objective (DYN) quadratic. Eliminating the node translations yields the rotation-only problem (DYN-RO), whose
linear term is absorbed by one auxiliary rotation into the homogeneous problem (DYN-H). Its SDP relaxation is
solved with the same BM staircase as the static stage, with a certificate of global optimality and a suboptimality
gap, whereas a local solver can stall in a wrong local minimum.
5 The XM4D Pipeline
This section describes the end-to-end system that recovers both the static and the dynamic structure of the view graph
in Fig. 2. We present it for a monocular video, but the formulation only requires that each image carry a time tag, and an
image collection without time tags reduces the pipeline to static 3D reconstruction. The stages are preprocessing and
tracking (Section 5.1), the static stage (Section 5.2), EDG construction, segment solving and completion (Section 5.3),
and the final photometric stage (Section 5.4).
5.1 Preprocessing: Depth, Intrinsics, Masks, and Tracks
Every frame is processed by a monocular depth foundation model, with Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [38] as the default,
which provides the metric depths dit used in the lift (2) and, for uncalibrated videos, the camera intrinsics. Per-frame
masks of the moving objects are obtained with SAM 3 [14], prompted either by a text description or by a few hand-
selected keypoints on the dynamic object and then tracked across the video. Finally, both static and dynamic 2D
correspondences are produced by point trackers, with TAPNext [85] as the default. The trackers are operated in an
online fashion: as each frame arrives, new query points are sampled only at locations farther than a threshold from
existing tracks. Unreliable observations are discarded (Appendix E). Optionally, dynamic tracks can be assembled into
long-range trajectories for the EDG (Appendix E.5).
5.2 Static Stage: Certified Solve
The static stage solves (SBA) with the solver of Section 3, augmented with two robustness steps. First, we run
XM2 [20]: the solver is applied twice, with an outlier filter in between that keeps only the observations with the smallest
residuals under the first solution, so tracking failures or inaccurate depth predictions do not corrupt the final estimate.

5.3 EDG Construction, Segment Solve, and Completion
Starting from the lifted dynamic tracks of Section 4, we first build a time-independent graph connecting tracks that
remain spatially close over time. We then subsample this graph to obtain sparse EDG nodes and inherit its connectivity
to define the EDG edges. Because no single frame observes all nodes in long videos, we solve the EDG motion
independently on short overlapping segments using the certified solver of Section 4.3, and fuse the segment solutions.
Finally, missing EDG poses are filled by a completion step.

5.4 Photometric Stage
The recovered geometry initializes a dynamic Gaussian-splatting representation [31]. We use gsplat [80] as the
photometric training engine and add an EDG wrapper on top of it, which warps every dynamic Gaussian to frame t by
linear blend skinning with the EDG transformations. During training, the rest positions of the dynamic Gaussians are
optimized together with the EDG transformations (Rj,t, pj,t), initialized from the certified solve, under a photometric
loss and depth supervision.

and TUM, and in translation on Sintel; on Syn4D, NVIDIA, and DyCheck it is close to the best method, MegaSAM; and
on the unordered image collections of IMC, XM4D is outperformed only by the dedicated SfM pipeline COLMAP. Fig. 6
shows the trajectories on one scene per benchmark.
COLMAP is the most accurate on IMC, the unordered setting it is designed for, and remains competitive on Replica and
TUM, but degrades sharply on Sintel, Syn4D, and NVIDIA, where its ATE-T is 4–20× that of XM4D. The feed-forward
VGGT is roughly three times less accurate in translation. MegaSAM, designed for dynamic videos, is the most accurate
on Syn4D, NVIDIA, and DyCheck, with XM4D close behind, but is less accurate on Replica and TUM and in translation
on Sintel and fails on IMC, whose images lack the temporal continuity its optical-flow front end relies on. MoSca
is 3–10× less accurate than XM4D in ATE-T on the dynamic benchmarks and also fails on IMC; we attribute the
gap to the XM solver, which recovers the cameras by a certifiably optimal SDP on static tracks alone, so errors in
the deformation model cannot propagate into the trajectory. Overall, XM4D ranks first on Replica and TUM and in
translation on Sintel, second on Syn4D, NVIDIA, and in translation on IMC.

6.4 Novel-View Rendering
Table 4 and Fig. 7 evaluate novel-view synthesis on DyCheck, NVIDIA, and Syn4D. XM4D attains the best average

geometry misaligns their renderings with the test view and blurs the dynamic parts. Since the recovered dynamic
motion is hard to compare directly across methods, we further evaluate the geometry on Syn4D, whose moving objects
have ground-truth geometry, through the Chamfer distance of the dynamic object and the error of the rendered depth
(Table 7); XM4D is the most accurate on every scene.

Summary
XM4D gives the best overall results for three reasons. First, its certified cameras make it the most accurate on
Replica and TUM and in translation on Sintel, and close to the best elsewhere. Second, its solver is the fastest on
nearly every benchmark, making XM4D the fastest 4D reconstruction end to end, and scales to 2000 frames.
Third, its cameras and EDG motion give the photometric stage an accurate scaffold, yielding the best novel views
on all three dynamic benchmarks, even against methods with ground-truth poses.
7 Conclusions
We presented XM4D, a 4D reconstruction framework that recovers camera motion and non-rigid deformation through
certifiably globally optimal geometric optimization. Separating the two stages turns both into QCQPs over (scaled)
rotations: the static stage estimates camera poses, depth scales, and static landmarks, and the dynamic stage
estimates the motion of an embedded deformation graph. Both QCQPs are relaxed to SDPs and solved by a GPU
solver that returns a certificate of global optimality. Across seven benchmarks, XM4D estimates accurate cameras on
both dynamic videos and unordered photo collections, scales to thousands of frames, and initializes dynamic Gaussian
19


splatting for state-of-the-art novel-view rendering.
Limitations. The certificate covers the optimization, not its inputs: XM4D relies on the predicted monocular depth
and point tracks, and errors that survive the filters of Appendix E.2 bias the certified solution. The solver stores the
data matrices densely, so its memory grows quadratically with the number of frames; exploiting their sparsity [50] is a
natural next step. Finally, the EDG structure and skinning weights are fixed before the solve; estimating the graph
jointly with its motion is future work.

E.5 Long-Range Dynamic Tracks
Raw dynamic tracks are short and, on repetitive motion, swap the identity of similar-looking parts, so we optionally assemble them
into long-range tracks in three steps. (a) Skeleton. A sparse but more reliable long-range tracker, CoWTracker [32], is restarted
periodically from a grid inside the object mask and run forward and backward, so that every skeleton track spans the whole video;
tracks with large 2D jumps, inconsistent color, or low coverage are filtered out. (b) Attachment. Short online tracks [85] are attached
to every skeleton point whose percentile distance to them is small. (c) Averaging. Each skeleton point is replaced by the per-frame
mean of its attached tracks, giving one long track per skeleton point; unattached online tracks are kept as additional shorter tracks.
E.6 GPU Solver
XM4D implements RTR-TCG and the Riemannian staircase of Algorithm 1 on the GPU in double precision. The data matrices Q
and Qh are obtained by eliminating the linearly entering translations, landmarks, and node translations, an instance of variable
projection that stays efficient when sparsity is exploited [50]. We store Q densely, so each inner conjugate-gradient iteration costs
one dense matrix product for the Euclidean Hessian action 2 ˙UQ, computed with cuBLAS [49]. The tangent-space projections and
the retractions, a batched Gram–Schmidt QR for the Stiefel blocks and a renormalization onto the sphere for the scales, are custom
CUDA kernels; the dynamic problem (DYN-BM) reuses them without the scale variables.
