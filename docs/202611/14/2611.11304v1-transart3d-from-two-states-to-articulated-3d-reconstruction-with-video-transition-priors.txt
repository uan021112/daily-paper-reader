Abstract
Reconstructing interactable articulated assets from a few
images is key to robotics and simulation, yet highly un-
derconstrained. Existing optimization-based methods fit an
articulated asset under a static 3D prior that scores each
state independently, so per-state reconstruction errors leak
into the estimated moving part and joint as false motion.
Video diffusion models capture how objects move between
states, but only implicitly in 2D. To this end, we present
TransArt3D, an optimization-based framework that recon-
structs an articulated 3D asset from two endpoint images
by translating this video transition prior into explicit part
structure and joint kinematics. It first lifts frames generated
between the endpoints with a transition-coupled 3D consen-
sus that applies the static 3D prior jointly across frames,
stabilizing unchanging regions and initializing the parts
and joint. A rectified-flow kinematic refinement then lets the
video prior supervise a 3D motion field over the whole tran-
sition, while part assignment and a shared joint constrain
the field throughout optimization, keeping per-state errors
out of the articulation. Finally, a motion-aware canonical
appearance fusion uses the recovered articulation to map
both endpoint images onto one canonical surface, texturing
regions that only one state reveals. On PartNet-Mobility,
TransArt3D outperforms methods given the same two end-

1. Introduction
Articulated 3D assets are central to robotics and simulation,
where digital replicas support interaction and reconfigura-
tion. Building such an asset requires the geometry and ap-
pearance of the object, together with its movable parts and
the joints that drive them. A few images of the object at dif-
ferent states show only a small part of this articulation. The
reconstruction is therefore highly underconstrained.
Learned methods predict parts and joints from a few im-
ages [11, 14, 29] or from a static mesh [9]. Others generate
a part layout and retrieve part meshes [8, 13, 23]. Their
outputs are bounded by articulated training data, which
is orders of magnitude smaller than static 3D and video
data. Optimization-based methods fit an articulated asset
to the observed states under a pretrained static 3D prior.
FreeArt3D [3] scores each posed state with this prior inde-
pendently. Nothing in this score ties the states together, so
per-state reconstruction errors leak into the estimated mov-
ing part and joint as false motion (Fig. 2).
Video diffusion models offer a complementary source
of motion knowledge.
Trained on large video collec-
tions, they model how objects move between states. In-
betweening models generate the transition between two
given frames [21]. This knowledge is implicit in 2D. A
generated video does not specify which 3D elements move
together or which joint drives them. CHORD [17] distills a
video prior into a free-form 3D motion field. This field can
explain the transition with any motion, so it yields no part
and no joint. Lifting each generated frame to 3D on its own
brings back the per-state errors of the static prior, which a
joint fitted to the frames reads as motion.
Both shortcomings point to the same remedy. Per-state
errors change from frame to frame, and true articulation is
one rigid motion shared by all frames. A joint that must
explain every frame can therefore fit only the shared motion.
Fitting parts and one joint to the whole transition thus turns
the video prior into articulation.
We therefore present TransArt3D, an optimization-based
framework that reconstructs an articulated 3D asset from
two unposed endpoint images. It translates the video transi-
tion prior into explicit part structure and joint kinematics by
letting the prior supervise a 3D motion field over the whole
transition. A part assignment and one shared joint constrain
this field throughout optimization.

in motion and in appearance (Fig. 3). A transition-coupled
3D consensus lifts the frames generated between the end-
points to 3D. It applies the static 3D prior jointly across

Our main contributions are as follows.
• We present TransArt3D, an optimization-based frame-
work that reconstructs an articulated 3D asset from two
endpoint images by translating a video transition prior
into explicit part structure and joint kinematics.
• We propose a rectified-flow kinematic refinement in
which a part assignment and a shared joint constrain a
video-supervised 3D motion field throughout optimiza-
tion, keeping per-state errors out of the articulation.
• We introduce a transition-coupled 3D consensus that ap-
plies the static 3D prior jointly across generated frames to
initialize the parts and joint, and a motion-aware canoni-
cal appearance fusion that textures one canonical surface
from both endpoint images.
• Experiments on PartNet-Mobility and real-world captures
demonstrate that TransArt3D outperforms existing meth-
ods in geometry, articulation and appearance, and that two
endpoint images suffice to surpass methods given six ob-
served states.


2. Related Work
Articulated Object Reconstruction. Optimization-based
methods fit parts and joints to multi-view or multi-state ob-
servations with neural fields or Gaussians [5–7, 12, 27].
Most of them rely on many views per state and on calibrated
cameras. Learned methods predict parts and joints from one
or a few images [11, 13, 14, 23] or from a static mesh [9].
LARM [29] additionally receives the joint as a condition,
and Articulate-Anything [8] assembles the asset from a part
database. The outputs of these methods are bounded by ar-
ticulated training data and part libraries. FreeArt3D [3] lifts
this bound by fitting canonical geometry, texture and joint
parameters under a frozen static 3D prior. This prior scores
one posed state per query, so nothing in it decides which
points move together or which joint explains the differences
between states. TransArt3D keeps the frozen static prior
and obtains this cross-state evidence from a video transition
prior.
Appearance of Articulated Objects. Texturing meth-
ods paint a fixed geometry from text or images [15, 26,
28, 30], and articulated generation methods synthesize ap-
pearance together with structure [4, 16]. Surfaces that only
one state reveals receive no observation in either setting.
TransArt3D uses the recovered articulation to route each
endpoint image to the canonical surface it observes.
Video Priors for Articulation. Video diffusion mod-
els provide a prior over plausible transitions between
states [21]. ATOP personalizes this prior per object [22],
DreamArt fine-tunes it on articulated data [16], and SP4D
predicts RGB and kinematic-part videos [31]. AKD distills
the prior into a supplied rig [10]. In-2-4D and TwoSquared
lift image pairs to 4D with free-form deformation and re-
cover neither parts nor a joint [18, 20]. CHORD distills
the prior into a control-point motion field that has the same
limit [17]. TransArt3D builds on the field and distillation
of CHORD and lets a part assignment and one shared joint
constrain the field throughout optimization, which turns the
corrected motion into explicit articulation.
3. Method
3.1. Overview
Given unposed images I0 and I1 of an articulated object
at its start and end states and a text motion prompt c, we
reconstruct a textured articulated 3D asset. We assume a
single joint and take the start state as canonical. A part as-
signment splits canonical points into a static base B and a
movable part M. We infer the joint type and its parame-
ters J . A revolute joint has J = (a, o, ρ) with unit axis a,
pivot o on the axis and angular range ρ. A prismatic joint
has J = (a, ρ) with unit axis a and translation range ρ.
Each state has a joint state q ∈[0, ρ], with q = 0 at I0 and
q = ρ at I1. The transform Tq rotates M by q about the
axis through o, or translates it by q along a.
Figure 3 shows an overview of TransArt3D. A frozen
video diffusion model conditioned on I0, I1 and c guides a
3D motion field, which the part assignment and joint con-
strain. TC3C (Sec. 3.3) lifts frames generated by this model
to 3D with TRELLIS [25] and initializes the canonical sup-
port, part assignment and joint. RFKR (Sec. 3.4) jointly
refines the motion field, part assignment and joint. MACAF
(Sec. 3.5) uses the recovered part assignment and joint to
fuse I0 and I1 into one canonical appearance.

3.3. Transition-Coupled 3D Consensus
TC3C lifts frames of the generated transition to 3D and ini-
tializes the canonical support, part assignment and joint.
Lifting each frame with TRELLIS independently gives a
plausible shape per state, yet these shapes differ even on the
static base. These per-state reconstruction errors then leak
into the fitted part and joint as false motion. TC3C therefore
samples all states jointly, so that unchanging regions agree
across states, and fits the joint and part assignment to the
coupled result.
Cross-state coupling.
Intuitively, structure that persists
through the transition should agree across states, while the
region swept by M should keep state-specific structure. We
sample K frames uniformly from the transition, with frames
1 and K at I0 and I1, and condition the first TRELLIS stage
on each of them. All states share one noise draw, so token j
refers to the same location in every state. Sampling runs in
two passes. The first pass mixes the latent of each state with
those of the two endpoints during early steps. The second
pass re-noises this result and replaces self-attention with a
gated fusion of a state-specific and a cross-state response,
hk,j = gj Ak(j) + (1 −gj) ¯Ak(j),
(4)
where Ak(j) attends from token j to the tokens of state k,
and ¯Ak(j) uses the same query with keys and values aver-
aged over states. The motion-selective gate gj grows with
the cross-state disagreement of the first-pass occupancies
and of the current clean estimates ˆz. It shrinks on confi-
dent persistent structure and stays below 1. Decoding gives
binary occupancies Ok of the K states on a shared 643 grid.
Articulation initialization. Removing the voxels occupied
in nearly all states leaves the change region of each state.
A translation preserves the projection of the movable part
onto the plane normal to its axis, whereas a rotation gen-
erally does not. We compare these projections across states
for each axis direction of the canonical frame, which selects
the joint type and the axis of a prismatic joint. A grid search
followed by sub-voxel refinement then finds the joint state
qk of each state k. For a revolute joint, it also selects the axis
among these directions and finds the pivot o. These param-
eters maximize the overlap between the largest connected
part of the canonical change region, moved by Tqk, and the
voxels of Ok not occupied in every state. We fix q1 = 0
and orient a so that qK ≥0. This gives the initial joint
J0 with ρ = qK. Given J0, each voxel of the canonical
support U = O1 either stays fixed or moves with the joint.
We label it with the explanation that fewer states contra-
dict. Smoothness and collision terms regularize this label-
ing, which gives the initial base B0 and movable part M0.
The second TRELLIS stage, conditioned on I0, generates a
structured latent on U, which we decode into canonical 3D
Gaussians.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Dataset. We evaluate on 48 objects from twelve PartNet-
Mobility [24] categories, four per category. Each object has
one revolute or prismatic joint.
The two endpoint images show the resting state and the
fully articulated state.
Metrics.
Each reconstructed asset is posed at six uni-
formly spaced joint states.
One evaluator renders and
scores every method. We report part geometry at the rest-
ing state (RS) and averaged over the five articulated states
(AS). Part geometry is measured by dgIoU, dcDist and
dCD [13, 14]. AOR measures the interpenetration between
the two parts [13, 14]. Joint accuracy is measured by the
normalized range error and the axis-normal pivot displace-
ment. Appearance is measured by CLIP similarity over five
views and by foreground-masked LPIPS. Full metric defi-
nitions are provided in the supplementary material.
Implementation. TransArt3D uses frozen TRELLIS [25]
as the static 3D prior and frozen Wan2.2-Fun-A14B-InP [1,
2, 21] as the video transition prior. It takes the two endpoint
images and a text motion prompt as input. We set F = 41
and K = 6. Further settings are provided in the supplemen-
tary material.
4.2. Comparisons with Existing Methods
Baselines.
We compare with three groups of methods.
LARM [29] predicts parts and joints with a feed-forward
network.
SINGAPO [13], DIPO [23] and Articulate-
Anything [8] generate a part layout and retrieve part
meshes.
For a fair comparison, we remove the test ob-
jects from their retrieval databases.
PARIS [12] and
FreeArt3D [3] optimize each object. Under the dual-state
protocol, every method receives the same two endpoint im-
ages. Under the multi-state protocol, the baselines receive
six state images.
LARM receives one view per state to
match our input. Its released three-view setting is reported
in the supplementary material. SINGAPO supports seven
of the twelve categories. It is evaluated on those 28 objects
and marked in Tab. 1.
Quantitative Results.
Tab. 1 shows that TransArt3D
achieves the best part geometry and joint accuracy under the
dual-state protocol. Among methods covering all objects, it
attains the lowest RS and AS dgIoU, dcDist and dCD, the
lowest range and pivot errors and the highest CLIP. DIPO
attains the lowest AOR and Articulate-Anything the low-
est LPIPS. Retrieved part meshes rarely interpenetrate and
carry clean textures. Their range and pivot errors are still
two to seven times ours. A plausible layout does not re-

4.3. Ablation Study
Cross-state coupling.
Removing cross-state coupling
causes the largest drop in Tab. 2, and AOR nearly quadru-
ples. Each frame then keeps its own reconstruction errors,
which the joint initialization reads as motion. Consequently
the parts interpenetrate (Fig. 5), as for two-state FreeArt3D.
Endpoint correspondence initialization keeps geometry and
range and nearly doubles the pivot error. Two endpoints
fix how far the part moves and leave the pivot loosely con-
strained. Coupling the states is thus a prerequisite for esti-
mating the articulation.
Video guidance. Replacing video guidance with state-wise
static 3D guidance worsens geometry, AOR and pivot even
with six states. More states improve each per-state fit. They
cannot reveal which differences between states are motion.
A coarse prompt such as “open the drawer” yields a transi-
tion that barely moves or moves wrongly, and the articula-
tion follows it. The video prior thus helps through the mo-
tion it depicts, once that motion is read out. With the part as-
signment and joint fixed, video guidance alone barely helps.
Boundary refinement then cuts the range error by 40%, and
joint refinement cuts the pivot error by 70%. CHORD with
a post-hoc fit reads the motion out after optimization and
doubles the pivot error even on the ground-truth mesh. Its
free field absorbs the motion into non-rigid sliding (Fig. 5).
The joint must therefore constrain the field during optimiza-
tion.
Canonical appearance. With geometry and articulation
fixed, the variants in Tab. 3 differ mainly on surfaces re-
vealed only at the articulated endpoint (Fig. 6). A single
endpoint never sees them, and average pooling blurs them.

Multi-Joint Extension. For objects with several movable

Real-World Reconstruction.

can be posed between the two observed states. It needs
no articulated training data and therefore applies directly to

5. Conclusion
We present TransArt3D, an optimization-based framework
that reconstructs an articulated 3D asset with explicit parts


and joint from two endpoint images. It translates a video
transition prior into articulation by letting a part assignment
and one shared joint constrain a video-supervised motion
field over the whole transition, so per-state errors stay out
of the joint. Coupling the states also initializes the parts
and joint from jointly lifted frames and textures one canon-
ical surface from both endpoints. Experiments show that
TransArt3D recovers more accurate geometry, articulation
and appearance than existing methods, and that two end-
point images with the transition prior surpass six indepen-
dently observed states. It extends to real-world captures and
multi-joint objects, turning two photographs of an object
into an asset that a simulator can articulate.
