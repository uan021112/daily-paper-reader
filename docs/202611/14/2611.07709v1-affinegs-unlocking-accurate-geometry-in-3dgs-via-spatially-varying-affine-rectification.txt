Abstract
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become the leading approach for photorealistic
novel view synthesis, yet its underlying geometric accuracy lags behind its visual
quality. Existing depth-guided methods attempt to resolve this by regularizing
the optimization with monocular depth priors, typically relying on a global linear
alignment. Crucially, we reveal a fundamental flaw in this assumption: modern
monocular depth networks systematically exhibit region-dependent, piecewise-
linear distortions. This phenomenon invalidates standard global alignment strate-
gies, which inevitably warp scene geometry by enforcing a single scale-and-shift
transform. To address this, we introduce a spatially varying affine rectification
model that dynamically corrects monocular disparity into consistent metric depth
using regionally smooth, edge-aware fields. We further derive a normal-aware regu-
larizer that uses spatial gradients to couple the rectified disparity with 3DGS depth
and normals. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that our sensor-free approach
prevents structural collapse under extreme view sparsity and extracts highly accu-
rate 3D meshes, effectively bridging the gap toward sensor-supervised geometric
fidelity while preserving state-of-the-art photorealism.

Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [12] has emerged a preferred method for fast and photorealistic
rendering of novel views. However, while 3DGS excels at capturing high-frequency appearance
details, its underlying geometric representation remains inherently unstructured. Compared to implicit
neural surfaces [31, 15] or classical Multi-View Stereo, the geometry reconstructed by 3DGS is
significantly weaker. As noted in [7, 5, 24], Gaussian primitives often overfit to the training views,
leading to floating artifacts, multi-layered surfaces, and locally inconsistent geometry. This lack of
structural integrity is highly problematic for downstream applications requiring reliable 3D meshes,
such as autonomous driving, robotic perception, and AR/VR.
To address these geometric shortcomings, one line of work leverages physical sensor depth (e.g.,
DN-Splatter [24]) to directly constrain and align the Gaussian primitives. However, acquiring dense,
high-quality sensor depth is hardware-dependent and impractical in many real-world applications. As
a more scalable alternative, a highly promising line of research [5, 26] injects monocular depth priors
into the optimization process, leveraging the strong structural cues produced by modern foundation
depth models. These methods typically operate on a critical assumption: that predicted monocular
disparity can be aligned to metric depth using a global linear transform. While this global alignment
strategy successfully provides baseline geometric guidance, our extensive experimentation reveals
that this core assumption breaks down in complex real-world environments.
Specifically, we discover that modern off-the-shelf monocular estimators (e.g., Depth-Anything-
V2 [27], MoGe [25], Metric3D-V2 [10]) systematically exhibit region-dependent, piecewise-linear

to fit a single global scale-and-shift transform will either average the error across these conflicting
clusters—resulting in an entirely misaligned scene—or overfit to a single dominant region while
severely warping the rest. This phenomenon fundamentally limits the efficacy of global alignment
strategies, severely reducing their reliability for geometric supervision in sparse-view settings.
Motivated by this insight, we move beyond global assumptions by introducing a spatially varying
affine rectification of monocular disparity. Our key observation is that monocular depth exhibits
highly structured distortions: locally smooth, edge-aligned, and piecewise linear. We exploit this
structure by dynamically estimating scale-and-shift fields. Crucially, we elegantly recover these
spatially varying parameters without relying on any external sensor depth. Specifically, we estimate
the local scale factor by comparing the finite-difference spatial gradients of the monocular disparity
against the rendered 3DGS depth. We then refine these initial estimates using an edge-aware guided
filter, which enforces smoothness within continuous surfaces while preserving sharp discontinuities
across object boundaries. This self-contained process rectifies the monocular prior into consistent
metric depth prior to geometric supervision. We further integrate this rectified depth with normal-
aware constraints derived from projective camera geometry. By formulating our constraints directly
on the spatial gradients, we isolate the scale estimation from local shift ambiguities and couple the
structural boundaries of the disparity prior with the physical orientations of the 3DGS primitives.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We show that modern monocular depth predictions suffer from region-dependent,
piecewise-linear distortions in disparity space which limits global alignment in depth-guided
3DGS, as a single transform cannot simultaneously regularize distinct geometric clusters.
• We present a novel affine rectification model that corrects monocular disparity into consistent
metric depth using regionally smooth, edge-aware fields derived from spatial gradients.
• We introduce a gradient-domain regularizer derived from camera geometry that couples
corrected disparity gradients with 3DGS depth and surface normals.
• Across diverse experimental settings, our method maintains the strong photometric quality
of Gaussian Splatting while substantially improving geometric accuracy, bridging the gap
toward sensor-supervised methods without requiring physical depth data.

remain widely used, estimating camera poses and sparse 3D points through epipolar constraints

With the rise of deep learning, neural reconstruction methods have significantly advanced NVS.
Implicit scene modeling methods like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [15] represent scenes with
a multilayer perceptron and synthesize images via volume rendering. NeRF delivers impressive
photorealism and sparked extensive follow-up research tackling aliasing [1], efficiency [18, 29, 9],
and scalability [19]. Parallel to these developments, explicit or hybrid representations, such as
sparse voxel grids [13, 22], tensor decompositions [2], and polygonal primitives [4] have emerged to
improve rendering speed by exploiting localized computation. Among the explicit representations,
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [12] stands out for enabling real-time rendering with high visual
fidelity through alpha-blended rasterization of Gaussian primitives. Its combination of performance,
simplicity, and quality has made it a leading representation for novel view synthesis. However, 3DGS’s
geometric accuracy remains limited and geometry improvement is an active research direction.
3DGS with Depth Priors. Recent works integrate monocular depth estimation [17] into 3DGS to
strengthen geometric supervision. More advanced monocular estimators, such as Depth-Anything-
V2 [27], broaden the range of depth priors available. 3DGS pipelines [5, 24] typically incorporate
monocular depth by jointly optimizing a single global scale-and-shift transform during training. This
dynamically aligns the predicted disparity to the rendered depth, allowing the aligned prediction to
serve as a geometric regularization target. However, as we demonstrate in Section 3.1, this assumption
of global linear alignment does not hold in practice due to the region-dependent, piecewise-linear
distortions inherent in monocular depth predictions.
Beyond direct depth supervision, 3DGS extensions leverage geometric cues primarily by modifying
the underlying representation. Recent works achieve this by structurally adjusting the primitives into
2D patches or disks [7, 11], appending surface normals as explicit attributes [6], merging Gaussian
rendering with implicit signed-distance fields [3], or introducing joint normal-depth supervision
pipelines [32, 24]. In contrast, our method does not modify the Gaussian representation nor rely on
rigid global alignment assumptions. Instead, we extract reliable geometric supervision purely by
targeting and rectifying the spatially varying distortions inherent to monocular depth priors.
3
Method
To improve the geometric fidelity of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), our method leverages monocular
depth priors while explicitly accommodating their inherent spatial distortions. Modern monocular
estimators exhibit region-dependent, piecewise-linear inconsistencies that cannot be resolved via
standard global scale-and-shift alignment. Rather than treating these distortions as pure noise, we
exploit their locally smooth and edge-aligned structure, and introduce a spatially varying affine
rectification model (Section 3.1). We then couple these locally consistent metric depth signals with
surface-normal and camera-geometry constraints in the gradient domain (Section 3.2), and anchor the
overall scene scale using a direct metric depth re-alignment (Section 3.3). Together, these components
provide robust, geometry-aware supervision without modifying the underlying 3DGS representation.
Fig. 2 gives an overview of the full pipeline.

3.1
Spatially Varying Affine Rectification
Motivation. Existing depth-guided 3DGS methods typically assume that monocular disparity can
be rectified into metric depth using a single global scale-and-shift transform. However, to quantify
the validity of this assumption, we conducted a statistical analysis using the Depth-Anything-V2
prior on the MuSHRoom and ScanNet++ datasets. As shown in Table 1, a single global transform
(K = 1) leaves over 40% of the scene fundamentally misaligned. Yet, when partitioned into just
3 to 5 distinct local regions, the inlier ratios jump to over 84% and 91%, halving the RMSE. This
statistically proves that modern monocular estimators exhibit region-dependent, piecewise-linear
distortions. Consequently, an uncorrected global prior will inevitably warp the geometry of some
objects while aligning others. Rather than treating these distortions as pure noise, we exploit their
locally smooth and edge-aligned structure to introduce a spatially varying affine rectification model.

Edge-aware smoothing via guided filtering. To ensure regional coherence while respecting sharp
object boundaries, we smooth the estimated α and β fields using a Guided Filter [8]. We use
the monocular disparity d as the guide image, which naturally aligns the smoothing process with
the scene’s structural edges. This promotes local smoothness within coherent depth regions while
preventing blurring across object boundaries, effectively capturing the piecewise-linear nature of

the monocular distortions. We denote these filtered fields as αsmooth and βsmooth. We use a large
filter window (a radius of 550–650 pixels on MuSHRoom; Appendix Sec. C), so the filtered fields
follow the edges of the guide while their local linear relation to it varies only slowly across the image.
Because the guided filter is a local linear fit, it can push the filtered scale field outside the clamping
range, including to non-positive values for which the corrected depth in Eq. 9 is undefined. We
therefore clamp αsmooth to the same percentile range after filtering.

Normal-Aware Gradient-Domain Regularization
Depth values are zero-order measurements which are highly susceptible to shift ambiguities. In
contrast, surface normals encode first-order geometry. Therefore, we can use these physical surface
normals as a strong cue to support our gradient estimation. Specifically, by tying our rectified disparity
gradients directly to the expected depth gradients derived from the normals and camera intrinsics, we
create a supervision signal that naturally penalizes geometrically implausible local slopes.

Normal-guided projective constraints. While Lgrad transfers structural edges from the monocular
prior to the rendered depth map, it only constrains the positions of the Gaussian primitives. Because
3DGS is a volumetric representation, primitives can form a visually smooth alpha-blended depth map

orientation ambiguity severely degrades the quality of extracted 3D meshes.

Experiments
Implementation details. We train 3DGS using the following loss objective, which is a weighted
combination of our proposed geometric regularization:
Ltotal = Lphoto + λ1Lgrad + λ2Lnormal + λ3Ldepth.
(12)
We empirically set λ1 = 0.1, λ2 = 0.08, λ3 = 0.4 and Lphoto is the original photometric loss [12].
On the MuSHRoom scenes kokko and sauna, these default weights produce floaters, so we scale
all three weights by 0.5 and 0.25, respectively. On ScanNet++, we use the 0.25 scaling and train at
half resolution (960 × 720). Appendix Sec. C lists all per-scene settings. We train all models for
30k iterations. For densification, we use the unmodified adaptive density control of Splatfacto [23]
(gradient-based cloning and splitting, opacity-based pruning, and periodic opacity reset) with its
default settings, identical for our method and all Gaussian-based baselines. We change no densification
logic, so all reported gains come from our supervision signal alone. To obtain disparity maps, we
propagate RGB images through the pre-trained Depth-Anything-V2-Large model [27]. Crucially, the
spatially varying affine parameters (e.g., the scale factor α) used to rectify these monocular priors are
iteratively updated alongside the 3DGS scene parameters during optimization. By following [24],
we initialize the Gaussian scene using 1M back-projected points from training dataset sensor depths.
The Algorithm column of Tables 4 and 5 lists the meshing algorithm of each method. We mesh
our method, and DN-Splatter (Monodepth) on MuSHRoom, by fusing the depth maps rendered
at the training views with Open3D TSDF fusion (voxel size 0.01 and truncation 0.03, in scene
units). Methods meshed with Poisson reconstruction extract a total of 2M points from back-projected
depth/normal maps and use a depth level of 9; Neusfacto and MonoSDF use marching cubes, and
2DGS uses TSDF fusion. Appendix Sec. F.5 compares Poisson and TSDF meshes of the same trained
models. We refer readers to [24] for more details.
Baselines. We evaluate across implicit, explicit, and classical representations. Implicit baselines
include Nerfacto, Depth-Nerfacto [23], Neusfacto [30], and MonoSDF [31]. Explicit baselines feature
standard Splatfacto [23], geometry-focused SuGaR [7] and 2DGS [11], alongside depth-regularized
DRGS [5] and DN-Splatter [24]. For a fair sensor-free comparison, we introduce DN-Splatter
(Monodepth), which replaces its physical sensor data with dynamically aligned monocular priors
(Depth-Anything-V2 [27]). Finally, we include classical Poisson surface reconstruction from back-
projected sensor depth to contextualize upper-bound mesh performance [24].
Datasets. We validate our proposed method across a diverse set of challenging indoor environments.
For standard forward-facing captures, we utilize the NeRF-LLFF dataset [14], which comprises
distinct real-world scenes. To assess generalization to complex camera movements, we evaluate
on the MuSHRoom dataset [20], a real-world indoor benchmark specifically designed with disjoint
camera trajectories for training and testing. Finally, to evaluate geometric accuracy, we benchmark
on ScanNet++ [28], a large-scale real-world dataset providing dense RGB sequences paired with
high-fidelity 3D ground-truth geometry.

5
Conclusion
In this work, we identified a critical limitation in existing depth-guided 3DGS methods: the reliance on
global linear alignment fails to account for the region-dependent, piecewise-linear distortions inherent
to modern monocular depth priors. To overcome this, we introduced a novel spatially varying affine
rectification framework that dynamically corrects these monocular distortions into geometrically
consistent metric depth using edge-aware, locally smooth fields. By coupling this rectified depth with
normal-aware gradient constraints, our method explicitly regularizes the physical orientation of 3D
primitives without requiring any architectural modifications to 3DGS. Extensive evaluations across
the NeRF-LLFF, MuSHRoom, and ScanNet++ datasets demonstrate that our approach significantly
outperforms sensor-free depth-regularized baselines, as well as existing explicit baselines on NeRF-
LLFF and MuSHRoom. Ultimately, our method bridges the gap toward sensor-supervised geometric
fidelity.

A
Comprehensive Analysis of Monocular Disparity Distortions
In Sec. 3.1 of the main paper, we demonstrated that monocular depth priors suffer from region-
dependent, piecewise-linear distortions by evaluating RANSAC inlier ratios at a standard residual
threshold of 0.02. To rigorously prove that this piecewise behavior is an inherent property of
foundation depth models and not merely an artifact of a specific error tolerance, we expand our
empirical analysis here.
A.1
The Discovery of Piecewise-Linear Disparity Fragmentation
We uncover a previously uncharacterized, systematic failure mode inherent to modern foundation
depth estimators: piecewise-linear disparity fragmentation. While foundation models are highly
effective at capturing relative depth ordering, our analysis reveals that their predictions do not map to
true metric scale through a unified global transform. Instead, the networks systematically fracture
scenes into distinct, spatially coherent affine regimes.
At first glance, one might assume that allocating more degrees of freedom (increasing the number
of local regions, K) naturally reduces residual error, akin to fitting a higher-degree polynomial to
noisy data. However, it is crucial to distinguish the structured distortions we observe from complex
pixel-level noise. If the monocular priors simply suffered from random, unstructured inaccuracies,
partitioning the data into K linear models would merely overfit to random, scattered subsets of pixels.
Our empirical evidence, particularly the spatial coherence masks shown in the main text (Fig. 1),
proves otherwise. The K affine regimes are not random; they are highly structured and correspond
directly to coherent physical surfaces. The breaks between these linear regimes align strictly with
dominant object boundaries and large depth discontinuities. This proves that foundation models
encode relative depth smoothly within continuous physical surfaces, but apply fundamentally different
scale-and-shift parameters across different objects.
A.2
The Mathematical Impossibility of Global Alignment
This phenomenon fundamentally invalidates the core assumption of existing depth-regularized 3DGS
methods. A single global affine transform possesses exactly two degrees of freedom (scale and shift).
Consequently, it is mathematically impossible for a single transform to simultaneously reconcile
K fundamentally incompatible linear regimes. Global alignment in this context is not merely
suboptimal—it is a mathematically ill-posed operation. Attempting to fit a single line through the
fragmented disparity space will inevitably force the optimizer to average the error across conflicting
spatial clusters, resulting in the severe warping of physical geometry in some regions to satisfy the
alignment of others (see Fig. 7).
A.3
Empirical Validation Across Foundation Models and Datasets
To demonstrate that piecewise-linear disparity fragmentation is a systemic characteristic of monocular
priors and not an isolated artifact of a specific architecture or dataset, we expand our empirical evalu-
ation across three state-of-the-art foundation models: Metric3D-V2, MoGe, and Depth-Anything-
V2. As a direct extension to the visual evidence provided in our main text Fig. 1 (which utilized
Depth-Anything-V2), we visually confirm in Fig. 8 that both Metric3D-V2 and MoGe exhibit the
region-dependent fragmentation behavior.
For each model, we evaluate the affine alignment over the MuSHRoom dataset sequences using
iteratively fitted piecewise local linear regions (K ∈{1, . . . , 8}). To rigorously test the spatial
alignment, we evaluate the models across three separate residual error thresholds: a strict threshold
(0.005), a moderate threshold (0.02), and a forgiving threshold (0.05). Furthermore, to ensure these
findings generalize across different capturing hardware and indoor environments, we additionally
evaluate Depth-Anything-V2 on the ScanNet++ dataset.
As shown in Tables 7 through 10, the global alignment assumption (K = 1) fails significantly under
strict thresholds across all models and datasets. For instance, at a strict tolerance on MuSHRoom, a
global transform aligns only 25.6% of the geometry produced by Depth-Anything-V2. When tested
on the entirely different ScanNet++ dataset, it similarly aligns a mere 26.9% (Table 10). However, by
simply allowing the evaluation to independently align local affine regions (K ≥3), the inlier ratios

D
Definitions for Metrics
D.1
Depth Evaluation Metrics
To rigorously assess depth estimation performance on the ScanNet++ and MuSHRoom datasets, we
adopt standard evaluation protocols established by prior work [24]. Our quantitative assessment relies
on the following metrics:
• Absolute Relative Error (Abs Rel): Computes the mean ratio of the absolute depth
difference to the corresponding ground-truth depth, reflecting the average magnitude of
relative deviations across the scene.
• Squared Relative Error (Sq Rel): Similar to Abs Rel, but squares the relative depth
differences before averaging, which more severely penalizes larger, localized depth errors.
• Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): Calculates the square root of the mean squared
differences between the predicted and actual depth maps, providing a direct measurement of
the absolute error magnitude.
• Logarithmic RMSE (RMSE log): Computes the RMSE in the logarithmic domain. This
metric is particularly advantageous for mitigating the disproportionate influence of distant
background objects, which naturally exhibit larger depth values.
• Threshold Accuracy (δ < t): Evaluates the percentage of predicted pixels where the
maximum ratio between the predicted depth and ground-truth depth (or vice versa) falls
below a predefined threshold factor t.
D.2
Mesh Evaluation Metrics
For 3D geometric fidelity, we compare our extracted 3D geometry against ground-truth meshes using
widely accepted reconstruction metrics, with formal definitions provided in Table 12. To quantify
the structural overlap between the predicted and actual geometry, we compute Precision, Recall, and
the resulting F-score. Following standard practices, we enforce a strict distance threshold of 5 cm to
determine whether a predicted vertex successfully matches the ground truth. Furthermore, to ensure a
fair and relevant assessment, we strictly isolate our mesh quality evaluation to regions that fall within
the visibility frustums of the training camera views.

F.3
Sensitivity to Filters
Raw outputs from monocular depth networks, while structurally strong, inevitably contain local noise,
quantization artifacts, and high-frequency inconsistencies. If injected directly into the optimization
pipeline without regularization, these noisy coefficients force the 3D Gaussians to overfit to erroneous
local depth shifts, severely degrading both the extracted geometry and the novel view synthesis. To
address this, we apply a filtering mechanism to smooth the extracted alignment coefficients. In this
section, we ablate our choice of the Guided Filter against a standard Gaussian filter and a baseline
with no filtering.
22


In Table 15, we perform this ablation on the coffee_room scene from the MuSHRoom dataset using
the Depth-Anything-V2 prior. We keep all loss terms active (RGB + Disparity Normal Gradient +
L1 Depth) and isolate the effect of the filter type on the disparity alignment coefficients. The results
are evaluated across Novel View Synthesis (right split), Depth estimation (right split), and Mesh
extraction.
We show that disabling the filter entirely (“None”) results in a catastrophic drop in geometric accuracy;
the F-score collapses to 0.680, and the depth RMSE spikes to 0.269, proving that unregularized
monocular noise actively harms the spatial reconstruction. While applying a standard Gaussian blur
mitigates some of this noise and improves the F-score to 0.774, it does so indiscriminately. Because a
Gaussian filter smooths across physical depth boundaries, it inadvertently blurs distinct structural
edges, limiting the sharpness of the final geometry. In contrast, our chosen Guided Blur leverages the
prior itself as an edge-preserving guide. This ensures that noise is smoothed across flat surfaces while
sharp structural discontinuities are strictly preserved. As a result, our Guided Blur strategy firmly
dominates across all categories, yielding the highest photometric fidelity, the lowest depth error, and
a significantly superior geometric F-score of 0.855.

F.4
Sensitivity to Gradient Matching Formulation
We evaluate the sensitivity of our approach to the specific mathematical formulation of the disparity
gradient loss defined in Eq. 5. Using the coffee_room scene and the Depth-Anything-V2 prior, we
ablate our combined magnitude and directional alignment formulation (MSE + Cosine) against using
strictly Mean Squared Error (MSE) or strictly Cosine Similarity. The objective of this loss term is to
ensure that the spatial gradients of the rendered depth map closely follow the structural gradients of
the monocular prior.
The quantitative results in Table 16 demonstrate that relying exclusively on an MSE formulation is
insufficient for complex geometries. MSE alone struggles to enforce sharp orientational changes,
resulting in a significant drop in Normal Consistency (0.791) and overall F-score (0.781). While
Cosine Similarity alone provides a highly effective directional cue—matching our combined approach
in absolute depth accuracy—it lacks a constraint on the actual magnitude of the gradient change.
By combining both MSE (magnitude) and Cosine (direction), we ensure the most robust structural
regularization. This combined approach prevents local geometric collapse and yields the best overall
surface extraction (F-score of 0.855) alongside optimal photometric rendering quality (21.53 dB
PSNR).

G.1
Comprehensive Analysis of the DN-Splatter (Monodepth) Baseline
To fully demonstrate the limitations of global alignment strategies when utilizing monocular depth
priors, we provide a comprehensive scene-wise breakdown of the DN-Splatter (Monodepth) baseline
on the MuSHRoom dataset (Table 18). While DN-Splatter yields exceptional results when provided
with ground-truth sensor depth, its geometric integrity collapses when forced to rely on uncorrected
monocular priors using a global scale-and-shift alignment.
The scene-wise metrics reveal high volatility and catastrophic failures in structurally complex en-
vironments. While simpler or more uniformly constrained scenes like coffee_room and honka
manage to retain a loose semblance of geometry (F-scores of 0.516 and 0.409, respectively), environ-
ments with high depth variance and complex occlusion boundaries suffer from complete topological
collapse. Specifically, in the computer and kokko scenes, the baseline yields extreme depth errors
(RMSE > 1.95) and effectively zero structural reconstruction, plummeting to F-scores of 0.065 and
0.027. This erratic behavior perfectly illustrates the piecewise-linear fragmentation of foundation
depth models discussed in our main text. Because monocular distortions vary spatially, forcing a
single global linear alignment causes severe warping—optimizing to fit one local region inherently
breaks the geometry of the rest of the scene. These results empirically validate our core motivation:
to successfully harness monocular priors for 3DGS, spatially varying local rectification is strictly
required.

H
Limitations
While our proposed spatially varying affine rectification significantly improves the geometric fidelity
of 3DGS, our approach has several limitations:
Dependency on Foundation Model Reliability. Because our method relies on extracting geometric
cues from monocular depth estimators, our reconstruction quality is inherently bounded by the
reliability of the chosen foundation model. As discussed in Appendix Sec. F.2, if the monocular prior
completely fails to capture the underlying structure—such as in the presence of highly transparent
surfaces, massive specular reflections (mirrors), or severe domain gaps not covered by the prior’s
training data—our affine rectification cannot recover the lost geometry.
Absence of Absolute Physical Scale. Our method bridges the gap toward sensor-supervised geomet-
ric fidelity by enforcing consistent Euclidean geometry across the scene. However, because it operates
entirely without physical sensor data, the final reconstructed mesh still lacks an absolute real-world
physical scale. The global metric anchor (Ldepth) aligns the geometry to the arbitrary coordinate space
established by the initial SfM camera poses, rather than true physical dimensions. Moreover, because
Ldepth is re-aligned to the current rendering at every step, it cannot correct a global scale error that
is already present in the photometric reconstruction. On the ScanNet++ scene b20a261fdf, both
plain Splatfacto and our method render depth about 5% shorter than the sensor depth. At the 2–4 m
viewing distances of this scene, this amounts to 10–20 cm, beyond the 5 cm F-score threshold, which
limits our F-score on this scene to 0.18, compared with 0.46 on 8b5caf3398.
Sensitivity to Regularization Strength. The default loss weights are too strong for some scenes. On
kokko and sauna, they produce floaters, including directly in front of the camera, and we reduce all
three weights by factors of 0.5 and 0.25, respectively (Appendix Sec. C).

• It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should
preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
of Normality of errors is not verified.
• For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative
error rates).
• If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how
they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
