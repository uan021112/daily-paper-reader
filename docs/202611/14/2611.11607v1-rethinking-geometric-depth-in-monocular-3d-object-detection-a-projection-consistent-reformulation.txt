Detection: A Projection-Consistent Reformulation

Abhinav Kumar1

Abstract
Monocular 3D detection (Mono3D) hinges on recovering depth from a single
image, for which the geometric formulation z ≈f · H/h2D is widely adopted
due to its simplicity and direct grounding in projective geometry. We show that
this formulation harbors a systematic, projection-induced bias: substituting the 2D
bounding-box height h2D recovers the depth of an object’s visible front surface
rather than its center, causing size-dependent underestimation that persists even
under ground-truth inputs. We address this front-surface bias with MonoPCA,
a unified framework built on a projection-consistent reformulation. At its core,
PCDepth retains the measurable h2D and closes the gap to center depth through an
orientation- and size-aware correction derived from perspective geometry, elimi-
nating the bias without introducing ill-posed regression targets. Because PCDepth
couples depth to predicted object size and orientation, attribute perception be-
comes essential. We therefore introduce GeoAlign, a teacher–student framework
that transfers geometric representations from Vision Foundation Models into a
lightweight student, strengthening attribute perception at no inference cost. Across
four challenging benchmarks, MonoPCA achieves SoTA performance, including a
+1.44% AP3D gain on the KITTI leaderboard and +2.80% on Omni3DOUT. Code
is available at https://github.com/alanzhangcs/MonoPCA.

Introduction
Monocular 3D object detection (Mono3D) seeks to recover object location, size, and orientation from
a single RGB image, underpinning applications from autonomous driving [71, 49, 52] to augmented
reality [54, 58]. Yet a single image fundamentally lacks metric scale: projecting a 3D scene onto a
2D plane collapses absolute distance into a one-to-many ambiguity. Depth estimation thus emerges
as an ill-posed inverse problem and, in practice, the main bottleneck of Mono3D [49].
A substantial body of work has sought to tame this ambiguity, broadly along two complementary axes.
The first injects geometric and probabilistic priors into depth prediction, including keypoint-based
reasoning in MonoFlex [90], geometric decomposition in MonoRCNN [69], and uncertainty modeling
in GUP Net [47]. The second reshapes the depth representation itself, through scale-equivariant
features in DEVIANT [31], depth-guided decoding in MonoDETR [89], complementary depth
heads in MonoCD [83], decoupled object queries in MonoDGP [62], and inter-attribute coupling
in MonoCoP [94]. Despite their architectural diversity, these methods rest on a shared foundation:
when grounded geometrically, depth is recovered through the projection relation z ≈f · H/h, with
h instantiated as the predicted 2D bounding-box height h2D [69, 47, 31, 89, 62, 94]. This shared
foundation, we find, harbors a systematic bias that has gone unexamined.
We observe that the formula z = f · H/h2D exhibits a persistent depth error even under ground-truth
inputs (Fig. 1a), and this error grows monotonically with object size, an unmistakable signature of
a structural rather than statistical flaw. Tracing the formula back to its geometric origin reveals the
cause. Under perspective projection, h2D does not encode the depth of the object center but that

A consequence of PCDepth is that depth is no longer an isolated scalar but a function of the
predicted 3D size, orientation, and 2D height. This coupling is a double-edged sword: it removes
the projection bias but propagates attribute errors into depth, making accurate attribute perception
essential. Lightweight detectors typical of Mono3D often underfit the global semantic context
required to estimate size and orientation reliably, while simply scaling up the backbone forfeits
inference efficiency. We resolve this tension with GeoAlign, a teacher–student framework in which a
high-capacity teacher is built via a Mixture-of-Backbone (MoB) that fuses a convolutional hierarchy
with Vision Foundation Models (VFMs), and is then distilled into a standard ResNet student through
Hierarchical Feature Alignment (HFA). The student inherits the teacher’s geometric perception at no
additional inference cost, fully exploiting the potential of PCDepth.
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
• We identify a systematic, projection-induced bias in the conventional geometric depth formulation:
substituting the 2D bounding-box height h2D recovers the depth of the visible front surface rather
than the object center, causing size-dependent depth underestimation overlooked by prior work.
• We propose MonoPCA, a unified framework comprising (1) PCDepth, a projection-consistent
depth formulation with orientation- and size-aware corrections that eliminates the projection bias,
and (2) GeoAlign, a teacher–student distillation framework that transfers VFM-enriched geometric
representations to a lightweight student without inference overhead.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate that MonoPCA achieves SoTA performance on four challenging
benchmarks, including a +1.44% AP3D gain on the KITTI leaderboard and +2.80% on Omni3DOUT,
with consistent improvements across object categories and distance ranges.

Related Work
Mono3D. Existing Mono3D methods fall into two architectural paradigms. (1) CNN-based meth-
ods [67] extend 2D detectors to 3D using geometric priors and structural constraints [4, 32, 90,
2


45, 51, 5], with center-based pipelines [45, 51, 78, 44] predicting 3D boxes from image centers
and others modeling 2D–3D correlations [38, 47, 90, 82]. Complementary lines explore scale-
equivariant, camera-aware, and weather-robust designs [31, 64, 95, 34, 23, 17, 55], multi-scale
FPN structures [4, 32, 3], BEV segmentation for large objects [33], and auxiliary supervision
from CAD models [9, 46, 35, 30, 41], LiDAR [50, 79, 10, 66, 61, 15, 19, 22, 42], synthetic
scenes [39, 40, 20, 57], and dense depth maps [16, 63, 25, 48, 60, 65], as well as label-efficient
training with weak, sparse, or pseudo labels [87, 27, 73, 36, 53]; we refer readers to [49] for a
comprehensive survey. (2) Transformer-based methods [8, 11, 97] bring end-to-end detection to
Mono3D [21, 81, 98, 89, 62, 80, 59, 28], with MonoDETR [89] introducing depth-guided queries,
MonoDGP [62] decoupling visual decoding from error-based depth, and MonoCoP [94] modeling
inter-attribute correlations. MonoPCA is orthogonal to these architectural advances: rather than
redesigning the detector, it targets the depth formulation that all of them share, and can be plugged
into both CNN- and transformer-based detectors as a drop-in replacement for the depth head, as we
demonstrate in Fig. 7.
Geometric Depth Estimation in Mono3D. Geometric projection has long been used to compensate
for the lack of metric scale in monocular images, relating observable 2D cues to 3D distance
through camera intrinsics [1, 69, 2, 7, 47, 89, 62, 84]. Across these methods, the projection relation
z = fH/h2D is adopted as a given, with effort directed at the surrounding pipeline (e.g., keypoint
reasoning [90], geometric priors [69], or uncertainty modeling [47]) rather than the formula itself.
While related lines of work adopt a surface-plus-offset decomposition for regression-based depth [60,
29] or refine predicted depth post hoc [96], the analytical formula and its bias remain unaddressed.
We instead identify that substituting the 2D bounding-box height for the projected center height
introduces a systematic projection bias, a structural flaw of the formula that has gone unexamined
despite its broad use [47, 89, 62, 94], and derive an orientation- and size-aware correction directly
from perspective geometry.
Knowledge Distillation in Mono3D. Distillation is a common way to inject depth knowledge
into monocular detectors, typically from LiDAR-based teachers whose inputs are unavailable at
inference [15, 42]. GeoAlign differs in that its teacher consumes the same monocular image as the
student, and it transfers VFM-enriched features for attribute perception rather than depth.
Vision Foundation Models. Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) learn transferable representations
from large-scale vision–language or self-supervised pretraining, with representative families including
CLIP [76, 86] and DINO [56, 70]. While these backbones provide strong semantic priors and have
been adapted to 3D shape understanding [91], their use in geometry-sensitive tasks such as Mono3D
remains limited [95, 96, 85]. We leverage VFMs in a teacher–student framework to construct enriched
multi-scale representations that support projection-consistent depth modeling under PCDepth.

Method
3.1
Problem Setup and Overview
Task. Given a single RGB image and known camera intrinsics, Mono3D aims to detect every object
as a 3D bounding box parameterized by its center (x, y, z), physical size (W, H, L), and yaw angle
γ in the camera coordinate frame, alongside a class label C and a 2D bounding box on the image
plane. Among these unknowns, the depth z is the dominant source of error: a small relative error in z
produces a metric displacement that scales with depth, making it the central bottleneck of Mono3D.
Approach at a glance. We pursue accurate depth from two angles (see Fig. 3). First, we revisit
the geometric depth formulation that underpins most modern detectors and identify a structural
bias overlooked by prior work (Sec. 3.2). Second, we correct this bias with PCDepth, a projection-
consistent depth formulation that derives center depth from observable 2D evidence together with
predicted 3D attributes through geometric correction (Sec. 3.3). Because PCDepth couples depth to
the predicted size and orientation, depth accuracy now hinges on attribute accuracy. We close this
loop with GeoAlign (Sec. 3.4), which distills geometry-aware representations from Vision Foundation
Models into a lightweight student, strengthening attribute perception at no inference cost.

3.2
The Front-Surface Bias of Geometric Depth
The geometric depth formulation traces back to the pinhole camera model, under which the depth of
a point at 3D height H projecting to image height h is
z = f · H
h ,
(1)
with f the focal length. In the Mono3D literature [89, 47, 62], h is instantiated by the predicted 2D
bounding-box height h2D, yielding zgeo = f · H/h2D. This substitution is intuitive but, as we now
show (Fig. 2a), geometrically inconsistent.
Diagnosis. Eq. (1) is exact only when h is the projection of the line segment passing through the
object center perpendicular to the optical axis. For an object with non-negligible thickness, this
center-aligned segment is not what the 2D bounding box captures. As shown in Fig. 2a, the 3D
bottom center (red) is self-occluded by a nearer surface point (green) that projects higher on the
image plane; the visible bottom of the box corresponds to a still-closer surface point (yellow). The
effective center-aligned projective height h′
2D therefore satisfies h′
2D < h2D, and zgeo recovers the
depth of the visible front surface rather than that of the object center. We refer to the resulting offset
as the front-surface bias; Fig. 1a shows that it grows monotonically with object size and persists even
under ground-truth f, H, and h2D, confirming a structural rather than statistical origin.

3.4
GeoAlign: Strengthening Attribute Perception
PCDepth ties depth accuracy to the predicted size and yaw, shifting the bottleneck to attribute
perception. Both attributes are difficult to recover from a single image: size requires category-
level priors inferred without metric scale, and yaw relies on subtle appearance cues such as part
configuration and self-shading. Such cues call for representations that combine fine local geometry
with long-range semantic context, a regime where Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) excel, yet naive
backbone replacement would multiply inference cost. GeoAlign resolves this tension by distilling a
high-capacity teacher, formed by fusing a VFM with the convolutional backbone, into a lightweight
student, transferring attribute perception (Fig. 4) without inference overhead.

Experiments
Datasets. We evaluate MonoPCA on four representative Mono3D benchmarks. KITTI [18] contains
7,481 training and 7,518 testing images; following [12], the training split is further divided into 3,712
training and 3,769 validation images. nuScenes [6] provides 28,130 training and 6,019 validation
front-camera images, with annotations converted to KITTI format following [31]. Waymo [75]
provides 52,386 training and 39,848 validation front-view samples [31]. Omni3D [3] aggregates
multiple 3D detection datasets into a unified benchmark; we evaluate on Omni3DOUT, Omni3DKITTI,
and Omni3DnuScenes, and additionally report Omni3DSmall
OUT , Omni3DLarge
OUT , and Omni3DCar
OUT splits to
disentangle performance across object scales.
Evaluation Metrics. For KITTI and nuScenes, we follow [72] and report Car AP3D and APBEV at
IoU3D ≥0.7, with Moderate as the primary setting, and additionally report IoU3D ≥0.5 on nuScenes.
For Waymo, we report AP3D and the heading-aware APH3D [66, 31] at IoU3D ≥0.5 and 0.7 over
distance ranges [0, 30), [30, 50), and [50, ∞) meters. For Omni3D, we report AP averaged over
IoU3D thresholds τ ∈[0.05, 0.10, . . . , 0.50].
Implementation Details. We build MonoPCA on MonoCoP [94]. The student uses a ResNet-50
backbone, while the teacher fuses ResNet-50 with EVA-CLIP [76] through MoB and is discarded
after training. We use AdamW with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10−4 (2 × 10−5 for the teacher
backbone) and a weight decay of 10−4, and halve the learning rate at epochs 85, 125, 165, and 205.
The alignment loss weight is λalign = 5 (Appendix G.4). All models are trained with a batch size
of 16 on eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs; a full KITTI run takes about 12 hours. The full architecture,
training losses, and loss weights are given in Appendix A.

Standard 3D Benchmarks
We evaluate MonoPCA on three standard Mono3D benchmarks (KITTI [18], nuScenes [6], and
Waymo [75]), where each model is trained and tested on a single dataset.
KITTI Leaderboard (Test) Results. The KITTI leaderboard withholds ground-truth annotations and
evaluates submissions on the official server, providing a fully blind evaluation protocol. As shown
in Tab. 1, MonoPCA achieves new SoTA performance across all 3D and BEV metrics without any
extra supervision. Under the primary AP3D Mod. metric, MonoPCA reaches 20.55%, outperforming
MonoCoP by +1.44%. The improvement holds across all difficulty levels and extends to BEV
evaluation, indicating more accurate 3D localization. MonoPCA also surpasses methods that rely on
additional supervision such as LiDAR or depth maps (e.g., MonoTAKD and OPA-3D) on all AP3D
metrics. More results are provided in Appendix B.
KITTI Val Results. We observe similar trends on the Val split. MonoPCA achieves 26.22% under
AP3D Mod., exceeding MonoCoP by +2.24%. The gains hold across all difficulty levels, including
the Hard setting dominated by occluded and distant objects, suggesting that they stem from better
geometric depth rather than from a subset of easy cases.
nuScenes Val Results. As shown in Tab. 2, the gains carry over to nuScenes: MonoPCA leads on
every 3D and BEV metric and improves AP3D Mod. by +2.32% over MonoCoP at IoU ≥0.7. The
margin widens at IoU ≥0.5 (+4.20%), suggesting that PCDepth removes a systematic depth offset
that pushes predictions out of the matching range, rather than only tightening well-localized boxes.
Waymo Val Results. On Waymo (Appendix D), MonoPCA achieves the best overall AP3D and
APH3D at IoU ≥0.5, with gains concentrated within 50 m (+4.77% and +1.61% AP3D over Mono-
CoP at 0–30 m and 30–50 m). At these distances depth noise is small and the front-surface offset is a
dominant error source, so correcting it pays off most; farther away, noise that grows with depth limits
the benefit of any correction.

Ablation Study
We conduct ablation studies on the KITTI Val split, reporting AP3D under IoU3D ≥0.7 with the Mod.
setting as the primary metric. Additional results are provided in Appendix G.
Component Analysis. Tab. 5a reports the contribution of each proposed component. PCDepth
and GeoAlign alone improve the baseline by +1.09% and +0.75%, respectively. Combined, they
yield a +2.24% gain, exceeding the sum of the individual gains (+1.84%). The two components are
therefore complementary by design: PCDepth couples depth to the predicted size and orientation,
making attribute accuracy the new bottleneck, and GeoAlign addresses this bottleneck by distilling
VFM-enriched representations into the student.

front-surface bias since h2D encodes the projected front face rather than the object center. Replacing
h2D with the bias-free h′

5
Conclusion
We identify a systematic, projection-induced bias in the conventional geometric depth formulation:
substituting the 2D bounding-box height h2D recovers the depth of the visible front surface rather
than the object center. We address this with MonoPCA, which corrects the bias analytically via
PCDepth and supplies the attribute fidelity it requires through GeoAlign, a teacher-student distillation
strategy that transfers VFM-enriched geometric representations into a lightweight student. MonoPCA
achieves SoTA performance on KITTI, nuScenes, Waymo, and Omni3D without additional inference
cost. A consequence of this formulation is that depth is no longer regressed in isolation but coupled in
closed form to predicted size and orientation, making attribute accuracy a direct factor in depth quality.
We hope this work motivates further study of projection-consistent depth modeling in Mono3D.

Architecture and Implementation Details of MonoPCA
A.1
Model Details
We provide a detailed description of the architecture of MonoPCA. Built upon MonoCoP [94],
MonoPCA adopts a more compact design, as illustrated in Fig. A1. Compared with previous methods,
MonoPCA simplifies the overall framework by jointly encoding and fusing depth and visual features.
As a result, the model only requires a standard visual encoder and decoder, leading to a cleaner and
more efficient architecture.
Specifically, it consists of an image backbone for extracting visual features, a depth predictor for
generating the depth feature map, a visual encoder that processes the fused image and depth features,
and a visual decoder that further refines the encoded representations. On top of these components,
several prediction heads are employed to estimate the 3D object attributes, while the proposed
PCDepth formulation is used to produce the final depth prediction.
Image Backbone. Our framework employs two types of image backbones. The teacher model adopts
the proposed MoB, whereas the student model uses a standard ResNet backbone. Although their
internal architectures differ, both backbones share the same input–output interface. Given an input

A.3
Implementation Details
We train the network using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10−4 and
a weight decay of 1 × 10−4. The teacher backbone is optimized with a smaller learning rate of
2 × 10−5, while the student network uses a learning rate of 2 × 10−4. The learning rate follows a
step decay schedule with a decay factor of 0.5, applied at epochs 85, 125, 165, and 205, with a total
of 250 training epochs. All experiments are conducted on a single node with eight NVIDIA A100
GPUs (80GB) and a batch size of 16. A full training run on KITTI takes approximately 12 hours,
while training on the larger Waymo and Omni3D benchmarks takes one to two days.

Additional Ablation Study
G.1
Things We Tried That Did Not Make it into the Main Algorithm
From Deformable Attention to Full Attention. Following MonoCoP, we adopt deformable attention
in both the visual encoder and the decoder. Compared with vanilla Transformer attention, deformable
attention is significantly more efficient, as it attends to a sparse set of sampled key locations rather
than all spatial tokens. However, this design also limits the ability to capture global context. Motivated
by this observation, and by the success of full attention in modeling long-range and cross-modal
dependencies in other domains [92, 93], we explored replacing deformable attention with standard
full attention in both the encoder and decoder to enable global feature interactions.
In practice, however, this modification did not lead to improvements. Using full attention substantially
increases the computational cost and memory consumption due to its quadratic complexity with
respect to the number of tokens. More importantly, we observed a consistent performance drop
compared with the deformable attention design. We conjecture that the sparse sampling mechanism in
deformable attention provides an implicit inductive bias that helps the model focus on geometrically
relevant regions, whereas full attention introduces excessive global interactions that may dilute these
spatial cues. Therefore, we retain the deformable attention architecture in the final design due to its
superior efficiency and better empirical performance.
Predicting h′
2D for GeoDepth. To eliminate the projection-induced bias in the GeoDepth formulation,
a straightforward idea is to directly predict the projective height h′
2D and use it in place of the
conventional 2D bounding box height h2D. This modification is conceptually simple and can correct
the bias in the geometric relation.
However, in practice this approach does not improve detection performance and often leads to
unstable training. We observe that h′
2D is inherently depth-dependent and varies with object pose
and occlusion, making it difficult to estimate reliably from image features alone. As a result,
direct regression of h′
2D introduces additional prediction uncertainty, which propagates to the depth
estimation and degrades the final 3D detection accuracy. Therefore, instead of directly predicting

Integrating VFM without FPN. Another design we explored is to directly integrate the last-layer
features from the Vision Foundation Model (VFM) without constructing a multi-scale feature pyramid.
This approach simplifies the architecture and reduces additional feature processing, as the detection
head operates directly on the high-level semantic representation produced by the VFM.
However, we find that relying on a single-scale feature significantly degrades detection performance.
Object detection, especially in Mono3D settings, benefits from multi-scale representations to capture
objects at different sizes and distances. Using only the final VFM feature map removes this multi-
scale structure and weakens the model’s ability to localize objects accurately. Therefore, in the final
design we retain the FPN-based multi-scale feature construction when integrating VFM features,
which provides richer spatial representations and leads to consistently better detection performance.
Using Pixel Shuffle for Token Reduction. We also explored reducing the number of image tokens
using a pixel-shuffle style operation. Specifically, neighboring feature maps are first merged and
then projected through a lightweight projector to reduce the channel dimension. This operation
effectively decreases the number of image tokens and thus lowers the computational cost of subsequent
transformer layers.
However, we observe that this design consistently leads to a noticeable performance drop, typically
around 2% ∼3% in detection accuracy. We conjecture that merging neighboring features in this
manner removes fine-grained spatial details that are important for accurate object localization in
Mono3D detection. Therefore, we do not adopt this token reduction strategy.
Replacing PositionEmbeddingSine with RoPE. We also explored replacing the commonly used
PositionEmbeddingSine with Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), which has been widely adopted in
Transformer architectures for its ability to encode relative positional information.
However, this modification does not improve detection performance in our setting. In practice, we
observe a slight performance degradation compared with the original sine positional embedding. We
conjecture that RoPE is more suitable for sequence modeling tasks, while dense prediction tasks
such as Mono3D detection rely more heavily on explicit spatial priors. The PositionEmbeddingSine
embedding provides a stable and well-structured spatial encoding for image features, which appears
to be more effective for our detection framework. As a result, we retain PositionEmbeddingSine in
the final model design.

H
Limitations
MonoPCA has three main limitations. First, the closed-form correction in Eq. (6) assumes pinhole
projection and an upright box, and treats h2D as the tight projection of the eight 3D corners; deviations
from this last assumption (truncation, occlusion, annotation tightness) account for the residual error
of PCDepth under oracle inputs in Fig. 1a. Second, depth accuracy is now coupled to predicted size
and yaw, so residual attribute errors, especially on heavily occluded or distant objects, still propagate
into depth. Third, our evaluation focuses on outdoor driving benchmarks (KITTI, nuScenes, Waymo,
and Omni3D), and the behavior of MonoPCA under substantially different camera setups has not
been studied.
I
Broader Impact
MonoPCA targets Mono3D, which underpins applications in autonomous driving, robotics, and
augmented reality. By improving depth accuracy without additional sensors or supervision, our
work may extend the reach of camera-only 3D perception in settings where LiDAR is impractical
or cost-prohibitive. At the same time, any 3D detection technology carries potential for misuse in
unauthorized surveillance, and imperfect perception deployed in safety-critical systems can lead to
harm. Moreover, because GeoAlign distills representations from a foundation model, the student may
inherit spurious correlations or biases present in the teacher [13]. We position MonoPCA as research
on geometric depth modeling rather than a deployment-ready system: real-world use should be paired
with rigorous validation, redundant sensing where feasible, and adherence to domain-specific safety
standards.
