A forensic evaluation framework for egocentric foundation
models: an EFM3D case study for crime scene reconstruction

aSchool of Computing, Engineering & Technology, Robert Gordon University, Aberdeen, United
Kingdom
ABSTRACT
Foundation models increasingly combine geometry, semantics, and spatial context for three-dimensional scene
understanding, but their suitability for forensic crime scene reconstruction remains unclear. This study presents
a structured forensic evaluation framework for egocentric foundation models and demonstrates it using the
Egocentric Foundation Model for 3D (EFM3D). Four simulated indoor crime scenes were recorded with Meta
Project Aria Gen 1 at Robert Gordon University’s East Lodge House forensic training facility. The framework
evaluates room-scale reconstruction, semantic scene understanding, computational performance, and forensic
suitability. Across the four scenes, EFM3D generated meshes averaging 12,668 ± 2,430 vertices and 24,838 ± 4,903
faces, with 39,529 recorded semantic predictions spanning 21–24 categories at a mean confidence of 0.40. Because
reference meshes and expert-labelled 3D ground truth were unavailable, these statistics are treated as descriptive
outputs rather than measures of geometric or detection accuracy. Final inference runs processed 1,388 ± 203
snippets per scene at 0.69 ± 0.01 samples/s and required 50.77 ± 6.12 minutes per recording. A seven-criterion
capability profile indicated stronger support for room geometry, furniture localisation, scene context, and spatial
relationships than for fine-scale forensic evidence. The results position EFM3D as a complementary tool for
contextual documentation and scene review rather than a replacement for high-fidelity forensic capture. The
framework provides a reproducible basis for evaluating future egocentric foundation models in specialised forensic
settings.
Keywords: Foundation models, egocentric vision, machine vision, digital forensics, crime scene reconstruction,
EFM3D, Project Aria, 3D scene understanding
1. INTRODUCTION
Foundation models are reshaping machine vision by bringing geometry, semantics, and spatial reasoning into
increasingly unified representations. In contrast to conventional pipelines that treat reconstruction, localisation,
and object recognition as separate stages, recent multimodal models can reason jointly about the structure and
content of a scene. This shift is particularly relevant in applications where a useful representation must capture
not only where objects are located, but also what they are and how they relate to the surrounding environment.
Digital forensic investigation is one such application. Crime scene reconstruction preserves spatial relationships
after a physical scene has been released and supports later examination, communication, training, and courtroom
presentation.1 Photography, sketches, manual measurements, and total-station surveys remain important because
they are established and defensible, yet they often leave investigators with fragmented records rather than a single
explorable representation.1–4 Three-dimensional capture has improved this process: photogrammetry, terrestrial
laser scanning (TLS), and LiDAR can preserve geometry with high accuracy,3,5–7 while Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) can produce photorealistic scene representations and flexible novel
views.8–10 Even so, these approaches remain primarily concerned with geometry and appearance. Semantic
interpretation identifying objects and understanding their contextual role is commonly added later or performed
manually11,12 and remains an active area of methodological research in its own right, particularly with respect to
loss-function design for segmentation accuracy.13

Wearable sensing offers a different acquisition model. Meta Project Aria records synchronised visual, inertial,
calibration, and other sensor streams from the wearer’s perspective.14 Aria Machine Perception Services (MPS)
convert these observations into trajectories, visual inertial odometry, and semi-dense geometry. EFM3D builds on
this information through Egocentric Voxel Lifting (EVL), jointly predicting occupancy, object locations, and
semantic context in three dimensions.15 In principle, a natural investigator walkthrough can therefore support
both scene reconstruction and contextual interpretation.
The unresolved question is whether capabilities learned from general indoor environments transfer to forensic
scenes. EFM3D was developed and evaluated primarily on everyday activities, whereas crime scenes may contain
disturbed layouts, restricted viewpoints, severe occlusion, reflective surfaces, and small evidence whose position
and condition must be preserved accurately.1,15 A model can therefore perform well at room-scale understanding
while still failing on the details that matter most during evidential examination. Conventional machine-vision
benchmarks do not fully capture this distinction. From a digital-forensics perspective, the resulting egocentric
recordings, reconstructed geometry, semantic predictions, and spatial metadata form digital representations that
can support subsequent scene examination, visualisation, and investigative review.
To address this gap, the present study introduces a structured forensic evaluation framework for egocentric
foundation models and demonstrates it through an EFM3D case study using four simulated indoor crime
scenes. The framework considers four complementary dimensions: room-scale reconstruction, semantic scene
understanding, computational performance, and forensic suitability. Its purpose is not to replace established
benchmark metrics, but to extend them with criteria that reflect practical forensic use.
The contribution of this work lies not in modifying EFM3D’s architecture, but in introducing and demonstrating
a forensic-domain evaluation methodology that separates conventional model outputs from the requirements of
forensic use. The main contributions are:
1. a structured forensic evaluation framework for assessing egocentric foundation models in crime scene recon-
struction;
2. an EFM3D case study based on a four-room Project Aria dataset collected within a dedicated forensic training
facility;
3. a domain-specific evaluation methodology that distinguishes descriptive model outputs from forensic accuracy
and operational readiness; and
4. evidence-based recommendations for forensic datasets, evidence-aware semantics, hybrid reconstruction, and
future operational validation.
The remainder of the paper reviews relevant reconstruction and egocentric-vision research, identifies the
forensic evaluation gap, presents the framework and experimental methodology, reports the results, and discusses
their implications and limitations.
2. RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAP
2.1 Digital Crime Scene Reconstruction
Digital reconstruction allows investigators to preserve spatial information after a physical scene has been released
and supports measurement, review, and communication.1,2 Conventional documentation remains indispensable,
but its fragmented nature can make complex scenes difficult to revisit and interpret as a whole.2,3 Photogrammetry,
TLS, and LiDAR address this limitation by producing metrically useful 3D models,5–7 while recent forensic
platforms have begun integrating 3D reconstruction with analytical tools for scene and trajectory interpretation.16
NeRF and 3DGS further improve visual realism and novel-view rendering,8–10,17 with recent work extending
3DGS toward photorealistic immersive VR applications.18
These techniques differ in acquisition requirements and fidelity, yet most treat semantic understanding as
a separate problem. A reconstruction may show where an object is located without identifying what it is or
whether it has investigative relevance. Scene-labelling approaches can add semantics,12 but forensic interpretation
still depends heavily on expert review.11 This creates a clear opportunity for systems that combine geometry
with object-level reasoning.


2.2 Egocentric Wearable Sensing and Foundation Models
Egocentric devices capture a continuous first-person record as the wearer moves naturally through an environment.
Project Aria combines RGB and monochrome scene cameras, inertial measurement units, eye cameras, microphones,
and calibration data in a synchronised research platform.14 MPS estimates trajectories and semi-dense geometry,
providing the spatial basis for downstream perception.
EFM3D extends this pipeline toward learned 3D scene understanding. Its EVL component lifts image features
into a shared voxel representation from which the model predicts occupancy, oriented 3D bounding boxes, and
semantic categories.15 The original EFM3D evaluation used Aria Digital Twin (ADT) and Aria Everyday
Activities (AEA), which capture furnished apartments, offices, and ordinary activities rather than staged forensic
evidence.19,20 This domain difference matters because models trained on everyday environments are naturally
better represented for rooms, furniture, and common objects than for bloodstains, fingerprints, cartridge cases,
fibres, or other evidence classes. Recent work on multimodal and geospatial forensic reconstruction further
illustrates the need to preserve evidence-specific detail alongside scene-level geometry.4,21
2.3 Research Gap
Existing evaluations of egocentric foundation models emphasise reconstruction, detection, segmentation, and
scene understanding within general indoor environments. Those benchmarks are valuable for measuring technical
progress, but they do not ask whether outputs preserve evidential detail, provide meaningful forensic context,
or remain practical within an investigative workflow. Forensic suitability therefore cannot be inferred from
benchmark performance alone.
To our knowledge, structured evaluation of EFM3D under these forensic requirements remains limited. The
present work addresses that gap by combining conventional machine-vision measurements with an explicit forensic
suitability assessment. The resulting framework is intended as a reproducible evaluation methodology rather than
a claim of universal forensic validation.
3. FORENSIC EVALUATION FRAMEWORK AND EXPERIMENTAL
METHODOLOGY
3.1 Framework Motivation and Overview
A forensic reconstruction system must do more than recover visually plausible geometry. It should preserve
the spatial arrangement of the scene, provide semantically useful context, operate within a practical processing
workflow, and avoid overstating evidential detail that it cannot reliably represent. These requirements motivate a
broader evaluation than geometry-only or detection-only benchmarks.
The proposed framework therefore organises assessment around four dimensions: room-scale reconstruction,
semantic scene understanding, computational performance, and forensic suitability. Figure 1 shows how
these dimensions connect the model outputs to an overall operational assessment. The framework is deliberately
model-agnostic: EFM3D is used here as the case study, but the same dimensions can support future evaluations
of other egocentric foundation models.

3.2 Objectives and Research Questions
The framework is demonstrated using the official EFM3D pipeline and four simulated indoor crime scenes. Table 1
contrasts the forensic training-facility data with the principal datasets originally used to evaluate EFM3D.
The four research questions correspond directly to the framework dimensions:
RQ1 What room-scale reconstruction characteristics does EFM3D exhibit within simulated forensic environments?
RQ2 What semantic object predictions does EFM3D produce in simulated forensic environments, and what
limitations are evident for forensic scene understanding?
RQ3 What computational characteristics are observed when processing Project Aria recordings?
RQ4 To what extent are the generated outputs suitable for forensic crime scene documentation and interpretation?


3.3 Crime Scene Dataset and Acquisition
Data were collected at East Lodge House, Robert Gordon University’s forensic training facility. Four furnished
rooms with different layouts and object distributions were treated as independent simulated crime scenes. The
available dataset records room-level capture characteristics but does not contain a formal scene-by-scene inventory
of staged evidence; consequently, no additional scene composition or evidence-count claims are made here. Each
recording followed the same broad protocol: the wearer moved naturally around the room, covered the principal
viewpoints, and avoided abrupt head movements that could disrupt visual–inertial tracking.

3.5 Processing Pipeline
The official Meta workflow was retained without architectural modification (Fig. 3). MPS first generated camera
trajectories, visual–inertial odometry, and semi-dense geometry. EFM3D then applied EVL to form a 3D feature
representation and produced a reconstructed mesh, oriented bounding boxes, and semantic visualisations.14,15
These outputs were subsequently assessed using the four dimensions of the proposed framework.
Figure 3. Project Aria acquisition, MPS pre-processing, EFM3D inference, and forensic evaluation pipeline.
3.6 Evaluation and Analysis
Quantitative analysis used recording duration, frame count, mesh statistics, semantic predictions, confidence
scores, processing logs, throughput, and inference time. Qualitative assessment was used where the dataset lacked
manually annotated forensic ground truth. Because no reference meshes or expert-labelled 3D object annotations
were available, mAP, IoU, F1-score, and absolute reconstruction error could not be calculated. The analysis
therefore does not claim benchmark-level detection accuracy; instead, it characterises consistency across scenes
and evaluates practical scene-documentation value.


For the forensic-suitability dimension, seven operational criteria were examined using a categorical capability
profile. Each criterion was classified as supported, partially supported, limited, or not supported on the basis of
the observed outputs. These categories are descriptive rather than numerical: they make the assessment criteria
explicit and expose relative strengths and limitations within this case study without implying a validated forensic-
readiness score. No validated weighting scheme or multi-rater instrument currently exists for this application.
Future work should establish expert-derived criteria and inter-rater reliability before a formal forensic-readiness
instrument is considered.

consistency only: without a reference mesh they cannot establish geometric accuracy, completeness, or superiority.
The effective acquisition rate remained approximately 30 fps across all recordings, indicating stable capture.

The third framework dimension examines whether the workflow is practical for offline forensic processing. Table 7
reports the final inference measurements. EFM3D processed between 1,105 and 1,554 snippets per scene at a

increased with the amount of data processed, but throughput remained tightly clustered across the four scenes,
indicating stable computational behaviour under the final experimental configuration.

4.5 Relationship to Existing Reconstruction Approaches
EFM3D addresses a different part of the reconstruction workflow from geometry-first techniques. Photogrammetry,
TLS/LiDAR, NeRF, and 3DGS primarily preserve geometry or appearance, whereas EFM3D combines wearable
egocentric acquisition with learned 3D occupancy and semantic object predictions.2,3,5–10,15
The methods
were not benchmarked on a common reference scene in this study; consequently, no ranking of their geometry,
speed, or evidence-preservation performance is made. The present results instead motivate a hybrid evaluation
question: whether semantic egocentric scene understanding can complement established high-fidelity capture
while preserving clear boundaries between model inference and evidential observation. Comparative experiments
using common reference scenes and ground-truth geometry are required to test that proposition.


4.6 Summary of Findings
Application of the proposed framework shows that EFM3D produced interpretable room-scale reconstructions,
common-object semantic predictions, and stable offline computational behaviour across the four simulated scenes.
The framework also exposes an important domain gap: forensic-specific and fine-scale evidence was not adequately
represented in the observed outputs. These findings support further investigation of egocentric foundation models
as complementary tools for contextual documentation and scene review, while leaving evidential accuracy and
operational readiness to be established through ground-truth and practitioner-centred validation.
5. DISCUSSION
5.1 Interpretation of the Findings
The case study demonstrates that a general-purpose egocentric foundation model can transfer useful room-level
knowledge to a forensic setting without architectural modification. Across all four recordings, EFM3D produced
representations of the dominant indoor structure and predictions corresponding to common furniture, combining
room-scale geometry with semantic context. These observed capabilities motivate further evaluation for tasks
such as scene familiarisation, collaborative review, training, and preparation of immersive scene interfaces.
The results also show why good scene understanding should not be confused with evidential completeness. The
observed pattern is consistent with EFM3D’s general indoor-domain training and evaluation context: furniture
and common indoor objects were more readily represented, whereas forensic-specific and trace-level evidence was
absent or under-resolved. A coherent room-scale mesh can therefore coexist with the loss of precisely the details
that make a reconstruction evidentially useful. The distinction is central to forensic deployment.
Wearable acquisition is nevertheless attractive. A natural walkthrough can preserve a continuous first-person
record without occupying the investigator’s hands or requiring a scanner to be repositioned at multiple stations.
In time-sensitive settings, this may provide a rapid contextual record before targeted high-resolution evidence
capture begins. The present findings therefore support investigating EFM3D as a complementary rather than
substitutive component of forensic scene-documentation workflows.
5.2 Implications for Machine Vision
The findings have implications beyond digital forensics. Foundation models are commonly judged using general
benchmarks that emphasise reconstruction, detection, segmentation, or localisation. Those measures remain
essential, but they provide only a partial view of suitability once a system enters a specialised or safety-critical
domain. Application-specific requirements can expose limitations that remain invisible in benchmark scores.
The proposed framework illustrates one way to extend evaluation without discarding conventional metrics. By
combining technical measurements with explicit operational criteria, it separates model capability from domain
readiness. Similar reasoning may be relevant wherever foundation models are expected to support decisions in
environments where missing small details, misinterpreting context, or overstating confidence carries practical
consequences.
5.3 Implications for Digital Forensics
For forensic practice, the immediate value of egocentric foundation models lies in contextual documentation
rather than evidential replacement. EFM3D can help an examiner understand room layout, navigate a digital
scene, and identify major objects, but high-fidelity photography, photogrammetry, TLS, LiDAR, and specialist
evidence imaging remain necessary for precise measurement and detailed examination.2,3,5–7
A hybrid workflow is therefore more realistic: wearable egocentric capture provides rapid scene-level context,
while targeted high-resolution methods preserve evidentially important regions. Future systems could combine
these complementary representations within a common spatial frame, allowing semantic scene understanding
to guide investigators toward areas that require more detailed capture without presenting inferred semantics as
evidence.


5.4 Threats to Validity
Several factors constrain the interpretation of the study. First, the evaluation used four simulated indoor
crime scenes from a single training facility. The scenes provide controlled forensic context, but they do not
represent outdoor, industrial, public, or highly dynamic environments. Second, the study evaluates a single
egocentric foundation model using the official Meta implementation without task-specific fine-tuning on forensic
datasets. Although EFM3D represents one of the most advanced publicly available egocentric foundation models,
alternative models or domain-adapted versions may demonstrate different reconstruction characteristics and
semantic capabilities.
Third, the dataset does not contain manually annotated 3D forensic ground truth. This prevented calculation
of mAP, IoU, pose error, completeness, or metric reconstruction accuracy and required the study to combine
objective output statistics with structured qualitative assessment. Fourth, the forensic suitability ratings were
produced as an exploratory assessment rather than through multiple independent practitioners. Inter-rater
reliability and alternative weighting strategies therefore remain to be validated. The recordings were also produced
using a single acquisition procedure, so the effects of operator behaviour, viewing trajectory, walking speed,
viewing distance, and scene coverage were not independently evaluated. Finally, the study addresses technical
suitability rather than chain of custody, evidential provenance, explainability, repeatability, legal admissibility, or
end-user effectiveness.
These limitations do not invalidate the case study, but they define its scope. The proposed framework should
be regarded as a structured starting point for forensic evaluation rather than a validated standard.
5.5 Directions for Future Work
Future development should focus on four priorities. First, larger multi-site egocentric datasets are needed with
expert labels for evidence type, 3D position, visibility, and uncertainty. Second, domain adaptation should
introduce evidence-aware categories and contextual relationships rather than relying solely on everyday-object
vocabularies. Third, comparative studies should apply the same framework to photogrammetry, LiDAR, NeRF,
3DGS, and emerging foundation models using common reference scenes, complemented by systematic comparison
of object detection architectures suited to evidence-aware semantic categories.23 Finally, practitioner-centred
validation should assess inter-rater reliability, uncertainty communication, provenance, and XR-based review with
investigators.11
6. CONCLUSION
This study presented a structured forensic evaluation framework for egocentric foundation models and demonstrated
it through an EFM3D case study using four Project Aria recordings from simulated indoor crime scenes. The
framework brings together room-scale reconstruction, semantic scene understanding, computational performance,
and forensic suitability so that technical outputs can be interpreted in relation to operational forensic requirements.
The case study showed that EFM3D produced interpretable room-scale geometry and predictions for major
furniture while adding scene-level semantic context. Inference behaviour was stable across the four recordings,
with an average runtime of 50.77 ± 6.12 minutes under the final experimental configuration. At the same time,
the evaluation exposed a clear evidential boundary: fine-scale evidence and forensic-specific semantics were
not adequately represented in the observed outputs for detailed evidential examination. Within the conditions
evaluated here, the results support considering EFM3D primarily for contextual documentation and scene review
rather than as a replacement for validated high-precision capture methods.
More broadly, the findings reinforce the need to evaluate foundation models in the operational settings for
which they are intended. As these models move beyond general benchmarks into specialised and safety-critical
domains, application-specific evaluation will be essential for distinguishing technical capability from practical
readiness. The proposed forensic evaluation framework provides a structured basis for assessing future egocentric
foundation models within crime scene reconstruction and for designing comparative evaluations with ground-truth
geometry, evidence-level annotations, and practitioner assessment.
