Abstract
Hand avatar reconstruction typically relies on multi-view capture or per-subject
optimization, limiting its scalability. We introduce InstantHand, a feed-forward
framework that constructs an animatable 3D Gaussian hand avatar from a single
image using off-the-shelf MANO, camera, and mask estimates, without test-time
optimization. Image-averaged losses provide insufficient supervision for alpha
leakage, which thickens thin finger contours. To address this issue, we propose a
Soft Alpha Band regularizer that concentrates supervision on silhouette boundaries.
Furthermore, colors regressed from image features can drift toward a learned prior
even in observed regions. Our Reliability-gated Appearance Transfer preserves
observed appearance by transferring source colors according to each Gaussian’s
rendering contribution, while completing unobserved regions using the learned
prior conditioned on global source identity. InstantHand constructs an avatar in
2.84 seconds and renders at 161 FPS, matching per-subject-optimized baselines on
InterHand2.6M and remaining competitive in zero-shot evaluation on HanCo.

Introduction
The rapid evolution of digital human technologies is transforming immersive interaction across
augmented and virtual reality, telepresence, gaming, and social communication. This progress has
been driven by the combination of parametric human models, such as FLAME [1] for faces, SMPL [2]

and SMPL-X [3] for bodies, and MANO [4] for hands, with high-fidelity neural rendering techniques
including NeRF [5] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [6]. Among these components, hands play
a central role as the primary interface for fine-grained interaction and dexterous manipulation [7–
11]. However, despite their importance, creating personalized, animatable, and photorealistic hand
avatars from minimal input remains a key challenge for scalable digital human systems. While
substantial progress has been made in single-image 3D avatar reconstruction for head avatars [12–14]
and full-body avatars [15–18], high-fidelity hand avatar modeling from a single image remains
comparatively underexplored and particularly challenging. Hands are highly articulated, thin, and
frequently self-occluded [7, 8, 19], which makes reconstruction from sparse observations inherently
ill-posed. Existing hand avatar pipelines address this ill-posedness through additional observations
or per-subject optimization. Most methods rely on multi-view capture or monocular video with
subject-specific fitting [20–22]; single-image approaches still require optimization-based inversion
at inference [23]; and Gaussian-based methods remain dependent on multi-view or video-based
reconstruction [24]. Consequently, scalable single-image hand avatar reconstruction without test-time
optimization remains largely unresolved.
To eliminate the dependence on dense observations and costly test-time optimization, we aim to
directly predict an animatable hand avatar from a single image, given off-the-shelf MANO, camera,
and mask estimates, in a feed-forward manner. We adopt 3DGS [6, 25] as the output representation
for its efficient rendering and controllable geometry and appearance. However, feed-forward Gaussian
avatar methods developed for heads [14] and bodies [17] do not transfer directly to hands. First, hands
are thin, low-volume, and highly articulated, so even minor errors in Gaussian position, scale, or
orientation produce over-expanded or overlapping primitives, thickness artifacts, and degraded fidelity
under novel poses. Second, appearance from a single image is ambiguous: existing feed-forward
pipelines [14, 17] regress appearance directly from image features and tend to fall back on learned
priors even in observed regions, weakening input-specific cues such as skin tone and fine texture,
while unobserved regions must still be completed plausibly. Feed-forward hand avatar reconstruction
therefore requires (i) geometry stabilization for thin, articulated structures and (ii) source-conditioned
appearance modeling that preserves reliable input evidence while completing missing regions from
priors.
To address these challenges, we propose InstantHand (Fig. 1), a feed-forward 3DGS framework for
single-image animatable hand avatar reconstruction. For geometry, we constrain Gaussian placement,
scale, and orientation around MANO anchors and supervise rendered alpha on the silhouette band
to keep thin fingers compact. For appearance, we introduce Reliability-gated Appearance Transfer,
where Gaussians supported by input observations are selectively enhanced with reliable local ap-
pearance cues to preserve input-specific details such as skin tone and fine textures. For unobserved
regions, appearance is inferred using the learned prior together with a global identity representation,
enabling consistent completion under occlusion. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We present InstantHand, a feed-forward framework that reconstructs an animatable canonical
3D Gaussian hand avatar from a single image without per-subject optimization.
• We propose a Soft Alpha Band (SAB) regularizer that concentrates alpha supervision on
silhouette boundaries, reducing alpha leakage and preserving thin finger structures.
• We introduce Reliability-gated Appearance Transfer, which preserves observed appearance
based on each Gaussian’s rendering contribution while completing unobserved regions using
the learned prior and a global source identity.
• InstantHand achieves reconstruction quality comparable to per-subject-optimized baselines
and competitive zero-shot performance, with avatar construction in 2.84 s and rendering at
161 FPS.

Related Work
Hand Pose and Shape Estimation. Monocular 3D hand pose and mesh estimation provides the
geometric foundation for many hand-centric vision systems [26–37]. These methods either predict
mesh vertices directly or regress the parameters of the MANO model [4] from RGB images [26, 27].
For example, METRO [29] and Mesh Graphormer [30] use transformers to regress the vertices of a
MANO-topology hand mesh from a single image. More recent methods, including HaMeR [34] and
WiLoR [37], regress MANO parameters together with camera parameters from unconstrained images.

Our work takes off-the-shelf pose, camera, and mask estimates as input and focuses on reconstructing
and rendering an animatable hand avatar.
Hand Avatar Reconstruction. Hand avatar reconstruction aims to recover subject-specific geometry
and appearance for rendering under novel viewpoints and poses. Most existing approaches rely on
multi-view images or monocular video sequences and employ neural implicit representations [5, 38]
or explicit mesh-based pipelines [20–22, 39–41]. Representative methods include HandAvatar [20],
HARP [22], and LiveHand [21], which adopt implicit, mesh-based, and multi-view neural rendering
formulations, respectively. To reduce dependence on dense observations, single-image approaches
such as OHTA [23] incorporate learned priors for one-shot reconstruction, but still require test-time
fitting or inversion. Gaussian-based methods, including GaussianHand [24], use explicit 3D Gaussian
primitives for efficient rendering. As summarized in Table S5 of the appendix, existing methods
require either dense observations or per-subject optimization, regardless of representation. In contrast,
our method constructs an animatable Gaussian hand avatar from a single image without test-time
optimization.
Feed-Forward 3D Gaussian Avatar Reconstruction. Feed-forward avatar methods predict the
parameters of animatable 3D Gaussians from one or a few images, avoiding per-subject optimization.
This approach has been applied to full- and upper-body avatars by LHM [17] and GUAVA [42], and to
head avatars by GAGAvatar [13] and LAM [14]. However, reconstructing an animatable hand avatar
from a single image remains challenging, as thin finger boundaries are sensitive to alpha leakage and
the source view provides incomplete appearance information (Sec. 1; Appendix D). We address these
challenges with a Soft Alpha Band regularizer that targets boundary leakage and Reliability-gated
Appearance Transfer that combines observed appearance with a learned prior and a global source
identity.
3
Method
Given a single source image Is, InstantHand reconstructs an animatable 3D hand in a single forward
pass (Fig. 2). Source-image features are fused with MANO-surface query anchors to form per-
Gaussian tokens (Sec. 3.1), which parameterize a canonical Gaussian representation with surface-
anchored centers, learned scales, and base colors, while opacity and canonical rotation remain
fixed (Sec. 3.2). Reliability-gated Appearance Transfer then refines only the Gaussian colors using
contribution-aware local evidence and a global source appearance identity (Sec. 3.3). The resulting
Gaussians are animated via MANO-driven deformation and rendered from arbitrary viewpoints. The
two-stage training objective is described in Sec. 3.4.

Source-fidelity diagnostic. Aggregate novel-pose metrics combine source-observed and unobserved
regions, obscuring the fidelity of source appearance reconstruction. To isolate the effect of the
appearance module, we render each avatar in its source view while keeping all non-color Gaussian
attributes fixed at their canonical values, so metric differences reflect only the color update. With
each evaluation frame serving in turn as the source, the module improves source-view PSNR by 1.96
dB on InterHand2.6M and 3.41 dB on zero-shot HanCo (Table S2; protocol in Appendix C.5).
Comparison with feed-forward avatar recipes. Table 4 examines whether avatar designs developed
for heads and bodies transfer to hands under matched conditions. Compared with InstantHand, the
head-recipe port loses 2.61 dB, reduces the boundary F-score from 0.698 to 0.299, and increases
alpha leakage from 0.234 to 0.608, while interior coverage remains at 1.000. This pattern localizes
the failure to thin, articulated contours rather than the hand interior, consistent with Sec. 1. The head-
recipe port also has a lower boundary F-score and greater leakage than the constrained model without
SAB, indicating that both the constrained parameterization and boundary-band supervision contribute
to boundary quality. Even with boundary supervision retained, freeing per-axis scale, rotation and
opacity jointly, or all three reduces PSNR by 0.47–0.71 dB (Appendix F.2). The body-recipe port
performs similarly to the head-recipe port, within 0.24 dB in PSNR and with a boundary F-score of
0.267 versus 0.299, despite its canonical-space regularizers.
Pose and temporal diagnostics. Grouping test frames by pose change relative to the source reveals
that quality degrades primarily with finger articulation: PSNR drops by 2.69 dB on InterHand2.6M
and 1.93 dB on HanCo between the mildest and strongest articulation thirds. Changes in viewing
direction have a smaller effect, with drops of at most 0.97 dB. Under continuous pose driving on
zero-shot HanCo, frame-to-frame changes in our renderings exceed those in the ground truth by 4.7%
on average and at most 8.0% per camera. All views and frames are rendered from a single fixed set
of canonical Gaussians, providing a shared representation across views; we assess cross-view and
temporal consistency empirically through these diagnostics and the supplementary video. Appendix J
provides the full analyses and comparisons with baselines evaluated on the same frames.
5
Conclusion
We presented InstantHand, a feed-forward framework for reconstructing an animatable 3D Gaussian
hand avatar from a single image without per-subject optimization. A MANO-anchored canonical
representation, together with the Soft Alpha Band regularizer, preserves compact, thin articulated
structures, while Reliability-gated Appearance Transfer integrates observed source appearance accord-
ing to each Gaussian’s rendering contribution and completes unobserved regions using the learned
prior and a global source identity. Once constructed, the avatar renders at 161 FPS. Its Gaussian
colors and scales remain fixed across driving poses, preserving appearance consistency. InstantHand
constructs avatars over 400× faster than optimization-based baselines while maintaining competitive
reconstruction quality in zero-shot evaluation on HanCo and under fully estimated inputs.

Implementation Details
A.1
Training Protocol
We train InstantHand in two stages, canonical representation learning followed by source-conditioned
Gaussian appearance modeling. Both stages use AdamW (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95) with a learning-rate
schedule of 2,000 warmup steps, 10,000 hold steps, and 8,000 cosine-decay steps, gradient clipping
at norm 1.0, and bfloat16 mixed precision. Training is distributed over four NVIDIA RTX A6000
GPUs.
Stage 1: canonical representation learning.
We train the MANO-anchored canonical Gaussian
representation for 10 epochs of 2,000 optimization steps each (20,000 steps in total), with a per-GPU
batch size of 6, gradient accumulation over 2 steps, a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 that decays to 0.1×
its peak, and a weight decay of 0.05. This stage takes about 25 hours. The objective of Eq. 15 of the
main paper combines mask-aware RGB reconstruction, LPIPS, silhouette supervision, Laplacian,
offset, and scale regularization, and the Soft Alpha Band (SAB) regularizer of Eq. 16, with weights
λrgb = 1.0, λlpips = 0.2, λsil = 0.6, λlap = 10,000, λoff = 0.1, and λscale = 0.75, and with
λout = 0.25 and λin = 0.05 for SAB.
Stage 2: source-conditioned appearance modeling.
We initialize from the Stage-1 checkpoint
and freeze the entire Stage-1 canonical predictor, including the base-color head frgb. The canonical
geometry and base colors are therefore fixed, and only the source-conditioned appearance module of
Sec. 3.3 of the main paper is optimized. We train for 10 epochs of 2,000 steps each with a per-GPU
batch size of 4, a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 that decays to 0.2× its peak, and a weight decay of
0.01. This stage takes about 13 hours. The objective of Eq. 17 of the main paper combines source-
view reconstruction, novel-view reconstruction, and feature-statistic consistency, with λs1 = 1.0,
λsp = 0.5, λn1 = 1.0, λnp = 0.25, and λstat = 0.05. All loss terms are defined in Sec. A.2.
Data preparation.
Each training sample consists of one source image and six target frames of
the same subject, which provides supervision under varying poses and viewpoints. All images are
cropped around the hand using the bounding box of the 2D keypoints, enlarged by a factor of 1.3,
and resized to 256 × 256, and the camera intrinsics are adjusted accordingly. When available, we
use the provided masks, MANO parameters, and camera annotations. Otherwise, we estimate hand
masks with SAM 3 [54] and MANO and camera parameters with WiLoR [37]. Left-hand samples are
flipped horizontally to keep a consistent canonical hand orientation. The same flip is applied during
training and inference, so a single network serves both hands. Dataset construction and sampling are
detailed in Sec. B.

A.3
Soft Alpha Band Details
The bands Bout and Bin of Eq. 16 of the main paper are constructed from the ground-truth silhouette
M by morphological dilation and erosion. Bout is the 2-pixel dilation band outside M, and Bin
is the interior eroded by 2 pixels, with all widths defined at 256 × 256 resolution. We use the
tolerances τout = 0.03 and τin = 0.60 and smooth-L1 (Huber) penalties ψ(·) whose quadratic-to-
linear transition lies at 0.03 for the outer term and at 0.05 for the inner term. The tolerances are
deliberately asymmetric. Alpha in the outer band is penalized above a near-zero level, whereas the
17


interior is only required to stay above 0.60, because leakage is the failure mode being targeted while
interior coverage only needs a floor.
The schedule η(n) over the optimization step n is zero for the first 5,000 steps and then increases
linearly to 1 between steps 5,000 and 12,000 (25% and 60% of the 20,000 Stage-1 steps). The band
penalty is thus activated only after a coarse geometry has formed. Applying it from initialization
destabilizes early optimization, when the rendered silhouette is still far from the target.
Both bands are constructed per frame from the ground-truth silhouette. Under crossed fingers or
near-contact poses, every region inside M, including contact regions between fingers, is therefore
untouched by the outer penalty, which acts only on true background. Since hands are opaque, no
valid translucency is suppressed, and the interior floor τin prevents erosion near contact boundaries.
Figure S4 (Sec. H.1) shows the qualitative effect of SAB.
A.4
Observation Reliability Details
The observation reliability of Eq. 8 of the main paper fuses each Gaussian’s accumulated rendering
contribution Ci and foreground support mi into a confidence γi ∈[0, 1] through a square-root
transform, median-relative thresholding, and percentile normalization.
Design requirements.
The reliability is designed to satisfy four requirements. (i) γi should increase
monotonically with the evidence a Gaussian actually contributes to source pixels. (ii) It should be
insensitive to changes in projected hand size, camera distance, and image scale. (iii) It should be
robust to the heavy-tailed distribution of rendering contributions. (iv) It should be bounded in [0, 1],
so that the reliability-weighted term in Eq. 13 of the main paper interpolates convexly between
observed and base colors. Each component of Eq. 8 addresses one of these requirements with a
standard robust-statistics tool.
Square-root transform. The distribution of {Ci} is heavy-tailed, as a small number of Gaussians near
the silhouette and on prominent surface regions accumulate disproportionately large contributions.
Using Ci directly would let these few Gaussians dominate the normalization and push the confidence
of moderately observed Gaussians toward zero. The square root, the classical variance-stabilizing
transform for nonnegative count-like mass, compresses this tail (requirement iii). As a result, γi
reflects whether a Gaussian is reliably observed rather than how strongly it dominates rasterization.
Median-relative thresholding. Thresholding contributions relative to the median of the nonzero
contributions, rather than with an absolute cutoff, adapts to each source image and is therefore
insensitive to the global scale of the rendering contributions (requirement ii). Absolute thresholds are
brittle because the total contribution mass varies with hand pose, projected hand size, and camera
distance. A fixed sigmoid or softmax over Ci would instead require a temperature, which is not
scale-invariant across images.
Percentile normalization. Normalizing √Ci mi by its ζ-th percentile over O instead of its maximum
amounts to quantile normalization with winsorization at the ζ-th percentile (requirements iii and iv).
It is robust to outliers that survive the variance-stabilizing transform, and clipping to [0, 1] gives a
bounded confidence that is comparable across poses and image scales.
Hyperparameters.
With (κ, τm, ζ) = (0.5, 0.5, 95), a Gaussian enters O if its rendering contri-
bution exceeds half the median nonzero contribution and more than half of its projected footprint
lies on the hand, and the scores are normalized by the 95th percentile. Both thresholds are set to
the midpoint 0.5, and the 95th percentile is a conventional choice for robust normalization. Sec. F.4
reports the sensitivity around these values.
Graded weights and the observed-set boundary.
Unlike a binary visibility mask, γ assigns
graded weights within O and is zero outside it. Gaussians at the edge of O contribute only weakly to
the source image, so the transition from source-driven to prior-driven color takes place where the
source evidence is weakest. Alpha blending across many overlapping Gaussians with different γ
values usually smooths this transition in the rendered image. When the observed tone differs from
that of the prior, for example because shading is baked into the source (Sec. I), the transition can
remain visible as a seam, most noticeably where the boundary crosses a thin structure. Sec. J.3
quantifies the color step at this boundary. The source-level shift ∆aid

Role separation.
As noted after Eq. 13 of the main paper, the reliability γi and the appearance gate
gi act on the source evidence only through their product giγi, but the two factors differ in kind. γi is
a closed-form estimate of how reliably a Gaussian is observed. It is computed from the rasterizer
outputs and the source mask, receives no gradient, and does not predict appearance. Appearance itself
is predicted by the learned modules, namely the local evidence tokens, the global source identity
token, and the source-conditioned prior bank Ds, and the appearance gate gi decides how much of the
observed color to adopt where evidence exists. We do not learn γi, since a learned confidence could
be satisfied from dataset priors rather than from the observation process. A single learned scalar in
place of giγi would lose the one factor that training cannot adjust. The ablations confirm that neither
factor absorbs the other. With every variant retrained through the full two-stage pipeline, removing γ
costs 3.03 dB and removing gi costs 1.69 dB (Table 3 of the main paper).

C
Evaluation Protocol
We follow the single-image animatable hand reconstruction protocol established by prior hand avatar
benchmarks [20, 23], including their sequence, designated source-frame, cropping, and masking
choices, to ensure direct comparability with reported baselines. The InterHand2.6M source frame is

single source image, the model reconstructs a canonical Gaussian hand representation, which is then
rendered under novel target poses and viewpoints for evaluation. All methods receive the identical
source image, ground-truth annotations, and evaluation targets.

C.3

Following the HandAvatar protocol, pixels outside the ray mask are set to the background color
in both the rendered and the ground-truth image, and PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS are computed on
the resulting images, which reduces background bias. Because our rasterizer ignores the principal
point, we render with a centered principal point and map the render to the original crop with the
exact homography, so all methods are scored on the same pixels. All evaluations are performed at
256 × 256 resolution.
C.4
Boundary-aware Silhouette Metric Definitions
Boundary F-score and boundary IoU are computed between the binarized rendered alpha (A > 0.5)
and the ground-truth mask, with boundaries extracted as 1-pixel contours. The boundary F-score is
the standard contour-matching F-measure: predicted and ground-truth boundary pixels are matched
within a distance tolerance of 2 pixels via distance transforms. Boundary IoU is a variant of the
boundary-restricted IoU of Cheng et al. [56]: each 1-pixel contour is dilated by 3 pixels and the IoU
is computed between the two resulting bands. The band is thus symmetric about the silhouette rather
than restricted to the mask interior, so it also registers outward alpha leakage, which an interior-only
band ignores. Alpha leakage is the mean excess of the rendered alpha above a tolerance of 0.1 over
the outer band, constructed as a 5-pixel dilation of the ground-truth silhouette excluding the silhouette
itself, and interior coverage is the mean rendered alpha over the interior eroded by 5 pixels. The
evaluation bands are deliberately wider than, and distinct from, the 2-pixel training bands of Sec. A.3,
so the boundary evaluation does not simply re-measure the region that the SAB loss optimizes. All
metrics are computed at 256 × 256 and, unless stated otherwise, averaged over the InterHand2.6M
novel-pose/novel-view evaluation set. They are reported in Table 4 of the main paper and in Tables S8
and S11, the latter also on the HanCo evaluation set.

C.6
Timing and Efficiency Measurement
The 2.84 s reported in the main paper is the wall-clock avatar-network inference time on a single
NVIDIA A100 (80 GB), covering image encoding, MANO-anchored token computation, Gaussian
decoding, source-view rendering, and source-conditioned appearance composition. It excludes disk
I/O, data loading, input preprocessing, one-time model loading, and CUDA initialization, and it is
averaged over repeated runs after warm-up (standard deviation 0.007 s).
Cost breakdown.
Building the canonical Gaussians alone takes 1.38 s under the same proto-
col. Source-conditioned appearance composition, including the source-view rendering it requires,
therefore accounts for the remaining 1.46 s, about half of the avatar-network time.
End-to-end raw image to avatar.
Because the reported 2.84 s assumes that MANO, camera, and
mask inputs are already available, we additionally report the complete raw-image-to-avatar cost
on the same GPU (Table S3). Upstream estimation adds approximately 0.46 s (0.38 s for the SAM
3 foreground mask and 0.08 s for WiLoR MANO and camera estimation), giving an end-to-end
construction time of approximately 3.30 s. Upstream estimation is thus about 14% of the end-to-end
cost and is not the bottleneck. This entire cost is incurred once per avatar, for example when a user’s
hand is enrolled at the start of an AR/VR or telepresence session, and subsequent animation re-runs
neither network and renders at 161 FPS. The construction time is therefore not a per-frame latency,
and we do not claim real-time acquisition.
All baseline measurements in Table 2 of the main paper (per-subject fitting time, rendering speed, and
peak memory) are taken on the same NVIDIA A100 (80 GB). Relative to our 2.84 s avatar-network
inference, the fitting times of HandAvatar, OHTA, and BIGS∗correspond to speedups of 1964×,
1183×, and 486× (5579/2.84, 3360/2.84, and 1380/2.84). The “over 400×” figure quoted in the
main paper is the most conservative of these.
Rendering speed and memory.
FPS measures novel-pose rendering (MANO-driven deformation
and rasterization) at 256 × 256 with batch size 1 over the HanCo evaluation frames, excluding data
loading: 161 FPS (≈6.2 ms per frame). Peak GPU memory, including the CUDA context, is 1.79 GB
during rendering and 4.78 GB during avatar construction. Since all compared methods receive the
same ground-truth inputs (Sec. C.7), upstream cost is identical across the compared systems and
orthogonal to the reported comparison.

D
Extended Related Work
Hand avatar reconstruction.
A large body of work relies on multi-view inputs or monocular
video sequences, combined with neural implicit representations or explicit mesh-based pipelines [20–
22, 39–41]. HandAvatar [20] models pose-dependent appearance using implicit representations with
self-occlusion-aware texture modeling. HARP [22] employs a mesh-based formulation augmented
with learned reflectance maps to capture high-frequency details, while LiveHand [21] leverages multi-
view video and neural rendering to synthesize photorealistic hand appearance. Despite achieving
high visual fidelity, these approaches depend on dense observations and per-subject optimization,
leading to substantial computational overhead and limited scalability. Single-image methods such
as OHTA [23] introduce data-driven priors for one-shot hand avatar reconstruction, but still require
test-time fitting or inversion. Gaussian-based approaches such as GaussianHand [24] adopt explicit
3D Gaussian primitives for efficient rendering but continue to depend on multi-view supervision or
temporal sequences and often require subject-specific optimization. As summarized in Table S5,
existing methods remain constrained by dense inputs or per-subject optimization, whether they use
implicit fields, meshes, or Gaussian primitives.
Feed-forward 3D Gaussian avatar reconstruction.
Feed-forward avatar methods regress the
parameters of animatable 3D Gaussians from one or a few images, replacing per-subject optimization
with amortized inference. Among them, LHM [17] extends Gaussian representations to animatable
full-body avatars, while GUAVA [42] focuses on upper-body reconstruction. For head avatars,
approaches such as GAGAvatar [13] and LAM [14] demonstrate high-fidelity rendering via feed-
forward Gaussian prediction. Extending these designs to hands exposes the two failure modes

Baseline Adaptations
E.1
BIGS-based Hand Reconstruction Baseline
BIGS [47] originally targets bimanual hand–object interaction reconstruction from monocular videos,
and therefore does not directly match our single-image, single-hand evaluation setting. To construct a
reference optimization-based 3DGS baseline, we adapt BIGS to a single-hand setting by retaining
only the Gaussian hand avatar component and removing the object and second-hand branches.
The adapted baseline represents the hand in a canonical Gaussian space. Following the Gaussian hand
avatar modeling design, Gaussian attributes are decoded from a triplane feature representation using
MLP heads: the geometry head predicts Gaussian offsets, rotations, and scales, the appearance head
predicts colors and opacities, and the deformation head predicts MANO-related skinning weights.
The canonical Gaussians are then transformed to posed space using MANO-based linear blend
skinning and rendered with differentiable Gaussian rasterization.
The original formulation is designed for monocular videos without known camera poses, where the
camera coordinate system and hand trajectory must be estimated or aligned. In our benchmark setting,
ground-truth annotations are available, so for controlled fitting and evaluation we use the ground-
truth MANO parameters, camera parameters, and hand masks whenever available. This removes
errors from external hand-pose, camera, and mask estimation, and focuses the comparison on the
reconstruction and rendering formulation itself. Accordingly, the fixed virtual-camera construction
and the 2D-keypoint-based global trajectory alignment of the original pipeline are bypassed, and
the temporal smoothness regularization defined across consecutive video frames is inactive under
single-frame fitting.
The adapted baseline is fitted to the single source frame by minimizing a masked reconstruction
objective restricted to the foreground hand region, combining an L1 term and an SSIM term, together
with a silhouette loss on the rendered opacity and a regularizer that encourages the predicted skinning
weights to remain close to the corresponding MANO skinning priors. Fitting uses Adam for 15,000
iterations, with learning rates of 1.6 × 10−4 for the Gaussian position parameters, 1.0 × 10−4 for the
remaining Gaussian attribute parameters, and 1.0 × 10−3 for the triplane and MLP decoder weights.
During evaluation, the fitted BIGS-based baseline is rendered using the same target MANO poses,
target cameras, hand-centered crops, and foreground masks as InstantHand. Since this protocol adapts
BIGS to a single-hand benchmark and differs from the original bimanual hand–object interaction
setting, we report it as a reference optimization-based 3DGS baseline (BIGS∗) rather than as BIGS in
its original setting. Its results appear in Tables 1 and 2 of the main paper, and its input protocol in
Table S4.

InstantHand anchors Gaussian primitives on a subdivided MANO surface (Sk in Eq. 2 of the main

F.4
Inference-time Sensitivity of Observation Reliability
We keep the trained checkpoint fixed and vary the reliability hyperparameters of Eq. 8 of the main
paper one at a time around the default (κ, τm, ζ) = (0.5, 0.5, 95). For each setting, γ is recomputed
once from the Stage-1 source-view render and the complete appearance-composition forward pass is
rerun, while no network weights or canonical Gaussian attributes are updated. This is therefore a
local inference-time sensitivity analysis rather than a comparison of separately retrained models. We
run the sweep under two protocols, the InterHand2.6M protocol used for the other ablations and the
held-out HanCo protocol of Sec. C.2, so that the analysis covers both subject-level and dataset-level
generalization.
Table S9 shows a plateau rather than a peak. Across the seven settings, PSNR spans 0.03 dB (26.19–
26.22) and LPIPS 0.002, even though κ varies by a factor of four and τm by more than a factor of two.
For scale, removing reliability gating entirely costs 3.03 dB (Table 3 of the main paper), roughly two
orders of magnitude more than any hyperparameter change here. The behavior of reliability gating is
therefore governed by its structure, namely square-root compression, median-relative thresholding,
and percentile normalization (Sec. A.4), rather than by the exact constants. The same insensitivity
holds under dataset-level transfer: on the held-out HanCo sequence the same seven settings span
0.05 dB PSNR (20.16–20.21) and 0.001 LPIPS. In practice, reliability gating requires no per-dataset
tuning.

F.5
Rationale for Two-stage Training
The two-stage schedule enforces a structural separation between canonical representation learning
and source-conditioned appearance adaptation. Stage 1 learns the canonical predictor, and Stage 2
freezes it in full, including the base-color head frgb, while optimizing only the source-conditioned
appearance module of Sec. 3.3 of the main paper (training details in Sec. A.1). By construction,
source-view appearance supervision in Stage 2 cannot alter the canonical geometry or base colors, so
novel-pose rendering starts from exactly the Stage-1 representation.
The frozen canonical branch also gives the appearance module a well-defined prior to fall back on:
wherever source evidence is missing or unreliable, the composed color reverts to the Stage-1 base
color plus the source-level shift ∆aid
s
shared by all Gaussians. The full model improves source-view
fidelity (Table S2), and observation reliability controls what reaches novel views. A color taken
from the source is only as trustworthy as the evidence behind it. When transfer is not weighted by γ,
novel-pose PSNR falls from 26.21 to 23.18 dB (Table 3 of the main paper), which is consistent with
colors of weakly observed Gaussians being carried to novel views. The two stages thus keep the two
roles separate: Stage 1 provides a stable prior for every Gaussian, and Stage 2 learns only where, and
how strongly, source evidence should replace it, together with a source-level shift that propagates
global appearance cues to every Gaussian.

Discussion
G.1
Relation to Boundary Losses
Boundary-focused objectives and metrics are established in segmentation, where regional losses are
complemented by contour-based losses [59] and by boundary-aware evaluation [56]. Rendered alpha,
however, differs from the pixel-labeling setting those losses assume, and each element of SAB (Eq. 16
of the main paper and Sec. A.3) addresses one of the resulting differences. Per-pixel alpha is not an
independent prediction but a composite of many projected 3D primitives, so band supervision acts
through the rasterizer on primitive centers and scales rather than relabeling pixels. A one-sided outer
penalty admits a degenerate optimum with no segmentation analogue, namely shrinking the hand
until nothing crosses the contour. The paired interior coverage term closes this loophole, so leakage
cannot be “fixed” by shrinking the hand. Soft alpha at boundaries is correct anti-aliased rendering
rather than an error, and the tolerances and the robust penalty leave it unpenalized. Finally, the
schedule η(n) reflects the 3D setting by activating the penalty only once the bands are geometrically
meaningful for the evolving primitives. All SAB ablations keep the constrained parameterization of
Sec. A.1 fixed, so the boundary-aware silhouette evaluation (Sec. C.4 and Table 4 of the main paper)
measures the effect of SAB under identical constraints.
G.2
View-dependent Appearance, Illumination, and Multi-frame Extension
Our appearance model predicts view-independent Gaussian colors and does not employ view-
dependent representations such as spherical harmonics (SH). The two address different problems.
The ambiguity we target is observational. A single source image observes essentially one side
of the hand, so the core difficulty is deciding where source evidence is reliable and how to com-
plete unobserved regions, which is what reliability-gated transfer and the global source identity
address. A similar observational problem arises for human avatars built from monocular video, where
FlexiAvatar [45] optimizes only the visible body regions and completes entirely unseen regions
with diffusion-generated texture. View-dependent appearance modeling instead captures viewpoint-
and illumination-dependent reflectance after the underlying appearance has been recovered, and
it typically requires multi-view observations to be constrained. Under single-image supervision,
per-Gaussian SH coefficients would be severely underdetermined. The two mechanisms are therefore
complementary rather than alternatives: view-dependent terms could be attached to our composed
colors without changing the geometry stabilization or the reliability-gated transfer.
A related limitation is that shading baked into the source image is transferred as albedo and thus
carried to novel poses. This is as much a property of the setting as of the model: a single image
does not determine illumination and albedo separately. Explicit albedo–shading decompositions such
as those of HandAvatar [20] and OHTA [23] model self-occlusion shading learned from video or
multi-identity data, but the scene illumination of a single source image, such as its exposure or color

two to four frames, coverage grows by only 0.03 and PSNR is effectively unchanged (−0.01 dB),
whereas from four to eight frames coverage grows by 0.25 and all three metrics improve again. The
gain per unit of coverage nevertheless diminishes near full coverage, from +0.32 dB for the first 0.23
of added coverage to +0.05 dB for the last 0.25, for two reasons. First, overlapping observations of
an already covered region are handled by reliability-gated selection rather than learned fusion. The
reliability separates observed from unobserved Gaussians within a single view but is not designed
to rank several valid observations of the same region, so near-ties are resolved in favor of the base
view. Second, the last uncovered regions are visible only at grazing angles, where they receive low
reliability and occupy little area in the evaluation views. Learned multi-view fusion, trained with
explicit multi-view or temporal conditioning, could combine overlapping observations instead of
selecting among them and is a natural direction for future work.
G.3
Ethical Considerations
Hand imagery can act as a soft biometric: skin texture, vein-like patterns, and nail features may
be identifying, and a reconstructed avatar could in principle support impersonation in hand-centric
interfaces. All training and evaluation data come from published research datasets used under their
licenses, and our model neither identifies individuals nor is trained for recognition. Feed-forward
hand avatar reconstruction may benefit virtual reality, telepresence, digital human animation, and
human–computer interaction by enabling efficient avatar creation from minimal input. At the same
time, as with other avatar and generative modeling technologies, reconstructed avatars could be
misused for identity impersonation or deceptive synthetic media. Deployments that capture user
hands should obtain informed consent and safeguard reconstructed avatars as biometric-adjacent data.

Failure Cases and Limitations
Representative failure cases are shown in Fig. S10, in the input-documentation format of Fig. S8: the
input frame (Input), the WiLoR [37] pose overlay (MANO), the SAM 3 [54] source crop (Source),
and our reconstruction (Render).
First, our method inherits the inherent ambiguity of single-image hand reconstruction. Under severe
self-occlusion the occluded surface is never observed, so its appearance is completed from the
source-conditioned prior rather than transferred from evidence, and thin local structures that are only
weakly observed are reconstructed with reduced fidelity. The upstream pose estimate on which the
avatar is anchored also becomes less reliable in such views. Where the boundary of the observed
set crosses a thin or strongly foreshortened part, such as the thumb in a dorsal view, source-driven
and prior-completed colors meet on the same structure. If the observed tone differs from the prior,
as when shading is baked into the source (the third point below), the transition appears as a visible
seam rather than a gradual blend. Sec. A.4 discusses this boundary, and Sec. J.3 measures where such
seams can occur (Fig. S12).
Second, all Gaussian attributes are fixed after the single forward pass. This keeps the identity
consistent across poses, but the avatar does not model pose-dependent appearance. Wrinkles that
form and shading that changes under strong articulation are not synthesized, whereas wrinkles visible
in the source are transferred as texture. The representation also ends where the MANO template
ends, and the avatar has no forearm. When the hand is viewed along the forearm, as in the 360◦
rotations of the supplementary video, the open wrist boundary is visible, including the inner side of
the template surface on which the Gaussians lie. The MANO-based baselines likewise end at the
wrist. The anchor set is fixed as well, and the isotropic scales are predicted once in the canonical
pose. Under strong flexion, skinning stretches the surface on the convex side of a joint and spreads
neighboring anchors apart, but the splats are not enlarged to follow this stretch. Where the spacing
exceeds the splat footprint, neighboring rows of splats separate, and the lattice becomes visible as fine
striations at the knuckles. Enlarging the splats relative to the anchor spacing would close these gaps,
but the same splats also form the silhouette. Rendering the prediction on the next subdivision level,
which halves the anchor spacing, without shrinking the scales (row “k = 4, si” of Table S11) doubles
the footprint-to-spacing ratio. This raises alpha leakage from 0.234 to 0.293 and lowers the boundary
F-score from 0.698 to 0.661, which is exactly the leakage that the scale ceiling and SAB are designed
to suppress. The striations are a local effect, and since curvature is handled by density rather than by
anisotropy (Sec. F.2), a finer subdivision level reduces them. Training at k = 4, however, runs out of
memory on our training GPUs (RTX A6000, 48 GB) and was not feasible with our resources.

J.2
Temporal Stability
Table S14 reports temporal stability on the continuous HanCo sequence. Our avatar is evaluated
zero-shot, as neither the subject nor the dataset is seen during training and no test-time optimization
is performed. Every time step except that of the source frame shows a novel pose, and three of the
four cameras show a novel view. Under these conditions, our renderings remain within 8.0% of the
ground-truth change on every camera, with a mean absolute excess of 4.7%. To calibrate this value,
we add a per-frame brightness jitter of ±1% to our own renderings. The excess pooled over all pairs
then increases from +4.8% to +12.4%, and further to +22.0% and +43.1% for ±2% and ±4%.
A 1% flicker thus adds 7.6 points, which exceeds our mean absolute excess of 4.7%. By contrast,
the per-subject 3DGS fit BIGS∗exceeds the ground-truth change by 30.7–50.5% across the four
cameras, which is consistent with view-specific appearance noise from single-view optimization.
OHTA, which optimizes its avatar on this subject for 3360 s (Table 2 of the main paper), is closest to
zero, with a mean absolute excess of 3.6%, although its sign differs across cameras. Its stability is
therefore comparable to that of our feed-forward avatar. HandAvatar changes less than the footage on
every camera, by 8.7–23.3%, consistent with the uniform tone without shading noted in Sec. G.2.

Supplementary Video
We provide a single supplementary video (InstantHand_Supplementary_Video.mp4, 3 min 9 s)
in four parts. In every part, all of our renderings are projections of one fixed set of canonical Gaussians
per subject, with no view-dependent parameters, no image-space refinement, and no per-frame fitting
or update. The avatar representation therefore cannot drift over time: frame-to-frame changes in the
renderings come only from the driving pose and the rendering itself, and Sec. J.2 quantifies them on
HanCo. Temporal stability and multi-view consistency are difficult to convey with still frames, so we
encourage readers to view the video directly.
Parts 1–2: benchmark subjects.
Part 1 follows the InterHand2.6M protocol of Sec. C.1 and
Part 2 the zero-shot HanCo protocol of Sec. C.2, where the source subject is unseen and no test-time
optimization is performed. In both parts the avatar is reconstructed from the protocol-designated
source frame with its ground-truth MANO, camera, and mask. Each part shows (i) the single source
input image together with the reconstructed canonical avatar, (ii) full 360◦yaw and pitch rotations of
the canonical 3D avatar, and (iii) continuous novel-pose driving. In the rotations, the prior-completed
side comes into view as the hand turns away from the source view, so the transition between the
source-observed and prior-completed sides can be inspected directly. Each driving pose is rendered
synchronously from six fixed cameras: the source camera, the reference camera in which the driving
sequence is defined, and four fixed novel cameras at azimuth offsets of +60◦, +120◦, +150◦, and
−60◦from that reference. Both parts are driven by the same 35.2 s MANO pose sequence, assembled
from a diverse set of hand gestures with short holds and interpolation between them. The sequence
is disjoint from the evaluation frames of either dataset, covers a wider range of poses than either
evaluation sequence, and shows the InterHand2.6M and HanCo avatars in identical poses. No ground-
truth images exist for these poses, so the driving segments are qualitative. All quantitative novel-pose
results (Tables 1, 2, and 3 of the main paper) are computed on the evaluation sequences.

Reproducibility Statement
The full model is specified in the main paper (Eqs. 1–17). All loss terms are defined in Sec. A.2,
the Soft Alpha Band construction and schedule in Sec. A.3, the design rationale and thresholds of
reliability gating (Eq. 8) in Sec. A.4, and the network architecture in Sec. A.5. Notation is consistent
between the main paper and the appendix. All real training datasets and both evaluation datasets are
publicly available (Sec. B), with preprocessing, annotation sources, subject and frame counts, and the
complete sampling policy documented in Secs. A.1–B.3. The synthetic training data are generated
following the published protocol of Zhao et al. [53]. All loss weights, learning rates, schedules,
and batch sizes are reported with their final values in Sec. A.1. Evaluation sequences, designated
source frames, crops, masks, metric computation, boundary-aware silhouette definitions, the scope
of the self-reconstruction diagnostic, timing exclusions and the end-to-end runtime breakdown,
per-baseline input protocols, and the provenance of all baseline numbers are specified in Secs. C–E.
The protocols of the pose-stratified, temporal, and canonical analyses are given in Sec. C.8. The
source-side MANO and mask sensitivity protocol and the inference-time reliability sensitivity settings
are specified in Secs. F.3 and F.4, and the inference-time densification in Sec. I. All such variants
keep the trained checkpoint fixed. Training uses four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs (approximately
25 and 13 hours for the two stages), and all timing and efficiency measurements use a single
NVIDIA A100 (80 GB) under the protocol of Sec. C.6. The reported 2.84 s is the avatar-network
inference time once common MANO, camera, and mask inputs are available, and the corresponding
end-to-end raw-image-to-avatar time, including SAM 3 and WiLoR estimation, is approximately
3.30 s (Table S3). Code, pretrained models, and the evaluation scripts are available at https:
//github.com/coolho1129/InstantHand, and videos and further results are on the project page,
https://coolho1129.github.io/InstantHand-page/.
