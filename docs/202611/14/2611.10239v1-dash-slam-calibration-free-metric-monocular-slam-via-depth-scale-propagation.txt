# DASH-SLAM: Calibration-Free Metric Monocular SLAM via Depth Scale Propagation 

Otavio Ribas 1,2, Jacopo Panerati 3, and Pierre-Yves Lajoie 1,2

Abstract — Geometric foundation models estimate dense ge-ometry and camera parameters from monocular images, but reliable SLAM also requires consistent scale and accurate data association across successive reconstructions. We present DASH-SLAM, a monocular SLAM system that integrates metric depth predictions from pretrained Depth Anything 3 into a shared-keyframe pose graph without task-specific fine-tuning. DASH-SLAM derives scale corrections from depth ratios at shared anchor images and accumulates them across reconstructions, with a damping factor controlling their in-fluence. These corrections rescale odometry translations, loop constraints, and map geometry. Experiments on TUM, Replica, and 7-Scenes show that DASH-SLAM outperforms state-of-the-art methods. We evaluate performance using both rigid and similarity alignment to distinguish metric-scale fidelity from accuracy up to scale. We further evaluate DASH-SLAM on four challenging sequences in perceptually-degraded environments from the Unified Autonomy Stack field dataset, covering tun-nels, fog, and a frozen lake. On these sequences, DASH-SLAM outperforms the foundation-model SLAM state-of-the-art and maintains tracking where classical SLAM methods fail. 

I. INTRODUCTION Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) is a foundational task in robotics to simultaneously produce a 3D representation of a scene and estimate the poses of the agent in that map. While LiDARs and other modern types of sensors are getting more common, a single RGB camera is still the cheapest, lightest, and most available sensor, which makes Monocular SLAM particularly attractive. However, this setting carries a set of constraints: many of the main existing systems, both classical and learned [1–3], rely on calibration, and a single camera is physically unable to ob-serve absolute scale. Although these problems can sometimes be mitigated in a controlled environment, they pose major problems in real-world applications, where camera intrinsics are often unknown and the ambiguity on scale renders it impossible for a robot to measure the distance it has travelled. Geometric Foundation Models (GFMs) have greatly con-tributed to overcome the first limitation, i.e. calibration, by being trained to estimate a point map, camera poses, and intrinsics directly from uncalibrated images [4–7]. Conse-quently, SLAM systems that leverage these models as their backbone bypass calibration altogether, and even manage to surpass some calibrated pipelines on several benchmarks [8, 9], thus making it possible to run SLAM systems on arbitrary cameras.

The second limitation, i.e. scale, remains largely unre-solved and is fundamentally more complicated to mitigate. In fact, this limitation is often concealed in the standard evaluation protocol: Monocular SLAM accuracy is generally measured by its absolute trajectory error after a Sim (3) 

alignment to ground truth, which estimates and removes a global scale factor before any error is measured. In practice, this means that a map that is too large or too small is indistinguishable from a correctly scaled one. As almost none of the state-of-the-art monocular SLAM systems recovers metric scale, some methods that perform well under Sim(3) 

alignment can exhibit errors more than an order of magnitude larger when evaluated under rigid SE(3) alignment. Yet, accurate scale remains a requirement for robots to actually navigate real-world settings. The latest GFMs such as Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [7] have the capability of predicting depth in metric units, anchoring their reconstruction on scale priors learned from their real-world training data. However, harvesting this property into a proper metric SLAM system is not automatic. While some foundation models can operate over an arbitrarily large sequence of frames, they process them all at once. This means that simply feeding the frames to the model would not allow a robot to estimate its state online. Moreover, GFMs can reach memory constraints relatively fast, which is a real limitation in autonomous systems. This means a system based on a GFM is required to chain the estimations for each batch, but since each carries its own small scale error, these errors compound and the scale drifts. The question is how to use a metric GFM so that its scale estimates inform the whole sequence, without calibration and without returning to a hand-crafted feature tracking pipeline. While concurrent work injects per-keyframe DA3 depth priors into the back-end of a feature-based SLAM pipeline [10], we opted to use the foundation model’s joint multi-view reconstruction, which provides poses, intrinsics, and metric depth, as the tracking front-end itself. We present DASH-SLAM (Depth-Assisted Scale-

Harmonized Metric Monocular SLAM), an uncalibrated RGB SLAM system that uses DA3 as its backbone for tracking, mapping, and loop closure. A batch of keyframes is processed by DA3 into poses and metric depth, which are then used to produce a submap. Consecutive submaps share two anchor frames that are observed in both inferences, allowing two independent depth maps to directly measure the relationship between the scale of two consecutive maps. II. RELATED WORKS 

Visual SLAM and learned geometry. Feature-based sys-tems such as ORB-SLAM3 [1] estimate motion and struc-ture through correspondence search and geometric optimiza-tion. Early learned systems retain this coupling: DROID-SLAM [2] iteratively refines dense correspondences, camera poses, and depth through differentiable bundle adjustment, while DPV-SLAM [3] uses a sparse patch graph and efficient proximity loop closure. Their standard monocular formula-tions use camera calibration and do not determine absolute scale from geometry alone. DASH-SLAM instead obtains poses and depth jointly from a pretrained reconstruction model, with metric scale supplied by its learned prior. 

Foundation models for SLAM. In recent years, new machine learning architectures capable of learning from internet-scale datasets have had a major impact on computer vision. DUSt3R [4] predicts pointmaps from uncalibrated image pairs, MASt3R [5] adds learned matching features, and VGGT [6] jointly predicts geometry and cameras across multiple views. These priors support different sequential formulations useful for robotics. MASt3R-SLAM [8] com-bines two-view pointmap matching with tracking, fusion, and global optimization; ViSTA-SLAM [12] learns symmetric two-view geometry and relative poses for a Sim(3) graph. A different approach, FLOCC-SLAM [13], uses GFM to estimate inter-robot loop closures and optimizes their scales against existing metric odometry. DASH-SLAM instead ob-tains both odometry and loop measurements from DA3, whose metric scale comes from the model’s learned prior. More closely related to our approach are VGGT-SLAM [14], which aligns overlapping submaps on SL(4) ,and its successor, VGGT-SLAM 2.0 [9], which uses keyframe nodes, overlap constraints that reconcile calibra-tion and scale, and re-inferred loop submaps. While these approaches target up-to-scale reconstruction, DASH-SLAM leverages DA3’s metric priors to merge repeated anchor images into shared nodes, and applies depth-derived scale corrections with damping, to ensure metric accuracy and better consistency along the robot’s trajectory. A related approach, using the same inference backbone as ours, DA3-Streaming [15], reconstructs overlapping image batches and aligns them through similarity transformations derived from shared geometry and loop closures, repeat-edly processing the overlapping frames. In contrast, DASH-SLAM speeds up the pipeline using smaller overlaps and optimizes individual keyframe poses through scale-adjusted relative-pose constraints, enabling corrections within and across submaps. 

Metric reconstruction priors. Metric depth estimation has a long history in computer vision, and recent approaches like Metric3D [16] and UniDepth [17] can learn metric geometry across camera settings. MapAnything [18] and Depth Anything 3 [7] extend the available reconstruction priors to multi-view settings. Closely related to our approach, AMB3R [19] combines a learned metric-scale head with a compact volumetric back-end and introduces AMB3R-VO, a model-agnostic odometry pipeline that also supports DA3. In comparison, DASH-SLAM retains an explicit pose graph with retrieval-based loop constraints for correcting the trajectory. Another tech-nique PRISM-SLAM [10] incorporates DA3 depth priors into a feature-tracking system using uncertainty weighting, scale filtering, and metric optimization. DASH-SLAM uses instead joint multi-view inference itself to obtain relative poses, then converts both odometry and re-inferred loop measurements into a common scale. Thus, one of our contributions concerns the integration and propagation of metric predictions within SLAM. We assess this integration under both SE(3) and 

Sim(3) alignments. 

Field robustness and perceptual aliasing. Perceptual alias-ing can produce correlated false loop constraints that can lead to drastic failures, a longstanding challenge in SLAM [20]. VGGT-SLAM 2.0 [9] studies retrieval errors in repetitive offices and demonstrates outdoor driving reconstruction. CoMo3R-SLAM [21] addresses outdoor cross-agent associ-ation, while On-the-Fly3R [22] evaluates large UAV recon-structions and validates local estimates before integration. Our experiments complement these efforts with tunnel, fog, and frozen-lake sequences, examining the sensitivity, bene-fits and vulnerability of current metric-prior SLAM under challenging conditions, largely unsolved to this day. III. METHODOLOGY DASH-SLAM takes a stream of monocular RGB frames with unknown intrinsics and produces a metric camera tra-jectory and a dense point map. It comprises of four parallel threads connected by bounded queues (see the overall ar-chitecture in Fig. 1): a front-end that selects keyframes, an 

inference stage that builds submaps with Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [7] by consuming selected keyframes, a back-end that places each submap in a global metric frame and optimizes a pose graph, and a loop-closure worker that detects, revisits, and measures them with the same GFM. We describe each stage in its dedicated subsection. 

A. Keyframe Selection 

Typically, visual SLAM systems select a frame as akeyframe when its disparity with respect to the previous keyframe exceeds a threshold [5, 9]. In learning-based dense SLAM, keyframes are the input set for the feed-forward reconstruction. Therefore, a disparity-based approach causes the density of the input set to depend on scene depth and camera motion. Furthermore, since DA3 operates over a batch of keyframes, irregular density translates into batches that span too little motion to provide parallax, or too much to retain the view overlap its cross-view attention relies on. Therefore, instead of relying on frame-by-frame disparity, we select keyframes at the segment level [23]: the stream is partitioned into non-overlapping segments of NS = 128 

frames, and keyframes are sampled regularly within that segment with a stride that is dependent on the apparent motion. For consecutive frames i, i +1 in a segment, we estimate the mean displacement magnitude ¯di,i +1 through optical flow. The accumulated displacement DS = P

Optimization. All factors use an isotropic noise model with

E. Boundary Verification and Repair 

Each boundary is verified for consistency by using the anchor pair. We compare the relative pose between the two anchor frames as measured by submap m−1 and by submap m, and flag the boundary as broken when the two disagree by more than 15 ◦ in rotation, or when the ratio of their translation magnitudes lies outside [0 .5, 2] . When this happens, DA3 repairs the boundary by producing a third measurement using frames from both submaps, used to arbitrate between the two conflicting factors. 

F. Loop Closure 

Retrieval. Every keyframe is described by a DINO-SALAD global descriptor [25]. When a submap is completed, each of its keyframes is compared against all previous ones that are at least two submaps away, using the L2 distance between descriptors. Pairs closer than ldiff are candidates; only the closest candidate per earlier submap is retained, and at most one candidate is verified per query submap. 

Verification by re-inference. We verify the validity of a loop-closure by re-running DA3 on a small batch formed by the query keyframe, the matched keyframe, and neighbors of each one of them. A candidate is rejected if the mean predicted confidence over the two matched frames is below 

cgate . Otherwise, all frames of the new batch share one local frame, and the loop measurement is the relative pose between the two matched frames, Zloop = Eq (Ed)−1.

Metric scale of the loop. As with every DA3 inference, the verification comes with its own scale. Thus, to adjust it to the global scale, first it needs to be brought to the same scale as the query submap by using the median depth ratio between the query keyframe’s original depth map and its depth in the re-inference (as in Sec. III-C). Then, it can be brought into the global scale by using the query submap’s accumulated scale Sm (Eq. 4). 

Acceptance. Each measured loop is compared with the rela-tive pose the current graph predicts between the two frames, and accepted immediately if the rotation disagreement is be-low 90 ◦. Loops that fail this check are not discarded but held :if odometry breaks, the graph itself is wrong, so a correct loop can appear inconsistent. Held loops are re-evaluated whenever a new submap is processed, and accepted if a second held loop implies the same correction. Furthermore, a held loop that remains uncontradicted for five submaps is accepted on its own. Accepted loops enter the graph as between-factors with a Huber robust kernel ( kloop ), so an erroneous closure that passes all checks is down-weighted rather than warping the map. Boundary repairs follow the same path but bypass the geometric check, since their frames are adjacent by construction. 

G. Map Reconstruction 

Geometry is stored per keyframe, in camera coordinates, as the confidence-filtered back-projected points of Sec. III-B. The global map is obtained by expressing each keyframe’s points in the global metric unit and transforming them with the keyframe’s optimised pose, xw = Xi (Sm x), with an-chor frames taken from the first submap that contains them. Because points are re-projected from the current poses rather than fused into a fixed cloud, corrections from loop closures propagate to the map without re-running the foundation model. 

H. Implementation Details 

We use the DA3NESTED-GIANT-LARGE-1.1 model at a processing resolution of 504 px, with both ViT backbones cast to bfloat16, which reduces peak GPU memory by approximately 40% at no measurable accuracy cost. The pose graph is implemented in GTSAM. All parameters above are shared across experiments. IV. EXPERIMENTS

Metrics. We report the absolute trajectory error (ATE) RMSE under two alignments. Sim(3) is the conventional metric for monocular SLAM, computed using Umeyama alignment and the ground truth to adjust for a global scale factor and, therefore, to measure trajectory shape . SE(3) 

alignment fits rotation and translation but not scale, so it additionally penalizes a trajectory that is globally too large or too small. We also report the average frames-per-second (FPS) rate obtained over each dataset. 

Baselines. We compare against 6 uncalibrated systems that, like ours, receive no intrinsics. In our comparison, we include DA3-Streaming, the streaming reconstruction shipped with DA3 [7], to demonstrate our performance gain over the pure DA3 system. For reference, we also list 5 calibrated systems, which are given the ground-truth intrinsics. Every system is run through the same harness, with identical dataset loading, association, and scoring, so the numbers differ only in the tracker. Each system had its metrics computed as its average over 3 runs. 

Hardware. All experiments are conducted on a system equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4000 Ada (20 GB) GPU and an Intel Core Ultra 7 265K CPU. The configuration described in Sec. Configurations. On all indoor datasets, the system was run with τS = 650 px, nmax = 32 , nwarm = 16 , nstride = 4 ,

7-Scenes is the hardest of the three for foundation-model methods: the sequences are short, hand-held and contain rapid rotation. E. Metric Accuracy 

Across the three benchmarks, Table IV shows that the scale factor recovered by DASH-SLAM under Sim (3) align-ment remains within 2.1%, 5.0%, and 0.8% of metric scale, respectively, without calibration, an initialisation procedure, or an external reference. This is substantially closer to metric scale than approaches not based on DA3, which do not provide a native mechanism for estimating metric depth. DA3-Streaming, the native implementation of DA3 for long sequences, remains within 3–13% but does not perform scale chaining across windows. This comparison suggests that metric depth alone is not sufficient: the per-batch scales must also be reconciled, at least for smaller sequences, as provided by the anchor-frame chaining described in Sec. F. Unified Autonomy Stack (UAS) Dataset 

As the three benchmarks above cover indoor environments and traverse at most a few tens of meters, we use four se-quences of the NTNU-ARL UAS dataset to test our approach in much more difficult regimes: two tunnel inspections (fyllingsdalen , hornbill ), a frozen lake and a foggy campus flight, whose ground-truth paths are 659–1427 m long. The camera is fisheye, the scenes are textureless or fog-obscured over long stretches, and the flights are an order of magnitude longer. Table V compares DASH-SLAM with VGGT-SLAM and ORB-SLAM3, the latter provided with the fisheye cam-era calibration. DASH-SLAM achieves ATEs of 64.19 m and 75.84 m under Sim (3) alignment on the fyllin 

and hornbill sequences, respectively, substantially out-performing VGGT-SLAM, which obtains 196.93 m and 148.90 m. ORB-SLAM3 loses tracking on both sequences; it re-initialises a new map and retains only the largest one, which on hornbill covers only 5% of the route. On 

frozen lake , however, ORB-SLAM3 tracks the sequence to its end and achieves a substantially lower ATE, probably due to the abundance of visual texture, although highly self-similar, on the lake’s icy surface. DASH-SLAM and VGGT-SLAM obtain comparable errors on this sequence, at 74.35 m and 76.06 m, respectively. Finally, campus fog 

presents a particularly challenging trajectory. None of the systems successfully reconstructs the complete sequence. Nevertheless, ORB-SLAM3 and DASH-SLAM achieve sub-stantially lower ATEs when evaluation is restricted to the first half of the trajectory, at 1.68 m and 2.66 m, respectively, whereas VGGT-SLAM still exhibits a high ATE of 46.08 m. CONCLUSION We presented DASH-SLAM, a monocular SLAM system that integrates metric-depth predictions through anchor-frame scale chaining and a shared-keyframe graph. Depth-derived corrections enable scale accuracy and consistency across odometry, loop closure measurements, and map geometry. Across three indoor benchmarks, DASH-SLAM achieves the lowest reported metric trajectory error among the evaluated methods, while reaching real-time speeds (25.3 to 162.4 FPS). Our technique also obtains lower error than a compa-rable GFM-based method across four challenging sequences in perceptually-degraded environments, although substantial residual errors remain. These results point to a promising future for metric foundation models in SLAM and motivate further research toward robust operation in challenging real-world environments beyond indoor benchmarks.
