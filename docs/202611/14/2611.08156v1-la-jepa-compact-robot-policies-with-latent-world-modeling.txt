# ABSTRACT 

World-action models commonly adapt pretrained video generators, and vision-language-action models build on pretrained vision-language backbones; both tie policy architecture and inference cost to large transformers designed for genera-tion rather than control. While the knowledge accumulated through large-scale pretraining is certainly useful, it remains unclear how much generation-oriented architectural choices affect control performance, and whether the trade-off re-quired to access this pretraining is worth it. In this work, we introduce Latent Ac-tion JEPA (La-JEPA) , a compact world-action model whose policy transformer is trained from scratch in the feature space of a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 encoder, so that its architecture and capacity can be chosen for control. Following Fast-WAM, La-JEPA learns to predict future features jointly with actions during training and generates only actions at inference. Among policies without embodied pretrain-ing, La-JEPA achieves state-of-the-art performance in clean and randomized RoboTwin 2.0 scenes and on LIBERO-Plus with correct instructions, while using far fewer parameters than the strongest competing policies. It runs sub-stantially faster than Fast-WAM at comparable performance and outperforms the closest JEPA-based methods, LeapBot-WA and JEPA-WAM, on LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0 without their pretrained policy backbones. On four real-robot tasks, including contact-heavy pushing and long-horizon multistep manipulation, its average success is comparable to π0.5, outperforming non-embodied-pretrained baselines. Through a design study of the choices behind La-JEPA, we show that JEPA features outperform video VAE latents, most clearly out of distribution, and that training-time future prediction choices significantly impact the policy even when future prediction is skipped at inference. We also correct the LIBERO-Plus instructions, as the officially released evaluator corrupts them for most variants, and re-evaluate several strong open baselines. These results establish a compact, efficient foundation for JEPA-based world-action modeling: without a generative pretrained policy backbone, La-JEPA is better and faster than far larger policies built on video and language generation models, suggesting that robot foundation models may benefit from architectures designed specifically for control rather than inherited from generation.

Vision-language-action policies take a similar route using language-generation transformers aligned with visual inputs (Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025). Most world-action models (WAMs) are VAE-based because they build on pretrained video genera-tors, yet better-suited latent spaces are available. Video VAEs retain accurate information but spend much of their capacity on pixel-level fidelity that control does not require. JEPA representations, in contrast, are developed and evaluated for downstream tasks such as video understanding, depth estimation, and segmentation, which are closer to the needs of embodied agents (Assran et al., 2025; Mur-Labadia et al., 2026). Previous work aligns VLMs or video-generation models to the JEPA space (Lin et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) or learns a JEPA-style latent space jointly with the pol-icy (Vujinovic & Kovacevic, 2025); to our knowledge, no prior work trains a compact world-action policy from scratch in a pretrained JEPA feature space. We introduce Latent Action JEPA (La-JEPA) , a world-action model that learns future-feature pre-diction and action generation directly in the dense spatial and temporal feature space of a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 video encoder (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026). Its World DiT and Action DiT are initialized from scratch, allowing their architecture and capacity to be chosen independently of the visual en-coder. Following Fast-WAM and Faster-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026b; Ma et al., 2026), actions attend to observed context but not to future tokens, so predictive supervision can shape the policy during training without the cost of generating the future at inference time. Among policies without embodied pretraining, La-JEPA achieves the strongest clean and random-ized RoboTwin 2.0 results and the strongest LIBERO-Plus results with corrected instructions (Sec-tion 5.2), with about four times fewer deployed parameters than the next-best policies, while run-ning up to 5 × faster than Fast-WAM, whose design we follow (Figure 1, Tables 1 and 2). With the released instructions, it is second only to NowWAM, which is insensitive to the corrupted text (Table 14). It also outperforms the closest JEPA-based methods, LeapBot-WA and JEPA-WAM, on LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0 (Table 3). On real-robot tasks ranging from cube placement to contact-rich pushing and long-horizon manipulation, La-JEPA trained from scratch on the target demonstrations achieves the highest average success among the compared policies, comparable to the embodied-pretrained π0.5 (Table 5). Together, these results demonstrate leading benchmark per-2Preprint formance, efficient inference, and competitive physical manipulation without requiring a generative backbone. To understand which design choices enable this performance, we study the impact of the represen-tation space, different targets for predictive supervision, and robustness mechanisms. We adopt a 

compact first approach, making the architecture viable even for a policy with 0.1B trainable param-eters, before increasing policy capacity. Comparisons of JEPA features and VAE latents span both benchmarks and multiple model configurations, showing that JEPA features generally outperform VAE latents, with the clearest gains for out of distribution evaluations (Section 4.1, Appendix A.1). Action-only controls separately confirm the benefit of WAM-style future prediction, and studies of temporal encoding, dropout, augmentation, and encoder adaptation complete the recipe. These analyses support the central contribution: a compact, effective world-action policy, together with evidence to guide its design. As a secondary contribution, we show that the released LIBERO-Plus evaluator appends perturbation parameters to most instructions, so that most variants apply two per-turbations at once. This corruption affects policies to very different degrees, from no change to 19 points, and obscures the robustness study that the benchmark is designed for; we therefore re-evaluate the openly available baselines with corrected instructions under a single “clean” protocol that isolates each perturbation as intended (Section 5.2). 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Vision-language-action policies. Vision-language-action (VLA) policies extend pretrained vision-language models with action outputs, predicting discretized action tokens (Kim et al., 2024) or attaching a flow-matching action expert (Black et al., 2024), and broaden their training data to generalize further (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026a). All inherit the size and inference cost of a multi-billion-parameter vision-language backbone. 

World-action models. World-action models (WAMs) instead build on pretrained video or image generators whose representations drive action generation (Kim et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), reaching the strongest previously reported LIBERO-Plus results among policies without embodied pretraining. Because generating future frames slows inference, recent WAMs predict the future only during training (Yuan et al., 2026b) and reduce the action expert to a single layer that reads keys and values cached from all layers of the video backbone (Ma et al., 2026). All retain the generator backbone in the deployed policy. 

Joint-embedding predictive architectures. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn representations by predicting the embeddings of masked inputs rather than reconstructing pix-els (Assran et al., 2023). V-JEPA extends this objective to video (Bardes et al., 2024), V-JEPA 2 scales it (Assran et al., 2025), and V-JEPA 2.1 improves the dense features of V-JEPA 2 (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026), which makes it especially well suited to robotic policies. JEPA representa-tions have since been used in robot policies: ACT-JEPA learns a JEPA-style latent space jointly with the policy (Vujinovic & Kovacevic, 2025), VLA-JEPA and JEPA-VLA bring V-JEPA targets or features into vision-language-action models (Sun et al., 2026; Miao et al., 2026), and JEPA-WAM and LeapBot-WA build world-action models on V-JEPA features (Lin et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). These two world-action models are the closest to La-JEPA, but both initialize their predictors from pretrained vision-language or video generation models. 

Compact policies. Policies that are competitive without inheriting either a VLM or video back-bone are scarce. While Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., 2023), DP3 (Ze et al., 2024) and ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) exist, their performance is rarely close to VLAs and World Models. MINT predicts coarse-to-fine action tokens with a separately trained action tokenizer (Huang et al., 2026) and reaches much better success rates, but remains below the strongest WAMs and VLAs. La-JEPA fits in this category as it trains its policy DiTs from scratch while adding WAM-style future predic-tion in a pretrained JEPA feature space. 3Preprint La-JEPA Tube-motion encoding

# 3 LA-JEPA 

La-JEPA learns action generation jointly with future-feature prediction in a pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L representation space, following the separation of training-time world modeling from inference-time action generation in Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026b) and Faster-WAM (Ma et al., 2026). At decision time t, the policy maps multi-camera observations ot, proprioception st, and a language instruction ℓ to an action chunk A = ( at, . . . , a t+H−1). This section describes the fixed architecture and training procedure (Figure 2); Section 4 examines the choices within it. 3.1 ARCHITECTURE 

Visual representations. The pretrained V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L encoder (Mur-Labadia et al., 2026) processes each camera independently, producing observation features zctx from the most recent frames, and future targets zfut representing the 32 frames ahead. We normalize and spatially reduce these feature grids before projecting them into the World DiT’s embedding space. They are the model’s only visual input and prediction target, so the model gets no separate RGB pathway. Future predictions are matched to targets directly in V-JEPA space and require no pixel reconstruction. 

Language and proprioception. A frozen FLAN-T5-large encoder (Chung et al., 2022) embeds the instruction ℓ, and learned projections map proprioception st to additional tokens; together they form the conditioning sequence zcond , which both DiTs read through cross-attention. 

World–action architecture. The model is made of two diffusion transformers (DiTs) (Peebles & Xie, 2022): a World DiT over observation and future-feature tokens and an Action DiT over the action chunk. Both are initialized from scratch with separate attention projections and feed-forward layers, so pretrained knowledge enters only through the visual and language encoders. Rotary visual positional encodings (Su et al., 2021) preserve spatial and temporal information. The two DiTs inter-act at every layer, where action queries read the World DiT’s observation keys and values alongside its own. The attention mask keeps future information out of the action pathway: observation tokens attend only to observations, future tokens to observations and futures, and action tokens to observa-tions and actions. Actions therefore never access future features directly, yet predictive supervision still shapes the observation representations they read through the shared World DiT parameters dur-ing training. 3.2 TRAINING AND INFERENCE

Action inference. At inference, we drop the future-feature tokens, initialize the action chunk from Gaussian noise, and integrate the action velocity field from τa = 0 to τa = 1 with Euler steps, holding the World DiT’s timestep conditioning at a fixed value τctx = 0 (since we do not predict the futures anyway). Since observation tokens attend only to one another under fixed conditioning, their per-layer keys and values are computed once and cached, and only the Action DiT runs at each denoising step. Like most recent policies, we convert the predicted action tokens to the robot’s action space, execute the first k actions of the chunk ( k = 10 on LIBERO and 24 on RoboTwin, as in Fast-WAM), and then predict a new chunk from the latest observations. 

# 4 DESIGN ANALYSIS 

Experimental setup. We test the design choices behind Section 5 with the small (S) policy unless stated otherwise. We ask, in turn, whether to predict future features, in which feature space, how to encode motion (Section 4.1), and then how to make the policy robust to observation changes (Section 4.2). The studies use LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and clean-trained RoboTwin 2.0 (Liu et al., 2023; Fei et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025); in-distribution (IID) evaluation refers to LIBERO or clean RoboTwin scenes, and out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation to LIBERO-Plus or randomized scenes. Figure 3 reports each choice’s gain in success in percentage points (hereafter, points), grouped into blocks A to F. Each block has its own reference recipe, so comparisons hold within a block; Table 6 lists baselines and evaluation budgets, and Appendix A documents the protocols. 4.1 PREDICTION TARGET AND FEATURE SPACE 

Predictive supervision. We first test whether predicting future features benefits the policy, com-paring joint action and future-feature prediction with action-only training (Figure 3A). Future pre-diction raises RoboTwin success by 7.0 points in clean and 3.7 in randomized scenes. On LIBERO-Plus, it improves success for each of three training seeds, by 2.1 points on average, with every paired comparison significant ( p < 0.01 , McNemar; Appendix A.1).

Each policy learns action and future-feature prediction with the normalization and projections suited to its representation, so the comparison evaluates each representation together with its interface to the policy. Temporal encoding. Unlike LDA-1B’s DINO, which only encodes images, V-JEPA 2.1 can pro-cess videos, which raises the question of how the observations and future targets should be encoded. We tested three configurations. Static encodings treat every frame as an image: the observation is the current frame, and eight future targets, spaced four frames apart up to 32 frames ahead, are encoded separately. Tube encoding instead processes two-frame clips aligned with the encoder’s tubelets, ending at the current frame for the observation and at each target frame for the futures; the obser-vation clip thus gives the policy a motion cue without access to future observations. Tube-motion 

keeps the tube observation but jointly encodes the 16 future frames, spaced two frames apart, so each of the eight future feature grids attends over the whole predicted horizon (Figure 2, right). Relative 6Preprint to static encoding on RoboTwin (Figure 3C), tube encoding raises clean success by 1.1 points and randomized success by 3.6, and tube-motion preserves the randomized gain ( +3 .7) while raising the clean gain to 3.4; the final recipe therefore uses tube-motion. 

Token reduction. Before entering the World DiT, the encoded feature grids are reduced spatially, either by averaging neighboring features (mean pooling) or by concatenating them into one token (patch grouping). We initially used mean pooling for its simplicity, since it can be applied once to the precomputed features. On RoboTwin, patch grouping improved randomized success, so the final RoboTwin recipe uses it; LIBERO did not benefit from it (89.35% against 94.05% with mean pooling), so we kept mean pooling there (Appendix A.1). 4.2 ROBUSTNESS AND ENCODER ADAPTATION 

Camera dropout. To reduce reliance on any single view, we drop eligible cameras during training, always retaining at least one view and removing the dropped cameras’ future targets from the loss (Figure 3D). This is the condition dropout used to train classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022), although we sample without guidance. Dropping only the wrist cameras raises randomized RoboTwin success by 7.1 points, while clean success remains essentially unchanged. LIBERO benefits from this too: by allowing either camera to be dropped, we raise LIBERO-Plus success by 10.2 points, with large gains under camera-viewpoint and sensor-noise perturbations (Table 8), while in-distribution success slightly improves. 

Proprioception dropout. Dropping the proprioceptive conditioning with probability 0.1 during training, with the configuration otherwise unchanged, raises RoboTwin success by 3.8 points in clean and 4.2 in randomized scenes (Figure 3E). The final recipe therefore uses both camera and proprioception dropout. 

Data augmentation and encoder adaptation. Finally, we combine image augmentation of the observations with LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) adaptation of the observation encoder; future targets are still computed from unmodified frames by the original encoder for training efficiency purposes (Appendix B). Relative to a plain LIBERO policy (Figure 3F), LoRA alone adds 1.4 points on LIBERO-Plus, augmentation alone 5.8, and both together 7.2, while in-distribution success remains unchanged. The combined recipe also improves RoboTwin, raising clean success by 5.7 points and randomized success by 16.0 (Appendix A.2). 4.3 FINAL RECIPE 

These studies define the final recipe: joint action and future prediction in V-JEPA 2.1 tube-motion features, camera and proprioception dropout, and image augmentation with encoder LoRA. We train it at three sizes, S, B, and L (Table 9), which correspond to 0.10, 0.34, and 1.14 B trainable parameters respectively. Appendix B documents the remaining benchmark-specific settings.

La-JEPA also outperforms the two closest JEPA-based world-action models on LIBERO-Plus and RoboTwin 2.0, although both retain pretrained predictors (Table 3). LeapBot-WA, which also pre-trains on robot data and deploys 10.4B parameters, falls below all three La-JEPA sizes on LIBERO-Plus with both the released and the corrected instructions (Table 14 in Appendix B). JEPA-WAM, built on a pretrained Qwen2.5 model (Qwen et al., 2024), is exceeded on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus by all three La-JEPA sizes, S and B deploying fewer parameters. On RoboTwin, JEPA-WAM trains and evaluates on 20 of the 50 tasks, and on these tasks every La-JEPA size, trained on all 50, leads it by more than 15 points in clean and 9 points in randomized scenes. This subset is easier than the full benchmark, since per-task DP3 and ACT policies score about 20 points higher on it in clean scenes (Appendix B); because it was chosen by JEPA-WAM and applies equally to both methods, we do not consider the comparison biased in our favor. Even when JEPA-WAM’s scores are extrapolated to all 50 tasks using this difficulty gap, every La-JEPA size remains ahead in both regimes. LDA-1B, pretrained on over 30k hours of human and robot data with a 4B vision-language model (Lyu et al., 2026), remains far below on both benchmarks (Yan et al., 2026b). 5.4 COMPUTATIONAL COST 

La-JEPA updates 0.10 to 1.14 B parameters during training, far fewer than the video-generator and vision-language-action baselines (2.0 to 7.8 B; Tables 1 and 2) while showing lower replan latency at matched or better performance. We time complete policy calls, with ten denoising steps on one consumer GPU (Table 15, Appendix C). With optimized (compiled) builds, S and B run two to five times faster than Fast-WAM and Faster-WAM, and S with original-kernel CUDA graphs, which reproduce uncompiled actions bit-for-bit, remains 2.6 × faster than optimized Fast-WAM. Compiled builds do not significantly change closed-loop LIBERO success (Appendix C), and the physical-robot policy runs compiled. Reducing inference to three denoising steps lowers latency further while keeping success within about one point (Appendix E.2), consistent with evidence that diffusion policies do not require many denoising steps (Cl´ emente et al., 2025). 5.5 REAL -R OBOT EXPERIMENTS

# 6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Scope of recipe optimization. We aim to show the potential of compact world-action policies rather than to optimize them exhaustively: beyond the targeted design studies, we did not systemati-cally search learning rates, weight decay, dropout rates, LoRA configurations, or the relative capacity of the World and Action DiTs. This lack of scale-specific tuning, together with limited target data, may contribute to the non-monotonic RoboTwin scaling; our experiments do not isolate these ef-fects. We also recognize that many high-performing baselines have external supervision objectives and robot-specific architectures that we do not: while this work does outline the performance of a policy with a simple setup, we cannot be certain that our improvements would stack with other work’s policy upgrades. 

Large-scale pretraining. Our policies learn from target demonstrations alone, so how La-JEPA scales with embodied pretraining remains open. Adapting the policy and its visual representation across embodiments and environments would also test whether data, rather than capacity, limits the larger models. We consider this the principal future direction of research that should come out of this paper. La-JEPA remains below the strongest embodied-pretrained methods on randomized RoboTwin scenes, and its real-robot average, although comparable to that of π0.5, comes from four 10 Preprint tasks on one robot setup and does not establish parity across broader manipulation demands. The richer supervision, synthetic data, and additional objectives used by some stronger baselines are natural extensions of La-JEPA that we have not tested. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

We asked whether world-action policies must inherit the architecture of a generative backbone, and whether a compact transformer trained from scratch can compete with those that do. Trained in a pretrained JEPA feature space, La-JEPA reaches the strongest RoboTwin 2.0 clean and randomized results among policies without embodied pretraining, and the strongest LIBERO-Plus results once the benchmark’s corrupted instructions are corrected, while running up to 5 × faster than Fast-WAM. On four physical tasks, La-JEPA’s average success is comparable to that of the embodied-pretrained 

π0.5. Through our study, we empirically validate the benefits of the V-JEPA latent space for control tasks, provide some guidance on the benefits of different robustness design improvements and find that heavy generative models are not a requirement for high-performing learned policies.

# B TRAINING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS 

This appendix specifies the headline training recipe, the data and checkpoint selection behind the reported La-JEPA results, the benchmark protocols, and the sources of baseline results. 

Architecture and optimization. Table 9 summarizes the architecture, optimization, conditioning, and inference settings of the headline recipe at the three policy sizes. 

Observation augmentation. Table 10 lists the final augmentation recipe. After the gate that leaves a window unmodified, transformation gates are independent, and parameters are shared across frames. Only observations are augmented; future targets are computed from unmodified frames by the frozen encoder. 

Precomputed features and token reduction. Before training, we encode every demonstration once with the frozen V-JEPA 2.1 encoder and store the resulting features. Stored-feature policies, 19 Preprint used in most design studies, read both observations and future targets from these stores and never run the encoder during training. Encoder LoRA and image augmentation require encoding the ob-servations at every training step, so the final policies encode their observation clips online but still read their future targets from the stores. Encoding the targets online as well would dominate the encoder cost of each step, since each camera’s targets span 16 frames, eight times as many encoder tokens as its two-frame observation clip. Targets from the adapting encoder also do not improve success (Appendix E.1), so the targets remain those of the frozen encoder. The features are then reduced spatially before entering the World DiT. Mean pooling, the simplest reduction, averages each 2 × 2 neighborhood of a feature grid into one token. Because it can be applied once when the features are precomputed, it shrinks the stored features fourfold. Most of our development therefore used pre-pooled stores, which are much smaller and far easier to load during training than full-resolution V-JEPA features. Patch grouping instead concatenates neigh-boring features into one wider token, reducing the number of tokens without discarding any feature values, but the stored features keep their full size. Mean pooling suffices on LIBERO, whereas on RoboTwin the detail it averages away matters: patch grouping raises randomized success by 4.1 points (Appendix A.1), so the final RoboTwin recipe uses patch grouping. 

Training data and checkpoint selection. On LIBERO, all three sizes train on all 1,777 demon-strations retained by the revised pipeline (Appendix B) for 150,000 updates, and we report the final checkpoints’ performances averaged over three training seeds; L additionally uses full-depth LoRA. The final RoboTwin S, B, and L policies train on the 2,438 clean demonstrations retained by the revised pipeline (Appendix B) with three training seeds each. For each seed, the checkpoint is se-lected among 90k, 120k, and 150k updates on a 1,000-episode clean grid disjoint from the evaluation episodes, which picks 150k for every seed except one L seed (120k); the selected policies are eval-uated on 5,000 episodes per condition with action noise redrawn at every replan, and we report the mean over seeds. 

Benchmark protocols. LIBERO scores are success rates over the four standard suites. LIBERO-Plus scores are success rates over all 10,030 evaluation episodes of the seven perturbation categories, including background textures, weighting every episode rather than every category equally. Litera-ture aggregates are recomputed from reported per-category rates when needed. La-JEPA’s headline LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus evaluations use MuJoCo 3.3.2 and the upstream suite-specific step lim-its (220, 280, 300, and 520 steps, after 10 settling steps). The design studies, evaluation-setting sweeps, and compiled-build validation keep a flat budget in every suite, 700 steps except for the LIBERO pair of block B (600 steps after 30 settling steps; Appendix A.1), so each comparison is made within one protocol. RoboTwin policies are trained on clean demonstrations and evaluated in clean and randomized scenes, and the mean of the two regimes is the leaderboard aggregate. La-JEPA’s RoboTwin policies are conditioned on each episode’s own RoboTwin instruction in both training and evaluation. JEPA-WAM reports 20 RoboTwin tasks, so Table 3 restricts our evaluations to the same tasks, using 2,000 episodes per condition and training seed. JEPA-WAM trains only on these 20 tasks, whereas our policies train on all 50. The subset is easier than the full benchmark: per-task DP3 and ACT policies reach 73.9% and 51.8% clean success on it (Lin et al., 2026), against 55.2% and 29.7% over all 50 tasks (Chen et al., 2025). Projected to all 50 tasks, JEPA-WAM’s suc-cess is estimated at 65.5% clean and 25.0% randomized (see the full-suite estimate below), below every La-JEPA size in Table 2. Methods pretrained on robot trajectories beyond the target demon-strations are outside the main comparison and are explicitly identified in the latent world-action model comparison, the leaderboard figure, and the physical-robot comparison.

This appendix defines how we time policy calls and count parameters, the two cost measures of Section 5.4. C.1 LATENCY 

Protocol. Tables 15 and 5 time complete benchmark policy calls, not executed actions or control-loop cycles. Each timed call includes fresh instruction tokenization and encoding, input preparation, transfer, and host-side action output; loading, compilation, simulation, and transport are excluded. Text and vision encoders run on parallel streams where supported. Eager execution is uncompiled; original-kernel CUDA graphs reduce launch overhead without changing kernels; optimized builds also compile the computation. The head-to-head WAM rows use ten denoising steps and 32 pre-dicted actions, whereas the three-step rows and π0.5 (official JAX JIT, ten steps, native ten-action horizon) are separate operating points. Each method keeps its native inputs: La-JEPA uses two

Action noise is pre-drawn and copied inside the timed call for La-JEPA, and drawn on the CPU inside the call for Fast-WAM. LingBot-VA is timed on the same device under the same protocol with its native implementation and appears, rounded to 10 ms, only in Table 5. 

Execution variants. For La-JEPA, original-kernel CUDA graphs reproduce eager actions bit-for-bit across 36 replays per model on 12 inputs that vary observations, instructions, and noise. Complementing the token-reduction comparison (Appendix A.1), we keep patch-grouped observa-tions and mean-pool only the future targets (Table 20). In-distribution success stays near 61–62%, whereas out-of-distribution success is non-monotonic in pooling granularity. Because the settings also differ in positional geometry, target normalization, camera weighting, and checkpoint step, these scores describe the tested recipes rather than an isolated effect of target resolution. The poli-cies are small, frozen-encoder, static-feature policies trained on the 95% split. Observations keep patch grouping with spatial factors [2 , 4, 4] for the head, left-wrist, and right-wrist cameras, and the future targets are pooled by [2 , 4, 4] , [4 , 8, 8] , or global averaging, giving [80 , 20 , 20] , [20 , 6, 6] ,or [1 , 1, 1] spatial tokens per temporal position.
