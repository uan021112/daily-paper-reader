Abstract
Understanding building facades is central to urban digi-
tal twins, heritage modelling, and sustainable city analy-
sis, yet existing LiDAR benchmarks stop at geometry and
semantic segmentation and offer little support for reason-
ing about the functional role of each architectural element.
We introduce HFX3D, a large-scale urban facade bench-
mark of approximately 492 million LiDAR points from 26
buildings in Halifax, Canada, captured across four sea-
sons, day and night, and varied weather.
Each facade
is annotated at three tiers, semantic, instance, and per-
instance functional attributes, making it, to our knowledge,
the first facade dataset to pair geometry and semantics with
functional labels.
Annotations are produced by a semi-
automatic pipeline that combines geometry-guided heuris-
tics with expert validation. We benchmark representative
point-based, graph-based, convolutional, and transformer
architectures and find that semantic segmentation is already
challenging under severe class imbalance, while instance
separation of repetitive structures and functional reason-
ing from geometry are substantially harder.
The bench-
mark establishes a new foundation for function-aware 3D
urban understanding. Project page: https://jijup.
github.io/hfx3d-benchmark/; code: https:
//github.com/jijup/hfx3d-benchmark.
1. Introduction
Accurate 3D facade modelling is central to urban digital
twins and building information modelling (BIM) [3, 23].
Recent advances in point cloud segmentation [33, 34, 41,
48] can reliably label common facade components such as
walls, windows, and doors, but semantic labels alone are in-
sufficient for high-level applications. The functional role of
each element, whether a window is operable, whether a bal-
cony provides access, whether a column is load-bearing, is
what drives structural analysis, energy modelling, and BIM
reconstruction [28, 30]. Figure 1 illustrates this within-class
ambiguity: facade elements with the same semantic label
can exhibit contrasting functional roles.
Mobile LiDAR
scanning now yields dense, RGB-coloured point clouds that
capture facade geometry at centimetre resolution [5, 37], yet
no existing 3D facade dataset provides functional annota-
tions alongside semantic and instance labels.

Recent facade point cloud benchmarks have advanced
the field along complementary directions. ZAHA [46] in-
troduced the hierarchical Level of Facade Generalization
(LoFG) for fine-grained semantic parsing, City-Facade [5]
extended facade understanding to city-scale mobile LiDAR
with semantic and instance annotations, and UnderOne-
Facade [42] emphasized geographic diversity and cross-
domain generalization. These benchmarks improve seman-
tic granularity, instance understanding, and transferability,
but do not explicitly capture the functional roles of individ-
ual facade elements, leaving a key gap for downstream BIM
and digital-twin reasoning.

dataset with three tiers of annotation (Figure 2): per-point
semantic labels across 12 classes, instance-level grouping

2. Related Work
2.1. Facade Segmentation Datasets
Facade parsing has moved from 2D image benchmarks
such as eTRIMS [24] and Graz50 [35] to dense 3D point
clouds. General-purpose outdoor benchmarks such as Se-
mantic3D [18], Paris-Lille-3D [36], and KITTI-360 [25]
provide large annotated point clouds but treat buildings as
coarse semantic units, while indoor benchmarks such as
S3DIS [1] and ScanNet [10] advanced instance segmenta-
tion in controlled settings.
Dedicated facade datasets have emerged more recently.
ArCH [29] is a pioneering heritage benchmark with de-
tailed architectural classes, and TUM-FACADE [45], ex-
tending TUM-MLS-2016 [49], advances campus-scale fine-
grained labeling. ARCH2S [6] contributes controlled syn-
thetic scenes well suited to pretraining. ZAHA [46] is the
most comprehensive geometric facade benchmark to date,
and City-Facade [5] takes a further step by adding instance-
level annotation. The concurrent UnderOneFacade [42] as-
sembles a cross-continental benchmark spanning the UK,
Germany, and Singapore with a hierarchical semantic tax-
onomy, but does not provide instance or functional labels.
Building on these efforts, HFX3D introduces per-instance
functional attributes, as summarised in Table 1.
2.2. Deep Learning for 3D Facade Segmentation
Point-based networks progressed from PointNet [33] and
PointNet++ [34] to graph-convolutional DGCNN [41].
Later work added kernel-point KPConv [38], sampling-
based RandLA-Net [19] for large outdoor scenes, and self-
attention Point Transformers [43, 44]. Despite this progress,
facade segmentation remains challenging: severe class im-
balance and the geometric similarity between planar ele-
ments leave rare classes with low recall.

3. Dataset Acquisition and Annotation
3.1. Point Cloud Acquisition
We captured 26 buildings with a GeoSLAM ZEB Horizon
handheld mobile LiDAR scanner [14] and a co-mounted
ZEB Vision camera that supplies per-point RGB colour.
The buildings were drawn from 50 surveyed across five
neighbourhoods of Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada, with the
final subset chosen to capture diversity in facade complex-
ity, building height, construction material, and architectural
era. An operator walked each facade at ground level, fol-
lowing a closed-loop trajectory where access allowed. Cap-
ture spanned six to eight months and all four seasons, day
and night, under snow, fog, and clear sun, an environmen-
tal range not reported by any existing facade benchmark in

3.2. Semantic Annotation
Four trained annotators manually annotated the point clouds
in CloudCompare, using the co-registered ZEB Vision fish-
eye imagery as visual reference.
Each building was as-
signed to a primary annotator.
We define 12 semantic
classes: 0: wall, 1: window, 2: door, 3: balcony, 4: vegeta-
tion, 5: stairs, 6: terrain, 7: roof, 8: blinds, 9: other, 10: col-
umn, and 11: arch. As shown in Figure 3, the resulting point
distribution is highly imbalanced, with wall and terrain ac-
counting for the majority of labeled points.
Annotation
was particularly challenging in regions containing geomet-
rically or visually ambiguous elements, partial occlusions
from vegetation and adjacent structures, and sparse LiDAR
returns from reflective surfaces such as glass, a known diffi-
culty for facade segmentation [46]. These ambiguities were
further exacerbated under adverse acquisition conditions,
including snow cover and low illumination. To promote
consistency across buildings and annotators, all annotations
followed predefined class definitions and were periodically
reviewed for adherence to the annotation protocol.
Every building then underwent two rounds of cross-
review by annotators who had not originally labeled it, with
disagreements resolved by majority vote. For each build-
ing we logged the IoU against the original labels, Cohen’s
Kappa [7], and the number of regions added, deleted, or
modified, applying first-round corrections before the sec-
ond.
After first-round corrections were applied, second-
round mean Cohen’s Kappa reached 0.99; the most frequent
residual correction in the second round was confusion be-
tween wall and window labels, and window remains the

3.3. Instance Segmentation via Clustering
To obtain instances, we cluster each semantic class with
DBSCAN [12], with ϵ tuned per building from 0.09 to 0.7
so that individual elements, such as a single window in
a glass pane or a single bush, are separated into distinct
instances. This automatic step produced 8,586 candidate
instances across the 26 buildings; annotators then refined
these segments in CloudCompare, merging over-segmented
and splitting under-segmented elements and correcting se-
mantic labels in the same pass, yielding 6,418 final in-
stances, a 25.3% reduction that reflects DBSCAN’s ten-
dency to over-segment rather than under-segment on this
data. One building required net splitting rather than merg-
ing, confirming that review corrected in both directions
rather than uniformly pruning clusters. Residual unassigned
points were eliminated after refinement for all but two build-
ings. Each point then carries an instance ID alongside its
semantic label.

Human Validation.
The refined functional suggestions
were subsequently reviewed by annotators using the asso-
ciated ZEB scanner imagery and Google Street View [16].
During the review, each instance was highlighted in 3D to-
gether with its suggested functional attributes and corre-
sponding confidence scores. Annotators verified the sug-
gested assignments, corrected ambiguous cases, and added
attributes that were not reliably captured by the automatic
refinement. For example, annotators assessed window op-
erability using visible geometric cues and the associated
imagery, while they distinguished decorative columns from
structural columns by revising their load bearing and
aesthetic attributes.
The validated results were then
used as the final instance-level functional annotations.
Label Verification and Audit.
Each functional attribute
follows IFC 4.3 and Getty AAT definitions and is read from
the exterior LiDAR geometry and co-registered imagery, so
a property such as load bearing is assessed visually, not
verified against structural drawings. We audit the labels in
two ways. First, across the two review rounds on ten build-
ings spanning the train, validation, and test splits, the rounds
agree on 93.2% of applicable attribute decisions. Second, in
a test on two sampled buildings reviewed separately by two
second-round annotators, each starting from the first-round
labels, agreement reaches 99.0% of decisions, with a Co-
hen’s κ [7] of 0.93.
The final dataset associates each point with a semantic
label and instance ID, while each instance is assigned a 15-
dimensional functional attribute vector. This combination
of automatic refinement and human validation enables ef-
ficient instance-level functional annotation at dataset scale.
The dataset is partitioned at the building level into 18 train-
ing, 4 validation, and 4 test buildings, so no building con-
tributes points to more than one split. Point-wise seman-
tic and instance annotations are provided together with sep-
arate instance-level functional attribute files. The supple-
mentary material provides additional acquisition and anno-
tation details, per-building review and instance-clustering
statistics, implementation settings, extended qualitative
comparisons, and the complete cross-dataset evaluation ma-
trix.
4. Benchmark Evaluation
We establish a comprehensive benchmark for urban facade
parsing across three tasks: (i) semantic segmentation of
facade points, (ii) instance segmentation of individual fa-
cade elements, and (iii) functional attribute labeling at the
instance level. Urban facades combine strong geometric
regularities, such as coplanar windows and repeated bal-
conies, with substantial functional diversity, including ac-
cess, ventilation, and shading.
While most existing 3D
benchmarks focus on semantic labeling, instance-level and
functional reasoning remain comparatively underexplored
for large-scale outdoor point clouds. Sections 4.1 and 4.2
benchmark seven semantic architectures and three learned
instance methods. Because the learned instance methods
must infer object boundaries without the true semantic class
of each point, we additionally report a geometry-based clus-
tering baseline given ground-truth semantics; the two are re-
ported in separate tables rather than merged into one leader-
board. Section 4.3 measures cross-dataset transfer, and Sec-
tion 4.4 examines functional attributes.

All models are trained on the training split and evalu-
ated on the held-out test split defined in Section 3; the train-
ing, validation, and test splits contain 343.7M, 68.4M, and
79.7M points, respectively. To ensure fair cross-architecture
comparison, the evaluation constraint is 100% point cov-
erage before any metric is computed rather than one pre-
scribed sampling mechanism. Six of the seven models meet
it through a shared possibility-guided KNN-crop procedure:
crops are sampled iteratively around the least-covered point
via a cKD-tree over the full building, and per-point soft-
max probabilities from overlapping crops are averaged and
argmax-decoded.
Crop sizes follow each model’s native
input scale, ranging from 4,096 to 120k points. KPConv

4.2. Benchmarking Task: Instance Segmentation
Three learned instance segmentation methods are trained
from scratch on raw point features, without ground-truth
semantic labels: PointGroup [22], which groups points us-

which combines instance-aware sampling with box-aware
dynamic convolution; and SoftGroup [40], which performs
soft semantic grouping followed by top-down refinement.
SoftGroup leads with 0.29 AP50, ahead of ISBNet (0.09)
and PointGroup (0.04) (Table 4).
Instance AP here de-
pends heavily on the pipeline, not only the data. Scoring
subsampled predictions against full-resolution ground truth
caps mask IoU and gives near-zero AP: ground-truth masks
reach only 0.31 AP50 on one validation building, against
0.91 with masks propagated to every point. A 0.9 m group-
ing radius also chains neighbouring elements. We therefore
propagate masks to every point before scoring and group
PointGroup and SoftGroup at 7.5 cm.
The remaining difficulty is class-specific. PointGroup
over-fragments: the 52 trees of one test building receive 358
confident vegetation predictions, which outrank correct pre-
dictions elsewhere in pooled AP. ISBNet scores well only
on balconies (0.54 AP50). Column and stairs, with only 14
and 5 test instances, supply about 70% of SoftGroup’s mAP.
Windows, the most numerous class, are easier to locate than
to delineate: SoftGroup’s window AP falls from 0.56 at IoU
0.25 to 0.22 at IoU 0.50.

4.3. Cross-Dataset Generalization
To test how well the proposed benchmark generalises be-
yond its own facades, we compare it against ZAHA [46],
the closest published facade benchmark, remapping both
datasets to a shared 11-class taxonomy (vegetation, absent
from ZAHA, is dropped) and training with xyz-only input
for a feature-neutral comparison. All seven architectures
follow the same protocol as Section 4.1.
KPConv leads every setting in Figure 6, but its cross-
dataset transfer is markedly asymmetric:
training on
HFX3D and testing on ZAHA loses 29.5 percentage points,
while the reverse loses only one. This asymmetry holds
across architectures: every model tested on ZAHA does
worse when trained on HFX3D instead, a uniform domain
penalty absent in the other direction. Tested on HFX3D,
cross-dataset training is architecture-dependent rather than
uniformly harmful: Point Transformer v2 actually improves
when trained on ZAHA instead of HFX3D itself, and Point
Transformer v3 is essentially unaffected, while the remain-
ing architectures lose substantially.
This asymmetry likely reflects several factors rather than
a single cause: HFX3D’s broader environmental and ar-
chitectural variability, together with the label-space harmo-
nization (shared 11-class taxonomy, dropped vegetation and
colour) and annotation-convention differences between the
datasets. We therefore report it as an observation rather than
evidence of superior robustness.
Mixed training tells a similar story: combining both
datasets helps DGCNN and Point Transformer v3 on
HFX3D, but hurts KPConv, PointNet, and RandLA-Net,
with a smaller effect either way on ZAHA.

5. Conclusion
We introduced HFX3D, a large-scale LiDAR facade bench-
mark with per-instance functional annotations, spanning
approximately 492 million points across 26 diverse urban
buildings in Halifax, Canada. Beyond standard semantic la-
bels, the benchmark provides 15 functional attributes per in-
stance, derived through architectural ontologies, geometry-
guided heuristics, and human validation. We define three
tasks of increasing complexity: point-level semantic seg-
mentation, object-level instance grouping, and instance-
level functional attribute labeling.
We benchmark these
tasks across seven semantic architectures, three learned
instance methods plus four geometry-clustering baselines,
and an evidence ablation for functional attributes. Semantic
segmentation remains difficult under class imbalance and
glass-surface sparsity. Instance AP stays low, concentrated
in a few rare, well-separated classes, while dense repeated
windows resist delineation.
Functional labeling is hard-
est, especially for ambiguous attributes such as operable
and aesthetic and rare ones such as drainage and
vegetation support.
The benchmark establishes a new level of annotation
richness for 3D urban understanding and opens directions at
the intersection of point cloud learning, architectural knowl-
edge representation, and BIM. Future work includes ex-
tending functional annotations to interior scans, incorpo-
rating complementary sensing modalities such as thermal
infrared, and developing architectures that jointly optimise
semantic, instance, and functional reasoning through shared
functional-geometric representations.

classes with instance AP. The eight thing classes are win-
dow, door, balcony, column, arch, blinds, vegetation, and
stairs; the four stuff classes are wall, terrain, roof, and other.

B. Functional Attribute Priors and Rules
Each semantic class maps to a fixed set of candidate func-
tional attributes (the semantic-to-functional candidate map-
ping, Fig. 4 of the main paper), and each candidate carries
a semantic prior P(a | c). These priors are expert heuris-
tics set from architectural knowledge and fixed before the
human review round; attributes outside a class’s candidate
set are fixed to 0. Table 9 lists the priors for every class.
Instance-dependent attributes are then refined from geomet-
ric evidence G and contextual evidence C, fused with the
prior in the odds domain as described in the main paper; Ta-
ble 10 specifies the evidence used for each refined attribute.
Context relations are computed on a graph that links in-
stances whose bounding boxes lie within 1.5 m or that are
among each other’s 12 nearest centroids; all constants are
provided in the released configuration. Reviewers were not

D. Additional Qualitative Analysis
Figure 13 shows qualitative semantic segmentation predic-
tions on two test buildings for all seven benchmarked back-
bones. The ranking is visible throughout: predictions retain
progressively less fine structure from KPConv to DGCNN,
with the weakest models reducing each facade to its domi-
nant wall and terrain classes.
Figure 14 and Figure 15 show qualitative instance seg-
mentation results on the four test buildings. Learned meth-
ods (PointGroup, SoftGroup, ISBNet), trained end-to-end
from raw point features with no semantic labels supplied,
are shown separately from geometry-based clustering (DB-
SCAN, Mean Shift, K-Means, HDBSCAN) evaluated as an
oracle given ground-truth semantics.
The learned meth-
ods separate instances far less reliably than the oracle-
semantic clustering, fragmenting or missing repetitive fa-
cade elements such as windows and blinds; SoftGroup is
the strongest of the three (Table 4). Geometry-based clus-
tering given ground-truth semantics in Figure 15 instead re-
covers coherent, separable instances, with HDBSCAN the
most consistent across elements (Table 5).
E. Per-Class Instance Segmentation Results
Table 11 reports AP50 per thing class on the test split for all
seven instance segmentation entries. Learned scores vary
widely by class: SoftGroup ranges from 0.001 on arch to
0.857 on column, ISBNet is strong only on balcony (0.538),
and PointGroup peaks on window (0.170). PointGroup’s
over-fragmentation (Section 4.2 of the main paper) is espe-
cially costly in pooled AP: its 358 vegetation predictions on
BLD005 bury correct ones elsewhere, so vegetation AP50
is 0.52 on BLD026 alone but only 0.034 pooled.
Window is the weakest class for DBSCAN and HDB-
SCAN despite being the most numerous, because neigh-
bouring panes merge, whereas detached classes such as
balcony and column are recovered far more reliably under
HDBSCAN.
