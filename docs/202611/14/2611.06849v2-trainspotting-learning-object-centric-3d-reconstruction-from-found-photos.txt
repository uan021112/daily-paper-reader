Abstract
State-of-the-art feedforward reconstruction models perform
poorly on in-the-wild object-centric photo collections. They
are trained largely on single-session object captures whose im-
ages vary in viewpoint while the object and its environment
remain nearly fixed. Public photo archives contain a different
form of multi-view data. They hold found photos of the same
object or design: photos taken independently by many people
across years, environments, cameras, and sometimes physical
units. In this work, we introduce TRAINSPOTTING, a dataset
of 12,068 objects and 311,886 photos from Wikimedia Com-
mons. We reconstruct each object to recover camera poses,
multi-view stereo depth, and object masks. We categorize the
objects into three regimes: objects fixed at one site, objects
that move between sites, and designs shared by many physical
units. Evaluating recent feedforward reconstruction models
on TRAINSPOTTING shows that they fail most severely in the
last two regimes. Fine-tuning VGGT-Ωon TRAINSPOTTING
significantly improves camera pose, depth, and 3D geometry
on held-out objects. We observe similar improvements on the
independent NAVI in-the-wild benchmark, which contains pho-
tos of each object in different environments. Performance on
conventional benchmarks remains largely unchanged.
1. Introduction
Choose life. Choose a train, a statue, a pipe organ. Choose
a 1967 Volkswagen Beetle, a K6 red telephone box in London.
We chose not to choose. We reconstructed them all.
Found photos of objects like those listed above accumulate
in public archives such as Wikimedia Commons over many
years.
Feedforward reconstruction models [48, 49, 52],
however, obtain most of their object-level training data from
datasets that record each object in a single video or capture
session [28, 31, 55]. These captures densely sample nearby
views while the object and its environment remain nearly fixed.
The photos of an object on Wikimedia Commons follow no
shared capture plan. Their viewpoints are unevenly sampled,

Standard reconstruction pipelines are unreliable in such in-
the-wild settings, and so are feedforward reconstruction models,
which are largely trained on their output (Figure 2). Classical
structure from motion relies on repeatable correspondences
that agree with one rigid scene [35]. A changing environment
provides few such correspondences, and the object may occupy
only a small part of each photo. Changes to an individual
object over time and differences between physical units further
weaken geometric consistency. Together, these factors can result
in incorrect reconstructions, so building a dataset from these
photos requires identifying the photos of the intended object,
matching them robustly, and verifying the recovered geometry.
In this work, we propose a dataset and an evaluation protocol
for feedforward reconstruction of objects from in-the-wild photo
collections. To build this dataset, we collect candidate categories
from Wikidata and Wikimedia Commons, such as Cristo
Redentor, the Hubble Space Telescope, and the Ford Mustang.
We use a large language model (LLM) to retain the categories
that depict a rigid physical object or design (Section 3). To
address the failures of standard pipelines on these collections,
we propose a new object reconstruction pipeline, combining
dense learned matching with geometric verification of the
recovered camera poses and depth maps (Section 4). The
result of our pipeline is TRAINSPOTTING, an in-the-wild
object-centric dataset that contains 12,068 objects and 311,886
photos from Wikimedia Commons paired with camera poses,

TRAINSPOTTING and find that they perform substantially worse
on these in-the-wild collections than on conventional bench-

2. Related Work
3D datasets. 3D datasets have grown rapidly in scale. Synthetic
datasets such as Objaverse [9, 10] and ShapeNet [7] provide
textured 3D models. Datasets built with 3D scanners include
DTU [1], Google Scanned Objects [12], and OmniOb-
ject3D [53], and contain at most a few thousand objects.
Meanwhile, CO3D [31], MVImgNet [54, 62], and uCO3D [28]
reach a larger scale by crowdsourcing videos of objects and
annotating them with 3D information via structure from motion.
In such datasets, each object is recorded in one coordinated
capture [28, 31, 55, 62]. ScanNet [8], ScanNet++ [61], and
DL3DV [27] record scenes in the same way. Few datasets pho-
tograph objects in different environments. PASCAL3D+ [56],
ObjectNet3D [57], and Pix3D [40] align CAD models to
internet images of objects, but the models only approximate
the depicted objects. NAVI [19] is the closest dataset to our
setting. It photographs each object in different environments
with different cameras and aligns each image to a 3D scan of
that object. In all of these object-scale datasets, each image is
aligned to its corresponding 3D model by hand, and per-image
geometry such as depth is rendered from that model rather than
reconstructed from the photos. In contrast, TRAINSPOTTING
consists of multi-view photo collections of objects taken by

Reconstruction from internet photo collections. Photo
Tourism [38] and Building Rome in a Day [2] reconstructed
landmarks from unordered photos taken by many photographers.
Later datasets use such reconstructions as supervision for
learned models.
MegaDepth [24] reconstructs about 200
landmarks from photos on Flickr with COLMAP [35], filters
the resulting multi-view stereo depth, and uses it to supervise
single-view depth prediction. MegaScenes [42] reconstructs
80k Wikimedia Commons categories of scenes with COLMAP
using SIFT features.
MegaDepth-X [23] selects densely
photographed MegaScenes scenes and reconstructs them with
MASt3R-SfM [13] and doppelganger disambiguation [58].
All three contain scene-level rather than object-centric
reconstructions. Of the 12,068 objects in TRAINSPOTTING,
only 1,163 (9.6%) are reconstructed as scenes in MegaScenes
and 56 (0.5%) in MegaDepth-X, almost all of them objects
permanently installed at one site, such as statues and memorials.
Objects that move between sites or exist as many physical units
are almost entirely absent from these datasets.
Learned matching and structure from motion. Classical
structure from motion (SfM) methods like COLMAP [35] match
hand-crafted keypoints and register images incrementally. Super-
Point [11] and SuperGlue [34] replace traditional keypoint detec-
tion and matching with learned components. LoFTR [39] pro-
poses detector-free matching, predicting matches directly with
a transformer. Dense matchers such as DKM [14], RoMa [15],
and RoMa v2 [16] predict a warp for every pixel of an image pair,
aiming to recover correspondences under large changes in view-
point and illumination. Several SfM systems, such as Detector-
Free SfM [18] and Dense-SfM [21], build on such dense
matches to form multi-view tracks and refine the tracks together
with the geometry. MASt3R-SfM [13] builds on the matches
of MASt3R [22] and jointly aligns all images. VGGSfM [47]
learns the entire reconstruction pipeline, predicting tracks, cam-
eras, and points and refining them with differentiable bundle ad-
justment. We keep the incremental reconstruction of COLMAP
and supply it with multi-view tracks built from RoMa v2
matches that pass confidence and geometric checks (Section 4).
Feedforward 3D reconstruction. Feedforward models
that regress cameras, depth, and point maps directly from
a set of images have become a standard approach to 3D
reconstruction [20, 22, 25, 41, 48, 49, 51, 52, 59, 64]. Most
of their object-level training data come from single-session
captures and renderings of synthetic 3D assets.
They are
likewise evaluated on benchmarks built with such data. Two
recent works, MegaDepth-X [23] and MegaUnScene [65]
train and evaluate feedforward models on in-the-wild internet
photo collections, but only on scene-level, not object-centric,
collections. TRAINSPOTTING is an object-level dataset similarly
built from internet photos. We fine-tune VGGT-Ωon this
dataset and compare the result with recent feedforward models
on TRAINSPOTTING and NAVI in-the-wild (Section 5).

3.1. Objects from Wikimedia Commons
Wikimedia Commons organizes photos into user-curated
categories. Categories for physical subjects are often specific
to one object or design, such as Category:LNER Class A3 4472
Flying Scotsman. Each object in TRAINSPOTTING corresponds
to one such category, and we call the images filed under it a
collection. Many of these categories are linked from Wikidata,
the structured knowledge base supporting Wikimedia projects,
and 68% of the TRAINSPOTTING objects have a Wikidata item.
Objects represent either individuals or designs. An individual
is a single physical unit, such as one hull, one statue, or one
numbered locomotive. A design is a shape shared by many
physical units, such as a car model or a mass-produced
telephone box with the same underlying geometry. We pool
all units of a design into one single collection, where individual
units within the collection may differ in color, trim, and wear.
3.2. Curation at scale
We enumerate candidate categories in two ways. Some classes,
such as ships and locomotives, are enumerated from their
Wikidata items; others are found by traversing the Wikimedia
Commons category graph from root categories, such as
Cameras and Pipe organs.
The traversal records 155,744
categories after excluding branches that do not depict the object
itself, such as photos taken with a camera model, drawings, and
replicas of an object.
The traversal also reaches subcategories for places, events,
and parts related to an object, which we wish to exclude.
Title rules cannot reliably separate these categories from the
objects themselves. For example, Cannon Street station is
a railway station rather than a cannon. Therefore, we use
an LLM to classify each of the recorded categories from its
title and the categories above it in the category graph. The
classifier assigns three labels: (i) whether the category depicts

3.3. Capture variation
Different kinds of objects are characterized by different forms
of variation in context and capture. A statue fixed to its plinth
yields photos that share a common environment, but differ in
viewpoint, time, and lighting. Photos of a locomotive span
different stations with corresponding changes in the environ-
ment. A telephone-box design represents many physical units
whose exact wear and paint may differ. We denote these distinct
categories as fixed, roaming, and instance-pooled (Figure 3).
Fixed denotes an individual installed at one site, roaming an
individual that moves between sites, and instance-pooled a de-
sign shared by many physical instances. The labels follow from

4. Pseudo-Ground Truth via Reconstruction
A Wikimedia Commons category can contain photos of other
objects of the same kind, unrelated subjects, and crowded
scenes in which the object is small. In this section, we describe
how we robustly reconstruct each object to obtain camera poses,
MVS depth, and object masks via object gating, learned dense
matching, and geometric verification. Figure 4 follows one
object through the reconstruction pipeline.
4.1. Matching and structure from motion
Object gating. We use an LLM to assign a text prompt to
each object, similar to the classification in Section 3.2. We then
use SAM3 [6] with this prompt to identify images in which
the object is the main subject. We keep an image when the
largest detected box covers at least 10% of the image. Common
rejected images include crowd photos, close-ups of details, and
images of a different subject filed in the same category.
Dense matching. We compute point matches between photos
with the dense matcher RoMa v2 [16] due to its robustness
to environment and object variation. In the supplement, we
compare this choice with structure from motion from SIFT,
SuperPoint with LightGlue, and MASt3R matches on the same
images. We embed each image with the retrieval model of
MASt3R-SfM [13] and match it against the 20 closest images
in the embedding space. For each image pair, RoMa v2 returns
a dense mapping of every pixel of one image to the other along
with a certainty map. We sample candidate correspondences
from these outputs. We keep a correspondence between a point
x in one image and W(x) in the other only if

4.3. Statistics
We pass 17,470 objects with 1,251,986 photos through our
reconstruction pipeline. We report the number of objects and
photos that remain after each stage in the supplement. Object
gating removes the largest share of photos. Structure from
motion succeeds for 85% of the objects and registers 72% of
the gated photos.
Dataset. Our final dataset, TRAINSPOTTING, contains 12,068
objects and 311,886 photos. Out of all of its objects, 4,338
are fixed, 2,869 roaming, and 4,861 instance-pooled. Capture
dates are available for 84% of the photos. The median capture
span is 12 years and 90% of the objects have a capture span
of at least one year. A median object has photos by 7 different
photographers taken with 6 different camera models. Figure 5
shows these distributions by regime. For a coarse view of the
composition, we use an LLM to group the 337 object labels of
the dataset into 53 categories and 13 supercategories (Table 2).

5.1. Setup

released 1B-parameter checkpoint of VGGT-Ω[49] on the

training sample contains one object and between 2 and 24 of its
photos, chosen so that they form a connected set under covisi-
bility. We consider two photos covisible when at least 5% of the
object points of one project inside the object mask of the other.

depths of each sample in the coordinate frame of its first view.

3D points to the origin. We normalize the predictions in the
same way, following DUSt3R [51]. The model is trained with

5.2. Evaluation protocol
Samples. On TRAINSPOTTING, we draw one connected 4-view
sample from each held-out object and, when the object has
enough photos, one connected 16-view sample, for 1,635
samples from 1,000 objects. On each external dataset, we
use one sample of 8 views per scene, equally spaced along
the capture sequence when one exists and drawn at random
otherwise, since published protocols differ in the number and
selection of input views. Samples are drawn with a fixed seed.
Camera pose. We compare the predicted and ground truth
cameras on every pair of views in a sample. For each pair, we
compute the error of the relative rotation and the direction of
the relative translation and report the area under the accuracy
curve of the larger of the two errors up to 5◦and 30◦(AUC@5
and AUC@30), following PoseDiffusion [46]. All metrics are
computed per sample and then averaged over samples.
Depth. We evaluate the predicted depth maps on the pixels with
ground-truth depth. For models that only predict point maps,
we take depth as the z-coordinate of the point map in each
camera’s frame. We scale the predicted depth of all views in a
sample by a single median ratio to the ground truth. We report
the absolute relative error (AbsRel) and the inlier ratio δ<1.03,
the fraction of pixels whose predicted and ground-truth depths
differ by less than a factor of 1.03.
Point maps. For models that only predict depth, we obtain a
point map for each view by unprojecting the predicted depth
with the predicted camera. For models that predict point maps,
we use the predicted point maps directly. We align the predicted
points to the ground-truth points with a similarity transform
estimated from corresponding pixels and refined by iterative
closest point (ICP). We then report accuracy, the mean distance
from each predicted point to the nearest ground-truth point,
and completeness, the mean distance from each ground-truth

5.3. Results on TRAINSPOTTING
Table 3 reports evaluation results on TRAINSPOTTING.
SPOTTER improves on VGGT-Ωin every metric, most strongly
in camera pose and completeness, and substantially outperforms
the strongest baseline, π3. Table 4 shows that the difficulty
is concentrated in roaming and instance-pooled objects,
whose photos differ most in environment. VGGT-Ωperforms
considerably better on fixed objects than on the other two
regimes, whereas SPOTTER performs similarly on all three, and
performance further improves with more views. Figure 6 shows
reconstructions of four TRAINSPOTTING objects and two NAVI
in-the-wild objects. The baselines fail to register views taken
in different environments. Their point maps therefore contain
duplicated or fragmented copies of the object, and their cameras
are scattered. SPOTTER registers the same views consistently
and reconstructs a single object.

6. Conclusion
Current feedforward reconstruction models perform poorly on
in-the-wild object-centric photo collections, even for objects that
they reconstruct accurately from a single capture session. Such
in-the-wild collections are abundant in public photo archives,
and TRAINSPOTTING shows that they can be turned into train-
ing data at scale. Fine-tuning VGGT-Ωon TRAINSPOTTING
improves camera pose, depth, and 3D geometry on held-out
objects and on the independent NAVI in-the-wild benchmark,
while performance on conventional benchmarks remains largely
unchanged. The base models had already been trained on large
datasets of objects recorded in single capture sessions, and
we also show that training on fixed objects alone transfers
poorly to roaming and instance-pooled objects, so what these
models lacked was variation in how an object is photographed.
Beyond training, TRAINSPOTTING provides a benchmark
for reconstruction under changing environments, and its
reconstructions of objects photographed over decades allow
studying how objects and their surroundings change over time.

A. Dataset Curation
This section gives the details of the curation summarized in
Section 3.2 of the main paper.
Enumeration. Thirteen vehicle classes are enumerated from
their Wikidata items: aircraft, ships, locomotives, automobiles,
rail vehicles, buses, trams, military vehicles, trucks, motorcycles,
tractors, construction equipment, and other vehicles. Each
item gives one Wikimedia Commons category, 22,983 in total.
Whether a class names individual objects or designs is fixed by
the class: aircraft, ships, locomotives, and trams are identified
by registration, hull, or fleet number, while the remaining
classes are identified by model.
All other objects are found by traversing the Wikimedia
Commons category graph from 856 root categories. We chose
211 of them by hand, and the LLM screened the remaining 645
from the subcategories of broad hand-chosen categories. The
traversal does not descend into branches whose titles match one
of seven kinds of category that do not depict the object itself:
photos taken with a camera model, people with an object, media
about an object, replicas and scale models, institutions, parts,
and wiki maintenance categories. It prunes 37,930 branches
this way and records 155,744 categories.
Classification. Of the recorded categories, 49,252 contain at
least 25 photos, counting the photos of subcategories that depict
the same object. The LLM classifies each of them from its title
and the titles of the categories above it in the graph. The prompt
is reproduced in Section G. It keeps 40,254 categories as rigid
physical objects, rejects 8,956 for the reasons listed in Table S2,
and leaves 42 undecided. Of the kept categories, 13,347 name
an individual object or a design, and the remaining 26,907 are
categories of many objects, such as Statues in Paris, which
we do not use. After removing categories already covered
by the vehicle classes and merging subcategories that depict
the same object into their parent, 10,901 object categories
remain. Together with the vehicle classes, they form the 33,884
candidate objects of the main paper.

D. Evaluation Details
This section details the protocol of Section 5.2 of the main paper.
Samples on TRAINSPOTTING. The views of a sample form
a connected set under the covisibility relation used for training:
two photos are connected when at least 5% of the object points
of one project inside the object mask of the other. Each view
is resized to cover 512 × 512 pixels and cropped around its
principal point.
External datasets. For ordered captures, the 8 views are
equally spaced over the sequence, including its first and last
frame. For unordered collections, they are a fixed random
draw. Scenes with fewer than 8 usable frames are skipped. On
NAVI, images are center-cropped to a square, and frames that
the dataset flags as occluded are excluded. Inputs are resized
to each model’s patch multiple, and outputs are resampled to
the ground-truth grid.
Metrics. The depth scale of a sample is the median ratio of
ground-truth to predicted depth over the valid pixels of all its
views. The similarity transform of the point-map metrics is
estimated from corresponding valid pixels [43] before the ICP
refinement.

E. Additional Qualitative Results
Failure cases. Figure S2 shows four held-out samples on which
SPOTTER fails, one for each of the most common failure modes.
Views taken with long focal lengths are ambiguous between
focal length and depth, so their points can be misaligned
with the other views and appear as noise around the airliner.
Close-up views can be ambiguous in their placement on the
object, and the close-up of a wheel is localized at the wrong
position on the car. Symmetric objects produce doppelganger
ambiguities, and the cameras of the grave monument are
placed on the wrong one of its two identical pillars. Objects
far from the training distribution, such as the toy shark, whose
appearance changes drastically with its pose and placement
across the views, are reconstructed inconsistently.
