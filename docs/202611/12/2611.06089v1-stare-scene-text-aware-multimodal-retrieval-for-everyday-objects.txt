Abstract. In this study, we focus on service robots that retrieve and
transport everyday objects based on free-form instructions that may re-
fer to visual features and scene text. This is challenging because it re-
quires a multimodal understanding of the visual features and spatial
relationships of objects, as well as scene text embedded in the envi-
ronment. To address this, we propose Scene Text-Aware multimodal Re-
trieval for Everyday objects (STARE), which leverages visual and textual
cues in response to free-form instructions. STARE introduces Crosslin-
gual Visual Prompt to enhance scene text interpretation, and models
the complex relationships between named entities in the instructions and
their corresponding objects. We evaluate STARE using two novel bench-
marks, GoGetIt and TextCaps-test, both of which consist of real-world
indoor/outdoor images and instructions involving object manipulation
and navigation. The results show that STARE outperforms all baseline
methods on standard multimodal retrieval metrics. Furthermore, in phys-
ical experiments conducted under a zero-shot transfer setting, STARE
achieves an overall success rate of 80%. Our project page is available at
https://stare-projectpage.pages.dev/.
Keywords: Scene text · Multimodal retrieval · Service robots

Introduction
Service robots in indoor and outdoor environments such as households, retail
stores, and urban streets are becoming increasingly important due to labor short-
ages and aging populations. When interacting with service robots, it is natural
for users to refer to objects using scene text (e.g., labels, signage, packaging),
which provides a clearer and more direct way of specifying objects than relying
solely on visual features. Therefore, in real-world applications, it is crucial that
robots can retrieve target objects based on user instructions by leveraging scene
text and visual information.
In this study, we formulate this problem as Scene Text-Aware Multimodal
Retrieval (STMR). Given a free-form natural language instruction for object
manipulation or navigation and a set of images collected during pre-exploration,

This task is challenging because it requires an understanding of not only
the visual features and spatial relationships among objects but also scene text.
While existing methods [29, 35] have been successfully applied to multimodal
retrieval, their integration of scene text with other multimodal information is not
always sufficient. Linking named entities in instructions to scene text associated
with the corresponding objects remains challenging, limiting performance on
this task. Furthermore, because multimodal retrieval requires large-scale and
fast inference, relying solely on multimodal large language models (MLLMs) is
not practical.
To address these limitations, we propose Scene Text-Aware multimodal Re-
trieval for Everyday objects (STARE) for the STMR task. STARE consists of
three modules: a Multi-Form Instruction Encoder (MFIE) for encoding free-form
instructions, a Scene Text-aware Visual Encoder (STVE) for encoding candidate
images, and a Scene Text ReRanker (STRR) for reranking candidates based on
scene-text cues. Within STVE, we introduce Crosslingual Visual Prompt (CVP),
which visually associates scene text detected by optical character recognition
(OCR) with its image location while reducing confusion between the prompt
marks and the original scene text. The pre-exploration can be performed up
until query time to dynamically update the collected images.

– We construct the GoGetIt and TextCaps-test benchmarks, which contain

Related Work
Vision-language models (VLMs) for robotics and multimodal retrieval are ex-
tensively studied [4,14] and systematically reviewed [34,36]. Furthermore, inte-
grating scene text is effective for disambiguating visual representations [13,21].
Foundation Models for Robotics. Recent studies show that remarkable
generalization across manipulation and navigation tasks can be achieved by
leveraging large-scale foundation models, such as large language models (LLMs)
and VLMs [12, 14]. Vision-language-action models [4, 33] trained on large-scale
real robot trajectories enable scalable policy learning across diverse manipula-
tion tasks. However, these approaches are not well suited for scenarios involving
thousands of input images, as such large-scale processing incurs substantial in-
ference latency and often exceeds API token limits.
Multimodal retrieval. Numerous studies [23,39] have assessed the perfor-
mance of domestic service robots in mobile manipulation tasks based on natural
language instructions. Several existing methods [8,16,32,38] perform multimodal
retrieval to identify target objects in the environment, and output ranked lists
of relevant images based on natural language instructions. Other methods re-
trieve target objects and output text-based answers or waypoints [37]. Recent
benchmarks also evaluate multimodal retrieval in terms of reasoning and knowl-
edge [20]. Despite rapid advances, few methods leverage scene text (e.g., labels,
signage), although it often disambiguates the target. We address this gap through
scene text-aware multimodal retrieval.
Scene Text. Research on images containing scene text includes recognition
and detection [18], retrieval [9,40], and VQA/synthesis [2,11]. In particular, scene
text retrieval methods often integrate OCR-extracted textual and positional in-
formation with visual features to improve alignment with query texts and achieve
higher retrieval accuracy. Mishra et al. [24] study visual question answering by
reading text in images. ViSTA [9] embeds OCR-extracted text and positions
and fuses them with visual features via a fusion token in a transformer. In con-
trast to these approaches, STARE integrates text and position into a narrative
representation of object-level associations and introduces an auxiliary similarity
function for efficient matching.

Methodology
3.1
Problem Statement
In this paper, we define the Scene Text-Aware Multimodal Retrieval (STMR)
task as follows: a mobile robot collects images, which may include scene texts,
from a wide range of indoor or outdoor environments through pre-exploration.
Given a free-form natural language instruction to manipulate an object or navi-
gate, the model retrieves the target images from the pre-collected images. STARE
does not assume the presence of scene text and is applicable whether or not scene
text is present, as demonstrated by its performance on the LTRRIE benchmark.
The model is expected to rank images that correspond to the instruction highly
in the output list. Additional task details are provided in Section ?? of the
supplementary material.
The terminology used in this paper is defined as follows. An “instruction”
refers to a free-form natural language instruction for object manipulation or
navigation that includes referring expressions. A “target object” is the object
referred to by an instruction, and a “target image” is an image containing that
object. An instruction may have multiple target images. “Scene text” refers to
text that exists within an image. We assume that the target images contain
either objects that serve as navigation targets (e.g., buildings, signs) or everyday
objects that can be manipulated (e.g., kitchen utensils, bottles).

image-side processing during pre-exploration from instruction-side processing at

3.3
Multi-Form Instruction Encoder
At query time, STARE first performs query-side preprocessing on xtxt. Query
expansion and named-entity extraction are generated jointly in a single LLM
call. The expanded query is used by MFIE, whereas the extracted named enti-
ties are subsequently used by STRR. MFIE models complex expressions within
xtxt by incorporating text embeddings and LLM-based query expansion. Query
expansion, a technique for expanding a query with semantically related terms,
has been less explored in closely related studies. We do not use xtxt directly,
because named entities in xtxt (e.g., “Thayers Witch Hazel”) are often under-
represented in the training data of VLMs, which can result in inadequate multi-
modal embeddings. Instead, we apply query expansion to generate descriptions
capturing the visual characteristics of named entities (e.g., “A transparent glass
bottle with a red lid and ...”). Query expansion thus aligns named entities with
familiar visual attributes. It uses the LLM’s world knowledge to describe the
target’s appearance, complementing lexical matching when product names are
incomplete, paraphrased, or translated.
Based on xtxt, MFIE leverages a language encoder and an image-aligned text
encoder to extract various features associated with xtxt at multiple semantic
levels. The unified representation htxt ∈Rdtxt is obtained as follows:
  \b m {h }_{\text { txt}} = \text {SSM}\left ( \left [ f(\bm {x}_{\text {txt}}); f(\text {QE}(\bm {x}_{\text {txt}})) \right ] \right ) ,
(2)
where dtxt denotes the output dimension of the MFIE module, and f(·) repre-
sents multiple text encoders, such as Stella [42] and the CLIP text encoder [29].
QE(xtxt) is the LLM-based query expansion of xtxt. Stella extracts pure lin-
guistic features, whereas the CLIP text encoder produces image-aligned textual
representations. We use an SSM to model relationships among multi-granularity
features and to improve inference efficiency.

3.4
Scene Text-aware Visual Encoder
STVE combines image features extracted using image encoders, fine-grained im-
age features using overlapped patchification, and narrative representations that
consider scene text. The overlapped patchified features allow the module to cap-
ture finer details of the image. Overlapped patchification is used because stan-
dard patchification cannot accurately capture visual features of objects or scene
texts located at patch boundaries. Specifically, x(i)
img is divided into overlapping
cells, which are encoded by multiple image encoders (e.g., CLIP, DINOv2 [27])
to produce multimodal features h(i)
fine. Using multiple image encoders allows the
model to capture information across both semantic and spatial levels, as shown
in [30].
Next, we introduce the narrative representations, which are generated us-
ing Crosslingual Visual Prompt (CVP). Many everyday objects contain scene
texts, making it essential to extract image features that effectively incorporate
scene texts. However, existing methods [8,16,35,38] do not sufficiently integrate
scene texts with image features, limiting their effectiveness in multimodal re-
trieval tasks. To fully leverage scene texts, simply extracting scene texts and
their positions using OCR is insufficient. In environments where multiple scene
texts are present, OCR results are not enough to accurately associate each text
with the correct object. Occlusions or unfavorable orientations can also result
in inaccurate OCR-detected scene texts. To address these challenges, we use an
MLLM to extract linguistic features from images that capture complex referring
expressions, spatial relationships, and relationships between objects and scene
text.
However, relying solely on MLLMs can cause hallucinations, where incorrect
or nonexistent text is generated. We mitigate this issue by providing the MLLM
with OCR-detected scene texts and visually marking their locations within the
image. We do not use conventional visual prompting because inserted prompts
may be misinterpreted as scene text by downstream multimodal models. Un-
like typical visual prompting, CVP employs non-alphanumeric visual marks to
distinguish the marks from scene texts present in the image. These marks are
chosen for their suitability for enumerated list-style descriptions.
This representation is generated by first performing third-party OCR on

The CVP and scene texts are then passed to a multimodal large language model

instruction and scene text associated with candidate objects. This is particu-

and scene text. Moreover, retrieval settings require fast similarity calculations

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Benchmarks. We constructed the GoGetIt and TextCaps-test benchmarks for
the STMR task. The GoGetIt benchmark was constructed because existing mul-
timodal retrieval datasets [22,31] are not always suitable for robotic manipulation
or navigation tasks. Although the RefText dataset [5] uses scene texts to iden-
tify the target object, some instructions are overly simple, with several consisting
of only a single word. To address this, we annotated samples with longer and
more practical instructions that describe robotic actions suitable for execution
in real-robot experiments.
However, evaluating multimodal foundation models on RefText raises poten-
tial data-leakage concerns because some RefText images originate from public
datasets such as COCO [19], which may also be used during pretraining. Ref-
Text’s random train/validation/test split [5] adds to these concerns. This con-
cern is substantial: since 61.9% of RefText samples are from public datasets,
approximately 50% may overlap with the pretraining corpora of baselines like
Long-CLIP and BLIP-2. Thus, we required a different benchmark to enable
a fair comparison with foundation-model baselines. The original TextCaps cap-
tions are not robot instructions, so we created TextCaps-test from TextCaps [31]
with newly annotated instructions.
We also evaluated the performance of our method in settings without scene
text using the LTRRIE dataset [15], a multimodal retrieval dataset featuring
indoor environments. The instructions and images in the LTRRIE dataset were
collected from the REVERIE dataset [28] and the Matterport3D simulator, re-
spectively.
Statistics. The GoGetIt benchmark consists of 27,280 samples and 3,657
images containing scene text, collected from diverse indoor and outdoor environ-
ments. The GoGetIt-RefText subset contains 23,323 samples with instructions
sourced from the RefText [5] dataset, while the GoGetIt-Instruction subset con-
tains 3,957 referring-expression instructions newly collected from 129 annotators.
The TextCaps-test benchmark consists of 400 instructions provided by 10 anno-
tators and 105 images containing scene text. The LTRRIE [15] dataset consists
of 5,108 English instructions and 3,569 images, collected from 67 indoor environ-
ments. For LTRRIE, we use the original train/validation/test split and image
counts.
In the GoGetIt benchmark, the RefText and Instruction subsets were parti-
tioned following the same split as the RefText dataset. The training set contains
17,352 samples from the RefText subset and 3,126 from the Instruction subset.
The validation set contains 1,433 RefText samples and 266 Instruction samples.
The test set includes 4,538 RefText samples, 565 Instruction samples, and 400

TextCaps-test samples. Including LTRRIE, the training set consists of 31,071
samples, the validation set contains 2,096 samples, and the test set includes
6,004 samples. There is no overlap of samples across the training, validation,
and test sets. We used the training set to train the model, the validation set to
tune hyperparameters, and the test set to evaluate model performance.
Annotation. For the GoGetIt-Instruction subset and TextCaps-test bench-
mark, annotators were shown each image with the target object enclosed in a
bounding box, together with scene text extracted from the image. They were
asked to provide full-sentence instructions describing object manipulation or
navigation actions, optionally including scene text. Multiple experts manually
reviewed all test samples against predefined criteria. Each instruction had to be
a full-sentence manipulation or navigation command consistent with the target
object and its scene text.

4.2
Implementation Details
We used GPT-4o [26] for MFIE and STVE, and Google Cloud Vision API for
OCR. During pre-exploration, OCR, backbone feature extraction, and STVE
are executed once for the candidate images, and the resulting image embeddings
are stored. At query time, only instruction-side processing and STRR are exe-
cuted. STARE had approximately 40 million trainable parameters and 5 billion
multiply–add operations. We trained our model on a GeForce RTX 4090 with
24 GB of VRAM and an Intel Core i9-13900KF with 64 GB of CPU memory.
Training took approximately 1.2 hours. The similarity calculation between a sin-
gle instruction and 100 precomputed image embeddings took approximately 39.8
ms. We measured recall@10 on the validation set for each epoch. The evaluation
on the test set was performed using the model that had the highest recall@10
score on the validation set. We used the Double Relaxed Contrastive loss [38]
with the STRR score s, including the lexical matching term.
Baselines. Our zero-shot multimodal retrieval baselines were CLIP (ViT-
L/14), Long-CLIP (ViT-L/14), BLIP-2 (ViT-g), BEiT-3 (large), and Meta CLIP
2 [10,17,29,35,41]. We also included ReMEmbR, NLMap, and RelaX-Former due

4.3
Quantitative Results
Table 2 presents a quantitative comparison of STARE and the baseline methods.
We used recall@K (K=5,10), a standard metric for multimodal retrieval, and
treated recall@10 as the primary measure [7]. Scores for the frozen baselines are
reported from a single trial because their scores were consistent across repeated
runs. For the other methods, we report the mean and standard deviation ob-
tained from five trials. The highest score for each metric is in bold. The scores
for Long-CLIP, BLIP-2, and RelaX-Former are not reported on the GoGetIt
benchmark due to data leakage between their training data and the test set of
the GoGetIt benchmark. We used Katakana characters as the non-alphanumeric
characters for CVP, which are visually distinguishable from the scene texts (see
Table 3).
Table 2 shows that STARE achieved the highest recall@10 scores of 94.4%,
92.8%, 95.4%, and 86.6% on the RefText subset of the GoGetIt benchmark, the
Instruction subset of the GoGetIt benchmark, the TextCaps-test benchmark, and
the LTRRIE dataset, respectively. Our method outperformed the best baseline
scores of 81.2%, 84.6%, 91.8%, and 84.3% by 13.2, 8.2, 3.6, and 2.3 points, re-
spectively. Moreover, STARE outperformed all baseline methods across all other
evaluation metrics. These differences in recall@10 were statistically significant
(p < 0.01).

5
Conclusion
We study STMR, the task of retrieving images with or without scene text from
free-form instructions for robot manipulation or navigation. STARE combines
scene text-aware narrative representations with named-entity matching. We con-
structed GoGetIt and TextCaps-test to evaluate this task. STARE outperformed
the evaluated baselines and achieved 80% task success in zero-shot hardware ex-
periments.
