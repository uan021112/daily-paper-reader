Visibility-Aware Reference Generation for Benchmarking Visual 3D
Reconstruction in Vegetation-Rich Environments
Zhipeng Zhong, Matthew Coombes, and Cunjia Liu
Abstract—Reliable evaluation of visual 3D reconstruction and
depth estimation in vegetation-rich outdoor environments is
limited by the scarcity of geometric ground-truth data and
the cost of complete scanner surveys. We present a LiDAR-
based reference-generation pipeline for evaluating the zero-shot
performance of feed-forward visual-geometry models alongside
conventional structure-from-motion (SfM) methods. The pipeline
reuses camera images, LiDAR scans, and reference poses from
existing multi-sensor localization datasets to generate per-view
depth maps and visible reference point clouds. To address visibil-
ity errors in accumulated LiDAR scans, we introduce the Large-
Depth-Gap Visibility Score (LDGVS) for point-wise visibility
filtering and automatic scale selection in Hidden Point Removal
(HPR). Experiments on TartanAirV2 and TartanGround show
that, compared with Poisson surface reconstruction, which is
an approach suited to completed scan surveys, the combined
HPR and LDGVS filtering retains approximately three times
the point density at comparable absolute relative error. We also
apply the pipeline to the BotanicGarden dataset to systematically
compare depth estimation and 3D reconstruction methods. Feed-
forward methods produce dense depth predictions efficiently,
but their 3D reconstructions can contain duplicated structures.
Conventional SfM generally produces more coherent geometry,
but its reconstructions remain sparse and more prone to failure in
challenging vegetated scenes. The benchmark toolkit and datasets
will be released.
Index Terms—3D reconstruction, depth estimation, visibility
analysis, vegetation-rich environments
I. INTRODUCTION
Visual depth estimation and 3D reconstruction are essential
capabilities for agricultural and other field robots, providing
dense geometric information from lightweight, low-cost cam-
eras. Many studies [1]–[4] have sought to improve visual
geometric methods in vegetation-rich outdoor environments,
including forests, orchards, crop fields, and botanical gardens.
However, agricultural applications continue to rely predom-
inantly on conventional geometry-based SLAM [5], [6] or
SfM [7], [8], while the feed-forward methods [8]–[10] have
been less widely adopted. This pattern contrasts with general-
purpose 3D-reconstruction and depth-estimation benchmarks
[11]–[15], where feed-forward models are increasingly promi-
nent [16]–[22]. This motivates us to evaluate their zero-shot
performance in vegetation-rich outdoor environments.
However, benchmarking in these environments remains
challenging because publicly available datasets with suitable
ground-truth (GT) geometry are scarce and acquiring such
data is time-consuming and labor-intensive. A standard ap-
proach is to use high-accuracy terrestrial laser scanners to

Previous studies have demonstrated the use of LiDAR-derived

and road environments [11], [25]–[29], but extending this
approach to vegetation-rich environments remains challenging.
The difficulty arises because mobile LiDAR measurements

observations, occlusions, and camera–LiDAR parallax.
These limitations raise a central question: how can mobile
LiDAR data be converted into reliable camera-view geometric
references for benchmarking visual depth estimation and 3D
reconstruction in vegetation-rich environments? We therefore
develop a reference-generation pipeline and apply it to the rep-
resentative BotanicGarden [30] dataset, where the generated
references are used to compare feed-forward visual-geometry
models with conventional SfM methods. Our contributions are
summarized as follows:
1) We propose a pipeline that converts camera, LiDAR, and
reference-pose data from existing localization datasets
into per-view depth and visible point-cloud references.
2) We introduce the Large-Depth-Gap Visibility Score
(LDGVS) for point-cloud visibility filtering and auto-

providing greater reference density than Poisson surface

3) With the proposed pipeline, we construct a Botan-

geometry models with conventional SfM methods in
geometric quality, metric scale, cross-frame consistency,
robustness, and efficiency.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Agricultural Datasets for 3D Reconstruction and Depth
Estimation
Many studies have investigated 3D reconstruction using
agricultural data. Some studies use RGB-D cameras to obtain
GT data in apple orchards [42], a walnut-tree scene [43].
some studies evaluate performance through comparisons with
SOTA methods [9], [10], whereas others rely solely on vi-
sualization [5]. Several studies evaluate their results using
manual measurements [6], [44]. Laser scanners are more
commonly used to provide more accurate and denser GT data
for tree reconstruction [8], [23], [24]. To our knowledge, no
work has used LiDAR data as GT for 3D reconstruction or
depth estimation in this type of environment. Several LiDAR-
based depth-estimation benchmarks exist for urban and road
environments [11], [25]. And some localization datasets [26],
[27] has been used for 3D reconstruction evaluation [28],
[29]. Although many studies have explored this topic, the
availability of datasets for large-scale 3D reconstruction and
depth estimation in agricultural environment remains limited.
To filt this gap, we propose a LiDAR-based geometric
reference-generation pipeline for data collected directly by
lightweight mobile robot platforms. This eliminates the need
for dense, exhaustive scanning, thereby enabling the cost-
effective creation of large-scale 3D reconstruction and depth
estimation datasets.
B. Point-Cloud Visibility Analysis for Dataset Consturction
For fair comparison, we only can compare the point cloud
that can be seen in image data. Therefore the key problem of
datasets for 3D reconstruction and depth estimation using point
cloud data is to get correct visible the point-cloud. Specifically,
ETH3D and ScanNet++ datasets [14], [45] use Poisson sur-
face reconstruction on laser-scan point clouds [46] to render
depth rather than directly using projected point clouds. More
loosely, splatting-based methods [47], [48] render a primitive
projection. These approach can work well when dense and
complete geometry is available, but sparse LiDAR scans in
vegetation-rich scenes often contain missing areas and large
depth discontinuities. In such cases, surface reconstruction
may fail to represent the scene. Another approach relies on
consistency [11] or depth differences [49] between estimated
depth and projected point-cloud depth. These methods can
work well in urban and road environments, but depth esti-
mates are often inaccurate in vegetation-rich environments.
Consequently, these methods may also filter out too many
visible points. Hidden Point Removal (HPR) [50] analyzes
visibility on the point cloud without first reconstructing a
surface. However, we found that HPR cannot be applied
directly to environments with large depth variations. [51]
proposed visibility score can be computed directly in image
space. However, this work only evaluate their method on two
manual label points of city scene, can not generalize well in
our experiments.
Instead, in this work, we propose a modular point-cloud
visibility-analysis framework that introduces a large-depth-gap
visible score and uses it to adaptively select the HPR radius,

C. 3D Reconstruction and Depth Estimation in Vegetation-
rich and Agricultural Scenes
Recent reviews [1]–[4] indicates that most research on agri-
cultural applications still based on traditional SfM or SLAM.
For examples, some studies integrate existing vSLAM frame-
works with semantic-segmentation networks for semantic 3D
reconstruction of orchards [5]–[7]. SfM is the most widely
used method for 3D reconstruction in plant phenotyping. [52],
[53]. There is also work on applying the improved SFM
to agricultural scenarios, such as detector-free [8] and deep
learning matcher [54], [55].
On the other hand, due to the emergence of visual self-
supervised large models [56] these emerging feed forward
methods show a very strong generalization across different
scenes and benchmarks [17]–[19], [21], [57]. However, These
methods have not yet been fully benchmarked in agricultural
scenarios against traditional SfM methods.
Therefore, we construct a comprehensive 3D-reconstruction
and depth estimation benchmark to evaluate the performance
of SfM and feed forward methods in large-scale vegetation-
rich and agricultural environments. We also integrate recent
state-of-the-art learning-based feature and matching models
[58]–[60] into an SfM pipeline and compare their performance
within this benchmark.

[50]. HPR requires a radius parameter R, which is computed

a user-specified scale factor s:

V. EXPERIMENTS
We organize the our experiment into two primary branches.
The first evaluates the accuracy of the generated visible-depth
references simulated datasets. The second is to benchmarks
3D reconstruction and depth estimation using the generated
references in a real SLAM dataset.
a) Metrics: For point-cloud comparison, we report ac-
curacy (Acc), the accuracy inlier ratio (Acc τ), completeness
(Comp), the completeness inlier ratio (Comp τ), Chamfer
distance (CD), F1 score, and reconstruction density. For
depth comparison, we report absolute depth error (Abs), the
absolute-error inlier ratio (Abs τ, threshold 0.03), absolute
relative depth error (AbsRel), the relative-error inlier ratio
(Rel τ, threshold 1.03), and valid-depth density. We also
report runtime for efficiency comparison, which denotes the
processing time in seconds per image; Metric definition details
are provided in Supplementary Section S.III.

our pipeline and compare them with existing projection and

B. Benchmark Experiments
a) Datasets: We apply our reference-generation pipeline,
configured as AHPR (5%)+LDGVSF(0.7)+LD, to the Botan-
icGarden dataset [30] to construct references for the 3D
reconstruction and depth benchmarks. The pipeline supports
complete-sequence reference generation, but memory and run-
time constraints limit the reconstruction benchmark to 200
synchronized stereo frames (400 images), a scale broadly
comparable to established 3D-reconstruction benchmarks such
as ETH3D [14].
b) Common experiment settings:
For the 3D recon-
struction comparison, we apply a cross-frame reprojection-
consistency filter to VGGT, VGGT-Ω, DA3, MapAnything,
and MASt3R-SfM. For the depth-estimation comparison, this
filter is applied, where enabled, only to the accumulated 3D
visualizations; the quantitative depth metrics and 2D depth
maps are obtained before this filtering. To assess estimation
accuracy in high-confidence regions and within a limited
distance range, we include confidence-P50 mask and range
≤10 m variants in the benchmark tables. The confidence-P50
mask retains high-confidence predictions by thresholding the
model-provided confidence scores at their 50th percentile. A
range ≤10 m label denotes retaining predictions for which
0 < bd ≤10 m.
More details of preprocessing and common settings are
provided in Supplementary Section S.I-A.
c) Evaluation protocol: For both depth and reconstruc-
tion evaluations, we compare only pixels with valid reference
depth and a valid prediction. This restricts the comparison
to the part of the scene visible in the camera images and
avoids directly comparing unrestricted global point clouds.
Because full-sequence inference with the multi-view methods
exceeds GPU memory, we divide each sequence into chunks
of 16 frames with an 8-frame stride, yielding a 50% temporal
overlap. Following [28], adjacent chunks are registered using
a robust confidence-weighted Sim(3) transformation estimated
from pixel-corresponding back-projected points in their shared
frames, as detailed in Supplementary Section S.II-B. After reg-
istration, the newer prediction is retained for each duplicated
frame before evaluation and scene fusion.

structions across a sequence. Motivated by MonoRec [70], we
therefore back-project the retained depth maps, accumulate the
resulting point clouds across the sequence using the reference
camera poses, and visualize the accumulated geometry from
two fixed viewpoints as 3D View 1 and 3D View 2. These
views provide a qualitative assessment of cross-frame depth
consistency.
Most methods recover a reasonable relative-depth ordering
in the image plane, while SGM remains sparse and noisy.
Their accumulated geometry is considerably less consistent.
Repeated tree trunks and ghost surfaces appear when the
estimated depth of the same object varies across viewpoints
or camera-to-object distances, causing its observations to
occupy different 3D locations. These artifacts persist despite
per-frame depth alignment and, where enabled, cross-frame
consistency filtering. Similar artifacts remain in the multi-
view methods that process 16-frame inputs, indicating that
multi-frame context alone does not resolve the problem. DA2
and MapAnything produce the clearest second 3D view: DA2
retains a more restricted reconstruction, whereas MapAnything
preserves more scene content and shows the strongest cross-
frame depth consistency in this example.

VI. CONCLUSION
We presented a LiDAR-based pipeline that converts camera,
LiDAR, and reference-pose data from existing multi-sensor
localization datasets into per-view depth maps and visible
point-cloud references for benchmarking visual depth estima-
tion and 3D reconstruction in vegetation-rich outdoor environ-
ments. To address visibility errors in the accumulated LiDAR
measurements and enable their reuse as geometric references,
we introduced the Large-Depth-Gap Visibility Score (LDGVS)
for point-wise filtering and automatic scale selection in Hid-
den Point Removal (HPR). The resulting pipeline reduces
reliance on dedicated laser-scanner surveys. Experiments on
TartanAirV2 and TartanGround show that the combined HPR
and LDGVS filtering retains approximately three times the ref-
erence density of Poisson surface reconstruction at comparable
absolute relative error.
We instantiated the pipeline on BotanicGarden to com-
pare the zero-shot performance of representative feed-forward
visual-geometry models with conventional SfM in an under-
evaluated vegetation-rich outdoor setting. Feed-forward mod-
els generate dense geometry efficiently but can exhibit du-
plicated structures and cross-frame inconsistencies, whereas
conventional SfM produces more coherent geometry but re-
mains sparse and prone to failure. These findings demonstrate
the need for domain-specific evaluation and the value of
the benchmark in exposing trade-offs in density, consistency,
robustness, and efficiency beyond geometric accuracy alone.

S.I BENCHMARK INPUT AND DEPTH PROTOCOL
A. Common Experiment Settings
All methods operate on undistorted images. Each image
is uniformly rescaled and center-cropped to the target input
resolution of the corresponding method. For stereo methods,
each synchronized left–right image pair is counted as one
stereo frame, and dense predictions are evaluated only in the
left-camera view.
For the depth and reconstruction benchmarks, each evalu-
ated view is represented by camera-z depth and its associated
camera pose. DA2 (Metric), MoGe-2, VGGT, VGGT-Ω, and
DA3 use their native depth predictions. For DA2 (Relative),
we obtain a relative depth proxy by taking the reciprocal
of its predicted inverse depth. Disparities from SGM and
FoundationStereo are converted to camera-z depth using the
calibrated horizontal focal length and stereo baseline. For
MapAnything, camera-z depth is taken from the z-coordinate
of its predicted camera-frame point map. For SfM methods,
reconstructed 3D points are projected into the left image.
Camera parameters estimated by each method are used unless
calibrated intrinsics are provided as input.

S.IV VISIBLE-DEPTH REFERENCE ABLATION STUDY
We evaluate the effects of the LDGVSF threshold and the
AHPR target low-visibility ratio on the accuracy and density
of the generated visible-depth references.
Table V and Figure 9 show a consistent accuracy–density
trade-off. Increasing the AHPR target low-visibility ratio re-
tains more projected points but generally reduces accuracy.
At a fixed AHPR setting, applying LDGVSF removes low-
visibility-score points and generally improves the error metrics
at the cost of lower reference-depth density. Raising the
LDGVSF threshold from 0.7 to 0.9 filters more points, while
the additional changes in accuracy are modest and mixed. The
visualization shows the corresponding coverage trend: the 1%
configurations are sparse, the 10% configurations are denser,
and the 5% configurations provide intermediate coverage; a
threshold of 0.7 also retains more support than 0.9.
Several parameter combinations therefore provide a rea-
sonable balance between accuracy and density. We choose
AHPR(5%)+LDGVSF(0.7) to generate the reference depth for
our pipeline because it retains relatively dense reference-depth
coverage while limiting the retention of erroneous depth values
in the generated references.
S.V EXTENDED VISIBILITY COMPARISON ON
BOTANICGARDEN
Figure 10 extends the qualitative comparison to the chal-
lenging real-world BotanicGarden data acquired with a Livox
LiDAR. Compared with the simulated example, PSR in (d)
retains a denser visible-point projection in this scene, but it still
discards many points relative to the cumulation baseline in (b).
In contrast, AHPR(5%)+LDGVSF(0.7) in (j) preserves sub-
stantially more visible-point support than PSR and maintains
coverage close to the baseline while filtering likely occluded
points.
S.VI QUALITATIVE DEPTH-ERROR ANALYSIS
Figure 11 illustrates the sources of depth error. Even the
aligned left-view single scan in (b) has residual errors at object
boundaries: sparse LiDAR samples and discrete ground-truth
pixels can fall on different surfaces near a depth discontinuity.
The right-view cumulation baseline in (c) also retains incorrect
depths within objects because of camera–LiDAR parallax and
accumulated occluded points. PSR in (e) retains low-error
interiors but removes much of the difficult boundary support,
whereas AHPR(5%)+LDGVSF(0.7) in (k) preserves denser
support with residual errors mainly at edges. Since accuracy
is evaluated on each method’s retained pixels, these additional
boundary samples help explain the accuracy–coverage trade-
off.
