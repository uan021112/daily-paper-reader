Learning to Live: A Step Toward Lifelong Embodied Intelligence
Agibot Human Intelligence Project

Abstract
Embodied agents are usually organized around individual tasks, yet the physical world keeps changing
between tasks and during execution. We introduce LIFE (Lifelong Intelligence from Experience),
a framework in which a robot observes continuously, remembers selectively, and turns experience
into new capabilities. RoboBeats, a family of lightweight streaming gates, tracks conditions set by
the agent and discovers new events, deciding when an observation warrants a response or a memory
write. Selected observations enter Plastic Memory, a flat store of images and structured records that
keeps the source evidence, so new fields and indexes can be added when later questions need them.
When existing tools fall short, Explore–Distill–Mount (EDM) turns solutions found during problem
solving into validated, reusable tools and skills. Under matched protocols, RoboBeats substantially
improves condition tracking over its base models and, at the same writing budget, retains more useful
evidence for memory question answering than uniform sampling. A case study of limited scale,
replaying 24 hours of continuous video recorded by a real robot, illustrates how the integrated system
builds memory from a day of experience and uses it to answer everyday questions. LIFE takes a
step toward robots that become more familiar with their surroundings and more capable through
experience: learning not only to complete tasks, but to live in the world.

Introduction: From Individual Tasks to Lifelong Embodied Agents
We hope for robots to participate in everyday human environments over extended periods, understanding changes,
accumulating experience, and gradually learning to handle new situations. A robot on its hundredth day in a building
should be more familiar with it than on its first: the people it has encountered, the places it has visited, and the problems
it has handled should help it make better judgments and find more effective solutions. We call this goal Learning to Live
and study how embodied agents can continually build knowledge and capabilities through interaction with the world.
Many embodied-agent systems are organized around user-specified tasks: they interpret an instruction, select or compose
skills, and execute actions toward the requested goal (Ahn et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). Yet the physical world
continues to change both between tasks and during execution. Objects may move, people may arrive, and seemingly
incidental events may become relevant later. This creates a mismatch between the agent’s discrete reasoning and tool
calls and the world’s continuous evolution. Scene descriptions, success detection, and human feedback can help agents
revise their plans during execution (Huang et al., 2022). A relevant change may require a response before the next
planned decision, while a completed tool call may leave the intended physical outcome uncertain: reaching a location,
for example, does not confirm that the object sought is still there. The agent therefore needs ongoing observations to
update its understanding, bring relevant changes into its decision process, and verify outcomes throughout interaction.
Sustained observation must also be practical over long periods. Continuous visual streams contain both brief, conse-
quential events and large amounts of repetitive information, while the relevance of an observation to future tasks may
not yet be known (Gorlo et al., 2026b; Zeng et al., 2026). Processing every observation with an expensive model would
increase computational cost and response latency, while retaining and indexing every observation without selection
would make memory increasingly redundant. The system must identify when further analysis is warranted and decide
what experience to preserve. At the same time, memory should retain enough evidence to support future questions that
were not anticipated when an observation was made.
Recent work has explored continuous visual interaction and proactive memory, including systems that decide when
to respond or record evidence from video streams (Yao et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026). Embodied agents have also
used long-term visual and spatial memory to answer questions and guide navigation (Anwar et al., 2024; Hu et al.,
2026), while systems such as BetaMem and ABot-AgentOS study memory evolution and integrated robot reasoning (Liu
et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2026). These approaches provide a basis for studying how ongoing perception, experience, and
capability development can work together in a robot system.
To address these challenges, we introduce LIFE (Lifelong Intelligence from Experience), a framework that connects
proactive perception, long-term memory, reasoning, and autonomous learning. LIFE continuously screens observations
for changes relevant to the current context or potentially useful in the future. It turns selected experiences into retrievable
historical evidence and uses that evidence to support decisions, verification, and further exploration. As the agent
encounters problems that existing methods cannot handle, it can also develop and retain new tools and skills, allowing
accumulated experience to contribute to future capabilities.
LIFE’s proactive perception is implemented by RoboBeats, a lightweight gate that tracks specified conditions and
detects new events in streaming video. It decides when an observation should trigger a response, further perception, or
memory writing, so that a large model does not have to analyze every frame. Figure 1 summarizes its evaluation across
streaming perception and memory question answering benchmarks.
Selected experiences enter Plastic Memory, which stores observations with their temporal and spatial context and keeps
the source images (Anwar et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Structured records give fast access through people, places,
objects, and events, while the retained images let the agent recover details that were not extracted when the experience
was recorded. Writing only selected observations keeps memory compact, and keeping the images leaves it usable for
questions that were not anticipated.
The information needed for a new problem may already exist in retained observations even when the agent lacks a way
to extract or connect it. Building on skill accumulation in embodied agents (Lu et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2023), LIFE
uses the Explore–Distill–Mount (EDM) mechanism from ReSight (Che et al., 2026) to turn solutions developed for
individual problems into validated tools and skills that apply to both past experience and future tasks.
This report presents the design of LIFE and its core components: RoboBeats for proactive perception, Plastic Memory
for retaining and revisiting experience, and EDM for constructing and reusing tools and skills. We evaluate perception

LIFE Overview: From Experience to Knowledge and Capabilities
LIFE connects continuous observation, persistent memory, and capability development through an agent that reasons
and acts on its experience, building on work in proactive visual interaction and embodied memory (Hu et al., 2026;
Liu et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026). As shown in Figure 2, the agent sets attention conditions, queries
Plastic Memory, and invokes tools to answer questions or act in the world. RoboBeats screens incoming observations
for conditions that require a response and events worth recording. A writer turns selected observations into memory,
which supplies evidence for subsequent reasoning. When the available tools cannot meet a new need, EDM provides
a process for developing and retaining additional methods. The shared tool and skill layer exposes memory access,
physical actions, and reusable methods to the agent.
2.1
From Waiting for Instructions to Attending to the World
RoboBeats keeps screening the visual stream during task execution and between user requests. Its output feeds two
workflows: condition tracking supports responses to the current situation, and event discovery supplies observations for
memory writing. Both use lightweight screening to decide when further processing is needed.
For condition tracking, the agent or a standing interaction rule specifies what to watch for, such as a person starting a
phone call or the visible completion of a manipulation task. When the condition changes state, the system triggers a
response or returns the observation to the agent for reassessment. Monitoring continues while an action tool is running,
and the agent combines these observations with tool feedback and task context to decide whether to continue, finish, or
revise its next action. Section 3.5 shows this for an elevator door, a handover, a phone call, and a grasp.

For event discovery, RoboBeats identifies changes worth describing and passes the selected observations to the writer,

related records about people, objects, and activities. Event discovery decides what enters memory; the writer decides
how it is described. Section 6 applies this pipeline to a full day of robot video.
2.2
Making an Unfamiliar World Familiar
Plastic Memory retains selected images and associated JSON records in a flat file structure. Each saved observation
carries its acquisition time, its pose when available, and fields extracted from the evidence (Section 4.1), which
give access through people, places, objects, and events. The source images remain available for details the original
descriptions did not capture.
Given a question, the agent retrieves candidate records, follows their temporal or semantic links, and inspects the
supporting images when needed. When a question needs a detail that no field records, a program extracts it from the
retained images and adds it to their records (Section 4.4). Because the memory is a set of ordinary files, programs and
coding agents make these updates with standard file operations.
Navigation extends and refreshes this experience. A retrieved observation supplies a destination pose, the robot reaches
it with map-based or vision-and-language navigation, and on-site perception checks the current state; frames collected
along the way enter memory as new evidence.
Figure 3 shows two uses of shared history: the robot notices a person’s repeated snack-taking and offers help on a later
encounter; it also uses earlier interactions and sightings to identify the user’s bottle and where it was last seen.
2.3
Turning Experience into Reusable Capabilities
The agent first solves a problem with the evidence and tools already available: it retrieves structured fields, inspects
source images, or gathers a new observation through physical action. Ways of extracting or using evidence that prove
useful repeatedly become capabilities for later tasks.

3
A Proactive Gate for Streaming Visual Perception
Robots in continuous operation need to observe their surroundings and determine which changes warrant further
processing for the current task. A system may need to issue a timely reminder when a specified event occurs, or record
newly observed activities before future tasks are known. Streaming video perception must balance the need for timely
decisions with the computational cost of long-term operation.
Streaming systems have studied timely responses and query-independent memory formation (Wen et al., 2026; Yao
et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026), while selective episodic memory has also been explored for robots (Gorlo et al., 2026b).
ProactEgoMem evaluates proactive memory acquisition and later recall in egocentric video (Liao et al., 2026).
We introduce RoboBeats, a family of proactive gates based on Qwen3.5 for streaming visual perception. It processes
frames in temporal order and evaluates whether a specified condition holds or an event has occurred. These judgments
5


update its gating state, which determines when to pass observations to downstream modules. RoboBeats supports both
condition tracking and event discovery, providing a basis for proactive responses and memory writing.
3.1
Conditioned Streaming Perception
RoboBeats formulates continuous perception as a sequence of judgments about natural language conditions. As frames
arrive, the model scores each condition using the current frame and previous observations. It supports two modes.
Condition tracking monitors specific descriptions, such as “someone opens a door,” and judges whether each condition
holds over time. Event discovery uses the fixed query “Has a new event occurred?” to identify observations of new
activities or changes. Both modes use the same input structure and scoring procedure. Event discovery is the case with a
single monitored condition.
We develop 0.8B, 2B, and 4B variants using the Qwen3.5 family (Qwen Team, 2026a). The different sizes allow a
deployment to choose a model suited to the available on-robot compute and memory. The input sequence starts with
the monitoring conditions, followed by timestamped images in temporal order. Each image is followed by a decision
query for each condition, with its result read separately. Each image appears only once, so multiple conditions share its
visual representation and observation history. The input is causal. Each decision uses the condition descriptions, the
current image, and earlier observations within the bounded context. Predictions are maintained independently for each
condition, allowing several conditions to hold simultaneously.

3.2
Training and Online Inference
RoboBeats learns condition judgments from continuous frames and temporal labels. During online operation, it reuses
previous computation to process new frames incrementally. The system updates trigger states from the scores and groups
consecutive active observations into intervals.
Multi-Condition Training with Shared Frames. A training example consists of continuous frames and several
monitoring conditions. Each condition has temporal labels indicating whether it holds at each step. A single causal
forward pass predicts scores across time steps and conditions, and each score is compared with its label. The frames
appear only once, allowing conditions to share visual representations and observation history. Losses are computed
independently for each condition, so conditions with overlapping intervals can be trained together. Each training group
contains at most eight conditions. Larger sets are split into groups.
We adapt Qwen3.5 with a frozen visual encoder and LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) updates to the language model. The objective
combines per-step judgments with interval supervision to improve both individual predictions and their agreement with
annotated intervals.

Online Processing of Continuous Frames.
At the default 1 Hz input rate, RoboBeats processes timestamped frames causally and reuses cached attention and
recurrent states to avoid recomputing the full history. Overlapping chunks bound the context length while preserving
recent observations across chunk transitions. Appendix B.1 gives the chunk sizes and cache-update procedure.
Updating Trigger States. Per-step scores can fluctuate as the scene changes. To reduce repeated switching, the system
maintains an independent binary state for each condition and uses separate activation and deactivation thresholds. Their
default values are 0.5 and 0.3. Let pt,k be the score for condition ck at time t and st,k its updated state. All states start
at zero. The update rule is given in Appendix B.1. A state of one denotes an active condition, and zero denotes an
inactive condition. Between the two thresholds, the previous state is retained. A transition from zero to one records
an onset, and a transition from one to zero records an offset. Consecutive active steps form an observation interval.

3.4
Benchmark Construction
Existing online video benchmarks, such as OVO-Bench (Li et al., 2025), evaluate timely responses and questions about
past events. Robots operating in everyday environments face an additional challenge: observations may continue for
long periods, while activities and state changes are brief, sparse, and unevenly distributed. Processing every observation
and retaining all details would increase computation and storage as experience accumulates. Long-term operation
therefore calls for preserving potentially useful changes while filtering or compressing redundant observations.
We construct SSS-Bench (Sparse-event Streaming Suite) to study this setting using extended videos of everyday
activities. The benchmark focuses on sparse events and their temporal, sequential, and object relations, while also
including continuous activities and dense scenes for comparison. The system selects and organizes observations
without knowing future questions, then receives a question at a specified time; all answer evidence precedes that
time. Observation-selection strategies are compared under the same per-video writing-frame budget, so differences
in question-answering accuracy reflect which observations each strategy retains. Figure 6 illustrates perception and
memory question answering examples, while Figure 7 summarizes the benchmark’s sources and question types. SSS-
Bench questions and reference answers are constructed from continuous videos and event cues, then checked through
independent model verification and human review. Question-only and query-time-frame probes screen for potential
shortcuts. Appendix B.5.1 describes the construction and verification process.
The final benchmark contains 618 questions spanning 171 parent videos, six data sources, and 14 question subtypes,
with at most five questions per parent video. We check for overlap with the proactive gate’s training set at four levels
(videos, parent videos, person groups, and filenames) and find none. After independent verification, questions are
selected according to predefined quotas for each question subtype. The selection is not adjusted based on the evaluation
results of the methods being compared.

3.5
From Perception to Responses and Memory
In LIFE, the agent or a predefined rule supplies conditions to RoboBeats, which tracks them as observations arrive.
A change in a condition can prompt the system to respond or request further processing. The agent or task workflow
determines the response from the gate output and the current context.
Figure 8 illustrates three uses of condition tracking. At an elevator, the robot monitors the door and can enter while it is
open. Visual conditions can also connect short actions: in the handover example, the robot reaches toward an offered
bag and closes its hand when the giver lowers it. The same mechanism can pause speech when a person answers a phone
call and resume it when the call ends.
Conditions can also be monitored while an action tool is running. In the snack-grasping example, RoboBeats tracks
whether the grasp condition is met during execution. This observation provides feedback to the task workflow alongside
the tool’s own status, allowing the agent to assess the action in the context of the current scene. Similar monitoring
could support the fetching stage of the assistance scenario in Figure 3.
Event discovery supports a different use of the same perception mechanism. It selects observations for a writer,
which receives images and timestamps and describes visible events or states. The resulting records retain references
to supporting frames and can be organized into Plastic Memory (Section 4). Subsequent questions can retrieve the
descriptions and return to the images when more detail is needed. Section 6 describes the offline construction of memory
from a day of robot video.

3.6.1

We evaluate two capabilities of RoboBeats. With specified conditions, it must continuously judge whether they hold and

4
Plastic Memory: Turning Experience into Reusable Evidence
An experience retained today may only reveal its value tomorrow. A desktop seen during a patrol or a brief interaction
between a person and an object may initially appear incidental, yet later become essential to answering a question or
finding a target. Long-term memory must therefore support both efficient access to known clues and reinspection of past
observations for information that was not extracted when they were recorded.
Existing robot memory systems use spatial maps and scene graphs (Gu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Werby et al.,
2024), visual observations for later retrieval and localization (Hu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), and structured memories
that can be updated through experience (Liu et al., 2026). LIFE uses Plastic Memory, a flat memory built on retained
observations. Structured fields provide access paths for anticipated questions, while the images remain available for
reinterpretation when a later question needs information that was not extracted at recording time.
Plastic Memory adopts the flat, observation-based memory design of our earlier work ReSight (Che et al., 2026) and
applies it to experience that accumulates through robot operation. Section 3 describes how proactive perception selects
observations for further processing and recording. This section presents memory representation, access, and extension,
followed by ReSight’s base retrieval evaluation. Section 5 introduces ReSight’s EDM mechanism for constructing and
reusing tools, skills, and memory builders.
As shown in Figure 11, an execution agent accesses shared evidence through tools, while a coding agent reprocesses
observations through file and program interfaces. Both operate on the same retained evidence.
4.1
A Reinterpretable and Programmable Memory Representation
LIFE stores observations separately from their current interpretations. For each retained visual observation, the system
preserves an image and its available context, including acquisition time, pose, and source. Semantic descriptions, people,
objects, and events are organized as structured fields or associated records that provide access paths for later questions.
The memory is based on image files and associated structured records containing JSON fields; fields and derived
indexes can be added or updated as needs change. A coding agent can inspect the images and write programs to read
existing fields, extract new information, or construct additional indexes and tools. A field is a current interpretation of an
observation, not all the information contained in it. For example, an image may initially be recorded with a location and
object description. If a later task requires lookup by room number, retained images can be reprocessed to extract text
and add a field or index linked to the source evidence.

4.2
Filtering and Linking Evidence for Question Answering and Action
The same experience can be accessed from different directions. For “Which cups are on the eighth floor?”, a floor field
first narrows the candidates, followed by retrieval using object attributes. For “Where did this person go afterwards?”,
entity clues connect observations across time, and temporal ordering reconstructs the observed trajectory. Fields can
both exclude irrelevant evidence and link otherwise scattered records. Let R denote memory records with available
fields. We summarize these access patterns as
FilterP (R) = {mi ∈R : P(mi) = 1},
Linkk(S; R) = {mj ∈R : ∃mi ∈S, Rk(mi, mj) = 1}.
(9)
P specifies a query constraint, and Rk is a relation defined by a field or association rule, such as a shared entity identifier,
a shared event, temporal proximity, or a common spatial region. Unknown attributes should remain distinguishable from
17


known mismatches. Candidate identity and object associations also require inspection of the source evidence. Filtering
and linking can be composed with similarity search, ranking, aggregation, and image inspection:
Eq = (OpL ◦· · · ◦Op1)(M),
y = π(q, Eq).
(10)
Eq is the evidence selected for the current request, and π uses it to produce an answer or inform an action. This is a
common description of the access interface: individual operators may be fixed retrieval tools or programs executed on
demand. Field-based association does not require every relation to be enumerated in advance. A query can combine
conditions as needed, while recurring combinations can be packaged as tools.
For example, to “find Peidong’s cup”, the system may first retrieve identity or interaction records involving Peidong,
inspect related sightings, and examine desktops or drinking events to connect person-related clues to visual evidence of
the cup. A question can be answered with a candidate object and supporting evidence; a request to fetch it also takes the
pose of the most recent reliable sighting, and the robot goes there as described in Section 2.2.
Historical evidence describes past states. When an object passes between people, scattered observations constrain its
possible transfer history, but unobserved handovers remain inferences. Direct observations should remain distinguishable
from conclusions inferred from clues. If the current holder or location is uncertain, asking a person or observing again
can supply further evidence. Memory therefore supports questions that require no movement as well as tasks that require
physical action.

Linking Historical and Latest Observations by View

Rescanning History and Extending Fields on Demand
As experience accumulates, memory must accommodate both new observations and questions unforeseen at recording
time. States and events are linked to observations through timestamps, frame references, and source information. These
interpretations can be revised, while retained images provide evidence for reinspection. Updating memory therefore also
includes extracting new information from old experiences.
For example, existing fields may retrieve images of doorways without supporting lookup by room number. The system
can rescan retained images, extract text, and build an index so that the same historical evidence supports new queries.
For a new field builder b,

M(r+1) = (O, F(0), X (r) ∪Xb),
OS ⊆O.
(13)
OS is the historical scope to process, such as a patrol, a floor, or candidate segments relevant to a target. Builder outputs
keep their links to the source frames. Processing may run once or be repeated as relevant observations accumulate; each
new question need not trigger a rescan of the entire history.
Field backfilling changes how historical evidence can be accessed. OCR fields make old images searchable by text, and
a stable lookup interface makes these fields directly available to subsequent tasks. The fields LIFE adds on the robot fit
the same equation. The people visible in a frame, registered object instances, events, and view membership are each
produced by a builder over retained frames, and these builders form part of the capability set described in Section 5. A
builder written by hand and one produced by EDM play the same role: as in ReSight, initial and extended fields differ in
when they are introduced, not in their source.
The next section introduces ReSight’s EDM mechanism for constructing, validating, and retaining such methods in
response to capability gaps exposed by tasks.

5
EDM: From Solving Once to Reusing Later
The value of memory also depends on how effectively an agent can use it. Even when relevant evidence has been
retained, the execution agent may lack an appropriate retrieval operation or require many interactions to align times,
localize objects, or aggregate information across segments. A difficult task can therefore expose two different gaps: a
missing fact, or a missing method for obtaining that kind of fact.
Prior work has explored reusable tools (Cai et al., 2024), skill libraries for embodied agents (Lu et al., 2026a; Wang et al.,
2023), and adaptation of memory systems (Zhang et al., 2025b). Explore–Distill–Mount (EDM), introduced in our earlier
work ReSight (Che et al., 2026), converts task-specific exploration into executable, verifiable, and reusable capabilities.
This section describes EDM and reports its evaluation; all results here, including the robot-memory experiment, come
from the original ReSight experiments rather than new LIFE evaluations. Section 6 shows an integrated LIFE system
using memory and available tools to answer questions about a day of robot observations.
5.1
Identifying Capability Gaps from Task Difficulty
ReSight separates routine execution from capability construction. The execution agent answers queries using existing
tools within a bounded online decision budget. The coding agent can directly inspect retained observations, write
programs, invoke models, and construct intermediate artifacts to develop new solution paths for difficult tasks. These
asymmetric interfaces allow complex analysis and development to take place separately while preserving a concise
interface for routine execution.
For example, retrieval may identify images containing a target without returning the object’s center in world coordinates.
Segment descriptions may mention object pickups without reliably counting repeated interactions. Extending reasoning
with the same tools does not necessarily supply these missing operations. EDM treats such requirements as opportunities
to construct repeatable localization, counting, ordering, or retrieval programs.
C = (T , S, B),
(a, τ) = Execute(q, M; C).
(14)
T denotes callable tools, S denotes skills that guide their use, and B denotes builders that generate or update memory
fields and indexes; a and τ are the task output and execution trace. Adding a fact primarily changes memory, whereas
retaining a method for obtaining facts changes the capability set. Self-evolution here operates at the level of programs,
tool interfaces, and skills, without requiring base-model parameter updates after each task.

5.2
Explore, Distill, and Mount
During Explore, the coding agent inspects evidence for the current task, tests combinations of programs and models,
and produces runnable solutions. It may extract text from relevant frames, estimate target locations using depth and pose,
or organize events and revisit order. Exploration permits task-specific intermediate steps to identify missing operations
and workable implementations.
During Distill, effective parts of the exploratory solution are converted into capabilities usable by the execution agent.
This requires removing hard-coded scene paths and answers, specifying inputs, outputs, and applicability conditions,
and refining tools and skill instructions using execution feedback. Distillation closes the gap between a coding agent
solving a task once and a budget-limited execution agent invoking the same method reliably.
During Mount, validated tools, skills, and builders are integrated into the execution environment. Subsequent tasks
access these capabilities through registered interfaces, with builders producing fields and indexes for the relevant
memories when needed. For room-number lookup, constructing an OCR index processes evidence; registering a
text-search tool and its usage instructions mounts a capability. Together, they make relevant evidence more directly
accessible to future queries.

Benchmark Evaluation of Capability Expansion

OC-NaVQA.

frames. Table 7 compares the same ReSight-Sol execution agent before and after mounting.
Position and time errors decrease after mounting, matching the added spatial and temporal operations, while text
accuracy is unchanged and binary accuracy decreases: the gains concentrate on the operations that EDM constructed.

exposes operations for interaction enumeration, revisit ordering, duration comparison, and temporal alignment. Table 8
reports the results.

Reusing Frozen Capabilities on Robot Memories
We further evaluate whether constructed capabilities can support subsequent queries over memories recorded during
office patrols. Capability construction uses only three tasks on eighth-floor memory. Tools and builders are then frozen
and evaluated on six tasks, with five independent runs per task. Subsequent tasks include a new query about Room 805
and queries for patrol length and photo localization over first-floor memory collected on another day. Existing builders
may run on the new memory, but capabilities are neither synthesized nor modified. Both configurations use the same
execution model with at most ten tool-interaction rounds per run.
With frozen capabilities, ReSight answers all 15 runs on subsequent tasks, compared with 9 of 15 before EDM (Table 9).
Across the six tasks, successes increase from 12 of 30 to 30 of 30, while mean tool calls decrease from 5.10 to 1.20.

6
A Day of Experience: 24-Hour Memory Question Answering on a Real Robot
A robot that remains present in a shared environment can accumulate experiences before it knows what its users will ask.
We bring together proactive perception, Plastic Memory, and the agent’s tools to study how these experiences support
later questions on an A3 Ultra robot operating in an office building. Over the day, its front-facing camera recorded
work areas, a meeting room, and a pantry, as well as other floors of the building and outdoor areas, capturing arrivals,
meetings, meals, and object handovers. We use footage from 21:00 on October 21 to 21:00 on October 22, 2026, to
construct a memory of the day and ask the agent questions about the recorded experience.
6.1
From a Day of Observations to Memory
We reconstruct memory offline from the recorded video, combining the proactive perception of Section 3 with Plastic
Memory and its evidence-access design of Section 4. The RoboBeats event-discovery gate scores incoming observations.
Nearby triggers are combined into windows and passed through a background-difference prefilter before a local Qwen3.8-
27B writer describes visible events and states. These descriptions retain timestamps and references to supporting frames,
allowing a later question to lead back to the original observation.
A subsequent Qwen3.8-27B consolidation process organizes the writer’s output in half-hour blocks, linking records
of people, objects, places, and activities. Following the memory design in Section 4, these descriptions and indexes
provide access paths to timestamped visual evidence. Alongside the textual records, object detection and tracking
provide additional observations of people and objects. For a registered object, condition-based rescanning can locate
actions involving it across the recorded day. The agent uses tools for timelines, objects, and historical images to access
these records. This follows the separation in Section 5: evidence accumulates in memory, while tools and field builders
make that evidence usable for different tasks.

Answering Questions from Experience
The evaluation contains six questions about people, objects, times, and locations, one for each case in Figure 13. Two
of them, the jacket and the lunch break, are two-turn exchanges in which the user follows up on the first answer.
LIFE’s execution agent answers each question three times using the reconstructed memory and its tools, and the figure
shows examples from these runs. The examples follow the path from selected observations to organized memory and
tool-assisted answers: the agent can use a stored description, connect observations across events, and revisit a frame to
examine evidence.
Tracing two cups through a meeting.
A user asked who owned two cups left on a meeting-room table. The agent first
queried the timeline and examined images of the cups after the meeting. It then used historical video access to review
observations from earlier in the meeting and before attendees arrived. The green cup was associated with an attendee
who brought and used it, while the black cup was already on the table before the attendees arrived. The agent therefore
offered an attribution for the green cup and distinguished it from the black cup, whose owner was not established. This
answer depended on the order in which the objects and people appeared, rather than on their proximity in the final scene.
Tracking a cushion across events.
A user asked whom he had given his registered cushion to and who subsequently
carried it into Room 805. The agent used the registered object and its memory records to identify the relevant cushion.
It then used condition-based historical action evidence to connect an observed handover at approximately 10:51 with a
later carrying event at approximately 11:02. It answered that Che Lirong received the cushion and Su Jian carried it into
the room. The object record and historical action observations link the two questions to different people and times.
Finding a jacket.
At 12:30, a user asked where the robot had last seen his jacket, then asked to see that observation
and where the robot had been. The agent identified a chair back in the pantry, where the jacket was last recorded at
about 12:18, showed the historical frame, and returned the map pose from which it had been taken. The memory record
supplies the location, and the historical-image tool brings back the visual evidence and observation pose, giving the user
a view of the place to search.
Inferring the end of a lunch break.
Asked to estimate the lunch break from its observations, the agent combined records
of dining, people resting, and subsequent activity at workstations, and answered approximately 12:00–14:00. When
the user asked for a more precise end time and supporting images, it selected frames from approximately 13:41–13:50
and refined its answer: people began to resume work around 13:50. The exchange shows how a summary over several
periods can guide inspection of images near a transition.
Other questions about the day.
To answer whether anyone visited the office after 02:00, the agent found a brief visitor
at approximately 06:56 and distinguished the person from fixed displays in the background. It also combined identity
and time records to identify the first named arrival at the nearby workstations. These questions use the same day’s
experience as the object and activity examples, but access different fields and observations according to the information
requested.

7
Discussion
LIFE connects continuous perception, long-term memory, and capability development. Our experiments examine
proactive perception, memory question answering, and self-evolution, while the daily-life cases show how a robot can
use observations from different times to answer questions about people, objects, and activities.
Moving from these capabilities to long-term autonomous operation requires further evaluation. As the environment
changes, a robot must update its memory, resolve conflicts between old and new information, and use accumulated
experience in later actions. Whether perception, memory, and capability development can work together reliably over
long periods remains an open question.
Evaluating lifelong embodied intelligence also poses a challenge. Our real-robot study follows one robot through one
continuous day. A day in the real world cannot be re-enacted, so comparing configurations or repeating runs on the
same experience is possible only by recording the robot’s stream and replaying it; replay, however, does not test how
the robot’s actions change what it later observes. Simulation platforms support controlled evaluation of embodied
agents (Savva et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2026), but sustained environmental changes, natural human interaction, and
the long-term consequences of actions remain difficult to capture fully. Multi-day online deployments with questions
posed in situ, together with dynamic simulation, may help assess how robots accumulate and use experience in open
environments.

8
Conclusion and Future Work
We presented LIFE, an embodied intelligence framework that connects proactive perception, long-term memory, and
self-evolution. RoboBeats continuously monitors the environment and selects observations for memory writing; Plastic
Memory retains experiences that can be revisited and reinterpreted; EDM turns methods developed during problem
solving into reusable tools and skills. Under matched protocols, the gate improves condition tracking over its base
model and, at the same writing budget, retains more useful evidence for later questions than uniform sampling. In
ReSight’s experiments, capabilities built with EDM carry over to held-out episodes and to new robot memories without
further changes. At the scale of our real-robot case study, which replays one day of robot video with six questions, the
integrated system answers everyday questions about people, objects, and activities more accurately and much faster than
a coding agent working from the raw recording.
Future work will bring continuous perception, memory, long-term goals, and autonomous action closer together. A robot
could organize its activities around an ongoing mission, choose what to attend to and where to explore, and adjust its
actions in light of past experience. It could remember places it has visited, people it has met, and events it has observed,
learning through continued interaction with the world.
Our goal is for embodied agents eventually to live autonomously in the real world, learning from their own experiences
and building knowledge and capabilities over time. This is the direction we call Learning to Live.

B.1
Online Inference and Trigger States
Online processing.
By default, the system receives frames at 1 Hz. The model first reads the monitoring conditions,
then processes timestamped images and their decision queries in order. It outputs a score for each condition at every
step.
To avoid repeated computation, the model caches attention key and value states and recurrent states from the processed
history. When a new frame arrives, the system appends the image and decision queries to the cached history. It computes
the current predictions, updates the cache, and waits for the next frame.
Long streams are processed in chunks to bound the context length. Each chunk contains at most 100 frames, with 10
overlapping frames between adjacent chunks. At a chunk transition, the system rebuilds the cache from the condition
descriptions and the last 10 frames of the preceding chunk, then continues with new frames. The overlapping frames
retain their timestamps and provide preceding context. Only predictions for newly arriving frames are recorded,
preserving a continuous sequence of outputs.

B.2
Evaluation Datasets and Held-out Results
Dataset composition.
For condition tracking, we use a held-out condition judgment set and public benchmarks. The
held-out set contains 3,682 video windows excluded from training, with 70,027 supervised frames from eight data
sources. It covers fixed-camera, third-person, and first-person views. Each window retains the interval in which a
condition holds and the continuous observations before and after it. This tests generalization to held-out videos from
sources covered by the training data. Public benchmarks cover temporal localization, condition triggering, and proactive
responses. We evaluate online activity localization on 3,720 Charades-STA (Gao et al., 2017) queries and first-trigger
timing on 250 questions from the StreamingBench (Lin et al., 2024) proactive output task. The SPOT-Bench (Chatterjee
et al., 2026) action boundary detection task contains 2,340 response points across 322 videos and tests responses to
the starts and ends of action steps. ESTP-Bench (Zhang et al., 2025d) uses all 2,264 questions over 14 tasks (1,212
single-question examples and 1,052 contextual questions) to evaluate proactive responses from a condition gate coupled
to an answering model.
For event discovery, SSS-Bench tests whether observation selection supports subsequent memory question answering.
The system selects and retains information without knowing future questions, then answers using the memory available

B.4
SPOT-Bench Output Construction
For action-boundary detection, we rewrite each request as start and end conditions. The model scores whether each
condition holds at 2 fps, the input rate used by the benchmark’s streaming baselines; the other condition-tracking
benchmarks use 1 fps. Fixed state transitions turn those scores into responses. The readout uses activation and
deactivation thresholds of 0.5 and 0.3, takes the first rising edge for a start and the first falling edge after activation for
an end, and emits each response at most once per question. This output construction limits repeated responses.
Published methods instead decide when to respond to the original question directly. To examine the effect of the output
construction, we also evaluate general VLMs with the same rewritten conditions and fixed readout. Qwen2.5-VL-7B
scores 10.0 T-F1@5 in the published official setting and 42.3 with our construction. These results compare the complete
model-and-protocol configurations. The matched-protocol comparison isolates the effect of adaptation more directly:
the Qwen3.5-2B base scores 27.5 and RoboBeats-2B scores 43.8.

B.5.1
SSS-Bench Construction and Verification
As shown in Figure 5(B), construction comprises video and event-pool preparation, task and evidence selection, question
and answer generation, strong-model verification, human review, and shortcut probing. We select continuous public
video clips with recognizable actions and use native annotations and Qwen3.8-27B (Qwen Team, 2026b) teacher events
as clues. Humans define question types and rules. GPT-6.1-sol inspects the full observation range at 1 fps to determine
the task scope, question time, and evidence. Early questions and answers are generated with templates, rules, and
assistance from Qwen3.8-27B. Later questions, reference answers, and scoring rubrics are constructed by GPT-6.1-sol
from the frames. Questions about order, occurrence number, and counts are checked against the images. An unannotated
event is not assumed to be absent.
GPT-6.1-sol verifies candidates independently. It first answers from the full 1 fps observation range with reference
answers and event labels hidden, and reports evidence times. After the answer is saved, the references and scoring
rubrics are revealed to check facts, evidence, and wording. Disputed questions undergo further review. Humans inspect
cases and revise rules, followed by additional model verification. Qwen3.8-27B then answers using either the question
alone or the question with a single frame at question time to screen for language and static-image shortcuts. These
probes diagnose difficulty and do not guarantee that all shortcuts are absent.
Human review also refines scoring requirements and removes duplicates. The kitchen example in Figure 5(B) illustrates
the relation between historical evidence and a later question time.

Memory Interfaces and Implementation Details
This appendix expands the keyframe interface, view assignment, retrieval, and historical-access rules in Section 4.3.

C.1
Keyframe Fields and Writing Rules
Every keyframe has an index record with the same fields, whether it was sampled along a route or saved by proactive
perception. The source and trigger record how a frame was written; they do not determine which fields it has. Derived
fields are produced by builders that run over every retained frame, so an empty field means that the corresponding
builder has not yet processed the frame. Table 18 lists the fields.
A keyframe is written when it is expected to add information to memory (Equation (8)), and different triggers estimate
this gain in different ways. Along a route, the robot must have moved or turned far enough since the last accepted frame,
the image must be sharp and not captured during a fast turn, and candidates whose viewing frusta overlap accepted
frames are excluded; these checks estimate viewpoint gain. Proactive perception writes a frame when a state changes, an
event occurs, a condition fires, or a person arrives, which estimates event gain. When a perception inference finishes
without a state change, a state-maintenance frame records that the state still holds. Frames confirmed by a user on site
are written directly.
Any keyframe can serve as a spatial frame of its view, representing what the place looks like, if it passes the same quality
check regardless of how it was written: the image is sharp, the robot is neither turning nor moving, people occlude little

View Assignment and Viewing Direction
The assignment operator σ in Equation (11) uses position, direction, and an estimated gaze region within a map. Let
pi = (xi, ψi), where xi is a planar position and ψi is the viewing direction: body heading plus head rotation when
available, approximating the planar direction of the camera. Angular differences use the minimum circular separation to
avoid discontinuities across ±180◦:

∨1[∥ai −aj∥< ρ] ,

Here, dang denotes the minimum circular angle, and ai is a reference point at distance g along direction ψi, approximating
the gaze region. This point is geometrically constructed and is not a detected object’s position. The second criterion
permits observations from different positions to share a view when their reference points are nearby. Among views
satisfying the criteria, a new frame selects the anchor with the lowest position-and-direction matching cost; otherwise, it
creates a new view. Frames from different maps are assigned separately.
C.3
Retrieval Fusion and Appearance-Change Cues
The score s(q, i) in Equation (12) combines retrieval channels by rank fusion. Channels are defined by field type:
keyword matching on image text, text embeddings of descriptions and object lists, and image embeddings. Descriptions
written by different models are scored in separate channels because they do not share a score scale: English scene
descriptions and the writer’s Chinese state and event sentences score differently against the same English query. The
image-embedding channel scores every keyframe on one scale. Queries containing rare terms, such as room numbers,
prioritize frames containing all rare terms. Place queries rank views by their spatial frames: the highest-ranked spatial
frame of each view becomes rq(v), accompanied by the latest observation c(v). Other members of the view that match
the query are returned with it as evidence of what happened there.
Appearance-change cues compare SigLIP2 image embeddings. The comparison in Equation (12) is made only when
both embeddings are available and the latest observation is clearly later than the matching frame. Otherwise, the pair is
unassessed. The cue reflects overall appearance differences; identifying their cause requires source-image inspection.

If no suitable retained frame exists near a requested time, the tool can consult the pose timeline to check whether the
robot was at the relevant observation location, then attempt to read a frame from the raw-video buffer. Recovery depends
on the recording still being retained and accessible. A request for “now” can acquire a new frame when the robot is at
the specified observation location.
Historical comparison uses detections from the first and last frames. When the capture poses nearly coincide, same-
category boxes are matched by image position to indicate continued visibility, position differences, objects no longer
detected, and newly detected objects. For larger pose differences, only category counts are compared. Registered
objects additionally use their event ledger to list recorded appearances, movements, and departures during the interval.
Targets outside the vocabulary can be detected by supplying a temporary English category. These differences require
interpretation against source images so that occlusion or missed detection is not treated as evidence of disappearance.

24-Hour Real-Robot Experiment Details
Memory construction.
The recorded day runs from 21:00 on October 21 to 21:00 on October 22, 2026. The event-
discovery gate and the background-difference prefilter select 256 windows for the initial Qwen3.8-27B writer, six of
them during the night, and the resulting memory retains 1,296 keyframes. The consolidation process, which organizes
the writer’s output in half-hour blocks, uses 363 writer calls with approximately 8.04 million input and 0.14 million
output tokens on the local Qwen3.8-27B model. Object detection and tracking and the condition-based rescans add
further construction cost that these counts do not include.
Scoring and inputs.
Every configuration answers each of the six questions three times, and each answer is manually
scored against the recorded rubric, with partial credit for some answers. LIFE’s execution agent receives the two turns
of a two-turn question in sequence, whereas the coding agents receive both turns together as one numbered prompt.
