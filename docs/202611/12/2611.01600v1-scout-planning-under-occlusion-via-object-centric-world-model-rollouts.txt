Abstract
Robots manipulating in cluttered spaces must often plan from partial views where
foreground objects occlude the target, surrounding geometry, and usable free space.
Reactive policies often miss these hidden constraints, while exhaustive search
becomes computationally prohibitive in dense clutter. We present SCOUT, an
occlusion-aware planning framework that bridges high-level deliberation with
grounded physical rollouts. SCOUT leverages an object-centric scene representa-
tion to reconstruct hidden geometry and derive a relational planning state capturing
blocker hierarchies and accessibility corridors. To navigate the vast action space in
clutter, an LLM proposes structured action candidates which are then rigorously
evaluated via Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) using a learned action-conditioned
world model. Evaluations on ‘Reveal’ and ‘Placement’ tasks show that SCOUT
outperforms Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, embodied agents, and 3D
world models, with significant gains in long-horizon tasks requiring reasoning
over occluded geometry. We further validate the framework’s robustness through
real-world robotic manipulation in a cluttered shelf environment.
1
Introduction
Manipulation in cluttered, occluded spaces is fundamentally difficult because the objects and free
space that matter most are often not directly visible [16, 40]. A target may be buried behind front-row
blockers in a shelf, reachable only through a narrow corridor that is itself occluded or only after several
intermediate rearrangements [14, 3]. Reasoning about these unobservable spaces to manipulate in
clutter requires a robot to move beyond purely reactive visual feedback, necessitating deliberation
across possible environment and object layouts and their intricate relationships among each other.
A natural way to enable such deliberation is to plan in belief space, where the robot represents
unobserved scene structure and reasons over possible hidden configurations [24]. For occluded
manipulation, such a belief must capture more than the mere existence of hidden objects. The planner
must also reason about which object is likely to block the target and where free space may exist
around it. This suggests an explicit scene representation that captures object-level geometry and
spatial structure, rather than only symbolic hidden-state hypotheses. Moreover, representing the
hidden scene is only part of the problem. To manipulate in clutter, the robot must also reason about
how that scene representation will evolve under candidate actions. It therefore needs a world model
that predicts how an action reveals the target, creates free space, or simply changes the occlusion
pattern [19].
Recent progress in vision-language and foundation-model-based robotics has improved action selec-
tion from observed images and led to strong results in semantic grounding, skill selection, program
synthesis, and visually guided planning [23, 29, 38, 22, 33, 30]. These methods are effective when
the relevant objects and affordances are visible, showing that large models can provide useful high-
level structure for robotic decision making. World models and search offer a complementary route

We therefore present SCOUT, a planning framework for manipulation under occlusion that combines

model predicts how this representation evolves under discrete object-level actions [19]. An LLM
proposes structured action candidates, and a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) [6] module evaluates
simulated rollouts under the learned world model. The next primitive is selected via grounded search,
and planning repeats from the updated state. We evaluate the method on cluttered manipulation tasks
in which targets are initially occluded, partially observable, and require multi-step rearrangement
to access [16, 40]. In these settings, we find that planning over reconstructed scene structure and
simulated futures is more effective than relying on visible-only reasoning or one-shot language
predictions alone.
Our contributions are:
• A structured hidden-scene representation for occluded manipulation from sparse frontal RGB-D
views.
• A planning method that reasons over this representation using a learned world model and LLM-
guided search.
• Simulation and real-robot validation on cluttered multi-step rearrangement tasks.
2
Related Work
Planning under Occlusion with Hidden-Scene Models.
Planning under occlusion is classically a
belief-space problem [25]. In embodied AI, recent work has addressed hidden targets and incomplete
maps through semantic priors, diffusion-based planning, and uncertainty-aware task-and-motion
planning. PONI predicts potential locations of unseen targets from partial semantic maps [36], while
value-guided diffusion planning explicitly targets navigation under partial observability through
multi-step candidate generation and best-plan selection [44]. TAMPURA and related partially
observable TAMP methods reason under action uncertainty using symbolic belief representations and
information-gathering strategies [12, 25], with extensions incorporating LLM-guided state estimation
over hidden objects [26]. Occluded planning further depends on reconstructing hidden 3D structure
from limited observations. Prior work in semantic scene completion and open-world 3D scene
understanding reconstructs geometry and semantics beyond the field of view [8, 28, 34, 1]. Spatially
2


aware robot foundation models show that explicit geometric alignment improves manipulation quality
and generalization [35, 27], while recent work leverages robot trajectories and test-time adaptation
to improve action semantics and value estimation [11, 45]. In contrast, we address planning under
occlusion in cluttered 3D scenes by reasoning over a reconstructed hidden scene and using an
action-conditioned world model to evaluate how different actions reveal previously unobserved
structure.
Language and vision foundation models for robotics.
Foundation-model approaches have enabled
robots to select skills, synthesize code, and plan trajectories from language and images [23, 29, 38, 22].
PIVOT shows that iterative visual prompting can substantially improve spatial decision-making
by repeatedly refining proposals rather than committing to one-shot predictions [33]. OK-Robot
demonstrates how open-knowledge components can be composed into a robust mobile-manipulation
pipeline, while recent analysis highlights the importance of spatial grounding and operational detail
when integrating such modules [30]. Our method differs from these works in two key respects: it
explicitly reasons over a hidden-scene belief rather than only visible observations, and it uses the
language model as a search prior, while the world model and MCTS perform physical evaluation and
final action selection.
World models and search for embodied planning.
Learned world models have proved effective for
planning and policy improvement in games and control, including MuZero, Dreamer, and DayDreamer
[37, 20, 41]. More recent simulators and generative world models expand this perspective to real-
world video and navigation [43, 2]. Language-guided search has also been explored for object
rearrangement and code world-model generation [9, 13]. Our goal is not to simply attach an LLM to
search. Instead, the LLM narrows the object-action space using the relational planning state, while
MCTS and the learned world model evaluate candidate futures and select the final plan from grounded
rollout evidence.
3
Method
We address long-horizon manipulation under partial observability by combining an object-centric
scene representation, a learned world model, and an LLM that generates structured action proposals
for planning. Our formulation explicitly separates (i) action proposal generation from the relational
planning state, from (ii) physical feasibility and multi-step outcome prediction, handled by the
learned world model. Fig. 1 provides an overview of SCOUT, from hidden-scene reconstruction
and LLM-guided action proposals to world-model rollouts and final plan selection. The complete
execution flow of the system is detailed in Algorithm 1.
3.1
Problem Formulation
We consider manipulation under partial observability, where an agent must plan a sequence of
object-level actions from a limited set of observations. Each instance is defined by a hidden scene
state s, a goal specification g, and a finite action budget T. The agent receives a set of RGB-D
observations o = {o(1), . . . , o(V )} captured from frontal viewpoints, providing partial visibility of
objects, free space, and access corridors. The objective is to compute a finite-horizon action sequence
a1:T = (a1, . . . , aT ), where at ∈A, that achieves the task goal. The action space A consists
of object-centric manipulation primitives a = (i, u), where i indexes the manipulated object and
u ∈{shift_left, shift_right, shift_back, pull_forward}. In the initial-view protocol, the
scene is reconstructed once from o, and subsequent planning states are updated using the world model
without re-observation.
We consider four task families instantiated from a shared distribution of cluttered shelf scenes: Reveal,
Reveal_Multi, Place, and Place_Multi. Each task defines a binary success condition evaluated at
the end of the sequence. Reveal tasks require the target to be visible and accessible, while placement
tasks require the object to lie within a goal region, with Reveal_Multi and Place_Multi being the
counterparts over multiple objects.
The system operates over a reconstructed scene representation z0 obtained from observations, and a
derived planning state x0 capturing task-relevant structure (Sec. 3.2). A learned world model predicts
state transitions zt+1 = fϕ(zt, at) (Sec. 3.3), enabling simulation of action-conditioned future states.

As shown in Fig. 2, GHOST takes RGB-D views from the front-facing
viewpoints, together with camera intrinsics and extrinsics. For each
view, SAM3D [39] provides object-level priors from segmented object
regions, including coarse 3D geometry estimated from the mask and
depth, a confidence score indicating how reliable the object hypoth-
esis is, and a compact shape token that summarizes the object’s local
appearance and geometry in a learned feature vector. These per-view
object hypotheses are fused across views into object-level estimates
with uncertainty, producing visible slots for objects supported by ob-
servations. To account for occluded structure, GHOST also maintains a fixed-capacity set of hidden
slots initialized as learned queries. The visible and hidden slots are jointly refined using a slot
transformer with self-attention and cross-attention, allowing observed objects and latent hypotheses
to exchange spatial context.
The refined slots are rendered through a Gaussian-based 3D refinement module to produce an
occupancy volume, updated slot states, and a relational graph. To support reasoning about task
structure, we derive a relational planning state xt = P(zt, g) from the reconstructed slot set zt and
the goal g, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Each of the K slots contains 7 values: a 3D center, a 3D size,
and an existence probability. The resulting state xt includes (i) the target object index, (ii) blocker
relationships indicating which objects obstruct access to the target, (iii) visibility indicators, and (iv)
coarse spatial relations. These relations are computed deterministically from slot geometry using
geometric predicates. The planner operates on xt, while the world model evolves zt; the mapping P
serves as the interface between perception and planning.

4
Experiments
We conduct a comprehensive set of experiments to evaluate the effectiveness, generality, and ro-
bustness of SCOUT for manipulation under occlusion. Our evaluation is organized around four
questions: 1) Does SCOUT improve task success in cluttered, partially observable manipulation? 2)
How well does it generalize to unseen objects, view layouts, and harder occlusion settings? 3) Which
components of the planner contribute most to performance? 4) Can our method successfully transfer
to the real world?
Task suite.
We evaluate SCOUT on a cluttered shelf benchmark designed to isolate the hidden-scene
planning problem under controlled occlusion. Scenes are generated in ManiSkill2 [18] using a
procedurally constructed shelf and object assets from the ManiSkill2-YCB set [7], ProcTHOR [15],
and RoboCasa [32]. Each scene contains 8–16 objects, including task objects, foreground blockers,
and distractors; object poses are sampled so that the target object or placement region is initially
occluded from the frontal view. In the initial-view protocol, each scene is observed from a small
set of RGB-D viewpoints sampled around the shelf opening, and the planner must produce a finite-
horizon sequence of object-centric primitives from these sparse observations alone. We evaluate
the four task families introduced in Sec. 3.1 and illustrated in Fig. 5: Reveal, Reveal_Multi, Place,
and Place_Multi. Additional details on shelf generation, object assets, camera sampling, and task
construction are provided in Section A.1.
Implementation Details.
We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [42] as the open-source action-proposal
model in SCOUT. We set the rollout scoring weights to αvis = 1.0, αacc = 1.0, αblock = 0.25, and
αinvalid = 1.0. All learned components are trained with AdamW [31] on simulator-generated shelf
data. Experiments are conducted on four NVIDIA L40S-48GB GPUs with 64 CPU cores and 512GB
RAM; full architecture, hyperparameter, compute, and inference details are provided in Section A.
Baselines.
For the initial-view comparison, we adapt four reference methods to the same sparse-
observation, no-reobservation, and discrete-primitive setting. The VLA comparisons characterize
performance under this shared interface rather than native continuous-control performance.
• π0.5 [4] is an end-to-end vision-language-action policy designed for open-world robot manipulation,
mapping visual observations and task specifications to continuous low-level robot actions. For fair
comparison, we provide the same initial RGB-D views and a templated task specification, decode
each predicted action to the nearest valid primitive and execute the resulting primitive sequence
under the same no-reobservation protocol as SCOUT.
• Long-VLA [17] is a long-horizon VLA policy designed to extend vision-language-action models
to multi-step manipulation. Since it originally produces continuous robot actions, we evaluate it
with the same initial observation set and task specification as SCOUT, map its outputs to the shared
primitive vocabulary, and impose the same T = 12 action budget.
• OWMM-Agent [10] is an embodied agent that combines scene understanding, state tracking,
and action generation for open-world mobile manipulation. To adapt it to our stationary shelf
benchmark, we disable navigation and auxiliary tool use, provide only the same initial RGB-D
observations, and require all decisions to be emitted through the same object-centric primitive API.

Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate performance using task-completion success rates over 500
independent rollouts with randomly initialized shelf configurations. Since SCOUT outputs high-level
manipulation primitives rather than low-level controls, we measure trajectory length in primitive
actions and impose a maximum budget of T = 12 actions per episode. Reported success is computed
from simulator ground-truth geometry: Reveal rollouts are counted as successful if the target object’s
visibility and accessibility scores both exceed 0.6 within T actions, while Place rollouts are successful
if the specified object reaches its assigned goal region within T actions. During planning, these
quantities are estimated from the relational planning state xt = P(zt, g) for rollout scoring. In
addition to success rate, we report the mean number of primitive actions per successful episode, target
visibility gain, target accessibility gain, and invalid-action rate. The visibility score is computed
from a fixed front-facing evaluation camera. For Reveal tasks, it measures how much of the target
object is visible; for Place tasks, it measures how much of the goal region is visible, normalized by its
empty-shelf projection. The accessibility score measures whether the task site is reachable from the
shelf opening: it is high when the path to the target object or goal region is clear, and low when other
objects block this path. Actions are marked invalid when the predicted transition violates geometric
feasibility constraints. Metric construction details are provided in Section A.3.
4.1
Simulation Results
Figure 6 shows the success rates for the simulation tasks under in-distribution, novel-object, and
novel-view settings. SCOUT achieves the highest success rate across all task families and distributions,
reaching an in-distribution average success of 80.8% compared with 72.8% for the strongest baseline.
The gains are largest in the multi-object settings, where SCOUT improves over the strongest baseline
by 9.0 points on Reveal_Multi and 9.0 points on Place_Multi. These settings require reasoning over
hidden blockers, intermediate rearrangements, and narrow access corridors. The same trend holds
in the single-object tasks, where SCOUT reaches 86.8% on Reveal and 88.2% on Place, improving
over the strongest baseline by 7.2 points in each case. These results use the initial-view protocol;
Appendix A.7 reports closed-loop comparisons and additional proposal, search, calibration, and
reconstruction diagnostics.
Generalization Performance. SCOUT remains strong under distribution shift. On novel objects,
it reaches an average success of 75.4%, compared with 67.6% for the strongest baseline. On novel
views, it reaches 76.8%, compared with 69.3%. The smaller degradation under viewpoint and object
shifts suggests that planning over reconstructed object-centric structure provides a stable basis for
manipulation under occlusion.

4.2
Failure Analysis and Qualitative Results
Failure Modes. To analyze where unsuccessful rollouts break down, we categorize failures in
Fig. 7a into four sources: hidden-scene errors, transition-prediction errors, geometric-validity errors,
and search-selection errors. The largest source is hidden-scene error, accounting for 7.6% of all
rollouts, where the reconstructed state misses a blocker or overestimates the free corridor to the
task site. Transition-prediction errors account for 5.2%, typically when the rollout predicts that
an action will clear visibility or access but the executed transition leaves the site partially blocked.
Geometric-validity failures contribute 4.8%, while search-selection errors are the smallest category
at 1.6%, indicating that most failures arise before final action selection rather than from the scoring
rule itself.
Panels (b)–(d) show representative failure cases behind the decomposition in Fig. 7. In (b), the world
model predicts that the selected action will create enough clearance, but execution leaves the target
only partially exposed and still not retrievable. In (c), the target is too small and occluded in the
back row, so it is not recovered by the hidden-scene reconstruction. In (d), the scene is so congested
that the available shift and pull primitives cannot open a feasible access corridor without an explicit
remove action.

with greedy depth-1 selection reaches 70.6% success and uses fewer actions on average, but the
shorter plans are less reliable because they do not evaluate longer rearrangement effects. The full
model achieves the best success, 80.8%, while maintaining a moderate action length of 7.5 primitives.
4.3
Real-World Experiments
To evaluate real-world performance, we deploy SCOUT on a Boston Dynamics Spot platform [5]
equipped with a 6-DOF manipulator and its integrated gripper-mounted 4K RGB and Time-of-Flight
(ToF) depth sensors. Observations are captured from static frontal viewpoints to maintain simulation-
consistent partial visibility, with action candidates executed via a kinematic motion controller. We
perform 10 trials each for the Reveal and Place tasks using a variety of household objects on a
cluttered shelf.
Across these 20 trials, SCOUT achieves a 60% (12/20) success rate. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the
system successfully reasons over hidden topology to plan strategic rearrangements, such as shifting a
blocking container (orange arrows) to expose an occluded target (red to green highlight) or clearing
an access corridor to reach a goal region (yellow arrow). Failures were distributed between low-level
hardware issues, such as gripper slippage on compliant surfaces, and plan-level errors. The latter
primarily involved the world model proposing physically invalid actions or MCTS rollouts failing
due to noisy GHOST reconstructions in high-glare environments. Detailed hardware specifications
and individual failure case analysis in Section A.8.
5
Conclusion and Limitations
In this work, we presented SCOUT, a planning framework for manipulation under partial observability
that integrates hidden-scene reconstruction, object-centric action abstraction, and world-model-guided
rollout evaluation. SCOUT enables structured reasoning over occluded environments by combining
LLM-guided action proposals with grounded evaluation of action-conditioned futures. We show
that this approach improves task success across cluttered shelf manipulation settings, including
multi-step rearrangement and generalization to unseen configurations. The framework operates from
sparse RGB-D observations and a discrete primitive interface, supporting consistent behavior across
simulation and real-world deployment.
9


Broader Impact. SCOUT improves the ability of robotic systems to operate in cluttered and partially
observable environments, with potential applications in warehouse automation, assistive robotics, and
service tasks. By reasoning about hidden objects and access corridors, it can reduce manipulation
errors and improve reliability in real-world settings. The structured planning interface may also
support more interpretable decision-making in human-robot interaction scenarios.
Limitations. Several limitations remain. The performance of SCOUT depends on the accuracy
of hidden-scene reconstruction, and errors in estimated geometry, object existence, or blocker
relationships can propagate through planning. The learned world model may produce inaccurate
predictions under complex contact interactions or out-of-distribution object arrangements. In addition,
the current formulation assumes a static scene during planning and relies on a fixed set of discrete
manipulation primitives, which may limit applicability to dynamic environments or tasks requiring
continuous contact-rich control.

A
Implementation Details
This appendix provides additional implementation details for SCOUT, complementing the method and
experimental setup in the main paper. We describe the simulator, data generation, scene reconstruction
module, relational state construction, world-model training, MCTS rollout evaluation, and real-robot
deployment.
A.1
Simulator and Dataset Generation
Shelf environment.
All simulated scenes are generated in ManiSkill2 with SAPIEN physics [18].
We use a procedurally instantiated cluttered shelf with approximate dimensions 0.36 m × 0.90 m ×
0.40 m and a fixed shelf-frame convention: depth points from the shelf opening toward the back wall,
the lateral axis spans left–right, and the vertical axis points upward. Candidate object placements are
sampled from a 2 × 6 slot grid inside the shelf volume.
Object assets.
We construct cluttered shelf scenes from ManiSkill2-YCB objects [7], ProcTHOR /
AI2-THOR assets [15], and RoboCasa kitchen assets [32]. YCB provides everyday graspable objects
such as cans, boxes, mugs, and bottles, while ProcTHOR and RoboCasa provide additional household
and kitchen objects. Objects are sampled from shelf-scale categories including mugs, cans, boxes,
bottles, and jars. For stable simulation, visual meshes are rendered from the original assets, while
collision geometry is approximated by box collision shapes. We use 77 YCB objects, 97 ProcTHOR
objects, and 16 RoboCasa objects.
Scene sampling.
Each scene is sampled by choosing a difficulty level and populating the shelf with
objects from the asset pool. Easy, medium, and hard scenes contain 4–8, 7–12, and 10–16 objects,
respectively; the benchmark settings in the main paper use 8–16 objects. Each scene contains task
objects, foreground blockers, and distractors. Distractors are objects that are not directly part of the
task but may still affect planning if they block the access corridor. Single-object tasks contain one
task object, while multi-object tasks contain two task objects. Each scene contains 2–5 foreground
blockers, with the remaining objects treated as distractors. Candidate poses are sampled with jitter and
rejected if they violate shelf bounds, intersect other objects, or fail the initial occlusion requirement.
Task families.
As described in Sec. 3.1 and illustrated in Fig. 5, we evaluate four task families:
Reveal, Reveal_Multi, Place, and Place_Multi. Reveal specifies one occluded target object, while
Reveal_Multi specifies two targets. Place specifies one object and an axis-aligned goal region, while
Place_Multi specifies two objects and a shared goal region. In the reported experiments, the goal
region occupies the front 30% of the shelf depth, spans the valid lateral workspace, and uses the
lower half of the shelf height as the placement volume. All tasks use a maximum episode budget of
T = 12 primitive actions.

A.2
GHOST Scene Reconstruction
Input representation.
GHOST receives RGB-D observations, camera intrinsics, camera extrinsics,
and object-level priors. For each view, the input consists of an RGB image, a depth map in metres,
an intrinsic matrix K, a world-from-camera transform Twc, and object masks. In simulation, object
masks are obtained from simulator segmentation when available; otherwise SAM3D is used as an
object-level prior [39]. The planner uses up to 5 views from the candidate view set described above.
Fig. 10 shows representative RGB-D observations and segmentation masks used as input to GHOST
before cross-view fusion.
Object priors and cross-view fusion.
For each RGB-D view, SAM3D provides an object mask,
coarse 3D geometry from mask and depth, a confidence score, and a learned shape token. We use
a confidence threshold of 0.5 and shape-token dimension 64. Per-view hypotheses are associated
in 3D using coarse object boxes. Detections with 3D-IoU above 0.25 are merged into a single
visible-slot initialization. Object centers and sizes are averaged using confidence weights, existence
probability is set to the maximum confidence in the cluster, and shape tokens are aggregated by
confidence-weighted averaging.
Visible and hidden slots.
Visible slots are initialized from fused observed object hypotheses.
Hidden slots are initialized as persistent learned queries and allow the model to represent unobserved
objects that may affect planning. We use 16 hidden slots per scene. During training, hidden slots are

Visible and hidden slots are refined using a slot transformer with model dimension

A.5
World Model Training
Inputs and targets.
The learned world model receives object features, a padding mask, global
action features, and a hallucinated-slot mask. Object features have dimension 13 and include
normalized center, normalized size, existence probability, visibility fraction, target flag, acted-object
flag, hallucinated-slot flag, back-row flag, and a slot bias. Global features have dimension 11 and
include the acted-object summary, primitive one-hot encoding, and primitive direction vector.
The world model predicts per-object displacement, moved-object logits, and transition validity:

where ∆µi ∈R3 is the normalized displacement, mi is a moved-object indicator, and v is a transition-
validity flag. Object sizes and existence probabilities are passed through unchanged.
Architecture.
The world model uses an object-token encoder, a global-feature encoder, self-
attention interaction layers, and prediction heads. The object encoder is a two-layer MLP with GELU
activations and hidden dimension 96. The global feature is encoded and broadcast to all object tokens.
The interaction module contains 2 transformer-style blocks with 4 attention heads, LayerNorm, and
feed-forward dimension 2 × 96. The trunk hidden dimension is 128. The displacement head outputs
a 3D displacement per slot, the moved head outputs one logit per slot, and the valid head outputs one
transition-validity logit from the pooled global feature.
Loss.
The world-model loss is
LWM = w∆L∆+ wmLm + wvLv + 1{phase=2}whLh.
(13)
Here, L∆is a masked Smooth-L1 displacement loss, Lm is binary cross-entropy for moved-object
prediction, Lv is binary cross-entropy for transition validity, and Lh penalizes nonzero motion on
hallucinated slots. We use weights (w∆, wm, wv, wh) = (1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.5).
Training schedule.
World-model training uses the transition dataset described above. Phase 1 trains
on oracle simulator states, and Phase 2 fine-tunes on GHOST-reconstructed states while keeping
oracle pre/post states for supervision. Phase 1 trains for 8 epochs with batch size 32 and learning rate
3×10−4. Phase 2 initializes from Phase 1 and trains for 4 epochs with batch size 32 and learning rate
10−4. We use AdamW [31], weight decay 10−5, gradient clipping at 5.0, and fp32 loss accumulation.

A.8
Real-World Hardware and Protocol
Figure 11: Real-world shelf observation setup. Left: Spot observes the cluttered shelf using its

Robot platform.
Real-world experiments in Fig. 9 are conducted on a Boston Dynamics Spot
robot equipped with the Spot Arm [5]. The Spot Arm is mounted in the standard configuration and is
controlled through the Spot SDK. We use the standard Spot Arm gripper, which provides actuated
gripper jaws and integrated sensing rather than a custom parallel-jaw end effector [5].
Observation.
The real system uses the wrist-mounted gripper sensors. The Spot Arm gripper
includes RGB and ToF depth sensing, which we use for frontal shelf observation [5]. The RGB
stream is captured at 3840 × 2160 resolution and resized for perception. The ToF depth stream
is aligned to the RGB frame and filtered to the shelf workspace. We capture 3 −5 frontal wrist
20


observation at the start of each planning episode. Fig. 11 shows the real shelf observation setup and
representative robot-captured RGB-D views used for scene reconstruction.
Shelf setup.
We construct real cluttered shelf environments using household objects. The real shelf
has approximate dimensions 0.36 m depth, 0.90 m width, and 0.40 m height, matching the simulated
shelf scale. Each trial contains 6–10 household objects, including bottles, cans, boxes, mugs, and
containers.
Task setup.
We evaluate Reveal and Place tasks. Reveal requires exposing and accessing a partially
occluded target, while Place requires moving a specified object into a marked goal region after
clearing nearby clutter. We run 10 trials per task, for 20 total trials. SCOUT succeeds in 12/20 trials,
corresponding to 60% overall success, with 7/10 Reveal successes and 5/10 Place successes.
Primitive execution and success.
Each primitive is executed as a Cartesian end-effector motion
in the shelf frame. Push primitives use a straight-line motion of 0.08 m at approximately 0.05 m/s.
Pull primitives establish contact near the front face of the selected object and retract by 0.08 m along
the negative depth direction. After each primitive, the end effector retracts upward by 0.04 m. The
real-world action budget is T = 12, matching simulation. Reveal succeeds if the target is exposed
and reachable from the shelf opening; Place succeeds if the specified object is moved into the marked
goal region. A trial is marked unsuccessful if the robot exhausts the action budget, executes an invalid
primitive, or leaves the target or goal region blocked.
Real-world failures.
Among the 8 unsuccessful real-world trials, 3 failures are due to execution
errors such as slipping or insufficient object displacement, 3 are due to perception or reconstruction
errors, 2 are due to transition-prediction mismatch.
