Abstract
Monocular depth estimation (MDE) has advanced rapidly with the emergence of
foundation models such as Depth Anything. Their transformer-based architectures
provide strong generalization across diverse scenes and domains, but also incur
high computational and memory cost, making efficient deployment challenging.
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) provides an efficient and practical solution
for model compression, yet low-bit PTQ remains challenging for MDE models.
When applying PTQ to Depth Anything, we identify two key challenges: (i) in-
dependently minimizing the quantization error of the query and key projections
fails to preserve the attention maps induced by their interaction, and (ii) quantiza-
tion errors accumulate across layers, resulting in intermediate feature distribution
shifts. To address these issues, we propose SPA-Q, a Structure-Preserving Adaptive
PTQ framework for MDE, consisting of two main components. First, Attention-
Preserving Calibration (APC) determines query and key quantization parameters
by matching the full-precision attention distribution. Second, Channel-Wise Distri-
bution Alignment (CWDA) learns channel-wise affine transformations to mitigate
quantization-induced distribution shifts, and the learned parameters are absorbed
into the weights after training. Experimental results show that SPA-Q consistently
outperforms existing PTQ methods under 4-bit quantization, achieving an average
25.9% reduction in AbsRel and a 17.3% improvement in δ1 across NYUv2 and
KITTI datasets.

Introduction
Monocular depth estimation (MDE) is a fundamental task in computer vision, with applications in
robotics [1, 2], autonomous driving [3, 4], and 3D scene understanding [5–7]. Recent advances in
Vision Transformers (ViTs) [8–12] have significantly improved MDE, leading to strong foundation
models such as MiDaS [13] and Depth Anything [14, 15]. Depth Anything achieves strong zero-shot
generalization through large-scale training with labeled and pseudo-labeled data, while leveraging
multi-scale intermediate features for dense depth prediction. However, its large model size and high
computational cost hinder real-world deployment in resource-constrained environments.
Model quantization [16–21] is an effective approach for reducing the memory and computational
cost of deep neural networks. Existing quantization methods can be broadly divided into quantization-
aware training (QAT) [22–24] and post-training quantization (PTQ) [25–28]. QAT incorporates
quantization effects during training and often achieves higher accuracy, but it requires additional
training and access to training data. In contrast, PTQ applies quantization to a pre-trained model

without full retraining, making it suitable for practical deployment. Although PTQ has been widely
studied for recognition models, its application to MDE remains underexplored. Unlike recognition
tasks, depth estimation requires preserving fine-grained geometric and structural information in
intermediate features, making it more sensitive to quantization-induced distortions.
In our study, we identify two key limitations of low-bit PTQ for transformer-based MDE models. (i)
Independent quantization error minimization for query and key projections is not well aligned
with preserving the resulting attention distribution. Existing PTQ methods [25–28] typically
calibrate each quantizer by minimizing the discrepancy between full-precision and quantized outputs
of each projection. However, in self-attention, the attention map is determined by the query-key
interaction passed through the softmax operation, rather than by the fidelity of each projection.
Therefore, independently minimizing the quantization error of the query and key projections does
not guarantee preservation of the resulting attention map. This issue is especially critical for MDE,
as distorted attention maps can corrupt features aggregated by the decoder, thereby degrading scene
geometry and object boundaries in depth predictions. (ii) Quantization errors accumulate across
layers and induce distribution shifts in intermediate features. Since the DPT decoder fuses features
from multiple encoder stages, even small shifts in feature distributions can propagate through the
decoder and degrade depth prediction. In practice, low-bit quantization induces noticeable distribution
shifts in intermediate features, making them increasingly mismatched with full-precision counterparts.
To address these limitations, we propose SPA-Q, a novel Structure-Preserving Adaptive PTQ frame-
work for transformer-based MDE models. SPA-Q is designed for architectures whose decoders
aggregate multi-stage encoder features to produce dense depth predictions. It consists of two com-
plementary components. Attention-Preserving Calibration (APC) determines the quantization
parameters of the query and key projections by directly preserving the full-precision attention distri-
bution instead of minimizing projection errors independently. In particular, APC minimizes the KL
divergence between the full-precision and quantized attention distributions, thereby better capturing
their discrepancy after softmax. Channel-Wise Distribution Alignment (CWDA) addresses distri-
bution shifts in intermediate features through learnable channel-wise affine transformations, which
are optimized with feature-level and depth-level objectives in an end-to-end manner. The learned
affine parameters are then fused into the weights, introducing no additional inference overhead. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:
• We identify two limitations of low-bit PTQ for transformer-based MDE models. First,
independent quantization error minimization for query-key projections fails to preserve the
resulting attention map. Second, accumulated quantization errors induce distribution shift in
intermediate features.
• We propose SPA-Q, a Structure-Preserving Adaptive PTQ method for MDE. SPA-Q intro-
duces Attention-Preserving Calibration (APC), which calibrates query and key quantizers
with an attention-level objective, and Channel-Wise Distribution Alignment (CWDA), a
channel-wise alignment scheme for correcting intermediate feature distribution shifts.
• Extensive experiments on relative depth estimation benchmarks show that SPA-Q outper-
forms existing PTQ methods under 4-bit quantization across all evaluated settings.

Related Work
2.1
Monocular Depth Estimation Models
MDE [29, 30] refers to the task of predicting a dense depth map from a single image, where each pixel
reflects the 3D structure of the scene. Recent advances driven by large-scale data and transformer-
based architectures have significantly improved depth prediction, leading to models such as MiDaS
[13] and Depth Anything [14, 15]. MiDaS improves generalization by training on multiple datasets
with varying scales and distributions, reducing dependence on a single dataset. Building on this,
Depth Anything adopts a DINOv2 [11]-pretrained ViT encoder. Depth Anything v1 leverages both
labeled and unlabeled data, while v2 further improves performance by incorporating large-scale
synthetic and pseudo-labeled data. Both MiDaS and Depth Anything are typically built on the DPT
[31] architecture, which aggregates multi-scale features using a transformer encoder and reconstructs
them with a multi-scale decoder. We focus on Depth Anything v1 and v2 for evaluation, as they
represent state-of-the-art MDE models with strong generalization performance.
2


2.2
Post-Training Quantization
PTQ [16–18] has been widely studied as an efficient approach for compressing neural networks
without requiring full retraining. Most existing PTQ methods are designed for recognition tasks such
as image classification. BRECQ [25] minimizes quantization error via block-wise reconstruction
using a second-order approximation. By randomly dropping activation quantization during reconstruc-
tion, QDrop [26] mitigates overfitting and improves generalization. RepQ-ViT [27] further reduces
inter-channel variation through scale reparameterization, enabling channel-wise calibration with
tensor-wise inference. However, these methods have been primarily designed for and evaluated on
classification tasks [8, 9, 32], and do not adequately address dense prediction tasks. In MDE, predic-
tions rely on multi-scale intermediate features, making the task more sensitive to quantization errors.
To address this limitation, QSCA [33] introduces lightweight auxiliary modules for residual error
compensation. Despite its effectiveness, this approach incurs additional parameters and computational
overhead, which is undesirable for efficient deployment. In this work, we propose a PTQ method
for MDE that preserves intermediate features required for dense depth prediction by maintaining
attention maps and mitigating feature distribution shifts.

3.3
Attention-Preserving Calibration
As discussed in Sec. 3.2, local quantization error minimization for query and key projections is
not well aligned with preserving the resulting attention map, and logits-level calibration is still
insufficient for matching the attention distribution after softmax. Based on this observation, we
propose Attention-Preserving Calibration (APC), which determines the query and key quantization
parameters by directly minimizing the discrepancy between the full-precision and quantized attention
distributions. Unlike objectives defined on individual projections or pre-softmax attention logits, APC

normalized probability distribution over tokens, KL divergence is more appropriate than Euclidean
error for measuring discrepancies in probability mass and capturing distortions in the attention
distribution. Accordingly, APC minimizes the row-wise KL divergence between the full-precision

Experimental Setup
Models, datasets, and evaluation.
We use Depth Anything v1 [14] and v2 [15], built on the
DINOv2 [11] encoder with ViT-S and ViT-B backbones, as representative foundation models for
monocular depth estimation. We evaluate SPA-Q on five MDE benchmarks: NYUv2 [36], KITTI

depth estimation metrics, including the absolute relative error (AbsRel) and threshold accuracy δ1.
AbsRel measures the mean relative difference between predicted and reference depth values, while δ1
measures the percentage of predictions satisfying a threshold ratio of 1.25.

Implementation details.
For fair comparison, we follow the experimental settings of prior work
[33]. We adopt the widely used percentile method for calibration, using channel-wise quantization for
weights and tensor-wise quantization for activations. The calibration set is constructed by randomly
sampling 16 images from the training set of NYUv2 for indoor scenes or KITTI for outdoor scenes.
The CWDA parameters are initialized such that all elements of α are set to 1 and all elements of β
are set to 0. We optimize all CWDA parameters in an end-to-end manner for one epoch using 5% of
the training images without ground-truth depth, with λSILog = 0.5. We use the Adam optimizer with
a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and no weight decay. All experiments are conducted with a batch size of
1 on a single RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU.

Experimental Results
Quantitative results.
As shown in Table 1, we compare SPA-Q with existing PTQ methods,
including BRECQ [25], QDrop [26], RepQ-ViT [27], and QSCA [33], under 4-bit quantization on
five zero-shot relative depth estimation benchmarks. Overall, our framework consistently achieves the
best performance in both AbsRel and δ1 across all evaluated datasets, outperforming all compared
PTQ methods. For Depth Anything v1 with the ViT-B backbone, the improvement is substantial
on the two representative benchmarks, NYUv2 [36] and KITTI [37]. On NYUv2, our framework
reduces AbsRel from 0.1875 to 0.1021 and improves δ1 from 0.6845 to 0.8991. On KITTI, AbsRel

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we present qualitative
comparisons of predicted depth maps in Figure 4. Compared with QSCA [33], SPA-Q produces qual-

[38] and DIODE [39], thin structures such as wooden poles and ropes, as well as the boundary of
the wall opening and object silhouettes, are well recovered, whereas QSCA largely fails to preserve
them. Sintel [40] is particularly challenging due to its domain gap from real-world imagery and
rendering effects such as atmospheric haze and motion blur. While QSCA collapses into a nearly
radial depth pattern that loses the character entirely, SPA-Q recovers its contours, including the
raised hand, consistent with the full-precision prediction. These results demonstrate that the proposed
framework consistently preserves depth structure across diverse domains, including indoor, outdoor,
and synthetic scenes.

Efficiency analysis.
We further compare model size, parameter count, and bit operations (BOPs)

restores the parameter count to that of the original quantized network. These results show that SPA-Q
improves depth estimation accuracy without introducing additional inference overhead.

5
Conclusion
In this paper, we addressed low-bit post-training quantization for MDE. We identified two key
limitations of existing PTQ methods. Independently minimizing quantization error for query and
key projections does not adequately preserve the resulting attention distribution, while accumulated
quantization errors induce distribution shifts in intermediate features. To address these issues, we
proposed SPA-Q, a structure-preserving adaptive PTQ framework that combines APC and CWDA
to preserve attention distributions and mitigate feature distortion. Experimental results on multiple
zero-shot depth estimation benchmarks showed that SPA-Q consistently outperforms existing PTQ
methods under 4-bit quantization.
Limitations & Future Works.
Although the proposed method consistently outperforms existing
PTQ baselines under 4-bit quantization and yields improved qualitative results, the quantized depth
maps remain visibly less clean than their full-precision counterparts. We conjecture that this limitation
stems from the restricted set of depth values representable under low-bit quantization, which is more
discrete than in full precision and can reduce the smoothness of predicted depth maps. Our study is
limited to MDE with Depth Anything v1 and v2, partly due to GPU memory constraints for larger
foundation models. Extending SPA-Q to larger foundation models and other depth estimation settings,
such as stereo and video depth estimation, remains an important direction for future work.

depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.

is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be

and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to

reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
