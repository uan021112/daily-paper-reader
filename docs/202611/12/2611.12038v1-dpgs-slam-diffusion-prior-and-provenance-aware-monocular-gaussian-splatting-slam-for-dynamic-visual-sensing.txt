nity. Many traditional SLAM systems, such as ORB-SLAM
(2015) [3] and DSO (2017) [4], are built upon the assumption
of a rigid scene and therefore tend to suffer from tracking
drift or reconstruction instability when independently moving
objects are present.
Recently, NeRF (neural radiance fields) (2020) [5] -based
volumetric representations have been explored as a dense-
mapping paradigm for SLAM, due to their strong photometric
modeling capability and high-quality novel-view synthesis.
However, NeRF-SLAM methods typically incur high computa-
tional costs and limited rendering efficiency, and often rely on
additional depth sensors or predefined dynamic priors, such as
DN-SLAM (2023) [6] and RoDyn-SLAM (2024) [7], to handle
dynamic regions. These constraints restrict their applicability
in purely monocular RGB settings and limit their robustness
in complex dynamic scenes.
More recently, 3DGS (3D Gaussian splatting) (2023) [8] has
emerged as an efficient and expressive scene representation for
dense scene modeling. By modeling scenes as a collection of
learnable 3D Gaussian primitives and enabling fast, differen-
tiable rasterization, 3DGS has been widely adopted in recent
SLAM systems, such as SplaTAM (2024) [9] and MonoGS
(2024) [10], achieving high-fidelity mapping in static environ-
ments at substantially lower computational costs. Nevertheless,
existing 3DGS-SLAM approaches typically assume a static
scene, leading to temporally inconsistent updates of Gaussian
primitives in dynamic regions, which accumulate over time
and contaminate the reconstructed map.
This behavior results in tracking instability, persistent noise
in long-term mapping, and ghosting artifacts, thereby limit-
ing the applicability of 3DGS-SLAM in real-world dynamic
scenarios.
In dynamic SLAM systems such as MaskFusion [11],
DG-SLAM [12], and RoDyn-SLAM [7], detected dynamic
regions are excluded or downweighted during tracking and
mapping. While this strategy reduces the influence of moving
objects from directly contaminating camera-pose estimation
and map optimization, it does not supply static-background
observations for the masked regions. Consequently, back-
grounds that remain insufficiently observed, whether under
persistent or intermittent occlusion, may be missing or only
sparsely represented in the reconstructed map. When only
sparse or inconsistent static observations are available, their
reconstructed appearance and geometry may also be weakly
constrained, resulting in local blur, ghosting, or unstable
Gaussian structures. Therefore, suppression alone removes
unreliable dynamic observations but does not provide the
missing background information needed for reconstruction.
To address this limitation, our DPGS-SLAM introduces
asynchronous diffusion-based background completion together
with provenance-aware Gaussian mapping. Here, provenance-
aware denotes the explicit labeling of Gaussian primitives as
observation-supported or diffusion-generated, with each label
determining whether a primitive participates in optimization
and how it is updated.
For selected keyframes, a diffusion inpainting model gen-
erates plausible background content for regions masked as
dynamic, providing an appearance prior where direct obser-
vations are unavailable. The completion process runs asyn-
chronously, without blocking camera tracking or observed-
map optimization. Metric3D v2 (2024) [13] predicts met-
ric depth for the completed regions, allowing the generated
content to be lifted into 3D and represented by provisional
Gaussian primitives.
These generated Gaussian primitives remain fixed and are
excluded from camera-pose estimation and observed-map
optimization. When the corresponding background becomes
visible in subsequent keyframes, a depth-aware projective
rule removes eligible generated Gaussian primitives, while
observation-supported Gaussian primitives are independently
initialized from real RGB observations and predicted depth. In
this way, DPGS-SLAM provides provisional representations
of insufficiently observed static backgrounds while keeping
generated Gaussian primitives separate from camera tracking
and observation-supported map optimization.

II. RELATED WORKS
A. Diffusion-Based 3D Reconstruction and Scene
Completion
Diffusion models learn data distributions through iterative
denoising [1], while latent diffusion models perform this
process in a compressed latent space to enable efficient con-
ditional image generation and inpainting [2]. Their ability to
generate plausible content in unobserved regions has motivated
their application to 3D reconstruction. GS-GS (2025) [19] uses
diffusion-generated views to refine Gaussian representations,
while Zhong et al. (2025) [20] introduce scene-grounded video
diffusion for sparse-view reconstruction. RI3D (2025) [21] em-
ploys separate diffusion priors for repairing observed regions
and inpainting missing content.
Diffusion-based 3D scene completion further addresses
missing or occluded regions. NeRFiller (2024) [22] uses
multi-view diffusion inpainting for NeRF completion, while
InFusion (2024) [23] introduces diffusion-based depth com-
pletion for initializing missing Gaussian primitives. SplatFill
(2026) [24] combines diffusion inpainting with depth-aware
supervision, and Lai et al. (2025) [25] employ diffusion-
based depth completion for egocentric reconstruction under
dynamic occlusions. Although these approaches improve re-
construction completeness, diffusion-generated content may
lack direct observation support, requiring careful integration
into incremental SLAM to avoid affecting camera tracking
and observed-map optimization.
B. 3D Gaussian Splatting SLAM Systems
3DGS [8] represents scenes as collections of anisotropic
Gaussian primitives and enables efficient differentiable raster-
ization without ray marching. Owing to its rendering efficiency
and reconstruction quality, 3DGS has been widely adopted
in dense SLAM systems, including SplaTAM (2024) [9],
MonoGS (2024) [10], and Photo-SLAM (2024) [26], achieving
accurate tracking and high-quality mapping in static environ-
ments. However, these systems are primarily developed and
evaluated under static-scene assumptions.
To handle dynamic environments, recent works extend
3DGS-SLAM to dynamic scenarios, including DG-SLAM
(2024) [12] and GassiDy (2025) [27]. These approaches
identify and suppress dynamic observations through motion
masks, rendering residuals, or adaptive Gaussian primitive
management. WildGS-SLAM (2025) [18] further incorporates
learned per-pixel uncertainty into tracking and Gaussian map
optimization to reduce the influence of dynamic observations.
DRG-SLAM (2026) [28] combines geometric motion verifica-
tion and temporal consistency to exclude dynamic observations
from RGB-D tracking and Gaussian mapping. While these
strategies reduce contamination from moving objects, dynamic
suppression alone does not provide observation support for
persistently occluded static backgrounds. As a result, such
regions may remain missing, sparsely represented, or weakly
constrained in the reconstructed Gaussian map.
C. Dynamic Visual SLAM Systems
Many classical visual SLAM systems, such as DSO [4]
and ORB-SLAM3 (2021) [29], are developed under the as-
sumption of static scenes. The presence of dynamic objects
contradicts this assumption by disrupting feature correspon-
dences and photometric consistency, often resulting in tracking
drift and reconstruction failure. To address this limitation, a
common strategy is to identify and exclude dynamic regions,
reconstructing only the static components of the environment.
Dynamic region detection is commonly based on geometric
consistency checks, such as StaticFusion (2018) [30] and
ReFusion [16], or optical-flow-based motion estimation, such
as Motion removal (2018) [31] and FlowFusion (2020) [32].
However, these approaches can produce noisy and temporally
unstable masks under occlusions or inaccurate geometry and
motion estimates. Semantic-based methods further exploit
object detection or semantic segmentation priors like Mask-
SLAM (2018) [33], but semantic category information alone
cannot reliably distinguish between moving and stationary in-
stances. Hybrid approaches combining geometric, motion, and
semantic cues, such as DynaSLAM (2018) [34] and SG-SLAM
(2022) [35], improve dynamic-region identification, although
consistent separation of dynamic and static regions remains
challenging under occlusions and intermittent observations.
More recently, DROID-W (2026) [36] introduces uncertainty-
aware bundle adjustment to jointly refine camera poses, depth,
and dynamic uncertainty.
In many dynamic SLAM systems [7], [12], [34], dynamic
observations are excluded or suppressed during tracking and
mapping. While hard exclusion prevents detected dynamic pix-
els from directly contributing to pose estimation and map op-
timization, masking alone does not provide static-background
information for regions that remain unobserved.
Beyond hard exclusion, Zhou et al. (2025) [37] combine dy-
namic pixel-set masking with residual losses for soft suppres-
sion, while DROID-W (2026) [36] incorporates dynamic un-
certainty into bundle adjustment. Dy3DGS-SLAM (2025) [38]
addresses artifacts associated with pruning dynamic Gaussian
primitives. These strategies reduce dynamic interference but
do not supply missing static-background observations; soft
suppression may also retain residual dynamic contamination.
DPGS-SLAM therefore combines optical-flow-based mo-
tion discovery with temporally propagated segmentation and
performs asynchronous diffusion-based background comple-
tion to provisionally represent backgrounds with insufficient
real observation support. Provenance-aware mapping isolates
generated Gaussian primitives from camera-pose estimation
and observed-map optimization; when real static observations
become available, eligible generated primitives are removed
and observation-supported primitives are independently initial-
ized.

C. Provenance-aware 3DGS-SLAM Pipeline
1) 3D Gaussian-based Map Representation: We represent
the scene using the 3DGS formulation, in which the en-
vironment is modeled as a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian
primitives:
G = {Gi = (µi, Σi, oi, hi, qi) | i = 1, . . . , N}.
(15)
Here, N is the current number of primitives. Each Gaussian
primitive is parameterized by its center µi ∈R3, covari-
ance Σi ∈R3×3, scalar opacity oi ∈(0, 1), and spherical
harmonic coefficients hi ∈R3×(L+1)2 representing view-
dependent appearance, where L denotes the maximum order of
the spherical harmonics expansion [8]. The provenance label
qi ∈{OBSERVED, GENERATED} distinguishes primitives
initialized from real static RGB pixels and diffusion-generated
RGB pixels. Accordingly, we denote the observed and gen-
erated Gaussian subsets by GO and GG, respectively, with
G = GO ∪GG.

selected as a keyframe.

IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
A. Experimental Setup
1) Datasets:
We evaluated our DPGS-SLAM on two
widely used dynamic RGB-D benchmarks: (a) TUM RGB-
D (2012) [15] dataset and (b) BONN RGB-D dynamic
(2019) [16] dataset. Both datasets capture indoor scenes using
handheld RGB-D cameras and provide ground-truth camera
trajectories. Following prior work on dynamic SLAM such
as GassiDy (2025) [27] and WildGS-SLAM (2025) [18],
we selected sequences involving human motion and object
interactions to evaluate robustness in dynamic scenes.
We additionally evaluated static-background reconstruction
on three sequences from the StaDy4D (2026) [17] dataset.
StaDy4D is a synthetic dataset that provides paired static
and dynamic RGB sequences rendered along identical camera
trajectories. The dynamic sequences were used as SLAM
inputs, while the corresponding static sequences provided
ground-truth (GT) references for evaluating the reconstructed
background and were not involved in tracking or mapping
optimization.
For qualitative ablation comparisons, we used the wander-
ing sequence from WildGS-SLAM iPhone (2025) [18] and
freiburg3 sitting halfsphere from TUM RGB-D [15], repre-
senting limited background observations under rapid viewpoint
changes and prolonged human occlusion, respectively.
Across all evaluations, our DPGS-SLAM used only monoc-
ular RGB images and camera intrinsics as input; neither sensor
nor ground-truth depth was used for tracking, mapping, or
background completion. Ground-truth depth from StaDy4D
was used only for evaluation, including the construction of
regional masks and quantitative assessment of geometric ac-
curacy.
For LoRA-based fine-tuning of the diffusion inpainting
model for static-background completion (Section III-B), we
used indoor scene images sampled from ScanNet (2017) [44]
and ScanNet++ (2023) [45] datasets. Synthetic occlusion
masks were applied to these images to construct the masked-
image training pairs described in (4)–(9).
2) Metrics: Camera tracking accuracy was evaluated us-
ing the ATE (absolute trajectory error), reported as RMSE
(root mean squared error) in centimeters. Following WildGS-
SLAM [18] and DROID-W [36], we evaluated the complete
camera trajectory, including non-keyframes, after aligning
the estimated trajectory with the ground-truth using evo
(2017) [46] with Sim(3) Umeyama alignment [47].

we report standard photometric rendering metrics commonly

B. Camera Tracking Accuracy Analysis
We evaluated the camera tracking accuracy on dynamic
scenes from the BONN RGB-D dynamic and TUM RGB-D
datasets using the ATE RMSE metric.
1) Quantitative Results: As shown in Table I, our DPGS-
SLAM achieved the lowest average ATE RMSE of 2.7 cm
on the four selected BONN RGB-D sequences. On TUM
RGB-D, it achieved an average ATE RMSE of 1.2 cm, match-
ing DROID-W at the reported precision. This tracking per-
formance is supported by motion-mask-based exclusion of
dynamic observations and photometric–geometric pose opti-
mization, as further examined in Table IV. Camera tracking
uses only observation-supported Gaussian primitives, while
diffusion-generated primitives remain excluded from pose op-
timization.
2) Trajectory Analysis: As shown in Fig. 6, our DPGS-
SLAM maintains closer alignment with the ground-truth tra-
jectory and exhibits reduced drift in the illustrated sequences,
compared with SplaTAM and MonoGS. On the wk.xyz se-
quence of TUM RGB-D, DPGS-SLAM and DROID-W ex-
hibit similar trajectory alignment, while WildGS-SLAM shows
larger local deviations. On the ps.tr2 sequence of BONN RGB-
D, all three methods closely follow the ground-truth trajectory,
with minor local deviations.

E. General Discussion and Limitations
The proposed system DPGS-SLAM relies only on monoc-
ular RGB input. The diffusion inpainting model was fine-
tuned offline using LoRA [40], without additional scene-
specific fine-tuning on the evaluated sequences. Metric3D
v2 [13] provides predicted depth for tracking, mapping, and
provisional Gaussian initialization. Periodic optical-flow-based
motion discovery may introduce a brief delay in detecting
newly entering moving objects, allowing temporary dynamic
interference. Such interference may be mitigated by subse-
quent motion detection and incremental map updates.
Provenance-aware mapping excludes generated Gaussian
primitives from camera-pose estimation and observed-map
optimization, and selectively deletes them when valid static
observations become available. However, persistently unob-
served regions may retain inaccurate generated appearance or
geometry. Asynchronous background completion also incurs
computational overhead and does not guarantee real-time
performance.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We present DPGS-SLAM, a monocular RGB 3D Gaussian
splatting SLAM system for dynamic environments that pro-
vides provisional representations of static backgrounds with
insufficient support from real RGB observations. The system
combines periodic optical-flow-based motion discovery with
SAM2 to obtain temporally consistent motion masks.
For map reconstruction, diffusion-based inpainting enables
asynchronous background completion, while provenance-
aware Gaussian mapping integrates the generated content
as provisional Gaussian primitives. Generated primitives are
excluded from camera-pose estimation and observed-map op-
timization. When valid static observations become available,
eligible generated primitives are removed using a depth-
aware projective rule, while observation-supported Gaussian
primitives are independently initialized. Experimental results
demonstrated competitive camera tracking, with average ATE
RMSE values of 1.2 cm on TUM RGB-D and 2.7 cm on
BONN RGB-D. On the three evaluated StaDy4D sequences,
DPGS-SLAM improved average PSNR by 4.60 dB over
WildGS-SLAM, while ablation studies further support the
benefit of observation-based replacement for static-background
reconstruction. Future work will explore tighter integration
of dynamic object modeling and unified optimization for-
mulations to further extend the applicability of the proposed
framework.
