ABSTRACT
The spatial intelligence of large vision-language models (LVLMs) requires the
ability to combine local visual observations into a geometrically consistent rep-
resentation of the physical world. Many static benchmarks assess isolated spa-
tial judgments, such as relative direction or distance, while interactive embodied
evaluations combine perception with exploration, planning, control, and simulator-
specific execution. To bridge this gap, we introduce RECONSPATIAL1, a bench-
mark centered on multi-view 3D scene reconstruction across indoor, tabletop, and
outdoor environments. Given real multi-view images and target-object queries,
LVLMs are required to jointly predict oriented 3D bounding boxes in a shared,
camera-anchored metric coordinate frame. Reconstructions are evaluated along
two complementary axes: Structure for relative layout and Metric for physical
scale and camera-anchored placement. Nine atomic spatial QA tasks, grouped into
Geometric Perception and Spatial Relations, probe spatial capabilities involved
in joint reconstruction. Offline navigation extends evaluation to path planning.
Evaluation of 18 frontier LVLMs reveals a consistent gap between relative scene
structure and metric reconstruction; even GPT-6-Astra scores 81.4% on Structure
but only 58.7% on Metric. Tracing this gap, diagnostic analyses link atomic spatial
capabilities to reconstruction quality and show that relational consistency alone
does not ensure metric accuracy. Experiments with 3D map injection further sepa-
rate scene construction from path planning, revealing how these geometric errors
limit navigation success. Finally, spatial fine-tuning yields broad transfer across
both spatial and general vision-language benchmarks.

INTRODUCTION
A central requirement of spatial reasoning in large vision-language models (LVLMs) is the abil-
ity to combine partial, viewpoint-dependent observations into a coherent understanding of three-
dimensional space. Although contemporary LVLMs have made substantial progress in visual-
semantic understanding (Bai et al., 2025; Comanici et al., 2025; Achiam et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2025;
2026), their spatial intelligence remains severely limited, particularly in understanding complex 3D
environments (Yang et al., 2025; 2026a; Liu et al., 2025a).

individual spatial judgments, such as relative direction and distance (Yang et al., 2025; 2026a; Wang
et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025a). However, independent queries fail to test whether
a model can jointly recover a coherent scene: for instance, knowing which object is closer does

benchmarks evaluate spatial understanding through interaction in simulated environments (Majumdar
et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2026), where outcomes jointly depend on perception, exploration, planning,
control, and simulator-specific execution. This leaves an unmet need: evaluating whether LVLMs can

To bridge this evaluation gap, we introduce RECONSPATIAL, a benchmark for evaluating cross-view
spatial reasoning in LVLMs through joint prediction of object-level 3D bounding boxes. Given
multi-view observations and target object queries, an LVLM is tasked with predicting oriented 3D
bounding boxes in a shared, camera-anchored metric coordinate frame. This joint objective requires
models to associate objects across viewpoints, resolve relative camera geometry, and estimate metric
extents simultaneously. Providing queried 2D regions controls for open-set detection, isolating spatial
reasoning as the primary point of evaluation. We assess each reconstruction along two complementary
axes: Structure, which evaluates relative layout and topological arrangement, and Metric, which
measures physical scale and camera-anchored 3D placement across indoor, tabletop, and outdoor
scenes.
RECONSPATIAL organizes evaluation into four levels: Geometric Perception, Spatial Relations,
Reconstruction, and Navigation (Figure 1). The first two levels comprise nine spatial QA tasks that
probe individual spatial capabilities, including cross-view correspondence, camera pose estimation,
and relative distance judgments. Their scores help us examine how these capabilities relate to joint

Evaluation of 18 frontier LVLMs reveals a consistent gap between relative scene structure and metric

that relational consistency alone does not ensure metric accuracy. Experiments with 3D map injection

55.2 to 69.7 percentage points. This gap indicates that downstream navigation is primarily bounded
by perceptual accuracy rather than planning capability. Finally, spatial fine-tuning confers broad
performance gains across both spatial and general vision-language benchmarks.
The contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• Joint 3D reconstruction for spatial evaluation. We introduce RECONSPATIAL, which
establishes a new evaluation paradigm that benchmarks LVLMs on jointly recovering object-
level 3D bounding boxes from real multi-view observations. Complementary Structure and
Metric scores distinguish relative scene coherence from physical scale and placement.
• A hierarchical diagnostic suite across diverse domains. Covering indoor, tabletop, and
outdoor scenes, RECONSPATIAL unifies 1,388 atomic items, 840 reconstruction instances,
and 346 offline navigation episodes to evaluate local spatial capabilities, joint scene recon-
struction, and navigation.
• Diagnostic insights and transferable supervision. Evaluating eighteen frontier mod-
els uncovers a pervasive Structure–Metric disparity. Controlled interventions show that
object-level metric depth yields larger Metric gains than relational cues. Downstream map
substitution decouples spatial perception from planning competence, and spatial fine-tuning
confirms transferable reasoning benefits.

RELATED WORK
Benchmarking Spatial Reasoning.
Spatial reasoning benchmarks have primarily targeted local
geometric queries (Yang et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2026; Li et al.,
2025a; Yang et al., 2026a) or embodied task execution (Majumdar et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2026;
Zhao et al., 2026b). Recent efforts also explore 3D room generation (Yang et al., 2026b), inverse
rendering of synthetic single-image scenes in IR3D-Bench (Liu et al., 2025a), top-down metric
layouts in Video2Layout (Huang et al., 2025), and 2D floor plans from apartment photographs in
Blueprint-Bench (Petersson et al., 2025). In contrast, RECONSPATIAL evaluates joint object geometry
from real-world multi-view observations, directly scoring predicted 3D bounding boxes against
annotated ground truth. Complementary Structure and Metric scores explicitly disentangle relative
scene layout from physical scale and camera-anchored placement.
Spatial Representations for Reasoning and Planning.
Explicit spatial representations provide
essential intermediate abstractions between perception and action. Recent agentic approaches support
spatial reasoning with intermediate information obtained through code execution, tool calls, or visual
sketching (Marsili et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2026b; Dai et al., 2026; Cho et al.,
2026), while cognitive-mapping approaches study structured representations that scaffold mental
navigation (Yang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). Complementary work examines data and training
strategies for spatial reasoning (Ouyang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2024; Li et al.,
2026; 2025b). We build upon this insight by treating reconstructed 3D bounding box maps as an
explicit spatial reasoning representation, directly probing how their geometric fidelity supports path
planning.

3.3
DIAGNOSTIC HIERARCHY AND EVALUATION DOMAINS
Task design and diagnostic hierarchy.
The benchmark tasks are designed to distribute relevant
spatial evidence across multiple views, requiring LVLMs to compare observations and reason about
geometry in shared reference frames. Because an aggregate reconstruction score cannot isolate
constituent failure modes, RECONSPATIAL complements holistic reconstruction with nine atomic
spatial QA diagnostics organized into two task families (Figure 2). Geometric Perception examines
cross-view correspondence, camera pose, depth ordering, and metric depth. Spatial Relations assesses
camera- and object-anchored directions, distance ranking, object correspondence, and proximity. In
total, RECONSPATIAL includes 840 joint reconstruction instances, 1,388 atomic spatial QA items,
and 346 independent offline navigation episodes (Figure 1(b)). Counts refer to task instances rather
than distinct physical scenes. Detailed task formulations appear in Appendix A.
Evaluation domains.
Reconstruction draws on ScanNet and CA-1M for indoor scenes (Dai
et al., 2017; Lazarow et al., 2025); YCB-Video, T-LESS, HomebrewedDB, and NOCS for tabletop
scenes (Xiang et al., 2018; Hodaˇn et al., 2017; Kaskman et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019); and nuScenes
for outdoor scenes (Caesar et al., 2020). Reconstruction scores are first averaged within each domain
and then equally across the three domain means, preventing larger domains from dominating the
aggregate score. Data composition and construction details are given in Appendix A and Table 5.

In this section, we evaluate 18 frontier LVLMs across the four levels of RECONSPATIAL. We examine
the overall benchmark landscape, the disparity between Structure and Metric fidelity, and the gap
between atomic capabilities and joint scene reconstruction. We further investigate these findings
through information injection, verification probes, and qualitative examples, and examine whether
cross-view spatial supervision transfers to broader multimodal tasks.
4.1
EVALUATION SETUP
The cohort comprises GPT-6-Astra (OpenAI, 2026), GPT-5.6-Sol, and GPT-5.5; Gemini-3.1-Pro
(Comanici et al., 2025), Gemini-3.5-Flash (Google, 2026), Gemini-3.7-Flash, and Gemini-3.8-Flash;
Claude-Opus-4.7, Claude-Opus-4.8, Claude-Opus-5, and Claude-Fable-5; Qwen3.7-Plus (Bai et al.,
2025) and Qwen3.8-Max (Qwen Team, 2026); Kimi-K3 (Team et al., 2026); Doubao-Seed-2.1-Pro
(ByteDance Seed, 2026); MiniMax-M3 (MiniMax, 2026); Grok-4.6; and GLM-5.3-Flash (Z.ai,
2026). All models use the same task definitions, parser, and scoring protocol. Output-format and
reproducibility details are given in Appendix A.3. The Overall score equally weights the four
capability levels—Geometric Perception, Spatial Relations, Reconstruction, and Navigation—and
Reconstruction equally averages Structure and Metric across the three physical domains, preventing
split sizes from biasing aggregate results.

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
Table 2 illustrates the overall benchmark landscape. GPT-6-Astra sets the performance frontier,
leading all four levels with 59.5 Overall, followed by Qwen3.8-Max at 48.4. Across capability tiers,
runner-up rankings vary: Qwen3.8-Max ranks second in both atomic levels, while GPT-5.6-Sol
places second in reconstruction and ties with Kimi-K3 for second in navigation. Farther down the
leaderboard, Claude-Fable-5 ranks third in reconstruction (54.1) but drops to seventh Overall (42.5).
Close aggregate scores thus frequently mask divergent competencies across local geometric reasoning,
scene reconstruction, and planning. Across all models, navigation remains universally difficult: even
GPT-6-Astra succeeds in only 30.3% of episodes under scale-normalized evaluation, motivating our
map-injection experiments to probe the origins of planning failures in Section 4.3.
Table 2 shows a persistent gap between relative layout recovery and metric reconstruction, even for the
strongest models. Figure 3 shows that this disparity holds in all 54 model–domain combinations and
is largest on tabletop scenes for every model. Indoor gaps are comparatively uniform across models,
while tabletop and outdoor gaps vary more substantially. Recovering recognizable object relations
therefore does not guarantee calibrated geometry for distance-sensitive planning. Per-domain results
and scorer robustness checks are provided in Appendices B.1 and A.3.
4.3
DIAGNOSTIC ANALYSIS
Atomic capabilities and reconstruction.
To examine task-specific associations across the eighteen
LVLMs, we compute partial correlations controlling for the mean score on the other eight atomic
tasks. Camera pose is associated with projected box overlap (r = 0.62), while depth ordering
tracks front–back layout consistency (r = 0.63). Pixel correspondence is associated with Metric
(r = 0.46) but barely with adjacency (r = 0.01), suggesting that detailed cross-view matching is
more closely linked to metric geometry than to local neighbor relations. These patterns distinguish
the geometric demands of joint reconstruction beyond an overall spatial score. The full correlation
matrix and stability analyses are provided in Appendix B.2. Building on this analysis, we use
information-injection experiments to examine how supplied geometry affects reconstruction and
navigation.
Geometric information injection.
Using the ScanNet two-view reconstruction set, we augment
prompts for GPT-5.6-Sol, GPT-6-Astra, Qwen3.8-Max, and Gemini-3.8-Flash with ground-truth
metric depth cues or relational cues. Metric depth cues specify object depths in metres along the
reference camera’s viewing axis; relational cues describe depth order, pairwise directions, distance
ranks, and camera motion. Compared with the same prompts without added cues, both cue types
improve Structure, while metric depth cues produce larger and more consistent Metric gains across
all four models (Figure 4). For GPT-5.6-Sol, depth alone raises Metric by 18.1 percentage points.

Layout verification and revision.
We investigate whether visual feedback can help models assess
candidate layouts and improve their own reconstructions. In the verification task, GPT-5.6-Sol,
Claude-Opus-4.8, Gemini-3.1-Pro, and Gemini-3.5-Flash choose between two rendered layouts: one
preserves the reference geometry, while the other perturbs object arrangement. Providing the scene
photographs raises selection accuracy to 95.0–97.5% across the four models, showing that they can
use image evidence to judge the candidate layouts.
We then examine whether rendered feedback also helps a model produce a better reconstruction.
GPT-5.6-Sol revisits its prediction produced without added cues, using the original photographs and
its previous scene estimate; one condition additionally provides a rendering of that estimate. Both
revision conditions leave Metric essentially unchanged from the original prediction, and adding the
rendering provides no additional gain over text-only revision, with a difference of −0.3 percentage
points. Revision requires coordinated changes to object positions, sizes, and orientations, while
verification supplies two fixed alternatives. A rendering makes the current estimate visible without
adding external geometric measurements. High verification accuracy alone therefore does not
establish that rendered feedback is sufficient for effective metric correction.

SPATIAL SUPERVISION AND TRANSFER
To evaluate the effectiveness and transfer of our spatial supervision, we fine-tune Qwen3.6-35B-
A3B2 on up to 50k examples spanning Geometric Perception, Spatial Relations, and Reconstruction,
using reasoning traces generated by Qwen3.8-Max and filtered against the ground truth, without
general-domain training data. On external evaluations, the 50k model improves nine of ten spatial
and all eight general benchmarks (Table 4). Across the ten public spatial benchmarks, including
SITE-Bench (Wang et al., 2025), BLINK (Fu et al., 2024), ViewSpatial-Bench (Li et al., 2025a), and
ERQA (Gemini Robotics Team, 2025), the mean score rises from 60.98 to 65.13. General benchmark
gains include 7.9 points on VisualPuzzles (Song et al., 2025) and 3.6 points on BabyVision (Chen
et al., 2026a). Most external transfer gains are already present at 10k examples, with smaller gains
from further scaling. The full benchmark list and evaluation settings are provided in Appendix B.6.
On RECONSPATIAL, performance improves steadily with training scale, from 24.39 to 37.17 Overall,
with monotonic gains in the aggregate Geometric Perception, Spatial Relations, and Reconstruction
scores. Navigation success rises from an 8.1% baseline to at most 11.8% and varies non-monotonically
with data scale. Cross-view spatial supervision thus yields broad external transfer and steady gains in
perception and reconstruction on RECONSPATIAL, while translating these gains into reliable planning
remains an open challenge.

CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
In this work, RECONSPATIAL establishes multi-view 3D scene reconstruction as a principled bench-
mark connecting local spatial perception with holistic scene coherence and downstream planning.
Across 18 frontier LVLMs, our evaluations uncover a persistent gap between relative scene struc-
ture and metric reconstruction. Diagnostic analyses show that relational consistency alone cannot
guarantee metric accuracy, while downstream map substitution demonstrates that navigation failures
stem primarily from perceptual geometry rather than planning logic. Furthermore, cross-view spatial
fine-tuning confers broad transfer to both spatial and general vision-language benchmarks.
Limitations and Future Work.
Several limitations outline avenues for future work. First, physical
objects are represented via oriented 3D bounding boxes, omitting fine-grained surface meshes,
articulated parts, and continuous free-space occupancy. Second, our navigation evaluation focuses on
offline, open-loop planning within single rooms to decouple execution noise, leaving closed-loop
recovery and multi-room exploration unexamined. Finally, spatial fine-tuning was evaluated on
a single model family. Scaling these evaluations to broader architectures and interactive physical
environments represents a critical frontier for building dependable embodied spatial intelligence.
ETHICS STATEMENT
Data Sourcing and Privacy.
RECONSPATIAL builds upon existing open research datasets and
in-house robot recordings (Appendix A). All asset curation and redistribution strictly comply with
the original licenses and permissible use policies. To protect privacy, all real-world scenes have been
screened to ensure they contain no personally identifiable information.
Broader Impact and Safety.
This benchmark aims to advance foundational 3D spatial perception
and reasoning. All navigation tasks are evaluated strictly offline using pre-recorded observations and
maps, involving no physical robotic actuation or safety risks. While enhanced metric accuracy is
essential for building more dependable embodied agents, real-world deployment requires independent,
rigorous safety verification and failsafe mechanisms.
AI USE STATEMENT
We used LLMs to assist with manuscript editing, code development and figure preparation. Separately,
Qwen3.8-Max was employed to generate reasoning traces and target responses for spatial fine-tuning
(Appendix B.6). All AI-assisted outputs, code, and synthesized data were manually verified by the
authors, who take full responsibility for all methods, results, and claims presented in this work.

observations from multiple viewpoints, since no single view provides the complete task evidence.

all scores are computed analytically from these annotations. For each task, we describe its inputs,
reference frame, answer space, and construction; for reconstruction and navigation, we also give the
evaluation protocol. Table 5 reports task-level domains, counts, and input views, and Section A.5
gives complete examples with visual inputs, prompts, and reference answers.

A.1
GEOMETRIC PERCEPTION
Pixel correspondence.
The input contains two views and a query marker in the first. The second
view contains five to eight colored candidate markers, and the model selects the marker corresponding
to the same physical surface point. Candidate locations are sampled around the projected corre-
spondence so that image proximity alone does not identify the answer. Items are scored by exact
multiple-choice accuracy. The data cover indoor scenes from ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017), outdoor
scenes from Sekai (Li et al., 2025c), and robot observations from AgiBot World (AgiBot-World-
Contributors et al., 2025), BridgeData V2 (Walke et al., 2023), RoboMIND (Wu et al., 2025b),
Galaxea Open-World (Jiang et al., 2025), RDT-1B-associated recordings (Liu et al., 2025b), and
in-house data. Robot examples pair distinct viewpoints from head-mounted, wrist-mounted, and
external third-person cameras, according to the cameras available in each source. Existing correspon-
dence annotations are used where available. For robot observations, we detect target regions and use
MASt3R (Leroy et al., 2024) to obtain cross-view matches and 3D point maps, retaining candidate
matches whose associated 3D points agree spatially.
Camera pose.
Two views are presented without intrinsics or field of view. Translation is quantized
into eight egocentric directions relative to the first camera; rotation is represented by its sense and mag-
nitude. The reported score is the mean of translation and rotation accuracy. This discrete formulation

avoids conflating geometric understanding with unstable free-form numeric formatting, and querying
translation as a direction accommodates sources with poses defined only up to scale. View pairs and
their relative camera transformations come from ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017), ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth
et al., 2023), ETH3D (Sch”ops et al., 2017), and Sekai (Li et al., 2025c).
Depth ordering and metric depth.
Both tasks use ScanNet scenes with EmbodiedScan annota-
tions (Dai et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2024a) and CA-1M (Lazarow et al., 2025). Target objects are
selected across views, and their annotated 3D centres are transformed into a designated reference
camera frame; their coordinates along its viewing axis define the reference depths. In depth ordering,
four objects are designated by boxes across two views, and the model orders their centres by depth
along the second camera’s viewing axis. The output is one of eight candidate permutations and is
scored by exact accuracy; every adjacent pair in the sorted sequence differs by at least 0.3 m to avoid
ambiguous orderings. In metric depth, the model receives two views with four named, boxed objects
and predicts each object’s depth in metres along a designated reference camera axis, as a JSON
mapping from object identifiers to real-valued depths. At least one object is marked in the other view,
requiring its depth to be expressed in the reference camera frame. We report the mean accuracy at
relative-error thresholds {5, 10, 20, 40}%; the ordering-specific separation criterion is not imposed.
A.2
SPATIAL RELATIONS
Spatial relation tasks draw on object-level geometry from ScanNet images (Dai et al., 2017) with
EmbodiedScan oriented-box annotations (Wang et al., 2024a), and from CA-1M (Lazarow et al.,
2025). Scene records associate object centres, orientations, instance identities, and visibility across
views, and each query distributes the required object evidence across views.
Camera-anchored relation.
Two objects are selected from different views so that neither view
contains both. The model adopts the first camera’s orientation and classifies the direction of one
object relative to the other into eight horizontal sectors; the reference answer maps their relative
displacement in that frame to a sector. The reference-frame requirement distinguishes cross-view
perspective taking from a comparison of image-plane positions.
Object-anchored relation.
The reference frame is defined by an anchor object’s own front direction
rather than by a camera. The position of a target observed in another view is transformed into this
frame to determine its direction relative to the anchor, using the same eight-direction output space as
the camera-anchored task. Anchors are restricted to ScanNet categories with a meaningful front, and
candidate examples are manually checked for orientation ambiguity.
Distance ranking.
Each item specifies three object pairs whose members may occur in different
views. The model ranks the pairs by the Euclidean 3D distance between their centres, and the six
possible permutations form the answer space. This task measures inter-object metric structure and
differs from depth ordering, which is defined along a camera axis.
Object correspondence (OC).
This task uses same-category, size-similar instances that cannot be
reliably distinguished by their names; instance identities and per-view visibility determine the answer.
In the matching variant, the first image marks a target with a red box and the query view marks
candidates with distinct colors; the model selects the same instance or reports that it is not visible. In
the missing-instance variant, context views assign colors to several instances and the model identifies
which one is absent from the query view. The two variants are micro-averaged into one score.
Nearest object.
A reference object and four candidates are distributed across three views. The
model selects the candidate whose annotated 3D centre is closest to the reference, scored by four-way
accuracy. Construction records the true distance margin and screens simple image-space cues such as
box area and image centre.

Scorer controls.
Controls built from the ground truth (Table 6) confirm that each score responds to
its intended property: random rescaling leaves Structure at 100.0 but lowers Metric to 76.9, depth
flattening lowers both, and shuffled assignments reduce both to near their floors. A fully corrupted
prediction, with shuffled assignments and a random scale, scores lower on Structure (8.9) than on
Metric (15.2), so the Structure–Metric gap does not arise from Metric having a lower floor.
Sensitivity to scoring choices.
Model rankings remain stable under alternative component weights
and scale tolerances, as shown in Table 7. All variants use the same 840-instance scores, retain failed
outputs as zero, and weight the three domains equally. Removing the scale term changes the headline
ranking most: position and overlap already allow a fitted scale, so removing its penalty also removes
the requirement for physical calibration.
As a completeness check, we multiply each instance’s Structure and Metric by the fraction of
queried objects matched, retaining the same domain aggregation. Rankings on both scores and their
reconstruction average remain unchanged; the average decreases by at most 0.17 percentage points,
and Structure exceeds Metric in every model–domain combination.

Collision checking.
The avoidability-aware check uses annotated ground-plane obstacle footprints.
At a sampled path point inside an obstacle, a shallow graze is tolerated if free floor exists within 0.2
metres on either side, perpendicular to the direction of travel. A crossing fails when neither side
offers such an escape. Room-boundary crossings are always failures, and the target object is excluded
from collision checking. This tolerance accounts for small lateral errors; it is not a requirement for a
0.2-metre empty buffer around the path. Any path satisfying both arrival and collision conditions is
accepted, without requiring agreement with a particular reference traversal. Unparseable paths score
zero for all models, without model-specific parser relaxation.

B.5
NAVIGATION MAP INJECTION
The study in Section 4.3 evaluates GPT-5.6-Sol, GPT-6-Astra, Qwen3.8-Max, and Gemini-3.8-Flash
on all 346 episodes, each model serving as its own planner. Maps use the reconstruction task’s object-
centric parameterization: IDs, categories, centres, dimensions, and yaw measured counterclockwise
from +x to the width axis. In the self-map condition, the model first selects navigation-relevant
objects and predicts their boxes from the episode observations, then plans from those boxes; GT
boxes come from EmbodiedScan (Wang et al., 2024a) annotations, with synthetic room-boundary
boxes of nominal height 2.5 m. The GT map leaves the model to associate the visual target with
a map entry, whereas GT + target ID identifies that entry. Paths are evaluated without endpoint
rescaling, qt = bpt, so the supplied metric geometry must support planning directly; for GPT-5.6-Sol,
image-only success is 21.7% under the scale-normalized leaderboard protocol and 15.6% here.
Table 12 reports success and its components by episode tier. GT maps improve success over self-
generated maps for all four models. Self-generated maps increase success for GPT-6-Astra, Qwen3.8-
Max, and Gemini-3.8-Flash, while GPT-5.6-Sol remains unchanged. Explicit target identification
raises success for GPT-5.6-Sol and Qwen3.8-Max, changes GPT-6-Astra little, and lowers success
for Gemini-3.8-Flash.
The outcome components show how the maps help. With images alone, failures stem mainly from
collisions: paths reach the target in 31–62% of episodes but are collision-free in only 16–29%. GT
maps raise both components above 85% for three of the four models, whereas self-generated maps
leave GPT-5.6-Sol’s success unchanged at 15.6%. Target identification lifts reach to 99–100% for all
models, so the remaining failures are collisions, which explains the slight drop for Gemini-3.8-Flash.
Detour episodes (Tier 2) are not harder than human-traversal episodes once GT maps are supplied.

B.6
SPATIAL FINE-TUNING SETTINGS
We fine-tune Qwen3.6-35B-A3B separately on 10k, 20k, and 50k examples spanning Geometric
Perception, Spatial Relations, and scene reconstruction, without general training data. Reasoning
traces and answers are generated by Qwen3.8-Max. Multiple-choice examples are selected by answer
correctness, while reconstruction examples are selected by ranking their reconstruction scores. Each
run starts from the same checkpoint and trains for one epoch with a global batch size of 512, a
learning rate of 7 × 10−6 with cosine decay; the three runs share the same data recipe and differ
only in the number of examples. We follow source-level training/evaluation splits. All variants share
decoding settings (temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95) and a common local inference pipeline.
Spatial evaluations include SITE-Bench and SITE-Bench-Video (Wang et al., 2025), BLINK (Fu et al.,
2024), ViewSpatial-Bench (Li et al., 2025a), ERQA (Gemini Robotics Team, 2025), OmniSpatial (Jia
et al., 2026), 3DSRBench (Ma et al., 2025), MMSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2026a), EmbSpatialBench (Du
et al., 2024), and RefSpatialBench (Zhou et al., 2025).
General evaluations cover the MMMU validation split (Yue et al., 2024), MMBench-CN v1.1 (Liu
et al., 2024), VisualPuzzles with direct answers (Song et al., 2025), BabyVision (Chen et al., 2026a),
the CharXiv reasoning task (Wang et al., 2024b), AI2D (Kembhavi et al., 2016), RealWorldQA (xAI,

Longer routes.
Two longer traversals expose complementary errors (Figure 12). In the lounge,
Qwen3.8-Max reaches the stool without collision, while the other models miss the target and intersect
obstacles. This reverses the outcome of the shorter counter case, illustrating that reliable target
grounding remains scene-dependent. In the conference room, GPT-6-Astra and Claude-Opus-5
follow the aisle around the central table. GPT-5.6-Sol reaches the blackboard’s vicinity but crosses
the furniture, whereas Qwen3.8-Max both crosses the furniture and misses the goal. The latter
example shows why arrival and collision avoidance must be assessed together: a plausible endpoint
can conceal an infeasible route. Across the longer traversals, models must preserve both the target
location and the obstacle layout over the full path.
