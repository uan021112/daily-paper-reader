Abstract — General-purpose mobile manipulation requires a combination of capable robot hardware, scalable demonstration collection, expressive whole-body control, and effective policy learning. Yet these components are often developed indepen-dently, creating mismatches that limit the capabilities of the complete system. We present Pixel, a co-designed full-stack system for learning general-purpose mobile manipulation. Pixel combines a low-cost wheeled humanoid with human-aligned kinematics and compliant grippers, multiple teleoperation and robot-free human data-collection interfaces, a unified whole-body controller, and a common policy learning pipeline across human and robot demonstrations. A hierarchical learning stack further enables language-conditioned visuomotor control and long-horizon task execution. We evaluate the system across hardware, data collection, and policy learning, and demon-strate mobile-manipulation behaviors requiring dexterity, active perception, navigation, and whole-body coordination. We hope that Pixel provides an integrated and extensible foundation for studying how robot hardware, data, control, and learning can be designed together for robot learning. More results at

I. INTRODUCTION Imagine asking a robot to tidy your bedroom. It might need to collect clothes from the floor, place objects into cabinets at different heights, or operate a vacuum. The robot might also need to understand your instructions, determine where objects belong, plan a sequence of behaviors, and adapt when you rearrange the furniture. Realizing this vision requires integration across a deep robot-learning stack. The hardware must provide the mo-bility, workspace, strength, compliance, and sensing needed to interact safely in human environments. Its morphology determines where it can reach and how effectively it can leverage different data sources, while its end effectors deter-mine which objects and tools it can operate. Broad capability also requires large, diverse datasets and the interfaces that make such data practical to collect. Yet demonstrations are useful only if the robot’s controllers can translate them into safe, physically realizable motions and learning algorithms can extract behaviors that generalize to new situations. Maximizing system-level capability therefore requires co-design across these layers, such that no individual component becomes the bottleneck on the performance of the complete system. Thus far, most robotics work focuses on individual parts of this stack. Open-source hardware platforms have made capable robots and end effectors increasingly accessible, but commonly leave scalable data collection, policy training, and deployment to downstream integration [1], [2], [3]. Con-versely, sensorized human interfaces enable demonstrations to be collected at scale without a physical robot, but transfer arXiv:2611.03648v1 [cs.RO] 2 Nov 2026 to a target platform remains constrained by its morphology, sensing, end effectors, and controllers [4], [5], [6]. Other systems connect hardware, teleoperation, and policy training to achieve impressive learned manipulation [7], [8], but use comparatively simple embodiments and rely primarily on demonstrations collected through teleoperation. What remains underexplored is a mobile manipulation system in which the embodiment, data interface, controller, action representation, and learning stack are designed jointly from the start. In this work, we present Pixel , a co-designed, open-source mobile robot and learning stack that enables broadly capable mobile manipulation. At the center of the system is a wheeled humanoid robot costing under $15,000. Its human-proportioned 17-DoF upper body is designed to align with human motion, while a holonomic mobile base, vertical lift, and articulating torso provide wide workspace coverage. Wide-aperture, high-force robot grippers are paired with lightweight, comfortable sensorized human-worn grippers, allowing demonstrations to be collected in the wild while retaining high action fidelity. This co-design extends from the hardware to the data-collection interfaces, control, and learning. Leader-arm tele-operation, VR teleoperation, and sensorized human demon-strations all produce task-space targets that are mapped to the robot through a shared whole-body controller that coordinates motion while accounting for joint limits and self-collision avoidance. We also introduce a common relative-base action space which represents motion consistently across human and robot demonstrations, enabling flexible policy training across data sources. We systematically ablate design choices across hard-ware, data-collection interfaces, and model training. Policies trained from sensorized human demonstrations achieve an average task progress of 82.0% across three challenging mobile-manipulation tasks, while policies trained from robot-teleoperated demonstrations achieve 78.7% across three ad-ditional tasks. By co-designing the robot and learning stack, Pixel sup-ports rapid iteration across embodiment, data, learning, and deployment within a single extensible platform. II. RELATED WORK 

A. Open-Source Robot Platforms 

Open-source robot platforms accelerate progress by low-ering entry costs and enabling researchers to repair, extend, and iterate on others’ designs. Yet most prior work addresses only one layer of the robot-learning stack, whether hardware components such as end effectors [1], [2], [9], [10], [11], demonstration interfaces for data collection [12], [4], [13], [14], [15], [16], or data and training infrastructure [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]. These projects broaden access, but building a complete system still requires integrating indepen-dently designed hardware, sensing, control, data collection, and learning infrastructure, often developed with different objectives and assumptions. Several platforms integrate multiple layers of the stack but retain important limitations. Some platforms offer more complete hardware systems but lack the morphology required for general-purpose mobile manipulation [22], [23], [24], [25]. Other open platforms demonstrate impressive physical capabilities but either lack policy-training and deployment infrastructure [3] or emphasize locomotion over capable manipulation [26], [27]. Systems that combine hardware, data collection, and policy training often rely heavily on teleoperation, limiting the scalability of data collection [7], [8], [28], [29]. Pixel aims to provide a complete, scalable system by co-designing all parts as one extensible stack. 

B. UMI Systems & Human Data Collection 

Human data-collection interfaces have grown rapidly in popularity because they enable data to be gathered easily and at scale without a robot. As a more scalable alternative to robot teleoperation, they have seen widespread adoption in both research and industry [30], [31]. Sensorized human data collection, however, introduces an embodiment gap: human actions must map to executable robot actions. Robot-compatible tools minimize this gap through a matching interface, ranging from early gripper-in-hand devices [32], [33], [34] to the UMI family [4], [35], [5], [14]. Other methods trade direct alignment for more natural collection: DexUMI constrains the hand with an exoskeleton [36]; DexCap and DexWild use wearable gloves with retargeting [37], [6]; and EgoMimic captures bare-hand egocentric video [38]. These designs balance natural interaction against action fidelity. While most prior systems capture primarily hand motion and target tabletop manipulation, recent work has begun using human demonstrations to learn richer behaviors involv-ing active perception, whole-body coordination, and mobile manipulation. ActiveUMI and EgoMI jointly capture head and hand motion to enable learning of active-perception be-haviors [5], [39]. For mobile manipulation, EMMA co-trains on egocentric full-body human demonstrations and static robot data, while HoMMI learns bimanual navigation and manipulation from head-and-gripper UMI trajectories [40], [41]. HuMI and HALOMI further extend human data collec-tion to commercial humanoids by incorporating lower-body motion [42], [43]. However, the co-design of these systems is generally localized to selected components or implemented on an existing robot embodiment. Pixel jointly optimizes the complete system for seamless transfer from human demonstrations to whole-body robot skills.

Compared with tabletop manipulation, mobile manipula-tion can introduce additional challenges for behavior cloning, including tighter coupling between perception and action, compounding errors over longer horizons, and the difficulty of deploying learned policies on complex robotic systems. In the simplest setting, when demonstrations directly contain robot actions, policies can learn coordinated whole-body motion end to end [8], [22], [45]. However, this approach requires collecting demonstrations on the complete robot and assumes that teleoperating the robot through the desired motions is feasible, which can itself be challenging. Direct imitation of robot actions, however, is not always feasible. On legged robots, small joint-prediction errors can violate balance or contact constraints, while human demon-strations do not provide robot joint states. Many systems therefore predict task-space motion and delegate joint coor-dination to retargeting or whole-body control. Legged plat-forms pass learned end-effector trajectories to controllers that maintain balance [46], [47], while wheeled and humanoid systems map keyposes or human head, hand, pelvis, and foot motion to feasible whole-body actions [48], [40], [41], [42], [43]. Long-horizon tasks may also require structure beyond low-level action prediction. Hi Robot decomposes instructions into atomic language commands executed by a low-level policy [49]; CoT-VLA predicts visual subgoals before gen-erating action chunks [50]; and π0.7 uses language, strategy metadata, and visual subgoals to steer and compose behaviors across tasks [51]. III. SYSTEM DESIGN We present Pixel , a low-cost wheeled humanoid and integrated robot-learning stack for general-purpose mobile manipulation. Rather than developing the robot embodiment, data-collection interfaces, and learning infrastructure inde-pendently, we design them jointly so that each component complements the others and the robot can realize its full capability as one complete system. Figure 2 summarizes the hardware co-design. In general, our design is guided by three principles: 

G1. Cost-effective, reproducible, and safe. The system should maximize manipulation capability while mini-mizing hardware cost and complexity, remain easy to reproduce, maintain, and extend, and be safe to operate in human environments. 

G2. Scalable and high-fidelity data collection. The robot and data-collection interfaces should support scalable, high-fidelity data collection and facilitate learning from multiple data sources, including teleoperated and hu-man demonstrations. 

G3. Broad everyday mobile-manipulation capability. 

The robot embodiment and learning stack should sup-port a broad range of everyday mobile manipula-tion behaviors, including dexterous, long-horizon, and whole-body tasks. In the following sections, we describe the three co-designed layers of the system: the robot hardware, the data-collection interfaces, and the policy-learning stack. 

A. Robot Hardware System 

We design Pixel to support a wide range of everyday mobile-manipulation tasks in the open world, while making deliberate tradeoffs to balance our design goals G1–G3. At the architectural level, we choose to retain the task-relevant advantages of a humanoid morphology while minimizing the mechanical and control complexity of a full humanoid robot. A human-aligned upper body provides a human-like bimanual workspace for interacting with objects, tools, and environments designed for people, while also facilitating the transfer of human demonstrations in support of G2. At the same time, a humanoid system with bipedal locomotion and multi-fingered hands would introduce substantial cost, control complexity, and maintenance concerns. Consistent with G1, we therefore replace legs with a holonomic mo-bile base and passively stable vertical lift, and use simple custom grippers in place of dexterous hands. These choices trade some terrain versatility, lower-body articulation, and in-hand dexterity for simplicity, while preserving most of the mobility, vertical reach, and grasping capability needed to support the diverse whole-body and dexterous behaviors targeted by G3. The complete hardware platform, shown in Figure 3, can be assembled for under $15,000. 

Holonomic Mobile Base. Pixel is built on a cus-tom holonomic mobile base. Our base has a footprint of 

45 .5 cm × 53 .5 cm , compared with 50 .0 cm × 54 .0 cm for TidyBot++ [22]. This compact footprint allows the robot to navigate cluttered indoor environments and pass through standard doorways. Holonomic mobility enables independent planar translation and rotation, improving maneuverability in confined indoor environments [22], providing a simple action space for whole-body teleoperation and policy learning, and facilitating the transfer of natural human whole-body motion to the robot in support of G2. The chassis is constructed from aluminum extrusions, forming a rigid superstructure that houses the batteries, power electronics, drivetrain, lift actuator, and networking hardware. The drivetrain consists of four MK4n swerve modules with Colson wheels from Swerve Drive Special-ties [52]. Motivated by our goal of reproducibility in G1, we exclusively use off-the-shelf parts. Unlike the offset powered-caster design used by Tidy-Bot++, we use zero-offset swerve modules, whose steering axes approximately intersect the wheel contact patches, thus decoupling the steering from chassis motion. This decoupling of steering and chassis translation is particularly important for mobile manipulation, where small steering inputs to the base can cause jerky motions in the end-effector pose and compromise more precise tasks. The resulting drivetrain does not require additional CNC-machined parts [22] and provides simpler kinematic control. Base control details are available in Appendix A. 

Vertical Lift. A large vertical workspace is essential for operating in human environments, where tasks may require reaching from the floor to high shelves. An anatomically accurate, multi-joint lower body would more closely match human kinematics and therefore better satisfy G2, but would substantially increase the robot’s mechanical and control complexity and cost. Given the mobility of the base, such additional articulation would provide only limited additional task-relevant workspace compared with a simpler vertical degree of freedom as shown in Figure 9. We therefore design a telescopic lift that provides 430 mm of travel, a maximum speed of 75 mm /s, and a rated payload of 40 kg . This design preserves the task-relevant vertical motion needed to transfer human demonstrations while substantially expanding the workspace of both arms with a simple and stable mechanism, balancing G2 and G1. The lift repurposes a commercial electric table column with a custom drivetrain. A Kraken X60 motor drives the column’s lead screw through a chain transmission, using a 10-tooth motor sprocket and a 36-tooth screw sprocket for a 

3.6:1 reduction. Consistent with the safety objective in G1, the lead-screw mechanism passively holds its position when unpowered, preventing the lift and its payload from falling in the event of power loss. Represented as a single prismatic degree of freedom, the lift also integrates naturally into the whole-body controller described in Section III-B. Lift sizing and control details are provided in Appendix B. 

Torso, Head, and Arms. To facilitate the transfer of human demonstrations to robot motion, Pixel uses a human-proportioned, 17-DoF upper body comprising a torso-pitch joint, two 7-DoF arms, and a 2-DoF head. Compared with mobile manipulators whose arms and cameras are rigidly mounted to the lift structure [3], [8], [53], these additional degrees of freedom allow the robot to easily reposition both its manipulation workspace and sensing orientation. In par-ticular, torso articulation extends the forward and downward reach of both arms, while the actuated head enables the robot to maintain task-relevant regions within view during manipulation. These degrees of freedom expand the robot’s whole-body manipulation capability (G3) while allowing it to reproduce torso pitching and gaze behaviors present in human demonstrations (G2). To avoid unnecessary mechanical and manufacturing com-plexity, the upper body is built around a simple, rigid torso structure consistent with G1. The torso is assembled from waterjet-cut, 6.35 mm -thick 6061-T6 aluminum plates and is cantilevered from a single torso-pitch joint driven by a MyActuator X10-100 actuator with a rated torque of 

50 N m . Coordinating this joint with the vertical lift and arms allows the robot to reach low or recessed objects while maintaining a stable base configuration. To reduce the kinematic mismatch between human and robot motion (G2), we use two AgileX NERO arms, each with 7 DoFs, a rated payload of 3 kg , and human-like kinematics. The redundant arm degree of freedom provides additional flexibility for satisfying end-effector targets while avoiding joint limits, self-collisions, and unfavorable configurations in confined bimanual workspaces. The head is actuated in yaw and pitch by two DAMIAO DM-J4310-2EC actuators, allowing the onboard cameras to be oriented independently of the torso. The gripper follows a finger-aligned design resembling the natural pinch motion of the thumb and index finger, making it intuitive for human demonstration while remaining capable of grasping a diverse range of objects during robot deployment [14]. The human-worn interface preserves the task-facing finger geometry and wrist-camera placement of the robot gripper, reducing observation and action mismatch between demonstration and deployment [4]. On the robot side, each gripper is driven by a DAMIAO DM-J4310-2EC actuator through a 14-tooth pinion and two 36-tooth finger gears, yielding a 2.57:1 reduction. At a motor torque of 3 N m , the transmission provides a nominal com-bined torque of 7.71 N m across the finger pivots and a mea-sured closing force exceeding 90 N . The pincer mechanism provides a maximum aperture of 240 mm while maintaining a compact and mechanically simple construction. Each finger consists of a rigid 3D-printed PETG structure, a compliant 95A-TPU membrane, and a Shore-A 30 Dragon Skin silicone contact surface. The distal portion is narrow and comparatively rigid, enabling precise grasps of small and thin objects. The wider, compliant proximal region conforms to larger objects and handles, increasing contact area and resistance to slip. Separating the rigid load-bearing structure from the compliant membrane allows the finger geometry and surface compliance to be tuned independently. Because deformation of the compliant membrane is visible in the wrist-camera image, it also provides visual cues about contact state and grasp force without requiring dedicated tactile sensors, providing additional information to the policy in support of G3. The policy commands the gripper using continuous aper-ture targets, which are tracked using an impedance con-troller with variable stiffness, damping, and torque limits. Lower gains provide compliant contact for delicate objects, while higher gains enable secure power grasps. Each robot-mounted gripper weighs 680 g and can be assembled for under $200. 

Sensing, Compute, and Electrical Architecture. Mobile manipulation in the open world requires tightly integrated sensing, computation, communication, and power delivery. Pixel therefore uses a fully onboard architecture designed to support real-time control and perception (G3) while remain-ing modular, low-cost, and easy to maintain (G1). The robot’s sensing suite consists of three Arducam B0385 global-shutter RGB cameras (one on each wrist and one on the head), a head-mounted Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera, and a base-mounted Livox Mid-360 LiDAR. The RGB cameras stream camera-native MJPEG at 640 × 480 

and 100 FPS, with a wide field of view of approximately 

160 ◦ diagonally. Global shutters reduce motion artifacts, and the onboard JPEG compression lowers the processing and storage overhead of multi-view data collection. The wrist cameras capture close-range contact information, and the head camera supplies broader scene context. The D435 provides aligned RGB and depth, which enables 3D recon-struction of the scene and the training of policies based on depth images or point clouds. The LiDAR and its integrated IMU can be used for SLAM or navigation (G3). Computation is divided between a Raspberry Pi 4 and an onboard GPU laptop. The Raspberry Pi controls the drive-train and lift, whereas the laptop handles whole-body control, data collection, and policy inference. The two computers communicate using ROS 2 over a private wired Ethernet network, with a Wi-Fi access point supporting wireless VR and leader-arm teleoperation. Motor communication is divided across four CAN networks: one for the drivetrain and lift, one for the torso and head, and one for each arm and gripper. To accommodate devices with different power require-ments, Pixel uses two complementary onboard power sources. A 48 V , 25 Ah lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery supplies the robot’s DC electronics, while a 288 Wh portable AC power source rated at 300 W powers the GPU laptop, networking equipment, and other peripherals through their standard power adapters. This separation allows compute, sensing, and networking hardware to be added or replaced without redesigning the robot’s DC power distribution, sup-porting the extensibility objectives of G1. LFP was cho-sen for its long cycle life and favorable thermal stability during frequent operation, making it a safe and reliable choice consistent with G1. Together, the two sources provide approximately 2.5–3 hours of untethered operation, with runtime primarily limited by the smaller AC source and the GPU laptop’s power consumption. 

B. Whole-Body Control and Data Collection Interfaces 

Everyday tasks such as retrieving objects from the floor, loading a dishwasher, and opening a drawer require coordi-nated motion of the whole body. Supporting these behaviors on a 23 -DoF platform presents challenges for both control and data collection. For many tasks, teleoperators must command the entire robot to produce coherent whole-body motions. Sensorized human demonstrations also need to be mapped effectively to robot motions without losing task-relevant behavior. Following our goal to facilitate learning from multiple sources in G2, we co-design a unified whole-body controller used by both teleoperation and task-space target tracking, custom teleoperation hardware and interfaces, and a sen-sorized human data collection system. These components enable policies to efficiently learn coordinated and expressive whole-body behaviors on Pixel. 

Whole-Body Controller. We use a shared whole-body controller to maintain consistent motion characteristics across demonstrations collected with different devices, thereby reducing the mismatch between data collection and learned policy execution. We use geometric fabrics [54], [55] to generate coordi-nated whole-body motion from task-space targets. Fabrics pair each local acceleration policy with a metric that de-termines its relative influence and preferred directions of motion. A key benefit is their state-dependent metrics , which allow motion priorities to adapt to the robot configuration, velocity, and targets as reachability or proximity to joint limits changes. The robot’s kinematics map the local policies into a common configuration space, where they combine into a single acceleration field. COWBOY [56] implements fabrics-based whole-body control for a mobile robot with one manipulation arm and an actuated camera arm. We extend fabrics-based whole-body control to a wheeled humanoid with two manipulation arms, an articulated torso, a vertical lift, and an actuated head. 

Hand and head pose tracking. Pose attractors draw the two grippers and the head toward desired positions and orientations, with damping near each target. These tasks act through the robot’s full-body kinematics, so tracking a hand or head pose can coordinate base motion, lift height, and torso posture. Head targets specify both viewpoint and viewing direction: VR teleoperation derives the orientation from a hand-based look-at point (Equation 1), while learned policies predict head poses directly. Gripper apertures are controlled separately. 

Reach-dependent base coordination. A base-translation term favors arm motion when the hand targets are nearby and allows greater base participation as they move beyond reach. We approximate each arm’s workspace by a sphere centered at its shoulder. When all active hand targets lie within these estimated workspaces, a large metric makes base acceleration more costly, encouraging the arms to accommodate the motion. As either target moves beyond its workspace radius, the metric decreases smoothly, allowing the pose attrac-tors to recruit more base translation. Damping moderates base motion throughout this transition. This term lets the controller move continuously between local manipulation and coordinated base–arm reaching without a separate base-position target. 

Posture and motion allocation. Posture attractors favor a nominal body configuration while the hand and head targets are tracked. Leader-arm teleoperation can supply arm and torso posture references to preserve the operator’s intended configuration. A separate motion-allocation metric assigns greater cost to lift and torso acceleration, biasing motion toward the arms while retaining whole-body flexibility. For base rotation, a yaw attractor follows a heading derived from the hand targets and desired viewing direction; a heading-dependent metric reduces unnecessary rotation near this heading. Together, these terms express weighted preferences for how the robot uses its redundant degrees of freedom. 

Avoidance and motion generation. Joint-limit repulsion steers joints away from their boundaries, while repulsion be-tween link-attached spheres discourages self-collision. These behaviors contribute soft motion preferences to the com-bined controller. The resulting acceleration field is damped, bounded, and integrated at 200 Hz into joint-position and base-velocity references tracked by the low-level controllers. Appendix C presents the mathematical formulation and con-troller parameters.

To accommodate diverse users and tasks, we design two forms of teleoperation devices for the robot: a leader arm system for dexterous tabletop tasks and a VR teleoperation system for whole-body manipulation skills. Figure 4 presents our two teleoperation approaches.

We design a kinematically matched, 65%-scale replica of the robot’s torso and two 7-DoF arms, with integrated triggers for continuous gripper control. We omit the robot’s actuated head from the replica and instead delegate gaze control to the whole-body controller, reducing the size and complexity of the interface without sacrificing gaze control. The joints are actuated and support features such as gravity and friction compensation, virtual joint limits, configurable null-space posture regulation, and bilateral force feedback [15], [16]. Gravity compensation reduces operator effort by counteracting the weight of the device, null-space regulation improves ergonomics by biasing each leader arm toward a comfortable configuration, and force feedback im-proves contact awareness by reflecting the robot’s interaction forces back to the operator. Buttons integrated into the han-dles raise and lower the lift, while thumb joysticks command planar base translation and rotation. A central design choice is that the measured leader-arm angles are not sent to the robot as joint-position targets. Instead, we use them in two complementary ways: we apply the leader configuration to the robot’s kinematic model to compute gripper poses that serve as primary task-space attractors, and we provide the joint angles as a secondary nominal posture objective. The task-space attractors allow the whole-body controller to coordinate the arms with the torso, lift, and base while accounting for joint limits and self-collision avoidance. Because the gripper poses do not fully specify the configuration of the robot’s redundant upper body, the nominal posture objective additionally biases the robot toward the operator’s intended torso and elbow configuration. These objectives preserve both the task-level motion and the configuration-level intent of the operator to enable more expressive teleoperation for whole-body behaviors (G3). Hardware and controller details are provided in Appendix D. 

VR Teleoperation System. An untethered interface that al-lows the operator to move freely can make many whole-body tasks easier and more intuitive to teleoperate than with leader-arm systems. For example, the operator may need to reposition themselves for a better view of the scene or walk alongside the robot as it navigates through the environment. A VR-based interface supports this mobility while allowing the operator to continuously control the robot. We use the Meta Quest 3/3S for its relatively low cost and self-contained 6-DoF tracking, making the system easier to reproduce and supporting G1. The headset is worn around the operator’s neck to provide body reference tracking, while the handheld controllers provide hand poses, continuous gripper commands, and auxiliary controls. Because the system is in-tended for local rather than remote teleoperation, the operator observes the robot and workspace directly instead of receiv-ing streamed robot-camera images through the headset. This preserves the wide field of vision of human eyes and avoids latency-sensitive visual feedback, which can cause dizziness and disorientation during immersive teleoperation [57], [58]. A custom OpenXR application based on [59] built in Unity transmits the headset and controller poses and inputs over the robot’s private Wi-Fi network through a ROS-TCP bridge, and the robot-side interface updates the whole-body targets at 60 Hz . Sensorized Human Data Collection 

To enable more scalable data collection than teleoperation, we co-design a wearable human data collection system that enables Pixel to learn whole-body skills directly from humans performing tasks, without the need for a robot. The complete system uses off-the-shelf sensors and primarily 3D-printed parts, costs under $1,000, and can operate untethered for approximately 2.5–3 hours, consistent with the cost and reproducibility objectives of G1. The complete system is shown in Figure 4. 

Hardware Co-Design. Central to our system is a co-designed gripper and VR headset that maintains strong alignment between the robot and the human (G2). Each wearable gripper preserves the task-relevant finger geometry and wrist-camera placement of the robot gripper, while minimally modifying the hand-facing structure for better human ergonomics. Users place their thumb opposite the index and middle fingers, forming a natural C-shaped pinch whose aperture directly actuates the gripper. Silicone pads, elastic straps, and a support ridge distribute loads across the hand, enabling the manipulation of heavier everyday objects. A weak torsional spring assists reopening when the user relaxes their grasp, reducing the finger-extension effort required between grasps. Because the wearable interface is human-powered, it does not require the same metal transmission used on the robot. We instead integrate each gripper finger and its gear into a single 3D-printed PETG part and use press-fit 3D-printed shafts, reducing part count, cost, and mass and simplifying fabrication and replacement in support of G1. We use VR controllers for wrist tracking and attach them through their existing battery-cover interface, allowing installation and removal without tools. The resulting wearable gripper weighs only 386 g , substantially less than comparable devices like the original UMI device at 780 g [4], the AgileX PIKA Sense at 550 g [60], and the GenRobot DAS Gripper at 520 g [61]. Our lightweight and ergonomic interface reduces operator fatigue during repeated demonstrations, supporting sustained data collection in G2. Software & Data. The software stack for human data collection is derived from the VR teleoperation stack, with major components such as the OpenXR Unity application, ROS 2 publishers and subscribers, and data collection code almost identical. During collection, users see a high-quality passthrough view while all menus remain hidden unless requested to reduce distractions. Distinct controller-haptic patterns communicate calibration, recording start and stop, ongoing recording, and recording failures. In parallel, the touchscreen user interface reports the health and update rate of every recorded stream, allowing sensor dropouts to be identified. For each episode, we record time-stamped head and bimanual gripper poses, continuous gripper apertures, the three RGB camera streams, and the head-mounted RGB-D stream. Recording can be started and stopped directly from the controller, so an operator does not need to interact with the touchscreen. Data storage, temporal synchronization, and training sample construction are detailed in Appendix G. During lightweight post-processing, we transform the tracked head and gripper poses from the Quest convention into the task-space convention used by the robot, preserving a common action representation across human and robot demonstrations (G2). We correct transient artifacts in the pose trajectories using heuristic jump detection and interpola-tion. Separately, we process the gripper-state signals using a Hampel filter [62] for outlier rejection followed by Savitzky– Golay smoothing [63]. Finally, we temporally rescale the trajectories to 0.5× the demonstrated speed, which we found to improve tracking reliability. For extensions of this sensorized human data system to more complex, dexterous tasks, we refer readers to our companion paper, Pixel-UMI [64]. 

C. Policy Learning 

Mobile manipulation presents a particularly challenging policy-learning problem because a single task may combine coarse whole-body motion, such as navigation, with precise manipulation, such as grasping, insertion, or alignment. These behaviors unfold over horizons substantially longer than those of typical tabletop tasks, increasing the effect of control error accumulation and making policies more likely to encounter observations that are poorly represented in the demonstration data. Moreover, open-ended tasks may require several distinct skills whose order depends on the user’s request and the current state of the environment. To realize broad everyday mobile-manipulation capabil-ity G3, we design a hierarchical system that separates task-level reasoning from continuous control. A language-conditioned low-level policy executes reusable mobile-manipulation skills, while a vision-language model (VLM) decomposes open-ended user requests into supported skills and monitors their execution. This hierarchy allows the low-level policy to specialize in closed-loop visuomotor control while the VLM handles skill composition, progress tracking, and interaction with the user, resulting in a powerful and general policy learning pipeline. Our policy architecture is shown in Figure 5.

High-Level Agent Harness. The low-level policy provides closed-loop control for individual language-conditioned skills, but it is not designed to interpret open-ended requests, reason across multiple subtasks, or track progress over long horizons. We therefore place it within a VLM-based agent harness that separates task-level reasoning from physical execution. The harness operates in two phases: planning, which constructs a user-approved sequence of sup-ported skills, and execution, which carries out this sequence while monitoring its progress. Separating these phases allows us to allocate more computation to planning and task-level reasoning before execution begins, enabling more reliable decomposition and management of long-horizon tasks, while favoring faster inference during execution monitoring in support of G3. We instantiate the high-level agent using Gemini 3.5 Flash. During planning, the VLM receives the user request, the current head-camera observation, and, when replanning, the unfinished portion of the existing plan. It then constructs an ordered plan using a predefined skill library. Each skill is specified by a semantic description, a fixed instruction for the low-level policy, and visually verifiable success criteria. Re-stricting planning to this library grounds the VLM’s decisions in the capabilities represented in the policy’s training data and allows unsupported requests to be identified explicitly. The proposed plan is presented to the user for confirmation before execution begins. After confirmation, the harness executes the plan one skill at a time. For each step, it maps the selected skill to its fixed low-level instruction, invokes the policy, and maintains the state of the overall plan. The VLM monitors execution using the skill’s success criteria and a sparse visual history spanning the rollout. We construct this history from the observation at the start of the skill, a temporally intermediate keyframe selected to capture scene changes, a recent observation, and the latest camera image. This history serves as a compact visual memory, allowing the VLM to remember previous attempts, track meaningful progress, and determine whether the current skill has been completed while preserving fast inference. If progress repeatedly stalls or an unsafe condition is detected, the harness pauses execu-tion and verbally requests assistance from the user. Users interact with the agent through speech and can confirm, pause, resume, stop, or revise the plan at any time. We use NVIDIA NeMo with the Parakeet Unified English 0.6B model for speech recognition and Piper for text-to-speech. Model configurations, prompts, and monitoring details are provided in Appendix I. When paired, the high-level agent and low-level policy allow Pixel to compose reusable skills into longer-horizon behaviors while keeping physical execution grounded in the learned policy. IV. EXPERIMENTS Having described the design of Pixel, we next evalu-ate how effectively the complete system realizes design goals G1–G3. Specifically, our experiments address the fol-lowing questions: 1) Do the co-designed hardware components provide the mechanical capability required for everyday manipulation? We evaluate how the robot’s different whole-body degrees of freedom contribute to its task performance, conduct analyses on its workspace, and further measure the gripper characteristics. 2) Do the control interfaces enable intuitive and com-patible collection of human and robot demonstra-tions? We compare usability, collection efficiency, and ability to capture coordinated whole-body motion of different data collection methods. 3) Can we enable policies to learn reliable, long-horizon whole-body behaviors from demonstra-tions? We explore different policy design choices including observation and action spaces to understand their effects on policy performance. These experiments evaluate the complete co-designed system, from its physical embodiment and data-collection interfaces to its policy-learning stack. 

A. Task Definitions 

Several experiments use a common suite of tasks, summa-rized below by data-collection source and evaluation setting. Figure 6 illustrates these tasks. Additional task images and progress-based scoring rubrics are provided in Appendices K and L, respectively. 

Sensorized Human Data Tasks. We collect sensorized human demonstrations and train policies for the following tasks: 1) Toy Cleanup (103 demonstrations). Starting from a randomized pose with the coffee table in view, the robot must approach the table while lowering its body, pick up a randomly placed toy, strafe toward a nearby basket, and deposit the toy inside. This task evaluates whole-body coordination and generalization across objects and locations. 2) Tidy Pillow (129 demonstrations). Starting from a randomized location with the couch in view, the robot must navigate around obstacles such as a desk, coffee table, and toy basket before placing the couch pillows upright against the seat back. This task evaluates long-horizon navigation and bimanual manipulation. 3) Trash Disposal (189 demonstrations). The robot begins in front of a couch with a coffee cup placed on the seat. After picking up the cup, it must back away, actively scan the room to determine whether the trash can is to its left or right, navigate to it, and dispose of the cup. This task evaluates the ability to learn active vision from human demonstrations. For Toy Cleanup and Tidy Pillow, we additionally collect 288 and 141 robot-teleoperated demonstrations, respectively, to study joint training with human and robot data. 

Robot Teleoperation Data Tasks. We collect robot-teleoperated demonstrations for the following tasks: 1) Vacuum Cleaning (133 demonstrations). Starting from a randomized location, the robot must retrieve a handheld vacuum, turn it on, navigate to a dining table, vacuum spilled pepper flakes, return the vacuum to its storage location, and turn it off. This task evaluates long-horizon mobile manipulation and interaction with household appliances. 2) Hang Jacket (89 demonstrations). The robot must locate and approach a person, wait to receive their jacket, transport it to a clothes rack, and hang it. This task evaluates navigation, human–robot interaction, and manipulation of deformable objects with varying materials. 3) Snack Retrieval (108 demonstrations). The robot must navigate to a drawer, open it, retrieve a snack, close the drawer, locate the requesting person, and hand over the snack. This task evaluates articulated-object manipulation, human interaction, and careful handling of fragile objects such as bags of chips. 

User Study Tasks. We use two tasks in the data-collection interface study: 1) Tidy Pillow. We use the same task definition given above to evaluate whole-body navigation and bimanual coordination. 2) Wine-Glass Pouring. Starting in front of a dining table, the robot must grasp one wine glass with each gripper and pour beads from the right glass into the left without spilling. This task evaluates precise grasping and coordinated bimanual manipulation.

A. Hardware Experimental Results 

Pixel’s set of degrees of freedom enables consistent performance across diverse mobile-manipulation tasks. 

As shown in Figure 10, the complete system achieved 

100% task progress on all three evaluated tasks. Averaged across tasks, progress decreased to 47 .4% when the torso was disabled, 71 .2% when the lift was disabled, and 52 .0% 

when head control was disabled. The importance of each degree of freedom depended on the task. For example, Toy Cleanup retained 100% progress without head control, suggesting that the visual encoder can tolerate the fixed camera viewpoint for some simple tasks. In contrast, Tidy Pillow progress decreased to 14 .3% without torso motion and 20 .0% without head control. The torso ex-tends the robot’s workspace when placing the pillow against the seat back, while active head control helps coordinate this whole-body motion. Trash Disposal progress similarly decreased to 36 .0% without head control. When collecting sensorized human demonstrations, humans naturally turn their heads to search for the trash can and keep it in view while approaching it. Disabling head control prevents the robot from realizing the head trajectories learned from this behavior, causing the camera viewpoint to diverge from the demonstrations and frequently preventing the robot from locating the trash can. Thus, although individual tasks may not require every degree of freedom, the complete embod-iment enables consistent reproduction of the coordinated manipulation, navigation, and active-perception behaviors learned from sensorized human demonstrations.

C. Policy Design Experimental Results 

Complementary head and wrist views enable both fine-grained manipulation and active perception. As shown in Figure 12, using all three cameras produced the highest average task progress for both data sources. For policies trained with sensorized human demonstrations, the complete observation space achieved 82 .0% average progress, com-pared with 66 .5% using only the wrist cameras and 39 .9% 

using only the head camera. The same trend held for robot-teleoperated data, for which the complete observation space achieved 78 .7% average progress, compared with 74 .0% 

and 59 .5% for the wrist-only and head-only configurations, respectively. The individual tasks illustrate why both perspectives are needed. Head-only policies achieved just 5.0% progress on Toy Cleanup and 0% on Vacuum Cleaning, consistent with the loss of close-range visual information needed to align grasps and interact with small controls such as the vacuum power button. Removing the head camera also prevents the policy from reliably reproducing active-perception behaviors present in the sensorized human demonstrations. During Trash Disposal, human demonstrators naturally turn their heads while backing away from the couch to search for and locate the trash can. Although the corresponding head motion remains part of the predicted action, a wrist-only policy does not observe the head-camera images that give this behavior its visual context. During Hang Jacket, the garment can cover both wrist cameras as it is transferred from the person to the robot. A wrist-only policy can therefore become completely visually occluded at a critical point in the task, whereas the head camera retains a view of the person, garment, and surrounding scene. Thus, the complete obser-vation space is not necessary for every individual task, but it provides the most consistent performance across behaviors requiring close-range manipulation, active visual search, and robustness to camera occlusion. 

A shared, ground-anchored action frame improves long-horizon coordination while remaining compatible with both human and robot data. On the sensorized-human tasks, our relative-base action space achieved 82 .0% 

average task progress, outperforming relative-pure at 67 .3% 

and relative-head at 25 .7% . The difference was most pro-nounced on Tidy Pillow, where relative-base achieved 95 .0% 

progress, compared with 47 .9% for relative-pure and 25 .0% 

for relative-head. Relative-pure remained effective on shorter or less tightly coordinated tasks, reaching 85 .0% on Toy Cleanup and 69 .0% on Trash Disposal. However, because the head and two end effectors are independently referenced at the beginning of each action chunk, we observed drift and loss of bimanual coordination during longer-horizon behav-iors. Relative-head provides a shared reference frame, but it couples the gripper trajectories to errors in the predicted head pose. We observed that vertical head-pose drift consequently propagated to both end effectors and frequently caused task failure. In contrast, relative-base expresses all three trajecto-ries in a common chunk-start frame while preserving ground-anchored height and orientation, preventing head-prediction errors from redefining the gripper reference frame. On the robot-teleoperation tasks, absolute joint-space actions achieved 86 .3% average progress, compared with 

78 .7% for relative-base end-effector actions. We therefore do not conclude that end-effector actions are intrinsically superior to joint-space control. Rather, we choose relative-base as it retains strong robot performance while providing a representation that is directly available from both sensorized human and robot-teleoperated demonstrations. VI. CONCLUSION & LIMITATIONS We presented Pixel, an open-source, co-designed wheeled humanoid and integrated robot-learning stack spanning hard-ware, whole-body control, data collection, and policy learn-ing. Rather than developing these layers in isolation, Pixel co-designs them around a central goal of enabling broad autonomous mobile manipulation. Our evaluations show that this full-stack approach supports dexterous, long-horizon mobile manipulation while keeping the system low-cost, reproducible, and safe to operate in human environments. Several limitations point to important directions for future work. First, Pixel’s wheeled, low-cost embodiment trades some mobility and dexterity for simplicity and safety: it cannot traverse stairs or match the range of motion and manipulation capabilities of a full humanoid with dexterous hands. Future work should systematically characterize these tradeoffs and explore how improved affordable actuators and mechanisms can expand capability without compromising reproducibility or safety. Second, the sensorized human in-terface records head and gripper trajectories but does not capture uninstrumented body motions or contacts, such as bracing a door with an elbow, and lacks tactile sensing. Richer full-body tracking, action estimation, and multimodal sensing could preserve more of the expressivity of human demonstrations. Finally, the high-level agent’s ability to compose learned skills has not been systematically evaluated. Because it operates over a predefined skill library, it cannot execute unsupported skills and offers limited compositional generalization. Expanding this repertoire, improving hierar-chical policy architectures, and evaluating them on longer-horizon tasks remain important directions. Steering changes the rolling direction but contributes no di-rect translation in the ideal model; arbitrary travel directions therefore require steering alignment. We choose this tradeoff to decouple steering from offset-induced chassis motion and retain unmodified commercial modules, simplifying both the mechanical design and the direction-and-speed control law. The resulting base is omnidirectional, with steering transients handled as follows. 

Steering alignment and drive coordination. We reverse wheel rotation and shift the desired steering angle by π when-ever doing so reduces steering travel, keeping the angular error within 90 ◦. A proportional steering controller tracks the selected angle through bounded steering-rate commands. During alignment, the signed wheel-speed target is scaled by 

max(0 , cos ei), where ei is the remaining steering error. This projects the desired velocity onto the current rolling direction and reduces unintended motion while the wheel turns. 

Low-speed hysteresis. Near rest, small velocity fluctu-ations can produce large changes in the desired steering direction. We therefore enter a hold state when the com-manded translational speed and absolute yaw rate fall below 

0.01 m /s and 0.07 rad /s, respectively. Hold is released when either reaches its larger threshold of 0.05 m /s or 0.08 rad /s.During hold, steering-rate and wheel-speed commands are zero. These separate entry and release thresholds suppress repeated switching and steering adjustments near rest. 

Base-state estimation. Measured steering angles and wheel speeds provide module rolling velocities, from which we estimate chassis velocity by solving the stacked Jacobian equations in the least-squares sense. Integrating this velocity yields wheel odometry. When available, SLAM pose mea-surements are fused with wheel-derived velocity in a planar Kalman filter to estimate the base pose.

D. Leader Arm System Details 

Hardware design. The 65%-scale leader uses 3D-printed links and 18 Dynamixel servos. Six XM430-W210-T servos provide the paired torso drive and actuate the first two joints of each arm; twelve XC330-T288-T servos actuate the remaining five joints per arm and the two gripper triggers. The torso and proximal arm joints support more downstream mass over longer lever arms, producing larger gravitational moments. We select larger motors at these joints to reduce the risk of overheating and stalling, and use two motors at the single torso pivot to share the load. Lower torque demands at the distal joints and triggers allow smaller motors, keeping the interface compact and lightweight. The powered servos operate in torque-control mode through motor-current com-mands, enabling gravity compensation and null-space posture regularization. 

Gravity compensation. We use Pinocchio’s recursive Newton–Euler algorithm with the leader’s rigid-body model, including its link masses and centers of mass. Evaluating inverse dynamics at the measured configuration q with zero joint velocities and accelerations isolates the gravitational torques g(q). The backward recursion accumulates the grav-itational forces and moments of downstream links to obtain the torque required at each joint. Joint-wise gains set the assistance level through τg = Kg g(q), and the torso support torque is divided equally between its two motors. 

Null-space posture regularization. Each arm is biased toward a comfortable nominal configuration using diagonal stiffness and damping gains Kp and Kd. The resulting posture torque is projected into its kinematic null space:

Friction compensation. When a joint’s measured speed falls below a small threshold, we apply a low-amplitude torque that alternates in sign on successive control cycles. Mapping to robot commands. At initialization, the operator aligns the leader with a reference posture before teleoperation begins. We also use the joint angles as nominal robot posture targets to preserve torso and elbow intent. Trigger positions provide continuous gripper commands, while handle buttons and joysticks control lift motion and base velocity, respectively.

H. Policy Deployment Details 

Asynchronous inference and execution. Policy inference runs in a background worker while a separate execution loop advances through queued actions at the policy data rate. Each request uses synchronized observations and the current language instruction, with the action reference frame cap-tured at launch. New requests are launched after a configured number of action steps, with one prediction evaluated at a time. During inference, the robot continues executing the previous chunk; an independent command loop supplies the current targets to the whole-body controller. If the queue is exhausted, the runtime retains a hold target until a new chunk becomes available. This separates variable inference latency from periodic command delivery. 

Real-time chunking. We use training-time action condi-tioning for real-time chunking (RTC) [71]. Let H denote the action horizon and d < H the estimated inference delay in policy steps. The next d queued actions form a committed prefix: these actions are expected to execute while the next prediction is computed. The flow sampler fixes this prefix throughout its Euler integration and generates the remaining actions conditioned on it. During training, randomly sampled prefix lengths expose the model to this conditioning, with supervision applied only to the remaining valid actions. The runtime estimates d from recent handoff delays, bounded by the delay range used in training; the initial prediction uses no prefix. When a prediction returns, the runtime discards the committed prefix and any additional steps already executed during inference before replacing the queue with the remaining actions. 

Reference-frame consistency. The local RTC implemen-tation constructs its prefix from queued world-frame targets and re-expresses them in the new chunk’s relative-base frame. Let Bt be the gravity-aligned pseudo-base frame derived from the most recent commanded head pose at request step t, initialized from the measured pose when no prior target exists. For a queued target of the head or hand

Local and remote inference. The reported policy eval-uations use local inference on the robot’s GPU laptop, typically using an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. Alternatively, a remote server executes the policy, receiving preprocessed camera arrays and the language instruction over a WebSocket connection and returning a predicted action chunk. J. Low-Level Policy Details 

Visual representation. The policy receives synchronized head and wrist images without robot-specific proprioception. Each view is encoded by a pretrained DINOv3 ViT-B/16 backbone [65]. We process head images at 384 × 384 and retain all tokens to preserve distant objects and spatial relationships across the scene. Wrist images are processed at 224 × 224 , retaining only the class and register tokens. These close-range views emphasize interactions around the grippers, allowing a smaller cross-attention token budget. Learned camera embeddings identify each view, and the con-catenated visual tokens are layer-normalized before language conditioning. 

Language conditioning. A frozen DistilBERT en-coder [67] maps instruction ℓt to a 768-dimensional em-bedding et by mean-pooling non-padding token features. A learned network produces scale and shift vectors for FiLM [68]: 

ezt,i =  1 + γ(et) ⊙ zt,i + β(et), (36) where zt,i is visual token i, γ and β are the predicted scale and shift, and ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication. The modulation is shared across visual tokens and initialized to the identity. 

FlowDiTX action generator. FlowDiTX projects a noisy action chunk Xs ∈ RH×29 into 768-dimensional tokens with learned temporal position embeddings, where H is the action horizon and s ∈ [0 , 1] is flow time. Each of six blocks applies temporal self-attention, cross-attention, and a feed-forward network. Action tokens supply the cross-attention queries, while language-conditioned visual tokens supply the keys and values. A sinusoidal flow-time embedding controls adaptive layer normalization and initially zero residual gates. The output head predicts an action-space velocity field, which is integrated to generate the action chunk. 

Action representation. We represent rotations by the first two columns of their rotation matrices [79]. This continuous 6D representation avoids Euler-angle wrapping and singular-ities and the sign ambiguity of unit quaternions. The target columns are orthonormal unit vectors with components in 

[−1, 1] , so we retain their fixed scale without dataset stan-dardization. Predicted columns are orthonormalized when decoded into rotations. Position and gripper targets are standardized using dataset statistics. 

Training procedure. We train for 150,000–200,000 opti-mizer updates, jointly fine-tuning the visual backbones with the action generator and FiLM network while keeping Dis-tilBERT frozen. Flow times are sampled from Beta(1 , 1.5) ,emphasizing small s and therefore noisier actions. The workspace comparison in Table I measures how closely each embodiment’s fixed-base reach corresponds to human reach. We estimate these workspaces by sam-pling feasible joint ranges, evaluating end-effector positions through forward kinematics, and refining the boundaries of the resulting volumetric representations. 

1) Kinematic sampling: For each embodiment, we sample the arm joints together with shared body joints, such as the torso and lift, while holding the mobile base station-ary. Shared-joint motion contributes to both arms’ reach. Joint ranges and mechanical couplings follow the respective kinematic models. Forward kinematics maps each sampled configuration to left- and right-endpoint positions, which are accumulated into separate workspaces. All models retain their physical dimensions and share a ground plane and forward direction, using the relative placement described in the main paper. We use scrambled Sobol sequences to cover the joint-limit domain. Additional samples concentrate near joint limits and on selected boundary faces, improving coverage of configurations that produce extreme reaches. This combines broad exploration of the configuration space with targeted sampling of regions that may be underrepresented by uniform sampling alone. 

2) Human lower-body motion: Human reach is evaluated using the canonical human model [72] across a family of symmetric, flat-footed postures ranging from standing to squatting. At each pelvis height, we solve for a consistent leg configuration that respects joint limits and keeps both soles on the ground. Torso and arm configurations are then sam-pled conditional on these lower-body postures. This captures the contribution of lower-body motion to vertical reach while preserving a consistent relationship between pelvis height and leg articulation. Subsequent refinement remains on the same family of squat poses. 

3) Workspace discretization and refinement: Endpoint po-sitions are discretized on an axis-aligned grid of 25 mm 

cubic voxels. A voxel is occupied if it contains at least one sampled endpoint position, and its volume contributes to the corresponding arm’s workspace. Left and right occupancy are maintained separately so that both combined reach and arm-specific correspondence can be evaluated. We improve boundary coverage through adaptive local sampling. Occupied voxels adjacent to empty space identify candidate boundary configurations. We perturb the associated shared and arm joints within their limits, evaluate the new endpoint positions, and merge them into the occupancy sets. Progressively smaller perturbations refine the sampled boundary; refinement ends when its sampling budget is exhausted or too few new occupied voxels are discovered. All quantitative comparisons use the unsmoothed occupancy sets. Surface smoothing is used only to visualize workspace shape. The reported comparison isolates positional kinematic reach subject to joint limits. It applies neither collision rejection nor static-stability filtering, and sampled endpoint positions below the ground plane are retained. These assump-tions are shared across the workspace metrics.
