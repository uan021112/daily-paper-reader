# Contact-Implicit Whole-Body Trajectory Optimization for Dynamic Humanoid Locomotion on Parameterizable Uneven Terrain 

Roberto Mauceri , Stefano Dafarra , Giulio Romualdi , Marco Gabiccini , and Daniele Pucci 

Abstract —This letter presents a trajectory planner for hu-manoid robots to perform dynamic motions on complex, pa-rameterizable terrains using a contact-implicit whole-body opti-mization framework. The planner generates feasible movements without predefined contact sequences, considering the robot’s full dynamics and kinematics. To plan bipedal locomotion on steps, ramps, and other terrains, we propose a mathematical model to approximate ground geometry with a smooth surface. The rigid interaction between the robot and the ground is modeled via novel contact constraints, extending the domain of applicability from flat and horizontal surfaces to any arbitrary smooth surface. Effectiveness is demonstrated using nonlinear programming to plan motions on various terrain topologies. We also explore dynamic trajectory generation by embedding high-level requirements in the cost function and the initial guess. Preliminary feasibility tests were conducted over non-coplanar terrains, both in simulation and on the real robot ErgoCub. 

Index Terms —Humanoid and bipedal locomotion, optimization and optimal control, whole-body motion planning and control. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# HUMANOID motion planning aims to generate coor-dinated, physically consistent trajectories that respect system dynamics and contact constraints. While early meth-ods focused on flat ground, real-world applications require handling uneven terrain , which demands a more sophisticated approach to selecting feasible contact locations and generating dynamic trajectories. When operating offline, the planner must anticipate variations in terrain geometry and generate feasible motions that maintain stability without real-time feedback by explicitly integrating terrain and contact models. TO offers a high level of abstraction and provides powerful tools for the explicit management of contact constraints (e.g., mixed-integer convex programming (MICP) [2] or linear comple-mentarity problem [3]). It has proven effective in robotics fields that require creating and breaking contacts with the environment, such as manipulation [4]–[6] and legged locomo-tion [3], [7]–[16]. Common terrain modeling approaches for legged locomotion rely on planar region extraction from point clouds to simplify foothold planning [17], [18], while others explore continuous terrain representations [19] or discretized heightmaps [15], enabling richer geometric descriptions. Traditional TO approaches rely on predefined contacts ,where contact locations and timings are fixed in advance. In [20], the authors explored robust walking on uneven ter-rain using convex programming (CP), which ensures global optimality. Given predefined footstep locations and timings, their approach regulates the robot centroidal dynamics (CD) while satisfying the contact wrench cone criterion [21], [22]. Footholds are selected based on the method proposed in [8]. The terrain is modeled as stepping stones with flat convex regions where foot placement is permitted. Recent work has advanced these ideas by integrating contact location determi-nation and motion planning into a unified CP framework, ad-dressing challenges of terrain adaptability and dynamic motion for quadrupeds [12] (Table I). To mitigate the combinatorial complexity of MICP, [13] leveraged the sparsity properties of L1-norm minimization and a center-of-mass (CoM) model to reformulate contact planning as a feasibility linear program. Their approach, validated in humanoid simulations, shows promise for online planning on non-flat terrain (Table I). To overcome the limitations of predefined contacts, contact-implicit approaches let the planner freely determine them, offering greater flexibility and adaptability [16]. A common technique is the use of complementarity conditions (CCs), which encode rigid contacts and allow smooth transitions between free motion and contact phases [3], [7]. However, CCs are non-convex and introduce nonlinearities, making optimiza-tion challenging and often ill-conditioned. Several strategies address these issues: relaxed CCs add a small tolerance in the contact constraints [23], penalty-based approaches penalize CC violations within the cost function [4], and dynamic CCs reformulate the conditions using Baumgarte stabilization [14].

NLP, on the other hand, accommodates system nonlinearities and enables the exploration of richer, more realistic dynamic motions [3], [7], [14]. However, NLP is challenged by multiple local minima induced by terrain morphology and locomotion patterns, requiring careful initial guess selection to enhance the likelihood of finding optimal solutions. Solving the nonlinear equations in NLP can be computationally intensive and prone to numerical instability, potentially increasing solution times and limiting online applicability. Many NLP solvers rely on gradient-based methods requiring derivatives of the cost function and constraints. When applied to legged locomotion, these techniques demand differentiable contact descriptions. The method in [19] implements an NLP problem that fixes the number of contact events while letting the solver determine step timings and footholds on a continuous heightmap. This approach avoids CCs but significantly limits the search space. Alternatively, [15] uses onboard sensors to capture terrain and represent it through a non-smooth heightmap. Terrain gradients are computed via numerical differentiation, but solver accuracy is limited by map resolution. Filtered maps are used separately for foothold placement, torso orientation, and edge avoidance. The gravito-inertia acceleration (GIA) model is used (Table I). Another methodology for nonlinear optimization is differ-ential dynamic programming (DDP). Traditionally it does not handle constraints explicitly, but extensions like Feasibility-driven DDP (FDDP [11] and BOX-FDDP [24]) manage infea-sible initial guesses and control constraints. DDP can achieve greater computational efficiency compared to NLP, making DDP-based planners suitable for online applications (Table I). In parallel to optimization-based methods, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool for discovering robust control policies in environments with high variability and uncertainty but often requires extensive training data and may perform inefficiently without demonstration priors. Unlike prior approaches, our method integrates the robot WBD with a differentiable terrain and a mathematically coupled CC-based contact model. A notable aspect of our terrain model is its ability to remain smooth while closely approximating discontinuities common in human-centric environments. Our approach formulates planning as an optimal control (OC) problem solved via NLP. The key contributions are:  

> •

The introduction of a differentiable representation of uneven terrain, combined with generalized contact constraints.  

> •

A mass-scaling of the system’s centroidal dynamics to improve efficiency and dynamic motion results.  

> •

The validation of our method by generating locomotion trajectories on non-flat surfaces, showcasing its ability to synthesize dynamic motions. We conducted preliminary tests to evaluate the feasibility of the generated trajectories, validating the approach through both simulations and real-world experiments on the humanoid robot ErgoCub over non-coplanar terrain (Fig. 1).

D. Optimal Control Problem and Nonlinear Programming 

A constrained OC problem involves finding a control policy for a dynamical system to minimize a cost functional J and satisfying constraints involving state and control time signals. NLP techniques are crucial for addressing the optimization challenges of OC problems, since the former represent a finite-dimensional approximation of the latter. An NLP problem consists of three main elements:  

> •

a p-tuple of optimization variables χ ∈ Rp, that can be freely chosen within its domain.  

> •

A scalar function in the optimization variables Γ( χ) : 

Rp → R to be minimized ( cost function ).  

> •

A set of k equations or inequalities that algebraically link the optimization variables gi(χ) ≥ 0 (constraints ). The solution of the NLP problem is the vector of optimization variables χ⋆ that simultaneously satisfies all constraints and minimizes the objective function: 

χ⋆ ≜ arg min   

> χ∈Ω

Γ( χ).

The set Ω ≜ {χ ∈ Rp : gi(χ) ≥ 0, ∀i} is called feasible set .

Γ( χ) and gi(χ) are assumed to be differentiable functions. The differentiability of all the problem functions enables the use of derivative-based optimization algorithms. This includes gradient-based methods, which directly compute gradients and Hessian matrices, and interior point methods, which indirectly use derivatives through the formulation and manipulation of barrier functions [30]. The infinite-dimensional OC problem can be transcribed into a finite-dimensional NLP problem by using the so-called

F. Choice of the Initial Guess 

Given the non-convex nature of the NLP problem, the choice of the initial guess plays a crucial role in shaping the resulting trajectories, as different choices may lead to distinct local minima . This work highlights the importance of the initial guess for the time evolution of contact forces. For instance, where these forces are set equal to zero, the computed solution results in realistic jumping motions. The initial guess is built by interpolating between two robot configurations (initial and final) constrained to static equilibrium.

Since the couple (φ, ϑ ) is generally non-unique, the solution is determined by minimizing Γ ≜ ∥φ∥2 + ∥ϑ∥2 to ensure least deviations in the ankle angles. The problem is constrained by the condition that P 1 and P 2 belong to the ground surface. The contact points’ positions are then used to solve a statics problem to evaluate feasible contact forces. This problem is hyper-static, meaning static equilibrium equations alone can’t determine the contact forces uniquely. Therefore, a criterion must be defined to select one feasible solution from the infinite possibilities. This criterion can be framed as an optimization problem with contact forces as variables, subject to contact constraints Eqs. (6) to (10). Once feasible contact forces are evaluated, we seek a robot configuration AqB with the CoM in the desired position and feet in the desired poses. This problem can also be framed as an optimization problem, where the variables are the components of AqB. Constraints include the desired foot poses, the CoM position, joint limits, and unit norm for the base quaternion. The objective function is a regularization task on the joint positions and base pose.

This section presents the results obtained by solving the discussed OC problem, taking into account all the details introduced in the previous sections. The transcription uses the multiple shooting method [33]. State-transition equality con-straints are derived by discretizing the differential equations (Section III-C) using the explicit Euler method. To preserve the structure of the group of unit quaternions, an integration over the Lie algebra followed by the application of the exponential map is used [36]. The integration step ∆t is assumed to be constant. The time horizon [ti, t f ] ⊂ R is finite and its length T has been varied according to the required task. It has been divided into N time intervals of the same length 

∆t = T /N . The NLP problem is solved using the software package IPOPT [37], employing the mumps and MA57 linear solvers [38]. The pipeline from the problem definition to its solution is implemented entirely in Python 3.10 with the help of the symbolic framework CasADi [39]. B. Tested Trajectories 

Low-dynamic motions have been tested both in simulation and on the humanoid robot ErgoCub (Fig. 1). The trajectories, generated offline, are provided as a reference to a joint position PID controller. Thus, loop closure occurs exclusively at the joint level, with no guarantee of correct tracking of the floating-base pose. Since the sampled trajectory frequency is around 10 Hz , we increased the sample density by a factor of 10 -20 through linear interpolation. The walking and step-climbing (height: 7 cm ) motions have been proven to be remarkably accurate and have been successfully reproduced in the simulation and on the real robot, even in the absence of a high-level closed-loop controller. The robot performed the tasks without losing balance, thus validating the physical feasibility of the trajectories generated by the planner. The optimization yields feasible trajectories that naturally involve edge contacts, without requiring explicit enforcement. In this case, heel contact precedes toe contact, resulting in a gait pattern more closely resembling that of humans. Some trajec-tories involving contacts with a ramp have been successfully reproduced in simulation. More complex trajectories cannot be adequately executed as long as an open-loop control archi-tecture is used. The supplementary video shows experiments conducted both in simulation and in the real world. V. C ONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

The paper presents an offline whole-body trajectory planner for humanoid locomotion on uneven terrain, framed as a constrained OC problem solved via direct transcription. The terrain was modeled using an originally devised representation for smooth surfaces, generalizing contact constraints beyond flat surfaces. The generated trajectories demonstrate the ef-fectiveness of this approach, which enables dynamic motions on complex terrain. Future work will extend the real-robot validation to more dynamic motions once a high-level closed-loop controller is in place to ensure accurate tracking. Another possibility is to integrate the planner with RL modules. For example, the generated trajectories could serve as valuable motion priors for training learning-based controllers. Regarding the limitations of the proposed model, it remains effective as long as real terrains can be approximated by rela-tively simple, easily parameterizable shapes. Additionally, its reliance on explicit functions for geometry description restricts the range of representable walking surfaces. Employing im-plicit functions could enable a richer set of geometric shapes. Nevertheless, while the current approach remains highly effec-tive in structured, human-designed environments, developing a method to generate differentiable terrains from point clouds would enable planning over arbitrary, unstructured terrains unknown a priori.
