Abstract —Depth-guided, cost-volume-based Generalizable 3D Gaussian Splatting (G-3DGS) encoders reconstruct scenes as sets of Gaussian primitives directly from multi-view images without requiring per-scene optimization, thereby facilitating feed-forward novel-view synthesis on edge computing platforms. However, their shared encoder architecture presents two primary bottlenecks. First, the per-pixel depth search disregards semantic similarity within the 2D feature space, causing depth search operations to account for over 65% of memory traffic due to redundant exhaustive candidate scans. Second, the sequential pipeline of depth prediction followed by Gaussian generation neglects local 3D geometric continuity, resulting in more than 52% of generated Gaussians being redundant prior to instantia-tion. In this paper, we introduce SCARF, a dataflow-co-designed accelerator that aligns each bottleneck with a corresponding scene-level regularity. To mitigate the first bottleneck, Feature Similarity Depth Reuse reconceptualizes depth search as aproblem of semantic locality: depth consistency is determined by affinity in feature space rather than physical address proximity. It transforms exhaustive per-pixel depth searches into anchor-centered local searches. To overcome the second bottleneck, Scene-Adaptive Early Sparsification disrupts the conventional serial depth-to-materialization boundary by probing each tile to infer local scene regularity. It then selectively activates only the minimally sufficient generation pathway, eliminating redundant Gaussian computations before primitive instantiation. On a 27.8 square millimeter 28 nm post-layout design, SCARF delivers a 2.94-fold geometric-mean end-to-end speedup with maximum PSNR degradation below 0.072 percent, 2.23 inferences per joule, and 34.5-fold GPU energy efficiency.

Second, the sequential pipeline comprising depth prediction and Gaussian generation fails to exploit the continuity inherent in local 3D geometry. SCARF combines Feature Similarity Depth Reuse (FSDR) 

and Scene-Adaptive Early Sparsification (SAES) . FSDR contracts full depth-candidate scans into semantic-guided local searches, while SAES selects the minimally sufficient gen-eration path before Gaussian materialization. Our principal contributions are as follows: 

(i) We introduce Feature Similarity Depth Reuse , which reconceptualizes per-pixel depth search as semantic locality rather than physical proximity. FSDR uses semantic signa-tures from locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) to index content-addressable memory (CAM). On a cache hit, it replaces the full candidate scan with a localized neighborhood search, reducing cross-view feature-sampling traffic. 

(ii) We design Scene-Adaptive Early Sparsification , which disrupts the traditional serial boundary between depth estima-tion and Gaussian materialization. With a small probe set, SAES evaluates per-tile feature and depth consistency and activates only the minimally sufficient generation path, pruning redundant Gaussian computations while the resulting impact on quality remains controllable. 

(iii) We develop SCARF 1, a 27 .8 mm 2 post-layout accel-erator in 28 nm that integrates FSDR and SAES for depth-guided, cost-volume-based G-3DGS encoder inference. II. B ACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION 

A. Generalizable 3DGS Encoders 

Generalizable 3D Gaussian Splatting (G-3DGS) en-coders [11]–[13], [15]–[17] generate scene representations as

sets of Gaussian primitives directly from multi-view images in a single feed-forward pass. Existing G-3DGS encoders fall into two categories. Geometry-consistent depth-guided encoders first estimate per-pixel depth through cross-view feature matching and cost-volume aggregation, then generate Gaussian primitives from the resulting depth map. For ex-ample, MVSplat [11] uses plane-sweeping cost aggregation to ensure multi-view geometric consistency, enabling robust depth localization even with sparse views. DepthSplat [12] en-hances this by incorporating monocular depth priors and self-supervised DINOv2 [18], [19] features, improving robustness in low-texture and occluded regions. TranSplat [13] adopts a Transformer-based, depth-aware scheme, integrating cross-view matching [20], [21] and Gaussian parameter prediction within an end-to-end framework. In contrast, direct or implicit encoders, including pixelSplat [15], latentSplat [16], and No-PoSplat [17], use probabilistic sampling, latent decoding. 

B. Depth-Guided G-3DGS Encoder Pipeline 

Despite differences in backbone and matching strategy, MVSplat, DepthSplat, and TranSplat share a common exe-cution pipeline. A CNN backbone [22], optionally enhanced with Transformer encoder layers [23], processes all input views to generate multi-scale feature maps through hierar-chical convolution and self-attention. For each reference-view pixel (u, v ), depth estimation is performed independently: each of the D depth hypotheses {dk} projects the pixel to the target view, where corresponding features are bilinearly sampled, accumulating per-hypothesis matching costs into a cost volume C ∈ RH×W ×D . A U-Net [24] refines C across depth and spatial dimensions, and a Softmax-based depth head converts the refined scores into a continuous depth estimate

Re10K [26], ACID [27], and DL3DV [28] datasets, revealing two challenges to accelerator efficiency. 

a) Challenge 1: Independent per-pixel depth search overlooks semantic similarity in 2D feature space, causing redundant memory traffic : Feature maps exhibit a consistent statistical property: pixels with similar feature vectors typically belong to the same object surface and have comparable depth values, based on two priors. First, co-surface pixels share texture and illumination, resulting in highly correlated features extracted by CNNs. Second, object surfaces are geometrically smooth, causing depth to vary continuously within local re-gions. As shown in Fig. 2(a), pixel pairs with cosine feature similarity above 0.7 account for 51.2% of all pixels on average, with 99.5% depth consistency. For DepthSplat, which uses self-supervised DINOv2 features, this proportion rises to 91.4%. These findings indicate that semantically similar pixels provide highly predictive depth estimates. Existing systems fail to effectively leverage semantic reuse. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the depth-search stage generates over 65% of LPDDR5 memory traffic, despite an average L2 cache hit rate of only 31.2%. This inefficiency arises from structural issues: cross-view feature sampling across D depth hypotheses produces access patterns that vary with both the depth hypothesis and the target view index, resulting in a lack of physical address locality. Conventional caches depend on address continuity rather than feature-space similarity, causing semantically similar pixels scattered throughout memory to each trigger independent full-candidate scans. 

b) Challenge 2: The serial depth-prediction and Gaussian-generation dataflow neglects local continuity in 3D geometry, causing redundant computations : Existing G-3DGS encoders employ per-pixel Gaussian generation to maintain generalization, resulting in depth prediction and Gaussian generation accounting for 54% of the total execu-tion time. To characterize redundancy inside encoder outputs, we analyze local attribute similarity among the generated Gaussian primitives. Fig. 3(b) shows the spatial distribution of attribute differences for the scene in Fig. 3(a), revealing that neighboring Gaussians are mostly similar. Figs. 3(c)– (e) plot the per-attribute similarity distributions and mark the high-similarity boundary used in our redundancy analysis. We count a Gaussian as highly similar on an attribute when the covariance or spherical-harmonic cosine similarity exceeds 0.7, or when the opacity L2 distance is below 0.2. Fig. 3(f) further shows that, across the nine model and dataset pairs, 67.8% of encoder-generated Gaussians on average satisfy all three attribute tests. Thus, adjacent pixels on the same surface produce Gaussian primitives with similar attributes. Furthermore, using local 4 × 4 tiles, we merge high-similarity primitives into representative primitives, yielding a 50.9% Gaussian reduction on average. Across models and datasets, the merged Gaussian sets keep the PSNR loss below 0.1% in scene rendering, indicating that these high-similarity primitives contribute highly overlapping content to the final rendering. Thus, encoder-generated Gaussian sets contain sub-stantial local structural redundancy. In fact, the fully optimized per-scene 3DGS reference reduces primitives by 51.3% on average, further showing the large compressible space in scene representation. However, the serial dataflow requires depth prediction for all pixels before Gaussian generation. This prevents depth prediction from observing Gaussian-primitive similarity and causes significant computational redundancy. 

D. Our Approach 

To address these challenges, we propose SCARF, adataflow-co-designed accelerator that leverages semantic sim-ilarity in 2D feature space and local geometric continuity in 3D space to reduce redundant memory accesses and redundant Gaussian generation: 

a) Feature Similarity Depth Reuse (FSDR) : To address Challenge 1, we propose FSDR, which replaces address-keyed lookups with semantic-signature-keyed lookups within the depth-search pipeline. Upon a hit, it narrows the candidate set to a cached-anchor neighborhood, replacing the full D-candidate scan with a guided local search, thereby eliminating the redundant feature-sampling traffic. 

b) Scene-Adaptive Early Sparsification (SAES) : To ad-dress Challenge 2, we propose SAES, a dataflow that fuses depth prediction with Gaussian materialization. It breaks the strict stage boundary between depth prediction and Gaussian generation. Each tile can activate only the minimally necessary materialization path before full primitive instantiation. the proposed approach maintains quality and reduces encoder workload and energy consumption. III. SCARF D ATAFLOW 

Building on FSDR and SAES, SCARF restructures the depth-guided G-3DGS encoder pipeline into four stages, as shown in Fig. 4. S1 extracts multi-view features, S2 predicts per-pixel depth, S3 materializes Gaussian primitive param-eters, and S4 converts network outputs into final Gaussian primitives. Unlike a serialized S2 →S3 pipeline, SCARF fuses S2 and S3 into a tile-level joint pipeline. 

A. Stage 1: Feature Extraction 

S1 extracts backbone features from all input views by sequentially applying CNN convolutional layers and Trans-former encoder layers to produce multi-scale feature maps. 

a) Model-agnostic runtime configuration : Depth-guided G-3DGS encoders vary in convolutional and Transformer layer counts and may include a Vision Transformer (ViT) branch. Memory-mapped input/output (MMIO) configuration registers manage these differences uniformly. This dataflow eliminates model-specific branches and supports depth-guided topologies on unified hardware. 

b) Tile-aligned writeback layout : Feature maps at all scales are divided into fixed-size tiles before off-chip write-back. A linear layout keeps data contiguous within each tile and stores tiles sequentially, placing cross-view features of the same tile adjacent in memory. The layout matches the probe-prioritized execution order of S2, and the critical pixel reads therefore touch only a few consecutive cache lines and avoid data reordering or inter-block movement before S2. 

B. Stage 2: Semantics-Guided Depth Prediction 

S2 uses FSDR-guided candidate contraction to reduce mem-ory traffic during per-pixel cross-view depth prediction.

Stage 4: Gaussian Conversion

a) Compact writeback format : S4 reduces output band-width by using a compact writeback format aligned with the Gaussian representation. It writes only the six independent upper-triangular elements of the symmetric covariance matrix and stores only the valid spherical-harmonics coefficients for the configured order. This design cuts writeback bytes per Gaussian by over 35% without altering computational semantics and ensures that output bandwidth exhibits linear scaling with scene complexity. 

b) Streaming commit : During S4 execution, results are committed to the writeback buffer in tile-local Gaussian order, avoiding the burstiness and queuing overhead of conventional batch-based writeback. For tiles routed to the L0 representative or L1 lightweight path chosen by SAES, S4 receives only the retained outputs from that path, thereby further reducing conversion and writeback traffic. IV. SCARF A RCHITECTURE 

The SCARF architecture (Fig. 5) comprises five inter-connected components: the Matrix Vector Unit (MVU), FSDR Cache, GGU Array, control subsystem, and scratch-pad memory (SPM) subsystem. The MVU shares a matrix-multiplication compute unit (MMCU) across S1 through S3, amortizing compute area across their diverse operators. The FSDR Cache intercepts per-pixel depth-search requests at the S2 front end, eliminating redundant candidate scans before they reach the MMCU. The GGU Array offloads geometric conversion from the MVU, allowing computation of the next tile while primitive construction proceeds concurrently. To-gether, these units keep the high-frequency dependency chain among features, depths, and Gaussians on chip, leaving only essential inputs and outputs on the DRAM path. 

A. Matrix-Vector Unit 

Across S1, S2, and S3, the operator mix shifts from matrix-intensive convolution and attention in S1, through irregular bilinear sampling and reduction in S2, to SAES-specific vector statistics in S3. Provisioning stage-specific arrays would frag-ment area and force costly data reordering at stage boundaries. SCARF instead organizes the MVU around a shared MMCU for convolution, general matrix multiplication (GEMM), and attention, augmented by a BilinearUnit for irregular cross-view sampling, a VectorALU for reductions and SAES path-selection statistics, and fused NormUnit and ActivationUnit to eliminate intermediate writebacks. 

a) S1: feature extraction : The MMCU operates in Conv, GEMM, and Attention modes. In Conv mode, convolutions are mapped via im2col with fused normalization and activation. The NormUnit and ActivationUnit handle these operations to eliminate intermediate writeback. In GEMM mode, the same array processes QKV projections and FFN blocks. In Atten-tion mode, the VectorALU executes scaled dot-product and normalization alongside the MMCU, enabling self-attention and cross-attention. 

b) S2: depth prediction : S2 reuses the MMCU across both Conv and GEMM modes for U-Net refinement and depth-head regression, switching between them via weight-switch commands to the WeightBuffer to avoid stalls caused by array reconfiguration. A dedicated 32-way BilinearUnit absorbs this irregular traffic concurrently, keeping the MMCU fully occupied with refinement. To produce ˆdu,v , the Vec-torALU then normalizes over depth candidates and computes the expectation over those candidates. 

c) S3: feedback execution and Gaussian-parameter pre-diction : The single-instruction, multiple-data (SIMD) data-path of the VectorALU computes the L0 feature variance, 

σ2feat (PT ), and L1 depth standard deviation, σd(PT ), online through a reduction tree with constant path-selection latency for any probe count. In the representative-generation path, the VectorALU performs weighted moment matching to in-corporate non-probe pixels into representative Gaussians. In the Full path, the MMCU Conv mode is reused for parameter prediction with fused NormUnit and ActivationUnit execution.

Pixels with nearly identical features and depths may have distant memory addresses, so address-domain caches miss most reuse opportunities regardless of capacity. CAM instead indexes signature values through parallel associative lookup across all entries, locating semantic matches regardless of physical address. Each 41-bit entry stores a valid bit, a 16-bit LSH signature, an FP16 depth anchor, and an 8-bit least-recently-used (LRU) counter.

Random hyperplane projection preserves the cosine-similarity structure of CNN features: pixels lying on the same surface cluster into nearby regions of signature space, so a small Hamming distance reliably predicts depth agreement. C. Gaussian Generation Unit 

The GGU Array performs the final geometric mapping, con-verting raw per-pixel network outputs into renderable Gaussian primitives. Since these geometric operations are independent across Gaussians, the array comprises 32 processing elements, each executing a three-stage pipeline: PositionCalc back-projects and applies extrinsic transformations to obtain the world-space position μ. CovBuilder expands the quaternion into a rotation matrix R and constructs the covariance matrix 

Σ = RSS ⊤R⊤. SH OPGenerator rotates SH coefficients into world coordinates via per-band Wigner-D multiplication and applies sigmoid activation to opacity. 

a) Rotation-matrix bypass : Within a GGU processing element, spherical harmonics rotation is the most memory-intensive sub-stage, comprising about 43% of total GGU mem-ory traffic. Since the required rotation matrix R is mathemat-ically identical to the one computed in CovBuilder, SCARF directly connects CovBuilder’s R-Regs to SH OPGenerator’s input via a bypass. This eliminates the downstream stall while waiting for them to be reloaded before Wigner-D multipli-cation, enabling SH OPGenerator to start immediately after CovBuilder has completed its work. 

b) Precision-sensitive mixed-precision strategy : 3DGS rendering is sensitive to geometric precision: multi-stage accu-mulation in back-projection and covariance construction can amplify FP16 errors into visible artifacts, whereas spherical harmonics rotation is more tolerant of reduced precision. SCARF therefore applies FP32 selectively to geometry-critical steps, including PositionCalc, CovBuilder, and the rotation matrices in SH OPGenerator, preserving geometric fidelity without the bandwidth cost of full FP32. 

D. SCARF Scheduling and Model Adaptation 

The algorithmic evolution of depth-guided, cost-volume-based G-3DGS encoders outpaces hardware iteration cycles. SCARF bridges this gap with a programmable architecture that enables a single hardware instance to efficiently sup-port multiple models within the same depth-guided, cost-volume-based encoder family. Its programming model uses instruction sequences to define the computation graph, with each instruction specifying the operator type, input/output buffer addresses, and hardware configuration. At runtime, the PipelineFSM reads instructions sequentially, dispatches tasks Fig. 7. S2/S3 instructions for TranSplat and DepthSplat. 

to hardware units based on the operator type, and manages data movement among the buffers. SCARF defines six hardware primitives: CONV for convolu-tion and deconvolution, GEMM for matrix multiplication, NORM 

for BatchNorm and LayerNorm, ACTIVATION for ReLU, GELU, and Softmax, BILINEAR for bilinear sampling, and 

REDUCE for Mean, Max, and Sum. Higher-level operators such as Transformer attention and epipolar sampling are decomposed into these primitives. This abstraction maintains a stable hardware base while adapting to model variations through instruction scheduling. Fig. 7 compares the S2 and S3 instructions of TranSplat and DepthSplat. The comparison exposes two concrete schedule differences. First, DepthSplat carries the mono ViT feature branch from S1 into S2, where cost-volume refinement fuses CNN, multi-view, and mono features, while TranSplat refines the epipolar cost volume and regresses depth. Second, DepthSplat adds DPT-based upsampling before Gaussian prediction, whereas the lighter depth and feature outputs go directly into the Gaussian heads that belong to TranSplat. V. M ETHODOLOGY 

a) Experimental Platform : We implemented the SCARF RTL design using the TSMC 28 nm process and the Open-ROAD physical-design flow, including synthesis, placement, routing, and post-layout timing analysis [32]. On-chip SRAM blocks were generated using a commercial memory compiler within the same technology node. The design operates at 1 GHz and features a fully pipelined architecture. To evaluate SCARF under realistic workloads, we developed a cycle-accurate simulator that models the SCARF microarchitecture and integrates Ramulator [33] to capture detailed DRAM timing for Micron LPDDR4X-4266. The simulator performs both functional and timing simulations, accepts multi-view images as input, and outputs predicted 3D Gaussian pa-rameters. Performance metrics are derived from simulator cycle counts. Logic power is estimated from the post-layout gate-level netlist, TSMC 28 nm Liberty timing and power files, and workload switching activity generated by the cycle-accurate simulator. The switching activity is annotated in the OpenROAD and OpenSTA analysis flow to estimate dynamic power and leakage power. On-chip SRAM energy is esti-mated from simulator access counts and memory-compiler energy numbers. Using DRAM activity traces generated by the cycle-accurate simulator, DRAMPower [34] provides the corresponding off-chip memory energy estimate. 

b) Workloads : We selected three representative depth-guided, cost-volume-based G-3DGS models: MVSplat [11] (MV), TranSplat [13] (Tran), and DepthSplat [12] (Depth), which differ in architecture and computational characteristics. MVSplat is the lightest, TranSplat has medium complexity, and DepthSplat is the most compute-intensive due to its heav-ier ViT backbone and larger depth search space. The datasets used include Re10K [26], ACID [27], and DL3DV [28], covering diverse indoor/outdoor, driving, and dense multi-view scenes. All models use official pretrained weights. 

c) Baseline : Previous accelerators have primarily fo-cused on per-scene NeRF rendering [35], [36] and 3DGS rasterization [37], [38], but none have targeted feed-forward G-3DGS encoder construction. We use the Jetson Orin NX 16 GB as the system-level baseline, a common edge AI platform in robotics and autonomous driving. It features an Ampere GPU with 1,024 CUDA cores, 16 GB of LPDDR5 memory at 102.4 GB/s, and a 2 MB L2 cache, running at 918 MHz in MAXN mode (25 W TDP). Encoder and rasterizer latencies were measured separately using Nsight Systems. The optimized software stack employs PyTorch [39] with cuDNN [40], cuBLAS, optimized epipolar sampling, cost-volume and attention paths, and custom CUDA kernels for model-specific operators. The implementation uses FP16 precision and CUDA Graphs throughout. VI. E VALUATION

D. Area and Power Analysis a) Area Analysis : As shown in Table IV, SCARF occu-pies 27.8 mm 2 in 28 nm, with the area distribution directly reflecting the workload hierarchy. The MMCU dominates at 50.4% (14.0 mm 2) because it serves as the shared compute substrate across S1 through S3. Concentrating compute into one 48 × 48 array avoids the duplication of stage-specific designs. The GGU Array accounts for 17.3% (4.80 mm 2), sized to run its 32 geometry-conversion PEs concurrently with the MVU. The FSDR subsystem occupies only 0.08 mm 2 (0.3%), including only its 32-entry CAM, confirming that semantic indexing imposes negligible silicon cost relative to the 41% to 62% DRAM traffic reduction it enables. On-chip buffers (1.26 mm 2, 4.5%) sustain inter-stage data reuse, while the LPDDR4X PHY (3.50 mm 2, 12.6%) reflects the unavoidable fixed analog cost of high-speed I/O. 

b) Power Analysis : In Table IV, SCARF consumes 2.1 W at 1 GHz and 1.0 V, enabling fanless operation. Dynamic power mirrors the area hierarchy: the MMCU dominates at 52.2% (1,016 mW) as the primary active-compute fabric, while I/O and PHY account for 12.5% (244.0 mW) as the fixed cost of off-chip bandwidth. Despite consuming only 15 mW ( <1%), the FSDR subsystem delivers outsized savings. By cutting DRAM traffic by 41% to 62%, it suppresses the I/O switching energy that would otherwise grow with every redundant depth-search cycle. Neo [43] targets real-time on-device 3DGS ren-dering and exploits temporal redundancy in depth ordering across consecutive frames through reuse-and-update sorting. SCARF targets depth-guided, cost-volume-based G-3DGS encoder inference, where cost-volume depth search and Gaussian materialization dominate encoder construction. b) 3DGS memory scaling and training systems : A sep-arate line of systems work addresses memory capacity limits in large-scale 3DGS reconstruction and training. CLM [44] offloads Gaussian parameters to CPU memory and loads the active working set into GPU memory when needed, using pipelining and access-pattern-aware communication reduction to support very large scenes on a single consumer GPU. GS-Scale [45] similarly stores Gaussian parameters, gradients, and optimizer states in host memory, then reduces training overhead with selective offloading, parameter forwarding, and deferred optimizer updates. These systems expand the scale of per-scene 3DGS optimization and rendering, but they do not change the computation inside feed-forward G-3DGS encoders. SCARF targets this different stage by reducing cost-volume depth search and redundant Gaussian materialization before the final Gaussian set is emitted. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

SCARF targets inference in depth-guided G-3DGS encoders with cost-volume depth prediction. It exploits semantic locality through Feature Similarity Depth Reuse, which narrows depth candidates by feature-space affinity, and local geometric re-dundancy through Scene-Adaptive Early Sparsification, which activates the minimal materialization path before full primitive instantiation. Across three depth-guided, cost-volume-based models and three datasets, SCARF delivers a 2.94 × geometric-mean end-to-end speedup, 2.23 inferences per joule, and a maximum PSNR drop under 0.072%. Implemented in 28 nm, SCARF occupies 27.8 mm 2 and consumes 2.1 W, demonstrat-ing practical potential for edge novel-view synthesis. For downstream integration, SCARF serves as the Gaussian producer before a rasterizer. S4 writes primitives to a buffer that an existing 3DGS rasterizer reads through its input in-terface. G. Hardware proxy and scope 

Reviewers without Orin hardware can use the portability proxy after capturing same-workload CUDA timings. The proxy reports labeled latency, FPS, and speedup estimates without independent Orin power or temperature evidence. The released public hardware path routes the RTL with iFlow and ASAP7, then reports the technology-normalized estimate defined by the paper, with raw and scaled fields kept sepa-rate. Commercial TSMC 28 nm and LPDDR collateral are outside the archive. The Results Reproduced badge applies to the evidence evaluated during AE within these stated scope boundaries, not to measurements that depend on proprietary technology files outside the archive.
