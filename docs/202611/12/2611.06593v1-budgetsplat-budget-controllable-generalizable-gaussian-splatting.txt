# Abstract 

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting enables efficient gen-eralizable novel view synthesis, but existing pixel-aligned methods often generate highly redundant Gaussian primitives, causing the final representation size and rendering cost to grow rapidly with image resolution and the number of input views. We propose BudgetSplat, a budget-controllable feed-forward framework that adapts the final Gaussian representation to a user-specified preservation ratio. Rather than treating com-paction as simple importance ranking, we formulate it as a joint problem of importance estimation, spatial coverage, and cross-view redundancy. A single trained model supports vary-ing numbers of context views and multiple test-time budgets without per-budget retraining or per-scene compression. To realize this formulation, Intra-Cross Mask Supervision pro-vides structure-, coverage-, and redundancy-aware guidance for learning Gaussian importance, while budget-conditioned feature modulation adapts Gaussian opacity and attributes to the requested preservation ratio. Coverage-Aware Budget Se-lection further combines patch-wise pre-selection, view-wise opacity ranking, and 3D redundancy suppression to construct compact yet spatially distributed Gaussian representations. Experiments on indoor reconstruction and cross-dataset gener-alization demonstrate consistent quality–size trade-offs across different input view counts and preservation ratios, substan-tially reducing final representation storage and rendering cost while preserving rendering quality. 

# 1. Introduction 

3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) [9] represents scenes using explicit anisotropic Gaussians and enables high-quality real-time novel view synthesis. However, it requires costly per-scene optimization. Recent generalizable methods predict Gaussian representations from input images in a feed-forward manner, substantially improving reconstruction efficiency and generalization to unseen scenes [2, 4, 11, 20, 23]. Most feed-forward 3D-GS methods decode one or more Gaussians from each pixel. Although this pixel-aligned formu-lation provides strong geometric anchoring, its representation size grows rapidly with image resolution and input-view num-ber. Overlapping observations additionally produce many re-dundant Gaussians, increasing the storage and rendering costs of the final scene asset. Existing methods reduce pixel-aligned Gaussian redundancy through different designs. Geometry-aware fusion [20, 21] and view compression [18] consolidate overlapping pixel-aligned predictions across views; voxel-aligned prediction [19] predicts Gaussians in a shared 3D volume, whereas global scene encoders [1, 8] compress multi-view observations into a compact set of scene tokens before decoding the Gaussian representation. The latter demonstrate that a fixed-capacity Gaussian set can be sufficient on some benchmarks, but its output size remains tied to the scene token capacity. These developments raise a practical question: Can a single feed-forward model adapt its final Gaussian representation to different user-specified budgets? Existing approaches gen-erally fall into two categories. Fixed-capacity methods [1, 8] tie the representation size to a particular token or decoder configuration, so substantially larger or more complex scenes may require retraining a higher-capacity model. Post-hoc compression methods [7] reduce an already reconstructed Gaussian set, but introduce additional per-scene processing or optimization. In practice, deployment conditions may re-quire the same model to operate under different representation budgets. Simply applying a budget-unaware Top-𝐾 rule to a conventionally trained pixel-aligned model is suboptimal: it may over-allocate Gaussians to salient or high-opacity regions, leaving weakly textured areas under-covered. We therefore view budget-controlled Gaussian compaction as a joint prob-lem of importance estimation, spatial coverage, and cross-view redundancy, rather than simple candidate ranking. These limi-tations motivate a feed-forward model whose final Gaussian representation can be adjusted at test time while retaining the geometric advantages of pixel-aligned reconstruction. To this end, we propose BudgetSplat, a budget-controllable feed-forward 3D-GS framework that treats compact Gaussian allocation as a joint problem of importance estimation, spatial coverage, and cross-view redundancy. A single trained model supports varying context-view counts and user-specified test-time budgets without retraining or per-scene compression. 1arXiv:2611.06593v1 [cs.CV] 4 Nov 2026 Intra-Cross Mask Supervision provides structure-, coverage-, and redundancy-aware guidance for learning Gaussian impor-tance, while Budget-Conditioned Feature Modulation adapts Gaussian prediction to the requested preservation ratio. Fi-nally, Coverage-Aware Budget Selection combines local pre-selection, view-wise opacity ranking, and 3D redundancy suppression to construct the compact representation. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We formulate budget-controllable feed-forward 3D-GS as a joint problem of importance estimation, spatial coverage, and cross-view redundancy, and propose a single pixel-aligned model that supports different context-view counts and user-specified test-time budgets without retraining or per-scene compression. 

• We introduce Intra-Cross Mask Supervision to preserve informative structures, maintain sparse support in low-frequency regions, and reduce repeated cross-view su-pervision, together with Budget-Conditioned Feature Modulation that adapts Gaussian opacity and attributes to the requested preservation ratio. 

• We introduce Coverage-Aware Budget Selection, which combines patch-wise pre-selection, view-wise global opacity ranking, and 3D redundancy suppression to pro-duce compact yet spatially distributed Gaussian represen-tations. Experiments on ScanNet and Replica demonstrate robust quality–size trade-offs across different input-view settings and test-time budgets. 

# 2. Related Work 

Generalizable novel view synthesis. NeRF [14] and 3D Gaus-sian Splatting (3D-GS) [9] achieve high-quality novel view synthesis but typically require per-scene optimization. Gener-alizable methods instead learn scene priors for feed-forward reconstruction. Early approaches predict image-conditioned radiance fields using pixel-aligned features, feature matching, or cost volumes [3, 5, 12, 17, 24]. Recent methods extend this paradigm to 3D-GS by directly predicting Gaussian geome-try and attributes from input images [2, 4]. DepthSplat [23] and MonoSplat [13] further incorporate pretrained monocu-lar depth priors to improve geometry and generalization, but introduce additional dependence on external foundation mod-els and feature adaptation. More importantly, these methods primarily target reconstruction quality and do not explicitly control the size of the final Gaussian representation. 

Compact Gaussian representations. Pixel-aligned predic-tion provides strong geometric anchoring, but its Gaussian count scales with image resolution and input-view number, causing substantial cross-view redundancy. Geometry-aware fusion [20, 21] and view compression [18] reduce repeated information, whereas voxel-aligned prediction [19] and scene-level decoders [1, 8] redesign the prediction space to produce more compact representations. However, their output size is determined by the underlying fusion or representation design rather than a user-specified test-time budget. Post-hoc methods such as LightGaussian [7] compress an already reconstructed Gaussian set but require additional per-scene processing. EcoS-plat [15] learns Gaussian importance and selects primitives through opacity ranking, but the final selection does not explic-itly reason about spatial coverage or cross-view redundancy. In contrast, BudgetSplat treats controllable compaction as a joint importance–coverage–redundancy problem, introduc-ing explicit low-frequency support, cross-view redundancy weighting, coverage-aware selection, and target-view alpha completion.

## 3.2 Budget-conditioned Gaussian Compaction 

The reconstruction backbone produces 𝑁 𝐻𝑊 pixel-aligned features and Gaussian centers. Given a requested preservation ratio 𝜌 , we condition the features used for Gaussian prediction and select a subset for rasterization. This compaction remains part of the feed-forward inference pipeline and requires neither per-budget retraining nor per-scene optimization. 

Budget-Conditioned Feature Modulation (BCFM). Chang-ing only the selection budget would apply different truncation levels to budget-independent Gaussian predictions. We there-fore inject the requested ratio 𝜌 into the feature used by the Gaussian prediction heads. Specifically, 𝜌 is spatially broadcast and transformed by a lightweight convolutional embedding network into a budget feature 𝑬 𝑖 ∈ R𝐶 ×𝐻 ×𝑊 , which is added to the base feature 𝑭 𝑖 ∈ R𝐶 ×𝐻 ×𝑊 before Gaussian parameter prediction: 

 ˜𝛼 𝑖 , ˜𝚺 𝑖 , ˜𝒄 𝑖 = MLP (𝑭 𝑖 + 𝑬 𝑖 ) . (3) Here, ˜ 𝛼 𝑖 , ˜ 𝚺 𝑖 , and ˜ 𝒄 𝑖 denote the predicted opacity, covariance, and color, respectively. The activated opacity is used both for rendering and for candidate ranking. This conditioning allows a single model to adapt its Gaussian predictions to different test-time budgets. 

Coverage-Aware Budget Selection (CABS). Directly ranking all candidates within a view may concentrate the retained Gaus-sians in salient regions and leave smooth or weakly textured areas under-covered. We therefore perform coverage-aware selection in three steps. First, each input view is divided into local patches, from which an oversampled set of high-opacity candidates is retained to construct a spatially distributed candi-date pool. Second, all candidates retained from the patches of the same view are ranked jointly according to their predicted opacity. We refer to this step as view-wise global opacity rank-ing , since the ranking is performed across the candidate pool of one view rather than independently within each patch. Finally, the ranked candidate pools from all views are jointly filtered by 3D non-maximum suppression to remove geometrically redundant candidates according to the requested budget. The retained candidates form the compact Gaussian representation 

G𝜌 . Detailed budget allocation and selection procedures are provided in the supplementary material. 33.3 Learning Gaussian Importance

## 3.4 Training Strategy and Objective 

We train BudgetSplat using ground-truth target RGB images in two stages. The first stage learns dense pixel-aligned recon-struction without budget-controlled selection. Following prior works [2, 4, 20, 23], we optimize the rendered target image using 

Lrender = 𝜆 MSE LMSE + 𝜆 LPIPS LLPIPS . (14) This stage establishes a reliable dense reconstruction before budget-controlled compaction is introduced. 4After the dense prediction converges, we activate the budget-controlled branch and continue training with top-𝐾 rendering. During this stage, 𝜌 is randomly sampled and is injected into the prediction branch through BCFM. Its sampling range is gradually shifted toward lower preservation ratios. The com-pact representation produced under the sampled budget is rasterized, and the model is jointly optimized using the recon-struction, mask supervision, and alpha completion objectives: 

L = Lrender + 𝜆 mask Lmask + 𝜆 alpha Lalpha . (15) Through this two-stage procedure, the model first learns reli-able dense reconstruction and then learns to adapt its Gaussian prediction and selection to the requested budget while preserv-ing spatial coverage. 

# 4. Experiments 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. Following FreeSplat [20], we train on the same ScanNet [6] split with 100 training scenes and 8 test scenes, and evaluate zero-shot generalization on 8 Replica [16] scenes. We report standard image quality metrics, including pixel-level PSNR, patch-level SSIM [22], and feature-level LPIPS [25]. 

Implementation details. We train using Adam [10] with a learning rate of 10 −4 and a batch size of 1 on a single RTX 4090 GPU. Unless otherwise specified, images are resized to 384 × 512. We first train the dense reconstruction model for 300K iterations. Starting from this checkpoint, we train the budget-controlled stage for another 300K iterations. The final model therefore undergoes 600K iterations in total. We set 𝜆 MSE = 1.0, 𝜆 LPIPS = 0.05, 𝜆 mask = 0.1, and 𝜆 alpha = 1.0. Detailed training schedules are provided in the supplementary material. 

Baselines. We group compared methods into native-capacity (pixelSplat [2], MVSplat [4], DepthSplat [23], FreeSplat [20], ZPressor [18], VolSplat [19], and GlobalSplat [8]) and budgeted-compaction (EcoSplat [15]) methods. Unless oth-erwise noted, we use official checkpoints and configurations under the same view splits. We include the published 2- and 3-view results of FreeSplat++ [21], but omit it from the 10-view comparison because its implementation is unavailable for reproducing our 256 × 256 protocol. ## 4.2 Comparisons with Existing Methods 

Results on ScanNet. We evaluate our method on ScanNet [6] using 2, 3, and 10 context views. All settings use the same trained model, which also supports arbitrary preservation ratios at test time without retraining. Note that BudgetSplat 100% 

contains more Gaussians than native FreeSplat [20] because our model operates on the dense pixel-aligned candidate set and does not use its native geometric fusion. The lower-budget variants therefore reflect compaction by our selector rather than additional pruning of FreeSplat’s already fused output. Figure 2 compares BudgetSplat with recent feed-forward 3D-GS methods. While competing methods exhibit incomplete coverage, structural discontinuities, or local over-smoothing, BudgetSplat better preserves scene completeness and fine details under a compact budget. The supplementary material further visualizes both the selected Gaussian centers and the corresponding renderings across different preservation ratios. As shown in Table 1, BudgetSplat achieves the best ren-dering quality among the evaluated budgeted-compaction methods. At the 25% budget, it matches EcoSplat [15]’s Gaussian counts, while substantially improving all rendering metrics. Compared with FreeSplat [20], it uses over 60% fewer Gaussians with PSNR differences of only 0 .24 and 0 .38 dB. Table 2 further reports interpolation and extrapolation re-sults with 10 context views. All methods are evaluated at 256 × 256 because several pixel-aligned baselines run out of memory at 384 × 512 on an RTX 4090. BudgetSplat 25% 

achieves the best PSNR and SSIM among the budgeted-compaction methods and remains close to FreeSplat [20] in PSNR and SSIM, while using approximately 45% fewer Gaussians. Even at the aggressive 5% budget, BudgetSplat uses only 33k Gaussians, with PSNR decreasing by 0 .22 dB for interpolation and 0 .35 dB for extrapolation relative to BudgetSplat 100% .

Representation compactness. Because recent methods use different architectures and inference pipelines, we compare the output-side cost of the final Gaussian representation. Table 2 also reports the Gaussian count and raw storage required for rendering. GlobalSplat [8] produces the smallest native representation, while BudgetSplat 5% achieves higher PSNR and SSIM with a comparable budget of about 33k Gaussians. At the 25% budget, BudgetSplat also clearly outperforms EcoSplat [15] using the same number of Gaussians. These results show that the preservation ratio enables a controllable trade-off between representation size and rendering quality. 

Zero-shot generalization on Replica. We further evalu-ate cross-dataset generalization by applying the models to Replica [16] without finetuning. As shown in Table 3, we distinguish methods evaluated at their native output capaci-ties from methods that explicitly perform budgeted Gaussian compaction. Among the budgeted-compaction methods, Bud-getSplat achieves the strongest rendering quality. The results also show that absolute cross-dataset performance remains strongly influenced by the underlying reconstruction archi-tecture and scene representation, as evidenced by the strong performance of VolSplat [19] and GlobalSplat [8] in different settings. More importantly, BudgetSplat maintains stable rendering quality when the budget is reduced from 100% to 25%. # 5. Conclusion 

We present BudgetSplat, a budget-controllable feed-forward 3D-GS. Instead of keeping all pixel-aligned Gaussians, Bud-getSplat learns to compact them according to a user-specified budget ratio. BudgetSplat can flexibly adjust the number of Gaussians while preserving rendering quality, without per-budget retraining or a separate offline compression stage. Experiments show that BudgetSplat provides an effective 7trade-off between visual quality, representation storage, ren-dering memory, and rendering efficiency. 

Limitations and discussions. BudgetSplat relies on image gradients and estimated geometry for pseudo supervision, so degraded image quality or depth errors may affect selection under very compact budgets. Moreover, while BudgetSplat reduces the final representation and rendering costs, it retains the dense pixel-aligned prediction backbone. Future work will explore more robust supervision and budget-aware candidate generation.
