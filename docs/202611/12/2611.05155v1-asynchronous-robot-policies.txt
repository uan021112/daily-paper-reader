ABSTRACT 

Scaling vision-language foundation models has significantly advanced general-ist robot manipulation, but the increased model scale introduces computational latency that degrades real-time physical reactivity. Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architectures execute high-level semantic reasoning and action ex-ecution sequentially within the primary control loop, creating a fundamental ten-sion between semantic-reasoning capability and robot agility. To address this, we propose Asynchronous Robot Policy (ARP), an architectural framework that de-couples semantic reasoning from low-level action generation using asynchronous processes operating at independent frequencies. In ARP, a high-capacity Vision-Language Model (VLM) asynchronously updates a semantic memory at low fre-quency, while a lightweight Vision-Action Expert, conditioned on this semantic memory, generates robot actions at a much higher control frequency. Such a for-mulation, enables having a fast perception-action loop without compromising on the semantic capability. To overcome asynchronous memory staleness, ARP trains the vision-action expert on the semantic staleness and execution delay, with tem-porally mismatched observation across processes. On Franka manipulation tasks, ARP achieves an 18% higher success rate and 20% 

less time need for execution in quasi-static setups, while 3-5 × baseline perfor-mance in dynamic simulation environments. Furthermore, ARP enables operating the VLM at 10 × lower than its nominal frequency with no significant performance loss making it much more efficient than the baseline. These results highlight the architectural advantages of modeling robot policies as asynchronous processes, enabling real-world reactive deployment without sacrificing the scale of founda-tion models. 1 INTRODUCTION 

Scaling has become the dominant paradigm of modern artificial intelligence (Kaplan et al., 2020). However, scaling model size incurs a fundamental trade-off: increased capability at the cost of 1arXiv:2611.05155v1 [cs.RO] 3 Nov 2026 Arxiv Preprint higher computational latency and degrading real-time responsiveness. While higher latency may be tolerable in static or text-based applications, it presents a critical failure mode in robotics. Physical robotics requires tight perception-action loops to handle real-time contact dynamics and sudden environmental shifts, such as grasping falling objects or absorbing unexpected bumps. Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) (Brohan et al., 2022; Zitkovich et al., 2023) models fall victim to this bottleneck, creating a persistent tension between model capability, computational overhead, and closed-loop reactivity. Consequently, scaling the vision-language backbone improves high-level reasoning at the expense of motor agility, yielding a system that is semantically rich but physically lag-bound and depends on open-loop deployment. To resolve this friction, we propose 

Asynchronous Robot Policies (ARP) . As illustrated in Fig. 1b, ARP breaks the reactivity-capability trade-off, enabling the seamless integration of significantly larger foundational models into robot pipelines without significantly compromising dynamic reactivity or real-time performance. Existing generalist VLA policies execute the vision-language foundation model and the action ex-pert sequentially within the primary control loop. Their latencies therefore add up, giving a total perception-to-action latency of tVLM + tAE , as shown in Fig. 1(a). In contrast, ARP decouples VLM and action expert into asynchronous processes running at different frequencies. The VLM operates at a lower frequency f1 for high-level semantic reasoning, while a compact vision-action expert operates at a higher frequency f2 ≫ f1. Under ARP, the effective perception-action loop latency is strictly reduced to the expert execution time ( tVA-E ), so that control reactivity no longer grows linearly with the VLM scale. Importantly, this decoupling allows ARP to benefit from larger, more capable VLMs while maintaining reactivity, as shown in Fig. 1 (b). Making robot policies asynchronous, however, introduces several new challenges. The fast action expert must continuously act on a compact semantic memory produced by a slower VLM, while the robot state evolves during both VLM and action expert inference. This creates two sources of mis-match: the semantic memory may be stale relative to the current observation, and newly predicted actions may arrive after execution has already progressed. Performing the two loops asynchronously can therefore lead to outdated conditioning, delayed actions, and discontinuities across replanning cycles. ARP addresses these challenges by compressing rich VLM activations into fixed-width asynchronous memory, explicitly conditioning the action expert on semantic staleness and execu-tion delay, and training with temporally mismatched memories and action-prefix conditioning to support smooth continuous replanning. Asynchronous paradigms have been explored in prior work, mainly for cloud-to-edge VLA deploy-ment, extending the action expert’s contextual history, or smoothing execution through asynchronous action chunking (Hirose et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025). However, these approaches still retain dependencies on the main model pipeline. To our knowledge, ARP is the first truly asynchronous robotic control framework, where one can vary the rate of two processes, featuring an autonomous Vision-Action Expert. This expert is capable of executing high-frequency reactive motor commands independently while continuously synthesizing semantic updates from the asynchronous VLM. Evaluated on quasi-static Franka robot manipulation tasks, ARP achieves an 18% higher success rate while taking 20% less time than conventional VLA baselines. In dynamic environments, ARP achieves 3-5 × the performance of the baseline, successfully handling target perturbations where synchronous policies fail. Furthermore, ARP reduces computational demand by running the VLM at 10 × lower frequency with no significant loss in performance owing to the asynchronous nature of ARP. These results indicate that ARP offers a viable blueprint for bridging the gap between large-scale semantic reasoning and agile physical control. 

2 RELATED WORKS 

2.1 GENERALIST ROBOT POLICIES 

Inspired by the scaling laws and multi-modal pretraining paradigms established in Large Language Models (LLMs) (Touvron et al., 2023; Team et al., 2024; Alexandrov et al., 2024) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) (Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), VLA paradigm extend these general-ist representations to robot control. Typically, VLAs adapt pretrained VLM backbones to map visual 2Arxiv Preprint and natural language instructions directly to continuous or chunked robotic motor actions (Brohan et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Team et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Nikolov et al., 2025). Although VLA architectures represent the current state of the art in policy gener-alization, their real-world deployment is frequently bottlenecked by catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning (Dey et al., 2024) as well as limitations in heterogeneous training data scale and di-versity (Padalkar et al., 2023; Walke et al., 2023; Khazatsky et al., 2024). Another line of work relies on LLM and VLM driven program to ground the semantic reasoning in robotic primitives via code generation (Arenas et al., 2024), TAMP-based approach (Shen et al., 2026) or agentic-harness-style approaches (Chen et al., 2026b). A complementary research frontier leverages world-models (NVIDIA Cosmos Team, 2026) and video-generation models (Wan et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025) as alternative backbones in World-Action-Model (WAM). These paradigms learn predictive environ-mental representations by forecasting future states in pixel space (Hu et al., 2023), latent feature space (Chen et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2024), or directly in action space to carry out planning (As-sran et al., 2025). While this work primarily benchmarks against standard VLM-based VLA archi-tectures, ARP can be extended to make WAMs asynchronous, by incorporating world-models as the backbone for the semantic expert. 2.2 ASYNCHRONOUS SYSTEMS 

AR-VLA (Hu et al., 2026) proposes an autoregressive action expert to decouple the VLM cache from action generation. Similarly, AsyncVLA (Hirose et al., 2026) shares architectural similarities with ARP by pairing a local action expert with a high-latency remote VLA. However, while AsyncVLA is deployed to handle latency from cloud server to edge devices, ARP investigates unconstrained, process-level asynchronous execution and training approach in an end-to-end manner. Meanwhile, VLASH (Tang et al., 2025) and Training-Time RTC (Black et al., 2025) introduce training-time augmentations to compensate for VLM latency, optimizing specifically for sequential action-chunk alignment. In contrast, ARP addresses latency at the inter-process boundary, conditioning a high-frequency vision-action expert on temporally mismatched semantic memory representations. More broadly, streaming VLMs (Xu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026) and duplex-audio architec-tures (Venus Team(Ant Group), Tsinghua University, 2026) have demonstrated the benefits of asyn-chronous processing for video understanding and conversational assistants. In robotics, the Robot Operating System (ROS) (Macenski et al., 2022) established non-blocking publisher-subscriber ab-stractions as a standard paradigm. Drawing direct inspiration from ROS, ARP adopts this publisher-subscriber model to decouple high-level VLM reasoning from real-time policy execution. 

3 METHOD 

Generalist robotic manipulation requires rapid sensory-motor feedback, yet real-time execution of-ten conflicts with the heavy computational footprint of high-capacity generalist Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Coupling action generation directly to the VLM forward pass tightly ties the robot’s control frequency to the scale and latency of the underlying language backbone. To resolve this bottleneck, we propose Asynchronous Robot Policies (ARP) , an architecture that factors pol-icy execution into two asynchronous computational loops operating at distinct rates: 1. Slow Semantic Loop (Semantic Expert): Periodically processes observations through a VLM to produce a compact, fixed-dimensional semantic memory representation. 2. Fast Action Loop (Vision-Action Expert): Predicts low-level motor control actions at a high control frequency by conditioning a lightweight policy on real-time sensory inputs and the most recently available semantic memory. 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION AND SYSTEM OVERVIEW

Xt = [ Vt, S t, R, A t] ∈ RNX ×D , where Vt ∈ RNv ×D represents the visual tokens, St ∈ R1×D

This joint conditioning mechanism enables clean context tokens and noisy action tokens to be processed cohesively during iterative trajectory generation while maintaining robustness to execution delays. 3.4 TRAINING STRATEGY 

Training with Stale Semantic Memory. To render the policy robust against variable inference latencies during asynchronous execution, the Vision-Action Expert is trained on temporally mis-matched observation-memory pairs. For each training timestep t, the active visual observation It is paired with a historical observation frame Iτ sampled from an earlier timestamp τ ≤ t within the same demonstration trajectory. 4 EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

To systematically evaluate the feasibility, efficiency, and benefits of Asynchronous Robot Policies (ARP), our experiments are designed to address the following primary Research Questions :• RQ1 (Quasi-Static Performance): How does ARP compare against the standard VLA paradigm on conventional manipulation benchmarks? • RQ2 (Closed-Loop Reactivity): Does asynchronous execution improve reactivity and tra-jectory recovery under dynamic real-time perturbations? • RQ3 (Frequency Robustness): How robust is ARP when operating under varying VLM inference frequencies? • RQ4 (Training Methodology): What role does cache staleness distribution during training play in enabling policies to act on delayed semantic context? 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

LIBERO Simulation Benchmark We employ LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023) as our primary simu-lation platform for controlled ablation studies. Following the multi-task protocol, a single policy is trained across all suites, 40 tasks totaling 2,000 demonstration episodes, and evaluated with 500 episodes per suite. Rather than purely optimizing for state-of-the-art metrics, we leverage LIBERO as a controlled testbed to isolate the specific contributions of the asynchronous VLM across both quasi-static and dynamic manipulation settings. For fine-tuning, the backbone models are updated using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to maintain parameter efficiency. Regarding visual obser-vations, the VLM receives single-view inputs from the third-person agentview camera to remain strictly within the pretraining distribution of single-image vision-language models, whereas the Ac-tion Expert (AE) fuses multi-camera streams (agentview + wrist camera) to supply the fine-grained spatial details necessary for precise physical control. 

LIBERO-Perturb To evaluate real-time policy reactivity under sudden spatial shifts, we introduce this diagnostic benchmark spanning 10 tasks selected from both the Spatial and Object suites of LIBERO. This benchmark applies a deterministic 5 cm mid-episode displacement to the target object orthogonal to the approach vector once the end-effector comes within 10 cm , testing trajectory re-planning without changing task specifications. Implementation details, collision-checking rules are provided in Appendix B. 5Arxiv Preprint 

Dynamic Bump Benchmark To evaluate continuous tracking and dynamic trajectory synthesis, we introduce this benchmark, built on Robosuite simulation framework. The robot must track and grasp a moving target object (e.g., a rolling sphere or cylinder) amidst static visual distractors. During rollouts, a mid-episode velocity impulse rotates the target’s heading by up to ±100 ◦ (with probability p). Complete environment configurations, metric details, and dataset parameters are provided in Appendix C. 

DROID Dataset & RoboLab Evaluation To evaluate the scalability of ARP on large-scale, het-erogeneous robotic data, we train policies on the DROID dataset (Khazatsky et al., 2024), which comprises diverse in-the-wild real-world manipulation trajectories recorded at 15 Hz. After apply-ing standard trajectory filtering, we benchmark ARP against standard VLA architectures. Policies trained on DROID are evaluated in two distinct settings: simulation evaluation is conducted on the RoboLab evaluation framework (Yang et al., 2026), a rigorous, photorealistic simulation environ-ment that closely correlates with physical real-world performance and provides direct comparisons against state-of-the-art generalist policies. Second, we deploy these policies trained on DROID on Franka robot following the DROID-hardware setup on 3 challenging tasks. ARP vs. Vision-Gated VLA We investigate whether simply equipping the Action Expert with di-rect visual observations is sufficient to achieve latency-robust control. We find that fast-path vision alone cannot withstand semantic delay: when trained exclusively on synchronized data ( dt ≡ 0), the policy collapses under slow VLM refresh rates, as shown in Tab. 4. Substantial performance recovery emerges only when this dual-path architecture is paired with stale-cache sampling dur-ing training, confirming that staleness augmentation is an indispensable counterpart to the asyn-chronous architecture itself. Crucially, staleness training does not merely confer delay robustness, it surprisingly yields a notable boost in overall baseline performance, even under fully synchronized execution. We hypothesize that training under variable memory staleness forces the slow VLM to construct temporally invariant, anticipatory representations that act as long-horizon semantic priors, while delegating real-time, reactive visual servoing to the fast Action Expert.

5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS 

In this work, we introduced Asynchronous Robot Policies (ARP), an alternative paradigm that fundamentally models the physical robot control from traditional synchronous or state-machine pipelines toward decoupled, multi-rate asynchronous processes. By decoupling high-level semantic reasoning from low-level motor execution, ARP breaks the latency-capability trade-off inherent in generalist VLA architectures. Our extensive empirical evaluations demonstrate that ARP achieves superior closed-loop reactivity under dynamic perturbations, scalable compute efficiency. Further-more, we observe that the proposed end-to-end training pipeline of ARP leads to enhanced overall task success, which we suspect is because both proccess need to account for discrepancy. The action expert needs to act in both stale and fresh memory scenarios, and the semantic expert needs generate features which models the task at both current timestamp as well as to anticipate the future states. While ARP provides a practical framework for asynchronous policy execution, several critical de-sign questions remain open for future investigation. Immediate implementation challenges include optimizing inter-process communication, establishing full-duplex memory streams, and evaluating transient cached features against persistent memory structures for long-horizon tasks. Beyond these technical hurdles, asynchronous representations eliminate the rigid temporal synchronization con-straints that currently bottleneck Physical-AI. By enabling the seamless integration of models and multi-rate sensors operating across disparate frequencies and compute budgets, ARP opens new possibilities for heterogeneous robotic architectures. Intervention Protocol The target object is automatically identified via BDDL goal predicates. Frame sampling for the VLM and action expert. Because the VLM accounts for most of the training compute, we decouple the observation sampling frequencies between the VLM and the action expert. For a sampled frame, the VLM processes the image, state, and prompt in a single for-ward pass, producing the language-action loss and populating the key-value (KV) cache. From the same episode, we then sample multiple subsequent frames as action-expert targets. Their temporal offsets match the staleness distribution encountered during deployment, with one target aligned with the VLM’s initial frame to maintain coverage of synchronous execution. Each target carries its own camera images, proprioceptive state, action chunk, and realized age. The action expert evaluates all targets in parallel against the single cached VLM prefix. Conse-quently, one expensive VLM forward pass supervises multiple lightweight expert passes, pairing an older VLM context with fresh real-time observations, which closely mirrors the asynchronous deployment. To ensure training quality, target frames are snapped to valid frames that pass dataset quality filters, preventing the expert from training on idle or padded segments. Finally, frame selec-tion occurs on encoded images directly within the input pipeline, decoding only the selected frames to prevent data loading from becoming an I/O bottleneck. DROID ∩ RoboLab To test whether an advantage depends on a task being represented in pre-training, we partition the RoboLab tasks by how well DROID covers them. Each RoboLab task is hand-annotated with the concrete object nouns it manipulates and a goal key naming its target or interaction — a container (bin, bowl, shelf, crate, mug, pot), a relation (stack, upright, off-shelf), or bare prehension — and each key expands to a synonym list, so bin also matches basket, trash and container, and the prehension key matches pick up, grab, lift and take. We scan the natural-language instruction of every DROID episode and count that episode toward a task when the instruction con-tains a word-boundary match for at least one of the task’s object nouns and at least one of its goal phrases. Before thresholding we drop every task whose constraint type has no DROID analogue — counting, colour or size comparison, spatial relations, reorientation, ordering and category sorting — because for these the object can be well covered while the instruction the policy must satisfy is not. A task is DROID-covered when at least 100 DROID episodes co-occur with it, giving 31 tasks; intersecting with RoboLab’s own simple tier gives the 18-task split we report.
