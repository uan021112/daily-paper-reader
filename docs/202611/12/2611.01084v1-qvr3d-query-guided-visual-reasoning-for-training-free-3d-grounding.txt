Abstract
3D visual grounding localizes objects in 3D scenes from
natural language descriptions, requiring reasoning over
semantics, spatial relationships, and multi-view observa-
tions. Training-free approaches avoid costly annotations
but often rely on explicit scene transformation or scene-
level frame selection, which may waste limited visual ca-
pacity on query-irrelevant regions. We observe that effec-
tive grounding should prioritize observations that provide

1. Introduction
3D visual grounding [1, 7] aims to localize objects in a
3D scene from natural language descriptions, providing a
fundamental interface between language understanding and
spatial perception. This capability is essential for embod-
ied applications such as robotics, autonomous navigation,
human-computer interaction, and augmented reality, where
an agent must identify objects not only by appearance but
also by their spatial context and relationships to surrounding
objects. Despite the strong visual understanding capabili-
ties of recent vision-language models (VLMs) [3, 13, 42],
extending grounding from 2D images to 3D environments
remains challenging because the model must reason over
geometry, object relationships, and observations distributed
across multiple viewpoints.
Existing 3D visual grounding methods primarily follow
supervised [7, 18, 21, 23, 28, 36, 38, 40, 43, 45, 46, 49, 53]
or training-free [16, 25, 27, 47, 50] paradigms.
Super-
vised approaches learn language-to-geometry alignment
from large-scale annotated 3D data, but acquiring object-
level grounding annotations is expensive and difficult to
scale. Recent training-free methods instead leverage pre-
trained foundation models without task-specific optimiza-
tion: they typically convert 3D scenes into rendered views,
textual spatial representations, or structured scene graphs
that can be processed by 2D VLMs. While effective, these
approaches often require explicit scene transformation and
are largely designed for single-object grounding. Moreover,
frame-based 3D vision-language systems typically select
observations according to global scene coverage. Under a
limited frame budget, maximizing scene-level coverage can
allocate substantial capacity to regions unrelated to the lan-
guage query, leaving fewer informative observations for re-
solving the referred object. This raises a key question: can
training-free 3D grounding reason directly from RGB-D ob-
servations while allocating visual evidence according to the
query rather than the entire scene?
Motivated by this need, we introduce QVR3D, a query-
guided visual reasoning framework for training-free 3D
grounding, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Our key observation is
that effective grounding does not require uniformly repre-
senting the entire scene; instead, the visual budget should
concentrate on objects and regions that provide evidence for
the query. QVR3D therefore operates directly on RGB-D
video observations and detected 3D instances without ad-
ditional scene rendering or task-specific adaptation. It first
identifies candidate target and anchor objects from the lan-
guage query and introduces Query-Guided Maximum Cov-
erage (QGMC) to select complementary views that cover
query-relevant regions. The selected observations are then
used by Grounding Probability Estimation (GPE) to com-
bine instance confidence with attribute and relationship
consistency, providing complementary evidence for VLM-
based grounding selection. This formulation naturally han-
dles both single- and multi-object queries within the same
inference pipeline.
Extensive experiments on ScanRefer [7] and Nr3D [1]
demonstrate strong training-free 3D grounding perfor-
mance.
QVR3D achieves state-of-the-art results among
zero-shot methods on ScanRefer and remains competitive
with several supervised approaches.
Across challenging
splits involving multiple same-category objects, our re-
sults show that concentrating visual observations on query-
relevant regions improves fine-grained object disambigua-
tion.
We evaluate QGMC as a plug-and-play sampling
strategy for frame-based 3D vision-language systems and
observe consistent improvements across visual grounding,
dense captioning, and question answering, indicating that
query-guided observation selection generalizes beyond the
grounding task. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 1, scene-level
Maximum Coverage (MC) selects 32 frames, including 16
irrelevant views, whereas the QGMC of our QVR3D se-
lects 5, all query-relevant. On ScanRefer with Qwen3-VL-
8B in the frame-only setting without GPE, QGMC reduces
the average frames from 21.52 to 10.16 (-52.8%), and im-
proves Acc@0.25 from 42.6% to 56.1% (+13.5%). Overall,
our QVR3D achieves 56.9% with all the proposed modules,
demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed approach.

rectly operates on RGB-D observations using off-the-

2. Related Work
2.1. 3D Vision-Language Scene Understanding
Recent vision-language models (VLMs) [3, 13, 42] have
been extended from 2D images to 3D environments by in-
corporating geometric structure, multi-view observations,
or video representations.
These models support a range
of 3D vision-language tasks, including question answer-
ing [2], dense captioning [11], and visual grounding [1, 7].
Video-3D LLM [54], for example, represents a 3D scene
as a video sequence and incorporates 3D positional encod-
ing to preserve spatial information across views. It also in-
troduces maximum coverage sampling to reduce redundant
observations while maintaining scene coverage.
Despite
their strong performance, such 3D VLMs typically rely on
large-scale instruction tuning [26] and substantial computa-
tional resources. Our work instead focuses on training-free
3D grounding with off-the-shelf foundation models and di-
rectly operates on RGB-D observations without adaptation.
2.2. 3D Visual Grounding
Supervised Methods.
Supervised 3D visual grounding
methods [6, 9, 18, 20, 22, 28, 36, 38, 39, 43, 44, 48, 49]
learn language-to-geometry alignment from annotated 3D
scenes. TSP3D [18] improves grounding efficiency through
text-guided pruning, which suppresses irrelevant voxel fea-
tures, together with completion-based addition to recover
useful geometric information.
PC-CrossDiff [38] mod-
els cross-modal interactions at both point and cluster lev-
els using differential attention to enhance localization cues
and suppress spatial ambiguity. While supervised methods
achieve strong performance, they require task-specific 3D
annotations and training, motivating approaches that can di-
rectly leverage pretrained foundation models.
Training-Free Methods.
Training-free 3D grounding
methods [5, 16, 25, 27, 31] avoid task-specific training by
transforming or organizing 3D scene information into rep-
resentations that pretrained language and vision-language
models can process.
SeeGround [25] converts a 3D
scene into query-aligned multi-view renderings and com-
bines them with explicit spatial descriptions for VLM-based
grounding. Language-to-Space Programming (LaSP) [31]
formulates grounding as language-guided spatial reasoning
and uses LLM-generated programs to analyze relationships
among detected objects. View-on-Graph (VoG) [27] rep-
resents the scene as a multimodal graph and enables the
model to retrieve and reason over relevant nodes and rela-
tions. These approaches demonstrate that pretrained foun-
dation models can perform 3D grounding without task-
specific training, but generally rely on rendered views, exe-
cutable programs, or structured scene representations. Our
framework instead operates directly on RGB-D observa-
tions and detected 3D instances, avoiding additional scene
rendering or explicit scene conversion. Existing training-
free approaches also primarily target single-object ground-
ing, whereas our framework supports queries referring to
multiple target instances within the same inference pipeline.
2.3. Visual Observation Selection for 3D Reasoning
Frame-based 3D vision-language systems must select lim-
ited observations from long RGB-D sequences. Uniform
frame sampling is computationally simple but ignores scene
content and may retain redundant observations. Coverage-
based strategies address this issue by selecting views that
collectively observe a larger portion of the scene. Video-
3D LLM [54], for example, applies maximum coverage
sampling over scene voxels to improve frame utilization.
IVSGround [14] learns to select query-relevant views using
VLM-generated feedback for 3D grounding.
Scene-level coverage is independent of the language
query, so selected frames may devote limited capacity to
regions unrelated to the referred objects. This motivates
query-aware observation selection that prioritizes visual ev-
idence relevant to grounding.
Our method follows this
direction by selecting RGB-D observations according to
query-relevant scene content, as described in Sec. 3.2.
3. Method
3.1. Overview
Given an RGB-D video sequence F = {f1, f2, ..., fn} cap-
tured in a static scene and a textual description referring to
one or more target objects, our goal is to localize the de-
scribed object(s) in 3D space. We first apply an off-the-
shelf 3D instance segmentation model [37] to obtain a set
of object instances O = {o1, o2, ..., oN}. Each instance oi
is associated with a voxel-level binary mask mi, a seman-
tic category label yi, and a confidence score ci. The task is
then to identify the instance(s) that best match the textual
description d and return their corresponding 3D bounding
boxes.
Our framework proceeds in four stages (Fig. 2). First,
a large language model (LLM) parses the textual descrip-
tion into a target category, anchor categories, attributes,
and spatial relationships, which are used to retrieve candi-
date target and anchor instances from O. Second, we in-
troduce Query-Guided Maximum Coverage (QGMC) sam-
pling (Sec. 3.2) to select informative RGB-D frames by
maximizing the coverage of query-relevant objects rather
than the entire scene. Third, our Grounding Probability Es-
timation (GPE) module uses the selected frames to estimate
attribute and relationship consistency for each candidate tar-
get and combines these cues with the instance segmentation
confidence to obtain its grounding probability (Sec. 3.3).

3.2. Query-Guided Maximum Coverage Sampling
Recent training-free 3D grounding methods often convert a
scene into bird’s-eye-view (BEV) renderings or other com-
pact visual representations before reasoning with a VLM.
In contrast, we directly represent the scene using RGB-
D frames sampled from the input video.
Because mod-
ern VLMs can process only a limited number of frames,
the choice of visual observations is critical. Existing frame
sampling strategies typically maximize coverage of the en-
tire scene, which preserves global context but may allocate
much of the frame budget to regions unrelated to the lan-
guage query. This introduces redundant visual content and
reduces the amount of query-relevant evidence available to
the VLM. To address this issue, we propose Query-Guided
Maximum Coverage (QGMC) Sampling, which concen-
trates the frame budget on candidate target and anchor ob-
jects derived from the query. Rather than maximizing cover-
age over all scene voxels, QGMC maximizes coverage only
over query-relevant voxels, yielding a compact set of frames
with more concentrated query-relevant evidence and less ir-
relevant context.
Query Parsing.
Given a textual description d, we ex-
tract the semantic information required to identify the re-
ferred object(s). An LLM parses d into four components:
a target object category t, a set of anchor categories A =
{a1, a2, . . .} that provide contextual references, a set of at-
tributes P = {p1, p2, . . .} that describe properties of the tar-
get, and a set of relationships R = {r1, r2, . . .} that char-
acterize its relations to surrounding objects. For example,
given the query “It is a gray trash can. The trash can sits in
the corner by where the TV is.”, the LLM identifies “trash
can” as the target category, “TV” as an anchor category,
“gray” as an attribute, and “in the corner by TV” as a rela-
tionship.
Candidate Object Retrieval.
We next retrieve candidate
target and anchor instances by matching the parsed object
names against the semantic category labels predicted by the
instance segmentation model. Because the parsed names
may not exactly match the predicted labels due to word-
ing differences, pluralization, or minor spelling variations,
we combine fuzzy matching [24] and stem matching [35].
Fuzzy matching handles lexical and typographical varia-
tions through string similarity, while stem matching cap-
tures grammatical variants that share the same word stem.
We apply both strategies to the target category and each an-
chor category and take the union of their matched labels.
Instances associated with the matched target labels form
the candidate target set Ot, while those associated with the

3.4. VLM-Based Target Selection
Given the sampled frame set E and the grounding probabil-
ity P(oti|d) for each candidate target oti ∈Ot, we use a
VLM to make the final grounding decision. We annotate
the sampled frames with candidate object IDs and provide
them to the VLM together with the textual query and esti-
mated grounding probabilities. The VLM selects the object
ID(s) that best match the query, and the corresponding 3D
bounding boxes are returned as the final grounding results.
4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmark Datasets.
We use two popular benchmark
datasets to evaluate 3D visual grounding approaches.
ScanRefer [7] contains 51,583 human-annotated refer-
ring expressions describing 11,046 objects across 800
ScanNet [15] scenes,
with descriptions covering ob-
ject attributes, spatial relationships, and contextual cues.
Nr3D [1] contains approximately 41,503 free-form refer-
ring expressions collected on ScanNet scenes through an
object reference game, emphasizing natural language varia-
tion and fine-grained disambiguation among similar object
instances. These benchmarks evaluate 3D object localiza-
tion from language under diverse and context-dependent re-
ferring expressions.
Evaluation Metrics.
We evaluate single-object 3D visual
grounding on ScanRefer [7] and Nr3D [1] using localiza-
tion accuracy. A prediction is considered correct when the
3D Intersection over Union (IoU) between the predicted and
ground-truth bounding boxes exceeds a threshold τ, and the
metric is denoted as Acc@τ. Following the standard eval-
uation protocols, we report Acc@0.25 and Acc@0.5 for
ScanRefer, and Acc@0.25 for Nr3D.
Implementation Details.
Our framework is training-free
and uses only off-the-shelf models without task-specific
fine-tuning. We use Mask3D [37] for 3D instance segmen-
tation, X-VLM [51] to compute visual-text similarity for
attribute probability estimation, and InternVL2-8B [12, 13]
for binary relationship verification. Query parsing and fi-
nal grounding selection share the same VLM backbone; we
evaluate Qwen2-VL-72B [42] and Qwen3-VL-8B [3], us-
ing text-only prompts for query parsing. For QGMC sam-
pling, we set the maximum frame budget to K = 32 and
the coverage threshold to r = 0.95. The reference attribute
set contains 617 common attributes.

4.5. Transferability of QGMC Sampling
We evaluate the transferability of QGMC by integrating
it into Video-3D LLM [54], a frame-based 3D vision-
language model for visual grounding, dense captioning, and
question answering.
We replace its frame sampler with
QGMC while keeping all other components unchanged.
This isolates the effect of frame selection and evaluates
QGMC as a plug-and-play component without retrain-
ing or task-specific adaptation. Tab. 5 reports results on
Multi3DRefer [52]. QGMC achieves the best F1@0.25 and
F1@0.5 among the evaluated sampling strategies while us-
ing only 10.53 frames on average.
This corresponds to
less than half the frames required by Maximum Cover-
age (MC) with a coverage threshold of 0.95 and approx-
imately one-third of the 32-frame budget, demonstrating
that query-guided sampling can improve grounding accu-
racy while substantially reducing visual input. Additional
results across 3D vision-language tasks are provided in Sec.
B of the supplementary material.
5. Conclusion
We introduce QVR3D, a query-guided visual reasoning
framework for training-free 3D grounding.
By combin-
ing QGMC for query-relevant view selection with GPE
for integrating instance, attribute, and relational evidence,
QVR3D supports both single- and multi-object grounding
without task-specific adaptation. Experiments on ScanRe-
fer and Nr3D demonstrate state-of-the-art zero-shot per-
formance. QGMC generalizes as a plug-and-play sampler
across frame-based 3D vision-language tasks, improving
frame efficiency.

B.6. Generalization of QGMC Across 3D Vision-
Language Tasks
We evaluate the generality of QGMC by replacing the orig-
inal frame sampler in Video-3D LLM [54] while keep-
ing its architecture, training, and inference pipeline un-
changed.
This isolates the effect of frame selection and
evaluates QGMC as a plug-and-play component across vi-
sual grounding, dense captioning, and question answering.
Because different sampling strategies yield different num-
bers of input frames, we evaluate baselines across multiple
frame budgets and compare task performance against the
average number of frames actually processed by the model.
As shown in Fig. 4, QGMC consistently achieves a
stronger performance-efficiency trade-off across bench-
marks, recovering the performance of substantially larger
frame budgets with far fewer observations. The gain there-
fore stems from allocating limited visual capacity to query-
relevant evidence rather than processing more input. This
advantage is largest for dense captioning, where the referent
is spatially localized, and smaller for question answering,
which often requires broader scene context beyond target
and anchor regions. These results indicate that the visual
budget required for 3D vision-language reasoning is more
closely tied to the spatial extent of query-relevant evidence
than to overall scene size.

Viewpoint-Dependent Reasoning.
QVR3D does not ex-
plicitly model the reference viewpoint implied by a lan-
guage query. As shown in Fig. 5, expressions such as “the
far left” may depend on an observer orientation specified
by the query rather than the camera viewpoint. Without
explicitly recovering this reference frame, directional re-
lations such as left, right, front, and behind can be am-
biguous. Incorporating viewpoint estimation and reference-
frame-aware spatial reasoning could improve grounding for
such expressions.
Global Scene Context.
QGMC prioritizes candidate tar-
get and anchor objects to concentrate the frame budget on
query-relevant regions. This improves sampling efficiency
but may discard global context required for holistic compar-
isons. For example, identifying the leftmost object among
several similar instances requires understanding their rela-
tive arrangement across the scene. A hierarchical sampling
strategy that combines query-focused observations with a
small number of global views could better balance local ev-
idence and scene-level context.

Limited Use of Depth.
Although QVR3D takes RGB-
D observations as input, depth is mainly used for geomet-
ric operations such as 2D–3D projection, while subsequent
VLM reasoning relies primarily on RGB appearance. As
a result, depth cues are not fully exploited when reasoning
about geometric relationships. Future work could incorpo-
rate depth-aware visual representations or explicit 3D geo-
metric reasoning to better utilize the RGB-D modality.
Attribute-Aware Frame Sampling.
QGMC retrieves
candidate objects primarily through semantic category
matching before frame selection. Objects from the same
category are therefore treated similarly even when the query
contains discriminative attributes such as color, shape, or
size. Incorporating attribute consistency into candidate fil-
tering or the coverage objective could further focus sam-
pling on visually plausible targets and reduce unnecessary
observations.
These directions point toward a broader extension of
query-guided 3D reasoning that jointly considers viewpoint,
global context, geometry, and fine-grained semantics. Ex-
tending QVR3D beyond static scenes to dynamic and em-
bodied settings is another promising direction.
