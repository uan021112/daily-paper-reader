Streaming 3D reconstruction models keep past video frames in a recurrent state, fast weights from test-time training, or a key-value (KV) cache. Each written frame shifts the coordinate frame of later cameras or occupies GPU memory. Training-free rules decide what to write from a signal of the current frame. How much they write accounts for their mean accuracy: spending the update budget of a gate at uniformly spaced frames, with no signal, matches nine published gates, and the budget bounds the drift of the state wherever the writes fall. We propose SceneBound, a training-free rule that writes a frame only when its predicted camera pose lies at least a fixed radius from every stored keyframe. Its keyframe count stays within a constant factor of the covering number of the visited poses, the fewest views that keep a keyframe near every camera, so the count grows with the places visited and stays flat when the camera returns. In large scenes, SceneBound closes a submap once decoding its first frame again reveals drift. SceneBound uses one radius across four released models and three memory types. ## 1. Introduction 

A home robot vacuuming an apartment or augmented re-ality glasses worn around an office record video for hours, spending most of that time in the same few rooms. To act in that space, the device must recover 3D scene structure and track the camera. Feed-forward networks map a set of images to camera poses, the position and orientation of each view [38, 76, 79, 86], and a 3D point per pixel in one pass [35, 45, 80]. In a video stream, frames arrive in se-quence and the full video cannot fit in memory; streaming 

1arXiv:2611.00756v1 [cs.CV] 31 Oct 2026 3D reconstruction predicts the pose and 3D points of each frame online from the frames seen so far [15, 72, 77, 95]. To relate the current view to earlier views, a streaming model equips a feed-forward network with an online memory of past frames [8, 43, 49, 82]. These are the same memories that language models use to carry context. CUT3R [77] compresses past observations into a fixed-size recurrent state 

of latent tokens updated at each step, following recurrent networks and state-space models [28, 30]. ZipMap [32] stores past frames in fast weights , layer parameters updated by gradient descent at inference through test-time training [5, 46, 66]. StreamVGGT [95] and STream3R [37] append the attention features of each frame to a growing key-value (KV) cache , the memory that transformers use to attend to past tokens [70, 83, 92]. Writing a frame into any of these memories carries a cost. Each update to a recurrent state or fast weights shifts the coordinate frame in which later cameras and points are expressed, so the trajectory drifts from the true path. In a KV cache, each stored frame occupies GPU memory, and the cache of StreamVGGT fills 80 GB within 10 seconds of video. Both costs grow with the number of frames written, and the rooms stay the same size. Training-free rules manage the memory of a frozen net-work at test time by reading a signal from the current frame to decide how much to write. In recurrent states, update gates scale each update by a gain computed from attention weights or state uncertainty [14, 21, 33, 93], reliability and information content [4, 40, 42, 58], or motion and geometric cues [1, 19, 61, 71]. In KV caches, compression rules keep the highest-scoring tokens of each frame [48, 53, 54, 65], keep a fixed number of tokens or frames [12, 51, 85, 89], or index kept frames by place [78, 87, 96]. These rules share a premise: a sharper signal of the current frame makes a better memory. Like a visitor sketching the living room on a fresh page every time they pass the sofa, a camera returning to a known room produces the same signals, so these memories write the room again and grow with the video, or, at fixed capacity, forget the earlier visit (Fig. 1). To test this premise, we remove the signal and keep the amount written, following budget-matched baselines in net-work pruning and language-model caches [47, 50, 75]. Our 

budget-matched baseline spends the update budget of a gate, the sum of its gains on a video, at uniformly spaced frames with no image signal. On our long-sequence benchmark, this blind schedule matches nine published gates with a geometric-mean error ratio of 0.99, so their signals add no mean accuracy beyond their budget. We prove that when the expected coordinate shift per write keeps a direction, the drift of every rule stays within a constant factor of its budget wherever writes fall, and measured drift grows almost in pro-portion to the budget. In the fast weights of ZipMap, a sparse schedule cuts the error of writing every frame by nearly an order of magnitude. The right question for a memory rule is therefore how much to write. A fixed interval answers it by counting frames, rewriting a revisited room on every visit and leaving views uncovered when the camera moves fast. We propose SceneBound, a training-free rule that lets the scene set the amount, following the keyframe prin-ciple of visual simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM) [9, 18, 25, 36]. Visual SLAM stores a view as a keyframe when the camera moves far enough from stored ones and culls redundant keyframes so the map grows with the scene [10, 24, 34, 55]. SceneBound writes a frame into the memory of a frozen network as a keyframe when its predicted pose lies at least a fixed radius from every stored keyframe, and decodes closer frames from the unchanged memory. Because each frame is compared with all stored keyframes, the keyframes lie near the best placement for their count, which stays within a constant factor of the cov-ering number of the visited poses, the fewest views keeping a stored view near every camera [22, 27]; the count thus grows with the places visited and stops growing on revis-its, regardless of video length. The poses come from the model’s own pose head, so the test needs no extra network pass and applies to any memory that can keep or drop a frame. In large scenes, where one state drifts as the visited region grows, SceneBound splits memory into submaps , as SLAM does [6, 20, 23, 52], and closes the active submap once its first frame, decoded again by the frozen model, moves beyond a threshold, a check needing no ground truth. With one radius across CUT3R, ZipMap, StreamVGGT and STream3R, SceneBound achieves the lowest mean abso-lute trajectory error (ATE) among the published training-free rules we evaluate in the recurrent state of CUT3R and the KV cache of StreamVGGT, in one pass and over nine passes of a scene. Over those repeated passes, its keyframes grow by 3% to 6%, published gates multiply their initial budget, and fixed-capacity caches multiply their error. 

## 2. Related work 

Streaming 3D reconstruction. Streaming models extend multi-view feed-forward networks [35, 38, 86] with online memories of spatial tokens, keyframes or KV caches [37, 43, 49, 82]. Models trained for long videos learn what to keep through persistent tokens, hybrid memories or fast weights [16, 39, 46, 90], and recent ones read their memory through test-time training, geometric context or long-horizon attention [13, 17, 29, 32]. SceneBound decides, without training, what enters the memory of a released model. 

Training-free memory management. Training-free rules filter the updates of a recurrent state [19, 61, 71], evict or merge cache tokens [2, 31], or index memory by place in a state [91] or a cache [12, 78, 87, 96]. Keyframes and submaps. Classical SLAM bounds its map by keyframe insertion and culling [10, 24, 25] and by submaps in large scenes [6, 23], and learned systems optimise keyframes or chunks in a back end that closes loops [20, 52, 56, 68] across local and global maps [41, 74]. SceneBound applies the test against all stored keyframes to the memory of a frozen network and bounds it by the covering number of the predicted poses.

3.2. The budget determines the drift 

To see what a gate signal adds, we first ask how much drift the update budget W causes by itself. The state increments 

ξt are nearly uncorrelated, so the change that adds up lies in the coordinate frame that st encodes, and we measure it through a read-out ϕ, the pose of x0 decoded again from st

with no write. With B the largest increment norm and L the Lipschitz constant of ϕ, a write of gain βt moves st by at 3most Bβ t and ϕ by at most LBβ t. When each write pushes 

ϕ on average along a common direction, the coherent parts add in proportion to W , and the unpredictable parts add like a random walk.

The budget thus confines expected drift to a factor of 

LB/κ , wherever writes fall and whatever signal sets the gains, and a larger budget raises drift without limit. Compar-ing a constant gain W/N (Q = W 2/N ) with full updates (Q = W ) at equal budget separates the two terms: on 7-Scenes and Neural RGB-D, full updates at our keyframes have 22 times the Q of a constant gain and after 500 frames shift the pose of x0 by 0.92 and 1.11 times as much. Across five constant gains and three full-update rules, this shift grows with exponent 1.2 in W , against 0.5 for increments without a common direction, and stays within a factor of 2.4 per unit budget (Fig. 2a), as in the fast weights of ZipMap. Within this band, a signal can lower drift at a fixed budget by writing where the expected change is small or giving small gains where the random part is large. To test whether published signals do this, our budget-matched baseline takes the sums (W, Q ) of a gate on a video of N gated frames and applies one gain g = W/J at J uniformly spaced frames, with J the integer in [⌈W ⌉, N ] closest to W 2/Q . Its budget is W , and its sum of squared gains W 2/J lies within 0.3% of Q across all 549 gate-sequence pairs. Reading no frame, its gap to the gate measures what the signal adds at the gate’s chosen budget; we mark constructed baselines with †. On the 61 benchmark sequences, the baseline matches nine published gates from eight papers (Fig. 2b): the ratio of its mean ATE to the gate’s ranges from 0.92 for AFG-Img to 1.06 for TTSA3R, with a geometric mean of 0.99, and lies within resampling noise for seven of the nine. This ratio is 1.04 in the second released checkpoint at 224 pixels, 0.98 on held-out Replica and 0.96 under the periodic reset of TTT3R. Under that reset on full-length KITTI drives [26] and TUM RGB-D sequences, the signals add accuracy, and the baselines reach a geometric mean of 1.12 and 1.13 times the gate error. The budget thus sets mean accuracy on the benchmark, and the increments set the form of update: at equal budget, full updates every 10 to 150 frames reach 1% to 18% lower ATE than a constant gain (Fig. 2b), whose increments 8 to 16 frames apart repeat one push at a gain of 0.05, with a mean inner product of 0.46 times their mean squared norm. 

3.3. Spatial keyframes 

The decoder localises a frame against stored views of the same place, so a state needs one view near every camera, and by the budget bound further writes add drift. SceneBound therefore lets the visited poses set the budget (Fig. 3). When the predicted pose of frame t lies at least M from every stored keyframe, we apply a full update, as CUT3R is trained with, and store the pose as a keyframe. Closer frames skip the update and are decoded from the unchanged state; as for the gates, the pose memory receives every frame. Because each keyframe lies at least M from earlier ones, no open ball of radius M/ 2 holds two keyframes.

3.4. Submaps from measured drift 

On a long trajectory, the covering number grows with the visited region, and with it the drift bound of one state. Pe-riodic resets and the chunks of VGGT-Long [20] close a state at fixed frame counts; SceneBound closes it when it measures drift. is the single-submap variant. After each keyframe write, the

3.5. Fast weights and the KV cache 

ZipMap [32] replaces global attention by test-time training: each frame updates the fast weights of every global block by a normalised gradient step and is decoded with them. After decoding a non-keyframe, we restore the fast weights to their values before it, so each frame applies a full update or a skip, as the budget bound treats. In the KV caches of StreamVGGT [95] and STream3R [37], every camera is expressed in the coordinate frame of x0, whose keys and values never change. A written frame leaves the coordinate frame in place and costs memory, and the number of re-tained frames sets the error (Fig. 2d, e). ## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental setup 

Implementation. Methods in the recurrent state share one evaluator and the released 512-pixel CUT3R checkpoint. Cache methods and ZipMap run released checkpoints with official streaming settings in the RegVGGT [54] evaluator, ZipMap with a one-frame online window. AFG-Img and AFG-Pose are the image and pose gates of AFG, Info3R in full adds its resets to its gate, TTT3R with reset restarts every 100 frames, and cache memory counts the GPU memory beyond the stored input frames. Where papers report TUM-dynamics at 1,000 frames, our runs lie within 13% of their values, OVGGT within 22%, our implementations of AFG-Pose and Info3R included. Constructed baselines (†) replace the keyframe test of SceneBound: a fixed interval writes every 50 frames, the best of seven intervals from 10 to 150 frames in the state on the test sequences, uniform sampling 

spreads the count of SceneBound evenly over each video, a count known only after the run, and the latest keyframe tests the radius against the most recent keyframe alone. We chose the keyframe radius M = 0 .3 from 0.15, 0.2 and 0.3 on 15 TUM sequences including the eight from TUM-dynamics; a later benchmark sweep from 0.2 to 0.5 is lowest at 0.3 and within 1% up to 0.4. We set the depth unit of d on the benchmark and τ = 0 .1 on seven TUM RGB-D sequences, fixing all three across models and datasets. Inference is deterministic, so each configuration runs once. 

Datasets and metrics. Our long-sequence benchmark applies the TTT3R [14] pose protocol (stride 1, up to 1,000 frames) to 61 sequences (51,256 frames) from TUM-dynamics [64], Bonn [57], 7-Scenes [62] and Neural RGB-D [3]. Revisit sequences play 24 sequences forward and backward nine times, 17 from TUM-dynamics and Neural RGB-D in every memory and seven from 7-Scenes in the state. Concatenated rooms stream all sequences of six 7-Scenes rooms in turn, returning to each along a new path. At full length, we evaluate on 19 TUM RGB-D and 11 KITTI odometry sequences [26], and once with fixed constants on four held-out sets: eight Replica scenes [63], 11 EuRoC [7], 61 ETH3D SLAM training [60] and 19 further TUM RGB-D sequences. We report ATE in metres after similarity align-ment [69], fused-point accuracy, memory and frame rate. 

4.2. Main results 

We ask whether the count set by the visited poses is as accurate in one pass as the best tuned amount, and whether memory and accuracy hold on revisits (Tab. 1). In one pass, SceneBound has the lowest mean ATE in both memories, 9% below Info3R in full and 18% below RetrieveVGGT, and ranks first or second in each of the eight dataset columns. On TUM-dynamics, where we chose M , both its values lie below every value reported at 1,000 frames, including that of the trained LoGeR [90]. Every 50 frames reaches 0.084 in the state, and the best interval in the cache, every 25 frames, matches SceneBound with 2.7 GB more memory (Fig. 2b, e); outside TUM-dynamics, SceneBound lies within 2% of every 50 frames in the state. SceneBound thus finds online the amount that a sweep of intervals tunes on the test sequences. Placement also matters: at the same count, uniform sampling and random frames raise the state’s error by 12% and 13%. Over nine passes, the keyframes of SceneBound grow by 3% in the state and 6% in the cache, and every other rule pays in update budget, memory or accuracy. In the state, published rules end at 1.5 to 9.7 times its error, and the closest, the Info3R gate, spends 11 times its budget. In the cache, the fixed-capacity InfiniteVGGT and GHOST forget the room, their error more than doubling after the first pass; FrameVGGT takes over twice the memory of SceneBound, and OVGGT holds less at 2.4 times its error. On the concatenated rooms, where new paths bring new poses, SceneBound reaches 0.139 against 0.142 for AFG-Pose and every 50 frames. On the Bonn sequences where a person moves boxes, it reaches 0.085 against 0.094 for the Info3R gate, since each frame’s points come from its own image. 4.3. Four released models and four frame rates 

We test whether one radius transfers across memory types and camera speeds. In ZipMap, SceneBound writes 24 frames per sequence and cuts the error from 0.590 to 0.089 (Fig. 2c); every 50 frames and uniform sampling at its count reach 0.083 and 0.084, so the count brings the gain. In STream3R, SceneBound reaches 0.075, ahead of every fixed interval and of uniform sampling at its count (Fig. 2d), and in both models its writes grow by at most 1% over nine passes. To vary camera speed along the same paths, we feed every second, fourth and eighth frame of the benchmark, scaling the motion per frame by ℓ (Fig. 2f). At stride 8, SceneBound writes about 15% fewer frames in every model and changes its error by at most 6%; every 50 input frames more than doubles its error, and a fixed time interval stays within 5% of SceneBound and writes again on revisits. A first frame facing a far scene raises the error by 3%, and one facing a near wall, at half or a quarter of the depth unit, writes more frames and raises the error by 11% and 20%, as the budget bound predicts at larger budgets. A unit that rises with the median depth of later keyframes, never falling, keeps the count bound and brings these to 2% and 3%. One radius thus serves three memory types and an eightfold range of camera speeds. 

4.4. Long sequences and held-out data

## 5. Conclusion 

The update budget of a training-free memory rule fixes its drift up to a constant factor wherever the writes fall and sets 8its mean trajectory error on the long-sequence benchmark, so a rule must first decide how much to write. SceneBound lets the visited poses decide, placing keyframes near the best for their count, so the amount it writes follows the scene and stops growing on revisits, with one radius across recurrent states, fast weights and KV caches in four released models and an eightfold range of camera speeds. Its drift check cuts the drift of one state on long trajectories, where the remaining error lies in placing submaps, a task for a back end with independent loop verification, and scenes that change call for a test that retires stored views.

B.4. The increments 

We decode frame 0 again with the current state and no write, which returns the pose that the state now assigns to its first camera, and the distance of this pose from the pose at the start measures the displacement of the coordinate frame. Over five constant gains and three rules of full updates, the slope of the logarithm of this displacement versus the logarithm of the budget is 0.63, 1.00, 1.41 and 1.76 on TUM-dynamics, Bonn, 7-Scenes and Neural RGB-D after 500 frames, as in Fig. 2a, and 0.98, 0.98, 1.34 and 1.37 after 1,000 frames. Over all four datasets the slope of the geometric mean is 1.2 after 500 frames, and the shift per unit budget stays within a factor of 2.4 across the eight rules, the band of Theorem 1; increments without a common direction would give a slope of 0.5. For the ratio test of Sec. 3.2, SceneBound writes 23.0 and 22.5 full updates per sequence over the first 500 frames of 7-Scenes and Neural RGB-D, so a constant gain of equal budget has Q = W 2/N , 22 times less, and a dominant uncorrelated random part would give √22 ≈ 4.7 times its shift. Interpolated in log budget between the constant gains of 0.03 and 0.05, the keyframes shift the first camera by 0.92 and 1.11 times as much after 500 frames and by 0.93 and 1.53 times after 1,000 frames. The Gram matrix of the increments in Tab. 5, which we compute from one increment in eight along each sequence, is short-ranged and grows as the gain falls. At gains of 1 and 0.3, the state increments are therefore nearly uncorrelated, with a common part near zero, and the change that grows with the budget is the coherent change of the read-out that Theorem 1 bounds. At small gains, neighbouring increments repeat one push, and the moments of the increments thus differ between schedules. Two experiments test what the gains say about the to-kens. Permuting the gains of a gate over the state tokens of each frame preserves their distribution, their mean and their course over time, and results in 0.92 to 1.11 times the error of the gate for TTT3R, TTSA3R, FILT3R and AFG-Pose, with MeMix at 0.96 for random tokens of its count. A keyframe that reaches part of the state, either with each token at the TTT3R gain of the frame or as a full update of the half of the tokens with the largest gain, is above the full update of SceneBound (0.0805) in all forms. The half with the largest gain is also above a random half at both radii tried, with 0.230 versus 0.123 and 0.263 versus 0.116.

C.5. The KV cache 

We run the cache rules in the released StreamVGGT check-point through the code and evaluator of RegVGGT (com-mit 3ec82dbe , the 518-pixel crop of its pose evaluation, bfloat16) and score each predicted pose as the pose of the camera in the world. The pose script of RegVGGT scores the inverse, the pose of the world in the camera. Scored that way, our run at 1% of keys reproduces the nine values of its Table 1 at 800, 900 and 1,000 frames within 0.5%. The rules run the cache unregulated, with all keys of all layers, and decide once per frame, after the camera head, whether the frame stays. SceneBound stores the frame when, with the depth unit of frame 0, its predicted camera is at pose distance 0.3 or more from every kept frame. Uniform sam-pling selects frame 0 and the same number of later frames at the spacing of Proposition B.1, and a fixed interval selects frames a fixed number apart. A frame that leaves the cache is replaced by an empty tensor, which frees its storage. The camera head of StreamVGGT keeps its own cache, four re-finement steps of four blocks per frame; the fixed intervals, uniform sampling and SceneBound of the main tables prune it, so a frame that leaves the aggregator’s cache leaves this cache too. Pruning the camera head lowers the mean ATE of SceneBound from 0.0870 to 0.0831 and of a frame every 25, 50, 100 and 200 frames by 2% to 5%; with the camera head kept whole, as the published caches run, SceneBound is 15% below RetrieveVGGT and ahead of all seven published caches; the radius sweep keeps the camera head whole. We retuned nothing for this model. The peak GPU memory of a run includes the model and its activations, the cache and the input frames, which the eval-uator of RegVGGT stores on the GPU in float32 at 2.35 MB per frame for 378 by 518 pixels. We report the peak less the stored input frames in all tables. A least-squares fit over the runs of SceneBound, its uniform sampling and the interval of 50 frames yields about 255 MB per kept frame, and the full cache fills the 80 GB GPU at frame 287 on all 61 benchmark sequences. XStreamVGGT (commit 3ec1d8d6 )keeps, in every global layer, the first frame, the current frame and the tokens between whose keys best match the queries of the frame, up to 2,048 tokens, and prunes the cache of its camera head to the same length. D.2. Protocol and published values 

We run all rules of the recurrent state through one evaluator, the pose, depth and reconstruction scripts of the CUT3R re-lease as packaged with ReCal3R, at 512 pixels and stride 1, with at most 1,000 frames on the long-sequence benchmark and at full length elsewhere. The checkpoint, the preprocess-ing, the frames and the alignment of the metric are identical across rows, and inference is deterministic. On the full-length TUM RGB-D sequences, all RGB frames are fed to the model and the frames with a true pose within 20 ms are scored. For KITTI, we use the grey left camera of the odom-etry benchmark, staged from a public mirror and checked against the official archive. Rows with official code reproduce the published values within 13% and OVGGT within 22%, for the reasons in Sec. C.5 (Tab. 18). Table 19 sets SceneBound beside the values that published methods report on TUM-dynamics at 1,000 frames, trained ones included. FILT3R reports 0.057 on TUM-dynamics at 800 frames, our port obtains 0.074 at 1,000, and MeMix and AFG-Img report no value at 1,000 frames. Since AFG-Pose and Info3R have no released code, we implement their equations, and our implementations agree with the papers on TUM-dynamics. On KITTI, our AFG-Pose with the periodic reset of TTT3R is within 20% of the paper on six of the 11 sequences, and our Info3R with the constants its paper specifies for driving is above the published value on 10 of the 11 sequences, with 72.3 against 30.5 m in the mean. The releases of CUT3R and TTT3R with reset, run on the same grey frames, lie within 7% of the values that AFG reports for them on drives 02 to 10, so the camera changes little for the model, and the gap lies with the two implementations. MeMix, the two AFG gates and BAT3R scale the token gain of TTT3R by a mask, a frame gain or further factors, and five of the nine gates therefore share that token signal. RayMap3R resets its state at intervals of 100 frames and smooths the output translation, and the similarity alignment of its resets calls an unimported function, so in the release each segment of 100 frames keeps its own coordinate frame. Our runs import this function; as released, RayMap3R reaches 0.3797 on the benchmark against 0.1699 with the function imported. The gate of BAT3R needs an untrained network that the CUT3R checkpoint lacks, and its release therefore draws this network afresh in each process. Our port fixes draw 0, which yields 0.069 on TUM-dynamics, compared with 0.157 and 0.143 for draws 1 and 2. With full updates at a fixed interval after an opening window, we reproduce the published TTT3R baseline rows at intervals of 100 and 150 within 15%; the published row at an interval of 50 is 0.150 and ours is 0.090.

D.8. Frame rate 

We feed the first 1,000 frames of each benchmark sequence to all four models at every first, second, fourth and eighth frame, so that the camera visits the same poses in 1,000, 500, 250 and 125 frames, and every constant stays as it is. At every frame, the runs give the values of Tab. 16 for SceneBound, AFG-Pose and the gate of Info3R. D.11. The assumptions beside their measurements 

We measure each assumption of the theorems on the stored runs (Fig. 8). • A read-out that drifts in a fixed direction (Theorem 1 and Proposition A.4). The shift of frame 0 grows with exponent 1.2 in the budget and stays within a factor of 2.4 per unit budget across eight rules (Sec. B.4). • Consecutive poses less than δ < M apart (Theorem 3). On each stream, 99.3% to 100% of the steps fall below M ,and one step exceeds M on 13 of the 38 streams. • Covering numbers of the predicted poses (Theorem 2). In the state, 1,421 ≤ Nd(X, M/ 2) ≤ 2,231 , and in the cache, 571 ≤ Nd(X, M ) ≤ 965 compared with 1,433 keyframes, a ratio of 1.49 to 2.51. • A place receives the same predicted pose on a return (The-orem 2). In the cache, 99.5% of later-pass frames are within M of their first-pass pose (Fig. 7). • Predicted poses within ε < M/ 2 of the truth (Proposi-tion A.2). This holds for 72% of Replica frames, for nine in ten frames on four of the 19 TUM RGB-D sequences and on no KITTI drive. • The amount stays fixed as the video grows. The revisit sequences take 678 writes in one pass and 698 in nine. Of the 49,823 frames that SceneBound skips on the bench-mark, 15.4% are nearest to a keyframe other than the latest one, 33% on TUM-dynamics and 2.6% on Neural RGB-D. The benchmark thus tests exploration, and the revisit sequences test the returns. On the revisit sequences, the covering number of the predicted poses at half the radius grows by 23% from one pass to nine, and the number of writes by 3%. In one state, submaps lower the median dis-tance between predicted and true pose on 13 of the 19 TUM RGB-D sequences and on all 11 KITTI drives, where the median over the drives falls from 9.6 to 1.4. 

## E. Limitations 

Our analysis covers the recurrent state of two released checkpoints of CUT3R, the fast weights of ZipMap and the KV caches of StreamVGGT and STream3R. STAC, Re-trieveVGGT and OVGGT report that scored retention beats random retention; of the scored caches, we compare only RegVGGT with a signal-free rule at equal budget. The keyframe test and the drift check act on predicted poses, so their guarantees carry pose accuracy as a condition. A frame whose predicted pose jumps beyond M from its predecessor is written as a new view, and on one of the 17 revisit sequences in the cache such writes raise the error of all later passes (Sec. B.5). Every later pass reads these misplaced views, as errors in the experience that a language-model agent distils into skills persist into its later tasks [88]. A first frame that sees the scene closer than the frames after it sets a finer depth unit for the whole video and adds writes (Tab. 9). A return finds a stored submap by predicted pose up to an accumulated drift of one keyframe radius, and place recognition on the stored keyframes extends returns beyond this radius. The error of SceneBound on long drives lies in placing its submaps (Tab. 12); a pose graph whose loops the frozen model confirms by its own reads raises this error (Tab. 13), so global placement needs a back end with inde-pendent place recognition and geometric verification. In the KV cache on Neural RGB-D, RetrieveVGGT and OVGGT, which read tokens of every earlier frame, fuse more accurate clouds than SceneBound (Sec. D.10). SceneBound keeps one state of about 18 MB per submap, so its memory grows with the visited region; a closed submap is read only on a return and can wait in host memory, where each return adds a host-to-device transfer, a cost that lies on the critical path of decoding when mixture-of-experts language models offload their experts [11]. Moving people enter memory as the rest of the scene does, and a stored view stays in memory after the scene it shows has changed; longer-term changes call for a test that retires stored views, as the persistent memory of a language-model agent resolves a new fact that contradicts a stored one [81].
