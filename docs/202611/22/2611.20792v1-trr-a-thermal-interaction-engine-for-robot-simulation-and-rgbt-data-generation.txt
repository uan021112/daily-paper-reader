ABSTRACT 

A robot’s actions change the thermal conditions of its next interaction: heat retained after release, carried with objects or stored beneath the surface can change a later contact’s outcome despite similar current temperatures. Training trajectories must capture how these effects connect earlier actions to later observations and task outcomes. We present TRR, a Thermal Robot Renderer that attaches spatial and depth-resolved thermal state to both contact partners, updates local heat exchange through contact, transport and separation, and generates synchronized RGB–thermal (RGBT) observations and event-local task checks on one execution clock. Controlled interventions show why retained heat, spatial transport and internal storage matter for subsequent contact outcomes. From the same state, TRR produces a paired six-task benchmark of 2,224 static–dynamic pairs, that is, 4,448 trajectories with synchronized dual-view observations, states and actions. Visible thermal history raises safe contact-site selection from 75.0% to 91.7% on a dedicated learning corpus, and training on dynamic histories raises simulated transfer completion by 50 percentage points under a new heating procedure with shared feedback execution. We demonstrate RGBT observation adapters on MuJoCo and Isaac Sim, report an engineering inventory of ten prepared robot configurations and six demonstrated camera viewpoints, and describe a developer workflow that carries an existing robot scene to exported RGBT records. The benchmark, the workflow and the platform resources described in Section 6 form the first items of a planned public release. Demonstrations and resource updates are available on the project page. 

# 1 Introduction 

A robot lifts a hot container, carries it across a workspace and sets it down. The gripper exchanges heat with the container, the original support retains a thermal trace, and the destination meets a surface altered by the journey. The robot has changed the thermal conditions of its own next action. Thermal manipulation already uses temperature to choose actions [40, 54, 56], and training data for these decisions has to record how each action changes the conditions and outcomes of later contacts. States that look equivalent under the current sensor can lead to different outcomes under the same next action. A trajectory generator that generalizes across these effects has to carry thermal state on the material itself, through changing contacts and visibility, so that its observations and task labels agree with the state each action meets. For a developer, this is what turns a robot scene into a source of thermally grounded RGB-thermal (RGBT) data: the same frame sequence that shows a robot carry an object also explains why the later contact succeeded or failed. We present TRR, a Thermal Robot Renderer built around persistent material-associated thermal state. Object-local thermal cells retain spatial patterns and internal storage as bodies move. Changing contacts couple local regions on both partners through equal-and-opposite heat exchange; separation removes the coupling while preserving both fields. The same evolving state supplies camera observations and event-local task checks on a shared execution clock, and parameterized assets and camera models initialize and calibrate a scene from real measurements. A user gains three things from this design: RGBT trajectories whose labels reflect the thermal conditions each action actually met, paired static–dynamic comparisons that isolate the effect of thermal evolution on a fixed motion, and a labeled view of where in a scene heat is retained, transported or stored. 2.1 Thermal interaction in robot simulators 

Thermal simulation and sensing cover household state and scalar temperatures [24, 25], decision-relevant evolution [57], contact-dependent response [1, 2, 8] and fire–radiation coupling [46]. Tactile Genesis diffuses heat in robot-link grids, coupling sensing voxels to a scalar opposing-link temperature [7]; TRR resolves local patches on both partners and ties their surfaces to imaging and task events. 

2.2 Thermal imaging and measured scenes 

Gazebo and Stonefish render thermal observations from assigned temperatures [10, 11]; DIRSIG, ThRend and OKTAL-SE add physical scene and sensor models [5, 23, 34, 35, 37]; digital twins integrate finite-element thermal solvers [20]. PhysIR-Splat models temperature, emissivity, radiation and camera response [9]; neural fields reconstruct thermal views [14, 27, 52], and ETGS models their evolution [49]; HEARTH maps measured temperatures for fixed-field rollouts [42]. Many of these systems supply physically rich thermal or infrared observation for a prescribed temperature field; TRR couples measured initialization, camera modeling and the action-dependent evolution of the observed surface during grasping, transport, separation and recontact. Visible–infrared datasets and fusion pair observations [13, 16, 18, 38, 43]; manipulation also requires matching observations to contact events. A surface can be occluded during exchange and visible only later; TRR retains the observations and checks the intervening contact. 

2.3 Thermal manipulation and data 

Thermal robotics studies contact and multimodal decisions [3, 4, 40, 41, 54–56], servoing [15] and partially observed safety [22]. Data-generation systems reuse motion and vary tasks [6, 19, 26, 30, 32, 44, 51]; TRR augments trajectories with thermal validity checks and RGBT labels. Task- and language-conditioned benchmarks organize goals [17, 28, 31], while recorded datasets add scale [21, 33, 47]. Appendix A compares broader workflows. 

# 3 Method 

3.1 How TRR works in plain terms 

TRR keeps a temperature map that belongs to each body rather than to a fixed region of the scene. Every body carries a grid or layered set of thermal cells, each holding a temperature and a heat capacity, and the cells travel with the body as it moves. During an execution, three steps run on every control cycle and a fourth runs at observation times and at contact events: 1. Read the mechanical state. The backend reports body poses, which surfaces touch, how hard they press, and the elapsed simulation time. Contacts are translated into pairs of material cells, one on each body. 2. Exchange heat where bodies touch. A touching pair of cells swaps energy in proportion to their temperature difference and to the contact area, with equal and opposite amounts added to the two partners, so exchange between the two is conservative. Heat entering from sources and leaving to the environment is accounted for separately, as step 3 describes.

Separating two bodies stops step 2 for that pair and retains both temperature maps for continued evolution, so a later contact anywhere reads the heat left earlier. A scene-specific thermal baseline and camera calibration set the starting map and the mapping to image values from real measurements, and changing these inputs adapts TRR to a new scene without changing the update above. 3.2 Interaction-centered design 

A task specifies geometry, materials, initial temperatures and mechanical and thermal requirements; controllers or demonstrations supply actions, and the robot backend supplies poses and contacts. TRR maps each interface to material regions on both bodies, updates their heat exchange, and retains both states through motion and separation (Figure 1). At time 𝑡 , mechanical state x𝑡 places the material cells, and active contacts couple their temperatures T𝑡 . Thermal integration advances over elapsed mechanical time; Section 3.3 gives the update, Section 3.4 the observation sampling, which runs at each configured observation time, and Section 3.5 the checks, which are evaluated at contact events. This gives a heated region a stable material identity while its world position, partners and observation change. Scene configuration specifies geometry, layered materials, contact resistances, task requirements and camera settings. Real measurements supply a scene-specific thermal baseline and camera calibration. Adapting a scene changes these inputs while retaining the contact mapping and conservation equations below.

3.5 Task-conditioned RGBT generation 

Matching actions to conditions. Each task defines targets and thermal requirements at decision events. A source demonstration or task-specific controller supplies the target and grasp; Cartesian perturbations with inverse kinematics vary the path, and selection variants obtain condition-matched targets from the motion source. Every candidate and replacement is executed and checked against the evolving field. A controller can also use thermal state to change timing: in cooling-and-transfer, a rule-based expert waits and starts grasping when object temperature reaches a threshold; the expert reads physical state while the camera separately generates observations, so the evolving physical state determines timing and the camera records the corresponding appearance during the same executed motion. 

Event-local checks. Object checks test the selected identity; region checks query the actual contact location at contact time; timing checks specify a region, statistic, threshold and window and require the action within it; mechanical checks assess grasp, transport, release and placement. # 4 Experiments 

Experiments trace the connection from thermal state to observations, task outcomes and learned decisions. Following analytical contact and diffusion checks (Appendix C.1), interventions ask which state distinctions change the next contact. Measured observations and camera-driven timing test the connection to sensing. Paired trajectories then isolate changes in task validity, and separate learning corpora connect thermal training data to safe contact choices and successful robot transfer under a new heating procedure.

Spatial state before and after transport. We first shift a 24 × 24 mm footprint between L and R on a stationary plate, keeping material, initial temperature, area and 20 s contact duration fixed. After 40 s of separation, patch means are 34 .41 /25 .00 ◦C for left contact and 25 .00 /34 .41 ◦C for right contact (Figure 5a). The same distinction matters when the material moves. We heat a plate’s left patch for 20 s, translate it by 80 mm at either 0 ◦ or 180 ◦ yaw, and contact the same receiver. Just before contact, the warm and opposite patches are 39 .56 and 25 .00 ◦C. Rotation changes initial receiver heat flow from 2.72 W to below 0.001 W; a scalar plate with matched capacity, conductance and exposed area predicts 0.139 W for both orientations (Figure 5b). The receiving interface must query the arriving material region, rather than a world-fixed field or body mean that omits the transported heat pattern. 

Internal storage behind a similar surface. Two otherwise identical 1 cm 2, 6 mm slabs start at 25 ◦C. One receives a 0.05 s surface pulse and the other 180 s heating, followed by controlled cooling and 0.1 s natural release. 5.2 An end-to-end MuJoCo use case 

The transfer execution of Appendix E.1 runs on this platform, so we follow it end to end. It also appears in Figure 3 and Figure 13. 

Input. A Piper arm in MuJoCo handles a hot aluminium shell at 70 ◦C with two layered supports and two finite gripper pads, in a 25 ◦C environment and a 10,503-node thermal network. Layered materials set heat capacity and contact resistance, initial temperatures set the shell and support fields, and one external camera sets the equivalent observation model and its display range. 

Execution. The controller waits for the shell to cool to 40 ◦C. At the step before 168.8 s the shell reads 39 .993 ◦C, so the expert starts its approach there, grasps and lifts the shell, which uncovers support A at 174.8 s, transfers it and later exposes the destination trace at 217.3 s. The motion runs at 500 Hz. At each step the backend reports contacts, TRR maps each footprint to cells on both bodies, and conservative exchange, lateral conduction and convective cooling advance the thermal state on the mechanical clock, while the camera samples the configured observation times and the checks read the contacted cells.

5.4 Scene-specific calibration and asset reuse 

Parameterization lets the same thermal representation serve different scene configurations. Material properties and initial temperature distributions specify the physical state, while camera parameters specify how that state appears in an observation. This separation gives developers distinct controls for changing an interaction and changing its observation. A scene can therefore be adapted through its configuration while retaining the underlying state update and record generation. The Piper example illustrates a practical calibration route. Measured thermal images, the robot geometry and camera alignment are used to construct a spatial thermal asset and fit the observation response. The resulting field is attached to the robot’s material coordinates, allowing articulated pose changes to reuse it. The earlier demonstration includes compact, extended and raised configurations; the measured comparisons and fitting procedure are provided in the experimental sections and appendices. For engineering use, this provides a concrete path from a measured setup to a reusable scene asset. 

5.5 Backend integration and configurable observations 

The backend adapter connects scene geometry, body poses, contact information and camera configuration to the thermal representation. The robot simulator supplies mechanical evolution; TRR evolves the corresponding thermal state and renders observations at the configured camera times. MuJoCo and Isaac Sim examples in Figure 3 show this observation interface on different robot scenes; the two adapters cover RGB and thermal observation, while the dynamic interaction studies of Section 4 run in MuJoCo. Camera placement and response are separate configuration choices. External views can show the whole task, while wrist views follow the local interaction. The recorded temperature arrays support numerical processing; rendered thermal images support inspection and camera-response studies. RGB, thermal observations and execution records retain their temporal association, so a developer can inspect the image that accompanies a particular contact or action interval.

5.6 From execution records to demonstrations and learning data 

Records reach downstream use through three routes: a contact trace shows when and where exchange occurred, thermal observations show its visible consequences, and task checks connect those consequences to the selected object and interaction interval. For demonstrations, the existing material covers concurrent contacts, transfer between supports, cooling after separation and later recontact. For data generation, the paired static–dynamic protocol holds the mechanical trajectory fixed while changing thermal evolution. Downstream packaging then selects the observations, robot states and actions required by the learning interface; the LeRobot and robomimic loading checks provide concrete examples of this last step. Together, the workflow diagram and its case, the asset inventory, the configurable thermal and camera models, the backend adapters and the structured records give three entry points into TRR: a robotics developer can build a thermally instrumented scene; a perception researcher can study observations across views and camera settings; and a learning researcher can use generated trajectories or decision windows. All three use the same connection between interaction, evolving thermal state and recorded observations that underlies the research results.

6.5 Outlook 

The results point to several directions that follow directly from the engine. 

• Calibrated dynamic digital twins. Extend measured initialization and camera calibration to identify dynamic heat-transfer parameters from aligned real and simulated records. 

• Heating-dependent task programs. Generate heating, quenching and reheating histories systemati-cally to study how prior use changes the next decision. 

• Thermal data for embodied policy learning. Extend the decision studies to full robot policies, more objects and materials, and real RGBT observations. 

• Force- and roughness-dependent contacts. Introduce contact-resistance models that respond to normal force and surface roughness, evaluated through the same contact-power interventions. 

• Broader platform coverage. Add robot configurations, backends and data interfaces while retaining the shared workflow. 

# 7 Conclusion 

TRR attaches thermal state to material regions on both contact partners and carries the spatial and internal heat storage that actions leave behind. Contact, reset, transport and depth interventions show how this state changes a later exchange and its safety consequence. A common execution clock connects that evolving state to camera observations, event-local checks and paired RGBT records. A dedicated TRR-generated image-to-site corpus supports safe choices from visible thermal history. Regional decisions with shared feedback execution improve simulated transfer completion with dynamic training data under a new heating procedure. Through reusable assets, two backend adapters, a configurable thermal camera and structured records, the same engine takes a robot scene to thermally labeled data and to executed task evaluation. The paired benchmark, the developer workflow of Figure 11 and the resources of Section 6 give external teams a concrete starting point, and the project page brings demonstrations and resource updates together.

B.7 Model boundaries and validation scope 

Physical approximations. Interface conductance uses a fixed area-normalized resistance per material pair. Normal force can activate contact, but neither force nor roughness changes this resistance. Convection uses a fixed coefficient; radiation and reflected radiance use broadband approximations. The present checks therefore do not constitute an exhaustive sweep of convection coefficients, emissivities or view factors. 

Measured observation validation. The Piper study evaluates a scene-adaptation workflow: measured thermal fields define an asset baseline, measured temperature–grayscale pairs fit the camera response, and the resulting configuration is fixed for ten scoring poses excluded from fitting. The scoring images come from one M600 capture session. Apparent-temperature and grayscale errors are reported separately in Appendix C.8. The mixed manifest also contains 28 real recordings used for reader and integrity checks. 

Dynamic validation. Analytical contact and diffusion references check numerical accuracy and energy conservation (Appendix C.1); controlled interventions test the consequences of retained thermal state. Measured post-separation cooling traces provide an ordinal check on decay (Appendix C.9). The available physical setup differs from the simulated contact scenes in task objects, contact geometry and observation viewpoints. These measurements support thermal-asset calibration, camera-response evaluation and exposed cooling comparisons. Joint identification of dynamic heat-transfer parameters would require aligned real and simulated records of object geometry, contact footprints, boundary conditions and measurement times. Such matched contact records are outside the current acquisition.

C.7 Native regional decisions with shared feedback execution 

This study evaluates the task utility of simulated thermal training data with mechanical execution shared across arms. The coupon study evaluates contact-site choices; this study evaluates their consequences during robot execution. The task is bottle cold-region transfer. A learned infrared classifier chooses site 0 or site 1 once, before motion. Its action dispatches the corresponding original geometric primitive, whose feedback controller supplies subsequent joint targets from the native state. No task oracle corrects the choice. The controller’s legacy names “cold” and “hot” identify geometric primitives, not their actual temperatures or the correct answer. Heating can reverse which primitive is safe.

# F Reproducibility Manifest 

The release package is organized into six parts: (i) simulator core with layered assets, contact exchange, state transport and the camera/observation model; (ii) asset and measured-calibration data; (iii) task definitions and label generation; (iv) mixed-corpus manifests and fixed train/test splits; (v) training and evaluation scripts for forecasting and policy diagnostics; and (vi) scripts that regenerate each table and figure. Each study records its source split, training seed or deterministic model specification, evaluation cohort, and code/model versions. Saved per-decision records retain the inputs, choices and execution references used to regenerate the reported results. Environment versions and command lines are listed in the release README. Together, the saved records and regeneration scripts identify the configuration, seed and scoring unit behind each reported table and figure.
