Abstract
Robot policies trained by imitation generalize poorly outside the environments and objects seen
in training. Published data-scaling studies find that generalization grows with the diversity
of training environments and objects rather than with more demonstrations per environment.
Robot data is costly to collect across many environments, whereas egocentric human video
can be recorded in the wild at scale; existing egocentric video, however, mostly shows daily-life
activity and contains little skilled work in real workplaces. We present HubEgo-100K, a dataset
built for diversity. This version of the paper covers the first release, v1.0: 10,124 hours of 1080p
egocentric video with synchronized 100 Hz inertial (IMU) data from one head-mounted phone
camera. In v1.0, 12,227 contributors record their own unscripted work in 15,548 environments
(each one contributor’s own setting of one type, such as their kitchen or workshop) across 11
setting types, 30 work categories, 56 task types and 35 action families, with 83% of hours in
workplace settings. The release comprises 72,754 episodes with machine-generated action and
language annotations, in LeRobot and MCAP formats under CC BY 4.0. We measure diversity
along each axis, show that inside v1.0, with episodes in random order, new contributors and
words keep appearing, and relate the measurements to published evidence on six data properties
tied to policy performance, including hand visibility and inertial sensing. We also document
collection, curation, privacy measures and data format, and provide a datasheet. Later versions
of this paper will add benchmarks and cover further releases toward 100,000 hours.

Introduction
Robot manipulation policies trained by imitation gener-
alize poorly outside the environments and objects seen in
training (Khazatsky et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024). Pub-
lished data-scaling studies find that generalization grows
with the diversity of the training data rather than with its
volume alone. Lin et al. (2025) report that generalization
follows a power law in the number of training environ-
ments and objects, while additional demonstrations per
environment saturate; experiments with DROID (Khaz-
atsky et al., 2024) and π0.5 (Black et al., 2025a) show
the same dependence on scene diversity (Section 4.1).
Robot data, however, is costly to collect across many
environments.
DROID gathered 350 hours across 564
scenes over 12 months with 50 data collectors (Khazatsky
et al., 2024), and generalist policies are still trained on
pooled robot data: 970k demonstrations from Open X-
Embodiment for OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024), and data
from 7 robot configurations and 68 tasks together with
Open X-Embodiment for π0 (Black et al., 2025b).
Egocentric (first-person) human video can be recorded
at scale without robot hardware (Hoque et al., 2026), and
robot foundation models have begun to use it: GR00T N1
trains on human egocentric video alongside synthetic and
real-robot data, and learns from this action-free video
through latent actions (NVIDIA et al., 2025). Egocen-
tric human video improves visual representations for ma-
nipulation (Majumdar et al., 2023; Nair et al., 2022; Ra-
dosavovic et al., 2022) and, when co-trained with a small
amount of robot data, improves policy generalization to
new scenes, objects and tasks (Kareer et al., 2025, 2026;
Qiu et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025).
Existing egocentric datasets were mostly collected to
study daily-life activity (Grauman et al., 2022), kitchens
(Damen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2018), or scripted and
instrumented tasks, mostly in lab or tabletop settings
(Hoque et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2022; Sener et al., 2022;
Wang et al., 2023b). Skilled manual work in real work-
places, such as bricklaying, wiring, plumbing, garment
sewing, microsoldering, hair cutting or engine repair, in-
volves tools, materials and multi-step procedures, and is
not the primary focus of these collections. Ego-Exo4D
(Grauman et al., 2024) targets skilled activity explic-
itly, with a multi-view capture rig in 123 scene contexts.
Egocentric-10K and Egocentric-100K (Build AI, 2025a,b)
release head-mounted video of factory and manual labor
at 10,000 and 100,405 hours.
HubEgo-100K spans 30
work categories, of which manufacturing and assembly is
one (5.4% of hours), and pairs every episode with per-
frame data from the phone’s inertial measurement unit
(IMU) and with task types and machine-generated action
and language annotations.
HubEgo-100K is an egocentric video dataset of skilled
work recorded in the wild, and it is built for diversity
(Fig. 1). Contributors record a named, unscripted task
with a head-mounted phone during their own work, in
their own workplace or home, with their own tools and
materials.
By skilled work we mean trade, craft and
service tasks done as an occupation.
This paper de-
scribes the first release, v1.0 (10,124 hours of 1080p video
with synchronized inertial data from one head-mounted
phone camera), and measures its diversity axis by axis
(Fig. 10, Table 4): 12,227 contributors; 15,548 environ-
ments across 11 setting types, with 83% of hours in work-
place settings and 17% in households; 30 work categories
and 56 task types; 35 action families; machine-generated
descriptions that use 1,429 verb and 3,709 noun lemmas;
and 56 phone models. An environment is one contribu-
tor’s own setting of one type (their kitchen, their work-
shop), so environments of the same type are separate
physical places recorded by different contributors, and
their count is a close stand-in for the number of sites
(contributors who share a workplace count once each).

Related Work
Egocentric
video
datasets.
EPIC-KITCHENS
(Damen et al., 2018, 2022) recorded unscripted activity
in participants’ own kitchens with head-mounted cam-
eras and introduced verb and noun classes for actions.
EGTEA Gaze+ (Li et al., 2018) paired cooking video
with gaze.
Ego4D (Grauman et al., 2022) collected
3,670 hours of daily-life video from 931 camera wearers
in 74 locations and defined benchmarks over episodic
memory, hands and objects, and forecasting. Ego-Exo4D
(Grauman et al., 2024) captured skilled activities from
synchronized first- and third-person views. Assembly101
(Sener et al., 2022), HOI4D (Liu et al., 2022) and HoloAs-
sist (Wang et al., 2023b) focus on procedural assembly,
category-level hand-object interaction and guided tasks,
respectively, with 3D hand annotations. EgoDex (Hoque
et al., 2026) recorded 829 hours of tabletop manipula-
tion with Apple Vision Pro hand tracking.
EgoVerse
(Punamiya et al., 2026) pools egocentric demonstrations
from academic and industry groups for robot learning,
and Egocentric-10K and Egocentric-100K (Build AI,
2025a,b) release head-mounted video of manual labor.
Robot datasets and generalist policies.
Large
robot datasets (Brohan et al., 2023; Khazatsky et al.,
2024; Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024) report
the effect of data diversity on generalization, as does π0.5
(Black et al., 2025a). Lin et al. (2025) measured how im-
itation learning generalization scales with environments,
objects and demonstrations, using Diffusion Policy (Chi
et al., 2023), the policy class also used in DROID. Gener-
alist policies such as OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) and π0
(Black et al., 2025b) are trained on pooled robot data,
and GR00T N1 (NVIDIA et al., 2025) adds synthetic
data and human video to the mixture.
Human video for robot learning.
Human video
serves as a source of supervision in several ways: pre-
trained visual representations (Dasari et al., 2023; Ma-
jumdar et al., 2023; Nair et al., 2022; Radosavovic et al.,
2022), affordances and hand-object contact mined from
video (Bahl et al., 2023; Srirama et al., 2024), retar-
geting of human hand motion (Bahl et al., 2022; Qin
et al., 2022), co-training of policies on human and robot
data (Kareer et al., 2025; Qiu et al., 2025; Tao et al.,
2025; Wang et al., 2023a), latent-action pretraining (Ye
et al., 2025), and vision-language-action (VLA) models
pretrained on human video (Kareer et al., 2026; Long
et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Yang
et al., 2025). We review their quantitative findings in
Section 4.

The HubEgo-100K Dataset
This section describes how v1.0 was collected, curated,
annotated, packaged and released. Table 2 lists its head-
line statistics, and a datasheet (Gebru et al., 2021) is
given in Appendix A.
Terminology.
A recording is one continuous capture
by one contributor. A segment is a continuous part of a
recording kept after curation, and an episode, the unit of
the dataset as in LeRobot, is a segment or, for a segment
longer than 30 minutes, one of its equal parts. A con-
tributor is the person wearing the camera, called camera
wearer or participant in other egocentric datasets; con-
tributor is the usual word for people who supply data
to a crowd-sourced collection. A setting type is one of
11 kinds of place, such as a workshop or a construction
site (Fig. 7), comparable to the scene types of DROID
(Khazatsky et al., 2024); ten of them are workplace set-
tings and the eleventh is the household. An environment
is a distinct pair of contributor and setting type, that is,
one contributor’s own setting of one type. A contribu-
tor’s sites of one setting type count as one environment,

2. Head-mount check. Footage that was not recorded
with the phone worn on the head is detected from
motion and camera signals and removed.
3. Dense temporal segmentation. A vision-language
model segments every recording in time, and only the
segments in which the contributor’s hands perform the
named task are kept. The model-estimated on-task
share of each episode is stored with its annotations
(on_task_share; 96.9% hours-weighted).
4. Hand-visibility gating.
A panel of two vision-
language models describes each recording at one frame
per second and labels every sampled frame as showing
no hand, the left hand, the right hand, both hands, or
unclear. A frame counts as showing a hand when at
least one model reports one, and unclear frames count
as showing none. Recordings in which the hands are
mostly out of frame are dropped. Two further rules ap-
ply: a recording must reach a minimum share of sam-
pled frames with both hands, and every long stretch
without hands is cut out. The model-estimated shares
of sampled frames with at least one hand and with
both hands are stored in the catalog (hands_share,
hands_share_both).

dren, faces of other people and private text (docu-
ments, screens, cards, license plates). Every flagged
frame of a child, and every run of two or more flagged
frames of a face or private text, is cut out with a 5-
second margin; segments with a single flagged face or
text frame are withheld (Section 3.4).
6. Episode assembly.
Each remaining segment be-
comes an episode.
Recordings made at 30 fps are
stream-copied without re-encoding, starting at the
first keyframe at or after the segment start, and their
frames keep the recorded capture times. Recordings
made at 60 fps are re-encoded to constant 30 fps
(libx264, CRF 18, one keyframe every 30 frames); out-
put frame k is the source frame nearest to t0 + k/30 s.
Segments longer than 30 minutes are split into equal
parts.
7. Integrity and synchronization checks.
For ev-
ery episode the pipeline measures the median and
maximum frame interval, counts the intervals longer
than 1.5 times the nominal interval and the frames
repeated by re-encoding, and computes the drift be-
tween the real duration and the frame count at 30 fps
(median absolute drift 0.017 s).
Sensor timestamps
are re-expressed in nanoseconds from the first video
frame of the episode, so video and sensors share one
timeline (Section 3.5). These signals are stored per
episode in the catalog.
8. Task rebalancing. Hours are rebalanced across the
56 task types so that no task type dominates; the
median task type holds 69.5 hours.
9. Annotation and packaging. The annotation model
labels every episode (Section 3.3), and the episodes are
written as a LeRobot v3 dataset with one MCAP file
of native sensor streams per episode (Section 3.5).
A recording yields one episode per kept segment or part:
v1.0 contains 41,688 recordings and 72,754 episodes, 1.75
episodes per recording on average, and every episode
lasts at most 30 minutes.

Privacy and Ethics
Each contributor agreed that their recordings may be
used to train and evaluate AI and robotics systems. Be-
fore release we removed:
• segments where a child, another person’s face or pri-
vate text (documents, screens, cards, license plates)
was detected, cut with a 5-second margin; and record-
ings in which faces fill a large share of the footage;
• episodes in which the video annotation detected a mi-
nor;
• care tasks performed on another person;
• audio, location, device identifiers and contributor
identity. Contributor and recording identifiers in the
release are pseudonymous strings; the phone make and
model are kept as capture metadata.

Which Data Properties Matter:
Evidence and Measurements
This section reviews the published evidence on six data
properties. Table 3 sets selected findings next to the cor-
responding measurements on HubEgo-100K v1.0, which
Section 5 reports in full. Numbers about other work are
quoted from the cited papers; numbers about HubEgo-
100K are computed from the released tables and are
hours-weighted unless noted. Several of the cited studies
vary robot or hand-held-gripper demonstrations rather
than egocentric human video (Black et al., 2025a; Khaz-
atsky et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025); we use them to select
the properties to measure.
4.1
Environment and Object Diversity
Evidence. Lin et al. (2025) collected over 40,000 hu-
man demonstrations with hand-held grippers and exe-
cuted more than 15,000 real-world rollouts to measure
scaling in imitation learning. They report that “the gen-
eralization performance of the policy follows a roughly
power-law relationship with the number of environments
and objects” and that “the diversity of environments and
objects is far more important than the absolute num-
ber of demonstrations”. For 8, 16 and 32 environment-
object pairs, performance plateaus at 400, 800 and 1,600
demonstrations, about 50 per pair, and collecting in 32
environments with one object each and 50 demonstra-
tions yields a policy with about 90% success on new en-
vironments and objects. In their environment-by-object
sweep (their Fig. 6), the normalized score on Pour Wa-
ter with one object per environment rises from 0.14 with
one training environment to 0.74 with sixteen, while with
sixteen environments, adding objects per environment
no longer helps (0.74 with one object, 0.73 with four).
The authors recommend collecting “in as many diverse
environments as possible”. A power-law fit against the
total number of demonstrations is weak (correlation co-
efficients r = −0.62 and −0.79 for the two tasks in their
Fig. 7).
Robot datasets point the same way. DROID (Khaz-
9


atsky et al., 2024) contains 76k trajectories (350 hours)
from 564 scenes and 52 buildings;
co-training with
DROID improves success by 22 points in distribution
and 17 points out of distribution over the next best al-
ternative, and a size-matched subset with fewer scenes
performs worse out of distribution, which the authors
read as “the strength of DROID lies in its size and espe-
cially in its diversity”. Open X-Embodiment pools data
from 22 robots and reports that RT-2-X, trained on the
pooled data, improves emergent-skill success from 27.3%
to 75.8% (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024),
which is evidence for cross-embodiment training rather
than for environment diversity.
For π0.5, Black et al.

number of unique samples and find that performance in
unseen homes “generally improves with more training
locations”, with the 104-location model approaching a
model trained on the test environments. Xie et al. (2024)
find that factors well covered in training data, such as
backgrounds, stop being a bottleneck for generalization,
while camera position remains the hardest factor.

Evidence. R3M (Nair et al., 2022) pretrained on Ego4D
improves success on 12 simulated manipulation tasks by
over 20% compared to training from scratch and by over
10% compared to CLIP and MoCo; on a real Franka
arm with 20 demonstrations per task, it reaches 56%
against 24% for CLIP, and the authors attribute part
of the gain to the data itself (34% to 42% when switch-
ing from ImageNet to Ego4D under the same objective).
MVP (Radosavovic et al., 2022), whose 4.5M pretraining
images include 2.6M Ego4D frames, outperforms CLIP
by up to 75% and supervised ImageNet pretraining by
up to 81%, and notes that “larger models require big-
ger datasets”.
VC-1 (Majumdar et al., 2023), trained
on more than 4,000 hours of egocentric video, finds that
scaling data size and diversity helps on average but not
universally, and that adding diversity helps more than
adding size. Dasari et al. (2023) report that ImageNet,
Kinetics and 100 Days of Hands are competitive with
Ego4D and that the image distribution matters more
than size, a caution that motivates curation (Section 4.3).
For policies, EgoMimic (Kareer et al., 2025) reports
that adding human embodiment data improves task score
by 34 to 228% relative to robot data alone, and that 2
hours of robot data plus 1 hour of human data outper-
forms 3 hours of robot data (128 vs. 74 points), with
1,400 human demonstrations per hour against 135 robot
demonstrations.
Co-training with human data in Qiu
et al. (2025) raises out-of-distribution success from 34.7%
to 59.4%, and a human demonstration takes 4.8 to 7.6
times less time to collect than humanoid teleoperation.
DexWild (Tao et al., 2025) reaches 68.5% success in un-
seen environments, nearly four times robot-only training,
with human data from 93 environments; it also reports
that too high a human-to-robot ratio degrades perfor-
mance, so robot data remains necessary for grounding.
MimicPlay (Wang et al., 2023a) uses 10 minutes of hu-
man play per environment, which the authors equate to
about 3 hours of teleoperation. LAPA (Ye et al., 2025)
pretrains on human manipulation video through latent
actions and outperforms a VLA pretrained on robot data
from Bridge. Among VLA pretraining efforts, EgoVLA
(Yang et al., 2025) raises long-horizon success on unseen
backgrounds from 11.21% to 28.79% in simulation, Being-
H0 (Luo et al., 2026) reports real-robot gains of 10 to
45 points per task from human-video pretraining, and
GR00T N1 (NVIDIA et al., 2025) includes 2,517 hours
of human egocentric video in its pretraining mix. Kareer
et al. (2026) find that human-to-robot transfer “emerges
once the VLA is pre-trained on sufficient scenes, tasks,
and embodiments”, nearly doubling performance on set-
tings seen only in human data (for example 32% to 71%).

4.3
Hand Visibility and Curation
Evidence. Hands are the interface between a human
demonstration and a robot policy.
Shan et al. (2020)
built 100 Days of Hands from 131 days of internet footage
with 100K annotated frames (189.6K hands) labeled with
contact state.
HaMeR (Pavlakos et al., 2024) recon-
structs 3D hands as MANO meshes (Romero et al., 2017)
from 2.7M training examples, of which only 5% come
from in-the-wild datasets, and reports a 2 to 3 times im-
provement in PCK@0.05 on in-the-wild data over prior
work.
VISOR (Darkhalil et al., 2022) provides dense
hand-object segmentation for 36 hours of kitchen video.
VRB (Bahl et al., 2023) learns affordances from hu-
man hand-object contact and reaches 57% average suc-
cess against 25% for the next method; HRP (Srirama
et al., 2024) injects hand, contact and active-object la-
bels mined from 150K Ego4D videos into pretrained en-
coders and improves real-robot success by at least 15%
across five tasks, raising an Ego4D-MAE baseline from
0.51 to 0.73.
Direct evidence on curation comes from Long et al.
(2026), who co-train a VLA on robot data and action-free
Ego4D video. Of 3,976 raw hours, a camera-stability gate
keeps 1,893 hours (48%) and an additional visible-hand
gate keeps 975 hours (25%). “Training with raw Ego4D
clips underperforms the robot-only baseline, whereas the
clips retained after both gates surpass it”; the stability
gate alone “provides at most a small improvement”, and
adding the visible-hand gate gives a further gain. The
reported differences are about one to two percentage
points.

4.4
Task Breadth and the Long Tail
Evidence.
In RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2023), “removing
25% of the tasks while keeping 97% of the data achieves
an equivalent generalization performance to reducing the
dataset size by as much as 49%”, and the authors con-
clude that “data diversity is more essential than data
quantity”. BC-Z (Jang et al., 2021) scaled data collec-
tion to more than 100 tasks and performed 24 unseen
tasks with 44% average success without demonstrations
for them. VC-1 finds that adding a different kind of data
(navigation) to Ego4D helps more than adding more ma-
nipulation data (Majumdar et al., 2023), and EgoVLA
reports that increasing the scale and diversity of human
pretraining data “consistently improves downstream per-
formance” (Yang et al., 2025). In the opposite direction,
at a fixed budget of 200K clips, Long et al. (2026) did not
observe a consistent gain from increasing scene, object or
interaction diversity alone.
Measured in HubEgo-100K. See Sections 5.2, 5.5
and 5.6.
4.5
Contributor Diversity
Evidence. The evidence on the diversity of demonstra-
tors is less direct than for environments. Ego4D made
diversity of “people, places, objects, and activities” an
explicit design goal and recruited 931 camera wearers, in-
cluding occupations such as bakers, carpenters, landsca-
pers and mechanics (Grauman et al., 2022). For robot im-
itation at small scale, the robomimic study (Mandlekar
et al., 2021) finds that 300 demonstrations from six op-
erators of varying proficiency yield lower success than
200 demonstrations from one experienced operator, and
Belkhale et al. (2023) find that less consistent actions at
a given state are often associated with lower policy suc-
cess.
BC-Z (Jang et al., 2021) similarly observes that
pooling data from several robots and operators makes a
single task harder to learn, and that pooling across many
tasks overcomes this. No controlled study has yet varied
the number of human demonstrators at fixed hours for
egocentric pretraining; the per-contributor identifiers of
HubEgo-100K make such a study possible, for example
with contributor-disjoint splits.
Measured in HubEgo-100K. See Section 5.1.

Work, tasks and actions.
The recordings span 30
work categories and 56 task types, all unscripted, and
the annotation model assigns 35 action families and
18 skill groups.
A task type names a kind of job, a
broader unit than the single manipulation tasks counted
by task-oriented datasets, which report 20 guided tasks
for HoloAssist, 194 tabletop tasks for EgoDex and 1,965
tasks for EgoVerse (Hoque et al., 2026; Punamiya et al.,
2026; Wang et al., 2023b); within a task type, the finer
grain is carried by the descriptions.
Language, objects and devices.
The verb and noun
vocabulary of the descriptions has a long tail (Sec-
tion 5.5), and its nouns reflect the diversity of objects.
The footage comes from 56 phone models, which behave
like 15.8 equally used ones.

5.5
Language
The annotation model writes one free-text description
per episode, so most episodes receive their own descrip-
tion (the 72,754 episodes carry 62,605 distinct descrip-
tions, 93.6% of which occur once; 80.5% of episodes have
a description shared with no other episode), and we mea-
sure breadth on their vocabulary rather than on the num-
ber of distinct descriptions. Verb and noun lemmas are
extracted from the descriptions with a part-of-speech tag-
ger (spaCy en_core_web_sm): the descriptions use 1,429
verb lemmas and 3,709 noun lemmas, of which 685 verbs
and 2,110 nouns appear in at least five descriptions. The
most frequent verbs are trim, wipe, assemble, cut, apply
and pack, and the most frequent nouns wall, car, floor,
machine and brush. Word frequencies follow a Zipf law
with slope −0.99 between ranks 10 and 1,000 (Fig. 15,
right).
To measure how quickly new content appears
inside v1.0, we shuffle episodes at random and count dis-
tinct descriptions, verbs and nouns as hours accumulate
(Fig. 15, left, shows five such orders). The number of
distinct descriptions rises almost linearly along such an
order (a power-law fit beyond the first 100 hours gives ex-
ponent 0.97, and new descriptions appear at 5.8 per hour
after 90% of hours), as expected for free text written per
episode. Verb and noun lemmas accumulate sublinearly
(exponents 0.34 and 0.29), and new words keep appear-
ing: the last 10% of hours adds about 39 verb lemmas
and about 84 noun lemmas on average over 20 random
episode orders.

5.7
Episodes and Recordings
v1.0 contains 10,124 hours and 1,093,194,072 frames of
1920 × 1080 video at 30 fps, recorded from the contribu-
tor’s head with an ultra-wide lens (Section 3.1). Episodes
are long: the median is 4.9 minutes, the 90th percentile
21.4 minutes, and 72.8% of hours lie in episodes longer
than 10 minutes (Fig. 11, left). Episodes cut from one
recording share a recording identifier and carry their start
and end time within it, so longer procedures can be re-
assembled. The step-level instruction of each episode pro-
vides a language target of the kind used in VLA pretrain-
ing.
5.8
Hand Visibility and On-Task Share
Hand visibility and on-task share are model estimates:
hand visibility is the share of sampled frames in which
the model panel of the curation pipeline reports a hand,
and the on-task share comes from the temporal segmen-
tation model (Section 3.2). The pipeline keeps the seg-
ments in which the contributor’s hands perform the task.
Over all hours, at least one hand appears in a model-
estimated 94.7% of sampled frames and both hands in
76.3% (Fig. 2b); for the median episode the share with
at least one hand is 95.8%. 70.6% of episodes, holding
83.0% of hours, show a hand in at least 90% of their
sampled frames. The model-estimated on-task share is
96.9% hours-weighted (median episode 98.9%; 39.1% of
episodes are estimated to be entirely on task), and 92.2%
of hours lie in episodes with an on-task share of at least
90% (Fig. 2c). Both estimates are stored per episode, so
users can apply stricter thresholds. The model-estimated
share of sampled frames with both hands varies by action:
91.0% for Peel & shell, 87.2% for Fasten & connect and
86.3% for Sew & thread, against 52.2% for Sweep & vac-
uum.

Applications
GR00T N1 organizes its training data as a “data pyra-
mid” (NVIDIA et al., 2025): web data and human video
form the base layer, which provides “broad visual and
behavioral priors”, synthetic data the middle, and real-
robot data the top, which grounds the model in real-
robot execution. HubEgo-100K is intended for that base
layer. The data is action-free; established ways to use
such data for policy learning are co-training with a small
robot dataset, pretraining on human data followed by
fine-tuning on robot data, and training a visual encoder
on the human data (Hoque et al., 2026). A contributor-
disjoint train, validation and test split, stratified by task
type, is available on request, so that evaluation measures
generalization to unseen contributors and environments.
• Visual representation pretraining for manipula-
tion, following R3M, MVP and VC-1 (Majumdar
et al., 2023; Nair et al., 2022; Radosavovic et al., 2022),
evaluated on established robot benchmarks.
• Hand and hand-object pose. Pseudo-labeling 3D
hands with HaMeR (Pavlakos et al., 2024) as MANO
parameters (Romero et al., 2017) and contact with the
100DOH detector (Shan et al., 2020), which in turn
supports affordance learning (Bahl et al., 2023; Sri-
rama et al., 2024) and hand-motion pretraining (Luo
et al., 2026).
• Action recognition over the 35 action families or the
56 task types, with video, IMU or both (Gong et al.,
2023; Moon et al., 2023).
• VLA pretraining and co-training with latent ac-
tions (NVIDIA et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025), future
visual-state prediction (Long et al., 2026) or human-
to-robot co-training (Kareer et al., 2025, 2026), using
the step-level instructions as language targets.
• Video prediction and world models.
Minutes-
long episodes of real work with step-level language can
serve language-conditioned video prediction and world
modeling, a use that EgoDex also names for egocentric
manipulation data (Hoque et al., 2026); GR00T N1
uses video generation models to synthesize additional
training trajectories (NVIDIA et al., 2025).
• Head motion and visual-inertial odometry from
the aligned camera and IMU (Qin et al., 2018). The
catalog gives the lens and diagonal field of view of
every episode.
7
Broader Impact
HubEgo-100K is meant to help robots learn manual
skills from people who practice them.
Models trained
16


on recordings of trade work may automate parts of the
trades shown. Contributors agreed to this use of their
recordings, and the privacy measures of Section 3.4 are
applied to every episode before release. After rebalanc-
ing, the task mix of v1.0 does not reflect how often each
task occurs in the world, so models trained on it should
be evaluated in the conditions in which they will be used.
8
Conclusion
We presented HubEgo-100K, an in-the-wild egocentric
dataset of skilled work whose main property is diversity,
and described its first release, v1.0: 10,124 hours of un-
scripted work recorded by 12,227 contributors in 15,548
environments across 11 setting types, 30 work categories
and 56 task types. We measured this diversity axis by
axis; inside v1.0, with episodes in random order, new
contributors and words keep appearing. We related the
design of the dataset to published evidence on environ-
ment and object diversity, egocentric human video, hand
visibility, task breadth, contributor diversity and iner-
tial sensing, and reported the corresponding measure-
ment for each property from the released tables.
The
dataset, its catalog and its annotations are available un-
der CC BY 4.0.
Discussion and future work.
Subsequent releases
will extend HubEgo-100K to 100,000 hours (Section 3.6).
The release layout is built to grow: later releases can
add other capture setups as sibling folders of mono/ (Sec-
tion 3.5), and annotation layers such as 3D hand pose
and hand-object contact (Section 6) can be added per
episode under the same episode_index key. Our next
step is to train baselines on the data: visual encoders
pretrained on HubEgo-100K and policies co-trained with
robot data, evaluated on contributor-disjoint splits, to
measure how the diversity reported here carries over to
policy performance. Later versions of this paper will add
these experiments and cover subsequent releases.

A
Datasheet for HubEgo-100K
This datasheet follows the questions of Gebru et al. (2021) and
describes HubEgo-100K v1.0 (10,124 hours), the first release of a
dataset planned to reach 100,000 hours.
Motivation
For what purpose was the dataset created?
Robots that manipulate objects in homes and workplaces need
training data that covers the variety of those places, objects and
tasks. Existing egocentric datasets focus mostly on daily-life activ-
ity, kitchens, or scripted and instrumented tasks. HubEgo-100K
was created to provide egocentric video of skilled manual work (for
example bricklaying, wiring, plumbing, garment sewing, microsol-
dering, hair cutting or engine repair), recorded by contributors in
their own workplaces and homes. It is intended for research and
development of perception, representation learning, action under-
standing and robot learning systems (Section 6).
Who created the dataset and on behalf of which entity?
Hub Labs (Hub Data, Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA). The authors
of this paper are listed on its first page. All recordings are first-
party: every hour was recorded by a Hub contributor, and the
release contains no third-party episodes.
Who funded the creation of the dataset?
Hub Data, Inc.
Composition
What do the instances that comprise the dataset repre-
sent?
An instance is an episode: a continuous segment of head-mounted,
egocentric video in which a contributor performs a named manual
task, together with the phone’s sensor data and a set of annotations.
Episodes are cut from recordings; one recording can yield several
episodes. Recordings and contributors are referenced by identifier
(recording_id, contributor_id).
How many instances are there in total?
v1.0 contains 72,754 episodes from 41,688 recordings (1.75 episodes
per recording on average), for 10,124 hours and 1,093,194,072
frames, recorded by 12,227 contributors.
We count 15,548 envi-
ronments, defined as distinct pairs of contributor and setting type
(Section 3). The video totals 43.5 TB and the native sensor logs
1.29 TB.
Does the dataset contain all possible instances or is it a
sample of instances from a larger set?
It is a curated subset of what contributors recorded.
The cura-
tion pipeline keeps the segments in which the contributor’s hands
perform the task, privacy filtering removes content (see Preprocess-
ing), and hours are rebalanced across task types so that no task
type dominates.
What data does each instance consist of?
One MP4 video (1920 × 1080, H.264, 30 fps, monocular), the
accelerometer and gyroscope reading at each frame in LeRobot
v3 parquet, the native sensor streams in MCAP, one row in
catalog.parquet and one row in meta/annotations.parquet (Sec-
tion 3.5; fields in Appendix C). Episodes last at most 30 minutes
(median 4.9 minutes).
Is there a label or target associated with each instance?
Yes. Each episode has a task type (56 types) in a work category
(30 categories); the task type is the task the contributor was asked
to record.
The annotation model adds, by dense video annota-
tion, a short free-text description, a step-level instruction, an ac-
tion family (35 families), a skill group (18 groups), a difficulty
level (easy, medium or hard), a setting type (11 types) and the
share of the episode spent on task. meta/subtasks.parquet and
meta/subtask_occurrences.parquet store the instruction of each
episode in the LeRobot subtask layout.
Is any information missing from individual instances?
Four of the 72,754 episodes have empty annotation fields, 22 have
no setting type and 209 have no time-of-day label. All episodes in-
clude accelerometer, gyroscope and device-motion data; the magne-
tometer and heading streams are present in 99.1% of episodes and
the barometer in 93.6%.
Audio, location, device identifiers and
contributor identity were removed on purpose (see Preprocessing).
Are relationships between individual instances made ex-
plicit?
Yes. All tables are keyed by episode_index, the same identifier
used in the LeRobot data and the videos.
recording_id links
episodes cut from the same recording, and recording_start_s
and recording_end_s give the position of the episode inside it.
contributor_id is stable across episodes and links all episodes of
one contributor.
Are there recommended data splits?
We recommend contributor-disjoint train, validation and test splits,
so that evaluation measures generalization to unseen contributors
and environments; the identifiers needed to build them are in the
catalog. A contributor-disjoint split stratified by task type is avail-
able on request.
Are there any errors, sources of noise, or redundancies in
the dataset?
(i) The annotations are machine-generated and have not been au-
dited by human reviewers; hand visibility and on-task share are
model estimates. (ii) 5.5% of episodes contain at least one frame
interval longer than 1.5 times the nominal interval; the median
absolute drift against the nominal frame rate is 0.017 s over an
episode and the 95th percentile 0.155 s; re-encoded episodes can
contain duplicated frames. All three are logged per episode in the
catalog. (iii) Native inertial rates differ by operating system: about
100 Hz on iOS devices and a median of about 497 Hz on Android
devices. (iv) Recordings come from 56 phone models, so the field of
view varies across episodes, and camera calibration is not provided.
(v) Video and inertial data share one timeline (Section 3.2); the
19


residual time offset between the camera and the inertial sensors
has not been measured. Redundancy: several episodes can come
from one recording, and 6.4% of the distinct descriptions are used
by more than one episode.
Is the dataset self-contained, or does it link to or otherwise
rely on external resources?
It is
self-contained and
hosted
in
one
repository (https://
huggingface.co/datasets/HubLabs/HubEgo-100K). Reading it re-
lies only on standard formats (MP4 with H.264, parquet, LeRobot
v3, MCAP).
Does the dataset contain data that might be considered
confidential?
The footage shows the inside of workplaces and homes. Segments in
which private text was detected (documents, screens, cards, license
plates) were cut out before release.
Does the dataset contain data that, if viewed directly,
might be offensive, insulting, threatening, or might oth-
erwise cause anxiety?
No. The footage shows manual work.
Does the dataset identify any subpopulations (e.g., by age,
gender)?
No. The release contains no demographic attributes; contributors
are identified only by a pseudonymous identifier.
Does the dataset contain data that might be considered
sensitive in any way?
The data is video of people at work in their own workplace or home.
It contains no location data, no audio and no contributor identity.
Care tasks performed on another person, episodes with a detected
minor and segments with private text were removed.
Collection Process
How was the data associated with each instance acquired?
Video and sensor data were recorded by the contributor’s phone.
The task type is the named task the contributor was asked to
record. Descriptions, instructions, action family, skill group, dif-
ficulty, setting type and on-task share were produced by the an-
notation model, and hand-visibility shares were estimated by the
model panel of the curation pipeline.
What mechanisms or procedures were used to collect the
data?
Contributors record a named, unscripted task with a head-mounted
phone during their own work, in their own workplace or home
(Section 3.1). v1.0 was recorded on 56 phone models.
If the dataset is a sample from a larger set, what was the
sampling strategy?
Curation followed by rebalancing: the pipeline keeps on-task seg-
ments, applies privacy filtering, and rebalances hours across task
types.
Who was involved in the data collection process and how
were they compensated?
12,227 Hub contributors recorded the data. Contributors are com-
pensated by Hub for their recordings.
Over what timeframe was the data collected?
The recording date of every episode is given in the catalog
(recorded_date).
Were any ethical review processes conducted?
The dataset was collected by a company and not as an institutional
human-subjects study. Each contributor agreed to the use of their
recordings (see below), and the privacy measures of Section 3.4 are
applied to every episode before release.
Did you collect the data from the individuals in question
directly, or obtain it via third parties or other sources?
Directly. Contributors recorded themselves at work; no third-party
footage is included.
Were the individuals in question notified about the data
collection?
Contributors made the recordings themselves for this purpose.
Did the individuals in question consent to the collection
and use of their data?
Yes. Each contributor agreed that their recordings may be used to
train and evaluate AI and robotics systems.
Has an analysis of the potential impact of the dataset and
its use on data subjects been conducted?
The privacy measures of Section 3.4 limit the impact on contribu-
tors and on other people present during recording. Section 7 dis-
cusses the broader impact.
Preprocessing, Cleaning and Labeling
Was any preprocessing, cleaning or labeling of the data
done?
Yes, in the stages of Section 3.2 (Fig. 5): a head-mount check that
removes footage not worn on the head, dense temporal segmenta-
tion that keeps the segments where the contributor’s hands perform
the task, hand-visibility gating, privacy detection and cutting (Sec-
tion 3.4), episode assembly, integrity and synchronization checks,
and rebalancing of hours across task types; human review deci-
sions take precedence where available. Episodes recorded at 60 fps
(15.9%) are re-encoded to 30 fps; the others keep the phone’s orig-
inal video stream. Episodes are then annotated by the annotation
model (Section 3.3).
Was the “raw” data saved in addition to the preprocessed,
cleaned or labeled data?
The release keeps the phone’s original video stream for 84.1% of
episodes and every native sensor sample of every episode in MCAP.
The footage removed by curation and privacy filtering is not part
of the release.
Is the software that was used to preprocess, clean or label
the data available?
The curation and annotation pipeline is not released. Every statis-
tic in this paper is computed from the released catalog and anno-
tation tables, and the analysis script is available from the authors
on request.
Uses
Has the dataset been used for any tasks already?
This paper reports the statistical analysis of Section 5 and no model
training results.
Is there a repository that links to any or all papers or
systems that use the dataset?
Not yet.
What (other) tasks could the dataset be used for?
Those of Section 6: visual representation pretraining, hand and
hand-object pose, action recognition, VLA pretraining and co-
training, video prediction and world models, and visual-inertial
odometry.
Is there anything about the composition of the dataset
or the way it was collected and preprocessed, cleaned or
labeled that might impact future uses?
See the answer on errors and sources of noise above. In addition,
recorded time varies across contributors (the median contributor
recorded 21.3 minutes), and task types were rebalanced, so their
shares do not reflect how often the tasks occur in the world.
Are there tasks for which the dataset should not be used?
No restrictions beyond the license.
