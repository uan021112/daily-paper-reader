## Abstract 

Pretrained video representations provide rich temporal infor-mation for robotic manipulation, yet effectively integrating them into existing vision-language-action (VLA) policies re-mains challenging. We present Flow-Aware Visual Residual Alignment (FAVR), a lightweight framework for flow-aware visual conditioning of frozen VLA policies. FAVR maintains a compact temporal visual state through linear Test-Time Training (TTT) and retrieves action-conditioned visual fea-tures throughout flow-based action generation. By exploiting the affine structure of the frozen action head, we explicitly characterize how injected visual features influence predicted action velocities and formulate flow-aware channel selection as a box-constrained optimization problem. We develop a FISTA-based solver for joint gate optimization and derive an efficient closed-form approximation through diagonalization and rescaling. With only 1.74–2.19M trainable parameters and 5,000 adaptation steps, FAVR improves pretrained VLA policies across benchmarks. On RoboTwin 2.0, FAVR im-proves LingBot-VLA from 87.12% to 90.04% under clean conditions and from 86.88% to 89.38% under randomized conditions. A controlled ablation on five RoboTwin tasks fur-ther shows that flow-aware gating increases average success from 72.0% with a gate shared across flow steps to 82.0%, demonstrating the benefit of adapting visual conditioning throughout action generation. ## 1. Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models adapt pretrained vision-language representations to robotic control. Flow-based policies such as π0 and π0.5 generate continuous ac-1arXiv:2611.20971v1 [cs.CV] 16 Nov 2026 tion chunks conditioned on visual observations and language instructions [5, 13]. Video pretraining provides a comple-mentary source of visual knowledge by learning represen-tations of scene structure, object interactions, and temporal evolution [2, 3, 24]. Recent studies demonstrate the bene-fits of incorporating video representations into VLA poli-cies [23, 34], raising the question of how to effectively use them within existing action policies. Incorporating video representations requires more than aggregating temporal information and retrieving action-relevant features: the retrieved features must also benefit action generation. For flow-based policies, action chunks are constructed through successive velocity evaluations. Al-though the visual context remains fixed within an action chunk, the noisy action representation and corresponding policy features evolve across flow steps, so the appropriate visual contribution may also change. While state-dependent retrieval can implicitly learn this contribution, it does not explicitly account for the induced corrections in the action-velocity space. This motivates explicit velocity-residual alignment across flow steps. We introduce Flow-Aware Visual Residual Alignment 

(FAVR), a lightweight framework that integrates pretrained video representations into frozen VLA policies. FAVR em-ploys linear Test-Time Training (TTT) [30] to aggregate fea-tures from a frozen video encoder into a compact temporal vi-sual state, queried by the current action features at each flow step. Learned projections adapt state construction and re-trieval to the control task. A lightweight predictor estimates the target velocity residual, while the frozen affine action head provides an exact mapping from gated visual features to velocity corrections. We formulate flow-aware channel selection as a box-constrained velocity-residual alignment problem and employ a FISTA-based solver to jointly opti-mize channel weights while accounting for their interactions. The gated visual features are injected through a residual connection immediately before the action head. Both pre-trained backbones remain frozen, with only the auxiliary visual interface and residual predictor trained. Joint gate optimization, however, requires iterative com-putation at each flow step. To reduce this overhead, we derive an approximate closed-form gate through diagonalization and analytic rescaling, simplifying channel interactions and calibrating the resulting correction. A coupling-dependent error bound characterizes the effect of the omitted interac-tions. The approximation reduces computational cost at the expense of a modest portion of the joint solver’s performance gains. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the flow-aware gating employed by FAVR adapts channel weights across flow steps, unlike a shared gate. On five RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, this design improves average success from 72.0% to 82.0%. We evaluate FAVR on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-world dual-arm manipulation tasks. With only 1.74–2.19M additional trainable param-eters and 5,000 adaptation steps in simulation, FAVR achieves 98.6% and 85.05% success on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, respectively, using π0.5. On RoboTwin 2.0, it improves LingBot-VLA from 87.12% to 90.04% under clean conditions and from 86.88% to 89.38% under randomized conditions. Real-world experiments further show improvements in block pick-and-place and paper-cup grasping over continued fine-tuning. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce FAVR, a lightweight framework that inte-grates temporal video representations into frozen VLA policies through action-conditioned retrieval and flow-aware visual conditioning. • We formulate visual channel selection as a box-constrained velocity-residual alignment problem, with a FISTA-based joint solver and an approximate closed-form solution sup-ported by a coupling-dependent error bound. • We validate FAVR across three manipulation benchmarks and real-world experiments, with controlled ablations demonstrating the benefits of flow-aware gating and velocity-residual alignment. 

## 2. Related Work 

Vision-Language-Action Models. Vision-language-action (VLA) models transfer pretrained visual and language representations to robotic manipulation, as exemplified by OpenVLA [16]. OpenVLA-OFT improves action generation through parallel decoding, action chunking, and continuous regression [17]. Flow-based policies such as π0 [5], π0.5 [13], and LingBot-VLA [32] combine pretrained vision-language backbones with action experts trained using flow matching [20], generating continuous action chunks through successive velocity evaluations. 

Video Representations for Robot Control. Video pre-training captures scene structure and temporal dynamics, as demonstrated by the V-JEPA series [2, 3, 24]. WorldVLA and DreamVLA incorporate visual prediction into action generation through autoregressive modeling and diffusion conditioning, respectively [6, 33]. JEPA-VLA integrates frozen V-JEPA 2 features through input fusion or gated cross-attention [23], while PVI injects auxiliary visual features through a trainable action expert conditioned on the current noisy action [34]. Both studies show that video-pretrained representations can outperform image-pretrained features in robotic manipulation [23, 34].

## 3. Method 

FAVR augments a frozen flow-based VLA policy with tem-poral visual conditioning and flow-aware residual alignment (Fig. 2). A frozen video encoder and linear Test-Time Train-ing (TTT) aggregate observed history into a state queried by current action features. A residual predictor guides channel-wise gating of the retrieved readout before the frozen affine action head. We develop a FISTA-based joint solver and an approximate closed-form alternative, training only the visual interface and residual predictor.

History-dependent manipulation. TTT has been explored as a memory mechanism for sequential modeling [30], with recent robotic approaches employing recurrent states to cap-ture interaction histories [15, 31]. Since FAVR also employs a TTT-based temporal visual interface, we further exam-ine its ability to support memory-dependent manipulation through two tasks requiring information unavailable in the current observation. In covered-block retrieval, the robot must place a pre-viously covered block into a container after its cover is re-moved. In history-conditioned cup selection, the robot must select the cup concealing a block of the instructed color, whose location was visible before covering. Both tasks sepa-rate observation from execution, with the robot remaining stationary during observation to prevent target information from leaking through its own movements. Without action suppression, small arm movements during the observation phase can reveal the selected side through proprioception, allowing the policy to bypass the intended use of visual history. After 5,000 training steps, FAVR correctly selects the target in 7/10 trials on each task. While exceeding the 50% chance level for binary selection, the improvement remains modest. These results suggest that the TTT state can provide useful temporal context, while its primary role in FAVR is to support visual conditioning rather than serve as a dedicated memory module. 

Dependence on pretrained manipulation capability. To test dependence on the base policy, we freeze an unadapted bimanual π0.5 and V-JEPA checkpoint and train only FAVR for 5,000 steps on block pick-and-place demonstrations. The resulting arms repeatedly move above the workspace with-out reliably approaching or grasping the blocks. Unlike adaptation from a task-fine-tuned checkpoint, this does not establish stable manipulation: FAVR improves visual con-ditioning on a base policy that already possesses relevant task-level capabilities. 

4.4. Ablation Studies 

We investigate the key design choices of FAVR on five RoboTwin 2.0 tasks: hanging mug , move can pot ,

click bell , place bread skillet , and 

place can basket . All variants are trained for 1,000 steps with a batch configuration of 2 × 4 and a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5, and evaluated over 50 trials per task. The frozen LingBot-VLA baseline achieves 68.8% average success (Table 5). 

Flow-aware gating. As illustrated in Fig. 1, FAVR adapts visual channel weights to the current flow state. Since the predicted velocity evolves throughout flow integration, chan-nel weights selected at the initial step may no longer provide suitable corrections at later steps. To examine this, we com-pare FAVR with a variant that reuses the initial gate across all flow steps while retaining the action-conditioned visual read-out. Recomputing the gate increases average success from 72.0% to 82.0%. On move can pot , success rises from 66% to 86%, illustrating how a gate selected at one flow state can become less suitable as the velocity field evolves. This improvement highlights the importance of adapting visual corrections to the evolving action-generation state. 

Injection position and action-head geometry. A key prop-erty of FAVR is that the visual correction passes directly through the frozen affine action head, yielding the exact map-ping ∆vτ = W diag( xτ )gτ . directly in velocity space. At earlier injection positions, how-ever, subsequent nonlinear layers invalidate this mapping. Applying the same head-derived optimization at layers 12, 24, and 36 yields success rates of 67.2%, 66.8%, and 65.8%, respectively, all below the frozen baseline. We therefore also evaluate learned gates that implicitly model the downstream nonlinear response. These improve success to 75.2%, 79.6%, and 74.4%, but remain below FAVR’s 82.0% (see supple-mentary material). Under the limited adaptation budget, final-head injection is preferable because the known channel-to-velocity mapping permits direct gate optimization instead of learning the downstream nonlinear response. 

Correction mechanisms. We test whether the gains arise from velocity-residual alignment rather than simply adding a correction branch. We compare three alternatives: Learned Gate , which directly predicts channel weights; Direct Output ,which predicts bounded velocity corrections without gated feature injection; and Hidden-Only , which removes the tem-poral video-state interface and relies solely on VLA hidden features. Their average success rates are 69.6%, 78.8%, and 79.6%, respectively, compared with 82.0% for FAVR. 

Temporal visual state. We test whether recurrent visual states outperform independent video windows. Resetting TTT after each window reduces average success from 82.0% to 77.6%, while replacing recurrent updates with static fea-ture pooling further reduces success to 68.8%. These results indicate that accumulating observations across windows pro-vides more useful visual context than independent window processing or static aggregation. 

Video representation and multi-view conditioning. We first examine the contribution of video-pretrained representa-tions. On LIBERO, replacing V-JEPA with I-JEPA reduces average success from 98.6% to 97.8%, with the largest differ-ence on LIBERO-Long (97.2% versus 94.8%). This suggests that video-pretrained features are particularly useful for ma-nipulation tasks involving extended temporal interactions. We further investigate whether additional camera views improve the visual information available to V-JEPA. On the full 50-task RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, conditioning V-JEPA on the head camera alone yields 88.48% average success, whereas incorporating the head and two wrist cameras in-creases success to 90.04%. The VLA policy retains its three-view observations in both settings. The open microwave task illustrates why multi-view conditioning can be valuable (Fig. 5). During manipulation, the gripper may slip from the handle, requiring the robot to recover its grasp before continuing. Such contact changes are difficult to distinguish from the head camera but clearly visible from the wrist camera. With head-only video con-ditioning, the auxiliary visual representation may miss this local interaction change and continue to favor nominal task progression. Incorporating wrist observations provides addi-tional evidence of the actual contact state, allowing visual corrections to better reflect the need for recovery. On this task, three-view conditioning improves success from 45% to 64% (+19 pp). This example helps explain why multi-view video representations can substantially benefit manipulation tasks requiring precise contact monitoring and recovery. 

Inference cost. For inference, V-JEPA updates every ten ob-served frames, avoiding five concentrated encoding passes at action time. Average inference latency is 1117.7 ms for the frozen baseline, 1134.3 ms for FAVR-CF, and 1487.3 ms for 8FAVR. By replacing iterative joint optimization with approx-imate closed-form gating, FAVR-CF incurs only 1.5% addi-tional latency over the frozen baseline while reducing infer-ence time by 23.7% relative to FAVR. This efficiency comes with a modest performance trade-off: FAVR-CF achieves 98.25% on LIBERO and 84.60% on LIBERO-Plus, trailing FAVR by only 0.35 and 0.45 percentage points, respectively, while still outperforming the frozen baselines. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented FAVR, a lightweight framework for integrating pretrained video representations into frozen vision-language-action policies. FAVR constructs a compact temporal visual state through TTT and performs flow-aware visual residual alignment by exploiting the affine structure of the frozen ac-tion head. This formulation enables direct gate optimization in velocity space, for which we develop a FISTA-based joint solver and an approximate closed-form alternative, FAVR-CF. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-world manipulation demonstrate consistent im-provements with few trainable parameters, while FAVR-CF offers a favorable trade-off between performance and infer-ence efficiency. Future work may extend velocity-residual alignment to more general action mappings and explore joint adaptation of visual representations and action policies.
