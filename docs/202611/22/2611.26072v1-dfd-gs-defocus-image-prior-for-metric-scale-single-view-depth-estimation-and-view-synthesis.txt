DfD-GS: Defocus Image Prior for Metric-Scale Single-View Depth
Estimation and View Synthesis
Shiori Ueda1
Rinto Yagawa1

1 Keio University

Abstract—Recovering metric-scale geometry from observations
at a single viewpoint remains fundamentally ill-posed, limiting the
reliability of novel view synthesis. Recent methods rely on learned
monocular depth priors, which provide strong structural esti-
mates but can suffer from scale ambiguity and dataset-dependent
biases. We propose DfD-GS, a feed-forward framework that
predicts a metric-scale 3D Gaussian scene representation from
a single-view all-in-focus–defocused image pair. The key idea is
to exploit defocus blur as a physical metric depth cue. DfD-
GS first estimates a defocus-based metric disparity map from
the all-in-focus–defocused image pair and the corresponding
defocus strength. This estimate is used to align and refine a
monocular depth prior to metric scale. Visible and hidden 3D
Gaussians are then predicted for occlusion-aware novel view
synthesis. Experiments on synthetic-defocus datasets constructed
from Booster and Middlebury show that DfD-GS outperforms
single-image baselines in metric depth estimation and novel view
synthesis. On WildRGB-D, DfD-GS achieves lower AbsRel and
LPIPS but higher depth RMSE than the single-image baselines.
Additional analyses show that DfD-GS maintains a more stable
metric scale under field-of-view changes. Qualitative results
on a real dataset show recovery of the main scene structure
under optical defocus. Code is available on our project page:
https://sh1027.github.io/dfd-gs/.

INGLE-VIEW 3D reconstruction and novel view syn-
thesis aim to infer the geometry and appearance of a
scene from a single viewpoint. This problem is fundamental
to image-based rendering and immersive applications such
as augmented reality, virtual reality, and digital twins [1],
where only limited observations are available. Recent methods
have addressed single-view novel view synthesis by lifting
a monocular image into 3D scene representations, such as
layered representations [2], [3] and 3D Gaussians [4], [5].
However, recovering a metrically accurate 3D scene from a
single image remains ill-posed because the same image can be
explained by multiple scene scales and depth configurations.
A typical strategy is to rely on monocular metric depth pri-
ors learned from large-scale datasets. Recent monocular metric
depth foundation models provide strong geometric estimates
and generalize across diverse scenes [6]–[8]. Nevertheless,
metric depth estimation from a single RGB image remains fun-
damentally under-constrained. Camera parameters can reduce
projection ambiguity, but absolute scale and local geometry
must still be inferred from learned, data-driven priors, which

Defocus blur provides a complementary cue for metric 3D
reconstruction. Depth from defocus (DfD) estimates scene
depth from optical blur, whose magnitude is physically re-
lated to the distance from the focus plane [9]. This image-
formation cue is directly tied to metric geometry, but it is also
inherently ambiguous. Blur estimation is texture-dependent
and less reliable in flat or saturated regions. This motivates
a hybrid approach that combines the global structural prior of
monocular depth foundation models with the physical metric
cue provided by defocus blur.
We propose DfD-GS, a feed-forward framework for metric-
scale single-view novel view synthesis from a single-view
all-in-focus–defocused image pair (Fig. 1). Our key idea is
to combine the global structural prior of a monocular depth
foundation model with local metric cues from defocus blur.
DfD-GS estimates a defocus-based metric disparity map from
the all-in-focus–defocused image pair and the corresponding
defocus strength and uses it to align and refine the monocular
depth prior to metric scale. It then lifts the refined source-view
geometry into an occlusion-aware 3D Gaussian representation
that separately models visible and hidden scene content for
novel view rendering. This unified formulation enables both
metric depth estimation and single-view novel view synthesis
in a single forward pass.
To evaluate metric depth estimation and novel view syn-
thesis, we construct synthetic-defocus datasets from RGB-D
datasets with metric depth. DfD-GS outperforms the single-
image baselines on Booster [10] and Middlebury [11] for both
tasks. On WildRGB-D [12], it achieves the lowest AbsRel
and LPIPS, but has higher depth RMSE than the single-image
baselines. We also analyze the effect of aperture size and show

II. RELATED WORK
A. Single-view Novel View Synthesis
Single-view novel view synthesis aims to render unseen
viewpoints from a single viewpoint. A common approach is to
lift the input image into an explicit 3D-aware representation,
such as layered depth images or multiplane images, and render
target views from the reconstructed scene [2], [3], [13]–[15].
Recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting [16] has motivated feed-
forward Gaussian prediction from single images [4], [5], [17].
Splatter Image [17] predicts object-level Gaussians from a
single image, while Flash3D [4] extends monocular depth
priors to feed-forward scene reconstruction using layered 3D
Gaussians. Although these methods achieve efficient single-
view reconstruction and view synthesis, metric scale is not
explicitly considered. SHARP [5] explicitly targets high-
resolution photorealistic view synthesis from a metric 3D
Gaussian representation. However, its metric scale is still
inferred from a single RGB image in a data-driven manner. In
contrast, our method does not infer metric scale solely from
RGB appearance. It exploits defocus blur as a physical cue to
estimate metric-scale depth and construct a metric-scale 3D
Gaussian representation.
B. Metric Monocular Depth Estimation
Monocular depth estimation seeks to infer dense scene
geometry from a single RGB image. Large-scale relative
depth models have improved cross-dataset generalization by
learning robust monocular depth priors [18], [19]. Metric
monocular depth estimation further aims to recover depth
in absolute scale. Early zero-shot metric methods addressed
scale-aware prediction by learning geometric scale priors [20]
or combining relative and metric depth supervision [21].
Recent foundation models improve zero-shot metric depth
by explicitly modeling the relationship between depth and
camera properties in different ways. Metric3D [22] and Met-
ric3D v2 [6] transform images and depths into canonical
camera spaces, Depth Pro [8] predicts canonical inverse depth
with focal length estimation, and Depth Anything 3 [23]
introduces a metric model that predicts canonical metric depth.
UniDepth [24] and UniDepth v2 [7] estimate dense camera
representations with pseudo-spherical outputs. MoGe [25]
predicts affine-invariant point maps for open-domain 3D ge-
ometry. Although these methods reduce ambiguity arising
from camera parameters, absolute depth remains inherently
ambiguous from a single RGB image and is therefore inferred
largely from learned data-driven priors. In contrast, our method
complements the monocular prior with defocus blur as a
physically grounded metric cue.
C. Depth Estimation from Defocus Cues
Defocus cues have long been used for depth estimation
through depth from focus (DfF) and depth from defocus
(DfD). DfF estimates depth from a focal stack captured with
different focus settings. Classical DfF methods infer the depth
at which each scene point appears sharp using local focus
measures [26]–[28]. Learning-based methods replace hand-
crafted focus measures with neural networks and estimate
depth from focal stacks. Early methods relied on direct depth
supervision [29], while later approaches introduced defocus-
based intermediate supervision [30], all-in-focus image su-
pervision [31], differential focus-volume representations [32],
self-supervised learning from sparse focal stacks [33], and
defocus models for camera-setting invariance [34]. Hybrid-
Depth [35] further combines DfF cues from focal stacks with
single-image depth priors to produce robust metric depth maps.
These methods exploit rich focus variation across multiple
images, but they generally require focal stacks, which increase
capture cost and computational memory.
DfD, in contrast, does not require a dense focal stack and
estimates depth from defocus blur in one or a few images.
Classical methods established this idea by relating defocus
blur to scene depth using analytical image-formation mod-
els, spatial-domain transforms, or filter-based relative defocus
measurements [9], [36], [37]. Recent learning-based methods
have used defocus either as a supervisory cue for monocular
depth learning [38] or as an input cue for joint depth estimation
and focused-image restoration [39]. These methods demon-
strate that defocus provides useful geometric information with
limited inputs. Inspired by DfD, our method uses a single-view
all-in-focus–defocused image pair to refine a monocular depth
prior and construct a metric-scale 3D Gaussian representation
for novel view synthesis.
III. METHOD
We introduce DfD-GS, a feed-forward method for predict-
ing a metric-scale 3D Gaussian scene representation from
a single-view all-in-focus–defocused image pair. As shown
in Fig. 2, the input consists of an all-in-focus image I, a
defocused image Idef, and the corresponding defocus strength
kdef. We additionally assume that the focus distance zf is
known from the camera configuration. In this work, the focus
plane is placed at the nearest scene depth. Our objective is to
estimate a metric-scale 3D Gaussian representation G that can
synthesize novel views. The main notation used in this section
is summarized in Table I.
Our method consists of three stages: (1) DA3-based monoc-
ular depth prior extraction, (2) defocus-guided metric depth
prediction, and (3) occlusion-aware Gaussian parameter pre-
diction (Fig. 2).

A. DA3-based Monocular Depth Prior Extraction
We extract multi-scale image features FDA and a depth
map from I using the frozen Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [23]
backbone. We convert the depth map to disparity and linearly
rescale it to [0, 1], mapping the nearest and farthest predicted
points to 0 and 1, respectively. The resulting normalized
disparity-offset map DDA serves as a relative-geometry prior
for defocus-guided metric alignment in the next stage.
B. Defocus-guided Metric Depth Prediction
Defocus provides a metric depth cue through the physical
relationship between blur radius and scene depth. We use this
cue to globally align and locally refine the DA3 prior DDA.

disparity as inverse depth, D(p) = 1/Z(p), measured in m−1,
and let df = 1/zf denote the focus disparity. Under the nearest-
focus assumption, the disparity offset ∆D(p) = df −D(p)
is nonnegative. The blur radius in pixels is modeled as

C. Occlusion-aware Gaussian Parameter Prediction
To handle disocclusions in nearby novel views, we predict
two 3D Gaussians for each source-view pixel: a visible Gaus-
sian for the observed surface and a hidden Gaussian for scene
content that may become visible from nearby viewpoints. For
an input image of size H × W, the predicted Gaussian set
contains 2HW Gaussians and is denoted by G.
1) Feature fusion and parameter prediction: To predict
Gaussian parameters, we fuse RGB image-token features,
multi-scale DA3 features FDA, and depth-token features. I
and Zref are first embedded into patch tokens. They are
combined with FDA and fused by an MLP to produce a
dense feature map at the input resolution. The hidden Gaussian
parameters are predicted from the fused feature map. For the
visible Gaussian, we additionally use local RGB cues from I,
since its appearance is directly supported by the source-view
observation.
2) Lifting to 3D and rendering: Each predicted Gaussian
is lifted to 3D using the refined metric depth and the source-
view camera parameters. Each Gaussian in G is parameterized
by a 3D mean µ, a covariance matrix Σ, an opacity value α,
and spherical-harmonic color coefficients h. The mean µ is
initialized by back-projecting the corresponding source-view
pixel with the refined metric depth Zref and is further adjusted
by learned subpixel and depth offsets. The covariance Σ is
parameterized by anisotropic scale and rotation. The resulting
Gaussian set G is rendered using a differentiable 3D Gaussian
splatting renderer [16].

E. Training Data Generation
To obtain diverse training data with ground truth at scale,
we constructed a synthetic dataset. We expect defocus cues to
support synthetic-to-real generalization due to their relative in-
variance to scene appearance, as suggested by prior work [30].
The dataset provides all-in-focus images, defocused images,
ground-truth depth, and corresponding target views.
The dataset comprises 11 000 multi-view RGB-D scenes
generated using Blender1, with 10 000 scenes for training
and 1 000 for validation. Each scene contained randomly
arranged primitive objects with varied materials and lighting.
We rendered one source view and four target views per
scene. Defocused source-view images were synthesized using
the official pretrained BokehMe model [41], with the focus
plane set to the nearest scene depth and varying defocus
strength. Additional generation details are provided in the
supplementary document (Section S2).

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We compare our method against existing baselines in terms
of metric depth estimation accuracy and novel view synthesis
(NVS) quality. Beyond these comparisons, we investigate the
effects of f-number and field of view and conduct ablation
studies to assess the contributions of individual components.
We further evaluate our method on a real dataset captured with
optical defocus, examining reconstructed scene structure and
selected physical distances.
A. Datasets
We prepared RGB-D datasets with synthetically generated
defocus and a real dataset captured with optical defocus.
All input images were resized to 256 × 256. Additional
dataset details are provided in the supplementary document
(Section S3).
1) RGB-D Datasets with Synthetic Defocus: We created
synthetic-defocus datasets from existing stereo and multi-
view datasets with metric depth. We used Middlebury [11],
Booster [10], and WildRGB-D [12], comprising 12, 38, and
460 scenes, respectively. These datasets provide real images
with ground-truth disparity or depth. We generated defocused
source-view images using BokehMe [41], setting the focus
plane to the minimum valid scene depth. Unless otherwise
noted, we used f/4.0 for Middlebury and Booster and f/2.8 for
WildRGB-D.
2) Real Dataset: We captured five real-world scenes with
optical defocus. All-in-focus–defocused image pairs were ac-
quired from the same viewpoint at focal lengths of 24–34 mm
or 70 mm, using f/22 for the all-in-focus images and f/4.0 for
the defocused images. Since dense ground-truth depth maps
and target-view images were unavailable, we qualitatively
assessed reconstructed scene structure and evaluated metric
scale using selected physical distances measured in each scene.
B. Implementation Details
We implemented our method in PyTorch 2.11. We used
Depth Anything 3 Large as the frozen monocular prior
and trained the defocus-conditioned U-Net fU1, disparity-
refinement U-Net fU2, and Gaussian parameter prediction
module. The two U-Nets shared the same architecture, with a
base channel width of 8 and 6 encoder–decoder stages. We set
∆dmax to 10.0 m−1. For Gaussian prediction, the RGB image,
DA3, and depth tokens were embedded into dimensions of
64, 256, and 64, respectively. The fused token dimension was
256, and the decoded dense feature map had 128 channels.
We used spherical harmonics of degree 2 for view-dependent
color representation.
We optimized all trainable modules using Adam with a base
learning rate of 1.5×10−4 and a linear warm-up over the first
2 000 steps. Training was run for 100 000 steps with a batch
size of 4 and gradient accumulation over 8 steps, resulting in
an effective batch size of 32. Gradients were clipped to a norm
of 1.0. The loss weights were set to λr1 = 0.8, λrs = 0.2,
λrp = 0.1, λd1 = 0.8, λds = 0.2, λdg = 1.0, and λdefocus =
10.0. Training and inference were performed on an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 4080 GPU with 16 GB of memory.

C. Metric Depth Evaluation
We compared our method with state-of-the-art single-image
metric depth estimation methods, including Depth Pro [8],
Metric3D v2 [6], MoGe [25], UniDepth v1 [24], UniDepth
v2 [7], and Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [23]. For DA3, we used
its metric-depth variant, DA3Metric-Large. Since WildRGB-
D is used to train UniDepth v2, we excluded UniDepth v2
from the WildRGB-D evaluation. We also included Pent-
land’s classical DfD method [9], using the same all-in-focus–
defocused image pair as our method. We evaluated depth
accuracy on the source views using absolute relative error
(AbsRel), root mean squared error (RMSE), and threshold
accuracy δ1, defined as the fraction of valid pixels satisfying
max(ˆZ/Z, Z/ˆZ) < 1.25. For Pentland, we evaluated only
pixels with valid predicted and ground-truth depths. We also

2) Effect of field of view: We analyzed the effect of field-of-
view changes by varying the crop ratio used to generate the
input image. Specifically, we center-cropped the all-in-focus

then synthesized defocused images at this resolution, keeping
kdef fixed across crop ratios for each scene. A smaller crop

for extracting small center crops while maintaining the target
resolution of 256 × 256. Compared with the baselines, our
method is substantially more stable under changes in crop
ratio. This stability can be attributed to the use of defocus
as a physical cue. When the crop ratio becomes small, the
field of view is narrowed and the semantic and contextual
cues available to monocular depth baselines become limited.
In contrast, defocus blur is tied to metric depth, allowing our
method to maintain a more stable metric scale even under large
field-of-view changes.

E. Ablation
We conducted ablation studies to evaluate the contribution
of the DA3 prior, scale-map-based depth refinement, and hid-
den Gaussian representation. We compared the full model with
three variants. In w/o DA3 prior, we bypass global alignment
and dense disparity refinement and directly use the defocus-
based U-Net disparity as the final disparity, i.e., Dref = DU.
In w/o scale map, we remove the dense disparity refinement
and set the refined disparity to the globally aligned disparity,
i.e., Dref = Dalign. In w/o hidden Gaussian, we predict only
the visible Gaussian for each source-view pixel and remove the
hidden Gaussian branch. The quantitative results are reported
together with the main comparisons in Tables II to VII, and
the qualitative results are shown in Figs. 11 to 13.
Scale-map refinement improves depth accuracy on Booster
and Middlebury and all three NVS metrics across datasets. On
WildRGB-D, it improves AbsRel and δ1 but increases RMSE.
Without refinement, the rendered depth maps show patches
of large depth values, while novel views exhibit geometric
distortions. Residual local depth errors affect the initial Gaus-
sian positions, and the predictor may attempt to compensate
through learned position offsets. These compensatory shifts

geometry that is difficult to recover from defocus cues alone.

decrease on Booster and Middlebury. Separating visible sur-

the hidden layer must predict the colors and textures of
surfaces not visible in the input. Even when the reconstructed
geometry improves, errors in these predictions may lead to
lower PSNR and SSIM.

V. CONCLUSION
We presented DfD-GS, a feed-forward framework that
predicts metric-scale 3D Gaussians from a single-view all-
in-focus–defocused image pair. Experiments on synthetic-
defocus datasets showed that DfD-GS outperformed the single-
image baselines in both tasks on Booster and Middlebury.
On WildRGB-D, it achieved lower AbsRel and LPIPS but
higher depth RMSE than these baselines. Additional analyses
showed more stable metric scale under field-of-view changes
and dataset-dependent sensitivity to f-number. Experiments
with optical defocus demonstrated recovery of the main scene
structure.
Despite these improvements, several limitations remain. The
nearest-focus assumption restricts capture conditions. Extend-
ing the formulation to arbitrary focus positions is a direction
for future work. Residual depth errors and uncertainty about
unobserved surfaces limit novel view rendering quality. Future
work should better preserve reliable monocular geometry dur-
ing refinement and improve hidden-Gaussian prediction. Er-
rors in real captures may arise from limited defocus sensitivity
and differences between synthetic and optical blur. Evaluation
with dense ground-truth depth would help characterize these
limitations and guide further improvements.

S3. EVALUATION DATA
A. RGB-D Datasets with Synthetic Defocus
We created synthetic-defocus datasets from Middlebury [11]
and Booster [10], which provide stereo images with ground-
truth disparity, and WildRGB-D [12], which provides multi-
view RGB-D sequences. For Middlebury and Booster, we used
the official training splits, retaining 12 of the 15 Middlebury
scenes after excluding those with inconsistent lighting between
stereo images and all 38 Booster scenes. For WildRGB-D, we
used the validation split and randomly selected up to 10 scenes
from each of the 46 categories. The resulting evaluation sets
contained 12, 38, and 460 scenes from Middlebury, Booster,
and WildRGB-D, respectively.
For Middlebury and Booster, we used the left and right
images as the source and target views, respectively, and
converted the ground-truth disparity maps into metric depth
using the provided calibration parameters. For WildRGB-D,
we sampled two frames from each sequence with a fixed
temporal stride of 10 frames, using the earlier frame as the
source view and the later frame as the target view. Depth
estimation was evaluated against the source-view ground truth,
while NVS was evaluated by comparing the synthesized target-
view image with the corresponding ground-truth image. All
images and depth maps were center-cropped to a square aspect
ratio and resized to 256 × 256.
We synthesized defocused source-view images from the
all-in-focus RGB images using BokehMe [41]. The focus
distance was set to the minimum valid scene depth, placing the
