# Driving the Style: Velocity-Conditioned Whole-Body Control from Motion Demonstrations 

Murad Dawood 1 Oezge Drama Berkenkamp 1 Antonio Gonzales Marin 1 Nutan Chen 2

Abstract — Achieving human-like behavior on humanoid robots has advanced rapidly by leveraging motion-capture demonstrations rather than hand-crafted cost functions. Adver-sarial imitation supplies a style signal that lets a policy fulfill a task while adhering to a desired style, but is difficult to transfer to hardware. Whole-body tracking through reinforcement learning instead deploys reliably and imitates demonstrations faithfully, but the resulting policies are conditioned on the future reference, so they require a reference trajectory at runtime and can fail when the motion differs from the training data. We train a humanoid policy directly with whole-body tracking rewards while restricting its actor to compact locomotion commands and robot observations. Reference motion supervises the reward and privileged critic, without supplying reference joint trajectories to the actor or requiring teacher distillation. The demonstrated style is thereby encoded in the policy through the tracking reward, so the robot reproduces the whole-body style under commanded velocities. Trained on a single random-walk motion and commanded with velocity profiles from held-out motions, our policy reaches at least 98% success in simulation and completes every trial on the Unitree G1. On held-out slow walking, whole-body tracking drops to 2% success and command-conditioned distillation to 56%, and both fail on the real robot. On the trained motions, the joint position error of our policy stays within 8% of whole-body tracking, and it has a higher style coverage than adversarial motion priors (AMP), which fell on the real robot without a safety crane. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humanoid robots have shown the ability to successfully perform a variety of tasks by imitating human motions [1], [2]. This has motivated the capture and generation of extensive motion datasets through motion-capture systems [3], [4], [5]. However, these datasets do not account for the physical constraints of the robot, such as its kinematic and dynamic limits [6]. As a result, imitating the datasets directly through supervised learning yields policies that fail to execute reliably on the robot. Reinforcement learning (RL), on the other hand, is able to learn locomotion policies through exploration that exploits the robot’s own dynamics and kinematics [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. This has made RL a promising approach for learning stylized locomotion behaviors from motion references [12], [13], [14]. Existing pipelines for learning stylized locomotion follow one of three strategies. Adversarial training provides the RL agent with a reward signal about the style [12], [15], [16], [17]. Whole-body tracking instead combines multiple rewards to imitate the reference motion by tracking joint positions, joint velocities, and the positions and velocities of key body

A third strategy distills a privileged, reference-conditioned teacher into a student that observes only deployable inputs together with a history of past observations [19]. Each of them has a cost in reliability, in controllability, or in how the policy is trained. Adversarial approaches have seen great success in computer graphics [12], [16]: beyond imitating a style, they allow the policy to be driven at runtime without a reference trajectory. However, their deployment to the real world is challenging because of the weighting between the style reward and the task reward. Emphasizing the style can lead to unsuccessful deployment on the real robot (e.g. jittery motions and stumbling), while emphasizing the task reward enables successful deployment at the cost of weaker style imitation. Whole-body tracking, on the other hand, can deploy reliably on real robots while imitating the reference motion to a great extent. However, these policies are conditioned on the future reference of joint positions and velocities, so they require the reference trajectory at runtime and the whole-body motion must come from a recorded clip. Command-conditioned distillation can give the student a low-dimensional command interface, but the student learns by imitating the actions of a reference-conditioned teacher rather arXiv:2611.20888v1 [cs.RO] 16 Nov 2026 than from the task reward, so its behavior is limited by what the teacher does. To close this gap, we present a velocity-conditioned whole-body controller that deploys reliably and can be steered at runtime, without distillation from a privileged teacher. We train a tracking policy with an asymmetric actor-critic: the critic observes the full reference motion, while the actor observes only proprioception, the anchor orientation error, and a planar base velocity command. The demonstrated style is thereby encoded in the policy through the tracking reward, so the robot reproduces the whole-body style under a commanded velocity (Fig. 1). Our key observation is that different locomotion motions often share the base velocities of the training motion while their joint trajectories differ: forward walking, sidesteps, and turning all reduce to base-level goals. A policy conditioned on the velocity command, and not on the reference joints, is therefore less dependent on the joint trajectories of the training clip and can transfer to such motions. At deployment, its inputs can be computed from the base trajectory of a motion clip or, without a clip, from a gamepad. In our experiments, we drive it with velocity profiles from held-out motion clips, so that tracking and style can be measured against ground truth. We compare our approach with all baselines on the Unitree G1, in simulation and on real hardware.. In summary, our contributions are:

A. Humanoid Whole-Body Motion Tracking 

RL-based motion tracking has shown great success recently in imitating natural human movements on humanoids. [13] presented an RL framework that trains policies to imitate recorded motions via a set of rewards for tracking the positions and velocities of selected body links. [14] generated motions that preserve the interactions with the environment, including the objects to be interacted with, so that tracking policies can learn to interact with the environment while imitating the motions. [20] added a residual action model to account for the dynamics mismatch between simulation and the real world and improve sim-to-real transfer. [6] applied motion filtering, feet contact masks, and adaptive rewards to enable learning of highly dynamic motions. [21] combined adaptive sampling of motions based on difficulty with a virtual assistive wrench to help stabilize the training of athletic moves. [19] applied a teacher-student DAgger [22] with historical observations to learn the motions from a privileged teacher, and also let a joystick command the root velocity and pose, while the policy still receives the whole-body joint and keypoint targets. These approaches imitate the recorded motions faithfully, but the policies require the reference trajectory at runtime. Different from these approaches, our goal is a policy that is controlled by a velocity command and does not need a reference trajectory at deployment. 

B. Style Imitation from Demonstrations 

On the other hand, several works aimed to make the imitation controllable without a reference trajectory at runtime. [12] utilized adversarial training to make physically simulated characters imitate a given style without the need for reward shaping. Rather than imitating a single clip, [16], [15] learned reusable motion priors that encode a repertoire of skills from large unstructured datasets, via a latent skill embedding and a score-matching (diffusion-based) prior respectively, which a downstream controller can then use to perform new tasks. However, [17] showed that adversarial training requires careful tuning of the task and style rewards to avoid sub-optimal behaviors; the authors formulated the problem as a constrained Markov decision process and deployed it on a real quadruped. This tuning is even harder on humanoids: [23] reported that adding an adversarial style reward leads to stumbling and jittering that is not feasible for sim-to-real transfer. [24] distilled an RL velocity-tracking teacher into a student with discriminators against both the teacher and motion-capture data, but the style still relies on adversarial rewards that must be balanced against the task. To make the imitation robust for deployment, other works used hybrid controllers that split the body. [23] trained the lower body to track a base velocity robustly while the upper body imitates a reference motion. [25] applied a similar split, where the lower body tracks a velocity command and the upper body tracks a motion, using adversarial learning between the two policies so each stays robust to the other’s movements. These controllers deploy reliably and can be steered through the velocity command, but the style is limited to the upper body while the lower body follows a plain velocity-tracking objective. Unlike these approaches, we train the whole body with the dense tracking rewards of [13], so the style is reproduced across the whole body, and we condition the policy on the base velocity commands to keep it steerable at deployment. III. A PPROACH 

Our pipeline has two stages: (i) we record human motions with a motion-capture system and retarget them to the robot, and (ii) we train a whole-body tracking policy with reinforcement learning under an asymmetric actor–critic, where the actor is restricted to deployable observations conditioned on a base-velocity command. At deployment the velocity command is supplied online (e.g. from a game-pad) to steer the robot while it reproduces the demonstrated style. 

A. Motion Acquisition and Retargeting 

We record source motions with a motion-capture system and retarget them to the robot with GMR [26], which solves an inverse-kinematics problem mapping the captured human trajectory onto the robot kinematics while respecting its joint limits. For each clip this yields a per-frame reference trajectory of joint positions and velocities and of the position, orientation, and linear/angular velocity of a set of tracked bodies, expressed in the world frame. A designated anchor body (the pelvis) defines the reference base frame.

F. Ablation: Smoothed Motions 

The base velocity of the recorded motions oscillates with the gait, while a gamepad command is smooth. We therefore also train our policy on the random walk with a smoothed base trajectory. As for the plain policy, the random walk is the only training motion, and backward, side, and slow walking are held out. We smooth the base position and the frame-to-frame base rotation of each clip with a Savitzky-Golay filter [29] (window of 100 frames), and keep the joint trajectories unchanged. The joint errors are therefore computed against the same targets. Smoothing changes the velocity command and also the reference base position and orientation. Each policy is evaluated on the four motions with the base trajectory it was trained with: plain motions for the plain policy (P →P) and smoothed motions for the smoothed policy (S →S). 

a) Simulation.: Success remains high for both policies (Table IX; 100% for the plain and 99% for the smoothed policy on average). With smoothed training, MJPE rises by 

11% (4.16 ◦ to 4.62 ◦), MJVE rises by 14% , and the style changes slightly (MMD 0.19 to 0.21 , COV 0.36 to 0.34 ). 

b) Real robot.: Both the plain and the smoothed policy complete all three trials on the random walk and on the three held-out motions (Table X). As in simulation, the smoothed policy tracks slightly worse on average (MJPE 4.54 ◦ vs. 

4.26 ◦) with a similar style (COV 0.39 for both, MMD 0.18 

vs. 0.20 ). On slow walking, the smoothed policy has a lower MJPE ( 5.65 ◦ vs. 5.96 ◦), MJVE, and MMD, and a similar COV. Training on smoothed motions therefore costs little tracking accuracy and gives a policy that can follow smooth, gamepad-like velocity commands. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a velocity-conditioned whole-body tracking policy that is trained directly with RL and an asymmetric actor-critic. The critic and the tracking reward use the full ref-erence motion, while the actor observes only proprioception, the anchor orientation error, and a planar velocity command. On the motions it was trained on, our policy stays close to whole-body tracking, with a mean joint position error about 

8% higher in simulation. When trained on the random walk and commanded with velocity profiles from held-out motions, it is the only tracking-based method that keeps working on all of them, in simulation and on the Unitree G1, while whole-body tracking and command-conditioned distillation fail on slow walking. Compared with AMP, our policy has a higher style coverage and runs on the real robot without a safety crane. The input analysis shows that most velocity commands of the held-out motions are close to the training data, while far fewer of the reference joint frames are. In the ablations, mirrored training data or symmetry training, alone or combined, avoids the failure of the baseline policy on mirrored motions, and training on a smoothed base trajectory costs little tracking accuracy, in simulation and on the real robot.
