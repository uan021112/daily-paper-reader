# From Temperature-Limited to Battery-Limited Legged Locomotion 

Rapha¨ el S´ egas Bal´ azs Opra L´ eo Ghafari Thomas Stewart 

Abstract — On legged robots, sustained locomotion often ends in thermal shutdown rather than battery depletion: the heat of walking concentrates in a few joint motors, which overheat long before the battery is empty. We train thermally aware locomotion policies by reinforcement learning without ever observing temperature, and demonstrate this on a Unitree Go2 quadruped. We first identify a coupled 13-node lumped RC thermal network, fitted on two runs of a single robot and calibrated against the measured electrical power, that predicts all twelve motor temperatures with a mean RMSE below 1 °C across different robot instances, gaits, payloads, and speeds. Under a sustained gait, the temperatures the motors will settle at follow directly from the gait-averaged squared torques and joint speeds. Our core contribution is a reward that drives down the hottest predicted asymptotes: thermal time constants exceed the credit-assignment horizon of reinforcement learning by orders of magnitude, but predicted asymptotes respond to torque and joint speed within a single gait cycle, so the policy learns to lower and spread heat with no temperature input. On the robot, the policy turns sustained locomotion from temperature-limited to battery-limited: with a 5 kg payload at 1 m/s, a learned baseline reaches thermal shutdown after 19 minutes (1.1 km), whereas our policy runs the battery down after 174 minutes (10.0 km) with its hottest motor 14 °C below the shutdown threshold in a 26 °C room; on an unloaded factory circuit with stairs and ramps, the baseline overheats after 25 laps (6.2 km), while our policy completes 47 (11.2 km) on one battery with 22 °C of margin. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Learned controllers have made legged robots agile and robust [5], [9], [12], [14], [16]: they climb stairs and cross unstructured terrain on proprioception alone [9], [14]. With this surge in capability, these robots are increasingly asked to perform long-horizon tasks: patrolling a factory, running an inspection circuit, or carrying parcels up the stairs of an apartment block, often under heavy payloads. In practice, such runs often end long before the battery is empty, because a joint motor overheats. Electric legged robots are typically driven by permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSMs) whose dominant loss, Joule heating, grows with the square of the torque [2], [22]. In a compactly packaged leg, that heat escapes only slowly. Nahrendra et al. [14] had to slow their robot down in summer trials to keep its front-leg motors from overheating, Matsuzawa et al. [11] found heat concentrating in the hip joints of their legged-wheeled robot until the load was redistributed, and the humanoid that beat the human half-marathon record in April 2026 ran on liquid-cooled leg motors [3], a remedy that adds mass, power draw, and cost. In this paper, we address the problem of making learned locomotion policies thermally aware, so that sustained lo-comotion is limited by the battery rather than by motor temperatures. Two properties set this apart from energy arXiv:2611.22231v1 [cs.RO] 17 Nov 2026 minimization. First, overheating is a distribution problem: the robot shuts down when any motor crosses its threshold (around 86 °C on the Go2), so what matters is the hottest joint, not the sum over joints that an energy objective minimizes [2]. A gait can be efficient and still thermally fatal when it loads a single joint, a pattern Lin et al. [10] observed on a quadruped where a few joints overheated while the others stayed cool. Second, thermal time constants are tens of minutes, whereas the Joule heating that primarily drives them follows torque within a control step. Over a reinforcement learning (RL) episode of a few seconds, temperatures barely change, making credit assignment of temperature-based rewards exceedingly challenging. Thermal limits are typically handled in three ways. Hard-ware cooling pushes the thermal envelope [19], [24] at the price of mass and power. Model-based control enforces temperature limits through torque caps, thermally constrained trajectory optimization, or recovery postures [4], [6], [7], [8], [20], [21], mostly on fixed-base or slowly moving systems where capping torque does not endanger balance. In learned locomotion, the issue has often been addressed by heuristics, such as the power distribution term of Nahrendra et al. [14] that penalizes the variance of mechanical power across joints and, more recently, by feeding motor temperatures to the policy as observations with a reward derived from a control barrier function (CBF) [1] on the temperature derivative [13], [15]. M¨ uller et al. [13] keep the neck actuator of an animated character below its limit with a decoupled first-order thermal model, and Qian et al. [15] combine the same reward with the whole-body thermal network of Lin et al. [10] to carry 3 kg on a Unitree A1 for 27 minutes, up from 7 without the reward. Both grapple with the long thermal time scale. Qian et al. [15] note that the CBF term “typically exhibits limited variation” within an episode, initialize temperatures in a band around the threshold to obtain a learning signal, and report that the policy “tends to adopt conservative walking postures even when motor temperatures are low”, which hinders performance on slopes and stairs. Wan et al. [23] soften this with a residual policy conditioned on the thermal state, at the cost of a second training stage; the credit-assignment difficulty reported by Qian et al. remains, since the thermal state barely moves within an episode and the learning signal can only come from its variation across episodes. We take a different route and do not observe temperature at all. Our key observation is that, under sustained locomotion, the thermal steady state the motors settle into is set by a single gait cycle: once a coupled thermal model of the robot is identified, the temperatures at which heating and cooling equilibrate follow from the gait-averaged squared torques and joint speeds via one small linear solve. Unlike the temperatures themselves, these predicted asymptotes respond to a change in torque or joint speed within a single gait cycle. This collapses the credit-assignment horizon from thermal to gait time scales, leaves the policy input unchanged, and keeps evaluation independent of the initial thermal state. We first identify a compact, coupled 13-node lumped RC thermal network of the Go2, anchored in the measured electrical power balance so that fitted parameters are physical, and we turn it into a reward by penalizing a soft maximum of the predicted asymptotes, which lowers the hottest joints and spreads the load across the others instead of pulling down a sum. We incorporate this reward into a strong blind locomotion pipeline [2], [14], [16] and train policies using proximal policy optimization (PPO) [17] on diverse terrains including stairs and slopes, so the thermal margin does not come at the expense of terrain traversability. Nothing in our approach is specific to blind policies, quadrupeds, or the on-policy algorithm we use. The main contribution of this paper is a steady-state-aware thermal reward that makes learned locomotion policies ther-mally aware without temperature observations: it penalizes a soft maximum of the thermal asymptotes predicted from gait-averaged squared torques and joint speeds through a single linear solve of an identified thermal network, and thereby incentivizes the policy to lower and spread the thermal load across joints. Two further contributions support it: a measurement-first identification methodology that pins the quiescent power, the Joule heating coefficient, and the temperature coefficient of resistance electrically before any temperature is fitted, so that the parameters stay physical; and a compact 22-parameter, 13-node thermal network of the Unitree Go2, identified from two runs of a single robot and reported in full (Tab. II). We make five claims. (i) Our identified network predicts all twelve motor temperatures of a Go2 with a mean RMSE below 1 °C on runs it was not fitted on, including a second robot, learned gaits, payloads up to 10 kg, speeds up to 1.4 m/s, sustained walks, and stop-and-go runs. (ii) Electrical anchoring keeps the identified parameters physical, whereas a temperature-only fit of the same model drifts to nonphysical values and generalizes worse. (iii) Our reward lowers the hottest predicted thermal asymptote at every payload in simulation, and its weight sets the margin. (iv) The resulting policy retains terrain traversal capability over steep stairs and slopes, in simulation and on a factory circuit. (v) Without temperature observations, it shifts Go2 locomotion from temperature-limited to battery-limited operation, both with a 5 kg payload on flat ground and unloaded on the same circuit. Sec. III supports the claims in this order: (i) and (ii) on twenty-one held-out runs, (iii) and (iv) in simulation, and (iv) and (v) on the robot. II. M ETHODOLOGY

Thermal system identification 1) Experimental protocols: We conduct all experiments indoors to prevent wind and direct sun exposure, in a large flat 60 by 25 m room, so that straight stretches dominate the data. We ensure that the robots are at room temperature before every recording. We sample all motor temperatures, electrical quantities, joint torques and velocities at 50 Hz and log their statistics every 2 s. We record ambient tempera-ture at the same cadence with a Sensirion SHT40 sensor (±0.2 °C), and measure robot velocity independently with a stopwatch. In the back-and front-splayed static stances, the rear and front legs are pulled apart by an extra 5 ± 0.5 cm, respectively.

Each protocol concludes with a 90-minute damp segment where the robot rests on a plastic stand under its torso, so that only its feet touch the ground. We do not rest the robot directly on the ground, to avoid an additional torso-to-ground conduction path. Our training set for fitting the thermal model consists of two runs of over four hours (one per protocol) of a single robot and the stock Unitree locomotion controller (Unitree’s sport mode, commanded through the Motion Services Inter-face V2.0 of the Go2 SDK) for ease of reproducibility by the community. Segments, individually or in combination, help identify key thermal and electrical parameters introduced in Sec. II-A.2 and Appendix A: damp phases give the electrical quies-cent power directly, the splayed static stances predominantly excite hip nodes and their cool/hot pair helps identify the temperature coefficient of the resistance, shifted payloads break symmetries, the two payload levels (0 and 5 kg) disambiguate secondary heating terms, and the 60-minute sustained walk anchors the thermal asymptotes while the velocity sweep sets the forced-convection terms. We additionally record nine validation runs, used for cross-validation and structure selection (Sec. II-A.4), and twelve held-out test runs, scored once with the final model. Together they cover about 39 hours on two different Go2 robots (we fit on one of them), three controller families (the stock controller, DreamWaQ baselines of Sec. II-C, and checkpoints of our thermal policy), payloads from 0 to 10 kg including off-center loads, body speeds from 0.4 to 1.4 m/s, sustained walks on flat ground (Sec. III-D) and on the factory circuit (Sec. III-E), and stop-and-go runs with stand-lock or damp phases in between.

B. Steady-state-aware thermal rewards 

Thermal time constants are tens of minutes, far longer than an RL episode and the horizon over which PPO assigns credit: a 10 W change in a motor’s Joule heating moves its temperature by about half a degree over 20 s, while its asymptote moves by roughly twenty degrees. A reward on the temperature level thus barely distinguishes actions that increase heating from those that reduce it, and a reward on its derivative [15] conflates the quality of the gait with the thermal state the episode started in, since the same gait heats fast from cold and not at all near equilibrium. The asymptote, in contrast, is an instantaneous function of the gait alone, and we penalize a soft maximum of the asymptotes predicted by the identified model. Our thermal policy. Our thermal policy keeps the DreamWaQ reward terms and weights, adds the steady-state-aware thermal reward of Sec. II-B with weight wth = −0.2,and drops three terms that it renders redundant. Power distribution and joint power are both proxies for the heat that the thermal reward penalizes directly; body height is unnecessary because supporting weight on straighter legs lowers calf torque, so a high-body stance emerges on its own. We switch the thermal reward on, at full weight, after 5,000 iterations once terrains are already solved.

D. Endurance on flat ground 

We command the baseline and our thermal policy at 1 m/s with a 5 kg payload in the same large room as Sec. II-A.1 until thermal shutdown or battery depletion. While the baseline shuts down in 19 minutes (1.1 km), our thermal policy lasts 174 minutes (10.0 km) until battery depletion (Fig. 1), with 14 °C thermal headroom. These runs were at 22 and 26 °C room temperature (cooler day for the baseline). Lower Joule losses also make the policy more economical: at the same speed it draws 142 W against 244 W for the baseline, an electrical cost of transport of 0.71 against 1.24 (43 % lower, quiescent power included and Go2 measured mass of 16.2 kg plus payload). The supplementary video shows the stark contrast in posture: level and upright for our thermal policy, crouched for the baseline. E. Factory circuit 

We command our robot along a continuous factory cir-cuit (Fig. 6) combining a steep staircase climbed upward (19.5 cm risers with ±1 cm variability, 25.2 cm tread depth), a walkway, a gentle downhill ramp (3.2 °average slope), flat stretches, and sharp turns (239 m per lap; 0.7 m/s velocity command on stairs, 1 m/s otherwise), until thermal shutdown or battery depletion. In Fig. 7, the baseline reaches thermal shutdown after 111 minutes (25 laps, 6.2 km); our thermal policy lasts 193 minutes (47 laps, 11.2 km) before its battery is empty, its hottest joint peaking at 64 °C (22 °C margin). The cost of transport drops from 1.27 to 0.83 (34 % lower), or 8.8 instead of 13.4 Wh per lap. The circuit runs were at 23 and 25 °C room temperature (cooler for the baseline). On hardware, our policy thus traverses the same stairs and ramps as the baseline but lasts a full battery with 22 °C of margin (claims (iv) and (v)). IV. C ONCLUSION 

We have shown that a learned locomotion policy can be made thermally aware without observing temperature. In-stead of penalizing temperatures, which barely move within an episode, our reward penalizes the thermal steady states the current gait is heading toward: with an identified thermal network of the robot, they follow from gait-averaged squared torques and joint speeds through one small linear solve and respond to the policy within a gait cycle. The 22-parameter network of the Unitree Go2 behind it, calibrated against the measured electrical power, predicts all twelve motor temperatures with a mean error below 1 °C on held-out runs across robots, gaits, payloads, and speeds. On hardware, the Go2 went from temperature-limited to battery-limited: 174 instead of 19 minutes at 1 m/s with 5 kg of payload, with 14 °C of thermal headroom, and 47 laps of a factory circuit with steep stairs and ramps on one battery, where the baseline overheated after 25, and with no loss of terrain traversal capability. In future work, we plan to (1) close the sim-to-real gap we observed at large payloads, (2) build an electrical power reward on the identified power balance to turn thermal headroom into range, and (3) add a governor that adjusts the commanded velocity from the model’s real-time predictions.
