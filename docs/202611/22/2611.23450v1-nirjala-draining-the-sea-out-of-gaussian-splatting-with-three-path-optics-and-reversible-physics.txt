# ABSTRACT 

Water does not just tint an underwater photograph; it rewrites it. Each pixel mixes attenuated scene radiance, a veil of backscattered light, and scene light that was scattered forward on its way to the camera, and many combinations of scene and water explain the same pixels. Most Gaussian-splatting methods for underwater scenes model only the first two effects and remove the water by dividing by a transmission that decays exponentially with range, so their inversion gain grows without bound. We present NIRJALA (Sanskrit for “without water”), a 3D Gaussian Splatting framework that recovers water-free appearance and geometry together. NIRJALA has three parts. TRIVENI is a three-path image formation model that adds a range-saturating forward-scattering contribution to direct transmission and backscatter; we prove that this keeps the inversion gain bounded at every range and derive bounds on how errors in the estimated backscatter and attenuation propagate into the recovered colours. PRATIBIMBA is a reversible physics anchor that ties a flexible neural water model to a ten-parameter global physical model in both the recovery and the re-formation direction, so scene content cannot quietly hide inside the water. SAMARASA collects the physics-informed objectives, including a colour prior that balances channels separately at each range. To score water removal against ground truth, we also built SAJALA (“with water”), a 30-scene synthetic evaluation suite path-traced in Blender Cycles with water-free images, depth, and normals under controlled water colour and turbidity. On SAJALA, NIRJALA raises rendering PSNR from 24.34 to 24.76 dB and lowers depth AbsRel from 0.109 to 0.091 against the strongest baseline; on the real SeaThru-NeRF scenes it lowers LPIPS from 0.194 to 0.175 while rendering at 44 FPS. We close with a discussion of where appearance metrics and geometry disagree underwater, and of the limits of our own evaluation.

# 1 Introduction 

Marine ecologists, archaeologists, and the engineers who run underwater robots all want the same thing from a camera survey: a 3D model of the reef, wreck, or pipeline whose colours belong to the object and whose shape can be measured [1–3]. Neural radiance fields [4] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [5] have made such models routine in air. Under water, the camera never sees the scene directly. Every pixel blends light from the object, weakened by absorption, with light scattered by the water itself, and the blend depends on wavelength and distance [6–8]. A splatting model fitted naively to these images bakes the water into the scene: colours drift with distance, and the optimiser fills the water column with semi-transparent “floaters” to explain the haze. The usual fix is to place an underwater image formation model inside the renderer [9–12]. Nearly all of these methods build on the two-term model of Sea-thru [13], I = J e −βD r + B∞(1 − e−βB r ), which keeps direct transmission and backscatter. Two problems remain. The first is physical: the model leaves out scene light that is scattered through small

The second is numerical, and it matters more for 3D. Removing the water means dividing by e−βD r, so the gain applied to the data grows exponentially with range. A small mistake in the estimated backscatter or attenuation at the far end of the scene turns into a large colour error in the water-free output, and the photometric loss then pushes that error back into the geometry. On top of this, scene and water are not identifiable from the images alone [8, 15]: a flexible neural water model can absorb scene texture and still match every observation. NIRJALA is built around these two failure modes. Its first ingredient, TRIVENI (Sanskrit/Hindi for the confluence of three rivers), is a three-path image formation model that adds a range-saturating forward-scattering contribution to the classical two terms (Figure 1). We show that this single extra term keeps the inversion gain bounded at every range (Proposition 1), and we derive how errors in the estimated backscatter and attenuation propagate into the recovered colours under both models (Corollary 1). The second ingredient, PRATIBIMBA (“reflection”), is a reversible physics anchor. We keep an expressive neural water model for rendering, but tie it to a compact physical model with only ten global parameters through a recover-then-re-form round trip. A model that small cannot reproduce scene-specific structure, so any texture the neural branch tries to hide in the water shows up as an inconsistency and is penalised. The third ingredient, SAMARASA (“harmony”), gathers the physics-informed objectives, most notably a grey-world prior applied separately inside range bins so that colour is balanced at each distance rather than on average. Judging water removal is itself hard. Real underwater datasets such as SeaThru-NeRF [9] have no ground truth for the water-free scene or its depth, and a recent controlled study shows that appearance scores and geometric accuracy can move in opposite directions under water [16]. We therefore built SAJALA (“with water”), a set of 30 synthetic scenes path-traced in Blender Cycles under controlled water colour and turbidity, each paired with water-free images, depth, and normals. Our contributions are: • TRIVENI, a three-path underwater image formation model for Gaussian splatting, with a proof that its inversion gain is bounded at every range and explicit bounds on how backscatter and attenuation errors reach the recovered colours; under the two-term model both quantities grow exponentially with range. 2NIRJALA: D RAINING THE SEA OUT OF GAUSSIAN SPLATTING 

• PRATIBIMBA, a reversible physics anchor that couples a flexible neural water model to a ten-parameter physical model in both the recovery and the formation direction. • SAMARASA, a set of physics-informed objectives that includes a range-binned colour prior and joint appearance–geometry supervision. • SAJALA, a 30-scene path-traced evaluation suite with water-free, depth, and normal ground truth under controlled water colour and turbidity. • The best rendering quality on SAJALA and SeaThru-NeRF and the best depth on SAJALA among nine methods at 44 FPS, followed by a frank discussion of what these numbers do and do not show. 

# 2 Related Work 

Neural scene representations. NeRF [4] renders a scene by integrating an MLP radiance field along camera rays. Mip-NeRF [17] adds anti-aliasing, and NeRF-W [18] handles changing appearance with per-image embeddings, an idea we reuse. 3DGS [5] swaps the implicit field for explicit anisotropic Gaussians and tile-based rasterisation and renders in real time. Later work sharpens its geometry through surface alignment [19] and planar multi-view regularisation [20]. All of these assume clear air. 

Single-image underwater restoration. Physics-based restoration grew out of scattering models for haze [21, 22] and their underwater counterparts [6, 7]. Akkaynak and Treibitz showed that the coefficients governing direct transmission and backscatter differ and depend on range [8, 15], and Sea-thru [13] turned this revised model into a practical restoration method. Polarisation provides another physical handle [23]. Learning-based methods range from CNNs [24, 25] and Transformers [26] to physics-guided unsupervised models [27, 28]; see [3, 29] for surveys. These methods treat one image at a time and do not enforce consistency across views or with 3D geometry. 

Underwater neural reconstruction. SeaThru-NeRF [9] placed the revised model inside volume rendering and recovered water-free views, and WaterNeRF [30] refined the medium parameterisation. Splatting methods soon followed, and they differ mainly in where the water lives. WaterSplatting [10], Aquatic-GS [31], AquaGS [32], and Plenodium [33] keep it in a separate field rendered alongside the Gaussians; Plenodium also conditions that field on position. SeaSplat [11], 3D-UIR [12], and DualPhys-GS [34] predict attenuation and backscatter from rendered depth and image features. WaterClear-GS [35] stores the optical coefficients on the primitives themselves, while SeaFree-GS [36] and UW-GS [37] give each Gaussian a clean and a degraded colour. Gaussian Splashing [38] changes the compositing rule itself, and RestorGS [39] handles underwater, night, and haze in one depth-aware framework. Other work targets compactness [40], caustics [41], sparse views [42], and floaters [43], and Li and Fu [44] combine splat fields with physically based water rendering for few-shot synthesis. Almost all of these methods build on the two-term model. NIRJALA adds a forward-scattering term whose effect on the inverse problem we analyse formally, and it checks the learned water against a compact physical model in both directions instead of trusting the neural estimate alone. 

Evaluating underwater reconstruction. Most methods above report PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS on the underwater views of a handful of real scenes [9]. Álvarez-Tuñón and Graßhof [16] recently re-ran five systems under one protocol across four water regimes. They found that photometric and geometric quality can move in opposite directions, that published numbers are hard to compare because protocols differ, and that depth priors and fitted water parameters do not always behave as intended. Their study shapes how we evaluate (Section 4) and how we read our own results (Section 6).

# 4 The SAJALA Evaluation Suite 

Real captures such as the SeaThru-NeRF scenes [9] only let us score how well a method reproduces the degraded 

views. They cannot tell us whether the water was removed correctly or whether the recovered shape is right, because the water-free scene and its depth are unknown. A method can score well on underwater novel views while hallucinating colour in its water-free output, and, as Álvarez-Tuñón and Graßhof show, while building wrong geometry [16]. To measure what we actually care about, we built SAJALA, a synthetic evaluation suite path-traced in Blender Cycles. 

Content. SAJALA contains 30 scenes in four categories: Coral Reef (7), Cave (6), Ruin (8), and Sunken Ship (9). Each scene has 120 views captured along several camera paths and a standard colour checker placed in the scene. For every view, we render the underwater image together with the ground-truth water-free image, depth map, and 8NIRJALA: D RAINING THE SEA OUT OF GAUSSIAN SPLATTING 

normal map (Figure 1c). The scenes cover open seafloor, enclosed caves, cluttered reef geometry, and large man-made structures with thin parts such as masts and rigging, which are a common source of floaters. Because Cycles is a physically based path tracer, scattering and absorption in the water are simulated by light transport rather than added as an image-space effect, so none of the methods we test, including ours, has its own image formation model built into the data. 

Controlled water. To study how methods respond to the medium, the Ruin V scene is rendered under two water colours (blue and green), each at four increasing concentrations, giving eight versions of the same geometry. We also render a variant with spatially non-uniform turbidity, which breaks the homogeneous-medium assumption shared by every method evaluated here. 

Protocol. We use fixed training and test camera splits per scene. Underwater rendering is scored with PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS on all 30 scenes, and depth with RMSE, AbsRel, and the threshold accuracy δ < 1.25 , i.e., the fraction of pixels with max( ˆd/d ⋆, d ⋆/ ˆd) < 1.25 for predicted depth ˆd and reference depth d⋆. Water-free appearance is scored against the ground-truth water-free images with the same image metrics; in this paper we report it on four representative scenes, one per category. 

# 5 Experiments 

5.1 Setup Datasets. We evaluate on the four real scenes of the publicly available SeaThru-NeRF dataset [9] (IUI3-RedSea, Curaçao, J.G. RedSea, and Panama, with 29, 20, 20, and 18 images) and on all 30 scenes of SAJALA (Section 4). Fol-lowing [41], real images are downsampled to roughly 900 × 1400 and white-balanced, poses come from COLMAP [50], and every eighth image in filename order is held out for testing. 

Baselines. We compare with NeRF [4], SeaThru-NeRF [9], 3DGS [5], PGSR [20], RecGS [41], SeaSplat [11], WaterSplatting [10], and 3D-UIR [12]. All baselines are retrained with their official code and settings on the same splits. Because protocols differ between papers [16], our numbers should be compared with each other, not with numbers reported elsewhere. 

Implementation. NIRJALA builds on 3DGS with degree-3 spherical harmonics and is trained for 30k iterations per scene with Adam on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti. The position learning rate decays from 1.6 × 10 −4 to 

1.6 × 10 −6; the appearance, opacity, scale, and rotation learning rates start at 2.5 × 10 −3, 2.5 × 10 −2, 5 × 10 −3, and 

10 −3, and the optical modules use 10 −3 to 10 −2. Densification and pruning run every 100 iterations between iterations 500 and 15k. 

Run selection. We trained NIRJALA several times per scene and report, for each scene, the best run we obtained; this selection was made using the evaluation metrics reported in this paper. The baselines were trained with their official settings and are reported as single runs. Reporting the best of several runs favours our method relative to single-run baselines, so the numbers for NIRJALA should be read as an optimistic estimate of typical performance, and small margins should be read with care (Section 6).

# 6 Discussion and Threats to Validity 

Appearance and geometry can disagree. Álvarez-Tuñón and Graßhof [16] ran five splatting systems under one protocol and found that photometric and geometric quality can move in opposite directions under water. In their turbidity sweep, vanilla 3DGS gained about 4 dB of held-out PSNR (32.0 to 35.9 dB) while its surface depth error grew from 99 mm to 848 mm. Our water sweep (Section 5.3) shows the same kind of divergence from a different angle: as the water thickens, underwater PSNR rises while water-free PSNR falls by 4 dB. Two independent studies with different data and different methods therefore point the same way. Underwater rendering scores, which most of the field reports, can reward a model for reproducing the water rather than the scene. This is why SAJALA carries water-free and depth ground truth and why we report depth for every method on it. It also means that our gains on the real SeaThru-NeRF scenes, which are scored only on underwater views, say less about water removal than the SAJALA results do. 

Depth priors are not free. The same study found that removing SeaSplat’s depth supervision cost 2.8 dB of PSNR but reduced the opacity floating in front of surfaces by about a third, because the cheapest way to satisfy a depth loss can be to hang semi-transparent layers in the water [16]. NIRJALA also uses a monocular depth prior. Water parameters are site-specific. [16] also found that medium networks fitted on one site lose about 9 dB of PSNR when reused on another, so the fitted parameters describe the training images rather than the water. We draw the same conclusion for PRATIBIMBA: its ten parameters are per-scene anchors that constrain the neural model, not calibrated optical properties of the water, and we make no claim that they transfer. 

Poses come first. Like every method we compare, NIRJALA needs camera poses from COLMAP on real data. [16] report that COLMAP registers 99.5% of frames in clear water but none at 12 NTU on identical trajectories. Our method does not address this regime; in very turbid water, reconstruction fails before any image formation model is reached. 

Limits of our own evaluation. Several limits apply to our results. (i) SAJALA is synthetic. Path tracing in Blender Cycles simulates absorption and scattering physically, but the scenes do not contain suspended particles, caustics, or artificial lights that move with the camera, and gains on SAJALA may not carry over unchanged to real water. (ii) The water-free comparison covers four scenes and two baselines. (iii) Our ablations measure underwater rendering on real scenes only. (iv) For NIRJALA we report the best of several training runs per scene, selected on the reported metrics, and we do not report run-to-run variance. Our numbers are therefore an optimistic estimate of typical performance, and small differences, such as the 0.09 dB water-free margin on Cave or the 0.03 dB PSNR margin on Panama, should be read as ties. (v) The forward term is pointwise and does not model blur, and Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 concern the conditioning of the inversion, not whether real water follows Equation (3). (vi) The neural direct weight T D, neu is a bounded gain rather than a transmittance (Section 3.3), so its maps should not be read physically.

# C Additional Analysis 

This section collects four further properties of the three-path model and of the range-binned prior. Like Proposition 1 and Corollary 1, they are statements about the model and the losses, not about real water, and they involve no experiments. Throughout, we write q(r; βD , β f , η ) = e−βD r + η(1 − e−βf r ) and drop the channel index. 19 NIRJALA: D RAINING THE SEA OUT OF GAUSSIAN SPLATTING 

C.1 The bound does not depend on the unit of range 

PRATIBIMBA works on the normalised range ¯r, while the scene lives in metric units. The following lemma shows that this choice does not change the guarantee. 

Lemma 1 (Scale invariance) . For every s > 0 and r ≥ 0, q(r; βD , β f , η ) = q(sr ; βD /s, β f /s, η ). The constant κ of Proposition 1 is unchanged by this rescaling. Proof. Both exponents depend only on the products βD r and βf r, which are unchanged when r is multiplied by s and both coefficients are divided by s. κ depends only on η and the ratio βf /β D , which is also unchanged. Consequently, Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 hold in metric and in normalised units with the same κ, and the shaded region in Figure 3 (left) does not depend on how the range is normalised. 

C.2 Noise amplification 

Corollary 1 bounds a deterministic error. The same argument bounds random noise. 

Corollary 2 (Noise variance) . If the backscatter-corrected signal is observed as ˆN = N + ε with E[ε] = 0 and 

Var[ ε] = σ2, and q is exact, then the recovered radiance ˆJ = ˆN /q is unbiased and satisfies Var[ ˆJ] ≤ σ2/κ 2 at every range. Under the two-term model, Var[ ˆJ] = σ2e2βD r .Proof. ˆJ = J + ε/q , so E[ ˆJ] = J and Var[ ˆJ] = σ2/q 2 ≤ σ2/κ 2 by Proposition 1. For the two-term model, 

q = e−βD r .In words, the signal-to-noise ratio of the water-free radiance degrades by at most a constant factor under the three-path model, whereas it decays exponentially with range under the two-term model. Sensor noise and small fluctuations of the veiling light are exactly the kind of zero-mean errors this covers. 

C.3 Far-range radiance keeps a gradient 

The inversion gain describes the backward problem. The same quantity q also controls the forward problem that the optimiser actually sees. 

Proposition 2 (Gradient floor) . Under Equation (3) , ∂I/∂J = q(r), and therefore κ ≤ ∂I/∂J ≤ 1 + η for every r ≥ 0.For any per-pixel loss ℓ(I, I gt ), the gradient with respect to the radiance is ∂ℓ/∂J = q(r) ∂ℓ/∂I , so its magnitude is at least κ |∂ℓ/∂I |. Under the two-term model, ∂I/∂J = e−βD r → 0 as r → ∞ .Proof. Equation (3) is linear in J with coefficient T D + T S = q. The lower bound is Proposition 1; the upper bound follows from e−βD r ≤ 1 and η(1 − e−βf r ) ≤ η. The chain rule gives the gradient. Under the two-term model, the colour of a distant Gaussian receives an exponentially small share of the photometric gradient, so far-range appearance is learned slowly and is easily explained away by the backscatter term instead. The forward path guarantees a fixed fraction of the signal at every range. We note that the neural direct weight T D, neu of TRIVENI is itself bounded below by 0.5 (Section 3.3), so the neural rendering branch never suffers a vanishing gradient either; Proposition 2 explains why such a floor is desirable and shows that the physical three-path model provides one by construction.

# D Reproducibility Summary 

• Real data: SeaThru-NeRF [9], four scenes; images downsampled to roughly 900 × 1400 and white-balanced following [41]; COLMAP poses; every eighth image (filename order) held out. • Synthetic data: SAJALA, 30 scenes path-traced in Blender Cycles with fixed training and test camera splits (Section 4). • Training: 30k iterations per scene with Adam; all weights and schedules in Table 7; one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti. • Runs: several training runs per scene for NIRJALA, best run reported (Section 5.1); baselines trained with their official settings. • Metrics: PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS on held-out views; RMSE, AbsRel, and δ< 1.25 for depth on SAJALA; water-free metrics against ground-truth water-free images. • Cost: 1 h 45 min training, 3499.1 MiB GPU memory, and 44 FPS rendering per scene. 

# E Broader Impact 

Better underwater reconstruction supports reef monitoring, archaeology, and the inspection of submerged infrastructure, where colour fidelity and measurable geometry both matter. The main risk is over-trust: a water-free rendering looks like a measurement but is the output of a model, and as Section 6 shows, appearance and geometry can disagree. Results from NIRJALA, like those of any water-removal method, should not be used for quantitative ecological or structural assessment without validation against independent measurements.
