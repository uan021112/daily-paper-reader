# Stereo Visual Odometry with In-the-Loop Geometric Feed-Forward Models Towards Dense Metric Mapping 

Fahira Afzal Maken 1, Sagun Man Singh Shrestha 1, Edward Vanderfeen 1,2, Lucas Carvalho de Lima 1,Rongxin Li 1, Changming Sun 1 and Milad Ramezani 1,2∗

Abstract — We present a stereo visual odometry (SVO) frame-work that integrates geometric feed-forward models (GFMs) for dense depth prediction with factor-graph-based visual odometry to achieve accurate localisation and dense metric mapping. Our key idea is to exploit sparse but reliable metric geometry estimated from stereo and temporal multi-view observations to calibrate dense GFM predictions, while retaining the ro-bustness and efficiency of model-based SVO. Rather than directly conditioning the GFM on potentially noisy stereo depth, optimised 3D landmarks are used to jointly estimate a scale–shift transformation over all keyframes within each local submap, yielding metrically consistent dense depth predictions. To balance accuracy and computational efficiency, we introduce a hierarchical factor-graph architecture. At the local level, sliding-window optimisation estimates high-frequency camera poses and multi-view-refined 3D landmarks. Dense predictions are generated only for selected keyframes and registered within each submap using SE(3) Generalised ICP. Consecutive submaps are further aligned in Sim(3) using their shared geometry, allowing residual scale inconsistencies to be corrected. These intra- and inter-submap constraints are incorporated into a global factor graph to produce a consistent trajectory and dense metric reconstruction. Extensive experiments on public and in-house datasets demonstrate that the proposed frame-work achieves competitive odometry accuracy against geometric and learning-based baselines while providing accurate dense metric reconstruction and improved robustness to failures in learned depth prediction. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in deep learning have transformed visual simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM), with geo-metric feed-forward models (GFMs) showing strong capabil-ity in recovering dense 3D scene structure directly from im-ages. Building on these advances, systems such as MASt3R-SLAM [1] and VGGT-SLAM [2] have demonstrated impres-sive localisation and reconstruction performance. However, dense inference remains computationally demanding, incon-sistencies across local reconstructions must be reconciled over long trajectories, and predicted depth may still exhibit residual scale inconsistencies with metric geometry. While recent feed-forward SLAM systems can recover dense scene geometry, maintaining globally consistent and metrically scaled reconstructions over long trajectories re-mains challenging. We focus on visual-only odometry and SLAM, and there-fore exclude approaches that rely on additional sensing modalities such as inertial measurements. We review classi-cal visual odometry, recent feed-forward geometric models, and learning-based SLAM systems relevant to our approach. 

A. Visual Odometry 

Visual odometry (VO) estimates camera motion from con-secutive images using geometric constraints derived from vi-sual observations. Early stereo VO demonstrated the benefits of directly measuring metric scale for applications ranging from planetary exploration [7] to autonomous ground vehi-cles [8]. Modern feature-based systems typically detect and associate sparse visual features, initialise their 3D structure from stereo observations, and jointly refine camera poses and landmarks using multi-view geometric optimisation. ORB-SLAM2 [4] extended feature-based SLAM to monocular, stereo, and RGB-D cameras, while ORB-SLAM3 [5] fur-ther improved multi-map and long-term operation. Related graph-based systems such as RSLAM [6] and OpenVS-LAM [9] similarly exploit sparse landmark observations to maintain geometrically consistent camera trajectories. These approaches provide accurate and computationally efficient metric estimation when stereo observations are available, but their maps are inherently sparse and capture only a small fraction of the observed scene geometry. 

B. Deep Scene Reconstruction 

Recent vision foundation models have substantially advanced image-based depth and 3D reconstruction. Transformer-based models such as Depth Anything [10] learn strong monocular and multi-view depth priors, while MVSAnywhere [11] combines monocular and multi-view cues for generalisable multi-view stereo. More general mod-els such as MapAnything [12] accept images together with optional geometric information, including camera parame-ters, depth, and partial reconstructions, to predict metric scene geometry. A parallel line of work directly reasons about 3D struc-ture across images. DUSt3R [13] predicts dense pointmaps from pairs of overlapping images without requiring known camera calibration, while MASt3R [14] extends this for-mulation with improved geometric matching. These mod-els provide powerful dense reconstruction priors without requiring conventional feature extraction and triangulation. However, applying pairwise or window-based predictions over long sequences requires the resulting local recon-structions to be aligned and reconciled globally. Related depth-completion methods also demonstrate the benefit of combining learned priors with sparse metric observations. For example, Marigold-DC [15] guides an affine-invariant monocular depth prior using sparse metric depth measure-ments, although its iterative diffusion-based inference is not designed as a feed-forward VO framework. These works motivate exploiting sparse geometric measurements to met-rically constrain learned dense predictions. 

C. Deep Visual SLAM 

Deep learning has also been integrated directly into vi-sual SLAM. DROID-SLAM [16] jointly updates pixel-wise depth and camera poses through a recurrent neural network and differentiable dense bundle adjustment, and can exploit monocular, stereo, or RGB-D imagery. More recently, geo-metric foundation models have been incorporated into hybrid SLAM pipelines. MASt3R-SLAM [1] builds a real-time dense monocular SLAM system around MASt3R, combining learned pointmaps with camera tracking, local fusion, loop closure, and global graph optimisation. VGGT-SLAM [3] instead forms local dense submaps using feed-forward VGGT [17] predictions and globally aligns them through factor-graph optimisation. To account for reconstruction ambiguity with uncalibrated monocular imagery, it estimates 15-DoF projective transformations be-tween submaps on the SL(4) manifold. VGGT-SLAM 2.0 [2] improves this formulation through a keyframe-level factor graph that constrains the high-dimensional projective ambi-guity and reduces the drift and degeneracy associated with unconstrained 15-DoF submap alignment. ScaRF-SLAM [18] decouples feature-based visual SLAM from GFM-based dense reconstruction, using the former for tracking and the latter for mapping, with frame- and submap-level scale optimisation for consistency. The resulting metrically aligned depth maps are back-projected into dense point clouds and registered across keyframes to form submaps. By performing local geometric optimisation at ev-ery frame while invoking dense feed-forward inference and global registration only at selected keyframes, the proposed hierarchy aims to preserve the geometric accuracy of multi-view stereo while reducing the computational cost of dense reconstruction.

A. Implementation Details 

Our system is implemented using GTSAM [24] for factor-graph-based stereo visual odometry and MapAnything [12] as the GFM for dense depth prediction. In the front-end, we use GFTT [25] for feature detection and track them temporally using KLT [19] as described earlier. As new frames arrive, the local factor graph is incrementally updated using iSAM2 [26]. Keyframes are selected based on camera motion and scene covisibility: a new keyframe is created when the estimated translation or rotation of the current frame relative to the previous keyframe exceeds predefined thresholds, or when the number of covisible features falls below a predefined threshold. Once the local factor graph contains a fixed number of keyframes, the global factor graph is triggered and updated. We perform dense reconstruction using the multi-view configuration of MapAnything on the selected keyframes in each inference window. B. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics a) KITTI Odometry: We use the KITTI Odometry benchmark [27] to evaluate large-scale trajectory estimation. KITTI provides calibrated stereo imagery together with reference trajectories over urban, residential, and highway environments. Its long sequences allow us to evaluate the scalability and long-term consistency of the proposed hierar-chical factor-graph formulation. Due to space limitations, We report quantitative results on the odd-numbered sequences, while sequence 00 is used for qualitative analysis of the estimated trajectory and dense reconstruction. 

b) Newer College Dataset: The Newer College Dataset (NCD) [28] contains stereo imagery with a resolution of 

848 × 480 , captured using an Intel Realsense-D435, together with high-accuracy reference collected over diverse outdoor areas. Its varied outdoor structure provides a suitable set-ting for evaluating visual odometry performance and dense depth prediction for complex scene geometry. We use the Short Experiment run and process every third stereo frame, corresponding to an effective input rate of 10 Hz .

c) In-house Run: Our in-house platform (shown in Fig-ure 1) is equipped with a ZED-X stereo camera with a 

12cm baseline, capturing 960 × 600 images at 15 Hz . A Livox LiDAR and its integrated IMU are used by an inde-pendent LiDAR–inertial SLAM system to obtain a reference trajectory. The resulting LiDAR reconstruction is used as a geometric reference for evaluating dense-map accuracy. We selected a ∼ 40 m sequence whose start and end positions coincide, providing a controlled setting for assessing both local map accuracy and accumulated consistency over the full run. 

d) Evaluation Metrics: Trajectory accuracy is evaluated using relative pose error (RPE) functions implemented in the evo package [29]. We report the root mean squared error (RMSE) of the translational ( trel ) and rotational ( rrel )components separately, over travelled-distance intervals of 5,

10 , 50 , and 100 m , taking all pose pairs separated by each interval rather than consecutive segments. For quantitative dense-map evaluation, we use the in-house run and compare the reconstructed map against the reference LiDAR map using the Chamfer distance, the mean of the two average nearest-neighbour distances between the clouds, together with precision, recall and the F-score at a 10 cm thresh-old. Precision is the fraction of reconstructed points whose nearest reference point lies within this distance, recall is the fraction of reference points whose nearest reconstructed point lies within it, and the F-score is their harmonic mean. We score only points within 6 m of the estimated trajectory and voxel downsample the reference map to 5 cm , while each reconstruction is scored at its native density. Although this work focuses on odometry, we additionally report the abso-lute trajectory error (ATE) as RMSE after rigid alignment to the reference trajectory to provide an overall measure of accumulated error and deviation from ground truth. 

C. Baselines 

Using VSLAM-LAB [30], we compare against represen-tative classical and learning-based visual SLAM systems. ORB-SLAM3 [5] serves as a strong feature-based geometric baseline operating directly on calibrated stereo imagery. DROID-SLAM [16] represents learning-based visual SLAM with recurrent pose and depth refinement and differentiable dense bundle adjustment. VGGT-SLAM 2.0 [2] is included as a recent hybrid approach that combines feed-forward geometric prediction, using VGGT [17], with global factor-graph optimisation. For evaluating dense-map consistency on the in-house run, we compare against ScaRF-SLAM [18] and DROID-SLAM [16] as two separate baseline methods. As our approach currently focuses on odometry and does not incorporate loop closure, we disable loop-closure detec-tion and relocalisation in the baselines for a fair comparison. For all baselines, we use the same image sequences and camera calibration whenever supported. Before evaluation, estimated trajectories are aligned to the reference frame using Umeyama alignment implemented in evo . For metric stereo methods, only rigid alignment is applied, without scale correction. Since VGGT-SLAM 2.0 operates on monocular input and does not inherently recover metric scale, we additionally allow a global scale correction during alignment.

Table I reports the translational and rotational RPE on the selected KITTI and NCD sequences. On KITTI, our method remains competitive with ORB-SLAM3 and DROID-SLAM on sequences 03 , 05 , and 09 , while showing substantially greater robustness than the learning-based baselines on the more challenging sequences 01 and 07 . In particular, at the 100 m interval on sequence 01 , our method achieves a translational RPE of 7.12 m , compared with 60 .27 m for DROID-SLAM and 59 .28 m for VGGT-SLAM 2.0. The difference becomes more pronounced on NCD, where the learning-based baselines degrade considerably under the more complex motion and scene geometry. Our method maintains substantially lower translational and rotational errors across all evaluated intervals, while ORB-SLAM3 fails to complete the full sequence, tracking only 480 of 1410 .5 m 

due to feature-tracking failure. We hypothesise that this is caused by the substantial illumination variation in NCD, particularly in the garden section, together with the weaker stereo geometry provided by the Intel RealSense D435. Accordingly, the ORB-SLAM3 results reported in Table I are computed only up to the failure point. These results suggest that retaining model-based stereo odometry while exploiting learned geometry at the submap level provides greater resilience when either feature-based or learning-based SLAM becomes unreliable. Additionally, Figure 3 compares the RPE distributions of our full method against the learning-based baselines and our model-based SVO variant without dense-map constraints (denoted by Ours ∗). Incorporating dense-map registration substantially reduces translational drift, particularly over longer intervals, demonstrating the effectiveness of metric depth calibration and the benefit of integrating the resulting metric dense maps into the VO framework through intra- and inter-submap alignment. Overall, the ICP-based constraints improve odometry consistency, though their benefit depends on the quality of the dense geometry used for alignment. Future work will investigate asynchronous execution, GPU-based backend optimisation, and model acceleration to improve deployment efficiency. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a stereo visual odometry framework that integrates geometric feed-forward depth prediction with a hi-erarchical factor-graph architecture for accurate localisation and dense metric mapping. At the local level, stereo and temporal multi-view observations provide metrically scaled camera poses, and 3D landmarks. These optimised landmarks are then used to estimate a scale-shift transformation that metrically correct dense depth predictions from a geometric feed-forward model. At the global level, dense keyframe reconstructions are registered using GICP, while consecutive submaps are aligned in Sim(3) to correct residual scale inconsistencies. These geometric constraints are incorporated into a global factor graph, enabling long-term trajectory consistency and dense metric reconstruction while limiting expensive dense processing to selected keyframes. Experiments on KITTI, Newer College, and in-house runs demonstrate the potential of combining sparse but reliable geometric estimation with dense learned scene reconstruc-tion. The proposed formulation retains the metric consistency and robustness of model-based stereo visual odometry while exploiting feed-forward models to obtain substantially richer scene geometry. Our current system focuses on odometry and does not yet incorporate loop closure. Future work will therefore investigate loop-closure constraints for improved long-term global consistency, as well as computational op-timisation and asynchronous processing toward real-time deployment.
