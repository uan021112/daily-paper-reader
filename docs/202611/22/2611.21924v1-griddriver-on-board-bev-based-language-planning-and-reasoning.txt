# GridDriver: On-Board BEV-Based Language Planning and Reasoning 

Katharina Winter 1, William Engel 1, Zain A. Alishammat 1, Fabian B. Flohr 1

Abstract —Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV)-conditioned large language models (LLMs) enable language-interactive driving, but onboard deployment requires efficient perception and continuous planning alongside autoregressive language generation. We present Grid-Driver, an efficient BEV-conditioned LLM framework combining trajectory prediction, structured driving indicators, natural-language navigation, driving explanations, and scene question answering. Lightweight spatio-temporal BEV perception and spatially structured tokens provide scene context to a shared LLM, while frame-wise scheduling interleaves trajectory predic-tion with asynchronous language generation. GridDriver achieves competitive performance on public benchmarks, reaching 87.9% PDMS on NAVSIM and 57.6% accuracy on nuScenes-QA with a shared planning and QA checkpoint. Spatial representation analyses demonstrate improved geometric information retention and trajectory prediction compared with Q-Former compression. The system achieves 32.7 ms standalone planning latency and 9.4 plans/s in an onboard ROS 2 pipeline. We further evaluate planning on our own data recordings and demonstrate open-loop onboard operation on a research vehicle. Across public benchmarks and real-world evaluations, we identify strengths and limitations of the BEV-based approach. 

Index Terms —Autonomous Driving, Bird’s-Eye-View Percep-tion, Large Language Models, Language-Conditioned Planning, Real-Time Onboard Inference 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) have extended autonomous driving with natural-language instructions [1]–[3], question answering [4]–[6], and driving descriptions [7]. These capa-bilities allow driving models to combine trajectory prediction with language understanding and generation. Grounding such models in the driving scene requires spatial information about road geometry, surrounding agents, and their motion from multiple camera views. Bird’s-eye-view (BEV) representa-tions fuse multi-camera observations into a shared ego-centric frame [8], providing spatial and temporal context aligned with trajectory prediction. This makes BEV a suitable interface for language-conditioned planning [1], [6], [9], [10]. Moving BEV-conditioned LLM planning toward real-vehicle operation introduces additional requirements. Complex urban scenes demand rich spatial representations, while on-board computation requires efficient perception and inference. At the same time, the language capabilities motivating the use of an LLM should remain useful in practice, from natural-language navigation to passenger-facing driving explanations. M ETHOD 

GridDriver adopts the BEV-conditioned language planning framework established by BEVDriver [1], integrating spatial BEV features with natural-language instructions for trajec-tory prediction. A modular perception stack employs BEV-Former [8] to aggregate spatio-temporal features across camera views. As in RDADriver [9], grid tokens preserve spatial scene structure while enabling efficient integration with a reasoning and planning decoder. The decoder jointly generates trajectory waypoints and textual scene descriptions. 

a) Perception: For efficient inference, we employ BEVFormer-tiny [8] with a ResNet-50 backbone, three en-coder layers, and half-resolution multi-view images. It pro-duces a 50 × 50 × 256 BEV representation covering ±51 .2 m(2.048 m per cell). Temporal information is incorporated through recurrent ego-motion-aligned BEV features. An ego-motion MLP injects an 18-dimensional vehicle-state vector into every BEV query to account for ego dynamics in the scene representation, coming from vehicle CAN bus or INS/IMU. The encoder is trained for 3D detection with BEVFormer’s DETR-style head and additionally fine-tuned with a drivable-area head, which regresses the signed distance rasterized from the HD map to the drivable-area boundary. Both heads shape the BEV only during encoder training. A traffic-light head predicts four states (none, red, amber, green) for each of three directions using features from the encoder’s frozen ResNet-50 backbone. The resulting 12 channels are concatenated with the BEV map and projected back to 256 dimensions. After layer normalization and padding to 54 × 54 , non-overlapping 

3 × 3 patches are flattened, augmented with learned posi-tional embeddings, and projected through a two-layer MLP (2304 → 2048 → 2048 ), yielding 18 × 18 = 324 BEV tokens. 

b) Reasoning and Planning Decoder: The LLM input is the navigation instruction, the 324 BEV tokens, a learned 

<plan> token and an optional explicit ego-state token. The latter is used in all NAVSIM configurations and ablated on nuScenes. In the NL variant, the four command slots are zeroed, so the route reaches the model only through the text. Under causal self-attention, every BEV token attends to the instruction and to the preceding cells, and the <plan> token, which attends to the whole sequence, is the read-out. A two-layer MLP maps the hidden state at the <plan> token to six waypoints (x, y ) in the ego frame at 0.5s intervals over 3s. For video visualizations and real-vehicle deployment, a causal Kalman filter smooths predicted trajectories to reduce frame-to-frame jitter. All quantitative planning evaluations use the raw, unfiltered predictions. Icons and trajectory are read from the same hidden state. The icons therefore expose what the state that produces the plan encodes about the scene. The LLM additionally generates free-form explanations of planning decisions. Within the same checkpoint, explanations can either follow the predicted plan, describing the decision, or precede it, allowing the plan to attend to the generated reasoning. IV. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP 

A. Datasets and Evaluation Protocols 

nuScenes. We evaluate open-loop planning on 6,019 valida-tion keyframes (150 scenes) on L2 (m) and collision rate (%). Following the GPT-Driver [23] ST-P3 scorer, where missing future steps contribute zero error and vehicle collisions are evaluated against future occupancy with ground-truth overlaps masked. We also evaluate nuScenes-QA on 83,337 questions from the same validation frames. 

NAVSIM. We evaluate NAVSIM v1.1 navtest PDMS [24] and v2 navhard two-stage EPDMS [25] using 4s trajectories. PDMS assesses collisions, drivable-area compliance, time-to-collision, progress, and comfort through non-reactive simula-tion with recorded agent trajectories. EPDMS adds traffic-rule, lane-keeping, and extended-comfort metrics. Second stage evaluates precomputed follow-up states, weighted by prox-imity to the first-stage endpoint, to approximate closed-loop behavior. Unlike L2 error, these scores assess driving quality without requiring exact imitation of the human trajectory. 

In-house urban dataset. Our in-house dataset comprises approximately 251 km of urban driving recorded in a single city recorded with six cameras, LiDAR, and a NovAtel INS at 10 Hz, organized into 20s scenes with 2Hz keyframes. It contains 1,812 training scenes (4,240 manually annotated and 68,240 pseudo-labeled keyframes), 18 validation scenes (630 evaluated keyframes across three geographically held-out areas), and 268 additional unannotated scenes. We plan to release the dataset publicly in the future. 

a) Navigation and Explanation Supervision: Natural-language navigation annotations is generated for nuScenes, NAVSIM, and the in-house dataset from driven trajectories and map topology. Map-matched junction manoeuvres (left, right, straight, or U-turn) are combined with available landmarks and varied sentence templates, e.g. “Take a left at the T-junction”. Unlike conventional direction commands, our anno-tations describe junction decisions rather than road curvature: bends without junctions receive “Follow the road”, reducing direct cues about future trajectory shape. On nuScenes, 44% of conventional left/right commands have no corresponding junction turn in our annotations, illustrating this distinction. We use these annotations for nuScenes and our in-house dataset. Explanation labels are generated for nuScenes and in-house data from annotated 3D boxes, maps, and ego motion using rule-based templates, without human- or LLM-written text. Geometric rules identify interactions and causes of ego-motion. An independent reviewer checked 200 sampled vali-dation frames per dataset against camera images. Labels were fully correct in 89.9% of nuScenes frames and 90.4% of in-house frames, with missed agents the main nuScenes error. On the same nuScenes frames, model-generated explanations without explicit ego-state input were fully correct in 80.0%, with 87.0% correct main statements. For NAVSIM, we use ReCogDrive’s released QA-derived action and reason descrip-tions [16]. 

B. Implementation and Training Details 

We use six cameras and Qwen3-1.7B with LoRA ( r = 64 ,

α = 128 ) on attention and MLP projections. All BEVFormer-tiny encoders initialize from the official nuScenes-pretrained checkpoint, are fine-tuned per dataset, and frozen for planner training. nuScenes uses 6 encoder epochs with a drivable-area head; NAVSIM uses 9 epochs on navtrain detection labels, using six of eight cameras at half resolution. Drivable-area tar-gets on nuScenes come from the map-expansion drivable and car-park layers, on NAVSIM from the scorer’s own drivable-area polygons; supervision is only applied during training. In-house training uses up to 12 epochs without drivable-area supervision (missing map labels) on manually labeled training frames, selecting by validation mAP; ego-state comes from NovAtel INS. BEVFormer provides recurrent temporal fusion, restricted on NAVSIM to four 2Hz frames (current + 3 past). With cached BEV features, we train the grid tokenizer, ego-state MLP, waypoint head, and LoRA adapters for 30 epochs using mixed-precision AdamW (peak LR 10 −4, 2,000 warm-up steps, cosine decay, weight decay 0.06, batch size 64). Training runs on NVIDIA A40 and A100 GPUs. Encoder fine-tuning takes about 28 A40 GPU-hours on nuScenes. With cached BEV features, one 30-epoch planner run takes about 12 A100 GPU-hours on nuScenes (2 GPUs, 6 h) and about 43 A100 GPU-hours on NAVSIM (4 GPUs, 11 h). We predict ego-frame waypoints at 0.5 s intervals: six (3 s) for nuScenes and the in-house dataset and eight (4 s) for NAVSIM. NAVSIM uses ego status and waypoint headings are obtained by central differences of predicted positions. The official scorer tracks these poses using LQR and a kinematic bicycle model, without test-time simulator access. We report navtest PDMS (v1.1) and non-reactive EPDMS (v2). Evaluations average three seeds unless stated otherwise. nuScenes uses seeds 42,1,2 with checkpoints selected by minimum validation planning L2 (23,23,18, also for QA). NAVSIM trains on all navtrain with seeds 42,43,44, using the final epoch (29). In-house evaluation uses seeds 42,2,1 with best val epochs 21,22,25 with ego-state and 26,8,15 without planner ego-state respectively. Benchmark evaluations and onboard inference use post-plan, language interventions use pre-plan explanations. The training objective combines waypoint regression with optional language, icon and navigation supervision: 

L = Lwp + λtext Ltext + λicon Licon + λnav Lnav , (1) where Lwp is the mean L1 waypoint error, Ltext is next-token cross-entropy for teacher-forced explanations, and Licon 

is binary cross-entropy over 18 driving indicator icons cover-ing road conditions, following, vehicle states, watching, and yielding. Lnav is class-weighted cross-entropy over maneuver (none, straight, left, right, U-turn) and phase (none, approach-ing, turning). Their combinations determine four navigation display icons: preparing-left/right and turning-left/right. The indicator and navigation heads are linear classifiers on a shared two-layer trunk that reads the same LLM hidden state as the waypoint head, adding no decoding step. We set λtext =

λicon = 1 and λnav = 0 .2 when labels are available and 0 otherwise. We sample pre-plan and post-plan explanations equally. indicator and navigation losses apply only to post-plan samples, preventing target leakage from the explanation. Explanations average 9 tokens (maximum 32). 

C. Baselines and Comparability 

Baselines primarily comprise published VLA models, alongside non-VLA methods such as DiffusionDrive [26] and SimWAM [27], with fixed-policy configurations with varying training data, backbones (0.5B–8B for language models), and evaluation protocols. GridDriver uses a nuScenes-pretrained BEV encoder and benchmark-specific imitation learning with no separate large-scale driving-VQA pretraining of the LLM or test-time adaptation. The NL evaluations replace standard direc-tion commands with junction-aware, map-derived instructions, while retaining the same planning architecture.

Removing or replacing explanations alters planned motion to a shorter vs. longer plan, particularly when the vehicle is waiting. We hypothesize that the model associates explanatory sentences with the onset of movement, as such sentences often follow a cue to proceed. The effects are similar across models with and without explicit ego-state input. Hazard warnings shorten planned travel even when no hazard is present, whereas clearance messages increase it even when the path is blocked. Clearance examples highlight the risk of language dominating visual evidence, motivating stronger visual grounding. Navigation instructions also steer the plan: Reversing a turn shifts the trajectory toward the requested direction, while replacing a turn with straight increases planned travel. Impos-sible turns leave travel distance largely unchanged but induce small lateral shifts, suggesting that scene geometry moderates, but does not prevent, responses to invalid instructions. D. Architecture Ablations 

We conduct ablations on nuScenes without explicit LLM ego-state input, while retaining BEVFormer’s ego-motion alignment and CAN-bus conditioning. We replace 32 Q-Former queries with 18 ×18 spatial grid tokens and use scene-disjoint linear probes to examine whether BEV layout remains accessible throughout the LLM and reaches the waypoint head. Scene-disjoint ridge probes (three seeds) in Table VI show that grid tokens retain more linearly decodable vehicle oc-cupancy and drivable area than Q-Former queries ( R2: 0.37 vs. 0.10 and 0.62 vs. 0.32 at LLM input.) The advantage persists through the LLM, including at the plan token (0.08 vs. 0.03 and 0.30 vs. 0.15). With 28× fewer tokenizer parameters (9.7M vs. 270M), grid tokens preserve 3.7× higher vehicle-occupancy R2 and have faster inference (59ms vs 87ms at batch 1 on an A40 GPU). Grid tokens also improve lead-vehicle recall in generated explanations from 49% to 64% at similar precision (71% vs. 73%) and reduce distance error from 4.1m to 3.0m. Under identical training settings, grid tokens reduce planning L2 from 0.50 ±0.01 m to 0.47 ±0.00 mwith unchanged collision rates (0.15%). A. Real-time Inference and Scheduling 

Figure 5 illustrates the schedule, which interleaves token generation with subsequent planning passes to avoid delaying trajectory prediction, completing sentence k in frame k + 2 

on 10Hz sensor inputs. For each frame, the planning pass produces waypoints, the first sentence token, and a KV cache. Subsequent single-token passes extend the sentence across frames while the next frame’s BEV encoder runs on a separate GPU: while the planner computes the plan and continues the explanation for frame t, the encoder already processes frame t+1 . This overlap affects only the pipeline throughput. The latencies in Table VII are measured on a single GPU without it. Once EOS is reached, the sentence is published with its originating frame’s timestamp and the cache is discarded. Table VII reports encoder and planner latency on an RTX 4090. Planning and reasoning share one forward pass, yielding the first explanation token 0.1 ms after the plan. Subsequent tokens use Key Value caching and take approximately 12 ms each, with ten tokens per sentence on average. LoRA merging, FP16, and CUDA graphs reduce planning latency from 56.0 to 32.7 ms (30.6 Hz), with 153 ms mean sentence generation, providing substantial computational headroom for 10 Hz planning. The optimizations preserve output fidelity over 4,405 unseen in-house frames: Relative to the model as trained, ADE (0.443 m) and FDE (0.948 m) change by less than 0.2 mm, maximum waypoint deviation is 3.2 cm, and maneuver/phase agreement reaches 99.8%. All 237 compared explanations remain identical. The integrated ROS 2 pipeline achieves 9.4 plans/s, with remaining overhead from sensor transport, preprocessing, synchronization, and scheduling, which requires further system-level optimization. B. Live On-Board Demonstration 

We evaluate GridDriver in open-loop mode during real-world driving. As shown in Fig. 6, the in-vehicle interface continuously overlays predicted trajectories onto the front-camera view achieving real-time updates. Frequent reasoning output and too many icons overwhelm the passenger, thus plan tokens are visualized as intuitive icons indicating detected road users, traffic infrastructure, and driving intentions, such as yielding or following. While some context is expected immediately (e.g. traffic light turning green), repeated expla-nations of a longer maneuver should be avoided. The model’s textual reasoning is displayed upon completion and, together with Mapbox-triggered navigation instructions, communicated through synthesized speech. This setup highlights the chal-lenge of providing correct, timely, contextually relevant, and appropriately detailed reasoning during real-world driving, where delayed or excessive explanations may reduce their usefulness to passengers. VII. D ISCUSSION 

GridDriver achieves competitive BEV-based language-conditioned planning, while spatial grid tokens enable more accurate linear probing of scene information than Q-Former compression. However, coarse BEV resolution (2.048 m/cell), limited coverage (±51.2 m), and imperfect cross-domain per-ception constrain scene understanding. Low trajectory error also does not guarantee suitable driving behavior, motivating complementary safety, progress, and comfort evaluation. Language interventions reveal that explanations influence trajectories, even when they contradict visual scene evidence. Shared planning features couple trajectories and structured indicators, but free-form explanations may not faithfully rep-resent driving decisions. Real-world shadow-mode operation does not steer the vehicle but demonstrates computational fea-sibility. It also enables evaluation of system requirements and passenger interactions under real driving conditions, without establishing closed-loop safety. Future work includes closed-loop validation, improved visual grounding, more diverse navigation instructions, and onboard question answering. Re-inforcement learning and improved reasoning methods may further enhance planning robustness and causal understanding in diverse driving scenarios. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented GridDriver, a BEV-conditioned language plan-ner that jointly predicts trajectories, driving indicators, and language explanations. The model achieves competitive plan-ning and QA performance on public benchmarks, while real-time open-loop deployment on a research vehicle demonstrates onboard feasibility. Language interventions and real-world observations reveal both the influence of language on planning and remaining challenges in visual grounding and reasoning consistency. Code and reproducibility resources will support further research on onboard BEV–based LLM planning.
