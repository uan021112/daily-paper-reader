# Abstract 

Feed-forward 4D driving-scene reconstruction enables ef-ficient modeling of dynamic autonomous-driving scenes, but current methods suffer from two complementary fail-ure modes: semantic leakage of traffic participants into the static representation and geometric drift of Gaussian locations across viewpoints. We propose IGRecon4D ,a feed-forward dynamic scene reconstruction framework that addresses these failures with training-time instance-prior distillation and LiDAR-anchored Gaussian position-ing. Specifically, an instance-level semantic branch pro-vides a potentially dynamic participant prior, which is distilled into dynamic-mask learning through the Instance-Dynamic Consistency Constraint to encourage high-recall localization of traffic-participant regions prone to dynamic-static leakage. In parallel, sparse LiDAR measurements guide dense depth estimation, and the resulting depths are back-projected to anchor Gaussian positions for im-proved multi-view geometric consistency. Experiments on the Waymo Open Dataset and zero-shot evaluation on nuScenes demonstrate improved full-image reconstruction on the main benchmark and stronger traffic-participant-region reconstruction under cross-dataset evaluation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Dynamic scene reconstruction aims to recover time-varying 3D scenes from visual observations, which is es-sential for autonomous driving, simulation, and embodied perception. Early implicit methods such as NeRF-based dynamic reconstruction [1, 2, 3] model spatiotemporal variations through neural fields, while explicit methods such as 3D Gaussian Splatting [4] and its dynamic ex-tensions [5] improve rendering efficiency with Gaussian primitives. However, these methods typically rely on costly per-scene optimization, limiting their scalability to long driving sequences and large-scale datasets. To reduce per-scene optimization, feed-forward geo-metric estimation methods have emerged as an efficient alternative. Unlike traditional SfM pipelines such as COLMAP [6], these methods directly predict geomet-ric information from one or multiple input views through a single network forward pass. Representative methods such as DUSt3R [7], VGGT [8], and π3 [9] have shown strong geometric prediction ability, providing a foundation for recent feed-forward reconstruction methods. Building on these advances in feed-forward geomet-ric estimation, recent methods further predict Gaussian primitives in a feed-forward manner. In recent years, large reconstruction models (LRMs) [10] based on the Transformer architecture have demonstrated remarkable progress in 3D reconstruction, particularly multi-view re-construction methods built upon the Vision Transformer (ViT) [11]. Furthermore, several studies have extended feed-forward reconstruction to Gaussian-based scene rep-resentations, including Flash3D [12], MVSplat [13], No-PoSplat [14], AnySplat [15], and DepthSplat [16]. These methods achieve efficient 3D reconstruction and high-quality rendering but are primarily designed for static scenes. For dynamic scenes, recent efforts have begun to explore feed-forward 4D reconstruction. L4GM[17] introduced the first feed-forward framework for 4D Gaussian reconstruc-tion, but it mainly focuses on object-level modeling and does not readily generalize to complex scene-level recon-struction. Subsequently, STORM[18] proposed a feed-forward framework for dynamic scene reconstruction, sig-nificantly improving reconstruction efficiency. DGGT[19] further extends feed-forward reconstruction to multi-view autonomous driving scenarios. Nevertheless, existing feed-forward dynamic reconstruction methods still face difficul-ties in complex scene-level autonomous driving scenarios, where accurate geometry, multi-view consistency, and reliable localization of traffic-participant regions are all required. Beyond geometry and appearance reconstruction, sev-eral feed-forward reconstruction methods also predict ad-ditional scene modalities such as instance segmentation. IGGT [20] and EPS3D [21] predict instance-level seman-tics through feature clustering, while SegVGGT [22] fur-ther improves segmentation quality with a query-based for-mulation. However, these methods rarely exploit instance-level semantic cues to improve reconstruction itself. We use such cues as a potentially dynamic participant prior for high-recall localization of traffic-participant regions that are prone to dynamic-static leakage. Building upon these observations, we propose IGRe-con4D , a feed-forward framework for dynamic scene re-construction that jointly addresses Gaussian prediction 1arXiv:2611.21126v1 [cs.CV] 16 Nov 2026 and traffic-participant-region localization. Our method employs a ViT [11] encoder followed by a series of alternat-ing attention layers to extract hierarchical scene features, which are shared by both the reconstruction branch and the instance-level semantic branch. For dynamic scene reconstruction, a set of prediction heads is used to estimate Gaussian parameters and dy-namic masks. To improve the geometric accuracy of the reconstructed scene, we further leverage LiDAR priors to predict more accurate depth, providing more reliable Gaussian positions. The predicted dynamic masks allocate traffic-participant regions that may cause dynamic-static leakage to per-frame Gaussians, while the remaining re-gions are accumulated into the static Gaussian set. During rendering, each frame is synthesized by jointly rendering the static Gaussians and the dynamic Gaussians corre-sponding to the current timestamp. To improve traffic-participant-region reconstruction, we introduce an instance-level semantic branch that provides a potentially dynamic participant prior for dynamic Gaus-sian modeling. Specifically, the learned instance-level rep-resentations supervise dynamic mask prediction through an Instance-Dynamic Consistency Constraint, encourag-ing high-recall localization of traffic-participant regions that are prone to dynamic-static leakage. Extensive experiments on the Waymo Open Dataset demonstrate that IGRecon4D improves overall reconstruc-tion quality, traffic-participant-region reconstruction, and novel-view consistency over representative feed-forward reconstruction baselines. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: 1. We propose IGRecon4D , a feed-forward framework that unifies dynamic 4D Gaussian reconstruction and instance-level semantic learning for autonomous driving scenes. 2. We introduce an Instance-Dynamic Consistency Con-straint that exploits instance-level semantic cues as a po-tentially dynamic participant prior to supervise dynamic mask prediction, leading to improved high-recall local-ization and reconstruction of traffic-participant regions prone to dynamic-static leakage. 3. We incorporate LiDAR-guided depth estimation to derive reliable Gaussian positions, enabling LiDAR-anchored Gaussian positioning and improving geometric accuracy and multi-view consistency as validated by novel-view synthesis and ablation studies. 4. Extensive experiments on Waymo and zero-shot nuScenes evaluation demonstrate improved full-image re-construction on the main benchmark and stronger cross-dataset traffic-participant-region reconstruction. 

# 2 Related Work 

# 2.1 Optimization-based Reconstruction 

Dynamic 3D scene reconstruction aims to recover the ge-ometry, appearance, and temporal motion of a scene from monocular or multi-view videos. Compared with static reconstruction, this task must additionally handle mov-ing objects, occlusions, and temporal consistency across frames. Early RGB-D reconstruction methods exploit depth measurements for non-rigid geometry recovery[23], but their performance depends heavily on sensor quality and often degrades under occlusion, rapid motion, or noisy depth observations. Neural scene representations have significantly advanced dynamic reconstruction [24]. NeRF-based methods [1] commonly model dynamics by introducing time-dependent deformation fields or canonical-space mappings, as in D-NeRF [2], NeRFies [25], and Neural 3D Video [26]. More recently, dynamic Gaussian-based methods build upon 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [4] by introducing temporal parameters or motion-aware Gaussian representations [27, 28, 29]. These representations improve rendering efficiency and dynamic-scene fidelity, but they still typically rely on per-scene optimization, which limits their scalability to large autonomous-driving datasets. To improve geometry and temporal consistency un-der sparse observations [30], recent dynamic reconstruc-tion systems incorporate additional visual priors such as depth, optical flow, tracking, and semantic or instance cues [5, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. These priors help resolve ambi-guities in dynamic regions, but they are often integrated through multi-stage pipelines, for example, sequentially estimating depth or flow before reconstruction, resulting in additional system complexity and computational cost. This motivates reconstruction frameworks that can com-bine efficient feed-forward inference with reliable geomet-ric cues and high-recall localization of traffic-participant regions. 

# 2.2 Feed-forward Neural Reconstruction 

Feed-forward 3D reconstruction aims to directly predict scene representations, such as NeRFs [1] or 3D Gaussian primitives [4], through a single forward pass of a neural network, thereby eliminating the costly per-scene opti-mization required by conventional reconstruction meth-ods. Compared with optimization-based approaches, feed-forward methods offer significantly higher inference effi-ciency and stronger cross-scene generalization, making them an increasingly important research direction in re-cent years. An important precursor to feed-forward 3D reconstruc-tion is feed-forward geometric estimation [36], which aims to recover scene geometry with a single neural network forward pass. Unlike traditional per-scene SfM pipelines such as COLMAP [6], these methods take one or multiple images as input and directly predict geometric informa-tion such as depth maps, point maps, camera poses, or tracks. DPT [37] estimates dense depth from a single image with a ViT-based architecture, while DUSt3R [7] extends feed-forward geometric reconstruction to two in-put views and enforces two-view geometric consistency. VGGT [8] further supports reconstruction from more in-2put views and jointly estimates depth, camera poses, and tracks, whereas π3 [9] decouples reconstruction from the ordering of input views, improving the robustness of feed-forward geometric prediction. These methods provide effective geometric priors for 3D reconstruction and mo-tivate subsequent feed-forward methods to move beyond geometry prediction toward renderable 3D representation reconstruction. Building on this direction, early feed-forward recon-struction methods mainly focus on object-level reconstruc-tion. Representative approaches such as LRM [10] and LGM [17] learn ViT-based reconstruction priors and can efficiently recover high-quality 3D representations from only a few input views. By learning rich geometric pri-ors from large-scale datasets, these methods substantially improve reconstruction speed while maintaining competi-tive reconstruction quality. More recently, feed-forward reconstruction has been extended from object-level to scene-level modeling. Methods such as Flash3D [12], MVSplat [13], NoPoSplat [14], DepthSplat [16], and AnyS-plat [15] leverage feed-forward prediction of 3D Gaussian representations to achieve an effective balance between reconstruction quality and computational efficiency. Un-like optimization-based Gaussian reconstruction methods, these approaches directly infer Gaussian parameters from input observations, enabling efficient scene reconstruction and rendering. Nevertheless, they are primarily designed for static scenes and cannot be readily generalized to dynamic environments. To address dynamic scene reconstruction, recent studies have begun to investigate feed-forward 4D scene modeling. L4GM [17] introduced the first feed-forward framework for 4D Gaussian reconstruction, demonstrating the feasibility of directly predicting dynamic Gaussian representations. However, its design mainly targets object-level reconstruc-tion and does not readily generalize to complex scene-level environments. More recently, DrivingForward [38], STORM [18], NoPo4D [39], 4DGT [40], DrivingRecon [41], DrivingScene [42], DynamicVGGT [43], and StreetFor-ward [44] have extended the feed-forward paradigm to dynamic scene reconstruction, significantly alleviating the computational burden of per-scene optimization. DGGT [19] is closely related to our setting, as it sup-ports feed-forward dynamic reconstruction from multi-view autonomous-driving videos. Nevertheless, existing feed-forward dynamic reconstruction methods still face difficulties in complex driving scenes, where accurate geom-etry, multi-view consistency, and traffic-participant-region localization are all required. In particular, insufficient localization of traffic-participant regions can cause regions prone to dynamic-static leakage to be modeled as static content, while inaccurate geometry weakens cross-view consistency. Overall, dynamic-static separation remains a central challenge for dynamic reconstruction methods, since the ability to disentangle and model moving ob-jects and the static background is critical to the final reconstruction quality. 

# 2.3 Joint Geometric Reconstruction and Multimodal Prediction 

Some feed-forward reconstruction methods go beyond scene reconstruction by predicting additional modalities, such as instance segmentation, while inferring scene geom-etry and appearance in a single forward pass. Built upon the feed-forward architecture of VGGT [8], IGGT [20] and EPS3D [21] reconstruct the scene and obtain instance segmentation by predicting instance features and clus-tering them. To alleviate the segmentation degradation caused by the instability of clustering-based methods, SegVGGT [22] adopts a query-based formulation com-monly used in instance segmentation and queries multi-level features to further improve segmentation quality. Al-though these methods predict instance-level information during reconstruction, the interaction between different modalities remains limited. MVGGT [45] further takes an additional text prompt as input and localizes the cor-responding target in static scenes, while G2T AM [46] extends this capability to dynamic scenes. These methods show that feed-forward reconstruction can be extended toward multimodal scene understanding; however, they do not fully exploit auxiliary modalities such as instance seg-mentation to improve reconstruction itself. For dynamic scene reconstruction, we treat instance-level semantic cues as a potentially dynamic participant prior rather than true motion-state labels. Leveraging this prior for high-recall localization of traffic-participant regions that are prone to dynamic-static leakage can improve traffic-participant-region reconstruction and overall reconstruction quality. 

# 3 Method 

Given multi-camera videos, camera parameters, and sparse LiDAR depth maps from an autonomous driving scene, IGRecon4D reconstructs a dynamic 4D Gaussian representation in a feed-forward manner. The framework contains two coupled branches: a reconstruction branch that predicts Gaussian attributes, dynamic masks, and Gaussian positions derived from LiDAR-guided depth esti-mation, and an instance-level semantic branch that learns instance-level cues as a potentially dynamic participant prior for mask supervision. An overview of the proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. The reconstruction branch first extracts hierarchical spatiotemporal features with a ViT backbone and alternat-ing attention layers. It predicts Gaussian attributes and dynamic masks from these features, while dense depth esti-mated from sparse LiDAR measurements is back-projected to obtain geometrically reliable Gaussian positions. The resulting Gaussians are then decomposed into dynamic and static components and rendered with a differentiable rasterizer. # 4 Experiments 

# 4.1 Datasets and Evaluation Protocol 

We conduct comprehensive experiments on the Waymo Open Dataset[52]. The dataset covers a wide variety of driving scenarios, including urban streets, highways, and complex traffic environments, and contains abundant traffic participants (e.g., pedestrians, vehicles, and cy-clists) under diverse illumination and weather conditions. These characteristics make it well suited for evaluating the generalization ability and robustness of dynamic scene reconstruction methods in challenging real-world scenar-ios. During training, we use the entire training split, which consists of 798 scenes. Each scene contains approximately 190–200 consecutive frames, providing sufficient temporal information for learning dynamic scene representations. During evaluation, we report results on the validation split containing 202 scenes. Unless otherwise specified, all reconstruction methods are evaluated under the same validation split, input camera configuration, and metric computation protocol. Before training and inference, we perform several pre-processing steps. To obtain instance-level semantic an-notations, we employ SAM3[53] to automatically gener-ate instance masks for each scene. Specifically, language prompts corresponding to traffic participant categories, including pedestrian , vehicle , and cyclist , are used to ex-tract the corresponding instances. These masks are used as a potentially dynamic participant prior rather than as ground-truth motion-state labels. In addition, we gener-ate sky masks for every frame using LISA[54]. Benefiting from its language-guided semantic segmentation capabil-ity, LISA accurately identifies large semantic regions with weak texture, such as the sky, and produces reliable sky masks for supervision during training. 

# 4.2 Implementation Details 

Our training procedure consists of two stages. In the first stage, we train the instance-level semantic branch to learn instance-aware semantic representations. In the second stage, we leverage the learned semantic representations to improve dynamic scene reconstruction through the pro-posed Instance-Dynamic Consistency Constraint. Both stages are trained on eight NVIDIA B200 GPUs. Follow-ing DGGT[19], we adopt the same data sampling strategy by uniformly sampling four frames from every consecu-tive 20-frame sequence captured by the three front-facing cameras. In both stages, the AdamW[55] optimizer is em-ployed together with a linear warm-up schedule followed by cosine learning rate decay. In the first stage, the batch size is set to 8 per GPU, and the model is trained for 10,000 iterations. We initialize the backbone with the pretrained VGGT[8] weights and perform LoRA[56] fine-tuning (rank = 32) on the alter-nating attention layers. Meanwhile, the cross-attention layers, self-attention layers, MLPs, and the instance pre-diction head are fully fine-tuned. We use 100 learnable instance query embeddings in the instance-level semantic branch. The learning rates are set to 6 × 10 −5 for the LoRA parameters and 2 × 10 −4 for the fully trainable parameters. We set λcls = 0 .5 and λinst = 1 .0 in this stage. In the second stage, the batch size is reduced to 1 per GPU, and the model is trained for 50,000 iterations. The depth predictor is initialized with the pretrained InfiniDepth[48] weights. We freeze the LoRA-adapted alternating attention layers as well as the modules trained in the first stage, and optimize only the Gaussian predic-tion head and the dynamic prediction head. The learning 7rate in this stage is set to 4 × 10 −5. We set λlpips = 0 .05, 

λdynamic = 0 .05, λreg = 0 .01 and λsky = 1 .0 in this stage. During inference, we use 20 consecutive frames captured by the three front-facing cameras as input and evaluate the performance of our method. We conduct our evaluation on a single NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000 GPU. 

# 4.3 Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods 

We compare the proposed method with several repre-sentative feed-forward reconstruction approaches to com-prehensively evaluate its performance on dynamic scene reconstruction. Specifically, the compared feed-forward methods include AnySplat[15], STORM[18], DGGT[19], NoPo4D[39], and DrivingForward[38]. For a fair compar-ison, all methods are evaluated using the same number of input frames, namely 20 consecutive frames captured by the three front-facing cameras. Comparisons with additional methods are provided in Appendix B.1. For quantitative evaluation, we report image quality, geometric consistency, and computational cost. Image quality is measured using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS[51]. As shown in Table 1, our method improves PSNR over the strongest baseline DGGT[19] by 2.58 dB and reduces LPIPS from 0.116 to 0.102, while also achieving a slightly higher SSIM. To assess geometric consistency with Li-DAR observations, we additionally report the average per-scene depth root mean square error (D-RMSE). Fol-lowing a protocol similar to DGGT[19], we linearly align the depths rendered from the reconstructed Gaussians to sparse depths obtained by projecting LiDAR points, compute RMSE at pixels with valid sparse depth, and av-erage the resulting per-scene errors. Our method achieves the lowest D-RMSE of 4.610, compared with 7.470 for DGGT. We also report the runtime and GPU memory usage of each method. Although our method does not have the lowest runtime or memory usage, it completes feed-forward dynamic Gaussian reconstruction in 16.20 s. We further conduct qualitative comparisons with exist-ing feed-forward reconstruction methods. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the compared methods exhibit noticeable dif-ferences in traffic-participant-region reconstruction and texture preservation. Specifically, DGGT[19] provides insufficient high-recall localization of traffic-participant regions that are prone to dynamic-static leakage, caus-ing some pedestrians or vehicles to be absorbed into the static representation and degrading reconstruction quality. STORM[18], DrivingForward[38] and NoPo4D[39] exhibit noticeable information loss during texture modeling, re-sulting in blurred surface details. Although AnySplat[15] achieves competitive performance on static scenes, its lack of explicit per-frame participant-region modeling makes it difficult to faithfully reconstruct traffic participants in dynamic environments. In contrast, our method derives more reliable Gaussian positions through LiDAR-guided depth estimation while better localizing traffic-participant regions that should be modeled by per-frame Gaussians. Consequently, it improves the reconstruction of both static backgrounds and traffic participants, such as pedestrians and vehicles, within a unified framework. This leads to clearer object boundaries and better geometric alignment, as further demonstrated by the qualitative results in Fig. 3. 

# 4.4 Novel View Synthesis 

To further evaluate the multi-view consistency of the proposed method, we conduct a novel-view synthesis ex-periment. Specifically, during inference, we reconstruct the dynamic scene from videos captured by three syn-chronized cameras and obtain the corresponding dynamic Gaussian representation. Instead of rendering from the input viewpoints, we synthesize images from interpolated viewpoints. We denote the front-facing camera as Camera 0, the front-left camera as Camera 1, and the front-right camera as Camera 2. Novel views are rendered from the interpolated viewpoints between Camera 0 and Camera 1, as well as between Camera 0 and Camera 2, to evaluate the reconstruction quality under unseen viewpoints. As a baseline, DGGT is evaluated under the same setting by giving the same camera parameters as input and rendering from the identical interpolated viewpoints. Qualitative comparisons are presented in Fig. 4. As can be observed, DGGT[19] suffers from inaccurate depth estimation, causing noticeable geometric misalignment and spatial shifts of objects that should remain geomet-rically consistent under novel viewpoints. In contrast, our LiDAR-guided depth estimation derives more reliable Gaussian positions for LiDAR-anchored Gaussian posi-tioning, resulting in improved geometric alignment and stronger structural consistency under novel-view render-ing. Quantitative results are reported in Table 2. Because ex-act ground-truth images at the interpolated camera poses are unavailable, we use the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) as a proxy metric and compute the distributional distance between rendered interpolated-view images and real images from the input-view distribution. As shown in Table 2, our method achieves lower FID scores than DGGT[19] under this protocol, suggesting better distri-butional consistency for interpolated-view rendering. Overall, these results suggest that more accurate depth estimation improves the spatial localization of Gaussian primitives, leading to stronger multi-view geometric con-sistency and higher-quality novel-view synthesis.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented IGRecon4D, a feed-forward framework for dynamic scene reconstruction in au-tonomous driving scenarios. The key idea is to lever-age instance-level semantic guidance and LiDAR-guided depth estimation in a feed-forward Gaussian reconstruc-tion pipeline: the instance-level semantic branch provides a potentially dynamic participant prior for mask super-vision, while LiDAR-guided depth estimation derives re-liable Gaussian positions for LiDAR-anchored Gaussian positioning. Experiments on the Waymo Open Dataset show that IGRecon4D improves full-image reconstruction quality over representative feed-forward baselines and yields clear gains on traffic-participant regions in the ablation study. Novel-view synthesis further supports the benefit of LiDAR-anchored Gaussian positioning for multi-view consistency. Under zero-shot evaluation on nuScenes, IGRecon4D achieves the best traffic-participant-region re-construction metrics, although full-image metrics remain influenced by static and background-dominated regions. The current framework is evaluated mainly with three front-facing cameras, sparse LiDAR input, and short video clips. Extending the method to longer temporal windows, broader camera configurations, and larger-scale scenes is an important direction for future work. In addition, the instance-level prior is semantic rather than motion-state supervision, and therefore cannot distinguish static and moving objects of the same category, such as parked and moving vehicles. Reducing training and inference cost would further improve its practicality for large-scale autonomous-driving reconstruction.
