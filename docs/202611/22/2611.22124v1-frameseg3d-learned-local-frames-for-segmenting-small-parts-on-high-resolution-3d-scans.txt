ABSTRACT
Segmenting small parts on high-resolution 3D scans, such as nails on hand scans and teeth on
intraoral scans, poses three challenges: 1) each part covers a tiny fraction of the scan, 2) its
boundary is only a subtle change of height and curvature, and 3) it faces its own direction. Existing
methods fall into two groups, and both fall short. The first group processes the whole scan at once
and sees each part too coarsely. Point and mesh networks must shrink the scan to fit in memory, so
a small part keeps only a few of its points. Multi-view methods render images of the whole object,
so each part fills only a few pixels and a tilted part is seen from the side. The second group first
finds each part and then segments it up close. But these methods find the parts with knowledge
about one kind of part, for example the shape of the dental arch for teeth. Such knowledge does
not carry over to other kinds of parts. We find that accuracy is limited by how a part is seen,
from which direction and at what scale, not by how many points land on it. We therefore present
FrameSeg3D. It first predicts a local frame for every part, its center and the direction it faces,
from a low-resolution view of the whole scan. It then segments the part in views around its frame
normal at the scanner’s resolution, so covering the whole scan no longer costs resolution. Because
every part arrives centered, facing the camera, and at a fixed scale, one segmentation network
can serve different kinds of parts and learn from few labeled masks. We test FrameSeg3D on two
kinds of parts, nails and teeth. It outperforms state-of-the-art methods on hand scans (mean Dice
0.970 on nails) and on the Teeth3DS+ test fold (per-tooth IoU 96.5), with the most accurate tooth
boundaries (boundary IoU 70.5).

Introduction
Small parts on high-resolution scans. 3D scanners capture surfaces in fine detail, from large
statues [28] to the inside of the mouth [34], and many scans are taken to measure one small part of a
larger surface. Examples are a fingernail on a hand scanned for a custom-fit artificial nail [8, 12] and
a tooth on an intraoral scan used to plan a crown [2, 3, 35]. Parts in shape collections [4, 5, 36, 61]
are large, have sharp edges, and often come on aligned shapes. In contrast, these parts are tiny: a
nail holds about 6,000 of a hand scan’s two million vertices (0.3%). Their boundaries are subtle
changes of height and curvature, with no color to help. Each part also faces its own direction. On a
hand lying flat, the thumb’s nail faces a median 56◦away from the mat. Segmenting them vertex by
vertex requires seeing each part at the scanner’s resolution and from roughly the direction it faces.
Existing methods. No existing family of methods sees every part at the scanner’s resolution and from
the direction it faces. Point and mesh networks [15, 29, 38, 39, 45, 54, 56, 64] fit the whole scan into
a fixed budget of samples. On a hand, this leaves 41 to 671 samples on a nail, too few to trace its
boundary. Multi-view methods [3, 25, 33, 49, 58], including zero-shot ones built on 2D foundation
models [1, 30, 50, 67], place their cameras for the whole object. A part gets few pixels, and a tilted
part is seen at a grazing angle. On our hand scans, finer pixels even make a whole-hand render
slightly worse. Two-stage dental methods first find each tooth, then segment it in a 3D crop [3, 7, 53],
a landmark-aligned patch [27], a 2D parameterization [42], or views placed by the dental arch or
the occlusal plane [20, 23]. They come closest to seeing each part on its own, but they have two
limits. They find the teeth through the structure of the dentition, such as the arch, landmarks and
tooth positions, which other parts lack. And their crops or views are set by the whole jaw, not by
the direction each part faces. On our nails, ToothGroupNetwork, the winner of the 3DTeethSeg’22
challenge [3], reaches a mean Dice of only 0.797.
How a part is seen. We argue that small parts are limited by how they are seen, from which direction
and at what scale, rather than by how many samples land on them. With about as many pixels on a
nail, views around the nail’s own normal reach a mean Dice of 0.970, against 0.956 for views of the
whole hand. In images and medical volumes, small objects are handled by zooming in [11, 37, 59, 66]
or by processing each object at a fixed scale [17, 46, 47]. On a 3D surface, a small part must also
be turned toward the camera. Views centered on a nail but placed around the mat’s normal fall
to 0.927 on nails tilted more than 45◦, against 0.965 around the nail’s own normal. So each part
needs a frame: its center and the direction it faces. Finding the frame is hard. It is defined by the
part itself, yet needed before the part is segmented. So local reference frames computed from the
surface around a point [18, 51, 52] cannot provide it, and learned alignment [21, 38, 44] and view
selection [14] act on the whole input, not on each part. It must be found in a view where the part is
small. And it must not rely on the structure of one kind of part, such as the dental arch.
Segmenting each part in its own frame. We present FrameSeg3D, which predicts a coarse frame for
every part and segments each part in its own frame (Fig. 1). Frame prediction looks at the whole scan
once, at low resolution. A frame network [43] on a top-down render finds every part as a heatmap
peak [65], names it, and predicts the direction it faces. Unlike two-stage dental methods, it needs
2


no arch curve, landmark detector, or shape model, and no extra labels: we compute the training
frames from the segmentation masks. In-frame segmentation renders each part from 12 directions
around its own frame normal at 0.08 mm per pixel, finer than the scan’s vertex spacing. So the pixel
size no longer depends on the size of the scan, and the view direction no longer depends on where
the part lies. A 2D network segments every view, and we lift the masks back to the vertices [25]. A
coarse frame is enough, because the segmentation network is trained on perturbed frames and we
re-center the frame once on the first result. Every part then arrives centered, facing the camera, and
at a fixed scale. So one segmentation network serves nails and teeth alike and learns from few labels.
FrameSeg3D reaches a mean Dice of 0.970 on the nails of held-out people, ahead of point, mesh,
and multi-view networks. On the Teeth3DS+ test fold [2], it has the best per-tooth IoU (96.5) and
the most accurate tooth boundaries. With both stages trained on a quarter of the hands or a tenth of
the patients, it still beats the strongest baselines trained on all of them.
Contributions. Our contributions are as follows:
• We propose FrameSeg3D, which segments small parts on high-resolution 3D scans in learned
local frames. It predicts a coarse frame for each part, its center and the direction it faces, from a
low-resolution view of the whole scan, with no domain prior and no labels beyond the segmentation
masks. It then segments 2D views around the frame normal at the scanner’s resolution and lifts
the masks back to the vertices.
• We observe, in controlled experiments, that small-part accuracy is limited by viewing geometry,
not by the number of samples. At matched pixels per part and the same crop center, the part’s
own frame wins. On hand scans, finer pixels help only inside the frame, and the gain grows with
the part’s tilt.
• We present a segmentation network that does not depend on the part and learns from few labels.
Trained on nails and teeth together, it matches two dedicated networks.
• Our experimental results show that FrameSeg3D is the most accurate method on nails and on
the Teeth3DS+ test fold, and draws the most accurate tooth boundaries on both Teeth3DS+ test
sets. On the 3DTeethSeg’22 challenge test set, it trails ToothInstanceNet [53] by 0.4 per-tooth
IoU, mostly on third molars, which are rare in the training fold, so our frame network often places
no frame on them.

Related Work
Segmenting the whole scan. Point networks [38, 39, 54, 56, 57, 62, 64] and mesh networks [15, 45]
segment a scan from sampled points, faces or vertices. On intraoral scans (Teeth3DS+ and the
3DTeethSeg’22 challenge [2, 3]), MeshSegNet [29] and TSGCNet [63] label a downsampled mesh,
and recent methods add boundary-preserving sampling [58] or a B-spline model of the dental
arch [55]. All of them fit a scan into a fixed sample budget, so a small part gets samples only in
proportion to its share of the surface. Multi-view methods render the shape, segment the images, and
fuse the 2D predictions on the surface [22, 25, 49], also with 2D foundation models for zero-shot
part segmentation [1, 30, 31, 50, 60]. For teeth, they render the jaw from random [3] or fixed
viewpoints [33, 58, 67]. Their cameras are placed for the whole object, so a part’s pixel count and
3


viewing angle depend on where it lies. FrameSeg3D keeps the render-and-lift pipeline but places the
cameras per part, around its own normal, at a fixed metric resolution.
Finding the parts first. Zooming into a region is an established idea for images [17, 59]. Two-stage
dental methods bring it to intraoral scans. They locate the teeth and segment each one in a 3D
crop [3, 7, 41, 53], a landmark-aligned patch [27], a 2D parameterization of the crown [42], or
views along the dental arch [20]. Closest to our design, LMVSegRNN segments each tooth from
views that circle the occlusal axis above two detected landmarks [23]. These methods rely on dental
structure to find the teeth, and their crops or views are set by the whole jaw, not by the direction
each tooth faces. For nails, prior work made custom-fit artificial nails from 3D scans [12] and grew
the nail region on a finger scan [8], our classical baseline. Canonical frames are common in learning:
alignment modules align a whole input [21, 38], local reference frames make descriptors invariant
to rotation [52], tangent convolutions view each point along its normal [51], Frustum PointNets
rotate each detected object [40], and FrameNet predicts a frame at every pixel [19]. FrameSeg3D
instead learns one frame per part, its center and facing direction, from a coarse view of the whole
scan, with no dental structure. The frame places the cameras, the scanner’s resolution sets the pixel
size, and the frame labels come for free from the segmentation masks.

3.1 Frame prediction
Goal. Frame prediction Φ takes the whole scan and returns a name 𝑘and a frame ˆT𝑘= (ˆc𝑘, ˆz𝑘) for
every part present. It must find every part, including tilted ones, and name it correctly. The frame
only needs to be close: in-frame segmentation tolerates a center off by 4 mm or a normal off by 20◦
(Sec. 4.5).
Part size. One number, the part size 𝐷, sets every length in frame prediction and the crop radius
in Sec. 3.2. We set 𝐷to the median, over the labeled parts of the training scans, of twice the 90th
percentile of the distance from a part’s centroid to its vertices (𝐷= 11.5 mm for nails, 9.2 mm for
teeth). Twice the 90th percentile approximates a part’s diameter while ignoring stray vertices, and
the median over parts ignores unusual ones. So 𝐷is measured from the labels, not tuned. Nothing
else in the method depends on the kind of part: nails and teeth share every other setting.
Overview image. We take the scan’s up direction u to be its area-weighted mean normal, because it
needs no landmarks: for a hand on a mat it is the mat’s normal, and for a jaw it lies 9◦from the biting
direction. We ray-cast the scan orthographically along u, center the image on the scan’s centroid
and align it with the scan’s principal axes. To keep every part about 20 pixels across, enough to
find it but not to segment it, we set the pixel size to 𝛿0 = 𝐷/20 (0.58 mm for hands, 0.46 mm for
jaws). The overview image I0 has three channels: the height above the local floor, the upward normal
component |n · u|, and coverage.

4
Experiments
The method rests on one idea: a small part is segmented best in its own frame, at the scanner’s
resolution. We test it on two kinds of parts that differ in size and layout. A nail covers a tiny share of
a hand scan and often faces away from the scanner, while teeth fill much of an intraoral scan and sit
close together. Both use the same settings; only the part size 𝐷, which we measure from the labels,
differs from one kind of part to the other.
4.1 Setup
Data. We test on two datasets, one per kind of part.
• Nails. 95 hand scans of 51 people: both hands of 44 people and one hand of the other 7. Each
scan has about two million vertices, spaced 0.2–0.3 mm apart. Human annotators outlined every
nail by hand, and the vertices inside an outline take that nail’s label. The annotators also rated
each nail as usable or not; nails rated unusable are ignored in training and scoring. We split by
person into 30 training people (57 hands, 213 usable nails), 8 validation people (15 hands, 52
nails) and 13 test people (23 hands, 83 nails), and we score every usable test nail.
8


• Teeth. Teeth3DS+ [2] intraoral scans (100–150k vertices, teeth labeled by their FDI number)
with ToothGroupNetwork’s patient folds [3]: 420 training, 90 validation and 90 test patients, two
jaws each. We also test on the 3DTeethSeg’22 private test set, the challenge test set: 600 scans of
300 other patients, disjoint from all training scans.
Both networks are trained with augmentation (Appendix C); validation and test scans are never
augmented. The segmentation network sees mirror images, smooth surface noise and the perturbed
frames of Sec. 3.3. The frame network sees rotations, scalings, mirror images, and scans with smooth
surface noise, holes and small tilts. The corrupted test scans of Tab. 11 are made separately: we
decimate the test scans, add smooth noise to them, or cut one hole into each nail (Appendix B).
Metrics. For nails, we report Dice per nail (mean and median over nails), the average symmetric
surface distance (ASSD), the boundary F-score at 0.25 mm (BF0.25) and the share of nails with
Dice below 0.7. For teeth, we report the per-tooth IoU, the boundary IoU [6] at 0.25 mm and
ToothGroupNetwork’s tooth identification rate (TIR). The per-tooth IoU matches each ground-truth
tooth to the predicted tooth overlapping it most, so a missed tooth scores 0. Our models and the
trained baselines use 3 seeds, and all tables report the mean over the seeds (Appendix A).
Baselines. Whole-scan methods on hands: Point Transformer V3 (PTv3) [56], MeshSegNet [29]
and ToothGroupNetwork [3], all retrained on our hands, and a whole-hand render. It uses our
segmentation network, channels, 12 views within 60◦of the mat normal and lifting, but on the
whole hand. Per-part methods in our predicted frames: region growing [8], DGCNN [54] and
PTv3 on the crop. On teeth: the released TSegNet [7] and ToothGroupNetwork checkpoints;
ToothInstanceNet [53] and the 2026 methods TeethSplineSeg [55] and 3DTeethSAM [33], retrained
on the training fold and selected on the validation fold; and a whole-jaw render like the whole-hand
one. Every baseline trains on the same training scans as FrameSeg3D. Appendix D gives details and
reproduction checks for every baseline.
Implementation details. We use PyTorch, segmentation_models_pytorch, and Open3D ray casting.
One segmentation network serves all kinds of parts, in our experiments nails and teeth. Both networks
train with AdamW [32] and a one-cycle schedule [48], and we keep the checkpoint that is best on
the validation set. All kinds of parts share every setting; only 𝐷, which we measure from the labels,
differs. We use a server with eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs (80 GB each), two Intel Xeon Platinum 8468
CPUs (128 cores), 2 TB of memory and a 2 TB disk; each training run uses one GPU. Appendix C lists
the hardware and software, the training schedules and times, the data augmentation and the values
of the remaining constants.

4.4 One network, few labels
One network for nails and teeth. We train one segmentation network on crops of both kinds, drawn
with equal probability. It matches the two dedicated networks (Tab. 6): mean Dice 0.970 vs. 0.970
on nails, per-tooth IoU 96.48 vs. 96.46 on the test fold, and 95.84 vs. 95.73 on the challenge test set.
It trains for 24,000 steps, twice as many per domain as a dedicated network. Initializing one domain
from the other’s network does not help (Appendix G). The two kinds of parts share a network but do
not help each other.
Few labels. In its canonical view, the segmentation network needs few masks (Tabs. 7 and 8). We first
use frames from the full frame network. Trained on the nails of 3 of the 30 people, the segmentation
network already beats the whole-hand render trained on all 30 (0.966 vs. 0.964). With one person,
it leads the render trained on that person by 0.061 (0.949 vs. 0.888). It also needs fewer labels
than a point network in the same frames: with 1, 3 and 8 people, it leads DGCNN in our frames by
0.014, 0.010 and 0.004 mean Dice. Without a frame, PTv3 on the whole hand falls to 0.568 with
one person. On teeth (Tab. 8), cutting the training patients from 420 to 42 or 5 costs it 0.07 or 0.10
per-tooth IoU, but costs the whole-jaw render 0.54 or 2.41. ToothGroupNetwork, retrained with its
own code on the same 42 or 5 patients, falls to 89.40 or 87.18, from 95.96 for its released checkpoint
on all 420. With 5 patients, our segmentation network still beats that checkpoint (96.38 vs. 95.96).
The frame network is the weaker stage. Trained on the subset too, it loses two nails at 3 people
(mean Dice 0.943), and at one person it finds only 81% of the nails (0.772). Still, the whole method
needs only a fraction of the labels. With both stages trained on 8 of the 30 people, FrameSeg3D
beats the whole-hand render trained on all 30 (0.967 vs. 0.964). With both stages trained on 5 of
420 patients, FrameSeg3D leads ToothGroupNetwork trained on the same patients by 8.98 per-tooth
IoU (96.16 vs. 87.18) and still beats it trained on all 420 (96.16 vs. 95.96). With 42 patients, a tenth
of the training fold, it reaches 96.36, within 0.12 of training on all 420.

Scan quality and cost. FrameSeg3D keeps the highest mean Dice on test hands that are decimated,
made noisy or cut by holes (Tab. 11). Its mean Dice stays at 0.964 or above under every corruption
we test. The whole-scan models fail where their input changes most: PTv3 falls to 0.461 with an
eighth of the faces, and the whole-hand render to 0.891 under 0.1 mm of smooth noise. FrameSeg3D
takes 15.5 s per hand on one GPU, half the time of the whole-hand render at the same 0.08 mm per
pixel. Most of this time goes to the in-frame segmentation, which renders and segments 12 views of
each part in two passes (Appendix B).
5
Conclusion
Small parts on high-resolution 3D scans are limited by the direction and scale at which they are
seen, not by how many samples land on them. FrameSeg3D turns this into a simple method. It
predicts a frame for every part from a coarse view of the whole scan, then segments each part in
views around its frame normal at the scanner’s resolution. With the same settings, it outperforms
whole-scan and two-stage methods on nails and on the Teeth3DS+ test fold, and it draws the most
accurate tooth boundaries. On hands, the gain over the whole-hand render grows with the nail’s
tilt and is largest on the thumb, which faces sideways. The frames also help other networks: in our
frames, DGCNN comes within 0.004 mean Dice of FrameSeg3D on nails. The canonical view also lets
one segmentation network serve both kinds of parts and learn from a fraction of the labels: a quarter
of the hands or a tenth of the patients. Between nails and teeth, only the part size changes, and we
measure it from the labels. So we expect a new kind of part to need labeled scans, not a new design.
Limitations
The frame network is the weak stage. It needs more labels than the segmentation network: trained
on the hands of one person, it misses a fifth of the nails. It misses parts that are rare in training:
on the challenge test set, it places no frame on many third molars, which the training fold almost
lacks. There, ToothInstanceNet segments and names teeth more accurately. The frame network also
assumes that each part faces a clear direction. On jaws, where the crops of neighboring teeth overlap,
FrameSeg3D is slower than rendering the whole jaw and than the dental methods (21.9 s per jaw,
against 1.3 to 8.8 s). We test only two kinds of parts, and all hand scans come from one 3D scanner.
We simulate lower scan quality by decimation, noise and holes, but we have not tested scans from
other scanners. Learning the frame without frame labels, for example from the segmentation loss
through a differentiable renderer, is a natural next step, since the two stages could then be trained
together, end to end.

Appendix
A
Data and metrics
Hand scans. Each hand lies flat on a mat and is scanned with one 3D scanner (about two million
vertices, 0.2–0.3 mm apart). Human annotators outlined each nail by hand and labeled every vertex
as nail (with the finger’s name), skin, or ignore (doubled scan layers and loose fragments). Ignored
vertices are excluded from scoring and from all losses. The annotators also rated each nail as usable
or not. Seven people have one hand in the dataset (three training, one validation and three test
people), and the others both hands. The 83 test nails (11 thumbs) are those with labels rated usable.
The split is by person: 30 training, 8 validation and 13 test people, with 57, 15 and 23 hands and
213, 52 and 83 usable nails.
Nail metrics. Each ground-truth nail is scored against the predicted nail pieces that touch it, whatever
their finger label, so finger names do not affect the scores. Dice weights each vertex by its area. ASSD
is the mean distance between the predicted and ground-truth outlines, in both directions. BF0.25 is
the F-score of outline vertices within 0.25 mm of the other outline: precision counts the predicted
outline vertices near the true outline, and recall the reverse.
Tooth metrics. ToothGroupNetwork’s evaluation code averages IoU and F1 over predicted teeth, so a
missed tooth does not count. Its TIR is the share of predicted teeth with the right number. So we also
report our per-tooth IoU. It matches each ground-truth tooth to the predicted tooth that overlaps it
most, ignoring tooth numbers. A missed tooth scores 0. Boundary IoU [6] is computed on the mesh.
We score all teeth results with one scorer, from per-vertex predictions on the same meshes. Per-tooth
IoU and boundary IoU are means over ground-truth teeth. ToothGroupNetwork’s metrics are means
over scans, as in its evaluation code.
Seeds. Our models and the trained baselines use 3 seeds; tables give the mean. The segmentation
networks trained on label subsets (Appendix G) use one seed. A frame network on a subset is still an
ensemble of 3, as in the full method, with each member trained on the same subset from a different
random seed, and their outputs are averaged before finding peaks.

noise along the normal, or cut one hole into each nail and close it with flat triangles (Tab. 11).
FrameSeg3D is best under every density, noise level and hole size, and its mean Dice never falls
below 0.964, also with an eighth of the faces, under 0.1 mm of smooth noise and with a 3 mm hole
on every nail. The whole-scan models fail where their input changes most: PTv3 falls to 0.461 with
an eighth of the faces, and the whole-hand render to 0.891 under 0.1 mm of noise.

Cost. Table 12 gives the inference cost of every method with the final networks, measured on one
GPU in the same way. On hands, FrameSeg3D takes 15.5 s per hand, half the time of the whole-hand
render at the same 0.08 mm per pixel (30.6 s), with a third of its allocated GPU memory. The
whole-hand render at 0.25 mm and PTv3 are faster but less accurate (Tab. 1). The GPU memory of
FrameSeg3D stays small on hands and on jaws, 1.3 GB of allocations, because every view has the
same 384 × 384 pixels whatever the size of the scan. On teeth, it is the slowest method (21.9 s per
jaw, about 1.5 s per tooth), because the crops of neighboring teeth overlap and are rendered one at a
time; batching them would make it faster. Most of its parameters belong to the three frame networks
of the ensemble.
C
Implementation details
Hardware and software. We use a server with eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs with 80 GB each, two Intel
Xeon Platinum 8468 CPUs with 128 cores in total, 2 TB of memory, and a 2 TB disk for the data and
caches. Our code uses Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.5.1 with CUDA 12.4, segmentation_models_pytorch,
and Open3D for ray casting; the baselines run in their own environments. Each training run uses
one GPU, and several runs share the server at a time. With other runs on the server, one seed of
the shared segmentation network (24k steps) trains in about 6.4 h on one GPU, and one seed of
the frame network (20k steps) in about 2.7 h on hands and 0.6 h on teeth. Appendix B gives the
inference cost of every method.
Overview image. The image spans 1.2 times the 90th percentile of the training scans’ extent, so
that nearly every scan fits with a margin. We find the local floor by a grayscale opening of the height
image over 12𝐷, much wider than a part, so that the opening removes the parts and keeps what they
rest on; the overview’s height channel is measured from this floor.
Frame network. The heatmap Gaussians have a standard deviation of 𝐷/8. Twice that is 𝐷/4, well
below the distance between the centers of neighboring parts, so their peaks stay apart. The normal
loss uses the pixels within 𝐷/6 of a part’s center (𝑤(q) = 1 in Eq. (4)), where the surface faces most
like the part as a whole. Pixels with an ignore label give no loss, and pixels within 𝐷/2 of one give no
heatmap loss, because an ignored part still looks like a part. A part is a local maximum of ˆ𝐶above 0.3
that lies at least 𝐷/3 from a stronger one; 𝐷/3 is well below the distance between neighboring parts.
The Hungarian algorithm scores each name by the mean of ˆS within 𝐷/6 of a peak. The network
never sees scan tags, such as whether a hand is left or right or a jaw upper or lower; a tag only maps
a predicted name to the dataset’s label, for instance a tooth position, counted from the front of the
jaw, to its tooth number in the upper or lower jaw. We set these constants once and use them for
every kind of part.
Rendering. We divide the height channel by 5 mm and clip the curvature to [−1, 1] to keep the
channels in a similar range as the normal components, which lie in [−1, 1].
Training schedules. The segmentation network trains for 24,000 steps of 16 crops (peak learning
rate 5×10−4), so that each kind of part gets twice the steps of a network trained on one kind (6,000).
We keep the checkpoint with the best mean, over the kinds of parts, of the median crop Dice on
24


up to 300 validation parts each. The frame network trains for 20,000 steps of 16 overview images
(learning rate 5 × 10−4); at 6,000 steps its validation detection score was still rising. We keep its
checkpoint with the best detection F1 on validation, with the right name within 𝐷/3.
Data augmentation. We augment training scans only. Each training crop of the segmentation
network is mirrored with probability 1
2. With probability 1
2, we also move it along its normals by a
smooth field 𝜂, v𝑖←v𝑖+ 𝜂𝑖n𝑖: white noise smoothed over the mesh graph (correlation length about
0.6 mm) with a robust standard deviation drawn from U(0, 0.12 mm). We then recompute normals
and curvature, so the network cannot rely on the noise level of one scanner. The frame network sees
any rotation, scaling by 0.9–1.1 and mirroring. A mirror image renames the parts by a fixed map: a
tooth position changes sides, while a finger keeps its name. Half of its training images come from
one of eight augmented copies of each training scan. Each copy has smooth surface noise as above, a
hole on each part with probability 1
2, and a tilt of up to 15◦about a random axis perpendicular to u.
A hole is a ball of radius up to 0.3𝐷around a random point of the part, cut out and closed with flat
triangles, much like Cutout [10] on a surface. We fixed these settings in advance and did not tune
them. The whole-scan baselines are trained with the same noise and holes (Appendix D).

On hands. All hand baselines train on the same 30 people and select checkpoints on the same 8.
PTv3 [56] segments nail vs. not nail on the hand above the mat, at its 0.5 mm grid (median 671
points per nail). We drop nail pieces under 20 mm2. MeshSegNet [29] sees the hand decimated
to about 10,000 faces by vertex clustering, because quadric decimation stalls on most hands. Its
output is upsampled to every vertex by nearest neighbors. We could not run its graph-cut refinement.
Its input resolution limits it: even perfect labels at that input reach a mean Dice of only 0.877.
ToothGroupNetwork [3] samples 24,000 points (median 98 per nail). For hands, we use full rotation
augmentation, a fixed 70 mm scale, and a label vote per cluster instead of tooth numbers. We
select its DBSCAN parameter on validation and run it three times with a time-capped schedule. The
whole-hand render runs our segmentation network, channels and lifting on the whole hand, with 12
Fibonacci views within 60◦of the mat normal and 0.25, 0.125 or 0.08 mm per pixel. Each image
covers the hand. DGCNN [54] and PTv3 in the frame segment the same crops as FrameSeg3D, trained
on the same perturbed frames. Region growing [8] grows the nail from the frame center over the
surface. All baselines train on the same training scans as FrameSeg3D. DGCNN and PTv3 in the
frame train with our surface noise, like FrameSeg3D. PTv3 and the whole-hand render at 0.25 mm
per pixel see surface noise and holes like those of the frame network (Appendix C), drawn from
16 precomputed augmented versions per hand, because per-step noise was too slow to compute on
whole hands of two million vertices.
On teeth. We run the released checkpoints of ToothGroupNetwork and its baselines on its own test
fold. They match the published table within 0.01 IoU for ToothGroupNetwork (with and without its
boundary-aware sampling), Point Transformer and PointNet. TSegNet is 1.1 below and DGCNN 5.0
below. We retrain TeethSplineSeg [55] from scratch on the training fold with its full pipeline and
post-processing, and select on the validation fold. Its own evaluation on our test fold gives 93.55
T-mIoU and 70.87 B-IoU, close to its paper’s 92.62 and 71.36. We retrain 3DTeethSAM [33] from
SAM2.1 Hiera-L with its two-stage schedule (30 + 70 epochs), seven views and graph-cut smoothing.
Its own evaluation gives 93.44 T-mIoU, against 91.90 in its paper. Its released checkpoint saw 120 of
our 180 test scans in training, so we do not use it. We retrain ToothInstanceNet [53] from scratch with
its released code and full inference pipeline (pose alignment, tooth instances, then FDI numbers).
Its released checkpoints were trained on Teeth3DS scans that may include our test scans, so we do
not use them. Of the two training configurations in its repository, we keep the one with the higher
validation F1. The whole-jaw render predicts 17 classes (background and 16 positions) per pixel,
because neighboring teeth touch. Each tooth keeps its largest piece, as in FrameSeg3D. We found no
public code for Render2Seg [27], TSegLab [42] or IOSSAM [20], so we do not compare with them.

Frame prediction on teeth. A ground-truth tooth counts as named correctly if the frame with its
number lies within 3 mm of its centroid. On the test fold, 99.6% of the teeth are named correctly,
with 20 extra detections in 180 scans. The median center error is 0.52 mm (90th percentile 1.24),
and the median normal error is 5.4◦(90th percentile 12.1◦). On the challenge test set, 97.9% are
named correctly, with 109 extra detections in 600 scans. There, 1.6% of the teeth have no frame
within 3 mm. Where the nearest frame has the wrong number, the number is off by one, that is, it
names the neighboring tooth, in 404 of 431 cases, as is also the case for ToothGroupNetwork.
Teeth by position. On the challenge test set, ToothInstanceNet’s lead comes from the back molars
(Tab. 16). Of its 0.39 points of per-tooth IoU, third molars account for 0.42 and second molars for

the training fold has 0.01 third molars, the validation and test folds 0.02, and the challenge test set
0.25. For second molars, the numbers are 1.25, 1.47, 1.32 and 1.79. Of the 87 teeth where our first
run scores below 0.5 IoU, 84 have no frame, and 41 of these are third molars. The validation fold
does not show this failure: it holds 3 third molars, and every tooth in it gets a frame.
