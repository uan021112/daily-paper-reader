Abstract 

Cooperative mobile manipulation requires robots to coordinate their motions while ensuring whole-body collision avoidance and preserving task-relevant behaviors. We propose CALDS (Coopera-tive Adaptation of Learned Dynamical Systems), a framework that enables mobile manipulators to adapt learned single-agent behaviors for coordinated execution. Building on individual dynam-ical systems (DSs) learned from demonstrations, CALDS enables cooperative adaptation through two key components: Cooperative Policy Selection, which identifies compatible combinations of learned policies, and Temporal Adaptation, which ensures collision-free cooperation while pre-serving the spatial structure and convergence properties of the original behaviors. We evaluate CALDS across three diverse scenarios: assistive dressing, restaurant cleaning and serving, and drawer opening and object placement. Quantitative results demonstrate its effectiveness in improv-ing task success and enabling safe, coordinated simultaneous execution while maintaining fidelity to demonstrated behaviors. 

Keywords: Multi-agent Collaboration, Learned Dynamical Systems, Mobile Manipulation 

1. Introduction 

Mobile manipulators are versatile platforms capable of assisting humans in household tasks (Wu et al., 2023), and performing labor-intensive jobs, such as operating in greenhouses (S´ anchez-Molina et al., 2024) or construction sites (Hartmann et al., 2022). In these domestic and professional environments, efficiency rises when robots collaborate with other agents, anticipating on their ac-tions and intentions. For instance, consider a robot assisting a human in putting on a jacket while another shows how the outfit looks using a mirror, or a restaurant where one robot places a glass on the table-area cleaned by another robot. Such scenarios require robots to proactively adapt their behaviors to other agents, ensuring both task effectiveness and safety. Learning from Demonstration (LfD) has emerged as a powerful framework for transferring hu-man skills to robots, with successful applications in manipulators (Reuss et al., 2023; P´ erez-Dattari et al., 2024), dual-arm systems (Zhao et al., 2023), and mobile manipulators (Fu et al., 2024; Sun-daresan et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2025). However, extending these skills to collaborative behaviors involving multiple mobile manipulators presents several challenges. Firstly, collecting multi-agent demonstrations is inherently complex, making even a small number of demonstrations difficult to obtain. Secondly, mobile manipulators operate in cluttered and dynamic environments, requiring whole-body collision avoidance with other robots and surrounding obstacles. These challenges motivate frameworks that enable collaborative mobile manipulation from small amounts of demon-strations, while accounting for whole-body constraints and anticipating the actions of other agents. Rather than depending on extensive multi-agent demonstrations, recent approaches have ex-plored composing multi-agent behavior from single-agent demonstrations. Singh et al. Furthermore, single-agent demonstrations can be combined with multi-agent cost functions to train coordinated dual-manipulator diffusion policies (Peters et al., 2026). Extending this approach to mobile manipulators, however, requires reliable visual perception for both diffusion-based policy generation and whole-body collision avoidance. Other approaches learn collaborative behaviors from multi-manipulator demonstrations, while focusing on maintaining global consistency during decentralized execution (He et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2026). Learning-free task and motion plan-ning (TAMP) methods, such as those using temporal logic (Saha and Julius, 2017) or long-horizon planning frameworks (Hartmann et al., 2022), also offer solutions for collaborative tasks incorpo-rating whole-body collision avoidance. However, their computational demands can limit real-time applicability. Moreover, collision-free collaboration does not necessarily imply efficient execution: sequential motion or waiting for other robots to pass can cause unnecessary delays, while concurrent execution requires explicit coordination to avoid collisions (Rudy et al., 2022). To address these challenges, we propose CALDS ( Cooperative Adaptation of Learned Dynam-ical Systems ), a framework that enables mobile manipulators to coordinate learned single-agent be-haviors in shared workspaces while ensuring whole-body collision avoidance and task constraints (Fig. 1). Each robot is equipped with a set of learned dynamical systems (DSs), which encode di-verse strategies for accomplishing the assigned task. These learned DSs, such as proposed in (Urain et al., 2022; P´ erez-Dattari et al., 2024; Bakker et al., 2026), can be generated from only a few (e.g. 1–10) demonstrated end-effector pose trajectories in SE (3) , while guaranteeing convergence to the

CALDS comprises two centralized stages: Cooperative Policy Selection , which iden-tifies compatible combinations of single-agent policies, and Temporal Adaptation , which adjusts their temporal profiles to achieve collision-free collaboration while preserving the spatial structure, interpretability, and convergence of the original learned DSs. 2. Preliminaries on Learned Dynamical Systems 

DSs provide an effective framework for encoding motion behaviors either from human demonstra-tions (Billard et al., 2022) or through manual design by combining multiple sub-behaviors (Ratliff et al., 2018). A motion is represented as an autonomous continuous-time DS of the form, 

˙x(t) = f (x(t)) , (1) where x(t) denotes the system state and x∗ is the equilibrium point corresponding to the task goal, such that f (x∗) = 0, with 0 denoting the zero vector of appropriate dimension. The learned behav-ior is typically defined in the end-effector state space, which enables adaptation to changes in goal pose and the robot’s embodiment. In this work, we represent the end-effector pose as x = [ p, ξ],where p denotes the Cartesian position and ξ denotes the orientation represented as a quaternion. The output of the DS, ˙x, therefore corresponds to the end-effector twist. Stability of learned DSs can be enforced in several ways. In (Urain et al., 2022), stability is achieved through a diffeomorphic mapping between a simple stable system and task-space dynam-ics, extending the formulation to Lie groups. Other approaches enforce stability through the training objective of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) (P´ erez-Dattari et al., 2024), or guarantee convergence toward a geometric curve learned from demonstrations (Bakker et al., 2026). More recently, (P´ erez-Dattari et al., 2026) incorporated Lyapunov stability constraints into flow-matching-based DSs, in-cluding Lie group formulations.

4. Cooperate Adaption for Learned Dynamical Systems 

In this section, we introduce CALDS (Fig. 1). First, we learn distinct DSs from a small set of trajec-tory demonstrations to obtain single-agent policies. To enable cooperative multi-agent adaptation, CALDS comprises two layers: Cooperative Policy Selection (Sec. 4.2) and Temporal Adaptation (Sec. 4.3). Both layers rely on generating robot trajectories along a horizon, as described in Sec. 4.1.

Notes on stability. In continuous time, temporal scaling with α(t) > 0 reparameterizes time with-out changing the spatial trajectories of the learned DSs. Consequently, their asymptotic convergence properties are preserved. However, the stability of the complete robot system in (10b) also depends on the low-level controller, robot kinematics, and constraints, which may prevent reaching the goal. In discrete time, large scaling factors may cause numerical errors or overshooting, for which stabi-lizing bounds can be derived for certain DSs (Lee et al., 2025). The bounds in (10e) and (10f), on the desired end-effector velocity and α(k) respectively, help mitigate these effects and support stable execution. By restricting adaptations to temporal scaling and explicitly enforcing goal-convergence and safety criteria during optimization, our approach aims to preserve the desirable properties of the learned behaviors while enabling safe collaborative execution. 

5. Experimental Results 

We evaluate the proposed framework in three cooperative mobile manipulation scenarios, describing the experimental setup and quantitatively analyzing the results. The supplementary material com-prises an Appendix with additional details, and accompanying video (https://youtu.be/9AiIJNsJPlQ). 

5.1. Experimental Setup Simulation Setup. Experiments are conducted in simulation using two mobile manipulators, each consisting of a Clearpath Dingo omnidirectional base and a Kinova Gen3 Lite arm, resulting in a 9-DoF system. The FK and LLC are parallelized using JAX (Blondel et al., 2022b) and FRAX (Mor-ton and Pavone, 2026), a tailored version of MuJoCo XLA (MJX) (Todorov et al., 2012) for robot kinematics. Each robot is approximated by 8 capsules, slightly over-approximating its geometry while enabling efficient collision-avoidance computations. 

Scenarios. We consider three cooperative scenarios (Fig. 2): (1) Restaurant : Robot 1 (R1) cleans a table while Robot 2 (R2) places a cup on the cleaned portion, (2) Dressing : R1 dresses a human arm while R2 uses a mirror to show different viewpoints, and (3) Drawer : R1 opens a drawer while R2 places an item inside. To focus on cooperative motion adaptation, we simplify the simulated tasks: Dressing involves moving a cylinder over the human arm, while the drawer is attached to R1 by a flexible string, avoiding rigid grasp interactions. In Restaurant, R2 may place the cup only once R1 has cleaned the intended placement area. For mirror-showing, table-cleaning, and drawer-opening, the corresponding robot must first approach the task’s starting region within a margin of 

dstart , preventing the robot from starting the task midway. 

Learned DSs and Comparison. We consider two methods for learning the single-agent DSs used by CALDS: Curve-induced Dynamical systems on Smooth Manifolds (CDSM) (Bakker et al., 2026) and Policy via neUral Metric leArning (PUMA) (P´ erez-Dattari et al., 2024). CDSM fits a curve on the manifold to demonstrated trajectories and defines a DS that attracts towards the curve while progressing along its direction. PUMA instead learns a DS using a DNN, enforcing conver-gence through its loss function and connecting latent-space dynamics to task-space dynamics. Both methods support pose representations on SE (3) , guarantee convergence to the goal under their stated assumptinos, and can be learned from few demonstrations (1–10). We compare against ONCol-DMP (Singh et al., 2025), using its open-source implementation. We retain its collaborative adaptation framework but replace its skill library and policy selection

5.2. Experimental Analysis 

When analyzing Fig. 3, we observe that the proposed CALDS-CDSM and CALDS-PUMA enable cooperative adaptation of learned DSs in all three scenarios, considerably improving the success rate (91-100%) considerably, compared to Nominal CDSM and Nominal PUMA, while achieving small path deviations. Further improvements could be achieved through longer planning horizons, more parallel runs with diverse initial guesses, increased optimization time, and allowing multiple fallbacks to lower-ranked policy combinations. However, these improvements would come at the expense of real-time adaptability. An example of the resulting trajectories is shown in Fig. 1 and is further illustrated in the accompanying video. Compared to CBF-CDSM and CBF-PUMA, respectively, the proposed CALDS-CDSM and CALDS-PUMA achieve smaller path deviations, indicating that temporal adaptation remains closer to the path of the nominal learned DSs. Moreover, the success rates of the CBF-based approaches drop drastically in the Restaurant scenario, as their local collision-avoidance constraints can lead to conflicting requirements when avoiding both the static obstacle and the other robot, and task-ordering constraints are not enforced. 6. Conclusion and Future Work 

This work introduced CALDS, a framework for cooperative adaptation of learned DSs for mobile manipulators, integrating cooperative policy selection and temporal adaptation. We demonstrated its applicability to collaborative tasks involving two mobile manipulators across three distinct sce-narios. The resulting trajectories preserve the behavior encoded by the learned DSs, while satisfying whole-body collision avoidance and task requirements. Furthermore, exploiting the stability proper-ties of the learned DSs enables learning from few demonstrations and supports pose representations on SE (3) . Future work will investigate integrating the framework into a single neural network conditioned on discrete markers representing different behavioral modes (Sun et al., 2021), as well as extending it to human-robot collaboration, where increased uncertainty and behavioral diversity introduce additional challenges.
