## Abstract 

Whole-body motion tracking has enabled humanoid danc-ing, athletic motions, and teleoperation, providing a foun-dation for capability demonstrations and manipulation data collection. However, existing tracking policies lack aware-ness of nearby humans and proactive avoidance capabili-ties, while motion substitution and local constraint-based corrections may compromise reference motion integrity and tracking continuity. We propose ALERT, a general lightweight pipeline for extending motion tracking to shared human–robot environments. Through lightweight adapta-tion, it integrates human perception and proactive avoid-ance into pretrained tracking policies, balancing avoid-ance demands with reference motion tracking while pre-serving existing motion capabilities. Simulation and real-world experiments across tracking backbones demonstrate reliable collision-free execution through localized human avoidance, preserving whole-body coordination and en-abling rapid recovery of reference tracking, while retaining the ability of pretrained policies to track unseen motions. 

## 1. Introduction 

Recent advances in reinforcement learning for whole-body motion tracking have substantially improved humanoid mo-tion capabilities, enabling robots to reproduce complex hu-man motions such as dance and athletic movements on real hardware and perform real-time whole-body teleopera-tion [1–3]. These capabilities support robot performances, manipulation data collection, and human skill transfer. However, existing motion-tracking policies generate con-trol commands primarily from proprioceptive states and reference motions, without perceiving or actively avoiding

Deploying these capa-bilities in shared human–robot settings therefore requires human-aware motion tracking that accommodates nearby people throughout whole-body motion execution. Mainstream pretrained trackers provide strong tracking capabilities across diverse motions through learned motion representations and coordinated whole-body control, which should be fully utilized when learning human avoidance. Meanwhile, large-scale joint retraining for motion tracking and human avoidance requires substantial data and compu-tational resources. This motivates a lightweight adaptation pipeline that extends pretrained trackers with human avoid-ance rather than relearning whole-body control. The challenge is to add human avoidance while preserv-ing pretrained motion-tracking capabilities. Corrections should remain small and brief, concentrate on body parts at risk, and allow reference tracking to resume as the risk sub-1arXiv:2611.22258v1 [cs.RO] 17 Nov 2026 sides. Each correction changes subsequent tracking states, coupling avoidance with whole-body coordination and sup-port contacts. CSF [4] uses visual scenes and language rules to replace unsafe references with safe continuations, sacri-ficing original motion content and continuity. Constrained-Mimic [5] uses control barrier functions and single-step constrained optimization to modify reference motions and low-level control outputs. However, corrections satisfying current geometric constraints can disrupt balance and sub-sequent tracking when avoidance requires longer-horizon coordination or contact transitions. These limitations call for learning avoidance directly within the control policy to coordinate avoidance and tracking over longer horizons. To this end, we propose ALERT (Adaptive Low-Rank Extension Pipeline for Reference Trackers), a gen-eral lightweight training pipeline that extends pretrained motion trackers with human avoidance while preserving their motion-tracking capabilities. We freeze the pre-trained backbone and introduce potential-conditioned low-rank branches into its action network to learn local avoid-ance corrections using existing whole-body coordination. Each tracker retains its native motion-conditioning mech-anism, while a shared potential-field representation and velocity-flow training objective make the pipeline applica-ble across mainstream tracking frameworks. We use omni-directional LiDAR to detect and track nearby humans and estimate their occupied space with convex envelopes that adapt to body shape and pose. Superposing repulsive poten-tials from these envelopes provides a fixed-size representa-tion of the surrounding avoidance demands across varying human numbers and arrangements. To coordinate avoid-ance with reference tracking, we combine potential-guided avoidance velocities with the reference motion flow and train the adaptation branches to track the resulting target flow. This guidance preserves ongoing motion trends, lim-its interference with unaffected limbs, and recovers the ref-erence flow as clearance increases. As illustrated in Fig. 1, ALERT adjusts an arm or leg to avoid nearby people while preserving the overall reference pose on real hardware. Using only 500 motion sequences that intrude into the surrounding space and a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, we extend multiple mainstream tracking frameworks with human avoidance. The adapted policies largely retain their original tracking accuracy without nearby humans and con-centrate avoidance deviations on limbs at risk when people approach. On real hardware, the robot avoids nearby people during dance and kicking, then rapidly resumes reference tracking. During teleoperation, affected limbs adjust au-tonomously while the rest of the body follows the operator. Compared with CSF and ConstrainedMimic, ALERT com-pletes more motions without collisions or falls and achieves lower tracking errors throughout avoidance and recovery. Our contributions are fourfold: • We develop a general lightweight training pipeline that adds human avoidance to pretrained trackers while pre-serving their motion-tracking capabilities. • We combine LiDAR-based adaptive human envelopes with potential superposition to represent varying human numbers and arrangements using fixed-size inputs. • We formulate potential-guided target velocity flows and a tracking reward that guide local avoidance while main-taining the continuity of reference motion. • We validate sim-to-real transfer through human avoid-ance and motion preservation during real-world dance, kicking, and whole-body teleoperation. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Learning-Based Whole-Body Control 

Learning-based whole-body control enables humanoids to reproduce diverse human motions. ExBody and Ex-Body2 [6, 7] learn expressive control from human motion data, while WoCoCo [8] learns control with sequential con-tacts. DreamControl [9] uses guided diffusion for scene interaction. Whole-body controllers and teleoperation sys-tems [2, 10–12] connect human motion inputs to learned policies for real-time imitation and manipulation data col-lection. SONIC [1] and BFM-Zero [13] scale motion track-ing and reusable behavior representations across diverse skills. However, these controllers primarily follow refer-ence motions and lack proactive avoidance of nearby people during execution. ALERT extends these pretrained track-ers through a lightweight training pipeline, enabling human avoidance at low adaptation cost. 

2.2. Safe Collision Avoidance for Humanoid Robots 

Collision avoidance for humanoids spans local planning and whole-body control. Prediction-aware potential fields [14] and vision-based motion planning [15, 16] anticipate mov-ing obstacles in human–robot collaboration. Control bar-rier functions impose whole-body safety constraints [17, 18], while recent methods explore constrained tracking, collision-aware policy learning, and generative safety fil-tering [4, 5, 19, 20]. Potential fields also guide humanoid traversal through cluttered scenes [21]. However, geometric clearance alone does not specify how to adjust body motion while continuing reference tracking. ALERT superposes potentials from adaptive human envelopes to form a general representation across varying human counts, shapes, and arrangements. The resulting velocity-flow tracking reward provides dense, directional guidance before contact, coordi-nating local avoidance with the ongoing reference motion. 

2.3. Adaptation of Pretrained Control Policies 

Policy adaptation extends learned skills to new tasks and dynamics. Residual reinforcement learning [22] adds cor-2rections to base controllers, while Robot Trains Robot [23] adapts dynamics representations on hardware. Humanoid applications include loco-manipulation [24], fast task adap-tation [25], long-horizon tracking [26], and sim-to-real alignment [27]. AdaMimic [28] adapts tracking speed and low-level actions, while ADP [29] uses low-rank up-dates to adapt diffusion policies. Rapid motor adapta-tion [30], simulation-guided fine-tuning [31], and meta-reinforcement learning [32] address changing deployment conditions. ALERT introduces potential-conditioned low-rank branches into the frozen action network, learning local avoidance corrections while preserving pretrained tracking skills and whole-body coordination. 

## 3. Method 

As shown in Figure 2, ALERT extends a pretrained whole-body motion-tracking policy with conditional low-rank adaptation for human avoidance (Sec. 3.1). We esti-mate adaptive human envelopes from real-time point clouds (Sec. 3.2) and use them to construct repulsive potential fields. The resulting avoidance flow is added to the refer-ence motion flow to form a target velocity flow (Sec. 3.3). Guided by this target flow, the policy learns local motion ad-justments conditioned on potential-field features (Sec. 3.4).

3.2. Omnidirectional Adaptive Human Envelopes 

Avoiding nearby humans requires continuous and reliable perception of human positions and occupied space. How-ever, LiDAR measurement noise and human self-occlusion introduce local jitter and missing observations, making it difficult to reliably delineate a safe envelope for limb mo-tion from a single frame. To address this issue, we com-bine temporal human tracking with parametric convex-body modeling to estimate the human center trajectory and outer envelope, respectively. For human tracking, we first detect humans and extract a reference point cloud using Person-MinkUNet [35]. A par-ticle filter [36] then registers this reference cloud to the lat-est observations around the target to estimate its current po-sition, while an α–β position–velocity filter [37] produces a smooth, continuous center trajectory. ## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate our method in simulation and on real hardware, focusing on four questions: • Q1: Can our method improve human avoidance across different motion-tracking backbones? • Q2: Can avoidance skills learned in simulation transfer to a real robot? • Q3: Can our method outperform the baselines in both human avoidance and motion tracking? • Q4: How do the key design choices affect avoidance and motion preservation? 

Experimental Setup. We fine-tune SONIC [1], TWIST2 [2], and BFM-Zero [13] for human avoid-ance, starting from their officially released pretrained weights. Fine-tuning is performed in Isaac Sim [41] and Isaac Gym [42] on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU in each case. We select 500 representative motion sequences that intrude into the surrounding space from the BONES-SEED dataset for fine-tuning. During training, humans are represented by fixed cylindrical and prismatic templates. The LoRA adaptation rank is set to 16 . The potential-field input OU uses 21 body sampling points: 1 on the pelvis and 10 each on the arms and legs. For real-world experiments, we deploy the fine-tuned SONIC policy on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot and evaluate avoidance and motion tracking as nearby humans move and change poses. 

Baselines. We compare three baselines. 

SONIC [1] tracks reference motions without human per-ception or avoidance. 

CSF [4] rejects unsafe actions when risk is detected and replaces the remaining reference with safe motions. 

ConstrainedMimic [5] uses kinematic and dynamic safety filters based on control barrier functions to modify the refer-ence motion and low-level control commands, respectively.

Potential-Field Representation. We replace OU with distances and directions to human centers (w/o PF in Ta-ble 3). Potential-field inputs improve avoidance and track-ing, with M nearer 1, indicating better coordination of avoidance magnitude and direction with fewer deviations. Figure 8 shows local avoidance among multiple humans while preserving overall motion. Each sample encodes combined potential energy, steepest-descent direction, and normal and tangential flow components. By superposing human potentials, we represent varying human counts and arrangements with fixed-size inputs, enabling multi-human generalization without changing the network size. 

Flow Reward. Removing the velocity-flow tracking re-ward (w/o Flow in Table 3) lowers success and increases tracking errors in both body groups. The higher M indi-cates a larger normalized avoidance margin. The target flow guides avoidance direction and magnitude while preserving reference-motion components tangent to equipotential sur-faces. This guidance coordinates avoidance with tracking while limiting excessive corrections and interference with other limbs. Without the directional guidance provided by this reward, training also converges more slowly. 85. Conclusion 

We introduce ALERT, a pipeline that extends pretrained hu-manoid motion trackers with human avoidance. LiDAR-based human envelopes define repulsive potentials, whose velocity flows guide conditional low-rank adaptation. Ex-periments across three backbones and on Unitree G1 demonstrate local avoidance, rapid tracking recovery, and preserved tracking of unseen motions. ALERT outperforms CM and CSF in success rate and tracking accuracy. However, convex envelopes cannot represent gaps be-tween human limbs, potentially making close-range avoid-ance conservative. Future work will incorporate articulated human geometry for finer local avoidance.
