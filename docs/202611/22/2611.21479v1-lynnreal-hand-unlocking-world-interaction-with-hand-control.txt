LynnReal-Hand: Unlocking World Interaction with Hand Control
LynnReal AI∗
Interactive video world models enable exploration through text and camera controls, yet direct object
interaction requires finer control. Hands provide a natural interface, but translating continuous hand
movements into coherent visual responses remains challenging because of complex hand-object dynamics
and demanding real-time computation. To address these challenges, we introduce LynnReal-Hand, a
hand-controlled video world model that unifies precise interaction modeling with efficient streaming
generation. We curate 100K interaction clips spanning diverse real and synthetic environments and
develop hand-aware supervision to improve fine-grained hand motions and object interactions. Building
on this foundation, we learn a causal flow map for few-step generation and introduce region-aware
score distillation that combines complementary video and image priors to refine interaction dynamics
and visual appearance. To further reduce inference latency, we propose Ultra-Fast-VAE, a compact
video decoder that preserves high reconstruction fidelity with substantially lower computational cost.
Extensive evaluations on HandBench demonstrate superior hand-following accuracy and visual quality
compared with existing approaches. Our full-sequence model achieves an FVD of 181.67 and a MANO
Dice score of 93.02, while the three-step causal model maintains a MANO Dice score of 92.49. For
real-time interaction, 540p streaming on a single H200 reaches 18.03 FPS with 741 ms latency and an
optimized peak GPU memory footprint of only 34.89 GB. We will release the datasets, model weights,
and the full training and inference code to support research on hand-controlled video world models.

Introduction
Interactive video world models are transforming video generation from passive content synthesis into envi-
ronments that respond to user actions. Early systems such as Genie [1] demonstrated action-controllable
generation, while recent advances in Matrix-Game 3.0 [2], PAN [3], and open world models [4–7] have improved
interactivity, temporal consistency, and accessibility. Yet most interaction paradigms remain centered on
navigation, camera motion, or high-level instructions. These controls determine where users look or move, but
offer limited ability to manipulate the objects they encounter. Enabling such manipulation requires a world
model to respond not only to changes in viewpoint, but also to the fine-grained actions through which users
interact with their surroundings.
Hands offer a direct and expressive interface for this purpose: reaching, grasping, releasing, and coordinated
bimanual movements communicate intentions that are difficult to specify through text or navigation commands.
Recent efforts, including HandWorld [8], Hand2World [9], Generated Reality [10], Dexterous World Models [11],
and JointControlVideo [12], have begun to connect articulated hand motion with visual generation. However,
reliable hand-controlled interaction presents challenges that are distinct from conventional video synthesis.
First, hands and their contact regions occupy only a small portion of each frame, so global generative objectives
can underemphasize precisely the details that determine control fidelity. Second, accelerating a high-quality
video model into a few-step causal generator can amplify local motion errors: the student must preserve
articulated interactions while conditioning on its own imperfect history. Finally, even when denoising is
accelerated, decoding high-resolution video latents can remain a major obstacle to responsive interaction.
These challenges call for a training and inference pipeline that preserves interaction fidelity throughout the
transition from full-sequence learning to streaming generation.
We introduce LynnReal-Hand, an open-source hand-controlled video world model developed to address
these challenges end to end. At its core is Interaction-Preserving Training (IPT), a supervision strategy
that explicitly prioritizes fine-grained interaction fidelity at two critical stages: full-sequence pretraining
and causal distribution matching.
We first build a dataset of 100K interaction clips with aligned
hand trajectories, camera poses, and textual descriptions across diverse real and synthetic scenes. During
full-sequence learning, we go beyond global flow matching with hand-aware multilevel supervision: a hand-
region latent objective penalizes local reconstruction errors directly, while decoded LPIPS and DINOv2
constraints reinforce perceptual structure and hand appearance. This targeted supervision counteracts the
weak contribution of small interaction regions to the global training signal and establishes a stronger teacher
for subsequent acceleration.
Building on this pretrained model, we learn a causal flow map [13] that generates each video chunk in
only three denoising transitions. Teacher-forced training with clean history provides an efficient initialization,
but leaves a mismatch with inference, where each new chunk depends on previously generated content. We
therefore refine the causal student on self-generated rollouts using distribution matching [14, 15]. However,
global score matching alone does not distinguish the visual evidence needed for interaction dynamics from the
evidence needed for scene appearance. To allocate these complementary signals, we formulate region-aware
dual-score distillation: a hand-conditioned video teacher and critic guide the entire generated sequence, with
higher-noise evaluations emphasizing interaction-related corrections, while a complementary image prior
supplies appearance guidance only outside hand regions. This spatial separation is essential because the
image prior lacks hand conditions and should not override the specified motion. In this way, IPT addresses
complementary failure modes of the two training stages: insufficient hand-local learning during pretraining
and degraded interaction fidelity during causal acceleration. For open-ended streaming, a bounded attention
window and local temporal-position reset keep the student’s history and positional range finite.
Efficient generation also requires efficient reconstruction. We therefore introduce Ultra-Fast-VAE,
a compact Transformer decoder that preserves the generator’s native latent interface while compressing
attention and feed-forward branches and recovering reconstruction quality through representation and temporal
distillation. We evaluate the resulting system with HandBench, covering ten hand-interaction sources,
including two held-out sources for out-of-distribution testing. On the main benchmark, LynnReal-Hand-
Base achieves an FVD of 181.67 and a MANO Dice score of 93.02; the evaluated LynnReal-Hand-Fast
model, trained with video-score distillation but without the image-guidance branch, retains 92.49 MANO
Dice with three denoising steps. In a separate optimized 540p streaming evaluation on one H200, Fast reaches
18.03 FPS.

Related Work
2.1
Interactive Video World Models
Video world models use learned visual prediction to make an environment respond to user inputs. Genie [1],
Matrix-Game 3.0 [2], and PAN [3] develop action-based interaction and long-horizon simulation. WorldPlay [4]
and RELIC [5] study geometric consistency and memory, while LingBot-World [6] and SolarWM [7] advance
open training and deployment. BiWM [16] provides an open training framework for bidirectional autoregressive
world models. These advances provide a foundation for interactive synthesis. Our focus is the additional
control and supervision needed when a user specifies articulated hand motion within the generated scene.
2.2
Hand-Controlled Video Generation
Hand-conditioned generation represents actions using meshes, joints, or other spatial controls. HandWorld [8]
jointly models video and hand actions; Hand2World [9] studies hand-driven interaction; and Generated
Reality [10] combines hand and camera conditioning. Dexterous World Models [11] grounds interaction in
rendered static scenes and hand trajectories, while DexAC [17], P3-WAM [18], and JointControlVideo [12]
study complementary structured action representations. DreamDojo [19] and EgoWM [20] further connect
action-conditioned prediction with embodied learning. We build on this problem setting and study its full
training-to-delivery path. In particular, our method emphasizes local hand supervision, separates temporal
and appearance score gradients during causal distillation, and reduces the codec cost. Section 5 compares the
available native model paths under their recorded conditions.
2.3
Efficient Generation and Open Recipes
Few-step generation reduces the repeated computation of diffusion sampling. AnyFlow [13] learns finite flow
transitions; DMD [14] estimates distribution-matching updates from real and fake scores; and Self-Forcing [15]
trains autoregressive generators on their own histories. PackForcing [21] bounds long-video history through
partitioned KV caches, compression, and context selection. DuoMatching [22] combines video-joint and
image-marginal supervision. For hand control, these objectives must preserve a small, moving interaction
region as well as global visual appearance. Our score separation assigns these responsibilities explicitly. We also
address the complementary rendering cost: Ultra-Fast-VAE retains the native generator latent interface and
reconstructs each completed chunk with narrower attention and feed-forward branches, connecting denoising
acceleration to a measured reconstruction–latency trade-off.

3.5
Ultra-Fast-VAE: Efficient Video Decoding
Although the preceding stages substantially reduce the number of denoising steps, decoding the generated
latents into video frames remains a significant bottleneck for real-time interaction. High-fidelity Transformer-
based VAE decoders incur substantial computational overhead, even with recent streaming optimizations [32,
33]. Lightweight CNN alternatives offer lower latency but often sacrifice fine-grained reconstruction quality [34],
particularly for articulated hands and manipulated objects. To address this trade-off, we propose Ultra-Fast-

targeted reconstruction distillation.

3.6
Data Engine Construction
We construct a unified data pipeline for hand- and camera-conditioned egocentric video generation. The
pipeline converts heterogeneous videos into interaction clips with temporally aligned 3D hand parameters,
hand renderings, camera trajectories, and textual descriptions. It combines visual quality filtering, motion
annotation, interaction verification, and diversity-aware sampling to obtain a training corpus of 100K clips.
Video collection and preprocessing.
We collect videos from public egocentric datasets and online sources,
supplemented with first-person interaction videos synthesized by MiniMax-H3 [26]. Source videos must have
a resolution of at least 720p, a frame rate of at least 24 fps, and more than 200 frames; only egocentric
views are retained from multiview recordings. We use OmniShotCut [37] to identify shot boundaries and
exclude transition intervals. Within each continuous shot, we extract 125- or 240-frame clips at 24 fps. To
provide a sharp conditioning image, we select the starting frame with the highest grayscale Laplacian variance
among up to ten valid frames following each candidate cut position. Clips are encoded as H.264 videos with a
768-pixel short side while preserving aspect ratio. We additionally enhance a subset of videos using SparkVSR
to improve visual quality. We reject poorly exposed clips using brightness statistics, then retain the top 80%
8


ranked by the overall COVER quality score [38]. PaddleOCR detects persistent subtitles in the lower third of
the image. Subtitled landscape clips are discarded; portrait clips are consistently cropped only when subtitle
removal requires trimming at most 25% of the image height. All cropping precedes motion annotation.
Hand annotation and detection completion.
We apply the WiLoR hand detector [39] frame by frame, remove
duplicate and temporally inconsistent detections, and discard clips with hands detected in fewer than 50% of
frames. For retained clips, short gaps bounded by valid same-side detections are filled by interpolating box
centers and sizes when the preceding hand is away from the image boundary. Remaining gaps are addressed
with SAM 3 [40], prompted with “hand” within an enlarged region around the preceding same-side box. We
convert the largest predicted instance into a box, preserve the track’s handedness, and limit consecutive
SAM-based completions. After further cleanup and smoothing, clips with hand coverage below 90% are
rejected. WiLoR then estimates MANO [41] pose, shape, global orientation, and camera-space translation
from the completed boxes. We temporally smooth parameters within valid intervals and render the resulting
meshes on a black background, matching the video clip’s frame count, resolution, and frame rate.
Camera annotation.
We estimate camera intrinsics and per-frame poses with ViPE [42], using GeoCalib
for intrinsic initialization and MoGe-2 metric depth priors to constrain scale during geometric optimization.
To reduce interference from moving hands, we extract binary silhouettes from the hand renderings, apply
morphological closing and dilation, and exclude these regions from the geometric constraints. Reusing hand
annotations replaces ViPE’s generic foreground detection and segmentation stages. We reject trajectories
with missing or invalid poses, inconsistent frame indexing, translation jumps, abrupt rotations, or repeated
large direction reversals. Translation steps exceeding five times the local mean, adjacent rotations above 60◦,
and relative rotations above 100◦within approximately 1.2 seconds trigger rejection.
Semantic annotation and interaction verification.
We use Qwen3.8-Flash-Next [43], deployed with vLLM,
to assign background and activity labels and generate interaction descriptions from chronologically ordered
frames sampled at 1 Hz, including the first frame. Descriptions establish the initial scene and summarize
visible actions and state changes without inventing unobserved events. Camera motion and viewpoint changes
are excluded from the text, leaving them to the camera condition. In a separate verification pass, the model
examines frames sampled every 25 frames and checks three criteria: an egocentric viewpoint, only the camera
wearer’s hands with no other people visible, and purposeful hand–object interaction. We retain only clips that
explicitly pass all three checks. Valid interactions include grasping, placing, assembly, tool use, and sustained
manipulation; hovering, empty gestures, and static display are excluded.
Training-set selection.
Finally, we sample without replacement using weights proportional to the product of
inverse background-label and activity-label frequencies. This increases the selection probability of less frequent
environments and actions. Each selected sample contains a video clip, aligned hand parameters and renderings,
camera intrinsics and poses, and an interaction caption, providing visual targets and complementary motion
and language conditions for generation training.
4
HandBench: Evaluating Hand-Controlled Interaction
4.1
Benchmark Construction
Evaluating hand-controlled video generation requires assessing not only visual realism but also whether the
generated interactions faithfully follow the specified hand motions. Existing video quality metrics alone cannot
adequately capture such fine-grained control accuracy, particularly for complex hand-object interactions.
To enable a more comprehensive evaluation, we construct HandBench, a benchmark that jointly assesses
hand-motion fidelity, visual quality, and scene consistency across diverse interaction scenarios.
HandBench comprises 200 video sequences collected from ten sources, with 20 sequences each from
EgoDex [44], H2O [45], HOI-4D [46], OpenAoE [47], TACO [48], ARCTIC [49], CaptainCook4D [50],
Ego4D [51], EPIC-KITCHENS-100 [52], and synthetic scenes generated by H3 [26]. These sequences encompass
diverse interaction patterns, including fine-grained object manipulation, coordinated bimanual actions, tool
use, and interactions with articulated or deformable objects. For each sequence, we preserve the original video
9


interval, spatial crop, and temporally aligned hand and camera conditions to ensure consistent reference-based
evaluation. We use 160 sequences from eight sources for the main benchmark. H2O and TACO are excluded
from world-model training and reserved for out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation, with 20 sequences from
each source.
4.2
Evaluation Metrics and Protocol
A reliable evaluation of hand-controlled generation must distinguish accurate motion following from visual
plausibility, as realistic-looking videos may still deviate substantially from the prescribed hand trajectories.
We therefore adopt projected MANO [41] mesh-silhouette Dice as our primary control metric, measuring the
spatial agreement between reference hand geometry and hands independently reconstructed from generated
frames. This metric evaluates whether the generated hand configurations follow the intended movements
while accounting for their projected positions and shapes.
To complement control accuracy, we evaluate reconstruction fidelity using PSNR, SSIM [53], and LPIPS [24],
and assess video distribution quality using FVD [54]. We further measure semantic alignment and temporal
consistency through weighted CLIP [55] similarity and VBench [56] subject and background consistency
metrics. Camera-motion adherence is evaluated using background motion agreement, allowing us to distinguish
viewpoint following from hand articulation. Together, these metrics provide complementary assessments of
interaction fidelity and visual coherence without reducing them to a single aggregate score. For fair comparison,
we preserve each method’s native temporal sampling and align generated frames with their corresponding
reference frames. All methods are evaluated on common valid support, with scalar metrics aggregated using
equal weights across data sources and FVD computed from pooled video features. Detailed metric definitions
and evaluation procedures are provided in Appendix A.

Limitations and Future Work
While LynnReal-Hand demonstrates promising performance in hand-controlled video generation, several
directions remain open for further exploration. First, our current causal model operates with a limited temporal
context and does not explicitly model long-term memory, which may constrain interaction consistency over
extended horizons. Incorporating persistent memory mechanisms could enable more coherent and continuous
interactions in long-running virtual environments. Second, we observe that the causal flow-map model learned
in Stage II already exhibits promising generation quality within the training sequence lengths, even before
subsequent score distillation. This suggests an interesting direction toward directly learning high-quality few-
step generators through flow-map objectives, potentially eliminating the need for computationally expensive
DMD training. We leave the exploration of these directions to future work.
7
Conclusion
We introduce LynnReal-Hand for continuous visual interaction through natural hand motion. Hand-aware
supervision, few-step causal generation, and Ultra-Fast-VAE combine accurate control, high visual fidelity,
and efficient decoding. Experiments across diverse interaction scenarios support the effectiveness of this
framework.

B
Training and Implementation Details
B.1
Stage-I Implementation
The full 33B transformer is optimized in BF16 with frozen condition encoders and FlashAttention-3 [60].
Qwen encodes only the first image and caption; the native H3 VAE encodes the hand video. Native hand
and target planes contain 24 × 42 tokens with 1 × 2 × 2 patches. Camera conditions use fixed intrinsics and
first-relative extrinsics. H3’s data-directed prediction has the opposite sign to the noise-directed velocity in
the main text, with t = 1 −σ.
All target planes are noised and supervised. Anchor and hand tensors use 0.999X + 0.001ϵ with time label
0.999. Hand perceptual supervision samples a 16 × 16 latent crop, decodes five predicted latents with two
detached context latents, and evaluates LPIPS and DINOv2 on the corresponding 256 × 256 hand region.
The ROI margin is 0.2.
B.2
Stage-II Implementation
Stage II trains rank-384 LoRA [30] and interval-conditioning parameters (2.08B trainable parameters). FP32
AdamW uses (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.95), cosine decay, and EMA 0.999; CFG is disabled.

B.3
Stage-III Score Distillation
The image bridge maps five 24-channel H3 planes through a width-256 projection, eight spatial residual
blocks, and 2× pixel-shuffle upsampling. Physical-offset embeddings select the corresponding frame. The
bridge is frozen after fitting, while its input derivative remains active. Four temporal locations supply image
supervision. Qwen-Image [31] uses CFG4 and native norm rescaling without hand or camera conditions; a
Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B [61] critic fits one-frame image latents. After the bridge VJP, image gradients are zeroed
inside the hand ROI.
The dual-score configuration uses video CFG3, shift-3 student rollout, and shift-12 video-score noise. The
negative branch retains image and camera conditions while omitting hand and text conditions. Critics receive
five optimizer updates per generator update after 10 warmup updates; the image weight ramps from 0.001 to
0.1 during warmup. Student EMA is 0.999. Earlier generated histories are detached when replaying trainable
rollout exits.
The evaluated Fast model uses video-score training with uniform noise, paired hand supervision, and a
non-hand adversarial term; its image-score branch and image critic are disabled.

C.1
Evaluated Model Paths
LynnReal-Hand-Base uses joint CFG3 and the native H3 VAE at 1344 × 768. Its 30-point shift-3 schedule
performs 29 Euler updates with two evaluations per update. Fast uses three shift-3 flow-map transitions,
W2 history, native H3 anchor encoding, Tiny-H3 [34] hand encoding, and FP16 Ultra-Fast-VAE for quality
evaluation. The same Fast model supplies the pairwise preference results.
Streaming Hand2World [9] and JointControlVideo [12] use released models. Bidirectional Hand2World
adapts rank-256 attention LoRA and its camera adapter on the adaptation corpus (84,305 examples; 32,000
sample visits), retaining 50 native sampling steps, CFG1, and the full Wan VAE without TeaCache [62].

Generated Reality [10] adapts rank-32 LoRA and a motion encoder in the official 5B model on 1,146 ARCTIC
clips from subjects s01–s09, reserving s10 for reproduction validation. All methods retain native source-frame
alignment without frame repetition or playback changes.
Schedule-specific AdaLN storage.
For both LynnReal-Hand-Base and LynnReal-Hand-Fast, per-block AdaLN
modulation depends only on the prescribed time conditions. A fixed sampling schedule therefore needs only
the modulation values at its visited times and clean-conditioning boundaries; Fast uses the corresponding
start/end-time pairs. The inference export stores these values directly and omits the learned block projection
matrices, avoiding their loading and device residency. The default Base and three-step Fast tables occupy
535.3 and 55.4 MiB, respectively. This storage specialization leaves the sampling rule unchanged; total memory
also includes activations, codecs, KV caches, and graph buffers.

C.3
Optimized Streaming Deployment
The accelerated Fast path combines quantized projections, fused operators, condition caches, full 50-block
DiT compilation, CUDA graphs, and W8A8 Ultra-Fast-VAE on one H200. Native 960 × 544 computation is
vertically cropped to 960 × 540 output. Three transitions and W2 history are retained.
One warmup rollout and three measured repeats on the 124-frame example give 15 steady full chunks,
yielding 18.0295 FPS and mean latency 740.69 ms. Latency starts with available hand/camera conditions
and ends when synchronized frames reach host memory; it includes online hand encoding, prefill, generation,
and decoding. Continuous FPS additionally includes the preceding clean KV commit. Loading, initial-image
conditioning, cold compilation, warmup, video encoding, acquisition, and display are excluded. The Fast
peak in Table 2 comes from a separate measurement of the released sampling-specialized model on the same
124-frame input geometry. After one full warmup and release of unused allocator cache, three rollouts reach
34.89 GiB maximum PyTorch-allocated device memory. Conditions are prepared in advance, with the native
H3 VAE and Qwen encoder off device during generation. The peak includes the quantized transformer, online
hand encoder, Ultra-Fast-VAE, KV caches, and graph buffers; cold loading and conditioning preparation are
excluded. Timing and the 768p quality evaluation use separate model weights and protocols.

Architecture and causal interface.
The decoder retains 26 blocks and a 2048-channel residual stream, with
eight 64-dimensional attention heads and 1536-channel SwiGLU branches. It has 361.217M parameters. As in
the H3-compatible LightVAE [35], 28 overlapping 16 × 16 latent tiles are blended spatially. Each tile processes
the five current latents using the seven-position reference RoPE clock; the readout crops 20 native positions
to 17 video frames. Attention is joint within a chunk and causal between chunks, with no future latents or
decoder history cache.
E.2
Training and Export
Initialization and supervision.
Energy-ranked heads/channels and ridge-fitted output projections initialize
the reduced branches, as defined in Section 3.5. Reconstruction fine-tuning updates the student decoder while
frozen full-width branches provide detached local and feature targets. Neither the teacher branches nor the
image bridge appears in the deployed decoder.
Training uses native-H3 latents and original frames from 100,722 videos across 12 sources. A disjoint
1,394-video pool is excluded from fitting and calibration; 200 stratified videos from it select the export. Each
update accumulates two videos, each with two consecutive 17-frame chunks. Dense MSE supervises all pixels;
sampled spatial and perceptual losses emphasize hand appearance. Temporal and boundary losses match
changes in reconstruction error across adjacent frames, including chunk transitions. Table 7 lists coefficients.
LPIPS [24] and DINOv2 [25] provide frozen perceptual features; the loss families build on LynnReal-Omni [35].

late-training parameter states. Input-channel smoothing [63] with α = 0.75 and the folded color affine are
calibrated on training videos only; validation frames do not supply fitting targets.
