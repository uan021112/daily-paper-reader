UNITE: Consolidating Embodied VLM Specialists
via Shared-Anchor One-Bit Factorization
Tao Tao, Anke Tang, Enneng Yang, Zhi Hou, Xiaogang Wang, and Dacheng Tao
Abstract—Embodied capabilities such as visual grounding, spa-
tial reasoning, navigation, and driving understanding are often
developed in separate vision-language specialists fine-tuned from
a common pretrained model. Deploying the specialists separately
multiplies storage, whereas merging them into one dense model
forces conflicting task updates to share parameters and loses part
of each specialist’s expertise. We introduce UNITE, a framework
that consolidates such specialists into a compact shared-backbone
mixture of experts. UNITE keeps the feed-forward block of each
specialist as a routable expert and represents the expert bank
through a shared-anchor one-bit factorization: a shared floating-
point anchor plus expert-specific, row-scaled binary deviations.
The anchor is optimized jointly with the scales and signs through
closed-form alternating updates, using only source weights and
no calibration data. Router adaptation then adjusts token-
level expert selection with the backbone and experts frozen,
updating fewer than 0.6M parameters (< 0.01% of the 8.8B
active parameters). Built from four Qwen3-VL-8B specialists, the
complete UNITE model occupies 20.3 GB, 1.16× the storage of
one dense specialist and 59.6% below the uncompressed MoE.
Across 22 benchmarks and R2R Val-Unseen, UNITE matches
six static merging methods in mean retention of best-specialist
performance, leads all of them on Scan2Cap, ScanQA, and all
four navigation metrics, and surpasses every source specialist
on all five 3D scene understanding benchmarks (up to +19.1 on
Scan2Cap) and on R2R navigation. Relative to the uncompressed
MoE, factorization is nearly lossless on 21 of 22 benchmarks; the
exception is VSI-Bench, where frame-count experiments suggest
impaired use of cross-frame evidence. UNITE thus consolidates
complementary embodied capabilities near single-model storage.
Index Terms—Vision-language models, model merging, mix-
ture of experts, one-bit quantization, embodied intelligence.

component plus a compact expert-specific deviation removes
this redundancy [21], [22]. Prior work fixes the shared com-
ponent in advance, typically at the pretrained base or at a
merged weight, and then compresses the deviation around
it. The key observation is that the shared anchor determines
how well the expert-specific residuals can be represented at
one bit per weight. A row-scaled binary deviation represents
only one magnitude per row, so the mean expert weight,
which minimizes unquantized squared residuals, need not
minimize binary reconstruction error. Fitting the anchor jointly
with the deviations accounts for this constraint and reduces
reconstruction error by 20–22% relative to a fixed mean anchor
on the Qwen3-VL-8B expert bank, at identical storage.
We introduce UNITE, a framework that integrates this
jointly optimized representation into a shared-backbone MoE
for embodied VLM specialists. Following the dense-to-MoE
construction of Branch-Train-MiX [20], each decoder layer
retains a complete feed-forward network (FFN) block from
each specialist as a separately routable expert, while the
remaining modules are merged into a shared backbone. The
factorization jointly fits a shared floating-point anchor, expert-
specific row scales, and binary signs through closed-form
alternating updates, each of which minimizes its conditional
subproblem. Fitting requires only source weights, with no
calibration data or gradients, and each additional expert costs
approximately one bit per weight. Supervised router adaptation
then adjusts token-level expert selection with the backbone
and experts frozen, updating fewer than 0.6M parameters
(< 0.01% of the 8.8B active parameters).
We evaluate UNITE built from four Qwen3-VL-8B spe-
cialists [23] for grounding, spatial reasoning, navigation, and
driving understanding, against the source specialists and six
static merging methods on 22 understanding benchmarks and
R2R Val-Unseen. The complete four-expert UNITE model
occupies 20.3 GB, 1.16× the footprint of one dense specialist
and 59.6% below the uncompressed MoE (Table I). At this
footprint, UNITE matches static merging methods in mean
retention of the best specialist’s score across the 22 bench-
marks, and it leads all static merging methods on Scan2Cap
(102.1 versus 86.4), ScanQA (105.6 versus 101.8), and all
four navigation metrics. UNITE also surpasses every source
specialist on all five 3D scene understanding benchmarks
and all four navigation metrics, improving Scan2Cap by 19.1
points over the best specialist and reaching 59.6% R2R
success rate. Stage-wise evaluation shows that factorization
is nearly lossless relative to the uncompressed MoE on 21
of 22 benchmarks; on the exception, VSI-Bench, frame-count
experiments suggest impaired use of cross-frame evidence.
Our main contributions are threefold: (1) a shared-anchor
one-bit factorization with closed-form updates that jointly
optimize the anchor and expert-specific binary deviations
using only source weights; (2) UNITE, a shared-backbone
MoE framework with compressed specialist FFNs and su-
pervised router adaptation, achieving 1.16× the storage of a
single dense model; and (3) a stage-wise evaluation across
22 embodied understanding benchmarks and R2R navigation,
which identifies the gains and losses from expert construction,
factorization, and router adaptation.
II. RELATED WORK
Embodied foundation models and capability reuse.
Vision-language-action (VLA) and world action models pursue
broader embodied capabilities through multimodal learning
and predictive modeling, as exemplified by π0.5, WorldVLA,
and DreamZero [2], [5], [24]. These models acquire breadth
through large-scale joint training across tasks. Parameter reuse
provides a complementary route that composes already-trained
skills. RETAIN interpolates a pretrained VLA policy with its
fine-tuned counterpart to retain general capabilities during task
adaptation [25], and MergeVLA combines masked parameter
merging, cross-attention action experts, and task routing to
merge multiple task-specialized VLA models for skill compo-
sition [26]. Both produce a single merged model and do not


3
address the storage cost of holding many specialists. UNITE
targets the many-specialist setting: it consolidates existing
dense VLM specialists derived from a common pretrained
model, with an emphasis on compact expert storage and
learned token-level selection.
Static and dynamic parameter merging. Parameter merg-
ing methods differ in whether they produce a fixed model or
compose expertise according to the input. Model Soups aver-
ages fine-tuned model weights, while Task Arithmetic com-
bines task vectors relative to a shared pretrained model [18],
[27]. Fisher Merging and RegMean use parameter impor-
tance and activation statistics, respectively [28], [29]. TIES-
Merging resolves conflicts among update signs, and DARE
sparsifies and rescales task updates [19], [30]. All of these
methods produce a single dense weight set, so capabilities
whose updates conflict must share the same parameters; the
resulting interference is evident in Section IV-C, where every
static method falls below the navigation specialist on R2R.
Dynamic variants reduce interference by conditioning on the
input. WEMoE constructs input-conditioned FFN weights
from pretrained weights and task vectors, while Twin-Merging
dynamically combines a shared merged model with low-rank
task residuals [31], [32]. These methods synthesize a new
weight matrix per input, which requires the task vectors or
residuals to be stored and combined at inference. UNITE
instead composes the outputs of complete feed-forward experts
through token-level routing, so each expert is stored once in
a compressed form and evaluated only when selected.
Expert construction and routing. Constructing mixtures
of experts (MoEs) from dense checkpoints reuses learned
parameters to form separately routable expert branches. Sparse
Upcycling initializes experts by replicating feed-forward net-
work (FFN) blocks from a single dense model [33]. Branch-
Train-MiX assembles FFN blocks from independently special-
ized models and averages the remaining parameters into a
shared backbone [20]. MergeME addresses both homogeneous
and heterogeneous expert models, using projection layers
and sequence-level routing to connect heterogeneous model
branches [34]. These constructions retain every expert at full
precision, so the expert bank grows linearly with the number of
specialists; in our setting, the resulting 50.2-GB uncompressed
MoE is 2.9× the size of one specialist. UNITE adopts this
construction and reduces its storage through jointly fitted
anchors and row-scaled binary deviations.
Routing determines how the constructed expert branches
are selected and used. PHATGOOSE learns gates post hoc on
each specialist’s source data with the backbone and parameter-
efficient modules frozen, then combines these gates for token-
and module-level routing [35]. SMILE selects experts using
projection norms onto SVD-derived task-update subspaces
without additional router training [36]. MADE-IT uses feature-
subspace alignment for training-free routing within an adap-
tive continual model-merging framework [37]. Training-free
routers are fitted to the uncompressed specialist weights and
do not see the reconstruction error introduced when the experts
are compressed. UNITE therefore uses singular directions of
the centered source weights only to initialize the routers, then
fine-tunes them on the compressed bank with supervision.
Compact specialist and expert representations. Com-
pact parameter representations reduce storage by separating
shared weights from specialist-specific deviations. BitDelta
compresses fine-tuning updates around a fixed pretrained base
using binary signs and matrix-level scales, while T-Switch
represents task vectors through binary masks, signs, and
scalar magnitudes [21], [38]. At the expert-bank level, DeRS
represents upcycled experts using their common pretrained
initialization and sparse or quantized deviations [22]. Low-
rank approaches offer another compact representation: D2-
MoE constructs a shared base through Fisher-weighted expert
merging and factorizes expert-specific residuals [39]. In all
of these methods the shared component is fixed before the
deviations are fitted, either at the pretrained base or at a
merged weight. When deviations are constrained to one bit per
weight, this fixed choice determines the residuals the binary
code must approximate, and the shared component cannot
compensate for the representation’s limited expressiveness.
UNITE removes this restriction by treating the anchor as an
optimization variable: it jointly fits a floating-point anchor,
expert-specific row scales, and binary deviation signs through
alternating closed-form updates, allowing the shared anchor
to adapt to the one-bit deviation budget while preserving a
separate reconstruction for each expert.
III. METHOD
We consider E dense vision-language specialists for com-
plementary embodied capabilities, including grounding, spatial
reasoning, navigation, and driving understanding. They share
an architecture and are fine-tuned from a common pretrained
model. UNITE consolidates them into a shared-backbone
mixture of experts (MoE), reducing weight duplication while
retaining conditional access to specialist transformations.
The three stages serve distinct roles (Fig. 3). First, expert
construction retains complete specialist feed-forward blocks
and merges the remaining parameters into the shared back-
bone. Second, shared-anchor one-bit factorization compresses
the expert weights using source weights alone. Finally, super-
vised router adaptation learns token-level expert selection with
the backbone and compressed experts frozen.

the gate, up, and down projections of its source specialist;

and output dimensions allow the experts to process token
representations within a shared decoder.

B. Joint Shared-Anchor One-Bit Factorization
We represent every expert matrix as a shared anchor plus
an expert-specific binary deviation. The anchor determines the
residuals that the binary deviations must approximate, and with
one nonnegative scale per output row, a binary deviation can
represent only a single magnitude within that row. An anchor
fixed in advance therefore leaves reconstruction error that the
deviations cannot absorb. We instead treat the anchor as an
optimization variable and formulate reconstruction as a joint
optimization over the anchor, row scales, and binary signs.

rows into one weight group and treat its down projection as
a second group. For a fixed layer and weight group, expert e
has a weight matrix We ∈RO×I with O output rows and I

backbone and routers are counted separately. Because the
anchor is shared, the marginal cost of adding one more expert
is only 1+16/I bits per weight, so the representation becomes
more economical as more specialists are consolidated.

Supervised router adaptation adjusts expert selection to the
compressed model. The shared backbone, anchors, row scales,
and binary signs remain frozen. Training combines causal
language-modeling loss on target answer tokens with an aux-
iliary load-balancing loss, without explicit expert-assignment
labels. During router adaptation, we select multiple experts per
token so gradients from the language-modeling loss can pass
through the selected mixture weights.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
Our experiments answer four questions. (i) How much
storage does UNITE save relative to the alternatives of keeping
separate specialists or an uncompressed MoE (Section IV-B)?
(ii) At that storage, does UNITE retain the capabilities that
static merging loses, and does consolidation add capability
beyond the source specialists (Section IV-C)? (iii) At which
stage do gains and losses arise, and how does router adaptation
interact with one-bit compression (Sections IV-D and IV-E)?
(iv) Does the factorization transfer to a second model family
without router training (Section IV-F)?

accuracy for MMSI and DriveAction; MindCube uses the

F1@0.25, respectively. For R2R, we report navigation error

weighted by path length (SPL). NE is in meters; OS, SR,
and SPL are percentages. ShareRobot reports mean normalized
discrete Fréchet distance (nDFD) for trajectory prediction.
Lower is better for NE and nDFD; higher is better otherwise.
The supplement details benchmark variants and protocols.

For context, we also evaluate general-purpose and embodied

metrics. They differ from UNITE in architecture, scale, and
training data. Tables II and III compare their task-level scores
with UNITE, while Tables IV and V compare methods built

baseline implementations and calibration settings.
Training and evaluation. We apply ten alternating factoriza-

gradients through the selected mixture weights, whereas the
normalized top-1 coefficient in Eq. (1) is constant. Uncom-
pressed and compressed router-tuned models share the same
training budget and supervision mixture; full optimization
settings are provided in the supplement.

B. Weight Storage and GPU Memory
Table I places UNITE between the two deployment options
discussed in Section I. Four standalone specialists require 70.1
GB. Sharing the non-FFN backbone alone reduces this to 50.2

43.5 to 13.6 GB; adding 6.7 GB for shared modules and

reduction relative to the uncompressed MoE and a 71.0%

the 17.5-GB footprint of a single dense specialist or any static
merging baseline, while retaining four routable experts.

D. Stage-wise Analysis
We trace when capability gains and losses arise through
the three consolidation stages: the uncompressed MoE, the
shared-anchor one-bit MoE, and the router-adapted one-bit
MoE. Tables IV and V compare these stages with source
experts and static merging on understanding and navigation.
Figure 6 shows the change contributed by each stage across
benchmarks. All three stages use top-1 routing, so the number
of activated expert branches is fixed.
Source specialists are complementary. The navigation and
embodied spatial specialists record 57.5% and 56.8% R2R
SR, whereas the grounding specialist reaches 28.2% and the
driving specialist 0.1%. Conversely, the driving specialist leads
on NuScenesQA (58.8), LingoQA (65.8), and SAT (92.0),
where the other specialists are far behind. A consolidated
model must preserve capabilities held by a single source.
Expert construction already composes capabilities. Before
any compression or router training, the uncompressed MoE
scores 104.3 on ScanQA and 97.1 on Scan2Cap, exceeding
every source specialist. Its R2R SR (55.9%), NuScenesQA
(27.7), RoboSpatial (58.0), and MME-RealWorld (65.6) scores
are below the corresponding best specialists (57.5%, 58.8,
68.0, and 71.2). Router adaptation later recovers R2R and
most of NuScenesQA, whereas the RoboSpatial and MME-
RealWorld gaps persist; they therefore arise at expert con-
struction rather than from compression.
One-bit factorization is nearly lossless on 21 of 22 bench-
marks. Factorizing the expert bank reduces total model weight
storage from 50.2 to 20.3 GB, lowers no score other than VSI
by more than 1.2 points, and improves 16 of the 22: ScanQA
increases from 104.3 to 106.6, Scan2Cap from 97.1 to 100.6,
RefSpatial from 56.1 to 59.4, and ShareRobot (Traj.) from
0.34 to 0.26. R2R SR also improves, from 55.9% to 57.0%.

The one-bit MoE gains little from additional frames on
VSI. To characterize this deficit, we evaluate both expert banks
on VSI while varying the number of sampled frames from 4
to 64 (Fig. 7). At four frames the uncompressed and the one-
bit MoE score 48.1 and 46.9, a gap of 1.2 points in line with
the image benchmarks. With more frames the uncompressed
model improves monotonically to 55.2, 59.2, 62.4, and 65.0,
while the one-bit model stays between 46.8 and 48.8 with no
trend; the gap therefore widens to 17.2 points at 64 frames.
The sub-task breakdown is consistent with this reading. On
object counting and object-size estimation, scores change by
at most 5 points across frame counts for either model, and
the two models differ by at most about six points at any
frame count. By contrast, the uncompressed model improves
from 49.4 to 80.3 on relative direction, from 51.0 to 77.8
on appearance order, from 28.3 to 49.1 on absolute distance,
and from 45.6 to 62.2 on room size, whereas the one-bit
model varies by less than six points on each of them. These
results suggest that one-bit factorization limits the model’s
use of additional cross-frame evidence. At the same total
model weight storage, the INT4 bank retains VSI at 61.9
while trailing on most other benchmarks, showing that the two
representations have different error profiles. Router adaptation
does not recover the VSI deficit (Table VI); testing larger bit
budgets in selected experts or layers is left to future work.
Router adaptation refines selection. With the backbone and
compressed experts frozen, router adaptation raises SAT from
87.3 to 90.7, LingoQA from 59.2 to 63.0, MAPLM from 92.0
to 93.8, NuScenesQA from 29.7 to 48.7, and R2R SR from
57.0% to 59.6%. It lowers 12 of the 22 benchmarks, all but
RefSpatial (59.4 to 57.2) by at most 1.2 points.
Compression and router adaptation interact. To separate
the effect of compression from that of adaptation, we also
train the routers of the uncompressed MoE under the same
supervision mixture and training budget (Table VI).
After adaptation, the 20.3-GB compressed MoE outper-
forms the 50.2-GB uncompressed one on Scan2Cap (102.1
versus 94.1), NuScenesQA (48.7 versus 24.4), and R2R SR
and SPL, while the uncompressed model leads on ScanQA

ScanRefer and Multi3DRef. Two observations follow. First,
one-bit compression does not systematically limit what router
adaptation can achieve: on four of the nine reported metrics,
the compressed model is the better one after adaptation.

V. CONCLUSION
We presented UNITE, a framework that consolidates dense
embodied VLM specialists into a shared-backbone mixture of
experts whose expert bank is stored through a shared-anchor
one-bit factorization. Under a one-bit deviation budget, the
shared anchor is optimized jointly with the expert-specific
row scales and binary signs through closed-form alternating
updates that require only the source weights. Combined with
router adaptation of fewer than 0.6M parameters (< 0.01% of
the 8.8B active parameters), UNITE consolidates four Qwen3-
VL-8B specialists into a model whose total weight storage is
1.16× that of a single specialist. Across 22 embodied under-
standing benchmarks and R2R navigation, UNITE surpasses
every source specialist on all five 3D scene understanding
benchmarks and all four navigation metrics; the factorization
itself is nearly lossless relative to the uncompressed MoE on
21 of the 22 understanding benchmarks.
For the remaining exception, the video benchmark VSI-
Bench, frame-count experiments show that the compressed
model gains little from additional frames, suggesting impaired
use of cross-frame evidence. These findings motivate inves-
tigating whether a larger bit budget for selected experts or


12
layers can recover this capability. Extending the framework to
vision-language-action policies with action heads and to more
divergent specialists is a natural direction for future work.

M2. IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING DETAILS
Model construction and compression.: The embodied model uses four Qwen3-VL-8B specialists, each with 36 decoder
layers, hidden dimension d = 4096, and intermediate dimension m = 12288. Each layer contains two independently factorized
weight groups, gate-up and down, each spanning all four experts, giving 72 groups in total. For each group, we initialize the
anchor with the expert mean, perform T = 10 alternating updates in FP32 (Algorithm 1), and round the final anchor to BF16.
We then recompute the binary signs and row scales relative to the stored anchor and store the recomputed row scales in BF16.
For storage, we encode each sign Se,o,i ∈{−1, +1} as a bit be,o,i = (Se,o,i + 1)/2 ∈{0, 1}, mapping negative residuals to
0 and nonnegative residuals to 1. We pack the bits into 32-bit words along each matrix’s input dimension and decode them
at inference time as Se,o,i = 2be,o,i −1. For a group of E expert matrices with shape O × I, the anchor, signs, and row
scales require 16OI + EOI + 16EO bits before packing alignment. This is 1 + 16/E + 16/I bits per logical expert weight,
or approximately five bits for the four-expert bank, including the amortized anchor and scales.

Router adaptation.: We train only the 36 router matrices (589,824 parameters) with target-token language-modeling loss
and a load-balancing loss weighted by 10−3. Training runs for one epoch on eight A800 GPUs with learning rate 10−3,
cosine decay, warmup ratio 0.03, and seed 42. The per-device batch size is one, with four gradient accumulation steps, giving
an effective global batch size of 32. Training selects two experts per token, while the exported model uses top-1 unless the
evaluation explicitly varies k. Under Eq. (1) of the main manuscript, top-2 routing permits gradients through the selected
mixture weights, whereas the normalized mixture coefficient is identically one for top-1.
Router supervision mixture.: Router adaptation uses 55,757 supervised samples from eighteen sources spanning navigation,
3D referring, grounding, affordance, trajectory, video spatial reasoning, spatial question answering, egocentric planning, and
general visual question answering. The uncompressed MoE (Table VI) uses the same mixture and training budget.
