Abstract—Human manipulation data are widely used to train
robots for human-like dexterity. However, low-cost, large-scale
collection in the wild remains challenging. Existing methods re-
main limited in geometrically grounded metric-scale reconstruc-
tion, real-world generalization and inference speed. We present
WinDEX, integrating (1) geometric–temporal optimization for
metric 3D hand annotation from in-the-wild egocentric stereo
videos, combined with visual–inertial SLAM for world-frame
reconstruction; (2) WinDEX-Net, an end-to-end, fisheye-native
network combining calibrated stereo, local detail and temporal
context, with a data–model flywheel of model-assisted annotation
and retraining; (3) distilled models for offline reconstruction
and causal streaming, supporting reinforcement learning and
teleoperation; and (4) low-cost CyberCap stereo–inertial capture,
forming the WinDEX-Hand dataset with 1,000 hours of train-
ing annotations and held-out human-annotated in-the-wild 2D
and independent optical motion-capture evaluation sets. Offline
reconstruction runs at 43.61 FPS and causal streaming at 171
FPS. On held-out data, offline reconstruction achieves state-of-
the-art accuracy: 14.5 pixels in-the-wild reprojection error and,
with visual–inertial odometry, 37.29 mm world-frame joint error.
We achieve 7.6× and 3.6× the task success rates of reinforcement
learning policies trained with WiLoR and HaWoR motion refer-
ences, respectively, and demonstrate real-time arm–hand teleop-
eration. Project website: https://fruitrabbit.github.io/WinDEX/.
Index Terms—World-frame 3D hand pose estimation, ego-
centric stereo vision, learning from demonstration, teleoperation.

OBOTICISTS have long sought to guide dexterous
robotic manipulation using human motion trajectories.
Hand poses and trajectories guide teleoperation [1], [2], [3],
[4] and provide demonstrations, pose priors, and tracking
references for reinforcement learning (RL) [5], [6], [7]. Hand
trajectories also provide action supervision for video-based
policies and vision–language–action models [8], [9], [10],
condition world models that predict manipulation outcomes
[11], and supply geometric information for grasp synthesis
[12], [13]. These applications share a need for reliable 3D
hand motion in relation to the surrounding scene. We adopt
metric 3D hand poses and their trajectories in a gravity-aligned
world frame as our 3D hand representations, describing wrist
motion and finger articulation independently of the moving
camera (Fig. 1). Recovering this representation across every-
day environments at scale is our central problem.
Existing acquisition routes include optical motion capture,
calibrated multi-camera rigs, sensor gloves and wearable track-
ers, and video recording. Optical systems and multi-camera
datasets such as ARCTIC, DexYCB, HOT3D and Assembly-
Hands provide detailed reference measurements [14], [15],

demonstrate the potential of wearable multi-view sensing
[23], [24], [25]. Scalable in-the-wild capture still requires

To recover metric hand trajectories from these recordings,
both model architecture and training supervision must accom-
modate wearable capture. Monocular image and video estima-
tors, including HaMeR, WiLoR, HaWoR and Dyn-HaMR [26],
[27], [28], [29], use appearance and motion priors to resolve
single-view ambiguity. POEM and its successor exploit cal-
ibrated multi-view geometry [30], [31]; ViDiHand integrates
localization and reconstruction without an external detector
[32]. For compact fisheye stereo, an integrated architecture
must additionally use the known baseline and viewing rays,
preserve local finger detail, and distinguish hand motion from
camera motion. On the data side, laboratory capture limits
environmental coverage, and tracker-generated labels require
independent accuracy evaluation [16], [22]. Dense, reliable 3D
supervision across diverse natural recordings remains costly.
These needs motivate joint localization and reconstruction with
stereo and temporal context, coupled to a scalable pipeline for
producing and checking training annotations.
We present WinDEX, a system integrating wearable capture,
geometric–temporal annotation, learned reconstruction and
robot interfaces (Fig. 2). Low-cost CyberCap collects fisheye
stereo RGB and inertial data without hand instrumentation. To
obtain metric supervision, geometric–temporal optimization
fits hands to manually reviewed 2D keypoints, filters 3D labels
by reprojection error, and uses SLAM camera trajectories to
recover world-frame motion.
To address depth ambiguity, preserve finger detail and
account for camera motion, WinDEX-Net combines calibrated
stereo, local crops and odometry-conditioned temporal features
for end-to-end, fisheye-native localization and reconstruction.
Predictions accelerate annotation, and retraining closes the
data–model flywheel. WinDEX-Hand separates generated 3D
training labels from evaluation references: human-annotated
in-the-wild 2D references and independent optical 3D ground
truth (Section V-C).
To balance accuracy and response time, distillation yields
offline WinDEX-Window and causal, real-time WinDEX-
Stream. Hand2Policy uses pose-tracking rewards for reinforce-
ment learning; Hand2Teleop coordinates arm–hand teleop-
eration using only CyberCap stereo and IMU for operator
tracking.
Against human-annotated in-the-wild 2D references and
optical 3D ground truth from held-out recordings, WinDEX-
Window achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction accuracy and
temporal smoothness. Under matched training settings and
budgets, Hand2Policy reaches 61% mean simulated grasp–lift–
place success versus 8% for WiLoR and 17% for HaWoR, with
policy transfer to physical robots.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
1) To obtain metric 3D hand annotations from in-the-
wild egocentric stereo videos, we combine geometric–
temporal optimization with visual–inertial SLAM to re-
cover hand articulation and metrically scaled trajectories
in a consistent world frame.
2) To accelerate reconstruction, we introduce WinDEX-
Net, an end-to-end, fisheye-native network combining
calibrated stereo geometry, local hand detail and tempo-
ral context. Model-initialized optimization and retraining
form a data–model flywheel that expands supervision.
3) To support offline learning and live control, we dis-
till WinDEX-Net into WinDEX-Window and WinDEX-
Stream. Hand2Policy and Hand2Teleop further connect
the models to reinforcement learning with policy transfer
and real-time arm–hand teleoperation, respectively, with
validation on physical hardware.
4) To make collection accessible at scale, we use the
low-cost CyberCap stereo–inertial device for everyday
manipulation and construct WinDEX-Hand from the
annotated recordings, with held-out human-annotated in-
the-wild 2D references and independent optical motion-
capture ground truth.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Human Hand Motion for Robot Control and Learning
Human hand motion supports teleoperation, policy learning,
and action representations for vision–language–action (VLA)
and world models. AnyTeleop [2] converts visual hand esti-
mates into robot commands through learning-free retargeting
and collision-aware motion generation. DexCap [4] matches
human and robot fingertips through inverse kinematics and
learns point-cloud diffusion policies from wearable demon-
strations. For policy learning, DexMV [5] reconstructs hand–
object poses from paired RGB-D views in a configured record-
ing volume, translates them into robot states and actions, and
trains policies in corresponding simulated tasks. ManipTrans
[7] pretrains a generalist trajectory imitator and learns residual
corrections for object interaction, assuming supplied hand–
object trajectories. EgoVLA [9] and Being-H0 [10] use hand
trajectories as action supervision for VLA pretraining from
human videos. Dexterous World Models [11] condition visual-
dynamics prediction on hand motion. These uses depend on
reliable motion representations. WinDEX addresses this shared
need by recovering metric, world-frame hand motion from
everyday scenes.
B. Hand Motion Capture and Egocentric Datasets
Hand-motion datasets differ in capture hardware, image
modality and label reliability. DexCap combines electromag-
netic gloves with SLAM cameras [4]; DexUMI uses joint
encoders in a robot-specific exoskeleton and ARKit wrist
tracking [18]. Both retain hardware on the hands.
Headset datasets also differ from our RGB stereo setting.
UmeTrack uses four monochrome fisheye cameras and marker-
based optical ground truth [25]. HOT3D records Aria and
Quest 3 streams with optical ground truth in an instrumented

C. Image-Based 3D Hand Reconstruction
Many image-based pipelines first localize the hand and
then estimate its pose from a cropped image [17], [24],
[25]. Monocular image and video methods [26], [27], [28],
[29] recover detailed hand pose and shape using learned
appearance and motion priors. Metric depth remains dependent
on additional assumptions because a single view does not
directly determine absolute scale. POEM and POEM-v2 [30],
[31] use calibrated observations to constrain 3D reconstruc-
tion, whereas HGGT [35] jointly estimates hand meshes and
camera poses from uncalibrated images. Applying multi-view
reconstruction to a head-worn stereo pair requires accounting
for a short baseline, fisheye distortion, coupled camera and
hand motion, and occlusion.
Whole-image estimation [36] and recent video-based meth-
ods [32], [37] explore reconstruction without a separate hand-
detection stage. WinDEX-Net builds on this direction for na-
tive fisheye stereo input. It combines calibration-aware features
with temporal context to estimate hand articulation and metric
wrist position. Hand localization and iterative crop refinement

separately trained detector. The architecture is described in

D. Model-Assisted Annotation and Iterative Learning
Model-assisted data collection and self-training couple an-
notation with repeated model updates [38], [39]. These strate-
gies can extend supervision to larger datasets, while introduc-
ing the risk that prediction errors propagate across training
rounds. WinDEX uses model predictions to initialize anno-
tation, then refines them against stereo observations before
adding accepted results to the training set (Section V-B).
Geometric optimization and filtering by reprojection error
constrain labels using the recorded image evidence. A sep-
arate motion-capture set, excluded from training, provides an
independent measure of model accuracy throughout the iter-
ations. This combination links annotation scale to geometric
consistency and external evaluation.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
WinDEX connects egocentric recordings of everyday ma-
nipulation to robot control and learning through metric, world-
frame hand motion. Extending egocentric corpora [19], [20],
[22] with this representation requires both geometric recon-
struction and annotation at scale. Calibrated stereo supplies
depth constraints, and inertial measurements support camera
tracking. An offline optimizer uses these observations to gener-
ate supervision for WinDEX-Net; model predictions then ini-
tialize further annotation. Distillation yields WinDEX-Window
for offline reconstruction in Hand2Policy and WinDEX-Stream
for causal inference in Hand2Teleop.

B. System Design
Reliably modeling every factor in (2) across in-the-wild
conditions is difficult. We therefore learn reconstruction from
supervision obtained through tractable geometric and hand-
prior constraints. The workflow proceeds through data collec-
tion, annotation, learned inference and robot deployment.
1) Low-Cost Capture and WinDEX-Hand. To make collec-
tion accessible at scale, we use low-cost CyberCap to
record synchronized stereo images and inertial measure-
ments during everyday manipulation. WinDEX-Hand
organizes recordings for annotation and model training,
with held-out human-annotated in-the-wild 2D refer-
ences and independent optical motion-capture ground
truth for evaluation (Sections IV-A and V-C).
2) Geometric–Temporal Annotation. To obtain metric su-
pervision, we fit hand motion to manually reviewed 2D
keypoints under the observation, hand-prior and tempo-
ral factors in (2). Reprojection-error filtering selects 3D
annotations, and visual–inertial SLAM camera trajecto-
ries place hand motion in the world frame (Section IV).
3) WinDEX-Net and the Data–Model Flywheel. To accel-
erate reconstruction, WinDEX-Net combines calibrated
stereo, local hand detail and odometry-conditioned tem-
poral context in an end-to-end, fisheye-native model.
It approximates the hand-state MAP solution in (2),
with SLAM camera poses held fixed. Predictions ini-
tialize further optimization against manually reviewed
2D observations; refined 3D labels passing the same
reprojection-error threshold expand training, closing the
data–model flywheel (Section V).
4) Distillation and Robot Deployment. To balance accu-
racy, computational cost and response time, we dis-
till WinDEX-Net into WinDEX-D. WinDEX-Window
uses bidirectional context for accurate offline recon-
struction; WinDEX-Stream retains causal state for real-
time inference without future frames. Combined with
SLAM camera poses, the estimates support Hand2Policy

IV. DATA COLLECTION AND GEOMETRIC–TEMPORAL
ANNOTATION
We recover hand articulation and metric placement by
combining stereo evidence, temporal hand priors and visual–
inertial camera poses. The following collection and annotation
stages implement these factors and produce filtered training
supervision.
A. Recording Configuration and Data Collection
The binocular observation term in (3) requires synchronized,
calibrated views of the same hand; the camera-pose factor
in (2) requires aligned visual and inertial measurements. Cy-
berCap supplies both. Its cameras share a calibrated 60.7 mm
baseline and capture hardware-synchronized RGB fisheye im-
ages at 1920 × 1200 pixels per eye and 30 fps. A rigidly
mounted IMU records at 300 Hz on the same clock, with mea-
surements embedded in the video stream. Calibrated fisheye
intrinsics [41], stereo extrinsics and camera–IMU extrinsics
connect these observations geometrically.
We adapt occupational and work-activity information from
O*NET [42] to organize recording activities into collection
categories. WinDEX-Hand separates training recordings from
human-annotated 2D-only in-the-wild evaluation and inde-
pendently captured optical motion-capture ground truth; Sec-
tion V-C details their composition and splits.
B. World-Frame Odometry
Hand reconstruction alone describes motion relative to a
moving camera. We estimate the camera-pose factor p(T |
I, A) in (2) with cuVSLAM [43], combining calibrated stereo
observations with inertial measurements to constrain camera
motion during occlusion and rapid head turns. This follows
visual–inertial estimation [44], [45] using native fisheye im-
ages and the camera and IMU calibration. Poses take the first
recorded frame as the world origin and carry covariance and
confidence estimates; initial poses remain low-confidence until
gravity estimation settles.
Composing camera and hand poses makes odometry error
part of the world-frame reconstruction error. Against indepen-
dent optical motion capture, the camera trajectory has a root
mean square absolute trajectory error of 0.150 m and a root
mean square orientation error of 11.69◦.
C. Annotation by Optimization
The observation factor pobs(ul

binocular 2D keypoints the reference evidence for fitting.
Pose estimators supply these observations, and human review
selects reliable 2D keypoints. Fitted 3D annotations are then
filtered by their reprojection error against the accepted key-
points. Pose, shape and motion priors constrain ambiguities in
image evidence. The pipeline (Fig. 2(a)) therefore fits shared
shape and per-frame pose and placement, then refines motion
across frames.
1) Adaptive Virtual Projection: The available hand detec-
tors and pose estimators operate on pinhole images. In our
recordings, hands frequently occupy the fisheye periphery
(Fig. 6c). A single centered virtual view either omits these
regions or stretches them near its boundaries. We therefore
render five virtual pinhole views centered on the image center
and four quadrant centers. YOLO11-pose [46] and ViTPose
[47] estimate hand keypoints in these views, and the highest-
confidence hand detection selects the virtual view for each
hand. This provides suitable input to the estimators while
retaining native fisheye coordinates for the subsequent fitting
objective.
Within the selected virtual view, we fuse the two estimators
joint by joint. Candidates with confidence below 0.3 are
discarded. If both remaining estimates lie within 10% of the
hand-box side length, their coordinates are averaged with
confidence weights; otherwise, we retain the higher-confidence
estimate, using the hand-box score to resolve near-ties. Joints
without a valid candidate are excluded. The fused keypoints
are mapped to native fisheye coordinates and checked for
stereo depth and hand-structure consistency before fitting.
2) Initialization: Initialization combines an appearance-
based hand estimate with a geometric depth estimate. WiLoR
[27] predicts articulation and shape (θ0, β0) from the selected
view in each eye; triangulated wrist disparity from a stereo
matcher provides metric depth z0. The fused keypoints supply
the observations in (3), with residuals evaluated under the
calibrated fisheye projection model.
3) Stage One: Frame-Wise Optimization: The first stage
uses WiLoR’s pose and shape estimates (θ0, β0) and the
triangulated depth z0 as optimization initial values, then fits
MANO parameters and depth to the keypoints. MANO defines
the articulated geometry used to evaluate pobs, and DPoser-
X [48] supplies the articulation prior ppose in (2). A single
shape vector β is estimated for each recording: allowing shape
to vary frame by frame would let it absorb depth errors
and violate the shared-shape state in (1). We estimate β by
independently fitting the 64 highest-confidence frames, which
need not be consecutive, and averaging their shape estimates.
With β fixed, each frame is then optimized for articulation θt,
global orientation and depth zt.
4) Stage Two: Temporal Optimization in Three Modes: The
motion factor pdyn in (2) must suppress fluctuations without
erasing rapid hand motion. We use three modes—smooth mo-
tion, occlusion and rapid motion—to adapt its strength relative
to the observation term in (4). Smooth motion is the default.
Low 2D keypoint confidence, even in the better-observed
eye, indicates occlusion. When 2D confidence remains high
but reprojection error is large, we select rapid-motion mode:
reliable image observations disagree with the current tempo-
rally constrained estimate, suggesting that smoothing may be
restricting a fast pose change.
For smooth motion, temporal regularization and a 6 Hz low-
pass filter reduce detector jitter. During occlusion, we hold
the last high-confidence pose until a new high-confidence
frame becomes available. This avoids unstable fits but does
not recover unobserved articulation. For rapid motion, weaker
temporal weights allow the 2D observations to preserve fast

only and model-assisted annotations, keeping the acceptance
criterion fixed across flywheel rounds. Human review validates
the 2D reference points; this reprojection test selects the 3D
annotations (Section V-B).
D. Annotation Validation
We evaluate held-out WinDEX-Hand recordings to assess
calibrated RGB stereo under a consistent validation proto-
col. Existing headset benchmarks use monochrome tracking
streams or tracker-generated labels [16], [22], [25]. Indepen-
dent optical motion capture quantifies 3D accuracy; human-
annotated in-the-wild 2D references assess image consistency
and hand localization. Model comparisons (Section VII-C)
follow the same protocol.
An independently recorded optical motion-capture set pro-
vides 3D reference measurements (Section V-C). A rigid target
observed by both systems establishes the transform from the
capture frame to the reference camera frame. This set is
excluded from model training and iterative relabeling.
For motion-capture validation, we report absolute wrist-
position error in the reference camera frame, wrist-relative
mean per-joint position error (MPJPE), and Procrustes-aligned
MPJPE (PA-MPJPE), all in millimeters. MPJPE removes wrist
translation; PA-MPJPE aligns rotation, translation and uniform
scale. Compared with our optical motion-capture ground truth,
the retained annotations achieve mean errors of 9.6 mm for
wrist position, 10.1 mm for MPJPE and 6.1 mm for PA-
MPJPE across both hands. These results quantify metric wrist-
placement and hand-articulation accuracy against independent
optical ground truth.
For in-the-wild validation, we pool all annotation results,
including those obtained by optimization alone and by model-
initialized optimization, and report the mean 2D reprojection
error in pixels. The resulting error of 7.9 pixels indicates
close agreement between the optimized annotations and image
observations across diverse everyday scenes.

B. Iterative Annotation and Independent Audit
Starting with 20 hours of recordings annotated by opti-
mization, Algorithm 1 expands the labeled corpus. WinDEX-
Net predicts initializations for unlabeled recordings. Reviewers
first select reliable, independently detected 2D keypoints. The
pipeline of Section IV-C refines the initial 3D estimates against
these references and filters the results by reprojection error.
The accepted image–3D-label pairs enter the next training
round.
Each refinement round re-evaluates the factors of (2) on the
model-initialized trajectory: independent 2D keypoints provide
pobs targets, the frozen pose prior constrains articulation, and
temporal refinement enforces cross-frame consistency. Repro-
jection against human-screened keypoints selects the refined
3D labels. Network fitting and geometric relabeling alternate
in this expectation–maximization-style schedule (Section III).
An independent motion-capture set is excluded from fitting
and audits each round; iteration stops when audit error no
longer improves. Better initializations can allow additional
clips to pass optimization and filtering, expanding training
coverage. The annotation corpus C excludes the held-out,
human-annotated in-the-wild test set (Section V-C).
C. The WinDEX-Hand Dataset
WinDEX-Hand separates training supervision from evalua-
tion references through three data components. The training
corpus contains 1,000 hours of in-the-wild pseudo-ground-
truth annotations from the data–model loop, collected from
more than 200 operators across more than 1,000 scenes
and more than 5,000 distinct short-range tasks. Drawing on
O*NET occupational and work-activity information [42], we
organize collection into 13 scene categories: food prepara-
tion, retail, logistics, production, cleaning, healthcare, repair,
personal care, construction, office work, farming, craft and
education (Fig. 6a).
For held-out in-the-wild 2D evaluation, we human-annotate
20 hours collected separately across the same scene cate-
gories. Sapiens2 [51] and YOLO11-pose [46] initialize 2D
keypoints. Ordered from low to high detection confidence,
samples undergo human annotation of both keypoints and
bounding boxes, followed by manual review to establish the
test references. The set provides 2D annotations only, with no
3D ground truth, and is excluded from model training, baseline
fine-tuning and the annotation flywheel.
The optical motion-capture set supplies independent 3D
ground truth from 10 volunteers performing 38 task types
over 20 hours. We recreate six everyday settings within the
optical capture volume: food preparation, logistics packing, a
retail counter, workshop repair, office desk work and cleaning.
Participants wear CyberCap while optical tracking provides
per-frame 21-keypoint hand positions and validity flags. Ac-
tivities follow the same task inventory as the in-the-wild
collection. A hardware trigger synchronizes recordings at each
session’s start; wrist-velocity cross-correlation refines temporal
alignment offline. The set is excluded from model training and
iterative relabeling. Spatial registration and accuracy assess-
ment are described in Section IV-D.
Analysis of the collected in-the-wild data shows a median
wrist depth of 318 mm, with the central 80% of hand-frames
between 215 and 455 mm (Fig. 6b,c), consistent with the near-
field manipulation activities represented in the corpus. Half of
hand-frames are more than 25◦off the optical axis, and one in
ten exceeds 43◦, underscoring the need to reconstruct hands
in peripheral fisheye regions.

A. The WinDEX-D Family
The two members use different visual backbones, temporal
architectures and distillation targets. WinDEX-Window is a
116M-parameter, 32-frame student that compresses WinDEX-
Net through per-joint feature matching while retaining its
reconstruction stages. WinDEX-Stream is an 8.92M-parameter
causal student trained from a frozen WinDEX-Window check-
point through feature and motion-output supervision. The
former preserves window-based MANO reconstruction for
offline use; the latter learns recurrent prediction of metric 3D
joints for live control.
1) WinDEX-Window: Offline Reconstruction:
The main
cost of WinDEX-Net lies in the VGGT-Omega trunk’s global
attention over both views’ patches. To reduce this cost while
retaining the learned hand reconstruction stages, WinDEX-
Window replaces the trunk at its per-joint token interface.
A DINOv3 ViT-S/16 [49] encodes each eye, followed by an
image encoder structurally identical to the teacher’s for cross-
view fusion. The crop branch, geometry, motion encoder and
decoder are retained. The student inherits the teacher’s cross-
eye and read-out weights at initialization; among the retained
modules, only the crop branch’s input projection is learned
anew.
This interface also makes distillation practical at corpus
scale: per-joint teacher tensors are compact enough to cache,
avoiding repeated teacher inference during frame-wise stu-
dent training. We compute targets from clean images and
augment only student inputs, encouraging consistent features
under appearance changes. Interface matching is followed by
end-to-end fine-tuning under (17), because crop processing
and geometric refinement also consume features outside the
distilled interface.

B. Hand2Policy and Hand2Teleop
Retargeting separates wrist-based arm targets from wrist-
relative hand articulation to map the shared motion representa-
tion to each robot’s kinematics. In Hand2Teleop, a local server
runs WinDEX-Stream alongside real-time VIO. WinDEX-
Stream reconstructs metric 3D keypoints, and VIO camera
poses transform them into a global coordinate frame to reduce
jitter caused by head motion. The controller uses global wrist
motion relative to a session anchor for arm targets and wrist-
relative articulation for hand targets. Filtering, transient jump
rejection and retention of the last accepted target reduce the
influence of unreliable observations on commands.
In Hand2Policy, WinDEX-Window reconstructs recorded
trajectories for reinforcement-learning transfer. Retargeting
each clip once produces a robot-specific reference that a
tracking policy can reuse for replay, reset or training.
Both interfaces obtain arm targets from wrist displacement
through damped least-squares inverse kinematics. Hand targets
use analytical retargeting of the dex-retargeting family [1],
[2]. The live Wuji hand uses the vendor implementation [55],
and the simulated Inspire and Wuji hands use the trajectory
imitator’s retargeting stage [7] (Section VII-D).

end to end as described in Section VI-A1. WinDEX-Stream
uses MobileNetV3-Large encoders and causal recurrent fusion,
trained with the feature and motion-output distillation objec-
tives in Section VI-A2.
For the WinDEX-Net ablations, we evaluate the teacher’s
depth-feature tap under matched initialization and fine-tuning,
changing only the selected layer. We assess crop refinement
on held-out clips and group results by projected hand size to
test whether local resampling benefits hands that are poorly
resolved in full-frame features.
The offline evaluations in Sections VII-B and VII-C use an
NVIDIA RTX 4090 with FP16 inference, FP32 crop geometry,
sequence batch size one, and 32-frame windows at stride 16
with overlap crossfading. WinDEX-Net uses the same offline
inference protocol for the speed–accuracy comparison. Recon-
struction comparisons use WinDEX-Window with two crop
iterations. Reconstruction is evaluated against optical motion-
capture ground truth and human-annotated 2D references from
the held-out in-the-wild test set (Section VII-C).
WinDEX-Stream updates from each incoming stereo pair
and retained state, without future observations. Table III re-
ports its causal per-frame throughput alongside source-frame
throughput for the offline models. Its use for live control is
evaluated separately in Hand2Teleop (Section VII-E).
Hand2Policy (Section VII-D) uses WinDEX-Window trajec-
tories with the simulation imitator of [7] and Inspire and Wuji
hands. Hand2Teleop (Section VII-E) uses WinDEX-Stream on
a live platform comprising a 20-joint Wuji five-finger hand,
a seven-joint robot arm and an operator-worn stereo capture
device.

D. Hand2Policy
Hand2Policy uses reconstructed human motion to learn
robot policies. We study reinforcement learning (RL) as one
implementation, using the ManipTrans trajectory imitator [7]
driven by retargeted hand-motion references. We evaluate
trajectory following and grasp–lift–place trials in simulation
and on hardware. Fig. 11 shows the experimental setup and
the five object assets. We create the object assets using GPT6-
astra and Blender, and set their physical parameters from
measurements of the corresponding real objects.
All compared guidance methods use identical training set-
tings within each hand embodiment, differing only in hand-
motion references: 2,000 iterations for the electrically driven
Wuji hand and 5,000 for the tendon-driven Inspire hand.

E. Hand2Teleop
Unlike DexPilot, which uses a four-camera external RGB-
D rig [60], and BiDex, which uses motion-capture gloves and
teacher arms [61], our system tracks the operator using only
CyberCap’s stereo cameras and onboard IMU. Hand2Teleop
maps reconstructed wrist motion and finger articulation to
coordinated arm–hand commands for live teleoperation.
On an NVIDIA RTX 4090 server, WinDEX-Stream (Sec-
tion VI-A and Fig. 7) reconstructs hands from CyberCap
streams alongside real-time VIO camera-pose estimation. VIO
poses place the hands in a world frame to reduce head-motion
jitter. Global wrist motion drives robot-arm targets through in-
verse kinematics, and the Wuji SDK maps finger articulation to
hand-joint commands. The arm and hand receive coordinated
commands at 30 Hz, matching CyberCap’s stereo capture rate,
with an approximately 33.3 ms update period.

VIII. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
We identify three directions for future improvement. First,
prolonged occlusion removes visual constraints on articula-
tion and position. Stronger temporal reasoning and scene-
interaction context are needed for persistent tracking and
recovery.
Second, training on CyberCap recordings limits device
diversity. Data spanning different optics, stereo geometries
and mounting configurations are needed to improve and assess
cross-device generalization.
Third, combining object geometry, pose and intended in-
teraction with wrist motion and articulation could improve
teleoperation precision through object-aware motion mapping.
IX. CONCLUSION
WinDEX connects scalable in-the-wild egocentric data to
metric, world-frame 3D hand motion and physical robot
control and learning. Large-scale data collection, geometric–
temporal annotation and the WinDEX-Net data–model fly-
wheel form a capture-to-reconstruction pipeline. WinDEX-
Hand combines in-the-wild training annotations with held-
out human-annotated 2D references and independent optical
motion-capture ground truth. WinDEX-D provides fast offline
inference with WinDEX-Window and causal streaming infer-
ence with WinDEX-Stream. Reconstruction, Hand2Policy and
Hand2Teleop evaluations demonstrate the recovered motion’s
utility for perception and robotic manipulation.
