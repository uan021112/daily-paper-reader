# VIEW PACT: Multi-View Geometric Augmentation and Pose–Action Co-Training for Viewpoint-Robust Manipulation 

Chenglang Tu 1,∗,‡, Feng Chen 2,∗, Ruizhe Liu 2, Pei Zhou 2, Binbin Huang 2,Chen Xu 2, Yanchao Yang 2,†, Yi Ma 2,†, Shenghua Gao 2,†

Abstract — Image policies are scalable but remain brittle to camera viewpoint changes. On LIBERO, we identify propri-oception as an input shortcut: masking robot states sharply degrades several policies, while retraining from images alone recovers much of the loss. We introduce VIEW PACT, an image policy trained with privileged geometric supervision. Auxiliary objectives recover end-effector and task-relevant object poses in the camera frame, while deployment requires only RGB observations. Joint geometric supervision improves mean simulation success from 37 .4% to 78 .4% under unseen external-camera viewpoints, with more modest gains under separately evaluated object, scene, and camera-intrinsic shifts. Across four real-robot tasks, combining simulated and real-image geometric supervision achieves 68 .1% mean success, versus 3.1% for fixed-view behavior cloning and 30 .6% for the strongest evaluated alternative. These results support geometric supervision for viewpoint robustness in the evaluated fixed-view demonstration setting. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Image-based policies are attractive because RGB observa-tions are inexpensive and abundant in robot demonstrations and large-scale video. Yet they often generalize less effec-tively to unseen camera viewpoints than policies using ex-plicit 3D representations [1], [2]. While 3D representations encode robot–object configurations in geometrically stable coordinates, image appearance varies with camera pose and intrinsics. The challenge is to introduce geometric knowl-edge without requiring 3D observations or reconstruction at deployment. Existing approaches transfer pretrained geometric features through feature fusion or representation alignment [3], [4], or broaden training coverage through demonstration gener-ation [5], [6], novel-view synthesis [7], and scene recon-struction [8]. These approaches introduce geometry through pretrained representations or augmented observation–action data. We investigate a complementary transfer strategy: using simulation to supervise task-relevant robot–object ge-ometry rather than synthetic actions, while retaining real demonstrations as the source of action supervision. We find that image-based policies can develop strong dependence on proprioceptive inputs [9] even when train-ing without those inputs achieves higher task success. On LIBERO [10] (Sec. III), test-time state masking substan-tially degrades several state-conditioned policies, whereas retraining without the ablated inputs recovers substantial

These findings motivate using robot-state information to supervise visual representations rather than directly condition action prediction. Since auxiliary robot-state prediction alone does not consistently improve control, we introduce privileged geometric supervision of both end-effector and object poses during training, explicitly targeting robot–object geometry while retaining image-only action prediction at inference. We introduce V IEW PACT, which uses end-effector and task-relevant object poses as privileged training targets rather than policy inputs. Actions are predicted solely from images, while auxiliary objectives require the visual representation to recover these poses in the camera frame, encouraging it to encode robot–object configurations and spatial relationships. Deployment requires only visual observations, without pose labels, proprioception, or simulator states. We evaluate V IEW PACT in controlled simulation and on four real-robot tasks. In simulation, we isolate joint pose supervision and interaction-aware state sampling and test robustness to viewpoint, object, scene, and camera-intrinsic shifts. Joint end-effector and object supervision raises mean success from 37 .4% to 78 .4% . On the real robot, we compare simulated geometric supervision with simulated action augmentation and novel-view synthesis, and examine zero-shot real-image pose augmentation as a complementary source. Combining simulated and real-image geometric supervision achieves 68 .1% mean success, versus 3.1% for fixed-view behavior cloning and 30 .6% 

for the strongest alternative. These results show that, in the studied low-cost fixed-view regime, simulation transfers more effectively through geometric than action supervision, while simulated and real-image geometric supervision pro-vide complementary benefits. II. RELATED WORK

B. Geometry-Grounded Learning for Manipulation 

Geometry-grounded policies represent depth or 3D ob-servations as voxels, point clouds, virtual views, or end-effector-centric features [17], [18], [2], [19], [20]. These representations promote viewpoint robustness, but their deployment-time depth requirements introduce sensing over-head and sensitivity to measurement noise. Another line transfers geometric priors through feature distillation or geometric-backbone fine-tuning [21], [22], [23], with additional computational cost. Other methods learn action-relevant representations, including semantic– geometric features, keypoints, view-consistent features, mo-tion latents, and camera-frame actions or SE(3) trajectories [24], [25], [20], [26], [27]. Learning these representations often requires multi-view or RGB-D observations, and some methods additionally rely on manual geometric annotations. VIEW PACT instead learns from simulated pose labels and automatically annotated fixed-view real images. At deploy-ment, it uses only RGB observations, without pose annota-tions or 3D sensing. 

C. Sim–Real Co-Training for Robot Manipulation 

Classical sim-to-real methods address the reality gap through visual randomization or domain adaptation [28], [29]. Recent approaches combine simulated and real action trajectories or pair simulated robot experience with real human observations [30], [31], while others align joint observation–action distributions across domains [32]. Trans-ferring simulated action supervision requires addressing discrepancies in dynamics, embodiment, and action distri-butions. Our method instead uses simulation to supervise camera-frame end-effector and object poses, rather than actions. This provides task-relevant geometric supervision without requiring simulated actions or dynamics to transfer to the physical system. III. P ROPRIOCEPTIVE DEPENDENCE AND VISUAL

These results motivate a distinction between removing a direct state-input dependency and providing effective supervision for visual control. Robot-state reconstruction supervises the robot configuration but does not explicitly supervise the manipulated objects. Our method instead re-moves proprioceptive policy inputs and jointly supervises end-effector and object poses during training, directing the visual representation toward the robot–object geometry re-quired for manipulation. SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS We design our experiments to answer the following ques-tions: (1) Does pose–action co-training improve closed-loop manipulation under unseen camera viewpoints? (2) What are the individual and joint contributions of object-pose and end-effector-pose supervision? (3) Does interaction-aware state sampling improve the geometric data-augmentation pipeline over random-state augmentation? (4) Does the learned rep-resentation remain robust under object, scene, and camera-intrinsic distribution shifts? 

A. Experimental Setup 1) Task: We conduct simulation experiments in RoboTwin 2.0 [36], which is built on the SAPIEN physics simulator. We select five tasks: Hanging Mug , Move Playing Card Away , Open Laptop , Place Empty Cup ,and Put Object in Cabinet . We adapt the original benchmark configurations for both data collection and evaluation to construct a controlled testbed for viewpoint generalization. 

2) Data: For each task, we collect 100 successful motion-planned demonstrations using the ALOHA-AgileX embod-iment. Each demonstration contains action labels and RGB observations from a fixed external camera and a wrist cam-era. Geometric data are generated using the augmentation pipeline described in Sec. IV-B. 

3) Evaluation: All methods use the same Action Chunk-ing Transformer (ACT) backbone. For each task and su-pervision setting, we select the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. 

B. Generalization to Unseen Camera Viewpoints 

We first evaluate whether geometric data augmentation and pose–action co-training improve generalization to un-seen camera viewpoints. As shown in Table III(a), supervising either object pose or end-effector pose alone yields task-dependent gains and can even degrade performance; for example, end-effector-pose supervision performs worse than the baseline on Open Laptop. In contrast, joint supervision achieves the best per-formance across all tasks. A possible explanation is that the object and end effector together capture the core semantics of manipulation: the object specifies what should be acted upon, while the end effector specifies the acting body. Table III(b) further shows that the gain from joint supervi-sion is consistent across viewpoints. The single-target vari-ants can still underperform the paired baseline at particular camera configurations, whereas the joint model matches or exceeds it across all evaluated task–view pairs and remains stronger under the most challenging views. C. Random versus Interaction-Aware Augmentation 

We compare interaction-aware state sampling with both the unaugmented baseline and a random-state variant. The two augmented variants use the same object assets, scene and camera distributions, data budget, pose supervision, and training configuration; they differ only in state selection. Random-state augmentation independently samples object states and robot joint configurations, whereas interaction-aware augmentation draws feasible states from manipulation trajectories. Our method substantially and consistently improves the success rate across tasks. In contrast, random-state aug-mentation is unstable and can even perform worse than the baseline on some tasks, demonstrating the importance of interaction-aware state sampling and joint object–end-effector supervision. 

D. Generalization to OOD Variations 

Finally, we study whether the benefit extends beyond camera-extrinsic changes to object, scene, and camera-intrinsic distribution shifts. We compare the baseline with our method. Overall, our method provides modest improvements in mean SR under all three OOD conditions, but the gains do not extend to every task. This may be due to the distribution gap between the object-augmentation data and the expert demonstrations, together with the limited capacity of the policy model. VI. (3) What are the respective roles of simulation-derived and real-image-derived geomet-ric supervision in improving policy performance across viewpoint shifts, and are their effects complementary? (4) Does geometric co-training encourage the learned policy representation to focus on task-relevant image regions?

Q2: Without a digital twin, does simulation transfer better through geometry than actions? In this non-digital-twin setting, Sim Pose Aug. achieves a higher overall mean than Sim Action Aug. ( 28 .8% vs. 17 .5% ). Although the gains vary by task, the average improvement indicates that simulation transfers more effectively through geometric supervision than through action labels. Qualitatively, when the sim-to-real object gap is small, Sim Action Aug. can transfer local primitives such as grasping but often fails at later interaction stages that are difficult to reproduce in simulation, preventing completion of the full task. 

Q3: What are the respective roles of simulation-derived and real-image-derived geometric supervision, and are their effects complementary? Despite similar overall means ( 28 .8% and 28 .1% ), the two sources exhibit different sensitivity to camera shift. Averaged over tasks, Sim Pose Aug. achieves 37 .5% on the smaller shifts (Views 1–2) and 

20 .0% on the larger shifts (Views 3–4), a drop of 17 .5

percentage points. Real Pose Aug. is stronger on the smaller shifts ( 41 .3% ) but falls to 15 .0% on the larger shifts, a larger drop of 26 .3 points. This trend suggests that simulation-derived supervision contributes broader viewpoint coverage, whereas real-image-derived supervision primarily strength-ens grounding in the real scene near the data-collection view. Notably, the two sources are strongly complementary: Real+Sim Pose Aug. reaches 78 .8% on Views 1–2 and retains 57 .5% on Views 3–4. 

Q4: Does geometric co-training focus the representation on task-relevant regions? Figure 5 compares Grad-CAM responses computed over action queries 0–9 for the baseline and V IEW PACT on matched real-robot frames from camera viewpoints unseen during training. Across all four tasks, the baseline often activates on background surfaces or other off-task regions, whereas V IEW PACT concentrates more strongly on the manipulated object, end effector, and their interaction region.
