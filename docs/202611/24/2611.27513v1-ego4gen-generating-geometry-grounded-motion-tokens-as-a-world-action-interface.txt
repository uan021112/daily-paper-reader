Abstract
We present Ego4Gen (Egocentric 4D Future Generation),
a world model for 4D future generation in robotic manipu-
lation. Given a single egocentric image and a text prompt,
Ego4Gen jointly generates temporally aligned RGB frames,
dense point maps, and dense scene flow, explicitly pre-
dicting the evolution of scene appearance, spatial struc-
ture, and motion. Built on Cosmos3-Nano, Ego4Gen ex-
tends the pretrained generative backbone through modality-
routed LoRA and asymmetric attention, using geometric
pseudo-labels from MotionCrafter to learn to predict future
geometry and motion latents. These latents can either be
decoded into explicit 3D structure and motion or mapped
to robot actions. To this end, we also introduce FlowIn-
verse, a lightweight inverse dynamics network trained in
two stages to translate predicted 4D latents into robot ac-
tions. Experiments show that these geometry and motion
latents outperform the evaluated latent-action and video-
latent baselines in out-of-distribution settings.
Closed-
loop policy evaluations further demonstrate their utility for
translating predicted 4D futures into robot control.
To-
gether, these results support geometry-grounded motion to-
kens as a world-action interface, connecting 4D future gen-
eration with robotic manipulation and suggesting a path to-
ward reduced reliance on action-labeled data.
1. Introduction
Visual world models offer a new way to learn interaction
dynamics from large-scale video data for robotic manip-
ulation. By predicting how a task may unfold, they sup-
port action planning and generation [7, 26]. However, RGB
videos encode task-relevant state changes together with ap-
pearance factors such as color, texture, lighting, and back-
ground. Even scenes with similar spatial relationships and
motions can differ substantially in pixel appearance, creat-
ing appearance shifts that a controller must accommodate.
The representation connecting future prediction to action
execution should therefore preserve control-relevant infor-
mation while reducing interference from task-irrelevant vi-
sual variation.
Recent works explore latent-space interfaces between vi-
sual priors and robot actions. Some approaches perform
planning with predicted video latent representations [1],
while others use latent action models (LAMs) that com-
press inter-frame changes into compact action representa-
tions [3, 8]. However, these compact representations may

still retain visual information unrelated to robot control.
Prior analysis shows that latent action representations can
encode exogenous noise alongside action-induced changes
[28]. This suggests that compactness alone is insufficient to
ensure robustness to appearance shifts.
Geometry offers a promising direction for reducing the
influence of task-irrelevant visual appearance. Recent stud-
ies have explored mapping predicted geometry and motion
to robot actions. Some methods use explicitly constructed
geometric representations [25, 30, 31], while others extract
features from within world models [12, 29]. The former
introduces additional computation to construct and process
explicit representations, while the latter can still be affected
by shifts in the visual input distribution. We therefore seek
an interface that contains as little task-irrelevant informa-
tion as possible, motivating our focus on geometry-motion
representations.
Our experiments show that the geometry-motion latents
of MotionCrafter’s 4D VAE [32] are well suited to this role:
they support both explicit 4D scene reconstruction and ro-
bust mappings to robot actions. Building on this finding, we
develop Ego4Gen upon Cosmos3-Nano [18]. Given a single
egocentric image and a text prompt, Ego4Gen jointly pre-
dicts future RGB video and temporally aligned geometry-
motion latents, which can be decoded into dense point maps
and dense scene flow. To connect these predictions to robot
control, we introduce FlowInverse, a lightweight inverse
dynamics network that enables closed-loop robot control
with Ego4Gen through two-stage training: learning from
real videos and adapting to predicted representations. We
further show that adapting the world model with action-
unlabeled videos can improve generalization in closed-loop
control while keeping FlowInverse fixed, highlighting the
potential to benefit from additional video data without ad-
ditional action supervision.
Overall, our contributions are as follows:
• We validate geometry-motion latents as a world-action
interface. Through comparisons with latent action and
video representations, we show that MotionCrafter’s 4D
VAE latents support both explicit 3D structure reconstruc-
tion and robot action prediction, with strong robustness
under the out-of-distribution conditions.
• We introduce Ego4Gen, a 4D world model that jointly
predicts temporally aligned future RGB frames, dense
point maps, and dense scene flow from a single egocentric
image and a text prompt.
• We introduce FlowInverse, a lightweight inverse dynam-
ics network that maps Ego4Gen’s geometry-motion la-
tents to robot actions for closed-loop control. We also
demonstrate that world-model adaptation with action-
unlabeled videos can improve downstream policy perfor-
mance, suggesting a way to reduce reliance on action-
labeled data.
2. Related Works
2.1. 4D World Action Models
Visual world action models have made substantial progress
in transferring knowledge from large-scale video data to
robotic manipulation by combining visual generation and
action modeling [18, 20]. Building on these advances, re-
cent work incorporates geometry and motion to better sup-
port robot control. Some methods jointly model depth and
other geometric modalities to provide explicit spatial infor-
mation [9, 30], while others introduce motion cues through
optical flow prediction [29] or action flow conditioning
[12].
We seek to introduce more explicit temporal and motion
representations into world-model outputs. However, exist-
ing work represents 4D scene dynamics in different ways:
some methods use dynamic point maps to describe evolving
3D geometry [15, 17], while others represent motion in the
image plane through optical flow or action flow [12, 29].
We adopt dense 3D scene flow [16, 32], which directly de-
scribes the displacement of scene points in 3D space and
provides an intuitive physical description of motion.
2.2. World-Action Interface
The world-action interface which connects world model
prediction to action execution is central to world action
models. Existing approaches employ several forms of cou-
pling.
Some jointly model video and actions within a
unified architecture [18, 20], while others extract internal
world-model features for action prediction [12, 29]. Hier-
archical approaches first predict the future and then trans-
late the predictions into actions using a separate controller
[7, 30].
Research on world-action interfaces also explores repre-
sentations suited to action prediction. LAMs learn action
representations by compressing changes between frames,
providing a bridge to learning from videos without action
labels [3, 8]. However, these representations can also en-
code visual changes unrelated to control [28]. Other meth-
ods connect future generation to robot control through ex-
plicit object motion predictions [25], involving the unnec-
essary generation, processing, and subsequent encoding of
motion representations.
We use geometry-motion latents from a pretrained 4D
VAE [32] as the world-action interface. We use a genera-
tive model to predict future latents and a lightweight inverse
dynamics network to convert them into robot actions. This
interface preserves the ability to reconstruct explicit 3D ge-
ometry and motion while directly supporting robot control
in latent space.

We choose Cosmos3-Nano [18] as the pretrained back-

vision, we retain the latent representation produced by the
Wan2.2 VAE [22]. For geometry and motion, we adopt the
latent representations provided by MotionCrafter.
3.2. Unified Multimodal Generation
As shown in Fig. 2, we adapt the MoT architecture of
Cosmos3-Nano to the task defined in Sec. 3.1. We retain
its text reasoner and image-to-video (I2V) generation path-
way, while disabling audio and action generation. We then
freeze the pretrained backbone weights and introduce ge-
ometry and motion branches to generate dense point maps
and scene flow, respectively. The three continuous modal-
ities share the pretrained backbone, use modality-routed
LoRA for modality-specific adaptation, and are jointly de-
noised with asymmetric attention.
3.2.1. Spatiotemporal Alignment
During training, the text prompt is tokenized and processed
by the frozen reasoner. RGB frames are encoded by the
Wan2.2 VAE, while geometry and motion latent targets are
obtained from the downsampled frames using a frozen Mo-
tionCrafter predictor. We duplicate the initial frame I0 as
the first frame of the prediction target and use its timestamp
as the start of the four-second prediction horizon. This al-
lows the model to predict geometry and motion for the con-
ditioning frame as well, yielding a complete 4D representa-
tion from the initial timestamp onward.
For the vision modality, the conditioning frame I0 oc-
cupies a separate temporal slot in the latent space, while
the future frames supervised during training are temporally
compressed by a factor of four following Wan2.2, result-


ing in 25 vision slots in total. MotionCrafter’s geometry
and motion latents have no temporal compression and use
a different spatial compression factor. We therefore apply
modality-specific spatiotemporal patchification to the ge-
ometry and motion latents to align the three latent token
spaces. This results in 25 vision slots and 24 slots for each
of the geometry and motion modalities, with spatial tokens
of the same shape in every slot.

3.3. FlowInverse: Latent-to-Action Translation
To convert the predicted geometry-motion latent represen-
tation into robot control commands, we introduce FlowIn-
verse, a lightweight inverse dynamics network. Given the
current robot states and the future geometry-motion latents,
FlowInverse directly predicts the corresponding robot ac-
tion increments.
As illustrated in the lower-right of Fig. 2, we process
the geometry and motion latents with two separate con-
volutional encoders and add spatiotemporal positional en-
codings and modality embeddings to the resulting tokens.
We then use action queries to perform cross-attention over
tokens from the two latent streams and the proprioceptive
state. This design allows the action queries to incorporate
information from the robot’s proprioceptive state and selec-
tively read action-relevant features from the spatially struc-
tured geometry representation.
For output supervision, we use the joint-action incre-
ments and gripper states along the future trajectory as the
primary supervision signals, while using end-effector dis-
placement prediction as an auxiliary supervision signal.
The network is trained with a weighted MSE regression
loss:

3.4. Datasets & Training
During Ego4Gen pretraining, we use EgoDex [11], Ag-
iBot [21], and clean-task trajectories from the ALOHA-
AgileX robot in RoboTwin 2.0 [4]. All data are organized
into four-second training windows. We use modality-routed
LoRA adapters with (r, α) = (32, 64) for the vision branch
and (r, α) = (64, 128) for both the geometry and motion
branches.
We train the model on 8 NVIDIA RTX PRO
6000 GPUs with a global batch size of 24 for 40K opti-
mizer steps, using a warmup-stable-decay (WSD) learning-
rate schedule.
FlowInverse is trained in two stages. In the first stage,
we pretrain it on the 50 clean tasks in RoboTwin 2.0, where
geometry-motion latents are extracted from expert videos
using a frozen MotionCrafter model. In the second stage,
we fine-tune a separate model for each target task using
paired data consisting of Ego4Gen generated geometry-
motion latents and the corresponding action trajectories.
Ego4Gen remains frozen throughout FlowInverse training.
Both stages use clean-scene data, while randomized scenes
are reserved for evaluation.

4.3. Closed-Loop Robot Control
To validate Ego4Gen’s closed-loop control capabilities, we
conduct experiments on RoboTwin 2.0 using the ALOHA-
AgileX embodiment. We first post-train Ego4Gen for 10k
steps on clean demonstrations of all 50 tasks. The post-
trained generator then produces geometry-motion latents
for clean demonstrations, which are paired with the corre-
sponding action trajectories for the second-stage training of
FlowInverse. During execution, Ego4Gen generates future
latents from the current head-camera observation every 40
control steps. FlowInverse then uses these latents to predict
actions. We evaluate Ego4Gen on five tasks under the Easy

5. Conclusion
We present Ego4Gen, which generates geometry-motion
latents as an interface between world prediction and ac-
tion execution. Given a single egocentric image and a text
prompt, Ego4Gen jointly generates temporally aligned fu-
ture RGB video, dense point maps, and dense scene flow.
Together with FlowInverse, a lightweight inverse dynamics
network, it enables closed-loop robot control. Experiments
validate the utility of geometry-motion representations for
both explicit 4D scene reconstruction and robot action pre-
diction, as well as their robustness in out-of-distribution set-
tings. We further show that adapting the world model with
videos without action labels improves the mean success rate
in randomized scenes while keeping FlowInverse weights
fixed. These results support decoupled training of future
generation and action mapping through a geometry-motion
interface, providing a path to improving downstream con-
trol with more action-unlabeled videos and reducing re-
liance on action-labeled data.
