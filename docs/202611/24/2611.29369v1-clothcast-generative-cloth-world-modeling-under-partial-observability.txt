## Abstract 

World models support robotic manipulation by predicting how the scene evolves under a planned action. For gar-ments this prediction is notoriously difficult: deformations are complex, and self-occlusion leaves much of the cloth unobserved. Planned gripper trajectories provide a useful motion prior because cloth held by a gripper moves with it, while the remaining deformation must be predicted. We introduce ClothCast, a world model that applies this prior to the cloth’s points and learns the residual motion. ClothCast takes one RGB-D observation, the camera pose, the robot’s own model and the planned gripper trajectories, with no cloth mesh or material parameters. It lifts the observation to a layered point set that includes hypothesized occluded layers, carries the points with a blended rigid prior derived from the gripper poses, and predicts the residual motion with a video-diffusion point-flow model. Over entire 4- to 11-second episodes at 10 Hz, the moved points are rendered into depth, from which a geometry-conditioned completion model synthesizes RGB; after release, a hand-designed settling step replaces the learned motion. We further release Cloth-CastBench, 10 ,000 simulated dual-arm cloth-manipulation episodes with the ground-truth cloth mesh, including oc-cluded layers, at every frame. On 492 held-out episodes, ClothCast achieves 2.5 × lower sequence-averaged visible-surface Chamfer distance than Flex-π, the strongest baseline in geometry (14.0 mm vs. 35.6 mm), and 3.7 dB higher cloth-region PSNR than Cosmos-Predict2.5, the strongest baseline in cloth PSNR. On a 50-episode ablation subset, the rigid prior alone, applied to ClothCast’s learned initial geome-try with the same post-processing, already outperforms both geometry-capable baselines, and the learned residual lowers its error by about a third. 

## 1. Introduction 

World models let a robot evaluate a planned action before executing it, by predicting how the scene will evolve. For garment manipulation this prediction is hard: a garment has no single pose but a wide range of folds, wrinkles, and lay-ered self-contact [19, 28], and a single RGB-D image shows only part of it [15]. We study forecasting the RGB-D obser-vations of a cloth item throughout a dual-arm manipulation episode lasting several seconds, from the state observable at the start (one RGB-D observation, the camera pose and the robot’s own model) and the planned gripper trajectories, without a supplied cloth mesh or material parameters. Our starting observation is that the action is more than a control signal: it constrains where the grasped cloth will be. Cloth attached to a gripper moves with it, which gives a motion prior and leaves only the remaining deformation to be predicted. Geometry-capable world models such as Flex-

π [27] and DepthWorld [2] receive the action as a vector without explicit alignment to image pixels or cloth points; others ground it in the camera view [12, 23] or as 3D robot point flows [9], but still learn the resulting scene motion. Many cloth dynamics models apply grasp motion to the cloth state directly, but need a category-specific reconstruc-tion [10] or, when physics-based, explicit geometry and material parameters [33]. We apply a blended rigid prior directly to an estimated cloth point set: on our 50-episode ablation subset, this prior, with ClothCast’s learned initial ge-ometry and the same post-processing but no learned motion, already gives a lower geometry error than both geometry-capable world models (21.2 mm against 34.2 and 67.2 mm; Sec. 5.4). We build ClothCast on this observation. It represents the cloth as a set of 3D points and expresses the planned action as their motion: the blended rigid prior moves the points with the gripper poses, and a video-diffusion backbone built on the Ctrl-World architecture [6] predicts only the residual, how the cloth departs from this prior, as dense flow fields in image-space canvases, with the gripper trajectories also rendered as spatial channels. ## 2. Related Work 

Video world models predict scenes from images, and cloth dynamics models from an explicit cloth state; Tab. 1 com-pares both on how the action enters and what each must know about the cloth at the start. 

Action-conditioned world models. World models fore-cast the consequences of actions so that plans can be eval-uated before execution [5, 7], for fabric as RGB-D video in VisuoSpatial Foresight [8]. Ctrl-World [6] adds frame-level action conditioning to Stable Video Diffusion [3], Cos-mos [17] generates video, and V-JEPA 2-AC [1] predicts the features of a self-supervised video encoder, while Depth-World [2], Flex-π [27] and TesserAct [32] add depth, point maps or normals of the visible surface, aligned with the image. 

How the action enters. These models take the action as a vector per frame or clip, as we also condition Flex-π, or, for TesserAct, as text, without alignment to pixels or scene points. EnerVerse-AC [12] and MTV-World [23] project the end-effector pose or trajectory into the camera view, and PointWorld [9] represents the gripper motion as 3D point flows and regresses the displacement of the observed scene points in one-second chunks. ## 3. Method 

3.1. Setting and Overview 

Setting. At inference, ClothCast predicts the RGB-D ob-servations of a whole episode from only the state observable at frame 0 (time t0) and the plan: one 256 × 256 RGB-D observation with the camera calibration, the robot’s own model (kinematics and meshes), and the planned gripper trajectories of both arms, a pose and a binary closed flag per frame at 10 Hz. The camera pose is known when the camera is mounted on a tracked rig or on the robot, and can otherwise be recovered from the t0 frame, as we show in simulation (Sec. 5.4). The calibration and the robot model serve only geometry, including a robot mask for every frame rendered from the meshes posed by inverse kinematics on the planned trajectories; at t0, the pixels where the observed depth lies in front of the rendered arm are removed from it. Nothing else about the cloth is given: no mesh, template, category or material. 

Overview. The cloth state is a set of 3D points: a rigid prior moves them with the grippers that hold the cloth (Eq. (1)), and Stage 2 learns the residual. Since Stage 2 never creates points, Stage 1 lifts the observation to a layered point set that already contains the occluded cloth; Stage 3 renders the moved points to depth and RGB. Only the three diffusion models are learned, not the prior, the settling after release or the depth rendering. 

Shared architecture. The three stages share one architec-ture and are trained separately on ClothCastBench (Sec. 4). Every map a stage reads or writes, except Stage 2’s gripper heat maps, which enter at latent resolution, is a 256 × 256 

image in the camera view, encoded by the frozen Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) [3] VAE into a 4 × 32 × 32 latent tile; as in DepthWorld [2], tiles are placed side by side to form each stage’s canvas. The denoiser is the SVD UNet as extended by Ctrl-World [6], with history-frame and frame-level conditioning and an action encoder. Stages 1 and 3 feed the action encoder a constant and start from the SVD weights; Stage 2, the only stage in which the action input varies, starts from DepthWorld’s released UNet. 

3.2. Stage 1: Layered Geometry at t0

Stage 1 estimates the cloth geometry at t0 from the single RGB-D observation as a layered depth image [20], a repre-sentation previously predicted from a single view for view synthesis [21, 25]: for each pixel, the depths of up to four cloth surfaces that its camera ray crosses. The conditioning canvas holds only the RGB image, the scene depth and a second copy of the RGB, matching the three generated tiles, and the history frame holds the same observation; no mask or other label enters the generator. It generates three tiles: the depth of the surface layer, the cloth surface nearest to the camera; a gap tile whose three channels give the depth off-sets of up to three hidden layers, each beneath the one above it; and a visible-cloth mask. Decoding. At decode time the residual is added back to the prior and applied to the points. Because cloth held by a closed gripper moves with it, the surface-layer flow is also 4pulled toward the prior in a small neighborhood of each holding gripper: at every frame in which the arm holds, a pixel at distance d (in pixels) from the gripper’s position in the image at the window’s first frame takes (1 − w) times the predicted flow plus w times the prior, with the Gaussian weight w = exp( −d2/32) , which is 1 at the gripper and below 0.15 beyond 8 pixels. Post-release settling. After the last frame in which any arm holds the cloth, the cloth falls with no planned motion to follow and the learned motion is less accurate (Sec. 5.4), so we replace it by settling the point set onto the table, whose height is read from the observed t0 depth around the pre-dicted cloth mask, outside the robot mask. From the release frame on, every point keeps its horizontal position, and the points of each 8-mm cell of a horizontal grid are stacked, in their height order at release, over a height proportional to their number and to h0, the cloth’s median height above the table at t0, so denser cells pile higher, as folded cloth does; each point’s height relaxes exponentially toward its target, with a time constant of 0.15 s. 

3.4. Stage 3: RGB-D Completion 

Depth rendering. Projected directly into a depth image, the predicted points leave holes where the cloth has stretched, and isolated depth outliers. A gap fill before projection inserts points, on pixels left empty, along stretched links between neighboring points of the frame-0 point set (supple-ment); after projection, which keeps the nearest depth per pixel, a depth repair keeps the main connected cloth region, replaces depth spikes and fills the remaining holes inside it. The depth repair and the settling of Sec. 3.3 form the post-processing that Sec. 5.4 ablates. The result is the predicted depth sequence, and the same surface, unprojected, is the cloth geometry we evaluate. 

Depth-to-RGB synthesis. The completion network syn-thesizes the RGB frames and sees the plan only through its conditioning. Its conditioning canvas is a single tile per frame: the predicted depth of the cloth surface alone, log-encoded over 0.2–1.5 m, with the robot mask of Sec. 3.1 painted in a fixed color. Rollouts use windows of one con-text frame and four predicted frames, each conditioned on its own canvas; the first window’s context is the observed RGB frame at t0, and every later window takes its context from the model’s own last predicted frame. During training, the canvases are built from the ground-truth cloth surface depth with the dataset’s robot masks painted in. 

## 4. ClothCastBench 

ClothCastBench provides the geometry of occluded cloth and the motion of every cloth point for 10,000 simulated episodes in which two Franka Panda arms manipulate one cloth item, 2,500 for each of four classes: square sheets and procedurally generated t-shirts, long-sleeve shirts and pants. The cloth is simulated as a mass-spring system with self-collision and table friction, using GPU kernels adapted from PhysTwin [11], and rendered with Blender’s EEVEE engine; each item has one of ten fabric classes, which set its stiffness, mass, friction and texture. Every episode starts from a crum-pled state and runs one of three scripted, open-loop dual-arm programs on two exposed grasp points: an inward page fold, with the arms synchronized or staggered (4,000 and 2,000 episodes), or a lift–swivel–carry–drop (4,000); each closed gripper attaches the cloth vertices within 3 cm of it by virtual springs. A fixed pseudo-random split by episode holds out 516 test episodes and keeps 9,484 for training. Construction details are in the supplement. Each episode provides 256 ×256 RGB-D frames at 10 Hz from a calibrated camera, fixed within an episode and placed differently in each, together with visible-cloth and robot masks. The simulator adds, at every frame, the ground-truth cloth mesh and a layered depth decomposition of the camera view: at each pixel, the depth of the nearest cloth surface, including cloth hidden behind the robot, and of up to five cloth layers beneath it, each carrying the mesh face index and barycentric coordinates of the surface point it images. A pixel on any layer of any frame therefore identifies a fixed point on the cloth, whose ground-truth position is known at every later frame. Each episode also records, per frame and per arm, the gripper pose and a binary closed flag, and a 14-D action of per-arm increments in gripper translation, rotation and closure. Episodes are replayed kinematically, so the planned robot state is also the achieved one, and the action stream holds only gripper states; the cloth enters it only through the grasp points, which the scripted programs 5choose on exposed cloth. The arms are posed by inverse kinematics on the gripper trajectories, and arm–cloth contact is not simulated: the robot acts on the cloth only through the grasp springs, so another arm reaching the same gripper poses can be rendered in its place. The cloth mesh, the layered depth, the visible-cloth mask, and all frames after t0

are used only for training and evaluation. 

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Setup 

Test set and protocol. We evaluate on the 516 held-out test episodes, which span the four classes and last 44–110 frames. Cosmos-Predict2.5 produced no output for 24 of them, so all comparisons use the remaining 492 episodes. The split is by episode, and no test episode is used to train any of the compared models. Each method starts from the observation at t0 (RGB-D for ClothCast, Flex-π and DepthWorld, RGB otherwise) and rolls out the whole episode autoregressively, conditioned on the planned gripper trajectory: the baselines receive it as the benchmark’s 14-D actions, with the initial gripper state for Flex-π and the gripper state at every frame for V-JEPA 2-AC, and ClothCast as gripper poses and grasp states. None of the baselines receives the camera pose or the robot model, and only Flex-π receives the camera intrinsics, which all episodes share. 

Inputs. The robot’s kinematics give Stage 3 the arm sil-houette along the planned trajectory (Sec. 3.1), whereas the baselines must generate the arm themselves. Stage 2 receives neither the robot mask nor the robot model at any frame, only the planned gripper trajectory, which the camera pose places in camera axes; the mask enters the geometry only at t0,where it keeps arm pixels out of the observed cloth depth and the table-plane estimate. We therefore compare on the cloth. The geometry metric and cloth PSNR exclude the robot pixels, and cloth SSIM windows reach them only at the region boundary; the cloth LPIPS crop is on average 22% robot, and pasting the true arm into ClothCast’s frames lowers its cloth LPIPS by only about 0.005; we did not test how much of the baselines’ cloth LPIPS comes from the arm they must generate. The full-frame metrics are not a like-for-like comparison, since the arm fills 19% of the frame and ClothCast is given its silhouette; the geometric and RGB cloth metrics also favor ClothCast wherever a baseline draws the arm over cloth. 

Metrics. For geometry, we measure the Chamfer distance on the visible cloth surface: in each predicted frame, the ground-truth visible-cloth pixels, excluding the robot, eroded twice with a 3 × 3 kernel and restricted to valid ground-truth depth, are back-projected from the prediction and from the ground truth, and the Chamfer distance is the mean of the two directed mean nearest-neighbor distances, in millimeters, without subsampling or alignment. Predicted cloth outside these pixels is not scored, so the metric does not penalize cloth predicted where the ground truth shows none. An evaluation pixel left without a predicted depth is not filled: its ground-truth point is matched to the nearest predicted point elsewhere on the surface, so every uncovered pixel adds its distance to the predicted surface. We report the coverage, the share of evaluation pixels with a predicted depth; the baselines predict every pixel. For RGB, we report PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS (VGG) [31] on the full frame and on the same cloth region, where SSIM is averaged over the region and LPIPS is computed on a crop around it. Every metric is averaged over the frames of an episode, then over the episodes of a garment class, then over the four classes, excluding the observation at t0.

Baselines. We compare against four action-conditioned world models, each scored on its own output without our depth repair or settling. Flex-π [27] and DepthWorld [2] predict RGB and per-pixel geometry, so they enter both com-parisons; Cosmos-Predict2.5 [17] (Cosmos in Tab. 3) and V-JEPA 2-AC [1] predict RGB only, V-JEPA 2-AC through an RGB decoder trained on the benchmark. Each baseline starts from its released checkpoint and is adapted to the 9,484 training episodes. Every 500 updates, a checkpoint is scored on a fixed probe of 20 training episodes, by cloth Chamfer distance for Flex-π and DepthWorld and by cloth LPIPS for Cosmos and the V-JEPA 2-AC predictor; train-ing was stopped manually once these scores had leveled off, and the V-JEPA 2-AC decoder was trained for the full 10 epochs. The supplement gives the probe protocol, every probe score and the training details. No test episode is used for training or checkpoint selection. MEDOR [10] predicts pick-and-place outcomes rather than observations along a trajectory, and UniClothDiff [24] needs a garment template and a history of estimated states, so we do not compare against either. PointWorld [9] shares our inputs but forecasts only the observed points, in 1-second chunks and without images; we do not retrain it on ClothCastBench, and drop-ping our hidden-layer points (Sec. 5.3) only approximates its restriction to observed points.

Geometry. 5.5. Real-robot evaluation 

Our real data has 418 episodes in which two WidowX AI arms carry, drag, lift, pull apart or swing seven items (two gingham cloths, a towel, a polo shirt, a t-shirt and two pil-low covers), recorded as 256 × 256 RGB-D at 10 Hz by a RealSense D455 with the camera calibration, the measured gripper trajectory and approximate masks of the visible cloth (grown from color) and of the robot (rendered from the mea-sured joints), but no cloth mesh. We hold out 84 random episodes for testing (Fig. 3); the same items appear in the 334 training episodes. Stage 2 is used as trained in simulation, and Stage 1 is fine-tuned only on simulated training episodes re-rendered with WidowX arms, so neither geometry stage is trained on a real image. Stage 3 is fine-tuned for three epochs on the real training episodes, conditioned on the sensor depth of the visible cloth and the robot mask. Two inference settings differ from simulation: the forecasts are conditioned on the executed trajectory, measured rather than commanded, and the reference height h0 of settling is fixed at 20 mm, chosen on a training episode on which the value computed from the Stage-1 points (44 mm) stacked the released cloth up to 0.36 m high. As in simulation, the future robot masks given to Stage 3 are rendered from the joints alone, without the sensor depth. Against the sensor depth, with the Chamfer distance of Sec. 5.1 averaged per item and then over the seven items, the predicted visible surface of the 84 test episodes has an error of 5.6 mm at 0.1 s, 32.6 mm at 1 s, 34.7 mm at the last frame and 35.3 mm over all frames, at a coverage of 0.66; the cloth PSNR is 14.6 dB and the cloth LPIPS 0.137. The rigid prior alone (Sec. 5.4), with the same Stage-1 points and post-processing, reaches 5.0, 54.5, 30.8 and 44.9 mm at the same horizons, at a coverage of 0.67. Over all frames, the learned residual thus lowers the error by 9.6 mm; it helps while the cloth is moved and is worse than the prior at the first and the last frame. The error is lower on the items closest to the benchmark’s classes, flat cloths and shirts: 30.2 mm over all frames on the 64 episodes of the gingham cloths, the towel and the shirts, against 48.1 mm on the two pillow covers, which are sewn two-layer sleeves and whose test episodes are mostly carries. Per-horizon and per-item results are in the supplement. 

## 6. Limitations 

ClothCastBench is a preliminary benchmark, not adeployment-ready one. It covers three scripted dual-arm programs, in which the robot acts on the cloth only through grasp springs, and needs more cloth types, motions, fab-rics and real data. On real data, the error is highest on the two pillow covers, whose test episodes are mostly carries, and carries have the highest error for every item; the real 8RGB forecasts use a completion stage fine-tuned on footage of the same items. The four modeled layers leave 13% of the labeled layer pixels at t0 unrepresented, and the hidden-layer points come to the front in about the right amount but not reliably where hidden cloth is revealed: at any given horizon, they show at revealed and at previously visible pix-els at similar rates. Settling, gap fill and depth repair are hand-designed (Sec. 5.4), and points drift along the surface over long horizons. Finally, each forecast is a single sample that takes minutes per episode, so planning with ClothCast, which needs several samples per candidate action sequence, is left to future work.

## A. ClothCastBench construction 

This section completes Sec. 4; every value is read from the generator’s code and the released episode files. 

Items. Sheets are 31 × 31 -vertex grids with 1 cm spacing. T-shirts (short sleeves), shirts (long sleeves, a collar band and an overlapping front placket) and pants (two legs) consist of masked quad-grid panels with 1 cm spacing, whose front panels are stitched to the back panel along the outline ex-cept at the openings. Their dimensions are drawn uniformly from ranges of real garments and then halved; before halv-ing, t-shirts have a body 34–44 cm wide and 45–58 cm long with 10–16 cm sleeves, shirts a body 38–46 cm wide and 58–70 cm long with 48–56 cm sleeves, and pants a 32–40 cm waist and a length of 60–74 cm. Every panel has structural, shear and two-hop bending springs. 

Simulation. The mass-spring kernels, adapted from Phys-Twin [11], integrate 167 substeps per 30 Hz frame ( ∆t ≈

0.2 ms) with dashpot and drag damping. Cloth–cloth and cloth–table contacts are inelastic, with the fabric class’s Coulomb friction; the cloth–cloth contact distance stands in for ply thickness, and self-collision ignores vertex pairs that are close in the rest shape. Table S1 lists the ten fabric classes. Stiffnesses are log stiffness-to-mass ratios in the simulator’s units, the log dashpot damping is drawn from 3.5–4.8 for every class, and the mass is scaled by the item’s areal density relative to 150 g/m 2, clamped to [0 .4, 3.5] . The class names indicate which fabrics the ranges imitate; the values are not calibrated material constants, and masses are not in SI units. 

Grasps. The cloth vertices “within 3 cm” of a closed grip-per (Sec. 4) are those inside a vertical cylinder of 3 cm radius around the grasp point, reaching 2.5 cm below it. Control springs attach the gripper to the connected patch that con-tains the grasp point and to the patches of lower layers inside the cylinder; a lower patch is released once its pull exceeds a friction limit. The two cylinders may not share vertices, and an opened gripper does not grasp again. 

Initial states. The initial states are the starting states of an earlier dataset generated with the same simulator and garment generator, in which each flat item was folded by page-turn arcs about a crease and crumpled by lifting it at one point and releasing it in the air; shirts begin with their sleeves folded across the body. They are used with zero velocity. In plastic items (47% of the episodes), bending springs that were compressed below 75% of their rest length during this preparation keep the compressed length, so the creases persist; shirts always keep the creases of their folded sleeves. A state qualifies if, relative to the flat item, its top-view footprint is at most 0.6 times as large and its overlap factor, the summed top-view area of its triangles divided by the footprint, at least 1.35 times as large; if the area-weighted dispersion of its normals (one minus the largest eigenvalue of their second-moment matrix) is at least 0.12; if at least 12% of its area is tilted by more than 45 ◦; and if the spread between the 5th and 95th percentiles of its area-weighted height is at least 5% of the square root of the flat footprint. 

Grasp selection and programs. Grasp candidates are the vertices visible from the earlier dataset’s camera in the initial state; on shirts, only the interior of the back torso. A pair must be at least 12 cm apart and at least max(18 cm , d ) apart in the flat item, where d is its current distance; pairs far apart horizontally are preferred. Each program starts 0.2– 0.5 s into the episode. Along a page-fold arc with stroke

Arms. The Panda bases stand 1.2 m apart on opposite sides of the table, facing each other. Collisions within an arm and between the arms and the cloth are not checked; the arms do not act on the cloth in the simulation. 

Rendering. Blender 4.0.2 renders every third 30 Hz simu-lation state, giving 10 Hz, with EEVEE at 24 samples, ambi-ent occlusion and soft shadows, under the same three disk area lights and dim world light in every episode. Each fab-ric class has one CC0 physically based texture set (color, roughness and normal maps) from the ambientCG library. The intrinsics are fixed (focal length 351.7 px, 40 ◦ vertical field of view). The 18 candidate viewpoints combine the two sides of the grasp pair, three azimuth offsets along it and elevations of 55, 65 and 75 ◦, which are selected for 8,358, 1,337 and 305 episodes. Each candidate is scored by the projected cloth area it sees after ray tests against the arms and the cloth, summed at the start, at 40% and at 85% of the episode. 

Labels and validation. Along each pixel’s ray, the first six cloth surfaces are labeled, and deeper ones are dropped. Every released episode passes the generator’s validation of its arrays, which checks, among others, the ordering and sentinels of the layer labels, that each face–barycentric la-bel reprojects to its pixel (largest error 4.2 × 10 −7 px) and to its depth ( 6.0 × 10 −8 m), that the robot mask excludes the cloth, and that the actions equal the differences of the recorded gripper states (largest error 6.0×10 −8). An episode is re-planned with a new attempt when planning fails, the simulator’s stability guard trips, the cloth leaves the rendered table, or an export stage fails repeatedly; 1,129 of the re-leased episodes come from a second or later attempt. No episode is selected for its task outcome. 

Split. The test episodes are those whose name has an MD5 hash whose first eight hexadecimal digits, read as an integer, are divisible by 20: 516 episodes, namely 127 sheets, 134 t-shirts, 125 shirts and 130 pants, or 203 synchronized and 105 staggered page folds and 208 lift–swivel–carry–drops.

## C. Metrics 

Geometry. ClothCast and the baselines are scored with the same pixel selection and Chamfer computation. In each predicted frame, the evaluation pixels are the ground-truth visible-cloth pixels outside the robot mask, eroded twice with a 3 × 3 kernel and restricted to valid ground-truth depth, and both depth maps are back-projected at these pixels into camera coordinates. Flex-π and DepthWorld predict geom-etry at every pixel. A pixel at which ClothCast predicts no depth has no predicted point, while its ground-truth point is matched to the nearest predicted point elsewhere. The 6 of 29,759 predicted frames in which ClothCast covers no evaluation pixel have no Chamfer distance and count only in the coverage. 

RGB. All methods are scored with the same implementa-tion of PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS (VGG) [31]. Cloth PSNR excludes the robot pixels; cloth SSIM is averaged over the cloth region, and its windows reach robot pixels only at the region boundary, which gives them 0.2% of the window weight on average; cloth LPIPS is computed on a box around the cloth region, which is on average 22% robot and 23% cloth. Pasting the true arm into ClothCast’s predicted frames lowers its cloth LPIPS by 0.005 and raises its cloth SSIM by 0.003. 

Aggregation. Every metric is averaged over the predicted frames of an episode, excluding t0, then over the episodes of a class, then over the four classes. The horizons of 1 to 4 s are the 10th to 40th frames after t0, and Last is each episode’s final frame.

## F. Real-robot evaluation 

Data. The 418 real episodes show two WidowX AI arms carrying, dragging, lifting, pulling apart or swinging seven items: two gingham cloths, a towel, a polo shirt, a t-shirt and two pillow covers. Each episode provides 256 × 256 RGB-D frames at 10 Hz from a RealSense D455 camera, the camera calibration, the measured gripper poses and jaw states, and approximate masks of the visible cloth (grown from color) and of the robot (rendered from the measured joints, without the pixels where the sensor depth is at least 2 cm closer than the arm); there is no cloth mesh, so the hidden layers have no ground truth. The forecast starts at the frame at which the grasp is established. The data has no predefined split, so we draw 84 test episodes at random and keep the other 334 for training; the same seven items appear in both. 

Adaptation and inputs. Stage 2 is used as trained in sim-ulation. Stage 1 is fine-tuned from its simulation weights for one epoch on simulated training episodes re-rendered with WidowX arms (Tab. S2), so neither geometry stage is trained on a real image. Stage 3 is fine-tuned from its simu-lation weights for three epochs on clips of the real training episodes, conditioned on the sensor depth of the visible cloth, with sensor holes filled by the nearest valid depth and the dataset’s robot mask painted in, and supervised with the real images; at test time, it receives the predicted cloth depth and, at future frames, robot masks rendered from the joints with the WidowX meshes alone, without the sensor depth. ClothCast receives the measured gripper trajectory, normal-ized and clipped with the same bounds as in simulation, and settling uses a fixed h0 = 20 mm (Sec. 5.5). 

Metrics. The geometry is scored with the Chamfer dis-tance and coverage of Sec. 5.1 against the D455 depth, on evaluation pixels taken from the dataset’s approximate visible-cloth and robot masks; frames are averaged per episode, episodes per item, and then over the seven items. The sensor depth is noisy and the masks are approximate, so these errors are not directly comparable with the simulated ones.
