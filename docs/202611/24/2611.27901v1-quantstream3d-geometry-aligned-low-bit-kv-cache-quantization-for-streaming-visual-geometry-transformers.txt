## Abstract 

Streaming visual geometry transformers retain historical key–value (KV) states as cross-view geometric memory, but the growing cache increasingly limits long-sequence infer-ence. Low-bit quantization reduces its storage cost while altering both the historical evidence and the geometric com-putation that consumes it. We systematically character-ize KV quantization in streaming geometry and uncover two linked challenges: Nonuniform Geometric Sensitivity and Cache–Geometry Mismatch. Geometric sensitivity to KV quantization varies across precision choices and cache locations, while cross-view retrieval perturbations alter geometry-head inputs and propagate through subsequent predictions and KV writes. Guided by these findings, we propose QuantStream3D, to our knowledge the first dedi-cated KV quantization framework for streaming visual ge-ometry transformers. Geometry-Guided Mixed-Precision KV Quantization selectively protects sensitive cross-view ev-idence, while Geometry-Aligned Quantization-Aware Distil-lation jointly adapts transformer blocks and geometry heads

1arXiv:2611.27901v1 [cs.CV] 20 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Feed-forward models now jointly recover camera parameters, depth, and scene structure from images, as exemplified by the Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer (VGGT) [24]. Streaming models such as StreamVGGT and STream3R ex-tend this capability to online inference by reusing historical key–value (KV) states as new views arrive [9, 36]. This geometric memory carries cross-view evidence into later predictions, while its growing storage and retrieval costs constrain long-sequence inference and concurrency. Recent efforts address this bottleneck through cache pruning, with InfiniteVGGT and FrameVGGT retaining a compact subset of historical states [30, 32]. Quantization offers a comple-mentary route by reducing the bit width of retained KV states, an approach widely explored in large language mod-els (LLMs) [7, 12, 20]. Although XStreamVGGT explores combining pruning with quantization, it focuses primarily on 4-bit KV quantization using LLM-oriented methods [21]. How lower-bit history affects cross-view geometric compu-tation remains insufficiently understood, hindering accurate and efficient KV quantization for streaming geometry. In this work, we systematically characterize KV quan-tization across two streaming geometry backbones. Our study spans quantization granularity and bit width, sensitiv-ity across layers, frames, and special tokens, and represen-tation transforms, revealing two linked challenges. Nonuni-form Geometric Sensitivity spans representation choices and cache locations: K is not uniformly more sensitive than V, challenging LLM-derived key-priority assumptions [6, 20]. Global attention in layer 14 also forms a shared sensitivity peak across both backbones. Cache–Geometry Mismatch 

arises when quantized cross-view retrieval alters the features supplied to geometry heads trained with native-precision history. Because streaming inference repeatedly consumes and updates this memory, these perturbations can propagate through later queries, KV writes, camera states, and geomet-ric predictions. Together, these findings motivate selective protection of geometric evidence and joint adaptation of history generation, retrieval, and geometric prediction. We propose QuantStream3D (Figure 1) to preserve sen-sitive geometric memory and align the computation that consumes it. Guided by this analysis, we develop Geometry-Guided Mixed-Precision KV Quantization , combining per-channel K/V quantization, selective 2/4-bit precision alloca-tion, and paired Q/K rotation to protect sensitive cross-view evidence. To align geometric computation with this quan-tized history, we introduce Geometry-Aligned Quantization-Aware Distillation (QAD), jointly adapting transformer blocks and geometry heads under a frozen native-precision teacher. The student generates and consumes its own quan-tized history, allowing later-view geometric supervision to shape earlier KV writes within each clip. Across five benchmarks and two backbones, many QuantStream3D configurations surpass their unquantized counterparts. Relative to LLM-oriented KV quantizers, we reduce absolute trajectory error and reconstruction complete-ness error by up to 57 .4% and 69 .7% , respectively. We further integrate QuantStream3D with existing native cache-pruning methods using the same quantization policy and adapted weights, pushing the limits of compression. To translate low-bit storage into practical inference gains, we implement fused packed-cache kernels for StreamVGGT that reduce intermediate memory traffic and launch overhead. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Revealing the geometric impact of KV quantization. 

We systematically characterize KV quantization in stream-ing visual geometry transformers, uncovering Nonuniform Geometric Sensitivity and Cache–Geometry Mismatch. We expose the limits of LLM-derived KV quantization as-sumptions and establish an empirical and analytical basis for geometry-guided quantization and joint adaptation. • Unifying geometry-guided quantization and geometry-aligned adaptation. We introduce QuantStream3D, to our knowledge the first dedicated KV quantization framework for streaming visual geometry transformers. It couples Geometry-Guided Mixed-Precision KV Quantization with Geometry-Aligned QAD, preserving sensitive cross-view evidence while jointly adapting transformer blocks and geometry heads to student-generated quantized history. • Pushing the limits of compression beyond pruning. ## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Streaming 3D Geometry 

Feed-forward models infer geometry directly from im-ages: DUSt3R predicts point maps [26], while VGGT jointly estimates cameras, depth, and scene structure [24]. StreamVGGT and STream3R extend feed-forward geometry to causal inference, retaining historical KV states for cross-view reasoning [9, 36]. Recent approaches optimize his-tory through diversity-aware retention (InfiniteVGGT) [32], 2coherent memory organization (FrameVGGT) [30], spa-tiotemporal caching (STAC) [25], and pose-guided writes (SceneBound) [27]. QVGGT quantizes weights [15]. Most closely related, XStreamVGGT combines cache pruning with LLM-oriented KIVI quantization, evaluating geometric tasks primarily at 4-bit precision [21]. Its released quantiza-tion path uses simulated quantization and retains dequantized floating-point KV rather than packed low-bit codes. Ultra-low-bit geometric sensitivity and adaptation to quantized history remain insufficiently addressed. QuantStream3D combines systematic geometric characterization with packed low-bit KV storage and optimized inference kernels. 

2.2. KV Quantization and Adaptation 

KV cache quantization reduces long-context LLM mem-ory costs by storing lower-precision history. KIVI uses per-channel key and per-token value quantization [12]; KVQuant combines per-channel keys, pre-RoPE quanti-zation, and nonuniform formats [7]. Beyond granularity, per-token methods mitigate outliers through Hadamard ro-tations (QuaRot) [1] or channel reordering and clipping (SKVQ) [4]. OScaR addresses token-norm imbalance in per-channel quantization using canalized rotation and token scaling [20], whereas OSCAR derives attention-aware rota-tions from covariance statistics [35]. TurboQuant limits re-construction and inner-product errors through rotated scalar quantization and residual correction [33]. Complementary attention-aware strategies protect salient tokens [17, 18]. For model adaptation, LLM-QAT uses teacher-generated train-ing data [11], while NVFP4 QAD distills a higher-precision teacher [29]. Streaming geometry requires preserving cross-view evidence and adapting geometric computation to quan-tized history. QuantStream3D couples geometry-guided KV quantization with Geometry-Aligned QAD, jointly adapt-ing transformer blocks and geometry heads using quantized history generated by the student.

3.2. Quantization Granularity and K/V Precision 

We compare four K/V granularities in both backbones us-ing affine round-to-nearest quantization with groups of 64, without protection or transforms. Both K and V use 2 bits, isolating granularity in this challenging low-bit regime. Per-channel K/V improves trajectory estimation over per-token K/V in both backbones (Tab. 1). On StreamVGGT, KCVC also yields lower trajectory error than the LLM-oriented asymmetric KCVT layout adopted by KIVI [12]. Given its strong overall performance, we use KCVC for subsequent analyses unless noted otherwise. Fixing KCVC, we compare K4V4, K4V2, K2V4, and K2V2 to isolate bit-width and K/V sensitivity. Without protection or transforms, 4-bit caching is comparatively ro-bust, whereas 2-bit caching can substantially increase ge-ometric error (Tab. 1). The relative ranking of K4V2 and K2V4 reverses across geometric outputs and backbones. The key-priority tendency reported in LLM studies [6] does not consistently transfer: K is not uniformly more precision-sensitive than V in streaming geometry.

3.3. Layer, Frame, and Token Sensitivity 

We next examine how quantization sensitivity varies across layers, frames, and token types. We scan all 24 global-attention layers in both backbones, reducing K, V, or both from 4 to 2 bits at one layer while keeping all others at 4 bits. Global attention in layer 14 yields the largest error increase under all three interventions and across all three TUM trajectory metrics in both backbones. Joint K/V sen-sitivity likewise peaks at this layer on Sintel δ1 (Figure 2). Independent clip checks confirm the peak (Appendix A.1). To examine historical roles and special-token sensitiv-ity [22], we separately restore all tokens in the first frame, camera tokens across history, or register tokens across his-tory to native precision under otherwise 2-bit caching. First-frame protection lowers translational and rotational relative pose errors in both backbones. Camera- and register-token protection can also improve geometric outputs, but their benefits vary across backbones and metrics (Appendix A.1). 

Takeaways. 

• Layer sensitivity. Global attention in layer 14 is a shared sensitivity peak across both backbones. • Frame sensitivity. First-frame protection yields repeatable gains in both backbones. • Token sensitivity. Camera and register tokens can benefit from protection, with gains that vary across geometric outputs. 

3.4. Quantization Transforms 

Transformation-based methods reshape activation distribu-tions to ease low-bit quantization. QuaRot and RotateKV re-distribute channel outliers through orthogonal mixing [1, 19]. SmoothQuant rescales activations with compensating weight transforms, while QServe applies reciprocal Q/K scaling through SmoothAttention [10, 28]. We examine whether these benefits transfer to streaming geometry. Under unprotected 2-bit caching, we compare paired Q/K Hadamard rotation, V-only rotation, their combination, and fixed SmoothQuant-style channel scaling. Paired Q/K rota-tion reduces all three TUM trajectory errors in both back-bones. V rotation can improve individual outputs, but adding it to Q/K rotation increases all three STream3R trajectory errors. Fixed channel scaling likewise offers no consistent benefit across tasks and backbones (Appendix A.2). 

Takeaways. 

• Q/K rotation. Paired Hadamard rotation yields repeatable gains under 2-bit quantization. • Value rotation. V rotation can help, with gains that depend on the backbone and accompanying transforms. • Channel scaling. Fixed channel scaling does not yield con-sistent gains across the tested settings.

4.3. Fused Execution with Packed KV 

Packed KV reduces persistent storage but introduces de-coding and input-assembly overhead. We implement fused Triton [23] kernels for StreamVGGT to eliminate redundant history materialization and reduce launch overhead [13, 20]. On each read, a single kernel unpacks mixed 2/4-bit his-tory, applies affine reconstruction, and copies current native-precision K/V directly into the final contiguous attention operands. Direct placement eliminates per-frame decoded buffers and a separate concatenation step while retaining the original scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) and its autocast behavior. For a combined decoded historical K/V payload of DF bytes, direct placement avoids 2DF logical bytes of intermediate writes and reads. We fuse the Q/K Hadamard transforms in a shared launch after normalization and RoPE, leaving V unrotated. Current-frame attention uses native-precision K/V, so each layer stages its writes after the FFN and defers packing until the backbone finishes. Before geometry prediction, one launch per layer jointly performs per-channel min–max reduction, affine quantization, and bit packing for current K/V, while earlier history stays packed (Appendix C). 

4.4. Composition with Native Cache Pruning 

Beyond quantizing unpruned KV caches, we integrate QuantStream3D with existing cache-pruning methods to push the limits of KV compression. Pruning reduces the number of retained states, while quantization lowers their bit width. We instantiate this composition with InfiniteVGGT, XStreamVGGT, and FrameVGGT [21, 30, 32], reusing the same adapted StreamVGGT weights. Each integration pre-serves the native selection rule, selection schedule, auxiliary state, and feature basis. Given decoded KV candidates Zτ

and selector state aτ at event τ , the cache update is 

Iτ = Sbase (Zτ , a τ ),Hπτ = Pack[ Qπ (Gπ (Zτ [Iτ ]))] . (9) Here Sbase is the native selector, Gπ forms 64-token groups for each layer, head, and bit width, and Qπ applies the per-channel quantizer in Sec. 4.1. We assign precision using each retained token’s original frame and layer identity. Regroup-ing may combine retained tokens from different frames, so we recompute per-channel min–max statistics for the result-ing groups. Quantized history can also change later selection. We therefore evaluate geometric quality and physical KV storage on each realized rollout (Sec. 5.3).

We systematically characterized low-bit KV quantization in streaming geometry, revealing the need to preserve cross-view evidence and align geometric computation with quan-tized history. Global attention in layer 0 uses 36 or 89 historical frames, with the first protected at 4 bits and others at 2; layer 14 uses 36 all-4-bit frames. The timed pipeline covers 64-dimensional Hadamard Q/K rotation, history reconstruction with current-K/V appending, original SDPA, and current K/V encoding at fixed history lengths. Each control disables history-read fusion, Q/K pairing, or K/V pairing individually while holding inputs and precision fixed. Separate K/V encoding retains fused range reduction, rounding, and packing within each operand, isolating the effect of pairing. After JIT compilation, Q/K rotation and K/V encoding use 12 warm-ups and 30 calls per round; history reads and the pipeline use five warm-ups and 15 calls. Five interleaved rounds randomize variant order, using synchronized CUDA-event timing that includes format validation and allocation. Numerical agreement is documented in Appendix C.2. Our fused reader drives the pipeline gain by reconstructing packed history and appending current K/V directly into final attention operands, eliminating per-frame decoded buffers and a separate concatenation step. We also pair Q and K rotation within one kernel launch and encode K and V together, reducing the number of kernel launches.
