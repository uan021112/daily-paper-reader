Abstract— Online surface reconstruction from LiDAR scans
requires observation models that respect the sensor’s physical
measurement geometry. Unlike unordered point sets, LiDAR
returns carry anisotropic range noise along known rays, which
also implicitly identify free space. We introduce the Occu-
pancy Planar Patch (OPP), an incremental bounded planar
patch representation that evaluates surface likelihoods and
free space directly in range-space, allowing sensor noise, plane
uncertainty, and ray clearance to be explicitly modelled during
reconstruction. A single ray-to-patch association weight jointly
updates plane geometry, refines surface boundaries, and blends
camera imagery. The method is evaluated using six sequences
from the Oxford Spires and Multi-Campus datasets. OPP
achieves the highest precision and lowest reconstruction error
among evaluated methods, while producing stored maps among
the most compact. By conservatively admitting surfaces only
where geometric evidence is certain, OPP prioritises structural
precision over unconstrained completeness, which is well suited
for architectural modelling. Geometric reconstruction operates
in real time, requiring 81 ms per frame on a representative
sequence, with texture projection adding 134 ms.
I. INTRODUCTION
Mobile robots and handheld LiDAR scanners can map
a large building in a single walk-through [1], [3]. How-
ever, raw point clouds lack explicit representations of solid
surfaces and free space. Recovering explicit surfaces is
necessary to make the data usable downstream: for dig-
ital twinning [4], construction monitoring [5], simulation,
motion planning [6], and localisation [7]. However, down-
stream applications impose differing requirements on surface
reconstructions. Physics simulation and collision checking
typically require a single watertight mesh. By contrast, for
architectural modelling, surfaces should be separate and
individually parameterised so that walls, window reveals, and
structural elements remain distinct objects [4]. In this work,
we focus on architectural modelling and perform surface
reconstruction in real time during scanning.
Conventional surface reconstruction often treats scans as
unordered point sets with isotropic noise [7], [8]. A LiDAR
scan, however, is a collection of range measurements from
the sensor origin along known rays, with noise acting pre-
dominantly along those rays and the space in front of each
return known to be empty [9], [10]. As a result, we formulate
our method directly in range-space to exploit the sensor
measurement geometry, preserving directional and free-space
cues without voxelising 3D space.
Building interiors, facades, corridors, and urban streets are
predominantly piecewise planar [11], [12], making bounded
planar patches an effective map representation. Unlike local
volumetric voxels [2], [13], a plane pools range measure-
ments over its entire physical support, continuously refin-
ing the estimate as the surface is observed from different
viewpoints during a walk-through. Planar parameterisations
can also represent large structures with minimal storage [14]
and directly provide the geometric primitives required by
downstream architectural and CAD pipelines [4].
We introduce the Occupancy Planar Patch (OPP), an
incremental mapping framework designed to reconstruct
clean, structured geometry in real time (Fig. 1). OPP models
architectural surfaces as supporting planes whose spatial
extents are continuously refined by sensor visibility and
free-space carving. By formulating inference directly in

the LiDAR’s measurement space, the reconstructed model
evolves recursively with each incoming beam.
In summary, our contributions are:
• Occupancy Planar Patch: a bounded planar patch rep-
resentation coupling an infinite supporting plane with a
continuous 2D occupancy field whose boundary is delin-
eated by its iso-contour, preserving depth discontinuities
and maintaining structural elements as distinct surfaces.
• Unified recursive inference in range-space: evaluat-
ing predictive likelihoods directly in range-space models
anisotropic noise and uncertainties in closed form; a single
per-ray responsibility jointly drives recursive plane refine-
ment, continuous boundary learning, and texture projection
while maintaining a valid map after every scan.

sentation rather than as raw points. Common representations
include volumetric occupancy [13], signed distance fields [2],
[15], [16], surfels [17], edge and plane features [18], and
meshes, either extracted offline [19] or maintained on-
line [20]. Conceptually closest to our approach is the normal
distributions transform [21], [22], which also models the
scene as a Gaussian mixture; it places one Gaussian per
cell of a fixed grid describing point distributions, whereas
we assign one component per planar surface and evaluate
it along the ray, directly incorporating the sensor noise
model and ray clearance. Because built environments are
predominantly planar, planar representations are widely used,
either as the primary map representation [23] or as geometric
features for odometry [18]. Existing formulations differ in
spatial extent: unbounded infinite planes [24], voxel-bounded
planes [25], or explicitly bounded planar segments [23],
[26]. We build on bounded representations. The following
subsections review the three core decisions in bounded-plane
mapping: data association, plane and boundary parameteri-
sation, and appearance integration.
A. Data Association
Existing planar mapping pipelines predominantly associate
measurements via RANSAC [8], [27], range-image region
growing [28], or voxel subdivision [25]. While compu-
tationally simple, RANSAC fits infinite planes and fails
to separate disconnected coplanar surfaces; region growing
assumes pixel adjacency and breaks at depth discontinuities;
and voxelisation imposes an arbitrary grid scale where cell
size dictates boundary resolution. Recent bounded-plane
frameworks perform per-ray association against candidate
surfaces [14], but hard assignment forces premature deci-
sions when multiple surfaces explain an observation with
comparable likelihood. We adopt soft assignment inspired
by mixture models [29], [30], evaluating candidate respon-
sibilities directly along each ray so that sensor uncertainty
and plane geometry dictate association confidence.
B. Plane Fitting and Boundaries
Plane parameters are conventionally extracted via PCA [8]
or planar bundle adjustment [25]. Both minimise Euclidean
point-to-plane distances under an isotropic noise assumption,
introducing systematic bias when applied to the anisotropic
range noise of LiDAR sensors. Evaluating residuals di-
rectly in range-space along measurement rays eliminates this
bias. For spatial extent, previous methods rely on explicit
boundaries such as convex hulls [23], polygons [14], or
alpha shapes [26]. These binary representations force hard
inside/outside decisions regardless of observation density. In
contrast, an implicit continuous field retains partial evidence
and naturally accommodates sparsely sampled regions.
C. Appearance
Downstream robotic tasks such as visual localisation,
simulation, and semantic mapping benefit from geometry
carrying appearance or semantics [15]. In real-time dense
mapping, colour is typically fused via running averages [31]
or radial-distance weights [32]. Surface visibility is conven-
tionally resolved by Z-buffering to select the closest surface,
whereas the A-buffer [33] maintains a depth-sorted list of
intersections per ray to allow multi-layer depth composition.
III. METHOD
We present the OPP reconstruction framework, which in-
crementally processes LiDAR point clouds Pt, RGB images
It, and sensor poses Tt ∈SE(3) to reconstruct a collection
of textured planar patches K. As illustrated in Fig. 2, each
LiDAR return is motion-undistorted and traced as a ray
to query intersecting candidate patches (Sec. III-B). It is
then evaluated in range-space to compute a soft ray-to-patch
assignment weight (Sec. III-C), and used to concurrently
update the supporting plane (Sec. III-D) and its continuous
occupancy boundary (Sec. III-E). Finally, incoming images
are projected incrementally onto the texture atlas (Sec. III-F).
A. Scene Representation: The Occupancy Planar Patch

patches K = {(πk, Ok, Ak)}. Each patch k couples an
infinite supporting plane πk with a continuous occupancy
field Ok defining its finite spatial boundary, alongside a
texture atlas Ak capturing surface appearance (Fig. 3). This
formulation mirrors planar surfaces in real-world architec-
ture, which are physically flat across their span yet strictly
bounded by corners, edges, and openings.

D. Incremental Update: Supporting Plane
Following ray-to-patch assignment, each ray recursively
updates the associated candidate patches. Rather than accu-
mulating measurements for offline batch fitting, we update
each patch’s supporting plane using an extended Kalman fil-
ter (EKF) [9], maintaining a Gaussian belief π ∼N(π∗, Σπ)
that remains valid after every scan. Each update is linearised
at the current estimate using the plane Jacobian, Eq. (4).
The measurement noise corresponds to Eq. (5) excluding
the plane covariance term, which is supplied by the filter
state covariance. The measurement information is scaled by
γk, following standard expectation-maximisation weighting
for a mixture of components. This produces an incremen-
tally refined Gaussian estimate of each plane, maintaining
geometry without accumulating historical point clouds.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Baselines and Experimental Setup
We compare OPP against four distinct LiDAR recon-
struction baselines, each evaluated at a 10 cm resolution:
Poisson meshing (Puma [7]), neural signed distance fields
(PINGS [16]), direct voxelised meshing (ImMesh [20]),
and truncated signed distance fields (VDBFusion [2]). All
pipelines use identical ground-truth trajectories to isolate
reconstruction fidelity from odometry drift, with ImMesh
adapted to accept external poses. We omit PlanarMesh [14]
because it was designed as an in-memory proof-of-concept
without paging and exhausts system memory after approxi-
mately 300 scans; in contrast, OPP uses disk-backed paging
(Fig. 6) to maintain a strictly bounded memory footprint.
Geometric fidelity is evaluated against millimetre-accurate
reference scans from a survey-grade tripod scanner (Leica
RTC360). Runtime and memory benchmarks (Fig. 6, Fig. 7)
were recorded on an Apple M2 laptop (8-core CPU, 10-core
GPU, 24 GB unified memory).
B. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
Experiments are conducted on six sequences from two
datasets: Keble03, Observatory01, and Bodleian02 from the
Oxford Spires Dataset (OSD) [1], alongside kth day 10,
tuhh day 02, and ntu day 02 from the Multi-Campus Dataset
(MCD) [36]. The OSD platform provides a Hesai QT64
LiDAR and three synchronised cameras. MCD features one
or two forward cameras paired with an Ouster OS1-64
LiDAR (KTH, TUHH) or an OS1-128 LiDAR (NTU).
We measure reconstruction quality against ground-truth
scans using standard distance metrics in metres: accu-
racy (reconstruction-to-reference error) and completeness
(reference-to-reconstruction error), reporting both mean and
mode. In addition, surface precision, recall, and F1 scores are
evaluated at 1 cm and 5 cm thresholds. Storage requirements
are assessed using each method’s native disk format: meshes

C. Reconstruction Results
Table I reports quantitative comparisons across all se-
quences, and Fig. 5 illustrates qualitative reconstructions on a
representative selection of sequences spanning both datasets.
Across all six benchmark sequences, OPP consistently
achieves the highest precision at 5 cm and the lowest mean
accuracy error, while matching or exceeding the best mode
accuracy on four sequences (Table I). Free-space carving
also naturally rejects dynamic obstacles (Fig. 5, insets in
row 3). While walking pedestrians leave dense motion trails
in the raw LiDAR scans and the volumetric mesh, OPP’s
transmission term carves away these transient artifacts as
subsequent rays observe the ground plane.
The primary trade-off of our method lies in completeness
and recall. At long ranges, beam divergence and sparse
returns increase the in-plane uncertainty δ. Rather than
fitting false planes to noisy points or vegetation, OPP’s
gating and adaptive allocation suppress surface generation
in uncertain regions. While this lowers recall on distant
foliage (e.g., 28.2% on kth day 10 vs. 60.5% for ImMesh),
it ensures architectural boundaries remain cleanly defined
without bridging across depth discontinuities.
OPP also produces compact maps, achieving the smallest
exported file size on Keble03 (30.2 MiB) and Bodleian02
(104.6 MiB), and ranking second on Observatory01 and
kth day 10. This efficiency stems directly from our rep-
resentation: each supporting plane condenses thousands of
coplanar points into a few geometric parameters, while 2D
CSRBF kernels delineate surface boundaries without the
heavy overhead of 3D grids or meshes.
Beyond compact stored maps, OPP maintains a strictly
bounded memory footprint during online operation (Fig. 6).
As newly explored facades expand the cumulative map on
disk, disk-backed patch paging keeps resident RAM below
7.6 MiB (median 5.0 MiB), decoupling working memory
from trajectory length. Similarly, texture projection offloads
patch atlases to GPU memory (7.4 GiB), keeping the live
CPU heap constant at ∼0.8 GiB.
Fig. 7 reports runtime distributions of OPP, with and
without texture projection. On OSD, OPP runs comfortably
within the 10 Hz sensor rate (median 62 ms to 81 ms for
∼30k points per frame). On MCD, runtimes increase as
input point count increases: 142 ms to 153 ms for the OS1-64
sequences (∼57k points per frame) and 301 ms for the denser
OS1-128 sequence (∼94k points per frame). Texture projec-

V. CONCLUSIONS
We presented OPP, an online incremental LiDAR surface
reconstruction framework that couples supporting planes
with continuous 2D occupancy fields. By formulating likeli-
hoods, geometric updates, and ray clearance in range-space,
OPP models anisotropic sensor noise and parameter uncer-
tainties in closed form. Across six benchmark sequences,
OPP achieves the highest precision and lowest accuracy error
while producing stored maps among the most compact of the
evaluated methods, providing cleanly parameterised surfaces
suitable for architectural modelling.
In future work, we will perform quantitative evaluation on
the textured reconstruction and investigate methods to join
planar patch edges into closed architectural geometry.
