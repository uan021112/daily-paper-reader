Abstract
Understanding a scene requires both geometry and seman-
tics: geometry recovers its spatial structure, and seman-
tics separates it into objects. Current models obtain the
two from separate networks or separate passes. We present
PINE, a one-step Omni Diffusion Transformer that predicts
the point map, the surface normals, and the instance map of
an image in one forward pass. PINE treats each map as a
token stream, and a stream mask decides which streams in-
teract. A camera-aware ray encoding positions every token
by its camera ray rather than its pixel. Grounding attention
adds the affinity of pretrained DINOv3 features to the atten-
tion logits. Trained on only 139K synthetic images for ge-
ometry, PINE achieves state-of-the-art point maps whether
or not intrinsics are given and the best average rank on
surface normals.
It also exceeds SAM on large objects
with less than 1% of its training images, and it predicts all
three maps 5.7× faster than MoGe-3 with SAM. Website:
https://aigeeksgroup.github.io/PINE
1. Introduction
Understanding a scene requires both geometry and seman-
tics [17, 75]. Geometry describes where each surface lies
and how it is oriented, and semantics describes which sur-
faces form one object. Specialist models [68, 82] now re-
cover each part from a single image with high accuracy. The
Depth Anything [30, 71, 72] and MoGe [24, 58, 59] series
predict depth, point map and normals. Diffusion estima-
tors such as Marigold [19, 42] and Lotus [14] predict de-
tailed depth and surface normals, and GenPercept [64] and
E2E-FT [37] reduce them to one sampling step. The SAM
series [21, 49] segments any object under a prompt, and
gen2seg [20] colors every instance with a generative model.
None of these models predicts all three maps in one for-
ward pass. A full parse of one image chains a geometry
model and a segmentation model. Their latencies and mem-
ory add up, and their maps do not share one camera. Point-
map models [24, 30, 33, 45, 59] can take the intrinsics,
whereas diffusion normal estimators [11, 14, 19, 37, 73]
take the image alone, even when the intrinsics are given.
arXiv:2611.29389v1  [cs.CV]  22 Nov 2026


Generalist models such as 4M [40], Unified-IO [35], DI-
CEPTION [78] and OneDiffusion [25] cast multiple tasks
into one network. They do not remove the cost: DICEP-
TION runs one forward pass per map, and OneDiffusion
needs multiple sampling steps for each map. A direct so-
lution is one Diffusion Transformer that predicts all maps
together in one step. Yet a Diffusion Transformer [43] is
designed for RGB images. The three maps break three of
its assumptions. The instance map has no fixed target, be-
cause it is correct under any permutation of its colors. The
point map places each depth along the camera ray of its
pixel, but a transformer indexes its tokens by pixel [43]. A
full view and a zoomed crop then give one scene point two
positions. The normals and the instance map are constant
over surfaces and objects, but attention recovers this group-
ing from the tokens alone. In practice, the attention of a
patch spreads over other objects.
Based on these observations, we present PINE, a one-
step Omni Diffusion Transformer that predicts the point
map, the normals and the instance map in one forward pass,
as Fig. 1 shows. PINE revises these three assumptions. For
the target, PINE represents each map as a token stream.
The geometry streams follow flow matching at their own
timesteps, while the instance stream regresses its map with
a clustering loss. A stream mask keeps the instance stream
apart from the geometry noise. For the position, the camera-
aware ray encoding positions the tokens of every stream by
the rays of one camera. When the intrinsics are unknown, a
default camera takes their place. For the grouping, ground-
ing attention writes the affinity of DINOv3 features [54]
into the attention logits of the normal and instance streams.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We present PINE, a one-step Omni Diffusion Trans-
former. One forward pass replaces one specialist model
per map and multiple sampling steps of diffusion estima-
tors.
• We introduce a camera-aware ray encoding, which posi-
tions tokens by camera rays whether or not intrinsics are
given, and grounding attention, which gives the normal
and instance streams the grouping of their pixels.
• PINE achieves state-of-the-art point maps whether or not
intrinsics are given and the best average rank on surface
normals, and it exceeds SAM on large objects with less
than 1% of its training images. It runs 5.7 times faster
than MoGe-3 combined with SAM.
2. Related Work
Monocular Geometry Estimation.
MiDaS [47, 48] and
Depth Anything [71, 72] train on mixed or large unlabeled
data to predict affine-invariant depth.
DUSt3R [60] re-
gresses point maps directly, and MoGe [24, 58, 59] predicts
affine-invariant point maps from a single image. Without
intrinsics, the field of view is ambiguous. Metric3D [74],
UniDepth [44, 45], Depth Pro [3], DA3 [30] and Founda-
tionGeo [33] estimate the intrinsics or take them as input.
DSINE [1] feeds per-pixel ray directions to a normal esti-
mator. All of these models index their features by pixels and
predict geometry only. GTA [39] and PRoPE [28] encode
the relative cameras of multiple views in attention, whereas
PINE positions the tokens of one image by their rays.
Diffusion for Dense Prediction.
Marigold [19] fine-tunes
a pretrained latent diffusion model [16, 51] into a zero-shot
depth estimator. GeoWizard [11], StableNormal [73] and
Marigold V2 [42] extend this recipe to normals. FE2E [56]
starts from an image editing model. GeoNeXt [69] starts
from a video generative model and predicts depth and nor-
mals as the next frames. GenPercept [64], Lotus [14] and
E2E-FT [37] reduce the multiple sampling steps of these
estimators to one deterministic step. TransNormal-2 [27]
corrects the decoding errors of the autoencoder at object
boundaries with pixel-space losses and a refinement mod-
ule. PPD [65], PointDiT [66] and Lapis [32] avoid the de-
tail loss of the autoencoder by denoising in pixel space [29].
Each of these estimators predicts one or two geometric
maps, whereas PINE predicts all three maps in one step.
Instance
Segmentation.
Instance
segmentation
has
moved from closed label sets [6, 15] to class-agnostic
entities [36]. SimpleClick [34] segments the object under
a click. The SAM series [21, 49] accepts any prompt and
scales this ability with a large mask corpus. TokenCut [63],
Deep Spectral Methods [38] and CutLER [61] group
pixels into objects by the affinities of self-supervised
features [41, 54].
gen2seg [20] fine-tunes a generative
model [51] to color every instance with a clustering loss [9].
PINE predicts instances as one stream of a geometry model
and writes the affinity of DINOv3 features into its attention.
Omni Models for Dense Prediction.
Multi-task net-
works such as UberNet [23] and Omnidata [10] share one
backbone across geometric and semantic tasks.
Taskon-
omy [75] measures how these tasks transfer.
Generalist
models cast multiple tasks into one interface, such as im-
ages in Painter [62] and token sequences in 4M [40] and
Unified-IO [35].
DICEPTION [78] trains one diffusion
model for all tasks but runs one forward pass per task. Gen-
Ception [57] turns a video generation model into one per-
ception model, where a text prompt selects the task. Uni-
Diffuser [2] and OneDiffusion [25] give each modality or
view its own timestep, so that any of them can condition
the others over multiple sampling steps. PINE is an omni
model of a different form: each map is a token stream, each
geometry stream has its own timestep, and one forward pass
gives all maps.

3. Method
We introduce PINE, a one-step diffusion model that takes
an RGB image and, optionally, its intrinsics, and predicts
an affine-invariant point map, surface normals and an in-
stance map. Figure 2 shows its pipeline. The Omni Dif-
fusion Transformer of Sec. 3.1 processes the three maps as
token streams in one sequence. The camera-aware ray en-
coding of Sec. 3.2 positions every token by its camera ray.
Grounding attention in Sec. 3.3 adds the DINOv3 affinity
to the attention logits of the normal and instance streams.
Section 3.4 describes the training, and Algorithm 1 lists one
training step and the one-step inference.
3.1. Omni Diffusion Transformer
Given an RGB image I and, optionally, its intrinsic matrix
K, PINE predicts the three maps as

where P is an affine-invariant point map whose z channel is

permutation of its colors and has no fixed target to denoise

3.3. Grounding Attention
A normal is constant over a planar surface, and an instance
label is constant over an object. Both maps thus depend on
how pixels group into surfaces and objects. Self-supervised
ViT features already encode this grouping in their affini-
ties [38, 63]. Yet in Eq. (4), the DINOv3 features enter
only the tokens, and attention infers from them which to-
kens should exchange information. PINE therefore writes
the affinity into the attention logits as a bias. A layer nor-
malization and a linear projection map the DINOv3 feature
of patch j to a grounding code gj. For normal-normal and
instance-instance pairs, every layer adds a bias to ℓjl as
bjl = (Ugj)⊤(V gl),
(8)
where each head of each block has its own learned low-rank
projections U and V . The bias raises the attention between
patches that look alike regardless of their positions and of
the current geometry estimate. Figure 4 shows that ground-
ing attention moves the attention of a patch onto its own
object. The point stream does not use grounding attention,
because depth also depends on the global layout: two walls
that look alike can lie at different depths.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setups
Implementation Details.
PINE starts from the pretrained
weights of PointDiT [66] and trains on the 139K synthetic
and 24K real images of Tab. 7. Its B, L and H models use
a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16, ViT-L/16 and ViT-H+/16 [54].
The reference camera has the focal length fref = 420 pixels
and its principal point at the image center. We clamp the
denominator 1−ts of Eq. (3) at 0.05. We crop each training
image at a random aspect ratio in [0.5, 3.5], flip it and jitter
its color, and Sec. A lists the full augmentation. The loss
weights are λrel = 0.1, λgrad = 0.5 and λinst = 0.02, and the
sky pixels add the error of Eq. (10) with a weight of 0.01.
We train for 20 epochs with AdamW, a constant learning
rate of 10−5 and a batch size of 64 in bfloat16. Training
PINE-L takes about 20 hours on 8 A100 GPUs.

4.3. Ablation Study
Table 5 ablates the three components of PINE: the camera-
aware ray encoding, the stream mask and grounding atten-
tion. To limit the cost, we train each variant as a PINE-L
model on Hypersim and Virtual KITTI 2 only, and the other
settings follow Sec. 4.1. To separate the gain of PINE from
the additional training, we also train the point-only Point-
DiT architecture with the same recipe. The point maps, the
depth and the normals use the benchmarks of Sec. 4.1, and
the instances use COCOexc and DRAM.
Camera-Aware Ray Encoding.
In Tab. 5, without the en-
coding, intrinsics have no effect, and Relp stays at 5.05 in
both settings.
With the encoding, intrinsics reduce Relp
from 4.82 to 3.94 and the mean angular error from 18.23
to 17.81. Without intrinsics, the encoding still reduces Relp
from 5.05 to 4.82. We split the error of each point into a
lateral part εxy and a depth part εz. Averaged over the valid
pixels with intrinsics, the encoding reduces εxy from 2.28
to 1.27, but εz only from 3.22 to 2.95. Figure 6 illustrates
this split on one image row. The gap in lateral error grows
with the distance to the principal point, where a wrong fo-
cal length moves a point the most. During training, Relp
with intrinsics stalls near 5.0 without the encoding but keeps
dropping to 3.94 with it.
Stream Mask.
In Tab. 5, letting the instance tokens also
attend to the geometry tokens raises Relp without intrinsics
from 4.82 to 4.93 and lowers the COCOexc mIoU from 33.0
to 32.4. PINE therefore isolates the instance stream. Re-
moving the normal-to-point attention raises the mean angu-
lar error with intrinsics from 17.81 to 17.94. The normal
stream thus keeps its attention to the point stream.

5. Conclusion
We have presented PINE, a one-step Omni Diffusion Trans-
former that predicts the point map, the normals and the in-
stance map of an image in one forward pass. It achieves
state-of-the-art point maps and the best average rank on nor-
mals. It exceeds SAM on large objects but still trails it on
medium and small objects. Camera-aware ray encoding ex-
ploits intrinsics when given, and grounding attention groups
pixels by object. Unlike chains of specialist models, its one-
pass design suits interactive applications such as robotics.
Its point maps lack metric scale. Future work can add met-
ric scale and semantic classes as new streams.

A. Implementation Details
This section complements Secs. 3.4 and 4.1 of the main pa-
per with the full training and inference setup. Table 7 lists
the training data. The geometry streams train on the four
synthetic datasets, and the instance stream also trains on the
instance labels of EntitySeg and ADE20K. On Hypersim,
the instance loss uses only the scenes with at least 10 anno-
tated objects, as in gen2seg [20], which leaves 87K images
with instance labels. We remove the 555 EntitySeg images
that come from COCO val2017, so that no test image of
COCOexc enters training. Tables 8 to 10 list the model sizes,
the training hyperparameters and the inference settings.
A.1. Training Losses
The loss weights in Eq. (9) are λrel = 0.1, λgrad = 0.5 and
λinst = 0.02. The point-map target xp is normalized for
shift and scale as in PointDiT [66]. In Eq. (10), the sky pix-
els add the same error, averaged separately and weighted by
λsky = 0.01. The gradient loss Lgrad is the multi-scale gra-
dient matching loss of MiDaS [47] on the log-depth error,
which sharpens depth edges. The normal loss Lsink follows
Marigold V2 [42]: inside every 5 × 5 window, a Sinkhorn
plan matches the predicted normals to the target normals
before the L1 error is taken. The instance loss Linst follows
gen2seg [20], which adapts the discriminative loss of [9]. It
pulls the predicted colors of every instance to their mean,
pushes all other pixels away from that mean, pushes the
means of different instances apart and pulls the background
to black. Given that it uses the instance ids only to group
pixels, the loss ignores which color an instance takes.

its own timestep ts = sigmoid(ξ) with ξ ∼N(−0.8, 0.82).
With probability 0.1, ts is set to 0, the timestep of one-
step inference. Each training image keeps its intrinsics with

B.4. Normals
The accuracy within 11.25◦is the fraction of valid pixels

B.5. Instances
The point prompt of each ground-truth instance is its inte-
rior point farthest from the instance boundary. All meth-
ods use the same point prompts. For PINE and gen2seg,
we select a mask from the instance map by the rule of
gen2seg [20].
This rule takes the query color q as a
Gaussian-weighted mean around the point prompt, with
σ = 0.01. It scores every pixel of color c by 1/∥c −q∥
and rescales the scores to [0, 255]. A joint bilateral filter of
diameter 9, with both σ at 75, smooths the scores, and the
pixels with a score above 3 form the mask. SAM [21] takes
the point prompt as one positive point, and we keep the out-
put mask with the highest predicted IoU. We split COCOexc
into small, medium and large instances by the COCO area
thresholds of 322 and 962 pixels.

D. Grounding Attention
This section extends the grounding attention ablation of
Sec. 4.3. Table 11 and Figs. 11 and 12 analyze how the
grounding attention of Sec. 3.3 changes the attention.
D.1. Region Mass
The region mass of a query patch is the share of its attention
that falls on its own region. For the instance stream, the re-
gion is the ground-truth instance that covers more than half
of the patch. For the normal stream, the region is the con-
nected set of patches whose normals lie within 10◦of the

E.7. Sampling Steps
Table 15 evaluates PINE with one to four sampling steps.

ery model, and more steps raise all errors slightly. The mean
angular error rises with every added step. PINE therefore
uses one sampling step.
