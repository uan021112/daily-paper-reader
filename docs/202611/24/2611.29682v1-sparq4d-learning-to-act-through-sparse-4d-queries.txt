Abstract
Successful manipulation requires a policy to understand
where things are and how they will move, yet action demon-
strations leave this spatiotemporal structure implicit. While
explicit 4D supervision has proven effective, reducing the
cost of dense 4D prediction remains challenging. This pa-
per introduces SparQ4D, a lightweight yet expressive de-
coder that efficiently injects holistic 4D scene understand-
ing into policies and improves success rates. At its core
is a query-based decoder attached to a pretrained policy,
which casts many spatialtemporal tasks into one unified in-
terface (e.g. depth, 2D/3D point motion, multi-view corre-
spondence, and hand-eye relations). This design enables
efficient training and inference without sacrificing expres-
sivity: the policy learns rich 4D structure from a small num-
ber of sampled queries during training, while the decoder
can be removed at inference with zero additional overhead.
Across multiple backbones, simulated environments, and
real-world settings, SparQ4D achieves state-of-the-art ma-
nipulation performance, outperforming competing methods
while training up to 5× faster. Probing analyses further
reveal that this 4D structure is recoverable from the policy.

1. Introduction
Successful manipulation is inherently spatiotemporal: it de-
pends on knowing where things are and how they will move.
To hang a mug, a robot must grasp it, align its handle with a
rack, and relate wrist and external camera views as it moves.
Action demonstrations supervise the desired behavior but
leave these relationships implicit. Efficiently teaching a pol-
icy this spatiotemporal structure is thus a central question.
One approach to supervising spatiotemporal understand-
ing is through future prediction.
World action models
(WAMs) jointly model future visual observations and robot
actions [26], while X-WAM [11] extends this paradigm to
multi-view RGB-D prediction. Reconstructing the full fu-
ture supervises scene dynamics richly, but requires dense
prediction over many visual tokens, substantially increas-
ing training cost. Fast-WAM [27] removes future genera-
tion at inference yet retains strong performance, suggesting
that much of the benefit comes from the training signal it-
self. Another line injects geometric knowledge into vision-
language-action models (VLAs) through pretrained 3D rep-
resentations:
R3DP [30] incorporates foundation-model
features via a fast-slow perception system, and Spatial Forc-
ing [14] aligns VLA representations with 3D teacher fea-
tures during training. Although effective, these methods
likewise rely on dense, per-token supervision. This leads
to our central question: can sparse, explicit supervision
teach a robot policy both geometry and motion at sub-
stantially lower training cost, without additional infer-
ence overhead or sacrificing expressivity?
We introduce SparQ4D, a lightweight framework that in-
jects holistic 4D understanding into robot policies through
unified sparse supervision (Fig. 1). Inspired by D4RT [29],
our key idea is to formulate diverse spatiotemporal tasks
as a single query: where is a selected point at a speci-
fied time, in a specified view or coordinate frame? Given
a query specifying the point, time, and reference frame, a
lightweight decoder predicts its location. By varying these
attributes, the same decoder provides supervision for depth,
2D/3D point motion, multi-view correspondence, and hand-
eye relations. We attach this decoder to intermediate VLA
representations and jointly optimize auxiliary predictions
with action learning, encouraging the policy to encode rich
geometric and temporal structure. Crucially, this unified
formulation eliminates the need for dense reconstruction.
During training, we sample only a small budget of queries
across points, times, and views, directly controlling auxil-
iary decoding cost while retaining diverse spatiotemporal
supervision. At inference, the decoder is simply removed,
leaving the original policy architecture and computation un-
changed. Thus, SparQ4D decouples rich training-time su-
pervision from inference-time complexity.
Across simulation benchmarks and real-world tasks,
SparQ4D achieves state-of-the-art manipulation perfor-
mance with both π0 and π0.5, reducing failures by up
to 60%.
These gains span RoboTwin 2.0 [6], LIBERO-
Plus [9], and five real-world tasks, demonstrating the effec-
tiveness of our unified supervision. Beyond performance,
SparQ4D requires up to 5× fewer training GPU-hours than
WAM-based approaches, while introducing no additional
inference overhead. Finally, probing analyses reveal recov-
erable 4D structure, suggesting that the policy itself inter-
nalizes this structure rather than relying on the auxiliary de-
coder. Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce SparQ4D, a lightweight yet expressive
framework that injects holistic 4D understanding into
robot policies through a unified query-based supervision
interface.
• We enable efficient training through a small, controllable
budget of sparse 4D queries, reducing auxiliary super-
vision cost without sacrificing expressivity or inference
speed.
• We demonstrate improved manipulation performance
across multiple backbones, simulation environments and
the real world. We also systematically examine supervi-
sion content, density, placement and internal representa-
tion through controlled ablations.
2. Related work
Geometric representations and supervision for policies.
Prior work incorporates geometric knowledge into robot
policies through explicit 3D inputs, such as depth, point
clouds, and voxels [10, 19, 28], or pretrained 3D fea-
tures [30].
These methods leverage geometric informa-
tion at inference time. Alternatively, training-time repre-
sentation alignment improves spatial understanding without
additional geometric inputs at deployment. Spatial Forc-
ing [14] aligns VLA representations with pretrained 3D fea-
tures, while Temporal Forcing [7] extends it to 4D represen-
tations. FLARE [32] similarly encourages predictive rep-
resentations through future latent alignment. Unlike these
methods that supervise implicit features, SparQ4D directly
supervises explicit geometric and motion quantities through
a unified, lightweight query decoder.
World modeling and sparse 4D supervision.
Video-
prediction policies such as UniPi and GR-1 [8, 23] use fu-
ture observations to guide action learning, while world ac-
tion models (WAMs) jointly model video and actions [15,
26]. Recent approaches extend world modeling with 3D/4D
information through geometric prediction or alignment [11,
16, 25, 31].
Fast-WAM [27] removes future generation
at inference, while AHA-WAM [4] accelerates execution
through asynchronous world modeling. Despite these ad-
vances, future prediction often incurs substantial training
cost. Separately, D4RT [29] introduces unified 4D queries

3. Method
SparQ4D adds an auxiliary objective to policy learning:
besides predicting actions, its backbone is also trained to
answer sparse 4D queries about the current observation
(Fig. 2). Designing this objective involves two main ques-
tions: what to ask the policy and how to read out its answers.
Sec. 3.1 answers the first by defining the query that uni-
fies depth, motion, multi-view, and hand-eye supervision.
Sec. 3.2 answers the second with a lightweight decoder on
the policy’s backbone. Sec. 3.3 presents details in training
and inference.

3.2. Query decoder
Architecture.
To answer 4D queries, we introduce a
lightweight transformer decoder Dϕ that reads from the
policy’s intermediate representations Zt.
Each query is
encoded as a single token by summing a linear embed-
ding of the p × p RGB patch around (u, v) in Ic
t, a po-
sitional encoding of (u, v), and learned embeddings of c,
∆t, and f. Query tokens cross-attend to the prefix tokens
Zt (image tokens from all cameras and language tokens),
but not to each other, allowing independent decoding. A
linear layer maps each decoded token to a predicted patch
ˆYq = Dϕ(q; Zt). The decoder adds only 1.6% to the pol-
icy’s parameter count.

sparse 4D supervision. At each time step, we sample M
queries, distributed equally among the four types. Spatial
locations (u, v) are sampled preferentially near depth dis-
continuities, with uniform sampling to ensure broader cov-
erage. For motion queries, ∆t is sampled uniformly from

4. Experiments
Our experiments test both effectiveness and efficiency, and
examine why SparQ4D works. We organize them around
five questions:
• RQ1 (Effectiveness, Simulation): Does SparQ4D im-
prove manipulation success over strong VLA and WAM
baselines in simulation?
• RQ2 (Effectiveness, Real World): Does this improve-
ment hold on a physical robot?
• RQ3 (Efficiency): Is SparQ4D cheaper to train and faster
to deploy than dense video-based and implicit-alignment
baselines?
• RQ4 (Ablation): Which query types and architectural
choices drive this improvement?
• RQ5 (Analysis): Does SparQ4D make the VLA repre-
sentation geometry-aware?
4.1. Experimental setup
Training.
On RoboTwin 2.0 [6], we use 50 clean-setting
and 500 randomized-setting demonstrations per task, train-
ing for 5 epochs on eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs, following
Fast-WAM [27].
On LIBERO-Plus[9], we train on 130
tasks with 50 demonstrations each. In simulation, depth
queries are supervised with rendered ground-truth depth.
For real-world evaluation, we collect 50 demonstrations for
each of five tasks and supervise depth queries with MoGe-

Evaluation.
In simulation, we roll out each method 100
times per task under both Clean and Randomized settings.
On the real robot, we roll out 25 episodes per task, due to
the time-intensive nature of real-world evaluation. We re-
port success rate (SR) throughout. For real-world tasks, we
additionally report progress score (PS), a partial-credit met-
ric defined in App.C.
Baselines.
All baselines are initialized from their publicly
released pretrained weights and fine-tuned on RoboTwin
under the same protocol as ours.
We compare against
three groups: (i) π0 and π0.5 [1, 2], action-only VLA base-
lines; (ii) Spatial Forcing [14], an implicit-alignment base-
line (Sec. 2) built on π0, for which we use the implementa-
tion from XPolicyLab [24]; and (iii) X-WAM [11] and Fast-
WAM [27], two WAM baselines built on the Wan2.2 [21]

backbones, although the magnitude of improvement varies.
Gains are pronounced in geometry-sensitive settings.
On RoboTwin 2.0, SparQ4D brings particularly large im-
provements to tasks requiring precise spatial alignment be-

gains 23 points under randomization. On LIBERO-Plus,
a similar benefit emerges under spatial perturbations: with
π0, success rates under camera and robot perturbations im-
prove by 50.6 and 45.7 points, respectively. Even with the
stronger π0.5, robot perturbations improve by 8.9 points, al-
though gains across other perturbation categories are mixed.
Together, these results suggest that explicit 4D supervision
is particularly beneficial for manipulation requiring accu-
rate spatial reasoning and alignment.

4.4. RQ3: Is SparQ4D more efficient to train and
deploy?
SparQ4D is cheaper to train and deploy.
Compared
with the two WAMs, SparQ4D (π0.5) trains 3–5× faster
and runs inference 1.7–6.6× faster, with about a quarter
of their parameters and 24–41% of their training mem-
ory (Tab. 6).
Importantly, these efficiency gains do not
come at the expense of performance: with sparse 4D su-
pervision, SparQ4D outperforms both WAM counterparts
on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO-Plus, demonstrating the ef-
fectiveness of explicit geometric and motion supervision as
an efficient alternative to dense future prediction. Since the
query decoder is used only during training, it adds no in-
ference overhead to the backbone. Compared with Spatial
Forcing, whose costs are otherwise similar, SparQ4D also
avoids a frozen VGGT teacher [22], reducing training over-
head and implementation complexity.

5. Conclusion
We presented SparQ4D, a lightweight framework that in-
jects holistic 4D understanding into robot policies through
unified sparse queries. A shared decoder supervises depth,
point motion, multi-view correspondence, and hand-eye re-
lations from intermediate VLA representations. A small
query budget avoids dense prediction, while removing
the decoder after training incurs zero inference overhead.
Across RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO-Plus, and five real-world
tasks, SparQ4D improves both π0 and π0.5, particularly in
geometry-sensitive settings, outperforming WAMs at sub-
stantially lower cost. Ablations confirm the benefits of com-
plementary queries, while probing reveals recoverable 4D
structure within the policy. These findings show that rich
spatiotemporal understanding can emerge from sparse su-
pervision without costly world prediction.

A. Implementation details of SparQ4D
This section details the query construction and sampling,
target generation, decoder architecture, and training con-
figuration of SparQ4D. To ensure fair comparisons, we
first optimize the underlying VLA baselines and then tune
SparQ4D on top of these configurations. All tunable hyper-
parameters are carefully examined, with the final settings
selected based on empirical performance and training effi-
ciency.
A.1. Query construction and sampling
Query tokens.
A query token is constructed from 5
summed parts.
The RGB patch is cut from the 224 ×
224 input image of the source camera after the back-
bone’s image preprocessing, with the best choice p = 14,
which equals the patch size of the SigLIP image encoder
in PaliGemma.
The positional encoding applies random
Fourier features [20] to each pixel coordinate, normalized
to [−1, 1]. The learned embedding tables have three entries
for the source camera, H = 50 entries for the time offset,
and five entries for the reference frame. The five terms are
summed and layer-normalized.
Query allocation and sampling.
We sample M = 96
queries per frame, evenly allocated across the four query
types (24 each). For spatial sampling, we favor depth dis-
continuities using an importance map computed from the
depth gradient magnitude, smoothed with a 5 × 5 box filter,
averaged over 4 × 4 pixel cells, normalized by its 99th per-
centile, and clipped to [0, 1]. A cell i with importance wi is
sampled with probability α/|Ω| + (1 −α)wγ
i / P
j∈Ωwγ
j ,
where Ωcontains valid cells, α = 0.1, and γ = 2. Pixels
are then sampled uniformly within the selected cells. For
temporal sampling, we draw the time offset ∆uniformly
from valid future frames within the 50-frame horizon.
A.2. Query targets
Spatial query targets.
The simulator provides depth
maps for all cameras, camera intrinsics, per-frame camera
extrinsics , and gripper poses.
Targets are computed on
the 224 × 224 canvas seen by the backbone, onto which
images are resized with padding. Depth maps are resized
with nearest-neighbor interpolation, which avoids interpo-
lating depth across object boundaries. We set s = 1.2 m
and clip normalized coordinates to [−1, 1]. For the real-
world setting, camera parameters and gripper poses are ob-
tained through hand-eye calibration and forward kinemat-
ics. Depth is obtained through MoGe-3 [13].

C.1. Robot platform
All real-world experiments are conducted on a dual-arm
PiPER-X mobile manipulator platform, as shown in Fig. 5.
Each arm consists of six joints and is equipped with
a parallel-jaw gripper controlled by a continuous-valued
gripper command.
Three synchronized Intel RealSense
D435 cameras—one head-mounted camera and one wrist-
mounted camera on each arm—are mounted on the plat-
form.
C.2. Data collection
Our data collection and deployment pipelines are built
upon LeRobot [3]. For each task, we collect 50 success-
ful teleoperated demonstration trajectories. Each trajectory
contains synchronized multi-view RGB observations with
size 640×480, robot proprioception and joint-space action
streams at 30 Hz. When required by a policy’s action rep-
resentation, we convert joint-space actions to end-effector-
space actions using forward kinematics.
C.3. Evaluation protocols
To control variation in scene initialization and enable paired
comparison across methods, we construct a fixed set of 25
evaluation configurations for each task and reuse the same
configurations for every evaluated method. Before every
rollout, the evaluator manually restores the objects to the
corresponding reference configuration using a visual lay-
out overlay, following the layout-replay protocol of Ro-
boDojo [5].
The evaluation configurations span diverse object po-
sitions, orientations, and relative arrangements within the
tabletop workspace visible to the head camera. These vari-
ations require policies to adapt their grasps and manipula-
tion motions to different initial layouts, including changes
in towel orientation, container placement, and the spatial ar-
rangements of mugs and bowls. Fig. 6 shows representative
configurations for all five tasks.
We report success rate (SR) and a task-specific progress
score (PS). Each trial is assigned a score in [0, 100] accord-
ing to the task-specific rubrics in Tab. 11. A trial is success-
ful if and only if it receives 100 points. SR is the propor-
tion of successful trials, and PS is the mean per-trial score
across the 25 evaluation configurations. Both metrics are
computed under the same fixed set of initial configurations
for all compared methods.
All methods are deployed using synchronous chunked
inference. At each policy query, the model receives the lat-
est observation and predicts an action chunk, which is exe-
cuted open-loop in its entirety. The next policy query then
uses the latest available observation. We use an action hori-
zon of H = 50 for the π-series VLA policies and H = 32
for WAM-based policies, at the control frequency of 30 Hz.

Fill Pen Holder
A pen holder and four pens, consisting of
two flat highlighters and two slim gel pens, are placed on the
table. The robot must pick up the holder and insert all four
pens into it. The holder is initialized in the left part of the
head camera’s field of view, while the pens are initialized in
the right part with randomized positions and orientations.
The prompt is Pick up the pen holder and place all the pens
into it.
Fold Towel
A flattened towel is placed on the table, and
the robot must perform two sequential folds to form a com-
pact rectangular shape using bimanual manipulation. The
required procedure consists of two steps. First, the right
arm holds down the right side of the towel while the left arm
grasps its lower-left portion and folds it in half. Second, the
left arm remains raised while the right arm lifts slightly and
opens its gripper, then grasps the middle of the towel’s right
side and folds it in half again. The prompt is Fold the towel.
Hang Mugs
Three mugs and a mug rack are placed on the
table, and the robot must pick up the mugs and hang them
securely on the rack. The prompt is Hang the mugs on the
mug rack.
Place Fruits
A strawberry and a persimmon are placed on
the table, and the robot must place both fruits into a bucket.
The prompt is Place both fruits into the bucket.
Stack Bowls
Three bowls are placed on the table, and the
robot must manipulate them one at a time to form a single
vertical stack at the center of the workspace. The bowls are
initialized with randomized left-to-right ordering and posi-
tions within the head camera’s field of view. The prompt is
Stack the bowls on the table.
