Abstract
World action models (WAMs) excel at robot manipulation
by imagining future observations and decoding actions from
them, yet remain confined to a single embodied agent. In
practice, manipulation often requires multiple robots to col-
laborate across disjoint workspaces, coupled timing, and
even heterogeneous embodiments. Such coordination de-
mands both spatiotemporal alignment of future interactions
and awareness of individual embodiment and role identities,
whereas existing decentralized per-robot policies, flat cen-
tralized models, and multi-robot VLAs fail to unite both. To
bridge this gap, we present MAWAM, a multi-agent world
action model that jointly predicts the future observations of
multiple embodiments and decodes each agent’s actions from
them for collaborative manipulation. Through an agent-axis
curriculum, MAWAM expands a foundational single-agent
WAM along an agent dimension to coordinate teams under
shared weights. Within one unified causal sequence, joint
self-attention spans all agents to spatiotemporally align their
imagined futures, while hierarchical language, identity en-
codings, and embodiment masks bind each stream to its role,
agent identity, and embodiment. We further characterize col-
laborative manipulation, formalizing multi-agent-necessary
tasks along three axes (why, when, and who) for systematic
study. Building on this formalism, we develop MA-Task Har-
ness, a coding-agent pipeline where mutually constraining
generators and evaluators co-evolve to interactively build
customizable simulation-data and evaluation packages. Us-
ing this pipeline, we design MA-Bench, following these
principles across all axes with seven tasks and pairing joint
success rate with individual role and collaboration scores
for fine-grained analysis. On MA-Bench, MAWAM reaches
82.7% mean joint success, 30.7% above the strongest evalu-
ated baseline, and achieves real-time closed-loop collabora-
tion on a real heterogeneous robot team.
1. Introduction
World action models (WAMs) [1, 23, 25, 54–56] advance
robot manipulation by coupling control with future visual

modeling, imagining how observations evolve to ground
actions in physical foresight. Yet existing WAMs remain
confined to a single embodied agent, whereas real-world
manipulation often requires multiple robots collaborating
beyond one agent’s limits.
These limits stem from what one agent can observe,
where it can reach, what it can actuate and so on, often
under tight temporal coupling (e.g. four-arm tray transport)
and across heterogeneous embodiments. Thus, the future is
no longer rolled out by one agent against a passive scene,
but co-authored across space, time, and distinct embodied
agents with specialized views, kinematics, and roles.
Existing multi-robot methods fall into three categories,
yet none unites spatiotemporal alignment of future inter-
actions with awareness of individual embodiment and role
identities. First, LLM planners [6, 30, 33, 44] negotiate sub-
tasks while delegating physical coupling to motion planners,
struggling with closed-loop contacts. Second, decentralized
policies [11, 13, 41] condition each robot on local views,
leaving partner cues invisible and failing to align coupled fu-
tures across agents. Third, centralized or coupled multi-robot
VLAs [53, 58, 62] map observations reactively to actions
via multi-arm prompting, peer feature exchange, or pooled
views, lacking visual foresight while blurring agent, role,
and embodiment identities.
Our key insight is straightforward: let a team imag-
ine together, then act individually (Fig. 1). We present
MAWAM, a multi-agent world action model that jointly pre-
dicts the future observations of multiple embodiments and
decodes each agent’s actions from them for collaborative
manipulation (Sec. 4). Through an agent-axis curriculum,
MAWAM expands a foundational single-agent WAM along
an agent dimension from single-role trajectories to synchro-
nized multi-robot episodes under shared weights. Within one
unified causal sequence, self-attention spans all agents to
spatiotemporally align their imagined futures, while hierar-
chical language, Fourier identity encodings, and embodiment
masks bind each stream to its role, identity, and embodiment
across homogeneous and heterogeneous teams.
Studying such collaboration also exposes a benchmark
gap: most manipulation suites focus on a single embodied
agent (e.g. RoboTwin [7, 35] and RoboCasa365 [36, 37]),
whereas multi-robot suites [33, 41] design collaborative tasks
case by case: they neither verify that a task is beyond any
single agent, nor systematically cover why collaboration
is needed, how tightly agents couple in time, and which
embodiments take part, while success-rate scoring cannot
separate individual role competence from genuine collabora-
tion. We therefore formalize multi-agent-necessary manipu-
lation (Sec. 3.1) along three tested axes: why collaboration is
needed (information, spatial, or capability complementarity),
when intervals couple (asynchronous, partially synchronous,
or synchronous), and who executes each role (homogeneous
or heterogeneous embodiments), paired with milestone met-
rics separating Role and Collaboration Scores from Success
Rate. Building on this formalism and capable coding agents,
we build MA-Task Harness (Sec. 3.2) to co-evolve simula-
tion generators and deterministic evaluators into customiz-
able data-synthesis and evaluation packages, upon which
we propose MA-Bench (Sec. 3.3) with seven tasks (700
certified pairs) across all axes. Our contributions are:
• We formalize multi-agent-necessary manipulation to char-
acterize genuine multi-robot collaboration for systematic
study. Grounded in this formalism, we develop MA-Task
Harness, a coding-agent pipeline where mutually con-
straining generators and evaluators co-evolve to interac-
tively synthesize customizable simulation-data and eval-
uation packages. Using this pipeline, we construct MA-
Bench, covering all three axes across seven collaborative
tasks and pairing joint success rate with individual role and
collaboration scores for fine-grained diagnosis (Sec. 3).
• MAWAM, a multi-agent WAM that jointly predicts spa-
tiotemporally aligned future observations across embodi-
ments and decodes each agent’s actions by its embodiment
and role identity via agent-axis expansion (Sec. 4).
• On MA-Bench, MAWAM reaches 82.7% mean SR
(30.7 points above the strongest evaluated baseline), while
detailed component ablations and analyses show how joint
imagination and agent-axis conditioning enable cross-
agent information sharing, 3-D future alignment, and
timely adaptation. Finally, it achieves real-time closed-
loop collaboration on a real heterogeneous robot team
(Sec. 5).
2. Related Work
Single-agent VLAs and WAMs.
Generalist VLAs map
observations and language to actions at scale [2, 3, 21, 22,
45], while WAMs ground control in foresight by modeling
future visual dynamics [1, 4, 14, 19, 23, 25–27, 50, 54–56].
Both lines extend to diverse morphologies through cross-
embodiment pretraining [10, 12, 48] and to two arms through
bimanual policies [20, 63], yet they still control a single
embodied agent per rollout. MAWAM instead expands a
single-agent WAM along an agent axis to model a team.
Multi-robot policies.
Beyond single bodies, LLM plan-
ners negotiate subtasks and waypoints [6, 8, 30, 33, 44]
while leaving physical coupling to motion planners. Learned
policies output low-level actions via constrained or decen-
tralized diffusion [11, 17, 41], identity-prompted VLAs [13],
multi-arm VLAs [53, 62], or arm-local VLAs with learned
peer interaction [58]. Yet all act reactively without visual
foresight, whereas MAWAM conditions actions on a jointly
imagined team future.
Multi-agent world models.
Conversely, existing multi-
agent world models separate simulation from manipula-
2


tion: video models predict multi-view futures given ac-
tions [9, 29, 34, 43, 51], MARL models learn game or
low-dimensional dynamics [15, 60, 61, 64], COMBO plans
high-level subgoals [59], and driving WAMs predict trajec-
tories [32, 40]. MAWAM instead jointly denoises multi-
manipulator visual latents and outputs closed-loop actions.

3.2. MA-Task Harness
Turning this formal definition into working tasks normally
demands heavy manual engineering of multi-robot planners,
timing windows, and matching evaluation functions, where
any mismatch yields uncertified demonstrations or flawed
scores. To automate this process, MA-Task Harness (Fig. 2)
is a coding-agent framework that converts a task idea into a
verified, reusable package in three stages.
(1) Specification via Why–When–Who. Starting from a
high-level idea (e.g. “put a pot into a spring-door cabinet”),
the coding agent clarifies the task with the user over several
rounds (Fig. 2a). Who: it fixes the team {Ek}, decomposes
role milestones Mk and drafts a global team instruction g and
per-robot role instructions ℓk. Why and When: it identifies
why a single agent fails (κ) and how the critical intervals cou-
ple, which defines Mcollab (e.g. the door stays held while the
pot is placed). When simulation reveals a physical bottleneck
(e.g. a doorway too narrow for IK), it returns a diagnosis with
a proposed fix. The dialogue yields a structured TaskSpec
with labels, team, prompts and milestones.
(2) Co-evolution of generation and evaluation. From the
TaskSpec, the harness spawns two sub-agents that refine
each other over rounds n = 1, . . . , K in a RoboTwin-derived

3.3. MA-Bench
Following Sec. 3.1 and using the MA-Task Harness, we
build MA-Bench, a suite of seven collaborative tasks (Fig. 3).
Asynchronous: in Joint bowl placing (spatial) one ALOHA
moves a bowl into a shared zone and the other carries it
to a goal outside the first robot’s reach; in Find common
color (information) two ALOHAs 2.7 m apart see disjoint
sets of blocks sharing exactly one color, which each must
pick on its own side. Partially synchronous: in Hold and
scan (spatial) one ALOHA presents a box while the other
aligns a scanner with it; in Open door, place pot (capability)
a single-arm Panda holds a spring-loaded cabinet door open
while an ALOHA carries a pot inside with both arms. Syn-
chronous (capability): two ALOHAs carry a tray with four
arms without toppling the stack on it (Lift tray, keep blocks)
or tilt it in unison to pour its contents into a bag (Lift tray,
pour food); in Pour and catch a Panda tilts a hopper while a
UR5 tracks the moving stream with a cup.
Across the suite, five tasks pair homogeneous ALOHAs
and two pair heterogeneous embodiments; in every task, each
robot records three RGB views (head and two wrists, with
missing single-arm wrist views masked), proprioception,
and native end-effector actions at 25 Hz. In total, MA-Bench
provides 700 accepted demonstration pairs (100 per task),
with every task’s axis labels certified by its evaluator’s ne-
cessity probes (details in Appendices A and C). MA-Bench
spans all temporal regimes, complementarity types, and team
compositions in closed loop (Tab. 4, Appendix C).

4.2. Architecture
Backbone. We build on LingBot-VA [25], an autoregres-
sive WAM on a video diffusion transformer [39, 46] with 30
blocks and about 5 B parameters. Each block applies self-
attention with 3-D rotary position encoding, cross-attention
to a frozen T5 text encoder [42] and an FFN; separate heads
predict video and action velocities. Both modalities are
trained with flow matching [28, 31]: for clean x, noise ϵ and
level σ, the model receives xσ = (1−σ)x+σϵ and regresses
u = ϵ −x, with an independent σf per latent frame as in
diffusion forcing [5].
Causal structure. Training uses teacher forcing with two
copies of every frame, a noisy prediction target and a clean
history. A stage index s = 2⌊f/C⌋+ µ, with µ=0 for
video and µ=1 for action, orders video before action within
a chunk. A query token q may attend to a key token v iff
|sq −sv| ≤W and

actions are an inverse-dynamics readout of the imagination.
The agent axis: joint causal attention.

robots as a token axis and shares all parameters θ across

projected by shared query/key/value maps, and processed by

never gates an attention edge, directly realizing Eq. (6):
(i) robot k’s noisy video attends to all robots’ clean histories
and same-stage noisy videos, denoising team futures jointly
into one imagination; (ii) robot k’s noisy actions attend to
all robots’ clean videos in the chunk, acting on partners’
imagined futures alongside its own. At N=1 this recovers
the backbone, with parameter count independent of N.

4.4. Closed-Loop Inference
At deployment, one MAWAM instance controls the team
in closed loop via a sliding joint KV cache (W=64 latent
frames) storing all robots’ clean history (Fig. 4b). Every
chunk (C=2 latent frames, 32 steps or 1.28 s) runs four steps:
(1) Imagine jointly: denoise all robots’ future video latents
together; (2) Act: cache imagined latents as clean tokens
and jointly denoise all robots’ action blocks, re-applying
mek each step; (3) Execute: map slots to native end-effector
commands; (4) Ground: overwrite the imagined chunk in
the cache with encoded real observations and executed ac-
tions. Benchmark evaluation uses 25/50 video/action steps;
an accelerated 4/8-step real-time setup with fused QKV pro-
jections, compiled RoPE, and cached text KVs runs in 0.72 s
per chunk on one GPU for pause-free 30 Hz control (details
in Appendices D and G).
5. Experiments
5.1. Setup
We evaluate seven policies across all seven MA-Bench
tasks (100 independent trajectory trials per task under syn-
chronous evaluation) on Role Score Sk, Collaboration Score
Scollab, and joint Success Rate SR (Sec. 3.1): (i) Per-robot
(π0.5 [21], LingBot-VA [25]/Stage 1), running independent
instances on each robot’s views, history, and role ℓk; (ii) All-
view (π0.5 (joint), LingBot-VA (joint)), pooling all views
and prompts into one flat stream—LingBot-VA (joint) thus
serves as a backbone-matched WAM control lacking our
agent axis; (iii) Multi-robot VLAs, covering centralized
decomposed-prompt MA-VLA [62] and peer-coupled arm-
local VLA-Fabric [58]; and our (iv) MAWAM. All methods
share identical demonstrations, action interfaces, and evalua-
tors (details in Appendices D and E).

5.3. Ablations and Analysis
Joint spatiotemporal imagination. As shown in Tab. 2(a),
removing cross-agent joint self-attention over the upcom-
ing chunk so that each robot denoises its future indepen-
dently from shared history Hf causes the policy to fail
(0.0% SR) across all three temporal regimes. Specifically,
Scollab drops to 0.0% on asynchronous Joint bowl placing
(77.0%→0.0%) and partially synchronous Open door, place
pot (94.5%→0.0%) because neither robot anticipates zone
clearance or door holding, while on synchronous Lift tray,
pour food, symmetric starts lift the tray (50.0% Scollab) be-
fore unaligned tilt predictions drift out of phase and spill
(91.0%→0.0% SR).
Hierarchical language and identity encoding.
Under
weight-shared agent streams, local roles and identities are
foundational: removing local roles ℓk (Tab. 2c) causes com-
plete failure across all three tasks, and retaining ℓk without
identity/role encodings (Tab. 2d) similarly collapses perfor-
mance, showing that the agent axis is vital to multi-robot
collaboration by binding each role and visual-action stream
to its corresponding embodiment branch. Meanwhile, global
instruction g (Tab. 2b) proves indispensable for unifying de-
coupled subtasks: in Joint bowl placing, agents act without
physical coupling, so removing g loses shared team intent
and halves SR (66.0%→30.0%, Scollab: 77.0%→40.0%),
whereas under physical coupling via distinct bodies or co-
grasps (Open door, place pot, Lift tray, pour food), the gated
global pathway stays non-intrusive.
Does MAWAM leverage other agents’ information? In
Find common color, each robot sees only its three blocks to
pick the color shared with its unseen partner; keeping own
initial views fixed, we recolor only the partner’s blocks to
shift the shared color (50 trials). In Fig. 6a, MAWAM flips to
the new target in 75.7% of solved scenes (82.0% accuracy vs.
33.3% chance), vs. 28.6% (36.0%) for LingBot-VA (joint)
and 0.0% w/o joint imagination, confirming that MAWAM
genuinely leverages information from other agents.

6. Conclusion
In this work, we present MAWAM, a multi-agent world
action model that jointly predicts the future observations
of multiple embodiments and decodes each agent’s actions
from them for collaborative manipulation. To systematically
study multi-robot collaboration, we formalize multi-agent-
necessary tasks along three axes (why, when, and who), build
MA-Task Harness to co-evolve customizable simulation-data
and evaluation packages via coding agents, and establish
MA-Bench across seven collaborative tasks with fine-grained
role and collaboration metrics. Extensive experiments quali-
tatively and quantitatively validate MAWAM’s effectiveness
over prior baselines, while component ablations and diagnos-
tic probes verify how joint imagination and agent-axis con-
ditioning drive cross-agent information sharing, 3-D future
alignment, and timely partner adaptation; finally, real-time
closed-loop deployment on a heterogeneous physical robot
team demonstrates reliable real-world collaboration.

A. Task Definition: Verification Probes
The why–when–who labels in Sec. 3.1 are operationally de-
fined by deterministic verification probes executed in sim-
ulation by each task package’s evaluator over the expert
generator, rather than assigned by informal inspection.
Necessity and complementarity (why). Single-agent fea-
sibility Feas(V ; E) is probed by re-planning the task for a
single robot with the task’s expert planner under the un-
changed goal predicate V . A task satisfies multi-agent ne-
cessity (Eq. (2)) when the team expert succeeds while every
single agent fails alone. To identify the complementarity
source κ (Eq. (3)), the evaluator applies three single-agent
relaxations in order of increasing physical substitution cost,
O ≺W ≺U: (i) RO exposes the privileged state observ-
able from the partner’s cameras (e.g. block colors on the
far side of the table) while leaving reach and actuation un-
changed; (ii) RW additionally permits re-placing the single
agent’s base across the union of both workspaces between
motion segments; (iii) RU additionally grants the partner’s
effectors, payload, and simultaneous contact budget to one
planner. The first relaxation that restores single-agent fea-
sibility determines κ: Information (κ=O, Find common
color), Spatial (κ=W, Joint bowl placing and Hold and
scan), or Capability (κ=U, the remaining four tasks).
Temporal coupling (when).
To probe temporal overlap,
the evaluator first serializes the team expert so that agent j
finishes its critical phase and retreats to rest before agent k
begins. Tasks that remain solvable under serialization are
asynchronous (Joint bowl placing, Find common color). Oth-
erwise, the evaluator performs a delay scan by holding the
first agent at its commanded pose while delaying the part-
ner’s critical phase by δ: if success persists across arbitrary
waiting delays, the task is partially synchronous (Hold and
scan, Open door, place pot); if success breaks beyond a nar-
row tolerance δ⋆of a few control steps, an instant-by-instant
physical constraint holds and the task is synchronous (Lift
tray, keep blocks, Lift tray, pour food, Pour and catch).
Embodiment (who) and scope.
A team is homoge-
neous when both robots share identical URDFs, actuation
spaces, and camera configurations, and heterogeneous oth-
erwise; for heterogeneous teams, a role-swap probe verifies
Feas(Mk; Ej) = 0, certifying that labor division is imposed
by physical embodiment rather than language alone. By
design, Eq. (2) excludes tasks where a second robot merely
accelerates a single-agent-feasible job (e.g. parallel table
clearing), where per-robot policies already suffice. When
a task contains multiple interaction segments, it is labeled
by its strongest temporal coupling, and each MA-Bench
task is constructed around a single complementarity type
for unambiguous evaluation. Whereas classical multi-robot
task-allocation taxonomies [16, 24, 38] classify high-level
assignment and precedence, our three axes characterize the
physical necessity, timing constraints, and body heterogene-
ity faced directly by a learned low-level policy.
B. MA-Task Harness Details
B.1. TaskSpec
A TaskSpec is the single source of truth shared by the
generation pipeline G(n) and evaluation pipeline E(n). List-
ing 1 shows an abridged specification for Open door, place
pot. Every predicate declared in role milestones and
collab milestones is compiled into a deterministic,
LLM-free detector over simulator state (contacts, poses, joint
angles, and velocities); the exact same detectors gate demon-
stration acceptance and score learned policies.
B.2. Acceptance Checks and Probes
Physical checks. A candidate demonstration is rejected if
any of the following fails: (i) no robot–robot, robot–fixture,
or self-collision outside whitelisted grasp contacts; (ii) in-
verse kinematics is continuous (no joint-branch jump be-
tween consecutive control steps) and strictly within joint
limits; (iii) grasps form real finger–object contacts (both
arms for bimanual holds) that persist throughout transport
without pose overwriting or synthetic attachments; (iv) all
objects remain physically supported and at rest in the termi-
nal window.
Logical checks and necessity probes. (v) every role mile-
stone in Mk is reached in causal order within its critical inter-
val ϕk; (vi) every collaboration milestone in Mcollab holds
across its entire shared window ϕjk (e.g. the spring-loaded
door stays above its angle threshold throughout insertion);
(vii) the terminal goal predicate V holds. A trajectory is

B.3. A Detailed Example of Co-Evolution
Fig. 8 illustrates a complete four-round co-evolution trace
of the generation sub-agent G(n) and evaluation sub-agent
E(n) on the heterogeneous task Open door, place pot, where
a single-arm Panda opens and holds a spring-return cabinet
door (hinge stiffness 0.35 N·m/rad, damping 0.6) while a
dual-arm ALOHA lifts a two-handled kitchen pot into the
cabinet.
Round 1 (v0→v1): top-down grasp slip.
Although the
initial 60◦top-down pinch grasp (native grasps) pro-
duced nominal open-loop demonstrations, E(1)’s closed-
loop physical audit diagnosed severe millimetre fragility:
under 5.6 mm hand offset and +7.7 mm hand spread, the left
fingers lost pot contact at 27.36 s, pot tilt spiked to 44.6◦–
78.9◦, and both fingers closed on empty air. In response,
G(1) replaced the top-down pinch with a RoboTwin contact-
frame two-sided handle grasp (side grasps at 0.9 rad),
introduced +y staging to clear the held door, and added
joint-limit filtering to the Panda IK solver.
Round 2 (v1→v2): aperture clearance and floor over-
hang. Auditing the new side-grasp trajectories, E(2) caught
four geometric failures: (i) at 0.9 rad wrist orientation,
ALOHA’s wrist cameras exceeded the 44 cm cabinet open-
ing by −2.5 mm (left) and −0.7 mm (right); (ii) lateral ap-
proach caused a −0.05 mm SAT overlap with the held door at
147.6◦; (iii) center-only placement at yrel=4.5 cm left the pot
mesh overhanging the cabinet floor by −1.1 mm; (iv) low-
ering directly to shelf height triggered left-arm IK fail-
ure. G(2) added continuous exterior TCP wrist compaction
(0.9→1.5 rad over 120 quintic steps, opening +10.0 mm /
+11.5 mm aperture margins and +6.5 mm door gap), shifted
the layout and placement target to yrel=2.5 cm, and switched
to low gravity release at z=0.83 m (+9.8 mm lowest-vertex
clearance above the shelf floor).
Round 3 (v2→v3): bidirectional counter-audit (G→E).
Co-evolution is strictly bidirectional. When v2 rejected
physically valid nominal trajectories (e.g. seed100000
with +21.9 mm floor clearance and 0.0 m/s terminal speed),
G(3) counter-audited E(3) and surfaced two evaluator bugs:
(i) the schema validator asserted (4, 4) camera extrinsics
whereas SAPIEN exports native (3, 4) matrices; (ii) legacy
demo timeline checks matched hard-coded expert phase
strings, falsely failing trajectories with shifted execution tim-
ing. E(3) fixed the (3, 4) contract, separated physical rejec-
tions from infrastructure faults, and replaced phase strings
with a phase-independent physical carry window (verifying
door angle > 140◦and continuous Panda finger contact
across every carry frame, 654/654 frames, min 147.6◦). Si-
multaneously, G(3) added a bounded 50-step guarded Carte-
sian IK fallback for short screw-plan segments under ±2 mm
layout perturbations.
Round 4 (v4): held-pot leveling and convergence.
Fi-
nally, E(4) flagged 2.1–2.6 cm/s terminal micro-oscillation
on perturbed seeds that failed the 50-frame (2.0 s) continu-
ous rest check. G(4) added exterior shared-SE(3) held-pot
leveling (reducing carry tilt to ≤0.15◦under 60/60 bilateral
contact) and a bounded settling window until 50 consecutive
zero-contact rest frames aligned to the 16-tick VAE grid.
The converged v4 package froze 100 accepted demonstration
pairs (62.08 s, 98 latent frames, zero collisions, full-mesh
front-floor clearance +20.6–22.5 mm, and 0.0 m/s terminal
drift).

C. MA-Bench Details
C.1. Simulation and Recording
Tab. 4 compares MA-Bench against existing collaborative
benchmarks across the When–Why–Who taxonomy. MA-
Bench is built on RoboTwin 2.0 [7] and the SAPIEN en-
gine [52]. Physics runs at 250 Hz and control at 25 Hz.
Expert motions are planned in end-effector space and ex-
ecuted through a motion planner; learned policies are exe-
cuted through the same controller interface. Three embod-
iments are used: the dual-arm ALOHA-AgileX platform
(two 6-DoF arms with parallel grippers), the 7-DoF Franka
Panda, and the 6-DoF UR5. Every robot records three RGB
views. A dual-arm robot records a head camera and two wrist
cameras; a single-arm robot records a head (third-person)
camera and its wrist camera, and its missing second wrist
view is filled with a black image and masked out of the
loss (Appendix D). Each demonstration pair stores, for both
robots and on a common clock, the RGB streams, joint po-
sitions, end-effector poses, gripper states, native-dimension
end-effector actions, the hierarchical prompts, and the full
simulator state used by the evaluator.

C.3. Instructions
Each task provides one global team instruction g and one
role instruction ℓk per robot. The stored prompts are written
in Chinese; English translations of two representative tasks
are as follows. For Lift tray, pour food: g = “The two robots
put everything into the garbage bag together.”; ℓA = “You are
robot A. Grasp the side of the tray near you with both arms;
the tray holds an apple, a carrot and a cucumber. Together
with robot B, lift the tray, move it to the table edge, tilt it
to pour all three items into the open bag, and keep holding
the tray until the food has settled.” (ℓB is symmetric.) For

arm places the pot.”; ℓA = “Open the cabinet door and hold
it open.”; ℓB = “Lift the pot with both hands and place it
inside the cabinet.”
C.4. Training Data and Evaluation Protocol
Training uses the 100 accepted demonstration pairs per task
listed in Tab. 5. For evaluation, we perform 100 independent
trajectory trials per task directly in the simulator: each
trial instantiates a fresh scene configuration (randomized
object poses, colors, and identities distinct from the training
demonstration trajectories) and rolls out the learned policy in
closed loop under the task’s deterministic evaluator. Before
benchmarking policies, we verify that the scripted expert
solves the evaluation layouts. Because joint success is binary,
we report 95% Wilson score confidence intervals alongside
point estimates (Appendix F).

D. MAWAM Implementation Details
D.1. Token Layout and Masks
Video tokens. Each robot’s three RGB views are resized
and tiled onto one canvas before VAE encoding: the head
view occupies the top 16×20 latent cells and the two wrist
views occupy the two 8×10 cells below it, producing a la-
tent zk
f ∈R48×24×20 per latent frame (48 channels) that is
patchified with a 1×2×2 kernel into 120 tokens. Any view
absent for a single-arm embodiment is encoded from a black
image and excluded from the video loss by a spatial view
mask. Video is sampled at stride 4 from the 25 Hz control
stream (6.25 Hz) and the causal video VAE compresses time
by 4×, so one latent frame spans 16 control steps.
Action tokens. Actions are end-effector targets: per arm,
a 3-D position, unit quaternion, and gripper command. The
backbone’s shared 30-D action slot layout places two 7-D
arm blocks at channels 0–6 and 7–13 and two gripper chan-
nels at 28 and 29. Dual-arm robots (ALOHA) use channels
{0, . . . , 13, 28, 29} (16 active); single-arm robots (Panda,
UR5) use {0, . . . , 6, 28} (8 active). Inactive channels are
zeroed in clean actions, injected noise, noisy/conditioning
inputs, flow targets, and losses, and are re-zeroed after ev-
ery denoising step at inference; without re-zeroing, noise in
unused slots leaks through attention and causes jitter. Poses
are expressed relative to the first-frame end-effector pose,
normalized to [−1, 1] via per-task 1%/99% quantiles, and
clipped. Each control step forms one action token (16 action
tokens per latent frame).
Sequence.
For robot k and latent frame f, the sequence
comprises four token groups: noisy video, clean video, noisy
action, and clean action. Over F latent frames, each robot
contributes L = 2F(120 + 16) tokens during training and
the joint sequence has length N·L. All robots share iden-
tical 3-D rotary coordinates (frame, height, width) and are
distinguished solely by the fixed identity and role encodings
of Sec. 4.2.

D.5. Real-Time Configuration
Consistent with Sec. 4.4 and Appendix G, our real-time de-
ployment modifies only the sampler and attention kernels
without altering model weights: (i) 4 video and 8 action Euler
steps paired with asynchronous parallel decoding; (ii) fused
QKV linear projections and a compiled 3-D RoPE kernel
in every self-attention block; (iii) episode-level caching of
global and local text keys and values; (iv) sliding joint KV
cache with CUDA Graphs so each denoising step processes
only the current chunk. On a single GPU, this reduces me-
dian chunk inference latency from 3.92 s (25/50 steps) to
0.72 s (5.4× speedup), enabling pause-free closed-loop real-
robot execution (Appendix G).
