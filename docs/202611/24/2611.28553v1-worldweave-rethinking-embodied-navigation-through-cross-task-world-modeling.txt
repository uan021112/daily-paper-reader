## Abstract 

Mobile embodied agents continuously alter their observa-tions through their own motion, while external dynamics further shape future interactions. Different mobile embod-ied tasks therefore capture complementary patterns of state evolution, yet jointly learning world dynamics across such interactions remains less explored. We investigate this prob-lem through Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) and Embodied Visual Tracking (EVT), which respectively in-volve navigation toward a stationary goal and interaction with a moving target. We propose WORLD WEAVE , a cross-task world model that couples shared predictive dynamics with task-specific adaptation. Instead of conditioning state transitions on heterogeneous physical actions, WORLD -WEAVE learns latent actions from observed state changes via inverse-forward dynamics, establishing a shared tran-sition representation across tasks. The resulting future-state predictions are adaptively integrated into task-specific representations for downstream decision making. Experi-ments across multiple VLN and EVT benchmarks demon-strate consistent improvements over specialized baselines. 

## 1. Introduction

Effec-tive mobile decision making therefore requires not only un-derstanding the current interaction state, but also anticipat-ing how that state may evolve as the agent acts. The dynam-ics underlying this evolution can vary substantially with the task goal and interaction setting. While an agent’s own mo-tion continuously changes its visual observations and spatial relationships with the environment, a moving target intro-duces an additional source of state variation. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) [1, 22] and Embodied Visual Tracking (EVT) [37, 50] represent two complementary set-tings that capture these different aspects of mobile embod-ied interaction. In VLN, an agent grounds natural-language instructions in visual observations and navigates toward an instruction-specified goal that remains fixed within an episode. Its state evolution is primarily driven by the agent’s 1arXiv:2611.28553v1 [cs.CV] 21 Nov 2026 own motion as it progresses through environmental struc-tures over extended trajectories. EVT, in contrast, requires a mobile agent to actively follow a moving target through continuous perception and control, where the evolving in-teraction state is jointly shaped by the agent’s movement and the target’s independent motion. Although VLN and EVT differ in task objectives and in-teraction patterns, both involve mobile agent-environment interaction. In VLN, state evolution primarily reflects the agent’s progression through environmental structures over extended trajectories [41, 49], whereas in EVT, it addition-ally involves the continuously changing agent–target rela-tionship shaped by both agent and target motion [40]. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (a), the two tasks offer complementary perspectives on embodied state evolution. Together, they provide an opportunity to study world modeling beyond the dynamics of either task alone. Despite their distinct goals and action spaces, can these related interaction patterns be leveraged to learn shared predictive dynamics? A natural framework for modeling such predictive dy-namics is a world model [16], which learns how latent states evolve through interaction. Conventional world models typ-ically model state transitions conditioned on predefined ac-tions, i.e. , p(st+1 | st, at) [17, 18]. While well suited to individual tasks, extending it across heterogeneous tasks introduces additional challenges, as the actions condition-ing state transitions may differ across task settings [44]. In our setting, directly conditioning a shared world model on the heterogeneous actions of VLN and EVT would tie its learned dynamics to task-specific action definitions. Latent-action learning provides an alternative by inferring latent transition variables from state changes encoded in consec-utive observations, rather than relying on predefined action labels [6, 14]. This motivates a shared transition represen-tation across heterogeneous embodied tasks. Establishing such a transition representation, however, does not determine how the shared dynamics should sup-port each task. VLN and EVT rely on predictive dynamics differently for downstream decision making, while naive sharing across heterogeneous tasks can introduce interfer-ence and negative transfer [20, 21]. Our preliminary exper-iments make this issue explicit. As shown in Fig. 1 (b), us-ing a single shared adapter preserves VLN performance but yields little effective tracking capability for EVT. Introduc-ing task-specific adapters substantially recovers EVT per-formance, whereas directly incorporating the shared world representation into VLN’s decision pathway degrades navi-gation performance. These observations suggest that effec-tive dynamics sharing requires not only a common transi-tion representation, but also task-specific adaptation of the shared predictions to the distinct representations and deci-sion processes of each task. Motivated by these observations, we propose W ORLD -WEAVE , a cross-task world-modeling framework that cou-ples shared predictive dynamics with task-specific adapta-tion for VLN and EVT. During pretraining, W ORLD WEAVE 

learns state transitions through inverse–forward dynamics: latent actions are inferred from state changes encoded in consecutive observations and used to predict subsequent states. Because these latent actions characterize observed state transitions rather than task-specific physical actions, the learned dynamics are not tied to either task’s action def-inition and can be pretrained from videos without action an-notations. To enable prediction without access to future ob-servations at inference time, the model further learns to in-fer these latent actions from current interaction context. The resulting future-state predictions are then adaptively inte-grated into each task’s representation, allowing shared pre-dictive dynamics to support distinct downstream decision processes. In this way, W ORLD WEAVE shares how state transitions are modeled while preserving how predictive in-formation is used for task-specific decision making. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We investigate cross-task dynamics sharing in embodied navigation, focusing on Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) and Embodied Visual Tracking (EVT) as two complementary interaction settings with distinct forms of state evolution and decision making. • We propose W ORLD WEAVE , which couples shared la-tent dynamics with task-specific predictive adaptation. It learns latent transitions from state changes in consecutive observations without relying on task-specific action defi-nitions, while adapting shared future-state predictions to distinct task representations and decision processes. • Extensive experiments across VLN and EVT benchmarks show consistent improvements over task-specific base-lines, supporting the effectiveness of cross-task dynamics learning across complementary embodied interactions. 

## 2. Related Work 

Vision-and-Language Navigation. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires agents to follow natural-language instructions through visual environments, evolv-ing from discrete navigation graphs to continuous envi-ronments [1, 22]. Subsequent methods incorporate spatial representations and observation history for navigation rea-soning [9, 10, 29, 38], while recent LLM/VLM-based ap-proaches improve instruction understanding and action pre-diction [7, 12, 54, 55]. Predictive modeling has also been explored, e.g., NavMorph [41] learns latent state transitions to anticipate future navigation states. 

Embodied Visual Tracking. Embodied Visual Tracking (EVT) extends active visual tracking to embodied agents that actively follow moving targets through egocentric per-ception and closed-loop control [37, 50, 51]. Recent meth-ods leverage vision–language–action models for target un-2derstanding and motion decision making [28, 31, 37], while USS [40] introduces latent predictive dynamics for tempo-ral modeling. Despite predictive modeling in both VLN and EVT, existing approaches model their dynamics separately, leaving cross-task dynamics sharing largely unexplored. 

World Models. World models learn predictive state dynam-ics for sequential decision making, traditionally through action-conditioned latent transitions [16–18]. Recent latent-action approaches instead infer transition variables from temporal visual changes, enabling dynamics learning with-out predefined action labels [6, 14, 26, 43]. World models have also been applied to embodied tasks such as naviga-tion and tracking [8, 40]. Beyond individual tasks, recent work has explored multi-task world modeling through mod-ular latent dynamics, as exemplified by Mixture-of-World Models [44], which combines task-conditioned dynamics experts with a shared backbone. While this approach orga-nizes multi-task dynamics through modular specialization, WORLD WEAVE focuses on sharing predictive dynamics across complementary embodied interactions, while retain-ing task-specific state representations and predictive adap-tation to accommodate distinct decision processes. 

Unified Embodied Models. Recent generalist embod-ied models aim to accommodate diverse tasks and em-bodiments within shared architectures or control inter-faces [5, 13, 15, 19, 32, 35]. In navigation, NavFoM jointly addresses multiple navigation and tracking tasks, while Qwen-RobotNav unifies navigation settings through a shared waypoint-prediction interface [46, 48]. These efforts explore cross-task generalization through shared models or task interfaces. In contrast, W ORLD WEAVE investigates the sharing of predictive dynamics across complementary mo-bile embodied tasks, while retaining task-specific adapta-tion to their distinct temporal and decision requirements. 

## 3. Our Approach 

We present W ORLD WEAVE , a framework that jointly learns predictive world dynamics across Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) and Embodied Visual Tracking (EVT) while preserving their task-specific state representations and decision processes.

Framework Overview. VLN and EVT exhibit comple-mentary patterns of state evolution, but their different action semantics and parameterizations make conventional action-conditioned dynamics p(sτt+1 | sτt , aτt ) [16–18] difficult to share directly across tasks. Rather than aligning their het-erogeneous action spaces, W ORLD WEAVE represents state changes through a latent action zt, providing a shared tran-sition space for modeling state evolution across tasks with-out directly conditioning on task-specific physical actions. As illustrated in Fig. 2, we first learn the shared dynam-ics from diverse temporally ordered videos without requir-ing task-specific action annotations. Despite their hetero-geneous motions, these videos share a common learning signal: how the represented world state changes over time. WORLD WEAVE encodes these changes into a shared latent transition space, allowing heterogeneous video transitions to jointly supervise the same transition model. Given con-secutive visual representations, the model infers a latent ac-tion from their temporal change and learns how the repre-sented state evolves under this latent action. Since future observations are unavailable during online decision mak-ing, we further learn a causal latent-action predictor that es-timates zt using only information available up to the current step. The resulting predictive dynamics can therefore antic-ipate future states without accessing future observations at decision time. We detail the learning of these shared dy-namics in Sec. 3.1. The predicted future state is then in-corporated differently into each task. For VLN, W ORLD -WEAVE combines the predicted state with historical con-text to support navigation, whereas for EVT, it supports structured estimation of target position, ego-trajectory, and continuous control. These task-specific integration mech-anisms are described in Sec. 3.2, followed by the learning objectives and training procedure in Sec. 3.3.

Learning Future-Informed Latent Actions. During dy-namics learning, consecutive observations provide direct evidence of the transition that occurred. We leverage this information through an inverse dynamics model I, which takes the contextual representations before and after the transition to parameterize a future-informed posterior over the latent action: zt ∼ q (zt | ht, ht+1 ) , zt ∈ RNz ×dz . We refer to zt as a latent action , which characterizes the ob-served transition between consecutive states. Here, q de-notes the posterior over the latent action, parameterized by the inverse dynamics model I. We then use this latent ac-tion to model how the current state evolves into the subse-quent state via the forward dynamics model F, which pre-dicts st+1 ∼ pF (st+1 | st, zt).The inverse and forward dynamics therefore form a cou-pled transition-learning process: I identifies the transition from consecutive observations, while F models how that transition evolves the current state. Importantly, zt is not re-quired to recover the task-specific physical action executed between the two observations. Across tasks, these latent actions lie in a shared transition space Z, allowing F to be shared without aligning the heterogeneous physical ac-tion spaces of VLN and EVT. Meanwhile, sτt remains task-specific, so sharing F does not require a unified state rep-resentation or assume identical state evolution across tasks. 3.3. Two-Stage Joint Learning 

We train W ORLD WEAVE in two stages: shared dynamics learning and task adaptation. In the first stage, we learn shared predictive dynamics from heterogeneous real-world and simulated videos without requiring physical-action an-notations, enabling the model to capture state-transition pat-terns across diverse embodied interactions. In the second stage, the learned dynamics are transferred to VLN and EVT through lightweight task-specific adaptation using em-bodied task data. This two-stage design separates general dynamics learning from downstream task adaptation, allow-ing the shared dynamics to be reused while preserving the distinct state representations and decision processes of each task. Details of data composition and training configura-tions are given in Appendix A of Supplemenary Material. 

Stage I: Shared Dynamics Learning. We first learn the shared world dynamics introduced in Sec. 3.1 from hetero-geneous real-world and simulated videos, without requir-ing physical-action annotations.This stage jointly adapts the pretrained vision-language backbone [3] and learns the dy-namics modules using LDyn in Eq.(5), which optimizes state prediction, representation reconstruction, and latent-action alignment. During training, latent actions are sam-pled via reparameterization from the posterior and causal prior distributions, while the KL divergence aligns the causal prior with the posterior. The posterior latent action is used to learn the shared forward dynamics F, while the causal latent action is used to reconstruct the future obser-vation representation. After Stage I, the inverse dynamics model I and representation decoder d are discarded. The causal latent-action predictor C and shared forward dynam-ics model F are retained to predict future states using only information available at the current step. 

Stage II: Task Adaptation. We incorporate the pretrained world dynamics into VLN and EVT through the task-specific adaptation described in Sec. 3.2. The dynamics modules are frozen, while the vision-language backbone is adapted via separate LoRA adapters for each task, along with their respective action heads. Although both tasks share the same predictive dynamics, their learning objec-tives reflect their distinct decision processes. ## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate WORLD WEAVE on VLN and EVT tasks through comparisons with existing methods, ablations of cross-task dynamics sharing and task-specific adaptation, and real-world robot deployment. More details, experi-ments, and further analyses are given in Appendix. Datasets. We evaluate on R2R-CE [2, 22], RxR-CE [23], 

VLN-PE [36], and VLNVerse [27] for VLN, and EVT-Bench [37], UnrealZoo [53], and Gym-UnrealCV [33] for EVT. Training details are provided in Appendix A.

Evaluation Metrics. For VLN, we report Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), Navigation Error (NE), Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (NDTW), and Success weighted by NDTW (SDTW) [1]. For EVT, we report Success Rate (SR), Tracking Rate (TR), and Col-lision Rate (CR). Details are provided in Appendix B.3 .

4.2. Ablation Studies 

What Should Be Shared Across Tasks? A central de-sign choice in W ORLD WEAVE is to share predictive dy-namics while preserving task-specific representations and adaptation. We first establish two single-task references: the task-specific baselines without explicit world model-ing (Baseline), and their corresponding variants augmented with independently trained task-specific dynamics models (Ind-WM), which incorporate C and F without cross-task parameter sharing. We then examine four joint-training variants with different sharing boundaries: (a) a shared LoRA adapter, (b) task-specific LoRA adapters, (c) task-specific LoRA adapters combined with shared causal latent-action predictor C and forward dynamics model F, and (d) WORLD WEAVE , which further incorporates task-specific predictive adaptation Rτ . As shown in Table 4, inde-pendently trained world models (Ind-WM) improve both tasks over their respective baselines, demonstrating the ben-efits of predictive modeling even without cross-task shar-ing. For VLN, Ind-WM improves SR / SPL from 46.9 / 41.9 to 48.1 / 45.7. For EVT, it substantially improves SR from 41.1 to 60.1, while also increasing TR from 71.2 to 72.3. These results establish the benefits of predictive modeling within individual tasks, motivating further investigation into cross-task knowledge sharing. Among the joint-training variants, (a) favors VLN but severely degrades EVT ( e.g. , SR / CR to 29.1 / 25.1), while (b) restores EVT performance at the cost of VLN. These suggest that sharing task representations alone does not guarantee beneficial cross-task transfer. In contrast, adding shared C and F in (c), while retaining task-specific repre-sentations, improves the success rates of both tasks over (b), supporting the potential of shared predictive dynamics as a basis for cross-task knowledge transfer. Finally, (d) further improves upon (c) through task-specific predictive adapta-tion Rτ , achieving the best VLN performance and the high-est EVT tracking success and tracking ratio. 4.3. Discussion 

Recent methods in the gray rows achieve higher absolute performance on some metrics, but at substantially larger training scales ( ∼8M–15.6M samples versus 500 hours of video and 2.5M task samples). We therefore focus on the controlled question of what should be shared across het-erogeneous embodied tasks . Our results suggest that shar-ing representations induces cross-task trade-offs, whereas sharing predictive dynamics with task-specific adaptation improves both VLN and EVT over their single-task base-lines. This provides evidence that predictive dynamics can be shared without requiring unified task representations or decision processes. More importantly, the design naturally lends itself to scaling: Stage-I can exploit heterogeneous videos without unified action annotations, while Stage-II 8accommodates task-specific adaptation. Due to computa-tional constraints, we leave larger-scale training to future work, which will investigate whether such scaling further improves transferability, particularly to distinct embodied interactions such as manipulation. 

4.4. Real-World Robot Experiments 

We further deploy W ORLD WEAVE on a TITA wheel-legged robot for both VLN and EVT in previously unseen in-door environments. As shown in Fig. 3, the robot per-forms closed-loop navigation and tracking under progres-sively challenging instructions and target-motion patterns, achieves 66.7% and 66.0% SR on VLN and EVT, respec-tively, compared with 46.0% and 48.7% for the matched baselines. Detailed task protocols, and results are provided in Appendix D of Supplemenary Material. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We introduced W ORLD WEAVE to investigate shared pre-dictive dynamics across heterogeneous embodied tasks, us-ing VLN and EVT as complementary testbeds. By coupling shared dynamics with task-specific adaptation, our method consistently outperforms single-task baselines. These re-sults demonstrate the potential of cross-task world model-ing for learning reusable dynamics while accommodating distinct decision requirements. Our findings offer a differ-ent perspective on embodied navigation: rather than learn-ing task-specific dynamics independently, related embodied tasks can provide complementary interaction experiences for learning shared predictive dynamics. As embodied mod-els expand to diverse tasks, key questions arise: which com-ponents can be shared, and how should shared predictions be adapted to different interaction patterns? Extending this approach beyond mobile embodied tasks to scenarios such as manipulation remains an important future direction. We will release our code and models upon acceptance.
