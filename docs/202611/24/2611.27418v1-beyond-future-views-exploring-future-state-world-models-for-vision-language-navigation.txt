Abstract 

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) requires an embodied agent to follow language instructions through unseen 3D envi-ronments, where the target spaces often lie beyond the current observation. Navigation world models address this challenge by predicting future views either as pixels or latent represen-tations, providing valuable look-ahead information for action decision. Both paradigms define the future primarily through what the agent may observe, raising a fundamental ques-tion: is predicting future views the ultimate form to represent the future for VLN? Cognitive studies show that the human hippocampus prospectively represents both future goals and intervening locations along planned routes. Inspired by this predictive-map view, we organize the navigation-relevant fu-ture around goal-directed spatial states and route structure. We instantiate this perspective with two complementary abstrac-tions: a short-horizon waypoint that specifies the next local state to reach, and a mid-horizon corridor that captures the spatial support of the upcoming route. Based on this design, we propose FS-Nav, a future-state world modeling framework that predicts waypoint-corridor states from spatial memory and language instructions, and communicates them to a VLM navigator through top-down and first-person interfaces. Ex-periments on standard VLN benchmarks demonstrate that FS-Nav achieves state-of-the-art performance. This highlights navigation-relevant future-state modeling as a promising di-rection for representing the future in VLN. 

## Introduction 

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) (Anderson et al. 2018; Ku et al. 2020) requires an embodied agent to follow natural-language instructions and navigate to a target location in previously unseen 3D environments. Advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have driven remarkable progress in VLN through their strong multimodal understanding and reasoning capabilities. Recent methods (Wei et al. 2025b; Zhang et al. 2024b; Wu et al. 2026) therefore adopt VLMs as navigation backbones, directly mapping visual observations

Despite these advances, directly predicting actions from current observa-tions remains largely reactive (Li et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2026), without explicitly anticipating what may lie ahead. Navigation world models address this limitation by pre-dicting possible future outcomes before action selection. Early approaches synthesize future egocentric views at the pixel level, providing explicit look-ahead signals for naviga-tion (Bar et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025a). Such fine-grained generation requires predicting every visual detail, which is difficult to anticipate and may be unnecessary for down-stream decisions. More recent methods (Yao, Gao, and Xu arXiv:2611.27418v1 [cs.RO] 20 Nov 2026 2025; Assran et al. 2025) move beyond pixel reconstruction by predicting future visual representations in latent space. By abstracting away low-level appearance variations, they focus prediction on more stable, high-level visual content. These advances make world models a powerful foundation for em-bodied intelligence. Despite this progression, both pixel- and latent-level approaches define the future through observa-tions. We wonder whether predicting what the agent will see is the ultimate form to represent the future in VLN. A principled answer is suggested by predictive cogni-tive maps. Brown et al. found that the human hippocampus prospectively represents both future goals and intervening locations along the planned route during goal-directed nav-igation (Brown et al. 2016). Predictive-map theory further describes a spatial state through the future states expected to be occupied under a behavior policy (Stachenfeld, Botvinick, and Gershman 2016). Together, these findings suggest that navigation can organize the future around goal-directed spa-tial states and route structure, rather than only future views. Inspired by this perspective, we represent the navigation-relevant future at two complementary horizons: a short-horizon waypoint that specifies the next local state to reach, and a mid-horizon corridor that captures the spatial sup-port of the upcoming route. Together, they form a compact, goal-conditioned abstraction of future navigation states, de-scribing where the agent should move next and how the route should unfold, as illustrated in Figure 1(a). Based on this representation, we propose FS-Nav, a future-state world modeling framework for VLN. FS-Nav consists of three components: a Future State World Model predicts waypoint-corridor states from the agent’s accumulated spa-tial memory and language instruction; a Future State Ren-derer converts the predicted future state into two visual in-terfaces: a top-down canvas for explicit spatial planning and a first-person visual overlay for egocentric grounding. Fi-nally, a Future-State-Guided Navigator consumes the result-ing guidance together with visual observations and the in-struction to predict navigation actions. This design separates what the world model predicts from how the prediction is vi-sually grounded for navigation. As previewed in Figure 1(b) and (c), this compact representation is both efficient to pre-dict and effective for navigation. Our contributions are summarized as follows. 

• Drawing inspiration from predictive cognitive maps, we introduce a cognitively grounded perspective on naviga-tion world modeling that organizes the anticipated future around goal-directed spatial states and route structure. 

• We propose FS-Nav, which instantiates this perspective with a short-horizon waypoint and a mid-horizon cor-ridor, predicts them from spatial memory and language instructions, and renders them through top-down and first-person interfaces to guide a VLM navigator. 

• Extensive experiments on R2R-CE and RxR-CE demon-strate that FS-Nav achieves state-of-the-art performance, validating the effectiveness of navigation-relevant future-state modeling for VLN. 

## Related Works 

Vision-Language Navigation. 

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) requires an embod-ied agent to follow natural-language instructions in unseen environments (Anderson et al. 2018; Lyu et al. 2026c). Earlier methods rely on modular perception and planning pipelines (Li et al. 2026b) or select actions from prede-fined navigable candidates (An et al. 2024). Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have enabled more uni-fied navigation agents that directly map language instructions and visual histories to actions (Wei et al. 2025b,a; Li et al. 2026a). These methods improve embodied decision making through stronger temporal modeling, instruction grounding, and action prediction. However, they primarily reason from observations collected so far to determine what to do next, making navigation largely reactive. The spatial structure of the upcoming route is not explicitly modeled before action generation, but remains implicit within the VLM’s reasoning process. In contrast, FS-Nav explicitly predicts the upcoming navigation space as a waypoint–corridor future state, provid-ing the VLM navigator with predictive route guidance before action generation. 

Waypoint- and Route-Guided VLN. 

Continuous VLN methods commonly introduce intermediate spatial outputs to bridge instruction following and low-level control. Learned waypoint methods predict local navigable candidates or motion targets that are selected by a naviga-tion policy and executed through a controller (Krantz et al. 2021; Hong et al. 2022; An et al. 2024; Lyu et al. 2026b). Recent approaches extend this formulation through image-grounded goals, short trajectories, or global route prediction. Goal2Pixel (Bao et al. 2026) predicts a visible goal pixel that is back-projected into a waypoint and tracked by a local plan-ner, while NavOne (Zhan et al. 2026) predicts global path and goal distributions from top-down maps. FS-Nav also pre-dicts waypoint–corridor geometry, but assigns it a different role. Rather than treating the prediction as the navigation decision or a directly executable plan, FS-Nav represents it as a multi-horizon future state that provides predictive con-text to a closed-loop VLM navigator. This design makes the upcoming route explicit while preserving the VLM’s ability to adapt its actions to the instruction, real observations, and navigation history. 

World Models in VLN. 

World models enable navigation agents to anticipate pos-sible outcomes before acting. Pixel-level world models ex-plicitly synthesize future views. PathDreamer (Koh et al. 2021) generates observations at unvisited viewpoints, while Navigation World Models (NWM) (Bar et al. 2025) pre-dict action-conditioned egocentric videos. Although intu-itive, pixel-level generation requires reconstructing high-dimensional appearance details that may be redundant or irrelevant to navigation. We propose FS-Nav, a future-state world modeling frame-work that makes the upcoming route explicit for VLN. As illustrated in Fig. 2, FS-Nav contains three components: a Future State World Model, a Future State Renderer, and a Future-State-Guided Navigator.

Navigation Future-State Representation 

Waypoint. The waypoint Wt specifies the next local tar-get. We follow the remaining expert trajectory and select the first position whose cumulative path distance reaches a short-horizon distance dw; if the remaining trajectory is shorter, we use its endpoint. After transforming this target into the agent-centered frame, we rasterize it as a Gaussian heatmap HWt ∈ [0 , 1] H×W . This provides smoother spa-tial supervision than directly regressing a single coordinate. The predicted waypoint is recovered from the heatmap using differentiable soft-argmax (Liu et al. 2025b). 

Corridor. A waypoint indicates where to move next, but not how the route continues beyond it. We therefore de-fine a corridor Ct using the subsequent future trajectory between the waypoint horizon dw and a mid-horizon dis-tance dc, subject to a maximum number of future steps or the end of the episode. The trajectory is transformed into the agent-centered frame and rasterized as a soft path band 

HCt ∈ [0 , 1] H×W . We additionally retain the ordered points 

P Ct = ( pt, 1, . . . , p t,N ) for direct path supervision. We rep-resent the corridor as Ct = ( HCt , P Ct ), where the path band captures its spatial support and the ordered points preserve route continuity and direction.

Future State Renderer 

The world model predicts route geometry, whereas the nav-igator operates on images and text. The Future State Ren-derer connects the two without predicting new information. It deterministically converts the same metric waypoint and corridor into one of two visual inputs for the navigator. 

Top-down canvas. The canvas shows the current agent pose, accumulated local map, predicted waypoint, and pre-dicted corridor in a bird’s-eye view. It is appended to the observation sequence as an additional image. This view ex-poses relative position, free space, and route shape directly, making it suitable for spatial planning. 

First-person overlay. Using the current depth observation and camera geometry, we project the waypoint and corridor into the current RGB view. The projection is drawn over the observed image and passed through the navigator’s visual stream. Unlike future-image generation, this interface pre-serves the real observation and adds only route guidance. It links the prediction to visible passages, doors, and obstacles in the current view. Both interfaces contain the same short- and mid-horizon route information. The canvas emphasizes top-down ge-ometry, whereas the overlay emphasizes egocentric visual grounding. Comparing them isolates the effect of visual ref-erence frame without changing the future-state target. 

Future-State-Guided Navigator 

The navigator is built on Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct (Bai et al. 2025) and receives the instruction, observation history, cur-rent observation, and future-state interface Zkt . For the can-vas variant, the top-down canvas is appended as the final image. For the first-person variant, the overlaid RGB frame is supplied through the visual stream. The action space and supervision remain the same for both variants. Following our VLN backbone, the navigator autore-gressively generates a textual action sequence At =(at, 1, . . . , a t,L ). The generated tokens are parsed into exe-cutable forward, turning, and stop commands. We train the navigator with supervised language modeling (Zhang et al. 2024b; Wu et al. 2025a):

## Experiments 

Experimental Setup 

Datasets and Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate FS-Nav on VLN-CE benchmarks using the validation-unseen splits of R2R-CE (Krantz et al. 2020) and RxR-CE (Ku et al. 2020). Following prior works (Zhang et al. 2024b; Zhu et al. 2026), we report Navigation Error (NE), Oracle Success Rate (OSR), Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) and normalized Dynamic Time Warping (nDTW). 

Implementation Details. For future-state construction, we set the waypoint horizon to dw = 1 .5 meters and the cor-ridor endpoint to dc = 3 .0 meters, with at most 20 future steps. These distances are chosen to favor visibility from the agent’s current viewpoint: a shorter waypoint horizon often places the target below the camera’s field of view, whereas corridor geometry beyond 3.0 meters is more likely to be occluded or outside the visible range. We train the Future State World Model on R2R, RxR, and ScaleVLN training trajectories for 20 epochs using AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, a batch size of 16, and cosine decay. We set 

λw = 0 .5, λdice = 0 .5, λcl = 0 .5, λtv = 0 .1, λpts = 1 .0,and λcurv = 0 .1. After training, the world model is frozen throughout navigator training. The navigator is initialized from Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct and takes up to eight histori-cal RGB observations together with the current observation and language instruction to autoregressively predict textual action chunks. We first train it for one epoch on R2R and RxR, and then use the resulting checkpoint to collect DAgger tra-jectories on their training splits. Finally, we continue training the same navigator on the combined R2R, RxR, ScaleVLN, and DAgger data to obtain the final model. Navigator training uses a learning rate of 2 × 10 −5, an effective batch size of 128, a linear schedule, bfloat16 precision, and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2. Unless otherwise specified, we use both future-state interfaces. Depth, odometry, and predicted semantics are used only to update the spatial map.

For controlled and re-producible comparisons, we vary one factor at a time while keeping the remaining settings unchanged and use fixed ran-dom seeds throughout training and evaluation. Pred →Pred is exposed to such prediction errors during training and is therefore more robust at inference. GT →GT achieves 72.3 SR, showing that accurate waypoint–corridor states can provide highly informative guidance. The gap to Pred →Pred suggests that future-state prediction quality re-mains a major bottleneck.

## Conclusion 

In this work, we explored a cognitively inspired perspective on navigation world modeling that organizes the anticipated future around goal-directed spatial states and route struc-ture. We instantiated this perspective with FS-Nav, which predicts a short-horizon waypoint and a mid-horizon cor-ridor from spatial memory and language instructions and communicates them to a VLM navigator through comple-mentary visual interfaces. Experiments on standard VLN benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of this compact representation, highlighting navigation-relevant future-state modeling as a promising direction for VLN.
