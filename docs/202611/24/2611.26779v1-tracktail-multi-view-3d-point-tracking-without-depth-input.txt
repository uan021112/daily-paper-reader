# Abstract 

We introduce Tracktail, a novel architecture designed to efficiently track query points in 3D from multiple calibrated views without any depth input. Our architecture separates the task as a per-camera video encoding of features and a per-query decoder. Notably, camera cross-attention is only performed across queries in the decoder, allowing for ef-ficient inference (7 FPS and less than 4 GB memory for 10 views and 100 query points). We further introduce a per-camera scene scale computed from camera geometry, which allows us to normalize inputs and predictions appro-priately across a vast variety of dataset scales. We train on a synthetic Multi-View Kubric dataset as well as a novel Multi-View Point Odyssey benchmark dataset, which con-sists of synthetic renders of humans and animals across 499 frames. Tracktail achieves state-of-the-art results on the Panoptic Studio, DexYCB, and Multi-View Kubric bench-marks, outperforming methods that rely on estimated depth. Tracktail also demonstrates a strong ability to maintain ac-curate tracking over longer ranges compared to existing methods. Our code, data, and weights are publicly avail-able: https://ai-hhmi.github.io/tracktail/ 

# 1. Introduction 

Despite recent developments in 2D point tracking, long-term tracking of arbitrary points in 3D remains a funda-mental problem in computer vision. Multi-View 3D point tracking has many applications such as dynamic scene re-construction, robotics, and pose estimation. Current 3D tracking approaches typically rely on having arXiv:2611.26779v1 [cs.CV] 19 Nov 2026 access to depth information[27]. While methods like VGGT [29] or DUSt3R [31] can be used to acquire depth estimates, they are computationally expensive and often quite noisy, which can greatly hinder tracking performance. Existing methods are also unable to robustly predict on datasets with different units and scene scales, requiring arbitrary scaling per-dataset [27]. To overcome these challenges, we developed Tracktail, a multi-view 3D point tracking model that does not take any depth input and robustly handles various scene scales. Our model takes a multi-view video, corresponding camera pa-rameters, and arbitrary query points as input (Fig. 1). Apretrained video encoder encodes the frames independently per view to obtain a latent scene representation, which is then passed through a decoder. The decoder leverages cam-era self-attention across views and temporal self-attention across frames, to produce predictions for 2D and 3D posi-tions, visibility, depth, and confidences. Our key contributions are as follows: • We introduce Tracktail, a depth-free multi-view 3D point tracker with an encoder-decoder architecture • We robustly handle the scene normalization problem by introducing a scene scale parameter computed from cam-era parameters and query points • We establish a new benchmark dataset for 3D tracking, Multi-View Point Odyssey, to assess long-term tracking • We demonstrate that our model achieves state-of-the-art results on existing benchmarks in both positional and oc-clusion accuracy. • We show that inference with our model is both faster and more memory efficient than existing 3D point tracking methods and scales better with the number of views 

# 2. Related Work 

Datasets. Many point tracking benchmarks have rela-tively short test sequences, which limits the extent to which long-term tracking can be assessed. For example, DexYCB and Multi-View Kubric only have 24 frames. Panoptic Stu-dio has slightly longer sequences at 150 frames. The Point Odyssey benchmark [37] was created specifically to pro-vide a long range tracking training dataset and benchmark for 2D point tracking, with the test videos averaging 2000 frames. More recently, Kubric-1024 was established as an-other long-range training dataset [15]. While Point Odyssey and Kubric-1024 are great for assessing long-range 2D point tracking, there is not yet an established benchmark to assess long-range multi-view point tracking. We extend the Point Odyssey generation pipeline [37] to introduce a new benchmark dataset for 3D tracking, Multi-View Point Odyssey, consisting of human and ani-mal datasets. The dataset consists of synthetic scenes with naturalistic motion sourced from motion capture. The test sequences are 499 frames, making it a useful benchmark for advancing long-term tracking. We trained our model on this new benchmark in addition to Multi-View Kubric, en-abling our model to surpass state-of-the-art performance on real-world benchmarks. 

2D Point Tracking. 2D point tracking methods have greatly advanced over the past several years [6, 16, 17, 30, 35, 37]. One of the earliest 2D tracking methods, based on persistent independent particles (PIPs), was limited to an 8-frame window due to the MLP-mixer component of the architecture [12]. PIPs++ removes this 8-frame con-straint by replacing the MLP-mixer with a CNN and adds a template-update strategy to account for changing appear-ance in objects over time, enabling longer range tracking [37]. CoTracker introduces an efficient transformer archi-tecture and tracks points jointly to leverage spatial and tem-poral dependencies [16]. CoTracker3 simplifies CoTracker, improving efficiency and reducing the amount of data re-quired to train the model [17]. LocoTrack introduces a local 4D correlation to improve point correspondence, addressing point drift and ambiguities that are commonly encountered when tracking regions of uniform color or texture [6]. 

3D Point Tracking. More recently, developments have been made in 3D point tracking for both monocular and multi-view approaches [10, 22, 27, 32–34]. SpatialTracker extends CoTracker by using monocular depth estimators to perform tracking in 3D through a triplane representation [32]. SpatialTrackerV2 [33] and TAPIP3D [34] also take monocular approaches, the latter utilizing spatio-temporal feature clouds. Dynamic 3DGS leverages 3D gaussian splatting to reconstruct scenes and track points in 3D [22], but requires optimizing over a large number of cameras (e.g. 27). MVTracker leverages depth and a 3D feature point cloud for multi-view 3D point tracking in a setup with a practical number of cameras (e.g. 4) [27], but re-quires tuning arbitrary scene scaling factors empirically per dataset. D4RT uses an encoder-decoder architecture to en-code a global scene representation and decode the 3D posi-tion of a 2D coordinate at a desired target time and cam-era [35]. Most recently, ARROW takes inspiration from D4RT’s query formulation to introduce a multi-view point tracker that incorporates global context [10]. This is done through a global attention layer where every query attends to the full encoded context, making it computationally ex-pensive [10]. We extend the D4RT architecture to produce an efficient multi-view 3D point tracker that does not rely on depth in-formation. Our method also directly addresses the scene scale problem by internally applying scene normalization that allows our model to robustly predict 3D positions and occlusions on a variety of datasets regardless of scale. 3. Methodology 

3.1. Multi-View Point Odyssey Dataset 

We extend the Point Odyssey generation pipeline [37] to render each scene (Fig. 2) from 9 static cameras placed around the subject, with ground-truth 3D trajectories and per-camera visibility for every surface point. The human dataset consists of Mixamo characters [2] that are rigged with AMASS motions [23], and the animal dataset con-sists of DeformingThings4D animal models and animation sequences [21] with random fur textures from BlenderKit. Each scene has a random HDRI environment and distractor objects. Random forces are applied to these objects to push them around throughout the video. The human dataset has 113 training and 8 test sequences, and the animal dataset has 210 training and 10 test sequences. Each test sequence is 499 frames long and comes with 512 evaluation tracks stratified by speed. See Appendix A for further details.

Our architecture extends D4RT [35] to multi-view 3D point tracking (Fig. 3). A video encoder E encodes each video 

Vc independently into a scene representation Fc = E(Vc).We use the V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L/16 encoder [25] on 24-frame clips at 256 × 256 pixels, with hierarchical features pro-jected to 512 dimensions. A query encoder Q produces an embedding zcn,τ = Q(qn, t n, τ, Mc, Ictn ) for each camera 

c, query point n, and target time τ , from the query’s pro-jection into the camera, an image patch around it, its depth relative to sc, the camera intrinsics, the query and target times, and an optional per-camera occlusion state. A decoder D with 6 transformer blocks then processes the embeddings. Each block applies camera self-attention across views, temporal self-attention across target frames, and cross-attention from each view’s queries to that view’s scene tokens. Camera self-attention uses camera ray encod-ings rcn as relative positional encodings, following PRoPE [20, 36]. Camera self-attention is the only place where in-formation is exchanged between views, and different query points never attend to each other, so the model can track an arbitrary number of points.

The same network also accepts 2D pixel queries in a single view, which we use as an auxiliary train-ing task. See Appendix B for further details. 3.5. Inference on long videos 

At inference, each view is cropped to a box around the pro-jections of the query points, with a 20 pixel margin and a minimum size of 256 pixels. The crop is resized so that its longer side is 256 pixels and zero-padded to 256 × 256 , and the intrinsics and crop offsets oc are updated accordingly. Longer videos are processed in sliding windows of 24 frames that overlap by 8 frames. Each point is first queried in the window that contains its query frame. In each follow-ing window it is re-queried at the last overlapping frame, using the previous window’s 3D prediction as the query po-sition. Each window is re-cropped around the current query positions, expanded by the points’ recent velocity so that fast-moving subjects stay in view. Points predicted to be in-visible are excluded from the crop. For re-queried points, the occlusion state input is set from the previous window’s per-camera visibility predictions, thresholded at 0.1. 

# 4. Experiments 

4.1. Datasets 

Training data. We train only on synthetic data from three multi-view datasets with static, calibrated cameras: (i) Multi-View Kubric (our renders) [11, 27]: 4,989 scenes with 10 cameras, 24 frames each, at 512 × 512 (ii) Multi-View Point Odyssey Human: 113 sequences, and (iii) Multi-View Point Odyssey Animal: 210 sequences. We sample 24-frame training clips with a temporal stride of 1, 2, or 4. Datasets are balanced by the number of available clips. 

Evaluation data. We evaluate on the three multi-view benchmarks of MVTracker [27], using its four input cam-eras and its fixed track sets: Multi-View Kubric (30 test se-quences of 24 frames, 512 tracks each), DexYCB [5] (10 sequences of 24 frames, 384 tracks each), and Panoptic Studio [13] (6 sequences of 150 frames, 384 tracks each). Each track is queried at the frame given by the bench-mark. To assess long-range tracking, we introduce Multi-View Point Odyssey. It consists of the test splits of hu-man (8 sequences) and animal (10 sequences) datasets, each 499 frames long, with 512 tracks per sequence stratified by speed. Each track is queried at the first frame where it is visible in the cameras used. We evaluate with a subset of 4 cameras (views 2, 4, 5, and 7) and with all 9 cameras. None of these benchmarks provide depth or visibility as input to Tracktail. 

4.2. Evaluation Metrics 

We follow the evaluation protocol of MVTracker [27] and compute all metrics in 3D world coordinates, on the frames after each track’s query frame. A point is visible if it is visible in at least one input camera. We report the average Jaccard (AJ), which jointly measures position and visibility. We also report the position accuracy δavg , averaged over a set of distance thresholds per dataset, the occlusion accu-racy (OA), and the median trajectory error (MTE). On the long Point Odyssey sequences, we also report the survival rate of tracks over time. Each method uses its own visibility threshold (0.1 for Tracktail, see Appendix C for details). 

4.3. Training 

Losses. The training loss combines Huber losses on the fused and per-camera 3D predictions, normalized by the scene scale, with auxiliary Huber losses on the per-camera 2D, depth, and reprojected 3D predictions, cross-entropy losses on the 3D, 2D, and depth bins, binary cross-entropy losses on the per-camera and overall visibility, and a tem-poral smoothness penalty. The full loss and its weights are given in Appendix D.2. 

Optimization. We use Muon [14] for the weight matri-ces of the transformer blocks and schedule-free AdamW [7] for all other parameters. Both use schedule-free averaging, weight decay 0.002, a short linear warmup, and no learning rate decay. We train in two stages with PyTorch Lightning Fabric and distributed data parallelism in fp32, with one clip per GPU per iteration. We count iterations as the total num-ber of clips processed across all GPUs: 1. Pretraining. The V-JEPA encoder is frozen and we train the query encoder and decoder for 800k iterations on 4 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, which takes 49 hours. The learn-ing rate is 10 −3.2. Finetuning. We unfreeze the encoder and train end-to-end for 2.3M iterations on 8 H100 GPUs, which takes 8 days. The learning rate is 2.5×10 −4, with a 0.25 × learn-ing rate for the encoder, and Multi-View Kubric clips are upweighted 3×.In total, training takes about 1,700 H100 GPU-hours. The model has 351M parameters: 308M in the video encoder and projection, 6M in the query encoder, and 36M in the de-coder. We also leverage sampling and augmentations during training to improve model robustness (see Appendix D). 

4.4. Comparison to Existing Methods 

We compare Tracktail against monocular and multi-view 3D trackers on Panoptic Studio, DexYCB, and Multi-View Kubric in Tab. 1. All methods use the same cameras, tracks, and query frames. We ran the following methods with our evaluation code: • MVTracker [27] with its released weights, using either ground-truth depth or depth estimated per frame with DUSt3R, with the camera parameters fixed to the ground truth. For DexYCB, the ground-truth depth is the Kinect sensor depth. • ARROW [10]. ARROW predicts depth up to scale, so we fit its scale to the ground truth once per 24-frame window and provide the ground-truth pose of the first camera. • CoTracker3 baseline , a depth-free baseline that we con-struct. We project each 3D query into every view, track it in 2D with CoTracker3 [17] (offline model), and trian-gulate the per-view tracks with linear triangulation using the views where it is predicted visible. If fewer than two views see the point, no prediction is made. See Supple-mentary Tab. 5 for an analysis of these failures. We also compare against the above methods on Multi-View Point Odyssey (Tab. 2). 

Results on existing benchmarks. Table 1 shows the eval-uation results on Panoptic Studio, DexYCB, and Multi-View Kubric with 4 input cameras. While our tracker tends to achieve only slightly better OA in comparison to ex-isting methods, our model especially advances position-based metrics on these real-world benchmark datasets, set-ting a new state-of-the-art on Panoptic Studio and DexYCB. Notably, Tracktail achieves higher performance than MV-Tracker with GT depth on both Panoptic Studio and DexYCB despite not taking any depth input. It also per-forms considerably better than MVTracker with DUSt3R depth on all 3 existing benchmarks, emphasizing that noisy depth estimates can limit tracking performance. While MVTracker with GT depth achieves the highest performance on Multi-View Kubric, our model comes close while still performing considerably better than other exist-ing methods. This makes sense, as MVTracker purely trains on Multi-View Kubric, while we train on a combination of Multi-View Kubric and Multi-View Point Odyssey. Train-ing on the latter allows our model to generalize better to real world datasets, making a slight drop in performance on Multi-View Kubric a reasonable trade-off. Figure 4 shows a qualitative comparison between Track-tail and the baseline methods on DexYCB and Panop-tic Studio that corresponds to the results in Table 1. On DexYCB, the CoTracker3 baseline produces erratic trajec-tories, MVTracker’s trajectories don’t follow the proper di-rection of motion, and ARROW fails to track certain points. Our tracker produces point trajectories that match closest to the ground truth, visually showcasing Tracktail’s strong performance on DexYCB. For Panoptic Studio, the Co-Tracker3 baseline doesn’t capture the full range of motion, MVTracker produces very shaky trajectories, and ARROW shows some point drift in comparison to the ground truth. Tracktail also exhibits a little point drift, though this is ex-pected since the videos are 150 frames. 

Results on Multi-View Point Odyssey. Table 2 reports the performance of Tracktail and baseline methods on the Multi-View Point Odyssey test set. Tracktail outperforms all baseline methods for all metrics. This is somewhat ex-pected, as we were the only one who trained on Multi-View Point Odyssey. ARROW struggled on existing benchmarks (Tab. 1), but it generally outperforms the other baselines on this dataset, achieving an MTE of 3.1 on the animal set with all cameras (Tab. 2). This could be in part due to ARROW’s training mixture, which includes the original single-view Point Odyssey dataset [10]. MVTracker with GT depth consistently performs better than MVTracker with DUSt3R depth, again highlighting the importance of depth-free point tracking methods. In Appendix F and Supplementary Fig-ures 6 and 7, we report survival rate on Multi-View Point Odyssey further quantify the survival of points over the 499-frame test videos. 

Comparative speed and memory usage. Supplementary Figure 8 compares the inference speed and memory usage of Tracktail with the baseline 3D tracking methods. For 4 views and 100 query points, Tracktail processes 75 × more frames per second compared to MVTracker with DUSt3R depth and 3.5 × more frames per second compared to AR-ROW (Fig 8). Tracktail also maintains the lowest peak memory usage across all baseline methods, only slightly increasing with the number of camera views. In contrast, memory usage scales linearly with the number of views for MVTracker with DUST3R depth and ARROW. Figure 9 shows that Tracktail’s memory usage is primarily driven by the number of query points, while inference speed only slightly decreases. These results demonstrate that Tracktail is more efficient than existing multi-view 3D point tracking methods in terms of both speed and memory.

# 5. Discussion and Future Work 

One limitation of our method is that it does not account for camera movement, making accurate tracking on moving camera datasets difficult to achieve (Tab. 8). We have gen-erated moving camera versions of Multi-View Kubric and Multi-View Point Odyssey and plan to add moving cam-era support to Tracktail. Expanding our training set to in-clude these datasets will hopefully improve robustness and performance on moving camera datasets from TAPVid-3D (Appendix Sec. G). While Tracktail demonstrates a higher ability for long-range tracking on Multi-View Point Odyssey compared to baseline methods, there is still room for improvement. Highly accurate tracking is necessary for most applications, so reducing point drift is important. Memory-based en-coders and cross-attention modules have proven useful in the segmentation space, so they could be useful for multi-view point trackers too [4, 15, 28]. We hope that releasing the Multi-View Point Odyssey benchmark enables more de-velopment to improve long-range point tracking. Many point tracking models are trained on purely syn-thetic data. While this is great for generating large datasets with accurate ground truth, there is still a gap between syn-thetic and real-world data distributions that limits the per-formance of these point tracking models on real-world data. We are currently using our pretrained model as a founda-tion for finetuning on a diverse collection of real-world an-imal datasets. We are planning for this model to be part of a framework that will reduce the amount of manual key-point annotation that is needed to get accurate pose track-ing and advance the field of multi-view animal tracking and pose estimation. Even without finetuning on real-world an-imal datasets, Tracktail shows promising results on Rat7M (Fig. 1) that make us hopeful for the future of this field. 

# 6. Conclusion 

Tracktail is a multi-view point tracker that accurately tracks 3D query points without depth input. Our method advances the state of the art by enabling tracking on datasets that don’t have ground truth depth without relying on inaccu-rate depth estimates. Additionally, it addresses the scene normalization problem with a per-camera scene scale com-puted from the camera geometry, allowing for robust pre-dictions regardless of dataset or scene scale. We also in-troduce the Multi-View Point Odyssey benchmark dataset, which consists of naturalistic human and animal data for measuring the long-range tracking performance of 3D point trackers. We hope that our model and this benchmark will advance the field for long-term tracking developments and push tracking performance on real-world data even further.

# Appendix 

# A. Multi-View Point Odyssey details 

Rendering and assets We extend the Point Odyssey gen-eration pipeline [37] and follow their protocol to collect data assets for the subjects, environments, scenes, and objects. Scenes are rendered in Blender 3.3 from 9 static cameras placed around the subject, at 960 × 540 pixels and 30 fps. For every surface point, we export its ground-truth 3D tra-jectory and its visibility in each camera. The pipeline pro-duces ground truth depth and segmentation masks as well. For the human dataset, we downloaded a 108 humanoid characters from Mixamo [2] and 11 of the 24 AMASS datasets [23]. The generation pipeline randomly selects one of these characters and animates them with SMPL-X [26] motions from a random AMASS sequence. For the an-imal dataset, we downloaded DeformingThings4D, which contains 26 animal models and 1,772 animation sequences [21]. For each scene, the generation pipeline randomly sam-ples an animal model, applies one of 17 fur textures from BlenderKit [1], and concatenates 15 randomly sampled an-imation clips into one long motion. For the scenes, we downloaded a collection of 220 4k in-door and outdoor HDRI environment maps from PolyHaven [3]. The generation pipeline randomly selects an environ-ment as well as 5 distractor objects from Google Scanned Objects [8] and PartNet [24]. Throughout each video, the objects are pushed by random forces to ensure movement. 

Dataset splits The human dataset has 113 training se-quences (249–2,034 frames) and 8 test sequences. The an-imal dataset has 210 training and 10 test sequences. Each animal training sequence is a 256-frame window taken from the full rendered sequence of 589–2,815 frames based on the span of 256 frames with the most 3D motion. All test sequences are 499 frames long. 

Track cleaning We found that some generated tracks move extremely fast and out of the scene, so we removed them from the dataset. A track is removed if, in any cam-era, its projection moves more than 100 pixels between con-secutive frames or its depth falls below 1 scene unit. It is also removed if it moves farther from the scene center than 20 times the median distance of points to the center in the first frames. As a result, we discarded a median of 0.2% of tracks per human sequence and 75% per animal sequence. 

Evaluation tracks We select 512 tracks per test sequence, stratified by speed. Eligible tracks are visible in at least one of the 4 evaluation cameras (views 2, 4, 5, and 7), in both frames of at least 50 consecutive-frame pairs. We rank them by their mean 3D speed over visible frames. We then sample 128 slow tracks (below the 25th percentile), 256 medium tracks (25th to 75th percentile), and 128 fast tracks (75th to 95th percentile). We excluded the fastest 5% of tracks because they are much faster than the majority and likely dominated by tracking artifacts. The tracks are used for the 4-camera and 9-camera evaluations. 

# B. Architecture details 

Video encoder. Each view is processed independently by the V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L/16 encoder as a clip of 24 frames at 

256 ×256 pixels, giving 12 ×16 ×16 spatio-temporal tokens per view (tubelet size 2, patch size 16). We use hierarchical features: the outputs of blocks 6, 12, 18, and 24 are con-catenated and linearly projected to 512 dimensions. We add a learned spatial positional embedding to the projected to-kens. Temporal position is provided by rotary embeddings in the decoder cross-attention. 

Query encoder. The query encoder computes an embed-ding independently for each camera, query point, and target time. For camera c, the query point qn is projected into the image, and we embed: (i) a 9 × 9 pixel patch around the projection in frame tn, encoded with a small CNN, (ii) the normalized 2D projected position, (iii) the log of the query’s distance to the camera divided by sc, (iv) the camera’s prin-cipal point and focal length, normalized by the image size, (v) the query time tn, the target time τ , and their difference 

τ − tn, (vi) whether the projection lies inside the image, and (vii) a per-camera occlusion state at the query frame (visible, occluded, or unknown). Continuous quantities are embedded with Fourier features followed by a linear layer, and discrete ones with learned embeddings. The resulting terms are combined by a learned gated sum, in which per-term sigmoid weights are predicted from all terms, followed by an MLP that produces the 512-dimensional embedding 

zcn,τ .

Occlusion state. The occlusion state lets the model use visibility information when it is available. In all reported experiments it is set to unknown at the query frame, and it is only filled in from the model’s own predictions when tracking across windows. During training it is randomly set to unknown with probability 0.5. 

2D queries. The same network also accepts 2D pixel queries in a single view, which we use as an auxiliary train-ing task. For 2D queries the depth term is replaced by a learned token. The decoder is conditioned on the query type (2D or 3D), and each query type has its own output heads. 

Decoder. The decoder consists of 6 blocks with width 512 and 8 attention heads. Each block applies camera self-attention, temporal self-attention, query-scene cross-attention, and a feed-forward MLP (expansion ratio 4). For camera self-attention, the embeddings {zcn,τ }Cc=1 of each query point and target time across all cameras attend to each other, with the ray encodings rcn as relative positional en-codings as in PRoPE [20]. For temporal self-attention, the embeddings of all target times of each camera and query point attend to each other, with 1D rotary position embed-dings over the frame index. For query-scene cross-attention, the query embeddings of camera c attend to all tokens of its scene representation Fc. We apply 1D temporal rotary em-beddings to the queries (by target time) and keys (by token frame), so that attention is biased by the relative time be-tween the target frame and each video token. 

Output heads. After the last block, the per-camera em-beddings are passed through separate linear heads: • 3D : the motion of the point relative to its query position, expressed in a ray-local frame aligned with the camera’s central ray and normalized by sc times the image width, with 1024 logarithmically spaced bins per axis. • Depth : the log-depth along the ray, relative to scfc.• 2D : the absolute pixel position in the view (256 bins per axis). • Visibility : a per-camera visibility logit. • 3D fusion weight : a per-camera logit. The 3D motion is mapped back to world coordinates and added to the query position to obtain the per-camera 3D pre-diction pcn,τ . The fusion weights are not supervised directly and are learned end-to-end through the 3D losses. 

Bin decoding. All positional heads are per-axis classi-fiers, decoded by a soft-argmax restricted to a window of 60 bins around the argmax, with a learned temperature. The 3D head predicts the motion of the point relative to its query position in a ray-local frame of the camera’s cen-tral ray. The motion is divided by scW , where W = 256 

is the input width, and binned with 1024 bins per axis over 

[−1.8, 1.8] . The bins are uniform in the signed-log space 

sign( x) log(1 + |x|/0.1) , and the soft-argmax is computed in that space. The depth head predicts log( d/ (scfc)) with 1024 uniform bins over [−2.5, 2.5] , where d is the distance along the ray and fc the focal length of the resized crop. The 2D head predicts the absolute pixel position in the 

256 × 256 input with one bin per pixel. Besides the fused direct 3D prediction, two auxiliary 3D estimates are formed for supervision only. The first back-projects the per-camera 2D predictions along their rays to the predicted depths and averages them with per-camera 2D weights. The second triangulates the per-camera 2D predictions with the same weights. 

# C. Evaluation metrics 

We follow the evaluation protocol of MVTracker [27] and compute all metrics in world coordinates. A point is con-sidered visible at a frame if it is visible in at least one of the input cameras. Each track is evaluated only on the frames after its query frame. 

Position accuracy ( δavg ). δavg is the fraction of visi-ble frames whose 3D error is below a threshold, av-eraged over a set of thresholds: {1, 2, 5, 10 , 20 } cm for DexYCB, {5, 10 , 20 , 40 } cm for Panoptic Studio, 

{0.05 , 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8} scene units for Multi-View Kubric, and {0.3, 0.6, 1.2, 2.4, 4.8} scene units for Multi-View Point Odyssey. 

Occlusion accuracy (OA). OA is the fraction of frames in which the predicted visibility matches the ground truth. 

Average Jaccard (AJ). AJ jointly measures position and visibility. At each threshold it is TP /(TP +FP +FN ), where a true positive is a frame that is predicted visible, visible in the ground truth, and within the threshold. AJ is averaged over the same thresholds as δavg .

Median trajectory error (MTE). MTE is the median 3D distance between the predicted and ground-truth positions over the visible frames of a track. The units used for MTE on each dataset are described at the end of this section. 

Survival rate. For the long Point Odyssey sequences we also report the survival rate: the fraction of tracks whose 3D error has not yet exceeded a threshold at any visible frame up to time t.

Aggregation. Metrics are computed per track, averaged over the tracks of each sequence, and then averaged over sequences. Tracks with fewer than two visible frames from the query frame on are skipped. MTE uses the lower me-dian for an even number of frames. On Multi-View Kubric and DexYCB, we report the mean of the static and dynamic track scores, following MVTracker: a track is static if its ground-truth path length is below 0.01 and dynamic if it is above 0.1 (Kubric units and meters, respectively), and tracks in between count in neither group. Points for which a method produces no prediction ( e.g . failed triangulation) count as predicted occluded for AJ, δavg , and OA, and are skipped for MTE and survival. Visibility thresholds. Each method uses its own thresh-old on the predicted visibility probability. For Tracktail we use 0.1. For MVTracker we use its official thresholds (0.5 on Multi-View Kubric and 0.01 on DexYCB and Panop-tic Studio) and 0.5 on Multi-View Point Odyssey. For AR-ROW we use 0.5. The CoTracker3 baseline uses the binary visibility returned by CoTracker3, which applies its default threshold of 0.9. The visibility threshold only affects AJ and OA. 

Units. All errors are computed in the world coordinates of each dataset. Following MVTracker [27], MTE is reported in centimeters for Panoptic Studio and DexYCB, whose co-ordinates are in meters. Multi-View Kubric coordinates are not metric. We multiply its errors by 13, which reproduces the scale of the Kubric MTE values reported by MVTracker. The δavg thresholds for Multi-View Kubric ({0.05 , 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8}) are in unscaled Kubric units. Multi-View Point Odyssey errors, δavg thresholds, and survival thresholds are in Point Odyssey scene units. 

# D. Training 

D.1. Sampling and augmentation. 

Each training sample is a 24-frame clip from 1–8 randomly chosen cameras with 128 query points. 95% of the query points are moving points (faster than 3 pixels per frame). Each view is cropped to the bounding box of the projected points and resized to 256 pixels on its longer side, as at in-ference. Query times are sampled from the frames in which the point is visible, strongly favoring the first and last frame of the clip, so the model learns to track both forward and backward in time. With probability 0.25 we instead use a single camera with 2D pixel queries. We apply the follow-ing augmentations: • a random global 3D rotation of the scene and cameras. • with probability 0.4, restricting the query points to a ran-dom 3D sphere, whose radius is 5–75% of the scene ex-tent, to produce zoomed-in views. • per-camera in-plane rotations (±45 ◦), cutout occlu-sions, blur, color, gamma, and noise augmentations, and grayscale conversion. • setting the occlusion state inputs to unknown with proba-bility 0.5.

# F. Survival rate 

Figure 6 shows the survival of points on Multi-View Point Odyssey across all 499 frames for Tracktail and the base-line methods. Tracktail maintains a high survival rate for the first 50 frames, while the other methods exhibit a sharp decrease in survival almost immediately. For the entire 499-frame duration, Tracktail’s survival rate is far above the other methods for the, decaying to just over 40% survival by the final frame. Both MVTracker and ARROW decay to 0% survival, while the CoTracker3 baseline plateaus at around 18% survival. We tested various 3D distance thresholds for the survival rate and picked a threshold of 1.2 scene units, as it shows Tracktail decaying from good to poor performance. Figure 7 shows the effect of different thresholds on the survival rate for Tracktail. More relaxed thresholds allows track-ing to stay between 80-100% survival, while more stringent thresholds show performance decaying to 0-20% survival for the majority of frames.
