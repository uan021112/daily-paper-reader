Dr. Grasp-RL: Diverse and Robust Dexterous
Grasping via Reinforcement Learning
Kunqi Xu1, Xiaokang Sun1 and Xueyan Zou1
1Tsinghua University - IEI Lab,
https://xkq1qkx.github.io/Diverse-Manip-RL-web/
Human interaction with physical objects relies on a combination of dexterity and robustness. In robotic
manipulation, dexterity requires not only flexible end effectors but also a diverse repertoire of manipulation
strategies, while robustness requires closed-loop adaptation to disturbances and unexpected situations. Two
prominent research directions in dexterous grasping have largely addressed these capabilities separately.
Generative approaches model multimodal distributions to produce diverse grasp poses, but their reliance on
open-loop execution can limit robustness. Reinforcement learning approaches learn closed-loop grasping
policies, but can converge to a narrow range of successful grasping strategies, limiting behavioral diversity.
We introduce a time-reversed curriculum with a coordinated reset mechanism that enables a simple MLP
policy trained with PPO to capture multiple modes of the grasp-pose distribution while maintaining closed-
loop control. Experiments demonstrate both diverse grasping behaviors and robust closed-loop execution.
Trained on only approximately 400 objects, our policy further demonstrates strong generalization when
evaluated on more than 20,000 unseen objects.

1. Introduction
What is the essence of human’s ability to manipulate objects? How can we make robots exhibit human-
level intelligence in their object manipulation performance? This remains an unsolved and even poorly
understood problem [1, 17]. Humans can grasp the same object in many different ways, adapting their
wrist pose and finger configuration to the current situation [7]. This flexibility suggests a desirable
capability for robotic grasping: a single policy should support multiple effective grasping modes and select
suitable actions from the current hand–object state. Diversity and feedback are complementary [4, 18].

Alternative grasping modes provide options when an approach is unfavorable, while closed-loop control
allows the robot to adjust its actions and retry after failed contact [15]. The challenge is to learn a policy
that retains this repertoire while realizing its behaviors through continuous physical interaction.
A useful perspective on this challenge comes from the distinction between evaluating candidate solutions
and generating them [2, 8]. Generative modeling offers an expressive means of representing multiple
plausible solutions, as illustrated by advances in image and video generation [12, 13]. In dexterous
grasping, grasp-synthesis approaches have similarly explored large-scale grasp data and diverse grasp-
pose distributions. However, a grasp proposal alone does not specify how to reach, maintain, or recover
it through physical interaction. Conversely, some policy-learning approaches integrate grasping more
directly with feedback close-loop control, either through reinforcement learning or subsequent policy
distillation. Yet optimizing task success does not necessarily encourage a policy to preserve multiple
successful grasping modes: learning can concentrate on a narrow repertoire of effective behaviors [6, 11].
Despite progress along both directions, jointly learning diverse grasping strategies and reliable close-loop
execution remains a central challenge.
We revisit this challenge from a value-function perspective. Rather than explicitly generating a target
grasp pose, we seek to learn a state-value landscape—a scalar field over grasping states—with two key
properties: multiple high-value peaks and dense, informative guidance. Each peak should correspond
to a distinct effective grasp configuration, allowing the landscape to represent multiple valid ways of
grasping the same object. Far from the object, the value should generally increase as the hand approaches
a feasible grasp from different directions; near contact, it should distinguish effective wrist poses and
finger configurations according to the object’s geometry. Importantly, this guidance should extend beyond
successful grasp configurations to the intermediate states encountered during approach, contact, and
recovery. Intuitively, a controller could then select actions that produce the greatest expected increase in
value under the robot’s dynamics, repeatedly updating its decisions from the current state. Multiple peaks
would preserve alternative grasping strategies, while dense value guidance would support closed-loop
execution and adaptation after failures or disturbances.
This intuition naturally motivates an actor–critic approach [16]: the critic learns to evaluate states
associated with different grasping modes, while the actor learns feedback actions that lead toward
high-return outcomes. We therefore introduce Dr. Grasp-RL, which uses PPO [26] with simple multilayer
perceptron (MLP) networks to learn diverse, closed-loop dexterous grasping. Through a time-reversed
training curriculum and diverse, instructive reset states, we progressively extend learning from states
near successful grasps to earlier and more challenging stages of the interaction. We just use a simple
reward proportional to object lift height. Our experiments demonstrate diverse grasping behaviors,
robust closed-loop execution, and strong generalization across object geometries. Our contributions are
threefold:
• We introduce Dr. Grasp-RL, a framework for learning a diverse repertoire of dexterous grasping
behaviors within a single closed-loop policy using MLP-based PPO.
• We develop a time-reversed curriculum coupled with diverse, curriculum-aligned reset states to
facilitate learning across multiple grasping modes with a simple lift-height reward, without requiring
an explicit grasp-pose generator at execution time.
• We evaluate the learned policy on more than 20,000 unseen objects after training on no more than
420 objects, and analyze its grasp diversity, closed-loop recovery behaviors, and generalization.

2. Related Work
Grasp generation and demonstration editing.
Generative grasp models provide diverse proposals as
priors for downstream planning and execution. Optimization-based synthesis supplies grasp datasets
such as DexGraspNet [28] and the taxonomy-conditioned Dexonomy [3]. GenDexGrasp [19] uses
hand-agnostic contact maps, while DexGraspNet 2.0 [38] and DexGrasp Anything [40] extend diffusion-
based synthesis to cluttered scenes and physics-aware grasp generation, respectively. Dex1B [31] scales
generative demonstrations for grasping and articulation. At the trajectory level, DemoGrasp [34] and
DemoFunGrasp [21] use RL-based demonstration editing, with the latter conditioning on grasp styles
and affordances. Both also distill edited demonstrations into visual policies. However, generating grasp
or trajectory proposals separately from execution decouples planning from ongoing physical interaction.
Without feedback-driven proposal updates or replanning, fixed targets can become unsuitable under
object motion or unexpected contact, limiting robustness and real-time adaptation. Our approach learns
grasping within a single feedback policy, allowing actions to adapt continuously to the evolving hand-object
state.
Closed-loop dexterous grasping and policy learning.
DexMV [23] and DexVIP [20] use human
demonstrations and hand-pose priors to guide policy learning. UniDexGrasp [30] couples grasp proposals
with a goal-conditioned RL controller, while UniDexGrasp++ [27] combines geometry-aware curricula
with iterative generalist–specialist learning. UniGraspTransformer [29] distills object-specific experts
into a universal Transformer policy. RobustDexGrasp [37] combines hand-centric geometry with teacher–
student learning, and ResDex [14] uses residual learning and mixture-of-experts to support different
grasping styles. These approaches enable feedback control, often through specialized priors, expert
construction, or multi-stage learning. Nevertheless, closed-loop task success does not itself ensure
broad coverage of effective grasping modes: return maximization can concentrate learning on a limited
repertoire of successful behaviors. We address this exploration challenge through diverse resets and a
time-reversed curriculum, while keeping the policy architecture and reward simple. Our approach learns
multiple grasping modes within a single policy that adapts its actions through continuous feedback.
Curricula, reset distributions, and exploration in RL.
Reverse Curriculum Generation [10] expands
initial states outward from goals, while Backplay [24] moves initialization backward along demonstrations.
Go-Explore [5] returns to promising archived states before further exploration, and Goal GAN [9]
generates goals of appropriate difficulty. More recently, OmniReset [32] uses diverse, programmatically
generated resets to scale manipulation without an explicit curriculum. A Balanced Data Diet [39] allocates
training samples near the policy’s competence frontier; related difficulty adaptation also appears in
RLVE [36] for language models. These studies primarily use curricula and reset distributions to improve
exploration and broaden task coverage. Beyond discovering successful solutions, we investigate how
these mechanisms can help a single policy acquire and retain multiple effective grasping modes for the
same object. This is particularly challenging in dexterous grasping, where a single policy must coordinate
wrist motion and finger contacts across distinct grasp configurations.

3.1. Task and Feedback Control
We consider tabletop grasping with a seven-joint xArm7 and an XHand with 12 actuated finger joints.
The policy acts at 10 Hz in mjlab [35] running at 1,000 Hz, with a maximum episode duration of 20 s.

3.3. Privileged Actor–Critic Learning
Observations and architecture.
The teacher receives a 152-dimensional state vector containing relative
object and hand poses, robot proprioception, object dynamics and geometry, contact summaries, and
episode progress, together with 600 scalar tactile readings. A PointNet-style encoder [22] maps 512
points sampled from the object’s complete mesh surface to a 256-dimensional shape feature. Points
are expressed in the object frame, centered by the mesh bounding box, and normalized by its largest
extent; physical size is provided separately in the state vector. Both actor and critic receive the same
1,008-dimensional concatenation. They have separate ReLU MLPs with hidden widths (512, 256, 128)
and share the trainable shape encoder. The actor defines an 18-dimensional Gaussian followed by tanh;
the critic predicts a scalar value. Grasp-template labels and object IDs are not policy inputs.

PPO [26] jointly optimizes the teacher and shape encoder. The changing reset distribution supplies

estimates their expected discounted returns, and the actor learns feedback actions from PPO advantages.
This implements the value-function motivation through policy learning; it does not explicitly differentiate
the critic with respect to state to choose actions. Diversity is expressed by the policy’s behaviors across
interaction states, without an explicit multimodal action head or a diversity loss.
3.4. Distillation to Deployable Observations
The student removes simulated tactile, contact, and dynamics information. Its 142-dimensional state
contains current and previous object-to-hand poses, the current hand-to-base pose, current and previous
finger angles, finger control targets, seven mesh-geometry descriptors, and the four most recent nor-
malized actions. Poses use translation and a six-dimensional rotation representation. A copied, frozen
teacher shape encoder supplies 256 additional features. The resulting 398-dimensional input feeds
a ReLU MLP with widths (512, 256, 128) and an 18-dimensional tanh output. Real-world execution
uses calibrated motion-capture object poses, robot feedback, and known object meshes; it requires no
simulated velocities, contact forces, or physical-property parameters.
We first perform behavior cloning (BC) on teacher-controlled rollouts, then use a DAgger-style proce-
dure [25] with equal numbers of teacher-controlled and student-controlled environments. All distillation
rollouts use 3B initialization. In both groups, the fixed teacher labels the actual visited state with

Ablation on seed initialization.
We also evaluate two variants in Table 1. Ours (low quality stage1)
uses lower-quality seed poses generated by Lightning-Grasp [33]. Its reduced final performance suggests
that weak grasp acquisition in Stage 1 limits subsequent learning, with Stages 2 and 3 unable to fully
compensate. Ours (w/o seed poses) removes near-grasp initialization and instead uses dense geometric
rewards analogous to the optimization energies used for grasp synthesis. Despite using the same number
of training steps as the full method, this variant achieves substantially lower success, suggesting that
reward shaping alone does not substitute for learning from diverse, physically feasible near-grasp states.
Together, these comparisons highlight the importance of both seed quality and initialization strategy.
Success and cross-dataset transfer.
On the test partition, the teacher achieves 92.11% success with
an arm and 93.23% without an arm; the student achieves 88.06% and 91.87%, respectively. Both exceed
all baselines listed in Table 1, while the student’s performance shows that much of the teacher’s capability
transfers to deployable inputs. Table 2 further evaluates transfer without fine-tuning across DexGraspNet,
Objaverse, YCB, DGA, EGAD, and VisualDexterity. Both policies outperform the listed baselines on five of
the six arm-mounted asset sets and all six hand-only sets. These results support transfer across object
geometries while preserving strong performance under the constraints of an attached arm.

Closed-loop regrasping.
Table 3 reports overall success and the attempt on which each successful
episode first succeeds. An attempt begins with finger–object contact; losing all such contact for 0.3 s
ends the attempt, and subsequent contact starts another. Palm or arm contact alone does not count. Our
policy continues from the resulting state after a nonterminal failed grasp, without resetting the scene or
policy history. Attempt shares are conditioned on eventual success and must therefore be interpreted
together with overall success rate.
On the test partition with an arm, 14.1% of teacher successes and 19.4% of student successes occur
after the first attempt. Together with their overall SRs of 92.11% and 88.06%, these recovered episodes
contribute approximately 13.0 and 17.1 percentage points to total success. The corresponding shares
are larger than on the training objects, indicating that retries remain useful under shape variation. In
comparison, at most 0.8% of successes occur after the first attempt for the arm-mounted baselines listed
in the table. Recovery also persists without the arm: approximately 12.3% and 14.5% of teacher and
student successes, respectively, require retries. Thus, high episode success is accompanied by successful
recovery from intermediate failures, rather than only immediate grasp acquisition.
Real-world adaptation.
Figure 6 shows the student deployed with motion-capture object poses, known
object meshes, and robot feedback. The first row illustrates successful single-attempt grasps. The
remaining three rows show unsuccessful contacts followed by online changes in wrist pose, finger
configuration, or approach before lifting the object successfully. These sequences demonstrate that the
deployed policy reacts to the evolving interaction instead of replaying a fixed grasp trajectory.
Coupling diversity and robustness.
A diverse grasping repertoire provides alternative actions when a
previous approach becomes unsuitable, while feedback allows the policy to use these alternatives from
the state left by a failed attempt. The observed changes in grasp configuration during successful retries
are consistent with this connection: recovery can involve selecting a different contact arrangement,
rather than mechanically repeating the first unsuccessful motion. Together, the grasp distributions,
regrasping statistics, and real-world sequences support the value of learning diverse behaviors within a
single closed-loop policy.

5. Conclusion
We presented Dr. Grasp-RL, which uses diverse resets and a time-reversed curriculum to train a dexterous
grasping policy with PPO. The policy acquires multiple grasping modes without a generative architecture
and combines this diversity with closed-loop control for real-time adjustment and recovery. Simulation
and real-world experiments demonstrate strong generalization across object geometries and robust
execution.
Implications.
These findings motivate a control-centered view of robot learning. Research paradigms
and narratives borrowed from other domains may not be the best fit for the distinctive demands of robotics.
We therefore advocate developing models and architectures grounded in robotics’ own requirements for
physical interaction.

Privileged dynamics and contact inputs.
The 19-dimensional dynamics block contains mass in kg;
object-frame COM offset divided by 0.5 m; the six distinct inertia entries (𝐼𝑥𝑥, 𝐼𝑦𝑦, 𝐼𝑧𝑧, 𝐼𝑥𝑦, 𝐼𝑥𝑧, 𝐼𝑦𝑧) divided

Shape encoding.
For each object, 512 points are sampled from its mesh surface in 𝑂. The point set is
centered at the axis-aligned bounding-box center and divided by its maximum extent. A shared pointwise
MLP has widths 3–64–128–256, with ReLU after the first two layers. Concatenated max and mean
pooling give 512 values, followed by a linear 512–256 projection. Explicit geometry metadata preserves
scale information removed by point normalization. The teacher jointly trains this encoder with its actor
and critic. The student copies and freezes the final teacher encoder; the shape feature can therefore be
cached once per known mesh at deployment.
Teacher architecture.
Separate actor and critic MLPs have hidden widths 512, 256, and 128, with ReLU
activations. The actor produces 18 Gaussian means and has a learned, state-independent 18-dimensional
log standard deviation. Actions are sampled and passed through tanh, with the corresponding log-
probability correction. Deterministic evaluation uses tanh of the mean. The critic produces one scalar.
Including the shape encoder and log standard deviation, the teacher has 1,537,061 trainable parameters.
There is no recurrent module or explicit grasp-mode, object-identity, or reset-identity input.

Far, independent resets.
The 2C bank contains 200 feasible open-hand robot configurations, shared
across objects. Its 5–25 cm separation refers to a projected geometric clearance along the object-to-
palm direction, not simply the distance between frame origins. The bank is checked against all 418
training objects. Each environment independently and uniformly samples a bank entry on reset. During
Stage 3, separate 200-entry banks are constructed for 3A and 3B and checked against object displacement
envelopes. They can be farther away than the 2C bank; the 5–25 cm interval is not imposed on every 3B
pose. Object and robot reset draws are independent, including across GPU ranks.
Feasibility checks.
Opening/retreat candidates are checked for robot–object and moving robot–floor
clearance (1 mm), prohibited self-intersection, joint limits, and endpoint IK. Reset-bank IK validation
uses 0.5 mm position and 0.005 rad orientation tolerances. These are initialization checks, not successful-
trajectory filtering. The banks are generated before learning; subsequent failed trajectories remain part
of training.

Level 1 mixes seeds and 2A; level 2 mixes 2A and 2B;

D. Behavior Cloning and DAgger
Shared setup.
Both distillation phases use the final teacher and the 3B reset distribution only. Each of
four GPUs hosts 418×20 = 8, 360 environments, totaling 33,440. The student has the observation contract
in Table S2; only these deployable quantities enter the student. The teacher uses its privileged observation
only to provide action labels. At each visited state, the target is the teacher’s deterministic bounded
action tanh(𝜇𝑇(𝑠)). We minimize the mean squared error over all 18 normalized action components. The
shape encoder is frozen, and only the student actor is optimized.

BC on teacher-visited states.
All BC environments execute the frozen teacher. For every object and GPU,
15 worlds are assigned to training and five to validation. Training and validation therefore use separate
worlds and trajectories, but the same object identities. The rolling buffers contain up to 1,605,120
training and 535,040 validation transitions per GPU. We collect 64 steps, perform 256 updates using
uniform sampling with replacement, and continue collection. We do not first collect an unbounded static
dataset and train it to convergence. The final BC checkpoint follows 43 rounds and 92,026,880 collected
teacher transitions across GPUs. Failed trajectories are retained; invalid numerical transitions are removed.
The expert success recorded during BC describes the data-collection policy, not the autonomous student.
DAgger on teacher- and student-visited states.
DAgger starts from the final BC student with a fresh
optimizer. For each object and GPU, 10 worlds execute the teacher and 10 execute the current student.
Within each group, eight worlds are used for training and two for validation. Separate rolling buffers are
maintained for the two sources; each source has 856,064 training and 214,016 validation slots per GPU.
Each training minibatch takes 4,096 samples from each source. The teacher labels the student’s actual
visited states, including unsuccessful approaches and recoveries; it does not take over control of these
worlds.
The student is held fixed during each 64-step collection, then updated for 256 gradient steps. The
next collection uses the updated student. Episodes can span collection rounds, and histories record the
actions actually executed in each world. The final checkpoint follows 33 DAgger rounds and 70,625,280
additional collected transitions across both sources. The BC and DAgger losses use the same action units
and ordering, so their fitting errors are comparable, while the state distributions differ.

Why report more than reward and loss?
A change in reset difficulty changes the state and return
distributions. A temporary decline in success at a curriculum transition therefore does not by itself
imply that the policy has lost a previously learned skill. Group-wise success separates learning the new
setting from retaining the earlier one. PPO’s policy-surrogate loss also changes with the freshly collected
advantages, so it is not a supervised prediction error that must decrease monotonically. We prioritize the
task success, return, and critic loss curves, and add explained variance in Figure S3:
