Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models benefit from strong se-mantic representations that support object recognition and in-struction understanding. However, precise manipulation also depends on resolving object locations and workspace rela-tions, and on selecting actions with an estimate of how the scene will evolve. Existing geometry-aware methods make the current scene more explicit, while predictive methods model future observations or representations. Yet future information is often neither grounded in a control-relevant spatial state nor directly used to guide action generation. We introduce Spa-tially Grounded Latent Lookahead (SG-LL), which uses the grounded present as the reference for predicting an actionable near-future representation. A frozen spatial teacher first dis-tills geometric priors into policy-connected spatial tokens. A current-conditioned bottleneck then learns a compact latent that captures future information relative to the present scene. This latent is predicted from the current policy context and initial Action Expert features, without access to future obser-vations or the spatial teacher. Finally, zero-initialized residual fusion incorporates the predicted latent into the Action Expert to refine action generation. Experiments on LIBERO-Plus, CALVIN, and three real-world manipulation tasks demon-strate improvements over π0.5 in both simulation and real-robot settings.

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models provide a unified pol-icy that maps visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. Large-scale pretraining and robot data have produced increasingly capable generalist policies, including the RT, OpenVLA, and π families (Brohan et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2024; Black et al. 2024a; Physical Intelligence et al. 2025). These models execute diverse manipulation instruc-tions and transfer across task conditions, yet strong task per-formance leaves open whether their internal representations explicitly expose the scene geometry needed for control and whether action generation anticipates how the interaction is likely to evolve. This distinction matters under variations in viewpoint, robot state, object configuration, and observation noise, where a semantically competent policy must still re-solve precise spatial relations (Fei et al. 2025).

Reliable manipulation calls for both spatial grounding and predictive lookahead. Semantic visual features can identify an object without making its location, robot–object relation, or local configuration readily available to the Action Ex-pert. Conversely, directly mapping the current context to an Action Chunk does not explicitly represent the near-future scene evolution implied by the current task and policy con-text. This motivates the central question of this work: Can a VLA ground the present scene with control-relevant spatial structure, anticipate where the interaction is heading, and use that lookahead to refine control? 

Spatially aware robot policies address the first require-ment through depth, point clouds, explicit 3D representa-tions, or geometry-aligned features (Shridhar, Manuelli, and Fox 2021, 2023; Ze et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025; Qu et al. 2025). Such representations can make scene structure more accessi-ble, but they are often introduced through an additional online modality or a specialized spatial architecture. Our objective is instead to use a frozen visual geometry model solely as a training-time supervisory reference while retaining the stan-dard RGB policy interface. Its geometric priors are distilled into a policy-connected current-state representation, while the teacher itself is absent from the deployed policy. Predictive representations address the second requirement from another direction. Prior work predicts images, videos, latent futures, or world-action features for representation learning, visual subgoals, action conditioning, or rollout-based control (Du et al. 2023; Black et al. 2024b; Zheng et al. 2025; Su et al. 2026; Fang, Chen, and Cai 2026; Zhao et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2026; Miao et al. 2026). These studies establish future-oriented representation learning as a promising direction for robot control. This raises a key interface challenge: how can a compact, future-informative representation be integrated into an existing flow-matching VLA such that prediction directly informs action generation? In SG-LL, lookahead takes the form of a compact near-future latent predicted once from the current policy context and di-rectly used to refine the inherited action computation. We introduce Spatially Grounded Latent Lookahead (SG-LL), summarized in Figure 1. SG-LL first maps VLM Back-bone features into Grounded Spatial State Tokens under su-pervision from a Frozen VGGT (Wang et al. 2025) Spa-tial Teacher. Finally, zero-initialized Spatial Grounding Residual Injection and Future-Latent Residual Fusion connect these representations to the inherited policy. Together, these components implement a simple principle: ground the present, look ahead in latent space, and refine the action. Because both residual pathways are zero-initialized, SG-LL preserves the pretrained policy computation at initialization while requiring no future ob-servations, no online geometry model, and no additional 3D sensors at deployment. We evaluate SG-LL at both the system and mechanism lev-els. We further evaluate SG-LL on three real-robot manipulation tasks. Our contributions are as follows: 

• We introduce a spatial grounding interface that dis-tills geometric priors from a frozen spatial teacher into policy-connected Grounded Spatial State Tokens, en-abling control-relevant spatial grounding while retaining RGB-only deployment. 

• We propose a decodable latent lookahead mechanism that predicts a compact, current-conditioned near-future representation from policy-available context and directly couples it to action generation through zero-initialized residual refinement. 

• We validate SG-LL through paired simulation evalua-tions, targeted mechanism interventions, and real-robot experiments, demonstrating improved end-to-end control performance and showing that the trained policy makes functional use of its predicted future latent. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models for Robot Control 

VLA policies combine pretrained vision-language represen-tations with diverse action-generation mechanisms, rang-ing from autoregressive action tokenization to diffusion and flow matching for continuous control (Brohan et al. 2022; Zitkovich et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024; Team et al. 2024; Chi et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025; Black et al. 2024a; Physi-cal Intelligence et al. 2025). Large-scale robot datasets and multimodal backbones further support transfer across tasks and embodiments, while recent work improves action repre-sentations and adaptation efficiency through latent actions, efficient tokenization, or specialized policy heads (Collabo-ration et al. 2023; Bousmalis et al. 2023; Li et al. 2024; Bu et al. 2025; Pertsch et al. 2025; Kim, Finn, and Liang 2025). SG-LL targets a different axis: rather than redesigning the base VLA or its action parameterization, it augments the inherited policy with control-relevant spatial grounding and near-future predictive information that directly participate in action generation. 

2.2 Spatial Representations and Geometric Grounding 

Spatial structure has been incorporated into robot policies through factorized what/where pathways, volumetric repre-sentations, point-cloud inputs, and explicit 3D feature en-coders (Shridhar, Manuelli, and Fox 2021, 2023; Ze et al. 2024). Recent VLA methods bring similar spatial inductive biases into pretrained vision-language policies through 3D positional encoding, representation alignment, or geometry-aware supervision (Qu et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2026). SG-LL instead uses spatial grounding as part of a predic-tive control interface: frozen geometric priors supervise a policy-connected current spatial state that both feeds into the inherited action pathway and supports near-future latent pre-diction. Spatial grounding is therefore coupled with predic-tive lookahead and action refinement rather than introduced only as an additional spatial representation. 

2.3 Predictive Representations and World Models 

Predictive representation learning captures temporal struc-ture by predicting future information in representation space rather than reconstructing every image pixel (Assran et al. 2023; Bardes et al. 2024). In robotics, future observations, visual subgoals, latent actions, and predictive features have been used for representation learning, action conditioning, and goal-directed control (Lynch et al. 2020; Du et al. 2023; Black et al. 2024b; Ye et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025). More recent VLA-oriented methods align policy represen-tations with future visual features or latent futures, or gener-ate future-oriented conditions and imagined observations to guide action generation (Zheng et al. 2025; Su et al. 2026; Fang, Chen, and Cai 2026; Zhao et al. 2026; Hu et al. 2024; Tian et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2026; Sun et al. 2026; Miao et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026; Xie et al. 2026; Yuan et al. 2026). Related world-model approaches learn action-conditioned dynamics for prediction, rollout, and planning (Hafner et al. 2019; Hansen, Wang, and Su 2022; Zhou et al. 2024; As-sran et al. 2025), including spatially structured models that connect geometric scene representations to predictive robot control (Zhen et al. 2024; Qiu et al. 2026). SG-LL brings spatial grounding and predictive repre-sentation learning together through a decodable, current-conditioned near-future latent. Rather than using future pre-diction only as a pretraining signal or externally generated subgoal, SG-LL directly integrates the predicted latent into Action Expert hidden states through residual fusion, mak-ing near-future information actionable within the pretrained VLA pathway.

## 4 Experiments 

Our experiments address three questions. (Q1) Does SG-LL improve end-to-end policy performance on LIBERO-Plus and CALVIN under matched evaluation protocols? (Q2) What does the learned future latent represent, does the trained policy behaviorally use it, and how do the key design choices contribute? (Q3) How does SG-LL perform on real-world manipulation tasks? 

4.1 Simulation Setup and Baselines 

LIBERO-Plus. We use LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al. 2025) as our primary simulation benchmark, covering seven perturba-tion dimensions across the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites. Stage I is trained for 4,000 steps and then frozen. Stage II adaptation initializes from the official π0.5-LIBERO checkpoint, freezes the VLM Backbone and Action Expert, fixes ∆ = 10 , and trains the policy-side SG-LL modules for 10,000 steps with seed 42. We use the official π0.5-LIBERO checkpoint as the pri-mary reference because SG-LL freezes the pretrained VLM Backbone and Action Expert and adapts the policy exclu-sively through its newly introduced modules. Applying the same freezing rule to the original π0.5 architecture leaves no corresponding parameters to optimize, making the official checkpoint the natural frozen-policy reference. To separately control for exposure to LIBERO-Plus adaptation data, we additionally continue training the original π0.5 architecture for 10,000 steps using its standard trainable policy param-eters. This continued-adaptation control reaches 88.38% on Spatial, compared with 90.97% for the official checkpoint and 93.88% for SG-LL. All local evaluations use the same preprocessing, normal-ization, inference settings, and episode manifests. Results are paired by episode and evaluated with exact McNemar tests and paired-bootstrap confidence intervals. Overall scores are episode-weighted over the complete 10,030-episode mani-fest. 

CALVIN. We use CALVIN (Mees et al. 2022) as a sec-ondary cross-benchmark evaluation under a matched D-to-D adaptation protocol. SG-LL and the original-architecture 

π0.5 baseline initialize from the same π0.5-base checkpoint and use identical Task-D data and 10,000-step adaptation budgets. Both freeze the VLM Backbone and train the Ac-tion Expert. Evaluation uses the same fixed 1,000 five-task sequences with a 360-step episode limit and replanning in-terval 5.

4.3 Mechanism Analysis and Ablations 

What does the learned future latent represent? We first examine the representation learned by the Decodable Future Abstraction Stage. A frozen-stage probe predicts Z∗ from fu-ture spatial features with a loss of 0.011462, compared with 0.297392 when using only current features, showing that the latent is strongly future-informative. At the same time, fu-ture decoding depends on both the latent and the current reference: joint, current-only, and token-only decoding give losses of 0.074566, 0.093445, and 0.124070, respectively. Replacing the correct current reference with zeros further in-creases the frozen-decoder loss from 0.076249 to 0.789774. Together, these results characterize Z∗ as a future-dominant, current-complementary representation: it carries substantial information about the near future while being designed to de-code that future relative to the current spatial state. A sweep over 1–16 latent tokens shows the same future-dominant, current-complementary pattern, suggesting that this behav-ior is not simply determined by bottleneck size. 

Does the trained policy use the predicted lookahead? 

We intervene on the same seed-42 SG-LL checkpoint over the fixed 2,402-episode Spatial manifest. As shown in Ta-ble 2, disabling residual fusion or replacing the predicted latent with random alternatives substantially degrades per-formance. Norm-matched random tokens behave similarly to unconstrained random tokens, whereas cross-episode to-kens retain partial performance, showing that SG-LL relies on episode-specific latent content rather than merely residual magnitude. also significantly reduces success, confirming that lookahead prediction uses policy-derived ac-tion context. 

What is the architectural contribution of residual fu-sion? We compare SG-LL with a separately trained con-trol that disables Future-Latent Residual Fusion throughout Stage II training and evaluation, while retaining the same Spatial Grounding Adapter, data protocol, and training bud-get. As shown in Table 3, the contribution of fusion is task-dependent: it significantly improves Spatial, is neutral on Object, and degrades Goal and Long, resulting in no aggre-gate advantage over the fusion-disabled control. Together with Table 2, this reveals an important distinc-tion: a trained SG-LL policy behaviorally relies on the looka-head pathway, whereas a model trained without that pathway can partially compensate through its remaining components. Thus, fixed-policy intervention and independently trained controls measure different aspects of the fusion mechanism.

4.4 Real-World Experiments 

We compare SG-LL with task-matched π0.5 baselines on three real-robot tasks: fold clothes , pick up fruit , and organize desk . The baseline is obtained by fine-tuning the official π0.5

model using the official training recipe, with the same task demonstrations and training budget as SG-LL. The datasets contain 6.5, 0.26, and 5.65 hours of demonstrations, respec-tively. Experiments are conducted on the AIRBOT TOK2 platform shown in Figure 3; detailed training settings are provided in the supplementary material. As shown in Figure 4, SG-LL improves success on fold clothes and organize desk while matching perfect fruit-picking success, and also achieves lower mean completion time among successful trials across all three tasks. Given the modest number of trials, we treat these results as task-level real-world evidence rather than aggregate statistical compar-isons. Across all three real-world tasks, training and evaluation use independently sampled scene configurations from the same task-specific data distribution. Object arrangements, spatial placements, and other initial conditions therefore dif-fer between training and evaluation, while the object sets and task definitions remain fixed. These experiments evaluate in-distribution generalization to unseen scene configurations rather than out-of-distribution or novel-object generalization. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced SG-LL, which augments VLA policies with spatially grounded, current-conditioned latent lookahead that directly refines action generation. Geometric teacher targets and future observations are used only during training, pre-serving the standard RGB-based deployment interface. Ex-periments on LIBERO-Plus, CALVIN, and real-world ma-nipulation demonstrate system-level improvements, while targeted interventions show that trained SG-LL policies be-haviorally use the predicted lookahead. ## B Scope and Terminology 

This supplement documents Spatially Grounded Latent Lookahead (SG-LL), its implementation, and the com-pleted experiments supporting the submission. Frozen-feature probes motivate the term current-conditioned future latent : the target is strongly future-informative, while the cur-rent reference and latent remain complementary for future decoding. SG-LL implements a single fixed-horizon latent lookahead that directly refines the inherited action pathway. All quantitative entries below are drawn from completed, archived artifacts. Historical implementation identifiers such as Route A , Scheme4 , and delta refer to SG-LL in the released code (https://github.com/Cqhddyb/SG-LL).

## E Training and Evaluation Protocols 

E.1 LIBERO-Plus 

Stage I caches 2,197 usable examples from 1,180 teacher batches (cache batch 2) and trains for 4,000 AdamW steps with batch 8, seed 31, learning rate 3 × 10 −4, weight decay 

10 −4, gradient clipping 1.0, and a 1,941/256 train/validation split. The cache and tokenizer require about 10:44 and 2:44 on one RTX 5090, respectively. E.2 CALVIN D-to-D 

The reported CALVIN experiment is matched low-budget D-to-D adaptation, not the ABC-to-D leaderboard protocol. Both arms initialize from the same official π0.5 base, use task-D LeRobot data, normalization, state/action mapping, physical batch 4, training seed 42, 10,000 optimizer steps, 10-action chunks, and train the Action Expert while freezing the VLM. SG-LL adds benchmark-specific Stage-I features and its spatial/lookahead modules. Its Stage-I cache contains 1,650 usable examples; tokenizer training uses batch 8 and 4,000 steps. SG-LL/base training takes 4:53:30/1:24:47 and peaks at 19.33/11.91 GB allocated memory on different RTX 5090 hosts; the ratio is not treated as a controlled overhead measurement. Evaluation uses the fixed official 1,000 five-task D-to-D sequence indices 0–999, maximum 360 environment steps per subtask, replanning every five steps, and no videos. The metric is mean completed prefix length in each five-subtask sequence and the rate of completing at least q ∈ { 1, . . . , 5}

subtasks. Both methods have exact sequence coverage, zero duplicate indices, and zero per-sequence errors. 

E.3 Real Robot 

Experiments use the AIRBOT TOK2 platform with two six-degree-of-freedom AIRBOT Play arms, two-finger grippers, two 720p wrist-mounted RGB cameras, and one 1080p over-head RGB camera. The official π0.5 model is fine-tuned with its standard recipe to form the task-matched baseline; SG-LL uses the same task demonstrations and optimization-step budget. Fold clothes and organize desk use 6.5 and 5.65 hours of demonstrations, batch 32, and 100,000 training steps; pick up fruit uses 0.26 hours, batch 32, and 20,000 steps. Training and evaluation sample configurations indepen-dently from the same fixed task-specific distribution. Object arrangements and spatial placements change, while the ob-ject pool and task definition remain fixed. The experiment therefore measures within-distribution generalization to un-seen configurations, not novel-object or out-of-distribution generalization. Success is judged by task completion under the archived task-level criterion, and duration is averaged over successful trials only. The archived task-level record provides training volume, batch size, update budget, trial counts, success outcomes, and successful-trial mean duration. Because trials were not paired and per-trial duration dispersion was not retained, the real-robot results are reported as task-level descriptive evidence with Wilson intervals for success rates.

G.8 Additional Scope Checks 

Two completed checks further characterize the selected de-sign. and 370 .27 ± 1.85 ms per action-chunk request. Peak allo-cated memory is 7.647 and 8.156 GB.
