Entangle: Scaling Human-Video In-Context World-Action
Models across Unseen Tasks and Contexts
Chendong Xiang∗, Chengwen Zhang∗, Ruowen Zhao, Zeyuan Wang, Shuhe Huang
Rongxu Cui, Min Zhao, Fan Bao, Hengkai Tan, Jun Zhu
Tsinghua University, GensPI Technology, Motubrain
Abstract
In-context learning (ICL) enables foundation models to perform unseen tasks from context without
parameter updates. In robotic manipulation, a human video provides the context that specifies how a
task should be performed. However, scaling human-video ICL on world-action models (WAMs) faces two
challenges: task-level human–robot pairs are not temporally aligned with robot actions, and standard
action losses allow policies to rely on initial-scene or task-identity shortcuts rather than tracking the
context. We present Entangle, an in-context world-action model that maps a single human video
and current robot observations to future visual states and executable actions. To scale temporally
aligned training data, we convert recorded robot videos into matched human contexts via video-to-video
generation, supplemented by real paired recordings. For model training, we combine variable-length
video windows with an intra-task contrastive objective that distinguishes the paired action trajectory from
other executions of the same task, preventing task-identity shortcuts. Without task-specific fine-tuning,
Entangle improves steadily as aligned training data scales, reaching an average score of 83.5/100 across
six unseen tasks on an Astribot S1 robot, and achieves a 69.5% average success rate on seven unseen
RoboTwin tasks, outperforming Zero-WAM by 22.6 percentage points. Under Context-OOD, although
trained only on scene-aligned pairs, Entangle transfers context-specified behaviors across rearranged
layouts and substituted tools.

Introduction
In-context learning (ICL) enables foundation models to perform unseen tasks from context without updating
model parameters [1]. In robotic manipulation, language instructions often underspecify multi-step order,
spatial constraints, and fine-grained motion, whereas a single human demonstration video directly shows
how a task should be performed [2–6]. World-action models (WAMs) [7–11], which jointly model future
visual dynamics and robot control within a shared generative backbone, offer a natural foundation for such
video-conditioned policies: given a single human demonstration video as context, a WAM can predict future
visual states and executable actions for unseen manipulation tasks without parameter updates.
However, scaling human-video ICL on WAMs faces two core challenges. First, human and robot videos are
typically paired only at the task level. Whether collected independently or synthesized from an edited initial
frame via text-to-video generation [9, 12], task-level pairs share a high-level goal but diverge in motion paths,
contact timing, and execution speed. Without temporal alignment between the human video and ground-truth
robot actions, the model cannot localize which segment of the demonstration corresponds to a target action
chunk, reducing the video prompt to a coarse task label. Second, even when paired trajectories are aligned,
standard action-prediction losses supervise each trajectory in isolation and leave room for shortcuts: when
a task admits multiple valid executions (e.g., different manipulation orders or obstacle-avoidance paths), a
policy can lower its loss by inferring the task identity or relying on the initial scene layout rather than tracking
the specific progression shown in the demonstration.
To address these challenges, we present Entangle, an in-context world-action model that maps a single
human demonstration video and current robot observations to future visual states and executable actions.
First, to scale temporally aligned training data, we convert recorded robot videos into matched human
demonstrations via reference-conditioned video-to-video (V2V) generation, supplemented by real paired
recordings (Fig. 2). Unlike first-frame editing followed by text-driven generation, V2V translation preserves
the camera viewpoint, object interactions, and timing of the source robot trajectory, so each generated human
video remains temporally aligned with the recorded ground-truth actions. Second, we encode the human
demonstration and current robot observations in a shared video-action backbone and concatenate their tokens
along the temporal axis (Fig. 3). For model training, we combine variable-length video windows with an
intra-task InfoNCE contrastive objective [13] inspired by negative-aware fine-tuning [14]: by pulling predicted
actions toward the video-paired trajectory while repelling other trajectories of the same task, it prevents
task-identity shortcuts and forces the policy to follow the demonstrated motion.
We evaluate Entangle on an Astribot S1 robot and the RoboTwin benchmark [15, 16]. Without task-specific
fine-tuning, Entangle improves steadily as aligned training data scales, reaching an average score of 83.5/100
across six unseen tasks on Astribot S1, and achieves a 69.5% average success rate on seven unseen RoboTwin
tasks, outperforming Zero-WAM [9] by 22.6 percentage points. More surprisingly, although our training
pairs are strictly scene-aligned, Entangle goes beyond unseen tasks with the same objects (Fig. 1, middle): it
transfers the demonstrated behavior across rearranged layouts and substituted tools, such as using a banana
to sweep a wooden block into a cardboard box from a brush-and-dustpan demonstration (Fig. 1, top and
bottom).
Our main contributions are as follows:
• Scalable temporally aligned data: we turn recorded robot trajectories into temporally matched human
demonstrations with reference-conditioned video-to-video generation, so alignment scales with the robot-
video corpus without human reenactment.
• In-context WAM training: we concatenate demonstration and robot-video tokens along time, train on
variable-length windows, and add an intra-task InfoNCE loss that removes the task-identity shortcut.
• Data scaling and cross-scene transfer: unseen-task scores grow monotonically with aligned data; scene-
aligned training transfers to new layouts and tools.

Related Work
2


2.1
In-Context Learning for Robotic Manipulation
In-context learning enables a frozen robot policy to infer a task from deployment demonstrations, such as
sensorimotor trajectories in ICRT [3], demonstration graphs in Instant Policy [4], or retrieved segments in
RICL [5], extending one-shot policies based on task embeddings [17, 18], frame attention [19, 20], or skill
priors [21–23]. To leverage human videos, prior works learn visual features [24], latent actions [25], or
play plans [26], co-train on egocentric data in EgoMimic [27], retrieve play segments in RHyME [28] and
MimicDroid [29], or condition deployment on a single human video via video retrieval in ViP [30], language
grounding in BC-Z [31], and cross-attention in Vid2Robot [2]. However, reducing a demonstration to a
task identifier limits policies to recalling training behaviors [32], whereas generalizing to unseen procedures
and novel object uses requires tracking how the interaction unfolds, as explored through subgoal or trace
prediction [33–35], temporal alignment [36–39], intra-task keyframe separation in JEDI [40], layout shifts in
SimpleICL [41], and functional substitutions in The Imitator Game [42]. Entangle uses a single human video
to specify the procedure within a world-action model, generalizing across unseen procedures (Task-OOD) and
altered scenes or tools (Context-OOD).
2.2
World-Action Models
World-action models jointly predict future observations and actions [10, 43–46], extending visual fore-
sight [47], action recovery from visual plans [48–50], predictive representations for control [51–53], and
video generators adapted for policy learning [7, 11, 54–56] or rollout synthesis [57]. Recent WAMs fur-
ther incorporate context and demonstrations: ICWM [58] infers system properties from self-interaction,
WATCH [59] pretrains on task-paired human videos, LingBot-VA 2.0 [8] and WorldScape Policy 2.0 [60] accept
demonstration prompts, and Zero-WAM [9] generates task-matched human clips while predicting temporally
spaced future chunks to encourage prompt usage. However, conditioning on a demonstration alone does not
ensure that predicted actions follow the demonstrated execution over task-level cues; Entangle addresses
this by combining temporally aligned training with intra-task action contrast [13] to distinguish different
executions of the same task.
2.3
Test-Time Training
Meta-imitation adapts policy parameters from a demonstration [61, 62], while test-time training adapts
models or fast weights to test inputs [63–65]. WAM-TTT [66] updates fast weights from human videos before
rollout, and RoboTTT [67] updates them as the robot’s visuomotor history grows. Entangle keeps its weights
fixed at deployment, conditioning each prediction directly on the human demonstration and current robot
observation.
2.4
Motion Retargeting and Paired Data
Motion retargeting transfers object-relative motions [68, 69] or human hand trajectories [70–72] to a robot;
BEHAVE [73] records human–object contact, and CORE4D [74] retargets two-person rearrangement motions
to new object shapes. Robot training data can also come from paired human–robot recordings [75–77] or
synthesizing robot demonstrations from human footage [78–81], whereas Gen2Act [82] generates a human
video from an initial image and task description. Entangle uses the full robot recording as a motion reference,
verifies the generated human video for temporal and interaction agreement with the source, and combines
these clips with synchronized human reenactments so each sampled demonstration window pairs directly with
the recorded robot actions.

To supervise fine-grained action prediction from human video, each target action chunk requires a localized
visual reference in the demonstration. When human and robot videos are paired only by task label or
synthesized from an edited initial frame [9], subsequent motion paths and contact timing remain unaligned
with the ground-truth robot actions. To provide unambiguous supervision, we enforce strict pairing: using the
recorded robot trajectory as an anchor, the paired human video matches (i) the camera viewpoint and scene
layout, (ii) the manipulated objects and sequence of interaction events (e.g., reaching, grasping, transporting,
and releasing), and (iii) the timing of each event. This alignment requires the policy to track visual progression
instead of relying on initial-scene or task-identity shortcuts; we ablate strict pairing in Table 2 and evaluate
cross-scene transfer at deployment in Section 5.6.
3.2
Ent-Real
We construct real pairs by having humans reenact recorded Astribot S1 trajectories in the same workspace
under a viewpoint matched to the robot’s head camera. During recording, demonstrators watch the source
robot video playback and perform the manipulation in synchrony, matching the object interactions, motion
paths, and execution tempo.
3.3
Ent-Syn
To scale the paired corpus without per-episode human reenactment, we convert existing robot videos into
human demonstrations through a three-stage pipeline (Fig. 2(a)).
Keyframe editing. We extract atomic action descriptions from the robot-video annotations (including Qwen3.8-
4


generated descriptions) and sample source keyframes for appearance editing. Using human-hand images from
HaGRID [83] as appearance references, gpt-image-2 replaces visible robot arms and grippers with human
hands and forearms while preserving the scene, task objects, viewpoint, and contact relationships. The edited
frames pass visual quality checks before video generation.
Video generation. Using Seedance 2.5 or Wan 3.0, the video editor takes the complete source robot clip
as a motion reference, the edited keyframes as appearance references, and a text prompt with the atomic
action description. Supplying the full robot clip anchors action timing, object trajectories, and state transitions
throughout generation without requiring explicit segmentation masks, depth maps, or edge-map control.
Verification and pairing. A vision–language model (VLM) verifier filters generated clips for visual quality
(complete arm replacement and scene stability) and temporal action consistency with the source trajectory;
verified clips are paired with the original robot observations and actions (details in Appendix E.4).

4.3
Training
We initialize from MotuBrain [10] and continue training (CT) on the full Ent-Dataset (Sec. 3) with the
objectives below. We evaluate CT directly on the real robot; RoboTwin uses multi-task supervised fine-tuning
(SFT) for simulator adaptation.

Variable-length window training. Context window duration governs a trade-off between procedural following
and generalization: conditioning on full-episode clips encourages the model to infer global task identity and
complete the task semantically instead of following the context, whereas training only on short clips limits
temporal context and hurts generalization. To balance this trade-off, we randomly sample synchronized human
and robot windows across multiple durations (13, 25, or 37 frames, encoding to Fc ∈{4, 7, 10} latent frames)
with the matching action segment; the anchored RoPE in Eq. 3 keeps actions aligned at every window length.
Closed-loop inference with Adaptive Horizon (AH). When executing a predicted action chunk open-loop,
later steps can lose grounding in the human reference as future uncertainty grows, accumulating contact and
trajectory errors before the next observation. In each replanning round (Fig. 3(c)), we therefore monitor V →A
attention from predicted actions to the reference tokens: when later steps exhibit low reference-attention
weight and high temporal entropy (indicating weak, diffuse grounding in the demonstration), Adaptive
Horizon (AH) truncates execution to the reliable prefix (6–24 steps), advances the context window by the
executed duration, and replans from fresh observations. Appendix E.3 details AH and context synchronization.

5
Experiments
We evaluate Entangle on the RoboTwin benchmark [15, 16] and a physical Astribot S1 robot across two
generalization axes: Task-OOD, where the complete goal and multi-step procedure are absent from training
data, and Context-OOD, where the execution scene departs from strictly aligned training pairs through
rearranged layouts or substituted objects and tools.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Inference protocol. Both simulation and real-robot inference condition purely on a 13-frame human-video
window without language input, using 50 denoising steps and video/action guidance scales of 5 and 1.
Simulation (RoboTwin). We address the simulator domain shift with joint SFT on 43 RoboTwin training
tasks, then evaluate on seven unseen tasks. Context advances 13 frames per prediction round. Real robot
7


(Astribot S1). We evaluate the CT model directly without downstream SFT, predicting 24 action steps at
10 Hz (2.4 s) and executing 6–24 steps per round via AH (or all 24 steps without AH; Appendix E.3).
Metrics. We report binary success rates in simulation and graded task scores (0–100) on the real robot.
Real-robot scores are averaged over 10 trials per task and configuration, measuring completion of both the
demonstrated goal and procedural constraints (rubrics in Appendix A.3).
5.2
RoboTwin Benchmark Evaluation
We evaluate seven RoboTwin tasks excluded from SFT training under the Zero-WAM demo_clean protocol [9]:
three seeds and 100 episodes per task per seed, with one fixed generated human context per task. Entangle
reaches 69.52%, exceeding Zero-WAM (46.95%) by 22.57 percentage points (Table 1) and the agent-based
RoboICL (32.38%) by 37.14 points. While Zero-WAM competes well on single-object tasks (e.g., opening a
microwave, placing a cup), it scores 9.00% on three-block stacking (SB), versus Entangle’s 70.33%. This gap
indicates that task-matched clips generated from an edited initial frame suffice for short, single-stage actions,
whereas frame-aligned correspondence is critical for multi-step sequences.
5.3
Real-Robot Task-OOD Evaluation
The six Astribot S1 tasks are absent from pretraining and CT. They test block manipulation (object roles and
ordering), mouse use (novel grasping, surface motion, and clicks), towel wringing (bimanual coordination),
counterintuitive goals (unconventional destinations), sweeping (using a cup as a tool), and obstacle
avoidance (following a specified passage beyond reaching the goal; Fig. 4). Appendix A gives task definitions,
and Appendix C pairs human contexts with robot executions.

detour passage to the goal, reducing Avoidance to zero. Finally, executing all 24 steps open-loop (w/o AH,
61.3) hurts Avoidance and Towel, as executing later steps after reference attention weakens accumulates route
and contact errors. Thus, data-level alignment, training-level contrast, and deployment-level replanning are
all essential.

5.6
Context-OOD Evaluation
Because all training pairs are strictly scene-aligned, we evaluate four progressively harder Context-OOD
shifts (Fig. 6) to examine how Entangle establishes correspondence when the workspace departs from the
reference. Under spatial layout shift (reoriented pens) and within-category substitution (black to white
mouse), the policy adapts its grasp orientation and reproduces the same letter-tracing trajectories across mouse
appearances. Under affordance-preserving cross-object transfer and affordance repurposing, the policy
transfers interactions across object categories, adapting hold-and-wipe from a picture frame to a stand and
using a banana to sweep a wooden block into a box when a brush and dustpan are absent. Thus, supervising
strict temporal alignment on a world-action prior does not lock the policy into pixel-level copying; instead,
Entangle exhibits an emergent ability to transfer demonstrated interactions to novel objects and scenes, freeing
deployment from strict scene alignment to accommodate diverse demonstrations.

6
Limitations and Future Work
Our real-robot evaluation is limited to one embodiment, and the data pipeline relies on the fidelity of generated
human videos. Cross-scene transfer and generated-context deployment are demonstrated through qualitative
cases; broader quantitative evaluation is needed. Future work will explore cross-embodiment transfer, more
reliable data generation, and failure recovery for longer tasks.
7
Conclusion
We presented Entangle, which uses human video as an interface for specifying robot behavior without task-
specific parameter updates. Our findings suggest that learning from aligned human–robot experience can
support transferable demonstration following beyond familiar tasks and scenes. This offers a path toward
more accessible robot adaptation, where people can communicate new behaviors by showing how they should
be performed.

RoboTwin Tasks, Environments, and Scoring
D.1
Task Environments, Goals, and Evaluation Protocol
We follow the released Zero-WAM demo_clean protocol on seven RoboTwin tasks held out from the 43-task
simulation SFT split. Each task uses one fixed generated human-video reference. For each video-conditioned
checkpoint, we evaluate 100 expert-feasible scenes per task per seed. A scene qualifies if the scripted expert
20


can plan and complete the task. We report the mean and sample standard deviation over three evaluation
seeds, averaging the seven task rates within each seed for the overall score.
Task environments and goals. The task definitions and scene setup are identical to those of the original
RoboTwin benchmark.
OS: Object on scale (place_object_scale). A mouse, stapler, or bell and an electronic scale are placed on the
same side of the table. The object’s initial center has x ∈[−0.25, 0.25] m, y ∈[−0.20, 0.05] m, with a small
central exclusion region; the scale is at least 0.15 m away in the horizontal plane. Object and scale asset
variants and in-plane orientation vary. Grasp the object, move it onto the scale’s platform, and release it. The
score tests placement, not a numerical weight reading.
SS: Stamp seal (stamp_seal). A seal and a 7×7 cm colored target appear on the same side of the table, initially
separated by at least 0.10 m. Seal appearance and target color vary. Move the seal to the designated colored
target and release it. Although the task is called stamping, the implemented predicate checks horizontal
alignment and open grippers; it does not independently verify stamping pressure or an ink impression.
OM: Open microwave (open_microwave). A fixed-base microwave with a hinged door is placed toward
the back of the table, with model and position variation. Its base is sampled from x ∈[−0.12, −0.02] m
and y ∈[0.15, 0.20] m. Grasp/pull the handle until the door reaches the required opening angle. No object
insertion, button press, or microwave operation is required.
MP: Move stapler to pad (move_stapler_pad). A stapler and an 11 × 6 cm colored pad are placed on the
same side of the table, initially at least 0.10 m apart. Stapler model, yaw, and pad color vary. Grasp the stapler,
transfer it to the pad, and release it. The released Zero-WAM rule requires physical stapler–pad contact with a
horizontal positional margin; it does not impose the upstream task’s specific quaternion condition.
BB: Bread in basket (place_bread_basket). A basket is placed near the table center at (0, −0.20) m. Bread
pieces are sampled in the side regions, separated from the basket and from one another, with appearance and
orientation variation. The scene constructor attempts to place two pieces; the predicate applies to every piece
actually created. Transfer all pieces into the basket and release them. Leaving one piece outside does not
satisfy the task.
EC: Cup on coaster (place_empty_cup). An empty cup begins in a left or right side region, and a fixed coaster
is placed closer to the center, initially at least 0.10 m away. Grasp the cup, move it onto the coaster, and release
it. The score compares functional points on the cup and coaster; it does not separately test cup orientation.
SB: Stack three blocks (stack_blocks_three). Three 5 cm cubes, colored red, green, and blue, begin at
separated table positions with varied in-plane rotations. Form a three-block tower with red at the bottom,
green in the middle, and blue on top, then release both grippers. The native predicate tests relative block
alignment and vertical spacing; it does not require the tower to be at a particular table-center coordinate.

Model configuration. The model configuration has 24 transformer layers, hidden width 4,096, 32 attention
heads, and a 55-dimensional unified action representation. It uses additive embeddings for the head, left-wrist,
23


and right-wrist views. The three RoPE conditions use the same layout at training and inference: temporal

E.2
Inference Settings
Simulation. The simulation evaluation client combines the head and wrist views into a 384 × 464 image and
uses current-observation geometry features. At each action inference, the context contains 13 consecutive
raw frames, advances by 13 frames after a successful inference, and repeats its last frame once the context
is exhausted. The context cursor resets per episode; it does not use the future query trajectory or a learned
alignment signal. The client configures 50 video and 50 action denoising steps, video/action shifts of 5/1, and
context/action guidance of 5/1 with text guidance disabled. The released controller executes 16 commands
initially and 32 subsequently, interpolating from the source action rate of 10 Hz to simulator commands at
50 Hz.
The simulation action mapping uses camera-frame end-effector representations with 6D rotations and stepwise
q99 statistics from RoboTwin training data. Its gripper channel retains the raw [0, 100] convention. The
no-reference control uses a global batch size of 1,024 during SFT. At inference it receives zero context tokens
and uses a context guidance scale of 1.
Real robot. Recording resolutions and frame rates for the head and two wrist RGB views are given in Section C.
Each inference predicts 24 actions at 10 Hz (2.4 s); AH selects a 6–24-step execution prefix, while the no-AH
control executes all 24 steps. Context windows contain 13 frames at 5 Hz and advance by the executed prefix
duration. Section E.3 specifies synchronization, attention extraction, and guidance. The 10 Hz rate refers to
action waypoints.
External baselines. The main-paper WAN-Action, LingBot-VA, and Zero-WAM entries reproduce measurements
from Table 2 of the Zero-WAM paper cited in the main text. The source uses three evaluation seeds and 100
rollouts per unseen task per seed, reporting the across-seed mean and standard deviation. Our evaluation
follows the same seven-task split and released success checks. The results report means and standard deviations
over three evaluation seeds for each fixed checkpoint.
RoboICL evaluation. We evaluate the GPT-6 Astra agent with xhigh reasoning effort. We use the RoboTwin
task settings and success predicates described in Sections D and D.2. RoboICL retains interaction history
within an episode and can retry failed operations. For fairness, the starred entry counts success only when
every required operation succeeds on its first physical attempt. We evaluate seven tasks over three seeds, with
10 scenes per task per seed.

E.4
Paired Dataset Construction and Split Checks
Episode-level split. A target robot episode can be paired with multiple human videos. We assign all pairs
that share a target episode to the same split and verify that training and validation share no target episode
identifiers.
Synchronous human reenactment. Participants first practice following the source robot video, then record
while watching its playback and synchronizing the manipulation with it. They use a viewpoint matched
to the head camera and mimic the gripper’s grasping and release motions with their hands while avoiding
dexterous finger manipulations that the parallel gripper cannot reproduce. All human demonstrations record
only tabletop hand and forearm manipulations without faces or personally identifiable information, and were
collected with participant consent.
Keyframe editing and video generation. Source robot-video annotations, including Qwen3.8-generated
descriptions, provide the atomic action condition. Selected source frames are edited with gpt-image-2 using
HaGRID human-hand appearance references. The edit preserves the source scene, objects, viewpoint, and
contact relationships while replacing robot manipulators with human hands and forearms. Edited frames
are quality-checked before serving as appearance references for video editing with Seedance 2.5 or Wan
3.0. The complete source robot clip provides the motion reference, together with the action description and
scene-preservation instructions. No explicit robot-arm segmentation mask, depth map, or edge map is supplied
to the generator. Accepted human clips retain the original robot observations and actions as supervision.
Cross-embodiment action supervision. Both Astribot and AgiBot recordings supply paired video–action
training examples. AgiBot examples retain their own recorded actions under the shared action representation,
with dimension-validity masks identifying available action components. They contribute video and action
supervision during CT; real-robot deployment is evaluated on Astribot S1.
VLM screening. We sample frames at the midpoints of equal-duration intervals within each clip and present
them to a vision–language model (VLM) in chronological order. For paired comparisons, the source and
generated frames occupy two rows, with each column matching the same relative time in the two clips.
The visual-quality check tests for complete replacement of visible robot manipulators, absence of residual
robot geometry, anatomical hand and forearm appearance, scene stability, and motion continuity. Task tools
already present in the source are distinguished from robot residue. Skin-colored rigid robot links or gripper
shapes fail the check even when their surface appearance resembles skin.
For the source-alignment check, the verifier receives the paired frames and the source action description.
It checks action identity, left/right roles, timing of the main phases, trajectories, object states, and absence
of additional manipulation. Different hand poses, incidental contact after placement, and small differences
in release or withdrawal timing are allowed when the main manipulation and resulting object states agree.
Changing the target object, reversing a transport direction, or continuing to manipulate an object after the
source action ends fails this check. The verifier returns a pass/fail decision for each check. We retain a
generated clip only if it passes both.
