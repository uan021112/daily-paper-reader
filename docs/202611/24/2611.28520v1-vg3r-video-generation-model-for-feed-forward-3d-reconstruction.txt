Abstract
Video generation models are widely used as 3D priors for
novel view synthesis and 3D scene generation. Fine-tuned
for perception, generation models also excel at dense pre-
diction such as depth estimation. However, methods that
apply video generation models to 3D reconstruction either
predict depth without cameras or generate geometry and
estimate cameras through many iterative denoising steps,
which makes them slower. In this paper, we present VG3R,
which turns a pretrained video generation model into a
feed-forward 3D reconstruction model. Our key observa-
tion is that such a model already captures the depth and
camera motion of a video, but not in the form required for
reconstruction. We introduce two components: clip-level
shift prediction, which corrects depth distortion caused by
the unknown shift in decoded inverse depth, and a multi-
layer camera head, which predicts per-frame cameras pa-
rameters by combining features across transformer blocks.
Using Wan2.1 as its video backbone, VG3R reconstructs a
scene and estimate the camera of every frame without it-
erative denoising. It achieves the lowest ATE and recon-
struction errors among methods built on video generation
models, runs about 4 and 14 times faster than Aether and
Geo4D. VG3R also demonstrates competitive depth estima-
tion and 3D reconstruction performance compared with ge-
ometry foundation models.

Video generation models go further: they generate videos
that remain coherent as camera and objects move, which
suggests that they implicitly learn the 3D structure of
scenes. They have likewise been adapted to video depth
estimation [19, 52, 78, 85], and GenCeption [63] turns
Wan2.1 [58] into a single-step model that matches or ex-
ceeds Depth Anything 3 [34], SAM 3 [8] and VGGT-Ω[62]
on their own tasks with less training data. Many works also
use the 3D prior of video generation models, from objects to
scenes: they generate multi-view videos of objects [60, 74],
synthesize novel views of scenes [37, 84] and create world-
consistent videos and explorable 3D scenes [3, 22, 48].
However, directly recovering both scene geometry and
camera poses from a video remains challenging for video
generation models. The 3D generation methods above syn-
thesize views along given camera trajectories, whereas 3D
reconstruction must recover both geometry and cameras
from the input video. Video depth estimators [19, 52, 78,
85] predict depth or inverse depth up to scale and shift, but
require separate camera pose estimation and alignment for
3D reconstruction. GenCeption [63] predicts one output per
prompt, such as depth or a ray map, not depth and cameras
jointly. Methods that jointly generate geometry and esti-
mate cameras with video generation models [20, 24, 41, 90]
need many iterative denoising steps, which makes them
slower. Consequently, feed-forward reconstruction is domi-
nated by geometry foundation models [31, 64, 67, 86] such
as VGGT [61], π3 [70], MapAnything [29], VGGT-Ω[62]
and Depth Anything 3 [34].
These models encode each
frame with an image backbone such as DINOv2 [45] and
treat a video as a set of images (Fig. 1(a), left).
In this paper, we show that a video generation model can
be turned directly into a feed-forward reconstruction model.
Our key observation is that, used as a feed-forward network,
a video generation model already captures the depth and
camera motion of a video, but not in the form required for
reconstruction (Fig. 2). For depth, the deterministic depth
pipeline of DVD [85] decodes the inverse depth ˆx of every
frame from the video diffusion transformer without sam-
pling (Fig. 2(i)). However, ˆx is normalized over the clip
to [0, 1], from 0 at the farthest surfaces to 1 at the nearest,
so taking 1/ˆx as depth places the farthest surfaces at infin-
ity and exaggerates the depth of far surfaces relative to near
ones. Even with the ground-truth cameras and scale, the
room reconstructed this way bends away from the ground
truth (Fig. 2(ii)). Recovering depth requires estimating, for
each clip, the shift c removed by this normalization, such
that the inverse depth is proportional to ˆx + c [65, 66, 83].
This shift is the inverse depth of the farthest surfaces di-
vided by the range of inverse depth in the clip, so it depends
on the relative depths of the nearest and farthest surfaces,
while the remaining unknown scale only changes the over-
all size of the reconstruction. Beyond depth, reconstructing
the scene also requires camera poses. The transformer fea-
tures contain information about camera motion, but existing
depth pipeline does not estimate cameras (Fig. 2(ii)).
Based on this observation, we present VG3R, which fine-
tunes Wan2.1 [58] with LoRA [18] into a feed-forward
model that reconstructs a scene and estimates the cameras
of all frames from a video (Fig. 1(a), right), without it-
erative denoising or a separate pose estimator (Fig. 1(b)
and Fig. 2(iii)).
Building on DVD’s deterministic depth
pipeline, VG3R introduces two components to recover
depth and cameras jointly. For depth, clip-level shift predic-
tion (ClipShift) pools transformer features across the video
to predict a single shift, correcting the decoded inverse

For
cameras, the multi-layer camera head (LayerTap) combines
features from intermediate and final transformer blocks to
predict the camera parameters of every frame. The point
map of every frame then follows from its depth and cam-

lowest ATE on every dataset and the lowest reconstruction

with geometry foundation models.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We present VG3R, a feed-forward 3D reconstruction
model fine-tuned from a video generation model, which
predicts a scene and the camera of every frame from a
video without iterative denoising and thus establishes a
direct path from video generation to 3D reconstruction.
• We find that a video generation model already captures
the depth and camera motion of a video, and propose
ClipShift and LayerTap to make them available for re-
construction.
• We show that VG3R outperforms the methods built on
video generation models in ATE and reconstruction er-
rors, approaches geometry foundation models and runs
about 4 and 14 times faster than Aether and Geo4D.
2. Related Work
2.1. Generative Priors for 3D and Perception
Video generation models are widely used as 3D priors.
They synthesize novel views from one or a few images [37,
84], generate multi-view videos of static and dynamic ob-
jects [60, 74], enable camera-controlled generation [16, 48]
and create explorable 3D scenes [3, 22, 54]. Image diffu-
sion models have also been adapted for depth and normal
estimation [13, 26, 28], including deterministic single-step
variants [17, 43, 75]. Vision Banana [14] instruction-tunes
an image generator into a generalist vision model by cast-
ing the outputs of vision tasks as RGB images.
Unlike
these works, VG3R uses a video generation model as a feed-
forward 3D reconstruction model rather than for generation
or depth estimation alone.
2.2. Video Generation Models for Geometry
Video generation models have been adapted to predict ge-
ometry in several ways, ranging from direct regression to
iterative generation. GenCeption [63] adapts Wan2.1 [58]
for single-step perception, using text prompts to select tasks
such as depth or ray map estimation. Built on Stable Video
Diffusion [5], DepthCrafter [19], ChronoDepth [52] and
Depth Any Video [78] predict affine-invariant depth or dis-
parity, while GeometryCrafter [77] and MotionCrafter [91]
predict point maps. DVD [85], whose depth pipeline we
adopt, adapts Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B into a deterministic regres-
sor from the RGB latent at a fixed timestep. Geo4D [24],
Aether [90], Sora3R [41] and Gen3R [20] generate geome-
try and cameras through iterative denoising. Other works
obtain geometry through next-frame prediction [79], the
prediction heads of a reconstruction model [87], or joint
modeling with other modalities [21, 33, 72].
Beyond explicit geometry prediction, probing studies
examine the 3D information encoded in frozen features
of image, video and vision-language models [12, 42, 53].
VidFM3D [23] finds that frozen Wan2.1 features outper-
form DINO features for reconstruction, while Geometry
Forcing [71] aligns video diffusion features with a 3D foun-
dation model. Unlike methods that predict only depth or
rely on iterative generation, VG3R jointly predicts depth
and cameras with a deterministic feed-forward network.
Concurrent with our work, Gen4P [73] also fine-tunes
Wan2.1 with a rank-512 LoRA into a feed-forward model,
predicting video depth and camera poses alongside 3D point
tracking.
Gen4P uses a separate cross-attention decoder
to answer queries, recovers cameras from queried points
through Kabsch fits and pose-graph optimization, and can
incorporate DVD’s LoRA as a frozen prior. In contrast,
VG3R decodes depth with the video generation model’s
VAE and directly regresses cameras from its transformer
features.
Without a video generation prior,
CVD [40] and
MegaSaM [32] optimize consistent depth for each video
at test time, RollingDepth [27] aligns image diffusion pre-
dictions across short video snippets, and Video Depth
Anything [10] adds a temporal head to Depth Anything
V2 [80]. Inspired by CVD’s geometric consistency con-
straints, VG3R instead enforces cross-frame depth consis-
tency during training through clip-level depth alignment
and multi-view supervision using ground-truth correspon-
dences.
2.3. 3D Geometry Foundation Models
Geometry foundation models learn feed-forward 3D recon-
struction from multiple views. DUSt3R [67] trained one
network to regress the point maps of an image pair in
a common coordinate frame, and MASt3R [31] added a
matching head to the same backbone. MonST3R [86] and
CUT3R [64] extend this to dynamic scenes and to streams
of frames. VGGT [61] predicts the cameras, depth and point
maps of many views with one transformer, π3 [70] removes
its reference view so that the prediction is equivariant to
the order of the views, and MapAnything [29] accepts op-
tional inputs such as intrinsics and predicts metric scale.
All of them build on image backbones [45] and train on
many multi-view datasets with 3D supervision. VG3R re-
tains their feed-forward design and replaces the image back-
bone with a video generation model pretrained on frames in

3.2. ClipShift: Clip-Level Shift Prediction
The decoded inverse depth ˆx cannot be used directly for
3D reconstruction, as the missing shift distorts the scene
geometry (Fig. 2(ii)). Recovering depth requires adding a
clip-level shift c to ˆx, giving depth proportional to 1/(ˆx +
c). From Eq. (2), the corresponding ground-truth shift is
c∗= δlo/(δhi −δlo), giving δi = (δhi −δlo)(xi + c∗). The
remaining scale factor δhi−δlo only changes the overall size
of the reconstruction, while the shift determines its shape.
Importantly, the shift is invariant to scene scale and
shared across all frames of the input video clip. This allows
us to recover the scene geometry up to scale by predicting a
single shift from the video clip. We therefore introduce clip-
level shift prediction (ClipShift), which pools the tokens of
the last DiT block across all latent frames using attention
and predicts a positive shift ˆc with an MLP. The depth of

Training. We train VG3R on 11 public datasets: Scan-
Net++ [82], TartanAir V2 [68, 69], Matterport3D [9],
ScanNet [11], PointOdyssey [88], Dynamic Replica [25],
Spring [44], Virtual KITTI 2 [7], DL3DV [35], Waymo [1,
57] and RealEstate10K [89]. We initialize the LoRA from
the released DVD weights [85] and train it together with
the prediction heads using AdamW [39]. The LoRA has
rank 512, and training takes about 40 hours on 128 NVIDIA
GH200 GPUs. We will release the trained model weights
and code to support further research.
Evaluation. At test time, input frames are resized to ap-
proximately 288 × 512 pixels, preserving their aspect ra-
tio.
All methods receive videos of consecutive frames,
with no evaluation sequences included in our training data.
We evaluate video depth on Sintel [6], Bonn [46] and
KITTI [15, 59] using absolute relative error (AbsRel) and
the fraction of pixels with a depth ratio below 1.25 (δ <
1.25), after one alignment per video in each method’s out-
put space. Temporal consistency is measured by tempo-
ral alignment error (TAE) [78] on ScanNet. Camera tra-
jectories are evaluated by absolute trajectory error (ATE)
and relative pose error (RPE) on Sintel, the dynamic se-
quences of TUM RGB-D [56] (TUM-dynamics), and Scan-
Net. We evaluate 3D reconstruction on 7-Scenes [55] and
NRGBD [2] using accuracy, completeness, and normal con-
sistency, after similarity alignment and ICP.

4.3. Ablation
Table 3 evaluates ClipShift and LayerTap in otherwise iden-
tical runs of 10,000 steps, Tab. 4 examines inference win-
dows and training data, and Tab. 5 studies the effects of tem-
poral input and video pretraining in a separate experiment.
ClipShift. ClipShift predicts a shared shift for each video
clip to correct the geometric distortion caused by the de-
coded inverse depth. Without this shift, the reconstruction
F-score at 5 cm (F@5) [50] falls from 65.0 to 19.6 on eval20
and from 64.4 to 21.8 on ARKit43 (Tab. 3). A single con-
stant shift across all clips only partially recovers this drop,
reaching 44.3 and 55.2, respectively. These results show
that the shift must adapt to each clip to correct the distor-
tion in reconstructed geometry.
After full training, the final model improves both the re-
construction and the cameras (last three rows of Tab. 3):
with its predicted cameras, F@5 rises from 65.0 to 76.8 on
eval20 and from 64.4 to 79.3 on ARKit43, and RPE-r drops
from 3.79◦to 1.14◦and from 3.41◦to 1.29◦. To assess
the accuracy of the predicted shift independently of camera
poses errors, we also evaluate the final model with ground-
truth (GT) cameras. ClipShift then achieves an F@5 of 85.6
on eval20 and 92.4 on ARKit43, within 1.1 points of a per-
clip scale-and-shift fit of the same decoded inverse depth to
the ground truth (GT fit). This shows that ClipShift, with-
out access to the ground truth, recovers nearly all the recon-
struction accuracy of the GT fit.
LayerTap. LayerTap combines features from intermediate
and final transformer blocks to predict the camera of every
frame. Using only the final-block tokens increases RPE-
r from 3.79 to 7.80 on eval20 and from 3.41 to 7.78 on
ARKit43. Reconstruction F@5 also decreases from 65.0
to 61.6 and from 64.4 to 60.4, respectively. These results
show that intermediate-block features improve camera esti-
mation, which also benefits reconstruction.

5. Conclusion
We presented VG3R, which turns a pretrained video gener-
ation model into a feed-forward 3D reconstruction model.
ClipShift corrects the shift ambiguity in decoded inverse
depth, while LayerTap predicts per-frame cameras from
transformer features. Across video depth, camera trajec-
tory, and 3D reconstruction benchmarks, VG3R achieves
the lowest ATE and reconstruction accuracy and complete-
ness errors among methods built on video generation mod-
els. By avoiding iterative denoising, it runs about 4 and
14 times faster than Aether and Geo4D, respectively, while
achieving competitive depth and reconstruction accuracy
against geometry foundation models.
Future work. Our approach could extend to other 3D and
4D perception tasks, such as surface normal estimation,
scene flow, and 3D point tracking. More broadly, adapting
video generators through LoRA and prediction heads raises
the possibility of a unified model that both generates videos
and reconstructs their underlying 3D scenes.

VG3R: Video Generation Model for Feed-Forward 3D Reconstruction
Supplementary Material
This supplement first completes the description of
VG3R: the details of ClipShift, LayerTap and the inference
on long videos (Sec. A) and its two depth losses (Sec. B).
It then details the evaluation of the main paper (Sec. C),
shows that the video depth results of VG3R on Sintel and
Bonn hold under the evaluation code of π3 (Sec. D), and
shows more reconstructions, video depth and camera trajec-
tories (Sec. E). The analyses that follow show that the shift
predicted by ClipShift is nearly as accurate as a fit to the
ground truth and that the intermediate blocks read by Lay-
erTap halve the rotation error of the cameras in shorter runs
(Sec. F), and that video pretraining and temporal input con-
tribute substantially to the accuracy of the depth (Sec. G).
Section H lists the data and their licenses.
A. Implementation Details
This section gives the architecture of ClipShift and Layer-
Tap and the inference of VG3R on videos longer than the
training clips. Frames are scaled to [−1, 1] before the VAE
encoder.
ClipShift.
ClipShift reads the 1536-dimensional tokens
that the last DiT block passes to the output layer, N ×
(H/16)×(W/16) of them for a clip of N frames of H ×W
pixels. After a layer normalization and without a position
embedding, one learned query attends to all of them in one
multi-head attention layer with 12 heads. The pooled vector
passes a layer normalization, a linear layer to 256 channels,
a GELU and a linear layer to one value, and a softplus with
an offset of 10−4 gives the positive shift ˆc of the clip. The
head has 9.8M parameters, and τ = ϵ = 10−3 in Eq. (3).
One shift for the whole clip keeps all its frames on one affine
map, as the read-out of Sec. B.1 assumes, whereas a shift
per frame could absorb the drift between frames.
LayerTap.
Each of the three token sets passes a layer
normalization and a linear map before they are summed.
The head keeps all 576 tokens of every latent frame at
288 × 512 pixels, with a 2D sinusoidal position embed-
ding, and two learned embeddings mark latent frame 0 and
the latent frame paired with it. Learned queries, one for
latent frame 0 and four for every later latent frame, carry
an embedding of their frame index and attend jointly over
each pair in four transformer blocks. The camera head of
VGGT [61] then attends across the L frame tokens and re-
fines the camera of every frame in K = 4 iterations, as a
translation, a unit quaternion and the horizontal and vertical
fields of view. In Eq. (4), γ = 0.6, wt = 3 and wf = 0.5,
and Lpts uses the valid pixels of a grid of stride 4. Keeping
all tokens instead of pooling them by 2 × 2 gives the low-
est RPE-r on eval20 in the shorter runs, 2.75◦against 3.79◦
(Tab. 10), so the final model keeps them.
Inference.
All frames share the intrinsic matrix formed
from ˆf0, and a longer video takes the median of ˆf0 over
its windows. We chose the window length and stride be-
fore any benchmark run, with an earlier model trained on
three datasets, on 30 validation clips of ScanNet [11] with
97 frames each (Tab. 6). Windows of 37 frames give the
lowest AbsRel, and among them a stride of 24 gives a lower
ATE than a stride of 18 with fewer passes, and a lower Ab-
sRel and ATE than a stride of 30, so we use a window of
37 frames with a stride of 24. Processing the 97 frames in
one pass instead raises ATE from 0.090 to 0.350 and RPE-r
from 1.02◦to 13.96◦, so the windows are needed for videos
longer than the training clips.

C. Evaluation Details
This section describes how the comparisons of Tab. 1 and
the inference times of Tab. 2 are obtained. Every method
receives only the frames of a video, without cameras or
ground truth, and runs once on GH200 GPUs with the set-
tings of Tab. 7. Table 7 lists the input size, the window
and the output of every method, and the fit of its video
depth: a scale and shift for an output defined up to both
(s, t) and a scale otherwise (s). For video depth, we use
the 14 sequences of Sintel [6], 5 of Bonn [46] and 13
of KITTI [15, 59] with up to 110 frames, as prepared by
MonST3R [86], and align each method to the ground truth
by one fit per sequence in its own output space. The tem-
poral alignment error (TAE) [78] compares the depth of
consecutive frames after reprojection with the ground-truth
cameras, computed with the code of Video Depth Any-
thing [10]. Camera trajectories are evaluated by ATE and
RPE after a similarity alignment on Sintel, the 8 dynamic
sequences of TUM RGB-D [56] (TUM-dynamics) and the
100 test scenes of ScanNet [11], 90 frames at stride 3 each,
of which 94 have valid poses. For reconstruction, we take
the first 90 frames at stride 3 of the 18 test sequences of 7-
Scenes [55] and the 9 scenes of NRGBD [2], back-project
the predicted depth with the predicted intrinsics and cam-
eras, align the point maps to the ground truth by a similarity
transform and ICP, and report accuracy, completeness and
normal consistency.

D. Video Depth with the Evaluation Code of π3
To check that the video depth results do not depend on
our alignment, Tab. 8 evaluates VG3R and the geometry
foundation models with the video depth evaluation code of
π3 [70], run unchanged. The code aligns every output as
depth, by a scale and shift that minimize the L1 error or
by a scale alone, and its scale-only rows are the values of
the main paper. The main paper aligns each method in its
own output space instead, because the inverse of an affine-
invariant inverse depth is not an affine-invariant depth. Un-
der this code, VG3R still has the lowest AbsRel and the
highest δ1.25 on Sintel and Bonn after the scale-and-shift
alignment, and the highest δ1.25 on both after the scale
alignment, so its results on these benchmarks do not depend
on aligning each method in its own output space.

Decision rules.
Each model was evaluated once on these
clips with the same evaluation code. A model wins a com-
parison of F@5 when the median difference is at least 3
points in its favor, it is better on at least 70% of the clips,
and P@5 and F@10 agree. A comparison of G5 passes un-
der the same conditions on G5 if, in addition, G10 and the
spread of the per-frame scales agree and the F@5 of the
first model is at most 3 points lower. We set the margin of
3 points on development data, without reading any held-out
clip.
Results.
Table 11 gives the comparisons, and Tab. 5 of
the main paper the medians of Wan-LoRA, its use with
one-frame input and Wan-Scratch. The first model wins ev-
ery comparison of F@5, and Wan-LoRA with video input
passes the comparison of G5 against its use with one-frame
input. Wan-LoRA is far more accurate than Wan-Scratch,
which was still improving at the end of training. With one-
frame input, Wan-LoRA is less accurate and drifts more,
as in the main paper. Wan-LoRA-DVD, which also trains
at a lower learning rate, is more accurate than Wan-LoRA.
For deployment, the shift is solved from the known cam-
eras instead of fitted to the ground truth, by modeling depth
as s/(ˆx + c) with one s and one c per clip; with this shift,
Wan-LoRA-DVD is more accurate than the best released
DVD model. In sum, Wan-LoRA is more accurate than
Wan-Scratch after the same number of steps by a median
of 22.6 points of F@5 and than its use with one-frame input
by 20.0 points, and starting from the LoRA of DVD adds
10.5 points, so video pretraining and temporal input both
contribute substantially to the accuracy within our training
budget.
