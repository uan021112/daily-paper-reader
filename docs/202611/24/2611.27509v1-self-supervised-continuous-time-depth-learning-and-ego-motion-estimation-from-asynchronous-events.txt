## Abstract 

Event cameras register brightness changes pixel by pixel with microsecond precision, whereas the dense depth that could supervise them is costly to acquire and exists only at isolated instants. Condensing the stream into frames eases learning, but at the expense of the very timing that sets event sensing apart. We propose a self-supervised approach in which each event is assigned its own depth in continuous time, and the camera motion is recovered from the same ob-servations. Supervision arises from geometry: lifted into 3D by its predicted depth and displaced by the camera motion at the instant it fired, an event should coincide with others produced by the same edge, so any misalignment exposes an error in depth or motion. Differentiable rendering then turns these per-event estimates into dense depth maps at tar-get times within each window, faithful to the events behind them. On MVSEC, the approach outperforms prior event-only self-supervised methods in most sequences and depth ranges, with the widest margins at night, and recovers cam-era rotation more accurately than an earlier unsupervised event-based method. Ablations isolate the roles of local spa-tiotemporal context, the temporal backbone, and the align-ment objective. These results position motion-compensated event alignment as a principled source of supervision for continuous-time depth and ego-motion estimation. 

## 1. Introduction 

Each pixel of an event camera fires independently the mo-ment its brightness changes, producing a sparse stream with microsecond temporal resolution and high dynamic range that is well suited to depth estimation in high-speed and low-light regimes [5]. In this setting, scene structure and camera motion are coupled prediction problems: each event must be assigned a depth, and the continuous camera motion that carries the same 3D point across the sensor must be known at each event’s own timestamp. The difficulty is that event streams are irregular in time arXiv:2611.27509v1 [cs.CV] 20 Nov 2026 and sparse in space, whereas dense metric depth is usually available only at image timestamps. Assigning the same endpoint depth map to every event ignores the fact that the camera and the visible geometry evolve within the interval. Most learning pipelines respond to this mismatch by convert-ing events into event frames, voxel grids, or recurrent frame surrogates before estimating geometry [6, 7, 12, 13, 18]. This suits standard backbones but discards the fine-grained timing that is the main advantage of event sensing. We instead derive supervision from the geometry of the measurements themselves. A predicted depth and the camera motion together allow each event to be back-projected at its own timestamp and transported to a common instant. If both are correct, events generated by the same edge arrive at the same place with consistent timestamps. If either is wrong, the events smear across the image. Sharpness after motion compensation is therefore a measure of geometric quality that requires no depth label. Our method (Figure 1) learns per-event depth from motion-compensated event alignment using a local-global event-state encoder trained by dual-anchor alignment losses. A selective state-space model captures temporal context across the time-ordered events of each window, and a multi-scale local branch captures image-plane neighbourhoods. An occlusion-aware renderer then combines the per-event depths into a target-time map while preserving event-time geometry and gradients to the contributing depths. It anchors each pixel on its most recent observations and combines sam-ples through smooth weights, allowing the rendered map to enter the training objective. A camera-motion head predicts each window’s motion, which is also estimated by aligning its events under the predicted depths. 

Contributions. 

• A self-supervised formulation at event-time granularity :per-event depth, camera motion evaluated at each event timestamp, and dual-anchor motion-compensated align-ment in place of dense depth labels. • A local-global event-state encoder that pairs bidirectional Mamba temporal modelling with multi-scale local spa-tiotemporal aggregation returned to the original event or-der, a head that predicts a bounded depth per event, and a camera-motion head for the window. Ablations con-firm the contributions of the local branch, the state-space backbone, and the alignment objective. • Geometrically consistent dense depth from per-event depth :a differentiable occlusion-aware renderer transports each event depth to the target time. A recency-anchored log-depth consensus suppresses older samples that dis-agree with the most recently observed surface, limiting foreground-background mixing. The target-time map re-mains consistent with its contributing event depths and passes gradients to each of them. On MVSEC, the ren-dered depth is the most accurate among event-only self-supervised methods at every 20 m cut-off and at three of the four 10 m and 30 m cut-offs. 

## 2. Related Work 

Event representations. A common route to event-based learning converts the asynchronous stream into a dense sur-rogate for conventional backbones: learned event representa-tions and voxel-style encodings make the conversion train-able [6], event-to-video reconstruction makes events usable in frame-based pipelines [18], and binary event frames suit compact image-like inputs [1]. The window length, bin count, and normalisation partly determine which motion cues are retained for learning. Event-native alternatives retain more structure: time surfaces encode recent timing [14], voxel graphs preserve sparse neighbourhoods [3], asyn-chronous graph networks update features on irregular spa-tiotemporal graphs [21], and point-based EventMamba mod-els streams without first forming images [19]. Our method follows this event-native direction for per-event depth, keep-ing events as ordered samples until target-time rendering. 

Geometry from events. Contrast maximisation provides a unifying view in which correct motion, depth, or flow sharp-ens an image of warped events [4]. EVO estimates 6-DoF motion by optimising such alignment [17], and stereo and visual-inertial systems add geometric constraints or sensors [2, 15, 20, 23]. These methods show the value of continuous-time warping. In our setting, the learned component is the per-event depth field that, with the camera motion, explains the stream. 

Learned and self-supervised event depth. E2Depth pre-dicts dense monocular depth from accumulated events [12], recurrent multimodal networks combine events with frames [7], and transformer-style recurrent models strengthen tem-poral aggregation [13]. Label-free depth learning from video supervises geometry by warping frames and minimising re-construction error [8, 22], and event warping now drives self-supervised event flow and depth [11, 25–27], in some cases together with ego-motion [26]. Our contribution is therefore not the warping signal itself but the temporal gran-ularity at which it is applied: existing methods aggregate events into frames or fixed windows and enforce consistency at those discrete anchors, whereas our method applies align-ment per event under continuous-time camera motion and predicts depth at event time. 

Sequence models. Structured state-space models offer linear-time sequence modelling with persistent latent dynam-ics [10], and selective state-space models such as Mamba add input-dependent updates that scale to long sequences [9]. For event data, this is a middle ground between frame aggre-gation and global attention: a compact state summarises the time-ordered events of a window, while local spatiotemporal aggregation restores neighbourhood structure around each event.

## 4. Experiments 

The evaluation tests four claims. First, event alignment can supervise a useful continuous-time depth field without dense depth targets. Second, occlusion-aware rendering con-verts that field into target-time maps that are competitive under conventional depth metrics. Third, the local-global en-coder is both effective and practical for long event windows. Fourth, the learned per-event depth is accurate enough for the camera motion to be recovered from the same events. Metric depth maps enter only after inference, when the rendered predictions are compared with the benchmark reference.

4.3. Ego-Motion 

At test time, the ego-motion estimate for each window is the constant 6-DoF velocity that best aligns its events under the predicted per-event depths. It is also the training motion of the depth losses and the camera-motion head’s target. Its ac-curacy therefore depends on the predicted 3D event locations. The estimate is then refined in two steps. Temporal smooth-ing averages each estimate with up to four preceding ones. A vehicle prior uses the non-holonomic motion of a car: the translation follows the vehicle’s longitudinal axis, which is fixed in the frame of a rigidly mounted camera and is esti-mated once on the training sequence. Each translation keeps its estimated speed and sign. Among the depth baselines, only Zhu et al. [26] report ego-motion, on outdoor_day1 

alone, because their network failed under the flashing street lights of the night sequences. Table 2 uses their metrics: ARPE, the angle between the predicted and ground-truth translation directions, and ARRE, the rotation-error angle. On outdoor_day1 , the rotation error of our method is 35% below that of Zhu et al. [26] ( 5.67 vs 8.67 × 10 −3 rad), and its translation-direction error is similar to theirs (7.80 vs 7.74 deg). Rotation is the more informative of the two comparisons: on this driving data the translation direction is dominated by forward motion, and in our method it fol-lows the vehicle prior’s axis, whereas the rotation must be recovered from how the transported events align. The lower rotation error therefore extends the depth result of Table 1 to the camera motion: the per-event depths that render accurate target-time maps also place the events consistently enough in 3D for their alignment to determine the camera’s rotation. 4.4. Ablations 

Local spatiotemporal encoder. The third claim is exam-ined first by removing the local branch, so that only the selective state-space path remains. This tests the premise of the local-global design, that the ordered event sequence alone does not represent image-plane neighbourhoods. With-out the local branch the depth error on outdoor_day1 

rises substantially, so long-range temporal context does not substitute for local geometric context: depth from motion parallax depends on how an edge and its neighbourhood move together on the image plane, and on whether nearby events belong to the same surface or lie on either side of an occlusion boundary. These relations are expressed by spa-tial adjacency rather than by sequence order, and the local branch supplies them at several scales to each event. 

Temporal backbone. The selective state-space backbone is then compared with GRU, LSTM, and vanilla RNN al-ternatives on the same event windows, each run in one di-rection at a matched parameter budget. The state-space backbone processes the windows faster than every alterna-tive, which supports selective state-space modelling for long event sequences. Together, the backbone comparison and local-branch ablation support combining efficient long-range temporal context with local spatial structure. 

Supervision. Finally, temporally aligned event self-supervision is replaced with a photometric objective in the same window setting, asking whether the gain follows from the event-time geometric signal or merely from the architecture and training schedule. The event-alignment objective lowers the depth error on every outdoor test se-quence, with the largest reductions on outdoor_night1 

and outdoor_night2 , and each night sequence improves by more than the daytime sequence. A photometric objec-tive depends on stable image formation, which low light, exposure variation, and motion blur degrade. Event align-ment evaluates the timestamped contrast changes directly and therefore supplies a signal better matched to the sens-ing process, particularly in the conditions for which event cameras are most useful. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented a self-supervised continuous-time formula-tion for event-native depth estimation that also recovers ego-motion from the same events. Per-event depth is predicted by a local-global event-state encoder, learned from dual-anchor motion-compensated event alignment, and rendered to target times by a differentiable occlusion-aware renderer. Geomet-ric transport provides the supervisory signal by rewarding depth predictions that explain the observed event motion un-der the estimated camera motion. On MVSEC, our method is the most accurate event-only self-supervised method at every 20 m cut-off and at three of the four 10 m and 30 m cut-offs, with its largest margins at night, and the ablations attribute its accuracy to both the local branch and the align-ment objective.
