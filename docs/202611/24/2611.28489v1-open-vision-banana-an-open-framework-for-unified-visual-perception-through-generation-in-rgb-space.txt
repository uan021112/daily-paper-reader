## ABSTRACT 

Vision Banana showed that a closed image generator, instruction-tuned to paint perception outputs as RGB images, becomes a generalist vision model. Does this carry over to open generators, and if not, what fails? We answer with Open Vision Banana (OVB) , an open framework that adapts the 7B diffusion generator of Qwen-Image-2.1 into one instruction-driven model for metric depth, semantic, instance, and referring segmentation, using a single shared low-rank adapter and public data. Each task is a task contract whose RGB encoder, instruction, and decoder share one specification and are checked before training. Studying 42 externally evaluated checkpoints from two 100k-update runs, six exact weight-space merges, and eight controlled shorter runs, we find that the gap to the closed system is specific and measurable. Geometry is learned early while absolute scale is not: median-aligned NYUv2 δ1 stays at 0.89–0.93 throughout training as unaligned δ1 wanders between 0.11 and 0.50. The scale is not committed by a single sample: a ten-seed ensemble lifts unaligned δ1

from 0.348 to 0.516 without retraining. Depth and 2D segmentation compete for the shared adapter, and weight merging moves along this trade-off without escaping it. Finally, lower validation flow loss comes with lower Cityscapes accuracy (Spearman ρ ≈ − 0.9 between loss reduction and mIoU), so the loss cannot select checkpoints. We release code, contracts, training data, and all evaluation outputs.

# 1 Introduction 

Can one image generator, guided only by a task instruction, see depth, classes, instances, and referred objects? Generative pretraining makes this plausible: to synthesize realistic scenes, a generator must model appearance, object structure, and spatial layout. Marigold [18] showed that a diffusion prior transfers to monocular depth, and Vision Banana [10] went much further: it paints segmentation, depth, and normals as RGB images, instruction-tunes an image generator on them, and reports results on par with specialist models while preserving image synthesis. Image generation may thus become a general interface for perception. The evidence for this paradigm, however, rests on a closed generator (Nano Banana Pro) trained on in-house data, with neither weights nor evaluation tools released. Two questions are therefore open: does the paradigm carry over to open generators , and, if it falls short, where exactly does it fail, and why? We argue that the second question is the more useful one. A reproduction that matches a number tells the community little; a measured account of the failure modes tells the next model what to fix. We address both with Open Vision Banana (OVB) (Figure 1), an open, independent framework that

realizes the paradigm on Qwen-Image-2.1 [40, 44]—a unified generation-and-editing model whose 7B diffusion transformer is conditioned by a frozen 8B vision-language encoder—using public data. Building it first exposes an engineering obstacle that silently breaks generative perception: RGB is only a carrier, and a prediction is meaningful only if the target encoder, the instruction, and the decoder agree on units, color codes, and mask conventions. When they disagree, the training loss still decreases while the decoded predictions fail as a whole. OVB therefore writes every task as a task contract that derives encoder, instruction, and decoder from one specification and checks them before training. A frozen editing backbone with one shared low-rank adapter then serves all tasks, selected only by the instruction. The open setting lets us study the paradigm at a granularity that a closed system does not allow: two 100k-update runs that differ only in learning rate, with 42 checkpoints scored on six benchmarks by an external suite; six exact weight-space merges of these checkpoints; and eight shorter runs that vary the starting point, the task mixture, the depth colormap, and the adaptation mode. OVB stays below the closed reference on every benchmark (Table 2), but the shortfall is neither uniform nor mysterious. It decomposes into five findings: • Geometry is learned, scale is not. Median-aligned depth is near the reference from early on; almost all of the depth gap is one number per image (Sec. 5.1). • The scale is not committed. The same input yields different scales under different noise; a seed ensemble recovers much of the gap without retraining (Sec. 5.2). • Tasks compete for one adapter. Depth improves while Cityscapes and ReasonSeg decline, along training and across mixtures and learning rates; full fine-tuning erases referring ability (Sec. 5.3). • Merging does not escape the trade-off. Exact merges move along it; only semantic segmentation gains beyond interpolation (Sec. 5.4). • The training loss is blind to what fails. Validation flow loss falls while Cityscapes accuracy falls with it (Sec. 5.5). In summary, we contribute: 1. An open framework for generation-as-perception : the Vision Banana paradigm on a public editing backbone with one shared adapter for four tasks, made reliable by task contracts that are checked before training. 2. A diagnostic study that locates the gap to the closed system in absolute depth scale, sampling variance, and task competition under a shared adapter, with every claim backed by full-benchmark external evaluation. 3. A reproducible baseline : code, contracts, the 295,505-row referring-segmentation training pool, leakage-audited evaluation protocols, and every evaluation output with checksums, with all deviations from the reference protocol documented. 

# 2 Related Work 

Generalist vision interfaces. Painter [36] casts dense prediction as image inpainting with in-context examples. InstructCV [11] and InstructDiffusion [13] instruction-tune text-to-image diffusion models to emit task outputs as images, building on the editing interface of InstructPix2Pix [3]. Vision Banana [10] scales this idea to a strong closed generator and reports specialist-level results, and related studies find emergent perception in image and video generators [38, 45]. OVB follows the RGB formulation of Vision Banana on public models and data; everything the paper does not disclose (adapter, optimizer, schedule, mixture, resolution, sampling) is our choice. Unlike prior generalists, we target metric depth and analyze why the open recipe falls short rather than proposing a new architecture.

StableNormal identifies sampling variance as an obstacle for diffusion-based geometry; we find that in the metric setting this variance concentrates in absolute scale. Specialist metric-depth models reach high zero-shot accuracy with dedicated architectures, large mixed datasets, and often camera intrinsics [2, 4, 20, 25, 34]; OVB uses no intrinsics and no depth head. 

Referring segmentation. Recent systems couple a language model with a mask decoder [19, 32, 37] or a promptable segmenter [5]; OVB paints the mask as an image with the same adapter that paints depth. 

Adaptation and weight averaging. OVB trains a rank-stabilized LoRA [16, 17] with conditional flow match-ing [21]. Averaging fine-tuned weights that share an initialization can combine their abilities [39]; we test whether it resolves the task trade-off of a shared adapter. 

What this paper measures. Each failure we report has precedent—hard metric scale, sampling variance in diffusion geometry [42], capacity trade-offs—so what we establish is their size relative to one another inside one open system, over 42 externally scored checkpoints.

It then maps u to a piecewise-linear path C along seven edges of the RGB cube, providing 7 × 255 = 1 ,785 8-bit steps. At inference, each output pixel is projected onto C to recover ˆ u, and metric depth is reconstructed by ˆd = 10 ((1 − ˆu)−1/2 − 1).

Thus, changes in absolute depth scale alter the encoded colors through the nonlinear depth transform. For semantic segmentation, each pixel is assigned the class ID of its nearest palette color under Euclidean distance in RGB space. For instance segmentation, the decoder proceeds through five stages: background initialization, 16-connected seed-based color grouping, noise pruning, boundary-artifact removal, and spatially constrained merging; its stages and thresholds are listed in Sec. C. For referring segmentation, pixels whose mean RGB value exceeds 0 .5 are classified as foreground, and all connected components are retained. 

Validation before training. Before the first update, the framework checks the manifest and configuration against the contracts (Figure 2(b)): (i) targets lie on the declared code (depth targets survive a decode–re-encode round trip within 2 /255, referring targets are binary, storage is lossless); (ii) instructions match the protocol (a relative-depth instruction is rejected under metric decoding, and no color legend exceeds the backbone’s text-encoder length); (iii) the adapter covers the attention projections that read the instruction, since an adapter restricted to feed-forward layers can only memorize fixed colors; and (iv) supervision and splits are clean (depth frames with too few valid pixels or large encoding error are rejected, and training images that overlap an evaluation set are removed by image identifier). Each check targets a failure that otherwise appears only after training.

Conditioning. OVB builds on the editing model Qwen-Image-2.1, which accepts a source image natively: x

enters as visual tokens and reference latents, pq is the text condition, and the target is generated from noise under c(x, p q ), the interface the backbone already uses for editing. Training uses conditional flow matching,

Shared adapter and starting point. The transformer, autoencoder, and text encoder are frozen. The frozen transformer is a fused checkpoint: two adapters from earlier OVB stages, trained from the same parent checkpoint (the parent of Table 3), are merged into it with weights 0.6 and 0.4: the depth-heavy run C1 of Table 3 and a rank-128 adapter trained for 20k updates on a uniform mixture of depth, normals, semantic, instance, and referring segmentation (18% each) with 10% editing replay. On top we train one zero-initialized rank-stabilized LoRA ( r = 64, α = 64, scale α/ √r = 8) on all attention projections and feed-forward layers; it is the only trained parameter set, and switching tasks changes only the instruction. 

Optimization. Both main runs train for 100,000 updates with AdamW [22] (weight decay 0.01), global batch 32 on 16 GPUs, resolution 512, 2,000 warmup updates, cosine decay, gradient clipping at norm 1, conditioning dropout 0.05, and an adapter EMA with decay 0.9999. They differ only in the peak learning rate: 3 × 10 −5

(run A) and 1 × 10 −5 (run B). The validation flow loss is evaluated every 2,500 updates; “best” denotes the EMA adapter saved when this loss reaches a new minimum. All configurations are released.

3.3 Training Data and Target Construction 

The training pool contains 1,098,435 image–instruction–target records (Fig. 3). Multiple tasks or queries may share a source image, so these counts describe supervision pairs. Each update samples a task with probability 0.35 for metric depth, 0.20 each for semantic, instance, and referring segmentation, and 0.05 for image-editing replay; records are sampled uniformly within each task. The replay pool contains 16,492 editing pairs and is included with the intent of preserving generation ability. The 407,278 depth records combine TartanAir [35], Hypersim [26], LingBot-Depth [30], ARKitScenes [1], SUN RGB-D (excluding NYU-derived samples) [29], and DIODE [31] data. Native-depth admission checks cover 359,693 records, while 47,585 retained legacy records are explicitly exempt from these checks. For newly prepared indoor and DIODE targets, invalid depth pixels are encoded as black; validity masks support data admission but do not mask the training loss. The segmentation pools contain 146,027 semantic and 233,133 instance records, including category-conditioned targets reconstructed from COCONut [7] annotations. In this component, each image contributes one semantic view containing at most 12 eligible classes and up to three category-specific instance views; unselected regions are treated as background relative to the requested categories. Semantic legends use one of four formats, and colors are remapped with probability 0.5 to encourage reliance on the instruction-specified color mapping. Targets are stored as lossless RGB images consistent with the task contracts. The 295,505 referring records comprise 270,195 RefCOCO-family pairs [43], 11,812 Rea2Seg pairs, and 13,498 LLM-Seg COCO pairs. Rea2Seg [12] contributes its GQA, LISA+, and spatial subsets, excluding its ReasonSeg category. LLM-Seg [33] contributes released questions paired with native RLE masks, without answer or chain-of-thought conditioning. Its retained subset is selected through held-out image exclusion, native mask validity and foreground-area checks, and visibility filtering under the 512-pixel training crop. Source-provided

3.4 Inference and Evaluation Protocol 

Inference generates the target from noise with 50 sampling steps, true classifier-free guidance [15] 4.0 against an empty negative prompt, seed 42, and a 512 × 512 canvas; predictions are decoded and then resized to the native ground-truth resolution (nearest neighbor for labels, bilinear for depth). Metric depth is scored on NYUv2 [28] (654 images, Eigen crop [8], 10 m cap), ETH3D [27] (454, 80 m cap), and DIODE-Indoor [31] (325, no cap) with δ1 and AbsRel accumulated over pixels and without any alignment ; we additionally report a per-image median-aligned δ1 as a diagnostic that uses the ground truth; it corrects a scale but not a shift, unlike the affine-invariant protocol of [18], and is not comparable to the reference. Cityscapes val [6] (500) is scored with mIoU over 19 classes from a global confusion matrix, RefCOCOg [23] UMD val [24] (4,896 expressions) with cIoU, and ReasonSeg val [19] (344 queries, original queries, legacy_union masks) with gIoU. Training and evaluation images are disjoint by image identifier; NYUv2, ETH3D, and ReasonSeg contribute no training data at all, while DIODE and RefCOCOg contribute their training splits and are reported as same-source held-out results. All scores come from the same evaluation protocol; per-benchmark details are in Sec. A. 

Comparability with the reference. Depth, Cityscapes, and RefCOCOg follow the reference definitions, but the reference does not state crops, caps, aggregation, resolution, or sampling settings, so these are our choices. Two benchmarks are not comparable: Vision Banana rewrites ReasonSeg queries with Gemini 2.5 Pro before segmentation [10], and we pass the original query. We report the reference numbers as context, not as a matched comparison. 

# 4 Main Results 

Table 2 summarizes the performance of the fused initialization and two representative checkpoints from run B. Continued adaptation substantially improves unaligned NYUv2 δ1, from 0.278 at initialization to 0.407 at 17.5k updates and 0.497 at 100k updates. This improvement is accompanied by a decline in Cityscapes mIoU, from 0.386 to 0.355 and 0.298, respectively. Between the balanced and depth-oriented checkpoints, referring segmentation also deteriorates: RefCOCOg cIoU decreases from 0.518 to 0.481, and ReasonSeg gIoU from 0.431 to 0.333. Depth gains are themselves dataset-dependent: the final checkpoint achieves the highest unaligned scores among the reported OVB models on NYUv2 and DIODE-Indoor, whereas the initialization remains strongest on ETH3D. Thus, none of the three models dominates across all benchmarks. Qualitative examples are in Sec. I. The contrast between unaligned and median-aligned depth further clarifies the observed limitations. For the final checkpoint, per-image scale correction raises δ1 from 0.497 to 0.926 on NYUv2, from 0.225 to 0.974 on ETH3D, and from 0.353 to 0.926 on DIODE-Indoor. These large improvements indicate that absolute-scale calibration accounts for a substantial component of the depth error, while much of the relative scene structure is preserved.

5.3 Tasks trade off under one adapter 

Along training. In run B (Figure 4(b)), Cityscapes mIoU falls from 0.362 at 10k updates to 0.293 at 100k while unaligned NYUv2 δ1 rises from 0.111 to 0.485; ReasonSeg peaks at 0.440 at 20k and settles near 0.333. The balanced and depth endpoints of Table 2 are two points of this one trajectory. 

Across learning rates. The larger learning rate of run A does not move faster along the same path. At 100k updates A is worse than B on unaligned depth (NYUv2 0.329 vs. 0.485) and on Cityscapes (0.263 vs. 0.293), but better on ReasonSeg (0.427 vs. 0.333). 5.5 Flow loss does not select checkpoints 

Checkpoint selection by validation loss is standard, and our “best” checkpoints are selected that way, so the macro-averaged validation flow loss decreases over them by construction. Yet across the EMA checkpoints of each run, the Spearman correlation between loss reduction and Cityscapes mIoU is ρ = −0.88 (A) and −0.89 (B): lower loss, worse segmentation . For unaligned NYUv2 δ1 the sign differs between the runs ( −0.05 in A, +0 .84 in B), and likewise for ReasonSeg (+0 .78 in A, −0.42 in B); all correlations are listed in Sec. F. Across runs, A reaches a lower final loss than B (0.03079 vs. 0.03120) while being worse on unaligned depth and Cityscapes. The latent mean squared error is dominated by what is easy to reconstruct, whereas the errors that matter after decoding, the global hue that carries depth scale or a few mislabeled regions, are small in latent space. Decoded metrics on a held-out selection set are needed for model selection. 

# 6 Discussion and Limitations 

Implications. The findings translate into three recommendations for generation-as-perception on open models. 

(1) Report depth both ways and over seeds. Unaligned scores conflate geometry with an uncommitted scale; reporting aligned scores and seed spread separates the two. (2) Select checkpoints by decoded metrics. The flow loss rewards what is easy to reconstruct in latent space, not the global hue that carries scale or the few regions that decide a mask, so selection needs decoded metrics on a held-out set. (3) Treat scale and task competition as the targets. Scale can be supervised where it is decided: reconstruct the clean latent from the predicted velocity, decode it, project onto the cube-edge path, and penalize the per-image log-depth residual. We provide this optional loss in the released code but have not evaluated it, and make no claim about its effect. 

Limitations. Gap to the reference. Our results are below Vision Banana on every benchmark; we do not reproduce its numbers, and its base model, in-house data, and human evaluation are not public. One backbone. The framework supports other generators through configuration, but all findings are on Qwen-Image-2.1; we do not claim they transfer. Selection on evaluation sets. Checkpoints and merge recipes were inspected on the evaluation benchmarks, so the best reported scores are optimistic by construction: the P5 and P6 weights were fixed after seeing Table 5. Our claims are about the sign of the effect across all six merges, not about any single recipe. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We built Open Vision Banana, an open framework in which every perception task is a checked contract between an RGB encoder, an instruction, and a decoder, and one adapter on a frozen editing backbone serves all tasks. The open recipe does not match the closed system, but it fails in ways that are now measurable: depth geometry is nearly right while its absolute scale is uncommitted, segmentation competes with depth for

a shared adapter, weight merging moves along this trade-off without escaping it, and the training loss cannot tell good checkpoints from bad ones. These failure modes, more than any single benchmark number, mark what an open generator must overcome to become a generalist vision learner; the released framework and evaluation outputs make each of them testable.

# A Evaluation protocol 

Inference. The source image is resized so that its area matches a 512 × 512 canvas (side a multiple of 32), the target is generated from noise with 50 flow-matching steps and true classifier-free guidance 4.0 against an empty negative prompt, with seed 42. Every raw pipeline output is asserted to be 512 × 512, and for referring tasks every prompt is asserted to match the instruction template of Tab. 1. The generated image is decoded by the task decoder and the decoded label or depth map is resized to the native ground-truth resolution (nearest neighbor for labels, so that interpolated colors never create phantom classes; bilinear for depth). 

Metric depth. The decoder projects each pixel onto the cube-edge path and inverts the power transform (Eq. (2)). Valid pixels are those with finite positive ground truth inside the dataset mask, and with a finite positive prediction after clipping. NYUv2 uses the Eigen crop and a 10 m cap, ETH3D an 80 m cap, and DIODE-Indoor no cap. AbsRel, RMSE, and δ1 = Pr[max( ˆd/d, d/ ˆd) < 1.25] are accumulated over all valid pixels of the dataset (pixel-weighted), not averaged per image. The aligned diagnostic rescales each predicted map by med( d)/ med( ˆd) over its valid pixels before scoring; it uses the ground truth and is never reported as a headline number. Because the prediction-validity filter is applied after decoding, a sample that decodes to extreme depths can dominate DIODE AbsRel, which is why we treat DIODE AbsRel as a stability indicator. 

Segmentation. Cityscapes predictions are decoded by nearest palette color, label IDs are mapped to the 19 train IDs, and mIoU is computed from one confusion matrix over the 500 validation images using official 

gtFine annotations. For referring segmentation, the prediction is thresholded to a binary mask; gIoU is the mean IoU over queries, and cIoU is the summed intersection over the summed union. RefCOCOg uses the UMD split ( refs(umd).p , 4,896 expressions) with native COCO masks. ReasonSeg uses all 344 queries of the validation set with the original query text and the legacy_union mask rasterization; we also log the official LISA rasterization ( lisa_v1 ), which is 1–2 points lower.

# C Instance decoder 

The instance decoder turns a one-color-per-instance RGB prediction on a black background into binary masks in five stages. The parameters are fixed for every result in this paper. 1. Background initialization. Pixels within Euclidean RGB distance τ = 14 (8-bit scale) of the background color are background. 2. Color grouping. Foreground pixels are grouped by 16-connected, seed-based region growing: apixel joins a component when its color lies within τ of the component’s seed color. The grouping is implemented with a union–find. 3. Noise pruning. Components smaller than θsize = 2 × 10 −4 of the image area are removed. 4. Boundary-artifact removal. A component is removed when a 3 ×3 erosion keeps less than θerosion = 0 .1of its pixels, which removes thin halos along instance boundaries. 5. Spatially constrained merging. Two components are merged when their mean colors lie within τ

and the bounding box of their union is at most γ = 5 times the sum of their bounding-box areas; merges are applied transitively. The remaining components are the predicted instances; the released code implements the decoder in the 

SegmentationDecoder class.
