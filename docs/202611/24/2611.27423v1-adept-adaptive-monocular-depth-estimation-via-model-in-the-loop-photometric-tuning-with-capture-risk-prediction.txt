# ADEPT: Adaptive Monocular Depth Estimation via Model-In-The-Loop Photometric Tuning with Capture Risk Prediction 

GiHyun Kim 1, Jin Mo Yang 2, and Hyung-Sin Kim 2

Abstract — Exposure and gain determine the visual evidence available to monocular depth estimation (MDE), particularly when robot motion creates a tradeoff between light collection and motion blur. We present ADEPT , a framework that adapts these settings using learned feedback from a frozen depth model. During training, ground-truth depth identifies the lowest-error reference within a group of physically eval-uated camera settings. A compact predictor learns reference-relative disagreement (RRD) between each candidate’s depth prediction and the reference prediction using regression and ranking losses. At deployment, it scores observed captures using frozen-model features and camera, motion, and illumination context, without ground truth or reference images. A contextual Gaussian-process controller uses recent scores to select subse-quent exposure and gain settings under motion and photometric constraints. We evaluate offline camera selection with relative and metric Depth Anything v2 and demonstrate online control with the affine-invariant variant on a mobile robot. Across nine equally weighted scene–illumination conditions, ADEPT 

reduces mean scale-and-shift-aligned Abs rel by 14.6% relative to built-in auto-exposure and by 10.0% relative to the best-performing evaluated prior controller. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Monocular depth estimation (MDE) provides dense ge-ometric cues from a single RGB image, making it attrac-tive for mobile robots constrained by sensor cost, size, or payload [1]. Recent depth foundation models further expand the potential of RGB cameras as a source of geometry through improved generalization across diverse scenes [2]– [4]. Deploying these models reliably, however, depends on 

how RGB images are acquired as well as on the model that interprets them. Camera settings determine the visual evidence available 

before inference begins. Saturation can erase intensity differ-ences, while motion blur and low signal-to-noise ratios can obscure boundaries and texture [5]–[8]. Learned priors can compensate for degraded observations, but faithful recovery of details lost during capture is not assured [9]. These effects are particularly important for moving robots: a long exposure that reveals a dark scene at rest can blur it during a turn, whereas shortening exposure limits the collected light. These tradeoffs motivate adapting acquisition to preserve evidence useful to the depth model as motion and illumination change. The central challenge is obtaining feedback that distin-guishes camera settings by their usefulness to the deployed depth model, without ground-truth depth online. Classical

Depth uncertainty estimators provide relevant signals [14]–[20], but their usefulness for ranking physical camera settings must also be established. On a mov-ing robot, these rankings must be updated from successive observations as viewpoint and illumination change. We introduce ADEPT , a framework that adapts exposure and gain using reference-relative disagreement (RRD) as learned feedback for a frozen MDE model. During train-ing, ground-truth depth identifies the lowest-error reference within a group of captures at approximately matched view-points and different camera settings. A compact predictor learns disagreement between each candidate’s depth predic-tion and the reference prediction (RRD), with an auxiliary ranking loss. RRD serves as a surrogate for relative cap-ture quality, whose usefulness is evaluated through camera-selection experiments. At deployment, the predictor scores observed captures using frozen MDE features and camera, motion, and illumination context, without ground truth or reference images. A contextual Gaussian-process (GP) con-troller uses recent scores to select the next exposure and gain under motion-dependent and photometric constraints. We evaluate the resulting feedback across training and validation scene configurations and in online mobile-robot experiments. Our contributions are threefold:

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Camera Control for Robotic Perception 

Camera-control methods adapt image acquisition to im-prove visual perception. Classical approaches optimize hand-crafted image-quality metrics based on gradients, entropy, brightness, or noise statistics [5]–[8]. Shin et al. [5] combine these measures with Nelder–Mead optimization, while Kim et al. [6] use Bayesian optimization of an entropy-weighted gradient metric. Learned approaches incorporate richer su-pervision: Tomasi et al. [12] train exposure and gain control using feature matches from a VO or SLAM pipeline, Lee et al. [10] learn exposure policies with rewards for image attributes and flicker, and Zhang et al. [11] use image history and rewards that include visual-odometry performance. LENS [13] uses confidence from a frozen classification model to select among candidate captures in quasi-static environments, with candidate-subset strategies that balance accuracy and acquisition cost. In contrast, ADEPT learns 

RRD for MDE camera selection and uses sequential feed-back: its predictor scores the current capture, and a contextual GP uses recent scores to select subsequent exposure and gain settings as viewpoint, motion, and illumination change. 

B. Uncertainty and Risk Prediction for Monocular Depth Estimation 

MDE uncertainty has been studied through probabilistic prediction, learned estimation, and post-hoc analysis [14]– [23]. SLURP [24] uses a side learner to estimate regres-sion uncertainty from the main model’s outputs and inter-mediate representations. Gradient-based methods [17], [18] and test-time augmentation [19] offer training-free alterna-tives. ADEPT ’s contribution is to supervise an image-level predictor using disagreement with a ground-truth-selected reference capture at an approximately matched viewpoint. Its output serves as a camera-selection cost. We evaluate this acquisition-oriented use through groupwise selection regret and online depth accuracy, with a supervision ablation examining the choice of training target. 

C. Monocular Depth for Robotics 

FastDepth [1] studies embedded depth inference, while D3VO [23] integrates learned depth, pose, and uncertainty into visual odometry. Large-scale training improves relative-depth generalization [2], [25]–[27], and metric-depth models broaden the applicability of single-image geometry [3], [4], [28], [29]. ADEPT complements these model advances by 

adapting image acquisition (exposure and gain) for frozen depth models. III. E MPIRICAL MOTIVATION

V. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 1) Data Collection: We collected RGB-D sequences us-ing an AgileX Robotics LIMO Pro equipped with an Orbbec 336 camera and an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano. The robot repeatedly traversed a fixed trajectory in both directions at a maximum linear speed of 1 .0 m /s and angular speed 1.0 rad /s. We evaluated the 16 exposure–gain configurations in the discrete grid C = ( {4, 8, 16 , 32 } ms )×{ 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 }.The dataset contains approximately 51K frames, partitioned at the scene-configuration level into three training and three validation configurations, with all runs and traversal laps from each configuration assigned exclusively to one split. Each training configuration shares the structural layout of its validation counterpart but differs in object identity and arrangement. Validation Scene 1 uses an enclosed layout formed by temporary walls, Validation Scene 2 removes these walls, and Validation Scene 3 retains the open layout with additional textured wall coverings. Illumination was categorized as bright ( ≈ 70 lux), normal ( ≈ 10 lux), or dark (< 5 lux). Based on linear speed |v|, translational motion was categorized as stop ( < 0.05 m /s), slow (0 .05–0 .25 m /s), or fast (0 .25–1 .0 m /s). When translation was negligible, rotational motion was categorized as rotate or spin using an angular speed threshold of 0 .5 rad /s. Motion states are derived from the wheel encoders of the LIMO Pro, whereas illumination states are manually assigned. Within each scene, observations depicting approximately the same viewpoint under different camera configurations were registered using ground-truth depth, requiring at least 95% spatial overlap, an ECC score above 0.9, and maximum 32px translation error; failed registrations were discarded. The configuration with the lowest measured Abs rel was treated as the best evaluated reference, and the remaining configurations were treated as candidates. 

2) Implementation Details: We use Depth Anything v2 Small at 640 × 480 and extract encoder features for a risk predictor containing 165,910 parameters. The predictor is trained using AdamW with learning rate 5 × 10 −4 and batch size B = 4. The controller uses κ = 1.645, βGP = 0.5,

B. Offline Selection Performance via Risk Prediction 

We compare our learned risk predictor with two training-free uncertainty baselines for monocular depth estimation: test-time augmentation (TTA) [19] and gradient-based uncer-tainty [17]. As reported in Table II, our method consistently achieves the highest correlations, lowest selection regret, and highest selection accuracy for both the affine-invariant and metric variants of Depth Anything v2 [2]. Across all back-bone and scene combinations, our method reduces absolute selection regret by 59 .6%–92 .4% relative to the uncertainty baselines. This advantage remains substantial in validation scenes. For example, with Metric-DAv2, our method achieves a Pearson correlation of 0 .951, compared with 0 .313 for TTA and −0.141 for gradient-based uncertainty. These results indicate that the learned risk provides a more reliable ranking of camera configurations under the evaluated scene shifts than the training-free baselines.

CONCLUSION This paper presented a model-in-the-loop framework for adaptive exposure and gain control for monocular depth esti-mation. We defined excess ground-truth Abs rel relative to the best evaluated camera setting as the desired acquisition ob-jective, while training the predictor using reference-relative disagreement as a practical surrogate. A descriptive analysis of the collected camera sweep shows that camera settings and their interactions with scene type account for most of the observed variation. In validation scenes, the proposed surro-gate reduces selection regret relative to direct ground-truth error supervision, supporting its empirical transfer without assuming mathematical equivalence to the desired objective. Integrated with a contextual GP controller and admissibility constraints, the proposed feedback improves mean online MDE performance over auto-exposure and the evaluated prior controllers.
