Many questions about videos ask for a number, such as how tall a person
is, how far a cart travels, or how fast a ball leaves a ramp. We study this
task, quantitative physical reasoning from video, where the inputs are a
video, a question, and a prior of known facts such as object sizes, speeds,
and distances from the camera to named objects. End-to-end video-language
models lack an explicit metric scale and physical consistency, and their er-
rors cannot be inspected. We present SceneRuler, which instead recovers a
measurable physical scene and answers with that scene as evidence. Its cen-
tral principle, Minimal Sufficient Reconstruction, builds, for each question,
only the smallest scene that suffices to measure the asked quantity, and tests
sufficiency on measurements rather than assuming it. The scene is written
as a program whose structure a vision-language model writes and whose
magnitudes are left to measurement. Physics-guided Fitting measures them
through physical motion laws, so that gravity itself can anchor the metric
scale, and marks every parameter, and the read-out of the asked quantity,
as determined or undetermined. Each question receives a read-out and an
answer. The read-out is the asked quantity evaluated on the scene, and it is
empty when the scene does not fix that quantity. The answer is always given
by a model that takes the scene as evidence. We evaluate on QuantiPhy and
also apply the method to 27 in-the-wild videos that we collected, which have

Introduction
How tall is the person on the left? How fast is the ball when it
leaves the ramp? How far does the cart travel in two seconds?
Questions like these ask for a number in meters, meters per second,
or meters per second squared, and the only evidence is an ordinary
video together with a few facts the asker already knows. These
facts are the size of one object, the speed of another, or the distance
from the camera to an object at a given time. We study this setting,
quantitative physical reasoning from video, and present SceneRuler,
which treats it as a reconstruction problem. It recovers a measurable
physical scene from the video and reads the asked quantity off that
scene. The input is always the same three things, a video, a question,

and a prior. The prior collects every known fact that comes with the
question. These facts are the size, speed, or acceleration of a named
object, and, where given, the distance from the camera to a named
object.
The direct alternative is to ask a large video-language model for
the number, for example Gemini-4-Carbon [Google DeepMind 2026]
or Qwen3.5 [Qwen Team 2026]. Such models describe what happens
in a video well, but a metric answer needs two things they do not
represent explicitly. The first is a metric scale that ties pixels to
meters. The second is consistency with physics, so that positions,
velocities, and accelerations over time agree with one another and
with gravity [Chow et al. 2025; Puyin et al. 2026].
When an answer is produced end to end, there is also nothing to
inspect. One cannot tell whether an error comes from the wrong
object, the wrong scale, or the wrong motion. A reconstructed scene,
by contrast, makes every intermediate quantity explicit and check-
able against the video.
Recent monocular depth estimation [Bochkovskii et al. 2025] and
dynamic scene reconstruction [Li et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025]
recover dense geometry and camera motion from casual video. Full
metric 3D reconstruction of an arbitrary video is, however, more
than a question requires, and the images of a single camera do not fix
its metric scale. Our central idea is Minimal Sufficient Reconstruction.
For each question, it reconstructs the smallest scene that suffices
to measure the asked quantity, and nothing more. That scene holds
the objects the question and the prior refer to, what they rest on or
hang from, one camera, and their motion, with every object a shape
as coarse as the question allows. Appearance, the background, and
objects that play no role in the answer are left out. Sufficiency is
tested on measurements rather than assumed. A scene is sufficient
when the observations and the prior determine the asked quantity,
so that nothing it is read from is left open and its own uncertainty is
small, and the prior anchors the metric scale that the images leave
open. It stays minimal because an element is added only when the
question, the prior, or an element already present needs it, and a
parameter the data do not constrain is reported as undetermined
rather than filled in.
To make sufficiency testable, the scene must be explicit down to
every number. SceneRuler therefore writes it as a scene program, a
renderable description of the objects, their supports, their motion,
and the camera, with every prior fact bound to the quantity it states.
The program divides the labor between models and measurement. A
vision-language model (VLM) writes the structure. It decides which
objects exist, what supports what, and which physical law governs
each stretch of motion. Metric values enter only from the prior,
a convention such as gravity on Earth, a commonsense size held
softly, or the fit. Models decide what the scene is. Measurements
decide how big and how fast it is.
Physics-guided Fitting then measures the magnitudes against the
object masks of all frames at once. Motion is fitted through physical
laws rather than frame by frame, so a few physical parameters absorb
many noisy observations, and these parameters are the quantities
the questions ask about. Because free flight and swinging depend
on gravity, gravity becomes an anchor of the metric scale beside
the prior. A fit returns a value for every parameter, so it also states
which values it can vouch for. Every parameter, the metric scale,
and the asked quantity are marked determined or undetermined.
A structure written by a model can be wrong, for example by a
missed stop, a wrong support, or a wrong depth relation. The fit
exposes such errors as numbers, residuals that drift, stated facts that
do not hold, and a read-out that is empty. These numbers stay with
the scene and reach the answer as evidence.
Each question receives a read-out and an answer. The read-out
is the asked quantity evaluated on the fitted program. It is empty
when the scene does not determine that quantity. The final answer
is always given. A VLM sees the question, the prior, frames of the
video, the rendered program, and the reconstruction as evidence,
without being told whether the scene determines the asked quantity,
and returns one number. The read-out thus states, separately from
the answer, whether the reconstruction measures what was asked.
Each question is a self-contained unit. Its scene is built only from the
video, that question, and its prior, without borrowing information
from other questions on the same video.
We evaluate on QuantiPhy [Puyin et al. 2026], a benchmark
of quantitative physical questions over videos. We also apply the
method to 27 in-the-wild videos that we collected, with 54 questions
and no ground-truth answers (Section 4.4 and Appendix B.1). In
summary, we contribute:
• Minimal Sufficient Reconstruction, a principle for quantita-
tive video question answering. It reconstructs, per question,
the smallest scene that measures the asked quantity, with
sufficiency tested on measurements (Section 3.1);
• the scene program, an explicit scene whose structure a VLM
writes and whose metric magnitudes enter only from the
prior, conventions, class sizes, or the fit (Section 3.2);
• Physics-guided Fitting, which fits motion through physical
laws, with gravity as an anchor of the metric scale, and
marks every parameter and the read-out as determined or
undetermined (Section 3.3);
• a read-out and an answer per question. The read-out is empty
when the scene does not determine the asked quantity, and
the answer is always given and reads the reconstruction as
evidence (Section 3.4).

Related Work
2.1
Quantitative Physical Reasoning
Vision-language models (VLMs) already carry a good deal of
commonsense physical knowledge. From a single image they
can estimate how large, how tall, or how heavy an object is, and
models trained on metric spatial question answering, such as
SpatialVLM [Chen et al. 2024] and SpatialRGPT [Cheng et al. 2024],
estimate distances and sizes in real units. Video adds quantities that
no single frame contains, such as velocity and acceleration, and
with them the question of whether an estimate is consistent with
physical law over time. A line of benchmarks measures how well
models do this. CLEVRER [Yi et al. 2020] and Physion [Bear et al.
2021] test qualitative predictions such as collision outcomes and
stability. Physion++ [Tung et al. 2023] and PhysBench [Chow et al.
2025] extend the evaluation to latent physical properties such as
mass, friction, and elasticity. QuantiPhy [Puyin et al. 2026] asks

2.2
Video-based 3D/4D Scene Reconstruction
Monocular depth estimation has progressed from high-quality rela-
tive depth, as in DPT [Ranftl et al. 2021] and Depth Anything [Yang
et al. 2024], to metric depth, as in ZoeDepth [Bhat et al. 2023] and
Depth Pro [Bochkovskii et al. 2025], which also estimates the focal
length. DUSt3R [Wang et al. 2024] regresses 3D point maps and
camera poses from uncalibrated images, and MonST3R [Zhang et al.
2025] and MegaSaM [Li et al. 2025] extend such reconstruction to
dynamic videos, recovering per-frame geometry and camera tra-
jectories. Dense point trackers such as CoTracker [Karaev et al.
2024] supply long-range 2D correspondences. Closest to our set-
ting, PhysMind [Yang et al. 2026] answers quantitative questions by
reconstructing posed metric meshes of the question objects, com-
bining video segmentation, metric depth and intrinsics estimation
(MoGe-2 [Wang et al. 2025]), image-to-3D object reconstruction
(SAM 3D Objects [Chen et al. 2026a]), and 6D pose tracking (Foun-
dationPose [Wen et al. 2024]), and measures the asked quantity on
the meshes. Full 4D reconstruction of a dynamic monocular video
is costly and often ill-posed, and errors in parts of the scene that
the question never asks about still propagate into the answer. Ours
follows the principle of Minimal Sufficient Reconstruction. It recon-
structs only the objects, supports, camera, metric scale, and motions
that the asked quantity depends on, each object as a coarse con-
structive shape rather than a detailed mesh, and leaves the rest of
the scene unmodeled.
2.3
Agentic Workflows for Modeling
Tool-using VLM agents decompose visual reasoning into calls to
specialized modules. For example, the programs that VisProg [Gupta
and Kembhavi 2023] and ViperGPT [Surís et al. 2023] generate in-
voke detection, segmentation, and depth models and compose their
outputs. Closer to our task, Code as Worlds [Wang et al. 2026] has
an agent discover executable world representations for physical
reasoning, and PhysMind [Yang et al. 2026] turns a video into an
executable world for training-free physical reasoning. A complemen-
tary line builds explicit models of physical dynamics. The Neural
Physics Engine [Chang et al. 2017] learns compositional object-
based dynamics, PhyDNet [Le Guen and Thome 2020] disentangles
PDE-constrained physical dynamics from residual factors for video
prediction, and system identification fits parametric motion models,
such as projectile flight, rolling, or a pendulum, to observed trajec-
tories [Jaques et al. 2020; Kandukuri et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2015]. Our
approach combines the two with a strict division of labor. A VLM
writes only the structure of a scene program, while every metric
magnitude comes from the prior, a convention, a commonsense size
held softly, or the fit, and each object’s motion is parameterized by
the physical law that governs it instead of being fitted freely frame
by frame.

3.1
Minimal Sufficient Reconstruction
For each question we reconstruct the smallest scene that suffices
to measure the asked quantity. The scene contains the objects that
the question and the prior refer to, the supports and holders they
rest on or hang from, one camera, and one motion law per time
segment of each object. Every object is a coarse shape, as coarse
as the question allows. A ball is a sphere and a car a box or a few
boxes. Appearance, the background, and objects that play no role
in the answer are left out.

Both properties are tested on measurements rather than assumed,
and this is what shapes the rest of the method. Sufficiency is a
property of the fitted scene. Every parameter is either determined
by the observations and the prior or marked undetermined with
a reason, and the asked quantity counts as measured only when
nothing it is read from is left open and its own uncertainty is small
(Section 3.3). Minimality is a property of how the scene is built. An
element enters only when the question, the prior, or an element
already present needs it, and the fit reports a parameter the data do
not constrain as undetermined rather than filling it in. A scene that
is too small shows up as residuals and undetermined parameters.
A scene that is too large shows up as parameters the data do not
constrain.
3.2
The Scene as a Program
To make sufficiency testable, the scene must be explicit down to
every number. We therefore write it as a scene program. It consists of
named parameters, objects, and one camera, which can be rendered
on every frame and compared with the video. Figure 3 draws one
program as a tree, the form of the scene graphs of earlier work [Ar-
meni et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2015; Rosinol et al. 2020]. The support
plane is the root, and each body hangs from what it rests on or is
held by. Each body carries its shape and size, its segments with their
laws, and the marks of what the fit determines.
Parameters and their sources. Each parameter has a value, a unit,
an uncertainty 𝜎, and a source. The source is prior (stated in the
prior), convention (a standard value taken as given, such as 𝑔on
Earth), class (a commonsense size, held only softly), fit (constrained
by the observations), or vlm (a model’s estimate, used only as an
initial value). The source rule is the method’s central division of
labor. A metric value enters the program only from the prior, a
convention, a class size, or the fit. Models decide what the scene is.
Measurements decide how big and how fast it is.
Objects and motion laws. An object is a plane or a body. A body’s
shape is a coarse constructive solid built from a few primitives, with
its metric extents as scene parameters, so a size changes without re-
authoring the shape. An object’s presence over time is a sequence of
segments, each with a relation to an earlier object, such as resting on

Observations. The program is compared with two fixed inputs.
The first is one binary mask per tracked object and frame, from
an open-vocabulary detector [Liu et al. 2024] and a video seg-
menter [Ravi et al. 2025] (Appendix A.1). A candidate track is kept
only if it passes checks of identity, presence, size, and the image
border, and otherwise the next candidate is tried. Each object’s mask
noise 𝜎𝑜, the boundary jitter between consecutive masks, is the unit
in which every pixel threshold of the method is stated. The second
input is the prior, parsed into numbered statements whose values
the program never changes.
The model writes the structure. A VLM writes the categorical part
of the program from labelled frames, the question, and the state-
ments. It writes which objects exist and which track each is, their
shapes and proportions, their motion as segments with relations and
laws, and which parameter or derived quantity each prior statement
and the question refer to. Where masks alone leave the scale open, it
may add further evidence. This evidence can be bodies at the same
depth, commonsense sizes as soft votes, and image measurements
that tie a statement about an untracked feature, such as a lane’s
width, to marked points on the plane it lies on. A second call re-
views the plan. It may add objects or sharpen descriptions but not
remove them. All model output is checked in code before it becomes
structure, and every prior statement must be accounted for, so none
is dropped silently (Appendix A.2). Each authored shape is also
checked against the masks before any fit. Its silhouette is aligned
to the observed mask over a sweep of viewing directions, and the
boundary distance that remains is measured. The VLM judges each
body from the aligned outlines and these numbers. A body it judges
wrong is re-authored or given new proportions, and the change
is kept only when the boundary distance falls by more than the
mask noise. The number of rounds per body is capped. The check
never reads the question or the fit. The result is a structure whose
magnitudes are free except where the prior or a convention gives
them, and which the fit now measures.

the global scale, or a parameter without observation. Its value must
agree within its 𝜎over the equally good states of the fit. Its own
uncertainty from the observations must be at most a quarter of its
natural scale, the same bound as for parameters (Table 3). It must
cover its span with a law rather than a pose table. A parameter that
is undetermined only by its own 𝜎does not empty the read-out,
since the read-out’s 𝜎is judged on the read-out. Otherwise the
read-out is empty and records the reasons (Appendix A.5). This is
the sufficiency test of Section 3.1, made concrete. A question may
ask about a time at which the queried body is no longer observed.
The read-out then continues the body’s fitted motion law to that
time and marks the value as extrapolated. It is withheld when the
law stops holding before that time, as when a free body lands on a
support.
3.4
Answer
The fit measures magnitudes for a fixed structure, but the structure
itself can be wrong, for example by a missed stop, a wrong support,
or a wrong depth group. Section 3.3 already exposes such errors
as numbers, residuals that drift, gates that fail, rows that are vio-
lated, and a read-out that is empty. The method does not revise the
structure after the fit. These numbers stay with the scene, and the
answer reads them as evidence.
Read-out and answer. Each question receives a read-out and an
answer. The read-out of Section 3.3 is empty when the scene does
not determine the asked quantity. The final answer comes from one
VLM call. It sees the question, the prior, frames sampled uniformly
over the video with their times, and renderings of the program
over the video. It also reads the reconstruction as evidence. This
covers each object’s sizes with the source that gave them, the laws,
𝑔, the camera, what holds the scale, each prior statement beside the
value the reconstruction has for it, and the reconstruction’s value
for the asked quantity in the asked unit. The call is told that the
reconstruction can be right or wrong, and it is not told whether the
scene determines the answer, nor any 𝜎. It judges from the images
whether the reconstruction is credible for the question and always
returns one number. The read-out thus states, independently of
the answer, whether the reconstructed scene measures the asked
quantity.
4
Experiments
The experiments ask four questions. Does a measured scene give
better numbers than asking a model directly (Section 4.3)? Do the
reconstructions agree with the video, also outside the benchmark
(Section 4.4)? Does the method know when it has measured the
answer (Section 4.5)? And what do the scene and the final call each
add to the answer (Section 4.6)?
4.1
Setup
Data. We evaluate on the validation split of QuantiPhy [Puyin
et al. 2026], a benchmark of quantitative physical questions over
simulated, lab-captured, and internet videos. Each question asks for
one number, such as an object’s size, speed, or acceleration, and
comes with a prior that fixes the metric scale. The questions fall into
four categories along two axes. The first is the kind of prior. Static
(S) priors state a size, and dynamic (D) priors state a speed or an
acceleration. The second is whether depth enters. In 2D questions
the prior gives no distance from the camera, and in 3D questions it
also gives the distances from the camera to several named objects.
We score the 158 questions whose ground truth the benchmark

answers all of them (Appendix B).
Metric. We use the benchmark’s Mean Relative Accuracy (MRA),
the fraction of a fixed set of relative-error thresholds that a predic-
tion meets, with a missing answer scoring zero (Appendix B). We
report the mean MRA in each category and, as the overall score,
the mean of the four category means, so that each category weighs
equally regardless of its size.
Protocol. SceneRuler is run once per question, with no repeated
sampling and no selection among runs. The reported answer is the
final answer of Section 3.4, which is always a number. How often
the read-out of the same run is determined is reported in Section 4.5.
No decision of the method reads a ground-truth answer.
Baselines. We re-run every baseline on the same 158 questions.
The first is Code as Worlds [Wang et al. 2026] (4B and 9B). The second
is PhysMind [Yang et al. 2026], which measures the asked quantity on
posed metric meshes. The third is Thinking with Spatial Code [Chen
et al. 2026b], in which a language model reasons over one 3D box
per object and the objects’ tracked positions. The last two answer
by direct inference. Gemini-4-Carbon [Google DeepMind 2026] and
Qwen3.5-27B [Qwen Team 2026] both receive the whole video with
the prior and the question. Their settings are in Appendix B.
4.2
Implementation Details
Every model call of SceneRuler goes to one VLM, GPT-6.1 [OpenAI
2026] (gpt-6.1-sol), through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint at
temperature 0 with a fixed seed. It writes the object inventory,
chooses the tracks, writes and reviews the plan, authors the shapes,
and gives the final answer. Detection, tracking, and monocular depth
use Grounding DINO [Liu et al. 2024], SAM 2 [Ravi et al. 2025], and
Depth Pro [Bochkovskii et al. 2025].
The plan is written from a grid of nine labelled frames, the fit
uses at most 36 frames per object, and the final call sees 16 frames
at a long side of at most 768 px. The fit minimizes its objective with
a trust-region least-squares solver. Table 3 lists every constant. All
runs use one machine with 128 CPU cores and three NVIDIA H100
NVL GPUs. Table 6 gives the time of each step, and Appendix B
gives the model calls per question.

5
Conclusion
SceneRuler answers quantitative questions about video by recon-
structing, for each question, the smallest scene that suffices to mea-
sure the asked quantity, and answering with that scene as evidence.
The scene is a program whose structure a VLM writes and whose
magnitudes only the prior, conventions, class sizes, and the fit may
set. Physics-guided Fitting measures those magnitudes through mo-
tion laws, so that a few physical parameters absorb many noisy
frames and gravity can anchor the scale beside the prior, and it
states which parameters, and whether the read-out of the asked
quantity, the evidence determines. The final answer reads that scene
as evidence beside the frames. What this buys is an answer that
can be inspected. Every number traces to the objects, scale, and
motion behind it, and the read-out says on its own whether the
reconstruction measured what was asked. The limitations of the
approach are discussed in Appendix D.
Three directions follow. The first is more data. The in-the-wild
videos show that the method runs outside the benchmark, but they
cannot score it, so labelled datasets beyond QuantiPhy are needed to
validate it. The second is moving cameras, which the scene program
could treat as one more body with its own motion law, anchored by
the static background. The third is finer shapes, refined only where
a coarse solid leaves the asked quantity undetermined, in keeping
with the principle of reconstructing no more than the question
needs.

Method Details
This appendix gives the details behind Section 3, namely the inputs,
the rules on the plan, the motion laws, the mechanics of the fit,
the identifiability tests, and the answer step. Table 3 collects the
constants, and Figure 8 follows the question of Figure 2 through

A.1
Observations and Prior
A text-only model call lists the distinct objects that the question and
the prior name, merging different spellings of one object, and may
add a visible reference object, such as one of well-known size or a
ball in free flight. Each object is detected with Grounding DINO, the
VLM picks the prompt box among the detector’s candidates, and
SAM 2 propagates it through the video. A candidate track is kept
only if it passes named checks of identity, presence, size, and the
image border. An object whose candidates all fail stays untracked.
On every tracked frame, SAM 2 segments the object inside its track
box, giving one binary mask per object and frame.
The mask noise 𝜎𝑜is the median boundary distance between
an object’s masks on consecutive frames after their centroids are
aligned, measured on frames where the mask is neither cut by the
image border nor shared with another track. Every pixel threshold
is stated in units of 𝜎𝑜, with a floor of
√
2 px for two rasterized
boundaries.
The prior is parsed into numbered statements, each with a quan-
tity, an object phrase, an SI value, and, where given, a time. A dis-
tance statement becomes a statement of the quantity distance to
camera, the depth of the object’s center along the optical axis. The
program never re-detects objects and never changes a statement’s
value.
A.2
The Plan
Contents. The VLM sees a grid of nine frames, each labelled with
its frame index and with the tracks’ boxes and names, together
with the question and the numbered prior statements. It returns
the objects in build order (supports first, then what rests on them,
then what is held or flies), each bound to a track or to none, with
a shape kind, proportions 𝐿:𝑊:𝐻as ratios, the view in which the
camera sees it, and its motion as a sequence of states, each with a
frame range, a relation and its target, a law, and a direction (left,
toward the camera, turning, and so on). The plan also binds every
prior statement and the question, names the environment, and says
whether the camera moves. A second call reviews the plan. It may
add an object or sharpen a description, but may not remove, rename,
rebind, or reorder objects.
Re-asks and repairs. The plan is checked in code against a fixed
set of rules. A plan that breaks a rule is returned to the model
once with the reason. If the reply still breaks it, the rule is applied
deterministically and the repair is recorded. Among the rules:
• A statement binds only to a tracked object.
• A statement about a class (“the tennis balls are 6.7 cm”) binds
to every tracked instance of the class.
• A size question binds to the axis the view shows, so the
“width” of a body seen side-on is its length 𝐿.
Statement accounting. Every prior statement has exactly one ac-
count. It is a binding to a parameter or derived quantity, an image
measurement, or an explicit declaration that nothing observable
carries it, with the reason. No statement is dropped silently.
Depth groups, class sizes, and image measurements. A depth group
names bodies at about the same distance from the camera, at a given
frame or, for bodies that do not move relative to one another, over
their whole common presence. Each group becomes same-depth
ties between its members, and code rejects a tie that contradicts the
prior’s distance statements. A class size is a commonsense size of a
tracked body with its spread, which becomes a soft vote. A vote on
the quantity the question asks for is dropped, and a prior statement
on the same quantity replaces it. An image measurement binds a

A.3
Objects, Motion Laws, and Camera
Relations. Each segment of an object’s presence has a relation
to an earlier object. The relation is free, on, in, held by, or hangs
from. The relation fixes the frame in which the segment’s law is
written. For on, the object’s height comes from the top face of its
support instead of being estimated. A body that moves by a law on
another body stays within the support’s footprint. Its contact point
lies inside the footprint at both ends of the segment. When the next
segment is a ballistic flight entered with a continuous velocity, the
contact point at the switch lies on the edge its path crosses, which
also measures the support’s extent. These terms are tested on the
scene fitted without them. Those that agree with the plan up to
three frames of boundary freedom are held in one more joint pass.
A term that fails contradicts the plan, so the support is not distorted
and the departure is recorded for the path check.

Order of the fit. The focal length starts from the monocular metric
depth model Depth Pro [Bochkovskii et al. 2025], the median of its
per-frame estimates on 12 evenly spaced frames of the video, and is a
free variable of the fit from there. The focal length is held at its start
while the objects are fitted one by one, until the joint pass below
frees it. The first tracked body is fitted together with the camera’s
pitch, roll, and height from each pitch start. A start is invalid if
it puts the camera below a plane on which a tracked object rests,
or if the prior’s statements cannot hold at that camera. Each valid
start is refined and scored on the whole scene. Every start within
one noise unit per frame of the best, and at least three, is carried
through the complete fit, and the lowest final objective is kept. The
other bodies follow in build order with the camera and the earlier
bodies frozen, each from discrete heading starts. A joint pass then
refines all of them and frees the focal length. The fitted value is kept
only if the silhouettes and the prior determine it (relative 𝜎≤0.25,
Table 3) and it does not reach the bound of the physical field of
view. Otherwise the focal length is reset to its start, the joint pass is
repeated with it fixed, and it is reported as undetermined. Keeping
the fitted value does not vouch for it. Whether the focal length is
reported as determined is decided, as for every parameter, by the
identification after the fit (Appendix A.5). A body’s size starts as
one scale times the model’s ratios. After the joint pass, each extent
that the observations determine on its own is freed, and the others
remain ratio times scale and are reported as undetermined. Segment
boundaries may move by up to three frames.
Gates. Each fitted object passes or fails four gates. (1) The program
evaluates. (2) Its held rows hold to their tolerances. (3) Its relations
hold. (4) Its pose agrees with the masks. For the pose gate, the 2D
similarity (translation, scale, rotation) that aligns the observed mask
with the rendered one on each frame is summarized per segment
as a constant part, a drift over the segment, and a scatter about
that drift, each in boundary pixels. A constant offset points to a

A.5
Identifiability and the Read-out
Uncertainty estimates. In Eq. (3), class-vote rows are multiplied by
𝑠so that a vote enters with its stated𝜎𝑐. A parameter’s𝜎is the largest
of four estimates. They are (1) the classical covariance of Eq. (3), (2) a
frame-clustered sandwich covariance, robust to correlated residuals
within a frame, (3) a profile along the weakest directions, with the
other parameters re-optimized, and (4) the spread of the parameter
over the equally good starts of the fit.
Undetermined parameters. A parameter is undetermined if 𝜎ex-
ceeds a quarter of its natural scale (its own magnitude for a size or
a focal length, the object’s distance for a position, and for a velocity
the larger of its magnitude and the object’s length over the segment
duration), if it loads a direction of zero curvature, if it has no effect
on any frame or row, or if it ends at a bound.
Scale test. Every length, speed, and acceleration that no metric
statement anchors is multiplied by 1.01 about the world origin,
while the anchors (the prior’s statements and fixed sizes, 𝑔, image
measurements, and class votes) are held. If neither the silhouette
objective nor any statement changes, the scale is undetermined.
Otherwise each anchor is tested alone.
The read-out’s 𝜎. The covariance is propagated through the quan-
tity’s gradient. The written precision of each statement it rests on
is added, and the result is enlarged by the misfit of the frames it is
read from and by pose residuals above the gate threshold, converted
to the asked quantity (a drift of 𝑑px over a segment of duration 𝑇
at depth 𝑍is a speed error of 𝑑𝑍/(𝑓𝑇)). An image measurement
asked for is read per period.
Empty read-outs. The read-out is empty, with a named reason, in
each of these cases:
• A parameter it reads, or a component the fit does not vary, is
open for a structural reason along which its value changes.
Such reasons are a direction of zero curvature, an unbounded
profile, the global scale, a held or bounded variable, a table
cell, or a parameter without observation.
• Its value differs, beyond its 𝜎, over the equally good states
of the fit.
• Its own uncertainty from the observations, without the
prior’s written precision, exceeds 0.25 of its natural scale. In
the reported run, 40 of the 111 empty read-outs are empty
for this reason alone, such as a speed of 4.0 m/s with 𝜎=
1.02 m/s, 0.254 of its scale.
• It is a speed or acceleration read where the motion is a pose
table.
• A held row is violated in the final fit, since all objects share
the camera through their silhouettes.
• The scale rests only on a class vote that the fit pulls beyond
3𝜎𝑐.
• The question is bound to a quantity the program has no
expression for.
Extrapolated read-outs. When the asked time lies outside the
frames in which a tracked body is observed, the read-out evalu-
ates the law of the segment that holds the time, else of the body’s
last or first segment, beyond the segment’s end or before its start.
A pose table is not a law and gives no value. The law is continued
only while it stays valid. A free body that comes down on a plane or
on a support’s top face has left its law, since no bounce is modelled,
and so has a body whose contact point leaves its finite support. The
read-out is then empty and records the time and the surface. The 𝜎
adds the read segment’s pose residual as a velocity error times the
continued duration, and the determinacy rule is unchanged.
A.6
Answer Step
The final-answer call is one request. It receives 16 frames sampled
uniformly over the video, resized to a long side of at most 768 px,
each preceded by its frame index and time. It also receives up to five
overlays of the rendered program on the video, each labelled with
every object’s IoU against its mask. The text gives the question, the
asked unit, the prior, and the reconstruction. The reconstruction
lists each object with its shape, its sizes, and, for every segment, its
law, relation, and fitted values, each with the source that gave it (the
prior, the fit, a convention, or the model’s guess). It also lists 𝑔, the
camera, what holds the metric scale, each prior statement beside the
value the reconstruction has for it, and the reconstruction’s value
for the question. The prompt says that the reconstruction can be
right or wrong. It carries no determined flag, no 𝜎, and no verdict of
the read-out. The call judges whether the reconstruction is credible
for the question and returns one number in the asked unit with a
rationale and the inputs it relied on. Each prior statement is listed
with the reconstruction’s value converted to the statement’s own
unit and, for a counted feature, per period.

Experimental Details
Metric. For a prediction ˆ𝑦and ground truth𝑦, MRA is the fraction
of thresholds 𝜃∈{0.1, 0.2, . . . , 0.9, 0.95} for which the relative error
| ˆ𝑦−𝑦|/|𝑦| is below 1 −𝜃. A missing answer scores zero.
SceneRuler. Each question is run once, on its own, with the
constants of Table 3, on the setup of Section 4.2. Table 6 gives the
time of each step per question. The plan, the authoring, the shape
check, and the final answer make 7 model calls per question (median,
4 to 18). Perception adds the calls of the object list, the choice of
detection boxes, and the visibility labels of the presence check.
Shape check. Every reported program went through the shape
check of Section 3.2. It measured all 495 bodies of the 158 programs,
and the VLM judged 272 of them. It proposed 62 changes and kept
5, one body on each of 5 questions. The other 57 did not lower the
boundary distance by more than the mask noise and were reverted.
Scale anchors. Table 2 groups the questions by the scale test of
the fit. It reads the field held_by of the scale entry of the fit’s iden-
tifiability report, which is prior when only prior statements, prior
sizes, and image measurements of prior features fix the scale (127
questions, 7 of them through an image measurement), and prior and
convention when 𝑔taken as the environment’s convention fixes it
as well (31). The scale is determined on every question.
Code as Worlds. Both sizes are run with the authors’ released
evaluator on 16 frames, at temperature 0.01 with a fixed seed and
without thinking, on one NVIDIA L40S. Only the GPU memory
fraction is raised from the released setting, which is sized for 80 GB
GPUs.
PhysMind. We run PhysMind’s own stages, namely SAM 3 [Car-
ion et al. 2026] tracks from the VLM’s concept prompts naming the
question objects, MoGe-2 [Wang et al. 2025], Metric Video Depth
Anything [Chen et al. 2025], SAM 3D Objects [Chen et al. 2026a],
FoundationPose [Wen et al. 2024], and GeoCalib [Veicht et al. 2024].
The asked quantity is measured on the posed metric meshes without
the prior, as in PhysMind. Of the 158 questions, 90 are measured this
way. For the other 68 (no posed track of the question object in 46, no
detection in 15, no poses in 7) the answer is the VLM’s direct answer
on the whole video, as PhysMind’s own pipeline does. The VLM is
Gemini 3 Flash (preview) [Gemini Team, Google 2025]. Tracks that
SAM 3 returns without a tag are matched to the question objects
by the same VLM. The PhysMind panels of Figure 6 are its posed
meshes from this re-run of its released code, drawn onto the video
frame.
Thinking with Spatial Code. We run the released code (commit
cf7fffc) with the released Spatial Encoder weights on 32 frames
spread over the video. Gemini 3 Flash boxes each object on the ear-
liest of 8 shown frames in which it is visible, and the encoder turns
the boxes into the spatial code. The code holds one 3D box per object
and its per-frame box centres with timestamps, in metres in the first
camera frame. The reasoner, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 [Qwen Team
2025] at temperature 0 with at most 8192 output tokens, receives
the spatial code, the prior, the given distances, and the question.
Everything runs on one NVIDIA L40S. The Thinking with Spatial
Code panels of Figure 6 are its 3D boxes from this run, each pro-
jected with the encoder’s own camera of the sampled frame nearest
to the frame shown.
Direct inference. Gemini-4-Carbon [Google DeepMind 2026] and
Qwen3.5-27B [Qwen Team 2026] are queried without tools. Each
receives the video file itself, with no frame sampling on our side,
and one prompt with the prior, the given distances for 3D questions,
and the question, asking for the number alone. Both were run on
2026-11-13 through OpenRouter on the model owner’s own hosts.
Gemini-4-Carbon runs as google/gemini-4-carbon with its de-
fault reasoning setting. On 11 of the 158 questions it returned no an-
swer, and these score zero. Qwen3.5-27B runs as qwen/qwen3.5-27b
with thinking enabled. Its 138 questions on 20 videos ran on Al-
ibaba’s hosts with no fallback to another provider. These hosts
rejected the video input of the other four videos with an invalid-
parameter error. The 20 questions of those videos were run again
on 2026-11-14 through OpenRouter without pinning the provider.
On one question the model returned an empty answer, which scores
zero. On the 157 questions it answered, its MRA on D2 is 0.503 (36
questions). The other categories are as in Table 1.

D
Limitations
A static pinhole camera. The program has one static pinhole cam-
era with the principal point at the image center and no lens dis-
tortion. A video that pans, zooms, or follows a body breaks this
assumption, and the fit then explains the image motion the camera
causes as motion of the objects or as misfit.
Coarse solid shapes. Every body is a connected solid of a few
primitives with metric extents along its own axes. A body that
deforms or articulates is matched only on average, and a part that
the coarse shape does not separate cannot be measured.
The scale needs an anchor. Masks fix a scene only up to a common
scale. Without a prior statement, 𝑔in a free-flight or swinging
segment, or a class size, every metric read-out is undetermined.
A class size is only as good as a commonsense size, and a wrong
environment gives a wrong 𝑔.
The structure depends on the VLM. The objects, their shapes, sup-
ports, segments, and laws, and what each prior statement refers to
are written by the VLM. Code checks the plan against rules, and
the fit exposes errors that show in the measurements, but a wrong
structure that fits the masks as well as the right one is not detected.
Fitting time. The fit is an optimization per question, not a forward
pass. The method is therefore suited to offline measurement rather
than to interactive use.
Empty read-outs. The read-out has no value when the evidence
does not determine the asked quantity. This is by design, so the read-
out covers only part of the questions. The final answer is always
given.
