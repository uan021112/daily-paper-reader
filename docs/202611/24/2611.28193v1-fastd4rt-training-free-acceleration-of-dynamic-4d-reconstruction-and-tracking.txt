## Abstract 

Query-based 4D reconstruction models such as D4RT en-code a video once into a global scene representation and obtain outputs by decoding independent point queries. However, inference is often influenced by dense outputs and long videos. Although D4RT separates encoding from de-coding, a dense output still requires one decoder query for every pixel of every frame, and a long video is still encoded as many overlapping segments. In this work, we analyze the inference cost of D4RT and show that a large part of it is redundant. In particular, we find that the later decoder blocks attend to a small set of tokens shared by all queries. Based on these findings, we propose FastD4RT, a training-free framework that accelerates D4RT. By exploiting the smoothness of the outputs over time and across pixels, FastD4RT encodes and decodes only at sparse anchors and recovers the remaining outputs via interpolation. It decodes the remaining queries against a shared token set selected once per segment. We further characterize the errors intro-duced by both components. Experimental results show that FastD4RT accelerates D4RT by about 5 times on average across all its tasks with competitive accuracy, and further improves accuracy on long videos. Our code is available at 

https://github.com/Lycheemeng/fastd4rt .

## 1. Introduction 

Reconstructing the geometry and motion of a dynamic scene from a monocular video is critical for applications such as robotics [3, 50] and augmented reality [29]. Recent feed-forward models [44–46, 49, 55, 59] replace per-scene optimization [21, 25, 60] with a single network pass and have made this task far more practical. Typically, these models predict a dense output, such as a point map or a depth map, for every input frame through task-specific heads. However, such per-frame outputs do not directly de-scribe how a point moves over time, so 4D tasks such as 3D point tracking still rely on separate modules [32, 52, 53], and every frame has to be fully decoded even when only a few points are needed. To address this, query-based 4D reconstruction models such as D4RT [58] decouple en-coding from decoding. A transformer encoder maps the whole video to a global scene representation once, and a lightweight decoder then answers independent queries: given a 2D point in a source frame, it returns the 3D po-sition of that point at any target time and in any camera. In this way, different 4D outputs are obtained from a single model by choosing different queries, and only the queried outputs need to be decoded. However, this design has a key limitation: D4RT still en-codes every frame and decodes every output densely, even when most of this computation is redundant. A full-length 3D track needs one query per frame, and a dense depth map needs one query per pixel. Meanwhile, the encoder only processes clips of a fixed length, so a long video has to be encoded as many overlapping segments. As shown in Fig. 2, the encoder and the decoder each take a large share of the runtime in 3D tracking, while the decoder alone ac-counts for almost all of the runtime in dense depth estima-tion. This creates a severe bottleneck and limits the scala-bility of D4RT to long videos and dense outputs. To understand where this redundancy comes from, we present a detailed analysis of the inference of D4RT. Our analysis reveals that the redundancy lies at three levels. First, most decoder queries are redundant. A 3D trajectory usually changes smoothly over time, and a depth map is decoded from a scene representation with a much coarser grid of tokens, so most outputs can be recovered from their neighbors. In fact, D4RT already uses this property for camera parameters, which are estimated from a coarse grid of queries, but not for the other outputs. Second, the en-coded segments are redundant. Adjacent frames of a video are highly similar, yet a long video is encoded densely with overlapping segments. More importantly, the attention in-side each query is also redundant. We find that in the later decoder blocks, all queries attend to almost the same small set of tokens, although each query still attends to the whole scene representation. This suggests that the later blocks read a shared summary of the scene rather than searching for information specific to each query. Motivated by these observations, we propose FastD4RT, a training-free framework that accelerates D4RT. To re-duce the number of computations, we propose anchor-based sparse inference, which decodes each output only at sparse anchors and interpolates the rest. We further derive an up-per bound for the interpolation error. To reduce the cost of each query, we propose shared-token decoding, letting all queries in the later decoder blocks attend only to a small shared set of tokens. Furthermore, the same idea enables faster and more ac-curate inference on long videos. For long videos, FastD4RT encodes only sparse anchor frames, so the video is split into fewer segments. Since every segment adds one more align-ment to the chain, fewer segments also mean less accumu-lated error, making FastD4RT faster and more accurate on long videos. We evaluate FastD4RT on the tasks and benchmarks of D4RT to demonstrate its effectiveness on both sparse and dense outputs. Since the official model is not pub-licly available, all experiments are built on the community OpenD4RT weights. Our comprehensive experiments show that FastD4RT consistently accelerates D4RT while pre-serving its accuracy. For instance, FastD4RT is 6.1 times faster on dense prediction with comparable accuracy, and produces about 5 times more full-video 3D tracks at every target frame rate. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We provide a detailed analysis of the inference cost of D4RT, and identify where the computation is redundant. In particular, we find that most outputs change smoothly and can be recovered from sparse anchors, that the later decoder blocks attend to a small set of tokens shared by all queries, and that more segments lead to more accumu-lated error on long videos. • We introduce FastD4RT, a training-free framework that combines anchor-based sparse inference with shared-token decoding, whose speed-ups compose multiplica-tively. We further derive upper bounds for the errors in-troduced by both components. • We evaluate FastD4RT, across the tasks and benchmarks of D4RT. Results indicate that FastD4RT accelerates D4RT by 2.1–6.2 times with comparable accuracy, pro-duces about 5 times more 3D tracks at every target frame rate, and further improves accuracy on long videos. 

## 2. Preliminary and Challenges 

2.1. Query-Based 4D Reconstruction 

Query-based 4D reconstruction recovers the geometry and motion of a dynamic scene from a video V with an encoder and a decoder. Unlike feed-forward models that predict a dense output for every input frame [44, 45], it first encodes the video into a scene representation, and then obtains each output by querying this representation. In D4RT [58], the encoder E is a ViT-g with interleaved frame-wise and global attention, which maps a clip to a global scene representation

ttgt , expressed in the camera of frame tcam :

P = D(q, F ) ∈ R3. (3) The decoder also predicts the visibility and the 2D position of the point, which are used for tracking. It consists of eight blocks of cross-attention, so every query can be decoded independently. The core of D4RT lies in this query interface: different tasks differ only in the queries they issue. The 3D track is obtained by querying the same point at every target time, and a depth map is obtained by querying every pixel of a frame. Camera parameters are solved from the 3D points of a coarse grid of queries in each frame. As a result, tasks that previously required separate models or dedicated out-put heads are handled by a single encoder–decoder, which distinguishes D4RT from prior 4D reconstruction methods. 

Long-video inference. D4RT is trained on short clips. For longer videos, it splits the video into K overlapping seg-ments and encodes each segment once. Each segment is aligned to its predecessor by Sim(3) transform estimated on their overlap [42], and the transforms are chained to the first segment. This follows the first stage of VGGT-Long [11], without its loop detection and global optimization. D4RT does not specify how tracks are carried across segments; in our implementation, a query starts in the segment closest to its source frame and is handed over to the next segment through its decoded 2D position in the overlap.

## 3. Methodology 

In this section, we propose FastD4RT, motivated by a key limitation of D4RT inference: it encodes every frame and decodes every query densely. We show that most outputs can be recovered from a few sparse anchors, and that the later decoder blocks only need a small set of tokens shared by all queries. Building on these findings, we present anchor-based sparse inference to reduce the number of en-coded segments and decoded queries, and shared-token de-coding to reduce the cost of each query, together with their complexity. Finally, we describe the overall framework and derive its error bounds. 

3.1. Problem Reformulation 

D4RT aims to make 4D reconstruction efficient by encoding a video once and answering independent queries. However, we find that its inference computes far more than the outputs require. Our central claim is that this extra computation comes from redundancy: most outputs change smoothly and carry little new information beyond their neighbors, and the later decoder blocks attend to the same small set of to-kens for all queries. We reduce K and Nq in Eq. (4) with anchor-based sparse inference in Sec. 3.2, and reduce CD

with shared-token decoding in Sec. 3.3.

3.3. Shared-Token Decoding 

We introduce shared-token decoding, which reduces the cost of each remaining query while keeping the early de-coder blocks unchanged. 

Attention analysis. We first examine how the decoder uses the scene representation. For each decoder block, we select a small set of tokens with the largest mean attention over calibration queries, and measure how much of the attention of held-out queries falls on them. As shown in Fig. 3, in the first three blocks, this set captures only part of the at-tention, so each query attends to its own tokens. From the fourth block on, it captures almost all of the attention for ev-ery type of query. This suggests that the early blocks locate the information for each query, while the later blocks read a shared summary of the scene. Motivated by this observa-tion, we restrict the later blocks to a shared set of tokens.

3.4. Overall Framework 

Based on the above analysis of anchor-based sparse infer-ence and shared-token decoding, we propose FastD4RT, a training-free framework for accelerating query-based 4D re-construction, as illustrated in Fig. 4. FastD4RT follows the same segment-based pipeline as D4RT. On the encoder side, a long video is subsampled with stride s, so that fewer seg-ments are encoded. On the decoder side, calibration queries select a shared token set once per segment, and the queries of each task are decoded only at sparse anchors, with the last blocks attending only to the shared tokens. The re-maining outputs are then recovered by interpolation and chained across segments. FastD4RT applies this process to every segment, and detailed procedures are provided in Al-gorithm 1 and Appendix C. Since anchors reduce the num-ber of decoded queries and shared tokens reduce the cost of each one, the two components compose multiplicatively.

## 4. Experiments 

This section evaluates FastD4RT through extensive exper-iments on the tasks and benchmarks of D4RT. Following D4RT, we first evaluate 4D reconstruction and tracking in Sec. 4.2, and then 3D reconstruction in Sec. 4.3. We fur-ther examine how FastD4RT behaves as videos get longer in Sec. 4.4. All experiments are repeated three times, and we report the mean results. 

4.1. Experiment Setup 

Benchmarks. We benchmark FastD4RT against D4RT on all of its tasks, following the benchmarks and metrics of D4RT [58]. For 4D reconstruction and tracking, we evalu-ate on the three subsets of TAPVid-3D [22]: (i) DriveTrack, (ii) ADT and (iii) PStudio, using the official query points on full-length sequences. 4.3. 3D Reconstruction 

Point Cloud and Video Depth. We evaluate point cloud es-timation on Sintel and ScanNet, and video depth estimation on Sintel, ScanNet, KITTI and Bonn. As shown in Tab. 3, FastD4RT runs about six times faster than D4RT with al-most identical accuracy. Notably, this speed-up comes en-tirely from the decoder, which dominates the cost of dense prediction, so the reduction in queries and attention cost translates almost directly into speed. Qualitative results in Fig. 5 further demonstrate this. On both the static and the dynamic scene, FastD4RT recovers the same scene geom-etry and fine details as D4RT, while taking only about one sixth of its runtime. We also note that the community check-point is weaker than the official model on Sintel, which af-fects both methods equally. 

Camera Pose Estimation. We evaluate camera pose on Sintel, ScanNet and RealEstate10K. As shown in Tab. 4, FastD4RT consistently accelerates D4RT on all three datasets while maintaining comparable accuracy. On Sintel, it also lowers errors, since interpolation between anchors smooths frame-to-frame jitter. On ScanNet, the relative er-rors increase slightly, as the handheld camera rotates faster than the anchors can follow. On RealEstate10K, each video has only ten frames, so the speedup is smaller. These results demonstrate that FastD4RT extends to dense prediction and camera pose estimation, and that its speedup is largest where decoding dominates the cost.

## 5. Conclusion 

In this work, we proposed FastD4RT, a training-free frame-work that accelerates D4RT. By analyzing the inference, we found that it encodes every frame and decodes every query densely, even though most of this computation is redundant. In particular, most outputs change smoothly, and the later decoder blocks attend to a small set of tokens shared by all queries. To address this, FastD4RT encodes and decodes only at sparse anchors and recovers the rest by interpolation, and lets the remaining queries attend only to a shared set of tokens. Experiments on the benchmarks of D4RT show that FastD4RT achieves up to 6.2 times speedup while maintain-ing competitive accuracy, and further mitigates error accu-mulation on long videos. These results suggest that much of the cost of query-based 4D reconstruction can be removed at test time, bringing such models closer to real-time use. 8References

# Appendix 

## A. Related Work 

A.1. Feed-Forward 3D and 4D Reconstruction 

Classical pipelines recover geometry from images with Structure-from-Motion and Multi-View Stereo [37, 38]. They are accurate on static scenes, but they rely on per-scene optimization, which is slow and fragile on dynamic videos. DUSt3R [46] changed this by regressing point maps of an image pair with a single network, and MASt3R [23] further added dense matching. Later models extend this design to many views in one pass. Fast3R [55] processes over a thousand images at once, VGGT [44] alternates frame-wise and global attention to predict cameras, depth and point maps jointly, and π3 [49] removes the dependence on a fixed reference view. For dynamic scenes, MonST3R [59] fine-tunes DUSt3R on dynamic data, CUT3R [45] keeps a persistent state to process a video online, and Easi3R [7] reuses the attention of DUSt3R to separate motion without training. Other methods combine several models for depth, motion and camera [25, 28, 47]. However, these models predict a dense output for every input frame, so they do not directly give the correspondence of a dynamic point over time. D4RT [58] instead follows the query-based design of scene representation transformers [18, 35]. It encodes a video once and obtains every 4D output by querying the scene representation, which unifies depth, point tracks and camera parameters in one model. FastD4RT is built on this query-based design. 

A.2. 3D Point Tracking 

Point tracking estimates the motion of a query point through a video. Particle Video [36] first tracked points over long ranges, and learning-based trackers [12, 13, 17, 20] have made 2D tracking robust to occlusions. Dense trackers such as DOT [31] further track every pixel of a frame. A 2D track can be lifted to 3D with a depth map and camera intrinsics, as in the baselines of TAPVid-3D [22], but the result depends on the accuracy of each component. Recent methods predict 3D tracks directly. SpatialTracker [52, 53] tracks points in 3D space and later models camera motion explicitly, DELTA [32] tracks every pixel in 3D efficiently, and L4P [1] shares a video backbone across low-level 4D tasks. St4RTrack [14] extend the pairwise point maps of DUSt3R to reconstruction and tracking at the same time. D4RT obtains a 3D track by querying the same point at every target time, and proposes an algorithm that exploits spatio-temporal redundancy to track all pixels with fewer queries [58]. FastD4RT keeps this tracking interface unchanged. It decodes each track only at temporal anchors and recovers the remaining frames by interpolation. 

A.3. Efficient Inference for 3D Vision Models 

As feed-forward 3D models [45, 49, 55] scale to long sequences, their inference cost has become a key concern. Most existing work accelerates the global attention of VGGT-style models [44]. FastVGGT [40] observes that the global attention of VGGT collapses to similar patterns and merges redundant tokens. HTTM [48] merges tokens separately in each attention head and exploits temporal correspondence, and block-sparse attention [10, 43] computes only the blocks of the global attention map where the attention mass concentrates. StreamVGGT [64] turns the global attention into causal attention with a key–value cache, so video can be processed frame by frame. For videos longer than the model can take at once, VGGT-Long [11] splits the input into overlapping chunks and aligns them, which is also the protocol D4RT uses for long videos. These methods reduce the cost of building the scene representation in models that predict a dense output for every frame. In contrast, the cost of D4RT is split between encoding many segments and decoding a large number of independent queries, and the decoder alone accounts for almost all of the runtime in dense prediction. FastD4RT therefore targets both the number of encoded segments and decoded queries and the cost of each query. Since encoder acceleration methods [26, 34] change only the attention inside the encoder, they are orthogonal to FastD4RT and could be combined with it. 

A.4. Token Reduction and Sparse Attention 

Reducing the tokens that attention operates on is a common way to accelerate transformers [2, 8, 57]. In vision transformers, DynamicViT [34] and EViT [26] prune or fuse less informative tokens [30, 54, 56], and ToMe [4, 5] merges similar tokens without training. In large language models, the key–value cache is compressed by keeping only the tokens that receive most 1attention [15, 27, 41]. StreamingLLM [51] keeps a few initial tokens as attention sinks, and SnapKV [24] selects important tokens for each head from the attention of a small observation window. Shared-token decoding follows the same principle of keeping the tokens that receive most attention. Unlike these methods, it selects the token set once per segment from a small set of calibration queries, and shares this set across all queries and tasks of the decoder, since the later decoder blocks of D4RT attend to almost the same tokens for every query. Computing expensive outputs only at sparse locations is also a long-standing idea in video analysis [19, 39]. Deep Feature Flow [62], for example, runs the full network only on sparse key frames and propagates their features to the other frames [63]. D4RT already estimates camera parameters from a coarse grid of queries [58]. FastD4RT extends this idea to all outputs of D4RT and to the frames given to the encoder, and bounds the error introduced by interpolation.

B.2. Proof of Theorem 2 

Let a be the attention of a query over the N tokens in a head, as in Eq. (6), and let ϵ be its total weight outside the shared tokens. Let ¯vin and ¯vout be the attention-weighted means of the values inside and outside the shared tokens. The full attention output is 

o = (1 − ϵ) ¯ vin + ϵ ¯vout . (16) Restricting the attention to the shared tokens renormalizes the weights inside them, so ˜o = ¯ vin . Therefore, 

˜o − o = ϵ (¯ vin − ¯vout ). (17) Both means are convex combinations of the values, so their norms are at most V , which gives Esh ≤ 2ϵV . The error is zero when no attention falls outside the shared tokens, and small when the tokens outside carry values similar to those inside. Finally, linear and bilinear interpolation form convex combinations of the anchors, so the error of the interpolated outputs never exceeds the largest error at the anchors. Hence interpolation does not amplify the shared-token error. 

## C. Framework 

FastD4RT takes a video V of T frames and the frozen encoder E and decoder D of D4RT, and runs as in Algorithm 1. If the video is longer than the training clip length Tc and the output is not dense, FastD4RT keeps every s-th frame. The video is then split into K overlapping segments, and each segment Sk is encoded once into Fk, from which the keys and values of all decoder blocks are computed and cached. For each segment, FastD4RT decodes the calibration queries C with full attention, and keeps the m tokens with the largest mean attention as the shared set T hl of each head h in each block l > l 0 (Eq. (6)). It then decodes the anchor queries in parallel, at every r-th frame for tracks and camera poses and on a G × G grid for dense outputs. Each query attends to all tokens in the first l0 blocks and only to the shared tokens in the rest, and the outputs between anchors are recovered by interpolation (Eq. (5)). Each segment after the first is registered to its predecessor by a Sim(3) transform, estimated with Umeyama’s method [42] on the most confident points in their overlap, and tracks are handed over through their decoded 2D positions in the overlap. Finally, the transforms are chained to the first segment to give the full-video outputs Y.Overall, FastD4RT keeps D4RT itself unchanged, and only changes which frames are encoded, which queries are decoded, and which tokens the later blocks attend to.

D.2. Hyperparameters 

FastD4RT uses the same anchor-based sparse inference and shared-token decoding on all tasks. Tracks are decoded at every third frame ( r = 3 ). For videos longer than one clip, the encoder takes every second frame ( s = 2 ), and the video is split into segments with a stride of 40 encoded frames. Dense outputs are decoded on a 128 × 128 grid. Camera poses are solved at every third frame on a 16 × 16 grid, with each point weighted by the product of its predicted confidences in the two frames, and videos of at most 72 frames are sampled to one clip. Shared-token decoding uses full attention in the first l0 = 3 decoder blocks and keeps m = 512 tokens per head in the other blocks, selected with |C| = 768 calibration queries per segment.
