# RoboColiseum: Co-evolution of Simulation, Evaluation, and Models 

Chenghao Yin ∗, Da Huang ∗, Di Yang ∗, Jichao Wang ∗, Junhui Wu ∗, Nanshu Zhao ∗,Tong Ran ∗, Zhijun Li ∗, Gang Zhao, Linting Guo, Rui Feng, Sheng Zhang, Tianyu Jiang, Wenjun Sun, Yongxin Su, Zhaobo Liu, Zheyuan Xing, Sukai Wang, Yue Zhou, Qihan Jiang, Jianhua Jiang, Chuang Men, Yuxiang Yan, Daizhen Lv, Kunyu Yu, Yongjian Shen, Tianxiang Zhu, Siying Zhang, Wenhao Wang, Jun Zhao, Bowen Jiang, Zhi Zeng, Maoqing Yao †, and Qian Wang †

Abstract— Simulation-based evaluation of embodied models requires calibrated interactions, diverse tasks, and explicit execution constraints. We present RoboColiseum, a platform combining system identification, composable physics and rendering, and multidimensional benchmarking. Recorded-command replay characterizes robot dynamics and object interactions, while a shared interface connects rigid and deformable simulation to rendering. Across 22 paired real–simulation robot–task configurations, task scores have a reported Pearson correlation of 0.9686. Eight evaluation tracks span instruction following, spatial reasoning, general manipulation, robustness, dexterous manipulation, precision manipulation, whole-body coordination, and execution across robot embodiments. Budget-aware scoring combines task progress, resource use, and completion rewards. We report 12 selected Season I policies and all 11 systems on the public Season II leaderboard, comprising eight Agent systems and three VLA policies. Agent2Act connects language models to robot-control execution; the VLA study uses mixed-task supervised fine-tuning. The results reveal substantial variation across tracks within each evaluation category. RoboColiseum connects simulation development with capability-oriented evaluation and provides a foundation for extending robot interactions and model assessment.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models [1], [2] and world action models (WAMs) [3] motivate evaluation that connects instruction understanding with physical execution. Simula-tion provides repeatable task instances and access to inter-action diagnostics, but its usefulness depends on the relation between simulated and real behavior, the interactions it represents, and the information and computational resources available to the evaluated system. Three challenges motivate our design. First, physical plau-sibility of identified parameters does not by itself establish accurate task behavior. Physically consistent inertial identifi-cation [4] and object parameter estimation [5] provide tools for calibration, while paired real–simulation evaluation is needed to assess the resulting task scores. Second, rigid and

A common interface can make different backends accessible without assuming identical physical behavior or support for every backend combina-tion. Third, evaluation must distinguish reasoning, execution, and resource use. VLABench [8], EmbodiedBench [9], and EmbodiedEval [10] motivate broader interactive evaluation. Agent2Act [11] evaluates multimodal models by having them directly generate robot-control commands through shared ob-servation, execution, and feedback interfaces. The evaluated agent includes both the model and its execution harness. RoboColiseum focuses on three components. System iden-tification and recorded-command replay characterize robot response and object interaction under stated control and observation settings. A shared protocol separates physics arXiv:2611.27042v1 [cs.RO] 20 Nov 2026 from rendering and supports backend-specific rigid and de-formable representations. An eight-track benchmark retains instruction following, spatial reasoning, general manipula-tion, and robustness as Season I, and adds dexterous manipu-lation, whole-body coordination, precision manipulation, and cross-embodiment transfer as Season II. We assess RoboColiseum through paired Real2Sim exper-iments and public benchmark results. Season I retains four foundational tracks [15]; Season II introduces four additional capability dimensions. We report separate results for eight Agent systems and three VLA policies, preserving their re-spective training and execution settings. Agent2Act [11] con-nects language-model reasoning to robot-control execution. The evaluation protocol specifies hidden layouts, execution budgets, and submission review. These components support a development cycle in which task-level observations motivate changes to simulation, training data, and evaluation coverage. II. S YSTEM IDENTIFICATION AND REAL 2S IM

III. C OMPOSABLE PHYSICS AND RENDERING 

Robotic simulation combines interaction models with vi-sual observations whose requirements vary across tasks. RoboColiseum separates physical simulation, rendering, and application logic through a common interface. Physics and rendering components can therefore be composed according to the robot, material, and observation requirements, while retaining a shared control and state-exchange boundary for rigid and deformable manipulation. 

A. Decoupled Architecture and RoboColiseum Protocol 

The RoboColiseum Protocol defines the interfaces and data exchanged among control, physics, and rendering com-ponents (Fig. 6). It covers control commands, joint states, rigid-body poses, simulation time, and the geometry updates required to represent deformation. Physics owns the simu-lated state and publishes its evolution; rendering consumes that state to generate observations. Scene metadata associates physical entities with their visual representations, while simulation timestamps identify the physical states underlying the observations. Isaac Sim and OVRTX provide alternative rendering paths over this common boundary. Their camera and appearance settings can be configured independently of the selected physical model. Separate-process execution isolates physics and rendering dependencies and permits different update rates. For deformable objects, topology information and evolving vertex positions supplement rigid transforms, al-lowing shape changes to propagate to the visual scene. B. Pluggable Physics Engines 

Physics adapters implement a common contract for initial-ization, command application, stepping, state readback, and reset. Shared execution components coordinate these opera-tions without depending on a particular solver implementa-tion. Scene assembly prepares robot and scene assets together with a manifest that identifies the resources consumed by physics and rendering. Backend-specific conversion stages accommodate differences in asset representation while pre-serving the common application interface. The architecture integrates PhysX, Newton, and libuipc backends. PhysX provides an Isaac Sim physics path, New-ton supports standalone and Isaac-integrated execution, and libuipc supplies a dedicated rigid–deformable contact back-end. MuJoCo participates through solver integration and a cooperating CPU robot-dynamics worker. This distinction separates the engine adapter from the numerical solver or auxiliary dynamics component it uses. New engines can register an adapter, implement the execution contract, and provide the necessary assembly stages. Each backend retains its material models and solver settings, allowing extensions without imposing a single numerical formulation on all tasks. 

C. Rigid–Deformable Interaction 

The libuipc backend extends the engine to deformable manipulation through Incremental Potential Contact (IPC). Cloth and bag-like objects use triangular shell models for membrane deformation and bending. Paper additionally sup-ports plastic bending to retain creases after loading. Vol-umetric soft bodies use tetrahedral finite elements with a stable Neo-Hookean constitutive model, while rigid objects use affine-body dynamics. These representations place rigid and deformable task objects within the native libuipc contact system. Contact, friction, and self-contact connect object defor-mation to robot motion. Material properties, shell thickness, and collision geometry are specified by the scene, enabling different interaction models within the same execution frame-work. Geometry mappings transfer solved positions to the authored visual meshes so that rendering follows the phys-ical deformation. The supplied scene configurations cover cloth folding, paper folding, cloth-hanger manipulation, and drawstring-bag manipulation, illustrating how the same back-end represents different deformable interaction structures. D. Robot Dynamics and IPC Coordination 

Deformable manipulation combines articulated robot dy-namics with nonlinear contact and material deformation. RoboColiseum assigns these computations to cooperating components. A CPU MuJoCo worker advances robot dynam-ics and configured robot–static-environment contacts. Robot link poses and velocities then provide kinematic inputs to IPC, which advances task objects and deformable contact. The two components operate at different rates, with the IPC driver selecting the latest valid robot state for each native step. This decomposition is intended to reduce the robot-dynamics burden within the IPC solve and concentrate its iterations on deformable interaction. The libuipc backend owns native stepping and dynamic task state, while New-ton SDK utilities provide model construction, state arrays, and forward kinematics. Commanded forward kinematics or external joint measurements can also supply robot motion through the same input boundary. The current coupling is directional: robot motion drives IPC without feeding IPC contact forces back into the CPU robot model. Completed rigid and deformable states are subsequently exposed through the shared interfaces for rendering and data collection. IV. B ENCHMARK DESIGN AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

A. Season I: Foundational Tracks 

Season I organizes foundational interactive capabilities into Instruction Following , Spatial Reasoning , General Ma-nipulation , and Robustness . Tasks connect language and visual observations to physical actions while emphasizing target selection, spatial relationships, action sequences, or sensitivity to evaluation conditions. Published task-design principles combine motor primitives, cognitive requirements, and task complexity [15]. Longer interactions compose primitives and increase planning horizon or coordination demands. 

Instruction Following. The published instruction suite contains ten tasks. Object-selection tasks vary the language needed to identify a target among distractors. Instructions refer to color, shape, size, numerical identity, object cate-gory, common-sense needs, or logical alternatives. Examples include selecting a colored block or an object appropriate to a stated need. Retaining a pickup interaction while changing the requested attribute emphasizes language grounding and target disambiguation. 

Spatial Reasoning. Eight spatial tasks require interpreting absolute locations and relations between objects, then using those relations to select or construct a target arrangement. Examples include selecting an object by relative distance, placing an object to the left of another, ordering blocks by size, and stacking blocks. These interactions examine scene interpretation, relational target specification, and the geometric organization of successive actions. 

General Manipulation. Ten manipulation tasks extend pickup to coordinated sequences of grasping, transport, placement, and object-state changes. Examples include mov-ing a pot with both hands, clearing a desktop, pouring workpieces, scooping popcorn, and sorting packages. The tasks emphasize operation-specific grasp selection, object control during motion, and ordering dependent actions to complete an interaction. 

Robustness. Five perturbation categories retain the un-derlying goal while changing background appearance and illumination, camera pose or image quality, robot initial pose, or instruction phrasing. Configured image degradations in-clude noise, occlusion, and dropped frames. These variations probe the stability of target selection and execution across observation and initialization conditions. Organizing variants by perturbation type supports analysis of which changes are associated with performance sensitivity. Together, the four tracks relate instruction interpretation, geometric reasoning, action sequencing, and stability across conditions to observable task behavior. 

B. Season II: Task Design 

As model capabilities and research objectives change, additional tasks can expose limitations that established tracks no longer distinguish. Season II extends the foundational tracks with Dexterous Manipulation , Whole-Body Coordina-tion , Precision Manipulation , and Cross-Embodiment Trans-fer . These additions introduce functional finger interactions, coupled mobility and manipulation, fine alignment of small objects, and action adaptation across robot structures. Task design starts from the capability needed to complete an interaction. Within each added suite, tasks share a central capability requirement while varying the objects, geometry, and action sequence. Table I summarizes the four added suites. The following subsections describe the four Season II suites and their intended capability requirements. The benchmark accommodates VLA policies, WAM poli-cies, and model-driven agent systems. VLA models generate actions from visual observations and language instructions, optionally conditioned on robot state [1], [2]. WAMs such as DreamZero jointly model visual dynamics and actions [3]. Agent systems combine model-driven reasoning with ob-servations, planning, tool use, and execution feedback. In Agent2Act [11], models directly generate robot-control com-mands through shared observation, action-execution, and feedback interfaces. The model and its execution framework together form the evaluated system. Together, the eight dimensions connect task demands to track-level performance profiles. Their results support capability-specific selection of demonstration data and train-ing tasks. Comparisons across tracks describe performance patterns; isolating individual failure mechanisms requires controlled experiments.

A conventional parallel-jaw gripper cannot reproduce this finger-in-hole operating pattern. The task examines whether a model can infer how to use the tool, form an appropriate hand configuration, and drive its motion while maintaining the hold. Book retrieval instead begins with a constrained workspace: the robot tilts a book within a tightly arranged shelf before extracting it, linking contact selection to the sequence needed to create removal space. The remaining tasks broaden the coordination demands. Parcel transport requires both hands to control one object, while cup lidding and bowl stacking connect grasp config-uration to subsequent approach, alignment, and placement. Comparing these interactions helps identify relative weak-nesses in functional grasp planning, sustained manipulation, and bimanual coordination. These additions introduce deformable handling and articulated or dynamic object manipulation. 

2) Whole-Body Coordination: Whole-body tasks extend evaluation to interactions that require the mobile base, torso, and arms to work together. Base position and body posture determine the reachable workspace, while a held object con-strains subsequent movement. Tray delivery, trash collection, door interaction, and shelf placement are designed around this coupling over extended action sequences. Tray delivery connects pickup and transport to maintaining the load during motion. Trash collection links retrieval from different locations to eventual disposal, requiring the model to track progress and choose suitable approach positions. Door interaction couples base placement, articulated-object manipulation, and the order of passage and closing. Shelf placement requires body configuration to adapt to the desti-nation while connecting transport to final placement. These tasks examine the joint decisions of where to move, how to position the body for interaction, and how to connect the current operation to later actions. They help reveal whether local manipulation skills extend to complete mo-bile sequences, whether object control is maintained during transport, and whether approach, interaction, and departure form a coherent plan. The current hidden-set manifest instantiates eight whole-body task configurations: board wiping, conveyor pickup and shelf placement, door passage and closing, roller-shutter entry, trash collection, plate placement in a dish rack, tray-based tissue delivery, and carton transport and sorting. The examples above describe their coordination requirements; the manifest defines the evaluated coverage. 

3) Precision Manipulation: Precision tasks focus on inter-actions in which local geometry and small relative motions affect completion. The suite covers eight task categories: inserting a straw into a milk-tea cup, capping a pen, de-positing a coin, inserting a block, connecting an Ethernet cable, assembling a part on a shaft, packaging a phone, and connecting a phone charging cable. Apertures, ports, and mating parts require task understanding to translate into suit-able approach directions, alignment, and action transitions. The tasks cover three recurring demands. Straw insertion, coin deposition, and pen capping require perception of a small object relative to its target and selection of an ap-propriate approach. Connector mating and shaft assembly emphasize orientation and alignment between parts; phone charging additionally requires the two arms to handle the phone and connector separately. Phone packaging embeds precise placement within a sequence involving the phone, internal packaging components, and box lid. Varying mating geometry, manipulated objects, and action sequences examines fine-grained spatial perception, relative-pose control, bimanual alignment, and sequential assembly. Task comparisons help identify where execution becomes difficult: locating the target interface, converting an approach into fine alignment, or maintaining established part relation-ships through subsequent operations. 

4) Cross-Embodiment Transfer: The cross-embodiment suite places shared task intentions on ARX, AgileX, G1, and G2 Omnipicker configurations. Six shared tasks cover opening a trash bin and disposing of trash, scraping trash into a bin with a tool, hanging a cup, replacing a keycap, writing, and pouring water. They examine how models adapt articulated-object interaction, tool use, placement, fine manipulation, writing, and liquid transfer to different robot structures. Differences in kinematic structure, reachable workspace, and end-effector geometry require different action realiza-tions for a shared goal. Opening a trash bin and disposing of trash connects lid manipulation to subsequent placement. Tool-assisted scraping requires robot motion to produce the intended contact between the tool and trash. Cup hanging and keycap replacement require approaches and alignment compatible with the end effector and target geometry. Writ-ing and pouring water connect motion paths and object ori-entation to the intended interaction. Task instances adapt to each embodiment’s workspace while retaining the common semantic objective. This design adds an embodiment axis to capability pro-files. Shared task categories provide a basis for comparing which operations remain stable across robot configurations and which are sensitive to changes in reach, geometry, or control. They also support extending the suite to additional embodiments while preserving the task intent. Performance is interpreted in relation to the training data and adaptation available for each robot. The pooled multi-embodiment fine-tuning used in the present policy study measures execution across included configurations; evaluation on a robot with-held from training would require a separate protocol. 

C. Task Authoring, Initialization, and Controlled Variation 

The platform’s task-authoring workflow associates a lan-guage instruction with robot, object, and environment assets, spatial layouts, initial states, and task constraints. Agent-assisted asset composition and scripted orchestration support variants with different asset combinations, placements, and initial conditions. Episode records retain the task description, scene assets, execution configuration, robot and object states, timestamps, and task outcome. Retaining robot configuration, joint ordering, and camera parameters connects the semantic task to its physical and observational realization. These records support tracing differences between task variants and examining failed executions. The parallel-simulation path supports reproducible task initialization. Selected environments can restore recorded states and switch between compatible task instances at reset. Scene pre-baking caches settled object poses and support relationships, and layout perturbations use these relationships to keep objects on their supports and clear of one another. Per-environment seeds determine initial-state perturbations independently of how other episodes are scheduled. This task-management mechanism supports replay and controlled comparisons; it is separate from the scoring and inclusion conventions of the historical evaluations reported below. Controlled variation is organized into scene layout, robot, environment, and rendering factors. These cover object placement; initial arm configurations, base poses, and joint dynamics; lighting, materials, and backgrounds; and camera viewpoints, noise, occlusion, and frame loss. Independent random streams permit selected factors to vary while pre-serving unrelated random draws. Task-level profiles specify the variation settings, and readback distinguishes requested settings, resolved parameters, and applied changes. This or-ganization makes the perturbation configuration inspectable and supports experiments that separate sensitivity to individ-ual factors from performance across a combined variation profile. 

D. Leaderboard Protocol and Evaluation Integrity 

The benchmark protocol maintains a dedicated Agent leaderboard separately from the VLA/WAM leaderboard. These categories reflect different inference and action-generation procedures; grouping systems separately pre-serves their training and execution context. The Season II policy results contain VLA models only; the public Season I policy category also includes WAM submissions. Public task layouts and test-server layouts follow the same intended distribution but instantiate different scenes. The server protocol specifies a fixed official set for scoring and a dynamic hidden set that changes with each submission. Dynamic-set scores are excluded from Overall and are compared with official-set scores to identify discrepancies that may indicate test-set overfitting. Submissions with ex-cessive discrepancies may be restricted from leaderboard inclusion. Human review supplements these checks by exam-ining privileged-information access and evaluation-specific exploits. Together, these checks address layout memorization and inappropriate information access. The protocol follows the execution-budget and information-access considerations discussed by OpenRUA [16]. Its layout checks and submission review are design safeguards; the reported model scores are not an experimental assessment of their effectiveness.

The platform’s co-evolution principle is to use evaluation findings to guide simulation refinement and training-task se-lection; quantitative validation of that iterative loop remains future work. VII. C ONCLUSION 

RoboColiseum integrates system identification, compos-able physics and rendering, and eight-track embodied eval-uation. Paired task scores show strong real–simulation asso-ciation within the evaluated configurations. Public Season I and Season II results provide capability profiles for policy and Agent systems under their respective execution settings. Explicit progress, budget, and completion accounting makes those profiles interpretable.
