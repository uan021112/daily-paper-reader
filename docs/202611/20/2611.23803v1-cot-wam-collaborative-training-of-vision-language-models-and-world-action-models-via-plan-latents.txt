Abstract
General robot manipulation requires both semantic compe-
tence and physical foresight. World action models (WAMs)
acquire an understanding of and predictive capability over
the physical world by jointly modeling visual foresight and
action distributions. However, existing WAMs still lack se-
mantic competence: they have limited ability to perform
open-world scene understanding, semantic reasoning, and
long-horizon planning. Although vision-language models
(VLMs) provide these capabilities, effectively integrating
their semantic priors into WAM prediction and control re-
mains challenging. To address this challenge, we propose
CoT-WAM, a jointly trained WAM–VLM framework. CoT-
WAM uses a learnable embedding to exchange information
between the VLM and the WAM, which is supervised by tex-
tual and goal-image signals during training to encode rich
intent information. A tailored two-stage training strategy
enables joint optimization and more effective collaboration
between the VLM and WAM. We evaluate CoT-WAM on six
benchmarks covering control performance, zero-shot gener-
alization, and long-horizon planning. CoT-WAM achieves
SOTA performance on LIBERO, CALVIN, and RoboCasa-
Kitchen, and demonstrates stronger zero-shot generalization
on LIBERO-Plus and LIBERO-Pro without fine-tuning. On
RoboCasa365, it further improves long-horizon planning
over baselines with and without external planners. Real-
world experiments further demonstrate the effectiveness of
our method in practical deployments.
1. Introduction
Vision-language-action models (VLAs) have recently
emerged as a dominant paradigm in embodied intelli-
gence [2, 3, 10]. By transferring the powerful semantic and
scene-understanding capabilities of vision-language mod-
els (VLMs) to robot control signals through discrete action
tokens or dedicated action experts, VLAs achieve strong gen-
eralization. However, their reactive control paradigm cannot
model the evolution of the physical world or predict the con-
sequences of actions, both of which are essential capabilities
for open-world manipulation agents. World Action Models
(WAMs) address this limitation by jointly modeling future
visual-state transitions and action distributions [12, 13, 28].
By conditioning action generation on learned world dynam-
ics, WAMs provide a viable paradigm for pretraining on
large-scale videos and make it possible to build manipula-
tion agents capable of understanding the physical world.
Despite their ability to predict state evolution, WAMs lack
the open-world understanding, reasoning, and planning capa-
bilities provided by a VLM backbone. Existing WAMs, such
as Fast-WAM [28], LingBot-VA [12], and DreamZero [27],
typically rely on a separate frozen text encoder to provide
language grounding. This design is often insufficient for
tasks that require open-world scene understanding, semantic
generalization and long-horizon planning. Recent works,
such as ST-WAM [26] and SG-WAM [7], incorporate VLMs
to provide scene-aware semantic guidance for action genera-
tion. However, ST-WAM keeps its VLM frozen throughout
training, so its semantic representations are not jointly op-
timized with the WAM. This limits the adaptation of the
VLM’s semantic knowledge to the needs of future-state pre-
diction and action generation. SG-WAM jointly trains a
VLM-based semantic planner with the WAM, but relies on
additional frozen semantic and geometric teachers to super-
vise future feature prediction.
To address these limitations, we propose CoT-WAM, a
framework that seamlessly integrates the rich semantic prior
of a VLM into a WAM, thereby equipping WAM predic-
tion and decision-making with open-world understanding
and planning capabilities. Building upon Fast-WAM, CoT-
WAM replaces its conditioning input with a learnable hidden
state produced by the VLM. The VLM is responsible for
scene understanding or subtask planning, and conveys its
planning intent to the WAM through the latent embedding.
Conditioned on this compact yet semantically rich represen-
tation, the WAM predicts the corresponding future states

and generates control signals. During training, we supervise
the learnable embedding with two complementary signals:
a discrete textual task signal and a continuous goal-image
signal. Consequently, the information transmitted from the
VLM to the WAM is no longer restricted to “hard” text, but
can encode richer information about the predicted conse-
quences of an intended action. Through a two-stage training
strategy, we successfully endow the WAM with stronger un-
derstanding and planning capabilities, leading to improved
closed-loop control performance.
We evaluate CoT-WAM across six benchmarks cover-
ing control performance, zero-shot generalization, and long-
horizon planning. For control, CoT-WAM achieves state-of-
the-art performance on LIBERO, CALVIN, and RoboCasa-
Kitchen without additional embodied pretraining. For zero-
shot generalization, the same LIBERO-trained policy sub-
stantially outperforms Fast-WAM on LIBERO-Plus and
LIBERO-Pro without fine-tuning, while remaining competi-
tive with methods using additional embodied pretraining or
supervision from external visual priors. For long-horizon
planning, CoT-WAM outperforms baselines with and with-
out external planners on RoboCasa365, a challenging bench-
mark for mobile manipulation. Beyond these simulation
benchmarks, real-world experiments further validate the
practicality of CoT-WAM. Together, these results demon-
strate that integrating VLM semantic knowledge with world
modeling through joint training improves scene understand-
ing, semantic generalization, and long-horizon planning. In
summary, our contributions are:
• We propose CoT-WAM, a joint WAM–VLM training
framework that equips WAMs with scene understanding,
semantic generalization, and long-horizon planning capa-
bilities.
• We introduce dedicated latent tokens to enable richer and
more flexible information exchange between the VLM
and WAM, supervised by text and goal images. A staged
training strategy enables joint learning and collaboration
between the two models.
• Experiments across six benchmarks demonstrate that CoT-
WAM substantially improves long-horizon planning over
the baseline, while achieving state-of-the-art control per-
formance and stronger zero-shot generalization.
2. Related Work
2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models
Vision-language-action models transfer semantic knowledge
from pretrained VLMs to robot control. OpenVLA [10]
learns manipulation policies from multi-robot demonstra-
tions, while π0 [2] couples a VLM with flow-matching action
generation. X-VLA [32] studies scalable adaptation across
embodiments. π0.5 [9] combines robot data, web data, and
semantic subtask prediction to improve open-world general-
ization. These methods emphasize semantic understanding
and action generation without explicitly supervising future
visual-state prediction. We complement such semantic priors
with WAM-based visual prediction, so that the representa-
tions used for control are also trained to capture task-relevant
scene evolution.
2.2. World Action Models
World action models learn visual dynamics alongside robot
actions. LingBot-VA [12] couples video and action experts
through causal modeling. Fast-WAM [28] retains future-
video prediction as training supervision while omitting it
at inference, and Faster-WAM [19] further reduces com-
putation with a lightweight action module. However, lan-
guage conditioning in models such as Fast-WAM is en-
coded independently of the current observation, leaving
scene-dependent task interpretation to the downstream video–
action network. Building on Fast-WAM, we replace this
conditioning interface with continuous intent representations
produced by a VLM from both the observation and instruc-
tion, providing scene-aware semantic guidance for visual
prediction and action generation.
2.3. Integrating VLMs with World Action Models
Recent work incorporates VLM priors into WAMs through
different conditioning interfaces. DSWAM [34] introduces
an optional VLM planner that supplies explicit subtask in-
structions to a WAM executor, but this discrete text interface
does not provide a differentiable path from the executor to
the planner. ST-WAM [26] uses frozen VLM features to
retrieve task-relevant semantic history for action generation,
so WAM training cannot adapt the VLM itself. SG-WAM [7]
enables joint training of a VLM-based planner and the WAM,
but requires additional frozen semantic and geometric teach-
ers to supervise future feature prediction. Our approach
jointly optimizes the VLM and video predictor through a
continuous interface supervised by task text and future visual
observations, without additional external priors. Predicted
visual goals further ground the communicated intent in a
future state that conditions both video and action generation.
3. Overview
We consider embodied policy learning from visual obser-
vations and language instructions. Given the current ob-
servation ot, robot proprioceptive state st, and task instruc-
tion ℓ, the policy predicts an action sequence of length H,
At = (at, . . . , at+H−1). Following Fast-WAM [28], we
use future video prediction as auxiliary supervision to shape
visual representations during training, while omitting full
video prediction at inference time to accelerate execution.
As shown in Figure 1, our model consists of a world
action model based on a MoT architecture [15] and a VLM
planner. Given the current observation and task, the VLM
2


planner communicates its plan for the next step to the world
action model through a latent interface. The world action
model then generates the action chunk At conditioned on the
VLM’s intent and the current observation. To make the intent
signal transmitted from the VLM to the world model more
concrete and continuous, we further introduce goal-image
prediction on the video-model side, mapping the VLM’s
continuous hidden representations to a concrete target visual
state. The predicted goal image then conditions both action
and video generation, grounding the VLM’s intent more
directly in control. This grounds the WAM’s control outputs
in the VLM’s strong semantic priors, improving closed-loop
performance and generalization.
4. Method
4.1. Latent VLM–WAM Interface
To better incorporate the VLM’s understanding and plan-
ning of the current scene and task into action generation, we
introduce a learnable latent embedding interface for commu-
nication between the VLM and the WAM. Specifically, we
encode the current observation ot and task instruction ℓ, then
append K learnable intent tokens Q = (q1, . . . , qK) to form
the VLM input sequence. Through the VLM’s self-attention,
these tokens aggregate scene and task information. Their
final hidden states form a continuous representation of the
VLM’s intent, which is passed to the downstream WAM
through a projection layer:

The downstream WAM adopts a MoT architecture similar
to Fast-WAM [28], in which a video expert and an action
expert are integrated through joint attention. Both experts
access the intent information Ct from the VLM through
cross-attention to condition future-state and action genera-
tion. This communication mechanism allows future predic-
tion gradients to propagate through the interface to the VLM,
enabling joint training and closer collaboration between the
VLM and WAM.
4.2. Latent Interface Supervision
The differentiable interface establishes an information path-
way between the VLM and the WAM, but appropriate train-
ing objectives are still needed to determine what information
it should convey. We therefore introduce two complementary
forms of supervision: text and goal images. Text supervision
encourages the interface to retain high-level task semantics
or subtask planning objectives, while goal-image supervi-
sion explicitly expresses the VLM’s intent in visual form,
enriching the transmitted information and grounding it more
directly in control.

duce an auxiliary language model for text supervision [14],
we use the VLM planner’s own text generation capability.
Specifically, during training, we append the target text yt af-

while for long-horizon planning tasks, we use the provided
subtask descriptions as prediction targets. At inference time,
we obtain the VLM’s intent output Ct in a single forward

Goal-Image Supervision
Supervision based only on task
semantics or subtask planning is often too coarse to make the
latent queries encode the VLM’s rich scene understanding
and specific intent. We therefore additionally supervise the
latent interface using the image at the end of the training
window, gt = ot+H, as the goal image. As with text su-
pervision, we use the capabilities of the existing module:
the WAM’s video expert provides goal-image supervision
through its own visual prediction capability. Specifically,
we train the video expert to predict the goal image from
the current observation and the intent condition Ct given by
the VLM. This forces the latent interface to encode the con-
crete visual changes associated with the intent, grounding
the VLM’s abstract intent in a specific visual state.

video prediction in the VLM’s visual intent, we additionally
use the predicted goal image to condition both action and
video generation. During training, we predict the goal image

4.4. Staged Training and Closed-Loop Inference
4.4.1. Staged Training Strategy
We divide semantic interface learning and action policy learn-
ing into two stages. The first stage jointly learns intent repre-
sentations and visual prediction, enabling VLM outputs to
serve as effective generation conditions for the WAM. The
second stage fixes this interface and learns action generation
while retaining visual supervision.
Supervising the Latent Interface.
We first supervise how
the latent interface encodes information. The latent queries
4


should capture both (1) task or subtask semantics and (2)
the target visual state and its evolution. We therefore jointly
train the VLM and the video expert in this stage, without
incorporating the action expert. Specifically, we optimize
Lstage1 = λtextLtext + λgoalLgoal + λvideoLvideo.
(5)
Text supervision encourages the interface to preserve task
semantics or subtask planning information, while the goal
and video losses jointly propagate visual prediction gradients
to the VLM, encouraging the latent queries to encode con-
tinuous visual-state evolution. This stage teaches the latent
queries to encode the information needed for downstream
prediction.
Joint Optimization of Action and Video Generation.
In
Stage 2, we load all weights trained in Stage 1 and initialize
the action expert by linearly interpolating the video expert’s
weights. We freeze the VLM and the conditioning projection
layer, and jointly optimize the video and action experts:
Lstage2 = Lgoal + Lvideo + Laction.
(6)
Conditioned on the intent supplied by the frozen VLM, this
stage jointly trains the entire downstream WAM module to
translate the VLM’s abstract planning intent into concrete
control signals.

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks.
We organize evaluation around three capa-
bilities as follows. For control performance, we train sepa-
rate policies on LIBERO [17], CALVIN [20], and RoboCasa-
Kitchen [21]. LIBERO comprises 40 tasks across Spatial,
Object, Goal, and Long, with 50 trials per task. CALVIN
follows ABC→D, evaluating 1,000 five-instruction chains
in unseen environment D and reporting average successful
chain length (ACL). RoboCasa-Kitchen comprises 24 tasks,
with 50 evaluation trials per task. For zero-shot generaliza-
tion, we apply the LIBERO policy without fine-tuning to
LIBERO-Plus [6] and LIBERO-Pro [33], respectively. Plus
covers seven visual and language perturbations; Pro covers
Object, Position, Semantic, and Task variations. For long-
horizon planning, we use RoboCasa365 [22], whose compos-
ite tasks require the robot to execute and transition between
multiple subtasks. We use Atomic-Seen (18 tasks) as an
atomic-skill reference and Composite-Seen and Composite-
Unseen (16 tasks each) for long-horizon evaluation, with 30
trials per task. Detailed training and evaluation settings are
provided in Appendix A.
Implementation.
We initialize the VLM with Qwen3-VL-
4B-Instruct [1] and the video expert with Wan2.2-TI2V-
5B [25], connected through a latent interface with 8 intent
tokens by default. Unless otherwise specified, training uses
AdamW [18] with a global batch size of 64 and a cosine
learning-rate schedule. Except for the RoboCasa365 setting
detailed in Appendix A.5, Stage 1 runs for 10k steps at 10−5
and Stage 2 for 60k steps at 5 × 10−5. At inference time,
we first denoise the goal to a noise level of σ = 0.5 using
5 steps, and then fully denoise the actions using 10 steps.
Additional settings are provided in Appendix A.
5.2. Main Results
Control performance.
CoT-WAM achieves strong control
performance across all three benchmarks without additional
embodied pretraining (Table 1). It reaches 99.00% aver-
age success on LIBERO, an ACL of 4.54 on CALVIN, and
61.4% success on RoboCasa-Kitchen, the highest aggregate
scores among the compared methods. Compared with our
Fast-WAM baseline, CoT-WAM improves average success
on LIBERO by 1.40 percentage points, CALVIN ACL by
0.41, and success on RoboCasa-Kitchen by 19.1 percent-
age points. The improvements also extend to long-horizon
sequential control: LIBERO-Long success increases from
95.20% to 98.60%, while completion of five-task CALVIN
chains rises from 71.9% to 81.5%.
On LIBERO-Long,
CoT-WAM even achieves a slightly higher success rate than
LingBot-VA [12] (98.60% vs. 98.50%), which incorporates
observation history and additional embodied pretraining.
These results demonstrate effective manipulation and reliable
sequential instruction following across diverse environments.

Long-horizon planning.
CoT-WAM enables the WAM to
plan and execute long-horizon tasks (Table 3). It achieves
success rates of 20.42% and 25.42% on Composite-Seen
and Composite-Unseen, compared with 0.63% and 2.50%
for the Fast-WAM baseline, respectively. Atomic-Seen suc-
cess also improves from 16.48% to 29.63%. To account for
the additional subtask supervision used during training, we

5.3. Ablation Studies
To evaluate the contributions of our design choices, we
ablate the model architecture, training strategy, and latent
interface supervision on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus (Ta-
ble 4). The full model achieves the best performance on both
benchmarks, with larger gains over the ablated variants on
LIBERO-Plus.
Architecture design.
VLM conditioning and visual goals
that express the VLM’s intent improve both control perfor-
mance and generalization. Replacing the VLM with frozen
text embeddings reduces success by 9.95 percentage points
on LIBERO-Plus, the largest drop among the evaluated abla-
tions, and by 1.00 point on LIBERO. These results suggest
that VLM conditioning strengthens generalization across
semantic and visual variations while also improving control
performance. Removing the entire goal branch reduces suc-
cess by 1.20 percentage points on LIBERO and 4.95 points
on LIBERO-Plus, further supporting the benefit of grounding
the VLM’s intent in explicit visual states.
Training strategy.
We further evaluate end-to-end training,
in which both the VLM and WAM are trainable and jointly
optimized in a single stage. This setting yields slightly lower
performance than our staged training strategy. This supports
explicitly supervising the information conveyed through the
latent interface before training the WAM to generate actions
conditioned on the VLM’s intent. The modest performance
gap also suggests that our architecture is amenable to joint
optimization of the two models from the outset.
Latent interface supervision.
We evaluate the roles of
task text and goal image supervision in Stage 1 by remov-
ing each loss separately. Removing the text loss reduces
success by 0.55 percentage points on LIBERO and 1.90
points on LIBERO-Plus. This suggests that, beyond enabling
long-horizon planning through subtask descriptions, text su-
pervision also improves control performance and zero-shot
generalization on the shorter tasks evaluated here. Remov-
ing the goal image loss reduces success by 0.50 percentage
points on LIBERO and 4.75 points on LIBERO-Plus. This
decline, despite retaining goal supervision in Stage 2, high-
lights the value of grounding VLM intent in visual goals
during the initial stage of interface learning.

6. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced CoT-WAM, a framework that
connects VLM semantic reasoning with world-action mod-
eling through continuous plan latents. We jointly train this
interface in two stages, using text supervision to align the
latents with subtask semantics and goal-image prediction
to ground their intent in future visual states. These pre-
dicted goals then condition both video and action generation,
linking semantic planning to predictive control. Experi-
ments across six benchmarks demonstrate state-of-the-art
control performance and improved zero-shot generalization
and long-horizon planning. Real-world experiments further
validate its effectiveness in physical manipulation.

Stage 1 jointly optimizes the VLM, intent queries, pro-

Goal conditioning and inference.
During training, the
goal condition is clean with probability 0.5. Otherwise, its
noise level is sampled uniformly from [0, 0.5] and applied
according to Eq. 8. At inference, the VLM computes the
intent condition in one forward pass. We then denoise the
goal for 5 steps to σ = 0.5, hold this latent fixed, and denoise
the action chunk for 10 steps. Text decoding and full future-
video generation are omitted.
A.2. Training Windows and Supervision
For LIBERO, CALVIN, and RoboCasa-Kitchen, each train-
ing window spans 32 action steps and contains nine images
sampled at a stride of four steps, including the current obser-
vation and the endpoint. On LIBERO and CALVIN, external
and wrist-camera images are resized to 224 × 224 and con-
catenated horizontally. Actions retain each dataset’s native
parameterization and are normalized using training-set statis-
tics.
The text target is the task instruction for these three bench-
marks. For RoboCasa365 composite tasks, the text target is
the annotated subtask description associated with the current
state. For Atomic-Seen, the original task instruction serves
as the text target. In all settings, target-text tokens attend
to the intent queries and their own causal text prefix, while
the intent queries aggregate the observation and overall task
instruction.
A.3. Control Performance
We train separate policies on LIBERO, CALVIN, and
RoboCasa-Kitchen using their respective demonstration
datasets and evaluate them with closed-loop replanning.
LIBERO covers the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites,
while RoboCasa-Kitchen evaluates manipulation in kitchen
environments. CALVIN follows the ABC→D protocol, train-
ing on language-annotated segments from environments A,
B, and C and evaluating instruction chains in the unseen
environment D. We report task success rates for LIBERO
and RoboCasa-Kitchen, and average successful chain length
and chain-completion rates for CALVIN.
A.4. Zero-Shot Generalization
We evaluate the LIBERO-trained policy on LIBERO-Plus
and LIBERO-Pro without fine-tuning. LIBERO-Plus tests
robustness to visual and language perturbations, while
LIBERO-Pro covers Object, Position, Semantic, and Task
variations. We report success rates, averaging across varia-
tion categories for LIBERO-Pro.
Comparison fields.
In Tables 1 and 2, additional embod-
ied pretraining refers to pretraining on robot data beyond the
benchmark-specific training set. The external-prior field in
Table 2 denotes auxiliary pretrained semantic or geometric
models used to guide or supervise policy learning.
A.5. Long-Horizon Planning
Task suites and data.
In RoboCasa365, composite tasks
involve mobile manipulation across multiple linked sub-
tasks, requiring the robot to coordinate their execution and
transition between them. We evaluate atomic-skill execu-
tion on Atomic-Seen and long-horizon task completion on
Composite-Seen and Composite-Unseen. These suites con-
tain 18, 16, and 16 tasks, respectively. Each task uses 30
evaluation trials with seeds 0–29 and the official task hori-
zon, giving 540 Atomic-Seen trials and 480 trials in each
composite suite.
Standalone Fast-WAM and CoT-WAM policies are
trained separately for Atomic-Seen, Composite-Seen, and
Composite-Unseen using 10% of the demonstrations from

the target split (50 demonstrations per task): 900 demon-
strations for Atomic-Seen and 800 for each composite suite.
For CoT-WAM, Atomic-Seen uses the original task descrip-
tion for text supervision and does not involve subtask predic-

Training and control settings.
The CoT-WAM models for
Atomic-Seen, Composite-Seen, and Composite-Unseen use
16 intent queries and a 64-step action horizon. Their three
camera views are resized to 256 × 256 and concatenated
horizontally, and nine images are sampled at a stride of eight
control steps. Stage 1 runs for 30k updates at a learning rate
of 10−5, with unit weights for text, goal, and video losses.
Stage 2 starts from this checkpoint, uses a learning rate of
10−4, and is evaluated at 150k updates. The corresponding
Fast-WAM and skill-policy checkpoints are also evaluated
at 150k updates.
At inference, CoT-WAM follows the goal and action de-
noising procedure described above. Both CoT-WAM and
Fast-WAM predict 64-step action chunks and execute the
first 32 actions before replanning.
VLM-scheduled skill policy.
To account for the subtask
annotations used by CoT-WAM, we train separate Fast-
WAM skill policies for Composite-Seen and Composite-
Unseen, each using annotated subtask windows from 800
demonstrations covering 16 tasks. Each skill policy receives
a subtask description and generates the corresponding robot
actions. A Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct planner selects a subtask
every 64 control steps from the current scene and overall
instruction, while the skill policy replans every 32 steps us-
ing ten action-denoising steps. This comparison provides
the subtask information through an explicit planner–policy
interface.
Oracle-scheduled skill policy.
We then replace the VLM
planner with an oracle subtask scheduler constructed from
privileged simulator state, while retaining the same Fast-
WAM skill policy. The scheduler uses object positions, grasp
and contact states, fixture states, and task-progress predicates
to select the next subtask. It checks whether the required
state transition has occurred before advancing and rechecks
prerequisites after a failed grasp or placement. Subtasks are
updated every 64 control steps, and the skill policy continues
to generate all robot actions. This setting assigns subtask
selection and scheduling to the privileged-state oracle, allow-
ing the comparison to focus on how effectively the learned
policy carries out the resulting sequence.
A.6. Real-World Experiments
Figure 6 illustrates real-world task execution. The platform
has two seven-DoF Marvin M6 arms and provides a head-
camera view and an upper-wrist view from each arm. We
consider the following tasks:
• Produce sorting. The robot sorts fruits and vegetables
onto their designated plates according to their categories.
• Table wiping. The robot picks up a towel and wipes the
designated stain from the tabletop.
Data and action representation.
For each task, we use
100 demonstrations for training, all collected on our robot
platform.
The policy predicts 32-step bimanual action
chunks. Each arm’s action consists of a three-dimensional
translation, a six-dimensional rotation representation, and an
absolute gripper opening, giving 20 dimensions per biman-
ual action. For each arm, all predicted end-effector poses
are expressed relative to its measured pose at the start of the
action chunk.

Supervision on the latent interface.
The w/o text supervi-
sion variant removes the text loss in Stage 1 while retaining
goal and video supervision. The w/o goal supervision variant
removes the Stage 1 goal-image loss while retaining the goal
branch, text loss, and video loss. Both variants subsequently

action supervision. Thus, these ablations change interface
supervision in Stage 1, whereas removing the goal branch
changes the architecture in both stages.

D. Limitations
The visual goals in CoT-WAM are defined by a fixed pre-
diction horizon, which may not align with semantic subtask
boundaries. The framework also lacks an explicit mechanism
for assessing whether a predicted goal is achievable from
the current state. Future work could explore adaptive goal
horizons and goal feasibility assessment to support more
flexible planning and recovery from unsuccessful execution.
