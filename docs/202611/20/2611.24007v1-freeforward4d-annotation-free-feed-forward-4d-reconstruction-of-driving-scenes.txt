## Abstract 

Feed-forward 4D reconstruction enables efficient creation of renderable driving scenes for autonomous driving sim-ulation. However, most existing methods rely heavily on dataset-provided cameras, LiDAR depth, or 3D box an-notations, limiting their applicability to unannotated driv-ing videos. Pretrained visual models can predict cameras, depth, and object tracks from RGB alone, but their geome-try is mutually inconsistent: multi-view predictions come in short windows with independent scales and frames, while monocular depth is metric only per image. Our key insight is that, once reconciled into a single metric frame, these priors can replace dataset annotations for feed-forward 4D reconstruction. We present FreeForward4D , which cali-brates overlapping prediction windows against monocular metric depth and registers them into one frame across time. On this geometry, 2D tracks are associated across cam-arXiv:2611.24007v1 [cs.CV] 18 Nov 2026 eras in 3D, and objects move through hierarchical motion that combines a shared object velocity with local Gaus-sian corrections, so that both training and inference use only RGB videos. On Waymo, FreeForward4D achieves 27.66 dB PSNR and 0.836 SSIM while recovering metric-scale cameras and depth without scale alignment. It sur-passes STORM, SLARM-F, UFO, DGGT, and StreetFor-ward, which rely on dataset annotations, in full-image PSNR and attains the best RGB fidelity on 8-second win-dows. RGB-only fine-tuning further adapts the framework to nuScenes and Argoverse 2. Code will be released. 

## 1. Introduction 

Reconstructing renderable dynamic driving scenes from real-world videos is essential for scalable sensor simulation and closed-loop evaluation in autonomous driving. Exist-ing per-scene methods achieve high-fidelity reconstruction by optimizing a dedicated representation for each driving sequence [6, 45, 46]. However, their costly per-scene opti-mization, often requiring minutes to hours, makes scaling to large collections of driving videos impractical. This compu-tational bottleneck has motivated feed-forward approaches, which learn transferable reconstruction priors across scenes and directly predict renderable 4D representations without scene-specific optimization [19, 26, 30, 47]. Despite these advances in efficiency, existing feed-forward methods still rely heavily on dataset annotations that are costly to acquire at scale. They typically require dataset cameras (intrinsics and poses) to establish scene ge-ometry, LiDAR depth for geometric supervision, and of-ten 3D box annotations or the object trajectories derived from them for dynamic modeling. Recent pose-free meth-ods [3, 52] eliminate the need for camera poses at infer-ence, but still rely on dataset annotations during training. Moreover, pose-free reconstruction is not guaranteed to be metric; DGGT, for instance, aligns its depth to LiDAR be-fore evaluation [3]. Consequently, these dependencies limit the applicability of feed-forward reconstruction to unanno-tated driving videos, where neither dataset annotations nor metric-scale references are available. This raises a funda-mental question: Can feed-forward 4D reconstruction be learned from RGB videos alone, without dataset-provided cameras, LiDAR depth, or 3D box annotations during train-ing or inference, while recovering metric-scale geometry and coherent scene dynamics? 

Pretrained visual foundation models now estimate cam-eras and depth from RGB images [35, 36, 40] and provide object-level observations without 3D box annotations [18, 27]. Their geometry, however, is not directly usable for re-construction, because it is inconsistent at three levels: in 

scale , multi-view models predict relative depth within each temporal window while monocular models predict metric depth per image; in coordinate frame , each window has its own reference; and in image grid , predicted intrinsics re-fer to each model’s input resolution rather than the recon-struction grid. Image-derived object tracks additionally lack cross-camera correspondence and 3D motion, while each timestamp observes only part of a moving object’s surface. Combined naively, these priors yield cameras, depth targets, and tracks that disagree with one another. Our key insight is that these priors, once reconciled into a single metric frame, can replace dataset cameras, LiDAR depth, and 3D boxes for both reconstruction and dynamics. Building on this insight, we present FreeFor-ward4D , an annotation-free feed-forward framework for dynamic driving scene reconstruction using only RGB-derived conditions (Fig. 1). First, metric-consistent geo-metric prior integration resolves all three inconsistencies: it calibrates the scale of each overlapping window against monocular metric depth, registers the windows into one scene frame, and maps the predicted intrinsics onto the re-construction grid, yielding metric cameras and depth with-out ground-truth scale alignment. Second, geometry-guided dynamic reconstruction exploits this common frame to as-sociate 2D tracks across cameras in 3D and to move each object with a shared velocity refined by local Gaussian trajectories. Source-aware temporal fusion further inte-grates motion-compensated observations across timestamps for novel-time rendering. Extensive experiments on Waymo Open [29], nuScenes [1], and Argoverse2 [43] demonstrate the effec-tiveness of FreeForward4D. Pose-conditioned STORM, SLARM-F, and UFO require dataset cameras as input, and pose-free methods require dataset annotations for training; without either, FreeForward4D achieves higher full-image PSNR than these methods, with the highest RGB fidelity over extended 8-second windows. On Waymo, it further recovers metric-scale cameras without any scale fitting, and RGB-only fine-tuning suffices to adapt it to new datasets. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We present FreeForward4D , a feed-forward 4D recon-struction framework trained and run on RGB driving videos alone, and show that pretrained priors, once rec-onciled into a single metric frame, can replace dataset-provided cameras, LiDAR depth, and 3D box annota-tions. • We propose metric-consistent geometric prior integra-tion, which resolves inconsistencies in scale, coordinate frame, and image grid by calibrating overlapping win-dows against monocular metric depth, registering them into one metric frame, and aligning camera rays with the reconstruction grid, yielding metric cameras and depth without dataset cameras, LiDAR depth, or ground-truth scale alignment. • We propose geometry-guided dynamic reconstruction, which associates per-camera 2D tracks across cameras through their 3D centers in this metric frame rather than 3D box annotations, couples them through a shared ob-ject velocity refined by local higher-order Gaussian mo-tion, and fuses context times with per-object normalized weights. • Experiments on Waymo Open, nuScenes, and Argoverse2 show that, without any dataset annotations, FreeFor-ward4D surpasses STORM, SLARM-F, UFO, DGGT, and StreetForward in full-image PSNR, attaining the highest RGB fidelity on Waymo 8-second windows. 

## 2. Related Work 

Per-scene driving scene reconstruction. Per-scene driving reconstruction has been extensively explored using neural radiance fields [21] and, more recently, 3D Gaussian Splat-ting [15]. These representations have also been extended to autonomous driving sensor simulation [11, 32], typically requiring scene-specific optimization for each recorded se-quence. Existing methods model scene dynamics through two main paradigms. Object-centric approaches explicitly decompose driving scenes into static backgrounds and in-dividual moving actors, whose motions are modeled using object-level transformations [6, 24, 44, 45, 49, 55, 56]. Such methods typically rely on 3D bounding boxes or tracked object poses to initialize dynamic reconstruction. Scene-level approaches, in contrast, capture dynamics through spatiotemporal fields, scene flow, or time-varying Gaus-sian attributes, without requiring predefined object trajec-tories [5, 13, 25, 33, 46]. Despite their different motion representations, both paradigms require computationally expensive scene-specific optimization, often taking hours per sequence and limiting their scalability to large-scale driving datasets. 

Feed-forward driving scene reconstruction. Feed-forward methods replace per-scene optimization with net-works that directly predict radiance fields or 3D Gaus-sians from images [2, 4, 37, 51]. In driving scenarios, sparse surround-view observations and complex scene dy-namics pose challenges for both geometric reconstruction and temporal consistency. Existing methods address sparse-view geometry through depth estimation and multi-view feature aggregation [10, 20, 31], while dynamic recon-struction incorporates motion-aware aggregation, static– dynamic decomposition, and time-dependent Gaussian rep-resentations [19, 26, 47]. Other approaches explore re-current scene updates, object-centric representations, and future-time synthesis to improve temporal modeling and rendering flexibility [8, 28, 30]. Recent pose-free methods jointly estimate cameras and dynamic representations from unposed images [3, 52], but are still trained with dataset annotations. Flux4D [34] avoids 3D box annotations but requires dataset cameras and LiDAR as input. In contrast, FreeForward4D enables feed-forward 4D re-construction from RGB driving videos alone, without re-quiring dataset-provided cameras, LiDAR depth, or 3D box annotations during training or inference. 

Foundation models for geometry and object pri-ors. Large-scale visual pretraining has enabled trans-ferable representations for diverse geometric and seman-tic tasks [23]. For geometric reconstruction, monocu-lar models provide dense depth and metric geometry esti-mates [12, 39, 40, 48], while multi-view models recover cross-view correspondences and scene structure from un-calibrated images [17, 35, 41, 42]. Recent advances in model scaling, recurrent inference, and chunk-wise align-ment of long sequences have further improved multi-view geometric reconstruction [9, 36, 38]. Combining monoc-ular metric depth with multi-view geometry has been ex-plored to recover the metric scale of a single reconstruc-tion [7]; FreeForward4D builds on such calibration to rec-oncile scale, coordinate frame, and image grid across over-lapping temporal windows, yielding a single metric frame that conditions feed-forward reconstruction. Beyond geom-etry, open-vocabulary detection and promptable segmenta-tion provide object-level cues that can be propagated across video frames [16, 18, 27]. 

## 3. Method 

Problem formulation. We study offline 4D reconstruc-tion of a multi-view driving video I = {Ivt }, indexed by time t and camera v, without dataset-provided cam-eras, LiDAR depth, or 3D box annotations in training or inference, which we refer to as annotation-free. Frozen vi-sual priors process the full video to estimate camera intrin-sics bKvt , metric camera-to-world poses bT vt , and 2D object tracks, and provide monocular pseudo-depth for scale cali-bration and supervision. A feed-forward reconstructor then predicts dynamic Gaussians and their motion from a sparse set of context frames C ⊂ I and their derived conditions, which can be rendered at arbitrary target cameras and times. Given the precomputed priors, reconstruction and rendering run in real time (Sec. 4). 

Overview. As illustrated in Fig. 2, FreeForward4D con-sists of two components: metric-consistent geometric prior integration (Sec. 3.1), which unifies heterogeneous visual predictions into globally consistent metric geometry, and geometry-guided dynamic reconstruction (Sec. 3.2), which establishes cross-view object associations and models co-herent dynamics through hierarchical motion modeling and source-aware temporal fusion.

The resulting poses, rays, and metric depth targets serve as consistent conditions for Gaussian recon-struction and cross-view object association. 

3.2. Geometry-Guided Dynamic Reconstruction 

Feed-forward Gaussian prediction. Given the geomet-rically consistent camera estimates from Sec. 3.1, we em-ploy a Transformer-based reconstructor that encodes image patches, Pl¨ ucker ray maps, and timestamps into tokens [47]. To aggregate information across context frames, the scene tokens are recurrently updated [30] and decoded into pixel-aligned 3D Gaussians. Each Gaussian j originates from a context frame at time τj , with its initial center defined as 

μj = oj + dj rj , where oj and rj are the camera ray origin and direction, and dj denotes the predicted camera-frame depth. The Gaussian head additionally predicts scale, orien-tation, opacity, color, and lifespan [5]. We retain Gaussians from all context frames to preserve complementary surface observations across views and time, providing the basis for subsequent motion modeling and temporal fusion. These components follow prior designs [5, 30, 47]; Sec. 4.6 ab-lates our geometry conditions and the object-level dynamics below. 

Geometry-guided cross-view object association. 

Grounding DINO [18] and SAM 2 [27] produce 2D object tracks independently for each camera. Since the decoded Gaussian centers lie in the common scene frame of Sec. 3.1, we associate these tracks in 3D. Specifically, for each track at each context time, we compute its 3D center as the coordinate-wise median of the Gaussian centers within its mask. Tracks from different cameras are compared by the median distance between their 3D centers over shared context times, and mutual nearest neighbors within a distance threshold form a group, with at most one track per camera. Each Gaussian j inherits the group ID gj of the track covering its pixel, where positive IDs denote tracked foreground objects, including stationary ones, and zero denotes the background. 

Hierarchical motion modeling. To predict object mo-tion, backbone features pooled within the masks of each group across views and context times form a temporal de-scriptor, from which a velocity MLP predicts a shared ve-locity vg that couples the Gaussians of group g across cam-eras. Cross-view identity is thus established by the pre-dicted geometry, while motion is estimated from features. While the shared velocity captures the overall translation of an object, it cannot represent spatially varying motion, such as that of a turning vehicle. We therefore refine the foreground Gaussian attributes, including the center eμj , and predict a residual velocity δvj , acceleration aj , and jerk jj

3.3. Training Objectives 

The reconstruction backbone and dynamic heads are jointly trained through target-view rendering. We employ a photo-metric MSE loss Lrgb , assigning five times higher weights to pixels within object track masks to emphasize object re-gions. A full-image perceptual loss LLPIPS [54] further improves rendering fidelity. For geometric supervision, 

Ld measures the discrepancy between rendered depth and MoGe-2 pseudo-depth, with both depth maps normalized by the maximum valid pseudo-depth. We additionally intro-duce a motion regularization loss Lm that penalizes exces-sive Gaussian-specific residual velocity, acceleration, and jerk, encouraging local motion to remain consistent with the shared object-level motion. 4.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets and evaluation protocol. We conduct our pri-mary experiments on the Waymo Open Dataset [29] and further examine cross-dataset adaptation through RGB-only fine-tuning on nuScenes [1] and Argoverse2 [43]. On Waymo, we evaluate all 202 validation scenes using the front-left, front, and front-right cameras at 10 Hz. Follow-ing the temporal sampling protocol of STORM [47], each 2s clip uses frames {0, 5, 10 , 15 } as context and evaluates 12 interpolated timestamps {1 : 4 , 6 : 9 , 11 : 14 } and four extrapolated timestamps {16 : 19 }, yielding 48 target im-ages per complete three-view clip. The evaluation com-prises 96,360 target images at 160 × 240 resolution (supple-mentary Sec. D). As in pose-free evaluation [14, 22], target cameras are estimated from the images: ours come from the same full-video front end as the context cameras (Sec. 3.1), so target frames inform these conditions but never enter the reconstructor (supplementary Sec. E). 

Metrics and baselines. We report PSNR and SSIM on full images and on dynamic regions defined by Waymo 3D box annotations following STORM [47], averaging image-wise scores over the evaluation set. Geometric accuracy is measured by depth RMSE (D-RMSE) at pixels with valid LiDAR measurements, without scale alignment. Dataset cameras, LiDAR depth, and 3D box annotations are used only for evaluation. We compare with pose-conditioned feed-forward meth-ods STORM [47], SLARM-F [26], UFO [30], and Point-Forward [8], as well as pose-free methods DGGT [3] and StreetForward [52]. STORM and SLARM-F are locally reproduced under matched evaluation settings (supplemen-tary Sec. B); results for other methods are taken from their respective publications unless stated otherwise. 

Implementation details. We train the three-view 2s model for 100k iterations; the 8s model is initialized from the 2s checkpoint and trained for another 50k iterations on NVIDIA H200 GPUs (details in supplementary Sec. A.5). Given precomputed RGB-derived conditions, the 2s model reconstructs a three-view clip in 0.21 s and renders each tar-get timestamp in 13 ms on a single RTX 4090. 

4.2. Novel-Time Synthesis on Waymo 

Quantitative comparison. Pose-conditioned STORM, SLARM-F, and UFO require dataset cameras as input; with-out them, FreeForward4D achieves 1.81, 0.68, and 0.40 dB higher full-image PSNR, using frozen priors reconciled into a single metric frame instead. On dynamic regions, the gains over STORM and SLARM-F increase to 2.32 and 1.41 dB in PSNR, with higher SSIM (0.729 vs. 0.623 and 0.672), consistent with our object-level motion modeling. Among pose-free methods, FreeForward4D exceeds DGGT and StreetForward by 0.25 and 0.65 dB in full-image PSNR, although both are trained with dataset annotations, and remains higher in PSNR under the evaluation protocol of the official DGGT code (supplementary Sec. E). Only PointForward, which uses ground-truth 3D box trajectories, attains higher PSNR on both full images and dynamic re-gions. 

Qualitative comparison. Figure 3 compares extrapo-lated timestamps. FreeForward4D reconstructs sharper ve-hicle boundaries and finer appearance details than STORM and SLARM-F while preserving static structures, without dataset cameras. 

4.3. Metric Geometry and Motion from RGB 

Metric geometry evaluation. FreeForward4D is the only method in Table 1 that uses no dataset cameras, LiDAR, or alignment in either training or evaluation, drawing met-ric scale from the frozen MoGe-2 prior, whose training data include Waymo LiDAR (supplementary Sec. A): pose-conditioned methods inherit it from dataset cameras and mostly LiDAR, DGGT aligns its depth to LiDAR before evaluation [3], and StreetForward is optionally supervised with ground-truth depth. Its D-RMSE of 7.06 m on Waymo 2s therefore jointly reflects scale, camera, and depth errors. Camera accuracy tests the recovered scale directly: each 2s clip is anchored at its first front-camera pose without scale adjustment or trajectory fitting. Across 1,818 clips, the absolute trajectory error is 0.58 m in median (1.08 m mean, 2.55 m P90) over a median ground-truth path length of 11.0 m, while the median relative translation error over 0.5 s is 0.27 m and the median rotation error is 1.20 ◦ (sup-plementary Sec. C). Figure 4 visualizes both aspects: ren-derings from cameras elevated by 0.5 m preserve road lay-out and vehicle shapes, and Gaussians on the same vehicle follow parallel, smooth trajectories. 

4.4. Extended 8s Reconstruction 

We evaluate extended temporal windows on 8-second se-quences using 16 context frames sampled at 0.5-second in-tervals. As shown in Table 2, our method achieves the best RGB rendering performance on Waymo, with 27.57 dB PSNR and 0.836 SSIM, surpassing UFO by 0.18 dB and STORM by 3.09 dB. Evaluated without alignment, our D-RMSE (7.11 m) is lower than those of STORM (8.11 m) and GS-LRM (7.37 m) and above that of UFO (5.10 m), all of which use dataset annotations. It also merges static Gaus-sians over about 20 s without per-scene optimization (sup-plementary Sec. F).

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented FreeForward4D, an annotation-free feed-forward framework that reconstructs dynamic driving scenes from RGB videos. Reconciling frozen visual pri-ors into a single metric frame enables cross-view object as-sociation and shared object motion without 3D box anno-tations; replacing the calibrated cameras with uncalibrated ones costs 3.93 dB in full-image PSNR. Without dataset an-notations, FreeForward4D surpasses STORM, SLARM-F, UFO, DGGT, and StreetForward in full-image PSNR on Waymo and adapts to nuScenes and Argoverse2 through RGB-only fine-tuning. Its depth accuracy and dynamics are bounded by the frozen priors; needing no annotations, it can directly benefit from stronger priors and more unannotated videos.

## A. Implementation Details 

This section details the calibration, rendering, and training used by FreeForward4D. 

Pretrained priors. FreeForward4D uses four publicly re-leased models, all kept frozen and never fine-tuned on our data: VGGT-Ω [36] for relative cameras and depth, MoGe-2 [40] for monocular metric depth, Grounding DINO [18] for open-vocabulary detection, and SAM 2 [27] for mask propagation. Their training data are described in the re-spective papers; in particular, MoGe-2 is trained on a mix-ture that includes LiDAR depth from driving datasets such as Waymo and Argoverse2 [40], without a specified split. FreeForward4D itself uses no camera, LiDAR, or box an-notation of any dataset for training or inference.

A.3. Gaussian Construction and Object Motion 

Each 8 × 8 image patch produces 64 pixel-aligned Gaus-sians through a two-layer decoder. The ray used for the initial center has camera-frame z = 1 and is transformed to scene coordinates without unit-length normalization. Con-sequently the predicted depth is camera-frame z-depth. Unit directions are used for the Pl¨ ucker encoding. Each Gaussian retains its context time. 

2D object tracks. Tracks are computed offline, indepen-dently for each camera over the full video with Grounding DINO [18] and SAM 2 [27]. Every 10 frames, Ground-ing DINO detects objects with the prompt “car. truck. bus. motorcycle. bicycle. pedestrian.” (box and text thresholds 0.25), and SAM 2 converts the detected boxes into masks. A new mask inherits the ID of an existing track when their mask IoU is at least 0.8 and otherwise starts a new track. Masks are propagated forward for 10 frames, and objects first detected at a keyframe are additionally propa-gated backward for up to 20 frames. Consequently, objects first detected at a later keyframe obtain masks at earlier con-text frames from frames up to 2 s later, as permitted by the offline setting of the main paper (Sec. 3). Only the masks and track identities at the selected context timestamps are passed to the reconstructor; target-frame masks are used only to weight the training loss. 

Cross-view association. A track observation contributes a 3D center only when its mask contains at least 16 finite Gaussian centers. Coordinate-wise medians are detached before association. Cross-camera mutual nearest neighbors are accepted when their median Euclidean distance over common context times is at most 4 m and they share at least one context time. A group contains at most one track per camera. Tracks without a valid center keep distinct positive IDs. We evaluate association against Waymo 3D labels, used for evaluation only. Each track is assigned to a labeled object by 2D box overlap (IoU ≥ 0.3) on context frames, which matches 44.6% of the 83,701 tracks over 2,020 win-dows. Among cross-camera pairs of matched tracks that share a context time, the 4 m threshold gives a pairwise pre-cision of 0.867 and recall of 0.647; varying it from 3 to 

6 m changes the F1 score only from 0.720 to 0.751. Cen-ters of the same object seen by different cameras differ by a median of 1.53 m , and 83.2% of such pairs lie within 4 m ,compared with 1.4% of pairs from different objects. These values exclude unmatched tracks and include errors of the label assignment. 

Shared and local heads. Mask coverage weights fea-ture pooling over views at each context time. The shared-velocity MLP receives the temporal mean and least-squares temporal slope of pooled features, the observation duration, and log(1 + ¯ c)/5, where ¯c is the mean total patch coverage. Its output is bounded componentwise to (−20 , 20) m /s.Groups observed at fewer than two context times receive zero shared velocity. The attribute head uses patch features, input RGB, de-coded color, and opacity to refine foreground centers, scales, quaternions, opacities, and colors once per recon-struction. The local head takes patch features, RGB, the refined center, shared velocity, and the normalized pixel position within the patch. Residual velocity, acceleration, and jerk have componentwise bounds 4 m /s, 6 m /s2, and 

12 m /s3, respectively. The temporal trajectory changes Gaussian centers; refined scale, orientation, and color re-main fixed during target-time evaluation. Background cen-ters remain fixed.

## B. Reproduced Baselines 

STORM † and SLARM-F † in Table 1 of the main paper are retrained with the authors’ official open-source implemen-tations without code modification and evaluated on the same 96,360 target images under the same protocol as ours. With our deduplicated 96,360 targets, STORM † reaches 25.85 dB full-image PSNR, compared with 26.38 dB reported in [47]; FreeForward4D (27.66 dB) exceeds both. 

## C. Accuracy of the Estimated Cameras 

We score the estimated cameras on all 202 Waymo valida-tion scenes against the dataset poses, which are used only here. Each 2s clip (the nine non-overlapping 20-frame win-dows of each scene, starting every 20 frames) is re-based at its first front-camera pose for both trajectories; no scale, ro-tation, or translation is fitted. ATE is the translation RMSE over the three cameras and 20 frames. RPE is the trans-lation RMSE of 0.5 s relative motions of the same camera. Rotation error is the geodesic angle between the re-based estimated and dataset camera rotations, averaged over the three cameras and 20 frames. Table S5 summarizes the re-sults. The median ground-truth path of a clip is 11.0 m.

## E. Target Cameras 

Main-paper protocol. Following the pose-free evalua-tion of AnySplat [14] and Off The Grid [22], target cam-eras are estimated from the images while the scene is re-constructed from the context frames. Our front end pro-cesses the full video in a single pass, so the context cam-eras, the metric calibration, and the 2D object tracks are obtained with all frames available; target RGB never en-ters the reconstruction network, consistent with the offline setting of the main paper (Sec. 3). Rendering a pose-free reconstruction at dataset cameras would first require regis-tering the dataset frame to the frame of the reconstruction, and errors of this registration would enter the image metrics; estimating target cameras in the frame of the reconstruction keeps the evaluation on rendering quality. Unlike AnySplat, which passes the target images through its network to pre-dict their poses, our target frames inform only the front end. Comparing such renderings with pose-conditioned methods rendered at dataset cameras follows DGGT [3]. 

Protocol of the DGGT code. We also evaluate with the protocol of the official DGGT code [3]. From the context frames {0, 5, 10 , 15 } of each clip, frames 0–15 are rendered and the metrics are averaged over all 16, so the four context frames, which are inputs to the reconstruction, are scored together with the 12 intermediate frames, and the extrapo-lated frames {16 : 19 } are not evaluated. Target cameras are interpolated between the context cameras, with spher-ical interpolation for rotation and linear interpolation for translation, rather than estimated from the target images. We keep the clips and the 160 × 240 resolution of Table 1 of the main paper. Because the evaluated frames differ, these results are not directly comparable with Table 1 of the main paper. Table S7 reports the 16 frames together and the 12 intermediate frames alone. DGGT describes its evaluation as covering all frames, with inputs 0, 5, 10, and 15 and predictions for frames 0–19, and describes its re-ported novel-view synthesis results as computed on interpo-lated frames; its public code scores frames 0–15. On both frame sets, FreeForward4D attains higher PSNR than the 27.41 dB reported by DGGT. On the same 12 intermediate frames, front-end target cameras give 28.09 dB (Table S6) and interpolated cameras 27.57 dB; the two differ only in the target cameras. Pose-conditioned baselines render at the exact dataset cameras of the target frames, which are consis-tent with their dataset context cameras; the counterpart for a pose-free method is to estimate the target cameras in its own frame. These estimates are not more accurate than the dataset cameras: their median trajectory error is 0.58 m over 2s clips (Sec. C). Our context cameras are estimated in the same way, so pose errors among them, which the baselines do not incur, affect only our reconstructions.
