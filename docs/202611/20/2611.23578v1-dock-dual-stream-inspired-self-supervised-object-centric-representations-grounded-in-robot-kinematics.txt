Abstract 

Latent-space planners select actions by comparing the em-bedding of a predicted future image with that of a goal im-age. This assumes that distance in the embedding reflects how far the scene is from the goal. We profile this assump-tion with simulator-executed futures, which removes pre-diction error. For six frozen DINO and V-JEPA encoders, feature distance barely tracks the object’s distance to its goal: the rank correlation is at most 0.11, against 0.87 for object-mask centroids. It is dominated by background and robot patches, and frozen-feature readouts capture the arm far better than the object. Inspired by the ventral ( what )and dorsal ( where/how ) visual streams, we propose DOCK, which learns an object representation whose changes agree with measured physical motion. A grasped object moves with the gripper, so forward kinematics on the joint an-gles gives a proxy for the object’s displacement without an-notation. From 9,951 held-object transport trajectories in 21 simulated kitchen scenes, we mine 404K displacement pairs and train three components: an object token pooled from patch features ( what ), a spatial ruler that makes differ-ences of readout coordinates equal to measured displace-ments ( where ), and a dynamics predictor over object tokens (how ). Used as a planning cost, the learned representation places the object in 15 of 21 tasks, as many as an oracle that reads the true object position, while mask geometry reaches 10 and frozen feature distance 9. It still solves the most tasks when the unseen robot arm is visible, and its ranking transfers to held-out layouts with unseen object instances and categories without any adaptation.

This pipeline assumes that the distance between two im-age embeddings measures progress toward the goal. For manipulation, current world models have two drawbacks that put this assumption in doubt. (Drawback 1) Task-agnostic relevance: JEPA-style methods [3], such as V-JEPA [4, 5], learn by predicting latent visual representa-tions, aiming to retain meaningful information while sup-pressing irrelevant detail. However, what is relevant de-pends on the downstream task: object placement requires precise spatial localization of the object, yet the object cov-ers only a few image patches and is easily ignored next to the much larger background and robot arm. (Drawback 2) Action-free pretraining: Encoder pretraining is action-free and does not explicitly model how actions affect fu-ture states, which leaves open whether the learned content and appearance features provide sufficient spatial precision. Exposing and removing these drawbacks raises two chal-lenges. (Challenge 1) Diagnosing the representation in 

1arXiv:2611.23578v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 isolation: A planner can fail because its predictor is wrong or because its cost does not measure the task, and closed-loop success rates do not tell the two apart; the arm also moves with the object it holds, so a cost may follow the arm rather than the object. (Challenge 2) Metric supervision without annotation: Placement needs a representation that singles out one small object and measures how far and in which direction an action moves it, but current represen-tations largely ignore the object itself, and object-pose or depth labels are costly to collect. To address these challenges, we present a series of profil-ing experiments that evaluate the representation alone, and a self-supervised training framework that teaches the repre-sentation where an object is and how actions move it, using only the robot’s own measured motion. Our contributions are twofold, as summarized below. 

▷ A profiling evaluation of current representations (Sec. 3). In a placement benchmark with exactly sim-ulated futures, the goal-image feature distance of six frozen DINO and V-JEPA encoders is almost blind to where the manipulated object is (Fig. 1): its rank cor-relation with the true object-to-goal distance is at most 0.11, against 0.87 for mask centroids. It is dominated by background and robot patches, and frozen-feature read-outs capture the arm far better than the object. 

▷ DOCK, a self-supervised dataset and training frame-work (Sec. 4). The framework is inspired by the dual-stream of the primate visual system [11, 27]: the ven-tral pathway for content ( what ) and the dorsal pathway for location and action ( where/how ). An object-centric representation of pretrained features supplies the what ;a spatial ruler that reads out the object’s metric location adds the where , and dynamics prediction of the object’s motion under the robot’s actions adds the how (Fig. 3). The ruler and the predictor are trained without annotation on 9,951 held-object transport trajectories, using only the gripper motion that forward kinematics computes from joint angles. As a planning cost, the learned representa-tion places the object in 15 of 21 tasks, as many as an oracle that reads the true object position, against at most 10 for mask geometry and frozen feature distances. 

2. Related Work 

Visual representations for planning. Latent planners pre-dict future features of self-supervised encoders such as DINO [6, 31, 37] and V-JEPA [4, 5, 28] and minimize their distance to a goal image [39, 43]; others learn vi-sual rewards from value pretraining, temporal distance or demonstrations [15, 23, 24, 38]. We test whether fea-ture distance tracks physical progress and learn a met-ric cost instead. Spatial and object-centric representa-tions. Probes and 3D-aware fine-tuning study the geom-etry in pretrained features [7, 25, 42], and object-centric methods rely on slots, object dynamics, descriptors, key-points or masks [8, 9, 19, 21, 26, 35, 36, 41]. We train a mask-pooled object token for metric location readout and action-conditioned prediction. Self-supervision from mo-tion and interaction. Ego-motion [1, 18] and robot inter-action [2, 17, 32] supervise visual learning, and measured camera velocity [13] resolves the scale ambiguity of self-supervised depth [10, 44]. We use gripper displacement from forward kinematics as a metric proxy for a held ob-ject’s displacement.

3.2. Spatial attribution of the cost 

Experiment. We split each DINOv3 patch’s squared fea-ture difference among background, robot, and object ac-cording to the fraction of its pixels in each region, mea-sured by simulator segmentation masks. Pixel area is the fraction of image pixels occupied by a region; feature cost 

is its fraction of the summed patch cost. Score variation 

measures how the region’s cost changes with the total cost across candidates, using the signed covariance share defined in Appendix A.6. Background and robot positions account for 64.12% and 33.09% of cost magnitude, leaving 2.80% at object positions. The object’s covariance share is only 0.31%, so the object hardly changes the score (Fig. 2b). 

Finding 2. Most of the full-image cost accumulates at back-ground and robot token positions, motivating an object-focused representation. 

Design implication. We pool features over the manipulated ob-ject’s mask to form the token used by both the spatial readout and dynamics predictor (Sec. 4.2). 

3.3. Arm and object readouts 

Experiment. We freeze DINOv3 and fit ridge and MLP readouts on whole-image mean-pooled features, and an at-tentive readout on the full token grid. Reach and pick probes predict hand–object distance and object lift from candidate images. For placement, we apply the same position read-out separately to the candidate and goal images, then com-pute the Euclidean distance between their predicted 3D ob-ject positions. The MLP attains within-state correlations of 0.940 , 0.218 , and 0.010 on pure reach, pick, and place; ridge and attention yield 0.006 and 0.013 on place (Fig. 2c; protocols in Appendix A.3). The large reach–place gap sug-gests an arm-based proxy: the readout may estimate ap-proach from arm configuration without accurately localiz-ing the object that has to be placed. 

Finding 3. Strong reach decoding but weak placement decod-ing suggests that the readout captures arm configuration more readily than object position. 

Design implication. We train the visual representation on arm-free images while deriving displacement supervision from robot kinematics (Sec. 4.1). 

3.4. Candidate correlation across encoders 

Experiment. On the same candidate banks, we compare DINOv2-S/B, DINOv3-L, V-JEPA 2-L, and R3M [29] by the rank correlation between feature distance and the task-specific oracle cost, using grid, pooled, and available CLS features. No tested variant exceeds ρ = 0 .144 ; Fig. 2d shows the L2 comparisons. We also evaluate six frozen encoders on the separate 21-task placement benchmark used in Sec. 5, with 300 simulator-generated candidates per task. In arm-free images, full-image and object-pooled costs reach at most 0.111 and 0.346 , versus 0.868 for mask-centroid distance. On matched visible candidates, DINOv3 3object pooling remains far below the mask-centroid dis-tance ( 0.420 vs. 0.870 in rank correlation).

4. DOCK 

Inspired by shared early processing and the specialized ven-tral and dorsal pathways in human vision [11, 27], we learn an object representation that plays the role of the ventral pathway ( what ), with a spatial ruler ( where ) and an action-conditioned dynamics predictor ( how ) that play the role of the dorsal pathway. Held-object transport supplies the mo-tion supervision, and the learned ruler distance provides the metric planning cost at test time (Fig. 3). 

4.1. Dataset Collection 

Kinematic supervision. A person carrying an object can feel how far the hand has moved. A robot has the same signal: while it holds an object, its joint angles determine the gripper’s motion, which gives us displacement super-vision without annotation (Fig. 3a). For arm joint angles

Held-object transport. We collect 9,951 held-object transport trajectories in the 21 RoboCasa [30] scenes of the benchmark, yielding 3.09 million valid frames (Fig. 3a). Each trajectory starts from a saved post-pickup state and moves the arm while maintaining the grasp, with the robot base and camera fixed. Trajectories include random walks, persistent and directed transport, reverse paths, expert paths, rotations and surface sweeps , with additional runs expanding spatial and motion coverage (Appendix B). Each step records a 256 × 256 image, an object mask, joint an-gles and contact information. 5. Experiments 

5.1. Setup

5.2. Main results 

Planning. In arm-free images, the learned cost solves 15 of 21 tasks with both backbones, as many as the oracle (Tab. 2), at a mean final distance of 15.5 and 16.1 cm against the oracle’s 12.6 cm. Five of the six tasks our models fail also fail with the oracle, which points to the planner rather than the cost; the sixth has a hidden goal object, on which our cost abstains. Mask geometry solves at most 10 tasks, all of which our models also solve; frozen feature distance solves at most 9 and leaves the object 30 cm or more from the goal on average, and V-JEPA 2-AC with its own predic-tor solves only one of the 21 tasks. 

Ranking. The learned costs also order the initial can-didates best in arm-free images ( ρ = 0.915 and 0.894 ,against 0.868 for the mask centroid). The DINOv3 model halves the regret of the best baseline (12.1 against 22.6 mm for mask centroid + size), while the V-JEPA 2 model stays slightly above it (26.0 mm). Frozen feature distance stays far behind ( ρ ≤ 0.346 , regret of at least 127 mm). 

Mask pooling. Among the frozen-feature costs, pool-ing over the object mask, as our object token does (Eq. (3)), is the most effective use of the mask in arm-free images: it gives the highest correlation ( ρ = 0.346 and 0.256 ,against at most 0.045 ) and the best planning, whereas mask-weighted patch distances do not consistently improve on all patches; the other frozen encoders behave similarly (Tabs. 15 and 16). Pooling alone does not make the fea-ture metric, however: with the same pooling, our trained encoder and readout raise the correlation ρ from 0.346 to 

0.915 for DINOv3 in arm-free images. 

Robot-visible images. Our models never see the robot in training, yet with the arm rendered the DINOv3 model still solves 12 tasks at 20.7 cm and the V-JEPA 2 model 9, against 8 at 23.2 cm for the mask centroid. Decision-0 rank-ing is weak for every cost here, and the mask centroid even ranks the initial candidates slightly better than our models (ρ = 0 .452 against 0.435 and 0.395 ); yet over six decisions the DINOv3 model solves four more tasks. Frozen all-patch distance instead correlates better when the arm is visible ( ρ

from 0.045 to 0.200 for DINOv3), consistent with a cost dominated by robot and background patches (Sec. 3.2).

Co-trained versus fresh readout. Table 4 also com-pares the co-trained readout with a fresh one fitted later with the encoder fixed, which tests whether the metric structure lies in the token or only in its jointly trained head. The co-trained readout is the better cost: in every configuration it has lower test error and arm-free regret and solves at least as many tasks. The gap depends on the encoder: on an adapted encoder a fresh readout solves 15 (DINOv3) and 13 (V-JEPA 2) arm-free tasks against 15 with the co-trained one, while on a frozen encoder it loses four and three tasks. This suggests that adaptation places the metric structure in the token itself, not in a particular readout. 

5.4. Generalization to held-out instances and cate-gories benchmark 

We test transfer without any adaptation, readout fitting or calibration on two benchmarks whose target assets and kitchen layouts do not occur in the original 21 scenes: ten 

instance-held-out tasks, five of whose target categories still occur there, and nine category-held-out tasks whose target categories are new as well. 

Unseen instances and layouts. The learned costs keep ranking candidates well in new kitchens (Tab. 5): in AF, 

ρ = 0.697 –0.843 against at most 0.291 for frozen fea-ture distance, at about half the regret (48–55 against 91– 178 mm; Tab. 21). Planning gains are smaller and depend on the model: the best learned cost (V-JEPA 2, fresh read-out) solves 5–6 of ten tasks against four for the best frozen baseline (pooled DINOv3), while the co-trained V-JEPA 2 readout solves only 2–3. 

Unseen categories and layouts. The advantage holds for unseen categories: ρ = 0 .635 –0.740 in AF against at most 0.482 , and DINOv3 with a fresh readout solves six of nine tasks, twice as many as pooled frozen DINOv3. Nei-ther readout is consistently better on held-out tasks, and RV remains hard in both benchmarks: the learned costs solve 1–3 tasks against 0–2 for frozen features, so transfer does not remove the sensitivity to the visible robot. 

6. Conclusion 

Our profiling shows that feature distance, the cost of latent-space planners, fails for object placement: for six frozen en-8coders it is dominated by background and robot patches and barely tracks the object’s distance to its goal. Inspired by the ventral and dorsal visual streams, DOCK instead learns an object token ( what ), a spatial ruler ( where ) and a dynamics predictor ( how ) from held-object transport, supervised only by forward kinematics. With simulated futures, its metric cost solves 15 of 21 placement tasks, matching an oracle with the true object position, and still solves the most tasks with the unseen arm visible; its ranking transfers to unseen layouts, instances and categories. 9LLM Usage Statement
