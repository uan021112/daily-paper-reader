# Abstract 

Predictive world models for language-guided robot con-trol face a fundamental conditioning challenge: instruc-tions should influence action selection without altering the predicted consequences of fixed actions under identi-cal observations. To address this challenge, we introduce JEPA Dots (Dynamics-Oriented Task Separation), a com-pact dual-latent framework that jointly learns visual dy-namics and language-guided control through separate dy-namics and task representations. Language-conditioned task features guide a flow-matching policy, while masked at-tention restricts world prediction to instruction-independent dynamics history and supplied actions. In closed-loop eval-uation on 40 trained LIBERO tasks, JEPA Dots achieves 84.9% overall success with approximately 39.1M param-eters, compared with the published 78.7% of NanoVLA-S with 161.0M parameters under a different evaluation proto-col. In the reference experiment, JEPA Dots also improves overall success from 76.0% for our task-ID-conditioned adaptation baseline to 84.9%, with LIBERO-Goal success increasing from 67.1% to 88.5%. Instruction-switching ex-periments further confirm that task representations and pol-icy outputs respond to language changes, while dynamics representations and world predictions remain invariant un-der fixed observations and supplied actions. 

# 1. Introduction 

Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) [1, 7] have emerged as a promising approach to visual represen-tation learning by predicting future or missing informa-tion in latent space. Their ability to learn compact vi-sual representations and predictive dynamics offers con-siderable potential for downstream prediction and plan-ning [2, 26, 38, 41]. Recent studies have further ex-tended JEPA-based learning to language-guided robot con-trol, including vision–language–action modeling [19, 32],

stage-level prediction [23], and compact world–action poli-cies [11]. However, integrating language conditioning into JEPA-based world models poses a fundamental challenge: task in-structions should influence action selection without altering the predicted consequences of fixed actions under identi-cal observations. When language conditioning is applied to shared visual representations, task-dependent information may also influence dynamics prediction, making it difficult to maintain this separation. Recent advances in JEPA-based world modeling have explored subspace regularization [39], temporal center-ing [22], structured representations [17], flow-based dy-namics [18], and hierarchical or amortized planning [5, 16, 38]. LeWorldModel (LeWM) [25] jointly learns visual rep-resentations and action-conditioned dynamics directly from pixels, while LeWAM [13] extends this formulation to joint world–action modeling through masked attention. Build-ing on LeWM, TC-LeWM [22] applies Gaussian regular-ization to temporally centered residuals to reduce the dom-inance of persistent scene information and better preserve dynamics-relevant variations. Despite these advances in predictive representation learning and world–action model-ing, explicitly separating language-conditioned task infor-mation from instruction-independent dynamics prediction remains an important architectural consideration. We propose JEPA Dots (Dynamics-Oriented Task Sep-aration), a compact framework that explicitly separates language-conditioned task representations from instruction-independent dynamics representations within a jointly trained world–action model (Fig. 1). Building on LeWM’s end-to-end predictive learning [25] and LeWAM’s masked world–action modeling [13], JEPA Dots employs a shared ResNet-18 backbone [15] with two distinct latent pathways. The dynamics pathway extracts visual representations with-out language conditioning, while the task pathway applies language-conditioned residual FiLM [28] to produce task-specific representations. Ad-ditional matched ablations examine the effects of language-conditioned feature modulation and subspace Gaussian reg-ularization on control performance and latent prediction. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: • We design a dual-representation interface for joint world– action modeling, providing distinct conditioning paths for instruction-dependent action generation and dynam-ics prediction without direct instruction input. • We integrate this interface with JEPA future-latent predic-tion, a flow-matching policy, and subspace Gaussian reg-ularization, using attention masks to enforce prediction invariance for fixed observations and supplied actions. • We analyze closed-loop control, instruction switching, and component ablations on 40 trained LIBERO tasks, re-porting variation across training runs and task-dependent FiLM effects. 

# 2. Related work 

Joint world–action learning and language conditioning. 

Recent JEPA-based approaches have integrated predictive learning with robot control. LeWAM [13] combines la-tent world prediction and flow-matching action generation 2through masked attention. VLA-JEPA [32] incorporates la-tent prediction into vision–language–action learning, while JEPA-WAM variants explore stage-level prediction [23] and structured transition modeling [19]. ThinkJEPA [37] incor-porates vision–language reasoning features into predictive learning, and La-JEPA [11] develops compact world–action policies using pretrained V-JEPA representations. Despite these advances, the separation of language-conditioned task information from action-conditioned dynamics prediction remains insufficiently explored in jointly learned world– action models. 

Predictive representations and dynamics regularization. 

I-JEPA [1] and V-JEPA [7] establish latent predictive learn-ing for images and videos, while V-JEPA 2 and 2.1 [2, 26] extend pretrained representations toward downstream appli-cations. DINO-WM [41] models dynamics over pretrained visual features [27], whereas LeWorldModel (LeWM) [25] jointly learns visual representations and action-conditioned dynamics directly from pixels. Representation regulariza-tion has been explored through VICReg [6], LeJEPA [3], Sub-JEPA [39], and temporally centered SIGReg in TC-LeWM [22]. Other studies investigate dynamics–context separation [17], flow-based dynamics [18], and hierarchical or amortized planning [5, 16, 38]. However, these meth-ods primarily focus on representation quality and predictive modeling, leaving the distinct conditioning requirements of task-dependent control and world prediction less explored. 

Language-conditioned policies and compact control. 

FiLM [28] enables language-conditioned feature modu-lation, while BAKU [14] incorporates task-conditioned visual representations into multitask policies. MiniLM and Sentence-BERT provide compact language embed-dings [29, 30, 34]. Action generation has been stud-ied through action chunking [40], diffusion policies [10], and flow matching [20]. Recent methods, including NanoVLA [9], SmolVLA [31], VLA-Adapter [35], and SimVLA [24], further explore compact or efficient robot policies. While these approaches advance language-conditioned action generation and model efficiency, they do not directly establish how task conditioning should be sep-arated from dynamics prediction in a shared world–action architecture. 

# 3. Dynamics-Oriented Task Separation 

JEPA Dots jointly learns visual dynamics prediction and language-guided action generation while explicitly separat-ing their conditioning pathways. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the framework employs a shared visual encoder with two distinct latent representations to accommodate the different requirements of world prediction and task-dependent con-trol. Language information is incorporated into task rep-resentation learning and policy conditioning, whereas dy-namics prediction depends only on visual history and sup-plied actions. This design enables task-specific action gen-eration while maintaining instruction-invariant world pre-diction under fixed observations and actions. The shared visual backbone is jointly optimized through predictive and action objectives.

## 3.2. Masked world–action modeling 

Given the dynamics context Ht and language-conditioned goal tokens Gt defined in Sec. 3.1, we integrate world pre-diction and action generation through a masked mixture-of-transformers (MoT), following LeWAM [13]. The MoT consists of four layers with four attention heads and a hid-den dimension of 192, employing modality-specific pro-cessing and attention masks [33] to control information flow between the two branches. Although both branches oper-ate within a jointly trained architecture, they receive distinct conditioning inputs.

## 3.4. Compact closed-loop control 

During inference, JEPA Dots takes multi-view visual ob-servations, proprioceptive states, and language instructions as inputs. The shared visual encoder produces dynamics and task representations, which are combined with language embeddings to condition the flow-matching policy. At each control step, the policy generates an eight-action chunk us-ing eight ODE integration steps and executes the first two actions before acquiring new observations. This receding-horizon procedure enables closed-loop control without re-lying on world-model planning, as the prediction branch is used only for training supervision. The visual and control network contains 16.4M param-eters, including 197,632 parameters introduced by residual FiLM. With the frozen sentence encoder contributing ap-proximately 22.7M parameters, the complete model con-tains approximately 39.1M parameters, providing a com-pact architecture for jointly learned visual dynamics and language-guided control. 

# 4. Experiments and results 

## 4.1. Experimental setup 

Tasks and datasets. We evaluate JEPA Dots on 40 tasks from four LIBERO suites [21]: Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10, with ten tasks per suite. Language instruc-tions are obtained from the demonstrations, and numeric task identifiers are excluded from language-conditioned in-puts. We reserve 10.0% of demonstrations for validation at the trajectory level. 

Training configuration. The model is trained for 60 epochs using AdamW with a learning rate of 1.0 × 10 −4,an effective batch size of 64, and approximately 15,000 optimizer updates. Training jointly optimizes action flow matching, latent world prediction, and subspace Gaussian regularization. 

Baselines and ablations. We compare JEPA Dots with frozen V-JEPA 2.1-B/L encoders coupled with flow match-ing, as well as published NanoVLA-S [9] and SmolVLA-240M [31] results. The V-JEPA baselines share the action-flow architecture and update budget with JEPA Dots but differ in encoder training, prediction objectives, and pre-processing. We additionally evaluate a task-ID-conditioned reference and matched ablations of the task-visual branch, residual FiLM, and subspace regularization. Published VLA results retain their original evaluation protocols. 

Evaluation protocol. Closed-loop evaluation uses 50 of-ficial initial states per task under two simulation random streams, totaling 4,000 episodes per model in the refer-ence experiment. Each episode is limited to 600 environ-ment steps. The policy predicts eight-step action chunks and executes the first two actions before replanning. To as-sess training variability, we additionally report three inde-pendent training runs for the main ablations, with 12,000 evaluation episodes per variant. The reference result and repeated-run statistics are reported separately. Further im-plementation details, baseline configurations, and evalua-tion protocols are provided in the supplementary material. Differences and relative changes are computed from un-rounded records; displayed measurements are rounded to one decimal.

## 4.5. Effect of subspace regularization 

We next investigate the role of subspace Gaussian regular-ization in learning dynamics representations. As shown in Tab. 4, replacing full-dimensional regularization with eight-subspace SIGReg improves the three-run average success rate from 80.7% to 81.9% within the FiLM architecture. Meanwhile, the mean validation world-prediction MSE de-creases from 60 .0 × 10 −4 to 52 .6 × 10 −4, corresponding to a relative reduction of 12.3%. These results suggest that subspace regularization can improve latent prediction quality while benefiting down-stream control. By applying Gaussian regularization across multiple low-dimensional projections, the method provides additional constraints on the learned dynamics representa-tions without increasing inference-time computation. Al-though latent prediction error does not directly measure physical-state accuracy, the consistent MSE reductions across three runs support the usefulness of subspace reg-ularization for jointly learned world–action representations. 

## 4.6. Verification of task–dynamics separation 

Finally, we examine whether the proposed dual-latent ar-chitecture achieves its intended separation between task-dependent control and instruction-independent dynamics prediction. We switch language instructions while keep-ing the observations, proprioceptive states, supplied ac-tions, model parameters, and action-sampling randomness fixed. The resulting dynamics latents and world predic-tions exhibit zero maximum absolute change, confirming the instruction-invariant prediction behavior enforced by the masked attention mechanism. In contrast, the task representations and generated ac-7tions respond to instruction changes. With FiLM, task-latent L2 differences range from 3.2 to 6.7, while action-chunk differences range from 1.2 to 4.9. In the same-scene goal-switching evaluation, JEPA Dots successfully executes both requested goals in 50 out of 60 scenes. These results verify that the two latent pathways fulfill their distinct con-ditioning roles: dynamics representations support action-conditioned world prediction, while task representations en-able language-dependent action generation. This separation provides a structured interface for integrating JEPA-based predictive learning with language-guided robotic control. 

# 5. Discussion and outlook 

What the conditioning separation enforces. DOTS gives the two branches different conditioning inputs while training their visual backbone jointly. The dy-namics path predicts from history and supplied ac-tions; the task path can change with language. By separating instruction-independent dynamics prediction from language-conditioned task representations, this inter-face connects JEPA-based predictive learning with flow-matching action generation. The reference experiment and task-level analysis show useful control behavior with this interface. Instruction-switching experiments further confirm unchanged world predictions under fixed observa-tions and supplied actions, while policy outputs respond to language instructions. The repeated-run ablations lo-cate FiLM’s benefits more precisely: its effect depends on the task, and understanding that dependence is an impor-tant next step. Together with the compact-model compar-isons, these findings suggest that the organization of predic-tive and task-relevant latent information matters for efficient control. 

Self-improvement through policy adaptation. Beyond the current imitation-learning setting, the interface also sug-gests a way to adapt from experience: execute the policy, verify the outcome, combine successful trajectories with demonstrations, and update the task and action adapters. RoboCat and offline-to-online learning use collected expe-rience for later policy updates, while RISE studies improve-ment through model-generated rollouts [4, 8, 36]. An exploratory two-task pilot collected 40 rollouts, of which 34 succeeded. After 200 adapter-update steps with the dynamics path frozen, the mixture of successful ex-perience and demonstrations achieved 53/60 successes on held-out rollouts. The initial policy achieved 44/60, and an expert-data control with the same update budget achieved 49/60. World MSE remained 51 .0 × 10 −4 under this proto-col. These results motivate a larger study of selective adap-tation over repeated interaction cycles. Keeping the predictor fixed during adaptation requires freezing its entire computation path, including the shared visual encoder. Separating instruction inputs by itself does not isolate parameter updates. Outcome-verification ac-curacy and held-out evaluation will be central to assess-ing whether gains accumulate over successive cycles. The supplement describes the pilot separately from the 40-task benchmark. 

Evaluation coverage and extensions. The experiments cover 40 trained tasks with their canonical instructions. Evaluation on new instructions, object configurations, and real robots would establish how the learned interface trans-fers beyond this setting. Generalization to these settings re-mains untested. A matched policy-only control would help isolate the contribution of predictive supervision. Physical-state probes could test which scene variables are retained by the dynamics latent. Deployment currently samples actions directly; using predictions to evaluate candidate actions is a further planning question. Common-hardware latency mea-surements would add a practical resource measure to the parameter counts reported here. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We presented JEPA Dots, a compact dual-latent world– action model for language-guided robot control. It com-bines LeWM-style end-to-end predictive learning with masked world–action coupling drawn from LeWAM. Lan-guage conditions task perception and action generation, while dynamics prediction reads visual history and sup-plied actions. The control network has 16.4M parameters, or approximately 39.1M including the frozen sentence en-coder, and achieves 84.9% LIBERO success in the refer-ence experiment, with Goal success increasing from 67.1% to 88.5%. Repeated-run analyses show task-specific FiLM gains and lower latent-prediction MSE with subspace regu-larization. Together with the instruction-switching analysis, these results support structured task–dynamics separation for compact control and offer a concrete setting for study-ing selective policy adaptation.

# S5. Transfer, ranking, and adaptation studies 

The following studies use different initializations, trainable components, or inference procedures. Their results are reported separately from the end-to-end DOTS benchmark. 

Frozen transfer pilot. A previous task-ID model, evaluated on Object and Goal with its ID input removed, succeeds in 584/2,000 episodes (29.2%). Adding language goal tokens gives 1,268/2,000 (63.4%); adding both a task head and FiLM gives 1,714/2,000 (85.7%). The latter comparison changes both components. The frozen world path retains MSE 87 .4×10 −4.The initialization has already seen all 40 tasks. 

Separate LeWM+MoT ranking study. This study trains a ViT-based LeWM first, freezes it, and then trains the policy. Language queries retrieve goal images from training demonstrations. Four inference modes are each evaluated over 2,400 episodes across three runs. Direct flow sampling and random ranking each give 1,409 successes (58.7%). An action-shuffle control gives 1,414 (58.9%), and selecting among eight candidates by terminal predicted latent goal distance gives 1,211 (50.5%). The lower success of distance-based ranking shows the importance of evaluating a latent goal metric in closed-loop control. 

Small adaptation pilot. The two-task pilot collects 40 rollouts and retains 34 successful trajectories. Task and action adapters are updated for 200 steps with the dynamics path frozen. Each condition is evaluated on 60 held-out rollouts: the initial policy succeeds 44 times, the expert-data control with the same update budget 49 times, and the mixture of successful experience and demonstrations 53 times. ## S6.2. Adapters and action-only flow policy 

All adapters and flow-policy parameters are initialized from scratch; no trained DOTS policy weights are transferred. FiLM, action AdaLN, and the final proprioception projection use zero initialization. The history adapter mean-pools patch tokens, projects the encoder width to 192D, and adds a projected 15D proprioceptive state. The task adapter projects patch features to 512 channels, applies language FiLM, and uses a 32-keypoint spatial softmax followed by a 64 → 384 → 192 MLP. Each latest per-view task latent is concatenated with the projected 192D language embedding to form a 384D goal before token embedding. The policy separately implements the action-reachable MoT subgraph, retaining the history/goal state-stream projections. It has four transformer blocks, width 192, four heads, feed-forward width 768, and dropout 0.1. The sequence contains four history tokens, two goal tokens, and eight noisy-action tokens, each embedded to 192D. Actions attend to history, goals, and current or preceding noisy actions. A sinusoidal flow-time embedding and AdaLN-Zero condition the action tokens. World-state queries and clean-action slots are omitted because the action branch cannot attend to them under the mask. The sole objective is rectified-flow velocity MSE. For standardized expert actions A, draw independent ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ) and 

s ∼ U (0 , 1) , use (1 − s)ϵ + sA as input, and regress A − ϵ. Padded positions are masked. Neither world-prediction loss nor SIGReg is applied. Each policy outputs eight 7D actions. 16 S6.3. Demonstrations, preprocessing, and training
