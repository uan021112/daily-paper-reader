# Abstract 

VGGT reconstructs cameras and geometry from unposed images in one pass, but its global attention grows quadratically with the number of views. Training-free accelerators thin this attention and work well on redun-dant video, but lose pose accuracy when views share little geometry. This paper traces these losses to two kinds of cross-view information that a uniform sparse approximation can fail to preserve together: di ff use layers aggregate distributed context, whereas matching layers depend on query-specific interactions that carry little attention mass. A per-layer statistic measures the log-mass missed by a mean token. Its row-free proxy is the logit variance, and it selects the estimator for each layer. Di ff use layers use shared, importance-sampled hub keys, with log-weights that make the softmax numerator and denominator unbiased. Matching layers stay exact: every aggressive thinning tested cost accuracy. At equal cost, the selected layers outperform the four shifted windows tested. On 13 unseen low-redundancy scenes with 32 views, LMSA stays within 1.4 AUC@5 of dense attention, compared with 10.3 for AVGGT and 4.5 for SAF3R; its di ff erence from contemporaneous GPA-VGGT is not significant, and only its gains over AVGGT pass both scene-level tests. With 100 views, it stays within 1.9 AUC@5 while its aggregator runs 2.0 × faster. Used post hoc to choose the layers GPA-VGGT protects, the statistic improves it by 1.2 AUC@5 for 5% more FLOPs. Applied untuned to π3, it beats AVGGT but remains further from dense accuracy. 

# 1 Introduction 

Feed-forward reconstruction transformers [26, 44–46, 48, 52] predict cameras, depth and point maps for a set of unposed images in one forward pass. VGGT [44] alternates attention within each frame with global attention over the tokens of all 

N views, and the global part grows as N2. A fast-growing family of training-free accelerators therefore thins global atten-tion [12, 30, 36, 40, 41, 43, 47]. Many rely on the premise that cross-view information is redundant, so that most cross-view keys can be dropped, merged or summarised with little e ff ect on geometry. The premise often holds for overlapping video but breaks when views are few and far apart, a setting this pa-per calls low-redundancy and measures by ρ, the mean number of views that see a point. The resulting failure is severe: on ETH3D courtyard ( ρ ≈ 7.7), AVGGT [40], keeping one key per 3 ×3 patch cell ( σ=9), lowers the pose AUC@5 of a frozen VGGT-1B from 58.3 to 8.1 and its AUC@30 from 90.9 to 34.8, averaged over five frame subsets. AVGGT [40] observed that VGGT’s early global layers form no cross-view correspondences, its middle layers align the views and its late layers only refine. Thinning one group at a time (Table 1) shows that these roles require di ff erent cross-view information. The middle layers give the clearest example. Across three low-redundancy development scenes, removing the model’s small correspondence-key sets from layers 10–16 (on TUM fr1_room, 30 of 33,312 keys per query) costs 17.7– 49.2 AUC@5. Removing as many of the heaviest other keys costs 6.0–23.0, even though they carry 2.3–3.4 × more attention mass; removing random keys costs nothing. The keys with the highest query-specific lift cost the most, although only 13% lie in correspondence windows (Fig. 2c). A key can therefore carry little attention mass yet be critical to geometric reconstruction. The other layers fail in di ff erent ways. Making early layers 0–8 frame-local, as AVGGT does, costs 33.1 AUC@5 on courtyard because these layers carry distributed cross-view context. Lay-ers 9 and 17–23 are di ff use again, but thinning them accounts for 14.8 of the 22.1 points AVGGT loses on the redundant TUM fr3_long_o ffi ce scene: its mean token under-counts the dropped mass (Section 3.4). Attention approximation is therefore an estimation problem with two regimes. A di ff use layer needs an accurate estimate of a sum over many comparable terms, whereas a matching layer needs query-specific terms that a small shared sample does not contain. One per-layer statistic separates them: the log-mass Γ

that a mean token misses from a query’s softmax denominator is about half the logit variance s2 when the logits spread little (Prop. 1), and s2 needs only the key covariance, not attention rows. A cut-o ff of 1.5 nats ( s2 ≈ 3), chosen on the develop-ment scenes and fixed before any test run, labels layers 11–16 as matching; that these layers need exact attention, not just a better sample, is an empirical finding that Section 4.3 tests. LMSA (layer-matched sparse attention, Fig. 1) gives each regime its own estimator. Di ff use layers attend to their own frame, the special tokens and a few hundred shared hub keys, importance-sampled per input from the attention of 512 pilot queries, with log-weights that make the softmax numerator and denominator unbiased. Their per-query cost hardly grows with 

N, and they remove both the early failure and the late one, which is consistent with the mean token’s under-weighting of the dropped keys. On 13 unseen low-redundancy scenes at N=32, named before any test run, LMSA stays within 1.4 AUC@5 of dense atten-tion (95% CI [0.7, 2.0]), the lowest mean loss of the training-free methods tested, against 10.3 to 18.9 for AVGGT ( σ=4, 9), which is 7–18% faster, and 4.5 for SAF3R; with 100 views it stays within 1.9 AUC@5 while its aggregator runs 2.0 × faster. At equal cost, the statistic’s six exact layers beat the four shifted windows of six tested (Section 4.3). The contemporaneous GPA-VGGT, as fast, loses 2.4, so the margin of LMSA (1.0, CI [−0.3, 2.5]) is not significant, and on 8 unseen redundant scenes GPA-VGGT loses 0.1 against 0.7 (Section 4.2); protecting the statistic’s layers 11–16 instead of 13–16 improves GPA-VGGT by 1.2 AUC@5 (CI [0.4, 2.1], post hoc). The paper makes three contributions: 1. An interpretable criterion for choosing the atten-tion estimator per layer: controlled interventions show that reconstruction needs both distributed con-text and query-specific interactions that carry little at-tention mass, and the mean-token log-mass deficit, with a row-free logit-variance proxy, separates the layers that need each; simpler concentration statistics agree (Sections 3.2 and 3.3). 2. LMSA: probability-corrected shared hubs in di ff use layers and exact attention in matching layers, with no training and no known poses (Sections 3.4 and 3.5). 3. Validation: on 21 pre-named test scenes LMSA has the lowest mean loss of the training-free methods tested; at equal cost its exact layers beat the four shifted windows tested, the statistic also improves GPA-VGGT on the same backbone (post hoc), and a registered test on NRGBD, Sintel and long video con-firms a variant with more hubs (Section 4). 

# 2 Related Work 

Feed-forward 3D reconstruction. DUSt3R [46] and MASt3R [26] regress point maps from image pairs; VGGT [44], π3 [48] and Fast3R [52] process many views jointly with global attention, and CUT3R [45] with a persistent state. Bratuli´ c et al. [5] show that correspondence and epipolar structure emerge in the intermediate-layer attention of VGGT and related models, the behaviour that the matching layers of Section 3.5 rely on. LMSA keeps these models frozen and changes only which keys their global attention reads. 

Accelerating global attention in 3D transformers. 

FastVGGT [36], HTTM [47], Co-Me [9] and, with fine-tuning, LiteVGGT [38] merge tokens; QuantVGGT [16] quantizes the model, which is complementary. AVGGT [40] analyses VGGT’s global attention layer by layer and finds that early global layers form no cross-view correspondences, middle layers align the views and late layers only refine; it Preprint – L ayer -M atched Sparse Attention for Low -R edundancy 3D R econstruction 3makes the global layers below a hand-set boundary frame-local and, in later layers, keeps one key per σ-cell, the reference frame and a count-biased mean token. LMSA does not claim the role split, which AVGGT and RegimeVGGT establish; it asks which estimator each layer’s information needs, choosing between a shared sample and exact attention by a per-layer statistic, and replaces the frame-local early layers, which fail on low-redundancy input (Table 1), and the biased mean token by probability-corrected shared hubs. Block-sparse attention [43] keeps key blocks chosen by pooled scores, and HeSS [23] redistributes its budget among the heads of each layer by a gradient-based sensitivity score; RegimeVGGT [30] analyses VGGT’s layer regimes and merges tokens and subsamples keys and values on a layer-wise schedule, choosing how much to compress rather than which estimator to use, and Good Token Hunting [54] selects frames and prunes tokens per layer. Other methods sparsify whole frames [27], as does the contemporaneous BlazeVGGT [53], partition the views into chunks [33], or train and fine-tune [20, 34, 49]. The contem-poraneous GPA-VGGT [41] adds to the early frame attention a shared cross-frame set of per-head cluster summaries with a 

+ log(size) bias, as in the proportional attention of ToMe [4], and merges keys and values later; its merging operator is used in the merged variants of Section 4.3. The hubs of LMSA are an importance sample with probability-correcting log-weights rather than summaries, and the same estimator serves the late layers, where it removes an estimation bias (Section 3.4). SAF3R [12] assigns each head one of several sparse patterns from o ffl ine calibration and, for correspondence heads, keeps the top-K keys per frame by feature similarity. 

Sparse, sampled and summarised attention. HyperAtten-tion [18], Scatterbrain [7] and Support Basis [2] (beyond the bounded-entry regime of [3]) keep large entries exactly and es-timate the rest by uniform samples, low-rank factors or polyno-mials, MagicPIG [10] estimates attention by hashed sampling, and vAttention [14] adds a uniform sample of the remaining keys to exact top-k keys; Skeinformer [8] samples key columns from a pilot, the mechanism closest to the hubs of LMSA. FMMformer [32], Fast Multipole Attention [22], Multipole Se-mantic Attention [31] and Multipole Attention [19] treat distant or dissimilar keys coarsely (low-rank, hierarchical or by clus-ter centroids), the last also picking keys for exact attention by centroid. In LLMs and video models the estimator is chosen per head, by retrieval heads [42, 51], o ffl ine search or a diver-gence statistic [21, 25], sampled queries [56] or online profil-ing [50], and Kascade [15] computes exact top-k keys only in anchor layers chosen on a development set. LMSA makes such a choice per layer from a closed-form statistic calibrated o ffl ine on development scenes, without gradients or a search over layer windows, and pairs it with a probability-corrected estimator. 

Statistical physics and hubness. The random-energy model [13] has a freezing transition at which a partition function becomes dominated by its extreme terms; it has been mapped onto attention at initialisation [17] and onto retrieval in dense associative memories [29], and LMSA uses it at inference to motivate a choice of estimator. Hubs that attract many queries are a known failure of similarity retrieval, corrected in text–image matching by the inverted softmax [28];

3.5 Matching layers stay exact 

A matching layer keeps dense attention, K(t) = U and W(t) =

∅ in Eq. (2), at a price of N units per layer. Section 4.3 shows why: the truncated key sets tested there fail once the di ff use layers are also sparse. 

Query-specific relevance, not popularity. Writing attention as A(t, j) = π j L(t, j) separates the column mass π j of key j,its popularity and the quantity that hubs sample, from the lift 

L(t, j), its specificity to the query. Removing in layers 10–16, per query, as many highest-lift keys as the query has corre-spondence keys, which hold 1–14% of the attention mass per layer, drives AUC@5 to 0.05 / 0.13 / 0.59 on the three low-redundancy development scenes, although only 13% of these keys lie in correspondence windows; removing a comparable 7–11% of mass chosen by attention weight costs far less (Sec-tion 1). A shared sample of popular keys is therefore unlikely to contain what these layers need. 

Truncation amplifies whatever it keeps. A sparse matching layer is in e ff ect truncated attention: the smallest layer statis-tic here is ¯ Γℓ≈1.8, and a query with that deficit would give its mean token only e−1.8 ≈ 17% of the mass it stands for (an illus-tration, not a bound on individual queries), so Eq. (7) amplifies the kept keys by an estimated 2.0–3.8 × for a σ=9 lattice. With the right keys this sharpening helps: a σ=4 lattice with oracle correspondence keys matches dense accuracy as the only sparse group (loss −1.38, CI [ −3.81 , 0.29]). But it applies equally to every error in the kept keys, including errors that sparse up-stream layers pass in to the few query-specific, low-mass keys these layers depend on (App. D.2). # 4 Experiments 

The experiments ask whether the design holds on unseen scenes (Section 4.2), why the matching layers must stay exact (Sec-tion 4.3), what it costs and how far the selector transfers (Sec-tions 4.4 and 4.5). 

4.1 Protocol 

Model and inputs. All runs use the released VGGT-1B [44] with unchanged weights, the aggregator under bf16 autocast and the camera and depth heads in fp32. Each scene contributes 

N=32 views at 518 px, or n−4 of its n views if it has fewer (e.g ., 22 of 26 for ETH3D o ffi ce), and a seed is one frame sub-set (App. B.1); every number is a mean over 5 seeds unless marked. Ground-truth cameras only build and order the sub-sets, identically for every method, and never enter inference; in a random order the loss of LMSA changes by +0.08 AUC@5 (CI [ −1.05 , 1.21], App. B.1). 

Redundancy and scenes. The redundancy ρ of a configura-tion is one plus the mean number of other views in which a patch’s point is visible, with visibility taken from the dense model’s own depth and cameras because Tanks and Temples has no ground-truth depth; a configuration is low-redundancy if ρ < 10 .5. The 13 unseen low-ρ test scenes come from ETH3D [35] (8), Tanks and Temples [24] (4) and TUM [39] (1), and the 8 unseen high-ρ test scenes from TUM, 7-Scenes [37], DTU [1] and Tanks and Temples (lists in App. B.2). The 5 de-velopment scenes are TUM fr1_room, ETH3D courtyard and offi ce (low ρ), and TUM fr3_long_o ffi ce and 7-Scenes o ffi ce (high ρ). 

Development protocol. Every design choice in Section 3 was made on the development scenes, with acceptance thresholds fixed before the corresponding runs (App. B.4). Registered 

tests had their plan and decision rules hashed before any run (App. B.4). The 21 test scenes were named before any test run, and the frozen recipe was evaluated on them once, for Table 2; later analyses on them are marked post hoc; development re-sults, including the variants that failed, are reported separately (App. D.3, D.1). Three high-ρ test scenes (7-Scenes chess, DTU 24, 114) appear in the attention audit of Fig. 2a but in no end-to-end decision. 

Metrics and statistics. AUC@ k averages, over thresholds 1.. k degrees, the fraction of frame pairs whose larger rotation or translation-direction error is below the threshold. AbsRel is the median over frames of the per-frame depth AbsRel after median scaling; Tanks and Temples has COLMAP poses and no depth. A loss is dense minus method, paired by seed; 95% intervals come from a hierarchical bootstrap over scenes, then seeds (10,000 resamples). 

Baselines and implementation. AVGGT [40] runs at σ ∈{4, 9} in a re-implementation, since no o ffi cial code is released, and need not reach its published accuracy (App. B.3); the contemporaneous GPA-VGGT [41] runs at its paper defaults, SAF3R [12] at its repository default (lazy top-4, shipped head assignment) and FastVGGT [36] at its default (90% token merging, aimed at ∼10 3 views). All methods share checkpoint, precision, frame subsets and software environment (App. B.3), and each baseline but FastVGGT reproduces the dense pre-dictions bit for bit with sparsity disabled (FastVGGT within 0.2 AUC@5). SAF3R’s head assignment was calibrated on 11 ETH3D training scenes that include courtyard, o ffi ce and seven of the eight unseen low-ρ ETH3D scenes, which favours it on ETH3D. Development runs use a masked reference implemen-tation of LMSA and the rows of Table 2 a fused one, whose pooled losses do not di ff er significantly (App. A.2).

4.5 Transfer to a second architecture 

A dense pilot pass of the original π3 [48] on the development scenes labelled its 18 global layers with the VGGT cut-o ff s be-fore any sparse run: layers 11–15 and, only just ( ¯ Γ = 1.54), layer 7 are matching. Layer 7, which the row-free proxy does not select, loses nothing with hubs, so a statistic just above the cut-o ff is not su ffi cient. 

# 5 Limitations and Conclusion 

Effi cient attention for 3D reconstruction must preserve each layer’s information, not merely attention mass. Probability-corrected hubs preserve di ff use context; no tested aggressive thinning preserved the few high-deficit matching layers’ query-specific interactions. LMSA is not significantly more accurate than contemporane-ous GPA-VGGT. At equal FLOPs, its low-redundancy margin (1.0 AUC@5) misses the pre-registered +5; GPA-VGGT is bet-Preprint – L ayer -M atched Sparse Attention for Low -R edundancy 3D R econstruction 8ter on redundant views. LMSA is about as fast as GPA-VGGT and takes 13–18% longer than AVGGT(4) at N=64–100; its six exact layers bound its FLOP advantage over dense attention by 4 × (App. D.6). More hubs reduce run-to-run hub-sampling noise; π3 transfer is preliminary.

FastVGGT. FastVGGT [36] is run with its repository de-faults (o ffi cial code, commit 6526e27) and no tuning. It is designed for about 10 3 views, and its authors evaluate it at that scale, so its rows here, at N=32 and 100, need not reflect its best case. 

Not run. Other training-free accelerators were not run in the time available, among them RegimeVGGT [30], whose code is public. Their published speed-ups and accuracies use other checkpoints, hardware, sequence lengths and timing conven-tions, so they cannot be compared with Table S10 or Table 2.

So, as at stride 3, GPA-VGGT is slightly more accurate than LMSA on this redundant video, at a similar time. C.6 Outdoor scenes: RealEstate10K 

This test asks whether LMSA keeps its accuracy on outdoor scenes, a domain that none of the main-table datasets covers well. It is post hoc relative to the main table, but its scene list, frame rule, methods and predictions were registered before it ran, and it is reported in full. 

Data and scene selection. RealEstate10K [55] consists of real-estate video clips with camera poses estimated from the videos. The test clips come from the preprocessed release of the pixelSplat authors [6]; their full test split could not be down-loaded, so the candidate pool is the 41 test clips of their pub-lished subset. Every clip was labelled indoor or outdoor from thumbnails before any model was run, and the test set is every outdoor clip with at least 32 frames: 9 clips of yards, facades, pools, porches and decks. Each of 5 frame subsets takes N=32 frames spread over the whole clip. The poses are known only up to scale, so the scores use the relative rotation and translation direction, as in the main table; the dataset has no depth. 

Result. LMSA loses 0.22 AUC@5 against dense attention (95% CI [ −1.55, 2.33]) and 0.40 AUC@30 (CI [ −0.51, 1.33]), so on these clips it is practically as accurate as dense atten-tion. GPA-VGGT is 0.76 above dense attention, and the dif-ference between the two methods is not significant ( +0.98, CI [−1.73, 4.05]). AVGGT(4), AVGGT(9) and SAF3R lose 2.11, 5.14 and 2.49; only the gap to AVGGT(9) is significant ( −4.92, CI [ −9.89, −0.20]). Per clip, LMSA loses more than 3 AUC@5 on one clip (at most 7.0, a covered porch), as does GPA-VGGT, whereas AVGGT(9) does so on four clips. 

What this test does not show. Every clip is of high redun-dancy ( ρ between 16 and 30), because a real-estate video re-visits the same space many times. The test therefore shows that LMSA carries over to outdoor scenes, but it does not test the low-redundancy regime that the main table targets. With 9 clips the intervals are wide, and GPA-VGGT’s negative loss comes mostly from one clip with moving people, on which it beats dense attention by 14.4. Without that clip and one clip that fades to white, LMSA loses 0.82 (CI [ −0.85, 3.19]) and GPA-VGGT 1.05 (CI [ −0.22, 2.71]). The frames come from YouTube videos and are used for research only; the poses are released under CC BY 4.0.

Keys. Table S15 collects the knockouts, in which one set of keys is removed from the matching layers and the loss is mea-sured. Proposal of the importance sample . A layer-local audit measured the output error of each layer on its own, for the far-field keys of layers 10–16. In it, 256 samples drawn in propor-tion to the pilot’s column mass have about half the output error of 256 uniform samples (0.109 against 0.203), so the column mass is a useful proposal. 

Shared versus per-query blocks. This test asks whether one block of keys shared by a tile of queries can replace a separate window for each query. Blocks of 6 ×10 keys centred by the or-acle lose 0.93 AUC@5 (CI [ −0.84 , 2.80]) relative to per-query 3×3 oracle windows, pooled over the low-ρ scenes. On court-yard alone, the loss is 2.62 (CI [ −0.31 , 5.55]). The pooled result therefore supports shared blocks, but the support is weaker per scene. 

Denser lattices instead of routing. Without correspondence keys, the lattice must be dense to stay accurate: at σ=16, the loss relative to σ=9 with oracle keys is 3.86 AUC@5 (CI [1.45, 6.64]) on the low-ρ scenes. Making every visible frame dense also recovers less than oracle 3 ×3 windows do (courtyard 49.3 vs. 54.7), so on courtyard adding whole frames helps less than adding the few correct keys. 

Refining the router later does not help. The router finds correspondences from the features of one layer, so a natural question is whether deeper features would find them better. The best correspondence readout from a sparse pass recalls 0.450 of the 3 ×3 targets at layer 9 and also 0.450 at layer 12, so routing from deeper features gains nothing.

D.4.1 Break-even switch 

Consider one query and one routed slot with probability p of holding the query’s correspondence. If the query is an orphan (probability o) and the slot is right, the slot removes it from the orphan share. Pose accuracy rises by g per unit of orphan share, so the expected gain is p o g . If the slot is wrong (probability 1 − p), its keys are amplified like right ones and cost H. The expected value is p o g − (1 − p)H, and it is positive i ff 

p > p⋆ = HH + o g . (S3) The numerator is the cost of a wrong slot and o g the expected gain of a right one. The gain saturates per query, because one right block is enough to rescue an orphan, whereas the harm adds up over every wrong slot. On redundant input there are almost no orphans ( o → 0), so p⋆ → 1 and the switch should stay o ff . The three inputs g, H and o are estimated on courtyard at σ=4 from 5-seed runs. Oracle correspondences remove an orphan share of 0.193 and gain 8.07 AUC@5, so 

g = 8.07 /0.193 = 42. Similarity keys placed only in the 24.3 slots per query whose frame does not see the point cost 6.87, so 

H = 6.87 /24 .3 = 0.283. With o = 0.193 this gives p⋆ = 3.4%. Figure S2 shows the curve, and Table S20 lists the switch sig-nals measured on saved router outputs. Of these signals, the vote-peak rule is the one used by LMSA-fast. The break-even accounting is consistent with earlier runs: on courtyard it pre-dicts net changes of −1.05 and +0.3 AUC@5 for ungated simi-larity windows and vote blocks, against −0.69 and −0.02 mea-sured. The model’s predicted gain for the vote-peak switch (≈ +3.8) is only an estimate, because the switched blocks were measured only inside the composed recipe (Table S19), not on their own. 

D.5 Thinner matching layers 

This post-hoc study tests how far the matching layers can be thinned before accuracy su ff ers. 

These tests are post hoc: their plans were registered after the results of Table 2 were in, before any of their own runs, with predictions and no pre-registered bar. They ask whether layers 11–16 can be thinned in other ways than the truncated lattices of Section 4.3. In every arm, the other layers keep the key sets of LMSA (hub256 in layers 0–8, hub1024 in 9–10 and 17–23), so only the matching layers change. The merge arms apply the key merging of GPA-VGGT in layers 11–16 at merge ratios 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8, so that 80, 50 and 20% of the keys remain. As a check, the merge arm at ratio 0 reproduces LMSA bit for bit. The top-k arm uses, in layers 11–16 only, the per-query top-

k frame selection of the SAF3R heads, which keeps 21–27% of the keys. Its layers were checked to match SAF3R’s own forward bit for bit. Every arm ran on the 5 development and 21 unseen test scenes with 5 seeds and was scored as in Table 2 (Table S21). On the low-ρ scenes, accuracy falls steadily as more keys are removed, relative to LMSA: 

• keeping 80% of the keys is not significantly worse than exact attention; 

• keeping 50% costs 1.6 AUC@5, and keeping 20% costs 5.7; 

• top-k frame selection costs 4.2, which is no better than SAF3R with its own early and late layers ( −1.07, CI [−3.58, 1.48]). The most extreme arm replaces layers 11–16 by the shared hub keys of the di ff use layers, so that no layer stays exact. It costs 27.8 AUC@5 on the low-ρ scenes and 14.9 even on the high-

ρ scenes, yet its aggregator runs only 1.1, 1.4 and 1.5 × faster than that of LMSA at N=32, 64 and 100 (same session). This means that the matching layers depend on query-specific keys, which a shared sample cannot provide, and that keeping these layers exact is cheap relative to their e ff ect on accuracy. On the high-ρ scenes, all other arms stay within 0.8 AUC@5 of LMSA, so thinning matters mainly where views overlap little. The three arms that use over 4 × fewer FLOPs than dense atten-tion at N=100 lose 4.2, 5.7 and 27.8 AUC@5 against LMSA on the low-ρ scenes. Every arm that keeps accuracy there re-tains at least 80% of the keys in the matching layers, so its cost stays quadratic in N. In other words, no tested way of thinning the matching layers both keeps accuracy and removes their quadratic cost. A final arm replaces the early and late lay-ers of SAF3R by the key sets of LMSA. It recovers none of the baseline’s low-ρ loss, so that loss therefore sits in its matching layers. 

D.6 Why the matching layers resist thinning

A rule was screened as viable if, even for the worst 10% of (query, head) pairs, it kept at least 90% of the attention mass with at most 25% of the keys. No rule passed in any of the six layers. 

• Epipolar band. Even with true poses, a band of ±5patches (15% of the keys) kept only 21–52% of the mass of the median peaked pair, where a peaked pair is one with Γrow ≥ 1.5. 

• Each query’s own 256 heaviest keys (an oracle that no practical rule can beat) kept only about 60%. 

• Reuse across layers. Heavy keys do not persist from layer to layer: a query’s heaviest cross-frame key matched that of layer 11 in under 2% of cases, so keys chosen in one layer are a poor guess for another. 

• Block bounds and the s2 switch. Both reach exact accuracy, but only by keeping 51–99% of the keys, which saves at most 12% of the FLOPs of LMSA. The switch uses the proxy s2 ≥ 3 in place of Γrow ≥ 1.5; the two agree on 89–96% of (query, head) decisions ( κ = 0.71–0 .91). Table S22 shows why these rules fail. Even where attention is peaked, it is spread over many keys: half the mass of a peaked pair lies on 48–269 keys, and 90% lies on 349–2540, out of about 33,000. Most of this mass falls on other frames, and the geometric structure, although visible, is small. On peaked pairs, the 3 ×3 window around the true correspondence is enriched up to 74-fold in layer 14, meaning that it receives far more atten-tion than its size alone would give it, yet it holds only 1.6– 12% of the cross-frame mass; a ±9-patch epipolar band (16% of the keys) holds 29–56%. In contrast, layers 10 and 17, which LMSA serves with hubs, are mostly di ff use and show no such concentration. The matching layers thus sit between the two cases that cheap approximations handle well: for the approxi-mations tried, their attention is too broad for any small key set tested, and too focused for a shared sample. The bound of about 4× is therefore an observed bottleneck under the training-free approximations tried, not a lower bound on sparse matching attention. It suggests that learned compression, rather than se-lection, is the route past it.

E.3 The selector applied to GPA-VGGT 

This post-hoc test asks whether the selector helps GPA-VGGT on the same backbone. 

This test is post hoc: its plan was registered after the results of Table 2 were in, before any of its own runs, and it has no pre-registered bar. It asks whether the two parts of LMSA that can be moved to another method, the layer selector and the hubs, improve a published training-free baseline. GPA-VGGT al-ready protects layers 13–16 by merging only 20% of their keys, and it summarises keys more coarsely in the other layers. Three arms modify it:

• GPA +sel +hubs applies both changes. Each arm ran on the 5 development and the 13 unseen low-ρ

scenes with 5 seeds and was scored as in Table 2 (Table S25). Protecting the selected set improves GPA-VGGT by 1.20 AUC@5 and brings it level with LMSA, at the price of 5% more FLOPs and 1–6% more aggregator time at N=32–100. The test therefore supports the statis-tic as a layer selector that transfers to another method on the same backbone. It does not show that hubs are better than the early cluster summaries of GPA-VGGT. 

E.4 Component ablation 

This post-hoc ablation measures how much each component of the frozen recipe contributes, by changing it and measuring the loss. 

The development-scene arms were planned before the test runs. The test-scene arms are post hoc, added after the main results, and have no pass /fail rule. Each arm changes one component of the frozen recipe and keeps everything else unchanged (Ta-ble S26), so any change in loss can be attributed to that compo-nent. The ablation separates the contributions as follows. 

• Importance-weight correction. The hubs are drawn non-uniformly, so each must be reweighted by its log-weight. Without this correction the loss rises to 24 AUC@5 on the development scenes and 36 on the test scenes, so the hubs are useless without it. 

• Pilot proposal. Drawing hubs uniformly, while keep-ing the correct weights, costs 2.0 more on the test scenes than drawing them from the pilot’s column mass. Variance-reducing draws (systematic, heavy-tail, quantile) change little. 

• Early layers. They need cross-frame context: restrict-ing them to the own frame, with or without special to-kens, is costly. A count-biased mean token recovers most of the loss, and the hubs recover the rest. 

• Later di ff use layers. In layers 17–23, 1024 hubs beat both 256 hubs and AVGGT’s lattice. Making layers 9–10 dense brings no gain for 8% more FLOPs. 

• Matching layers. Every sparse key set tested in the matching layers costs 1.4–25 more than exact atten-tion, and tubes (routed correspondence windows) re-cover only part of what a lattice loses. 

• Selector cut-o ff . The 1.5-nat cut-o ff sits at the knee of the accuracy curve. Raising it to 2.0 nats removes layer 16 from the exact set and costs 2.9 on the test scenes; raising it to 3.0 nats keeps only layers 14 and 15 exact and costs 11.0. Lowering it to 1.0 adds layer 10 to the exact set without benefit.

F.2 Sources of run-to-run variance 

The loss of LMSA varies from run to run for two reasons: the random hub draw, and the choice of frame subset. This post-hoc analysis measures how much of the variation each source causes. On the five development scenes, LMSA was rerun with two frame subsets per scene and three hub draws per subset, giv-ing 30 runs; a new draw changes the seed of both the hub pilot and the hub sample. A nested method-of-moments decomposi-tion then splits the variance of the per-run AUC@5 loss into its sources (Table S28). It attributes 84% of the variance to the hub draw (SD 2.28), 16% to the frame subset (SD 1.00) and none to the scene, so hub sampling noise is the main source. Most of the draw variance comes from one courtyard subset, whose three draws span 12.7 AUC@5; without courtyard, the draw SD is 1.14 (70%). Over the 21 test scenes, with one draw per sub-set, the scene term explains 3.5% of the per-run variance. Since the noise comes from sampling, drawing more hubs should re-duce it. More hubs (1024 in layers 0–8 and 2048 in layers 9–10 and 17–23) indeed lower the draw SD by 44%, from 2.28 to 1.27, at an unchanged mean loss (0.36 against 0.39) and for 2.2% more FLOPs at N=32. With more hubs, the frame subset becomes the dominant source of variation.

Other samplers at equal cost (post hoc). Instead of drawing more hubs, the noise could in principle be reduced by drawing the same number of hubs more cleverly. A layer-local audit tested this by comparing the current sampler with five alter-natives on the 18 hub layers of the frozen recipe, using the 5 development scenes, 2 seeds and 2048 random queries, with 8 independent draws each. All alternatives keep the numerator and denominator unbiased, and the plan and its continue rules were registered before the run. 

• Fixed share of top-mass keys. Keeping 10, 25 or 50% of the top-mass keys exactly and sampling the rest from the renormalised residual raised the output error by 3, 13 and 33%, so it made the error worse. 

• Other proposals. An RMS proposal and a head-balanced proposal changed the error by less than 0.2%, and a value-aware proposal lowered it by only 1.4%. 

• More draws. The output error fell as m−1/2 with the number of draws; twice the hubs gave 0.71 of the error. The reason the proposal matters so little is that it is close to flat relative to the draw budget: about 254 of 256 draws are distinct keys, so almost no draw is wasted on a repeated key. Drawing more hubs is therefore the only e ff ective lever, which is why the post-hoc variant above changes the number of hubs rather than the sampler.
