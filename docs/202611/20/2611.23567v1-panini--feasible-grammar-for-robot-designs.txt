# ABSTRACT 

Symbolic approaches to robot design provide interpretable rules but often overlook continuous geometry, while learned approaches handle geometry but offer limited explanations of their decisions. We introduce a typed production grammar that connects the two through a shared representation of robot skills and morphologies. Its rules use Fillmore-style case roles, parameter inheritance, and specificity-based arbitration, while a geometric realization map translates rule composition into transformations in SE (3) . This allows a single grammar to describe both manipulation skills and robot bodies. We show that the grammar is substantially more compact than an untyped alternative and that its rule arbitration can be recovered from learned geometric containment, achieving 

0.905 ± 0.062 decision agreement across 10 seeds, compared with 0.611 ± 0.090 for a Euclidean control. We also characterize how inheritance affects the compositional properties of the geometric realization map by measuring its homomorphism defect. For morphology optimization, we introduce P ¯ An . ini ( Production-rule And role-typed compositio N for Intelligent, Novel robot des Ign), a cross-entropy search method operating directly on the grammar’s typed genome. Across 30 methods and 24 seeds, P ¯ An . ini achieves the best mean rank, but is statistically indistinguishable from strong generic optimizers, including IPOP-CMA-ES, CEM, PSO, and TuRBO. Evaluating final designs with reinforcement-learning-trained controllers leads to a similar conclusion: the grammar-based search does not consistently outperform generic optimization. Further search mechanisms designed to exploit grammar structure also fail to establish a general advantage. A curriculum over grammar depth is a promising exception, becoming significantly better than six of seven strong baselines at 48 seeds, although this result requires caution because multiple variants were explored. The grammar’s main advantage is therefore not superior optimization performance, but structured and feasible design generation. Every generated candidate satisfies the grammar’s structural constraints, whereas unstructured methods achieve legality rates of only 0–82%. Coordinated limb placement maintains 95% joint feasibility in a highly constrained design space, compared with 55% for range-constrained resampling and no feasible designs from 6,000 flat-parameter samples. The grammar also supports designs with different limb counts, which the compared alternatives do not reach. We further examine the limits of these guarantees: structural legality does not ensure locomotion, and geometric feasibility does not reliably predict simulator failure. Together, these results establish typed production grammars as a compact, interpretable, and constraint-aware framework for robot skill and morphology generation, while showing that their structural advantages do not automatically translate into better black-box optimization. # 1 Introduction 

Black-box optimizers are routinely applied to structured design spaces such as robot bodies, where each candidate must be both good and legal . Unconstrained continuous or graph-based parameterizations can express a wide range of bodies, but whether a body is valid must be checked after it is generated rather than guaranteed by construction. This arXiv:2611.23567v1 [cs.RO] 17 Nov 2026 Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs also affects recent LLM-based low-level design editors [1, 2, 3]. The alternative is to represent a body as a structured object governed by a discrete, checkable grammar [4], echoing the older trade-off in planning between explainable symbolic operators [5] and reactive loops that gain robustness by dropping explicit structure [6]. This raises a question that is rarely separated empirically: when a black-box optimizer searches a structured space, does the structure help it find better designs, or only restrict which designs it can propose? Existing grammar-based approaches address parts of this. RoboGrammar [7] and GLSO [8] use graph grammars to constrain robot morphology, but their rules lack checkable geometric semantics and are typed only by graph adjacency. Region and order embeddings show that asymmetric relations such as entailment or default-versus-override can be represented geometrically as containment [9, 10, 11, 12], but to our knowledge this has not been tested as a way to resolve competing rules inside a grammar. We build a typed production grammar that combines semantic roles, parameter inheritance, and specificity-based rule arbitration. For manipulation, rules carry Fillmore-style case roles [13] (Agent, Patient, Instrument, Location, Source, Goal); for robot morphology, a separate structural role set (Root, Limb, Segment, Joint, Terminal). A geometric realization map turns every valid derivation into a sequence of rigid-body transformations in SE (3) . Rather than assuming that this map preserves the algebraic structure of rule composition, we measure its composition error over sampled rule pairs and longer chains. We then evaluate the grammar as a search space for morphology optimization, asking two separate questions: does it make search find better bodies , and does it make the bodies it proposes structurally valid ? The answers differ, and we report both as measured. 

What we find. 

• Structure guarantees feasibility. Every candidate the grammar produces is legal by construction, against 

0–82% for unstructured edits. In a very-low-feasibility space, coordinated limb placement keeps 95% of edits jointly feasible, range-constrained resampling keeps 55% , and flat parameterization finds no feasible design in 

6,000 draws. Only the grammar reaches designs that require a different limb count. • Structure does not improve fitness over strong generic optimizers. A grammar-guided cross-entropy search has the best mean rank among 30 methods at n = 24 seeds but is statistically indistinguishable from IPOP-CMA-ES, CEM, PSO, and TuRBO (Nemenyi p = 1 .00 ). Re-scoring each method’s final designs with RL-trained controllers does not favor the grammar either (Friedman p = 0 .71 in a clean six-method re-run). • Mechanisms that give the grammar a role in the search dynamics mostly do not help. None separates from the strongest generic optimizers, and transfer to held-out limb counts is significantly worse. The exception is a curriculum over grammar depth, which is marginally better at n = 24 and better than six of seven strong baselines at n = 48 , although not every comparison survives correction for the number of variants tried. • The grammar’s internal semantics are measurable. The realization map is a monoid homomorphism up to a defect that we measure when inheritance is active, rule arbitration is recoverable as learned geometric containment ( 0.905 ± 0.062 decision agreement over 10 seeds, versus 0.611 ± 0.090 for a Euclidean control), and the grammar is far more compact than an untyped alternative. We read the consistent null on fitness as evidence that well-tuned generic optimizers are hard to beat on this landscape however the grammar enters the search. Both guarantees have limits that we also measure: a legal body is not thereby a locomoting one, and the geometric feasibility gate is a design-specification filter that does not predict simulator failure.

An approach height declared in the first step must be available to every later step that needs it, without being restated ( inheritance ). If the block has an attached tool, a more specific approach rule should take precedence over the generic rule without removing it ( specificity-based arbitration ). 2.2 Case-Role Schema 

Six case roles, following [13]: Agent, Patient, Instrument, Location, Source, Goal. A case frame assigns roles to scene entities, each carrying a pose in SE (3) . Robot body design (Section 2.5) reuses this mechanism with a separate set of roles for body parts: Root, Limb, Segment, Joint, and Terminal. The role sets are disjoint because they apply to categorically different kinds of entities. 

2.3 Production Rules, Inheritance, and Arbitration 

A production rule r is (cond , req , params , emit , consume , block , s ): a boolean condition, required case roles, default parameters, STRIPS-style markers for legality gating, and a specificity index s(r) ∈ N.

Inheritance. A parameter in r’s inheritance-export set is written into the shared derivation state. It stays available to every later rule, unrestated, until overridden. Inheritance is the only channel for cross-rule information flow. That is also why it is the only possible source of departure from strict compositionality in the geometric realization map Φ

(Section 4). 

Specificity arbitration. When several rules match, the most specific one fires; if more than one rule ties for most specific, the one declared first wins. A losing rule is not deleted. It remains available wherever no more specific rule applies. This default-versus-override relation is what Section 4 tests as geometric containment. Algorithm 1 (Appendix B) specifies the derivation procedure exactly. Given a plan – an ordered sequence of intended operations with role bindings – it resolves, for each step, which rule fires and with which parameters: the most specific match wins, ties are broken by declaration order, and an inherited value overrides the matched rule’s own default unless the step supplies an explicit override, which wins over both. The result is the full derivation that Φ subsequently realizes geometrically.

2.5 Extension to Robot Morphology 

An identical grammar generates robot bodies, using the disjoint role set of Section 2.2 and a seven-rule catalogue: AddRoot, AddLimb (with a specificity-2 override variant), Articulate, Extend (inheriting the current segment template), Terminate, and Symmetrize. Each AddLimb application mounts one limb on the torso. Applied nℓ times in a row (once per leg, where nℓ is the genome’s leg count, §2.7), with the i-th application placing its mount angle at 2πi/n ℓ

around the torso’s circumference (a deterministic function of the application index and nℓ, up to the genome’s bounded mount-angle jitter), it produces an nℓ-legged body with uniform angular spacing by construction, not by a post-hoc check that rejects unevenly spaced bodies. Segment geometry is declared once and inherited by every later Extend within a limb, so an nℓ-legged, k-segment body uses the same rules regardless of nℓ and k (Section 4). The full catalogue is in Appendix A. 

2.6 Geometric Feasibility Layer 

Before a derived body is compiled and simulated, five geometric constraints are checked against a task specification: workspace reach, ground clearance, mass budget, support-polygon area, and minimum inter-terminal separation. A body failing any constraint is rejected before simulation. Section 4 tests what this buys and what it does not. 

2.7 Searching the Grammar’s Genome 

A morphology genome has five fields: leg count nℓ ∈ [2 , 8] , segments per leg k ∈ [1 , 3] , segment length and radius, and mount-angle jitter. Every genome is derived through the grammar, realized by Φ, and gated (§2.6) before simulation. Two grammar-based searchers operate on it. Grammar-guided evolution (GGE) is a (μ + λ) loop that mutates one parent at a time. P ¯ An . ini replaces this with cross-entropy resampling [14]. Each generation fits a diagonal Gaussian to the top 30% of a population of 10, carries the two best genomes forward unchanged, and rounds the discrete fields when the genome is built; the last 10% of the budget is a trust-region refinement around the incumbent. The standard deviation of k is floored at 0.6. Without this floor the elite refit collapsed onto k = 2 in all 24 seeds; with it, the number of distinct (nℓ, k ) combinations reached rises from 3 to 6, and mean fitness changes from 0.403 to 0.411 . This default configuration is not an arbitrary setting: a sweep over population size, elite fraction, elitism, and refinement fraction left every variant within 0.011 mean fitness of the default (paired p = 0 .57 –0.96 , n = 24 , budget 60). Because every baseline in §3.2 also searches this genome through the same derivation pipeline, the grammar defines the search space for all methods. P ¯ An . ini differs from them only in optimizer mechanics. Section 5 tests variants in which the grammar also takes part in the search dynamics. A schematic of the full pipeline – grammar, realization map Φ, feasibility gate, physics, and search closing the loop back onto the grammar’s parameters – is given as Figure 1.

# 3 Experimental Setup 

3.1 Rule Catalogues, Task Specifications, and the Simulated Body 

The manipulation grammar (8 rules: Approach in two specificity variants, Grasp, Lift, Transport, Place, RotateTool, Retract) and the morphology grammar (7 rules, §2.5) were built independently. Both are given in full in Appendix A. The morphology task spec used throughout is forward locomotion. Fitness is center-of-mass displacement under a fixed sinusoidal control signal over a fixed horizon: a controller-agnostic proxy [4], chosen so that morphology search is not confounded with control-policy search. Every compiled body’s torso is attached to the world by a 4Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs six-degree-of-freedom free joint, genuinely free to translate and rotate under gravity, with per-joint damping, armature, and an implicit integrator for numerical stability under contact; on 100 freshly sampled 8-limb bodies this produces 0 numerical blowups and 0 degenerate (zero-actuator) bodies, with displacement over a 2-second horizon ranging −0.34 

to +0 .88 m (mean 0.13 m), a physically plausible range for a body of this scale. The feasibility specification (§2.6) is calibrated so that ≈ 7% of fresh random genomes pass the geometric check ( 6.8% of 500, for the gate ablation of §4) – a design-specification pass rate, not a simulator-failure rate (§4). Whether the proxy predicts RL-trained performance is tested in Appendix D: on a catalogue rebuilt after a limb-mounting geometry fix, the rank correlation is positive but borderline (Spearman ρ = 0 .355 , p = 0 .050 , n = 31 ), so the proxy is at best a weak predictor. 3.2 Search Baselines 

P ¯ An . ini and GGE (§2.7) are compared against 28 alternatives under one accounting rule: one fitness query is one unit of budget, whatever the method’s population size or update schedule. Twenty are classical, self-adaptive, or quality-diversity black-box optimizers: random search, PSO [15], simplified and IPOP [16] CMA-ES, separable NES [17], hill-climbing, CEM [14], Bayesian optimization [18], DE/rand/1/bin [19], SHADE [20], augmented random search [21], MAP-Elites [22], novelty search [23], simulated annealing, Nelder-Mead, (1+1) -ES, SPSA [24], coordinate descent, LHS with greedy refinement [25], and TuRBO [26]. Seven more are budget-matched reimplementations of recent morphology-design search mechanisms (§4), plus one intermediate grammar-based variant (GGE-v2). Section 5’s targeted experiments use a fixed reference cluster of seven non-grammar methods: the five ranking directly below P ¯ An . ini at budget 60 (IPOP-CMA-ES, CEM, PSO, coarse-to-fine, TuRBO), LHS+refinement, and the simplified CMA-ES, the cluster’s weak tail. 

3.3 Validity Baselines 

Grammar-guided mutation (edit one genome parameter, re-derive) is compared against 14 alternatives spanning three families. Direct representation edits at varying severity: an adversarial mixture (15% collapse-to-zero, 15% sign flip, 15% five-to-fifty-fold blow-up, 55% moderate rescale), mild-only and extreme-only variants of it, structure-blind uniform resampling, deletion/duplication of a compiled geometry element, naive subtree-swap crossover, a raw byte-level bit-flip, and the mixture compounded at 5 and 10 mutations instead of 3. Structure-blind but range-aware resampling : per-attribute-type plausible-range resampling (e.g. radii constrained positive) with no cross-field awareness, naive numeric-average crossover, and pure re-initialization through the grammar (a sanity floor, since it reflects the grammar’s own guarantee). A trained-but-unconstrained latent perturbation : a minimal linear autoencoder (PCA) fit to grammar-generated valid designs, perturbed in latent space and decoded – a simplified stand-in for the GLSO-style contrast between learned-space and construction-guaranteed validity. Validity requires the compiled model to load in MuJoCo [27] and step forward without non-finite state. 

3.4 Metrics and Statistical Protocol 

Homomorphism consistency is measured with the defect δ of Equation 3 (§2.4), on sampled rule pairs and on chains of length k = 2 , . . . , 6. Compactness is measured as rule count under three conditions – fully flat, typed without inheritance, and the full grammar – across a grid of 2–10 legs and 1–4 segments per leg. Specificity-as-containment is measured as agreement between a learned region embedding’s smallest-containing-box decision and the symbolic arbitration decision, on held-out states. For every multi-method search comparison we report per-method mean and standard deviation across seeds, then a Friedman test followed, where the omnibus test is significant, by a Nemenyi post-hoc test [28]; with 30 simultaneous methods, uncorrected pairwise tests would produce spurious significance. We treat n = 24 seeds as the decisive sample size throughout. The one result that needed more power was rerun at n = 48 ,and both sample sizes are reported. Paired t-tests with 95% confidence intervals appear only as uncorrected, secondary evidence and are labelled as such. For the validity comparison, we report raw pass rate out of 100 trials per condition. All results use one pinned simulator version; Appendix C.8 reports how sensitive the search statistics are to that choice. 

# 4 Results 

We test whether the grammar composes geometry consistently, reduces rule counts, generalizes to unseen compositions, supports learned rule selection, improves morphology search, and preserves structural validity. We distinguish these structural benefits from improvements in task performance. 

Compositional consistency of Φ. Does realizing a derivation match composing its separately realized parts (Eq. 3)? We tested both independently built catalogues with ninst = 50 –60 instances per pair and chains of length two through six. These are instance counts, not seed counts (Table 1); the results describe sampled derivations, not a general proof .

Composition is exact without active inheritance, with small defects when inheritance is active. The defect δ was zero for every sampled inheritance-inactive pair and 0.037 –0.038 for adjacent inheritance-active pairs. Across full chains, it ranged from 0.000 to 0.081 . Rather than growing steadily with length, it tracked whether the chain ended with Extend, which reads inherited segment length, or Articulate, which does not. For Articulate-then-Extend, the defect was entirely translational: 0.0◦ rotation and 3.75 ± 2.08 cm translation, consistent with Extend adding a segment by translation. Translating the Patient’s pose also translated the realized motion by the same group element, with zero deviation in every sampled case. 

Exact composition holds when the second rule does not use the first rule’s inheritance exports. Whenever r2 uses no parameter exported through inheritance by r1, Φ( r1◦r2) = Φ( r1)·Φ( r2). Under this condition, r2 receives identical parameters whether evaluated jointly or independently. 

Grammar compactness. Typing reduces rule counts, and inheritance provides a further reduction. Typing reduces counts by 2.1–4.1×, and inheritance adds another 1.3–2.0× reduction (Figure 8, Appendix I). For 4-leg ×2-seg, 6-leg ×3-seg, and 8-leg ×3-seg bodies, flat counts of 17/37/49 become 8/10/12 with typing and 6/6/6 with inheritance. The manipulation grammar represents 30 variants with 5 rules rather than 80 ( 16 ×). 

The compactness advantage grows with body size. Flat counts grow linearly, while the full typed grammar remains at six rules, reaching a 13 .5× advantage for the largest tested body. At the smallest, with 2 legs and 1 segment, the ratio is 6Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs

# 5 Searching for a Grammar-Native Search Advantage 

Can grammar structure improve search dynamics, rather than only define the shared genome? We tested heterogeneous per-limb spaces, fitness noise, subtree crossover, quality-diversity selection, motif reuse, complexity tempering, production repair, transfer, and low-budget regimes using the same harness and budget accounting (Appendix F). 

Most grammar-native search variants do not outperform strong generic optimizers; two perform worse. Except for the curriculum below, none establishes an advantage. In the larger heterogeneous space, P ¯ An . ini falls to 3rd of 4 methods (n = 10 ), contrary to expectations that grammar benefits grow with design-space size [7, 8]. A prior transferred from 2–5 to 6–8 legs also loses to cold-start IPOP-CMA-ES and PSO (Nemenyi p = 0 .012 , 0.038 ). Compositional structure can therefore hinder search.

# 6 Conclusion 

A typed case-role production grammar with inheritance and specificity-based arbitration admits a checkable geometric semantics: rule composition matches SE (3) composition wherever inheritance is inactive, with a small, measured defect elsewhere. The grammar is compact, and its conflict resolution is recoverable as geometric containment, robust to regularization, embedding geometry, and perception noise. As a search space the grammar is shared by every optimizer we tested, and over it P ¯ An . ini is tied with the strongest generic methods – best mean rank of 30, but statistically indistinguishable from IPOP-CMA-ES, CEM, PSO, and TuRBO – a tie that RL re-scoring of 672 final designs does not change. Further attempts to give the grammar a role in the search dynamics produced ties, one significant loss, and one small, marginal positive effect (the depth curriculum). We take this consistency as the result: on this task, well-tuned generic optimizers are hard to beat however the grammar participates in search. The grammar’s contribution is instead structural. It is legal by construction where 14 alternatives are not, and its coordinated limb placement keeps edits jointly feasible where range-constrained resampling does not; in a very-low-feasibility space, flat parameterization finds no feasible design in 6,000 draws, while only the grammar reaches designs 10 Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs with a different limb count. These advantages hold over flat parameterizations, not over a hand-written generator encoding the same coordination, and have measured limits: legality does not imply locomotion, and the geometric gate encodes a design specification rather than predicting simulator failure.

# B Implementation Notes 

This appendix documents implementation decisions and validation checks that materially affect the numbers reported in the main text. Algorithm 1 gives the derivation procedure Section 2 describes in prose, exactly. 

The floating-base torso and integrator stability. Every compiled body’s torso – the one part meant to move freely under gravity – is attached to the world by an explicit six-degree-of-freedom free joint. Forward center-of-mass displacement therefore reflects genuine translation of the whole body, rather than the angle of an actuated hip hinge measured against a kinematically fixed base. Allowing the torso to fall and swing under real dynamics makes roughly half of sampled bodies numerically unstable under a naive explicit integrator, since actuated hinge joints are otherwise asked to support a body actually falling under gravity with no damping or armature. We stabilize this with per-joint damping ( 0.3) and armature ( 0.01 ), a smaller timestep ( 0.002 s), and an implicit integrator: standard stabilizers for actuated hinges under contact, verified not to change which designs the geometric feasibility checks accept. On a smoke test of 100 freshly sampled 8-limb bodies, this configuration shows 0 numerical blowups and 0 degenerate (zero-actuator) bodies, with displacement over a 2-second horizon ranging −0.34 to +0 .88 m (mean 0.13 m) – a physically plausible range for a body of this scale. One residual imperfection remains and is disclosed rather than papered over. With the torso genuinely falling and limbs genuinely swinging under gravity, a folded limb settles into geometric interpenetration with the torso at the contact point. Actuator gain and fitness validity. The actuator gain is calibrated so that center-of-mass displacement under the fixed sinusoidal controller varies with leg count, together with an implausible-displacement guard; a gain too weak to drive real locomotion would give identical displacement across different leg counts, which is physically impossible for a genuine gait, so this check is run on every change to the controller or genome space. 

Budget accounting in the gate ablation. The gate ablation (Section 4) counts only completed physics evaluations against the shared budget, not every genome attempted; counting free gate rejections against the same budget would throttle gate-active search to a far smaller attempt count than gate-inactive search and confound the comparison. 

Severity of the adversarial mutation baseline. The adversarial mixture in Section 3.3 combines collapse-to-zero, sign-flip, and five-to-fifty-fold blow-up perturbations rather than mild rescaling alone, so that this baseline is not a low bar that produces a misleadingly high validity rate. 

Decoupling feature count from specificity. The specificity hierarchy in Section 4 is built across two independent rule families so that a general rule carries a feature its override does not share, decoupling feature overlap from true specificity; a hierarchy where every override is a literal feature superset of its general counterpart would let a Euclidean-nearest-neighbor control with no notion of containment score perfectly by coincidence. 14 Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs 

Noise model for the perception-noise test. The perception-noise sweep (Section 4) models noise as a discrete bit-flip on the actual binary features used to compute the candidate set, since the arbitration decision does not depend on continuous state once that candidate set is fixed – a continuous perturbation of state alone would not exercise the decision at all. 

Budget enforcement in IPOP-CMA-ES. The external CMA-ES implementation’s population-based stopping criterion is checked only between generations, which can overshoot a fixed evaluation budget (up to 49 evaluations for a budget of 40 in this genome space); an effective cap below the nominal budget, verified across four budget sizes, keeps every method’s accounting comparable. 

Unit normalization in SPSA and Nelder-Mead. Both methods operate on a normalized [0 , 1] 5 representation of the genome, converting to real units only at evaluation time, verified against a synthetic objective with a known optimum. This matters because the genome’s real-unit dimensions span a factor of 150 between the largest and smallest range; operating directly in real units, or mishandling SPSA’s internal scale factor, can inflate a method’s effective step size by up to 36 × in one dimension relative to the others. 

Structure-aware inheritance check. The per-limb inheritance check in Appendix C.1 compares each limb’s first-segment radius and length in the compiled body with the genome’s, limb by limb, rather than counting matching elements anywhere in the body (which coincidental matches can satisfy) or checking whether the value appears anywhere as a substring – a substring check passes unconditionally from the first limb’s explicit override alone and cannot detect whether any other limb actually reflects it. 

Per-evaluation feasibility measurement. The shrinking-feasible-region search comparison (Appendix C.5) measures validity directly at every evaluation call, rather than reading it off a method’s running best-fitness-so-far trace, which reads as feasible at every entry once any feasible genome has been found, regardless of whether a given draw was itself feasible, and so inflates whichever method happens to find a feasible point earliest. Measuring per-evaluation feasibility directly removes this confound and is the methodology behind every number in Appendix C.5.

C.1 Inheritance Ablation: Does It Actually Reach Every Limb? 

The compactness result (Section 4) is a rule-counting argument: inheritance reduces the number of distinct (operation, parameter-tuple) rules needed when every limb shares the same segment geometry. It does not, by itself, establish that inheritance is executing as described – that every limb’s compiled geometry reflects the genome’s current segment radius, not only the first limb’s. We test this with two checks: an ablation (every rule’s inheritance-export set forced empty) measured against mutation validity, and a structure-aware trace checking, limb by limb, whether each limb’s first segment in the compiled body carries the genome’s segment radius and length (to the compiler’s 3-decimal precision). Validity is unaffected by the ablation, as a mechanism-level argument predicts ( 100 /100 valid with inheritance active, 

100 /100 disabled, n = 100 ): MuJoCo’s load-and-step check has no way to know whether a geometry’s intended parameter was honored, only whether the resulting body is physically well-formed, which typing/legality gating (not inheritance) is responsible for. The second check depends on which value takes precedence when a rule’s own declared default and an inherited value share the same key. We give an inherited value precedence over a rule’s own default for the same key, with an explicit per-step override, used only by the first limb, taking final precedence over both. Table 7 reports the result. With inheritance active, every limb of every body carries the genome’s mutated radius and length. With inheritance disabled, limbs after the first fall back to the rule’s own defaults: no non-first limb matches the genome’s length, and the 8/415

C.2 Compositional-Generalization Anomaly: Full Diagnosis 

The most likely innocent explanation for held-out error being lower than in-distribution error (Table 2) is a length confound: held-out derivations might simply be shorter on average, giving twist-composition error less opportunity 15 Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs to accumulate. We test this across n = 5 seeds (600 training derivations, 100 in-distribution and 100 held-out test derivations per seed, 60 epochs each) and bucket every evaluated derivation by its exact length. Across seeds, the anomaly is stable: in-distribution error is 0.0612 ± 0.0120 and held-out error is 0.0124 ± 0.0018 ,held-out lower in all 5 of 5 seeds. Bucketing by length rules out the simple length confound, but in a way that itself needs explaining: the two splits barely overlap in length (in-distribution derivations are lengths 3, 4, 5; held-out are lengths 4 and 8), and at the one length they share, length 4, in-distribution error ( 0.1705 , n = 178 ) is over 14 × higher 

than held-out error ( 0.0118 , n = 244 ) – the opposite of a simple "held-out is shorter" story. Tracing this length-4 split resolves it completely. Of the three in-distribution training templates, exactly one has length 4 and includes a tool-rotation step, and every held-out template of length 4 contains no such step. Splitting every evaluated derivation by whether it contains a rotation step, rather than by length, shows why this matters: derivations containing it have twist MSE 0.1806 (n = 27 ) versus 0.0010 (n = 73 ) for derivations without one – a 180 × gap. Rotation is the only transformation whose twist depends on a per-scene, randomly sampled tool axis rather than a task-geometry-determined translation target, making it intrinsically harder to fit regardless of where it falls in a derivation. Because this one hard operation appears only in a training template and never in any held-out template, the in-distribution average is inflated by a task type the held-out split never has to handle. The original anomaly is explained, not merely narrowed: it is an artifact of unequal task-type composition between the training and held-out template sets, specifically that the one rotation-based operation was placed exclusively on the training side. It does not reflect compositional generalization behaving backwards, and it does not bear on the morphology-domain result in Table 2, which uses a structurally different held-out split (leg and segment count, not primitive-rule identity) and shows the expected direction. A future version of this diagnostic should include a rotation-bearing template in the held-out set as well, so task difficulty is balanced across splits.

C.6 Stricter Validity Criterion and Careful Hand-Coded Editors

C.8 Reproducibility: A Simulator-Version Sensitivity 

Replicating the search comparison on a second machine changed the eight-seed statistic substantially (4/8 seeds won rather than 7, p = 0 .576 rather than 0.082 for a two-method comparison) despite byte-identical code and seeds. We flag this as an open methodological consideration for anyone reproducing these statistics on a different installation, rather than assume a given magnitude transfers unchanged across simulator versions.

# G Closed Kinematic Chains 

Extending the morphology grammar to branching limbs needs only a new rule following the existing required-role gating pattern; extending it to closed kinematic chains needs a genuinely new verification step, which this appendix characterizes directly by building the simplest non-trivial closed chain: a planar four-bar linkage (a ground link of 21 Panini- Feasible Grammar For Robot Designs length L0 connecting two torso mount points, a crank of length L1 driven by angle θ1, a rocker of length L3 at angle θ3,and a coupler of length L2 that must physically connect the crank’s tip to the rocker’s tip). The crank and rocker are each ordinary open-chain limb derivations from the torso – exactly the branching-limb case the grammar already handles. What is new: in a closed chain, θ3 is not a free genome parameter. It is determined by the other three link lengths and θ1 via the loop-closure constraint |crank _tip( θ1) − rocker _tip( θ3)| = L2, which the inheritance mechanism – designed to carry a value forward from an earlier rule, not to solve a constraint between two independently-derived subtrees’ endpoints – has no analogue for. We solve this constraint numerically (bisection over θ3 ∈ [0 , 2π), refined with Brent’s method, xtol = 10 −12 ) for 500 randomly sampled configurations, link lengths drawn from the same range as this paper’s own segment-length bound ([0 .05 , 0.30] ). 

Assembly feasibility is a real, non-trivial question. Only 318 /500 (63 .6% ) of randomly sampled link-length combinations admit any real solution to the loop-closure equation at all – the remaining 36 .4% are not closeable by any 

θ3, a classical consequence of four-bar linkage geometry (related to the Grashof condition) that the grammar’s existing parameter clamping has no mechanism to detect or avoid. 

Where a solution exists, the verification step itself is sound. For every closeable configuration, we check the claim that two derivation paths must agree on a shared joint’s position, by building the coupler at the solved length L2 and measuring how far its endpoint lands from the crank’s own tip. The measured residual is mean 1.5 × 10 −15 , max 

6.3 × 10 −14 – floating-point precision, to the same standard of a measured, bounded defect this paper applies to Φ’s own homomorphism defect (§4). A closed chain therefore requires (a) an assembly-feasibility check with no analogue in the current grammar, and (b) a numerical verification step categorically different from inheritance or specificity arbitration. We integrate both into the grammar directly, rather than leaving the solver standalone, through one additive extension: a new rule whose parameters are computed by solving a constraint over already-derived state, after the normal inheritance/override merge, instead of only from defaults or inheritance. Every existing rule is unaffected by this extension and the full test suite still passes unchanged. The rocker’s mount angle becomes this rule’s resolved output rather than a free parameter, and an unsolvable configuration is rejected through the grammar’s own existing failure path for an inapplicable rule, with no new exception type introduced. We benchmark this integration on 300 random four-bar configurations, comparing the grammar-wrapped rule (with a bounded repair loop that shrinks the coupler/rocker lengths and re-derives, up to 5 attempts, on an initial failure) against the standalone solver used as-drawn with no repair. The two paths agree on the as-drawn configuration 

92 .7% of the time, confirming that wrapping the solver as a grammar rule changes nothing about its correctness; the grammar-wrapped path’s repair loop raises the overall closeable rate from 63 .0% to 70 .3% (7.3% repaired, 29 .7% 

rejected as unfixable within 5 attempts). The forward-kinematic residual at the solved angle remains at floating-point precision (mean 1.6 × 10 −15 , max 6.3 × 10 −14 ), matching the homomorphism-defect standard of §4. Beyond what the standalone solver could check at all, we also verify genuine downstream simulation: compiling the solved linkage with a native rigid-body equality constraint joining the crank and coupler tips, every closeable configuration loads and steps 100 physics steps without blowing up, with the equality constraint’s residual under real dynamics averaging 

3.65 × 10 −3 (max 3.98 × 10 −2) – small, but non-zero in a way the offline forward-kinematics check, which never touches a physics engine, cannot see. Wiring the solver into the rule-application machinery is therefore no longer future work; what remains open is generalizing this mechanism beyond this one hand-written constraint to a general library of closed-chain topologies. 

# H Manipulation Benchmark: Full Results 

Section 4 summarizes this result; this appendix gives the full setup and table. The benchmark is built around the existing Approach →Grasp →Lift →Transport →Place rule chain. The end effector is a kinematically-driven point (with no actuators or joint limits of its own, so it isolates whether the staged plan succeeds from whether a real controller can track it); the object is a free dynamic body under gravity; grasping is a rigid kinematic pin between object and end-effector pose, engaged within a tolerance radius of the object and released at the Place target – a simplified-grasp proxy with no finger-contact dynamics, chosen for stability under the end effector’s discontinuous-looking position updates. Three tasks of increasing length are run on the same 150 random scenes for the grammar-derived plan and two baselines of deliberately different strength. “Baseline” moves in one straight line from the end-effector’s start pose directly to the final target, with no knowledge of the object’s location, attempting a grasp only opportunistically if that line happens to pass near the object. “Informed” is handed the exact same ground-truth object and target positions the grammar’s own waypoints are computed from and deliberately routes through the object before the target (start-to-object, then object-to-target, same total step budget), but still lacks the grammar’s staged ordering – no separate hover-above waypoint, no separate grasp-depth waypoint, no separate lift-to-height waypoint before transporting. Both baselines attempt a grasp via the same tolerance-triggered mechanism as the grammar, so any gap is attributable to staging, not to a different grasp mechanism or time budget. No simulation failures occur in any of the 150 × 3 × 3 = 1350 trials.
