## Abstract 

Scene chronology aims to reconstruct a scene across vis-its separated by hours or days, during which objects may change in position, presence or shape. Such reconstruction must retain every earlier state, yet each revisit often provides only a few images. Storing a complete model for each visit duplicates the unchanged scene, whereas updating a sin-gle shared model alters earlier states. We present ReScene, which stores each visit as an immutable compact update, so that any earlier visit can be recovered exactly by applying the stored updates in order, without retaining past images. To localise each update, ReScene attributes the disagreement between the preceding reconstruction and the new images to individual Gaussians through their compositing weights. This attribution guides the suppression of outdated Gaus-sians and the refinement of persistent ones, while newly vis-ible surfaces are initialised from learned multi-view depth. We also introduce Multi-State, a benchmark of non-rigid deformation and partial material removal, including syn-thetic scenes with ground-truth geometry. ReScene achieves quality higher than sequential 3DGS checkpointing with 2.5–4.8 × less uncompressed history storage. At compara-ble PSNR, it also reduces foreground depth error by 3.2 ×

relative to sequential ChronoGS, showing that accurate ren-dering does not necessarily imply accurate geometry. Code and benchmark are available at GitHub. 

## 1. Introduction 

Revisiting a scene hours or days apart may reveal objects that change in position, presence or shape, while most of the scene remains unchanged. Retaining each of these states as a scene chronology (SC) supports retrospective inspection on construction sites or in warehouses, where the scene may need to be examined exactly as it was at an earlier visit. In practice, however, each revisit is often captured with only a few images, and earlier captures may no longer be available. Existing methods meet only part of these requirements. For instance, dynamic methods such as 4DGS learn continu-ous motion from densely sampled video [23, 25], whereas re-visits observe distinct states without the transitions between them. SC methods jointly optimise all visits [7, 12, 22] and therefore require the images of every visit during training. A simple sequential alternative is to fine-tune the reconstruc-tion at each visit and save a checkpoint, but this duplicates the unchanged scene, so storage grows redundantly with every visit. Another approach is to update a single shared model, which alters earlier states [26]. Restricting each update to the changed parts of the scene avoids both problems, but sparse revisits make this challeng-ing. With few views, disagreement between the existing re-construction and the new images may arise from either scene change or reconstruction error, and partial-object changes such as deformation confine it to small regions. Inaccurate localisation can therefore either prevent necessary updates 1arXiv:2611.20433v1 [cs.CV] 16 Nov 2026 or alter persistent surfaces. Moreover, newly exposed sur-faces, such as the interior revealed by opening a grill lid, must be initialised from these views alone. Recent continual methods update a reconstruction locally [1, 26]. GaussianUp-date matches instance masks and initialises new geometry from structure-from-motion points [26], whereas CL-Splats detects change from semantic features and assigns it to Gaus-sians by voting with their projected centres [1]. Such cues may miss changes confined to a small part of an object, and triangulation requires view overlap that sparse revisits may not provide [20]. In this paper, we introduce ReScene, which localises updates to individual Gaussians and stores each visit as a compact update rather than a complete model. We attribute the photometric disagreement between the preceding recon-struction and the new images to Gaussians through their compositing weights, so that each Gaussian receives change and stable support in proportion to its contribution to the rendered pixels. This support bounds the opacity reduction of each Gaussian. Gaussians of moved or removed surfaces are therefore faded, whereas those that mainly render stable regions are largely protected. Rather than optimising an independent removal factor for each Gaussian [26], a visit-specific network predicts suppression within this bound from Gaussian features, sharing it across Gaussians with similar features. Newly visible surfaces are initialised from learned multi-view depth that is consistent across the new views. Finally, each visit is stored as an immutable update of per-Gaussian opacities, selected attribute corrections and new Gaussians, so every earlier visit is recovered exactly without retaining past images or replaying earlier views. As shown in Fig. 1, ReScene approaches the quality of checkpointing (Seq. 3DGS) at a fraction of its storage. Evaluating SC under such changes also exposes a gap in existing benchmarks, which mainly capture objects being moved, added, removed or articulated, and thus preserve the geometry of each object or rigid part. Non-rigid changes instead alter the shape of an object itself, either partly, as when a bite is taken out of a tomato, or wholly, as when a tissue is crumpled. Moreover, as ground-truth geometry is difficult to obtain for real scenes at every visit, existing benchmarks evaluate rendered appearance but not geometric accuracy, which matters in applications such as product dam-age monitoring. We therefore introduce Multi-State , whose real scenes capture non-rigid changes under practical capture conditions and whose synthetic scenes provide ground-truth geometry at every visit (Fig. 1, top). On these scenes, we find that accurate rendering from held-out views does not necessarily imply accurate geometry. Our contributions are summarised as follows: • We present ReScene, which stores each visit as an im-mutable compact update, so every earlier visit can be re-covered exactly without past images. Across Multi-State, WAT and ChronoScene, it achieves reconstruction quality comparable to or higher than that of 3DGS checkpoint-ing with a 2.5–4.8 × reduction in uncompressed history storage, whereas a sequentially updated ChronoGS loses 4.16–8.67 dB on earlier visits. • We propose Gaussian-level change attribution through compositing weights, which bounds suppression to protect persistent surfaces, together with depth-seeded initialisa-tion of newly visible surfaces. Compositing-weighted at-tribution improves PSNR by 2.70 dB over projected-centre voting, and ReScene shows its largest advantage under the sparsest revisits. • We introduce Multi-State, to our knowledge the first SC benchmark with non-rigid deformation and partial material removal. Its real scenes capture changes such as a crushed can and a bitten tomato under practical capture conditions, whereas its synthetic scenes provide ground-truth geome-try at every visit for evaluating geometric accuracy. 

## 2. Related Work 

Scene reconstruction. NeRF [15] and 3D Gaussian Splat-ting (3DGS) [6] reconstruct scenes for novel-view synthesis, and subsequent extensions accommodate appearance varia-tion in unconstrained photo collections [8, 14]. When only a few views are available, sparse-view approaches regularise optimisation with geometric priors [10, 16, 27] or predict rep-resentations directly from the input images [3, 4]. Learned geometric models such as DUSt3R [21] and MASt3R [9] further support reconstruction and initialisation from sparse observations [5]. Beyond static scenes, dynamic methods model motion through deformation fields [17–19, 23, 25] or time-varying Gaussians [11, 13]. However, these methods reconstruct either a single scene configuration or motion within a continuous observation period. Scene chronology instead seeks to retain multiple historical scene states ob-tained during separate visits. New observations must be incorporated from only a few images, without overwriting previously recorded states. 

Scene chronology and continual reconstruction. Neural Scene Chronology [12] models temporal changes in scene content, ChronoGS [22] separates persistent and changing components through temporally modulated anchors, and LTGS [7] supports sparse-view updates by sharing object templates across visits. These methods jointly optimise the observations of all visits, whereas continual approaches incorporate new captures sequentially. Among the latter, CLNeRF [2] relies on generative replay, CL-NeRF [24] com-bines expert adaptation with knowledge distillation, and CL-Splats [1] performs local updates with reversible history records. GaussianUpdate [26] is closely related to our work, as both update an existing Gaussian reconstruction from new observations. At visit t, our goal is to up-date Gt−1 to a reconstruction Gt of the current scene while keeping every earlier visit exactly recoverable. Crucially, only Dt is available at this visit, as earlier images are not retained. Moreover, each revisit captures far fewer views than the first, so much of the scene may not be observed. As these regions provide no evidence of change, the update retains them as last reconstructed, and a change in such a region is incorporated only once a later visit observes it. As discussed in Sec. 1, restricting each update to the changed regions of the scene avoids both the redundant storage of checkpointing and the alteration of earlier states caused by updating a single shared model. Under sparse re-visits, however, this restriction requires distinguishing scene change from reconstruction error with limited observations and initialising newly visible surfaces from few views, both of which are challenging. To address these challenges, ReScene learns a compact update ∆t to Gt−1 that stores only what changes, consisting of Gaussian offsets and a visit-specific appearance correc-tion et that absorbs global changes such as exposure. To distinguish change from reconstruction error with few ob-servations, ReScene weighs change support against that of stability for each Gaussian before suppressing or refining it. To initialise newly visible surfaces from these few views, ReScene uses learned multi-view depth that is consistent across the new views, rather than triangulation, which re-quires greater view overlap. 3.1. Evidence from the New Visit 

Before updating the model, we gather evidence of disagree-ment and stability from the new observations, which guides suppression, refinement and the placement of new Gaussians. 

Pixel-level disagreement. If a region of the scene remains unchanged at the subsequent visit, the model updated at the previous visit should reproduce its appearance from the new viewpoints. We therefore compare each new image 

Itv with the corresponding rendering R(Gt−1, Πtv ) using the pixelwise absolute photometric error. However, global colour shifts between visits (e.g. changes in exposure) could increase the photometric error across the entire image. To handle this, we first subtract a per-channel colour offset from the image. The offset is estimated only from low-error pixels so that locally changed regions do not bias it. After this 3correction, we compute the change mask M c,t v by filtering and thresholding the recomputed error, and then removing small connected components to suppress noise. Low-error pixels outside this mask form the stable mask M s,t v , whereas the remaining ambiguous pixels are unassigned (Fig. 2). As inconsistencies may also arise from reconstruction errors rather than physical changes, we only consider these masks as evidence of change. 

Gaussian-level support. The change and stable masks are defined over pixels, whereas the model is updated at the level of individual Gaussians. As each rendered pixel is formed by alpha compositing the Gaussians along its ray, we transfer the evidence of each pixel to these Gaussians using their compositing weights. Depth support. The change masks localise disagreement in the image plane but do not determine where new sur-faces lie in 3D. To this end, we estimate a depth map and confidence scores for each view of the current visit using MASt3R [9], with the cameras fixed to Πtv to align the es-timates with the reconstruction. Retaining only confident depth estimates, we assess geometric support through two comparisons. First, we back-project estimated depths and reproject the resulting points into other views, checking their cross-view consistency with the corresponding depth esti-mates. Second, we compare estimated depths with depths rendered from Gt−1 at the same cameras. Cross-view con-sistency supports the placement of new Gaussians, while agreement with the preceding reconstruction supports refin-ing existing ones.

3.4. Compact History and Retrieval 

After optimisation, each update ∆t stores the opacity vec-tor of existing Gaussians, the refined attributes and their indices, the attributes of births and the appearance correction 

et. Unmodified geometric and colour attributes are reused from preceding states, which reduces storage compared with saving a complete model after every visit (Sec. 4.2). Before saving, negligible opacities of existing Gaussians are set to zero without changing their indices, and births with low opacity are discarded. As the update stores explicit attribute values, decoding requires no network evaluation. Our imple-mentation also retains the MLP weights and auxiliary arrays, which are included in all reported storage measurements. To recover visit τ , we apply ∆1, . . . , ∆τ sequentially to the initial reconstruction G0 and render with eτ . Later updates leave these updates unchanged, so the stored reconstruction of each visit can be recovered exactly. ## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate on our Multi-State benchmark, which contains both rigid and non-rigid changes, including partial-object changes rarely found in existing benchmarks, such as a tissue being pulled out and crumpled or a bite taken out of a tomato. We evaluate four registered real scenes (16 visits) and four synthetic scenes (42 visits) from Multi-State. Its synthetic scenes provide ground-truth geometry at every visit, which enables geometry evaluation, whereas its real scenes contain the imperfections of real-world capture, such as sensor noise and camera poses that are estimated offline and aligned across visits. The construction of both parts is described in the supplementary material. We further evalu-ate on World-Across-Time (WAT) [2], which contains real revisits of the same scenes, and ChronoScene [22], which contains larger environments. 

Baselines. We compare with sequential 3DGS, LTGS [7], ChronoGS [22] and CL-Splats [1]. Sequential 3DGS fine-tunes all Gaussian attributes from the preceding model using current images, with densification and pruning. LTGS sup-ports sparse-view updates through shared object templates and multi-period optimisation, whereas ChronoGS jointly represents visits using temporally modulated anchors. We adapt both to sequential access: Seq. LTGS uses current images and synthetic replay from its preceding model, while Seq. ChronoGS updates its shared representation using cur-rent images. Neither adaptation uses earlier or future cap-tured images. Sequential 3DGS and our LTGS adaptation save complete models for historical retrieval; Seq. ChronoGS queries its final shared model. CL-Splats locally updates its reconstruction and recovers earlier visits from reversible records. All methods share camera registration, selected training views and held-out cameras; differences in initialisa-tion, optimisation and replay are detailed in the supplement. GaussianUpdate [26] is closely related, but the official imple-mentation was unavailable publicly. The supplement com-pares our paper-guided WAT experiment with its published results and discloses the remaining protocol differences. 

Evaluation protocol. On every benchmark, the initial re-construction is trained on all training images of the first visit, after which each later visit provides up to 24 images, without access to the images of earlier visits. We measure reconstruction quality at arrival and at final recall . At arrival, each visit is evaluated immediately after its reconstruction is obtained, using the held-out images of that visit. At fi-nal recall, each method recovers every observed visit from its retained representation after all sequential updates are complete. The rendered images are compared with the same held-out images used at arrival. As both evaluations use identical held-out images, a decrease from arrival to final re-call indicates that subsequent updates have degraded earlier reconstructions. We report PSNR and SSIM alongside the total storage required to recover all visits. On the synthetic scenes of Multi-State, we additionally evaluate geometric accuracy as the error of rendered expected foreground depth with respect to the ground truth. All scene-wise results are provided in the supplementary document.

S1.2. Birth placement, splitting and capacity 

Section 3.4.2 of the main paper introduces new Gaussians, which we called births , to represent surfaces missing from the preceding reconstruction. Their placement uses depth estimates from the current visit, while subsequent splitting provides additional detail where needed. Here, we specify the placement thresholds and splitting settings, then exam-ine how the maximum number of births per visit affects reconstruction quality and history storage. 

Placement thresholds. We initialise births by back-projecting changed pixels using the estimated depth of the current visit. A candidate must have MASt3R confidence above 1.5 and depth support from at least two current views, including its source view. A supporting view must contain the reprojected point within its confident change mask, and the point’s camera-space depth must agree with the estimated depth within 3%. The candidate depth must also differ from the preceding rendered depth by more than 3% of the esti-mated depth. Differences in either direction are accepted, allowing births both in front of existing geometry and behind removed surfaces. We sample candidates at a pixel stride of two and re-ject non-finite positions. To exclude distant outliers, can-didates must lie within three times the preceding model’s 95th-percentile radial extent, measured from its mean cen-tre. We randomly select at most 30,000 candidates using the fixed experiment seed. Their initial colours are sampled from the source images, and their isotropic scales are derived from depth and focal length. Each birth starts with opacity 0.1 and identity rotation. 

Splitting settings. We split births every 500 steps from step 1,000 to step 4,500, giving eight splitting events during each 5,000-step update. Between events, we accumulate the gradient norm of each birth’s position-offset parameter. Births with opacity above 0.05 and positive accumulated gradient are ranked by this value. There is no additional fixed gradient threshold. Each event replaces up to 10% of the current births with two children per parent, subject to the limit of 60,000 active births. Existing Gaussians are never split. The children are displaced in opposite directions along the parent’s longest principal axis by half its scale along that axis. Their scales are divided by 1.6, and each receives opacity 1 − p1 − αparent . Optimiser moments are retained for unsplit births and initialised to zero for the children. The accumulated gradients are reset after each event. Births with opacity below 0.01 are discarded when saving the update. 

Birth-limit analysis. The birth limit bounds the number of active births at each visit, including children created by splitting. Each update first places at most 30,000 births, or fewer if the limit is lower. Births are then split every 500 steps from step 1,000 to step 4,500 of the 5,000-step update, giving eight splitting events. Starting after 1,000 steps allows births to accumulate meaningful positional gradients, and stopping at step 4,500 leaves the final steps for the newest children to converge. At each event, up to 10% of the current births are each replaced by two children, so the count in-creases by at most 10%. Starting from 30,000, the count can therefore reach at most 64,306 births after the eight events. A limit of 60,000 thus truncates only the final splitting event, whereas limits of 70,000 or more are never reached. In prac-tice, fewer births may be created, as only births with opacity above 0.05 and positive accumulated gradient are split, and births with opacity below 0.01 are discarded when saving. We vary the limit from 5,000 to 90,000 on both subsets of Multi-State, running complete histories with all other settings fixed, including the initial placement cap, splitting schedule, training views, optimisation budget and seed. Each variant updates its own preceding reconstruction, so differ-ences propagate through subsequent visits. Table S1 reports quality averaged over later visits together with complete-history storage. Increasing the limit improves reconstruction quality, with diminishing gains beyond 30,000–40,000 births. S1.3. Selection of Gaussians for refinement. 

Section 3.4.3 of the main paper introduces the refinement set Ft, which combines Gaussians selected through image-based evidence with a conservative depth-supported sub-set. For Gaussian i at visit t, Cti and Sti denote its accu-mulated compositing contributions within the change and stable masks, respectively, averaged across the current views. Please refer to Equation 2 in the main manuscript for the formulation. The image-based selection includes Gaussians for which Cti /(Cti + 1) or Sti /(Sti + 1) exceed 10 −6. This identifies Gaussians that contribute to changed or stable pix-els. For the depth-supported subset, a Gaussian must have opacity above 0.005. In each supporting view, its projected centre must lie within the image, and its camera-space depth must agree with the preceding rendered depth within 3%. At least two current views must also provide confident depth agreement at that location. Specifically, MASt3R confidence must exceed 1.5, the estimated depth must agree with the preceding rendered depth within 3%, and the pixel must lie outside the change mask used during depth-proposal genera-tion. A visible observation inside this change mask excludes the Gaussian from the depth-supported subset. We also exclude the suppression candidates identified during that proposal stage. These exclusions apply only to the depth-supported subset. The final refinement set is its union with the image-based selection, so refinement and suppression may still affect the same Gaussian.

Images from previous visits are not used during the update of the current visit. ## S3. Implementation Details 

S3.1. Evaluated scenes 

We evaluate all four synthetic sequences and real sequences of Multi-State, which is described in previous section. We further evaluate ten scenes of WAT [2], with 51 states, 41 updates and 377 held-out images. The community scene is excluded as the sequential LTGS adaptation could not be validated on it in our experiments. On ChronoScene [22], we evaluate twelve scenes with 42 states, 30 updates and 999 held-out images, excluding three training images in Canteen and Lawncourt that lack a camera registration. For WAT and ChronoScene, we use the camera registrations and held-out images provided by the original benchmark, and all methods are evaluated on the same scenes of every dataset. 

S3.2. 3DGS Initialisation and Updates 

Across all datasets, only the initial reconstruction of each scene is trained on all training images of the first visit. On synthetic Multi-State, it is initialised from points back-projected from the ground-truth depth of these views, and the ground-truth geometry of later states is used only for evaluation as mentioned in the main manuscript. On the real (Multi-State and WAT) scenes and ChronoScene, it is initialised from SfM points restricted to tracks observed in at least two initial training images. Methods including ReScene, sequential 3DGS, sequential LTGS and CL-Splats share this initial model, which is fitted for 30,000 iterations. Meanwhile, sequential ChronoGS uses 40,000 iterations for initial fit, following its original protocol. Across all datasets, each later state provides at most 24 training images. These images were selected by farthest-point sampling of camera centres. Starting from the first image in lexicographic order, or on WAT from the three evenly spaced frames of the original sparse subset, we re-peatedly add the camera farthest from those already selected. The selection depends only on camera positions, and the selected images and held-out cameras are identical across methods. Each update runs for 5,000 steps of optimisation. 

## S4. Baseline optimisation and sequential adap-tations 

This section provides the implementation details for the base-lines introduced in Sec.4.1.2 of the main paper. We evaluate all methods using the same registered cameras, selected training images and held-out views. Each revisit supplies up to 24 current training images and a budget of 5,000 op-timisation steps. The methods differ in their initialisation, use of synthetic replay and representation of earlier visits. These adaptations therefore match the observation setting and update budget, rather than computational cost. 

S4.1. Sequential 3DGS 

Sequential adaptation. We extend static 3DGS to sequen-tial visits by initialising each update from the preceding fitted model. All Gaussian positions, scales, rotations, opacities and spherical-harmonic colour coefficients are optimised using the current visit’s images. We do not provide earlier captured images, synthetic replay or MASt3R-based birth proposals. This baseline tests whether conventional Gaus-sian fine-tuning can accommodate successive scene changes. 

Optimisation. Each visit uses a fresh Adam optimiser with the standard 3DGS parameter-group learning rates and nom-inal 30,000-step learning-rate schedule. We execute the first 5,000 steps of this schedule for each update. The reconstruc-tion loss combines L1 error and SSIM dissimilarity with weights 0.8 and 0.2, respectively. Native densification and 13 pruning occur every 100 steps from step 600 through step 4,900. Opacity resets and world-size pruning are disabled. 

Historical retrieval. Fine-tuning modifies the preceding reconstruction and does not preserve its earlier values. We therefore save a complete renderable model after each visit. Final recall loads the corresponding saved model, and the reported history storage includes the initial model and all subsequent checkpoints. 

S4.2. Sequential ChronoGS 

Sequential adaptation. ChronoGS [22] represents multi-ple visits within a shared temporal model. We adapt its joint fitting procedure by introducing visits sequentially. Each update starts from the preceding model and appends 16-dimensional anchor features and a global embedding for the new visit. These entries are initialised from those of the preceding visit. We then optimise the shared parameters and Gaussian topology using only the current visit’s images. Earlier tem-poral features remain trainable. We introduce no replay, com-pact adapters or additional persistence penalty. This retains the native shared representation while restricting captured-image access to the current visit. 

Optimisation. The initial reconstruction is fitted using the native 40,000-step ChronoGS procedure. Each subsequent visit uses a fresh Adam optimiser and restarts the native 40,000-step learning-rate schedule, executing 5,000 update steps. The native losses and parameter-group settings are retained. This initialisation differs from the standard 3DGS initial model shared by ReScene, sequential 3DGS and se-quential LTGS. 

Historical retrieval. Final recall queries the earlier visit indices in the final shared model. Later updates can there-fore affect earlier reconstructions through changes to shared parameters and topology. We count the final shared model in storage, rather than a collection of per-visit checkpoints. 

S4.3. Sequential LTGS 

Sequential adaptation. LTGS [7] uses object templates to reconstruct scene changes from sparse observations. We adapt its multi-visit pipeline so that each update begins from its own preceding reconstruction and processes only the newly available visit. We retain its change detection, instance matching, geometry initialisation and local refinement stages. Camera localisation is skipped because registered cameras are supplied consistently to all methods. The current visit supplies captured images for the update. To retain previously reconstructed content, the preceding model generates synthetic replay images at the initial and earlier visits’ training poses. Earlier camera poses are there-fore retained, but earlier captured RGB images are not used. Future visits are unavailable throughout the update. 

Optimisation. Sequential LTGS starts from the same stan-dard 3DGS initial model as ReScene and sequential 3DGS. At each revisit, we run the released refinement procedure with a budget of 5,000 optimisation steps, retaining its na-tive optimiser settings and losses. Synthetic replay remains enabled through the previous-viewpoint option. Replay and the preceding matching stages incur additional computation, so the common step budget does not imply equal runtime. Earlier WAT runs perform 4,999 optimiser updates because of the original terminal-step convention. Subsequent runs perform 5,000 updates. We apply integration repairs for numeric mask labels, visibility handling and variable camera dimensions. For images with different dimensions, masks are mapped to the corresponding image grid using nearest-label sampling before track lookup. These repairs preserve label identities and matching thresholds. 

Historical retrieval. Each fitted visit is exported as a com-plete renderable model and retained for final recall. This separates historical reconstruction from subsequent changes to the working model. The reported storage includes the ini-tial model and every saved visit. Thus, sequential LTGS uses synthetic replay during updating and checkpoint retrieval during historical evaluation.

## S7. Analysis on Number of Images per Visit 

We evaluate ReScene, sequential ChronoGS, sequential 3DGS and the refined CL-Splats adaptation on our real Multi-State dataset. Each method reconstructs a complete history using 3, 6, 12, 24, 36 or 48 current training views. Every revisit has enough training images for all six settings. 

Inputs and training. We construct nested subsets using farthest-camera-centre selection. Each subset starts with the first filename in lexicographic order and retains all images from the smaller subset. The 24-view subset is identical to that used in the main experiment. Within each method, the initial model and held-out cameras remain fixed. Ini-tial fitting uses all initial training images. Each update uses 5,000 steps, seed zero and its own preceding reconstruction. ReScene recomputes depth and evidence for each subset. All methods retain the optimiser and topology schedules described in Section S4. Updates do not use captured images from earlier or future visits. The offline camera-registration limitations and different ChronoGS initial fit remain un-changed. Equal iteration counts do not imply equal compu-tational cost.

## S8. Additional qualitative results 

Figures S2–S5 compare our method with the baselines on synthetic Multi-State, real Multi-State, WAT and ChronoScene, respectively. For each scene, one visit is rendered from the same held-out camera at arrival and at final recall, so that the two rows show both how a new visit is reconstructed and how well it is retained after all later updates. Overlays report whole-image PSNR. In these figures, ReScene preserves object shape and de-tail more clearly than LTGS, whose arrival errors remain in its stored reconstructions. ChronoGS exhibits additional dis-tortions at final recall, including altered object configurations in Multi-State, displaced or missing content in WAT, and changes to building appearance in ChronoScene. However, ReScene’s stored updates reproduce each arrival reconstruc-tion exactly, including any errors it contains. Exact recovery therefore preserves an earlier state but does not improve it. For example, the background of Street2 in ChronoScene, which is blurred at arrival, remains equally blurred at final recall. Meanwhile, figure S6 illustrates a challenging case involv-ing thin stems and leaves that occlude one another. Recon-structing these structures from sparse views remains difficult. All methods exhibit visible background artefacts, which are more pronounced in our reconstruction.
