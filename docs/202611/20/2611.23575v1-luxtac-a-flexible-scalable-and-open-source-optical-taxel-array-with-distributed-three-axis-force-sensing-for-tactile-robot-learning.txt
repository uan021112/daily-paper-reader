and built from off-the-shelf parts. Each taxel is a digital RGBC
light-to-digital converter under a translucent white silicone layer
capped by an opaque black film, so the back-scattered LED light
falls monotonically with local indentation depth. The same board

the color of the returned light, with no camera, magnets, or bump
overlay, making LuxTac, to our knowledge, the first optical taxel
array to resolve shear at individual taxels. Characterized with an
automated CNC indenter and a six-axis force/torque sensor, the
normal cartridge responds linearly to force (R2 = 0.999) from

and a taxel-to-taxel variation of 6–10% after normalization. With
the shear cartridge, per-taxel models estimate the three-axis force
at every taxel; across three indenter shapes and nine held-out
sessions, the summed estimates reach R2 = 0.96/0.96/0.92 in
x/y/z, each taxel’s estimate 0.92/0.93/0.94, and the median
shear-direction error is 4.8◦. Adding LuxTac pressure readings to
an Action Chunking Transformer policy substantially improves
peg-in-hole success over a vision-only baseline. Design files, code,
and videos are available at https://luxtac.github.io.

I. INTRODUCTION
T
OUCH tells a robot what vision cannot: whether it is
holding an object, how hard, and where the contact is
when the fingers occlude the scene [1]. Even low-cost tactile
arrays substantially improve learned manipulation policies
when fused with vision [2]–[4]. The most widely adopted
are piezoresistive: a force-sensitive film between orthogonal
electrodes, read as a resistance matrix [2], [3], [5]. They are
thin, flexible, and cheap, and their open-source releases have
lowered the barrier to tactile learning considerably.
Piezoresistive arrays have two weaknesses that matter once a
sensor leaves the bench. Each taxel reports a single number, so
they sense pressure but not shear. And that number is a contact
resistance through a polymer composite, which drifts, saturates
early, depends on the loading history, and differs between pads.
Optical transduction offers a different trade-off. Camera-
based sensors such as GelSight [6], DIGIT [7], and TacTip [8]
reconstruct the deformation field of an elastomer at high res-
olution, at the cost of bulk, bandwidth, and high-dimensional
observations. Miniaturized designs such as TacScope [9] and
MVP-Tac [10] shrink the sensing surface, but the camera’s
optical path still makes them thick along the viewing axis.
Between these extremes, discrete LED–photodetector pairs
embedded in elastomer have been used to sense contact and
proximity [11]–[13]. Such optical taxels measure a geometric
quantity, the deformation of the elastomer above them, through
a stable, linear photodetector with an on-chip digital converter.
We develop this idea into LuxTac (Fig. 1), a flexible, dense,
and scalable tactile array. Each taxel is a commercial light-to-
digital converter [14] under a translucent silicone layer and
an opaque black film; compressing the silicone moves the
absorbing film closer to the detector and reduces the returned
light. Because every taxel is an independent digital device on
its own bus segment, the same taxel cell tiles into arrays
of any size and aspect ratio without changing the readout
(Fig. 2). Because each detector also reports color, a second
gel cartridge with colored walls adds shear sensing at every
taxel without changing the electronics, which makes LuxTac,
to our knowledge, the first optical taxel array that resolves
shear per taxel. Our contributions are:
1) An approximately 3.5 mm thick optical taxel array built
entirely from off-the-shelf parts on a four-layer flexible
PCB, in which every taxel is an independent digital
RGBC detector on its own I2C bus segment, with no
analog front end, so the same cell tiles into arrays of
any size by adding multiplexers and buses (Sec. III).
2) Two interchangeable gel cartridges: the shear cartridge
encodes the local shear above each taxel in the color
of the returned light, so the same board senses either
normal deformation, or normal deformation and shear,
at every taxel (Secs. III-C and IV-C).
3) Characterization over 240 normal presses and shear tests
with three indenter shapes in nine sessions, evaluated at
contact positions and in sessions held out from training;
sim-to-real alignment of contact patterns; and two real-
world tasks, peg-in-hole insertion and cube sorting, with
an Action Chunking Transformer policy (Secs. IV–VI).
II. RELATED WORK
Piezoresistive textiles and laminates are the workhorse of
large-area tactile sensing [5]. 3D-ViTac [2] built 16×16 Velo-
stat pads for bimanual grippers and fused their readings with
vision as a 3D point cloud for diffusion policies [15], [16].
FlexiTac [3] replaced hand-laid conductive thread with FPC
electrodes, raised the readout to 100 Hz, and added GPU tactile
simulation for real-to-sim-to-real fine-tuning. OpenTac [4]
generates resistive skins automatically from robot geometry
and adds a bump overlay that converts shear into differential
pressure across several taxels. Magnetic skins such as ReSkin
and AnySkin [17], [18] offer replaceable three-axis sensing,
and uSkin [19] reports three-axis force at each taxel from mag-
nets above Hall sensors, at the cost of sensitivity to external
magnetic fields. SpikeATac [20] pairs a 4 kHz taxelized PVDF
array with capacitive pads to capture contact onset. LuxTac
keeps the thin, flexible, tileable form of these arrays but
replaces the resistive or magnetic transducer with an optical
one: each taxel reports the local indentation of the elastomer
above it, and the shear cartridge reads shear optically, from
color, rather than from magnets or bump patterns.
Vision-based sensors [6]–[8], [10] image the inside of an
elastomer and recover contact geometry at sub-millimeter
resolution, but need a camera and optical path that make them
thick and difficult to tile. Several groups instead embed dis-
crete emitter–detector pairs: Patel and Correll used commodity
proximity sensors under elastomer to measure distance and
contact force [11], [12], and Piacenza et al. learned contact
location and force from overlapping LED–photodiode signals
in a curved finger [13]. SpectraTac [21] placed three RGBC
color sensors and RGB LEDs under a transparent elastomer
with a black shielding layer and regressed one 3D force
and contact region for the whole sensor from its 12 color
channels with a neural network. These works rely on learned
models of coupled channels. LuxTac uses a regular grid of
independent, digitally read taxels under an absorbing top layer
that blocks ambient light, so each taxel is read and normalized
individually, maps one-to-one onto a simulated taxel, and
reports its own shear.
Simulators for camera-based sensors render the elastomer
image from contact geometry [22]–[24]. For taxel arrays,
FlexiTac converts the penetration of each simulated taxel
into force with a Kelvin–Voigt penalty model whose stiffness
and damping are tuned until the simulated force response
matches the real pad [3]; OpenTac pairs a MuJoCo sensor
model with per-sensor calibration [4]. Because a LuxTac taxel
responds to local indentation, our simulated taxel maps its
penetration depth directly to the normalized signal using the
depth response measured on the real pad, and we compare the
resulting contact patterns with real grasps (Sec. V).

normal deformation, and chromaticity encodes the direction
and magnitude of shear (Sec. IV-C).
B. Electronics
The sensing head is a 21.8 × 23.8 mm region of a four-
layer, 0.2 mm thick flexible printed circuit board (FPCB)
(Fig. 4). The prototype characterized in this paper carries 16
TCS34725 RGBC light-to-digital converters [14] on a rectan-
gular grid with pitches of 4.42 mm (columns) and 4.80 mm
(rows), and nine 0603 chip-scale LEDs arranged in a 3×3 grid,
with each LED centered between four adjacent photodiodes.
Each converter integrates an IR-blocking filter, a photodiode
array, and a 16-bit integrating ADC per channel, so the analog
signal never leaves the package. The converters operate at the
shortest integration time of 2.4 ms and 4× analog gain [14].
The same electronics serve both gel cartridges: with the
normal cartridge only the clear (C) channel is read, and with
the shear cartridge all four red, green, blue, and clear (RGBC)
channels.
All TCS34725 devices share one fixed I2C address, so the
head is connected through a narrow neck to two TCA9548A
eight-channel I2C switches [25], giving each sensor its own
bus segment with dedicated 4.7 kΩpull-up resistors. The
switches communicate with a Teensy 4.1 through two indepen-
dent I2C buses. A single NMOS transistor switch gates all nine
LEDs, which allows LED-off frames for ambient-light checks.
A Teensy 4.1 polls the sensors and streams frames containing
either one clear-channel reading or one set of RGBC readings
per taxel to the host over USB serial at 100 Hz in this work;
the I2C timing of an RGBC scan of all taxels (about 5.5 ms)
allows frame rates up to about 180 Hz (Table I). The complete
FPCB pad and PCB readout board are shown in Fig. 4.

C. Elastomer Stack and Fabrication
The elastomer stack is made in two variants with the
same materials (Fig. 5): Mode 1 (intensity-encoded) has a
continuous white layer under the black film, and Mode 2
(shear-encoded) divides it into one cube per taxel, separated
by colored silicone walls.

Both variants are cast in laser-cut acrylic molds as shown

experiments here use it flat.
1) Mode 1 fabrication: The degassed white translucent
mixture is poured slowly into the assembled mold to minimize
air entrapment as shown in Fig. 5 (a)(2). An acrylic plate is
pressed onto the mold to remove excess silicone and produce a
flat layer of uniform thickness (Fig. 5 (a)(3)). The mold is heat-
cured at 65 ◦C for approximately 20 min (Fig. 5 (a)(4)). The
mold frame is then removed, leaving the white layer bonded to
the acrylic base. The black mixture is poured over the white
layer and allowed to spread across the acrylic, covering the
top and sides of the white translucent layer (Fig. 5 (a)(5)),
and is heat cured for approximately 10 min (Fig. 5 (a)(6)).
The finished Mode 1 stack is shown in Fig. 5 (a)(7).
2) Mode 2 fabrication: Before casting, a grid-like dummy
wall insert is assembled in the mold where three straight
column walls are placed in the frame slots, and three row
walls with 1.25 mm half-lap notches are lowered on top so
that the two sets interlock and hold each other in position
(Fig. 5 (b)(2)). The white translucent mixture is poured around
the walls, leveled, and heat-cured at 65 ◦C for approximately
20 min.
Removing the grid leaves one translucent cube per taxel
separated by 1 mm slits as in Fig. 5 (b)(4). The prepared
pigmented mixtures (green, red, yellow, and blue) are injected
into the slits so that the slits between neighboring columns
alternate green/red and the slits between neighboring rows
alternate yellow/blue as shown in Fig. 5 (b)(5). The rows are
filled and heat cured first for approximately 10 min, followed
by the columns for 10 min (Fig. 5 (b)(6)). The frame is then
removed, and the black coating is applied (Fig. 5 (b)(7)) and
heat cured for 10 min (Fig. 5 (b)(8)), similar to Mode 1.
In both variants, the finished stack is aligned over the

IV. CHARACTERIZATION
A. Setup and Normalization
LuxTac is mounted on a rigid base under a three-axis GRBL
CNC gantry. A six-axis ATI Gamma force/torque sensor sits
between the spindle and the indenter, so it measures the force
applied by the indenter regardless of how LuxTac is mounted.
One script coordinates the gantry, the force/torque stream,
and the LuxTac stream and time-stamps every sample. The
force/torque sensor is tared over 1 s in free air, and the run
aborts if the unloaded force at any retracted position deviates
from this tare by more than 0.15 N. The LEDs are switched on
5 min before each experiment so that LED output and detector
dark current settle.

B. Normal Cartridge
Protocol. A flat circular indenter of 4.5 mm diameter,
roughly one taxel in size, is positioned over each taxel center in
serpentine order and lowered in 0.05 mm steps at 30 mm/min.
After each step it dwells 2 s so that the viscoelastic silicone
settles, and we average the last 0.5 s of force, depth, and
all taxel readings. Loading stops at 2.5 mm or 6 N, and the
indenter then retracts in the same steps to 0.5 mm above the
surface. The full-pad pass is repeated 15 times in five sessions
(240 presses). Because LED output drifts slowly, bi is re-
measured for every press from the unloaded frames just before
it, and xmin
i
is taken at the peak load of the taxel’s own presses
(≈6 N), leave-one-pass-out so that no pass normalizes itself.
All taxels are recorded on every press, which gives the spatial
response and the crosstalk directly.
Response. Raw baselines differ strongly across the array:
2364 ± 284 counts at the center, 1322 ± 203 at the edges, and
673 ± 100 at the corners, which collect scattered light from
fewer LEDs. Every taxel nevertheless responds monotonically
from the first loading step, with a detection threshold (smallest
force with bi−xi > 3σi) of 104±49 mN, limited in part by the
step size. After normalization the response is linear in force
(Fig. 6 (a)): the mean over taxels follows s = 0.02 + 0.170 F
with R2 = 0.999 from 0.1 N to the 5.8 N test limit, and no
taxel’s local sensitivity dsi/dF falls below half its median,
so neither a dead zone nor saturation is reached. Hysteresis,
the largest loading–unloading difference in s at equal depth,
is 5.9 ± 1.1% of full scale, and repeatability, the standard
deviation of s at fixed depth across passes, is 2.0 ± 0.3%
(Table III).

C. Shear Cartridge
Protocol. Three flat indenters are used. A 4.5 mm circle,
about one taxel in size, visits 57 positions: 49 on a 7×7 grid
at half the taxel pitch around the pad center, and eight random
off-grid test positions that are never used for training. At each
position it is lowered until it rests lightly on the gel, where
the force/torque reading is zeroed, pressed in 0.2 mm steps
(0.35 s dwell) to 2 mm or 6 N, and, at normal loads of 2 and
4 N, moved laterally in four directions out to 2 mm and back
in 0.4 mm steps. The sequence was repeated in three sessions
(15,522 hold steps). A 9 mm square repeats it at 33 positions (a
5×5 half-pitch grid and the same test positions) with shear at
up to 8 N in three sessions, the last of which presses positions

We simulate LuxTac so that tactile data can be collected

A. Simulated Sensor

B. Experimental Platform and Tasks
Experiments were conducted on a reBot Arm B601 with a
custom 3D-printed gripper that carries one LuxTac pad on one
finger, and a side-facing RGB camera. The policy receives the
per-taxel pressure signal of LuxTac; shear is not yet used as
a policy input. We consider two tasks.
Peg-in-hole: the robot grasps a peg, transports it to the
insertion location, lowers it into the hole, and releases it, which
requires accurate positioning and controlled contact.
Cube sorting: the robot sorts smooth cubes into a blue bowl
and dotted cubes into a red bowl, which tests whether touch
helps surface discrimination.
For each task, 50 human demonstrations with synchronized
multimodal observations and robot actions were collected on
this platform. Both policies use the default ACT implementa-
tion of LeRobot [28]. We train two ACT policies: a vision-
only baseline using the external RGB image and robot state,
and a visuotactile policy that additionally receives LuxTac
measurements, which isolates the contribution of touch.
C. Results
Success rates are reported with 95% Wilson score intervals,
and the two policies are compared with Fisher’s exact test.
Fig. 9 shows representative executions; the LuxTac heatmaps
show distinct contact responses during grasping that comple-
ment the external view.
For peg-in-hole insertion, LuxTac raised success from 6 to
13 of 15 trials, a significant improvement (Fisher’s exact test,
p = 0.021; Table V). For cube sorting, success changed from
5 to 6 of 15, which is not significant (p = 1.0); distinguishing
smooth from dotted cubes requires resolving surface features
that may be finer than the 4.4–4.8 mm taxel pitch of the current
prototype. Tactile feedback from LuxTac therefore helps most
where contact governs success, as in precision insertion.

VII. DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK
Because LuxTac measures deformation, its force estimate
depends on contact shape: a sharp object indents further than
a flat one at the same force, and a model trained only on
contacts larger than one taxel fails on the one-taxel circle
(Sec. IV-C). Training on several sizes, including the smallest,
closes this gap; for learning, the dependence is also a feature,
since the policy receives the same geometric quantity in
simulation and reality, whereas force control would need a
contact model such as Hertzian contact [29]. Because the
reading follows deformation, pairing it with force also reveals
how compliant the object is, which learned tactile sensing has
used to estimate Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio [30].
The silicone also creeps under sustained load, and our stepped
protocol characterizes the quasi-static response. LED output
drifts with temperature, so the baseline is re-estimated at
the start of each episode. The shear cartridge adds a three-
axis force estimate at every taxel without new electronics;
it has been evaluated with three flat indenters at held-out
positions and sessions, while curved, sharp, and deformable
objects remain to be tested. With the same 4.5 mm indenter,
the colored walls of this first shear cartridge lower the clear-
channel drop at 1.8 mm from 21.9% to 15.9% of baseline
and raise hysteresis from 5.9% to 9.1% FS, partly because
the shear protocol slides the indenter between loading and
unloading, while repeatability stays similar (2.7% vs. 2.0%
FS). The normal cartridge remains the default when only
pressure is needed.
Because a shared model adapts to a new taxel from a
few labeled sequences (Sec. IV-C), larger arrays need not be
calibrated from scratch taxel by taxel. On the prototype, most
taxels lie on the border, so each held-out taxel has a layout
absent from training; arrays with many interior taxels, and
indenters with several tips that label many taxels in one press,
should narrow the remaining gap.
The simulator reproduces the real contact patterns, while
force-controlled grasps, policy training in simulation, and
simulation of shear remain to be evaluated. In future work, we
will characterize larger arrays with more contact shapes and
objects, refine the wall geometry of the shear cartridge, use
per-taxel shear as a policy input on the real robot and use the
simulator to train policies in simulation, including simulated
shear, and transfer them to the real sensor.
VIII. CONCLUSION
We presented LuxTac, a flexible optical taxel array in
which each taxel reports the local indentation of a scattering


11
silicone layer under an absorbing film. Because every taxel
is an independent digital device, the same cell tiles into
larger arrays without changing the readout, and because each
detector also reports color, swapping the gel cartridge turns a
pressure array into one that resolves three-axis force at every
taxel, with no camera, magnets, or bump overlay. The normal
cartridge is linear and uniform after a two-point normalization,
and its depth response transfers directly into simulation; the
shear cartridge estimates normal and shear force per taxel
across contact sizes and sessions; and even pressure alone
substantially improved a learned insertion policy. Next, we will
use per-taxel shear in policies and train them in simulation.
We will open-source all hardware and software, including the
PCB design, fabrication procedure, firmware, characterization
scripts, and simulator integration.
