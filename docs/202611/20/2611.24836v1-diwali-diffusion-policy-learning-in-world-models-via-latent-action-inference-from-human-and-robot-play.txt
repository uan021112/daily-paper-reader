DiWALI: Diffusion Policy Learning in World Models
via Latent Action Inference from Human and Robot Play
Akshay L Chandra1, Iman Nematollahi1, Lingyue Zhao4, Daria Kulikova1,3,
Angela P. Schoellig4, Fabien Despinoy2, Abhinav Valada1
Abstract— Learning robot manipulation is constrained by
the availability of action-labeled robot data, for both imitation
and world models. Humans can demonstrate skills in seconds,
but their demonstrations lack the required robot actions. We
introduce DiWALI, a framework that learns and improves
diffusion policies within a world model trained on human
and robot play. DiWALI infers latent actions from consecutive
frames using an inverse dynamics model, enabling world model
training without action labels, and grounds them in robot
commands through a decoder trained separately on robot
play. Skill policies are trained over latent actions from action-
free human demonstrations and improved with reinforcement
learning in imagination. On the CALVIN benchmark, DiWALI
policies trained solely from human demonstrations substantially
outperform baselines trained with robot demonstrations. Incor-
porating human play into the world model training also yields
stronger policies after fine-tuning than using the same amount
of robot play alone. Experiments on a physical robot show that
skills learned primarily from human demonstrations are further
improved in imagination with a world model trained on human
and robot play. Together, these results demonstrate that human
experience can support both manipulation skill acquisition and
subsequent policy improvement, reducing reliance on action-
labeled robot data. Videos, dataset and code are available at
https://diwali.cs.uni-freiburg.de.
I. INTRODUCTION
Robot manipulation data is fundamentally constrained by
robot time. Demonstrations are typically collected through
teleoperation, with a single operator controlling a single robot
in real time. As a result, every hour of recorded data requires
roughly one hour of both human and robot time [1], leaving
even the largest robot datasets small by the standards of
vision and language. World models [2], [3] learned from play
can stretch this limited data budget. A single play dataset
collected in a scene can support a simulator in which policies
are trained and refined through imagined rollouts [4], [5].
However, both the play data and the demonstrations must
still include robot actions, since the learned dynamics are
conditioned on those actions.
Human demonstrations are not subject to the same robot-
time bottleneck. A person can demonstrate a skill in seconds,
yielding an order of magnitude more demonstrations per hour
than teleoperation. This makes human interaction a far more

We seek a single model that learns from such video and
subsequently serves as a simulator in which a robot policy can
be improved. Two lines of work each address one half of this
challenge. Latent action models [9]–[11] infer transformations
between consecutive frames of action-free video and use them
to pre-train policies, but the learned dynamics serve only as a
training signal rather than as a simulator. Action-conditioned
world models provide such a simulator [12]–[15], but require
action-labeled data. To our knowledge, no existing method
combines both capabilities in a single learned model that is
used to improve a robot policy.
We close this gap with DiWALI, which conditions the
world model on a latent action inferred from two consecutive
observations rather than on a robot command (Fig. 1). An
inverse dynamics model infers this latent action, which the
forward dynamics of a recurrent state-space model use to
predict the next latent state. Both are trained end-to-end

without action supervision, so the latent action captures
whatever aspects of a transition are needed to predict the next
observation, with the same representation used for human
hands and robot grippers. A single model can therefore
learn scene dynamics from both human and robot play. A
small decoder, trained only on robot play and conditioned
on the latent state, grounds latent actions in executable robot
commands, while a diffusion policy is trained and fine-tuned
entirely in the latent action space. The world model thus
serves both as the bridge between embodiments and as the
simulator in which the policy improves.
As imitation takes place in the latent action space, demon-
strations no longer require action labels. A skill shown by a
human is encoded as latent actions, imitated by the diffusion
policy and decoded into executable robot commands. On the
CALVIN benchmark [16], a DiWALI policy trained solely
from human demonstrations outperforms policies trained with
a few robot demonstrations, whether based on a world model
conditioned on ground-truth robot actions or a latent action
model with a jointly trained decoder. Human play further
broadens the world model’s training distribution, enabling
imagination-based fine-tuning to produce stronger policies
than with the same amount of robot play alone. Finally,
on a real robot, policies learned primarily from human
demonstrations improve further through imagination, without
additional real-world interaction.
In summary, our contributions are:
1) A latent-action world model for human and robot
play: a recurrent state-space model driven by an inverse
dynamics model and trained end-to-end without action
supervision, together with an action decoder trained
separately on robot play and conditioned on the latent
state. The ablations show that both design choices are
critical for making the latent actions decodable.
2) Skill policies from action-free human demonstra-
tions: diffusion policies over latent actions that, when
trained solely from human demonstrations, outperform
baselines trained with robot demonstrations.
3) Human play for policy improvement in imagination:
action-free human play is incorporated into the latent-
action world model that serves as the simulator, allow-
ing human experience to shape the imagined rollouts
used for policy improvement rather than only policy pre-
training. Policies fine-tuned in this simulator outperform
those fine-tuned with the same robot play alone.
4) Real-world validation: the full pipeline, from play data
to a fine-tuned skill policy, deployed on a physical robot.

II. RELATED WORK
Learning Robot Manipulation from Human Video: Learning
manipulation from human video requires a representation
that bridges the embodiment gap. Explicit approaches build
it from geometric cues extracted by off-the-shelf perception:
point tracks predicted from video and followed by the
robot [17], [18], object flow used as a cross-embodiment
interface [19], [20], and human hand poses retargeted to a
robot hand [6], [21]. These representations are geometrically
grounded, but inherit perception failures and impose a hand-
designed interface between embodiments.
Implicit approaches instead learn how human video should
transfer to robot behavior. They include visual encoders and
skill embeddings pre-trained on human video [22], [23],
policies co-trained on human and robot data [7], [24], video
generators that predict how a task unfolds [25], and latent
actions inferred between frames. Latent actions can be learned
from arbitrary video, but must later be grounded in executable
robot commands. DiWALI belongs to this family, with one
key distinction: the latent actions learned from human video
are also the world model’s action space, so the model bridging
the embodiments also serves as the simulator.
Latent Action Models: Vision-language-action models [26]–
[29] scale with action-labeled robot data [1]. Latent action
models offer a route beyond this dependence. An inverse
dynamics model infers a pseudo-action between consecutive
frames, and a forward model predicting the next frame
from it keeps the latent informative [30]–[33]. LAPA [9],
UniVLA [11], Moto [34], and villa-X [35] pre-train policies
on such latents from action-free video before fine-tuning on
robot actions. LAWM [36] replaces the pairwise inverse model
with a forward instruction-conditioned policy and LAOF [37]
introduces optical-flow constraints. Closest to our setting,
CLAM [10] learns continuous latent actions with inverse and
forward dynamics and jointly trains an action decoder on
a small amount of labeled data. In these methods, however,
the learned dynamics mainly serve representation learning.
DiWALI instead learns latent actions within a recurrent world
model trained on human and robot play, and trains the
action decoder separately, conditioned on the latent state;
our ablations show both choices are critical for decodability.
Policy Improvement in Imagination: Reinforcement learning
can refine skills acquired through behavior cloning [38]–[40],
but fine-tuning through physical interaction is costly. The
Dreamer family [2], [3], [12], [13] replaces the environment
with learned dynamics and learns behaviors from imagined
rollouts. DITTO and LUMOS [4], [41] learn imitation
policies inside world models trained on robot data, and
DiWA, World4RL, and World-Env [5], [14], [15] post-train
robot policies with reinforcement learning inside offline
world models. D2R [42] extends this to continual learning,
rehearsing earlier tasks in imagination to prevent forgetting.
All condition their dynamics on robot actions, requiring
every training sequence to include them. DGAC [43] instead
learns dynamics from human video, but improves the policy
through real robot failures rather than imagined interaction.
DreamDojo [44] conditions a world model trained on human
video on latent actions, but uses it to evaluate and plan rather
than to improve a policy. DiWALI instead conditions the
world model on inferred latent actions, so human play trains
the dynamics that later serve as the simulator, and the policy
is pre-trained and fine-tuned entirely in latent action space.

IV. DiWALI FRAMEWORK
We introduce DiWALI, which trains in four stages (Fig. 2):
(1) a latent-action world model is learned from robot and
human play, (2) an action decoder is learned from robot play
alone, (3) a diffusion policy over latent actions is pre-trained
on demonstrations, and (4) the policy is fine-tuned within the
world model and deployed through the decoder.
A. Latent-Action World Model
World models turn offline experience into a predictive
model in which behaviors can be learned without environment
interaction [2]. Following DiWA [5], we use a recurrent state-
space model (RSSM) [12]. Separate convolutional encoders
embed the scene and egocentric images into et, a recurrent
state ht summarizes the past, a stochastic posterior zt is
inferred from et while a prior ˆzt is predicted from ht alone,
both categorical with straight-through gradients [45], and a
decoder reconstructs xt from the model state ˆst = (ht, zt).
In action-conditioned world models, the recurrent state is
driven by the robot command at−1, which human play does
not carry. We therefore augment the RSSM with an inverse
dynamics model Iω, a tanh-squashed Gaussian conditioned
on two consecutive embeddings, whose sample replaces at−1:

C. Policy Learning from Action-Free Demonstrations
Skill policies are learned by imitating expert demonstra-
tions, which ordinarily must carry the robot’s actions. This
requirement makes them expensive. With DiWALI, the world
model reads a latent action off any two consecutive frames, so
demonstrations can be imitated in U instead of A, including
human demonstrations, which are cheaper to collect and make
up most of Dexp. Encoding Dexp with the frozen world model
yields, for every frame, a latent state zt and a latent action
ut ∼Iω(· | et, et+1). Robot demonstrations pass through
the same encoding, so their recorded actions are never read.
The diffusion policy πθ(ut | zt) is trained to denoise noise
into these latent actions [46]. Since U is bounded, samples
are clipped to it at no loss. Robot demonstrations therefore
become optional, since a skill shown by a human is executed
by the robot via the inverse model, the policy, and the decoder.
D. Policy Improvement in Imagination
A cloned policy inherits the coverage and quality of its
demonstrations. Reinforcement learning inside the world
model can improve it beyond them without a single real
interaction [5], but only as far as the model’s own coverage
allows. The DiWALI formulation makes that coverage broad,
because the model absorbs cheap human play, whereas a
world model trained on robot play alone can grow only
through costly teleoperation. Improvement requires a task
reward and a policy gradient through the denoising process.
The world model, trained on task-agnostic play, has no task
reward. We label the frames of each expert demonstration
after task completion as successful, train a binary classifier
Cψ(zt) on their latent states with these frames as positives,
and set Rψ(zt, ut) := Cψ(zt+1).

V. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
Our experiments address four questions. (i) Can a
policy trained solely from human demonstrations match
or outperform policies trained with robot demonstrations?
(ii) Which design choices make latent actions decodable
into executable robot commands? (iii) Does human play in
the world model yield better policies after improvement in
imagination than the same robot play alone? (iv) Does the full
pipeline transfer to a physical robot? We answer (i)–(iii) on
the CALVIN benchmark [16] and (iv) in a real-world setup.

A. Simulation Setup
We evaluate in environment D of the CALVIN bench-
mark [16], where a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda manipulates
a tabletop containing a drawer, a slider, a light bulb, an LED,
and colored blocks, observed by a fixed scene camera and a
gripper camera. CALVIN provides six hours of teleoperated
play with action labels, which we split evenly into two disjoint
sets of episodes. The first serves as robot play, DR
play. For
the second, we replace the robot with a rendered human arm
and remove all action labels, yielding our human play DH
play,
which we call CALVIN-H (Fig. 3). Its egocentric view is
rendered from a camera mounted on the human wrist. The
human arm differs from the robot in appearance, occlusion
patterns, and scene shading.
Tasks and data. We evaluate 11 tasks in five groups: open-
ing and closing the drawer, moving the slider left and right,
switching the light bulb and the LED on and off, and pushing,
lifting, and rotating the pink block. All world models are
trained from 64×64 multi-view RGB frames for 900k gradient
steps on batches of 50 sequences of 50 model steps, each
spanning H = 5 control steps. Latent actions have d = 16
dimensions, and robot commands da = 7, an end-effector dis-
placement and a gripper command. Each task provides an ex-
pert set Dexp of 50 human demonstrations and 0, 1, or 5 robot
demonstrations. DiWALI trains its world model on DR
play ∪
DH
play, its action decoder on DR
play, and its policy on Dexp.
Baselines. All methods train the same diffusion policy
and differ only in what it observes, what it predicts, and
the data used to train its representation. Diffusion Policy
(ViT) conditions the policy on multi-view RGB observations
encoded by a vision transformer and denoises robot actions,
without a world model. DiWA [5] instead conditions the policy
on the latent state of a world model trained on DR
play with
ground-truth actions, while likewise denoising robot actions.
We evaluate it both before and after fine-tuning in imagination.
Since both methods predict robot actions, they can imitate
only demonstrations that provide them and therefore train on
robot demonstrations alone. CLAM [10] is re-implemented
within our architecture rather than directly replicated, allowing
us to hold all components fixed except the decoder training. Its
policy denoises latent actions learned through inverse and for-
ward dynamics, while the action decoder is trained jointly with
the world model on DR
play. CLAM-X uses the same architecture
but trains the world model on DR
play ∪DH
play, with the decoder
loss applied to the robot half only. Neither variant conditions
its decoder on the latent state. Like DiWALI, both train
their policy on Dexp and can imitate human as well as robot
demonstrations, all encoded by their frozen world model.
Protocol. Each policy is evaluated from a fixed set of
task-specific initial configurations, taken from the first
frames of CALVIN’s validation episodes, with one rollout
per configuration. Success is determined by CALVIN’s task-
completion criterion, and we report the mean and standard
deviation over three seeds. Diffusion policies use K = 20
denoising steps. Fine-tuning runs PPO for 670 iterations with
50 parallel imagined rollouts, about one million world-model
steps or five million control steps, and updates only the
final Kft = 10 denoising steps, using γdenoise = 0.99 and a
smoothness coefficient of 0.05. The regularization weight αBC
in Eq. (8) is 0.1 for the open-drawer and move-slider-left tasks
and 0.025 for all other tasks, for DiWA and DiWALI alike.
For each fine-tuned policy, the reward classifier is trained
on the same demonstrations used for policy pre-training,
namely human and robot demonstrations for latent-action
policies and robot demonstrations alone for DiWA.

E. Real-World Results
We evaluate DiWALI on a Franka Emika Panda robot
in a tabletop environment containing a cabinet with a
drawer, a sliding door, and an LED controlled by a push
button. The scene is observed by a static RGB camera
and a gripper-mounted RGB camera (Fig. 1). Robot play
comprises two hours of teleoperated interaction collected
with a VR controller. Human play comprises two hours of a
person manipulating the same objects in the same workspace,
recorded by the static camera and a wrist-mounted camera
corresponding to the gripper view, without action labels.
Human motions therefore follow human rather than robot
kinematics, and the wrist-mounted camera differs from the
gripper camera in both placement and viewpoint. We evaluate
three skills: opening the drawer, moving the slider to the left,
and turning on the LED (Fig. 4). For each skill, we collect 50
human demonstrations and five robot demonstrations to train
the diffusion policy and reward classifier through the frozen
world model, while the action decoder is trained on robot
play alone. The world model is trained jointly on both play
datasets and produces accurate long-horizon predictions on
held-out trajectories. We then fine-tune the policies entirely
in imagination for 216K world-model steps, each spanning
five control steps, or about one million control steps.
We evaluate each skill over 20 rollouts from fixed scene
configurations and robot initial states, both before and
after fine-tuning, and report results averaged over three
seeds. Before fine-tuning, policies learned predominantly
from human demonstrations already succeed in 53% of
rollouts. Imagination-based fine-tuning raises this to 70%,
improving performance on every skill. These results show that
DiWALI can improve real-world skills learned from human
demonstrations using a world model trained in part on human
play, without requiring additional physical interaction during
policy improvement.


VI. CONCLUSION
We presented DiWALI, a world model driven by latent
actions inferred from consecutive frames, allowing human
and robot play to train the same dynamics. A decoder learned
from robot play grounds these latent actions in executable
robot commands, while a diffusion policy is trained and
improved entirely in the latent action space. On CALVIN,
policies trained solely from human demonstrations outperform
baselines trained with robot demonstrations. Incorporating
human play into the world model also leads to stronger
policies after imagination-based fine-tuning than using the
same amount of robot play alone. On a real robot, policies
learned primarily from human demonstrations further improve
through imagination, without additional real-world interaction.
Several future directions remain. DiWALI currently learns
from human play collected in the robot’s workspace. Extend-
ing it to in-the-wild human video could expose the world
model to broader experience. Latent actions also require robot
play for grounding, motivating the question of how little robot
data is sufficient. Finally, policy improvement is limited by
a frozen world model. Updating the model with real rollouts
from the fine-tuned policy could further raise this ceiling.

A.1. Hyperparameters and Training Details
Our model’s architecture and training settings generally
follow those of DiWA [5], and we summarize them here
alongside the differences. All values are shared across simu-

A.1.1. World Model
The world model is a DreamerV2-style RSSM [12], as in
DiWA [5] and LUMOS [4]. Separate convolutional encoders
embed the 64 × 64 scene and egocentric RGB images, and
their features are concatenated and fused by a fully connected
layer into the embedding et. The stochastic state zt consists
of 32 categorical variables with 32 classes each, and together
with the 1024-dimensional deterministic state ht it forms a
2048-dimensional model state. We use KL balancing [12]
and reset the recurrent state with a small probability to cope
with the scarcity of episode boundaries in play data.
The inverse dynamics model Iω replaces the robot com-
mand as the input to the recurrent dynamics model. It is
an MLP over the concatenated embeddings (et−1, et) that
outputs the mean and log standard deviation of a Gaussian,
whose reparameterized sample is squashed by tanh into a
d = 16 dimensional latent action ut−1 ∈[−1, 1]16. Iω is
trained end-to-end with the rest of the model through Eq. (5)
and receives no action supervision. Each model step spans
H = 5 control steps. Training sequences of 50 model steps are
sampled from random start indices within play episodes, and
every batch mixes robot and human play. All world models,
including DiWA and CLAM baselines, are trained for 900k
gradient steps. Tab. A.1 lists the hyperparameters. Fig. A.1
and Fig. A.2 show imagined rollouts driven by inferred latent
actions for both embodiments, in simulation and in the real
world, alongside the corresponding ground-truth rollouts.
