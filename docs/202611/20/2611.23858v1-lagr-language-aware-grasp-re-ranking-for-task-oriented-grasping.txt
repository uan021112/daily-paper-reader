## Abstract 

Grasping is a fundamental capability for robotic manipu-lation, with the appropriate grasp depending on the down-stream task. For example, a hammer should be held by its handle for hammering but by its head when it is handed over. Selecting such a grasp requires consideration of two criteria: physical feasibility on the object and suitability for the task. Developing such an approach requires dense grasp labels with both feasibility and task scores on real observations, but existing task-oriented grasping datasets are small, synthetic, or costly to annotate. To address this gap, we construct a dataset of 2.68 million scored 5-DoF grasp annotations over 100 , 911 image-task pairs from 17 

object categories. Additionally, we introduce LAGR, a task-oriented grasp detection method that explicitly addresses both these challenges. Given vision-language inputs, LAGR first generates task-independent grasp proposals along with a physical feasibility score, and then scores the generated grasps with a language-conditioned re-ranker that reasons over all proposals jointly. Experiments show that LAGR outperforms the next best method by 9.3 percentage points. We further validate LAGR on real objects by executing task-oriented grasps with a Franka Research 3 robot.

## 2. Related Work 

Grasping and Affordance datasets . Existing grasping datasets can be broadly categorized based on their super-vision and intended applications. Task-agnostic datasets, including Cornell [11], Jacquard [3], Jacquard V2 [13], and OCID-Grasp [1], provide explicit grasp annotations but do not account for downstream manipulation goals, po-tentially favoring physically feasible yet functionally un-suitable grasps. Language-driven datasets, such as Grasp-Anything [26] and Grasp-Anything++ [25], substantially expand object diversity and language-guided grasp super-vision through synthetic data, but do not explicitly distin-guish physical feasibility from task-dependent grasp suit-ability in real-world observations. Task-oriented 6-DoF grasping datasets, including TaskGrasp [18], LA-TaskGrasp [20], and LaViA-TaskGrasp [21], incorporate object–task relationships and semantic knowledge, but lack dense, image-aligned grasp annotations with independently quan-tified physical feasibility and task-dependent utility. Fi-nally, affordance datasets, such as RAGNet [28], HOVA-500K [15], and HANDAL [6], provide task-relevant in-teraction regions, affordance annotations, or reconstructed object geometry without directly supervising explicit task-conditioned grasp configurations. To bridge these gaps, we introduce a geometry-grounded dataset comprising 2.68 million scored grasps across 100,911 real-world image–task pairs, explicitly associating grasp geometry with physical feasibility and task-dependent suitability. 

Task-oriented Grasping . Existing task-oriented grasp-ing methods can be broadly categorized into three groups based on how they incorporate task information. Seman-tic reasoning-based approaches [7, 14, 19] leverage ob-ject–action relationships, affordance transfer, or geometric decomposition to identify functionally suitable grasp re-gions. However, these methods primarily emphasize se-mantic part selection and do not explicitly optimize grasp-level physical feasibility. Language-conditioned grasp gen-eration methods [22, 25] integrate visual and linguistic rep-resentations to directly predict grasp configurations. While effective at language-guided object localization and grasp synthesis, they do not explicitly separate task-independent physical grasp generation from task-dependent selection among feasible candidates. This can cause the grasp to be semantically appropriate for a task without being physi-cally feasible to execute and vice-versa. Affordance reason-ing approaches [15, 28, 31] employ vision-language mod-els, human demonstrations, and semantic reasoning to iden-tify task-relevant interaction regions. However, region-level affordance predictions do not directly specify executable grasp configurations or quantify their geometric feasibility. In contrast, LAGR explicitly decouples physical grasp gen-eration from task-conditioned selection, enabling joint rea-soning over multiple geometrically grounded grasp propos-als to identify configurations that satisfy both physical and functional needs.

Each observation comprises of an RGB im-age, an aligned depth map, a semantic segmentation mask of the scene, and a handle-part mask identifying the ob-ject’s functional region for grasping. Problem formulation . For each observation–instruction pair (V, q ), where V denotes the multimodal visual observa-tions and q represents a natural-language instruction speci-fying the task, our dataset provides a set of m ∈ N+ valid grasp candidates, denoted by G = {gk}mk=1 and their corre-sponding scores. Grasp generation . We generate candidate grasps eG =

At each sampled location, the gripper closing direction is determined from the object’s orienta-tion, while the grasp center is adjusted via opposing ob-ject boundaries. The estimated local thickness and gripper’s physical constraints determine the required contact depth and gripper opening. A geometrically valid grasp must es-tablish opposing contacts with sufficient surface support, satisfy the gripper’s opening constraint, and maintain ad-equate visibility and depth consistency between the recon-structed mesh and the observed depth map. We additionally reject candidates whose projected finger plates or approach regions exhibit excessive overlap with the object.

Task-specific utility score . For handover, we prioritize grasps on the functional region to preserve re-ceiver’s handle accessibility. Let GF (q) ⊆ G feas denote the set of functional-region grasps retained for task q. When 

GF (q) = ∅, we consider handle grasps as a fallback, as-signing higher utility to configurations leaving a greater por-tion of the handle accessible. 

Task-conditioned Annotation Score . Finally, we com-bine physical feasibility with task utility to obtain the task-conditioned annotation score ak = fk × u(gk, q ). This ensures that the final annotation jointly reflects geometric grasp quality and task suitability, while restricting the an-notated candidates to those that satisfying feasibility con-straints.

First, a task-independent grasp generator processes the multimodal observations using a pretrained visual encoder to generate a diverse set of physically plau-sible grasp candidates. We represent each grasp as an oriented rectangle parameterized by gk =(cy,k , c x,k , θ k, w k, h k), where (cy,k , c x,k ) denotes the grasp center in image coordinates, θk specifies the in-plane ori-entation of the gripper closing axis, wk represents the grip-per opening, and hk denotes the fixed jaw-contact depth. We adopt a 5-DoF image-space grasp representation instead of 3D 6-DoF grasp estimation, as single-view observations provide incomplete 3D geometry due to occlusions, making image-space prediction more directly grounded in the avail-able visual information. The grasp orientation is uniquely defined within the interval [0 , π ) as parallel-jaw grasps are invariant under a rotation of π. Task-independent proposal generator . Given a multi-modal visual observation V, our grasp generator Pϕ predicts a fixed-size set of n grasp proposals and their corresponding physical confidence scores Pϕ(V) = {(gk, p k)}nk=1 .We employ a MultiMAE [2] encoder to extract joint vi-sual representations from RGB images, depth maps, and se-mantic segmentation masks. Modality-specific patch em-beddings are jointly processed by the transformer, allow-ing cross-modal interactions before feature extraction. The resulting modality-associated token representations are re-shaped into spatial feature maps and adaptively fused us-ing learned modality gates. A convolutional fusion module produces a shared feature representation, which is progres-sively upsampled through a multi-scale feature neck. The highest-resolution feature map is used for dense grasp pre-diction, while all three resolutions are retained for subse-quent proposal-level feature extraction. A convolutional prediction head estimates grasp parameters over a spatial grid with discrete orientation bins. For each spatial loca-tion and orientation bin, the head predicts a grasp-center heatmap, physical feasibility, subpixel center offsets, an an-gular residual, gripper opening, and jaw depth. We combine the predicted center likelihood and physical feasibility to obtain the physical confidence of each dense hypothesis. This confidence serves as a task-independent measure of grasp plausibility and is used for proposal selec-tion and subsequent task-conditioned ranking. The dense head produces the grasp hypotheses. We first identify spatial local maxima of the physical-confidence map within each orientation bin and select the top hypothe-ses across all bins. Each selected hypothesis is decoded into an oriented grasp rectangle using its predicted center offsets, orientation bin and angular residual, gripper open-ing, and jaw depth. To promote diversity, we apply greedy suppression based on the spatial and angular proximity of decoded grasps – a candidate is suppressed when both its center distance and angular difference from a previously se-lected proposal fall below predefined thresholds. The re-sulting proposals are ranked by physical confidence, and 4up to n candidates are retained. When fewer than n can-didates remain, the proposal tensor is padded to maintain a fixed size. The resulting grasp proposals and their physi-cal confidence scores are subsequently passed to the task-conditioned reranking module. 

Grasp re-ranker . Given the instruction-independent grasp proposals Pϕ(V) = {gk, p k)}nk=1 and a natural-language instruction q, the task-conditioned reranker predicts the suitability score of each proposal for the specified task. For each candidate gk, we bilinearly sample features at its grasp center from the multi-scale visual representations. To incorporate grasp geometry, we separately encode the normalized grasp center, orientation, gripper opening, jaw depth, and physical confidence. The orientation is repre-sented by (cos 2 θk, sin 2 θk) to account for the π-periodicity. We encode the task instruction using a frozen MiniLM language encoder [27], followed by a learned projec-tion to obtain tq ∈ R256 . To capture interac-tions between grasp characteristics and task semantics, we construct a fused representation using concatena-tion, element-wise multiplication, and subtraction zk =

ψ ([ pk; tq ; pk ⊙ tq ; pk − tq ]) + pk, where ψ denotes a learned fusion network, ⊙ represents element-wise mul-tiplication, and [·; ·] denotes concatenation. pk = vk +Φgeom (rk) where vk is the visual feature vector that is ex-tracted at the center of the kth grasp proposal and Φgeom (rk)

is the learned geometry projection specific to geometry vec-tor rk of kth grasp. The fused proposal embeddings are sub-sequently processed by a proposal-context transformer, en-abling each grasp candidate to be evaluated in relation to the other candidates within the same observation. A prediction head maps each contextualized proposal embedding to a task-compatibility logit lk. The final grasp selection combines the predicted task compatibil-ity with the physical confidence pk, jointly accounting for grasp plausibility and task relevance. A two-layer MLP with a 16-dimensional hidden layer and ReLU activation maps the concatenated inputs [pk, l k] to the scalar rank-ing score Srank (gk, q ). Importantly, the reranker oper-ates exclusively on the generated candidates and does not modify their grasp geometry. The highest-ranked can-didate is selected from the original proposal set k∗ =arg max k∈{ 1,...,n } Srank (gk, q ) such that g∗ = gk∗ , where 

Srank denotes the combined ranking score. 

Loss Function . The grasp proposal generator is trained using a task-independent physical loss that combines a CenterNet-style heatmap objective [32], geometric regres-sion, Gaussian Wasserstein alignment adapted from rotated object detection [29], and feasibility supervision, Lphys =

Lheat + 2( Loffset + Langle + Lsize ) + 2 LGWD + 0 .5Lquality .Here, the heatmap loss supervises grasp localization, re-gression losses refine grasp geometry, Gaussian Wasserstein loss improves rectangular alignment, and the quality loss supervises geometric feasibility. This objective enables the generator to learn physically plausible grasps independently of task semantics. The grasp reranker is trained using a task-conditioned ranking loss, 

Lrank = Lcal + Llist (1) Here, the calibration loss Lcal aligns predicted task-compatibility scores with annotation-derived utility targets, while the listwise loss Llist encourages higher-utility grasps to rank above less suitable candidates. 

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Setup 

Benchmark . We evaluate LAGR on our task-oriented grasping benchmark derived from HANDAL [6], compris-ing 2.68 million scored grasp annotations across 100,911 image–task pairs, spanning 17 object categories and 51 cat-egory–task combinations. The grasp generator predicts 96 

candidate grasps per observation, which are subsequently evaluated by the language-conditioned reranker. 

Training . We train on NVIDIA L40S GPUs for 30 epochs, comprising five proposal-only epochs, five reranker-only epochs, and 20 joint-training epochs. We use AdamW opti-mizer with β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .999 , and weight decay 10 −4.The proposal and reranker learning rates are both 2 × 10 −4.

Real-robot Experiments . We evaluate LAGR in a sim-plified tabletop setting adapted from HANDAL [6], where objects are placed on a table under varying orientations and manipulation instructions. We use a Franka Research 3 arm equipped with a two-finger parallel-jaw gripper for grasp execution. For each object, we consider two distinct manip-ulation tasks and ten object orientations, resulting in 20 im-age–task pairs per object. This controlled setup enables sys-tematic evaluation of grasp prediction and execution across variations in object orientation and task requirements. We report the grasp success rate (SR) as the primary metric.

5.3. Baseline Comparison 

We compare LAGR against representative methods span-ning four complementary approaches to robotic grasp-ing. Task-agnostic grasp detection is represented by SE-ResUNet [30], which predicts grasp configurations based on visual and geometric cues without considering down-stream task requirements. For a fair comparison under task-conditioned evaluation, we apply our language-conditioned reranker to SE-ResUNet’s generated grasp proposals and report the resulting performance metrics. Task-oriented grasping methods, including GATER [19], OS-TOG [7], and ShapeGrasp [14], incorporate task semantics through object–action relationships, affordance transfer, and geo-metric decomposition, respectively. Language-conditioned grasping approaches, including CROG [22], LGD [25], and RealVLG-R1 [12], leverage vision-language representa-tions to predict grasp configurations from natural-language instructions. Finally, affordance-based approaches, in-cluding RAGNet [28], GLOVER++ [15], and A4-Agent [31], emphasize identifying task-relevant interaction re-gions through semantic grounding, human demonstrations, and multimodal reasoning. For approaches that predict only the affordance region (either in mask or heatmap form), we couple them with a simple PCA based grasp estimator that outputs a grasp rectangle similar to other approaches. To-gether, these baselines provide a comprehensive compari-son across physical grasp detection, task-oriented reason-ing, language-conditioned grasp prediction, and affordance localization, allowing us to evaluate the effectiveness of LAGR’s explicit separation of physical feasibility and task-dependent grasp selection. 5.4. Ablation Studies 

We investigate the contributions of our architectural compo-nents, encoder configurations, and input modalities towards LAGR’s performance. Table 2 reports our findings. 

Architectural Components . We ablate key architectural components to assess their contributions to LAGR’s per-formance. Table 2 showcases our results. Selecting grasps solely by physical confidence reduces accuracy from 82.06% to 53.03%, highlighting the importance of task con-ditioning on grasp selection. Removing explicit proposal geometry and the proposal-context transformer decreases accuracy to 74.00% and 77.76% respectively, demonstrat-ing the benefits of geometric representations and joint rea-soning over grasp candidates. Excluding the physical-quality score results in a smaller reduction to 80.79%, sug-gesting that visual and geometric features already capture substantial information about grasp feasibility. 

Reranker Loss Components . We investigate the contribu-tion of individual reranker loss terms to task-conditioned grasp prediction (Table 2). Using calibration loss alone achieves 77.52% accuracy, while incorporating listwise ranking improves performance to 82.24%, highlighting the importance of learning relative preferences among grasp candidates. Adding a margin loss provides negligible im-provement over calibration alone (77.94%) and does not improve performance when combined with listwise rank-ing. Consequently, our final objective (Eq. (1)) retains only calibration and listwise ranking losses, achieving a simpler formulation without sacrificing performance. 

Number of Grasp Proposals . We investigate the effect 7of varying the number of generated grasp proposals ( n)on task-conditioned performance and computational effi-ciency (Table 2). Increasing n from 1 to 64 improves accu-racy from 53.03% to 82.36%, highlighting the importance of providing diverse candidates for task-conditioned selec-tion. Beyond 64 proposals, performance largely saturates, with n = 128 achieving the highest accuracy (82.61%) and 

n = 192 offering no further improvement (82.55%). Mean-while, inference time increases with the number of propos-als, reaching 45.47 ms/sample at n = 192 . LAGR uses 

n = 96 as the default configuration, providing a practical balance between accuracy, and computational cost. 

Encoder Initialization and Adaptation . We investigate the impact of multimodal pretraining and encoder adapta-tion on task-oriented grasp prediction (see Table 2). Full fine-tuning achieves the highest accuracy (85.94%), out-performing the frozen pretrained encoder (82.06%) of pro-posed approach, highlighting the benefits of adapting visual representations to grasp-specific geometry. LoRA adapta-tion yields only marginal improvement (82.61%), suggest-ing that limited parameter updates provide less effective task specialization. Training from random initialization re-duces accuracy to 70.91%, with a further decline to 58.73% when the randomly initialized encoder is frozen. These re-sults underscore the importance of multimodal pretraining and the additional benefits of end-to-end adaptation. 

Input Modalities . We evaluate the contribution of indi-vidual visual modalities and their combinations to task-oriented grasp prediction (Table 2). Combining RGB and depth achieves 81.76% accuracy, closely approaching the full RGB–depth–semantic configuration (82.06%), while reducing inference time from 37.84 to 26.36 ms/sample. RGB-only (76.97%) and depth-only (76.73%) configura-tions perform worse, highlighting the complementary roles of appearance and geometric information. Semantic-only input yields substantially lower accuracy (30.06%), indicat-ing that semantic labels alone lack the fine-grained informa-tion required for precise grasp localization. Adding seman-tics to RGB or depth provides modest improvements, sug-gesting that semantic information offers limited additional benefit when visual and geometric features are available.

## 6. Conclusion 

We introduced LAGR, a task-oriented grasp generation framework that explicitly separates physical grasp feasi-bility from task-dependent suitability. To address the lack of dense task-conditioned supervision, we constructed a dataset comprising 2.68 million scored grasp annotations across 100,911 image–task pairs. Leveraging this dataset, LAGR combines multimodal grasp proposal generation with language-conditioned reranking to select geometri-cally plausible and functionally appropriate grasps. Exten-sive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of LAGR, achieving 85.94% task-conditioned grasp accuracy, with further validation through real-world robotic grasp execu-tion. However, LAGR’s performance depends on the qual-ity and diversity of the generated grasp proposals, and its current formulation is limited to two-finger parallel-jaw grasping.
