## Abstract 

Put a monocular mesh estimator behind every camera of a calibrated rig, and the reconstructed bodies land a meter or more apart. The cause is geometric: each estimate sits at the depth implied by a focal length the network assumed, not the one the camera has. Its error therefore points almost entirely along its own viewing ray, and the calibration tells us that direction exactly. To address this, we introduce the Ray-Lateral Agreement (RLA), which follows one rule: trust the ray, not the depth . RLA first slides every estimate along its own rays, which removes the depth bias and leaves the shape untouched. It then fuses the views one point at a time, under a Gaussian error whose precision follows each view-ing ray. The maximum-likelihood solution is closed-form, interpolates between averaging and midpoint triangulation, and stays well conditioned where triangulation is singular. Robustness is the harder half, because correct views also disagree along their rays. Any weight built on the full resid-ual therefore punishes good views together with bad ones. Ray-lateral agreement instead scores every view, at every point, by its residual across that view’s own ray alone. We evaluated RLA on multi-camera public benchmark datasets. Across two, three, and four cameras and three per-view pose estimators, RLA consistently achieves lower worst-case error than all triangulation baselines. 

## 1. Introduction 

Monocular human mesh recovery has advanced remark-ably. Given a single image, models such as HMR [17], HMR 2.0 [6], and SAM 3D Body [33] can reconstruct a complete, plausible 3D human body. At the same time, applications in gait assessment, sports science, and rehabili-tation are increasingly shifting from marker-based motion-capture studios to setups with a few calibrated consumer cameras [18, 25, 31]. A monocular network converts apparent size into metric depth through a focal length it has to assume, usually a fixed default [19], which real cameras rarely match: 1arXiv:2611.23513v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 each per-view body lands at the wrong distance along its own camera’s rays, by an amount that differs from camera to camera (Fig. 1a). On CMU Panoptic [15], 99.9% of the views place the person farther from their camera than the reference, each by a different amount (Fig. 3). Neither standard remedy resolves this. Averaging the bod-ies inherits the bias. Triangulating their 2D keypoints [7, 8], robustly with RANSAC [4] or reweighting [10], discards the 3D information each network provides, and is most fragile exactly where small rigs operate: with two cameras no test can find a bad view, and nearly collinear rays make the solu-tion degenerate. Learned multi-view fusion [9, 13, 27, 30] needs training data for the target rig and transfers poorly to new camera layouts [1]. Our key observation is that the error of a monocular es-timate has a known direction. Reprojected through its own assumed camera, the estimate fits the image closely: it is accurate across its viewing ray and inaccurate along it, and it stays so after the depth correction (Secs. 4.1 and 4.3). In a calibrated rig, that ray is known for every view and ev-ery point. We turn this into a principle, trust the ray, not the depth , and into a fusion operator, RLA (Fig. 2), that (i) corrects the depth by translating each estimate along its rays, which keeps every point on its observed ray and the body’s extent intact; (ii) fuses the views point by point with an anisotropic precision that trusts each camera fully across its ray and only by a factor λ ≪ 1 along it; and (iii) weights each camera at each point by its agreement with the con-sensus across its ray alone. A depth-ambiguous estimator disagrees along its own ray even when it is right; disagree-ment across the ray is the evidence that a view is wrong. RLA is closed-form and training-free, and costs three 3 × 3

solves per point. It applies to any per-view estimator whose outputs correspond across views, and we test it with three: SAM 3D Body, HMR 2.0 and CameraHMR. For an estimator that is given the true intrinsics, step (i) is the identity. Our contributions are: • an analysis of the focal-induced depth bias: the natural fix, rescaling the depth, compresses the body along its ray and degrades every single view, whereas translating along the ray does not; • RLA, a training-free operator with a maximum-likelihood interpretation, which combines an anisotropic per-view precision with a scale-free, ray-lateral agreement weight for every camera and every point; and • a camera-placement study against DLT, RANSAC, mid-point and reprojection-error triangulation, with the same inputs, camera subsets and held-out parameters for every method: thousands of camera subsets of two CMU Panop-tic sequences, with three per-view estimators, and every camera subset of the Fit3D fitness dataset. Our experiments show that RLA degrades gracefully where triangulation breaks down. With two to four cameras, its worst case is below that of every triangulation baseline on both Panoptic sequences, and its parameters transfer un-changed to the second sequence and to Fit3D, where it avoids the failures of triangulation between cameras that face each other. Ray-lateral agreement also complements RANSAC’s view selection: fused within RANSAC’s consensus set, it has the lowest median error on Panoptic with SAM 3D Body at every camera count we tested. 

## 2. Related work 

Multi-view 3D human pose. Calibrated multi-view cap-ture classically triangulates 2D keypoints [7, 8] or fits a body model to the keypoints and silhouettes of all views [11]. Learned methods replace these steps with trained compo-nents: algebraic and volumetric triangulation [13], cross-view feature fusion [27], epipolar attention [9], camera-disentangled latent codes [28], and volumetric [30], direct-regression [32] or cross-view matching [2] models for sev-eral people. They learn their fusion for the camera layouts they were trained on, and degrade on new ones [1]. In mark-erless biomechanics, OpenCap [31] and Pose2Sim [25] tri-angulate multi-view 2D keypoints to reconstruct 3D motion for musculoskeletal analysis, while studies of commercial markerless systems have evaluated their accuracy against marker-based motion capture [18]. All of these combine 2D evidence across views. We instead combine the complete 3D bodies that a monocular network now predicts in every view, without training for the rig, and study how the result depends on the number and placement of the cameras. 

Monocular mesh recovery and its depth. Monocular methods regress body-model parameters and a camera from a single crop [6, 17, 21, 33]. The true intrinsics are usu-ally unknown, so they assume a weak-perspective or fixed-focal camera, which ties the estimated depth to that assump-tion [19]. CLIFF [23] feeds the crop’s location in the full frame to the network, SPEC [20] estimates the camera, and SAM 3D Body [33] can be conditioned on given intrinsics. These advances improve each single view, yet depth remains its least certain part, because it follows from the apparent size of a body whose true size is unknown. We take the per-view estimates as they are, remove their depth bias with the known calibration, and design the fusion around the ambiguity that remains. 

Robust and uncertainty-aware fusion. RANSAC [4] and iteratively reweighted least squares with a robust loss [10, 12] are the classical answers to outlier views. Weighting each observation by its covariance is standard in photogrammetry and bundle adjustment [5, 29]. Depth-sensor noise models likewise separate the error along the viewing ray from the error across it [24]. Learned multi-view methods predict one scalar confidence per view [13, 34]. A scalar confidence cannot express that a view is reliable perpendicular to its viewing ray but uncertain along it. A fixed anisotropic noise model, in turn, cannot distinguish a consistent view from an outlier. RLA addresses both in closed form. It assigns low precision along each viewing ray and high precision perpendicular to it, and computes a weight for every camera and every point from cross-view agreement measured perpendicular to the camera’s ray. A view is therefore not penalized for its error along its own ray, which is precisely the depth error that dominates monocular estimates.

Baselines. All methods receive identical inputs: the same per-view estimates, corrected rays and calibration. Mean 

and median combine the corrected per-view 3D keypoints. The triangulation baselines use the per-view keypoints re-projected with Eq. (1) and undistorted. The direct linear transform (DLT) [7] solves the stacked linear projection equations of all views by SVD, minimising an algebraic error rather than a geometric one. DLT+IRLS refines DLT by iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) with binary weights: two rounds reject the views whose reprojection er-ror exceeds max(3 × median , 20 px ), reverting to all views whenever fewer than four would remain, so with four or fewer cameras it is plain DLT; IRLS-2 may reject down to two views. RANSAC [4] tries every camera pair as a hypothesis, keeps the largest consensus set within 20 px, breaking ties by total reprojection error, and refits with DLT. DLT+LM and RANSAC+LM refine these solutions by Levenberg–Marquardt minimisation of the reprojection error, over all views and over the consensus set respectively: the maximum-likelihood estimate under Gaussian image noise [7]. Midpoint triangulation is the point closest to the viewing rays in least squares, i.e . Eq. (5) with λ = 0 and equal weights; uniform anisotropic is Eq. (5) with equal weights; RANSAC set + RLA , the hybrid, applies RLA within RANSAC’s consensus set at each point. We also run the stan-dard 2D pipeline: a 2D pose detector, YOLOv8x-pose [14], on the same frames and boxes, triangulated by DLT, by confidence-weighted DLT [13] and by RANSAC (Sec. 6.4).

4.2. The depth bias and its correction 

All methods fuse the same depth-corrected estimates (Sec. 3.1), so we first test this shared step: how large the 5bias is, why we translate rather than rescale, and whether the comparison depends on the correction (full results in Tab. 4 in the supplement). Without correction the per-view bod-ies are far apart: two views place the pelvis 1.8 m apart on average, and averaging two views gives 1,079 mm MPJPE (median over pairs). Both corrections remove the bias, but rescaling the depth by kc also compresses the body along the ray: it raises the PA-MPJPE of every single view, by a median of 13.3 mm and up to 45 mm, and makes the mean of two views 9.1 mm worse. RLA is nearly insensitive to the choice, because it trusts each view’s depth only through λ;we use translation throughout. SAM 3D Body also accepts each camera’s true intrinsics as input, and then needs no correction (Tab. 4, right). This does not place the single views better: our correction of the default output has the lower MPJPE, 78.7 against 85.6 mm, on 28 of the 31 cameras, with a similar PA-MPJPE, and after fusion the two inputs differ by less than a millimetre. With the true intrinsics, DLT still fails badly on a few subsets (worst 220.8 mm with two cameras, 820.2 mm with three), so the triangulation failures in Sec. 4.3 do not come from the assumed focal length.

A 2D detector pipeline. Triangulating the keypoints of a 2D pose detector, YOLOv8x-pose, by DLT, confidence-weighted DLT [13] or RANSAC is the standard multi-view pipeline (Tab. 3). Its keypoints are as accurate in 2D as those of SAM 3D Body (median 9.4 against 8.0 px), but it drops 20.9% of them below its 0.5 confidence, among them 41% of the ankles, which lie outside the image in 44% of the views; we score its triangulations on the joints they produce (coverage 66–91%), which favours them. Even so, its best variant has a median MPJPE of 37.8, 30.4 and 26.8 mm with two, three and four cameras, against 29.4, 25.2 and 22.8 mm for RLA on every joint, and a far higher worst case (1,213.9, 137.4 and 46.6 mm against 56.0, 37.8 and 32.3 mm); confidence weighting changes the median by less than a millimetre. 

Selection and weighting are complementary. RANSAC decides which views to use at each point; RLA decides how far to trust each of them, and in which direction. The hybrid has the lowest median error in both metrics with three and four cameras, beating RANSAC on the same subset in 753 of 1,000 three-camera subsets in MPJPE and 923 in PA-MPJPE (860 and 975 with four cameras; the gain is not a better refit of the set, Sec. 6.4). It inherits RANSAC’s failures, since weights cannot repair a wrong consensus set: its worst case is higher than that of RLA (Spearman ρ = 0 .97 with RANSAC over three-camera subsets, against 0.72 for RLA), although up to the 95th percentile it is the more accurate (30.7 vs. 31.9 mm with three cameras, Fig. 6 in Sec. 6.5).

4.4. Camera placement and count on Fit3D 

Fit3D [3] records fitness exercises with four synchronised cameras in the corners of the capture area, 4.3–4.5 m from the subject, and a reference from marker-based motion capture. We use its 8 training subjects (17,970 frames) and score the 12 limb joints that both skeletons define (Sec. 6.10). Nothing is tuned on Fit3D: RLA and the hybrid reuse the setting selected on Panoptic for each camera count, and RANSAC keeps its 20 px. Table 1 (bottom) shows the results. 

Two cameras. Triangulation fails on the two pairs of cam-eras that face each other across the room, whose rays are within 17 ◦ of collinear: DLT reaches 81.9–95.8 mm MPJPE there, RLA 63.8–65.4 mm. RLA thus lowers the worst pair from 95.8 to 65.4 mm, below midpoint triangulation as well (77.7 mm), and on the other four pairs it is at most 2.3 mm worse than DLT. 

Three and four cameras. These subsets see the subject from well-separated directions, and all methods agree within 0.7 mm in median and worst case; RANSAC rejects only 0.3% of the camera-points. Errors are higher than on Panop-tic mainly because MPJPE here also contains the difference between the COCO keypoints of SAM 3D Body and the anatomical joint centres of Fit3D (Sec. 6.10).

Ablation. Table 2 in the supplement evaluates the con-tribution of each component (variants defined in Sec. 6.4). Anisotropic precision is essential: with uniform weights, depth-corrected estimates achieve 26.5 mm MPJPE using three cameras, while agreement-based weights with isotropic precision ( λ = 1 ) yield 63.4 mm, worse than the unweighted mean (56.9 mm with depth correction; 873.3 mm without). Computing agreement perpendicular to the viewing ray fur-ther improves accuracy over using the full residual: 25.2 ver-sus 26.1 mm with three cameras, and 22.1 versus 23.9 mm with five. This supports the central design principle of RLA: along-ray residuals are dominated by monocular depth errors and are therefore unreliable indicators of view consistency. 

Runtime. Fusing the 18,509 points of a frame takes RLA 53 ms with four cameras and 331 ms with 31 (NumPy, 12th Gen Intel Core i9-12900H), 4.3 and 1.6 times faster than DLT+IRLS; RANSAC and the hybrid take 416 and 54,356 ms (Sec. 6.8). Per-view inference takes 0.25 s per camera and frame on a GeForce RTX 3070 Ti Laptop GPU, the same for every method. 

## 5. Limitations and Conclusion 

Monocular estimators recover plausible 3D bodies but of-ten misestimate depth. RLA exploits the known viewing rays to fuse these estimates without training, using three 

3 × 3 solves per point: trust the ray, not the depth. Across two to four cameras, it reduces worst-case error relative to triangulation baselines on Panoptic and avoids two-camera triangulation failures on Fit3D, while remaining compet-itive with more cameras. RLA requires calibrated cam-eras and consistent mesh topology. It fuses points inde-pendently, without temporal or body-shaped priors. Evalu-ation is limited to two studios, nine Panoptic subjects, and eight Fit3D subjects, with no multi-person scenes. Its me-dian error is comparable to that of RANSAC’s, while the hybrid improves the median but inherits RANSAC’s worst cases.
