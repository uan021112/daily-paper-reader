Abstract
Underwater navigation requires translating visual spatial cues into motion, yet marine vision-language datasets rarely include
paired robot trajectories. We present NereidVLA, a vision-language-action model that transfers marine spatial knowledge to
language-conditioned 3D navigation. We first train Sea2Know on approximately two million marine examples to learn target
grounding, spatial relations, and scene structure without paired robot-motion supervision. We then adapt it using expert trajec-
tories executed under simulated underwater vehicle dynamics. Given RGB histories and a language instruction, NereidVLA
predicts discrete motion tokens that decode into local 3D trajectories for closed-loop tracking. Evaluation combines an inde-
pendent six-dimensional underwater navigation understanding benchmark with offline prediction and closed-loop navigation.
Under matched navigation training and execution settings, marine spatial pretraining increases simulation success from 72.4%
to 84.4%. Without fine-tuning on the real test environment, NereidVLA achieves 78.75% success in laboratory-pool robot
trials, compared with 47.50% for InternVLA-N1. These results show that marine spatial supervision can support downstream
motion learning and physical underwater navigation.

Introduction
Language-guided underwater navigation requires robots to
interpret a goal in the context of three-dimensional scene
geometry.
Following a pipeline requires understanding
its direction and the obstacles nearby, while cave naviga-
tion depends on recognizing entrances and the geometry of
traversable passages.
These decisions involve changes in
depth as well as horizontal motion: recognizing the target
is only one part of determining how to reach it. Turbidity,
light scattering, changing illumination, and occlusion further
complicate these spatial judgments [1].
Vision-language-action (VLA) models connect visual un-
derstanding and language to robot behavior [13, 22, 35], and
recent work has begun to explore this connection in under-
water environments [9, 25, 29]. Meanwhile, marine vision-
language datasets provide rich annotations for objects, spatial
relationships, and scene structure [28, 34]. These resources
offer an opportunity to learn about the underwater world, but
rarely include paired robot trajectories. Collecting underwa-
ter navigation demonstrations remains costly and difficult to
scale, and spatial understanding alone does not specify how
a robot should move. We therefore investigate a central ques-
tion: Can spatial knowledge acquired from large-scale ma-
rine observations be transferred to language-conditioned 3D
underwater navigation?
We propose NereidVLA, an underwater VLA model that
connects marine spatial learning with trajectory-supervised
navigation adaptation (Figure 1).
The first stage estab-
lishes spatial knowledge before learning robot motion. Us-
ing available annotations and question-answer formats from
NAUTILUS [28] and USI-Brain [34], together with under-
water simulation data, we construct approximately two mil-
lion marine spatial-understanding examples. We use these
examples to train Sea2Know, built on Qwen3-VL-8B [4], to
learn target grounding, spatial relationships, and scene struc-
ture. This stage makes marine observations useful for navi-

The second stage turns this spatially informed model into
a trajectory predictor for closed-loop navigation. Using 573
simulated scenes in MarineGym [7], we collect 5,117 ex-
pert demonstrations and derive approximately 100,000 navi-
gation examples spanning obstacle avoidance, target search,
pipeline navigation, and cave navigation.
An expert con-
troller executes geometric reference plans under simulated
vehicle dynamics, and the resulting robot motion supplies
the trajectory targets. We use these demonstrations to adapt
Sea2Know into NereidVLA, encoding local body-frame dis-
placements as motion tokens. Given RGB histories and lan-
guage instructions, the model autoregressively predicts local
3D trajectories, which a low-level feedback controller tracks
as visual observations are updated and the trajectory is re-
planned.
Our evaluation examines both the spatial knowledge
learned in the first stage and its value for navigation.
On a six-dimensional underwater navigation understanding
benchmark strictly disjoint from the spatial training data,
Sea2Know outperforms its backbone across all six dimen-
sions. NereidVLA improves offline trajectory prediction over
SimpleVLN [11] in unseen scenes. Under matched down-
stream training and execution settings, Sea2Know initializa-
tion increases closed-loop simulation success from 72.4%
to 84.4% compared with direct navigation fine-tuning of
Qwen3-VL-8B. On a BlueROVHeavy robot, NereidVLA
achieves a 78.75% task success rate in laboratory-pool trials
without fine-tuning on the test environment, compared with
47.50% for InternVLA-N1.
Our main contributions are as follows:
1. We propose NereidVLA, a two-stage underwater VLA
framework that transfers marine spatial knowledge to
language-conditioned local 3D trajectory prediction and
closed-loop navigation.
2. We construct a diverse underwater navigation dataset
from expert demonstrations executed under simu-
lated vehicle dynamics, and introduce an independent
six-dimensional underwater navigation understanding
benchmark.
3. We demonstrate improvements in offline trajectory pre-
diction and closed-loop navigation in simulation and on
a real robot. Matched comparisons establish the bene-
fit of marine spatial pretraining, while laboratory-pool
trials demonstrate transfer to physical execution without
test-environment fine-tuning.

Related Work
Underwater vision-language understanding. NAUTILUS
introduces NautData for underwater captioning,
visual
question answering, grounding, and detection, alongside
an imaging-prior-based visual feature enhancement mod-
ule [28].
USI-Brain develops spatial supervision cover-
ing object understanding, spatial relations, depth reasoning,
degradation-aware perception, and scene understanding [34].
UWBench evaluates underwater captioning, grounding, and
visual question answering, while MarineEval focuses on
marine-domain knowledge and reasoning [26, 31]. These ef-
forts advance underwater visual understanding and evalua-
tion but do not provide paired robot-motion supervision. We
use NAUTILUS and USI-Brain data to train Sea2Know and

introduce an independent benchmark for navigation-relevant
spatial judgments.
Vision-language-action learning and spatial reasoning.
OpenSpatial generates spatial supervision grounded in 3D
object boxes [15], while SpatialAxiom [16] and SenseNova-
SI [6, 18] advance general-purpose spatial reasoning. Zhu et
al. improve motion-grounded action understanding through
action-centric video–text alignment [33]. RT-2 represents ac-
tions as tokens and draws on vision-language and robotic
data [35], while OpenVLA provides an adaptable open-
source VLA model [13]. SpatialVLA introduces Ego3D en-
coding and adaptive action grids for spatially grounded action
prediction [22], and FAST explores efficient action-sequence
tokenization [19]. For navigation, SimpleVLN adapts multi-
modal models using visual histories and action chunks [11],
while InternVLA-N1 combines high-level goal planning with
low-level execution [12]. We examine how domain-specific
spatial knowledge acquired before navigation adaptation sup-
ports language-conditioned local 3D trajectory prediction.
Underwater language-conditioned behavior learning. Un-
derwaterVLA combines high-level task reasoning with low-
level control [25]. USIM provides automatically collected
underwater demonstrations, while U0 incorporates relative-
pose supervision for learning underwater behaviors [9].
AquaCap uses executable control programs and feedback-
based replanning [14], while AQUAACT contributes tele-
operation demonstrations and closed-loop policy evalua-
tion [27]. OceanGym supports language-grounded percep-
tion and sequential decision-making in underwater environ-
ments [29]. These approaches explore complementary as-
pects of underwater behavior and execution. NereidVLA fo-
cuses on transferring marine spatial knowledge to 3D trajec-
tory prediction through dynamics-grounded demonstrations,
followed by closed-loop navigation.
Underwater navigation and simulation.
Prior work in-
vestigates online 3D path replanning under motion con-
straints [10] and mapless obstacle avoidance through rein-
forcement learning [30].
HoloOcean provides underwater
vehicle and sensor simulation [21], while OceanSim ad-
vances underwater optical and sonar rendering [24]. Marine-
Gym supports GPU-parallel simulation, hydrodynamic mod-
eling, and domain randomization [7]. Using MarineGym,
we construct diverse navigation scenes and collect expert
trajectories under simulated vehicle dynamics.
Geometric
paths guide the demonstrations, but the trajectory supervision
comes from executed robot motion rather than the planned
paths themselves.

3.1
Problem Formulation and Overview

as local 3D trajectory prediction, covering obstacle avoid-
ance, target search, pipeline navigation, and cave navigation.
At decision time t, the policy receives four historical front-

expressed in the robot body frame fixed at decision time t.
The VLA uses only visual observations and language instruc-
tions; robot state estimates are used for trajectory supervision
and low-level feedback control, rather than as policy inputs.
Figure 1 summarizes our two-stage framework. We first
learn underwater spatial knowledge in Sea2Know, then ini-
tialize NereidVLA, our navigation model, with the learned
parameters and adapt it using expert trajectories. The pre-
dicted local trajectory is decoded into 3D waypoints and
tracked by a low-level controller under closed-loop visual
feedback (Figure 2).
3.2
Marine Spatial Knowledge Learning
The first stage learns navigation-relevant spatial knowledge
from marine visual data without paired robot trajectories. We
construct approximately two million vision-language training
examples using resources from NAUTILUS [28] and USI-
Brain [34], together with underwater simulation scene data.
We reuse available annotations, including object categories
and bounding boxes, and draw on existing marine question-
answer formats to organize supervision for target grounding,
relative spatial relations, depth ordering, and scene structure.
For sequential observations, we also construct multi-frame
questions about changes in target position, visibility, and ap-
parent size. Together, these examples provide supervision for
both static spatial relationships and temporal changes in un-
derwater scenes.
We train Sea2Know through full-parameter supervised
fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-Instruct backbones at 2B, 4B, and
8B scales [4]. This stage learns visually grounded spatial and
temporal representations without paired robot trajectories or
dynamics supervision. The resulting Sea2Know (8B) pro-
vides the initialization for subsequent trajectory-supervised
navigation adaptation.
3.3
Navigation Demonstration Generation
We build a navigation demonstration pipeline on Marine-
Gym [7], covering five environment families: seabeds, fixed
underwater facilities, pipelines, aquaculture cages, and caves.
Each environment varies task goals, obstacle layouts, and
traversable structures, together with water and lighting con-
ditions.
Expert references are generated according to the geometry
of each task. For obstacle avoidance and target search, we use
3D path search and collision checks to obtain feasible routes
around obstacles or toward targets. For pipeline and cave
navigation, reference paths additionally follow pipe axes or

3.5
Closed-loop Trajectory Execution
We execute navigation through receding-horizon prediction
and feedback tracking. After following a short segment of the
predicted five-second trajectory, the robot updates its visual
observations and replans. To account for inference latency,
the predicted trajectory is transformed into a fixed local exe-
cution frame using the estimated robot pose at image capture
time.
A low-level controller interpolates the predicted waypoints
and tracks them through state estimation, PID feedback, and
dynamics feedforward.
Since the VLA predicts positions
rather than control commands, the execution module deter-
mines attitude and heading references under vehicle motion
constraints and declared heading rules, and allocates com-
mands to eight thrusters. In simulation, replanning occurs at
1 Hz of simulated time.
4
Experiments
We evaluate Sea2Know on underwater spatial understand-
ing and NereidVLA on offline 3D trajectory prediction and
closed-loop navigation in simulation and on a real robot. We
also assess the contribution of marine spatial pretraining.
4.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and evaluation protocols.
Using MarineGym [7]
and high-fidelity underwater assets, we construct a train-
ing pool of 573 simulated scenes and collect 5,117 expert
demonstrations executed under simulated vehicle dynam-
ics.
The demonstrations cover obstacle avoidance, target
search, pipeline navigation, and cave navigation. We extract
100,000 training samples from 4,597 demonstrations across
516 scenes, with 18,726 samples for validation. Offline pre-
diction uses approximately 10,000 test samples. Both offline
testing and closed-loop simulation use 50 independent scenes
held out from the navigation training pool, with unseen high-
fidelity assets, layouts, and structures.
Our six-dimensional Underwater Navigation Understand-
ing Benchmark covers target grounding (TGA), spatial rela-
tions (SRA), passage structure (TSA), traversability predic-
tion (TPA), temporal understanding (TUA), and local action
decisions (LADA). TPA and LADA assess visual judgments
of traversable space and plausible local actions, without im-
posing robot-specific motion constraints. Three trained an-
notators independently label 4,500 candidate questions and
cross-check the annotations, retaining 3,646 after filtering
disagreements and low-quality items.
The benchmark is
strictly disjoint from the Stage 1 training data. Annotation
details and additional NautData and UWBench [28, 31] re-
sults are provided in the supplement.
For closed-loop simulation, we evaluate 25 distinct navi-
gation instances for each of the four tasks. Each instance is
tested under five lighting and water conditions, giving 500
executions per model. Models tested on the same robot use
the same low-level controller and evaluation conditions.
Baselines and adaptation.
For spatial understanding,
Sea2Know is evaluated after Stage 1; comparison models
are not adapted on our navigation data, and Qwen3-VL uses
its original Instruct checkpoints. For offline prediction and
closed-loop navigation, all baselines use the same navigation
training samples as NereidVLA, except U0 and π0.5-USIM,
which retain their original underwater checkpoints for trans-
fer evaluation. For the initialization ablation, Qwen3-VL-
8B uses matched downstream training and execution settings.
Model-specific details are provided in the supplement.
Metrics and implementation.
We report accuracy for spa-
tial understanding; ADE, FDE, Z MAE, ADE P95, and FDE
P95 for trajectory prediction; and success rate (SR), Grid-
SPL (based on SPL [2]), and Jerk RMS for closed-loop navi-
gation. Grid-SPL measures success-weighted path efficiency
against robot-aware A* reference paths on a 0.25-m 3D grid;
Jerk RMS measures translational motion smoothness. Details
are provided in the supplement.
In Stage 1, Sea2Know models (2B, 4B, and 8B) are initial-
ized from size-matched Qwen3-VL-Instruct backbones and
trained with full-parameter vision-language supervised fine-
tuning, jointly updating the visual encoder, vision-language
merger, and language model. In Stage 2, NereidVLA is ini-
tialized from Sea2Know (8B) and trained with autoregressive
trajectory supervision. We freeze the visual encoder while
fully updating the language model, token embeddings, and
LM head without LoRA. Further details are given in the sup-
plement.

4.5
Ablation and Analysis
Effect of marine spatial pretraining.
To isolate spatial
pretraining, we compare Sea2Know-initialized NereidVLA
with Qwen3-VL-8B fine-tuned directly on the same 3D
trajectory demonstrations, using matched representations,
supervision, and execution settings.
As shown in Ta-
ble 2, Sea2Know initialization reduces ADE from 0.29121 to
0.18082 (37.9%) and FDE from 0.55396 to 0.34970 (36.9%).
The gains extend to closed-loop navigation, where simulation
success increases from 72.4% to 84.4% and real-robot suc-
cess from 35.0% to 78.75%. These results show that marine
spatial pretraining improves 3D trajectory learning beyond
what is achieved through direct navigation fine-tuning alone.
Task-dependent navigation gains.
The benefits of spa-
tial pretraining vary across tasks.
In simulation, success
improves by 29.6 percentage points in target search and
15.2 points in pipeline navigation, compared with 1.6 points
in both obstacle avoidance and cave navigation.
Target
search requires updating the relative direction of a language-
specified goal as views change, while pipeline navigation re-
lies on structural direction and nearby free space. These de-
mands align with Sea2Know’s gains in spatial relations and
temporal understanding.
5
Conclusion
We presented NereidVLA, a two-stage framework connect-
ing marine spatial understanding with language-conditioned
3D underwater navigation.
Sea2Know learns navigation-
relevant spatial knowledge from large-scale marine obser-
vations; we then adapt it for 3D motion prediction us-
ing dynamics-grounded demonstrations. Encoding 3D tra-
jectories as discrete motion tokens enables autoregressive
prediction from visual histories and language instructions,
with feedback tracking for closed-loop execution.
Eval-
uations spanning spatial understanding, offline prediction,
simulation, and real-robot navigation validate this approach.
With matched downstream settings, Sea2Know initialization
improves simulation success from 72.4% to 84.4%, while
NereidVLA achieves 78.75% real-robot success without test-
environment fine-tuning.
These results demonstrate that
marine spatial knowledge can be effectively transferred to
language-conditioned 3D trajectory prediction and closed-
loop underwater navigation.

B
Data Construction and Understanding
Benchmark
B.1
Marine spatial training data
Sea2Know is trained on approximately two million ma-
rine vision–language examples from NAUTILUS [28], USI-
Brain [34], and underwater simulation scenes. Existing an-
notations and question–answer formats provide supervision
for target grounding, spatial relations, depth ordering, and
scene structure. Multi-frame questions additionally describe
changes in target position, visibility, and apparent size. None
of these examples requires paired robot trajectories.
The Underwater Navigation Understanding Benchmark is
disjoint from Stage 1 training. The external NautData test
split is also excluded.
B.2
Understanding benchmark
Our benchmark evaluates six navigation-related judgments
(Figure 3): target grounding (TGA), spatial relations (SRA),
passage structure (TSA), traversability prediction (TPA),
temporal understanding (TUA), and local action decisions
(LADA). TUA uses four-frame observations.
TPA and
LADA assess visually plausible passage and action choices,
rather than vehicle motion under dynamics constraints.
Three trained annotators independently labeled 4,500 can-
didate questions and cross-checked the annotations. After re-
moving disputed and low-quality items, 3,646 remain. Accu-
racy follows the answer format of each question. For multi-
answer LADA questions, a prediction is correct only if its
selected options exactly match the reference set.
B.3
Navigation demonstrations
We construct navigation scenes in MarineGym [7] across five
environments: seabeds, fixed underwater facilities, pipelines,
aquaculture cages, and caves. The training pool contains 573
scenes and 5,117 expert demonstrations. From 4,597 demon-
strations in 516 scenes, we extract 100,000 training samples,
with 18,726 additional validation samples. Offline evalua-
tion uses approximately 10,000 test samples; both offline and

C
Training and Baseline Adaptation
C.1
Stage 1: Sea2Know training
We train Sea2Know at 2B, 4B, and 8B scales through full-
parameter supervised fine-tuning of size-matched Qwen3-
VL-Instruct backbones. The vision encoder, vision–language
merger, and language model are all updated. Sea2Know (8B)
initializes the navigation model.
C.2
Stage 2: navigation adaptation
Given four RGB observations and a language instruction,
NereidVLA predicts ten motion tokens followed by EOS,
representing a five-second local 3D trajectory. We freeze the
vision encoder and fully update the language model, token
embeddings, and LM head, without LoRA. The loss is cross-
entropy over the motion tokens and EOS; visual and text con-
text tokens are masked. We use neither coordinate-regression
nor reasoning-text supervision.
Training uses 100,000 samples for one epoch (391 opti-
mizer steps) on 32 NVIDIA A800 80GB GPUs across four
nodes. A microbatch of one per GPU and eight gradient-
accumulation steps give a global batch size of 256. We use
AdamW (β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999; weight decay 0.01) with a
learning rate of 10−5, linearly warmed up over 80 steps and
then held constant. Training uses BF16 and fully sharded
data parallelism (FSDP). A fixed subset of 1,024 validation
samples is used for monitoring.
C.3
Motion tokenization
The ten future positions are expressed in the forward–left–
up body frame fixed at decision time. We cluster displace-
ments from the navigation training set into a fixed codebook
of 1,024 entries. At each step, the token is chosen from the
residual between the target position and the previously re-
constructed waypoint, allowing subsequent tokens to correct
quantization errors. At inference, cumulative codebook dis-
placements recover the local 3D trajectory. The encoding
equations are given in the main Method section.
C.4
Baseline adaptation
For offline trajectory prediction and closed-loop navigation,
Qwen3-VL (2B, 4B, 8B, and 32B), SimpleVLN, InternVLA-
N1, and NAUTILUS are adapted using the same navigation
training samples as NereidVLA. U0 and π0.5-USIM are eval-
uated in closed-loop simulation from their original under-
water checkpoints, without additional navigation fine-tuning.
These comparisons share training data where applicable, but
not necessarily model architectures or optimization settings.
To isolate spatial initialization, we compare NereidVLA
with Qwen3-VL-8B trained directly on the same trajectory
data under matched trajectory representation, supervision,
and execution settings. Only the initialization differs. For
the spatial-understanding benchmark, comparison models are
not adapted on our navigation data; Qwen3-VL uses its orig-
inal Instruct checkpoints.

D.2
Closed-loop simulation protocol
We evaluate 25 distinct instances of each of the four naviga-
tion tasks under five water and lighting conditions, totaling
500 runs per model. The model replans at 1 Hz of simu-
lated time. A run succeeds if the vehicle reaches the task
goal within the time limit and without violating safety con-
straints. Collisions and timeouts count as failures. Success is
determined from executed motion, not predicted waypoints.

E
Physical Robot System and Evaluation
E.1
Hardware and execution
We deploy a BlueROVHeavy with eight thrusters in a labo-
ratory pool. RGB observations from a RealSense D435i are
processed asynchronously on a remote RTX 4090 server. The
predicted waypoints are tracked by a low-level controller us-
ing IMU-supported state estimation, PID feedback, and dy-
namics feedforward.
To account for inference latency, each trajectory is trans-
formed into a fixed execution frame using the estimated robot
pose at image-capture time. The robot follows a short seg-
ment before acquiring new observations and replanning. No
data from the real test environment are used for fine-tuning.
E.2
Tasks and success criteria
We test four tasks with 20 trials per task and model, varying
target positions and obstacle layouts. Success requires com-
pleting the task without collision. Target approach (TA) and
goal approach with obstacles (GA) require reaching within
1.5 m of the target. Narrow-passage avoidance (NP) requires
crossing the specified endpoint; obstacle avoidance (OA) re-
quires reaching its assigned objective. All models share the
low-level controller and evaluation conditions. Results are
reported in Table 4.
