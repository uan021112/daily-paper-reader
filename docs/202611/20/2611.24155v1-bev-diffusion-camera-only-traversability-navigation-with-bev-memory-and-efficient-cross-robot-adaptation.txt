# BEV-Diffusion: Camera-Only Traversability Navigation with BEV Memory and Efficient Cross-Robot Adaptation 

Iana Zhura, Timofei Kozlov, Dmitrii Plotnikov, Ivan Valuev, Miguel Altamirano Cabrera, Dzmitry Tsetserukou 

Abstract —Robot navigation requires both an understanding of the scene and an efficient adaptation to new robots, yet current methods rely on expensive LiDAR sensors, long mapping and modular pipelines. We present BEV-Diffusion, a camera-only model that learns a bird’s-eye view (BEV) traversability map, its uncertainty and the path in a single network. A frozen DINOv2 backbone encodes consecutive RGB frames, which inverse perspective mapping and Bayesian fusion lift into the BEV, and a ranking loss keeps outdoor traversability graded rather than binary, separating asphalt from grass (median 0.94 vs. 0.75). On this map, a transformer denoiser generates polar waypoints in a few DDIM steps, and uncertainty-aware guidance refines the path and ends it where the map is no longer reliable. At deployment, a metric BEV memory keeps obstacles after they leave the camera view and warm starts each sample from the previous, ego-motion compensated path. Because the planner jointly learns the path, the map, and its uncertainty, it selects routes through terrain it can confidently traverse. In an unseen courtyard, a wheeled-legged robot autonomously traveled 120 m, completing 96% of the route without intervention, compared with 75–88% for the baselines. Even under low-light conditions after sunset, the proposed model completed the route without requiring any intervention. Retraining only the BEV head, about 3% of the parameters, adapts the model to a small legged robot in 2.3 min. Indoors, the memory gets this robot out of maze-like traps: it retains 62–66% of the nearby out-of-view obstacles, against at most 3% for the baselines, and the robot achieves 67–100% task progress, compared with 20–40% for the evaluated baselines. 

Index Terms —Visual Navigation, Diffusion Models, Bird’s-Eye-View Representation, Traversability Estimation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Legged robots are increasingly deployed in search and res-cue, inspection and exploration [1], where they must navigate unknown terrain without a prior map. This requires estimating robot-specific traversability and generating feasible paths from visual observations. For legged robots, the ability to step over and climb obstacles depends on the body, so that the same terrain can be crossable for one robot and blocking for another. Geometric approaches provide physically grounded terrain information, but they rely on depth sensing or LiDAR and often on separate mapping and planning stages [2], [3]. To avoid this, diffusion policies generate paths directly from visual experience. However, they either plan from images without a notion of robot-specific traversability [4], need depth to recover metric waypoints [5], or again rely on a map [6]. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Traversability Estimation 

Traversability tells a robot which terrain it can cross and which it should prefer, and thus which paths are safe. It is usually predicted in the image by a perception module and then projected into a map built from LiDAR or depth point clouds and the robot’s pose, on which a separate planner operates, as in WVN [2]. Geometric traversability methods estimate terrain suitability from slope, roughness, and clearance [3]. Learning-based approaches such as WVN and WayFAST [8] incorporate robot experience but rely on generalization beyond previously traversed regions. Many self-supervised methods, moreover, learn traversability as binary, labelling the ground the robot has walked on as traversable and the rest as not [9]. A binary map only separates free space from obstacles, whereas graded traversability also lets the planner prefer easier terrain, such as asphalt over grass, when several paths are free. Language-guided methods, including AnyTraverse [10] and EmbodiedDiffusion [5], introduce such semantic terrain pref-erences. BEV-Diffusion instead predicts graded traversability directly in the BEV from the camera, without a point cloud map, combining geometric and semantic supervision, with uncertainty estimates used during path refinement. 

B. BEV Reconstruction 

A camera observes the scene in perspective, while a ground robot moves and plans on the ground plane, where distances, free space, and its own footprint are metric. A bird’s-eye view (BEV) is a top-down projection of the scene onto this plane: maps from consecutive frames can be fused in it and paths planned on it directly, but lifting image features into it is ill-posed without depth. BEVFormer [11] learns spatial and temporal BEV features through attention, and SkyEye [12] learns BEV semantic maps from frontal view labels alone through a shared voxel representation. These methods, however, focus on perception rather than trajectory generation. TrajDiff [13] couples the two for autonomous driving: a diffusion transformer generates trajectories from BEV features that are supervised only by a heatmap of the future ego path. Its main model fuses camera and LiDAR at test time, and its BEV encodes where the vehicle drove rather than which terrain is traversable, without adaptation to a new robot. BEV-Diffusion instead predicts a BEV map of robot-specific, graded traversability from frontal RGB features, fuses it over time on top of planar IPM and plans on it. It uses LiDAR only to generate training labels and navigates from the camera alone. 

C. Diffusion Based Navigation 

Once traversability is estimated in a spatial representation, the next challenge is generating feasible trajectories from it. A scene often admits several valid paths, for example on either side of an obstacle, and a regressed path can average them into one that crosses the obstacle. Diffusion models [14], [15] instead learn the full distribution of paths, and their iterative sampling can be guided at test time by a cost such as collision or traversability, without retraining. NoMaD [4] learns goal-masked visual navigation policies, while DiPPeR [6] and SketchPlan [16] generate trajectories conditioned on spatial maps or sketches, and NaviDiffusor [17] incorporates collision-aware gradient guidance. EmbodiedDif-fusion [5] removes the mapping stage altogether by guiding diffusion with traversability predicted in the image, but it plans in the image plane, so it needs depth to recover metric waypoints and loses obstacles once they leave the camera view. BEV-Diffusion keeps this mapless design but learns traversability and paths jointly in the BEV, so its paths are metric without depth, are refined by the map uncertainty, and a metric BEV memory keeps obstacles after they leave the view. D. Trajectory diffusion 

The diffusion runs on standardised coordinates z0 = ( x0 −

μ)/s, with the mean and standard deviation of the training paths, so that the waypoint steps are not lost in the noise of the last DDIM steps. A DDPM [14] with Tdiff =1000 steps and a linear β schedule from 10 −4 to 0.02 adds the noise. The denoiser ϵθ is a Transformer with 4 layers, a width of 512 and 8 heads (26M parameters). Each waypoint token combines its noisy coordinates, the BEV feature sampled at these coordinates and a temporal context from the frame tokens. Each layer applies self-attention over the waypoints, cross-attention to the past trajectory tokens and gated cross-attention to the BEV tokens and to the start and goal anchors. 

E. BEV memory 

The predicted map covers only the camera view, so a passed obstacle vanishes from it. At deployment, an ego-centric metric grid of traversability log-odds L keeps it (for 4

the small robot, 2 cm cells from 1.5 m behind to 2.5 m ahead and ±1.5 m to the sides). Each step moves the grid by the ego-motion, estimated from the images alone: SIFT features [21] matched between consecutive frames are projected onto the floor with Eq. (2), and a rigid planar motion is fitted with RANSAC [22], which rejects points on walls and furniture. The grid then decays towards unknown, more slowly while the robot stands still, and fuses the new map in the camera view as L ← L+η (1 −u/ log 2) logit p, so that uncertain cells count less. The memory does not enter the denoiser. Instead, it warm starts each sample from the previous path, moved by the ego-motion and noised to 35 % of the diffusion time, unless the current map contradicts it. It also fills the camera blind zone, keeps the path straight until the body has passed remembered obstacles, and, when the robot makes no progress, turns it in place towards remembered free space.

H. Adaptation to a new robot 

To transfer the model to a new robot, we keep all weights of the source model and retrain only the traversability head, 1.1M of the 40M trained parameters, on the labels of the target robot with Lbev , Lσ and Lpath . The geometry is recomputed for the target robot, including the camera model, the projection of Eq. (2) and the BEV extent, while the frozen denoiser keeps the path statistics (μ, s) of the source robot. 

I. Inference 

We generate goal-free paths using five deterministic DDIM steps [24]. Predicted waypoints are refined through traversability- and uncertainty-based gradient guidance, fol-lowed by corridor snapping using the risk-adjusted score (p − 0.5 σ). The path is smoothed and repaired to maintain a minimum obstacle margin of half the robot width. Waypoints are truncated when their confidence p (1 − 0.7 u/ log 2) −

IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Robots and datasets 

We evaluate the method on two quadrupeds that differ strongly in size, camera and environment. 

Wheeled quadruped. The Agibot D1 Max (Inchbot ZSM-1) is a 41 kg quadruped with wheels on its feet ( 0.93 × 0.48 ×

0.585 m). Its front camera ( 1920 × 1080 , undistorted to 960 ×

540 ) sits about 0.60 m above the ground. A forward-facing RS-AIRY LiDAR, odometry and an IMU provide the training and evaluation labels only; navigation uses the camera alone. We recorded four flat outdoor scenes (asphalt, grass and two pebble areas), 4,841 frames in total. A contiguous 15 % segment inside each scene is held out for validation, giving 3,921 training and 688 validation windows. 

Small quadruped. The Dogzilla S2 is a small legged robot. Its fisheye camera ( 1920 ×1080 , rectified to 960 ×540 ) is only 0.162 m above the floor. We recorded four runs with 4,740 frames in an indoor room with mats, foam blocks, arches, a table on thin chrome legs, poles on round bases and boxes. One complete run (462 windows) is held out for validation, and the other three runs (1,782 windows) are used for training. Compared with the wheeled robot, the camera is almost four times lower, the lens and the scene type differ, and the BEV covers 2 m instead of 6 m. 

B. Evaluation protocol and metrics 

Each configuration is trained three times; we report the mean, and in the ablation also the standard deviation. All runs use the same validation windows and diffusion noise, and no planner goal (Sec. III-I). 

BEV reconstruction. As in semantic BEV segmentation [12], we score all labelled cells of the BEV, including those outside the camera view. Since 96 % of them are traversable, a binary IoU is uninformative, so we report the level mIoU over blocked ( τ < 0.3), rough ( < 0.6), moderate ( < 0.85 ) and easy cells. The blocked recall (Blk. rec.) is the share of blocked cells ( τ ≤ 0.15 ) predicted as blocked ( ˆpc < 0.5), on the whole BEV and outside the view (Out); we also report the expected calibration error of ˆpc.

Paths. Trav. is the mean label along a path, Easy the share of its length on easy terrain, Clear. the median minimum distance to a blocked cell, and Cross. the share of paths that enter one. As shorter paths keep more distance, we mark variants whose paths are much shorter than the recorded ones, which we score as a reference. Real-world metrics. LiDAR scans, labelled as in training, serve as ground truth. Besides the level mIoU and blocked recall in the camera view, we report the share of LiDAR obstacles within 1 m outside the view that the map marks as blocked (out-of-view obst. known) and the range error (Range err.), the median distance along camera rays between the first predicted and the first LiDAR obstacle. Path ok is the share of paths whose followed part keeps half the robot width from every LiDAR obstacle (8 cm over 0.1– 0.3 m for the small robot, 20 cm over the first metre for the wheeled one), Clear. the median of this distance, and <5 cm time the share of scans in which the body is closer than 5 cm to an obstacle. 

C. Ablation study 1) BEV representation: Table I shows that learned BEV reconstruction substantially improves blocked-cell recall over direct image projection (0.695 vs. 0.056). Polar and Cartesian representations achieve similar mIoU and path traversability, but the polar representation detects more blocked cells (0.695 vs. 0.624), at an additional cost of 2.6 ms per plan. The ranking loss keeps the map graded rather than binary: without it, the level mIoU drops from 0.319 to 0.304, and the share of correctly assigned rough cells from 41 % to 25 %. 

2) Uncertainty and temporal context: Uncertainty-aware planning increases obstacle clearance from 0.89 to 1.14 m by truncating trajectories in ambiguous regions. Additional frames improve out-of-view blocked-cell recall (0.746 with one frame vs. 0.808 with three), but correlated observations raise the calibration error from 0.043 with two frames to 0.051 with four. We therefore use two frames. 

3) Trajectory guidance.: Traversability guidance increases mean path traversability from 0.793 to 0.831, exceeding the recorded trajectories (0.779), while increasing the proportion of path length on easy terrain from 32.9 % to 45.7 %. This improvement comes at the cost of reduced obstacle clearance, reflecting a tradeoff between terrain preference and conserva-tive planning. 

D. Real-world experiments 

We evaluate closed-loop outdoor exploration with the wheeled-legged robot and indoor exploration with the small legged robot, both against the baselines. In all experiments, the robots navigate without a prior map and without localisation: the model plans from the camera stream alone, and the BEV memory of the small robot estimates its ego-motion from the floor in the images. 

1) Baselines.: For the modular pipeline, we adapt BEV-Former [11] to a single front camera. To exclude the backbone as a source of difference, it uses our frozen DINOv2 ViT-B/14 [18] with a simple feature pyramid [25], receives the same two frames and is trained with our labels and map losses (Sec. III-G). We combine its map with two planners. The first is A* [26] on a cost map, in which cells with a risk adjusted traversability ˆp − 0.5 σ below 0.3 are obstacles inflated by half the robot width. The second is a map conditioned diffusion planner in the spirit of DiPPeR [6], which shares our path representation, sampler and corridor snap. As in a modular system, it is trained on the labelled maps and receives the predicted maps at test time. As the end-to-end baseline, we use TG-Diff (Traversability-Guided Diffusion), a diffusion policy whose sampling is guided by a CLIPSeg traversability map instead of the RGB image, adapted from DroneDiffusion [27] and retrained on our robots. All methods are trained three times and evaluated with the protocol of Sec. IV-B.

Table II summarises the results. We count an intervention when a method’s path runs into a LiDAR obstacle at least 10 cm high within the first meter in view and is not re-planned away within 0.5 s, and report the share of the driven route (in 5 m stretches) without one. In Scenes 1 and 2, our model succeeds on 96 % of the route, against 75–88 % for the baselines, with no failure after sunset; its one failure coincides with the operator stopping the robot at a bush. The difference comes from how far the methods commit. Our paths end where the map becomes uncertain, after 1.5– 2.8 m, whereas the baselines plan 5–7 m ahead, 42–100 % of them through areas that our map marks as highly uncertain (σ > 1, orange in Fig. 3), against 14 % of ours. Our model therefore stops more often (17 vs. 0–2 % of the frames with free ground ahead in Scene 1), but at about 20 Hz a stopped plan costs only about 1 cm of travel. In Scene 3, both BEV maps grade the terrain rather than splitting it into free and blocked (our median 0.94 on asphalt, 0.75 on grass, 0.32 on blocked cells), whereas TG-Diff, relying on CLIPSeg alone, keeps the least of its path on easy terrain (53 vs. 65–93 %). Building the BEV geometrically (Eq. 2) also halves training time (21.3 vs. 38.8–41.0 min). Committing only to the reliable part of the map matters most where perception is hardest: after sunset, our model is the only one without an intervention.

We presented BEV-Diffusion, a camera-only navigation model that learns a BEV traversability map, its uncertainty and the path in a single network. LiDAR and IMU are needed only for the training labels; at deployment the robot navigates from the camera alone, without a prior map or localisation. Three insights follow. First, traversability should be learned in the BEV and graded: the learned map detects 69.5 % of the obstacles against 6 % for an image projection, and keeps asphalt and grass apart (median 0.94 vs. 0.75). Second, learning the path together with the map and its uncertainty lets the planner commit only to reliable ground: on the 120 m our model drove outdoors, 96 % of the route passed without an intervention, against 75–88 % for the baselines, with none after sunset. Third, a separate robot-specific BEV head makes adaptation cheap: retraining 3 % of the parameters for 2.3 min nearly matched training from scratch (89 vs. 96 % progress). On the small robot, the BEV memory, which also works with other BEV maps, raised the progress in trap-like scenes to 67–100 %, against 20–40 %. BEV-Diffusion thus shows that a single camera, a learned BEV and a diffusion planner trained together can replace LiDAR, mapping and localisation at deployment on low-cost legged robots.
