Merge Trees

ABSTRACT
We introduce DORMS (Depth-Ordered Region Merging for Segmentation), a purely algorithmic approach to image segmen-
tation from monocular depth maps, requiring no neural networks or learned parameters in the segmentation process. An
off-the-shelf monocular depth estimator provides the input depth maps. Our method constructs depth-ordered merge trees
through a deterministic union-find sweep, combining component area, depth persistence, and spatial contact constraints to
guide region merging. A threshold search determines the segmentation granularity according to a specified target region
count, while global depth detrending improves the separation of local geometric structures. Extensive experiments on
COCO Panoptic, Cityscapes, and ADE20K demonstrate consistent improvements over classical segmentation baselines and
competitive performance against zero-shot neural segmentation models. Notably, our method outperforms all evaluated
zero-shot models on Cityscapes, while remaining competitive with several approaches on COCO and ADE20K. These results
highlight the potential of purely algorithmic geometric reasoning for image segmentation without learned segmentation
models.

1 Introduction
Image segmentation is a fundamental problem in com-
puter vision and has been extensively studied for
decades. Early approaches relied on algorithmic tech-
niques, including graph-based segmentation, cluster-
ing, and superpixels, to group pixels based on low-
level image properties [1, 3, 12]. The development of
deep learning shifted the field toward learned repre-
sentations, enabling significant advances in semantic,
instance, and panoptic segmentation [15, 18, 23]. More
recently, transformer-based architectures and large
foundation models have further advanced segmen-
tation capabilities [8, 19, 26]. Despite these advances,
modern segmentation methods predominantly rely
on learned visual representations and often require
substantial training data or computational resources.
In this work, we investigate a complementary direc-
tion: can meaningful image segmentation be achieved
directly from monocular depth maps without using
neural networks for segmentation? Depth provides
geometric information about scene structure, includ-
ing relative distances, object boundaries, and spatial
relationships [11, 30]. Our motivation is to investi-
gate how much of this structure can be recovered
through algorithmic reasoning alone, without learning
segmentation-specific representations or relying on se-
mantic supervision. Beyond this investigation, we
aim to lay the groundwork for lightweight and prac-
tical segmentation methods that can leverage depth
maps obtained from different estimators or sensing
systems. While the input depth can be obtained from a
pretrained monocular depth estimator, the segmenta-
tion process itself uses no neural networks or learned
parameters.
Building upon classical merge-tree and topological
segmentation principles, we propose DORMS, a novel
algorithmic framework for image segmentation from
monocular depth maps. DORMS integrates depth-
ordered region merging, area–persistence-based scor-
ing, gated spatial-contact constraints, and global depth
detrending into a unified pipeline. Specifically, we
first remove the dominant linear depth trend to em-
phasize local geometric variations. We then process
pixels in depth order using a deterministic union–
find procedure, evaluating candidate merges based
on component area and depth persistence while us-
ing a gated neck-width constraint to prevent prema-
ture merging across narrow connections. A bounded
threshold search selects a segmentation according to a
specified target region count, followed by small-region
cleanup to produce the final partition. An overview of
DORMS is illustrated in Fig. 1.
We evaluate our approach on COCO Panoptic,
Cityscapes, and ADE20K [9, 18, 21, 31], comparing
against classical segmentation algorithms and neural
segmentation models under a unified class-agnostic
evaluation protocol. Using Hungarian-matched mean
Intersection-over-Union (mIoU), our method outper-
forms all evaluated classical baselines across the three
datasets and all tested zero-shot segmentation models
on Cityscapes. Stronger zero-shot methods achieve
higher mIoU on COCO and ADE20K, while Segmenta-
tion Covering reveals additional differences in region
quality and granularity. We further demonstrate that
global depth detrending consistently improves seg-
mentation performance across datasets, highlighting
its importance for depth-based geometric reasoning.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce DORMS, an algorithmic image
segmentation framework operating solely on
depth maps, combining depth-ordered merge
trees, area–persistence scoring, and gated spatial-
contact constraints without learned segmentation
parameters.
• We incorporate global depth detrending and
demonstrate its effectiveness across diverse
scenes, including those without a dominant pla-

depth structure for geometric segmentation.
• We conduct an extensive class-agnostic evaluation
across three segmentation benchmarks against
classical and neural approaches, demonstrating
both the capabilities and limitations of image seg-
mentation based on depth geometry alone.
2 Related Work
Classical and Hierarchical Image Segmentation.
Classical image segmentation methods group pixels
according to low-level visual and geometric properties.
Felzenszwalb and Huttenlocher [12] proposed an effi-
cient graph-based segmentation algorithm based on
local evidence for region boundaries, while SLIC [1]
introduced spatially coherent superpixels through it-
erative clustering. Hierarchical approaches further
organize regions across multiple segmentation scales,
including contour-based hierarchies [3] and merge-
tree representations [22]. In particular, Liu et al. [22]
constructed a hierarchy of superpixels using learned
boundary-merging decisions. Our method similarly
builds regions through hierarchical merging, but op-
erates directly on depth-ordered pixels rather than an
initial superpixel partition, and determines merges
using geometric criteria without a learned classifier.
Topological Segmentation and Persistence-Based
Clustering. Component trees and contour trees pro-
vide established representations of the evolution of
connected components in scalar fields [6, 17]. Per-
sistent homology extends this perspective by char-
acterizing the significance of components through
their lifetimes across filtration levels. These principles
have been applied to image segmentation through
persistence-guided region merging [20], topological
image analysis [4], and persistence-based cluster-
ing [7]. Our approach builds upon these foundations
by applying a depth-ordered component sweep and
evaluating candidate merges through the product of
component area and depth persistence. We further in-
troduce a gated spatial-contact constraint that delays
merging between sufficiently large components until
adequate contact has accumulated. Together, these
mechanisms define our depth-based region-merging
procedure rather than relying on persistence alone.
Depth-Based Geometric Segmentation. Depth infor-
mation has long been used to support image segmen-
tation through geometric relationships, occlusion cues,
and surface structure. Dimiccoli and Salembier [11]
incorporated depth ordering inferred from monoc-
ular image cues into hierarchical region representa-
tions. Other methods have explored joint color–depth
clustering and region merging [14], as well as depth-
image segmentation based on geometric surface prop-
erties [2]. More recently, monocular depth estimation
models such as Depth Anything V2 [30] have enabled
the extraction of dense depth maps from single images.
Our work investigates a complementary segmentation
formulation that uses such depth maps as its sole in-
put, without RGB features or semantic information
during segmentation. Rather than targeting specific
surface primitives, we combine global depth detrend-
ing with depth-ordered geometric merging to obtain
general class-agnostic image partitions.
Learning-Based and Foundation-Model Segmenta-
tion. Deep learning has substantially advanced se-
mantic, instance, and panoptic segmentation through
learned visual representations, including transformer-
based architectures such as Mask2Former [8]. Foun-
dation models such as SAM [19] and SAM2 [26] fur-
ther enable promptable segmentation using large-scale
pretrained networks. Other approaches, including
GroupViT [29] and CLIPSeg [24], leverage vision–
language representations, while DiffCut [10] performs
algorithmic graph partitioning over pretrained dif-

3 Proposed Method
We propose DORMS (Depth-Ordered Region Merging
for Segmentation), an algorithmic framework that seg-
ments monocular depth maps through global detrend-
ing and depth-ordered merge trees. A deterministic
pixel sweep evaluates region merges using component
area, depth persistence, and spatial contact, while a
bounded threshold search controls segmentation gran-
ularity, followed by conditional small-region cleanup.
The segmentation process requires no learned param-
eters. Detailed pseudocode and an algorithmic break-
down are provided in the supplementary material,
and the implementation code is publicly available in
our project page.

3.2 Depth-Ordered Merge-Tree Construction
We construct a depth-ordered hierarchy of connected
components through a deterministic pixel sweep. Let
π denote the permutation of pixels sorted from nearest
to farthest according to ˜d:
˜d(π(1)) ≥˜d(π(2)) ≥· · · ≥˜d(π(N)).
(3)
Ties are resolved using a fixed pixel index to ensure
deterministic processing.
Pixels are activated sequentially in this order, while
a disjoint-set (union–find) structure maintains the ac-
tive connected components. When a pixel is activated,
three cases are considered. If none of its 4-connected
neighbors is active, the pixel establishes a new compo-
nent. If its active neighbors belong to a single compo-
nent, the pixel is absorbed into that component. Other-
wise, the pixel connects multiple distinct components
and generates candidate merge evaluations.
For each component C, we maintain its current area
A(C) and birth depth b(C), corresponding to the
depth value at which the component was created.
Component areas are updated as pixels are added
and other components are absorbed. When multiple
components meet, the component with the earliest
birth in the sweep is designated as the elder, with ties
resolved by the smallest root index. Each other com-
ponent is treated as a junior, producing a candidate
merge evaluation with the elder. The newly activated
pixel always joins the elder, regardless of whether the
candidate merges are accepted.
Candidate merges are evaluated during the sweep
rather than automatically accepted. Importantly, re-
jecting a merge does not necessarily prevent the corre-
sponding components from merging later: a pair re-
jected by the spatial-contact constraint is re-evaluated
at subsequent bridging pixels and may be admitted
once sufficient contact has accumulated, whereas a
pair rejected by the area–persistence criterion remains
rejected for the remainder of the sweep (Sec. 3.3). Con-
sequently, the component hierarchy depends on the
sequence of merge decisions, rather than being a fixed
tree pruned only after construction. Figure 2 illus-
trates the evolution of connected components during
the depth-ordered sweep.

ponent pair may be encountered multiple times dur-
ing the depth-ordered sweep, but the outcome of a
rejected merge depends on which constraint prevents
it. A rejection by the spatial-contact constraint is tem-
porary: as contact accumulates, the pair may eventu-
ally satisfy the contact threshold and proceed to the
area–persistence criterion. In contrast, rejection by
the area–persistence criterion is permanent. The score
φ(e) = A(Cj) P(e) is monotonically non-decreasing
because the junior component’s area A(Cj) cannot
decrease, while its persistence P(e) = b(Cj) −˜d(p)

ing depth order. Moreover, the score is independent
of the elder component’s identity. Therefore, once
φ(e) ≥τ, the junior component cannot satisfy the
merge criterion at any subsequent encounter, even
with a different elder. Because the score cannot de-
crease while a pair is deferred, delaying the test can
only make the area–persistence criterion harder to sat-
isfy, never easier. Repeated evaluations thus allow
a contact-deferred pair to reach the area–persistence
criterion, but cannot reverse a rejection by it.
3.4 Target Region Count and Threshold Search
The merge threshold τ determines which candidate
merges are accepted and therefore influences the num-
ber of regions in the resulting partition. Rather than
choosing a fixed threshold across all images, we spec-
ify a target region count k and search for a threshold
that produces an appropriate partition.

3.5 Small-Region Cleanup and Final Segmentation
The neck-width constraint can leave small residual
components whose boundaries are too short to accu-
mulate the required contact for merging. To address
this, we apply a conditional cleanup step to remove
such regions.
We define a minimum region area threshold
Amin = max(Amin,abs, ρN),
(11)
where Amin,abs is an absolute minimum region size
and ρ is a minimum area fraction. In all reported ex-
periments ρN is the binding term; Amin,abs acts only
as a floor for degenerate inputs, guaranteeing a pos-
itive threshold when ρN < Amin,abs. Cleanup is per-
formed only when the neck-width constraint is en-
abled (m > 1) and the integer-valued area threshold
exceeds one pixel. In particular, no cleanup is applied
when m = 1.
When cleanup is enabled, each pixel belonging to a
region smaller than the threshold is reassigned the
label of its nearest surviving pixel, located by a Eu-
clidean distance transform. Reassignment is therefore
performed per pixel rather than per region, so a re-
moved region may be absorbed by more than one
neighbouring region. Region labels are then renum-
bered consecutively from zero, which is what makes
the reported region count well defined. This produces
the final segmentation Sfinal.
The number of regions reported in our experiments
is measured after the applicable cleanup step. Con-
sequently, the final region count can differ from the
count selected by the threshold search, including cases
where cleanup reduces it below the specified target k.

4 Experimental Setup
4.1 Datasets and Depth Inputs
We evaluate DORMS on three image segmentation
benchmarks: COCO Panoptic [18, 21], Cityscapes [9],
and ADE20K [31]. We use the COCO val2017 split con-
taining 5,000 images, the Cityscapes fine validation
split containing 500 images, and the ADE20K vali-
dation split containing 2,000 images. These datasets
cover diverse environments, from everyday objects
and indoor scenes to complex urban environments.

For DORMS, depth maps are obtained using pre-
trained Depth Anything V2 [30], while all subsequent
segmentation operations rely solely on depth values
without RGB features or semantic labels. We also
evaluate Cityscapes stereo disparity and conduct ab-
lations with different depth-estimator backbones. For
ADE20K, panoptic annotations are constructed from
semantic and instance masks. Further details on an-
notation construction, depth preprocessing, and back-
bone ablations are provided in the supplementary ma-
terial.
4.2 Comparison Methods
We compare against classical segmentation algorithms
and neural segmentation models under a unified class-
agnostic evaluation protocol. Classical baselines in-
clude Felzenszwalb’s graph-based segmentation [12]
and SLIC [1], evaluated using RGB and depth inputs
where applicable. Neural comparisons include the
zero-shot segmentation methods SAM [19], SAM2 [26],
GroupViT [29], CLIPSeg [24], and DiffCut [10]. We ad-
ditionally include COCO-supervised Mask2Former [8]
as a fully supervised reference on COCO.
For vocabulary-conditioned methods, GroupViT and
CLIPSeg receive the corresponding dataset’s cate-
gory vocabulary as text input. This provides dataset-
specific semantic information unavailable to the other
zero-shot and classical baselines, which should be
considered when interpreting their results. Overlap-
ping SAM and SAM2 proposals are converted into
complete, non-overlapping partitions, with uncovered
pixels retained as an additional region.
All meth-
ods are evaluated using the same ground-truth an-
notations and scoring implementation rather than
relying on metrics reported in their original pub-
lications.
Additional baseline configurations and
partition-conversion details are provided in the sup-
plementary material.

5 Experimental Results and Analysis
5.1 Comparison with Existing Segmentation Methods
Table 1 compares DORMS with classical and neu-
ral segmentation approaches on COCO Panoptic,
Cityscapes, and ADE20K. DORMS achieves H-mIoU
scores of 0.2332, 0.2086, and 0.2206, respectively, out-
performing all evaluated classical baselines across
the three datasets.
It also exceeds their H-mIoU-
selected Covering scores, although COCO Felzen-
szwalb achieves marginally higher Covering when
optimized separately (0.5124 vs. 0.5119), using sub-
stantially more regions (29.22 vs. 5.93). On Cityscapes,
DORMS achieves the highest H-mIoU, exceeding
the strongest zero-shot baseline, CLIPSeg (0.1506).
On COCO and ADE20K, stronger neural baselines
achieve higher H-mIoU, while the COCO-supervised
Mask2Former provides a substantially stronger super-
vised reference.
The metrics capture complementary properties: H-
mIoU uses one-to-one matching, whereas Covering
measures the best overlap for each ground-truth re-
gion. Consequently, DiffCut and CLIPSeg achieve
higher Covering on Cityscapes despite lower H-mIoU.
Table 2 further shows that our parameter-free seg-
mentation stage requires only 231 MB of CPU RAM
on COCO, increasing to 1,128 MB for the complete
pipeline including depth estimation with Depth Any-
thing V2 (DA-V2). Detailed baseline configurations,
computational measurements, and geometric abla-
tions are provided in the supplementary material.

5.2 Contribution of Global Depth Detrending
We evaluate global depth detrending by comparing
segmentation performance with and without this pre-
processing step. As shown in Table 3, detrending im-
proves H-mIoU from 0.2037 to 0.2332 on COCO Panop-
tic (+0.0295) and from 0.1753 to 0.2206 on ADE20K
(+0.0453). On Cityscapes stereo disparity, H-mIoU in-
creases from 0.0612 to 0.1045 at matched segmentation
parameters, although differing threshold-search set-
tings prevent attributing the entire gain to detrending
alone (Sec. 5.3).
Figure 3 shows statistically significant improvements
across all four COCO planarity quartiles, with the
largest gains in intermediate quartiles rather than the
most planar scenes. This suggests that detrending
benefits geometric merging beyond simply remov-
ing dominant planes. However, the effect is method-
dependent: on COCO, detrending marginally im-
proves Felzenszwalb (+0.0020) while reducing SLIC
performance (-0.0086).
These results indicate that
its benefits are closely linked to the proposed depth-
ordered merging mechanism. Additional analysis is
provided in the supplementary material.

6 Limitations and Future Directions
DORMS has several limitations.
Its segmentation
granularity depends on a dataset-specific target re-
gion count, which can lead to under-segmentation in
crowded scenes. Performance also depends on the
quality of the input depth map, particularly the ac-
curacy of geometric boundaries and depth contrast
between adjacent objects. Moreover, DORMS pro-
duces class-agnostic partitions without semantic su-
pervision.
Future work will focus on improving DORMS through
adaptive region-count selection, more efficient thresh-
old search, and enhanced geometric merging strate-
gies.
Beyond natural images, segmentation plays
an important role in medical imaging [13, 16], avia-
tion [25, 28], and industrial inspection [5, 27]. Investi-
gating the applicability of DORMS to these domains,
including suitable depth representations, presents an
important direction for future research.

7 Conclusion
We presented DORMS, an algorithmic framework
for image segmentation from monocular depth maps
that combines depth-ordered merge trees, area–
persistence-based scoring, gated spatial-contact con-
straints, and global depth detrending without learned
segmentation parameters or semantic supervision.
Experiments on COCO Panoptic, Cityscapes, and
ADE20K demonstrate improvements over all evalu-
ated classical baselines in Hungarian-matched mIoU
and over their mIoU-selected configurations in Seg-
mentation Covering, alongside competitive perfor-
mance against zero-shot neural models. Our analysis
highlights the importance of depth detrending and
geometric merge constraints, demonstrating the po-
tential of lightweight, algorithmic segmentation with
increasingly capable depth estimators and sensing
technologies.

A Implementation Details
All reported configurations of our method use global
depth detrending, area–persistence-based merge scor-
ing, and the gated spatial-contact constraint described
in Sec. 3. The segmentation stage operates exclusively
on depth values and does not require learned parame-
ters. This section provides the dataset-specific config-
urations, hyperparameter selection procedures, depth
inputs, and implementation settings used in our ex-
periments.
A.1 Hyperparameter Configurations and Selection
The principal hyperparameters are the target region
count k, the minimum accumulated contact threshold
m, and the area-gate fraction g. Table 5 summarizes
the configurations used for the main monocular-depth
experiments.
COCO Panoptic.
The target region count is se-
lected using a disjoint subset of COCO train2017,
with reweighting to reflect the natural distribution
of scene complexity. Table 6 presents the correspond-
ing Hungarian-matched mIoU values. The best result
occurs at k = 6, with lower performance at both neigh-
boring values k = 5 and k = 7. Thus, the selected
configuration is an interior maximum of the evalu-
ated range rather than a boundary value, and there is
no tie. The remaining hyperparameters, m = 16 and
g = 0.2%, were fixed beforehand based on separate
val2017 experiments and were not independently se-
lected using the held-out subset. Consequently, only
the selection of k uses the disjoint held-out data.
Cityscapes.
We evaluate the parameter grid k ∈
{4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15, 17, 20, 25, 30} and m ∈{1, 3, 16},
with the area gate fixed at g = 10%. The highest
Hungarian-matched mIoU is 0.2086 at k = 30. This
maximum is attained by both m = 3 and m = 16,
which produce identical recorded results to floating-
point precision across all 12 evaluated target counts.
We adopt m = 3 as the representative configuration.
The area-gate fraction was not swept on Cityscapes;
g = 10% is the only evaluated setting. These parame-
ters were selected using the Cityscapes validation set
itself rather than a separate held-out selection split.
ADE20K.
We evaluate k ∈{4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 15, 17},
m ∈{1, 3, 5, 8, 16, 32}, and g ∈{2%, 10%}. The highest
Hungarian-matched mIoU, 0.2206, occurs uniquely at
(k, m, g) = (8, 8, 2%), with no tied configurations. As
with Cityscapes, these parameters are selected from
sweeps on the evaluation split, rather than a separate
held-out set. Accordingly, the reported Cityscapes and
ADE20K results should be interpreted as validation-
selected configurations.
A.2 Threshold Search and Region Selection
The threshold search employs bounded adaptive sub-
division, as described in Sec. 3.4. The search inter-
val starts at τmin = 10−6 and extends to τmax =
max(106, 20smax), where smax is the maximum merge
score used to establish the image-dependent upper
bound. The reported runners use a maximum of 2,000
segmentation replays, with a minimum bracket ratio
of 1.0005.
To limit exploration of unnecessarily large region
counts, the search uses a cap of max(24, 3kmax), where
kmax is the largest target region count in the evaluated
grid. The cap is computed once for that grid. A search
bracket is pruned only when the region counts at both
endpoints exceed the cap. Consequently, the algo-
rithm does not exhaustively resolve all region counts,
particularly those substantially above the requested
target.
For each discovered region count, the search retains
a witness threshold that produced it. After selecting
the preferred count, the segmentation is reconstructed
through a fresh replay at the corresponding witness
threshold. This avoids assuming that an arbitrary
threshold within a bracket reproduces the same count,
which is not guaranteed because the region-count func-
tion can be non-monotonic.
We use the prefer_over selection policy: the small-
est discovered count greater than or equal to k is pre-
ferred, with the closest discovered count used only
when no such candidate exists. In the latter case, equal-
distance ties favor the smaller count. Across all three
primary evaluation datasets, the preferred-over-target
branch was selected for every image: 5,000/5,000
on COCO, 500/500 on Cityscapes, and 2,000/2,000
on ADE20K. Thus, all selected region counts before
cleanup were at least k; no fallback to a below-target
count was required.

B Detailed DORMS Algorithm
We provide procedural details of DORMS (Depth-
Ordered Region Merging for Segmentation). Its four
stages are global depth detrending, a depth-ordered
union–find sweep at a prescribed merge threshold,
bounded threshold selection for a target number of
regions, and conditional cleanup. The input depth
convention is that larger values indicate nearer loca-
tions; when a sensor reports metric distance, its or-
dering must be reversed before the sweep. All stages
after depth estimation are deterministic and require
no learned parameters.

D Evaluation Metrics and Scoring Protocol
We evaluate all segmentation methods using a unified
class-agnostic protocol based on Hungarian-matched
mean Intersection-over-Union (H-mIoU) and Segmen-
tation Covering. Both metrics operate on complete
image partitions and disregard predicted semantic cat-
egory labels. For each image, pixels marked as void
or unlabeled in the ground-truth annotations are ex-
cluded from evaluation. All methods are evaluated
using the same ground-truth partitions and scoring
implementation, rather than adopting results reported
under potentially different protocols in their original
publications.

D.3 Dataset-Level Aggregation and Instance AP
Both metrics are computed independently for each
image and then averaged across the evaluation split,
giving each image equal weight. We additionally re-
port the average number of predicted regions. For our
method, this count is measured after the applicable
small-region cleanup step.
We do not report instance Average Precision (AP),
since our evaluation concerns class-agnostic, full-
scene partitions rather than confidence-ranked object-
instance proposals. The predicted regions may rep-
resent objects, object parts, or background structures,
and do not naturally correspond to ranked instance
detections. Hungarian-matched mIoU and Segmen-
tation Covering are therefore better aligned with the
output and evaluation objectives of our segmentation
framework.

E.2 ADE20K Panoptic Annotation Construction
ADE20K [31] provides semantic segmentation anno-
tations and separate instance-level annotations, but
the publicly accessible annotations used in our experi-
ments are not supplied as a directly usable panoptic
partition. We therefore construct class-agnostic panop-
tic ground truth by combining the publicly available
150-class semantic masks with the instance-ID annota-
tions.
The construction follows four rules. First, the 100
instance-level “thing” categories are mapped to their
corresponding semantic categories using canonical
class names. We require exact canonical-name cor-
respondence rather than approximate substring or
synonym matching, which can incorrectly associate

distinct classes. Second, instance annotations are as-
signed individual segment identifiers, preserving the
distinction between annotated instances. Third, in-
stance annotations take priority wherever their labels
disagree with the semantic segmentation mask. Fi-

according to their remaining semantic category, with
one segment per semantic class per image. Pixels with-

Importantly, the semantic fallback does not split each
category into separate connected components.
A
single semantic segment can therefore contain spa-

sistent with panoptic annotations in which a segment
identifier need not correspond to one spatially con-
nected component. For reference, an inspection of
COCO Panoptic annotations found that approximately
33.7% of ground-truth segments contain disconnected
components. Splitting ADE20K semantic regions by
connectivity would instead substantially increase the
number of target regions and change the evaluation
protocol.
Using this construction, the ADE20K validation set
contains an average of 15.21 ground-truth segments
per image. As an internal consistency check, evalu-
ating each constructed annotation against itself pro-
duces a Hungarian-matched mIoU of 1.0. This con-
firms consistency of the constructed labels with the
scoring implementation, but does not independently
establish equivalence to an official panoptic annota-
tion release.
All ADE20K segmentation results use these con-
structed ground-truth partitions and DA-V2 Small
depth estimates. The same annotations and evalu-
ation implementation are used for our method and all
compared baselines, ensuring that the reported differ-
ences are not caused by using different ground-truth
constructions.
F Baseline Implementation Details
We compare our approach against classical segmenta-
tion algorithms, zero-shot neural segmentation meth-
ods, and a fully supervised reference under the uni-
fied class-agnostic evaluation protocol described in
Sec. D. Because these methods differ in their input
modalities, training supervision, and output represen-
tations, we document their configurations and the pro-
cedures used to obtain comparable image partitions.
All reported scores are computed using our evaluation
implementation rather than taken from the methods’
original publications.
F.1 Classical Segmentation Baselines
We evaluate Felzenszwalb’s graph-based segmenta-
tion [12] and SLIC [1] using RGB images and depth-
based inputs where available. For depth-based exper-
iments, the algorithms operate on scalar depth rep-
resentations rather than RGB features. We evaluate
multiple parameter configurations and report the con-
figurations achieving the highest Hungarian-matched
mIoU.
For Felzenszwalb, we vary the graph-segmentation
scale parameter, while for SLIC, we vary the requested
number of superpixels. These parameters influence
the granularity of the resulting partitions, and the
number of predicted regions may differ from the nom-
inal parameter values. The comparison tables report
the actual average number of regions produced.
We also examine depth preprocessing separately for
the classical baselines. On COCO, the reported depth-
based Felzenszwalb configuration uses globally de-
trended depth, while depth-based SLIC uses the origi-
nal depth representation, as these achieve the stronger
results in their respective parameter sweeps.
The
corresponding best H-mIoU configurations use scale
s = 1000 for Felzenszwalb and n = 6 for SLIC.
On ADE20K, the reported depth-based configura-
tions use detrended depth for both methods. The
Cityscapes SLIC depth-input comparison instead uses
the dataset’s sensor-derived stereo disparity, whereas
our main Cityscapes result uses monocular depth esti-
mated by Depth Anything V2.
The reported classical-baseline configurations are se-
lected according to H-mIoU rather than independently
optimizing each evaluation metric. These objectives
can favor different segmentation granularities. For ex-
ample, on COCO, depth-based Felzenszwalb achieves
H-mIoU of 0.1516 and Covering of 0.4901 in its H-
mIoU-selected configuration, whereas a separately se-
lected configuration reaches Covering of 0.5124 with
an average of 29.22 regions. We retain the H-mIoU-
selected configuration in the main comparison table
for consistency.
F.2 SAM and SAM2
We evaluate SAM [19] and SAM2 [26] using RGB im-
ages with point-based prompting configurations of
16 and 32 points. Both models rely on learned visual
representations, but do not receive dataset-specific se-
mantic category vocabularies during evaluation.
SAM and SAM2 produce mask proposals that may
overlap and need not cover the entire image. To eval-
uate these outputs as complete partitions, we apply a
common mask-assignment procedure. For each pixel
covered by multiple proposals, we select the covering
mask with the highest confidence score. Ties are re-

DORMS
solved in favor of the smaller mask. Pixels not covered
by any proposal are assigned to a single residual re-

The residual region can occupy a substantial fraction
of the image. Across the evaluated configurations, ap-
proximately 41–46% of COCO pixels and 61–80% of
Cityscapes pixels remain uncovered by the predicted
masks. In particular, SAM2 with 16-point prompting
leaves approximately 80% of Cityscapes pixels in the
residual region. Consequently, the resulting segmen-
tation metrics reflect both the predicted masks and
the additional residual region. This distinction is es-
pecially relevant for Segmentation Covering, where
a large residual region may provide substantial over-
lap with ground-truth background or other extended
regions.
We retain the uncovered pixels rather than excluding
them from evaluation, ensuring that SAM and SAM2
are scored as full-image partitions under the same
protocol as the other approaches.
F.3 Vocabulary-Conditioned Segmentation
We evaluate GroupViT [29] and CLIPSeg [24] using
RGB images and dataset-specific semantic category
vocabularies. The vocabularies contain 133 panop-
tic categories for COCO, 19 evaluation categories for
Cityscapes, and 150 semantic categories for ADE20K.
These models receive the corresponding category
names as text input, allowing them to exploit seman-
tic information about the categories present in each
benchmark. Although no dataset-specific model fine-
tuning is performed, this constitutes an information
advantage relative to methods that operate without
semantic category descriptions, including our depth-
based segmentation framework. The category vocabu-
lary is therefore treated as part of the evaluation con-
figuration rather than an unspecified implementation
detail.
Predicted semantic category identities are not used
when computing our class-agnostic evaluation met-
rics. Instead, the resulting segmentation partitions are
evaluated using the same region-overlap measures
applied to the other methods. The reported results
consequently measure partition agreement rather than
conventional semantic segmentation accuracy.
F.4 DiffCut and Fully Supervised Reference
We additionally evaluate DiffCut [10], which performs
segmentation using features extracted from a pre-
trained diffusion model. Our comparisons use the
SSD-1B-based configuration. Unlike GroupViT and
CLIPSeg, DiffCut does not receive a dataset-specific
category vocabulary during inference. Its segmen-
tation output is evaluated directly under our class-
agnostic partition protocol.
For COCO Panoptic, we also include Mask2Former [8]
trained with COCO panoptic supervision. This model
serves as a fully supervised reference rather than a
zero-shot baseline. Its training setting differs sub-
stantially from the other methods, and its results
are therefore reported separately. We do not include
Mask2Former results for Cityscapes or ADE20K.
F.5 Evaluation Consistency and Comparison Scope
All baseline predictions are evaluated against the same
ground-truth partitions using Hungarian-matched
mIoU and Segmentation Covering. We additionally
report the average number of predicted regions to
expose differences in segmentation granularity. The
reported metrics should not be directly equated with
semantic mIoU or other performance measures pub-
lished in the original baseline papers, since their
matching procedures, category definitions, and evalu-
ation objectives may differ.
All COCO and ADE20K comparisons use their com-
plete validation splits of 5,000 and 2,000 images, re-
spectively. On Cityscapes, all methods are evaluated
on 500 images except DiffCut, for which one image
failed during inference because of a recursion-depth
error, resulting in 499 evaluated images.
The comparison methods also differ in the information
available during inference. Our approach uses esti-
mated monocular depth without RGB features or se-
mantic category labels during segmentation. The clas-
sical methods use RGB or depth-based inputs, SAM
and SAM2 use learned RGB representations with point
prompts, and GroupViT and CLIPSeg additionally re-
ceive dataset-specific category vocabularies. DiffCut
uses pretrained diffusion features, while the super-
vised Mask2Former reference benefits from labeled
COCO training data. These distinctions should be con-
sidered when interpreting the relative performance of
the methods.

trending, focusing on its relationship with scene pla-
narity and its effect on classical depth-based segmen-
tation methods. These experiments complement the
main-paper results in Sec. 5.2 and investigate whether
the observed improvements arise primarily from re-
moving dominant planar structures or from a more
general interaction between depth detrending and ge-
ometric region merging.

global linear depth structure. Instead, the largest im-
provements occur at intermediate levels of planarity.
One possible explanation is that removing a global
depth trend makes local variations more influential
during component formation and persistence-based
merging. In images with weak global trends, there
may be relatively little structure to remove, while im-
ages dominated by a strong global gradient may con-
tain less residual geometric variation after subtraction.
Although consistent with the observed pattern, this
interpretation does not establish a unique causal mech-
anism.

H.2 Effect on Classical Depth-Based Segmentation
We additionally evaluate whether global depth de-
trending improves conventional segmentation algo-
rithms applied directly to depth maps. Specifically, we
compare Felzenszwalb’s graph-based segmentation
and SLIC on COCO Panoptic using depth representa-
tions with and without plane subtraction. Both meth-
ods use the configurations and parameter-selection
procedures described in Sec. F.1. The results are sum-
marized in Table 15.
Detrending has only a marginal positive effect on
Felzenszwalb, increasing H-mIoU from 0.1496 to
0.1516. In contrast, SLIC decreases from 0.1712 to
0.1626 after detrending. These results differ consid-
erably from the +0.0295 improvement observed with
our depth-ordered merge-tree framework on the same
dataset.
The contrasting behavior can be understood in terms
of how the algorithms use depth information. Felzen-
szwalb relies on local graph-edge differences and
component-based merging, while SLIC groups pixels
according to spatial and feature similarity. Removing
a global depth gradient changes the feature distances
underlying these decisions and does not necessarily
produce more favorable partitions. In our framework,
depth values additionally determine pixel activation
order and component persistence, making the inter-
action between detrending and subsequent merging
fundamentally different.
These comparisons demonstrate that global depth de-
trending is not a universally beneficial preprocessing
step for depth-based segmentation. Its contribution
depends on the segmentation mechanism, supporting
its use as an integrated component of the proposed
framework rather than as a generic enhancement ap-
plied indiscriminately to existing algorithms.

I.4 Performance Attribution and Interpretation
The original Cityscapes disparity experiment without
plane subtraction achieved H-mIoU of 0.0612 and Cov-
ering of 0.2963. Introducing global detrending raises
the best observed scores to 0.1045 and 0.4751, respec-
tively, while the additional disparity-specific prepro-
cessing raises them further to 0.1163 and 0.4994.
The largest numerical change is associated with the
introduction of global detrending. However, the origi-
nal no-detrending experiment and the later detrended
experiments used different threshold-search config-
urations. Therefore, the difference between their re-
ported maxima should not be interpreted as a strictly
controlled estimate of the isolated effect of plane sub-
traction. Comparisons at matched segmentation pa-
rameters support the same direction of improvement,
but the search-setting difference remains.
The additional preprocessing contributes a further
0.0118 H-mIoU when comparing the independently
selected maxima of the detrending-only and fully
preprocessed configurations. The corresponding ob-
served Covering difference is 0.0243, although this
value is an upper bound on the difference between
the true optimized maxima because the detrending-
only control was not evaluated below k = 7. No
paired confidence intervals were computed for these
full-validation preprocessing comparisons, so we do
not claim statistical significance for the additional im-
provement.
The corrected sensor-derived disparity configuration
exceeds the evaluated classical segmentation baselines
on Cityscapes in both H-mIoU and Covering, even
when their respective metrics are optimized indepen-
dently. Nevertheless, it remains below the main DA-
V2 Large configuration, which achieves H-mIoU of
0.2086 and Covering of 0.5866.
These findings illustrate that our framework can oper-
ate using geometric information from a stereo sensor
without a learned depth estimator. They also demon-
strate sensitivity to the numerical representation of
depth information: preserving pixel ordering alone is
insufficient to guarantee similar segmentation behav-
ior when depth-value spacing changes. The experi-
ments do not establish that newer or higher-resolution
depth sensors will necessarily improve performance,
since the interaction between sensor characteristics
and area–persistence-based merging requires further
investigation.
I.5 Experimental Scope and Limitations
The preprocessing variants were initially screened and
combined using a subset of 20 images, without con-
fidence intervals. Their rankings should therefore be
interpreted as configuration-selection evidence rather
than general conclusions about the relative effective-
ness of every operation.
The full-validation results use 500 images, but the se-
lected preprocessing configuration and target region
counts were determined using evaluation data rather
than an independent held-out validation split. The
resulting scores should be interpreted as validation-
selected performance. In addition, the disparity tie
statistics presented above were measured on one rep-
resentative image and should not be interpreted as
dataset-wide estimates.
Finally, the two reported metrics are optimized over
potentially different target region counts, and the
detrending-only Covering sweep does not bracket its
maximum. We retain these distinctions when report-
ing the results and avoid attributing the full improve-
ment to any single preprocessing operation.

J.1 Benchmark Protocol
For latency measurements, models are loaded before
timing and two warm-up passes are discarded. We re-
port the median runtime across repeated executions of
each image, followed by the median across evaluated
images. All GPU timings use explicit CUDA synchro-
nization on the device hosting the model. CPU bench-
marks allow 16 threads, although our depth-ordered
sweep executes sequentially within each image. Image
loading, evaluation metrics, and label-map conversion
are excluded from timing.
The GPU benchmarks use the first eight COCO Panop-
tic validation images, with three repetitions per im-
age. CPU benchmarks use the same ordered image
set, although computationally expensive models are
evaluated on fewer images. The precise sample counts
are reported in Table 18.
Memory is measured independently using a dedicated
process that imports the required libraries, loads one
model, processes one image, and exits. Peak CPU
memory is measured using the process resident set
size (RSS). For GPU inference, we separately record
peak allocated tensor memory, peak memory reserved
by the CUDA caching allocator, and GPU-process
memory reported by nvidia-smi after inference.
The last quantity additionally includes CUDA con-
text overhead. CPU RSS and GPU VRAM represent
different resources and should not be directly equated.
J.2 Inference Latency
Table 18 compares CPU and GPU inference times. Our
segmentation stage requires no GPU and operates di-
rectly on the supplied depth map. With the per-image
threshold search used for the reported segmentation
results, its median CPU latency is 1.1230 s. A single
sweep at a prescribed threshold takes 0.0328 s, approx-
imately 34 times faster. Thus, threshold selection ac-
counts for approximately 97% of the segmentation
stage’s measured runtime.
The fixed-threshold result characterizes the cost of
the underlying geometric segmentation operation, but
does not represent the configuration used to obtain
our reported accuracy results. In particular, a pre-
scribed threshold does not ensure that the resulting
partition approaches the specified target region count.
The additional computational cost of adaptive thresh-
old selection is therefore a tradeoff associated with
controlling segmentation granularity.
The CPU latency of Depth Anything V2 Small is
0.1333 s, compared with 1.1230 s for our segmentation
stage with threshold search. Adding these separately
measured medians gives an estimated CPU pipeline
latency of 1.2563 s; this value is not an indepen-
dently timed end-to-end measurement. Several neu-
ral models, including GroupViT and Mask2Former,
achieve lower CPU latency than our full-search seg-
mentation stage. Consequently, the primary compu-
tational advantage of our method is its parameter-
free, accelerator-independent segmentation operation
rather than uniformly faster inference.
For SAM and SAM2, increasing the point count from
16 to 32 substantially increases runtime. The GPU
latency of SAM rises from 0.2621 s to 0.8382 s, while
SAM2 increases from 0.2909 s to 0.8868 s. The point
count changes the number of processed batches rather
than the maximum working batch size, explaining
why the corresponding GPU memory measurements
remain unchanged.

M.1 Component Evolution During the Depth Sweep
Figure 6 shows six snapshots from the same fixed-
threshold sweep of COCO image 000000435081.
The fraction of activated pixels increases from 10%
to 100%. The number of live components in the shown
snapshots is 35, 34, 28, 9, 7, and 6, respectively. New
components may be born as the sweep reaches previ-
ously inactive depth maxima, while permitted merges
consolidate components encountered at bridging pix-
els. The displayed component count describes the cur-
rent sweep state, not the number of regions returned
by a separate threshold search.
M.2 Accepted Merges and Rejected Evaluations
Figure 7 shows two views of the instrumented merge
forest for ADE20K image ADE_val_00001000. The
first displays component lifetimes and approved
merges, whereas the second overlays the rejected
merge evaluations produced by the contact and area–
persistence criteria. The trace contains 122 compo-
nents, 114 approved merges, 28 contact-veto evalua-
tions, and 1,200 score-veto evaluations, leaving eight
surviving regions. These veto counts refer to evalu-
ations, not distinct component pairs: a pair may be
rejected at one bridging pixel and reconsidered at later
encounters with updated area, contact, and persis-
tence values.
The two views emphasize complementary aspects of
the algorithm. Approved merges describe the evolv-
ing partition at the selected threshold, while the veto
markers reveal that many potential merges are recon-
sidered as the sweep progresses. Their presence also
explains why recording only component births and
accepted merges is insufficient to reconstruct the full
sequence of merge decisions. The diagrams do not
separately visualize the result of toggling the contact
constraint, and no such visual comparison is claimed
here.
