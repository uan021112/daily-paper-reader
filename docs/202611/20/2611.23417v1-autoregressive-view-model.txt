Autoregressive View Model
Jensen (Jinghao) Zhou1,2, Samuel Rota Bulo1, Denis Rozumny1, Duncan Zauss1, Peter Hedman1,
Nikhil Keetha1, Philip Torr2, Christian Rupprecht2, Andrea Vedaldi1,2, Peter Kontschieder1
1Meta, 2University of Oxford
We present the Autoregressive View Model (AVM), which generates a scene as a stream of views
without constructing an explicit 3D representation. Through a unified in-context interface, AVM
conditions on images or videos to generate short clips along a requested camera path. Visual context
grounds reconstruction and interpolation, while a learned prior enables extrapolation into unseen
regions. AVM retrieves relevant views from inputs and generated history to form a bounded active
context. We fine-tune a pretrained bidirectional video diffusion transformer with flow matching in
an N-in-1-out formulation. We then tune a causal model on its own rollouts to improve photorealism,
reduce denoising steps, and limit long-horizon drift. We scale training to approximately 8M scenes
and video clips. We evaluate generation capacity and scene persistence across benchmarks for
camera control, text control, visual quality, dynamics, sparse and 4D reconstruction, and long-horizon
consistency. The results demonstrate that AVM adapts to diverse input and output settings through
one in-context interface.

Introduction
We seek to generate a video of a complex 3D scene along a requested virtual camera trajectory, given one or
more images or videos of the scene. The model must reconstruct observed regions, synthesize newly visible
content as the camera moves, and stay consistent with both the inputs and its own earlier outputs on revisits.
Reconstruction methods such as neural radiance fields [54] and 3D Gaussian splatting [38] fit 3D-consistent
representations to the input views and depend on dense captures for faithful rendering across viewpoints.
Video generators [6] can synthesize unseen content but may drift from the inputs and earlier outputs as
trajectories lengthen. Consistency becomes harder still once inputs and outputs accumulate more views than
the model can condition on at once.
We present the Autoregressive View Model (AVM), which generates a scene as a stream of views without
constructing an explicit 3D representation. Through a unified in-context interface, AVM generates short clips
along a requested camera path, conditioning on posed images or videos as prefill and on earlier generated
views as history. Visual context grounds reconstruction and interpolation, while a pretrained video prior

pool of prefill and history, keeping each target’s active context bounded even with dense inputs or long
histories (Section 4). Retrieval keeps earlier views accessible beyond the local window, persistent input views
anchor style and content, and recent motion context supports temporal continuity. By varying the context,
one model can reconstruct, explore, and revisit a scene.
We fine-tune a pretrained video diffusion transformer with flow matching in an N-in-1-out formulation, where
N context slots condition one target clip. We then derive a causal model, initialized off-policy with teacher
and diffusion forcing and tuned on its own rollouts. Distribution matching distillation and an adversarial
loss improve photorealism and reduce denoising steps. Context-augmented distillation conditions on prefill
and history just as inference does, and training on longer rollouts limits drift (Section 3.3). We train on
approximately 8M scenes and video clips from real-world captures and synthetic renderings (Section 3.4).
We evaluate generation capacity and scene persistence (Section 5). From one input image, AVM achieves
strong visual quality, and its camera control rivals that of view-synthesis models while substantially outper-
forming world models (Table 2). AVM also supports sparse reconstruction from images and 4D reconstruction
from video. Retrieval improves long-horizon revisits, where AVM leads on five of six metrics (Tables 3 to 6).
Text alignment and dynamic degree remain limitations. Figure 2 shows how AVM handles dense inputs and
long generated histories.
In summary, our contributions are: 1) unified in-context training for generation, reconstruction, and long-
horizon consistency across base training and causal tuning, 2) unified context management and retrieval
from prefill and history to support 3D consistency during generation, and 3) a unified benchmark protocol
for generation capacity and scene persistence across single-image, multi-image, video, and generated history
contexts.

Related Work
View-synthesis models.
NeRF [54] and 3D Gaussian splatting [38] typically require dense captures and time-
consuming per-scene fitting. Feed-forward regression predicts target views directly [34; 35; 70] or infers 3D
representations [10; 14; 97]. Diffusion models also predict views [23; 30; 87; 106] or 3D representations [2; 65],
and can additionally sample plausible unseen content. Conditioning on renders [16; 80; 86; 88; 99] or
geometric features [36; 40] can improve 3D consistency. 4D novel view synthesis must preserve dynamics
across viewpoints given single-camera captures [3; 62; 79; 83; 89] or synchronized multi-camera captures [8;
42; 52]. AVM supports text-conditioned generation of static and dynamic scenes from posed image sets or
synchronized video (Table 1). Causal tuning enables interactive streaming with few-step denoising and
key–value (KV) cache reuse across blocks.

Consolidated context (e.g., image #1, image #2, video clip #1) uses views from one scene without timestamps; synced
context (e.g., synced clip #1) provides simultaneous views from another camera at target timestamps. The transformer
processes context and target slots in one sequence.
World models.
World models generate driving video, game frames, and interactive observations from inputs
such as actions, text prompts, and a starting image or video prefix [9; 28; 29; 63; 75; 77; 108]. As rollouts
grow longer, the camera can revisit locations whose earlier views lie outside the model’s local context. For
scene consistency on revisits, models maintain rolling caches [63], condition on 3D renderings [90], encode
history as 3D-aware latents [100], or retrieve frames (by view overlap [19; 71; 98], position and yaw [13],
or point-cloud covisibility [66]). Likewise, AVM aims to preserve long-horizon scene consistency through
a unified in-context interface that retrieves prefill and history views for image-based reconstruction and
revisits.
Efficient video generation.
Repeated denoising over long spatiotemporal sequences motivates several ways
to accelerate video diffusion: 1) sparsifying attention with local windows [104], selected blocks [103], or
causal masks that block future frames or chunks and enable streaming with KV caching [32; 76; 96] as in
autoregressive language models, 2) distilling for fewer denoising steps via consistency along sampling
trajectories [41; 76] or distribution matching [94; 96; 109], and 3) applying inference-specific optimizations [48;
101; 102]. AVM starts causal tuning with teacher and diffusion forcing [11], then distills on-policy for few-step
generation.

3.1
Architecture
Diffusion transformer.
AVM fine-tunes a pretrained image-to-video diffusion transformer with 14 billion
parameters [81]. A spatiotemporal variational autoencoder (VAE) maps images and videos to latents z0, with
each latent representing an 8 × 8 × 4 video block. At diffusion timestep t ∈[0, 1], the transformer ingests
noisy latents zt = (1 −t)z0 + tϵ, where ϵ ∼N (0, I). The transformer processes the concatenated latents
[zctx

t ], as illustrated in Figure 3. It uses FlashAttention [101] for full attention (as in base training and
on-policy causal tuning) and FlexAttention [18] for sparse attention (as in off-policy causal tuning). Training
uses BF16 mixed precision, fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP2) [58], context parallelism (CP) with Ring
Attention [49], and selective activation checkpointing [59].

Training Objective
The model predicts the flow field ˆvi,t at each slot i, with ground truth vi,t = ϵi −zi,0. The flow-matching
loss [47] is LFM = E∥ˆv −v∥2
2, with the expectation taken over slots, timesteps, and noise draws.
In-context learning.
In-context learning lets language models adapt to examples without test-time parameter
updates [7]. AVM applies this principle through an N-in-1-out formulation: N context slots act as spatial
prompts for one target video clip. Images [23; 106] or video clips of the same scene provide consolidated
context; timestamps are discarded and temporal indices set to 0. Camera streams at target timestamps
provide synced context [3], sharing the target views’ temporal indices. Occasionally, initial frames of the
target clip provide prefix context for start-frame or motion conditioning during chunk-wise autoregressive
generation [76] (see Section 4). Context and target views are concatenated into one sequence, as shown in
Figure 3.
Error recycling.
Error-recycling fine-tuning (ERFT) [43] exposes the model to its own errors to reduce error
accumulation in long chunk-wise rollouts (e.g., those used as supervision for subsequent on-policy causal
distillation). At timestep t, the error e = −t(ˆvtgt −vtgt) is stored in a timestep-binned memory bank. Later
batches add stored errors to the noisy latents at all slots, ˜zt = zt + (1 −t)e, and to the reference latents at
context slots only, ˜cctx = cctx + e. A batch receives no recycled error with probability p (set to 0.5 in practice).
3.3
Causal Tuning
After base training, we use off-policy tuning as an initialization stage, followed by on-policy tuning on the
model’s own rollouts of varying length.
Off-policy tuning.
We retain the flow-matching objective with ERFT from Section 3.2 and initialize causal
tuning with a mixture of teacher forcing (TF) [85] and diffusion forcing (DF) [11]. TF conditions on clean
copies of the preceding blocks but halves the effective context length [32]. DF conditions on noisy preceding
blocks, creating a train–test mismatch with the denoised history used at inference [109]. AVM mixes TF and
DF to limit this mismatch while exposing the model to imperfect context [76]. Prefix context (Section 3.2) is
intrinsic to TF and can also be used with DF; Figure 8 illustrates the attention patterns.
On-policy tuning.
We continue to tune the model on its own rollouts using distribution matching distilla-
tion (DMD) [95] and an adversarial loss [24; 94] to sharpen the model’s output distribution and improve
photorealism. Few-step sampling with KV caching and classifier-free guidance (CFG) folding [32] enables
fast rollouts. To reduce error accumulation and encourage scene persistence over long horizons, we tune on
rollouts of gradually increasing length [15; 91], with reflected and synthetic camera trajectories (Section B).
Distillation mirrors sampling (Section 4): 1) context-augmented distillation [12; 75] conditions supervision on
prefill and history views (Figure 9), and 2) per-sample CFG distillation uses distinct guidance for static and
dynamic scenes. Table 6 ablates per-sample CFG distillation.

3.4
Data Modeling
Data sources.
We train on ∼6M labeled scenes and ∼2M unlabeled video clips. Labeled scenes span indoor
captures (e.g., ScanNet++ [93] and RealEstate10K [107]), outdoor captures (e.g., DL3DV [46]), posed in-
the-wild videos (e.g., SpatialVID [82]), object videos (e.g., uCO3D [51]), synthetic environment renderings
(e.g., Aria Synthetic Environments [1], ParallelDomain-4D [78], BlendedMVS [92], MVS-Synth [31], and
Spring [53]), game renderings, synchronized multi-camera video (e.g., MultiCamVideo [3]), and cubemap
renderings (e.g., Poly Haven [57]). Labeled scenes have metric-aligned camera poses and text annotations.
We use the unlabeled, unposed in-the-wild clips for camera-unconditional training to preserve the base
video model’s dynamics and text-prompt following. Training uses five-second clips and follows a resolution
curriculum, starting at 448 × 448 and then increasing to 720 × 1280.
Data augmentation.
Training samples are augmented to improve model robustness. Temporal downsampling,
applied only to static-scene videos, lets fixed-length video clips span longer, more complex camera trajectories.
FLUX edits [5] manipulate objects or transfer style in images, encouraging coherent target views despite
inconsistencies across context images. Original and augmented inputs are randomly mixed during training.
Data loading.
For each batch, a weighted sampler selects a task request specifying image count, video count,
clip length, and conditioning scheme. The request is routed to a matching dataset pool and is shared across
the batch; batch size is set per request. VAE latents for images and videos and T5 [61] embeddings for text
are precomputed offline.

Sampling
AVM samples view streams autoregressively, retrieving prefill and history views to condition target-view
generation.
Autoregressive generation.
Both the base and causal models can generate long videos autoregressively. Let
Lwin = Lmot + Ltgt denote the length of a local latent window: Lmot is the length of the motion context, which
supports temporal continuity, and Ltgt is the target length. The base model generates each target chunk
conditioned on motion context from the previous chunk (e.g., Lmot = 2, Ltgt = 19) and reprocesses its selected
context latents at every chunk. The causal model generates shorter target blocks within a fixed-size attention
window (e.g., Lmot = 15, Ltgt = 3) and reuses past states from a KV cache.

Context retrieval.
At each autoregressive step, AVM retrieves context using per-view metadata. 1) Camera
poses identify spatially adjacent views as consolidated context, with temporal indices reassigned to 0. Section E
details the selection, and Figure 10 compares Lrtr across policies. 2) Timestamps identify temporally aligned
views as synced context, with temporal indices matching the corresponding targets. 3) Other retrieval cues,
such as geotags on Internet images, are left for future study. Table 6 ablates retrieval.
Classifier-free guidance.
The base model uses 50 Euler steps with CFG. Let ˆv+ and ˆv−denote the flow
predictions under full and reduced conditioning, respectively; the guided flow is ˆvCFG = ˆv+ + (s −1)(ˆv+ −
ˆv−). For static scenes, ˆv−omits context and text conditioning, with s = 1.2 on context views and s = 5
on target views. For dynamic scenes, ˆv−omits only text conditioning, with s = 5 throughout. The causal
model uses 4 DDPM-style ancestral steps [27] with CFG folded in: during on-policy tuning, per-sample CFG
distillation bakes each training example’s static or dynamic guidance into the model (Section 3.3).

Evaluation
We evaluate AVM on a wide range of generation and reconstruction tasks. Ablations (Section 5.4) test model
and sampling choices.
5.1
Benchmark
Evaluation dimensions and metrics.
A view model must extrapolate beyond observed views, interpolate
between them, and maintain scene consistency. We therefore evaluate two complementary dimensions: 1)
Generation capacity assesses extrapolation through controllability (camera control and pose errors [20; 46],
object and text alignment [20]), visual quality (aesthetic and imaging scores [33], FID [26], VMAF [56],
DreamSim [22]), and dynamics (motion accuracy [20], dynamic degree and motion smoothness [33]); 2) Scene
persistence assesses interpolation through reconstruction from prefill views and consistency on revisits to
generated history, using PSNR [46], SSIM [84], LPIPS [105], place recognition (VPR) and geometric matching
(SP) [19], and 3D consistency (3D Cons.) [20]. See Section C for the protocol and Section D for metric
definitions.
Datasets, splits, and task setup.
We detail the datasets, splits, and task settings for generation capacity in
Section 5.2 and for scene persistence in Section 5.3.
Baselines.
As discussed in Section 2 and compared in Table 1, we evaluate against view-synthesis models
(GEN3C [62], Gen3R [30], SEVA [106], and ReCamMaster [3]) and world models (LingBot-World [63], HY-
WorldPlay 1.5 [75], and DreamX-World [19]). Metrics use generated frames directly unless stated otherwise.

5.2
Generation Capacity
We compare generation capacity using a starting image as context. For camera controllability and visual
quality, we use 1) 141 DL3DV-Benchmark (D) [46] scenes with the first 81 frames of each original trajectory;
and 2) 26 scenes from the static split (Ws) of WorldScore [20] with the 721-frame translation & rotation trajectory
(see Section A). For text and object controllability, we use 20 separate scenes from the static split, retaining the
original 241-frame trajectory and three-segment prompts. For dynamics, we use 32 scenes from the dynamic
split (Wd) of WorldScore [20], with a static camera and 81 frames.
Camera controllability.
Consistent with its view-synthesis formulation, AVM retains precise camera control
during autoregressive generation, remaining competitive with view-synthesis models and substantially
outperforming world models (70.67 vs. the best world model’s 3.91 on the Ws split). Figure 5 likewise shows
AVM following the camera trajectory more faithfully.
Text and object controllability.
AVM is competitive in object controllability but trails all world-model baselines
in text alignment (39.65 vs. 54.07 for the best baseline). In Figure 5, the garden-party prompt still produces
an indoor room while retaining elements of the input layout, illustrating weak text adherence alongside
structural continuity.
Visual quality.
AVM retains the strong visual quality of its pretrained image-to-video diffusion model
(Section 3.1), outperforming most view-synthesis models and world models across splits, notably in FID
(18.69 vs. the next-best 25.04 on the D split).
Dynamics.
AVM retains the pretrained video model’s capacity for motion, ranking second of the four models
evaluated on the Wd split in both motion accuracy and smoothness. However, its dynamic degree is the

lowest (24.88 vs. 35.55 for the best baseline), meaning that fewer of its videos are classified as dynamic. The
cow example in Figure 5 similarly preserves appearance across frames but shows only subtle pose changes.

6
Conclusion
AVM generates a scene as a stream of views, using one in-context interface for reconstruction, exploration,
and revisiting without fitting an explicit 3D representation. Observed views ground reconstruction and
interpolation, while a pretrained video prior supports extrapolation into unseen regions. Retrieval from
prefill and generated history lets the model use dense inputs and revisit earlier locations with a bounded
active context. Results across generation and reconstruction tasks show strong camera controllability and
visual quality, with retrieval improving revisit fidelity. Future directions include adapting stronger open-
weight video priors such as MiniMax H3 [55], adding optional geometric proxies for finer control over scene
structure, and integrating AVM into agentic workflows.

C
Evaluation Protocol
Metric resolution.
We rescale and center-crop generated frames and any corresponding reference frames to
448 × 448 before scoring. This lets us compare models with different native output resolutions at the same
scoring resolution.
Timestamp.
To hold a scene static, we use a constant timestamp across frames, as in static evaluations on the
D and Ws splits. For dynamic scenes, such as those on the Wd split, timestamps advance across frames to
represent scene motion.
D split.
We use all 141 DL3DV-Benchmark scenes.
Ws split.
For the translation & rotation trajectory (used to evaluate camera controllability and visual quality)
and the loop closure trajectory (used to evaluate revisits), we use the same 26 scenes. For the original three-
segment trajectory (used to evaluate text and object controllability), we use a separate set of 20 scenes. Both
subsets contain equal numbers of photorealistic and stylized scenes and are listed in Table 9.
19


Wd split.
For the original fixed-camera trajectory (used to evaluate dynamics), we use a 32-scene subset
spanning all five motion categories (Table 9).
MultiCam split.
For 4D reconstruction, we use the scenes listed in Table 9. We use camera 01 as input and
score all 81 synchronized frames from camera 02.
D
Evaluation Metrics
D.1
Controllability
Rotation error (Rot.).
Reconstruction methods (e.g., COLMAP [64]) can estimate camera poses from generated
clips. Following WorldScore [20], we measure the mean geodesic angle ¯r, in degrees, between the estimated
and requested rotations. Lower is better.
Relative translation error (Trans.).
We adapt WorldScore’s position error [20] by dividing the mean aligned
position error by twice the mean distance of the requested cameras from the starting pose. Both errors are
observable on translation & rotation paths. Lower is better.
Camera control (Camera).
We adapt WorldScore’s geometric mean [20] to combine rotation error ¯r and relative
translation error ¯t as 100
p
(1 −clip(¯r/15, 0, 1))(1 −clip(¯t/0.5, 0, 1)). Higher is better.
Object controllability (Obj.).
We use WorldScore’s open-set object-detection success score [20] at frames 0,
80, 160, and 240. TransNet V2 [68] flags the first cut or gradual transition whose probability exceeds 0.5;
normalized anchor scores at or after that transition are set to zero before all four anchors are averaged. Clips
without a detected transition retain WorldScore’s original aggregation. Higher is better.
Text alignment (Text).
We use WorldScore’s text alignment [20], which compares each anchor frame with
the full scene description using CLIP [60]. The same four anchors and TransNet V2 [68] transition gate used
for object controllability prevent a scene change from improving text alignment at the expense of continuity.
Higher is better.
D.2
Visual Quality
Aesthetic quality (Aesth.).
Following VBench [33], the LAION aesthetic predictor [39] scores every frame on a
0–10 scale; we divide by ten and average over all evaluated frames and scenes. Higher is better.
Imaging quality (Image).
Following VBench [33], MUSIQ [37] predicts the perceptual image quality of every
frame, including the effects of blur, noise, and exposure. We average the raw MUSIQ scores over all evaluated
frames and scenes. Higher is better.
Fréchet Inception distance (FID).
We pool Inception features [72] from all evaluated generated frames and
their reference frames, and compute one global FID [26] for each method and dataset. Lower is better.
Video multi-method assessment fusion (VMAF).
VMAF [56] computes a full-reference perceptual video score
between each generated sequence and its aligned target sequence. We average sequence scores over scenes.
Higher is better.
DreamSim distance (DSim).
DreamSim [22] measures the learned perceptual distance between each generated
frame and its corresponding target frame. We average over evaluated frames and scenes. Lower is better.
20


D.3
Dynamics
Motion accuracy (Acc.).
WorldScore motion accuracy [20] compares optical-flow magnitude inside the
prompt-designated moving region with flow outside it, discounting global motion caused by camera move-
ment. Higher is better.
Dynamic degree (Deg.).
VBench [33] uses RAFT [73] to estimate optical flow between adjacent frames,
averages the largest 5% of flow magnitudes, and classifies each video as static or dynamic. Dynamic degree
is the proportion of videos classified as dynamic. Higher is better.
Motion smoothness (Smooth.).
VBench [33] removes odd-numbered frames, reconstructs them with the AMT
frame interpolation model [44], and converts the reconstruction error to a normalized smoothness score.
Higher is better.
D.4
Scene Persistence
Peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR).
PSNR [46] compares corresponding generated and target RGB frames for
input-context reconstruction, or, for generated-history revisits, the two frames generated at the revisited pose.
Scores are averaged within each scene and then across scenes. Higher is better.
Structural similarity (SSIM).
SSIM [84] compares luminance, contrast, and local structure for the same frame
pairs used by PSNR. Scores are averaged within each scene and then across scenes. Higher is better.
Learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS).
LPIPS [105] measures deep-feature distance for the same
frame pairs. Scores are averaged within each scene and then across scenes. Lower is better.
Matching pixels (Mat. Pix.).
We use GIM [67] to match pixels between corresponding generated and target
frames and count correspondences above the benchmark confidence threshold. We average counts over
frames and scenes and report the result in thousands. Higher is better.
Place recognition (VPR).
Following DreamX-World [19], we extract one global MutualVPR descriptor [25]
from each image and report the cosine similarity between paired views. This metric tests whether two views
remain recognizable as the same place despite a viewpoint change. Higher is better.
Geometric matching (SP).
Following DreamX-World [19], we detect up to 1,024 SuperPoint keypoints [17] in
each image and match them with LightGlue [45]. For a pair with Ni and Nj detected keypoints and Nmatch
accepted matches, we report Nmatch/ min(Ni, Nj). The score is undefined when either image has no detected
keypoints, which occurs for six HY-WorldPlay revisit pairs. Higher is better.
3D consistency (3D Cons.).
Following WorldScore [20], DROID-SLAM [74] estimates dense depth and camera
motion, and the metric measures reprojection error between covisible pixels in consecutive frames. The
benchmark converts this error to a normalized consistency score. Higher is better.
