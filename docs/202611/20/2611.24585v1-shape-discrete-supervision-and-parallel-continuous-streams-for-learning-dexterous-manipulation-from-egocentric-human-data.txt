Abstract 

Egocentric human recordings are far cheaper than dexterous robot demonstrations, promising stronger pretraining and direct skill learning without task-specific robot data. Yet humans and robots differ in morphology and kinematics, so human recordings lack the joint positions and actuator commands that supervise robot policies, providing only retargeted end-effector (EEF) actions. This raises two questions: how human data should supervise the policy, and how a dexterous robot should read out actions at deployment. We address both in SHAPE (Supervision from Human Action tokens with Parallel Executable streams), a vision-language-action (VLA) model whose backbone predicts discrete action tokens of EEF chunks as an auxiliary task, while a shared flow-matching expert outputs two executable streams, EEF and joint. On a bimanual robot with 22-DoF hands, a matched study shows that discrete supervision helps learn from human data , driving backbone gradients and shaping action-informative representations across domains. Under standard control pipelines at deployment, direct joint readout excels at fine-grained control with robot demonstrations , scoring 0.80 across six tasks against 0.63 for 

π0.5, whereas IK-based EEF readout excels at human-to-robot transfer . Pretrained with human data and discrete tokens, SHAPE learns new tasks from human demonstrations without task-specific robot data . The project page is available here. 

# 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models learn general manipulation skills by pretraining on large collections of robot demonstrations (Brohan et al., 2023a; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2026). For dexterous bimanual manipulation such demonstrations are expensive: each one requires an oper-ator to teleoperate two arms and two multi-fingered hands. Egocentric human recordings with tracked hand poses are much cheaper to collect at scale (Liu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Banerjee et al., 2025; Hoque et al., 2026; Punamiya et al., 2026) and cover the same everyday activities. Such recordings hold two promises for dexterous manipulation: improving pretraining when downstream robot demonstrations are available, and enabling a policy to learn new tasks directly from human demonstrations alone ,with zero task-specific robot data.

Fulfilling both promises on dexterous hands is challenging because humans and robots differ in morphology and kinematics. Human recordings lack the joint positions and actuator commands that supervise robot policies: tracked hands can be retargeted to a shared end-effector (EEF) space, whereas mapping them to robot joints via inverse kinematics (IK) relies on embodiment-specific correspondences and yields out-of-distribution arm postures. This action gap creates two dilemmas. First, how should human data supervise the policy? Many methods supervise shared EEF actions via a continuous action decoder (Kareer et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a; Qiu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b; Zhao et al., 2026), sending gradients to the backbone only through that decoder; recent VLAs also train the backbone on discrete action tokens (Luo et al., 2026a; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; Driess et al., 2025), including on human data (Kareer et al., 2026). Yet because they differ in data and architecture at once, or combine both losses without separating them (Kareer et al., 2026), whether and how discrete supervision helps learn from human data remains unclear. Second, how should a dexterous robot read out actions at deployment? Unlike 1-DoF grippers, multi-fingered hands introduce high redundancy between fingertip poses and finger joints. Prior policies add a robot-only joint output beside a shared EEF output but execute only the joint output (Kareer et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026). Yet the two outputs excel in different settings: direct joint readout is more accurate when fine-tuned with robot demonstrations, while EEF readout via IK transfers better from human demonstrations. In this work, we find that auxiliary discrete action supervision helps the backbone learn from egocentric human data , and we pair it with a dual-stream action expert that keeps both EEF and joint streams executable : the joint stream for precise control with robot demonstrations, and the EEF stream for human-to-robot transfer. As shown in Fig. 1, in SHAPE (Supervision from Human Action tokens with Parallel Executable streams), the backbone predicts discrete FAST tokens (Pertsch et al., 2025) of each EEF chunk in one vocabulary for human and robot samples, supervising the backbone directly. In parallel, a shared flow-matching action expert independently outputs an EEF stream, supervised by both human and robot data, and a joint stream, supervised only by robot data in pretraining. Because both streams share the backbone and expert, human pretraining improves the joint stream without human joint labels, while either stream can drive the robot at test time. To evaluate both choices under controlled conditions, we pretrain four models in a matched 2 ×2 design that toggles human data and shared LFAST while fixing the architecture, robot data, and training budget. The human data comprise 2.8k hours of egocentric recordings, and the robot data 228 hours of teleoperation of two 7-DoF arms with 22-DoF hands. We evaluate the four models (reading out both streams) and two baselines in 1,680 real-robot trials, offline on held-out tasks, and via their representations and gradients. In simulation, 

2we also evaluate all four models alongside both baselines on a second robot absent from pretraining, across both real-to-sim and cross-embodiment gaps. Our contributions are: • We find that discrete action supervision helps a VLA learn dexterous manipulation from egocentric human data , driving backbone gradients and task-aligned action representations across domains. • We propose a dual-stream action expert with executable EEF and joint streams , transferring human pretraining to joint control without human joint labels while supporting flexible stream selection at deployment. • Pretrained with human data and discrete tokens, SHAPE excels in both downstream regimes: fine-tuned on human demos alone, it learns new tasks without task-specific robot data ; fine-tuned on robot demos, it scores 0.80 across six tasks against 0.63 for π0.5.

# 2 Related Work  

> Vision-language-action models.

Recent VLAs couple a pretrained vision-language model (VLM) with an action decoder, either by emitting discretized action tokens (Brohan et al., 2023a; Kim et al., 2024; Pertsch et al., 2025) or by attaching a continuous diffusion or flow-matching expert (Black et al., 2025; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024, 2026a), with extensions for tactile feedback or sensory prediction (Zhou et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) and cross-embodiment pretraining (Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2024b; Luo et al., 2026b). π0.5 with knowledge insulation (Driess et al., 2025) also trains the VLM on FAST tokens while an expert produces continuous actions, but stops expert gradients from reaching the VLM and learns actions from robot data only. We keep both paths trainable and ask a different question: what discrete supervision does for learning from human data.  

> Learning manipulation from human videos.

Human videos have been used to pretrain visual representa-tions (Grauman et al., 2022; Nair et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2022; Majumdar et al., 2023) or action priors (Shaw et al., 2022), predict affordances, flow, point tracks, or keypoints (Bahl et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2024; Bharad-hwaj et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026c; Cai et al., 2026c), learn latent actions or visual foresight (Ye et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b; Cai et al., 2026a), and, with tracked hands or motion-capture gloves, supervise actions in a shared EEF space (Wang et al., 2024a; Kareer et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026a; Luo et al., 2026a,b; Bi et al., 2026; Lepert et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b; Qiu et al., 2025; Kareer et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Punamiya et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026b; Guan et al., 2026). Co-training does not always help: gains require diverse pretraining (Kareer et al., 2026) or embodiment-specific networks (Li et al., 2026b). While Phantom (Lepert et al., 2025) renders a robot over the human arm and others align human and robot features (Punamiya et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2025), π0.5+ego (Kareer et al., 2026) uses both FAST tokens and flow matching on human data for grippers without separating the two, and EgoLAP (Zha et al., 2026) supervises the VLM with structured language actions and motion rationales on bimanual grippers while stopping flow-expert gradients at the backbone (Driess et al., 2025). Using neither rendering, explicit feature alignment, nor gradient insulation, our matched 2 ×2 pretraining study and continuous-head controls on a dexterous bimanual robot isolate what direct backbone supervision adds to learning from human data, when fine-tuning downstream tasks both with and without robot demonstrations.  

> Action representations for dexterous control.

Action chunks can be tokenized (Brohan et al., 2023b; Lee et al., 2024; Pertsch et al., 2025) or modeled continuously with diffusion (Chi et al., 2023) or flow matching (Black et al., 2025). For dexterous hands, policies act either in EEF and fingertip space, which transfers across embodiments via IK (Wang et al., 2024a), or in joint space, which is precise but embodiment-specific (Shaw et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2022, 2023; Handa et al., 2020); a large study on arms with grippers finds joint space more stable and task space better at generalizing (Feng et al., 2026). # 3 Method 

3.1 Problem setup 

We learn a policy that maps an observation ot and an instruction ℓ to an action chunk At = [ at, . . . , a t+H−1]

with H = 64 , from two data sources. Robot teleoperation Dr provides head and wrist camera images, proprioception, and actions in both EEF and joint space. Egocentric human recordings Dh provide head-camera images and tracked 3D hand poses, but lack executable joint states and actuator commands; we retarget the tracked hands to derive EEF actions. 

Action parameterization. Both action spaces are relative to the current state. For each hand, let Tt ∈ SE(3) 

be the wrist pose at time t and pt the positions of the five fingertips in the wrist frame, and let qt be the joint positions. For k = 0 , . . . , H − 1, the EEF action contains the wrist pose expressed in the current wrist frame, T −1

t Tt+k, and the fingertip offsets pt+k − pt; the joint action is the offset qt+k − qt. In pretraining, each data source is normalized with its own statistics. For human recordings, the hand is first estimated with the MANO hand model (Romero et al., 2017) and then retargeted to the robot hand, and the fingertip positions are those of the retargeted hand. Expressing EEF actions in the current wrist frame cancels camera extrinsics and decouples the action target from the underlying arm joint configuration. 

3.2 Backbone and dual-stream action expert 

As shown in Fig. 2, a single backbone and a single action expert serve both embodiments; only the output streams differ. The backbone is Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai et al., 2025). Its input contains image tokens, instruction tokens, and a state prefix of two tokens that embed the current EEF state and, for robot samples, the joint state. During training each state token is dropped independently with probability 0.5, so that the policy can run with or without it. Following recent VLAs, the backbone also predicts the current sub-task as text (Ltext ; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) and processes 64 learnable future queries that condition a Wan video 

4diffusion head (Team Wan et al., 2025) to predict frames about 1 s ahead ( Lvideo ; Yang et al., 2026b,c; Ma et al., 2026). The action expert is a 0.6B transformer that attends to the backbone keys and values at every layer (Black et al., 2025). Appendix A gives architectural details. The action expert is shared by two parallel output streams. The 55-d EEF stream represents each side by its 3D wrist position, a 6-d wrist rotation, and five 3D fingertip positions, and adds 7 torso and neck joints. The 65-d joint stream covers two 7-DoF arms, two 22-DoF hands, and the same 7 torso and neck joints. Each stream has its own input and output projections and is trained with a flow-matching loss (Lipman et al., 2023), LEEF FM or Ljoint FM . In the attention mask (Fig. 2, bottom right), each stream reads the image, sub-task, and query tokens and its own state token, but neither the instruction nor the other stream, so the two streams can be executed independently at deployment. A per-sample mask removes losses whose labels are missing: in pretraining, human samples supervise the EEF stream but not the joint stream, yet still shape the joint stream through the shared backbone and expert. 

3.3 Discrete action supervision 

With the continuous streams alone, action supervision reaches the backbone only through the action expert, and human samples provide no joint labels in pretraining. To supervise the backbone directly through its native token head on every sample, we predict discrete FAST tokens (Pertsch et al., 2025; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; Driess et al., 2025; Kareer et al., 2026) of each normalized EEF chunk as an auxiliary task. Avoiding dense per-step binning (Brohan et al., 2023b) or learned vector quantizers (Lee et al., 2024), FAST compresses chunks via a discrete cosine transform and byte-pair encoding, with its 1,024-token vocabulary mapped onto the backbone’s. Sharing one vocabulary across human and robot chunks lets the backbone predict them autoregressively under LFAST (compared against continuous backbone heads in Sec. 6.4). In the attention mask (Fig. 2), the action expert cannot read the FAST tokens, preventing shortcut copying so they can be omitted at deployment. 

3.4 Training 

The primary training objectives are optimized jointly: 

L = LEEF FM + mJ Ljoint FM + λF LFAST + λV Lvideo + λT Ltext , (1) where mJ masks samples without joint labels, λF = 0 .1, λV = 1 , and λT = 0 .2 (full hyperparameters in Appendix A). Pretraining samples human and robot data 1 : 1; robot-demo fine-tuning retains each model’s pretraining losses (Secs. 5.1, 5.4), whereas human-only fine-tuning disables LFAST across all models to isolate pretraining and compare both streams equally (Sec. 5.2). 

3.5 Deployment 

At test time, only the backbone and the action expert run; the auxiliary FAST tokens are omitted (see Appendix E.5 for an analysis of token decoding), and each rollout executes one of the two continuous streams (Fig. 2, top right). The joint stream commands the joint controllers directly, whereas the EEF stream converts predicted wrist poses and fingertip positions into joint targets via inverse kinematics. Keeping both streams executable within a single model allows flexible stream selection at deployment and lets us compare direct joint readout against IK-based EEF readout from the same checkpoint across both deployment regimes (Sec. 5.3).

Tasks. Six real-robot tasks have robot demonstrations (Fig. 3): precise insertion (Insert Battery), unplug-ging and reinserting a connector (Reinsert USB, Reinsert Type-C), a handover (Wipe Bowl), an articu-lated object (Open Cabinet), and in-hand twisting (Close Bottle Cap), each fine-tuned on 196–447 teleoper-ated demonstrations. Baselines. Both start from their public checkpoints and are fine-tuned on the same demonstrations in the same joint action spaces, for 30 epochs on the real robot and 100k steps (batch 256) in simulation. Where does the gain come from? The matched ablations show that discrete supervision and human pre-training contribute complementary gains rather than architectural advantages alone. Without human data, our model matches π0.5 (0.64 vs. 0.63). Including LFAST across pretraining and fine-tuning raises the six-task score by 0.22–0.33 across both streams, with or without human data ( p < 0.001 ; Secs. 5.2 and 6 isolate pretraining LFAST directly). Holding LFAST fixed, adding human pretraining data raises the joint-stream score from 0.64 to 0.80 (+0.16, p = 0 .002 ), improving all five non-ceiling tasks and lifting success on the four binary tasks from 76.3% (61/80) to 90.0% (72/80); together, both ingredients raise the joint-stream score by +0.38 over w/o both (0.42 to 0.80). 

5.2 Human-to-robot transfer 

Setup. For each H2R task we fine-tune every pretrained model on egocentric human demonstrations only, masking out wrist camera inputs as detailed in Appendix D.1, and deploy it on the robot with zero task-specific robot demonstrations (Fig. 4). Fine-tuning supervises the EEF stream with human EEF actions and the joint stream with retargeted arm and hand joints, and both streams are read out from the same fine-tuned checkpoint. Because FAST tokens encode only EEF actions, LFAST is disabled for all four models during fine-tuning, comparing both streams on equal terms and isolating the effect of pretraining. 

Combining human data and discrete supervision performs substantially best. Table 3 summarizes the H2R transfer results. Without downstream robot demonstrations, either ingredient alone achieves limited transfer: robot-only pretraining reaches 0/60 (0%) without LFAST and 4/60 (6.7%) with it, while human pretraining without LFAST reaches 7/60 (11.7%); combining both in SHAPE reaches 21/60 (35.0%), a +28.3-point gain from human data ( p < 0.001 ).

Pretraining LFAST drives transfer. On the human-pretrained models, pretraining with LFAST raises transfer success from 7/60 (11.7%) to 21/60 (35.0%, +23.3 points, p = 0 .004 ), improving every task. Because all four models share identical fine-tuning supervision with LFAST disabled, this +23.3-point gain is consistent with a pretraining effect of LFAST (analyzed in Sec. 6.2). 

The EEF stream adapts to object placement. With the EEF stream, human-pretrained models adapt to varied object placements: SHAPE reaches toward the object’s position and places it at the target across different layouts rather than replaying a fixed trajectory. Most failures occur at the grasp, where the hand reaches the object but closes too loosely to lift it, reflecting morphological and kinematic gaps that retargeting does not fully bridge. The joint stream instead replays the demonstrated motion, barely adapts to object position, and never succeeds (Sec. 5.3). 

5.3 Which stream to deploy? 

Shared training, flexible readout. Human recordings never supervise the joint stream in pretraining, reach-ing it only through the shared backbone and action expert (Sec. 3.2). Even so, human pretraining with LFAST 

raises the joint stream’s six-task score from 0.64 to 0.80 on the real robot (Sec. 5.1) and its success rate by +7.0 points in simulation (Sec. 5.4). Keeping both streams executable then lets us compare direct joint readout against IK-based EEF readout from the same fine-tuned checkpoint. 

With robot demonstrations, direct joint readout excels at fine-grained control. From the same check-points, direct joint readout outperforms IK-based EEF readout in 23 of 24 model–task pairs (Table 2), raising each model’s six-task score by 0.17–0.28. Gains are largest on contact-rich tasks such as Close Bottle Cap, Wipe Bowl, and Insert Battery. In open-loop evaluation on the 52 validation episodes of the six tasks (Ap-pendix A.5), however, both streams—converting joint predictions via forward kinematics—achieve the same mean 3D Euclidean error over 64-step chunks (right wrist: 11.6 vs. 11.5 mm; fingertips: 4.8 vs. 4.9 mm). The gap instead arises during execution, consistent with under-constrained fingertip-to-joint IK: five 3D finger-tip targets (15 values) under-constrain a 22-DoF hand, so small target or rotation errors can perturb the interphalangeal joints that govern grasp curl and contact. Direct joint readout commands all arm and finger joints without this inversion step. 

With only human demonstrations, wrist-frame EEF readout enables transfer. Under our human-retargeting pipeline, the advantage reverses: across the four models and three H2R tasks, 0 of 240 joint-stream rollouts succeed vs. 32 of 240 for the EEF stream (Table 3; Appendix B). In joint space, the 3D hand motion caused by an offset ∆q depends non-linearly on the arm posture qt via its Jacobian (Feng et al., 2026). Because per-frame retargeted human arm postures lie outside the robot’s posture distribution, as analyzed in Appendix D.2, joint offsets learned around them fail from the robot’s home posture. Even without a policy, replaying a demonstration’s ∆q open loop from this home posture leaves the right hand 10.3 cm from its EEF grasp target on average, stopping in front of and above the object. Wrist-frame EEF readout instead predicts 3D hand motion relative to the wrist and delegates joint resolution to IK at the robot’s own posture, letting one dual-stream model serve both settings.

5.4 A second robot in simulation 

Do human data and discrete supervision also help in simulation, on a robot absent from pretraining? We fine-tune the four matched models alongside π0.5 and GR00T N1.7 on the 20.8k teleoperated demonstrations of the 24 GR-1 tabletop tasks of RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025) under the same training budget and evaluate 50 episodes per task across three checkpoints (Table 4), with per-task results reported in Appendix F. This benchmark tests transfer across both a real-to-sim gap, as all pretraining data are real-world recordings, and a cross-embodiment gap: the GR-1 humanoid has only a head camera and a 29-DoF action space comprising two 7-DoF arms, two 6-DoF Fourier hands (instead of 22-DoF hands), and a 3-DoF waist. SHAPE reaches 72.2% overall, exceeding π0.5 (64.8%, +7.4 points, p < 0.001 ) and GR00T N1.7 (62.6%, +9.6 points), whereas w/o human matches π0.5 (65.2% vs. 64.8%) just as on the real robot. Across the 2 ×2 models, the two ingredients act nearly additively: human pretraining adds +7.0 points with 

LFAST (65.2% to 72.2%, p < 0.001 ) and +7.6 points without it (62.0% to 69.6%), while LFAST adds +2.6 points with human data and +3.2 points without it, for a combined +10.2-point gain over w/o both.

6.2 Action encoding and cross-embodiment task structure 

We next probe backbone features along two dimensions: linear action decodability and cross-embodiment task structure (Fig. 6). On last-layer prefix-mean features (Fig. 6a), models pretrained without human data carry negligible action signal on human frames ( R2 ≤ 0.002 , transfer r ≤ 0.05 ). With human data, adding

Beyond encoding motion magnitudes, LFAST organizes human and robot frames around shared task structure without explicit alignment loss (Fig. 6b, c). Across three task families recorded in both domains—evaluated under one shared task-agnostic prompt with per-domain means removed—57% of a human frame’s ten nearest robot frames at the last layer come from its matched task for SHAPE, vs. 37% for w/o FAST (+19 points, 95% CI [+15, +23]), 37–46% across all ablations, and 33% by chance (Fig. 6b). On head-camera image tokens from the vision tower through the language model (Fig. 6c; Appendix E.3), SHAPE exceeds w/o FAST by 8–21 points at every probed layer, staying at 55–64% in the deep layers vs. 40–45% without LFAST .

6.4 Gradient scale vs. discrete action tokens 

Does any strong continuous action target on the backbone suffice? Replacing FAST tokens with 64 learn-able queries in the same backbone slot—reading the identical prefix, mutually visible across the 64 steps, and masked from the action expert—to regress normalized EEF chunks via an L1 MLP head matched in initial backbone gradient norm ( λ = 0 .1) fails to sustain supervision: its backbone gradient norm shrinks to 

0.11 –0.13 × L FAST from 5k steps on, leaving layer-24 probes at the w/o FAST level (Appendix E.6). Sustained direct backbone gradients are therefore necessary. Dynamically rescaling the L1 weight ( 7–10 ×) to track LFAST ’s backbone gradient norm throughout pre-training (Table 5) separates the two representation properties of Sec. 6.2: it lifts layer-24 features, matches SHAPE on held-out robot error while lowering human error, and yields higher last-layer linear action probes (R2 = 0 .230 vs. 0.143 ) by emphasizing coarse chunk-mean displacement, yet erodes cross-embodiment task clustering from 66% at layer 24 down to 40% at the last layer (matching w/o FAST at 37%, vs. 57% for SHAPE). Closed-loop transfer follows task-structure preservation rather than linear probe R2 (Table 5; Appendix E.6). With zero target-robot demonstrations (H2R, auxiliary action heads disabled for all models), the gradient-matched head reaches 18.3% vs. 35.0% for SHAPE ( p = 0 .055 ); on RoboCasa GR-1 (20.8k demos), omitting the head during fine-tuning recovers 70.7% ( p = 0 .11 ), whereas retaining it interferes with fine-tuning and 

11 drops success to 68.9% ( p = 0 .001 vs. SHAPE’s 72.2%). Conversely, greedily decoding SHAPE’s own FAST tokens at test time leaves 15% of robot windows invalid and trails deterministic zero-noise integration of its EEF stream by 1.4 mm at the right wrist on valid windows (14.9 vs. 14.7 mm overall against stochastic sampling; Appendix E.5), confirming that discrete tokens work best as a self-sustaining, structure-preserving backbone target paired with continuous expert execution. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We presented SHAPE , a dexterous VLA that pairs auxiliary discrete action supervision on the backbone with a shared dual-stream action expert outputting executable EEF and joint streams. In a matched 2 ×2study, predicting discrete EEF tokens helps the policy learn from egocentric human data: it drives sustained action gradients on the backbone without dynamic reweighting or conflict with flow matching, and shapes action-informative representations while preserving last-layer task alignment across domains better than continuous regression controls. Sharing the backbone and expert transfers human pretraining to the joint stream without human joint labels, while keeping both streams executable serves complementary deployment pipelines: direct joint readout excels with robot demonstrations, whereas wrist-frame EEF readout via IK enables transfer from human demonstrations alone. Future work will explore scaling this paradigm to larger human video corpora and more robot embodiments.

# A Implementation details 

A.1 Architecture 

The backbone is Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai et al., 2025): a 0.42B vision encoder and a 4.02B language model with 36 layers and hidden size 2,560. The action expert has the same 36 layers, each attending to the backbone keys and values of its layer, with hidden size 768, feed-forward size 2,752, and 32 query and 8 key–value heads of dimension 128 (0.60B parameters). The flow-matching time enters through adaptive normalization, and inference integrates the flow in 10 Euler steps. The video head is Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B (Team Wan et al., 2025) (1.42B parameters with query adapters), with its DiT trained and its VAE frozen. Its 30 DiT blocks cross-attend to the 64 future queries—block k reads backbone layer k + 6 after shared normalization and linear projection—and four head-camera motion tokens (replaced by a learned null token when unavailable and in 30% of samples). Conditioned on the noise-free current latent frame, Lvideo is the flow-matching loss on the latent encoding of target frames t + 8 , t + 16 ,

t+24 , and t+32 at 30 Hz (arranged as a head-plus-wrists mosaic with wrist tiles masked on human recordings, and noise drawn from [0 .7, 1] in 80% of samples so the DiT relies on the queries). The sub-task text of a training chunk is the annotated sub-task that overlaps it most (e.g., “Reach with the right hand, grasp the matte black smartphone from the rear desk, and lift it”); at test time it is decoded greedily. 

A.2 Action and state spaces 

Actions are predicted at 60 Hz in chunks of 64 steps (1.07 s), and images are sampled at 30 Hz; at deployment the policy executes 50 steps (0.83 s) before re-planning. Table S1 lists the dimensions of both streams. Two of the seven torso and neck joints never move and are masked. Human recordings carry no torso or neck labels, so these dimensions are masked for human samples.

# C Statistical methods 

Score intervals. Each policy is run for n = 20 trials per task, and each trial earns a score x ∈ [0 , 1] : 1 for a success and 0 for a failure, or for Reinsert USB and Reinsert Type-C the credit of the last stage completed (Table S4). If μ is the mean score of a policy on a task, the variance of x is at most μ(1 − μ), because x2 ≤ x,with equality for binary scores. The six-task score of a policy is S = P 

> t

μt/6, where μt is its mean score on task t. Since the tasks are run independently, the variance of S is at most V = P 

> t

μt(1 − μt)/(36 n), and we report the 95% interval S ± 1.96 √V . For the total success rates in human-to-robot transfer (Table 3), we report exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95% intervals over the 60 trials of the three tasks. 

Comparisons. With robot demonstrations, two policies with six-task scores S1 and S2 and variance bounds 

V1 and V2 (or four-task scores restricted to the binary tasks) are compared by a two-sided z-test on z =(S1 − S2)/√V1 + V2. In human-to-robot transfer, two policies on one task are compared by a two-sided Fisher exact test on their success counts, and over tasks by the exact conditional Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) test stratified by task. Differences and their intervals are computed from unrounded values, so a reported difference can deviate from the difference of the rounded entries in the last digit.

# D Human-to-robot fine-tuning 

D.1 Setup 

Each pretrained model is fine-tuned from its EMA weights on the human recordings of one task only, for 14,261 steps of 128 samples, with LFAST disabled across all models (Sec. 5.2). Images are the head-camera frames of the human capture, cropped to match the field of view of the robot’s head camera; because the capture has no wrist cameras, wrist images are masked out of attention both in fine-tuning and on the robot, as for human samples in pretraining. Both streams are supervised: the EEF stream with tracked wrists and retargeted robot fingertips (Sec. 3.1), and the joint stream with retargeted joints. 

D.2 Why joint actions do not transfer 

As discussed in Sec. 5.3, replayed open loop from the robot’s home posture without any model, a human demonstration’s joint offsets ∆q place the right hand 10.3 cm away from where its EEF actions put it at the grasp, on average over the 745 Pick Eggplant demonstrations in Table S3 (Fig. S1). Quantifying the underlying posture gap, retargeted human arm postures lie outside the robot’s posture distribution: none is within 10 ◦ of a robot posture on all 14 arm joints, whereas 92% of the robot postures are within 10 ◦ of a posture from other robot episodes (median nearest distance 38 ◦ vs. 6 ◦). 

# E Offline, representation, and gradient analyses 

E.1 Offline evaluation 

For each evaluation window (a fixed starting frame and 64-step target chunk), every model and checkpoint samples one 64-step action chunk with the same random noise, and we compute the mean 3D Euclidean position error of the wrists and fingertips over the 64 steps (converting joint-stream predictions via forward kinematics), paired by task across models. The main test set has six splits and 14,100 windows, read out from the EEF stream: training episodes (808 episodes of 105 tasks, 2,392 windows), new episodes of these tasks (14 tasks, 211 episodes, 2,379 windows), held-out tasks (3 robot tasks absent from pretraining, 814 episodes, 2,395 windows; labeled held-out tasks (EEF) in Fig. 5), robot data of human tasks (5 robot tasks seen only in the human pretraining data, 536 episodes, 2,288 windows), and human recordings from training episodes (236 tasks, 852 episodes, 2,387 windows) and from new episodes (15 tasks, 100 episodes, 2,259 windows). The second test set evaluates independent teleoperated robot recordings through both the EEF stream and the joint stream (via forward kinematics) across three splits: unseen validation episodes of 58 pretraining tasks ( seen tasks ; 58 episodes, 577 windows), new-batch and new-scene captures of 4 pretraining task families (held-out setup ; 120 episodes, 1,088 windows), and 6 tasks absent from all pretraining data ( unseen tasks ; 180 episodes, 1,808 windows, whose joint readout is labeled unseen tasks (joint) in Fig. 5). We report position errors of the right wrist, right fingertips, and left wrist (Fig. S2); CIs are task-level bootstrap.

E.5 Decoding the FAST tokens 

As a deployment ablation testing why SHAPE executes the continuous expert rather than decoding its auxiliary tokens (Sec. 3.5), we greedily decode the FAST head on the 2,400 windows of Appendix E.1 (400 per subset) at 30k and 40k steps over the 1,024 FAST ids and EOS (up to 512 tokens) and detokenize valid sequences into 64 × 55 EEF chunks (returning the midpoint of the normalization range when invalid). On the same backbone pass, we compare against the EEF stream of the expert, both sampled with random noise and integrated deterministically from zero initial noise ( zero-noise expert ). Autoregressive token decoding is less reliable and less accurate than the continuous expert (Table S8): at 40k steps, 15% of robot windows fail to form a valid chunk, overall robot right-wrist error is 14.9 mm vs. 14.7 mm (sampled) and 12.4 mm (zero-noise), and even on valid windows decoded tokens trail the zero-noise expert by 1.4 mm; 30k shows the same pattern. Without human pretraining data, the FAST head decodes only 1 of 800 human windows (next-token accuracy 0.06 vs. 0.28 for SHAPE), whereas on robot data the two models decode alike. Discrete FAST tokens are therefore best used as a training-time backbone target while leaving test-time execution to the continuous expert.

E.6 Continuous-head controls on the backbone 

Control design. To separate the role of backbone gradient magnitude from that of discrete autoregressive prediction, we train two matched pretraining controls that replace the FAST tokens with 64 learnable queries in the exact same positions of the backbone sequence, mutually visible to one another and masked from the action expert as in SHAPE, and regress the normalized 64 × 55 EEF chunk from their last-layer hidden states via a two-layer MLP under an L1 loss (network initialization, random seeds, and data order match SHAPE and w/o FAST). The fixed-weight control sets λ = 0 .1 (trained to 15k steps) so that its unweighted backbone gradient norm matches LFAST at initialization. Because L1 regression saturates early, however, its backbone gradient norm shrinks to 0.11 –0.13 × that of LFAST from 5k steps onward: at 15k steps it supplies only 2% and 8% of the language model’s expected gradient energy on human and robot batches (vs. 58% and 61% for LFAST ) and 1% of the vision tower’s (vs. 26% and 50%). It recovers the last-layer prefix-mean probe ( R2 = 0 .117 vs. 0.121 for SHAPE and 0.082 for w/o FAST) and matched-task neighbor share ( 0.603 

vs. 0.608 ), but leaves intermediate probes at the w/o FAST level (layer-24 head-camera tokens: 0.042 vs. 

0.046 for w/o FAST and 0.083 for SHAPE) and recovers only half of LFAST ’s open-loop error drop on human training episodes ( 55 .63 mm vs. 54 .72 mm for SHAPE and 56 .72 mm for w/o FAST).

# F Simulation benchmark 

Tasks. The GR-1 tabletop tasks of RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024; NVIDIA et al., 2025) control a GR-1 humanoid with two six-DoF hands from its head camera. Six articulated tasks place an object into a cabinet, drawer, or microwave and close it (Close in Table 4); the other 18 are pick-and-place tasks that move an object from one receptacle to another (PnP). We fine-tune all models on the GR00T-Teleop-Sim collection (NVIDIA et al., 2025), which has 20,802 training and 480 validation episodes across the 24 tasks, with identical train/validation splits and normalization statistics. 

Fine-tuning. All four matched models use the exact same fine-tuning recipe, starting from their pretrained EMA checkpoints. The joint stream predicts the 29 joints of the GR-1 (7 per arm, 6 per hand, 3 for the waist) through its pretrained output layer, with the other dimensions masked, in chunks of 64 steps, of which 12 are executed; the policy receives no proprioceptive state. Models pretrained with LFAST keep it on EEF chunks computed by forward kinematics (wrist pose and five fingertips per hand, in the head-camera frame), and Lvideo predicts head-camera frames up to 32 steps ahead for all four models. We train for 100k steps with batch 256 and a base learning rate of 5 × 10 −5 (cosine decay after a 5% warmup), using 0.25 × the base learning rate for the action expert with its first decoder layer frozen, and 10 × for the joint output layer. Both external baselines ( π0.5 and GR00T N1.7) are fine-tuned on the exact same data split and 29-DoF relative joint targets under the same 100k-step, batch-256 budget (Appendix G). 

Evaluation. Every model is evaluated with EMA weights on 50 episodes per task (seeds 0–49, at most 720 simulation steps, executing 12 steps per inference); Table 4 and Table S10 report the mean across the 80k, 

27 90k, and 100k checkpoints. An episode seed fixes the initial scene, so any two models are compared by a paired t-test over the 1,200 task–seed pairs, each averaged over the three checkpoints. The 95% intervals of the overall success rates (Table 4) are normal intervals of the mean over the same pairs. Across task groups (Table S10), with 20.8k demonstrations ( ∼870 per task), single-stage pick-and-place (PnP) is largely saturated once human pretraining is included (74.1% for SHAPE vs. 73.8% for w/o FAST, both well above 66.1% for w/o human), whereas the two-stage articulated Close tasks still benefit substantially from LFAST 

(+9.5 points, 66.6% vs. 57.1%). 

# G Baselines 

Both baselines start from their public checkpoints and are fine-tuned in full; Table S11 lists their setups. On the six real-robot tasks, they train on the demonstrations of Table S3 for 30 epochs, as SHAPE does, using the same three cameras and predicting the same 65 relative joint actions as our joint stream (with the two immobile torso/neck joints masked), and are evaluated with their final checkpoints; each baseline retains its native pretrained chunk length and official fine-tuning learning-rate schedule (Table S11) for stable convergence. To keep the pretrained 32-d action projections of π0.5, each control step is split into a left-limb and a right-limb token as in Ego-Pi (Kim et al., 2026), so a chunk of 25 steps becomes 50 tokens. On the simulated GR-1 benchmark (Appendix F), both baselines train for 100k steps at batch 256 on the single head camera and the 29 active GR-1 relative joint targets (with unused slots masked) alongside their native state inputs (discrete state prompt for π0.5, state encoder with 0.2 dropout for GR00T N1.7), and are evaluated under the exact same EMA protocol as our models (executing 12 steps per inference).
