Abstract
Streaming 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) allows for online
reconstruction of dynamic scenes from multi-view videos for
Free Viewpoint Video (FVV). Current time and storage ef-
ficient methods rely on selecting a subset of anchor Gaus-
sians and neighborhood interpolation for fast streamable
3DGS. However, these methods can overlook object-specific
motion and introduce inconsistencies near object bound-
aries. We propose OCLDA-GS, an object-centric streaming
framework that dynamically reallocates object and motion-
aware Gaussian anchors to allocate anchor Gaussian sup-
port for objects undergoing large motion while at the same
time, restricting the gaussian neighborhood within object
groups for more precise object boundaries. Furthermore,
it estimates object-wise rigid transformations (e.g. a hand
moving) and leverages these estimates to learn per-anchor
local deformation correction for small deformations (e.g.
wrinkles on a shirt). Then, we conduct experiments on three
dynamic multi-view video datasets and demonstrate im-
provements over the previous baselines alongside improved
quality between refinement timesteps. Under matched re-
finement budgets, our method improves PSNR over Instant
Gaussian Stream (IGS) by 0.169 dB on N3DV, 0.202 dB on
Meeting Room and +0.199 on Google Immersive, while re-
ducing the mean PSNR drop between refinement timesteps
by 9.2% and 13.8% in N3DV and Meeting Room, respec-
tively.
Code is available via the supplementary zip.
1. Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [13] allows for photorealis-
tic reconstruction from RGB images and camera poses us-
ing 3D Gaussians as primitives.
4D Gaussian Splatting
(4DGS) or Dynamic Gaussian Splatting adds spatiotempo-
ral deformation in 3DGS. This enables realtime Free View-
point Video (FVV) for novel view synthesis [20, 33, 37].
FVV has applications in VR, immersive communication
and sports viewing [1, 35].

Splatting methods can be divided into two broad categories:
offline and online [8, 27, 33]. Offline methods optimize
spatiotemporal deformation by learning the motion features
throughout the entire video and emphasizes quality and re-
finement taking significant time and compute in the pro-
cess [36, 37]. In the online streaming setting, the scene rep-
resentation is updated as each set of new multi-view frames
arrive and the central concern is to make that update com-
putationally cheap while maintaining image fidelity [8, 27].
Recent time and storage-efficient Gaussian streaming
methods rely on selection of subset of Gaussians called an-
chors, whose motion features are then interpolated to up-
date the scene representation. Methods like IGS [35] and
ReconGS [6] work under fixed computational budget con-
trolled by the number of anchors and refinement per step
or keyframes making them extremely effective for real-
time dynamic scene streaming under limited computation
bandwidth.
IGS performs farthest point sampling (FPS)
and periodic refinement on pre-specified timesteps called
keyframes, while ReConGS [6] organizes anchors into an
adaptive hierarchy for compact motion optimization and
performs per-step refinements.
These methods assume neighboring Gaussians share the
3D deformation trajectories of each other. [11, 26]. How-
ever, this assumption fails for Gaussians near object bound-
aries. For example, in the N3DV dataset ( Fig. 1a), when the
chef’s hand moves near a stationary table, deformation via
interpolated anchor neighborhood can induce incompatible
motion in Gaussians belonging to the stationary table.
Existing anchor selection methods like FPS or hierarchi-
cal sampling also may not reflect the motion distribution of
the scene accurately. Under a limited anchor budget, these
methods can allocate excessive support to stationary re-
gions. Some related object and motion-aware control-point
methods address parts of this challenge [4, 9], but maintain-
ing accurate, object-consistent motion with limited refine-
ment remains challenging. Furthermore, motion within an
object often comprises a shared rigid component and local
non-rigid deformations (e.g., a moving body with wrinkling
clothes). Accounting for both components is important for
accurate motion prediction under limited refinement bud-
gets.
We introduce Object-Centric Local Deformation-Aware
Gaussian Streaming (OCLDA-GS). OCLDA-GS jointly ad-
dresses anchor selection, object-aware motion propagation,
and local deformation correction. First, OCLDA-GS uses
optical flow [28] observed over each completed keyframe
interval to redistribute a fixed anchor budget across object
groups, increasing support for groups with larger observed
motion. Second, it restricts motion propagation to anchors
within the same group, preventing cross-group interpolation
for supported Gaussians. Finally, it fits a rigid transforma-
tion to the predicted anchor correspondences of each group
and predicts an additional local deformation correction.
More broadly, OCLDA-GS highlights object-aware mo-
tion modeling as a promising direction for improving the
quality–efficiency trade-off in Gaussian streaming.
Ex-
tensive experiments on three
datasets demonstrate com-
petitive reconstruction quality and streaming efficiency in
both in-domain and cross-domain settings. Temporal anal-
yses against online streaming method like Instant Gaussian
Stream (IGS) further show reduced quality degradation be-
tween keyframes. In summary, our contributions are:
• Flow-guided anchor selection: We dynamically redis-
tribute a fixed anchor budget across object groups based
on observed optical flow, providing greater motion sup-
port for objects undergoing larger motion.
• Object-constrained motion propagation: We restrict
anchor-based motion interpolation to within object
groups, preventing incompatible motion from propagat-
ing across object boundaries.
• Rigid-conditioned deformation correction: We lever-
age object-wise rigid motion estimates to learn per-anchor
local deformation corrections, accounting for local non-
rigid deformations within objects without additional re-
finement.
2. Related Work
Dynamic novel view synthesis. NeRF established neural
radiance fields as a representation for novel view synthe-
sis [21]. Dynamic extensions model scene changes through
deformation fields, scene flow, and higher-dimensional em-
beddings [18, 22–24]. HexPlane and K-Planes improve ef-
ficiency through factorized spatiotemporal features [2, 5].
With an explicit Gaussian representation and differentiable
rasterization, 3DGS enables high-quality real-time render-
ing [13]. Dynamic Gaussian methods extend this represen-
tation using learned deformation fields and spatiotemporal
Gaussian formulations [19, 33, 36, 37]. These advances
improve dynamic scene representation and rendering, while
online reconstruction additionally requires efficient updates
as new observations arrive.
Efficient Streaming. StreamRF and ReRF investigate in-
cremental learning and residual representations for dynamic
radiance fields [16, 29], while NeRFPlayer and VideoRF
explore decomposed fields and feature streams for com-
pact video representation and playback
[25, 30].
For
Gaussian-based online reconstruction, 3DGStream trains
scene updates on the fly [27], and QUEEN uses sparse,
quantized Gaussian attribute residuals [8]. Complementary
compression approaches, including 4DGC and GIFStream,
target compact representations and efficient video delivery
[10, 15]. HiCoM and ReCon-GS use hierarchical motion
representations to reduce update and storage costs [6, 7].
IGS instead employs a generalizable anchor-driven motion
predictor with periodic Gaussian refinement at keyframes
2


[35]. OCLDA-GS builds on this inference-based setting to
improve motion support and Gaussian deformation.
Structured motion and object-aware deformation. Em-
bedded deformation represents motion through blended lo-
cal transformations [26], and SC-GS uses sparse control
points with learned interpolation and local rigidity regu-
larization [11]. Dynamic 3D Gaussians encourage coher-
ent tracking through local motion constraints [20].
Mo-
tionGS incorporates optical-flow-based motion guidance,
while MoDec-GS uses global-to-local motion decomposi-
tion [14, 38]. Closer to object-aware streaming, H3D-DGS
combines object-wise motion processing with flow-assisted
control points [9], and ComGS uses motion-sensitive key-
points and a motion-aware influence field [4]. Building on
these ideas, OCLDA-GS combines motion-aware anchor al-
location, explicit group-restricted propagation, and learned
rigid-motion-conditioned correction within keyframe-based
streaming.

4. Experiments
4.1. Configuration and Implementation Details
Datasets.
We evaluate on three datasets:
N3DV [17],
Meeting Room [16], and Google Immersive [1]. We train
the residual heads on four N3DV scenes (Coffee Martini,
Cook Spinach, Flame Steak, and Flame Salmon) and two
Google Immersive scenes (Dog and Truck). Cut Roasted
Beef and Sear Steak from N3DV, and Welder and Alexa
Meade Face Paint 1 from Google Immersive, are held out
for testing. Cross-dataset evaluation uses Discussion and
Trimming from Meeting Room.
Baselines.
We compare against four online methods:
IGS [35], ReConGS [6], 3DGStream [27], and QUEEN [8].
We reproduce these methods using their released implemen-
tations and evaluate them on common held-out scenes, cam-
eras, and frames. For context, we also include four offline
methods: Real-Time 4DGS [37], 4D-GS [33], STG [19],
and Swift4D [34]. Offline rows are shaded gray and serve
as contextual references.
Implementation Details.
Our implementation uses Py-
Torch and runs on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80 GB
memory. Ψϕ is the frozen AGM-Net from IGS [35]. It
receives four input views, and we use M = 8192 motion
anchors with eight same-group neighbors per Gaussian, as
defined in Eq. (9). The residual network Dθ contains two
hidden layers of width 256 with SiLU activations. We use
SAGA [3] to segment the Gaussian splat at timestep 0. De-
tails on SAGA grouping are provided in the supplementary.
We optimize Eq. (13) using AdamW with a learning rate
of 4 × 10−4 and weight decay of 0.05, setting α = 0.8,
λmag = 0.01, and λsmooth = 0.001. During streaming,
Gaussian refinement is performed every five frames, using
100 updates for N3DV, 200 for Meeting Room, and 300 for
Google Immersive. IGS and Ours share the same initializa-
tion and refinement budget for each scene.
Evaluation Metrics.
We report PSNR (dB) [31] and
structural dissimilarity, DSSIM = (1 −SSIM)/2 [32], to
measure reconstruction quality. Seconds per frame (s/frm)
denotes the average online reconstruction time per target
frame, including periodic keyframe refinement and exclud-
ing initial Gaussian reconstruction. Higher PSNR and lower
DSSIM and s/frm indicate better performance.

4.2. Results
In-domain Evaluation.
We evaluate on held-out scenes
from N3DV and Google Immersive for the in-domain eval-
uation. As shown in Tab. 1, our method achieves 33.046 dB
on N3DV and 23.798 dB on Google Immersive, improv-
ing over IGS by 0.169 dB and 0.199 dB, respectively. Our
method obtains the second-highest PSNR among the online
methods on both datasets, outperforming IGS, ReConGS,
and 3DGStream, while QUEEN achieves the highest PSNR.
Cross-domain Evaluation.
We evaluate the N3DV-
trained checkpoint on Meeting Room, without further
neural-weight training. Results in Tab. 1 demonstrate com-
petitive transfer on Meeting Room.
On Meeting Room,
our method achieves 30.290 dB, improving over IGS by
0.202 dB and obtaining lower DSSIM. This shows im-
proved cross-domain transfer of OCLDA-GS.
Reconstruction Efficiency.
As discussed in Sec. 3, we
do Gaussian optimization only at keyframes and use neu-
ral prediction for intervening frames. On N3DV and Meet-
ing Room, reconstruction requires 2.30 and 2.18 s/frm, re-
spectively, compared with 2.00 and 1.96 s/frm for IGS. The
added processing cost is approximately 0.30 and 0.22 s/frm,
accompanying the quality improvements on these datasets.
Across the completed comparisons in Tab. 1, our method
has the second-lowest reported reconstruction time on ev-
ery dataset.

5. Limitations
OCLDA-GS depends on the quality of object groups. In-
accurate grouping can limit object-aware motion propaga-
tion. More robust object grouping could alleviate this limi-
tation. Similarly, the rigid-motion mechanism assumes that
each object group results in a useful dominant rigid trans-
formation, which may be inaccurate for articulated objects.
Finally, our learned local deformation correction updates
Gaussian positions only but does not correct errors in ori-
entation, scale, and appearance.
6. Conclusions
We presented OCLDA-GS, an object-centric Gaussian
streaming framework that combines flow-guided anchor se-
lection, object-constrained motion propagation, and learned
local deformation correction conditioned on object-wise
rigid motion. Under a fixed anchor budget, these compo-
nents improve motion modeling without per-frame Gaus-
sian refinement. Experiments across three dynamic multi-
view datasets demonstrate competitive reconstruction qual-
ity and efficiency, with improvements over IGS on N3DV
and Meeting Room and reduced quality degradation be-
tween keyframes. These results highlight the importance
of object-aware motion modeling for efficient Gaussian
streaming.
