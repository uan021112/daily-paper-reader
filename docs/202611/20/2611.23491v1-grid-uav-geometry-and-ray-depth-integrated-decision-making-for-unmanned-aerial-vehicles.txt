## Abstract 

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) encode images with 2D encoders that capture little 3D structure. A common approach is to feed the policy estimated depth, but a UAV chooses its own altitude, must judge distances tens of me-ters away, where monocular depth is less reliable, and must relate its forward and downward views. On long aerial routes, adding depth features directly brings no significant gain (42.5% vs. 42.6% success) , so we study how 3D in-formation should be given to the policy. Analyzing flight failures, we find that two forms of 3D grounding fail in op-posite ways: explicit 3D lifting of depth collides less on the way but more often fails to finish at the target, while implicit alignment with a 3D geometry model finishes more often but collides more. We propose GRID , which combines both: (1) 

it lifts monocular depth into ray-corrected 3D positions in the UAV’s frame for both cameras, and (2) during train-ing only, it aligns its visual features with a frozen multi-view geometry model, at no inference cost. GRID adding explicit lifting to implicit alignment significantly increases how often the policy reaches the target ( 61.5 % → 69.5 %,

p < 0.001 ), and adding implicit alignment to explicit lift-ing significantly increases how often it completes the task once there ( 57.7 % → 61.4 %, p < 0.05 ). On TravelUAV (3,005 test episodes), GRID achieves the best success rate on every split, with relative gains over the state of the art of 9.5% (seen), 17.1% (unseen objects) and 25.4% (unseen map). github.com/anas2908/GRID-UAV. 

## 1. Introduction 

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are increasingly used for delivery and search-and-rescue operations, where an op-erator would state a goal in natural language, e.g . “fly to your left and find the white truck parked on a residential street,” and let the vehicle do the rest. In aerial vision-language navigation [36], the UAV must follow such an in-struction through a large outdoor environment, flying hun-dreds of meters without collision and stopping at targets it has never seen before. Because UAVs are battery-powered and payload-limited, heavy sensors such as LiDAR shorten flight time [1]. Thus, at test time we use only forward- and downward-facing RGB cameras and must infer 3D struc-arXiv:2611.23491v1 [cs.CV] 17 Nov 2026 ture from them [19, 26]. VLA models [3, 4, 13, 32], now used across many robot embodiments and recently on UAVs [30, 38], are a natural fit, but their 2D image encoders capture what is in an image rather than where it lies in 3D space [7]. This is a problem for a UAV, whose viewpoint changes rapidly in 3D, because a small tree nearby and a large tree far away can occupy the same part of the image. A common solution is to give the policy depth infor-mation. 3D-aware VLAs [25, 44] do this successfully in robot manipulation, where the relevant objects lie close to the camera. A UAV differs in three ways. First, it chooses its own altitude, so it must reason about what lies below as well as ahead. Second, the obstacles and targets that shape its route are often tens of meters away, where monoc-ular depth is less reliable [40]. Third, near the target, the same object often appears in both cameras at once, e.g., its side in the forward view and its top in the downward one, so it must be matched across two very different views. Depth input helps UAV policies in compact, short-range set-tings [20, 30], but on long outdoor flights ([36]) we find that feeding depth features directly brings no gain ( 42 .5% 

w/ depth vs. 42 .6% w/o depth success), whereas lifting the depth [39] into 3D [24, 37] raises success to 46 .6% . Hence, we study how 3D information should be given to UAV. We compare two forms of 3D grounding. In explicit 

grounding, we estimate metric depth with a monocular model adapted to aerial viewpoints, use each pixel’s view-ing ray to place every image patch at its 3D position in the UAV’s own body frame, for both the forward and downward cameras, and add these positions to the visual tokens. In im-plicit grounding, we align the policy’s visual features during training with those of a frozen 3D geometry model [35], so that it learns to represent geometry on its own. To com-pare the two, we look at how flights fail. A flight can fail in one of two ways: (T1) the UAV never reaches the target (comes within 15 m horizontally of it), usually be-cause it crashes on the way; or (T2) it reaches the target but fails to finish, for example by hovering high above it. The two forms fail at different stages (Figs. 4 and 5). Explicit grounding suffers less from T1: the UAV crashes less and reaches the target more often ( 69 .4% vs. 61 .5% ). But it suf-fers more from T2: once there, it finishes less often ( 57 .7% 

vs. 62 .3% ) and hovers above the target nearly twice as of-ten. Implicit grounding shows the reverse. We attribute this to what each form provides. Avoiding T1 requires knowing how far away things are, which explicit grounding provides directly. Avoiding T2 requires recognizing that the object in the forward view and the one in the downward view are the same, that is, relating the two current views, which we hypothesize implicit grounding learns. Since the two are complementary, we propose GRID 

(Geometry and Ray-depth Integrated Decision-making), a UAV VLA that combines both and needs only RGB cam-eras at test time. Unlike prior UAV VLAs, which either use no depth (AeroVLA [38]) or feed it through a Siamese projector without 3D lifting (AutoFly [30]), GRID grounds the policy in 3D both explicitly and implicitly. The explicit branch adds only 5.2M trainable parameters (2.2% of train-able and 0.07% of total parameters) on top of frozen depth estimator, the implicit branch adds no inference overhead. By combining the two (Sec. 4), GRID has fewer en-route failures (T1) than the implicit-only policy (reach 61 .5% →

69 .5% , p < 0.001 ) and fewer at-goal failures (T2) than the explicit-only policy (completion 57 .7% → 61 .4% , p < 

0.05 ). On TravelUAV benchmark, it raises overall success from 41 .7% to 48 .2% , is the best on every split, and gains most on the unseen map ( 25 .4% relative), which tests gen-eralization to new environments. Our contributions are as follows: • A failure analysis of UAV VLAs that separates failing to reach the target (T1) from failing to complete the task once there (T2). We also show that directly adding depth features brings no gain in long-horizon aerial navigation, and that explicit and implicit 3D grounding address dif-ferent failures. • We propose GRID, a UAV VLA policy that needs only RGB cameras at test time and combines explicit 3D lift-ing across forward and downward cameras with implicit alignment to a frozen geometry model, where the align-ment adds no cost at inference. • GRID achieves state-of-the-art success on all TravelUAV benchmark test splits, with the largest improvement on the unseen map. 

## 2. Related Work 

Aerial VLN benchmarks. Aerial VLN has been studied through dialog-guided (AVDN [8]), route-following (Aeri-alVLN [18]) and map-assisted (CityNav [14]) benchmarks, and at scale by OpenFly [9] and AutoFly [30]. OpenFly and AutoFly use short routes ( ∼99 m and ∼107 m, the latter in 

70 m × 70 m scenes); AerialVLN and CityNav have longer routes but discrete actions without flight kinematics. Trav-elUAV [36], which we use, combines long routes ( ∼256 mon average, up to 1.1 km) with continuous flight and only a coarse direction instruction with a target description, so the UAV must itself judge distances over hundreds of meters. 

VLAs for UAVs. VLAs [3, 4, 13] have been adapted to UAV racing [27], cognitive tasks [21] and naviga-tion [30, 38]. On TravelUAV, the benchmark’s own base-line pairs a navigation LLM that predicts hierarchical tra-jectories [36]; NavFoM [43] trains a single navigation foun-dation model across robot embodiments, including UAVs; and AeroVLA [38], maps forward-downward views and a coarse directional prompt directly to flight commands. All of them rely on 2D image encoders without 3D grounding. 3D grounding in VLAs. Several works add 3D informa-tion to manipulation VLAs. One line provides 3D informa-tion as input: 3D-VLA [44] builds on 3D scene features, SpatialVLA [25] adds egocentric 3D position encodings to visual tokens, and PointVLA [15] and GeoVLA [29] in-ject point-cloud features. Another line transfers geometric knowledge from a 3D foundation model [35], either by fus-ing its features into the policy (Evo-0 [17]) or by aligning the policy’s internal representations [41] with them during training (Spatial Forcing [16]). These works target table-top scenes close to the camera and follow one line or the other. GRID adapts both to long-range, two-camera aerial navigation and combines them. 

Depth for long-horizon UAV navigation. AutoFly [30] is the closest prior work: it feeds DAv2 [39] to a UAV VLA through a shared (Siamese) projector and reports a 3.9-point gain in success, in a setting with short routes in 70 m × 70 mscenes and a single forward camera, where nearby obsta-cles keep monocular depth reliable. The TravelUAV bench-mark is more demanding: obstacles and targets lie tens to hundreds of meters away, long routes let depth errors accu-mulate, and the target must be matched across two cam-eras. Feeding depth through AutoFly’s shared Siamese projector (Sec. 4.3) leaves success essentially unchanged (42 .6% → 42 .5% ), whereas lifting the same depth into 3D improves it; GRID builds on this and adds implicit geomet-ric alignment. 

## 3. Methodology 

GRID keeps a standard VLA policy and changes only what its visual tokens carry (Fig. 2). Explicit 3D lifting (Sec. 3.2) adds to each token the metric position of the surface it sees, and implicit geometric alignment (Sec. 3.3) aligns the same tokens inside the LLM with a frozen VGGT during training only. Both need only the RGB cameras at test time and leave the action interface unchanged. 

3.1. Task and Base Policy 

Task. At each step, the UAV observes a front image If

and a downward image Id. It is given a description of the target and, as in [38], a coarse bearing toward it (one of seven sectors, e.g. “forward-left”), recomputed at every step from the target’s true position, which makes it a coarse form of oracle guidance. The policy outputs forward, vertical and yaw commands, or LAND to end the episode; an episode succeeds if the UAV stops within 20 m of the target [36]. 

Base policy. We follow the formulation in [38] on an OpenVLA backbone [13]. The frozen vision encoder E

(DINOv2 [23] and SigLIP [42]) encodes each view c ∈{f, d} separately, with shared weights, into N =16 ×16 

patch tokens [5] Fc ∈ RN ×C , so every token covers part of exactly one camera. The concatenated tokens [Ff ; Fd] pass through a trainable projector into Llama-2 7B [33], fine-tuned with LoRA [12]. Each command is quantized into 99 uniform bins and written as a two-digit token, and training minimizes the cross-entropy Lact of these action tokens. 

3.2. Explicit 3D Lifting 

We add to every token the metric position of the surface it sees, in the UAV’s own frame (e.g., the utility pole in Fig. 2). The idea is inspired by egocentric position encod-ings for tabletop manipulation [25], but three properties of flight shape the steps below: wide-angle cameras, several views that must share one frame, and a wide range of depths within one view. We describe one token i of view c (in Fig. 2, the highlighted token on the pole) and omit c where it is clear. 

Aerial metric depth. Metric Depth Anything V2-Large [39] is trained on street-level driving scenes, far from aerial views. We fine-tune its encoder and head with the SiLog loss [6] on simulator depth [28] of front and down-ward images from the training split only, one view at a time as at test time, and freeze it as Φ. Since Φ learns from the simulator’s depth, which is the range along each pixel’s viewing ray, every value of Dc=Φ( Ic) is a metric range. At test time, depth comes from RGB alone, with no depth sensor or ground truth; Sec. 4 compares Φ with the off-the-shelf model. 

Four samples per token. As in [25], each token keeps four separate depth samples instead of a single depth. The map Dc is resized to 64 ×64 , so each token covers a 4×4

tile, which we split into 2×2 sub-tiles Ti,s . Each sub-tile gives one sample, di,s = mean p∈Ti,s Dc(p) for s =1, . . . , 4, located at its center pi,s = ( ui,s , v i,s ) in original image coordinates. We never average the four samples into one depth: each is lifted to its own 3D point. This is im-portant in flight, where depth varies widely within a token: the pole token’s samples span 11.2 m on the pole to 51.3 m on the background, and a single depth for the token (their mean, 34 m) would place it in empty space (red circle in Fig. 2).

3.3. Implicit Geometric Alignment 

Each token is lifted independently, so the policy must still learn from Lact alone which tokens show the same ob-ject. This is hardest near the goal, where the policy de-cides whether to land and the target is often split between the cameras, e.g . a car’s side at the bottom of the front image and its roof in the downward image. VGGT [35] computes each view’s features in the context of the other through global attention, so its targets carry both per-view geometry and cross-view context; we use it as a frozen teacher [11] during training. 

Standard VGGT teacher. VGGT is used as released (Fig. 2, bottom): both views enter it together at 518 px, DI-NOv2 splits each into 37 ×37 patch tokens, and 24 rounds of frame attention, within a view, and global attention, across views, compute each view’s features in the context of the other (in Fig. 2, global attention links the pole token to the downward image). Unlike Φ, VGGT never feeds the policy, so we do not fine-tune it. Targets and loss. Each token gets one VGGT target, which the policy learns to match:

In the causal LLM [34], the downward tokens follow the front tokens and attend to them, which lets them absorb the cross-view context of their targets. The gradient also reaches the earlier layers, the projector and the explicit branch, so both components are trained jointly. 

Inference. VGGT and hψ (12.6M parameters) are dis-carded after training, so the deployed policy is the model of Sec. 3.2 with no added parameters or computation. 

## 4. Experiments 

We evaluate GRID on TravelUAV: we compare it with the state of the art (Sec. 4.2), study how depth should be in-tegrated into the policy (Sec. 4.3), compare explicit and implicit grounding and analyze their failures (Secs. 4.4 and 4.5), and report the computational cost of each com-ponent (Sec. 4.6). 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmark. We use TravelUAV’s UAV-Need-Help task [36] with AeroVLA’s curated training set [38] (7,611 trajectories, 410k frames, 11 environments). The 3,005 test episodes form Seen (1,418), Unseen Object (629) and Unseen Map (958) splits, each divided into Easy (≤250 m) and Hard ( >250 m) by reference path length; routes average 256 m and reach 1.1 km. 

Metrics. We report the benchmark’s metrics [36]: suc-cess rate (SR; the episode ends within 20 m of the target), oracle SR (OSR; within 20 m at any time), navigation er-ror (NE; final distance in meters) and success weighted by path length, SPL = 1

Baselines. We compare with CMA [2], the TravelUAV model [36] and NavFoM [43], which use the benchmark’s dense oracle guidance, a more detailed form than the coarse bearing given to GRID (numbers from [38]). All of these baselines were evaluated on headless GPUs, i.e ., without an attached display, as is standard in data centers. The AeroVLA report lower success rates in this setting than with a physical display, which authors recommend, because off-screen rendering drops frames and destabilizes the physics. Display GPUs are rarely available on research clusters, so for a fair comparison with the other baselines we evaluate every method headless, using the checkpoint released by the AeroVLA authors (Supplementary, Section C) . AutoFly-depth follows AutoFly [30]: aerial-adapted DAv2 depth en-ters through the projector shared with the RGB features, without 3D lifting. 

Implementation and statistical testing. All our policies start from OpenVLA-7B [13] and are trained with identi-cal settings on four A100 (40 GB) GPUs; Tab. 4 lists all hyperparameters. All methods are evaluated on the same 3,005 episodes from identical start states, so we compare them with paired tests: an exact McNemar test [22] on per-episode success and 95% confidence intervals from a paired bootstrap over episodes (10,000 resamples). We re-peat every ablation evaluation three times and report the standard deviation across runs (Tab. 2), together with the largest p-value over runs. Completion is measured only on the episodes in which the UAV reaches the target, about 590–670 per model on the unseen map. Repeated evalua-tion runs of the same policy also differ (Table 2), and on fewer episodes this noise matters more. For quantities con-ditioned on reaching the target, we therefore pool five evalu-ation runs per model on the unseen map and resample whole episodes in the bootstrap, so that the extra runs reduce eval-uation noise without counting as additional episodes.

4.3. Depth Integration 

Table 2(a) and Fig. 3 test how depth should be given to the policy. All variants start from the same policy without depth (Base), which sees the front and down views separately. Base + AutoFly’s depth feeds depth through a Siamese pro-jector, as in AutoFly [30]. The explicit variants instead use depth to place each token in 3D. Supplementary, Section B.1 reports all metrics for every variant on the Full, Easy and Hard episodes of each split. 

Depth as input features. Base + AutoFly’s depth reaches 42.5% SR against 42.6% for Base ( p=0 .95 ), with no signif-icant difference on any split except the Hard routes of the unseen map. This holds even though its depth comes from our aerial-adapted Φ, which is far more accurate than off-the-shelf DAv2, so the lack of gain is not due to poor depth. AutoFly reports a 3.9-point gain from depth on routes of 107 m on average [30]; on TravelUAV’s routes, which are more than twice as long, appending depth to the visual in-put does not help the policy. 

Depth lifted into 3D. Lifting depth into egocentric 3D positions improves SR over Base by 5.7% with off-the-shelf DAv2 and by 9.3% with Φ (relative). Off-the-shelf DAv2 misjudges metric range badly from the air (Supplementary, Section A) , twenty times worse than Φ, yet still helps, which suggests that the encoding MLP (Eq. (2)) learns to correct a consistent scale error. The benefit of accurate depth is largest on the unseen map, where Φ improves SR over off-the-shelf DAv2 by 10.7% (relative).

4.5. Failure Analysis 

Decomposition. We split each episode at the first mo-ment the UAV comes within 15 m (horizontally) of the tar-get; the results also hold for 10 m and 20 m (Supplemen-tary, Section B.2) . Reach is the fraction of episodes that get there, completion the fraction of these that succeed, and 

SR = reach × completion. A failure is T1 if the target is never reached (collision, or lost: drifting away, stuck or out of steps) and T2 otherwise (hovering within 15 m horizon-tally but more than 20 m above the target, stopping in the wrong place after having been within the 20 m success ra-dius, or other). Labels are assigned automatically from the flight logs and confirmed by manually reviewing the replays of all 1,714 failed unseen-map episodes of the implicit-only, explicit-only and GRID models (about 60 hours). We ana-lyze the unseen map, where train-test environment overlap does not confound the effect of either form of grounding: in seen and unseen-object episodes, whose environments ap-pear in training, a policy can partly rely on familiar layouts instead of inferring 3D structure, whereas on unseen map it judges distances and positions from current images alone. 

Failures of explicit and implicit grounding. Explicit lifting reaches the target more often than implicit alignment (69.4% vs. 61.5%; +7 .9 points, 95% CI [4 .3, 11 .6] ; Fig. 4), mainly because it collides less on the way (28.0% vs. 35.0% of episodes, Fig. 5), but once there it completes the task less often (57.7% vs. 62.3%; −4.6, [−8.0, −1.2] ) and hovers high above the target nearly twice as often (9.8% vs. 5.1%). ## 5. Limitations and Future Work 

All experiments run in simulation; adapting GRID to real sensor noise, wind and lighting changes is the next step. Like prior UAV VLAs, we evaluate only on TravelUAV, since short-route benchmarks ( e.g ., about 100 m in Aut-oFly [30]) would not test the long-route failures we study; further long-horizon benchmarks are future work. Each pol-icy is trained once, so repeated evaluations and paired tests capture evaluation noise but not training variance, which would require retraining a 7B policy (up to 242 GPU-hours each; Tab. 3). Stopping in the wrong place remains an error source (Sec. 4.5). Like every VLA-based baseline, GRID inherits a large latency (674 ms per decision). 

## 6. Conclusion 

We studied how a UAV VLA should receive 3D informa-tion. On long aerial routes, appending depth features to the visual input brings no gain, whereas lifting depth into 3D does. Separating failures to reach the target (T1) from fail-ures to complete the task (T2) showed that explicit 3D lift-ing mainly reduces the first and implicit alignment the sec-ond. GRID combines both and, using only RGB cameras at test time, reaches the target as often as explicit lifting, com-pletes the task more often, and achieves the best success rate on every TravelUAV split, with the largest gain on the un-seen map. ## A. Aerial Depth Model 

Explicit lifting (Sec. 3.2) needs metric depth for both cam-eras. We start from the metric Depth Anything V2-Large model (DAv2-L) [39], which was fine-tuned for metric depth on driving scenes (Virtual KITTI). Seen from the air, and especially in the downward view, its metric scale is far off (Tab. 5), so we fine-tune it on aerial images and freeze the result as Φ for all our policies. 

Data. We use only the training set of Sec. 4.1. Its 7,611 trajectories are split into fine-tuning (6,090), validation (761) and test (760) trajectories, and no episode from the benchmark’s test splits is used. The fine-tuning trajectories provide about 327k front/down image pairs. The ground truth is the simulator’s depth of each image which, like the output of Φ, is the range along each pixel’s viewing ray. 

Training. We fine-tune the full model, encoder and DPT head, with the scale-invariant logarithmic (SiLog) loss [6] as used by DAv2. Front and downward images are pro-cessed one view at a time, as at test time: each 256 ×256 

image is resized to 518 ×518 with the same preprocessing as at inference, and the prediction is resized back to 256 ×256 

for the loss. We train for 3 epochs (61,329 steps) on four GPUs with AdamW, a batch of 16 front/down pairs, learn-ing rates of 5×10 −5 for the head and 5×10 −6 for the en-coder, weight decay 0.01, a 5% warm-up followed by co-sine decay (restarted after the first epoch), gradient clipping at 1.0, bfloat16 precision and random horizontal flips. This one-time cost amounts to 48 GPU-hours (Sec. 4.6). 

Evaluation. Following the official DAv2 evaluation, we compute the metrics for each image and average them over images. For predicted depth ˆdp and true depth dp at pixel p,AbsRel is the mean of | ˆdp − dp|/d p, δ1 is the share of pixels with max( ˆdp/d p, d p/ ˆdp) < 1.25 , and RMSE is the square root of the mean of ( ˆdp − dp)2.

Results. Table 5 compares Φ with the off-the-shelf model on the 761 validation trajectories. Off-the-shelf DAv2-L misjudges metric depth badly from the air: its AbsRel is 0.713 on the front camera and 0.873 on the downward cam-era, and only 18.6% and 14.6% of pixels lie within a fac-tor of 1.25 of the true depth. Fine-tuning lowers AbsRel to 0.044 and 0.035 (16 and 25 times lower), raises δ1 to 0.977 and 0.988, and reduces RMSE from 20.39 to 2.47 m on the front camera and from 13.19 to 0.85 m on the downward camera. For the policy, accurate depth helps most on the un-seen map, where explicit lifting with Φ raises SR over off-the-shelf depth from 36.22% to 40.08%, and from 37.47% to 42.69% when combined with implicit alignment (Tab. 6), whereas on the Seen split both differences are below 0.7 points.

B.2. Sensitivity to the Reach Radius 

We hypothesize that GRID reaches the target more often than the implicit model, thanks to explicit lifting, and com-pletes the task more often than the explicit model, thanks to implicit alignment. The reach radius of 15 m is a de-sign choice, so we test both parts of the hypothesis again with 10 m and 20 m (Tab. 7). As in Sec. 4.5, an episode counts as reached once the UAV comes within the radius (horizontally) of the target. At every radius, GRID reaches the target more often than the implicit model ( p< 0.001 , ex-act McNemar test) and completes the task more often than the explicit model ( p< 0.05 , paired bootstrap pooled over five runs). Across radii, the differences vary by less than one point (reach: +7.5 to +8.4; completion: +3.5 to +3.7). C. Headless Evaluation of AeroVLA 

TravelUAV simulates each episode in Unreal Engine with AirSim, which renders the camera images and steps the physics. Data-center GPUs such as the A100 have no display output, so the simulator renders off-screen ( head-less ). We evaluate every method this way (Sec. 4.1); for AeroVLA, we use the checkpoint released by its authors, without retraining or modification. 

Reported and reproduced results. With a physical dis-play, AeroVLA reports an SR of 47.96%, 56.60% and 37.58% on Seen, Unseen Object and Unseen Map (46.5% over all 3,005 episodes) [38]. Evaluated headless, the re-leased checkpoint reaches 43.86%, 48.49% and 34.03% (41.7%; Tab. 1). Running the graphics engine on compute-only GPUs, which drop frames; The collisions and sudden position jumps observed on headless GPUs are glitches of the AirSim physics engine. 

Choice of evaluation setting. Display GPUs are rarely available on research clusters, and the other baselines were also evaluated headless by their authors. More importantly, frame drops and physics glitches arise in the simulator, not in the policy. The simulator keeps running while a policy computes its next action. In the unseen-map flight logs, this has no visible effect: on steps without a forward command, the UAV moves horizontally by a median of 5 cm (95th per-centile at most 0.6 m) under every policy, and steps in which the UAV moves more than 5 m beyond its command oc-cur equally often for implicit alignment and GRID (3.6 per 1,000 steps), despite their different decision times. Only a common evaluation setup isolates the effect of the method, which is also what our paired tests require: every method flies the same episodes from the same start states under the same rendering. Comparing our headless results with dis-play results would confound the method. 

Comparison with the reported results. Even against AeroVLA’s display results, which favor AeroVLA, GRID evaluated headless has the higher SR on every split: 48.03% vs. 47.96% on Seen, 56.76% vs. 56.60% on Unseen Ob-ject and 42.69% vs. 37.58% on Unseen Map (48.2% vs. 46.5% overall). On the unseen map, which tests generaliza-tion, GRID is also better in NE (58.14 vs. 67.42 m), OSR (59.39% vs. 52.92%) and SPL (31.77 vs. 28.22).
