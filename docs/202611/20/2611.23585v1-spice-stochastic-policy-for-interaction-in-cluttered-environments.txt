SPICE: Stochastic Policy for Interaction in Cluttered Environments
Nidhi Parayil, Benjamin Greenland, Thierry Peynot, and Chris Lehnert
Abstract— Agricultural tasks such as harvesting and prun-
ing require robots to reach through dense foliage, where
contact is often unavoidable and vision is obstructed. In-
termittent contact creates an imbalance between free-space
and contact-rich observations, complicating the learning of
contact-responsive behaviours. We present SPICE, a tactile-
conditioned diffusion policy for reaching targets in dense
plant canopies using tactile sensing as the sole environmental
feedback. SPICE exhibits three behaviours: moving through
compliant foliage, manoeuvring around partially obstructing
stems, and manipulating branches that block the target path. To
the best of our knowledge, SPICE is the first tactile-conditioned
diffusion policy for target reaching through physically clut-
tered plant canopies. Across over 180 real-robot trials, SPICE
was evaluated against tactile-conditioned, image-conditioned,
and reactive-control baselines. Spatial tactile information and
explicit contact conditioning proved important, with SPICE
achieving 95% branch-manoeuvring success versus 45% for
the strongest tactile baseline. SPICE also achieved branch
manipulation beyond the reactive controller and remained
effective under visual occlusion, where the image-conditioned
DP failed. Zero-shot transfer across 40 trials on unseen tomato
and lime plants achieved 87.5% success rate. These results
demonstrate the potential of tactile-conditioned generative poli-
cies for interaction in visually occluded plant environments.
I. INTRODUCTION
Agricultural labour shortages are increasing demand for
robotic systems [1], yet many high-value crops, such as
sweet pepper, berries, and citrus, remain difficult to automate
because of dense, unstructured foliage [2]. Reaching a fruit
or pruning site within dense foliage often requires mov-
ing through leaves and branches with unknown geometry,
stiffness, and compliance. These structures create physical
obstruction by restricting access to the target and visual
occlusion by blocking the robot’s view. When multiple
nearby structures constrain the available path, the environ-
ment becomes cluttered, making contact difficult to avoid and
requiring the robot to adapt its motion using local physical
feedback.
Humans handle such situations naturally by moving through
compliant foliage, manoeuvring around partially obstruct-
ing stems, and displacing branches when there is insuf-
ficient space to move around them. In contrast, robotic
interaction often relies on predefined contact responses or
interaction models, limiting behavioural flexibility. Learning
from demonstrations (LfD) offers a way to acquire diverse
behaviours without explicitly modelling each interaction.

However, tactile-based LfD has largely focused on gripper-
level manipulation tasks such as grasping [3], [4], rather than
guiding whole-arm motion through clutter. Extending tactile
LfD to cluttered reaching is challenging because contact is
intermittent and the required response changes as the robot
progresses through the environment.
Existing methods for moving through plant canopies rely
mainly on collision avoidance, model-based interaction, or
predefined reactive contact responses [5], [6]. Learning
multiple contact-responsive behaviours directly from tactile
feedback remains underexplored. We therefore introduce
SPICE - Stochastic Policy for Interaction in Cluttered
Environments, a vision-free, tactile-conditioned diffusion
policy for reaching targets in occluded plant canopies. SPICE
generates action sequences from tactile feedback and replans
as contact evolves, enabling multiple contact-responsive be-
haviours within a single learned policy. Figure 1 illustrates
SPICE displacing an obstructing branch in a tomato plant.

II. RELATED WORKS
Reaching through clutter requires moving towards a target
while adapting to contact. Hybrid controllers combine po-
sition and force control using predefined objectives, while
Model Predictive Controllers (MPC) optimise motion us-
ing estimated robot–environment dynamics [7], [8]. Such
dynamics are difficult to model accurately in deformable
environments such as plant canopies. Reactive controllers
avoid explicit dynamics by responding directly to contact,
either through predefined behaviour switching [9] or by
combining target and contact objectives, as in RICE [10].
However, incorporating multiple interaction responses can
increase controller complexity and introduce conflicting ob-
jectives or local minima.
In agricultural robotics, predefined motions have been used
to displace obstacles [11], reveal targets [12], [13], and
regulate pruning contact [6], [14]. Model-based approaches
plan branch interactions using spring models [15], me-
chanical properties [16], or deformation parameters [17].
Reinforcement Learning (RL) has also enabled gentle ma-
nipulation [18] and avoidance or displacement of plant struc-
tures [5]. These approaches still require manually specified
motions, models, rewards, or interaction objectives. Learning
from demonstration offers an alternative without explicit
modelling. However, existing tactile agricultural methods
mainly detect branch contact [4] or regulate predefined
pushing motions [2], rather than learning multiple contact-
responsive behaviours for reaching through clutter.
Vision-language-action (VLA) models and Diffusion Poli-
cies (DPs) have shown strong performance in vision-based
manipulation [19], [20], [21], but have rarely been demon-
strated under severe visual occlusion. Tactile-conditioned
VLA and DP methods incorporate contact feedback [22],
[23], [3], [24], while TacDiffusion [25], Reactive Diffusion
Policy [26], and Tube Diffusion Policy [27] demonstrate
tactile-conditioned generation for insertion, assembly, and
local manipulation. However, these methods primarily ad-
dress gripper-level interaction rather than whole-arm reach-
ing through deformable clutter. Spatial tactile representa-
tions have also been explored using tactile skins such as
DexSkin [28], but much of this work relies on camera-based
tactile sensing. Tactile encoders for compact non-camera-
based sensors, such as magnetic taxel arrays, remain less
explored.
Taken together, existing methods either prescribe interac-
tion responses or apply tactile learning primarily to local
gripper manipulation. Learning multiple contact-responsive
behaviours for whole-arm motion through clutter from spatial
tactile feedback under visual occlusion remains underex-
plored. SPICE addresses this gap with a vision-free, tactile-
conditioned diffusion policy that learns multiple interaction
behaviours within a single policy.
III. METHODOLOGY
Moving through a cluttered plant canopy requires different
contact-responsive behaviours depending on how surround-
ing structures obstruct the path. We first formulate this
problem, then present SPICE, which combines spatial tactile
encoding with diffusion-based joint-space action generation,
followed by the demonstration collection and experimental
setup.
A. Problem Formulation
We consider a manipulator reaching a specified target
within a visually occluded and physically obstructed plant
canopy. The canopy geometry, arrangement, stiffness, and
contact dynamics are unknown. The robot observes its cur-
rent configuration qt, target configuration qtarget, and tactile
tensor Tt from sensors mounted on the End Effector (EE);
no visual observations or environmental model are provided.
Prior work on reactive tactile control has demonstrated

reliable target reaching and manoeuvring around partially ob-
structing stems [10]. However, these local contact responses
are insufficient when a branch fully blocks the target path
and leaves no space to manoeuvre around it; in such cases,
the robot must manipulate the environment to create access.
We therefore consider three contact-dependent behaviours
(Fig. 2):
• Target reaching: Moving towards the target through
compliant foliage;
• Branch manoeuvring: Moving to the target while
moving around a partially obstructing branch; and
• Branch manipulation: displacing a branch that fully
obstructs the path before continuing towards the target.


To represent different environmental structures, we use
branch configurations that create increasing levels of ob-
struction. With only compliant foliage and no obstructing
branch, the robot can move directly towards the target. A
vertical branch provides lateral free space, allowing the robot
to manoeuvre around it without deliberately displacing the
branch. In contrast, a horizontal branch blocks the available
lateral path, making manoeuvring infeasible under the robot’s
kinematic and sensing constraints. Because moving above
the branch increases the likelihood of contact with unsensed
robot surfaces, we use downward displacement as the ma-
nipulation behaviour. This creates access to the target while
keeping the interaction near the tactile end effector.
We learn these behaviours with a single closed-loop policy
from human teleoperation demonstrations, without providing
behaviour labels. Given recent robot and tactile observations,
the policy must move towards the target while adapting its
contact response to different branch orientations and levels
of obstruction.
B. Spatial Tactile Encoder and Contact Conditioning
Fig. 3.
SPICE is a tactile-conditioned diffusion policy in which a CNN

For reaching through clutter, the appropriate response
depends not only on contact magnitude but also on how
contact is distributed across the tactile surface. Preserving
the spatial arrangement of the taxels therefore allows the
policy to distinguish contact configurations that may require
different motions. SPICE represents tactile feedback using a
spatial embedding together with an explicit contact signal,
as illustrated in Fig. 3.
Each tactile sensor contains a 4 × 4 array of three-axis
taxels. Measurements from the two sensors are concatenated
into Tt ∈R3×4×8, with channels corresponding to the x-, y-,
and z-axis measurements. We use a CNN encoder with two
3 × 3 convolutional layers of 16 and 32 channels, followed
by a 2×4 layer with 64 channels. All layers use unit stride
and ReLU activation, with unit padding in the first two
layers. The resulting 64×3×5 feature map is flattened and
linearly projected to a 128-dimensional tactile embedding,

D. Data collection and Experimental setup
We used a controlled mock plant because real foliage is
cluttered and dynamic, making tactile demonstrations sensi-
tive to plant motion. As haptic feedback was not economi-
cally feasible, the operator relied on direct observation and a
tactile heat map; the mock setup was therefore occluded for
the robot but observable to the operator. The setup (Fig. 4)
used a UFactory xArm6 with two Xela uSkin uSPa44 tactile
arrays mounted on the gripper front to detect contact in
clutter. An artificial red fruit served as the target, while

We collected 240 demonstrations: 70 for each manoeu-

directions were balanced across both sides of the branch.
Because branch motion can alter the tactile gradient, failed,
noisy, or incorrect demonstrations were removed. Opposite-
sign lateral motion occurred in 54.2% and 64.2% of
timesteps for positive and negative tactile gradients, re-
spectively, while 69.8% of simultaneous two-sensor contact
timesteps showed negative end-effector pitch (Fig. 5). Only
30 obstacle-free demonstrations were included because about
70% of contact-rich trajectories already contained free-space
motion. Behaviour labels were not used during training.
Training used AdamW for 100 epochs with learning rate
10−4, batch size 32, cosine decay with 500 warm-up steps,
exponential moving averaging, and random seed 42.
IV. EVALUATION PROTOCOL AND METRICS
We evaluate SPICE using task success to determine
whether the robot completes the required behaviour, together
with interaction metrics that explain how the robot responds
physically during contact.
A. Experimental Protocol
We evaluated target reaching under three sequential levels
of interaction complexity: reaching the target without an ob-
structing branch, manoeuvring around a partially obstructing
vertical branch, and manipulating a horizontal branch that
blocks the target path and prevents lateral manoeuvring.
Success was defined according to the physical response
required in each task:
Fig. 5.
Distributions of end-effector positions and interaction behaviours
in the demonstration dataset.
• Target reaching: the final end-effector position is
within 3cm of the target, corresponding to the diameter
of the artificial fruit.
• Branch manoeuvring: the end effector moves laterally
around the branch before reaching the target.
• Branch manipulation: the end effector reorients above
the branch, displaces it downward by at least 5cm to
create sufficient access to the target, and then reach the
target.
A method advanced to the next task only if it achieved at
least 50% success within the first 10 trials of the current task.
Across experiments, initial and target joint configurations
were generated by independently sampling each joint from
the demonstration distributions. A pool of 180 initial–target
pairs was allocated across experiments, with branches placed
at eight predefined locations and contacts balanced between
the two tactile sensors.
Trials were terminated when continued execution risked
damaging the branch or tactile sensors, including sustained
loading without a contact-responsive change in motion, in-
creasing compression towards the contacted side, or repeated
motion without escaping contact for more than 5s. Termi-
nated trials were counted as unsuccessful. Because tactile
sensing covered only the end-effector contact faces, branches
moving beyond this region were manually released when
necessary after the evaluated response. Contact metrics were
computed before intervention.

V. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
We evaluated SPICE through five experiments examin-
ing tactile representation, encoder design, reactive-control
comparison, robustness under visual occlusion, and zero-shot
transfer to real plants. We first assess its overall performance
by aggregating 170 SPICE trials across these experiments,
comprising 40 target-reaching, 90 branch-manoeuvring, and
40 branch-manipulation trials. SPICE achieved success rates
of 100%, 98.9%, and 85.0%, respectively. The following
sections examine these results in detail.
A. Experiment I: Tactile Representation
Our first experiment evaluates how tactile representations
motivated by prior work affect SPICE performance in tar-
get reaching and branch manoeuvring. We compare SPICE
with mean three-axis tactile values (Txyz-DP), inspired by
TacDiffusion [25], and direct taxel input (TactDP), motivated
by Reactive Diffusion Policy [26]. Because Reactive Diffu-
sion Policy applies PCA to high-dimensional camera-based
tactile observations, we use the raw taxel measurements
directly. We also adapt the tactile-gradient formulation from
RICE [10] to form three-axis (RICE-DP) and y-axis (RICE-
y-DP) representations, with the latter aligned to the lateral
manoeuvring direction. SPICE, TactDP, Txyz-DP, and RICE-
DP were first evaluated on the no-branch task, where all
achieved 10/10 target-reaching successes. In branch manoeu-
vring, SPICE achieved 19/20 successes (95%), substantially
outperforming all alternatives. RICE-y-DP was next best
with 8/20 (40%), followed by TactDP without ct (7/20),
TactDP with ct (5/20), and RICE-DP (3/20). To assess the
contribution of ct, SPICE, Txyz-DP, and TactDP were tested
with and without contact conditioning. Both Txyz-DP variants
and SPICE without ct failed all 10 initial trials. Without ct,
Txyz-DP continued pushing after contact; with ct, it gener-
ated lateral motion but failed to clear the branch. TactDP
without ct occasionally succeeded through irregular motions,
whereas with ct it produced more consistent contact-directed
behaviour. SPICE also achieved the lowest mean target error

E. Experiment V: Zero-Shot Transfer to Real Plants
We evaluated SPICE for zero-shot transfer to previously

of 20 tomato trials; the remaining five tomato trials were
terminated by safety stops. Plant pots were repositioned

VI. DISCUSSION
The experiments validate SPICE as a single tactile-
conditioned policy for target reaching, branch manoeuvring,
and branch manipulation under visual occlusion, with 87.5%
zero-shot success on unseen real plants. It outperformed the
evaluated tactile and image-conditioned baselines, achiev-
ing 95% branch-manoeuvring success versus 40% for the
strongest tactile representation, while extending beyond
RICE to branch manipulation. The tactile ablations highlight
the importance of spatial structure. Aggregated forces can
remain high as contact shrinks, whereas gradients capture
changes in contact distribution. RICE-y-DP outperforming
RICE-DP suggests that behaviour-aligned cues are partic-
ularly useful, while the CNN’s stronger manipulation and
recovery performance indicates the value of richer local
taxel relationships. RICE achieved accurate reaching using
instantaneous tactile-gradient direction but did not regulate
interaction magnitude, leading to prolonged contact for mis-
aligned branches. SPICE instead adapted through contact re-
duction, retreat, and reorientation, supporting learned policies
when the appropriate response changes during interaction.
SPICE also produced behaviours not directly demonstrated,
including retreat, re-contact, and alternative manoeuvring
on unseen plants, suggesting adaptation beyond trajectory
reproduction. Limitations include single-operator, non-haptic
demonstrations and end-effector-only tactile coverage. Future
work will explore richer demonstrations, broader tactile
sensing, and visuotactile policies combining global visual
guidance with local tactile adaptation.
VII. CONCLUSION
This work presented SPICE, a tactile-conditioned diffusion
policy for reaching through visually occluded plant canopies.
From demonstrations collected using a simple, cost-efficient
setup, SPICE learned three contact-responsive behaviours:
target reaching, branch manoeuvring, and branch manipula-
tion, despite intermittent and unevenly represented contact
interactions. Across more than 180 real-robot trials, SPICE
was evaluated against alternative tactile representations, en-
coder architectures, reactive control, and image-conditioned
learning. It further demonstrated zero-shot transfer across
40 trials on lime and tomato plants with different branch
structures and interaction dynamics. To the best of the
authors’ knowledge, SPICE is the first tactile-conditioned
diffusion policy for reaching through physically obstructed
plant canopies, demonstrating that tactile-conditioned gener-
ative policies can support diverse physical interactions under
severe visual occlusion.
