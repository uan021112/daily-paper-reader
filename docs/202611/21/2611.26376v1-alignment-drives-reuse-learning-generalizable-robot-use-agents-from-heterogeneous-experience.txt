Alignment Drives Reuse:
Learning Generalizable Robot-Use Agents from Heterogeneous Experience
Ziyue Wang1
Yaopeng Yang2
Liyi Zhou1
1The University of Sydney

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) demonstrate broad gen-
eralization across tasks and domains.
However, compa-
rable generalization in robot manipulation remains chal-
lenging.
Heterogeneity in robot embodiments, task for-
mulations, geometric conventions, and action interfaces
fragments interaction experience across benchmarks and
hardware. We rethink robot skill learning through align-
ment: Can heterogeneous interactions across benchmarks
and embodiments jointly improve one shared skillset for
robot manipulation?
We propose UNICRAFT, a frame-
work for learning robot manipulation skills by aligning het-
erogeneous settings.
Specifically, UNICRAFT represents
observed scene geometry using consistent coordinate con-
ventions and aligns sensing and control interfaces across
robots. It organizes tasks from different benchmarks in a
canonical format, allowing interaction feedback to jointly
refine shared skills and the orchestration policy that guides
their selection and composition, without any demonstra-
tions or model weight updates. We interleave tasks from
3 benchmarks and 6 embodiments, collecting 2,700 learn-
ing rollouts per agent model.
During training, UNI-
CRAFT improves pooled task success on unseen scenar-
ios and tasks by 9–28 percentage points on LIBERO-PRO,
10.8–13.3 on RoboTwin 2.0, and 3.1–14.4 on RoboCasa365
(Epochs 1–3). After training, with the learned skills frozen,
UNICRAFT demonstrates zero-shot transfer across 4 new
robot embodiments and 5 additional agent models on robo-
suite and RoboDojo.

language, image, and video tasks [1, 4]. Natural language

However, comparable generalization in robotics remains

One of the main reasons is heterogeneity
(Figure 1(a)). Robot embodiments differ in their kinemat-
ics, observation spaces, and action representations; even the
same numerical action can produce different physical ef-
fects depending on the robot and its controller [33, 47].
These embodiment differences also shape how interaction
experience is collected and represented, while datasets in-
troduce further differences in coordinate conventions and
annotation practices [23, 47]. These differences fragment
experience across platforms, making it costly to collect and
difficult to transfer to new environments and embodiments.
These challenges also affect vision-language-action (VLA)
models, which extend pretrained VLMs to robot control by
learning to predict actions from task-specific demonstra-
tions [2, 3, 23, 49, 53]. Their improvements on manipu-
lation benchmarks do not consistently translate into gen-
eralization beyond training conditions: changes in scenes,
instructions, or embodiments can cause sharp performance
drops [13, 47, 50]. Qwen-RobotManip advocates aligning
heterogeneous manipulation data in representation, motion,
and behavior before VLA training [47].
Another line of work learns and evolves robot con-
trol policies through iterative interaction with the environ-
ment, reducing reliance on task-specific trajectory demon-
strations [7, 17, 27, 29, 48].
In this setting, LLMs or
VLMs write executable policy code and continually revise
it using interaction feedback [26, 29, 48]. Successful ex-
ecutions and validated repairs are distilled into program-
matic skills, accumulating cross-task knowledge and en-
abling agents to solve diverse tasks through skill compo-
sition. [17, 29, 48]. This line of work represents a prag-

Its demonstrated scope, however, remains bounded by task
definitions, hardware configurations, and benchmark suites.
For example, GaP formulates policy evolution for varia-
tional automation tasks, where a specified task is repeated
within a fixed workcell with bounded shape and pose varia-
tions [7]. SHAPER evaluates evolved skills under fixed exe-
cution interfaces, leaving cross-embodiment transfer under-
explored [42]. Skill2Real learns programmatic skills sepa-
rately on LIBERO-90 and robosuite [17]. These boundaries
and misalignments constrain the experience generalization
across tasks, scenarios and embodiments. Naturally, they
motivate three research questions. (i) Can alignment en-
able unified learning of manipulation skills from interac-
tion across heterogeneous tasks, benchmarks, and embodi-
ments? (ii) If so, does such learning improve generalization
to unseen tasks and scenarios? (iii) Can the learned skills
also transfer to unseen benchmarks and new embodiments?
Inspired by the alignment first, then scale principle [47],
we present UNICRAFT, a unified framework designed to
align heterogeneous settings (e.g., embodiments, environ-
ments, and benchmark cases) for learning robot manipu-
lation skills through iterative interactions without trajec-
tory demonstrations or updates to model weights. We re-
alize this principle by introducing (i) a unified environ-
ment representation that describes observed scene entities
as typed Objects and exposes robot and camera capabili-
ties through a shared Python package; (ii) composable skills
that use reusable library functions and declare the action
interfaces required for execution, with adapters providing
their hardware-specific implementations; (iii) a learnable
orchestration policy that guides the agent model to write
and execute programs in response to observations and roll-
out outcomes; and (iv) a canonical case representation that
consistently specifies task instructions and success condi-
tions, embodiments, scenes, and initial states across various
benchmarks. During the skill learning stage, mixed roll-
outs of these cases provide aggregated feedback for jointly
optimizing the shared programmatic components and the
orchestration policy.
This alignment enables each agent
model to learn one shared repository from 180 tasks across
LIBERO-PRO, RoboTwin 2.0, and RoboCasa365, cover-
ing six robot embodiments.
Learning interleaves these
tasks and embodiments, yielding 2,700 learning rollouts
per agent model (Figure 1(b)). UniCraft demonstrates im-
proved generalization across 66 unseen-scenario cases and
36 held-out tasks with continued refinement, as well as
zero-shot transfer capability to four new robot embodiments
and the unseen robosuite [52] and RoboDojo [9] bench-
marks. Our main contributions are:
• Methodology. We introduce UNICRAFT, which aligns
execution interfaces and learning cases, enabling joint
learning and refinement of one shared skillset and its or-
chestration policy across benchmarks and embodiments.
• Interleaved training. We interleave interaction experi-
ence from 300 task–embodiment pairs spanning 3 bench-
marks and 6 embodiments to learn one shared skillset per
agent model for reuse across heterogeneous settings.

2. Related Work
Cross-embodiment training. VLA models learn to pre-
dict actions from robot demonstrations [2, 23, 53]. Cross-
embodiment training combines demonstrations across
robotic platforms. Open X-Embodiment pools data from
22 robot embodiments and demonstrates positive trans-
fer across robots [33].
X-VLA uses source-specific soft
prompts to accommodate heterogeneous datasets [49].
Qwen-RobotManip further aligns state-action representa-
tions, motion, and behavior for large-scale training [47].
These approaches address heterogeneity within shared
action-prediction models. Our work examines alignment at
the level of interfaces for reusable manipulation skills.
Code-generated robot policies. Code as Policies (CaP)
turns language instructions into Python policies that call
APIs for perception and control, allowing explicit branches
and feedback loops [26]. Instruct2Act grounds generated
programs with visual foundation models [19], while Vox-
Poser writes code that composes 3D value maps for motion
planning [20]. RoboScript improves deployability through
a simulation-to-real interface, syntax checks, and Gazebo
validation [6].
CaP-Agent0, introduced in CaP-X, syn-
thesizes nine task-agnostic helper functions from success-
ful rollouts and uses execution feedback to recover fail-
ures [14]. These helpers provide reusable components for
subsequent program generation. RECENT extends reuse
across embodiments by preserving skill semantics while
adapting execution bindings through code refactoring [10].
Robotic manipulation skill learning.
Interaction-based
skill learning retains executable procedures and validated
repairs for reuse across tasks [7, 17, 27, 29, 48]. GaP refines
computation graphs built from modular skills through sim-
ulation rehearsal for variational automation in fixed work-
cells [7]. ASPIRE transfers guidance distilled from val-
idated repairs to support further debugging on a target
robot [29]. Skill2Real learns hierarchical skills in simula-
tion and transfers the frozen skills through a shared API,
while its LIBERO-90 and robosuite skill collections are
trained independently [17]. These studies differ in how ex-
perience is retained and what adaptation is needed in a new
setting. Our focus is on how aligned interfaces let experi-
ence from heterogeneous tasks, benchmarks, and embodi-
ments jointly refine one shared skillset.
3. Method
Here we describe alignment, joint refinement of skills and
their orchestration policy, and online skill composition.
3.1. Aligning Heterogeneous Manipulation Settings
Aligned geometry. Translating high-level cognitive plans
into real-world manipulation requires grounding abstract
semantic relations in metric physical space.
Although
VLMs can identify interaction candidates and reason about
spatial relationships, their predictions may require addi-
tional grounding in metric geometry and the robot’s cur-
rent state for reliable closed-loop execution [5, 30, 46]. We
therefore build on object-centric manipulation [18, 21, 36]
and represent observed scene entities as typed Objects car-

extent of their visible regions, with every measurement, tar-
get, and tolerance expressed in one episode-local frame.

ward z-axis, and the camera adapter reports intrinsics K
together with the capture-time rotation Rt and translation

3.2. Offline Learning and Evolution
Figure 3 summarizes our offline learning loop. We execute
aligned cases in batches, revise shared skills and their or-
chestration policy using rollout feedback, and carry vali-
dated updates forward to subsequent batches.
Aligning robot learning cases across benchmarks. To
support shared skill learning across benchmarks, we align
heterogeneous learning cases for unified rollout and eval-
uation across LIBERO-PRO [28, 50], RoboTwin 2.0 [8],
RoboCasa365 [32], and RoboDojo [9]. These benchmarks
differ in their embodiments and in how a case specifies
its task, scene, and initial state (e.g., declarative descrip-
tions in LIBERO-PRO vs. configurable procedural classes
in RoboCasa365). We define a canonical learning case as
c = (τ, r, e, x0). Here, τ specifies the instruction and the
success criteria. The embodiment r identifies the robot. The
scene configuration e specifies the layout, object and fix-
ture assets. The initial state x0 determines the starting robot
configuration, object poses, and articulated-fixture states.
These fields describe the task independently of each bench-
mark’s configuration format. For execution, each bench-
mark integration selects the native task and robot, config-
ures the scene, and initializes the starting state through the
benchmark’s existing setup routines.

3.3. Online Manipulation and Skill Transfer
Programmatic online manipulation. During online ma-
nipulation, action sequences adapt to the evolving scene
and execution outcomes. With M⋆fixed, the manipulation
policy π⋆guides the LLM/VLM in synthesizing a Python
program Ct conditioned on the task instruction g and ac-
cumulated interaction history ht. Online execution com-
bines two processes: (i) intra-program control flow, where
variables, branches, and loops compose skill calls and con-
dition subsequent operations on their returned results, and
(ii) inter-program decision-making, where the LLM/VLM
uses program outputs, exceptions, and explicitly requested
visual observations to synthesize Ct+1 within the same roll-
out. For example, a program can withhold placement when
grasp verification fails, while the subsequent model deci-
sion uses a new observation to plan a corrective retry. This
dual-level hierarchy enables dynamic runtime adaptation of
action sequences and parameters.
Cross-embodiment transfer. A target embodiment assem-
bles compatible modules from the frozen shared skills and
supplies device parameters and adapter bindings satisfy-
ing their action requirements. The learned implementations
and manipulation policy remain unchanged.
For exam-
ple, dual ARX-X5 can reuse grasping and placing modules
learned on Aloha-AgileX through its native action bindings.
Panda+Omron can combine manipulation modules shared
with a fixed Panda with navigation. An online program then
determines the call order and arguments for these modules.

4.2. Learning from Heterogeneous Experience
Agent models and learning cases. We use 3 agent models:
the closed-source GPT-6 Astra and Gemini 3.8 Flash, and
the open-source DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (1017).
All three
use high reasoning effort. The runs share a pool of 180 na-
tive tasks from three sources: the 90 LIBERO-90 tasks, 40
of the 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks under its hard setting, and
the 50 RoboCasa365 pretraining tasks (Table 1).
Cases
are randomly shuffled across the task pool, so each epoch
interleaves benchmarks and embodiments. The pool cov-
ers a fixed-base arm, 4 dual-arm platforms, and a mobile
manipulator across short-horizon tabletop, bimanual, and
long-horizon kitchen tasks. All three sources provide na-
tive task definitions and success checks, which the learning
loop uses in place of demonstrations. These assignments
give 300 task–embodiment pairs (90 + 50 + 40 × 4) over
6 embodiments, and Appendix Table 5 lists the tasks. We
reserve dual ARX-X5, Sawyer, Baxter, and Tiago, as well
as RoboDojo and robosuite, for the transfer experiments in
Section 4.4. Each run starts empty, evolving independently
in its worktree under matched budgets.
Epochs, batches, revisions, and budgets. Over 3 epochs,
each of the 300 task–embodiment pairs receives a fresh con-
figuration and initial state every epoch, yielding 900 distinct
cases and 2,700 base rollouts per agent model at 3 execu-
tions per case. Each epoch is split into 6 batches. All agent
models see the same cases in the same order, and one har-
ness revision follows each batch. Each case runs under a
horizon of 2× the pinned native evaluation budget of its
task, counted in native steps and identical across agent mod-
els, configurations, and embodiments. For example, a 400-
step LIBERO-90 task receives an 800-step horizon. The
steps provide a margin for correcting errors.

5. Discussion
How does UniCraft execute tasks with frozen skills?
We
present trajectories, programs using frozen skills, and
outcomes for five benchmarks in Appendix F.
Why do some cases remain unsolved after revisions?
We
conduct failure reason analysis on cases that fail across revi-
sions and repeated rollouts. Specifically, on RoboCasa365,
GPT-6 Astra achieves 15% success on Composite-Seen at
Epoch 3, with persistent failures on RinseSinkBasin and
SteamInMicrowave. We identify four major failure reasons
(Appendix D). (i) Revision may leave required capabilities
unavailable (Figs. 7–8).
(ii) Later actions can invalidate
completed subgoals (Figs. 9–10).
(iii) Agents struggle
to infer how movable parts respond to their actions and
whether the intended effect occurs (Figs. 11–12). (iv) Fine
manipulation remains unreliable when success depends on
precise alignment and stable contact (Figs. 13–14).
Can validated revisions cause regressions, and why?
Yes.
For example, Gemini 3.8 Flash scores 23.75%, 17.50%,
and 21.25% on RoboCasa365 Composite-Unseen across
Epochs 1–3 (Table 6). Reviser design involves a coverage–
context trade-off. Specifically, broader diagnosis increases
time, computation, and evidence volume, straining the
Reviser’s finite context for root-cause analysis.
On one
hand, the Reviser reviews 150 rollouts per batch and can
consult epoch-level execution and revision histories for a
broader perspective. On the other, to reduce context load,
we diagnose rollouts concurrently and have the Reviser
read case summaries before full traces. We analyze this
trade-off in detail in Appendix E.
6. Limitations
Comparison fairness. Differences in auxiliary tools and
pretrained components limit comparability across meth-
ods.
UNICRAFT uses SAM2.1 for prompted foreground
segmentation, whereas ASPIRE’s released stack provides
SAM3 segmentation, Contact-GraspNet grasp proposals,
and Molmo-based pixel grounding as a fallback [29]. We
control other sources of variation through three measures.
Model inference: For each agent model, compared methods
use the same API provider and high reasoning-effort setting.
Execution budgets: Within each evaluation setting, agent
methods share the same per-case limits on simulation steps
and model requests. Hardware configuration: All methods
are reproduced on the same machine and hardware configu-
ration. These controls standardize inference and execution
conditions.
Evaluation budget. We evaluate six agent models at high
reasoning effort and track learning for 3 of them over 3
epochs. Due to the cost of model inference and simulation
rollouts, we have not assessed other reasoning budgets or
longer learning schedules.
Empirical design choices. We fix the revision interval at
150 scheduled rollouts, comprising 50 cases with 3 training
executions each. We use 6 batches per epoch and 5 eval-
uation rollouts per case. These settings were not system-
atically optimized or assessed for sensitivity to batch size,
revision frequency, and repetition count.
Real-world validation. All evaluations are conducted in
simulation. Future work will evaluate transfer to physical
robots under real sensor noise, calibration errors, and dif-
ferences in contact dynamics.
7. Conclusion
We presented UNICRAFT, aligning cases and execution in-
terfaces to refine shared manipulation skills and their or-
chestration policy from heterogeneous feedback. Learning
spans three benchmarks and six embodiments without tra-
jectory demonstrations or model weight updates. Results
show improved generalization to unseen tasks and scenar-
ios, and zero-shot reuse on new robots and agent models.
Alignment offers a basis for reusable skills.

E. The Coverage–Context Trade-off
In CoffeeSetupMug, the robot must put a mug on the tray
beneath a coffee machine’s outlet. A revision added a check
intended to stop movement after the robot loses its grasp:
the arm stops when the gripper no longer detects a held ob-
ject. This change passed tests on eight training cases, with
no previous success lost. However, the newly successful
rollout never triggered the check. Figure 15 replays a pre-
viously successful placement program. With the check en-
abled, descent stops too soon and the later release leaves
the mug outside the tray. Bypassing only this check lets the
mug descend onto the tray and restores task success.

In our setting, the Reviser reviews 150 rollouts per batch
and can inspect epoch-level execution and revision histo-
ries. Looking across more cases can reveal regressions and
shared failure causes, but reading more trajectories con-
sumes time, computation, and context. We diagnose avail-
able trajectories while other rollouts continue and write
each case’s findings to summary.md. The Reviser reads
these summaries first and opens full sessions when it needs
more detail. The prompt requires testing target failures, re-
lated cases, and prior successes that the patch may affect.
Finding. The coverage–context trade-off.
Reviser design involves a coverage–context trade-off. On one
hand, examining more cases and past revisions can reveal re-
gressions. On the other, those records compete for finite con-
text during root-cause analysis. Therefore, the Reviser needs a
broad view of prior behavior while retaining enough detail to
explain individual failures.
