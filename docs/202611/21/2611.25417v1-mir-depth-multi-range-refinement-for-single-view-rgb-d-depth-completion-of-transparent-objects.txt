Abstract—Recovering accurate visible-surface depth from a
single RGB-D image and an object mask is essential for robotic
grasping and manipulation of transparent objects. Specular
reflections cause missing depth, while refraction corrupts nonzero
returns with background depth, making raw measurements
unreliable as surface anchors. Under a fixed sampling budget,
refining an initial single-view prediction within a single search
interval either misses large refraction offsets or sacrifices fine
local resolution. To resolve this single-view trade-off, MIR-
Depth constructs Coordinate-Aware Multi-Range Hypotheses:
a 3D-aggregated global inverse-depth volume and depth-axis-
filtered pixel-wise local volumes around a prior-guided initial
depth. Selective Recurrent Refinement then introduces out-of-
bounds lookup masking and validity-masked softmax fusion to
iteratively update inverse depth from these volumes using a
multi-scale ConvGRU. Finally, Spatially Gated Correction applies
a learned per-pixel spatial gate to the full-resolution residual,
preserving accurate initial geometry without imposing raw sensor

I. INTRODUCTION
Transparent objects are common in household and labora-
tory settings, yet recovering their surface geometry remains
challenging for robotic manipulation (Fig. 1). Accurate visible-
surface depth supports grasp planning [1], but reflection and
refraction cause missing or erroneous nonzero RGB-D mea-
surements that may correspond to background surfaces [2]–[4].
Opaque-scene depth completion often uses observed depths as
geometric anchors [5]–[7], whereas transparent-region read-
ings may lie behind the surface a gripper must contact [8].
An object mask localizes the target but cannot establish the
reliability of nonzero readings, so single-view completion must
fill missing depth and correct corrupted measurements without
treating them as reliable surface constraints.
Single-view networks predict dense depth from RGB-D
inputs in one forward pass [9]–[11], while prior-guided meth-
ods incorporate monocular depth estimates [12]–[14], as in
ReMake, which combines a relative-depth prior with an object

mask [8]. Despite providing a useful metric reference, prior-
guided predictions can have residual errors ranging from a few
millimeters along thin rims to tens of centimeters in strongly
refracted interiors. With a fixed per-pixel sampling budget,
narrow search intervals miss large refraction offsets, whereas
workspace-wide intervals reduce local sampling resolution.
Multi-range disparity volumes address a similar trade-off in
stereo matching [15], [16], but single-view RGB-D completion
lacks epipolar correlation and must score candidates using
monocular context and unreliable raw depth.
MIR-Depth (Fig. 2) iteratively refines single-view depth
across multiple inverse-depth volumes using metric initializa-
tion and multi-scale context from RGB, raw depth, the object
mask, and a frozen Depth Anything V2 prior [14]. Coordinate-
Aware Multi-Range Hypotheses combines one shared global
volume with two pixel-wise local volumes around the ini-
tial reference inverse depth, using validity-masked raw-depth
residuals as soft evidence. Since global bins share scene-wide
planes while local bins shift per pixel, a 3D hourglass aggre-
gates the global volume spatially, whereas depth-axis convo-
lutions filter local volumes without cross-pixel depth mixing.
Selective Recurrent Refinement uses out-of-bounds lookup
masking and validity-masked softmax fusion to combine fine
local detail and wide global corrections through a multi-scale
ConvGRU. Spatially Gated Correction applies a learned per-
pixel gate to the full-resolution residual, preserving accurate
initial regions without clamping to raw depth. Together, these
designs reduce depth error on TransCG and four unseen zero-
shot benchmarks while supporting reliable 6-DoF grasping on
a physical robot.
Our main contributions are:
• We propose Coordinate-Aware Multi-Range Hypotheses,
pairing a 3D-aggregated global inverse-depth volume
with depth-axis-filtered pixel-wise local volumes to cover
large refraction offsets and fine surface detail.
• We design Selective Recurrent Refinement, introducing
out-of-bounds lookup masking and validity-masked soft-
max fusion to iteratively update single-view inverse depth
from monocular context and soft raw-depth residuals.
• We introduce Spatially Gated Correction, learning a full-
resolution per-pixel spatial gate to blend recurrent depth
residuals into the initial estimate without treating nonzero
raw depth as a hard constraint.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Transparent Object Depth Completion
Conventional RGB-D and LiDAR completion methods re-
cover dense depth through spatial propagation [5], [6] or
learned feature fusion [7], [17], [18]. For transparent objects,
reflection and refraction cause missing depth and erroneous
nonzero measurements that may correspond to background
surfaces, requiring both hole filling and correction of unre-
liable observations [2], [3].
ClearGrasp [2] uses predicted surface normals and occlusion
boundaries to guide depth optimization. Other methods learn
local implicit representations over ray–voxel pairs [4] or
jointly complete point clouds and depth maps [19]. RGB-D
regression networks [9]–[11], [20], [21] predict dense depth
from image and sensor features. This line of work uses
synthetic data [20] and real-world datasets [9], [22]–[24] for
training and evaluation, with completed geometry supporting
6-DoF grasp detection [1].
Stereo-based methods [25], [26] use cross-view observa-
tions, while monocular multi-layer estimation [27] predicts
multiple surfaces along each viewing ray. For single-view
completion, pretrained monocular depth estimators [12]–[14]
provide scene priors that complement raw depth. ReMake [8]
combines a relative-depth prior and an object mask for RGB-
D depth prediction, while MIR-Depth refines a prior-guided
initial estimate to recover visible-surface depth from a single
RGB-D image and object mask.
B. Iterative Depth Refinement
PSMNet [28] aggregates disparity cost volumes with
stacked 3D hourglass networks, while CasMVSNet [15] pro-
gressively narrows depth intervals in a coarse-to-fine cascade.
Building on RAFT’s recurrent lookup framework [29], RAFT-
Stereo [30] and IGEV-Stereo [31] update disparity through lo-
cal volume queries and multi-level ConvGRUs. IGEV++ [16]
extends this approach with multiple disparity ranges to retain
fine detail while covering large disparities. These methods
derive candidate evidence from cross-view feature matching,
unavailable in single-view RGB-D completion.
In monocular depth estimation, IEBins [32] progressively
adjusts depth bins based on estimated depth and uncertainty.
For transparent-object completion, LIDF [4] refines its ini-
tial prediction through self-correcting updates, DCIRNet [33]
combines refinement with multi-stage supervision, and Trans-
Diff [34] uses iterative diffusion denoising conditioned on
RGB-D information.
With a fixed sampling budget, candidate-based refinement
trades off large-error coverage and fine local resolution. MIR-
Depth combines a shared global inverse-depth range with two
pixel-wise local ranges around a prior-guided reference for
single-view transparent-object completion. It scores candidates
using image features and raw-depth residuals, adapting volume
aggregation and recurrent lookup to shared global and pixel-
dependent local coordinates.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate MIR-Depth on TransCG [9], zero-shot trans-
fer to TODD [19], TRansPose [23], ClearPose [22], and
HouseCat6D [24], real-world 6-DoF grasp pose prediction,
and component ablations. MAE and RMSE are reported in
millimeters, REL is dimensionless, and δτ is in percent.
A. Implementation Details
We train MIR-Depth on the official TransCG split [9],
containing 34,191 single-view 640×480 RGB-D frames from
78 scenes, using RGB, raw depth, precomputed Depth Any-
thing V2 Small priors, and dataset-provided transparent-object
masks. The masks serve as input M and define ΩM; processed
training targets use V ∗(p) = 1[D∗(p) ≥0.01 m]. Following
ReMake [8], features from two Transformer encoders and
one CNN encoder are summed at each scale and passed to
a pyramid decoder and depth head. At quarter resolution,
a three-level 3D hourglass aggregates the global volume,
depth-axis convolutions filter local volumes, and a multi-scale
ConvGRU updates inverse depth before spatial gating. Raw
depth and validity masks use nearest-neighbor downsampling,
lookup uses linear interpolation, and D0 resizing and inverse-
depth and gate-logit upsampling use bilinear interpolation.

B. Evaluation on TransCG
Setup and baselines. The TransCG test split contains 23,524
frames from 52 scenes (11,766 D435 and 11,758 L515
frames). Predictions are bilinearly resized to 320 × 240 and
evaluated on Ωe = {p : Me(p) = 1, Ve(p) = 1}, where Me
is the evaluation transparent-object mask, Ve is the validity
mask retained before depth inpainting in the official prepro-
cessing, and D∗denotes the processed evaluation target. Per-
image MAE, RMSE, REL = ⟨| bD −D∗|/(D∗+ ϵe)⟩Ωe, and
threshold accuracy δτ are averaged across images. The official
implementation uses ϵe = 10−8 m and counts pixels satisfying
max( bD/(D∗+ ϵe), D∗/ bD) < τ for τ ∈{1.05, 1.10, 1.25},
normalizing percentages by |Ωe|. For nonnegative predictions,
zero predictions fail the ratio test; empty Ωe gives zero per-
image errors and 100% threshold accuracy. The baselines
cover three categories: DFNet [9] performs in-domain single-
pass regression without external priors; BP-Net [17] (NYUv2)
and PSD [18] (NYUv2 and KITTI with Depth Anything
V2) evaluate opaque-scene propagation under refraction; and
ReMake [8] provides prior-guided single-pass prediction using
the same Depth Anything V2 Small prior and mask inputs as
MIR-Depth.
Main results. Table I shows that BP-Net and PSD prop-
agate refracted background depth across foreground ob-
jects by treating nonzero sensor returns as reliable surface
seeds, yielding 37.855/61.796 mm and 37.693/59.735 mm
MAE/RMSE, respectively, even with Depth Anything V2
priors in PSD. Trained on transparent scenes, DFNet reduces
error to 12.567/17.543 mm, but its single-pass CNN without
monocular shape priors struggles with large specular holes.
ReMake combines the same Depth Anything V2 prior and ob-
ject mask to reach 8.040/11.363 mm, but its one-shot decoder
cannot jointly resolve large interior refraction and millimeter-
scale rim detail. Using this prior-guided initial anchor, MIR-
Depth redesigns single-view estimation through Coordinate-
Aware Multi-Range Hypotheses, whose 3D-aggregated global
volume captures large interior offsets, reducing MAE/RMSE
to 6.833/10.504 mm and REL to 0.01397, compared with
0.01660 for ReMake. Selective Recurrent Refinement fuses
active-range lookup features for inverse-depth updates, with
MIR-Depth achieving δ1.05 = 94.41% and δ1.10 = 98.76%,
compared with 93.63% and 98.65% for ReMake. Fig. 3 fur-
ther shows that global hypothesis aggregation pulls refracted
vessel interiors back to the visible surface in D435 captures
(Scenes 78 and 94, 2.50 and 2.07 mm MAE versus 4.46 and
3.55 mm for ReMake) and L515 captures (Scene 47, 4.29 mm
versus 5.96 mm), while depth-axis local filtering preserves
sharp container rims.
Hole filling and refraction correction. Table III separately
evaluates missing depth and nonzero returns. Anchoring pre-
dictions to raw measurements leaves PSD modifying 0.00%
of nonzero pixels by more than 1 mm, with MAE remain-
ing at the raw value, while BP-Net repairs only 0.80% of
outliers. MIR-Depth predicts positive depth at all evaluated
holes, whereas ReMake leaves 33 nonpositive predictions;
their within-20 mm hole accuracies are 92.32% and 91.26%,
respectively. Using the masked raw-depth residual δobs as soft
evidence, MIR-Depth repairs 89.74% of > 20 mm outliers
on nonzero returns and achieves 5.584 mm MAE (82.62%
reduction), compared with 88.52% repair and 7.137 mm MAE
(77.78% reduction) for ReMake. Relative to raw sensor depth,
MIR-Depth modifies 72.06% of nonzero pixels by more than
1 mm and reduces error at 64.80%.
C. Cross-Dataset Evaluation
To evaluate zero-shot generalization across sensors, ob-
ject categories, and clutter densities, we compare MIR-
Depth with general-purpose depth completion methods BP-
Net (trained on NYUv2) and PSD (trained on NYUv2 and
KITTI), and transparent-object completion methods DFNet
and ReMake (both trained on TransCG). Without fine-tuning,
all five models are evaluated on TODD [19] (4,339 frames),
TRansPose [23] (32,734 right-camera frames across 26 se-
quences), ClearPose [22] (132,598 frames across labels 1–
63), and HouseCat6D [24] (2,929 frames). Errors are aggre-
gated pixel-wise at native resolution over the intersection of
transparent-object masks and valid ground-truth depth (D∗≥
0.01 m on TODD, ClearPose, and HouseCat6D; CAD depth
D∗> 0.01 m for categories C, H, and T on TRansPose).
Table II shows that opaque-scene propagation in BP-Net
and PSD amplifies outliers under unseen refraction patterns,
reaching 1291.105 and 1305.920 mm RMSE on TODD and
306.267 mm MAE for PSD on TRansPose. Despite shar-

E. Ablation Study
Component ablations. Table IV compares MIR-Depth with
five ablated variants on 23,524 TransCG test frames at 320 ×
240. Removing iterative refinement increases MAE/RMSE
from 6.833/10.504 to 8.040/11.363 mm, showing the contri-
bution of recurrent candidate lookup and depth updates to cor-
recting initial errors. Replacing the medium and fine candidate
grids with the global grid, while retaining all branches, aggre-
gation operators, and per-branch candidate counts, increases
MAE/RMSE to 6.895/10.540 mm and reduces δ1.05 from
94.41% to 94.30%, supporting local ranges at a fixed candidate
count. Replacing learned selection with uniform weights over
active ranges, while preserving candidate grids and lookup
validity masking, increases MAE/RMSE to 6.906/10.597 mm
and reduces δ1.05 to 94.17%, showing the benefit of weighting
ranges using lookup features, entropy, and peak probability.
Removing intermediate supervision increases MAE/RMSE
to 6.882/10.554 mm, supporting supervision of intermediate
refinement outputs.
The w/o gating variant achieves the best TransCG results
(6.810/10.245 mm MAE/RMSE, 0.01396 REL, and 94.45%
δ1.05). Compared with full MIR-Depth, MAE and RMSE
decrease by 0.023 mm (0.34%) and 0.259 mm (2.47%), while
δ1.05 increases by 0.04 percentage points. Setting G ≡1
directly outputs DT , whereas G < 1 retains part of D0 (Eq. 7).
In the ungated variant, gate parameters are frozen and the
correction regularizer remains active on DT −D0. When DT
is accurate, residual attenuation can retain initialization errors,
which may contribute to the advantage of the ungated variant
on TransCG.
In real-world captures with predicted SAM masks (Fig. 6),
ungated output produces abrupt depth changes near the hol-
low container and fragmented surfaces in clutter, whereas
full MIR-Depth preserves more continuous contours. Spa-
tially Gated Correction uses recurrent features and candidate-
distribution cues to attenuate the residual, reducing boundary
depth jumps and surface fragmentation.
V. CONCLUSION
We present MIR-Depth for single-view RGB-D depth com-
pletion of transparent objects through prior-guided initial depth
refinement. Coordinate-Aware Multi-Range Hypotheses com-
bine shared global and pixel-wise local volumes to capture
large refraction offsets and fine geometry. Selective Recurrent
Refinement updates inverse depth through valid-range lookup,
while Spatially Gated Correction preserves accurate initial
regions. Experiments on TransCG, four zero-shot benchmarks,
and a real robotic grasping platform show that MIR-Depth
achieves the best results among compared baselines on multi-
ple transparent-object datasets.

Limitations. MIR-Depth recovers visible surfaces from a
single RGB-D frame despite mostly missing or refraction-
corrupted sensor measurements, but cannot reveal unobserved
geometry. A fully transparent object against a uniform back-
ground may have almost no visible boundaries, with its far
side hidden; even a human cannot determine its exact shape
from that frame. Future work will extend MIR-Depth to
temporal RGB-D streams, using views captured during robot
arm motion to reveal hidden surfaces and enable closed-loop
manipulation of transparent objects.
