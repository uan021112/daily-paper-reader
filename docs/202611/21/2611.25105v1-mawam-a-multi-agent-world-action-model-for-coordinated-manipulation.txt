Abstract
Every move in a robot team changes the world in which its teammates must act. Coordinated manipulation
therefore calls for predicting the team’s actions together with the physical future they jointly create. We
introduce Mawam, a multi-agent world-action model that jointly generates each robot’s future views and
executable action chunks in a single sample. Built from a pretrained single-robot world-action backbone,
Mawam jointly denoises all robots’ action and future-view tokens through cross-agent attention, while
preserving a separate control interface for each robot. Mawam achieves the highest success on all three
real-robot tasks (laundry-bag collection, bath-towel folding, and umbrella handover), with 67.8% mean
success, 18.9 percentage points above MA-VLA trained on the same demonstrations and cameras. In
simulation, Mawam reaches 90.3% mean success on eight tasks with teams of up to four arms. Ablations
show that cross-agent attention and team-stage future-video supervision both improve coordinated

1. Introduction
Robots will increasingly work in teams to tackle complex tasks in the physical world. In homes and factories,
robots may need to hand objects to one another, jointly manipulate deformable materials, or carry loads
together. In these tasks, each robot observes the scene from its own viewpoint, but its actions change the
shared environment and shape what its teammates can do next. A grasp may alter the shape of an object held
by another robot, while a handover may enable a teammate to begin its next operation (Mandi et al., 2024;
Qin et al., 2025). Successful collaboration therefore requires robots to coordinate their actions and timing to
achieve a shared goal. Viewed this way, collaborative manipulation is a process in which robots jointly shape
the future of their shared physical world. This motivates us to formulate it as a multi-agent world-modeling
problem: jointly predicting the team’s shared future and the action sequences that produce it.
Existing approaches to multi-robot collaboration largely fall into three categories. (1) Decentralized policy
coordination retains a separate execution policy for each robot while learning coordination mechanisms.
Although readily scalable, these policies may lead to idle waiting or oscillatory behavior in tightly coupled tasks
because of mismatched coordination patterns or desynchronized execution (Dong et al., 2026; He et al., 2025;
Park et al., 2026). (2) Joint trajectory and action generation methods model multi-agent trajectories or generate
coordinated controls but lack explicit predictions of their visual consequences (Wang et al., 2025; Zhu et al.,
2024). (3) Language- and logic-guided task coordination uses planning, dialogue, or temporal logic to organize
collaboration, but does not explicitly predict the visual consequences of joint actions (Chen et al., 2024; Kannan
et al., 2024; Mandi et al., 2024; Meng and Yin, 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). These methods either decentralize
decision-making, lack physical foresight, or rely on task-level guidance; none jointly generates executable team
actions and their corresponding visual futures.
World models offer a natural path toward addressing this lack of physical foresight, yet existing research still
separates multi-agent future prediction from executable joint control. Multi-agent world models can reconstruct
shared states or disentangle per-agent dynamics for planning (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026b), and
in video generation they can produce multi-view-consistent futures (Liu et al., 2026; Savva et al., 2026; Wu
et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026). However, these video models treat actions only as conditioning inputs and do
not produce policies that can be directly deployed in the physical world (Liu et al., 2026; Savva et al., 2026;
Wu et al., 2026). In robotics, recent world-action models jointly learn visual prediction and action generation,
demonstrating that future-prediction representations can improve control by capturing the dependencies
between actions and anticipated scene evolution (Guo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025; Ye et al.,
2026). However, these models are still organized around a single robot’s control stream. Multi-agent video
generation models thus predict without producing executable joint control, whereas multi-robot policies act
without jointly modeling the visual consequences that each agent will encounter.
This leads to a central question: how can a unified generative model jointly predict a robot team’s future
observations and generate coordinated actions for manipulation in a shared physical world? We introduce
Mawam, a multi-agent world-action model that learns coordinated control by jointly modeling each robot’s
actions and the team’s future camera views. A shared Transformer connects all agents’ action and future-view
tokens through cross-agent attention and jointly denoises them. Each generated sample pairs executable action
chunks with synchronized future views, predicting both how the team will act and how the scene will evolve
(Figure 1).
Joint prediction needs to preserve which observations and controls belong to each robot while distinguishing
its identity from its task responsibility. Mawam uses an explicit view–agent map to associate multiple private
camera streams with their owner, together with per-agent state and action tokens that retain each robot’s

control interface. Regular-simplex rotary identities assign the same identity code to all views of a robot,
an action-to-video bias ties its action tokens to those views, and separate task-role embeddings specify its
responsibility. This allows multiple robots to share a task role without merging their observations or controls:
for example, two camera holders can share a role while retaining separate states and action outputs.
To adapt GR00T 2 (NVIDIA et al., 2026), a single-robot world-action model, to team control, we use two
training stages. During warm-up, the model learns from one robot’s views, state, and actions at a time, adapting
to the cameras and controllers. Team training then enables cross-agent attention and role conditioning, using
synchronized demonstrations to jointly supervise the robots’ actions and future views. Randomized identity
assignments and complete-agent-group shuffling expose each role to varied identity codes and sequence
positions while preserving each robot’s observation–action correspondence. At deployment, joint denoising
produces the team’s future views and action chunks. The robots execute their actions in synchrony, then use
updated observations to condition the next joint prediction.
We evaluate Mawam on three real-robot tasks: laundry-bag collection, bath-towel folding, and umbrella
handover. Using three private cameras per robot and no shared global camera, Mawam achieves 67.8%
mean success, outperforming MA-VLA by 18.9 percentage points under the same demonstration and camera
setup. We further evaluate on eight RoboFactory tasks with two to four arms, including camera alignment,
collaborative photography, and multi-stage delivery, achieving 90.3% average success. Ablations show that
removing cross-agent attention reduces four-arm relay success from 95.0% to 39.0%, while removing future-
video supervision in team training lowers Place Food success from 78.0% to 47.0%. Additional ablations
demonstrate the benefits of task-role conditioning, randomized agent identities, and single-robot warm-up for
coordinated manipulation.
Our main contributions are:
• We propose Mawam, a multi-agent world-action model that jointly denoises all agents’ future-view and
action tokens through cross-agent attention. Joint video-action flow matching enables a single sample to
contain synchronized visual futures and executable actions for the entire team.
• We design an agent-attributed representation that explicitly associates each robot’s private views, state, and
actions, combining regular-simplex agent identities with separate task-role embeddings. This preserves
each robot’s observation and control interfaces while allowing multiple robots to share a task responsibility.
We further develop a two-stage training scheme: single-robot warm-up adapts the pretrained world-
action backbone to the target cameras and controllers, and synchronized team training learns cross-robot
interactions with randomized identity assignments and agent-group shuffling.
• We demonstrate coordinated manipulation on three real-robot tasks and eight simulation tasks with two
to four arms. Mawam improves mean success over MA-VLA by 18.9 percentage points on real robots and
exceeds its published simulation average by 6.9 percentage points. Ablations examine the representation,
future supervision, cross-agent interaction, and training recipe.
2. Related Work
Coordinating robot actions. Existing approaches can be organized around three broad coordination paradigms.
(1) Decentralized policy coordination. These methods retain a separate execution policy for each robot and learn
mechanisms for coordinating their behavior. LatentToM aligns consensus representations across agents, while
MIMIC-D jointly trains decentralized diffusion policies using local information (Dong et al., 2026; He et al.,
2025). CLS-DP distills collaborative dynamics into latent representations that condition each agent’s policy
(Park et al., 2026). (2) Joint trajectory and action generation. MADiff models joint multi-agent trajectories with
diffusion, supporting both centralized control and decentralized execution (Zhu et al., 2024). GauDP integrates
agents’ observations through a shared Gaussian scene representation for coordinated control (Wang et al., 2025).
(3) Language- and logic-guided task coordination. CMAS and SMART-LLM use LLMs for action assignment and
task planning, while RoCo coordinates robots through dialogue (Chen et al., 2024; Kannan et al., 2024; Mandi

et al., 2024). LTLDiff uses language-derived temporal logic to guide demonstration generation and multi-agent
diffusion policies (Meng and Yin, 2026). MA-VLA uses a planner to produce per-arm atomic prompts and a
shared VLA executor to generate all arms’ actions (Zhang et al., 2026b). Benchmarks such as RoboFactory
support systematic evaluation of collaborative manipulation (Qin et al., 2025). These approaches coordinate
robot actions or task plans, but do not jointly generate executable team actions and their corresponding visual
futures. Mawam unifies the two in a single joint sample, coupling all agents’ action chunks with synchronized
future views to model the visual consequences of the team’s actions.
Predicting multi-agent worlds. Shared-world prediction provides a complementary perspective on collabora-
tion. COMBO reconstructs shared state from egocentric observations for compositional planning, and SeqWM
models multi-robot dynamics sequentially with intention sharing (Zhang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026b).
Solaris, ShareVerse, MultiWorld, Gamma-World, and population-scalable video models develop consistent
multi-agent views and scalable video generation (Liu et al., 2026; Savva et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Zhao
et al., 2026a; Zhu et al., 2026). Action-conditioned video models predict how supplied robot or player controls
change the scene. For coordinated manipulation, the policy must also generate the controls that participate in
that change. Mawam learns a joint distribution over the team’s visual futures and low-level actions.
Learning actions with visual futures. Joint visual and action prediction connects generative world models
to robot control. DreamZero learns aligned visual futures and robot actions; Motus unifies world modeling,
policy learning, inverse dynamics, and joint video-action prediction, while Motubrain develops multiview and
cross-embodiment modeling (Bi et al., 2026; Team et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). Recent work also clarifies
how a policy can use future information. Fast-WAM and Self WAM retain future supervision with action-only
deployment paths, and RIFT constructs a future key/value interface in one backbone pass (Pan et al., 2026;
Yuan et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). These designs connect a robot’s actions to anticipated scene evolution.
In a team, that evolution also depends on what the other robots do. Mawam extends joint world-action
modeling to these cross-robot dependencies: all agents’ action chunks and private-view futures interact within
one generative process. Each robot’s predicted behavior is thereby modeled alongside the future that the team
jointly creates.
3. Mawam
Mawam predicts each robot’s actions together with the future the whole team will see. It extends a pretrained
single-robot world-action backbone to generate the whole team’s future views and action chunks in one sample
(Figure 2). Cross-agent attention couples the robots’ predictions; simplex identities attach every view to its
robot, and an action-to-video bias and role embeddings attach its actions and task role.

3.2. Coupling the team’s predictions
Cross-agent attention. One Transformer attends over all agents’ view, state, and action tokens (§ 3.3) and
jointly denoises their future views and actions. It uses dense bidirectional attention across agents and prediction
time, so each agent’s actions and future views are computed jointly with its teammates’ tokens. In Long Pipeline
Delivery, four arms relay a shoe down a line. Each arm can attend to its teammates’ action and future-view
tokens, allowing their handover motions to be generated jointly. The controls and visual consequences of all
participants are generated in the same sample. Shared global camera tokens, when available, serve as read-only
context.
Joint world-action learning. Modality-specific heads predict private-view latents and action chunks using

3.4. Training and closed-loop control
The model first learns how one robot acts through its own cameras and controller, then how the robots’ actions
interact. Both stages use Eq. (2). (1) Warm-up. Starting from a pretrained single-robot world-action backbone,
the model learns from one robot’s views, state, and actions at a time, with roles, identities, and cross-agent
attention disabled. This adapts it to the cameras and controllers. In simulation, Warm-up also predicts the
shared global camera like a private view. (2) Team training. Initialized from the Warm-up weights, the model
learns from synchronized team demonstrations with per-robot streams, roles, randomized identities, group
shuffling, and cross-agent attention enabled.
Closed-loop joint control. At deployment, joint denoising produces all private-view futures and each robot’s
action chunk. Each controller executes its first 𝑅actions in lockstep with the others. These actions change the
scene for the whole team, and the updated observations and states condition the next joint sample.

4.1. Real-world collaboration from private views
Tasks and platform. We evaluate three collaborative tasks using two Galaxea R1 Pro robots: laundry-bag
collection, bath-towel folding, and umbrella handover. In laundry-bag collection, both robots must keep the
bag lifted and open while a garment is placed inside; success requires the garment to be inside the bag
at termination, with neither robot grasping it. Bath-towel folding requires coordinated manipulation of a
deformable object, whereas umbrella handover requires transferring support from one robot to the other. Each
robot has one ego/head-left camera and two wrist cameras. The model therefore receives six private camera
views, with no shared global camera, and must integrate their complementary observations to predict the
team’s future observations and actions.

cameras and controller. We then train on synchronized demonstrations of the robot pair, jointly supervising
actions and future observations in all six camera views. Umbrella handover uses giver/receiver roles;
laundry and towel roles follow the collection setup. Demonstrations are split at the synchronized-pair level
after camera, timing, and action checks. At deployment, the robots execute the first 8 of the 24 predicted

5. Conclusion
A robot team’s actions jointly create the world in which each member must act next. Mawam generates every
robot’s next action chunk and future camera views in one sample. On two real robots that see the scene only
through their own cameras, it is best on all three tasks with 67.8% mean success, 18.9 percentage points above
MA-VLA; in simulation, it averages 90.3% on eight tasks with two to four arms. Cross-agent attention and
team-stage future-video supervision both improve coordinated control: without attention, a four-arm relay
falls from 95.0% to 39.0% and real-robot mean success from 67.8% to 52.2%; without this supervision, Place
Food falls from 78.0% to 47.0%. Training one model to predict each robot’s next moves together with the
future those moves create is a practical basis for coordinated manipulation.
6. Limitations and Future Work
Our experiments are limited to fixed robot teams of up to four agents. One joint sample serves the whole
team, and going from two to four arms raises its time only from 1.40 s to 1.85 s (Appendix Table 11). Future
work could extend Mawam to larger teams, allow team membership to change during execution, and support
collaboration among robots with different embodiments and sensors. The predicted future observations and
actions could help people understand a team’s intended behavior before it acts. Extending joint prediction to
include human partners could also help robots anticipate human actions and coordinate with them.

B.2. Real-robot observation and control layout
Each R1 Pro contributes ego/head-left, left-wrist, and right-wrist video. Mounted cameras are agent-local even
when they show both robots. There are six private views, zero shared views, two semantic agents, and two
assigned simplex vertices sampled from the four-vertex pool described below. All three views of one robot use
the same agent phase and role while retaining different view embeddings, one per input position, initialized
from the Warm-up embeddings of the ego, left-wrist, and right-wrist views. The action-to-video bias and the
role embedding are broadcast to all three owned views.
The physical interface exposes 44 state and 37 command coordinates per robot. The learned action tokens
activate 16 logical coordinates: left/right 7D relative end-effector commands and two absolute grippers. The
two-agent physical packet is therefore 88D state / 74D command, while the learned active space is 2 × 16D.
The action adapter writes inactive physical fields from the live controller state. The reported real-robot policy
predicts 24-step action chunks, executes the first 8 steps, and replans from live observations. Frames use
the same offsets as in simulation: five observed frames spanning the last 0.8 s and eight future frames at
𝑡+ 3, . . . , 𝑡+ 24, a 0.2 s interval and a 1.6 s horizon at 15 Hz. This gives two generated latent frames per view
and twelve across the six views. The last future frame is the observation after the final step of the predicted
chunk, so the visual and action horizons cover the same 1.6 s.

D. Ablation configurations
The ablation results appear in Section 4.3 and Table 3. This section specifies the interventions and evaluation
quantities.
Team-stage future-video supervision. The No team-stage future-video supervision variant sets the future-video
loss weight to zero during team training. Everything else is unchanged: Warm-up initialization, demonstrations,
training budget, each robot’s action tokens, the future-view tokens, the attention masks, and the denoising of
all tokens at inference. This control therefore matches Mawam in tokens and test-time compute and differs
only in whether the future views are supervised in team training. Both models start from the same Warm-up
weights, trained with the future-video loss one robot at a time, so the comparison isolates supervision of the
team’s joint future views.
Test-time video generation. Frozen future keeps every future-view token at its initial noise level for all
denoising steps, so the token layout, attention masks, and random draws match the full model while no future
view is formed. No future tokens removes the future-view tokens from the sequence and denoises the actions
from the observed views, states, and action tokens alone. Every training sample contains future-view tokens, so
this second control also tests an input layout the model never saw. On the real robots, the laundry-bag model
takes 0.320 s per inference with the full model, 0.215 s without future tokens, and 0.328 s with a frozen future
on one H100.
Information access. Independent agents use shared weights and retain each robot’s private views plus the
read-only shared global camera, with no cross-agent private-token attention. The No shared global camera
variant removes the shared global camera during team training and evaluation. These interventions address
cross-agent token exchange and shared visual context, respectively.
Centralized team. The Centralized team baseline uses the same backbone, demonstrations, and team-stage
training budget as Mawam, but concatenates all robots’ views into one visual context and all arm actions
into one vector, without identity codes or role embeddings. Each arm keeps a fixed position in training and

Robots. We use two Galaxea R1 Pro robots per task. Each robot has two 7-DoF arms with two-finger grippers,
a 4-DoF torso, and a wheeled base; the base and torso are not commanded by the policy, and the policy sends
end-effector poses and gripper commands only. The two robots in any pair face each other.

wrist rotated ±90∘, which places both grippers about 0.42 m in front of the torso and 0.25 m to either side,

and bath-towel folding, the role vocabulary and mapping are defined by the collection protocol and fixed
before training. Before splitting the data, we store task identities in the pair metadata and exclude records
with missing source identifiers or required camera streams.
E.2. Post-training and deployment
On the real robots, Mawam training starts from GR00T 2 and uses the same two-stage recipe and world-action
objective as simulation. The single-robot Warm-up uses the same three private views and 16D logical action
definition as team training. Team training receives synchronized team trajectories and supervises the futures of
all six private views together with both robots’ actions. Both stages use the robot-mounted cameras without
introducing a shared global camera.
At inference, one joint sampling call with two denoising forwards generates private-view futures and both
robots’ action chunks from the synchronized six-view input. Before execution, we jointly validate timestamp
skew, ownership and role metadata, validity masks, finite command values, controller ranges, command age,
workspace limits, and collision status. A failed check triggers a coordinated stop for both robots.
E.3. Task success and safety accounting
Before every trial, both robots return to the fixed pose in Table 10. We randomize the initial object pose,
garment position, and folding direction within a certain range. Every model is evaluated for 30 trials per task.
The task-specific terminal success criteria are:
• Umbrella handover: the receiver stably holds the umbrella, and the giver has released it.
• Laundry-bag collection: the garment is inside the bag at termination, and neither robot still grasps it.
• Bath-towel folding: the towel is folded in the intended direction with its edges and corners aligned, and
both robots have released it.
We report the success rate with its standard error over 30 trials per task.
