WTR-Drive: World–Task Decomposition with Structured Risk
Supervision for End-to-End Driving
Haoyu Xing∗, Shaobing Ding, Guanglei Zhao, Guanhua Zhao, Nawu Peng, and Mingyang Hou
Abstract— Proposal-conditioned scene predictions can lack
a common object-level reference across candidate trajecto-
ries. Aggregate planning scores also leave the objects and
times associated with violations implicit. We present WTR-
Drive (World–Task–Risk Driving), a Drive-JEPA-based plan-
ner that separates shared scene estimation from proposal-
conditioned reasoning. A proposal-independent World Memory
estimates object states, map structure, and future motion
by re-reading the original visual features at each refinement
round. Proposal feedback updates a residual Task branch
without writing back to World Memory. This branch guides
refinement through candidate-specific retrieval and gated visual
feedback. A final Task readout supplies candidate-conditioned
features for scoring. We derive structured risk targets from
existing candidate-scoring rollouts without additional simulator
calls. For at-fault collision and time-to-collision violations,
the targets encode event existence, association with shared
object slots, and the first violation time bin. On the NAVSIM
navtest split, WTR-Drive achieves 94.66 PDMS on v1 and
88.24 EPDMS on v2 (expert-frame sequential evaluation).
These scores are 0.96 and 0.44 points above the published
Drive-JEPA values, respectively. Candidate-level analysis shows
that higher best-candidate scores can coexist with lower
selected-trajectory scores, revealing a gap between candi-
date quality and selection effectiveness. Code is available at
https://github.com/xingxing9013605/WTR-Drive.
I. INTRODUCTION
Candidate trajectories represent alternative actions in the
same observed scene. Joint perception–planning systems
use intermediate scene representations [1], [2]. Drive-JEPA
combines video pretraining with multimodal trajectory distil-
lation [3]. iPad [4] retrieves visual features around candidate
waypoints. However, proposal-conditioned predictions can
assign different states to the same object across candidates.
Our approach uses a shared estimate of object states, map
structure, and object motion for all candidates. Each can-
didate then reasons about object relevance and potential
violations along its path.
For example, a vehicle may lie outside a lane-keeping
trajectory yet intersect a lane-change trajectory. Aggregate
planning scores summarize trajectory quality but leave the
involved object and first violation time implicit. Explicit ob-
ject and time labels supervise candidate–object interactions
and help diagnose object-association and event-timing errors.
iPad and Drive-JEPA already use mapping and collision-
related supervision [4], [3]. We extend this supervision to

II. RELATED WORK
We review scene representations, proposal conditioning,
and simulation-based risk supervision.
A. Multimodal Planning and Trajectory Selection
UniAD [1] and VAD [2] connect intermediate scene
representations to planning. Multimodal planners differ in
candidate construction and selection: Hydra-MDP [6] distills
human imitation and rule-based scores over a trajectory
vocabulary; DiffusionDrive [7] refines multimodal anchors
through truncated diffusion; GoalFlow [8] combines goal-
conditioned flow matching with goal and trajectory scoring.
DriveSuprim [9] couples progressive filtering with learned
scoring. iPad [4] aligns visual reads with candidate way-
points. WTR-Drive retains Drive-JEPA’s [3] pretrained en-
coder and multimodal training, while changing the scene
features used for refinement and scoring.
B. Predictive Representations and Driving World Models
V-JEPA [10] learns video representations through feature
prediction. Generative world models synthesize future driv-
ing video (GAIA-1 [11], DriveDreamer [12], Vista [13]),
and OccWorld [14] forecasts 3-D occupancy. Drive-WM [15]
generates maneuver-conditioned multiview futures and eval-
uates plans with image-based rewards. LAW [16] pre-
dicts future latent features conditioned on ego plans, while
World4Drive [17] predicts intention-dependent future la-
tents for candidate evaluation. WTR-Drive instead estimates
shared scene and object motion from observations, and
represents proposal-dependent interactions in Task Memory.
C. Structured Scene Queries and Planning Interaction
DETR3D [18] retrieves multi-view features through 3-D
references. BEVFormer [19] and MapTR [20] organize scene
features through BEV queries and structured map elements,
respectively. SparseDrive [21] links sparse perception, pre-
diction, and planning, while DriveTransformer [22] allows
direct interaction among agent, map, and planning queries.
WTR-Drive separates World updates from proposal feed-
back: World slots re-read the original visual features, and a
residual Task branch carries proposal-dependent interactions
without writing back to World Memory.
D. Simulation-Based Supervision and Structured Risk
nuPlan [23] evaluates repeated closed-loop interaction,
whereas NAVSIM [5] uses short-horizon non-reactive simu-
lation. PDM-style criteria [24], [5] also provide candidate-
score supervision [6], [9]. DROID [25] identifies objects
influencing driver behavior through response supervision and
object-removal interventions. RiskWorld [26] predicts object
risk through latent ego–object relation rollouts supervised
by logged futures. iPad [4] supervises passability and agent
futures at the proposal level, and Drive-JEPA [3] adds
mapping and collision targets to the same proposal-centric

III. METHOD
A. Problem Setup and Overview
Given multi-camera images and ego status, we predict P
candidate trajectories with T future poses (x, y, θ) and select
one by its predicted score. We build on Drive-JEPA [3],
which supplies the pretrained encoder, proposal initialization,
trajectory decoder, BEV refiner, proposal exchange, and
multimodal training. Our additions are the World/Task split
over I shared-parameter rounds (Fig. 2) and the structured
risk heads. Table I lists the notation; the batch dimension is
omitted.
We write MHAk(X; Y) for multihead-attention output
with query input X and key/value input Y. Blockk(X; Y)
additionally includes residual connections, layer normaliza-
tion, and a feed-forward network. The subscript identifies the
module. For self-attention, both inputs are the same.
B. Scene-Query World Modeling
The World Memory concatenates object and fixed-grid map
slots, W = [Wobj; Wmap]. Each round updates W(i) =
W(W(i−1), F0) from the original visual features without
direct proposal feedback; gradients still jointly train the
complete model. Scene-level targets supervise object states,
map semantics, and a time-conditioned object-motion head.
Thus, world modeling denotes scene estimation and motion
prediction: refinement revisits the same observations without
physical-time advance or a learned latent transition. Shared
object slots anchor correspondence across candidates, whose
attention weights and risk labels can differ.

slots. Object references follow current World box estimates;

E. Training Objective
We jointly fine-tune the encoder and downstream modules
with trajectory, score, scene, and risk losses. Best-of-N
trajectory supervision uses human demonstrations (weight 1)
and up to K = 4 simulator-scored pseudo-teachers (weight
0.5).
Each scoring head directly predicts a final score and five
component scores. Simulator targets supervise these with
binary cross-entropy (BCE): the final-score term has weight
1, and mean component BCE has weight 0.1. Components
are no-collision, drivable-area compliance, progress, TTC,
and comfort (C for v1; history comfort, HC, for v2). V1
averages the losses from its two heads.
Where present, inherited predicted-agent classification,
regression, and area terms retain coefficients 1/0.1/2. Auxil-
iary agent classification, box, and BEV-semantic terms retain
1/0.1/1. The added object classification, box, and future-
pose terms use 0.05, 0.01, and 0.02. Dense-map and slot-
map terms use 0.05 and 0.0125. Risk existence, association,
and time terms use 0.10, 0.05, and 0.05, respectively.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Evaluation Protocol
We evaluate on 12,146 NAVSIM navtest scenes [5] with
expert trajectories, reporting PDMS on v1 and EPDMS on
v2. Our v2 score uses Drive-JEPA’s expert-frame sequential
evaluation without human penalty filter (HPF) correction.
Published results retain their original protocols. Our v2
evaluation does not measure full two-stage pseudo-closed-
loop performance.
Component metrics follow NAVSIM [5]: NC (no at-fault
collisions), DAC (drivable area), DDC (driving direction),
TLC (traffic lights), EP (progress), TTC, C (comfort), LK
(lane keeping), HC (history comfort), and EC (extended
comfort).
EPDMSH applies HPF correction [27]. On a 0–1 scale, it
multiplies NC, DAC, DDC, and TLC by the weighted mean
of EP, TTC, LK, HC, and EC (weights 5, 5, 2, 2, 2), omitting
unavailable terms, and reports 100 times the mean over
non-missing expert-frame scores, separately from published
EPDMS. Differences use unrounded values; scores show two
decimals.
Candidate diagnostics during training use a distinct 512-
scene validation subset.
B. Implementation Details
A V-JEPA ViT-L encodes two frames from each of the left,
front, and right cameras at 256 × 512 resolution; projection
to d = 256 forms F0. We use P
= 32 candidates,
T = 8 poses at 0.5 s intervals, I = 4 shared-parameter
rounds, 32 object slots, and 8 × 8 map slots. Spatial ranges
are [−8, 56] × [−32, 32] × [−2, 6] m for scene queries and
[0, 56] × [−32, 32] m for the map. The optional global visual
attention in Proposal Interaction is disabled; World global
attention and BEV-refiner visual attention remain active.
We train for 50 epochs with Adam, a learning rate of
10−4, a 0.1 backbone multiplier, and mixed precision. The
global batch size is 576 (9 nodes, 16 devices per node,
and 4 samples per device), with no gradient accumulation.
A plateau schedule monitors the validation score. V1 clips
gradients at 1, whereas v2 uses no gradient clipping. Both
single-seed runs use seed 0; validation selects zero-based
epochs 41 and 47 for v1 and v2, respectively.
Inference Protocol: V1 averages the probabilities of two
scoring heads; v2 uses one. Sequential v2 inference inherits
the momentum correction ssel = (14spred+2cEC)/16 when a
valid preceding trajectory is available, where cEC is extended
comfort. Otherwise, or during training-time validation, se-
lection uses spred directly. We select the highest-scoring
candidate.

F. World–Task Representations and Structured Risk
We export World, Task, and risk outputs for all 32 candi-
dates in eight scenes; selected trajectories and scores match
the paired comparison. Risk diagnostics use the training roll-
out, scene matching, and validity masks. Association-label
coverage is 71.24%/74.67% at the selected v1/v2 epochs, i.e.,
the fraction of positive candidate–event pairs with known
object identities that map to matched slots, averaged over
batches rather than held-out accuracy.
Fig. 5 shows different responses within one scene. The
predicted map in (e), compared with GT in (f), captures
road regions but misses centerlines. Q14 denotes the same
object slot throughout (zero-based index 14). Under the
rollout criteria, the selected trajectory in (b), (g) has no at-
fault collision or TTC violation. For the collision in (c),
(h), the model assigns probability 0.84, identifies Q14, and
predicts the correct time bin. The collision in (d), (i) has
probability 0.20, below threshold 0.50, and an incorrect
object association. The event footprints come from the label
rollout and cannot validate learned motion forecasts.
Heatmaps and object-edge colors show attention over 64
map and 32 object slots, normalized jointly and averaged
over heads and trajectory times. Q14 receives 0.39% for the
selected plan, 0.22% for the detected collision, and 0.51%
for the missed collision, so correct detection occurs at the
smallest weight. Attention indicates feature retrieval, not a
calibrated collision probability.
The visualized failures motivate separate evaluation of
localization, forecasting, association, calibration, and event
timing. Matched ablations, repeated seeds, and efficiency
measurements remain outstanding. A matched risk-loss con-
trol would zero the three structured-risk weights while re-
taining subscore supervision. Reusing scoring rollouts avoids
additional simulation, though the added modules still incur
computation.
V. CONCLUSION
WTR-Drive combines shared scene estimation, residual
Task conditioning, and object/time risk supervision within
Drive-JEPA, improving on published Drive-JEPA scores on
NAVSIM v1 and v2. Diagnostics distinguish scene ground-
ing, trajectory refinement, and final selection, and a higher
best-candidate simulator score need not raise the selected
score. Future work will improve centerline geometry, object
localization, motion prediction, and track-to-slot association.
