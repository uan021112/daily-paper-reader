Abstract
Robot policy pretraining based on human videos is crucial for improving the
policy’s generalization ability. One main challenge for this task is the lack of
explicit action-relevant representations in such unlabeled data. Recent works tend to
estimate 3D hand motion trajectories from videos using 3D foundation models (3D
FMs). However, they keep solely the sparse trajectories as supervision, ignoring
the rich, fine-grained information produced during the trajectory extraction process.
To address this issue, we propose LSA, a hierarchical robot policy pretraining
framework that includes three phases: Lift, See, and Act. Specifically, the Lift
phase elevates 2D video observations into dense 3D representations by adaptively
aligning features in the policy’s shallow layers with the intermediate representations
extracted from multiple 3D FMs. Building upon these lifted representations, the
See phase equips the policy with explicit geometric and interactive awareness
through dual guidance. It introduces depth-map reconstruction to help the model
comprehend 3D spatial layouts and utilizes hand region cues to explicitly supervise
the encoder’s attention toward task-relevant interaction hotspots. Empowered
by the dense 3D representation learning and precise spatial guidance, our policy
achieves robust and accurate robotic manipulation in the Act phase. Extensive
experiments on diverse simulation and real-world tasks demonstrate that LSA
significantly outperforms current state-of-the-art approaches. The project page is
available at: https://yiyuan00.github.io/Lift-See-Act.

Introduction
Large-scale pretraining is crucial for improving the generalization ability of machine learning models
[1, 2]. In robotics, pretraining is equally important for policy learning, since high-quality manipulation
data are expensive to collect and therefore difficult to scale up. Human videos are a key source of
pretraining data, as they cover a wide range of real-world manipulation scenarios and contain rich
manipulation knowledge [3, 4]. However, using human videos to pretrain robot policies remains a
challenge, as such data inherently lack action-relevant representations [5].
Existing methods for extracting explicit action-relevant representations from human videos can
be divided into two categories. The first category of methods extracts 2D visual primitives from
video frames, such as contact points [6, 7], contact heatmaps [8], and object bounding boxes
[9], to characterize where contact occurs and which regions are potentially involved in manipu-
lation. However, these methods lack temporal representations of the 3D manipulation process.
The second category of methods attempts to estimate 3D hand motion trajectories from videos
[10–14]. These trajectories reflect both the displacement of the hand in the 3D space and the

representations into 3D, endowing the policy with more fine-grained geometric knowledge.
Building upon these lifted representations, the See phase equips the policy with explicit geometric
and interactive awareness. While the Lift phase establishes a dense 3D representation space, these
representations are implicit. Robot manipulation inherently requires an intuitive and precise under-
standing of the structure of the physical scene, along with a distinct visual focus on the manipulation
objectives. To achieve this, the See phase provides dual guidance: explicit structural guidance and
attentive guidance. First, leveraging the extracted depth maps, we introduce depth map reconstruction
as an auxiliary task, providing explicit 3D geometric constraints to enable the model to comprehend
3D spatial layouts. Second, we utilize the hand region as a cue to guide the encoder’s attention
toward task-relevant interaction hotspots. Specifically, we encode 2D hand keypoints into a Gaussian
heatmap to represent the hand region. We then select the attention map most similar to this heatmap
across all heads in the shallow layers as our target, and finally use it to supervise the mean attention
of all heads within a layer. The above dual guidance ensures the policy explicitly Sees the 3D scene
structure and concentrates on the task-relevant interaction regions.
To the best of our knowledge, LSA is the first approach to leverage multi-tiered 3D information
extracted from 3D foundation models for hierarchical robot policy pretraining on human videos.
Empowered by the dense 3D representation learning in the Lift phase and the dual guidance in the
See phase, our policy predicts 3D hand motion trajectories in the Act phase, ultimately achieving
robust and accurate robot manipulation. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our
design and show that LSA outperforms state-of-the-art methods on various simulation and real-world
tasks.

Related Works
Existing approaches to pretraining robot policies from human videos often construct action-relevant
representations to bridge the gap between passive visual observations and robot control. Based on the
space in which these representations are extracted, existing approaches can be broadly categorized
into 2D and 3D methods.
2


2D Methods. The first category of methods constructs action-relevant representations within the 2D
image space, which can be further divided into implicit and explicit methods. Implicit methods model
inter-frame relationships to extract abstract state representations. One line of work uses contrastive
learning [19, 20] to constrain the distance between visual embeddings across different time periods,
capturing the temporal dynamics of visual observations (i.e., how the current state transitions to the
next). To characterize these state transitions more precisely, other approaches learn discrete latent
actions between frames through VQ-VAE objectives [21], subsequently using these latent actions
as pseudo-action labels for unannotated videos [3, 22, 23]. Recently, some methods train video
prediction models [24–26, 4] to extract predictive visual representations, providing embodied agents
with dense information about future states.
In contrast, explicit methods directly extract 2D visual primitives at the pixel level. These approaches
typically identify contact points [6, 7], contact heatmaps [8], or bounding boxes [9, 27] to delineate
potential interaction regions. Furthermore, extracting human hand poses from video streams serves
as a simple but scalable approach to represent the interaction trajectories [7, 28].
3D Methods. The above 2D representations fundamentally lack explicit depth information, limiting
their ability to capture inherently 3D physical interactions. To address this limitation, the second cate-
gory of methods turns to explicit 3D action-relevant representations, such as 3D hand trajectories [12]
and parametric hand poses (e.g., MANO [29]) [30, 6]. Due to their 3D nature, these representations
align more tightly with the robotic action space.
These 3D methods can be further divided into two groups according to their data requirements. The
first group uses videos equipped with annotated 3D hand poses and camera parameters, typically
captured through AR/VR devices or RGB-D SLAM [31]. They directly adopt these ground-truth
3D hand poses as supervision for the pretraining of robot policies [30, 10, 32, 33]. However, these
videos are recorded in relatively controlled settings, which affects the scale of the video databases.
In comparison, the second group constructs 3D representations directly from unlabeled, in-the-wild
videos. Early efforts employ structure-from-motion and depth estimation models to recover camera
intrinsics and metric depth. They then combine these estimations with continuous 2D hand poses
to obtain 3D hand motion trajectories [11, 34]. Recently, the rapid advancement of 3D Foundation
Models (FMs) [15, 17, 16] has enabled the extraction of 3D hand trajectories with substantially
improved fidelity and efficiency [12, 10].
In this paper, we also use 3D FMs to construct action-relevant representations. Unlike trajectory-
based methods that focus exclusively on 3D hand trajectories and Spatial Forcing [35], which
distills dense geometric features from a single 3D FM, we extract hierarchical representations (e.g.,
geometric features and depth maps) from multiple 3D FMs during inference, providing multi-level
3D supervision for robot policy pretraining.

3.1
Problem Formulation and LSA Overview

As highlighted in Section 1, relying solely on estimated hand trajectories for robot policy pretraining
wastes the fine-grained geometric knowledge inherently produced by 3D Foundation Models (3D
FMs). To make the best use of this multi-tier knowledge, we propose LSA, a hierarchical pretraining
framework consisting of three distinct phases: Lift, See, and Act. First, the Lift phase elevates
2D video observations into a dense 3D representation space by aligning the policy’s shallow-layer
features with intermediate representations from 3D FMs. Building upon this, the See phase equips
the policy with explicit geometric and interactive awareness through structural and attentive dual
guidance. Finally, empowered by these representations, the Act phase translates visual inputs into 3D
hand motion trajectories for robust manipulation.
3.2
The Lift Phase: 3D Representation Elevation
Data Engine. To extract 3D hand motion trajectories, we develop a data engine using multiple 3D
FMs. Given an input video frame, we first extract the 2D wrist coordinates pw. Subsequently, we
employ VGGT [15] to estimate camera intrinsics K, and Depth Pro [16] to predict a metric depth map
Dg. The intermediate dense features extracted from VGGT and Depth Pro are denoted by Ec and
Ed, respectively. Using K and Dg, we lift the 2D wrist coordinates pw into the 3D space. Finally,
we conduct necessary data cleaning to obtain high-quality pretraining data. Details of data cleaning
and 3D lifting are provided in Appendix A.6.
Dense Representation Alignment. Geometric representations from 3D FMs have been shown
to benefit robot policy learning [35]. Given an input image Ih ∈RH×W ×3, the vision encoder
extracts visual features from it. Let Xa ∈RNa×Da denote the visual tokens from the l-th layer of
the encoder. To distill the fine-grained geometric knowledge of the two 3D FMs into the policy, we
align the shallow-layer visual tokens Xa with their geometric representations Ec ∈RNc×Dc and
Ed ∈RNd×Dd, respectively.

3.3
The See Phase: Dual Attentive and Structural Guidance
Although the Lift phase establishes a dense 3D representation space, these features remain largely
implicit. Robot manipulation inherently requires an intuitive understanding of the structure of the
physical scene, alongside a distinct visual focus on the manipulating agent and the target object. To
achieve this, the See phase provides dual guidance.
Attentive Guidance. Learning affordance knowledge from human videos benefits policy pretrain-
ing [8, 6]. To further improve the policy’s perception of hand-centric interaction regions, we design
an attentive guidance mechanism. Specifically, we first warm up the LSA visual encoder for several
epochs with the motion planning objective, allowing initial task-relevant attention patterns to emerge
(see Appendix A.5). Given the detected hand keypoints, we generate a Gaussian heatmap and
downsample it to the patch resolution, obtaining a spatial hand prior h ∈RNa, where Na denotes the
number of spatial patches. For each head in the first R layers, we extract the [CLS]-to-patch attention

Explicit Structural Guidance. To provide explicit 3D geometric constraints and enable the model to
comprehend 3D spatial layouts more precisely, we introduce depth-map reconstruction as an auxiliary
task. We utilize the image patch tokens from the m-th layer of the encoder, denoted as Xm, which
are processed by a transformer decoder to regress the depth map. This reconstruction is supervised by
the metric depth map Dg generated during the data engine stage, and the reconstruction loss Lrecon
is computed using mean squared error (MSE).
3.4
The Act Phase: Motion Planning
Empowered by the dense 3D representation learning in the Lift phase and the precise dual guidance
in the See phase, the Act phase predicts the 3D hand motion trajectory. For a training sample with
L + 1 sequential frames, we first lift the wrist locations into 3D space using the estimated camera
intrinsics and the metric depth map, and construct the ground-truth future trajectory T ∈RL×3 from
the subsequent L frames. We then average-pool the final-layer visual tokens of the initial frame into
a global visual embedding, which is fed into a single-layer MLP prediction head to predict the future
trajectory ˆT ∈RL×3. Finally, the motion planning objective Lmotion is optimized by minimizing the
ℓ1 distance between ˆT and T. By grounding action prediction in these rich 3D priors and refined
attention, the policy acquires robust, generalized motion planning capabilities, establishing a strong
foundation for downstream robot control.
4
Simulation Experiments
4.1
Setup
Evaluation Setup. We evaluate LSA on three simulation benchmarks: MetaWorld [41], DexArt [42],
and Adroit [43]. MetaWorld evaluates tabletop manipulation with a Sawyer arm and gripper, while
DexArt and Adroit focus on dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands. For MetaWorld, we
use eight tasks covering different difficulty levels: hammer, dial turn, assembly, hand insert, pick
place wall, shelf place, disassemble, and stick pull. For DexArt, we evaluate on bucket and toilet;
for Adroit, we evaluate on door, hammer, and pen. Following prior methods, the pretrained visual
encoder is frozen and combined with a lightweight behavior cloning policy head. We report success
rates averaged over three random seeds. Detailed data collection, training, and evaluation protocols
are provided in Appendix B.1.
Baselines. We compare LSA with three groups of baselines. First, we include general-purpose visual
representations, including CLIP [44] and DINOv3 [45]. Second, we compare with representative
robot visual representation pretraining methods, including MCR [46], VIP [47], R3M [19], VC-1 [48],
HRP [8], MPI [9], and R3M-A [37]. Third, we include baselines that directly take 3D point clouds as
inputs, including DP3 [49] and KAN-We-Flow [50]. For VIP, we additionally provide cropped target
images following its original protocol. More baseline details are provided in Appendix B.2.

As shown in Table 1, LSA obtains consistently strong results across MetaWorld, DexArt, and

Compared with direct 3D policy baselines such as DP3 [49] and KAN-We-Flow [50], LSA attains

that the proposed pretraining effectively injects useful 3D-aware representations into the visual
encoder. We further evaluate robustness by reducing image brightness by 40% and adding random
colored circular occlusions during testing. Tab. 2 shows that LSA suffers smaller drops under these
visual perturbations, decreasing by only 7.5% under brightness reduction and 18.7% under visual
disturbance. In contrast, the compared baselines show much larger degradation, indicating that LSA
is more robust to superficial appearance changes.

Analysis of LSA Variants. Tab. 4 analyzes key design choices in LSA. For motion supervision,
using only 2D hand motion achieves 77.3%, slightly below the 3D motion planning baseline of
81.0%. However, when the model receives 3D geometric supervision in the Lift and See phases
but uses 2D trajectory prediction in the Act phase, the performance drops further. We attribute this
to the semantic inconsistency between the lifted 3D representation space and the image-plane 2D
motion objective. Using only VGGT or Depth Pro for representation alignment is inferior to using
both, indicating that different 3D FMs provide complementary dense geometric cues. Removing
dynamic alignment weights or conditioning them on a single layer also degrades performance,
showing the need for adaptive multi-teacher weighting with multi-layer geometric context. For
attentive guidance, supervising the attention of all shallow heads suppresses attention diversity and
thus degrades performance. In addition, using a random head, the worst-matching head, or the hand
heatmap as the supervision target also reduces performance, indicating that the internally emerged
attention target selected by the hand heatmap more effectively captures interaction regions.

VGGT depth variants, e.g., 0.0364 m vs. 0.1155 m on DexYCB. When replacing only the Act-phase
trajectory labels, noisier VGGT-lifted trajectories reduce SR from 92.2% to 88.6%, while DexYCB
GT trajectories only improve SR to 93.5%. The limited improvement from replacing our pseudo-
trajectories with GT trajectories suggests that the adopted Depth Pro+VGGT pipeline provides
reasonably reliable supervision in our setting. We also validate the effectiveness of our labeling and
pretraining strategy on more diverse unlabeled datasets, such as Ego4D (Appendix C.5).

Real-World Experiments
5.1
Setup
We further evaluate LSA in real-world manipulation using a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 robot equipped
with a 1-DoF parallel gripper. RGB observations are captured by a RealSense D435i camera. As
shown in Fig. 4, we consider four daily-scenario manipulation tasks: Wipe PC, Pick Trash, Place Toy,
and Pluck Flower. For each task, we collect 30 demonstrations with variations in the initial poses of
the robot and objects. We evaluate each policy over ten trials per task and report the success rate (%).
To evaluate out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, we further construct two unseen variants of
Wipe PC. The policy is trained with one towel color and tested with two previously unseen towel
colors, denoted as unseen1 and unseen2. This setting tests whether the learned representation can
generalize beyond superficial appearance changes. More implementation details are provided in
Appendix B.4.
5.2
Evaluation Results
Tab. 6 shows that LSA achieves the highest average success rate among all compared methods, with
68.3% vs. 41.7% for the strongest baseline HRP. LSA outperforms the second-best method on Pick
Trash (70.0% vs. 40.0%), Place Toy (80.0% vs. 60.0%), and Wipe PC (80.0% vs. 50.0%), while
matching the best result on Pluck Flower (50.0%). On the two OOD Wipe PC settings, LSA obtains
70.0% and 60.0%, whereas the best baselines achieve 20.0% and 40.0%; notably, R3M-A drops to
0.0% on Wipe PC U1. These results suggest that LSA transfers better to real-world manipulation.
On one hand, its human-video pretraining learns 3D-aware representations from 2D observations,
reducing the burden of acquiring spatial relations from limited robot demonstrations. On the other
hand, its interaction-focused guidance helps the policy preserve task-relevant hand-object relations
under appearance changes.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we propose LSA, a hierarchical robot policy pretraining framework that systematically
decomposes the learning process into three synergistic phases: Lift, See, and Act. Unlike prior
works that rely solely on sparse 3D trajectories, LSA fully exploits the multi-tiered knowledge of
3D FMs by aligning policy features with dense, intermediate geometric representations. To bridge
9


the gap between implicit features and physical execution, we introduce a dual-guidance mechanism
in the See phase, combining explicit structural reconstruction with attentive guidance to focus on
task-relevant regions. Finally, the Act phase grounds the learned representation in future 3D hand
motion, which serves as an action-relevant proxy for downstream robot manipulation. Limitations.
LSA shares several limitations inherent to human-video-based robot pretraining methods due to the
human-robot embodiment gap. Broader Impacts. Looking forward, this work opens avenues for
scaling multi-tiered 3D knowledge extraction from human videos to broader robot manipulation
scenarios, with the potential to further enhance Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model pretraining.
To the best of our knowledge, this work has no obvious negative social impacts.

A.4
More Details of Depth Reconstruction
The decoder takes the 196 patch tokens from encoder layer m = 6, with shape B × 196 × 768,
corresponding to a 14 × 14 spatial grid. Each token is projected from 768 to 512 dimensions and
augmented with a fixed 2D sine–cosine positional embedding. The resulting sequence is processed by
eight self-attention Transformer blocks with 8 heads, preserving its shape of B × 196 × 512. A linear
head maps each token to 256 depth values representing one 16 × 16 patch, producing B × 196 × 256.
A non-overlapping Fold operation with kernel size and stride 16 assembles these patches into a
B × 1 × 224 × 224 depth map. Thus, depth is predicted patchwise and supervised pixelwise over the
full 224 × 224 resolution.

A.8
Pretraining Implementation Details
We adopt a pretrained ViT-B [52] initialized on ImageNet [53] as the visual encoder. To enable
parameter-efficient adaptation, we keep the original ViT-B parameters frozen and apply LoRA [54]
with rank r = 8 and scaling factor α = 16. Following our hierarchical design, we perform
representation alignment at the l = 3 layer of the visual encoder, while the visual tokens from the
m = 6 layer are routed to an 8-layer Transformer decoder for metric depth reconstruction. Attentive
guidance is applied to the first R = 6 layers. Attentive guidance is enabled after a 10-epoch warm-up
with the motion planning objective, so that the model can first develop initial task-relevant attention
patterns before the target attention map is selected. For the Act phase, the model predicts the future
3D wrist trajectory for the next L = 3 frames.
Following prior work [55], we use DexYCB [56] to pretrain LSA. After data cleaning, we retain
20K video clips for pretraining. Each clip consists of consecutive frames that depict human hands
performing tasks and moving within the scene. Optimization is performed using Adam with a cosine
learning rate schedule and an initial learning rate of 5 × 10−4. The model is trained for 50 epochs on
NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with 24GB memory each, using a batch size of 16 per GPU.

Experimental Protocols
This section provides detailed experimental protocols for simulation benchmarks, baseline implemen-
tations, 3D wrist lifting evaluation, and real-world experiments.
B.1
Simulation Evaluation Setup
We provide detailed simulation settings for the three benchmarks used in the main paper: Meta-
World [41], DexArt [42], and Adroit [43].
18


MetaWorld. MetaWorld provides tabletop manipulation tasks with a Sawyer arm and gripper. We
select eight tasks spanning different difficulty levels [36]: regular tasks including hammer and dial
turn, hard tasks including assembly and hand insert, and very hard tasks including pick place wall,
shelf place, disassemble, and stick pull. For each task, we use scripted policies to collect 25 expert
demonstrations. Following prior works [57, 58], the maximum rollout length is strictly limited to
175 steps, which makes the setting more challenging by requiring faster and more accurate task
completion. The behavior cloning policy is trained for 20,000 steps with a batch size of 32 and
evaluated every 2,000 steps.
DexArt. DexArt focuses on dexterous manipulation of articulated objects using an XArm6 arm and
an Allegro hand. We evaluate on two tasks: bucket and toilet. For each task, we collect 100 expert
demonstrations using pretrained reinforcement learning policies. Each rollout is capped at 100 steps.
The behavior cloning policy is trained for 3,000 epochs with a batch size of 64 and evaluated every
200 epochs. The pretrained visual encoder is frozen during downstream policy learning, and only the
policy head is trained.
Adroit. Adroit simulates a multi-fingered Shadow Hand in MuJoCo [59] and contains contact-rich
dexterous manipulation tasks involving rigid and articulated objects. We evaluate on three tasks: door,
hammer, and pen. For each task, we collect 10 expert demonstrations using a pretrained reinforcement
learning policy [60]. Each demonstration contains 100 steps. The training and evaluation protocols
follow the DexArt setting. We include this benchmark to further evaluate whether LSA can transfer
to complex dexterous manipulation with fine-grained hand-object interactions.
Reporting Protocol. For each evaluation checkpoint, we compute the success rate over all rollouts.
For each random seed, we average the success rates of the top-3 performing checkpoints. We then
report the mean and sample standard deviation over three random seeds, rounded to one decimal
place.
B.2
Baseline Details
We compare LSA with three categories of baselines.
General-purpose Visual Representations. We include CLIP [44] and DINOv3 [45] as strong
general visual representation baselines. CLIP is a vision-language model trained with contrastive
image-text supervision, which learns transferable semantic visual features from large-scale image-
caption pairs. DINOv3 is a self-supervised vision foundation model that learns strong image-level
and dense visual features without manual labels. Although both models provide powerful general-
purpose representations, their pretraining objectives are not explicitly designed for action-relevant 3D
reasoning or robot manipulation.
Robot Visual Pretraining Methods. We compare with MCR [46], VIP [47], R3M [19], VC-1 [48],
HRP [8], MPI [9], and R3M-A [37]. These methods represent strong robot-oriented visual pretraining
baselines, covering different ways of learning action-relevant representations for downstream robotic
control.
Direct 3D Policies. We further compare with DP3 [49] and KAN-We-Flow [50], both of which
directly take 3D point clouds as policy inputs and learn action prediction through diffusion- or flow-
matching-based objectives. These methods provide direct comparisons to LSA from the perspective
of 3D-aware policy learning. Following Lift3D [61], we compare the 2D and 3D policies under the
same evaluation protocol, using 224 × 224 RGB images and single-view point clouds with 1,024
points as inputs, respectively, while retaining the original method-specific settings of the 3D policies.
For all 2D baselines, we use the same downstream demonstrations. The pretrained visual encoder is
frozen and combined with the same lightweight behavior cloning policy head. The policy head is
implemented as a 3-layer MLP with hidden size 256, following prior works [46, 8]. We apply the
reporting protocol used for LSA to all 2D baselines.
B.3
3D Wrist Lifting Evaluation Set Construction
To quantify the noise of the lifted 3D wrist trajectories, we construct two small evaluation sets from
H2O [62] and DexYCB [56], respectively. For each dataset, we randomly sample 240 valid wrist
samples from 12 scenes, covering either left or right hands. Both datasets provide calibrated cameras
19


and ground-truth 3D hand poses. For each sample, we use the ground-truth 2D wrist projection as the
wrist location, estimate depth and camera intrinsics from different sources, and lift the wrist point to
the camera coordinate system. Following [63], we report the 3D Average Displacement Error (ADE)
between the lifted wrist and the ground-truth 3D wrist.
B.4
Real-World Implementation Details
Hardware Setup. We conduct real-world experiments using a 7-DoF Franka Research 3 robot
equipped with a 1-DoF parallel gripper. A RealSense D435i camera is used to capture RGB
observations from a fixed third-person viewpoint. During data collection, we record robot states and
RGB observations at 16 Hz.
Demonstration Collection. For each task, we collect 30 teleoperated demonstrations. The demon-
strations include variations in the initial poses of the robot and objects to avoid learning a fixed
execution trajectory. The four real-world tasks are defined as follows:
• Wipe PC: the robot grasps a pink towel and wipes the surface of a laptop.
• Pick Trash: the robot picks up a piece of trash and drops it into a trash bin.
• Place Toy: the robot grasps a toy and places it into a basket.
• Pluck Flower: the robot grasps a flower and lifts it upward.
Policy Training. Following prior real-world policy evaluation protocols, we freeze the pretrained
visual encoder and train only the downstream policy head. The policy head is a 3-layer MLP with
two hidden layers of size 512. We train the policy using a log-likelihood objective with a learning
rate of 1 × 10−4 and a batch size of 128 for 50k steps. The predicted actions are linearly interpolated
to 1000 Hz to satisfy the Franka control frequency.
Evaluation Protocol. For each task, we evaluate each policy over 10 independent trials and report
the success rate. A trial is considered successful if the robot completes the target manipulation goal
without human intervention. For the OOD variants of Wipe PC, the policy is trained with a pink
towel and evaluated with two unseen towel colors, purple and yellow. This setting changes the object
appearance while preserving the same task goal and interaction structure.

C.6
Hyperparameter Selection
As shown in Fig. 9, we investigate the optimal configuration of three critical hyperparameters: the
depth of attentive guidance R, the number of motion planning steps L, and the number of depth
reconstruction decoder layers C. For attentive guidance, applying it to the first R = 6 layers achieves
the highest success rate of 92.2%. Restricting guidance to only the first two layers (R = 2) lowers
the success rate to 85.8%, because this limited supervision makes it difficult to identify a reliable
target attention map and leaves other optimizable shallow layers unguided. In contrast, extending
it too deeply (R = 8) reduces performance to 87.8%, as it limits the ability of deeper layers to
learn abstract task semantics. For motion planning, L = 3 yields the best performance. A shorter
horizon (L = 1) severely degrades the success rate to 78.3%, because such a short horizon lacks
sufficient temporal context to represent meaningful hand motion. In contrast, a longer horizon (L = 7)
reduces performance to 85.0%, since predicting farther-future trajectories becomes overly difficult
and inaccurate. Finally, an 8-layer depth decoder (C = 8) best balances model capacity and training
stability. A shallower decoder (C = 2) leads to a lower success rate of 83.8%, because its limited
capacity is insufficient to reconstruct dense and accurate depth maps. In contrast, an overly deep
decoder (C = 16) also reduces performance to 83.8%, as it introduces optimization difficulties that
hinder overall training. Therefore, we adopt R = 6, L = 3, and C = 8 as our default setup.

D
Further Discussion on Limitations
Embodiment Differences.
LSA adopts future 3D wrist trajectories as action proxies primarily to
bypass the embodiment gap between humans and robots. In our preliminary experiments, additionally
supervising with fine-grained 3D hand meshes, such as MANO [29], degraded performance compared
with using wrist trajectories alone. We attribute this to the structural mismatch between human fingers
and robot end-effectors, such as parallel grippers. Embodiment-specific action representations impose
an additional burden on the downstream policy rather than providing transferable guidance. A
promising solution is to extract latent actions from human hand motion, so that the supervision
captures high-level motion intent rather than embodiment-specific action forms.
Object Interaction Discrepancies.
Although LSA’s attentive guidance effectively transfers region-
level attention from human hand–object interactions to task-relevant gripper–object regions, it
primarily provides coarse spatial guidance. Extending this supervision to finer-grained interaction
patterns is more challenging because differences in embodiment appearance and manipulation modes
may limit the direct transfer of such detailed cues to robots. One potential solution is to incorporate
explicit object-affordance supervision, such as object regions and candidate contact points. Such
object-centric and embodiment-agnostic signals could complement the current attentive guidance and
further strengthen the transfer of interaction knowledge to downstream robot tasks.
Real-World Distribution Shifts.
While our results suggest that 3D representations learned from hu-
man videos benefit downstream tasks, scene discrepancies between human-video data and real-world
robot environments may still limit their transferability. A possible solution is to jointly pretrain on
unlabeled human and real-robot videos using shared depth-reconstruction and 3D-feature-alignment
objectives, encouraging the visual encoder to learn a unified 3D scene-geometric representation
across both domains.
