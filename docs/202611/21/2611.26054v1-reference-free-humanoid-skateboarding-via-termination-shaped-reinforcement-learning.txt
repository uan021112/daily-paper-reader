Reference-Free Humanoid Skateboarding via Termination-Shaped
Reinforcement Learning
Ganeshvar Soenessardien, Michael Przystupa and Kevin Sebastian Luck
Abstract— Humanoid locomotion research has advanced
rapidly on walking and running, but object-mediated locomotion,
in which a robot must coordinate its body with a passive
external object, remains difficult. One challenging task is
skateboarding, which requires simultaneous interaction with
the ground and a passive but movable platform to achieve
forward motion. Current methods for tackling skateboarding
with humanoid robots either require human demonstrations
or utilise analytic steering models that require system iden-
tification. In this paper, we introduce a deep reinforcement
learning framework that does not rely on prior data such as
demonstrations and enables robots such as the K1 humanoid
to learn to skateboard using model-free learning. The method
combines task rewards with exploit-blocking terminations. We
introduce a number of constraints restricting behaviours such
as losing the planted board foot, standing with both feet
on the board, excessive twisting, separation from the board,
and unstable board motion. Their changing prevalence during
training produces an implicit progression from stance control
towards sustained robot–board locomotion. Experiments are
performed on a simulated K1 humanoid robot in Mujoco. The
results demonstrate the feasibility of reference-free humanoid-
object locomotion with modest hardware constraints while
identifying limitations in movement style, statistical precision,
reproducibility and eventual hardware transfer.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid robots can now walk, balance, recover from
pushes and traverse rough terrain using learned controllers
trained in massively parallel simulators [1]–[4]. Almost all
of these skills involve direct contact between the robot’s feet
and a static support surface. However, a large and practically
important class of behaviours is more difficult to master:
locomotion tasks in which a robot has to interact with a
passive object, that is neither actuated nor rigidly attached
to the body, to achieve movement. Pushing a cart, riding
a scooter, balancing on a mobility aid, or skateboarding all
share this structure. The object stores momentum, introduces
its own contact dynamics and can fail in ways the robot does
not always directly control [5]–[9].
Skateboarding is a particularly demanding instance of
object-mediated locomotion and therefore a useful bench-
mark for embodied artificial intelligence. To locomote with a
skateboard, a humanoid must keep one foot on a small, freely
rolling deck, and use the other foot to generate propulsion
against the ground while regulating the contacts among
three coupled bodies (robot, board and ground). Misplaced

This paper investigates applying reinforcement learning to
humanoid locomotion on a skateboard-like platform without
the use of task-specific priors used by recent humanoid
skateboarding systems. HUSKY, for example, achieves ag-
ile real-world humanoid skateboarding by combining Ad-
versarial Motion Priors (AMP) [13] from human pushing
demonstrations with a physics-guided lean-to-steer model
and trajectory-guided phase transitions, and validates the
resulting controller on real hardware [9]. Such reference data
and engineering are powerful when available, but they also
make the method depend on human demonstrations, physical
modelling and task-specific integration which are often not
available or hard to obtain. Furthermore, such data may be
hard to utilize due to the existence of the correspondence
problem between robot and human embodiment [14], [15].
This paper focuses on the harder and complementary case
of reference-free learning [16]. The controller receives no
motion-capture trajectory, no target joint-posture sequence,
and no explicit posture-tracking reward that tells it what
skating should look like. The skateboarding behaviour must
be selected and emerge by learning from the task reward,
the reset geometry and the early termination conditions. The
question is whether recognisable humanoid-board locomo-
tion can emerge from this minimal supervision alone and
what behaviour appears when no human motion prior is
present to shape its style.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Humanoid and Legged Locomotion
The current generation of learned legged controllers is
enabled by GPU-accelerated simulators that run thousands
of environments in parallel, including Isaac Gym [17] and
MuJoCo-based frameworks such as mjlab, which is used in
this paper [18], [19]. Rudin et al. [20] learned quadruped
walking in minutes with massively parallel PPO, and learned
humanoid walking controllers have since been transferred to
real hardware [1], [21]. These works establish the simulation-
and-PPO recipe adopted here, but target locomotion over
static terrain; this paper adds a passive, freely rolling object
into the support chain.
B. Reference-Based and Reference-Free Motion Learning
A common strategy for natural looking physics-based mo-
tion is to supervise the policy with human or animal motion
data. DeepMimic tracks reference clips using an imitation
reward [22], while Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) replace
explicit trajectory tracking with a learned discriminator that
rewards motions matching the statistics of a reference dataset
[13]. These methods are powerful because they inject a
strong prior over what motion should look like. The comple-
mentary line, to which this paper belongs, removes that prior:
Heess et al. showed that rich locomotion behaviours can
emerge from task reward and environment design alone [23].
This reference-free setting is often harder because the reward
can be underspecified and may admit many qualitatively
different solutions [16]. In particular, without a reference it is
difficult to converge to smooth motion that does not exploit
simulator artefacts. Characterising the solution that emerges
for humanoid skateboarding is a central aim here.
C. Robots on Dynamic Platforms and Skateboarding
Skating and rolling-platform locomotion have a long his-
tory in model-based robotics. Classical work analyses the
skateboard as a human-controlled non-holonomic system
[24], [25], while humanoid skating has been controlled
using model-based real-time balance and foot-force control
[11]. More recently, learning-based and optimisation-based
methods have been applied to legged robots on dynamic plat-
forms: quadrupeds have been taught to skateboard through
optimisation-based control [26] and to mount a skateboard
with reverse-curriculum learning [27]; hybrid automata learn-
ing has been applied to legged skateboarding [12] and
roller-skate-style swizzle gaits have been synthesised for a
humanoid [28].
The most direct point of comparison is HUSKY, a physics-
aware whole-body controller that enables a Unitree G1
humanoid to perform agile, humanlike skateboarding with
pushing, lean-to-steer turning and phase transitions, validated
in both simulation and the real world. The HUSKY system
is an instructive contrasting system to the work presented in
this paper. HUSKY achieves a natural stroke by learning an
AMP style reward from human pushing demonstrations [13],
derives steering from an explicit board-tilt/truck-steering
kinematic model, plans trajectories to bridge phases, and
performs sim-to-real identification of the physical board [9].
This paper instead removes the motion prior entirely, restricts
the task to a single push phase, replaces analytic steering
and trajectory planning with a termination-shaped feasibility
set, and stays in simulation on consumer hardware. The
reference-free result here is therefore best read as a lower
bound on what minimal supervision can achieve and provides
an empirical explanation on the value motion priors provide
for producing natural, agile skating.

IV. METHODS
We solve the previously described MDP using proximal
policy optimisation (PPO) [34] setup with two task specific
components: a reward stack that favours conservative skating
over standing still or kicking the board (Section IV-D)
and early terminations whose changing dominance during
training induces an implicit curriculum (Section IV-E).
A. Observation Design
The actor receives an 89 dimensional observation ot
concatenated with the three-dimensional command c, giving
a 92 dimensional input. Table I lists its components and
corruptions. The critic receives 102 inputs: noise-free obser-
vations together with privileged foot air times, contact states
and contact forces. Terrain-scan and foot-height inputs are
omitted because the terrain is flat. The board state and contact
flags in the actor input are not directly measured on hardware,
so deployment would require a board-state estimator, e.g.,
fiducial tracking of the deck [35], or providing them only to
the critic, as in HUSKY [9].

C. PPO Training Setup
The actor and critic are neural networks with hidden
dimensions (512, 256, 128), ELU activations, and input nor-
malisation. The actor parameterises a diagonal Gaussian
policy with an observation-dependent mean and a learned,
observation-independent log standard deviation for each of
the 22 action dimensions, initialised to 0 (standard deviation
1.0); exponentiation ensures positivity. The critic produces a
deterministic estimate of the value. PPO uses 24 rollout steps
per environment, 5 learning epochs, 8 minibatches, discount
γ = 0.99, GAE λ = 0.95, entropy coefficient 0.005, clip
parameter ϵ = 0.2, clipped value loss with coefficient 1.0,
maximum gradient norm 1.0 and an adaptive learning rate
initialised at 10−3 with a target KL divergence of 0.01. Each
run uses 6,144 parallel environments. The actor is initialised
from the generic walker policy; the critic and optimiser start
fresh, and no run is warm-started from a prior skateboarding
policy.

E. Termination-Shaped Curriculum
Unlike the standard fall and time-limit terminations, the
task-specific terminations below restrict which robot–board
interactions remain feasible. Such terminations act as hard
constraints; constrained RL formalises them explicitly [37]–
[39], and CaT [40] likewise enforces locomotion constraints
through terminations. We use plain terminations without a
constraint formalism and leave such a formulation to future
work. The principal curriculum driver is board_foot_-
displaced. At reset, the initial board-frame position of
the on-board foot is recorded and the episode terminates
if it moves by more than 5 cm. This condition dominates
early training and pressures the policy to plant its board foot
before collecting sustained board-motion reward. As stance
control improves and the board travels farther, board_-
out_of_bounds becomes more prominent. This shift in
the dominant failure mode creates an implicit curriculum
from stance control to locomotion without changing the
environment during training.
The remaining terminations remove specific failure modes
(Table III). both_feet_on_board blocks standing on
the deck; robot_board_yaw_misaligned blocks ex-
cessive body–board twisting; and trunk_too_low blocks
crouched and split-crawl solutions. Board inversion, exces-
sive trunk rotation and robot–board separation are handled by
board_inverted, spinning_out and robot_far_-
from_board, respectively. The expected learning pattern
is therefore that board-foot displacement dominates initially,
XOR stance rises, board and trunk speeds increase together,
and time outs become more frequent as episodes lengthen.

V. EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
A. Training Protocol and Seeds
Experiments started with a pre-trained walking policy as
warm-start, which defines a standard walking motion but
was learned without the moving platform being present. The
walker is never used as a tracking target but still introduces a
walking style prior; whether this warm start is necessary was
not tested. All experiments used identical high-level settings,
6,144 parallel environments and a target of 20,000 PPO
iterations and were repeated three times. Each run completed
approximately 19,800–20,000 PPO iterations.
B. Hardware and Compute Budget
We used a single workstation (NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti GPU,
AMD Ryzen 9 7900 with 12 cores/24 threads, 32 GB RAM).
At 6,144 environments, each iteration took ≈5.1–5.3 s once
warm, so one 20,000-iteration run took ≈28.7 h.
C. Metrics
Training progress is measured by average episode reward
and episode length. Skating mechanics are evaluated using
episode averages of four per-step metrics: board forward
speed, grounded body progress (forward trunk velocity in
XOR stance), trunk forward velocity and XOR-stance rate.
Failure modes are tracked through termination counts for
board out of bounds, board-foot displacement, yaw mis-
alignment, both feet on the board and time out. Curves
are smoothed only for visualisation; the exported CSV files
retain the raw logged values and final statistics are computed
from the unsmoothed final value of each seed.

D. Performance
Table IV lists the final performance for each experiment.
All three achieve high XOR rates and non-zero coupled board
and trunk progress, giving a consistent quantitative signature

E. Qualitative Behaviour
Visual inspection of representative checkpoints supports
and qualifies the quantitative results. The policies maintain
the push stance, produce forward board motion through
ground contact propulsion and remain upright for several
hundred steps. Both-feet-on-board and yaw-misalignment ter-
minations become rare, and no previously identified exploit
was observed during inspection. However, the motion is
conservative: the off-board foot lifts only slightly during each
push and remains near the ground for most of the stroke, with
a near-ground rate of approximately 0.92 in representative
diagnostic logs. The resulting motion is a small lift-and-
replant cycle instead of a fluid, high clearance leg swing.
Also, in rare cases, the ground leg rotates inward or pushes
with the side of the foot, briefly stalling propulsion. The
behaviour is therefore best described as recognisable but
conservative skateboarding. Section VII relates this style to
the incentives created by the reward design. Figures 6 and 7
show the propulsive stepping sequence and the resulting
motion through space.

Finally, the functional skill of skateboarding emerges con-

Why Conservative Skateboarding Skills Emerge: The gap
between the learned low-clearance push and a fluid stroke
may be partly explained by the reward design. The near-
ground term in Eq. (3) directly favours low foot clearance.
Relative to ground level, its weighted reward rate decreases
by approximately 0.24, 0.88 and 1.72 at foot heights of 0.05,
0.10, and 0.15 m, respectively. After multiplication by the
0.02 s control timestep, these correspond to per-step return re-
ductions of approximately 0.005, 0.018 and 0.034. Although
individually small, these reductions accumulate whenever the
foot remains elevated. The observed low-clearance motion
and near-ground rate of approximately 0.92 are therefore
consistent with this incentive. However, because the term was
not evaluated in a controlled ablation, it cannot be identified
as the sole cause of the conservative style.
This result also clarifies the contrast with reference-based
systems. HUSKY uses an AMP style reward to favour
humanlike pushing motions, together with physics guided
steering, transition planning and sim-to-real engineering [9].
HUSKY’s more natural motion likely reflects its complete
system, including both its motion prior and additional en-
gineering; i.e., it cannot be attributed to AMP alone. The
two studies represent different supervision choices: task-
based learning without a skateboarding motion prior versus
demonstration-guided style learning.
VIII. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
The paper marks a first step towards an end-to-end skill
learning framework for skateboarding with reinforcement
learning. Initial experiments are obtained in simulation and
future experiments will consider the investigation of possible
sim-to-real challenges of the proposed framework. Further-
more, the skateboarding skill has been trained on flat and
even terrain, albeit domain randomization has been applied
to various physical and control parameters to emulate and
compensate for real-world challenges. The board is also a
caster-wheeled transport roller board rather than a skateboard.
It has no trucks or kingpin, so leaning the deck does not
steer the wheels and the board cannot produce lean-to-steer
turning. Our results therefore do not cover truck dynamics,
and training on a modelled skateboard, as in HUSKY [9], is
future work.
Future work should first test the causes of the conserva-
tive style through controlled reward ablations. Reducing or
annealing the near-ground reward, possibly alongside a recov-
ery phase incentive, may encourage a higher-clearance stroke.
A lightweight style prior, such as a small AMP discriminator,
could instead be explored as a hybrid extension that retains
the task rewards and termination structure while adding
limited reference-based supervision. Reward and termination
ablations, training from scratch and additional seeds would
strengthen the causal and statistical evidence, while steering
could extend the task beyond straight-line propulsion.
IX. CONCLUSION
This paper investigated the extent to which a Booster K1
humanoid can learn to skateboard on a passive roller board
without a skateboarding motion reference or target joint-
posture trajectory and without more than a single consumer
workstation. The result is meaningful but bounded: functional
skateboarding emerges, but natural movement style remains
only partially developed. PPO training in MuJoCo/mjlab,
guided by task rewards and exploit blocking terminations,
produced policies that keep one foot on the board and
the other near the ground, propel the board forwards and
remain upright for hundreds of steps. Across three seeds,
the final XOR-stance rate was approximately 97.6%, mean
board speed was approximately 0.27 m/s and trunk motion
remained coupled to board motion.
Two lessons stand out. First off, early terminations are a
useful design tool for object mediated locomotion, because
they constrain non-skating, high-return solutions and help
direct learning toward the intended mechanics. Secondly,
reference-free learning recovered the functional outcome of
skating but not its full style: the off-board foot followed a
conservative, low-clearance motion rather than a fluid recov-
ery swing. This behaviour is consistent with the incentive
created by the near-ground reward, although a controlled


ablation is required to establish causality. Ergo this work
demonstrates a non trivial humanoid–object skill on mod-
est hardware while identifying clear directions for higher-
clearance strokes, steering, stronger experimental validation
and eventual sim-to-real transfer.
