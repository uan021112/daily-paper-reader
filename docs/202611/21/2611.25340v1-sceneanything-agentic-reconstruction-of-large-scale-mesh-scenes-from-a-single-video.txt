Abstract
Reconstructing an editable 3D scene from video requires
matching visible structure while plausibly completing un-
seen surfaces.
Agents can write scene-construction pro-
grams, but direct reconstruction attempts often produce er-
rors in layout, scale, and lighting.
We introduce SCE-
NEANYTHING, an automated system for reconstructing
mesh scenes from real and generated videos, enabling text-
to-scene construction when paired with video generation.
A software harness coordinates agents, pretrained vision
models, and graphics tools through an explicit reconstruc-
tion protocol.
Shared camera, depth, and segmentation
estimates ground component construction: a scene-owner
agent plans the environment and briefs authors, who write
parameterized Blender programs. A visibility- and fit-based
level-of-detail rule selects the construction level, while un-
seen regions are completed and marked as inferred. As-
sembled scenes use fitted lighting and supported motion,
and revisions undergo programmatic checks and indepen-
dent visual comparisons. Accepted programs and lessons
are reused across scenes. We evaluate on 100 scenes; sub-
set comparisons show improved original-view fidelity over
direct-agent reconstruction and higher novel-view content
similarity than the compared video-to-3D methods. We will
release the harness, agent instructions, reusable libraries,
and scenes.
1. Introduction
An editable 3D scene allows an environment to be explored,
relit, and modified beyond a recorded or generated camera
path. Recovering such a scene from video requires fitting
visible structures and spatial relationships, separating ma-
1

terials from illumination, and completing unseen surfaces.
Recent reconstruction and scene-generation models provide
useful geometric estimates and priors [16, 18, 22, 37]. We
build on these capabilities to reconstruct separate mesh ob-
jects, materials, and light sources from real and generated
videos.
Paired with text-to-video generation, the system
also enables text-to-scene construction. Unseen surfaces are
completed and marked as inferred.
Language and vision-language model agents can inter-
pret structures, write Blender programs, and revise them
from rendered feedback [11, 13, 39, 54]. Directly assign-
ing the entire reconstruction task to an agent leaves it to es-
tablish cameras and scale, choose constructions, and assess
its own result. In our zero-shot comparisons, two frontier
agents produce recognizable content but simplified geom-
etry and materials, with errors in layout, dimensions, and
lighting (Fig. 1, columns 3 and 4). Some attempts project
footage onto proxy surfaces, reproducing input views while
exposing artifacts from new viewpoints. These failures mo-
tivate a process that grounds agent construction in geomet-
ric estimates and evaluates revisions in the assembled scene.
We introduce SCENEANYTHING, an automated sys-
tem for coordinated component construction from video
(Fig. 2). Pretrained vision models supply shared camera,
depth, and segmentation estimates. A scene-owner agent
plans components and briefs author agents, which return
parameterized Blender generators. Agents choose scene-
specific constructions; the method specifies their shared in-
puts, authoring interfaces, level-of-detail policy, and revi-
sion procedure. The level-of-detail policy uses visibility
and fit to select procedural recipes, fitted instances, or part-
by-part construction. Components are assembled with fit-
ted lighting and supported motion, then revisions undergo
programmatic checks and render comparisons by separate
judge agents. A software harness coordinates agents, mod-
els, and graphics tools and applies the acceptance rule. Ac-
cepted generators and curated lessons are reused in later
runs. Section 3 details this structure and the decisions dele-
gated to agents.
Our contributions are:
• An observation-grounded reconstruction framework that
coordinates component authoring to produce editable
scenes from real and generated videos, enabling text-
to-scene construction when paired with video generation
(Secs. 3.1–3.4).
• A level-of-detail policy and scene-level revision proce-
dure that allocate individual modeling according to visi-
bility and fit, and evaluate changes through programmatic
checks and independent visual comparisons (Secs. 3.5
and 3.6).
• An evaluation across 100 scenes, including direct-agent
comparisons, a level-of-detail ablation, and open-weight
author substitutions (Sec. 4), accompanied by a planned
release of code, instructions, libraries, scenes, and evalu-
ation records.
2. Related Work
Reconstruction from video. Structure from motion and
multi-view stereo recover cameras and surfaces [30, 35,
36]; feed-forward models predict point maps, depth, and
poses [14, 22, 42, 45, 46]. Neural radiance fields [3, 28]
and Gaussian splatting [17, 18] represent appearance for
view synthesis, with surface-oriented variants supporting
mesh extraction [5, 12]. Dynamic methods recover time-
varying scenes [25, 44, 47], while generative models syn-
thesize explorable environments [16, 24, 37]. SCENEANY-
THING uses a video-to-3D model as a measurement prior,
then constructs separate, named mesh components with ex-
plicit materials, lighting, and supported animation.
Object-level reconstruction.
Methods retrieve and
align CAD models to scans [2], or generate objects us-
ing learned priors from an image [1, 6, 15, 26] or multi-
ple views [29, 34, 48, 50, 51]. Video2Game reconstructs
interactive mesh environments from video [49]. Our com-
ponents are constructed by parameterized programs fitted to
video observations, allowing their construction procedures
and parameters to be revised and reused.
Agentic scene construction. Language-model systems
arrange assets [10, 53], write scene programs [11, 39], and
coordinate generators with critics or verifiers [9, 23, 52].
Image-conditioned approaches predict structured scene de-
scriptions [21], refine programs through rendering [54],
orchestrate specialist agents [20, 40], or edit existing
scenes [13].
SCENEANYTHING grounds component au-
thoring in shared video-derived measurements and evalu-
ates revisions in a common assembled scene.
Separate
judges compare matched renders in both display orders,
with identical-image controls and a prescribed acceptance
rule. This procedure addresses the order sensitivity docu-
mented for language-model judges [43, 56].
Procedural generation, reuse, and level of detail. In-
finigen generates natural and indoor environments procedu-
rally [31, 32]. Voyager stores reusable agent programs [41],
and SceneCraft builds a scene-function library [11]. Our
library retains generators fitted to video observations for
adaptation to later scenes (Sec. 3.6). Rendering level-of-
detail methods select representations according to viewing
conditions [8, 19]. Our policy instead allocates construction
detail according to visibility and achieved fit; unresolved
detail and unseen surfaces are completed procedurally and
recorded as inferred (Sec. 3.5).
3. Method
Given a captured or generated video, SCENEANYTHING
reconstructs an editable Blender scene containing separate
2


mesh objects, materials, light sources, and animation data
for supported moving objects.
3.1. Overview and Terminology
A reconstruction run (or simply run) is one complete at-
tempt to reconstruct a scene; all agent sessions, vision
model inferences and compute jobs for that attempt belong
to the same run.
Scene representation and agent roles.
The current
scene is the reconstruction state at a given stage of the
run: its cameras and world coordinates, geometry, mate-
rials, lighting, and motion. A component is an authored
portion of the scene, such as a building or terrain surface.
A generator is a parameterized Blender program that con-
structs geometry and materials. A family groups objects or
parts that share construction logic. A family may span sev-
eral objects, such as windows on different buildings. An
instance is one occurrence of an object or part in a family;
different instances can have different generator parameters
and poses.
We use three types of agents. A scene owner agent co-
ordinates the run, maintains the current scene, plans com-
ponents, and assigns construction tasks to author agents.
Authors write and revise generators for particular families
or spatial regions. Separate judge agents compare candidate
reconstructions with the current accepted scene and the ob-
servations. All three roles are language or vision-language
model agents; our default owner uses Claude Opus 5.5, with
alternative author models evaluated in Sec. 4.4.

3.2. Video Processing and Geometric Initialization
Frames and initial reconstruction.
The harness ex-
tracts timestamped frames and detects shot boundaries;
each run reconstructs one continuous shot. Lyra 2’s recon-
struction branch [37] estimates cameras, depth, and a Gaus-
sian representation, providing initial geometry for spatial
measurements. These estimates serve as correctable priors
rather than final scene geometry.
Camera validation and world coordinates. Geomet-
ric diagnostic tools built with OpenCV and numerical rou-
tines, rather than large learned models, report reprojec-
tion error, trajectory discontinuities, vertical alignment, and
near-object parallax (Supp. S1). The owner uses these di-
agnostics to accept, re-estimate, or correct the cameras be-
fore construction. It establishes a gravity-aligned coordi-
nate system and estimates scale from two independent rec-
ognizable size references, checking their agreement. All
components use these shared cameras and coordinates.

3.3. Segmentation and 3D Association
Segmentation and tracking. The owner names target
classes and supplies text or box prompts. Mask2Former [7]
provides semantic regions, SAM 3.1 [4] segments prompted
instances, and SAM 2.1 [33] propagates instance masks
through time. Each covers a case that the others handle
poorly: Mask2Former labels continuous far regions that
prompted instances miss, and SAM 2.1 keeps instance iden-
tities in dense scenes (Supp. S1). A track associates masks
of the same instance across frames. Authors obtain masks
for constituent parts using the same prompting procedure.

Association with 3D geometry. Geometric tools project
reconstructed points into the views, filter occluded points,
and accumulate labels from overlapping masks. Plane fit-
ting estimates supporting surfaces and facades; cluster-
ing provides approximate instance locations and extents
(Supp. S1). This coarse 3D layout lets the owner plan the
scene and assign authors, and lets assembly place each com-
ponent at its estimated location instead of searching for its
pose. These estimates inherit camera, depth, and segmenta-
tion errors and describe only observed geometry. For exam-
ple, labeled building points constrain placement and visible
dimensions; an author constructs coherent walls, a roof, and
unseen sides.
3.4. Scene Decomposition, Component Authoring
Scene planning and briefs.
The owner plans com-
ponents, their relationships, and plausible completions of
unseen parts, keeping continuous surfaces such as terrain
within one component.
Unseen sides and surroundings
are built by the same generators as the observed parts and
recorded as inferred (Supp. S13). It builds a coarse environ-
ment, then delegates families or large structures to parallel
authors. Delegation separates the work into bounded tasks:
a family’s author shares one generator and its lessons across
all instances, and a smaller model can take such a task
(Sec. 4.4; Supp. S9). Each author receives a brief specify-
ing relevant observations, dimensions, coordinates, neigh-
boring components, support constraints (what the compo-
nent rests on or attaches to), and proposed level of detail
(Sec. 3.5), together with current lighting, tools, and relevant
generators and lessons.
Parameterized construction and fitting. Authors write
Blender generators with parameters for dimensions, place-
ment, orientation, and counts. They choose construction
procedures and revise code and parameters by compar-
ing renders with the footage, optionally invoking inverse-
rendering tools.
Components must remain separate and
movable, include unseen sides, and use materials without
copied footage pixels (Supp. S5). Material parameters de-
scribe reflectance under the fitted illumination (Sec. 3.6).
Author delivery. Authors deliver generator code, named
closed mesh objects, an object-label manifest, declared sup-
ports, and records of measurements and inferred parts. The
harness checks deliveries and compares rendered silhou-
ettes with observation masks. Failed deliveries return for
revision; passing permits assembly. Acceptance of changes
to the assembled scene follows Sec. 3.6.
3.5. Level of Detail
The video supports different amounts of detail for differ-
ent scene elements. Our level-of-detail policy chooses how
each element is constructed from its own observations, and
which detail is generated from regional statistics or struc-
tural assumptions. Components have a hierarchy of ele-
ments, such as a bed, its plants, and their leaves, or a district,
its buildings, and their windows. A construction level de-
scribes how an element is built; it is distinct from its depth
in this hierarchy. Part-by-part construction can be applied
recursively at successive tiers.
Construction levels. We use the following three levels,
abbreviated L0, L1, and L2 in the author briefs:
• L0: Procedural recipe. Generate the element on its sup-
port using density, size, and color estimated for its region.
• L1: Fitted instance. Fit a generator instance’s position,
scale, height, and orientation to the element’s own masks.
• L2: Part by part. Construct observed parts using their
4


individual masks and spatial measurements, applying the
policy recursively to those parts.
For example, a plant can be generated from regional statis-
tics, fitted to its own outline, or built from individually ob-
served leaves. The level is proposed during scene planning
and reconsidered at each revision. Two visibility tests pro-
pose the construction level before building; a subsequent fit
test checks a constructed part-by-part candidate.
Visibility-based proposal. Let T (e) be the frames in
which element e has an instance mask, and Mt(e) its mask
in frame t. Its frame share is at(e) = |Mt(e)|/(WH) for a
W×H image, and ˜a(e) is the median share over T (e). Let
m(e) count the masks of its parts over all frames of T (e),
and let w(e) be the median diameter of the largest circle
inscribed in each of these masks. Parts are not tracked at
this stage, so a part visible in k frames contributes k masks.
Tools compute these quantities.
Before construction,
the owner sets and records the visibility thresholds for
each family, with a reason; defaults are in Supp. S3. The
instance-visibility test S1 requires enough views and suffi-
cient median frame share. The part-visibility test S2 addi-
tionally considers sustained image size, part width, and the
number of part masks:

Construction, fitting, and fallback. For a proposed L2
construction, the author obtains part masks with SAM 3.1
and constructs and refines a candidate Ge. Authors deliver
a generator shared by the family and a parameter record for
each element; fitting tools adjust parameters against the cor-

tionally using consistent depth for bounded deformation; at
L2 the individual parts have their own masks and measure-
ments.

A part-by-part candidate that fails F is thus proposed as a

retained parts, so instances of one family can have different
construction levels. The author decides what is built: the
instructions allow it to build individual parts wherever those

the proposed level, and the built level.
The instructions emphasize separability as well as image
size: a small, cleanly outlined dormer can be fitted individ-
ually, whereas unresolved house fronts are represented by
their block’s estimated height, roof line, and window spac-
ing. The visibility tests do not measure separability: the
author judges it on the masks while building, and builds an
element whose outline merges with its neighbors at a lower
level than proposed. Detected elements remain represented
even if their masks or tracks cannot support individual fit-
ting.
Completion and novel-view detail.
Below the built
level, procedural rules generate unresolved detail using es-
timated density, size, and color.
Structural assumptions
and the scene plan provide plausible completions of un-
seen parts. Generated detail and unseen completions are
recorded as inferred. If an element’s placement is uncertain,
it remains generated within its parent’s constraints rather
than being dropped.
After assembly, a tool compares each element’s maxi-
mum projected size over the delivered views with its size
in the input frames. Where a novel view comes closer than
any input view, the author generates one additional tier of
finer elements. This tier is also inferred: a closer rendered
view does not supply new observations of the scene.

Lighting and materials.
The owner selects light
sources and calibration regions for fitting their strengths.
Outdoor scenes use sun and sky; indoor scenes use vis-
ible luminaires, with windows and doorways as sky por-
tals. Fitting tools sweep sun directions against observed lit-
and-shaded regions, initialized from date, time, and loca-
tion when available. Authors choose material models; dif-
fuse albedo is estimated under the fitted illumination and
checked for consistency between lit and shaded pixels. In-
consistency triggers lighting refitting. Reflective and trans-
missive surfaces use corresponding material models.
A fitted camera model maps scene-linear renders to
displayed frames through exposure, white balance, and a
bounded tone curve (Supp. S1).
Its parameters are held
fixed while lighting or material revisions are compared, pre-
venting candidate-specific exposure adjustments from com-
pensating for reconstruction errors.
Motion fitting. The owner selects a rigid-body, mecha-
nism, or water-surface representation; fitting tools estimate
its parameters. Rigid poses are fitted to tracked silhouettes
through the shared cameras, with trajectories constrained
by supports. Mechanisms use axes, periods, and speeds es-
timated from the sequence; water uses a wave surface fitted
to its extent, speed, and pattern. Animation uses the original
timestamps, and shape changes trigger pose refitting.
Candidate revisions and checks. The owner compares
the current scene with the original frames, lists defects, and
proposes or delegates a revision targeting the most severe
defect. Each comparison evaluates one declared revision
together with its necessary refits. Missing or inconsistent
observations can trigger additional segmentation, measure-
ment, or correction of derived estimates; the original frames
remain the reference for image agreement.
Before comparison, the level-of-detail policy is reconsid-
ered for affected elements and lighting and supported mo-
tion are refitted as needed. The harness runs support and
physical audits covering attachment relationships, interpen-
etration, emission from non-light materials, painted reflec-
tions, and implausible albedo; all but painted reflections,
which the owner inspects, are tested by programs. Each
audit counts findings on the assembled scene, and a candi-
date may not have more findings of a kind than its parent
(Supp. S8). For every moving object, median tracked-mask
IoU may decrease by at most 0.005. A candidate that fails
these scene or motion checks is rejected before visual judg-
ing.
Visual comparison and acceptance. Before judging,
the owner declares the render pairs and the primary view
group, a set of pairs for one kind of view, such as origi-
nal or novel views. Candidate and parent use a common
camera table, timestamps, observations, and rendering set-
tings. Separate judges, without construction history or can-
didate identities, see a reference frame and two renders and
choose the closer reconstruction or a tie. Judges are sepa-
rate because agents accept their own work too readily, and
the comparison controls two measured judge biases: judges
pick a side even between identical images, and prefer the
6


richer background (Supp. S2). Novel views use available
reference frames to assess consistency and plausibility, not
the correctness of unseen geometry.
Each pair is evaluated in both display orders and counts
as a win only when the same reconstruction wins both; a
pair with a tie or with disagreeing orders is left out of the
test. Identical-image controls test preferences unrelated to
scene differences. The harness accepts a candidate when
it wins the primary group under a two-sided sign test over
pairs at significance level 0.05, controls tie, and no other
evaluated group is lost, that is, won by the parent under
the same test. Supp. S2 describes the judging and early-
stopping procedure.
Corrections that the owner declares, before judging, as
required for physical validity undergo the same audits and
motion checks but are retained unless the parent wins a view
group under the same test. Accepted candidates replace the
parent; rejected candidates leave it unchanged. A run re-
turns its latest accepted scene when the owner has no further
listed defects or the time budget expires; if no initial assem-
bly passes the checks, it returns no scene (Algorithm 1).
Reuse across runs. The generator library Lg stores ac-
cepted construction programs by family. A curation agent
distills construction lessons into the experience library Le,
recording applicability conditions and supporting observa-
tions. Later owners and authors retrieve relevant generators
and lessons and adapt them to the new video’s observations.
Reused generators undergo the same fitting, checks, and ac-
ceptance process as new ones; reuse does not update model
weights. Generators are written for a family rather than for
one view, which also lets a scene be extended beyond the
filmed region.
4. Results
4.1. Setup
Scenes and evaluation subsets.
We reconstruct 100
scenes: 39 static and 20 moving-element Wikimedia Com-
mons videos, 17 generated videos, and 24 photo sets and
our own captures, including Mip-NeRF 360 [3], LLFF [27].
The videos span indoor and outdoor scenes captured by
handheld camera and by drone. Different experiments use
subsets of this collection. Video-to-3D methods were run on
21 scenes (8 real, 13 generated). SCENEANYTHING returns
a scene on all 21; the reconstruction of a compared method
can be registered to the original cameras on 19 scenes for
Pi3-X, 15 for PGSR, and 12 for COLMAP (Supp. S12). Ta-
ble 1 reports the 10 jointly aligned scenes (4 real, 6 gener-
ated). Direct-agent comparisons cover 30 scenes.
Metrics.
For video-to-3D comparisons, we report
LPIPS [55] and DINOv3 feature similarity [38] against the
input video at up to 8 original cameras per scene; these
frames are among the input of every method, and the table
averages per-scene means. Direct-agent comparisons use
101 frames across 30 scenes. At 8 novel cameras per scene,
spaced evenly on a path around the scene (Supp. S12), DI-
NOv3 similarity to the best-matching original frame mea-
sures content similarity, not geometric accuracy. Pairwise
comparisons follow Sec. 3.6, using Claude Opus 5.5 judges;

4.4. Author-Model Alternatives
We test DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Nemotron 3.5 Super VL
authoring for the same 14 scenes, with the owner model,
delivery requirements, and judges unchanged. While the
overall quality is lower (on the 11 scenes that also have
a run with the Claude Opus 5.5 author, mean LPIPS and
DINOv3 similarity at the original cameras are 0.55 and
0.66 for DeepSeek and 0.58 and 0.60 for Nemotron, against
0.47 and 0.79), the resulting scenes retain separate mesh
objects, fitted lighting, and qualitative coherence at novel
views (Supp. S9). All 28 runs delivered a scene.
4.5. Motion and Editing
Moving objects are animated: the owner identifies the kind
of motion and tools fit its parameters to the tracked masks.
For example, a high-speed train, a harbor boat, a funicular
car, an airliner, and a slewing crane follow the footage at
median tracked-mask IoU 0.89–0.98 and median centroid
error 2–13 px, measured over the frames in which each is
tracked. Explicit animation allows the fitted motion to re-
play from novel cameras (Supp. S11).
Separate meshes and explicit light sources also permit
relighting (Fig. 5A) and removal and repositioning of ob-
jects (Fig. 5B). Surfaces revealed by an edit remain present,
although their unseen geometry is inferred.
4.6. Limitations
Best results require commercial models; current open-
source author agents produce lower quality scenes. Recon-
struction of unseen regions is plausible but generally shows
lower quality and detail than observed regions. Our method
is built on top of imperfect camera, segmentation, depth,
and material estimates. Each run reconstructs one contin-
uous shot; unifying shots across cuts remains future work.
Human reconstruction is a hard researcch problem and we
do not attempt it; non-rigid motion other than water surfaces
remains outside the current scope.
5. Conclusion
We presented SCENEANYTHING, an automated system in
which a software harness coordinates agents, pretrained
vision models, and graphics tools to turn real and generated
videos into editable 3D scenes. The agents decide what
to build and which revisions to try; the harness keeps a
8


revision if it passes programmatic checks and separate
judge agents prefer its renders. At novel views, our scenes
agree better in content with the footage than those of three
video-to-3D methods that produce geometry, and they
remain coherent when smaller open-weight models author
the components.

S1. Models, tools, and camera model
Solvers and depth models (Sec. 3.2). Translating cam-
eras are solved with COLMAP incremental or GLOMAP
global structure from motion on semantically masked
frames, with the focal length free; where the median par-
allax stays below 30 px the focal length is held at the prior’s
value, because the data do not determine it. VIPE poses
are used as prior or fallback, and focal lengths are cross-
checked against VIPE, Depth Pro and MoGe-2.
A piv-
oting camera gives no parallax; its depth comes from the
prior where it agrees with the frames, and otherwise from a
monocular depth model (MoGe-2, Depth Pro or UniDepth)
or from known sizes on measured planes. When cameras
are solved again, moving objects are masked out, with la-
bels that the owner selects, and the result is aligned to the
prior’s world coordinates, so that the prior’s depth stays us-
able. Where the diagnostics ﬂag a local jump in the trajec-
tory, those cameras can instead be replaced by a bounded
interpolation between trusted ones. On one phone video, re-
placing the cameras of a ﬂagged run of frames by a bounded
interpolation between trusted ones lowers the mean image
error of a ﬁxed scene on those frames by 31%.
Locating masks in 3D (Sec. 3.3).
For the labeled
points: A depth-buffer test excludes points occluded by
nearer points, and the remaining points receive votes from
the masks that cover their projected pixels. The owner binds
masks to elements with its own script. The default clus-
tering tool works by density in the horizontal coordinates
(x, y) only; clustering in three dimensions would split a
tree at the gap between trunk and crown. For the plants
of a garden bed, in contrast, the owner clustered in three di-
mensions at a scale of centimeters. For each cluster the tool
reports the median 3D location and a robust axis-aligned
box. For the rays onto measured planes: when the base of
an instance is hidden, as for densely placed chairs or posts,
the rays through its top pixels are instead intersected with a
plane at the height measured for its class. The tool checks
the result in the other views. The two other tools: (ii) Rays
onto measured planes. An instance that stands on a mea-
sured plane can be located from a single view, by intersect-
ing the rays through the pixels where it touches the plane

Why three segmentation models (Sec. 3.3). We com-

trackers start from identical masks: in a dense scene of

SAM 3.1 is preferred in 8 comparisons to 0 (recall 0.93

on a city frame, SAM 3.1 ﬁnds 25 separate buildings, 8.6%

S2. Blind acceptance: judging sheets and early
stopping
Candidate and parent are rendered in the same ﬁnished
scene with identical timestamps, novel-view cameras, cam-
era parameters and sample count. Judges, independent sub-
agents, see one sheet at a time, which shows only the origi-
nal frame (or reference frames for a novel view) and both
renders in a random display order.
Every batch carries
identical-image controls. Pairs are sent in batches of four,
and judging stops once the probability that the full set would
reach the current verdict exceeds 0.95; on 183 stored com-
parisons this kept 99.1–99.6% of the full-judging decisions
with 66–91% of the sheets. The unit of the sign test is a
pair, which enters the test only when both display orders
give the same verdict. Judging does not stop before eight
pairs, and a control on which a side is chosen in both dis-
play orders blocks early stopping, so that the full declared
set is judged; no judge is replaced. The judges are separate
from the agent that builds because agents accept their own
work too readily: the directly prompted agents of Fig. 1 of
the main paper decide themselves when their scene is com-
plete, and on 30 scenes their LPIPS at the original cameras
is 0.66 and 0.69, against 0.51 for our runs (Sec. 4.2 of the
main paper). The comparison itself controls two biases of
the judges that we measured, by one ﬁnished scene for both
candidates, both display orders, and identical-image con-
trols. Judgments follow context: when two authors’ vegeta-
tion was compared in their own, differently ﬁnished scenes,
all 18 verdicts went to the richer background and reversed
once both were rendered into the same scene. A single com-
parison carries a positional bias: given a plain prompt, three
judges chose a side in 61 of 66 verdicts on pairs of identical
renders. A correction of the cameras themselves is validated
separately: one ﬁxed scene is rendered at the corrected and
at the uncorrected cameras, and both renders are compared
with the frames.
S3. Level of detail: tests and thresholds
Tests and thresholds.
The level-of-detail rule of the
main paper uses two visibility tests, S1 and S2, and a ﬁt
test F. The quantities in the visibility tests are computed by
tools from the masks. For an element located by a cluster
of the 3D points labelled with its class, the owner’s script
binds a mask to it in every frame. Its frame share in that
frame is the area of this mask divided by the area of the
frame (Eqs. 1 and 2 of the main paper use the median share
over the frames in which the element has a mask, and S2
also counts the frames in which the share reaches α0); its
part width is the median, over the masks of its parts, of the
diameter of the largest circle inscribed in each mask. The
part masks are pooled over the frames of the element: a
part seen in k frames counts k times in the number of part
masks and in the median width, a part without a mask con-
tributes nothing, and a mask that covers several parts counts
once; the number of part masks is thresholded only where
the owner sets m0. The owner sets the thresholds per family
and records the reason. The default visibility tests are per-
missive (n1 = 2, α1 = 0.1%; α0 = 1% in n2 = 3 frames,
w0 = 15 px); these are the start values of the instruction
quoted in Sec. S5, which sets no bound on α2 or m0. The
ﬁt test uses τ = 0.6 on the median silhouette IoU over the
track, which bounds the best frame as well; an author may
state a stricter test for a class. The silhouette of the ﬁt test is
the visible one, from a render of the assembled scene with
object identiﬁers; where several elements share one mask,
the union of their silhouettes is compared with it. The in-
structions ask for this overlap for every individually ﬁtted
element (Sec. S5). The built level can differ from the pro-
posed one: on the daylily bed, for example, the masks of a
sapling merge with the shrub behind it in part of its track;
the tests proposed part-by-part construction, and its author
built it as a ﬁtted instance.

S8. Method details
The passages of this section stood in the method section of
the main paper in an earlier, longer version and are given
here in the same order.
Observed, estimated and inferred data. We deﬁne ob-
servations to include both the original timestamped video
frames, and the quantities derived from them: segmentation
masks, object tracks, estimated cameras, depth, 3D points,
and spatial measurements. These quantities have different
reliability: frames are direct observations (though still con-
tain camera processing and exposure adjustments that are
not fully speciﬁed), whereas segmentation masks, camera
poses, and depths are model estimates that could contain
error (they come from the models and tools of Secs. 3.2
and 3.3 of the main paper).
We distinguish directly ob-
served structure, estimated geometric quantities, and in-
ferred scene completions. For example, a building’s visible
facade is directly observed, its distance is estimated, and its
unseen back wall is inferred.
System structure and responsibilities. It is natural to
ask which pieces of SCENEANYTHING are agentic, which
are a ﬁxed pipeline, and what is the paper’s contribution in
all of this. Language and vision-language model agents per-
form the owner, author, and judge roles; these roles can use
different models, including commercial and open-source
models (author substitutions are evaluated in Sec. 4.4 of the
main paper). Reconstruction models, including Lyra 2 and
its Depth Anything 3 backbone, estimate cameras, depth,
and 3D structure. Segmentation models produce seman-
tic regions, instance masks, and tracks.
Blender inter-
prets scene construction scripts generated by the agents,
and Cycles renders candidate scenes. The harness coor-
dinates these agents, models, and tools, maintains shared
records, and executes the checks and acceptance procedure.
The harness is the software that provides tools, coordinates
these roles, and applies programmatic checks and accep-
tance rules (blue in Fig. 2 of the main paper).
Our contribution is the formulation of video-to-3D re-
construction as the construction of a complete, editable
scene coordinated by a scene-owner agent, together with the
structure of the solution: a shared scene grounded in obser-
vations, initialization using recent vision models, decompo-
sition by the owner and delegation to author agents through
component briefs, a level-of-detail rule that builds each el-
ement as ﬁnely as its masks support, and revisions that are
kept only when judges prefer renders of the whole scene.
The harness implements the prescribed roles, interfaces,
construction requirements, and evaluation rules. Thus, the
solution structure is speciﬁed by the method, while the qual-
ity of the constructions and visual judgments also depends
on the underlying models, and could increase in quality with
future stronger models. Table S2 details these responsibili-
ties, and Sec. S5 details the agent instructions.

from the Mask2Former labels, the SAM masks, or their
union; instances take the SAM masks. The earlier, longer
wording of the paragraph on the models: The owner ﬁrst
looks at the frames and names the classes to segment (line 3

covers a case that the others handle poorly. Mask2Former

frames that the owner selects, and outlines what they name,
one tight mask per instance. SAM 2.1 propagates these in-

that need one identity across frames. A track is the set of
masks of one instance across frames. The owner then looks

supporting structures provide additional constraints.
Planning and authoring (Sec. 3.4). The decomposi-
tion is a judgment about the content of the scene: which
elements belong together, and what continues behind an
occluder. The owner’s instructions ask for a plan of “the
plausible whole this video belongs to”, split “by space and
level”, with inferred parts marked and every class written
as a hierarchy of elements (Sec. S5). On the embankment,
for example, the owner built the water itself and briefed ﬁve
authors. The instructions prescribe only the measurement
tools and the author’s render-and-compare loop (Sec. S5).
The script can be reﬁtted to the observations, edited after-
wards, and reused in later scenes. The owner writes a ﬁrst
plan while the segmentation models run, and ﬁxes the com-
ponents, their families, and their proposed levels of detail
(Sec. 3.5 of the main paper) in the author briefs. The owner
ﬁrst builds a coarse scene itself: the environment and the
“main masses as measured proxies” (Sec. S5). For each
family or large structure, the owner then starts one author
agent, and the authors work in parallel, each with the ob-
servations relevant to its task. Running a generator creates
named mesh objects with their materials, and a revision ed-
its the script rather than a stored mesh.
Lighting, materials, and motion (Sec. 3.6). Lamps,
windows, and mirrors have to be recognized, which the
owner does; directions and strengths can be measured, and
the tools ﬁt them. Source strengths are ﬁtted on owner-
selected calibration regions and checked on other regions.
The author of a component chooses its material model. The
kind of motion has to be recognized, which the owner does;
the tools ﬁt its parameters. The tools record mask IoU and
centroid error at every tracked frame, rather than only at
frames selected for visual judging. Spray, thin handrails and
fast rotor blades are reported as regions rather than rigid
poses. For diffuse surfaces, albedo estimates from lit and
shaded pixels are checked under the ﬁtted lighting; incon-
sistent estimates trigger lighting reﬁtting. People are not
built: their masks are excluded from ﬁtting and from the
comparisons, and the footage itself is left unchanged.
Revision and comparison (Sec. 3.6). Beyond what the
checks below ﬂag, an agent lists the defects; what is wrong
with a scene cannot be enumerated in advance. The owner
decides how the defect is ﬁxed: it can send a new brief to an
author, who revises its generator, or make the change itself.

described below. The owner propagates such corrections
itself: it publishes the corrected camera table or measure-
ments and re-issues the briefs of the components that used
the old values. The authors of these components revise their
generators before the next comparison. The owner decides
which reﬁts are needed. Each judge lists the differences it
sees and then names the render that is closer to the footage,
or a tie. Each revision is made on a copy of the current
6


scene, and its outcome is recorded as a lesson whether or
not the revision is accepted. The harness runs a comparison
procedure that we ﬁxed. The owner proposes the revision
and, before any judging, declares the render pairs to be com-
pared and the primary view group. A view group holds the
pairs of one kind of view, such as the original views or novel
views; the instructions ask the owner to declare 30–40 pairs
per view group (Sec. S5). The agent that proposes a revi-

report counts of ﬁndings. An object ﬂoats when the median
gap between its lowest vertices and the ﬁrst surface below
them exceeds 3 cm; glass and water never count as support.
Interpenetration counts overlapping triangle pairs between
objects of different components, apart from small resting
contacts. An emission ﬁnding is an emitting material on an
object that is not a declared luminaire. Albedo is measured
from the frames under the ﬁtted lighting, and a value out-
side 0.02–1 is a ﬁnding. Painted reﬂections are not detected
by a program: the instructions forbid them, and the owner

S13. Completion of unseen regions
Fig. S5 shows a whole scene. The video is ﬁlmed from a
boat on the river and shows the river fronts of the build-
ings (top row). The roofs, the sides of the buildings that
face away from the river, the courtyards, the canal behind
the arched gallery, the ground behind the quay wall, and the
city behind the frontage are never seen. We complete them:
these parts are produced by the same generators as the ob-
served parts, taken from the library or written during this
run, and are recorded as inferred (bottom row). This is one
reason why a generator is a general program for a family
rather than a ﬁt to one view: the generator that is ﬁtted to an
observed facade also builds its hidden sides, and it supplies
further buildings where the scene continues.
