LoongForge-Embodied: A Unified Framework
for Training Heterogeneous Embodied Models
LoongForge Team
Baidu, Inc.
Training Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World-Action Models (WAMs) is computationally ex-
pensive, while their heterogeneous architectures and fragmented training stacks make system optimizations
difficult to reuse across models. To address these challenges, we present LoongForge-Embodied, a native-
PyTorch framework that unifies and accelerates the training of heterogeneous embodied models. At its core,
LoongForge-Embodied introduces a common abstraction layer that decouples model-specific implementations
from shared training infrastructure, enabling cross-model reuse without constraining individual architectures
or execution requirements. Building on this unified infrastructure, the framework combines reusable accelera-
tion mechanisms with model-specific and hardware-aware optimization strategies across computation, execu-
tion, memory, data I/O, and distributed communication, including FP8-based communication optimizations for
DDP and FSDP. Beyond training acceleration, LoongForge-Embodied integrates a unified data infrastructure
with a pipeline for synthesizing robot data from egocentric data, and an efficient evaluation framework that
supports concurrency and optimized computation in simulator. Experiments across representative VLA and
WAM models demonstrate 1.47×–4.38× end-to-end training throughput improvements over their correspond-
ing open-source baselines, as well as 4.1×–23.2× faster evaluation under the same protocols.

1 Introduction
Recent advances in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World-Action Models (WAMs) have driven
embodied learning toward larger and more heterogeneous model architectures. VLA models such as Open-
VLA [21], π0.5 [18], and GR00T [40] integrate pretrained vision-language backbones with diverse action-
generation modules, while world-action approaches such as Unified World Models [64], Cosmos Policy
[22], and DreamZero [57] couple action prediction with video generation or future-state modeling. Mean-
while, large-scale multi-robot datasets [11, 20] have substantially expanded the volume and diversity of em-
bodied training data. Together, these developments give rise to heterogeneous training workloads with dis-
tinct computation graphs, memory footprints, communication patterns, and data-processing requirements.
Consequently, efficiently scaling embodied foundation models requires training infrastructure capable of
accommodating such diversity while delivering high performance.

Efficient training of heterogeneous VLA and WAM models faces two closely related challenges. First,
diverse model architectures and training objectives give rise to substantially different performance bottle-
necks. Variations in attention structures, computation graphs, activation-memory requirements, and dis-
tributed execution patterns shift the dominant costs among computation, memory, execution overhead, data
I/O, and communication. These bottlenecks also depend on hardware characteristics, making a fixed op-
timization strategy insufficient across models and hardware platforms. Second, the embodied learning
ecosystem remains fragmented. Despite recent efforts toward unified robot learning frameworks [7, 12],
many embodied foundation models still rely on independently developed training stacks, with customized
distributed execution, data processing, and evaluation pipelines. Although broadly reusable system opti-
mization mechanisms exist, including optimized attention [13, 15], graph compilation [3], CUDA Graphs
[38], and communication optimization [52], their effective integration and configuration often depend on
model architectures and hardware characteristics. Consequently, similar optimizations must be repeatedly
integrated, tuned, and validated across separate training stacks, limiting cross-model optimization reuse
and increasing engineering overhead. This raises a fundamental systems question: can heterogeneous
embodied models share a common training infrastructure while still enabling model-specific, high-
performance optimization?
We address this question with LoongForge-Embodied, a native-PyTorch framework that unifies and
accelerates the training of heterogeneous VLA and WAM models. The key design principle is to separate
model-specific computation from reusable training infrastructure. Although these models differ substan-
tially in architecture, learning objectives, and execution patterns, they share common system-level func-
tionalities, including training orchestration, distributed execution, data processing, optimization, checkpoint
management, and evaluation. To capture this commonality without imposing a fixed computational struc-
ture, LoongForge-Embodied introduces a unified abstraction layer built around configuration-driven model
registration, a lightweight model interface, and an extensible training engine. These abstractions allow
heterogeneous models to reuse shared training infrastructure while preserving their individual computa-
tion graphs and training requirements. More importantly, this separation provides a foundation for cross-
model optimization reuse: shared optimization mechanisms can be integrated once at the framework level,
while architecture-specific and hardware-aware optimization choices remain customizable. In this way,
LoongForge-Embodied combines the benefits of unified training infrastructure with the flexibility required
for model-specific, high-performance optimization.
Building on this unified abstraction, LoongForge-Embodied provides a composable acceleration toolkit
that combines reusable system-level optimization mechanisms with model-specific and hardware-aware
strategies. Guided by the performance bottlenecks of individual workloads, the framework integrates ef-
ficient attention kernels [13, 15, 46], mixed-precision computation, and fused operators and optimizers
to improve computational efficiency. Graph compilation [3] and configurable CUDA Graph capture [38]
reduce execution overhead, while activation checkpointing [9] and parameter-efficient adaptation address
memory constraints.
Precomputation, caching, and asynchronous checkpointing further mitigate data-
processing and I/O overhead. For distributed training, LoongForge-Embodied supports both Distributed
Data Parallel (DDP) and Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) with FP8-based communication optimiza-
tions, reducing gradient synchronization traffic in DDP and parameter AllGather traffic in FSDP, particu-
larly in bandwidth-constrained environments. Beyond communication compression, configurable synchro-
nization and prefetching strategies improve computation–communication overlap. Rather than imposing
a uniform optimization recipe, LoongForge-Embodied enables heterogeneous models to reuse shared ac-
celeration mechanisms while retaining the flexibility to select architecture-specific implementations and
hardware-aware configurations.
Beyond training acceleration, LoongForge-Embodied provides a unified data infrastructure and evalu-
ation framework. The data infrastructure of LoongForge-Embodied accommodates heterogeneous dataset

formats, model-specific processing requirements, and cross-embodiment data synthesis from egocentric vi-
sion to robot manipulation, while the evaluation framework enables composable model and benchmark in-
tegration through shared inference implementations. In addition, LoongForge-Embodied accelerates evalua-
tion through concurrency, reduced per-episode overhead, and reuse of computation. We evaluate LoongForge-
Embodied across representative VLA and WAM models with diverse architectures and training character-
istics. Compared with their corresponding open-source implementations, the framework achieves 1.47×–
4.38× improvements in end-to-end training throughput, demonstrating its effectiveness across heteroge-
neous embodied workloads. With the same protocols, the evaluation of LoongForge-Embodied achieves
4.1×–23.2× speedup over the baseline.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• A unified training framework. We introduce LoongForge-Embodied, a native-PyTorch framework
that unifies the training of heterogeneous VLA and WAM architectures through a common abstraction
layer, enabling reusable training infrastructure across models.
• System-level acceleration. We develop a composable acceleration stack spanning efficient attention,
compilation, CUDA Graphs, operator and optimizer fusion, data loading, and FP8-based distributed
communication for both FSDP and DDP.
• Evaluation acceleration. We accelerate the evaluation loop through concurrent episode execution,
reduced per-episode inference and rendering cost, and reuse of policy-independent computation, re-
ducing evaluation wall-clock time by 4.1×–23.2× under an unchanged benchmark protocol.
• Broad empirical validation. We evaluate LoongForge-Embodied across nine representative VLA/WAM
models and widely used embodied benchmarks, achieving 1.47×–4.38× training speedups while
maintaining task success rates comparable to official baselines.
2 Background and Motivation
This section introduces the modeling and training paradigms of VLA and WAM models, examines the
challenges arising from their heterogeneous architectures and workloads, and outlines the key design re-
quirements for a unified and efficient embodied training framework.

2.2 World-Action Models
WAMs extend action learning by incorporating predictive modeling of future world dynamics. While VLA
models primarily learn to generate actions conditioned on multimodal observations, WAMs additionally
learn the relationships between future world states and robot actions. Let Z+

future-world generation is not necessarily required during policy execution.

training without requiring explicit future-video generation at inference time. Cosmos 3 [41] further supports
multiple world-action generation tasks, including forward dynamics and action prediction. These formula-
tions share the objective of learning world-action relationships but differ in how world modeling and action
generation are coupled.
2.3 Training Challenges and System Requirements
Training large-scale VLA and WAM models is computationally expensive due to their complex multimodal
architectures and generative training objectives. Within individual models, vision-language backbones, ac-
tion experts, and world-modeling components exhibit different computational structures, attention patterns,
and gradient propagation paths. Across models, variations in component composition, training objectives,
and world-action coupling further introduce heterogeneous computation graphs and resource requirements.
4


Consequently, training performance is constrained by diverse bottlenecks in computation, execution, mem-
ory, communication, and data I/O, whose relative importance also varies across hardware configurations.
Addressing these bottlenecks requires targeted training optimizations, yet existing embodied models typi-
cally rely on fragmented, model-specific training stacks, making optimization techniques difficult to inte-
grate, reuse, and maintain across architectures. These challenges call for a unified training infrastructure
that enables efficient and reusable acceleration across heterogeneous embodied workloads.
Desiderata for Embodied Training Systems. The architectural heterogeneity and diverse training
workloads of VLA and WAM models motivate the following system design requirements:
D1. Unified. A common training infrastructure for heterogeneous VLA and WAM models, eliminating
fragmentation across model-specific training stacks.
D2. Flexible. Support for diverse model architectures, training objectives, and execution patterns with-
out imposing a fixed computational structure.
D3. Reusable. Cross-model reuse of optimization mechanisms, reducing redundant implementation,
integration, and tuning efforts.
D4. Model-specific. Targeted optimizations for specific model architectures, computational patterns,
and training characteristics.
D5. Hardware-aware. Hardware-specific optimization strategies tailored to different GPU architec-
tures, memory capacities, and interconnect bandwidths to enhance training efficiency.
D6. Faithful. Preservation of consistent training loss behavior and downstream task performance un-
der training acceleration.
D7. Data-integrated. Unified and extensible data infrastructure for multimodal data representation,
processing, loading, and generation.
D8. Evaluation-ready. Standardized evaluation interfaces and protocols enabling consistent and re-
producible evaluation across heterogeneous models and embodied benchmarks.
Guided by these desiderata, LoongForge-Embodied establishes a unified training infrastructure for het-
erogeneous VLA and WAM models. The following sections present the system architecture and its key
optimization techniques.
3 LoongForge-Embodied Overview
This section presents the overall architecture of LoongForge-Embodied and describes the unified model and
training abstractions that enable heterogeneous VLA and WAM models to share common training infras-
tructure while supporting model-specific execution and optimization.
3.1 System Architecture
Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture of LoongForge-Embodied. The central design principle is to
decouple model-specific computations from reusable training infrastructure. Rather than maintaining sepa-
rate training stacks for different VLA and WAM models, LoongForge-Embodied provides a unified system
in which heterogeneous models share common infrastructure while retaining their individual architectures,
training objectives, and execution requirements.

The system consists of several cooperating components. A configuration-driven model integration layer
manages model registration, construction, and model-specific interfaces. A unified data infrastructure pro-
cesses heterogeneous robotic datasets through common data representations and model-specific adaptations.
The shared training engine orchestrates training execution, while an acceleration toolkit provides reusable

These components are integrated into a common training workflow. Given a training configuration, the
framework resolves the corresponding model and trainer, constructs the data pipeline, and initializes the

distributed execution mechanisms. During training, the framework manages optimization, checkpointing,
and state recovery, while the evaluation subsystem provides a common interface for assessing trained mod-
els. This architecture separates model implementation from system-level execution, enabling both cross-
model infrastructure reuse and model-specific performance optimization.
3.2 Unified Model and Training Abstractions
Configuration-driven model integration. LoongForge-Embodied adopts a configuration-driven registra-
tion mechanism to integrate heterogeneous models without modifying the shared training infrastructure. A
structured configuration schema associates each model with its configuration types and corresponding data
configuration. A model registry maps model identifiers to implementation classes, which are instantiated
through a common construction interface. Model implementations are automatically discovered and regis-
6


tered, avoiding manual modifications to a centralized registry when introducing new models. Similarly, a
trainer registry selects the appropriate trainer implementation according to the training configuration. To-
gether, these mechanisms decouple model and trainer selection from their implementations.
Unified model contract. All integrated models expose a common interface consisting of model con-
struction and a forward operation that accepts a training batch and returns a loss and diagnostic metrics. This
contract allows the training engine to execute heterogeneous models without requiring knowledge of their
internal architectures or learning objectives. Model-specific behaviors are expressed through extensible in-
terfaces, including the selection of FP8 and LoRA target modules and model-specific distributed wrapping.
For example, individual models can exclude precision-sensitive components from FP8 conversion or restrict
optimization to selected network modules. Consequently, the framework standardizes how models interact
with the training infrastructure without constraining how they implement their computations.
Template-based training execution. The training engine follows a hierarchical template-method de-
sign that separates training orchestration from model-specific execution. At the highest level, a shared
training loop controls the iteration lifecycle. A common training-step abstraction handles operations such
as gradient initialization, optimizer execution, and gradient clipping, while an extensible forward-backward
interface encapsulates model-dependent computation. Most models reuse the default training procedure,
whereas specialized trainers can override selected execution hooks to support architecture-specific require-
ments, such as customized CUDA Graph execution. This design avoids duplicating complete training loops
while preserving flexibility for model-specific optimizations.
Composable execution and state management. LoongForge-Embodied further provides a common
mechanism for composing optimization and distributed execution strategies. Model wrapping follows an
explicitly ordered sequence: FP8 conversion, activation checkpointing, and distributed wrapping through
DDP or FSDP. This ordering preserves compatibility between module transformation and activation recom-
putation, preventing inconsistencies that may arise from applying these transformations in an incompatible
order. Models may specialize selected wrapping procedures through well-defined hooks without modifying
the shared execution framework. The same infrastructure also provides unified checkpoint management
across multiple serialization formats, supporting asynchronous saving and recovery of model, optimizer,
data-loading, and random-number-generator states. For distributed checkpoints, state restoration is coordi-
nated with model wrapping to support resharding across different distributed configurations. These mecha-
nisms maintain a common execution and recovery lifecycle across heterogeneous training workloads.
4 Training Acceleration
4.1 Computation Acceleration
Shared mechanisms and model-specific optimizations. LoongForge-Embodied separates reusable opti-
mization infrastructure from architecture-dependent implementations. Shared capabilities include optimizer
management, activation checkpointing, precision control, LoRA, and data loading, while attention kernels,
operator fusion, graph compilation, CUDA-graph capture, and feature caching admit model-specific im-
plementations. The framework unifies their configuration, registration, and compatibility management, al-
lowing heterogeneous models to compose shared mechanisms with specialized optimizations. We organize
these techniques by the bottlenecks they address.
Computation efficiency. Attention is a major computational bottleneck in VLA and WAM models.
LoongForge-Embodied provides a unified attention dispatch mechanism across PyTorch, FlashAttention-
2/3, and Transformer Engine, with SDPA fallback for unsupported backends or hardware.
Hardware-
aware selection accommodates the Ampere requirement of FlashAttention-2 and the Hopper requirement
of FlashAttention-3 [13, 46]. For model-specific attention patterns, FlexAttention supports compiled block
7


masks for joint attention and sequence packing, avoiding materialization of dense attention masks [15].
Additional efficiency comes from fused operators and optimizers. Model-specific fusion of rotary po-
sitional embedding (RoPE), normalization, modulation, and loss computation reduces kernel launches and
memory traffic [51]. A shared optimizer registry integrates fused AdamW implementations from PyTorch,
Transformer Engine, and Apex [30]; multi-dtype zero-redundancy optimizers shard optimizer states, and
foreach multi-tensor updates reduce per-tensor overhead.
Precision management supports module-wise BF16/FP16 computation with FP32 master weights, as
well as FP8 linear computation through two interchangeable backends: Transformer Engine modules and
TorchAO float8 linear layers, selected per model according to the backbone implementation and hardware
support [39, 42]. Under the Transformer Engine backend, configurable scaling recipes include blockwise,
current, delayed, and MXFP8, with FP8 autocast covering forward and backward computation [45]. This
compute-side FP8 optimization is distinct from the FP8 communication mechanism in Section 4.3. For con-
volutional workloads, cuDNN autotuning selects efficient algorithms for stable shapes, with explicit com-
patibility checks against deterministic execution and coordinated TF32 settings. Fake/meta implementations
further preserve torch.compile compatibility for custom cuDNN-backed kernels [10].
Beyond kernel selection and fusion, graph compilation and CUDA graphs reduce the cost of generating
and submitting kernels. torch.compile enables operator fusion and optimized kernel generation, with con-
figurable compilation scopes spanning the full forward pass, individual layers, or grouped layers with final
normalization and action heads. The grouped scope can facilitate overlap with DDP. Compilation mode, full-
graph execution, and dynamic-shape handling are configurable to limit graph breaks and recompilation [50].
Complementarily, CUDA graphs eliminate repeated CPU-side kernel launches through capture and replay.
The framework supports whole-iteration and per-microbatch capture, with warmup, sequence padding, and
optional in-graph gradient synchronization. Captured training graphs can also be pipelined with separate
frozen-backbone graphs, while subfunctions incompatible with capture are compiled separately and bypass
it [38]. Thus, compilation optimizes kernel generation, whereas CUDA graphs optimize kernel submission.
Memory efficiency. Activation checkpointing reduces memory consumption by recomputing selected
activations during backpropagation [9]. LoongForge-Embodied exposes module-level inclusion and exclu-
sion patterns, controlled through model configuration and consistently applied during distributed wrapping.
For parameter-efficient adaptation, shared LoRA integration supports configurable ranks, target modules,
and initialization, reducing trainable parameters, optimizer states, and gradient communication [17]. To-
gether with memory-efficient attention, these mechanisms address both activation and parameter memory
bottlenecks.
Data preparation and I/O. The framework reduces repeated preprocessing through a structured feature-
cache abstraction comprising configuration, artifact contracts, and dataset-level build, validation, and load-
ing utilities. Model-specific features, including text embeddings and episode statistics, can be precomputed
and reused through an explicitly configured cache directory [7]. The data pipeline additionally supports five
video-decoding backends, prefetching, persistent workers, and stateful loading. Asynchronous checkpoint-
ing overlaps disk I/O with training, with at most one in-flight save.
These optimizations are independently configurable and preserve the underlying training objectives.
Each model activates only the relevant mechanisms and supplies architecture-specific implementations
where necessary. Section 4.4 and Appendix A examine these model-dependent choices and their perfor-
mance implications.
4.2 Distributed Training Strategies
Unified Parallelism Abstraction. VLA and WAM models exhibit diverse parameter scales, architectural
structures, and memory requirements, making a single distributed training strategy insufficient for efficient
8


execution across models. LoongForge-Embodied addresses this heterogeneity through a unified parallelism
abstraction that decouples distributed execution from model-specific implementations. The framework sup-
ports DDP and FSDP2 through a common model-wrapping interface, allowing the parallelism strategy to
be selected through configuration without modifying the underlying model or training loop. DDP maintains
replicated model parameters and synchronizes gradients across workers, whereas FSDP2 partitions model
parameters, gradients, and optimizer states to reduce per-device memory consumption. The framework ad-
ditionally supports Hybrid Sharded Data Parallelism (HSDP), combining parameter sharding within device
groups with replication across groups. These strategies share the same distributed initialization, training-
loop control, and gradient accumulation infrastructure, enabling heterogeneous embodied models to adopt
different parallel configurations while reusing a common training pipeline.
Architecture-Aware Sharding and Memory Management. Efficient distributed execution requires
adapting parameter partitioning and memory management to the structural characteristics of individual mod-
els. Rather than imposing a fixed sharding granularity, LoongForge-Embodied provides an architecture-
aware FSDP2 wrapping mechanism that supports both explicit and automatic partitioning. Models may
specify module boundaries for fine-grained control, while the automatic strategy constructs sharding units
based on module structure and parameter counts, respecting architectural constraints that prevent certain
modules from being split. For models containing parameters with heterogeneous data types, the framework
further separates mixed-precision submodules into compatible sharding groups and organizes parameter
prefetching according to module execution order. These mechanisms allow sharding granularity and param-
eter materialization to be adapted to different model structures. Beyond parameter sharding, the framework
supports complementary memory-efficient configurations, including ZeRO-1 optimizer-state partitioning
under DDP and hybrid sharding under FSDP2. Together, these mechanisms provide flexible trade-offs
among parameter replication, memory consumption, and communication overhead without requiring sepa-
rate distributed training implementations for each model.
Precision-Aware Wrapping and Gradient Synchronization. Combining distributed parallelism with
mixed precision, activation checkpointing, and gradient accumulation introduces dependencies that must be
coordinated to preserve correct execution. LoongForge-Embodied separates original parameter precision,
computation precision, and FP8 conversion, allowing these mechanisms to be configured independently.
The framework enforces a consistent model-wrapping sequence in which FP8 conversion precedes acti-
vation checkpointing, followed by DDP or FSDP2 wrapping. This ordering is particularly important for
FP8 training, where applying checkpointing before precision conversion can introduce inconsistencies be-
tween tensors saved during the forward pass and those reconstructed during recomputation. The framework
also unifies gradient synchronization across parallel backends through an explicit synchronization context.
During gradient accumulation, communication is suppressed for intermediate micro-batches using backend-
specific mechanisms, while gradient synchronization is enabled for the final micro-batch before the op-
timizer update. This avoids redundant communication while preserving consistent gradient accumulation
semantics across DDP and FSDP2. By coordinating precision conversion, recomputation, and synchro-
nization within a shared execution pipeline, the framework enables these optimization mechanisms to be
composed across heterogeneous models.
Model-Specific Distributed Execution. Although the shared parallelism abstraction covers common
distributed training requirements, certain model architectures and execution patterns require specialized
strategies to achieve high performance. LoongForge-Embodied therefore exposes model-specific wrapping
and execution hooks that allow individual models to customize distributed behavior while retaining the
shared training infrastructure. For example, models employing distributed Muon optimization can explicitly
manage parameter ownership and FSDP sharding groups, enabling optimizer-specific parameter routing
and overlapping parameter prefetching with optimizer computation. Similarly, GR00T N1.7 integrates full-
iteration CUDA Graph execution with DDP by coordinating static gradient bucket construction with the
9


backward execution order. These extensions demonstrate that the unified distributed infrastructure does not
enforce identical parallel execution across models. Instead, it provides reusable parallelism mechanisms
while preserving the flexibility needed for architecture- and execution-specific performance optimizations.
4.3 Communication Optimization
Distributed training introduces substantial communication overhead, particularly for large-scale VLA and
WAM models, and its impact varies with model architectures, parallel configurations, and hardware in-
terconnects.
Rather than organizing communication optimization around individual parallel strategies,
LoongForge-Embodied targets the collective operations that occur at each stage of a training step: parame-
ter gathering in the forward and backward passes, gradient reduction in the backward pass, and parameter
synchronization in the optimizer step. For each class, the framework provides reusable mechanisms that
reduce transferred bytes or hide communication latency behind computation, while allowing model- and
hardware-specific configurations.
Parameter gathering in the forward and backward passes. When parameters are sharded, each
layer’s parameters are gathered through AllGather before they are used in the forward pass and, if released
after the forward pass, gathered again for backward computation. LoongForge-Embodied supports delta-
FP8 AllGather for this operation. Instead of repeatedly transmitting full-precision parameters, it quantizes
and communicates parameter updates relative to previously reconstructed values. Each rank applies the
same reconstructed increments to a shared reference, maintaining consistent parameter replicas while cor-
recting residual quantization errors across iterations. The implementation reuses existing AllGather buffers
as reference storage when the parameter layout permits, reducing additional memory overhead, and re-
tains full-precision communication for reference initialization and unsupported configurations. To hide the
remaining latency, configurable forward and backward prefetching issues each AllGather ahead of the cor-
responding parameter consumption, allowing communication to overlap with the computation of preceding
layers.
Gradient reduction in the backward pass. Gradients produced in the backward pass are synchronized
across data-parallel ranks, conventionally through AllReduce. LoongForge-Embodied provides configurable
communication hooks for this reduction, supporting standard AllReduce, reduced-precision communica-
tion, and FP8-based compression. The FP8 path decomposes AllReduce into asynchronous AllToAll and
AllGather operations: gradients are quantized blockwise and exchanged together with their scaling factors
through AllToAll, the received partial gradients are accumulated in FP32, and the reduced result is re-
quantized and redistributed through AllGather. This design reduces communication volume while limiting
numerical errors from repeated quantization. The hooks are selected independently of model implementa-
tions and fall back to standard AllReduce when message sizes, hardware capabilities, or memory constraints
make compression unsuitable. To avoid redundant reductions, cross-rank gradient synchronization during
gradient accumulation is deferred until the final micro-step, and an alternative gradient synchronization path
is provided for CUDA Graph execution.
Parameter synchronization in the optimizer step. The optimizer step can also involve parameter com-
munication, for example when optimizer computation is distributed across ranks, as in the distributed Muon
configuration described in the preceding subsection. For this stage, the framework supports asynchronous
parameter synchronization during optimizer execution, so that parameter exchanges overlap with optimizer
computation rather than being serialized after the update.
Hardware-aware adaptation. The benefit of these mechanisms is not uniform: communication com-
pression does not necessarily translate into lower training latency, as it depends on message sizes, commu-
nication scheduling, and interconnect characteristics. LoongForge-Embodied therefore exposes communi-
cation precision, compression thresholds, and synchronization strategies as configuration for each class of
10


collective, allowing different models and hardware platforms to select suitable optimizations. Unsupported
or inefficient compression paths fall back to standard collectives, preserving training compatibility while
avoiding unnecessary communication overhead.
4.4 Model-Specific Optimization Strategies
Although all models share the reusable acceleration mechanisms described in the preceding subsections,
profiling shows that the dominant training bottleneck differs substantially across architectures. We group
these bottlenecks into four categories: fragmented execution, in which short kernels and host synchroniza-
tion leave the GPU intermittently idle; memory–compilation coupling, in which activation memory, batch
size, compilation granularity, and communication overlap constrain one another; data delivery, in which
CPU-side decoding and preprocessing stall the GPU; and distributed communication, in which gradient or
parameter collectives are exposed on the critical path, particularly over bandwidth-constrained interconnects.
Table 1 summarizes, for each evaluated model, the dominant bottleneck and the principal model-specific op-
timizations. Per-model descriptions of the architectures, bottlenecks, and optimization designs are provided
in Appendix A, and the corresponding experiments are reported in Sections 8.7 and 8.8.
These model-specific optimizations follow a common design principle: rather than maintaining a sepa-
rately engineered training stack for each model, LoongForge-Embodied derives each model’s optimization
recipe by composing the reusable mechanisms described above, guided by profiling. For each model, we
first profile the original training implementation to locate the dominant bottleneck on the critical path, then
select and configure the mechanisms that address it—such as the compilation granularity, kernel-fusion tar-
gets, distributed strategy, communication precision, and data-path configuration—and finally verify numer-
ical fidelity against the reference implementation (Section 8.3). Model-specific code is confined to what the
reusable mechanisms cannot express, such as restructuring data-dependent control flow into static graphs or
adapting parameter resharding to the gradient-accumulation schedule. Consequently, the same mechanism
may be applied differently, or not at all, depending on where profiling locates the bottleneck.
5 Unified and Extensible Data Infrastructure
The heterogeneity of embodied models extends beyond their architectures to the data representations and
processing procedures required for training. Different VLA and WAM models rely on diverse dataset for-
mats, temporal observation structures, action representations, sampling policies, and preprocessing oper-
ations. In model-specific training stacks, these requirements are often embedded directly into individual
data-loading implementations, resulting in duplicated functionality and making data infrastructure difficult
to reuse across models. A unified training framework must therefore accommodate heterogeneous data re-
quirements without imposing a fixed data-processing procedure or altering the training inputs expected by
individual models.
LoongForge-Embodied addresses these challenges through a unified and extensible data infrastructure
that separates storage formats from model-specific data processing. Heterogeneous datasets are exposed
through a common sample contract, while model-specific behaviors are introduced through registries and
orthogonal extension interfaces. On top of these abstractions, the framework integrates efficient data de-
livery, validated caching, and stateful resumption to support high-throughput and reproducible training. It
further incorporates Ego2Robot [54] to synthesize robot-compatible trajectories from human interaction
data, with the generated data directly integrated into the same training pipeline.

5.1 Unified Data Abstraction and Contract
Format-independent data abstraction. Embodied training datasets differ in their storage organization,
metadata conventions, and mechanisms for retrieving multimodal observations and actions. Supporting
these differences through separate model-specific loaders would couple data storage to model implemen-
tations, limiting the reuse of shared training infrastructure. LoongForge-Embodied instead introduces a
format-independent dataset abstraction that separates the physical representation of data from the logical
samples consumed during training (Figure 2).
The framework currently supports LeRobot and HDF5 datasets through a common data-loading inter-
face, with automatic adaptation across LeRobot versions 2.0, 2.1, and 3.0. The underlying storage format is
selected independently of the model architecture. Each dataset implementation is responsible for interpret-
ing its storage-specific organization and constructing samples according to the shared representation, while
downstream training components operate without requiring knowledge of the original dataset format. This
separation allows different storage backends to share the same training infrastructure.
Unified training-facing contract. To decouple data storage from model execution, LoongForge-Embodied
defines a common sample structure consisting of visual observations (observation.images.*), robot
states (observation.state), actions (action), task information (task), and temporal metadata (timestamp).
The contract standardizes how these information categories are exposed without requiring all models to use
identical temporal windows, action horizons, or observation configurations.

5.2 Model-Specific Data Adaptation
A unified sample representation does not imply uniform data requirements. VLA and WAM models may
differ substantially in their temporal context, sample indexing, observation transformations, and batching
strategies. Directly embedding these differences into the shared dataset implementation would introduce
model-specific branches and undermine extensibility. LoongForge-Embodied therefore provides two com-
plementary mechanisms for injecting model-specific data semantics while preserving the common infras-
tructure.
Registry-based processing extensions. The framework maintains three independently extensible reg-
istries, keyed by model type, for transformation builders, preprocessing and collation components, and sam-
pling strategies. These registries separate the construction of model-specific data-processing components
from their invocation by the shared training pipeline. New implementations are discovered automatically
through module discovery, avoiding modifications to the central registration logic when additional models
are integrated.
Orthogonal dataset-level adaptation. Beyond sample transformations, models may require different
temporal retrieval and indexing semantics. To support these requirements, the LeRobot dataset abstraction
exposes three independent extension interfaces: delta_timestamps_fn, length_fn, and index_map_fn.
They respectively control the temporal observations retrieved for a sample, the logical length of the dataset,
and the mapping from logical sample indices to underlying data indices. The base dataset implementation
contains no model-specific branching logic, and models override only the interfaces corresponding to their
particular requirements.
For example, FastWAM customizes only temporal retrieval to construct multi-frame geometric obser-
vations, while retaining the default dataset length and indexing behavior. In contrast, Wall-OSS modifies
the dataset length and index mapping to implement its train/test partitioning without changing the temporal
13


retrieval mechanism. These two cases illustrate how different model requirements can be accommodated
independently: temporal context can be modified without changing dataset indexing, and the logical sample
space can be modified without changing observation retrieval.
5.3 Efficient and Reproducible Data Delivery
Beyond functional compatibility, a shared data system must provide sufficient throughput for GPU-intensive
training while preserving the data semantics of the original model implementations. LoongForge-Embodied
addresses these requirements through coordinated data-loading optimizations, cache validation, stateful re-
covery, and reference-equivalence testing.
High-throughput data loading. The framework combines block-oriented sample dispatch, prefetch-
ing, and host-memory staging to reduce data-loading overhead and overlap data preparation with model
execution. For video-based observations, a unified decoding interface supports five video backends, allow-
ing backend-specific decoding implementations to be selected without changing the surrounding dataset and
training logic. Pinned-memory handling additionally includes fallback behavior to preserve compatibility
when the preferred memory-staging path is unavailable.
Repeated preprocessing is reduced through the shared feature-cache mechanism introduced in Sec-
tion 4.1. Caching is a framework-level capability rather than a model-specific implementation: it is enabled
through configuration without changes to model code, and any integrated dataset can precompute and reuse
representations through it. The savings are largest for video-heavy workloads such as DreamZero, where
cache-aware retrieval avoids repeatedly decoding frames whose required representations have already been
generated. Cache reuse is guarded by compatibility checks covering the dataset manifest, preprocessing
configurations, and associated hashes. In particular, changes to operations such as cropping, resizing, or
color jitter are detected rather than silently reusing representations generated under different preprocessing
settings. This ensures that performance-oriented caching does not inadvertently change the intended training
inputs.
Stateful and deterministic resumption. Checkpointing a training run requires more than restoring
model parameters and optimizer states. The data pipeline must also recover its sample progression and
stochastic processing state; otherwise, restarting a run may change the subsequent training data even when
the model state has been restored correctly.
LoongForge-Embodied extends checkpoint recovery to the data subsystem through coordinated restora-
tion of sampler state, iterator progress, and dataset-level augmentation random-number-generator states. The
sampler maintains a minimal persistent state and reconstructs sampling behavior deterministically, while
the stateful iterator records sample-yielding progress. Together with the restored augmentation state, these
mechanisms support continuation of the data stream from its saved execution state, rather than restarting
sampling from the beginning of an epoch.
Reference-equivalence validation. A further challenge of unifying previously independent training
stacks is preventing silent changes to the samples observed by a model. Such changes can arise from dif-
ferences in temporal indexing, preprocessing, data types, or action representations, even when the resulting
tensors appear structurally compatible.
To address this issue, LoongForge-Embodied treats existing model-specific data implementations as
executable references and validates the unified pipeline against their outputs. For X-VLA, the unified data
pipeline has been verified to produce byte-wise identical training samples to the reference implementation.
This reference-equivalence approach provides a concrete correctness criterion for model integration and
helps isolate the effects of training system optimizations from unintended changes in data preprocessing.

5.4 Cross-Embodiment Data Synthesis with Ego2Robot
The preceding abstractions enable heterogeneous robot datasets to share a common training pipeline. How-
ever, the availability of robot-specific demonstrations remains a major constraint for embodied learning.
Human egocentric interaction data provide an additional source of manipulation experience, but differences
in kinematics, coordinate conventions, and embodiment prevent such trajectories from being directly used
for robot training.
LoongForge-Embodied re-implements and integrates Ego2Robot [54], a cross-embodiment data synthe-
sis pipeline that converts human interaction trajectories into robot-compatible demonstrations. The pipeline
organizes synthesis into four main stages: interaction reconstruction, human-to-robot action alignment,
robot retargeting, and trajectory validation and serialization.
During action alignment, human interaction representations are transformed into robot-compatible end-
effector targets using geometric alignment procedures, including virtual-finger alignment and tool-center-
point pose transformations. The retargeting stage then searches for feasible robot configurations and selects
compatible candidates through joint evaluation of the corresponding solutions. A registry-based embodi-
ment interface supports multiple robot morphologies, allowing robot-specific retargeting implementations
to be incorporated without restructuring the shared synthesis pipeline.
The generated trajectories undergo structural validation before being serialized into the native LeRobot
v3.0 format. Importantly, these outputs can be consumed directly by the unified data-loading infrastruc-
ture described in Section 5.1, without an additional format-conversion stage. Ego2Robot thus extends the
framework beyond the unified consumption of existing datasets to the generation and integration of new
cross-embodiment training data, forming a closed data-processing workflow within LoongForge-Embodied.
Experimental results are reported in Section 8.5.
5.5 Quality Assessment of Synthesized Trajectories
Synthesized cross-embodiment data has no ground truth to compare against. Unlike a teleoperated demon-
stration, a converted trajectory has no recorded robot execution that defines the correct answer, and the
structural validation of the preceding stage establishes only that the output is well formed. Downstream task
success measures the data indirectly and only after a full training run has completed, which is both too slow
and too coarse to guide the pipeline itself. A direct measure of synthesis quality is needed, computed on the
pipeline output itself and reported per episode.
LoongForge-Embodied therefore introduces an assessment standard for synthesized trajectories, orga-
nized along three axes (Table 2). Kinematic feasibility asks whether the retargeted joint trajectory is ex-
ecutable, through four lower-is-better metrics computed programmatically from the robot state and action
streams, with no model in the loop. Visual quality asks whether the rendered episode is a faithful pic-
ture of the interaction, covering event correctness, object integrity, residual human appearance, and spatial
consistency. Motion naturalness asks whether the resulting robot motion reads as purposeful over time, cov-
ering coherence, continuity, rhythm, path directness, pause behavior, and two-arm coordination. The three
are kept separate because they fail separately: a trajectory can respect every joint limit and still render as a
video in which a human hand performs the manipulation, and a visually clean episode can carry joint motion
no robot would execute.
The ten visual and motion metrics are graded on a three-level ordinal scale on which level 2 is best, by
a multimodal model that inspects sampled evidence frames together with the corresponding source frames
for visual quality, and continuous motion samples with short raw-frame windows for motion naturalness.
For reporting, the levels are mapped linearly onto a normalized score in which levels 0, 1, and 2 contribute
0%, 50%, and 100%, so that the score of a metric is (0.5n1 + n2)/(n0 + n1 + n2) over the episodes that

received a level; episodes the grader marks unknown or not applicable are excluded from both numerator
and denominator, and the number of scored episodes is reported alongside the score. The four kinematic
metrics stay in their original units and are macro-averaged over episodes. They are never folded into the
ordinal scale, because a violation rate and a perceptual grade are not commensurable quantities.

6 Evaluation Framework
Evaluating heterogeneous VLA and WAM models presents challenges beyond those encountered during
training. Different benchmarks expose distinct observation formats, action spaces, control protocols, and
16


runtime dependencies, while individual models require different input representations and action execu-
tion strategies. Maintaining separate evaluation implementations for each model–benchmark combination
introduces redundant integration efforts and risks inconsistencies between training and evaluation. To ad-
dress these challenges, LoongForge-Embodied extends its unified model abstraction to evaluation through
a training-aligned architecture and composable adaptation mechanisms. This design enables heterogeneous
models to share a common evaluation infrastructure while preserving model-specific execution behaviors
and benchmark-specific evaluation protocols.
6.1 Training-Aligned Evaluation Architecture
A central design principle of LoongForge-Embodied is to reuse the same model inference implementation
across training and evaluation. Rather than maintaining separate evaluation-specific model implementations,
the framework exposes a unified predict_action interface that accepts visual observations, language in-
structions, robot states, and dataset statistics. The evaluation framework invokes this interface through a
generic policy wrapper, allowing model-specific inference logic, including input processing, state normal-
ization, and action prediction, to remain within the shared model implementation. In particular, normaliza-
tion and denormalization associated with a model’s training configuration are handled by the model itself
rather than reimplemented in individual benchmark adapters. This alignment reduces duplicated inference
logic and minimizes the risk of inconsistencies introduced by independently maintained training and evalu-
ation implementations.
To accommodate heterogeneous runtime dependencies, LoongForge-Embodied adopts a decoupled client–
server architecture that separates benchmark execution from model inference. The benchmark client man-
ages environment initialization, observations, action execution, and episode termination, while an indepen-
dent inference server handles model loading and prediction. The two processes communicate through a
lightweight WebSocket-based RPC protocol using msgpack-numpy for structured numerical data exchange,
with protocol-version validation during connection establishment. This separation allows benchmark envi-
ronments and model inference services to operate with independent software dependencies, avoiding con-
flicts between simulation libraries and model-specific deep learning stacks.
Building on this architecture, the framework organizes evaluation into a standardized four-stage pipeline.
First, a benchmark-specific observation adapter converts raw environment observations into a canonical rep-
resentation. Second, a model-specific payload builder transforms the canonical observations into the inputs
expected by the corresponding model. Third, the inference server executes the shared predict_action in-
terface through RPC and returns the predicted actions. Finally, an action decoder converts the model outputs
into the action representation required by the benchmark environment. A unified entry point and configura-
tion interface coordinate these components across the benchmarks and model implementations currently in-
tegrated into the evaluation framework—five benchmarks (LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboTwin, and
ManiSkill) and seven model implementations—without requiring dedicated evaluation pipelines for individ-
ual model–benchmark combinations (Fig. 3). These counts describe the integration scope of the evaluation
framework and are independent of the models and benchmarks used in the experiments of Section 8.

Embodied introduces registry-based, composable adaptation mechanisms. Three complementary registries
manage model construction, input payload generation, and action decoding, respectively. Model factories
instantiate the corresponding inference implementations, while payload builders translate canonical obser-
vations into model-specific inputs according to declared model capabilities. Action decoding is further

Beyond input and output representations, embodied models also differ in action execution semantics;
enforcing a single strategy would either add redundant inference or alter a model’s intended closed-loop
behavior. LoongForge-Embodied therefore supports two execution modes, selected by a per-model cache
switch declared in its payload builder:
• Open-loop, cached execution. The client still issues one request per environment step, but the server
runs model inference only when a new action chunk is required and otherwise returns the next action
from the cached chunk. The model thus does not observe intermediate environment states while a
chunk is being executed, and redundant inference for chunk-based policies is avoided.
• Closed-loop, uncached execution. The cache is bypassed, so the model is invoked at every environ-
ment step with the latest observation. This preserves the native behavior of models that condition on
every observation and maintain their own action queues, such as DreamZero and LingBot-VA.
Additional model- and benchmark-specific parameters, such as embodiment identifiers, replanning horizons,
and action-step limits, are applied within the same execution framework, so evaluation orchestration remains
unified without imposing a uniform control policy.
A unified evaluation interface must also preserve the protocols defined by individual benchmarks so
that reported results remain comparable with official implementations. Benchmarks, however, differ in how
much of their evaluation loop they expose. For benchmarks that provide a programmatic environment API
(LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, and ManiSkill), a native runner owns the loop: it creates, resets, and steps
the environment, collects observations, and applies actions itself. Other benchmarks, such as RoboTwin,
ship an official evaluator that owns environment construction and rollout and exposes only a policy-plugin

callback; observations are delivered to that callback, but no external API is available for driving the environ-
ment. For these benchmarks, a bridge hosts the shared observation adapter, payload builder, policy client,
and action decoder inside the official evaluator as a policy plugin, so that the official runner drives the envi-
ronment while model inference still passes through the same shared pipeline. The two modes reuse the same
adaptation and inference components and differ only in which side owns the environment loop. To maintain
evaluation fidelity, we validate the protocol-critical components—observation preprocessing, prompt con-
struction, coordinate and action transformations, initialization behavior, and episode control rules—against
their official counterparts, and compare the resulting success rates with official reference runs (Table 3).
Such checks are particularly important because seemingly minor implementation differences can substan-
tially affect robotic task success rates, and they reduce the risk that unified adaptation introduces unintended
changes to benchmark semantics. Completeness and cross-run reproducibility checks on recorded episodes
further guard the reported results.
6.3 Evaluation Acceleration
Evaluation is an essential part of the embodied model training lifecycle. Reliable performance estimates
require repeated trials across many tasks, so a complete evaluation can consume a substantial share of the
lifecycle. LoongForge-Embodied accelerates evaluation while preserving the benchmark protocol: replan-
ning intervals, step limits, planned episode counts per task, and expert-screening criteria remain unchanged.
Only the execution of the evaluation loop is optimized.
Evaluation time has three main sources: the cost of executing episodes, including the degree of concur-
rency; fixed startup costs, such as starting processes and loading model weights; and work that depends on
the benchmark, task, and seed, but not on the policy being evaluated. LoongForge-Embodied addresses each
source separately.
• Parallel orchestration. A shared coordinator schedules episodes across S = G × N slots, where G
is the number of inference servers and N is the number of simulator workers attached to each server.
Episodes are dispatched in chunks to amortize process startup and scene initialization. Units are
queued longest-expected-duration first; for long-horizon tasks, every chunk is placed ahead of all
short-task work and split into smaller chunks, so the makespan is bounded by the long-task tail. Slots
are refilled as soon as a chunk completes. The capacity of each pool depends on the simulator’s host
resource: CPU-sim pools are limited by available CPU cores, while GPU-sim pools share device
memory with the inference server. For GPU-sim, the coordinator checks the memory budget before
a run starts. It also limits CPU threads per simulator process to prevent oversubscription when many
simulators share a host. Inference servers remain persistent across chunks, so model weights are
loaded once per run.
• Lower per-episode cost. The client holds a predicted action chunk and replans every K steps, reduc-
ing inference requests from one per environment step to one per chunk. It also skips camera rendering
within a chunk, since the model does not consume intermediate observations while that chunk exe-
cutes. On the server, requests from different slots can be merged into a batched forward pass. This
is enabled only for models whose inference cost is a bottleneck: for example, batching brings little
benefit when the mean batch size remains close to one, but can improve throughput when long model
requests leave the device idle between requests. Disabling per-step traces and video logging further
avoids serialization and video-encoding work during evaluation.
• Reusable policy-independent work. Some work depends only on the benchmark, task configuration,
and seed, and can therefore be computed without an inference server and reused across policies. For
19


RoboTwin, offline expert screening records accepted seeds and task attributes needed to construct the
scene. For LIBERO, scene snapshots capture the random-number generator state before each envi-
ronment reset. Under per-task seeding the scene depends on how many resets have already occurred
in the process, so snapshots are what allow an episode to run in any worker; they therefore enable
episode-level sharding rather than merely saving scene-rebuild time. These artifacts are reused across
later runs with the same task configuration. Their one-time generation cost is excluded from the eval-
uation times reported in Table 4; because the reference path repeats expert screening on each run, the
RoboTwin precomputation cost is recovered from the second run onward.
The simulator count N and the server-side batch size share the same GPU memory budget, so they
must be tuned together. Increasing N can reduce the memory available for batching, and a larger simu-
lator pool does not necessarily improve throughput once the pool is saturated. Raising N while shrinking
the merge batch size can therefore trigger out-of-memory scheduling units and lengthen a run rather than
shorten it; Section 8.4 reports a measured instance. The degree of concurrency and batching is calibrated
for each deployment, since throughput saturation depends on host core count, device memory, model size,
and rendering settings.
The protocol is held constant across the reference and accelerated paths, but individual episode outcomes
are not required to match. Because episodes run concurrently, an unstable scene may be replaced and the
evaluated seed set may consequently change. We therefore assess the correctness of accelerated evaluation
by comparing aggregate success rates under the same protocol, rather than by requiring episode-by-episode
reproduction (see Section 8.4).
7 Related Work
Embodied foundation models: VLAs and WAMs. Embodied foundation models have developed along
two complementary routes. VLA models, reviewed in [32], couple a vision-language backbone with an
action decoder and learn from demonstration data, whereas WAMs [31, 53] learn a generative model of the
world from which actions are derived. Within the VLA family, the field has moved from transformer policies
over demonstrated trajectories [5, 49] and web-scale vision-language transfer [6] toward open VLM-based
policies [21]; what mainly distinguishes the current generation is not scale but training-compute design. π0
and its successors [4, 18] replace a discrete action head with a flow-matching action expert, whose multi-step
denoising adds a distinct computation path to every training iteration; GR00T N1 [40] decouples a frozen
vision-language model from a separate action-generation module, changing which components receive gra-
dients; and RDT2 [29] scales a 7B VLM with flow matching for cross-embodiment training. WAMs are
even more heterogeneous in their generative machinery: GR-2 [8] conditions action generation on predicted
future video, unified world models [64] couple video and action diffusion within a single denoising pro-
cess, Cosmos Policy [22] transfers a video foundation model to control and planning, and recent systems
[24, 57, 59] explore diverse world-action couplings, from joint generation of world and actions to world
prediction as a training auxiliary, each with a different generative computation graph and memory pro-
file. These architectural choices translate directly into heterogeneous training workloads: attention patterns,
computation graphs, memory footprints, and training objectives (behavior cloning versus multi-step denois-
ing) vary across models, so no single optimization recipe applies uniformly and the training infrastructure
must accommodate this diversity.
Embodied training frameworks. Beyond model architectures, the community has built infrastruc-
ture that unifies model development, data processing, training, and evaluation. General-purpose frame-
works standardize training pipelines: LeRobot [7] integrates data collection, storage, training, and inference
for end-to-end robot learning, building on the modular imitation-learning foundations of robomimic [33];
20


RLinf-VLA [61] unifies VLA training with reinforcement-learning algorithms and simulators in a single
framework; and VLA Foundry [34] and FluxVLA Engine [26] extend this unification to the full LLM-to-
VLA training stack and the data-to-deployment workflow, respectively. Modular frameworks expose com-
posable components: StarVLA [12] provides pluggable VLM backbones and action heads in a Lego-like
design, StarVLA-α [56] reduces design complexity for reproducible training and comparison, AlphaBrain
[2] extends StarVLA with world-model backbones and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning, and OpenWAM
[55] modularizes backbone, representation, and video-action coupling for WAM pretraining. Alongside
these, model-family-specific pipelines such as openpi [18] and Isaac-GR00T [40] provide deeply integrated
training, fine-tuning, and deployment for their respective model series. Data and evaluation ecosystems
complement these frameworks: Open X-Embodiment [11] and DROID [20] aggregate large-scale hetero-
geneous demonstrations, and LIBERO [28], SimplerEnv [25], and RoboTwin [37] provide standardized
simulation benchmarks. In sum, existing frameworks have achieved substantial functional unification of
model interfaces, data pipelines, and evaluation flows, and some offer specialized training efficiency within
specific model families or training paradigms. However, they primarily emphasize functional modularity or
efficiency within such specific scopes, leaving systematic reuse of architecture-aware optimizations across
heterogeneous VLA and WAM workloads insufficiently explored; this is precisely the abstraction level at
which our system operates.
Training acceleration and distributed systems. The deep learning systems community has produced
a rich, mature body of optimization techniques covering compute, memory, and communication, organized
in system-level surveys [16, 23]. At the distributed level, Megatron-LM [47] introduced tensor and model
parallelism, ZeRO [44] shards optimizer states, gradients, and parameters, PyTorch FSDP [62] provides
PyTorch-native sharded data parallel training at scale, and TorchTitan [27] unifies these PyTorch-native
techniques in a single modular system. At the kernel level, FlashAttention [13, 14, 46] and its latest succes-
sor [60] make attention IO-aware and parallel, FlexAttention [15] compiles programmable attention patterns,
and Triton [51], torch.compile [3], and CUDA Graphs [38] address operator fusion, graph compilation,
and launch overhead. Memory and precision are addressed by gradient checkpointing [9], mixed preci-
sion [35], and FP8 formats [36, 43], while communication optimization includes gradient quantization and
compression [1], error-feedback mechanisms [19], ZeRO++’s quantized collective communication [52], and
planning and runtime adaptation for collective operations [48]. While existing systems provide powerful op-
timization primitives and composable training mechanisms, adapting and coordinating these optimizations
across heterogeneous VLA and WAM architectures remains a model-dependent engineering effort. TorchTi-
tan [27] is the closest system-level work to ours in spirit: it unifies PyTorch-native training techniques into
a modular system whose components compose, but it is designed around the homogeneous structure of
decoder-only language models, and its optimization interface does not couple to architecture-specific char-
acteristics of multimodal embodied models. Our contribution is therefore not a new optimization primitive
but an abstraction layer that adapts, composes, and reuses existing optimizations across VLA and WAM
workloads, making architecture-aware acceleration a reusable capability rather than per-model engineering.

8.1 Setup
Models and reference implementations.
We evaluate representative embodied foundation models span-
ning the model families discussed in Section 4.4: the VLAs π0.5 [18], GR00T N1.6 and N1.7 [40], X-
VLA [63], and Wall-OSS-0.5 [58]; the WAMs FastWAM [59], LingBot-VA [24], and DreamZero [57]; and
the omnimodal world model Cosmos 3 [41]. End-to-end speedups are reported for all nine models. Each
model is compared against its corresponding publicly released training implementation, namely the LeRobot
21


PyTorch implementation [7] for π0.5, the official Isaac-GR00T implementation for GR00T, and the official
or community releases for the remaining models. Each comparison uses the same model architecture and
training data. When a public release does not ship a configuration that matches the evaluated setting, we
adapt it minimally, keep its training code path, and note this in the corresponding appendix (for DreamZero,
Appendix A.7).
Benchmarks.
We evaluate downstream task performance on three simulation benchmarks. LIBERO [28]
comprises four task suites, Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long, which respectively probe spatial reasoning,
object generalization, goal-conditioned behavior, and long-horizon manipulation. RoboTwin [37] evaluates
bimanual manipulation under Easy and Hard settings. SimplerEnv [25] evaluates policies in simulated
counterparts of the real-robot Bridge and Fractal evaluation setups. For each benchmark, we follow the
official task definitions and evaluation protocols, including episode counts and action-execution settings,
and report success rates in percent. Each model is evaluated on the benchmarks for which its community
release provides the training data and hyperparameter configurations required for reproduction.
8.2 End-to-End Training Speedup
We evaluate the end-to-end training efficiency of LoongForge-Embodied across nine representative embod-
ied foundation models, covering diverse VLA and WAM architectures. For each model, we compare the
training throughput of LoongForge-Embodied against its corresponding open-source implementation and
report the resulting speedup, as shown in Figure 4.
LoongForge-Embodied achieves substantial training acceleration across all evaluated models, with speedups
ranging from 1.47× to 4.38×. Specifically, it achieves 3.00× on π0.5, 2.31× on GR00T N1.6, 1.79× on
GR00T N1.7, 1.79× on X-VLA, and 1.47× on Wall-OSS-0.5. For DreamZero (Wan2.2-TI2V-5B), Fast-
WAM, and LingBot-VA, the framework delivers speedups of 4.38×, 2.62×, and 2.20×, respectively. For
the omnimodal world model Cosmos 3, the speedup is 1.86×. Among these models, DreamZero exhibits
the largest improvement, reaching a 4.38× increase in end-to-end training throughput.
These results demonstrate that LoongForge-Embodied consistently improves training efficiency across
heterogeneous embodied models, despite substantial differences in their architectures and computational
characteristics. More importantly, the improvements are achieved at the end-to-end training level, demon-
strating that the combination of reusable system-level optimizations and model-specific optimization strate-
gies translates into substantial practical training acceleration.

8.7 Optimization Analysis
Section 4.4 groups the dominant training bottlenecks of the evaluated models into four categories (Table 1):
fragmented execution, memory–compilation coupling, data delivery, and distributed communication. This
subsection examines the first three using representative experiments; communication is examined in Sec-
tion 8.8. Complete per-model analyses, including additional profiles, ablations, and fidelity checks, are
provided in Appendix A.
Execution-bound training: GR00T N1.7.
Profiling of GR00T N1.7 shows that forward computation
of the frozen Qwen3-VL backbone dominates the step and that 78.2% of kernel launches last less than
20 µs, indicating that the workload is limited by fragmented execution rather than by individual matrix-
29


multiplication kernels. Table A2 and Fig. 8 report the throughput improvement obtained by successively en-
abling data, computation, and execution optimizations. The official Isaac-GR00T implementation achieves
58.44 samples/s/GPU in the supplied ablation setting.
Episode-aware indexing and deterministic asynchronous shard prefetching increase throughput to 64.64
samples/s/GPU (1.11×). Caching multimodal metadata, bypassing the unused vocabulary projection, and

Data-path bottlenecks.
For several models, a substantial part of the realized speedup comes from the
data path rather than from GPU computation. For DreamZero, first-frame-only processing (decoding only
the first frame per view when video latents come from the cache) reduces iteration time by 26.83% over
the full steady-state window (8.28% in an early window and 36.77% in a late window with input stalls in
the full-frame control) and lowers peak host shared-memory usage from 43.48 to 21.82 GiB; because the
control includes data-loading stalls, this gain should not be interpreted as a pure GPU compute improvement.

from 45% to 5%, a relative share rather than an end-to-end speedup. For X-VLA, increasing data-loading
parallelism from two to eight workers reduces step time from 1,081 to 704 ms, after which the steady-
state profile exhibits approximately 98% GPU busy time and 97.5% overlap between gradient AllReduce
and backward computation, indicating that data delivery rather than communication was the appropriate
optimization target. These measurements come from different models and protocols and should not be
compared directly.

Low-precision communication.
DreamZero’s Wan2.2-TI2V-5B recipe uses Delta-FP8 parameter All-
Gather under FSDP2, which transfers quantized parameter updates while preserving BF16 computation. To
understand the communication mechanism, we profile Delta-FP8 on the current FSDP2 recipe (Figure 11).
FP8 delta transfer decreases the AllGather time union from 308.39 to 179.72 ms per step, while its exposed
time falls from 142.31 to 31.90 ms/step. Correspondingly, the measured overlap ratio rises from 53.85%
to 82.25%. The quantization and dequantization kernels account for 24.59 ms/step in the profile, which
does not negate the reduction in communication exposure. In a separate, unprofiled 100-step comparison,
iteration time falls from 0.7410 to 0.6404 s, corresponding to a 1.157× speedup. This distinction between
the profiling trace and the steady-state timing is important: the reduction in exposed AllGather time is a
mechanism-level observation, not itself the end-to-end speedup.
Under DDP, FastWAM quantizes gradient buckets into block-scaled FP8 and exchanges them through
AllToAll–AllGather. In paired eight-GPU measurements, this lowers normalized communication traffic to
0.508 of BF16 AllReduce, reduces median iteration time from 1090.6 to 962.4 ms, and increases throughput
from 14.671 to 16.626 samples/s/GPU (13.3%).

Numerical fidelity.
The low-precision communication paths above show small deviations within short
training windows. For DreamZero, the 100-step total-loss mean absolute relative error (MARE) between
the Delta-FP8 enabled and disabled variants is 0.1084%, with no NaNs or skipped updates. FastWAM’s
FP8 gradient communication was reported to align in training loss with BF16, but per-step numerical dif-
ferences were not provided. These metrics differ across models and are therefore not combined, and neither
establishes long-horizon convergence or downstream task equivalence.
9 Conclusion
We presented LoongForge-Embodied, a native-PyTorch framework for unifying and accelerating the train-
ing of heterogeneous VLA and WAM models. Its key design principle is to separate model-specific compu-
tation from reusable training infrastructure: configuration-driven model registration, a lightweight model in-
terface, and an extensible training engine allow models with distinct computation graphs and training objec-
tives to share orchestration, distributed execution, data processing, checkpointing, and evaluation. On top of
this abstraction, shared acceleration mechanisms, spanning efficient attention, compilation, CUDA Graphs,
operator and optimizer fusion, data delivery, and FP8-based communication for DDP and FSDP, are inte-
grated once at the framework level, while architecture-specific and hardware-aware choices remain config-
urable for each model. The framework further provides a unified data infrastructure with cross-embodiment
data synthesis and a training-aligned evaluation framework with composable model and benchmark adap-
tation, supporting an integrated workflow from data preparation to evaluation.
LoongForge-Embodied
achieves 1.47×–4.38× end-to-end training throughput improvements over the corresponding open-source
33


implementations while maintaining comparable benchmark success rates. Applying the same approach to
the evaluation loop, whose serial execution cost dominates the development loop once training is acceler-
ated, reduces evaluation wall-clock time by 4.1×–23.2× on the benchmark runs reported in Section 8.4.

A Detailed Model-Specific Optimization Analysis
For each model, this appendix first describes the architecture, dominant training bottlenecks, and optimiza-
tion design summarized in Section 4.4 (Table 1), and then provides the complete analyses summarized in
Sections 8.7 and 8.8. Experiments that appear in the main text are referenced rather than repeated.
A.1 π0.5
Architecture and bottlenecks.
The π0.5 model uses a dual-branch architecture that couples a PaliGemma-
based vision-language backbone with a Gemma-based action expert for flow-matching-based action gener-
ation. Unlike conventional Transformer architectures, the two branches interact through joint attention at
each layer, where their query, key, and value representations are concatenated for shared attention com-
putation and subsequently separated for branch-specific processing. The attention mechanism follows a
structured prefix-action pattern: visual-language prefix tokens attend bidirectionally within the prefix, while
action tokens attend to both the prefix and the entire action sequence. This architecture introduces several
performance bottlenecks. First, conventional attention implementations incur substantial memory over-
head from explicit attention masks, grouped-query attention (GQA) key/value replication, and intermediate
tensor materialization across branches. Second, activation storage and fine-grained operations, including
normalization, positional encoding, and gated residual computation, increase memory pressure and exe-
cution overhead, constraining the feasible per-device batch size. Third, the coupled computation across
branches complicates distributed parameter sharding and communication scheduling. These challenges be-
come particularly pronounced on multi-GPU platforms without high-bandwidth GPU interconnects, where
gradient synchronization and insufficient communication-computation overlap can substantially limit train-
ing throughput.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied jointly optimizes attention
computation, activation memory, graph execution, and distributed communication. We first replace conven-
tional attention with FlexAttention, exploiting the structured prefix-action mask and native GQA support to
avoid explicit key/value replication and reduce attention-related memory overhead. The attention mask is
constructed once per training step and reused across layers, while cross-branch RoPE and adaptive normal-
ization operations are consolidated to reduce redundant computation and intermediate tensors. Building on
these memory savings, we employ selective activation checkpointing on the SigLIP vision encoder, whose
relatively low recomputation cost enables a more favorable memory-computation trade-off than uniform
checkpointing. Together with pruning invalid input slots, this reduces unnecessary computation and memory
consumption, allowing larger effective per-device batches with less reliance on gradient accumulation. To
improve execution efficiency, we introduce a multi-group compilation strategy that partitions Transformer
layers into separately compiled groups, enabling operator fusion while preserving scheduling opportunities
for overlapping gradient synchronization with backward computation. We further optimize DDP gradient
buckets, gradient-buffer reuse, and communication scheduling to reduce synchronization overhead, particu-
larly on bandwidth-constrained interconnects. For FSDP execution, we additionally constrain module wrap-
ping according to the cross-branch dependencies of joint attention, avoiding unnecessarily fine-grained pa-
rameter gathering. These optimizations are designed to work together: reducing attention memory overhead
enables more efficient recomputation and batch configurations, while compilation granularity is selected to
balance kernel fusion against communication overlap.

A.2 GR00T N1.7
Architecture and bottlenecks.
GR00T N1.7 is a VLA model comprising a Qwen3-VL backbone and
a 32-layer diffusion transformer (DiT) action head trained with flow matching. The backbone processes
multimodal observations and language instructions to extract visual-language representations, which are
subsequently combined with robot states to condition continuous action generation. In the standard training
configuration, the backbone remains frozen while only the action head is updated, resulting in an asymmet-
ric computational workload: the backbone performs expensive multimodal forward computation, whereas
the action head requires both forward and backward propagation. Despite eliminating backbone gradient
computation, several performance bottlenecks remain. First, the Qwen3-VL forward pass involves sub-
stantial redundant computation and fragmented GPU execution, including repeated construction of position
and attention metadata, unnecessary vocabulary projection, and numerous fine-grained RoPE and pointwise
operations. Dynamic metadata construction further introduces CPU–GPU synchronization, leaving GPU
execution pipelines intermittently idle. Second, multimodal data loading incurs redundant video decoding
across dataset shards, causing CPU resource contention and unstable GPU utilization. Finally, frequent
kernel launches, gradient processing, and distributed communication introduce additional execution gaps,
limiting the effectiveness of conventional operator-level optimizations. These characteristics indicate that
improving GR00T N1.7 training efficiency requires coordinated optimization across data preparation, back-
bone computation, and training execution.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied introduces a series of opti-
mizations tailored to the frozen-backbone architecture of GR00T N1.7. At the data level, we establish
episode-aware sample indexing to eliminate redundant video decoding and employ asynchronous shard
prefetching with deterministic augmentation replay, thereby overlapping data preparation with computation
without altering the original sampling semantics. At the computation level, we exploit the relatively stable
multimodal input layouts to cache position and attention metadata, avoiding repeated host-side construc-
tion and synchronization. We further bypass the unused language-model vocabulary projection, since action
training consumes backbone hidden states rather than language logits, and fuse RoPE, RMSNorm, and MLP
42


pointwise operations to reduce intermediate memory traffic and kernel launch overhead. Beyond these op-
timizations, we employ full-iteration CUDA Graph capture to eliminate CPU scheduling overhead across
forward propagation, backward propagation, gradient synchronization, and optimizer updates. More impor-
tantly, we exploit the frozen backbone to construct a cross-iteration execution pipeline that separates back-
bone inference from action-head training into two CUDA Graphs. Since the backbone output for batch n+1
is independent of the action-head parameter updates for batch n, the backbone forward pass of the next batch
can overlap with the current batch’s action-head training and gradient communication. This scheduling strat-
egy hides otherwise exposed execution and communication latency without introducing parameter-version
inconsistencies. Finally, we integrate gradient scaling into a capturable AdamW optimizer and eliminate
redundant gradient-buffer operations to reduce memory traffic during parameter updates. Together, these
optimizations transform the fragmented training execution of GR00T N1.7 into a more continuous GPU
pipeline while preserving the original training semantics.

A.3 X-VLA
Architecture and bottlenecks.
X-VLA adopts a VLA architecture that combines a Florence-2-based
vision-language backbone with a Transformer-based action prediction head. Its visual encoder, DaViT,
processes multi-view observations through a combination of convolutional layers, window attention, and
channel attention, while the language encoder integrates visual and linguistic representations for down-
stream action prediction. This heterogeneous architecture introduces substantial computational overhead
from attention operations, matrix multiplications, and numerous fine-grained memory-bound operators, in-
cluding normalization, element-wise operations, and tensor transformations. Beyond these computational
costs, the original implementation incurs additional CPU–GPU synchronization overhead during visual fea-
ture extraction and attention-mask processing. In particular, scalar extraction and data-dependent indexing
introduce host synchronization and dynamic tensor shapes, which interrupt graph execution and hinder ef-
fective compilation. Meanwhile, gradient communication is largely overlapped with backward computation
under DDP, making communication optimization less beneficial than eliminating synchronization and ex-
ecution overhead. These characteristics motivate optimization strategies that improve computational graph
continuity, reduce fine-grained execution overhead, and enhance data-loading efficiency.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied applies model-specific com-
putational graph restructuring together with reusable compilation and data pipeline optimizations. First, we
eliminate unnecessary host synchronization in multi-view image encoding by replacing scalar-dependent
validity checks and dynamic boolean indexing with fixed-shape feature extraction and tensor-based mask-
ing. We further simplify the Florence-2 encoder’s attention-mask handling by bypassing redundant validity
checks when all attention positions are valid, preserving the original attention semantics while removing
synchronization barriers. These modifications establish a more static and compilation-friendly computa-
tional graph, enabling effective application of ‘torch.compile‘. Building on this restructuring, TorchInductor
reduces Python dispatch and kernel-launch overhead through graph-level optimization and operator fusion,
particularly benefiting the numerous fine-grained operations in the visual backbone. In addition, we optimize
44


the multi-view data pipeline through parallel data loading, pinned memory, and asynchronous prefetching to
reduce GPU stalls caused by data preparation. Together, these optimizations illustrate how model-specific
graph restructuring can unlock reusable framework-level acceleration mechanisms while addressing the dis-
tinctive execution characteristics of X-VLA.

A.4 Wall-OSS-0.5
Architecture and bottlenecks.
Wall-OSS-0.5 is a VLA model built upon Qwen2.5-VL, comprising a
32-block vision Transformer and a 36-layer language model, together with a mixture-of-experts (MoE) ac-
tion prediction pathway trained with flow matching. Its training pipeline involves visual feature extraction,
multimodal representation processing, expert routing, and continuous action prediction, resulting in hetero-
geneous computation and communication patterns. Profiling reveals two major performance bottlenecks.
First, frequently invoked operators such as SwiGLU and RMSNorm introduce substantial memory traffic
and kernel-launch overhead due to intermediate tensor materialization and fragmented pointwise compu-
tation. These costs are particularly pronounced in the Transformer blocks and MoE components, where
repeated element-wise operations contribute significantly to iteration latency and activation memory con-
sumption. The latter is the binding constraint on the per-device batch size for this model, so reducing activa-
tion memory is as consequential as reducing step time. Second, the distributed training pipeline suffers from
exposed parameter communication and inefficient scheduling. In particular, the module registration order
differs from the actual forward execution order, causing parameter prefetching to miss critical dependencies
and introducing head-of-line blocking during FSDP parameter materialization.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied introduces model-specific op-
timizations targeting both operator efficiency and distributed execution. At the operator level, we implement
bitwise-exact CUDA kernels for SwiGLU and RMSNorm that preserve the rounding points and arithmetic
ordering of the original PyTorch eager implementations. The fused SwiGLU kernel eliminates intermedi-
ate activation materialization and recomputes activations during backward propagation, while the optimized
RMSNorm retains ATen reduction operations to preserve numerical behavior and fuses the surrounding
pointwise computations. We further extend both kernels to mixed bf16/fp32 execution and eliminate re-
dundant activation casting, reducing memory allocation and data movement without altering the original
computation semantics. We also implement visual indexing operations in CUDA and pack the QKV projec-
46


tions into a single layout, further reducing redundant memory accesses. At the distributed execution level,
we optimize FSDP resharding and distributed Muon parameter prefetching to overlap communication with
computation. In particular, we reconstruct the forward prefetch chain according to the model’s actual ex-
ecution order rather than its module registration order, preventing demand-driven parameter gathering and
communication-induced stalls. Topology-aware NCCL configuration and bucketed optimizer updates fur-
ther reduce per-step communication and launch overhead. Together, these optimizations improve training
efficiency while preserving the numerical fidelity of the original implementation.
End-to-end step-time ablation.
We evaluate the Wall-OSS-0.5 optimizations on LIBERO using eight

A.5 FastWAM
Architecture and bottlenecks.
FastWAM is a WAM
built upon the Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video DiT, employing a
Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture to jointly
model video and action generation through flow match-
ing. Its MoT backbone consists of two structurally symmetric but parameter-independent Transformer ex-
perts for video latents and action tokens. Each expert maintains separate attention projections, normaliza-
tion layers, and feed-forward networks, while cross-modal interaction is achieved by concatenating their
query, key, and value representations into a shared self-attention operation at each layer. During training, a
frozen video VAE encodes input frames into latent representations, while text embeddings are precomputed
offline, leaving the two DiT experts and proprioceptive state encoder to be optimized. This architecture
introduces two major performance bottlenecks. First, video encoding involves computationally expensive
3D convolutions, while the repeated normalization, modulation, and RoPE operations across MoT layers
incur substantial kernel-launch overhead and intermediate memory traffic. Second, the independent expert
parameters generate a large volume of gradients requiring synchronization across devices. This commu-
nication overhead becomes particularly pronounced on multi-GPU platforms without high-bandwidth GPU
interconnects, such as the bandwidth-constrained platform used in our experiments (Section 8.8), where lim-
ited interconnect bandwidth and additional parameter synchronization under ZeRO-1 can make distributed
training communication-bound.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied applies a combination of
model-specific computation optimizations and hardware-aware distributed training strategies. On the com-
putation side, we optimize the frozen VAE encoder using channels-last memory layouts to reduce unneces-
sary tensor layout conversions during 3D convolution, and employ Triton kernels for efficient RoPE com-
putation and fused LayerNorm–modulation operations in the MoT blocks, reducing intermediate memory
accesses and kernel-launch overhead. We further adopt selective graph compilation for MoT blocks and VAE
encoding, eliminate redundant all-true attention masks to enable more efficient attention kernels, and employ
fused AdamW with cuDNN autotuning to improve optimizer and convolution efficiency. On the distributed
training side, we replace the original ZeRO-1 strategy with DDP when device memory permits, avoiding
additional parameter synchronization while enabling customized gradient communication. To mitigate the
bandwidth bottleneck, we introduce an FP8 AllToAll–AllGather communication scheme that quantizes gra-
48


dient buckets into block-scaled FP8 representations, exchanges gradient shards through AllToAll, performs
local reductions in FP32, and redistributes the reduced gradients through FP8 AllGather. This design reduces
communication volume without lowering the precision of forward and backward computation. Crucially,
we preserve asynchronous communication–computation overlap by configuring GPU execution concurrency
and leaving the precision exemptions disabled, since the exemption path is currently synchronous and would
serialize gradient synchronization. Optional error-feedback compensation can further mitigate accumulated
quantization errors during extended training. Together, these strategies adapt both computation and com-
munication to FastWAM’s dual-expert architecture and the underlying hardware characteristics, improving
end-to-end training efficiency while maintaining training fidelity.

A.6 LingBot-VA
Architecture and bottlenecks.
LingBot-VA employs a Transformer-based architecture for joint video
and action modeling, with a backbone consisting of 30 WanTransformerBlocks. Its training computation
combines self-attention over video-action representations, cross-attention to textual conditions, and feed-
forward transformations. Unlike conventional fixed-length Transformer workloads, LingBot-VA processes
variable-length video sequences with structured attention masks that capture temporal and cross-modal de-
pendencies. This architecture introduces several performance challenges. First, varying sequence lengths
require dynamic attention-mask construction, introducing substantial host-side dispatch and kernel-launch
overhead. Second, full-block activation checkpointing reduces memory consumption but requires recom-
putation during backpropagation, amplifying the cost of frequently executed operations such as positional
encoding, normalization, and attention. Third, distributed training with gradient accumulation incurs redun-
dant parameter synchronization when FSDP repeatedly reshards and gathers parameters across consecutive
microsteps. Fourth, variable-length samples cause per-rank computation imbalance within each gradient-
accumulation step, so faster ranks wait for the slowest rank at gradient synchronization. Consequently,
efficient training requires not only accelerating the computationally intensive Transformer operations but
also eliminating redundant communication and fine-grained execution overhead specific to LingBot-VA’s
51


training workflow.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied introduces a combination of
model-specific computation, execution, and distributed-training optimizations. First, we adopt a gradient-
accumulation-aware FSDP2 strategy that retains unsharded parameters across consecutive microsteps and
performs resharding after optimizer updates, avoiding repeated AllGather operations at the cost of increased
parameter memory residency. Second, rather than compiling the entire Transformer block, we selectively
apply torch.compile to stable computational boundaries, including modulation, masking, and loss reduc-
tion, while dynamically compiling create_block_mask to accommodate varying sequence lengths. This
approach reduces Python dispatch and kernel-launch overhead without imposing restrictive static-shape
assumptions. Third, we replace Q/K RMSNorm with fused Transformer Engine kernels and implement
model-specific Triton kernels for both forward and backward RoPE computations, eliminating expensive
complex-number operations, redundant type conversions, and intermediate tensor materialization. These
optimizations are particularly effective under activation recomputation, where the same operations are re-
peatedly executed. Fourth, a cost-balanced sampler re-assigns the samples of each global batch across ranks
by estimated computation cost and aligns the cost of corresponding microsteps across ranks, reducing syn-
chronization stalls without changing the set of samples consumed per optimizer step. Together, they reduce
redundant computation and communication while preserving the original training semantics, contributing to

A.7 DreamZero
Architecture and bottlenecks.
DreamZero is a WAM that jointly models future visual dynamics and
robot actions through a video diffusion backbone. We consider its Wan2.2-TI2V-5B variant, which extends
a 30-layer DiT with action and state registers, allowing video and action tokens to interact through shared
transformer blocks with blockwise causal attention. The model jointly optimizes visual dynamics prediction
and action denoising, resulting in substantial computational demands from attention, feed-forward opera-
tions, and their backward passes. Beyond computation, its large transformer backbone introduces consid-
erable distributed training overhead. Under FSDP, repeated parameter AllGather operations and parameter
resharding between forward and backward execution can expose significant communication latency, partic-
ularly when communication cannot be effectively overlapped with computation. Furthermore, DreamZero
incorporates pretrained text, video, and visual encoders, whose execution requirements differ across training
stages. In particular, frozen components may incur unnecessary parameter communication when included
in FSDP-managed modules, while redundant video decoding and feature processing introduce additional
CPU, memory, and data-loading overhead. These characteristics make efficient DreamZero training depen-
dent on coordinated optimization of transformer execution, parameter communication, and model-specific
data processing.
Reference implementation.
DreamZero’s baseline is built from the official code release and its Wan2.2-
TI2V-5B training script, tuned where necessary for the evaluated setting; we refer to it as the official-code
reference.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, LoongForge-Embodied applies a combination of
communication-aware, computation-aware, and data-aware optimizations tailored to DreamZero’s execu-
tion characteristics. First, we optimize FSDP parameter management by disabling resharding after the
forward pass to avoid redundant parameter AllGather operations, and employ Delta-FP8 communication to
reduce parameter transfer volume through quantized parameter updates while preserving BF16 computa-
tion. We further adapt FSDP wrapping and parameter residency to the execution requirements of individual
components, excluding appropriate frozen modules from unnecessary sharding and communication while
retaining their required computation. Second, we optimize the computationally intensive causal attention
53


blocks through whole-block graph compilation, enabling compiler optimizations across a larger execution
scope and reducing overhead associated with fragmented execution. Compared with separately compiling
fine-grained attention sub-functions, this approach provides greater opportunities for operation fusion and
memory optimization. Finally, we streamline DreamZero’s data processing through precomputed video la-
tents and text embeddings, together with first-frame-only processing: when video latents are read from the
precomputed cache, the only remaining consumer of raw pixels is the first frame fed to the CLIP image
encoder, so the loader decodes one frame per view instead of the full clip, avoiding redundant video decod-
ing and unnecessary data movement. These strategies are complemented by a fused AdamW optimizer to
reduce parameter update overhead. Together, these model-aware optimizations address the interaction be-
tween computation, communication, and data processing while preserving the underlying training objective.

A.8 Cosmos 3
Architecture and bottlenecks.
Cosmos 3 is an omnimodal world model that is built on an MoT archi-
tecture that combines autoregressive reasoning with diffusion-based generation of continuous modalities,
including video and actions. Its training workflow involves multimodal data preprocessing, VAE-related
computation, transformer execution, and FSDP-based distributed training. This heterogeneous workflow in-
troduces several performance bottlenecks. First, CPU-intensive data preprocessing incurs substantial over-
head in batch preparation, causing GPU stalls and limiting effective utilization. Second, the large memory
footprint of model parameters and intermediate activations constrains the per-device batch size and conse-
quently limits training throughput. Third, although FSDP reduces memory consumption through parameter
sharding, repeated parameter gathering and inter-device communication introduce additional overhead that
cannot always be hidden behind computation. These bottlenecks exhibit different characteristics across GPU
platforms, motivating both model-specific and hardware-aware optimizations.
Optimization design.
To address these bottlenecks, we optimize Cosmos 3 across data processing, com-
putation, and distributed execution. We first migrate computationally intensive preprocessing operations
from the CPU to the GPU, reducing batch preparation overhead and improving GPU utilization. For model
execution, we employ torch.compile to optimize computational graphs and reduce memory consumption,
enabling larger per-device batch sizes. We further leverage FP8 computation to exploit hardware-supported
55


low-precision arithmetic and improve computational throughput. Beyond these optimizations, we intro-
duce a hardware-aware FSDP strategy that balances memory consumption against communication overhead.
Specifically, only frozen weights may skip sharding, since leaving a trainable weight unsharded would also
replicate its gradients and optimizer state. In Cosmos 3 SFT the reasoning expert of the MoT backbone
is frozen while the generation expert is trained. When device memory permits, we therefore exclude the
largest frozen tensors of the reasoning expert (token embeddings, the output head, the feed-forward pro-
jections, and the query, key, and value projections) from sharding, keeping them resident on every device
instead of re-gathering them at every step. This strategy effectively trades available memory capacity for
reduced communication overhead along the critical execution path. Because excluding a frozen tensor from
sharding changes only where its parameters reside and not the computation performed, training accuracy is
unaffected. Together, these optimizations address the distinct performance bottlenecks of Cosmos 3 while
preserving its original training objective.

Thread-pool capping.
When several simulator instances share one device, each worker process is capped
to a single thread for the numerical libraries and the tensor framework, rather than allowing each process to
size its pools from the host core count. On a 20-episode run of a single task, this reduces wall-clock time
from 239.7 s to 215.4 s, a 10.1% reduction, with no change in success rate.
Timing arithmetic.
The accelerated LIBERO run with FastWAM under software rendering totals 979 s,
composed of 791 s of worker execution and 187 s of model loading and health checks across eight inference
servers. Fixed startup cost is therefore about 19% of that run, whereas the same absolute cost is under 3%
of the 1.9 h RoboTwin run; counting worker startup as well, the fixed cost before the first episode is 0.09
to 0.10 h, or 5 to 6% of the RoboTwin runs. The two reporting conventions therefore diverge far more on
short runs than on full ones. On the reference path, model loading was not timed separately and is deducted
at the same magnitude when computing steady-state times, which amounts to approximately 190 s against
a 173.7 min LIBERO reference time (1.8%) and a negligible fraction of the 41.03 h, 28.6 h, and 32.0 h
RoboTwin reference times (below 0.2%).
Derived reference time.
The two RoboTwin reference times for FastWAM are not direct 5,000-episode
measurements. They are obtained from a single 10,000-episode reference run over 16 slots at a measured
94% slot occupancy. The half of its episodes that corresponds to the clean task configuration accounts for
457.7 slot-hours, that is 28.6 h on 16 slots, and the half that corresponds to the randomized configuration
accounts for 512.1 slot-hours, that is 32.0 h; these are the two entries in Table 4. For these two rows no
separate rollout-only measurement exists on the reference path, so the steady-state column repeats the end-
to-end value. The π0.5 reference time is a direct measurement with the same checkpoint and protocol over
eight slots.
Scheduling variants.
The π0.5 run was executed with two dispatch policies. Plain longest-processing-
time-first dispatch with a uniform chunk size of 25 episodes completes in 1.88 h. Placing all chunks of long-
horizon tasks ahead of short ones and reducing their chunk size to 10 episodes completes in 1.77 h, which is
the value reported in Table 4. Success rates under the two policies differ within run-to-run variation.
Portability of the configuration.
The value of N at which throughput saturates depends on host core
count, device memory, model size, and rendering settings. The sweeps above were obtained in the eight-
GPU configuration used throughout this report and indicate trends rather than a transferable setting. Reusing
a value of N calibrated elsewhere does not raise an error; it manifests as throughput below the calibrated
table, so the sweep is repeated whenever the hardware, the model, the chunk size, or the request-merging
batch size changes.
