ABSTRACT
Long-horizon world modeling in partially observable environments requires com-
pact memories that preserve spatial structure despite accumulated localization
drift. Inspired by hippocampal episodic and spatial memory and the multi-periodic
coding of entorhinal grid cells, we propose HiSTFormer, a hierarchical spatiotem-
poral world model, which adopts a layer-wise organization of local temporal RoPE
and multi-periodic spatial RoPE, together with content-conditioned address off-
sets, to capture recent temporal context and long-range spatial structure. A fixed-
capacity memory keeps KV storage and per-step attention cost bounded as the
sequence grows. On MemoryMaze and HM3D, HiSTFormer outperforms strong
world-model baselines within the training sequence length and generalizes sub-
stantially better to prediction horizons far beyond those seen during training, while
using a compact model. The hierarchical spatiotemporal design remains effective
across deeper Transformer stacks, with consistent gains as depth increases. These
results show that hierarchical spatiotemporal organization and content-adaptive
spatial addressing provide effective inductive biases for efficient long-horizon
world modeling.

INTRODUCTION
First-person world models must predict local dynamics from consecutive observations and actions
while maintaining scene consistency over long interactions using spatial information from past tra-
jectories. Studies such as MemoryMaze and TECO highlight the challenges of long-term memory
and visual consistency during scene revisits (Pasukonis et al., 2023; Yan et al., 2023). How to or-
ganize and effectively utilize spatiotemporal information has therefore become an important
question for long-horizon visual prediction.
Recent studies have increasingly recognized the importance of spatial memory and explored diverse
approaches. Geometry-based methods maintain historical scene information through explicit or
latent 3D representations. SpMem, 3D Persistent World Model, PERSIST, and Spatia employ 3D
maps, geometric indexing, or persistent scene states to maintain spatial consistency (Wu et al., 2025;
Zhou et al., 2026; Garcin et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). LSM-World and MosaicMem further asso-
ciate visual latent features with 3D space, recovering historical content through viewpoint transfor-
mations and feature alignment (Wang et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026b). While these approaches provide
cross-view spatial constraints, they typically require additional geometric estimation, 3D mapping,
or spatial memory maintenance.
Another line of research focuses on establishing spatial associations through pose information
and latent memory mechanisms. ReMind introduces camera-aware RoPE to establish spatial rela-
tionships across time (Xu et al., 2026); ReWorld combines pose-aware attention with a pose-indexed
Landmark Bank for long-term memory retrieval (Chen et al., 2026); WorldCrafter retrieves relevant
content from implicit 3D-aware memory conditioned on target camera poses (Yu et al., 2026a);
and MemoryVLN integrates pose, visual, and geometric features to enhance historical memory re-
trieval for navigation (Wei et al., 2026). Other studies explore the division of computational roles
across memory mechanisms. CoME combines short-term dynamics, long-term experience, and spa-
tial information through specialized memory experts (Stapf et al., 2026), while the concurrent work

relationships should be coordinated across Transformer depth remains insufficiently explored.
Biological neural systems provide insights into the organization and use of spatial information. En-
torhinal grid cells exhibit multi-scale spatial representations associated with path integration, while
the hippocampus integrates spatial, temporal, and episodic information (Hafting et al., 2005; Moser
et al., 2014; Eichenbaum, 2017). Computational models such as TEM, Hippoformer, and HPC-MEC
have further explored associations between spatial structure and experience-specific content (Whit-
tington et al., 2020; Kymn et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b).
We investigate two further questions. First, local temporal integration and long-range spatial
retrieval have distinct information requirements: the former relies on consecutive observations
and actions, while the latter accesses spatially related experiences across time. How to coordinate
them within a Transformer remains underexplored. Second, spatial addresses do not necessarily
correspond one-to-one with visual content: visual features may encode information from multiple
locations, and fixed camera-centered addresses may limit retrieval accuracy, motivating content-
adaptive spatial addressing.
Additionally, GridPE, nD-RoPE, M-RoPE, and VideoRoPE provide technical foundations for en-
coding multidimensional spatiotemporal relationships in Transformers (Li et al., 2024; 2026a; Wang
et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025).
Building on these insights, we introduce HiSTFormer, a hierarchical Transformer for long-horizon
visual prediction. Using grid-cell-inspired multi-periodic spatial encoding, HiSTFormer focuses
on hierarchical spatiotemporal organization and content-adaptive spatial addressing. First,
HiSTFormer organizes temporal and spatial processing into successive stages. Temporal layers use
standard temporal RoPE to integrate consecutive observations and actions, capturing local dynam-
ics (Su et al., 2021). Spatial layers then employ multi-periodic spatial RoPE to retrieve historical
experiences through path-integrated spatial addresses, enabling scene recovery across temporal gaps.
Second, HiSTFormer introduces content-conditioned per-head spatial address offsets, allowing
individual attention heads to adapt their retrieval addresses to the represented content. A fixed-
capacity structured memory further supports long-range retrieval without unbounded cache growth.
On long-horizon visual prediction in MemoryMaze, HiSTFormer, trained on 320-frame sequences,
generalizes reliably to 1,000-frame rollouts and outperforms the compared baselines both within
and beyond the training range. Further analyses show that hierarchical temporal–spatial encoding
outperforms single-type and within-layer joint encodings, with increasing benefits as Transformer
depth grows. Moreover, content-conditioned offsets introduce only 0.05M additional parameters,
yet improve historical memory retrieval and learn spatial adjustments that tend to align with salient
walls.
Overall, these results demonstrate that effective spatiotemporal organization and content-adaptive
addressing can improve long-horizon prediction and memory utilization in world models.

METHOD
HiSTFormer is a hierarchical spatiotemporal world model for long-horizon visual prediction, as
illustrated in Figure 1. At time t, the observation ot is encoded into a visual representation zt. Given
zt and the next action at+1, the temporal Transformer integrates local observations and actions into
a context-aware experience representation. The spatial Transformer then retrieves and integrates
relevant historical experiences to predict ˆzt+1.
Spatial addresses are maintained through path integration. A motion encoder estimates the dis-
placement ˆst+1 from at+1 and the visual representation zt+1 when available. The displacement is
transformed into world coordinates using the ground-truth heading and integrated into multi-periodic
spatial phases ct+1 for spatial RoPE. Additionally, a lightweight MLP predicts content-conditioned,
per-head offsets ∆ph from the temporal Transformer output, adapting spatial attention addresses to
the content represented by each experience.

2.1
HIERARCHICAL SPATIOTEMPORAL TRANSFORMER
Temporal and spatial RoPE are organized hierarchically. The temporal layers extract local contex-
tual information from recent actions and observations, whereas the spatial layers retrieve historical
information according to relative spatial relationships. At each step, the model takes the current
visual representation zt and the next action at+1 as input and produces the predicted ˆzt+1. Dur-
ing observation, zt is encoded from the ground-truth observation; during open-loop prediction, the
model instead uses the predicted representation ˆzt from the previous step.
Both layer types use standard pre-layer-normalization (Pre-LN) Transformer blocks composed of
multi-head self-attention and feed-forward networks, with a residual connection around each sub-
layer. Each attention module uses four heads, with 256-dimensional query, key, and value represen-
tations per head.
The temporal layers combine standard temporal RoPE (Su et al., 2024) with sliding-window atten-
tion and, by default, attend to the 32 most recent tokens. The spatial layers use a bounded KV cache
containing entries from the 32 most recent time steps and up to 96 earlier time steps. As shown in
Appendix A.4, performance is insensitive to the specific memory-selection rule provided that broad
temporal coverage is maintained.
Each retained spatial-layer entry already contains the local context extracted by the temporal layers
and can therefore carry information beyond that of a single frame.

2.4
TRAINING OBJECTIVE
Training is performed on 320-frame sequences divided into chunks of K = 16 frames. After ob-
serving each chunk, the model autoregressively predicts the next K frames using the observation
memory accumulated up to the current point, together with the actions and head directions from the
subsequent chunk. The model then observes the ground-truth frames of the predicted chunk, and this
observe–predict cycle repeats throughout the sequence. The training objective consists of an image-
reconstruction loss Lrecon, an open-loop prediction loss Lpred, a pairwise spatial representation loss
Lpair, and a teacher–student motion loss Lstudent. The complete objective is
L = Lrecon + Lpred + Lstudent + 0.03Lpair.

3
EXPERIMENTS
We collect trajectories from the 9×9 MemoryMaze and HM3D environments (Ramakrishnan et al.,
2021) and evaluate open-loop visual prediction conditioned on actions and head directions. Each
model observes historical RGB frames, actions, and ground-truth head directions, and autoregres-
sively predicts future visual sequences from future actions and ground-truth head directions. All
models are trained on continuous 320-frame trajectories using a K = 16 frame open-loop pre-
diction objective for 30,000 gradient steps on MemoryMaze and 100,000 steps on HM3D; CoME
instead retains its original diffusion-based training scheme. All models use a batch size of 16 and
are trained over three random seeds. Except for CoME (approximately 297M parameters), model
sizes are controlled to 7–8M on MemoryMaze and approximately 100M on HM3D. We consider
two continuous open-loop evaluation protocols: predicting 64 frames from 256 context frames and
predicting 500 frames from 500 context frames.
3.1
BASELINES
We compare HiSTFormer with recent world models equipped with spatial or long-term memory
mechanisms, as well as general-purpose sequence models with comparable parameter counts. All
baselines are adapted to a common observation, action, and head-direction interface, while their
original architectures and training procedures are preserved whenever possible.
HPC–MEC. We reimplement and adapt HPC–MEC (Zhang et al., 2026), a hippocampal–
entorhinal-inspired world model, using the same head-direction-based coordinate transformation
and data interface as HiSTFormer. The resulting model contains approximately 8.15 M parameters.
CoME. We adapt and train CoME on our dataset following its official implementation (Stapf et al.,
2026). To avoid discrepancies arising from different pose-estimation implementations, we pro-
vide ground-truth poses directly. CoME has substantially more parameters than HiSTFormer. Ap-
pendix E further reports results under a longer training budget; the additional training improves
performance but does not change the main conclusions.
General-purpose world models. We construct three latent-space world models based on Trans-
former, RSSM, and Mamba (Vaswani et al., 2017; Hafner et al., 2019; Gu & Dao, 2024; Hafner
et al., 2025). They share the same visual encoder–decoder framework and input and output inter-
faces. All three models are trained with the same open-loop prediction and image-reconstruction
losses, while retaining their architecture-specific auxiliary latent-space objectives. Each model con-
tains approximately 8 M parameters.

The qualitative comparison on MemoryMaze in Figure 2A further illustrates how prediction qual-

prediction target to the next 64 frames and progressively increase the available context length (Fig-

from long histories. HPC–MEC and CoME also benefit from additional context, whereas Mamba
and RSSM show only limited improvement. Transformer benefits from longer context within the
training range, but its performance degrades when the context is extended further.

4.2
HIERARCHICAL TEMPORAL–SPATIAL ENCODING ENABLES SCALABLE LONG-HORIZON
PREDICTION
Local temporal continuity and long-range spatial relationships exhibit different structures, motivat-
ing us to examine whether these two types of positional information should be organized differently
within a Transformer. We systematically compare temporal RoPE (T), spatial RoPE (S), within-
layer joint encoding (J) (Wang et al., 2024), no positional encoding (N), and different layer-wise
temporal–spatial arrangements, evaluating both in-range performance and long-horizon generaliza-
tion.
All configurations use the same 128-token memory budget. Temporal layers use a contiguous FIFO
window, whereas spatial layers use recent-plus-coverage (R+C): 32 recent tokens and 96 earlier
memories retained for temporal coverage. Unless cache organization is under study, each layer uses
its default policy; content-conditioned spatial offsets are disabled to isolate positional encoding and
layer-wise organization. We report LPIPS under the 256 →64 and 500 →500 protocols to assess
long-horizon generalization within and beyond the training sequence length.

Hierarchical organization consistently benefits from increased depth.
We further compare
temporal–spatial Transformers with different depths and layer arrangements (Figure 3B). As the net-
work is scaled from 2 to 4 and 6 layers, LPIPS consistently decreases under both the 256→64 and
500 →500 protocols, indicating that the hierarchical temporal–spatial design continues to benefit
from increased model depth. At six layers, the blockwise TTTSSS arrangement achieves lower pre-
diction error than the alternating TSTSTS arrangement. At both four and six layers, the blockwise
arrangements (TTSS and TTTSSS) also exhibit smaller variation across random seeds than their
alternating counterparts (TSTS and TSTSTS). Within the tested range of two to six layers, these
results show that placing local temporal processing before long-range spatial organization provides
stable depth scaling.
Temporal and spatial layers benefit from different memory organizations.
In the experiments
above, temporal layers access a contiguous window of recent history by default, whereas spatial
layers combine a recent window with long-term memories selected to provide broad temporal cov-
erage. To examine the effect of this asymmetric design, we further swap the cache organization used
by the two layer types (Figure 3C). Applying R+C coverage sampling to temporal layers does not
yield consistent improvements and leads to a clear degradation in long-horizon prediction. Likewise,
8


replacing the coverage-based memory of spatial layers with a pure FIFO cache degrades the perfor-
mance of TS. In contrast, the default configuration—contiguous recent history for temporal layers
and long-range coverage for spatial layers—achieves the best overall performance. This asymme-
try suggests that, under a limited memory budget, local temporal modeling relies more strongly on
contiguous context, whereas spatial retrieval benefits more from broad coverage over long histories.

Taken together with the spatial-cache organization experiments in Appendix A.4, these results show
that spatial indexing allows the model to exploit information spanning long temporal ranges without
retaining the full history. Consequently, KV storage and per-step attention cost remain bounded as
the sequence grows.
Spatial modeling is robust across encoding choices.
Keeping all other settings unchanged, we
compare the multi-periodic grid encoding with two alternatives: Cartesian, which uses orthogonal
wave directions, and Single-period, which retains the grid-like wave directions but uses a single
period (Figure 3E). We evaluate zero-shot transfer from the 9 × 9 training environment to larger
11 × 11 and 13 × 13 environments, averaging results over three training seeds. Under the 256→64
protocol, the multi-periodic encoding achieves the lowest LPIPS across all environment sizes. Under
the more challenging 500 →500 setting, the differences among encodings narrow, while the multi-
periodic representation remains stable and competitive across environment scales. Overall, these
results suggest that HiSTFormer’s spatial modeling is robust to the tested encoding choices, while
the multi-periodic grid-like representation provides a reliable default. Multi-scale spatial codes have
theoretical support for reducing periodic positional ambiguity (Mathis et al., 2012; Stemmler et al.,
2015). Further implementation details and distance-stratified analyses are provided in Appendix F.

Finally, we examine how these address adjustments contribute to visual prediction in practice. To re-
duce the effect of directly retrieving historical views from the same location, we select target frames

Overall, these results indicate that the learnable address offsets do more than simply introduce addi-
tional degrees of freedom. Instead, they provide content-dependent spatial adjustments to the base
positions obtained through path integration. These adjustments yield consistent prediction gains,
exhibit interpretable structure aligned with scene geometry, and influence the historical memories
actually used by the model, thereby enabling a more flexible binding between temporal context and
spatial memory.

CONCLUSION
We introduced HiSTFormer, a hierarchical Transformer that organizes local temporal integration
and long-range spatial retrieval into successive stages, with content-conditioned offsets for adaptive
memory addressing. Using a fixed-capacity memory and grid-like spatial encoding, HiSTFormer
improves long-horizon visual prediction both within and beyond the training sequence length.

A.3
MAIN MODEL CONFIGURATION
The main HiSTFormer model on MemoryMaze reported in Table 1 contains approximately 7.72
million trainable parameters and uses a two-layer TS Transformer, with one temporal layer followed
by one spatial layer. The temporal layer maintains a 32-entry FIFO KV cache.
For each spatial-attention head, the spatial layer applies RoPE to 128 of the 256 query and key
dimensions. Positional RoPE occupies 96 dimensions, using eight spatial periods with six rotary
channel pairs per period. Head direction occupies a further 32 dimensions by repeating the corre-
sponding rotation across 16 channel pairs. The remaining 128 dimensions are not rotated.
The spatial layer receives the complete output of the temporal layer. For each token, a content-
conditioned address network with dimensions 384→128→8 produces eight scalars(0.05M), which
are reshaped into four two-dimensional position offsets, one for each attention head. These offsets
adjust the spatial rotary phases used by the corresponding heads.
A.4
LONG-TERM MEMORY MANAGEMENT
By default, HiSTFormer manages its fixed-capacity long-term memory using temporal-redundancy-
based eviction. Let the retained entries be ordered by their timestamps. When the long-term memory
is full, we assign each interior candidate at timestamp τj the temporal-redundancy score
Cj = (τj −τj−1)(τj+1 −τj)(τj+1 −τj−1).
(11)
The entry with the smallest Cj is removed, while the two temporal endpoints are always retained.
This strategy preferentially removes temporally redundant entries and maintains coverage over a
broad temporal range.

We collect an egocentric video dataset in HM3D v0.2 using Habitat-Sim. Our exploration con-
troller, adapted from EgoEnv, initializes each episode at a sampled navigable location with a random
heading and navigates among up to eight nearby reachable goals, reshuffling the goals to induce re-
peated visits. Each trajectory contains 1,024 consecutive observations, with RGB images rendered
at 128 × 128 and downsampled to 64 × 64. The action space consists of moving forward by 0.25
m and turning left or right by 30°. We record the executed actions together with ground-truth agent
positions, camera poses, and headings. The final dataset contains 8,080 trajectories and 8,273,920
frames across 888 scenes. We use mutually disjoint sets of 788, 90, and 10 scenes for training,
validation, and testing, comprising 7,880, 180, and 20 trajectories, respectively. Training scenes
come from the HM3D training split, excluding 12 scenes with insufficient reachable goals. We
reserve HM3D minival for our internal test set and remove these scenes from the validation split.
All trajectories undergo geometric consistency checks and image/action replay verification at four
checkpoints per trajectory.
High Spatial Revisit Rates in HM3D. HM3D trajectories exhibit substantial spatial revisits, as
agents sequentially navigate to eight random goals. Figure 7 illustrates 20 test trajectories from 10
scenes and their historical observation coverage. We measure whether each predicted state matches
an observed position (distance ≤0.5), position and heading (angular difference ≤30◦), or state-
action transition (matching start/end states and action). Only observed history is considered, exclud-

D.3
GENERAL-PURPOSE WORLD MODELS
Transformer, RSSM, and Mamba take the previous visual latent, the current transition action, and
the ground-truth head direction at the target time as inputs. Each model updates its latent state and
autoregressively predicts the subsequent visual latents.
The Transformer baseline is a two-layer causal model with eight attention heads and a hidden width
of 480. It uses learnable absolute positional embeddings along the temporal axis. The Mamba
baseline uses three Mamba-1 layers with a hidden width of 464. The RSSM baseline uses a 768-
dimensional deterministic state together with a 234-dimensional continuous Gaussian latent state.
All three models contain approximately 8 million parameters.
Because learnable absolute positions may be unfavorable for extrapolation beyond the training se-
quence length, we additionally consider a more controlled relative-position comparison. The TT
configuration in Figure 3A is a two-layer temporal-only Transformer using relative temporal RoPE
and the same 128-token memory budget as the other positional-encoding variants. Despite removing
the absolute-position extrapolation issue, TT still performs poorly under long-horizon evaluation,
particularly the 500 →500 protocol. Thus, replacing absolute embeddings with temporal RoPE
alone does not yield strong long-horizon generalization; the gains of HiSTFormer additionally rely
on explicit spatial indexing and layer-wise temporal–spatial organization.

E.1
HPC–MEC
The original HPC–MEC formulation combines observation feedback with recurrent MEC-state
propagation. Our setting instead requires continuous open-loop prediction after a fixed observation
history, making it necessary to specify how the MEC state is updated when no new image observa-
tions are available. We therefore compare two update protocols: CI-R, which preserves recurrent

visual representations and never accesses future ground-truth images.

We therefore adopt SI in the main experiments, while reporting CI-R to make the effect of this
implementation choice explicit.

ADDITIONAL COMPARISON WITH ESWM
ESWM is a biologically inspired spatial world model that infers environmental structure by stor-
ing and integrating state–action transition memories (He et al., 2026). To compare different spa-
tial memory mechanisms for long-horizon visual prediction, we implement an adapted version of
ESWM following the original paper and pseudocode, and evaluate it on MemoryMaze and HM3D.
The original method constructs a sparse memory bank using a minimum spanning tree based on
environment topology. Since this requires explicit topological information, we retain all observed
transitions as episodic memory. For a controlled comparison, we match HiSTFormer’s visual en-
coder–decoder, 16-step autoregressive training protocol, and parameter count.
Table 8 presents quantitative comparisons. On MemoryMaze, HiSTFormer outperforms ESWM
across all metrics under the 256→64 protocol, reducing LPIPS by approximately 19.8%. Under
500→500, their performance is comparable, with HiSTFormer achieving lower LPIPS. On HM3D,
where spatial revisits are more frequent, both models perform similarly under 256→64, with HiST-
21


Former achieving slightly better mean values across all four metrics. Under 500→500, ESWM
achieves better results across all four metrics.
Historical revisit frequency may partly explain these differences. Under HM3D’s 500→500 proto-
col, approximately 96% of predicted frames have matching poses in the observed history, and 92%
have matching state–action transitions. Since ESWM retains all observed transitions, it may ben-
efit more directly from repeated experiences. In contrast, HiSTFormer remains highly competitive
under such extensive historical coverage while using fixed-capacity spatial memory.
Revisit-stratified analysis further reveals their different strengths. Figure 9 compares MSE and
LPIPS across groups defined by the number of historical pose matches. Both models generally
improve as revisit frequency increases. Under 256→64, HiSTFormer achieves lower LPIPS across
all revisit groups and remains competitive in MSE. Under 500→500, ESWM performs better on
samples with historical revisits. Notably, HiSTFormer achieves lower LPIPS under both protocols
for frames without matching historical poses, indicating stronger perceptual prediction when direct
historical correspondences are unavailable.
Overall, HiSTFormer demonstrates a clear advantage under limited historical coverage while
maintaining prediction quality comparable to a full-transition-memory model even in highly
repetitive environments, despite its fixed memory capacity. These findings further support the
effectiveness of HiSTFormer’s spatiotemporal organization and historical memory retrieval mecha-
nisms.
F
SPATIAL ENCODING DESIGN AND ROBUSTNESS
HiSTFormer adopts a biologically inspired multi-periodic grid-like encoding as its default spatial
representation. This section examines two key design choices: multiple spatial periods and hexag-
onal plane-wave geometry. The ablations in the main text show that HiSTFormer is robust to dif-
ferent spatial encodings, with the grid-like representation achieving stable and generally favorable
performance across the evaluated environment scales and prediction protocols. Here, we detail the
encoding variants and further examine their theoretical motivations and empirical behavior.
F.1
SPATIAL ENCODING VARIANTS.
For a controlled comparison, all three encodings use 96-dimensional representations and share the
same path-integration procedure based on predicted displacements and ground-truth head directions.
Only the encoding configurations differ:
Grid-like (HiSTFormer): Uses 8 logarithmically spaced periods, each containing 2 groups of 3
plane waves with directions separated by 120°. This yields 48 complex phases, corresponding to 96
real-valued dimensions.
Cartesian: Uses 2 orthogonal plane-wave directions (90°). To preserve the representation dimen-
sion, the number of periods is increased to 24, with 2 complex phases per period. The minimum and
maximum periods match those of HiSTFormer, resulting in denser period sampling.
Single-period: Retains the three-direction grid-like geometry but fixes the spatial period to nearly
64 environment units. It uses 16 groups of 3 plane waves with randomized initial phase offsets,
yielding 48 complex phases.
These variants examine the effects of wave geometry and period diversity under matched representa-
tion dimensions. Notably, Cartesian changes both the wave directions and period sampling density;
therefore, this comparison does not isolate the effect of hexagonal geometry alone.

effective encoding range and reduce large-scale localization errors (Mathis et al., 2012; Stemmler
et al., 2015). To examine the implications for visual prediction, we analyze the prediction-error dif-

generally positive and tend to increase with distance from the last observation. This trend is consis-

phase ambiguity, providing further empirical support for multi-periodic spatial encoding.

F.4
MOTIVATION FOR HEXAGONAL PLANE-WAVE GEOMETRY.
Hexagonal spatial structure has a well-established biological basis, as observed in the firing pat-
terns of entorhinal grid cells (Hafting et al., 2005). Theoretical and simulation studies also suggest
potential geometric advantages. Guanella and Verschure reported favorable position reconstruction
using triangular grid patterns compared with other regular two-dimensional tessellations (Guanella
& Verschure, 2007). Schøyen et al. further showed that hexagonal grid patterns can offer im-
proved spatial resolution and noise robustness over square patterns under conformal-isometry as-
sumptions (Schøyen et al., 2025).
However, these advantages depend on specific encoding models and theoretical assumptions and do
not necessarily translate into superior prediction performance in Transformer world models. Mir
et al. found comparable performance between hexagonal and square grid representations in spatial
memory storage and retrieval tasks (Mir et al., 2024). This agrees with our observations: although
24


Grid-like achieves lower errors in some settings, its advantage over Cartesian is modest and less
consistent, particularly under more challenging long-horizon prediction protocols.
Overall, HiSTFormer’s effectiveness is not tied to a particular spatial encoding. Multi-periodic
encoding is theoretically motivated by its ability to mitigate long-range positional ambiguity and
shows corresponding empirical benefits in our experiments. Hexagonal plane-wave geometry pro-
vides a biologically grounded design with potential advantages under specific theoretical condi-
tions. Considering these factors and the observed performance, we adopt the multi-periodic grid-
like encoding as a reliable default, while emphasizing hierarchical spatiotemporal organization and
content-adaptive spatial addressing as HiSTFormer’s primary methodological contributions.

INTERPRETABILITY ANALYSIS OF CONTENT-CONDITIONED SPATIAL
ADDRESSING
Content-conditioned offsets introduce only 0.051M additional parameters (approximately 0.67%),
reducing LPIPS by 9.00% and 5.56% under short- and long-horizon prediction, respectively. We fur-
ther investigate their role through motion representations, head-specific offset patterns, and targeted
memory interventions.
G.1
THE MOTION ENCODER LEARNS SPATIALLY MEANINGFUL DISPLACEMENT
REPRESENTATIONS
We first examine the relationship between the forward displacement predicted by the action-only
motion encoder and the ground-truth displacement. Figure 11 shows approximately linear relation-
ships across three training seeds, with seed-dependent differences in scale and sign. This suggests
that the encoder captures relative variations in physical motion. Since spatial phases are obtained
by integrating predicted displacements, with content-conditioned offsets further adjusting these ad-
dresses, this result provides a basis for interpreting the learned spatial addressing geometrically.
G.2
ATTENTION HEADS LEARN STABLE, CONTENT-ADAPTIVE SPATIAL OFFSETS.
Figure 12A shows the offset direction distributions of four heads across three seeds, together with
the relationship between offset magnitude and the nearest-wall distance along each head’s mean
preferred direction. Most heads exhibit stable directional preferences, distributed approximately
uniformly across the 80° field of view. Offset magnitudes are positively correlated with wall dis-
tances within approximately three environment cells, before gradually saturating. These results sug-
gest that individual heads maintain directional preferences while adapting their retrieval distances
to scene geometry. Figure 12(B) presents three representative examples illustrating the alignment of
learned offsets with visible walls.
G.3
SPATIAL OFFSETS EXHIBIT GEOMETRIC CONSISTENCY WITH RETRIEVED MEMORIES.
Figure 12(C–E) further examines the representative case presented in the main text. In Figure 12C,
H2’s adjusted address lies near the left red wall, while H0 points toward the distant green wall, albeit
with some distance error. Figure 12D shows historical frames receiving over 10% attention from H0
or H2. H0 retrieves visual cues from the green wall, whereas H2 primarily attends to the red wall,
suggesting complementary spatial information across heads.
G.4
TARGETED MEMORY INTERVENTIONS FURTHER REVEAL THE ROLE OF RETRIEVED
INFORMATION.
Figure 12E compares the full model, No Offset, and predictions obtained by replacing high-attention
memory slots in H0, H2, or both. The full model recovers the scene structure reasonably well,
whereas No Offset exhibits clear errors in both walls. Replacing H0’s high-attention slots primarily
degrades reconstruction of the distant green region, while replacing H2’s slots more strongly affects
the left red wall. Intervening on both produces larger prediction errors. These changes align with
