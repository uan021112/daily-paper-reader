Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have advanced
rapidly in language-conditioned robotic manipulation, yet
existing benchmarks mainly evaluate gripper–object con-
tact and largely ignore how objects interact with the en-
vironment through gravity, friction, inertia, and mass dis-
tribution. As a result, it remains unclear whether current
policies truly understand physics or merely interpolate from
their training data. We address this gap through the lens
of dynamic extrinsic dexterity and introduce PhyDEX, a
throwing benchmark in which a robot must infer an object’s
mass, center of mass, and friction to send it to a destination
beyond its kinematic workspace. PhyDEX contains 35K
trajectories (143 hours) across nine tasks and 100 scenes,
covering uniform, irregular, and dynamic-mass objects with
static, multiple, and moving destinations. All data are gen-
erated by a scalable, fully automatic pipeline that com-
bines grasp generation, motion planning, and closed-loop
throw optimization under randomized physical properties.

1. Introduction
Physical understanding has widely recognized as a corner-
stone of language-conditioned robotic manipulation sys-
tems [4, 15, 41, 56]. A broad spectrum of methods have
emerged, ranging from hybrid frameworks [28, 53] to uni-
fied systems [19, 41, 63], from vision-language architec-
tures [2, 20, 61] to multi-sensory models [38, 57], and
from static methods [2, 15, 20–22, 43, 44, 61] to dynamic
paradigm [17, 27, 31, 49, 62].
Despite this great progress, recent works predominantly
focus on the intrinsic gripper–object contact, whether in
standard manipulation tasks such as Pick-and-Place, Re-
orientation, and Opening & Closing Articulated Objects [7,
30, 37, 60], or in contact-rich tasks such as Insertion, Frag-
ile Grasping, and Wiping [11, 12, 16, 18, 26, 32, 38, 45, 57].
However, they largely overlook how the manipulators inter-
acts with its surrounding environment (e.g., gravity, fric-
tion, inertia, aerodynamics, mass distribution, etc.), namely
the newtonian mechanics underlying everyday physical in-
teractions, which substantially limits their ability to reflect
genuine physical intelligence.
To fill this gap, we recast language-conditioned robotic ma-
nipulation through the lens of dynamic extrinsic dexter-
ity [8], focusing on throwing-based object manipulation.
In this setting, the manipulator must estimate the object’s
physical properties, such as its mass, center of mass (CoM),
and friction, and plan the forces and contacts needed to
send the object to a target location outside its own kine-
matic workspace.
We introduce PhyDEX, a high-fidelity synthetic manipu-
lation dataset comprising 35k trajectories across 9 throw-
ing tasks and 100 scenes.
It covers rigid, irregular, and
dynamic-mass objects, as well as diverse destination types,
with all physical properties randomized. PhyDEX is gener-
ated by a fully decoupled, autonomous simulation pipeline
that separates asset specification, skill policies, task com-
position, and rendering.
Each task is built by retrieving
embodiments, scenes, and objects from an asset library
and composing scripted skill policies into complete behav-
iors. This compositional design enables automatic gener-
ation of object-level annotations, physical randomization,
and collision-aware motion plans, supporting systematic
VLA validation through fine-tuning and closed-loop eval-
uation.
To evaluate the physical understanding of current poli-
cies, we select three representative policies:
GR00T
N1.7 [3] as a general-purpose policy, DynamicVLA [54]
as a high-frequency policy, and ForceVLA [57] as a multi-
sensory VLA. Our experiments show that current policies
are not yet reliable for tasks requiring physical understand-
ing, with average success rates below 10%.
We further
identify generalization and the probing of object physical
properties as the main bottlenecks of current systems. To
confirm that this trend holds beyond a single setup, we ad-
ditionally conduct evaluations across multiple platforms, in-
cluding a humanoid robot and real-world experiments.
In summary, our contributions are threefold:
• We propose PhyDEX, a diverse and comprehensive
dataset of nine throwing tasks for studying physical un-
derstanding in robot manipulation.
• We introduce a scalable construction pipeline that gener-
ates diverse, end-to-end data for throwing tasks.
• We conduct extensive evaluations across multiple embod-
iments, including robot arms and humanoids, in both sim-
ulation and real-world experiments. Our results show that
data scarcity and physical blindness are the main bottle-
necks keeping current policies from performing reliably
on physical understanding tasks.
2. Related Works
2.1. Language-conditioned Robot Manipulation
Recently, language-conditioned robot manipulation sys-
tems have attracted much attention and achieved great
progress, owing to their generalizability, scalability, and
potential for deployment in the physical world.
Related
methods can be roughly categorized into two branches:
autoregressive-based approaches [5, 13, 19, 24, 25, 40, 50]
and diffusion-based approaches [2, 15, 20, 23, 41, 51, 56].
While these methods have significantly advanced overall
performance, their physical knowledge is interpolated from
the training data distribution and its reliability remains un-
verified.
2.2. Language-conditioned Dynamic Manipulation
Our work is closely related to dynamic manipulation, which
demands policies that are fast, reactive, and precise. Mod-
eling such dynamics is challenging, as it relies on physical
properties of both the objects and the manipulator that are
difficult to estimate [35]. In the language-conditioned set-
ting, most recent work tracks a target object under static
and predictable conditions [27, 62], adopts asynchronous
inference to hide latency [17, 31, 49], or extends control to
more precise 6-DoF manipulation [29, 54]. While effective,

physical property. In this work, we design a beyond-reach
manipulation benchmark in which the object must be moved
to a target location outside the kinematic range of the ma-
nipulator, making the understanding of physical properties
a decisive factor for success.
2.3. Physical Property Understanding
To throw an object accurately to a target, a robot must first
estimate the object’s physical properties. Studies in cogni-
tive science [36, 59] suggest that humans infer such prop-
erties not only from visual appearance but also from ma-
terial experience. Existing approaches to physical property
estimation fall into two main lines of work: those that en-
code an object’s properties implicitly [1, 42, 52, 59] and
those that predict them explicitly [33, 46, 48, 58]. While ef-
fective, these methods mostly target object-centric settings
in general domains, and robotic scenarios remain underex-
plored. A few works in robot learning [33, 55] have tack-
led this problem, but they are limited to simple pick-and-
place or contact-rich manipulation tasks. These tasks are
not demanding enough to properly evaluate a model’s phys-
ical understanding. To fill this gap, we explicitly randomize
object physical properties, such as mass, CoM, and friction,
to evaluate a model’s ability to accurately estimate object
properties and reason about physics.
3. PhyDEX
In this section, we present the task taxonomy, detailed
dataset statistics, and the data creation pipeline, which are
illustrated in Figure 2 and Figure 3, respectively.
3.1. Task Taxonomy & Statistics
PhyDEX comprises 9 tasks, 35,357 episodes, and 143.2
hours of data recorded at 10 Hz. It is designed to evaluate
how well policies estimate the physical properties of objects
from different perspectives.
Tasks.
In PhyDEX, policies perform a throwing task
at three levels of object difficulty:
(1) Uniform Object
(31.8%): objects with uniformly distributed mass and a cen-
tered center of mass (CoM); (2) Irregular Object (39.2%):
objects with non-uniform mass distribution and a CoM off-
set toward one end; and (3) Dynamic Object (29.0%): ob-
jects whose mass and CoM change during the interaction.
In addition, to test generalization and timing, we use three
destination settings:
static single destination (31.76%),
static multiple destinations (29.77%), and moving single
destination (38.47%).
Assets. We provide 107 curated object assets, sourced from
RoboCasa [37], RoboTwin 2.0 [7], and Objaverse-XL [9]:
20 destination objects, 46 uniform objects, 37 irregular ob-
jects, and 4 dynamic objects. For environments, PhyDEX
includes 100 RoboCasa kitchen scenes spanning diverse
layouts and styles.
3.2. Benchmark Construction Pipeline
In this section, we describe the data synthesis pipeline of
PhyDEX. We first construct the environment by specifying
the task, target object, destination, and scene. We then ap-
ply domain randomization to diversify these environments.
Finally, we generate approaching and throwing trajectories
and define the procedures for storing the resulting data.

3.2.2. Domain Randomization
To enrich the diversity of PhyDEX, we apply three types of
randomization.
Visual diversity. We perturb the poses of the two side cam-
eras and the wrist camera by up to ±5◦in rotation and
±5 cm in translation. We also build a library of 100 en-
vironment maps, each with randomized light temperature
and intensity, to simulate diverse natural illumination con-
ditions.
Trajectory diversity. We introduce additional randomness
in grasp pose selection output from GraspGen-X [14]. For
instance, our autonomous grasp pose generation pipeline
produces millions of grasp candidates. We retain the top
64 high-confidence grasps that are geometrically diverse,
requiring each selected grasp to differ from all previously
selected ones by more than 1 cm in position or 20◦in orien-
tation, and randomly sample the final grasp pose from this
set. We further randomize the object position by ±10 cm
along the x- and y-axes, the object yaw by ±180◦, and the
initial robot joint positions by ±0.09 rad.
Physical diversity. To evaluate whether policies can in-
fer physical properties, we randomize each object’s physi-
cal parameters independently of its appearance, so visually
identical objects may behave differently. Specifically, we
scale the object mass by a factor in [0.5, 2.0], offset the cen-
ter of mass by up to ±15 mm along each axis, and scale the
friction coefficient by a factor in [0.8, 1.2].
3.2.3. Trajectory Generation
To produce plausible and successful trajectories, we intro-
duce a closed-loop optimization procedure that executes
each candidate trajectory end-to-end and iteratively refines
it based on the resulting feedback. The procedure consists
of three main steps.

grasp candidates for each object. Given the object’s place-

collide with surrounding geometry, such as the supporting
surface or nearby objects. The remaining feasible grasps
are then ranked by confidence score and geometric diver-
sity, and one is sampled from the top-ranked candidates and
passed to the motion planning stage.
Motion planning. We then provide cuRobo [47] with the
scene point cloud extracted from the simulator and the tar-
get grasp pose, and it generates a collision-free trajectory
that approaches the object, grasps it and lifts it.

the release state (qr, ˙qr), and zero acceleration at both ends,

3.2.4. Data Storage
Each successful trajectory is stored in the standard LeR-
obot format [6]. A record contains the language instruc-
tion, multi-view RGB images with their camera parameters,
the robot’s proprioceptive states, and the action labels. It
also holds a 2 s window of wrist force/torque (F/T) read-
ings, which supports training multi-sensory VLA models
such as ForceVLA [57]. Other modalities, such as depth
maps, grounding annotations and bounding boxes, can be
added to the recording with a simple configuration option.
For each trajectory we also save an HDF5 file holding the
scene metadata and a replayable copy of the trajectory, so
the data can be reused and extended later.

ForceVLA [57] to assess whether simply adding a force
sensor to the policy is enough to give the model physical
intelligence. Following standard robot manipulation bench-

4.2. Implementation Details
We build our data generation and rendering pipeline on
NVIDIA Isaac Sim [39], a GPU-accelerated simulator
that provides photorealistic rendering and accurate physics.
Data generation runs on 8 NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs with
128 parallel environments. The physics simulation runs at
120 Hz, and data are recorded at 10 Hz. Each RGB cam-
era captures images at a resolution of 352 × 256. The robot
state consists of the 6 joint angles and the gripper open/close
state.
Wrist force/torque readings are stored over a 2 s
window. We fine-tune every policy on 16 NVIDIA A100
(40GB) GPUs using the default hyperparameters released
by its authors.
4.3. Overall Benchmark Results
As shown in Table 1, all three policies perform poorly on
our benchmark, with overall success rates below 10%. Most
successes occur on objects with uniform mass and static
destinations, and no policy completes a single task with a
dynamic destination. When objects have more challeng-
ing physical properties, such as non-uniform or dynamic
mass, the policies rarely complete a full trajectory. The
few successes by DynamicVLA and ForceVLA are likely
due to chance rather than real competence. We attribute
these failures to two main factors. (1) Limited robustness
of pretrained models: Current policies struggle to general-
ize to our highly diverse data, and only about 21.24% of
trajectories include a successful grasp of the object. This
is most pronounced for DynamicVLA, which builds on the
lightweight SmolLM2 [34] backbone to reach 25 Hz in-
ference. However, this backbone is too weak for out-of-

4.4. Further Analysis
Effect of Benchmark Diversity. As shown in Table 1, most
policies achieve near-zero success rates on PhyDEX. We at-
tribute this mainly to the high diversity of our benchmark.
Existing benchmarks either apply limited domain random-
ization, as in LIBERO [30], or evaluate on a small subset
of their data. For example, RoboSPA [10] provides 527K
trajectories but evaluates on only 250 trajectories per task.
Such settings can produce non-zero success rates that hide
how poorly pretrained policies generalize. To test this ex-
planation, we introduce a diversity score α ∈[0, 1] that
controls the strength of domain randomization. Each ran-
domized parameter is normally sampled from a range [a, b],
and we scale the width of this range by α around its nom-
inal value. Thus α = 1 recovers the full PhyDEX setting
and α = 0 disables randomization entirely. As shown in
Table 2, success rates rise steadily as diversity decreases.
ForceVLA improves the most, from 3.3% at α = 1 to
11.3% at α = 0.5 and 35.3% at α = 0, a more than tenfold
gain. GR00T N1.7 follows the same trend, rising from 2%
to 4.7% and then 9.3%, while DynamicVLA stays below
4% in every setting, which again points to the limited capac-
ity of its lightweight backbone. These results confirm that
the high diversity of PhyDEX is a major reason for the near-

Effect of Physical Properties.
As discussed above, we
hypothesize that current policies need a better way to in-
fer the physical properties of objects in order to complete
these tasks. To test this hypothesis, we extract the ground-
truth physical properties of each object and give them to
the policy by concatenating them with the robot propriocep-
tion embedding. As shown in Figure 4, adding this physical
prior clearly improves performance, raising SR by 12.5%
for GR00T N1.7 and 24% for ForceVLA. DynamicVLA’s
performance stays unchanged, which we attribute to its lim-
ited pretraining. These results support our hypothesis. We
believe this finding gives the community evidence to moti-
vate new designs, such as combining VLAs with reinforce-
ment learning, injecting VLM priors, or adding richer sen-
sor information.
Cross-Embodiment Evaluation. To check whether these
failures are specific to our UR5e arm, we build a small
cross-embodiment dataset on a Unitree humanoid using the
same generation pipeline. It contains 2,400 episodes with
static destinations: 640 for uniform objects, 1,280 for ir-
regular objects, and 480 for dynamic objects. We evaluate
each policy over 50 rollouts. As shown in Table 3, Dynam-
icVLA fails on nearly every task. Among the vision-only
policies, GR00T N1.7 performs best, but its average success
rate is only 6%. ForceVLA, which also uses force/torque
input, reaches 8.7%, and thanks to the simpler scene setup
it also succeeds on some dynamic-object trials (6%). Over-
all, the failure patterns on the humanoid closely match those
on PhyDEX, which suggests these failures are shared across
embodiments and are not caused by a single robot platform.

Real-World Evaluation. To test whether our findings carry
over to the physical world, we build a real-world setup
that mirrors our simulation tasks (Figure 5). It consists of
a UR10e arm with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and a wrist-
mounted F/T sensor. A base camera (RealSense D455) and
a wrist camera (RealSense D405) provide visual observa-
tions. Note that this arm differs from the UR5e used in sim-
ulation. For the target objects, we design three 3D-printed
cubes that look identical but have different physical proper-
ties (Figure 6): a standard cube with uniform mass, a cube
with a metal insert near one corner that shifts its center of
mass, and a hollow lightweight cube that we pair with low-
friction tape on the gripper fingers. Because the cubes look
identical, a policy cannot tell them apart from visual cues
and must instead infer their physical properties through in-
teraction. In each trial, the robot must throw the cube into a
basket placed outside its workspace. We collect demonstra-
tions following the same principles as the PhyDEX gener-
ation pipeline (see Appendix B), with 300 demonstrations
per object, fine-tune each policy on this data, and evaluate
it over 50 rollouts per object. As shown in Table 4, Dy-
namicVLA fails on every trial, and GR00T N1.7 reaches
an average success rate of only 10.7%.
Its performance
drops as the physics deviates from the standard cube: 16%
on the uniform cube, 12% on the shifted-CoM cube, and
only 4% on the lightweight low-friction cube. In contrast,
ForceVLA reaches 51.3% on average. We attribute this gap
to its force/torque input, which directly measures the ob-
ject’s weight and inertia once it is grasped. This finding
agrees with our ground-truth physics experiment: when a
policy has access to physical information, its performance

Failure Analysis.
To understand where current policies
break down, we sort every failed rollout into one of four
failure modes, ordered by manipulation stage: ineffective
reaching, where the arm never makes meaningful progress
toward the target object; approaching error, where the arm
reaches the object but arrives in a pose that does not al-
low a grasp; grasping error, where the gripper fails to se-
cure the object or drops it before release; and throwing er-
ror, where the object is grasped and released but misses the
destination. As shown in Figure 7, about 89% of all fail-
ures occur before the throw begins, and grasping error is
the most common mode (38.5%), followed by ineffective
reaching (28.8%) and approaching error (22.0%). These
pre-throw failures are consistent with the limited robustness
discussed in Section 4.3: under our domain randomization
of object poses, camera views, and scene layouts, policies
often cannot even complete the grasp. The breakdown also
differs across policies. DynamicVLA fails mainly in the
earliest stages, with ineffective reaching and approaching
errors together accounting for 64.2% of its failures, which
matches its weak SmolLM2 backbone. Its throwing error is
only 3.4% simply because it rarely gets far enough to throw.
GR00T N1.7 and ForceVLA reach the object more reliably
but still fail mostly at grasping (44.8% and 38.4%). Throw-
ing errors make up a smaller share of failures (10.6% over-
all), but this reflects how few rollouts reach the throwing
stage, not throwing competence: once a throw is attempted,
it rarely succeeds. Together, these results suggest that im-
proving physical understanding alone is not enough, and fu-
ture policies must also become more robust in the reaching
and grasping stages that come before it.
5. Conclusion & Discussion
In this work, we introduced PhyDEX, a benchmark that
evaluates physical reasoning in VLA models through dy-
namic extrinsic dexterity. Unlike existing benchmarks that
focus on gripper–object contact, PhyDEX requires poli-
cies to infer an object’s mass, center of mass, and friction
and to throw it to a destination beyond the robot’s kine-
matic workspace. Built on a scalable and fully automatic
pipeline, it provides 35K trajectories across nine tasks and
100 scenes, covering uniform, irregular, and dynamic-mass

evaluated policies.
objects with static, multiple, and moving destinations. Our
evaluation of three representative VLAs, GR00T N1.7, Dy-
namicVLA, and ForceVLA, shows that current policies are
far from reliable on this problem, with overall success rates
below 3%. We identify two main bottlenecks: limited ro-
bustness of pretrained models, which causes most failures
to occur before the throw begins, and physical blindness,
since policies cannot infer properties that are not directly
observable. Giving policies ground-truth physical proper-
ties roughly doubles their success rate, and experiments on
a humanoid robot show the same failure patterns, which
suggests these limitations are not tied to a single embodi-
ment. We hope PhyDEX will serve as a testbed for phys-
ically grounded robot learning and motivate new designs,
such as explicit physical property estimation, VLA train-
ing with reinforcement learning, and richer multi-sensory
inputs.
Limitations.
Our study has several limitations.
First,
PhyDEX focuses on a single dynamic skill, throwing, and
relies on simplified physical assumptions, randomizing only
mass, center of mass, and friction. Even so, current poli-
cies already fail at this level of difficulty, which shows a
clear lack of physical intelligence. Extending the bench-
mark to more skills, such as pushing, sliding, and pouring,
and to richer physics, such as elasticity, deformable objects,
and aerodynamic effects, would make it more comprehen-
sive. Second, we evaluate only three representative VLA
policies. Including a wider range of models would make
the conclusions stronger. Third, we discuss alternative ap-
proaches only briefly. Methods such as in-context learning,
which can adapt from a few interactions, and reinforcement
learning, which can discover physical properties through
trial and error, may be better at probing physical properties,
and we leave a systematic study of them to future work.
Broader Impact.
PhyDEX aims to advance robots that
understand and act on the physical properties of the objects
they handle. This ability is important for safe, reliable de-
ployment in homes, warehouses, and factories, where ob-
jects vary widely in mass, shape, and friction. By exposing
the physical blindness of current VLA policies, our bench-
mark can help the community build models that fail less
8


often and less unpredictably in real-world settings.
Be-
cause PhyDEX is fully synthetic and generated automati-
cally, it also lowers the cost of studying dynamic manip-
ulation, which is otherwise expensive and risky to collect
on real hardware. At the same time, throwing is a high-
speed, high-energy behavior, and a policy that misjudges
an object’s properties could release it on an unintended tra-
jectory and harm people or damage property nearby. Poli-
cies trained or validated on PhyDEX should therefore not
be deployed without additional safety measures, such as
restricted workspaces, speed and force limits, and human
oversight. Moreover, strong performance in simulation does
not guarantee safe behavior in the real world, so results on
PhyDEX should be complemented by careful physical test-
ing before deployment.
