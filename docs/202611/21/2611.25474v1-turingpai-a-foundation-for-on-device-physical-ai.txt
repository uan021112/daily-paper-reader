TuringPAI: A Foundation for On-Device Physical AI
Foundation Model Team, XPeng Inc.
Abstract
Physical AI requires models that understand spatial relationships and dynamic en-
vironments and translate observations into actions under limited onboard compute.
We present TuringPAI, a family of models for on-device Physical AI comprising
TuringPAI-VL for shared vision-language understanding, TuringPAI-Drive for au-
tonomous driving, and TuringPAI-Embodied for robotic manipulation. TuringPAI-
VL is a 0.65B model built on HybridViT-MLQ, which combines convolutional
local aggregation with Transformer-based global modeling. Multi-scale Latent
Queries integrate spatial features and observation history within the same visual
Transformer, providing a compact, structured interface to the language model.
Training combines general vision-language data with geometric, cross-view, and
temporal supervision. TuringPAI-VL supports general multimodal understand-
ing and improves quantitative geometric perception over Qwen2-VL-2B, while
providing a shared initialization for the two downstream VLA models. For au-
tonomous driving, TuringPAI-Drive maintains all evaluated ADE/FDE metrics
within 1.2% of a full-token HybridViT baseline using the MLQ interface and
achieves comparable aggregate scores in an in-house simulation evaluation. Us-
ing XPeng Native Runtime (XNRT) on a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC, TuringPAI-
Drive generates trajectories at 12 Hz in on-vehicle operation. For robotic manipu-
lation, TuringPAI-Embodied achieves a 96.8% average success rate across the four
LIBERO suites. These results demonstrate a shared vision-language foundation
for driving and robotic manipulation under practical on-device constraints.

Introduction
Physical AI systems must understand changing environments and translate observations into deci-
sions and actions under real-world computational and latency constraints [1–3]. Vision-language
models (VLMs) provide a reusable foundation by connecting visual perception with language in-
structions and reasoning [4–6]. Autonomous driving, a mature and practically important application
of Physical AI, serves as a major setting for developing and evaluating such models [7, 8]. It requires
spatial understanding, reasoning over observations across cameras and time, and timely responses
within limited onboard resources. Robotic manipulation presents complementary demands on object
geometry, interaction state, and instruction following [9].
Visual inputs in Physical AI applications often consist of high-resolution observations collected
across multiple cameras and time steps [10, 11]. These observations contain substantial redundancy,
yet local geometry and small temporal changes can affect downstream actions. VLM pipelines that
encode each observation as a dense patch sequence can spend substantial computation on spatially
correlated content [12, 13]. Forwarding separate visual sequences for successive frames further
expands the language-model context with overlapping scene information [14, 15]. Under limited
onboard compute and strict latency constraints, this redundancy increases the cost of using rich
observation histories. These requirements motivate VLM designs that aggregate local evidence
before global modeling and integrate complementary information across spatial scales and time,
while retaining the cues needed for downstream action.
Architecture alone, however, does not ensure that a VLM learns the physical information required
by these tasks. General image–text alignment and instruction learning support broad semantic un-
derstanding, but do not necessarily provide explicit supervision for metric geometry or temporal
changes. For example, a caption may identify a vehicle ahead without specifying its distance or
whether the gap is closing, although both quantities matter for driving decisions. Prior work shows
that explicit spatial and temporal training tasks can strengthen the corresponding capabilities of
VLMs [16, 17]. Architecture and training data thus play complementary roles: the architecture orga-
nizes observations efﬁciently, while the data guide the learning of information relevant to physical
interaction.
To address these challenges, we present TuringPAI, a family of models for on-device Physical AI
built around a shared vision-language foundation. At its core, TuringPAI-VL combines HybridViT-
MLQ with a training mixture tailored to Physical AI. TuringPAI-VL provides the pretrained foun-
dation for TuringPAI-Drive and TuringPAI-Embodied, which adapt it to autonomous driving and
robotic manipulation, respectively.
HybridViT-MLQ organizes local features, scene context, and observation history into a multi-scale
representation of the current observation. HybridViT ﬁrst uses a convolutional front end to form
locally condensed patch tokens, over which the visual Transformer models global relationships. We
then introduce Multi-scale Latent Queries (MLQ), a hierarchy of feature-conditioned queries that
aggregates visual evidence within the same Transformer. The queries occupy spatial grids at mul-
tiple resolutions and share a spatiotemporal positional encoding with patch tokens. For video, one
query hierarchy is initialized from the current frame and aggregates evidence from both current and
preceding frames. Only the updated query features are passed to the language model. For a ﬁxed
query layout, this interface incorporates observation history without increasing the output sequence
length. HybridViT thus reduces the sequence length used for visual encoding, while MLQ organizes
multi-scale and temporal evidence within a controlled language-model input budget. MLQ can also
be adapted to other ViT backbones with compatible feature, positional, and attention interfaces. As
an additional deployment beneﬁt, the convolutional front end supports heterogeneous execution on
NVIDIA Orin-X hardware [18], including DLA and GPU overlap in our implementation.
To train TuringPAI-VL, we complement general vision–language data with geometric, cross-view,
and temporal tasks. Following the data-construction approach of VLM3 [19], we use pixel-depth
questions and camera-conditioned 3D object localization to supervise distance, position, size, and
orientation, providing geometric cues for driving clearance and object placement. Cross-view cor-
respondence and relative camera-pose data support associations across observations, while multi-
frame driving and manipulation examples provide signals about motion and state changes.
We evaluate TuringPAI-VL on general multimodal benchmarks and spatial and geometric under-
standing tasks relevant to Physical AI. Driving evaluations of TuringPAI-Drive cover open-loop

Methodology
TuringPAI-VL is the shared vision-language foundation of the TuringPAI family. Its design centers
on three elements: HybridViT, Multi-scale Latent Queries (MLQ), and training data for Physical
AI. First, HybridViT combines convolutional aggregation of local image structure with Transformer-
based global modeling over condensed patch tokens (Section 2.1). Second, MLQ aggregates multi-
scale spatial features and observation history into a structured visual interface for the language model
within the same visual Transformer (Section 2.2). Third, we supplement general vision–language
data with geometric, cross-view, and temporal tasks to develop capabilities relevant to physical
interaction (Section 2.3). Together, these elements support spatial and temporal understanding while
enabling efﬁcient visual processing for downstream Physical AI tasks. Figure 2 presents the overall
architecture, downstream adaptation, and representative training examples. Figure 3 details the
MLQ mechanism.

2.2
Multi-scale Latent Queries
Backbone reuse and query construction.
Multi-scale Latent Queries (MLQ) provide a query-
based plug-in that aggregates visual features within a pretrained Transformer. MLQ reuses the
backbone’s attention and feed-forward weights without adding a separate Transformer stack. It can
be incorporated during VLM training without an additional visual pretraining stage. The design is
not speciﬁc to HybridViT; adaptation to another ViT requires compatible feature, positional, and
attention interfaces.
In HybridViT-MLQ, multi-scale pooling and linear projection of convolutional features initialize
latent query grids at different spatial resolutions, as illustrated in Figure 3. The grid sizes adapt to
the spatial dimensions of the input feature map. The grids are ﬂattened and concatenated in coarse-
to-ﬁne order, forming a feature-conditioned query hierarchy spanning global, regional, and local
spatial scales.
Uniﬁed spatiotemporal positioning.
We organize video inputs into four-frame observation win-
dows, taking the last frame in each window as the current observation. Each window uses one
latent-query hierarchy initialized from the current frame’s features. A single image is treated as
a one-frame window, with queries initialized from its own features. To position patch tokens and
queries consistently across scales and frames, we extend the DINO-style 2D RoPE [24] used by
TuringViT to three dimensions by introducing a temporal coordinate.

Query-based aggregation.
The asymmetric attention mask in Figure 3 prevents patch tokens from
attending to latent queries, leaving the backbone’s patch-to-patch attention pattern unchanged. La-
tent queries attend to all input patch tokens and to themselves and preceding queries in coarse-to-ﬁne
order. This allows queries to aggregate visual evidence and coarser context without feeding query
states back into patch-token updates. Only the updated query features are projected into the lan-
guage embedding space, forming multi-scale visual tokens that represent the current observation
with evidence from the available history.

2.3
Training Data for Physical AI Capabilities
To train TuringPAI-VL as a foundation for downstream driving and manipulation models, we com-
bine general vision–language data with tasks that provide explicit training signals for spatial geom-
etry, cross-view association, and temporal understanding.
The training data cover three complementary capabilities: general understanding, spatial perception
and reasoning, and multi-image and temporal understanding. These capabilities are learned jointly
within a single VLM. The resulting model directly generates spatial predictions from visual inputs
and task prompts, without additional task-speciﬁc ﬁne-tuning for spatial evaluation. Figure 2(b)
illustrates representative training tasks. Figure 4 summarizes the overall data-pool composition, and
Appendix A provides further examples.
General understanding.
General image–text and instruction data establish vision–language align-
ment and broad semantic understanding. They cover referring-expression grounding, visual ques-
tion answering, text and document understanding, knowledge and STEM, and language interaction.
These tasks support scene interpretation, environmental text reading, and instruction following, pro-
viding a semantic foundation for spatial, geometric, and temporal learning.
Spatial perception and reasoning.
Building on general visual grounding and spatial-relation un-
derstanding, we introduce quantitative geometric supervision following VLM3 [19]. Depth estima-
tion and 3D object localization provide information about distance, size, and orientation, offering
geometric cues for assessing following distances, safety margins, and driving clearance, as well as

Experiments
This section evaluates three models within the TuringPAI family:
TuringPAI-VL, the vision-
language foundation model, and two domain-speciﬁc extensions, TuringPAI-Drive and TuringPAI-
Embodied. TuringPAI-VL combines HybridViT-MLQ with a Qwen2.5-0.5B [26] language model
and is evaluated against comparable VLMs for general multimodal and physical-world understand-
ing. TuringPAI-Drive extends the foundation model with a trajectory planning head. It maintains
driving ADE/FDE metrics within 1.2% of the full-token HybridViT baseline, with comparable
driving-quality scores. On a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC, TuringPAI-Drive generates trajectories
at 12 Hz in on-vehicle operation. TuringPAI-Embodied integrates a ﬂow-matching action expert
with the same foundation model for robotic manipulation. It achieves 96.8% average success on
LIBERO, close to the published OpenPI π0.5 result. On a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC, its measured
inference stages after CNN patch embedding sum to 168.3 ms. Together, these results demonstrate
the versatility of the TuringPAI foundation across autonomous driving and robotic manipulation. All
on-device deployment and latency results reported in this work use a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC.

3.1
TuringPAI-VL: Foundation Model Training and Evaluation
TuringPAI-VL is the compact VLM designed to meet the on-device budget of XPeng’s Orin-X-
based in-vehicle computing platform while supporting real-time inference. It pairs HybridViT-MLQ
(156M) with Qwen2.5-0.5B [26], for approximately 0.65B parameters in total. Training proceeds
in two stages: visual-token alignment with the language model frozen, followed by joint training on
the full data pool. We then evaluate TuringPAI-VL on general multimodal benchmarks, including
image and video understanding, and on spatial-perception benchmarks. The pretrained TuringPAI-
VL checkpoint, including both the visual encoder and language model, initializes the driving and
robotic-manipulation VLAs.
3.1.1
Training Paradigm and Data Composition
To balance general-purpose vision-language capabilities with the physical-world understanding re-
quired by Physical AI, TuringPAI-VL adopts a two-stage training paradigm. The ﬁrst stage estab-
lishes vision-language alignment using a general image–text corpus, with the language model frozen
and only the visual encoder and adapter trained. Building on this initialization, the second stage
jointly optimizes all model parameters using a broader image/video–text mixture that combines gen-
eral multimodal tasks with Physical-AI-oriented supervision. In particular, the training data cover
geometric and spatial perception, cross-view understanding, and temporal reasoning, alongside cap-
tioning, VQA, text and document understanding, knowledge, and STEM. These tasks span both
autonomous-driving environments and robotic-manipulation scenarios. Furthermore, logit-level dis-
tillation from a larger TuringViT-based VLM is incorporated into the second stage to transfer its
multimodal capabilities to the compact model. Both stages use native dynamic resolution. Table 1
summarizes the training conﬁgurations for both stages, including their objectives, batch sizes, learn-
ing rates, and computational costs.

Benchmark Results
Following the three capability areas introduced in Section 2.3, we evaluate general understanding,
spatial perception and reasoning, and multi-image and temporal understanding. We use the same
jointly trained 0.65B-parameter TuringPAI-VL checkpoint for all tasks, without task-speciﬁc ﬁne-
tuning.
General understanding.
TuringPAI-VL supports a range of image understanding tasks (Table 2).
We use InternVL3-1B Base [27] as the primary reference because it is closer in parameter count to
TuringPAI-VL. TuringPAI-VL scores higher on chart interpretation (76.96 vs. 62.92 on ChartQA),
document understanding (80.52 vs. 74.75 on DocVQA), and visual mathematical reasoning (54.20
vs. 36.60 on MathVista mini). InternVL3-1B Base remains stronger on TextVQA and MMMU.
We include the larger Qwen2-VL-2B Base [5] as an additional reference. TuringPAI-VL retains
advantages in chart interpretation and visual mathematical reasoning, while Qwen2-VL-2B Base
is stronger in counting, document understanding, and several general VQA tasks, as illustrated by
CountBenchQA, DocVQA, and MMBench. For 2D referring-expression grounding, TuringPAI-VL
achieves an average score of 80.91 on RefCOCO, outperforming InternVL3-1B Base (77.88).

3.2
TuringPAI-Drive: Autonomous Driving VLA Training and Evaluation
TuringPAI-Drive is initialized from the TuringPAI-VL checkpoint described in Section 3.1, with a
trajectory-planning head added for end-to-end trajectory prediction. This section presents a com-
prehensive evaluation of TuringPAI-Drive from four aspects. We ﬁrst introduce the driving inputs
and training data, covering the multi-camera temporal observations and large-scale in-house driving
dataset. We then evaluate driving performance through open-loop trajectory prediction and XPeng’s
in-house CCES suite, with particular emphasis on comparing HybridViT and HybridViT-MLQ to
assess the impact of MLQ-based visual token compression. Next, we present the on-device deploy-
ment and runtime optimizations enabled by XPeng Native Runtime (XNRT), focusing on inference
efﬁciency and numerical accuracy on a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC. Finally, we conduct an attri-
bution analysis with qualitative visual comparisons between HybridViT and HybridViT-MLQ to
further examine the effects of visual representation compression.
3.2.1
Driving Inputs and Training Data
TuringPAI-Drive takes four consecutive frames, including the current frame from a seven-camera
surround-view system covering 360 degrees, sampled at 12 Hz, as input and predicts a 6 s ego trajec-
tory represented by 72 waypoints. The driving model uses the HybridViT-MLQ encoder described in
Section 2.2 to form a compact visual representation of the seven-camera input sequence. The model
is initially trained on XPeng’s in-house driving dataset, comprising approximately 298,000 hours
of recordings segmented into 35M clips. The data cover diverse driving environments, including
highways, urban roads, and parking scenarios. They encompass a broad range of driving behaviors,
including lateral maneuvers such as automatic lane changes (ALC), navigation lane changes (NLC),
U-turns, and sharp curves; longitudinal maneuvers such as stopping at red lights and starting from
a standstill; and challenging scenarios such as narrow-road negotiation, yielding to vulnerable road
users, and driver takeovers.

Open-loop trajectory prediction.
We evaluate trajectory prediction accuracy using Average Dis-
placement Error (ADE) and Final Displacement Error (FDE), measured separately in the lateral and
longitudinal directions over the full 6 s horizon and the ﬁrst 2 s. We also report mean per-point

We evaluate the effect of MLQ-based token compression by comparing HybridViT-MLQ under the
on-device token budget with the full-token HybridViT. At the 6 s horizon, HybridViT-MLQ achieves
1.2% lower lateral ADE and 1.1% lower lateral FDE, while incurring only marginal increases of
0.5% and 0.4% in longitudinal ADE and FDE, respectively. Across both prediction horizons, all
ADE and FDE metrics remain within 1.2% of those of the full-token HybridViT. Moreover, yaw
error and miss rate are slightly reduced. These results demonstrate that MLQ effectively compresses
the visual-token representation while largely preserving, and in some metrics slightly improving,
trajectory prediction accuracy (Table 5).

3.2.3
On-Device Deployment and Runtime Optimization
TuringPAI-Drive is XPeng’s VLA 2.0 Lite model and uses a 0.65B VLM backbone.
XPeng’s
NVIDIA Orin-X-based in-vehicle computing platform contains two Orin-X SoCs; TuringPAI-Drive
deployment and all latency measurements reported here use a single Orin-X SoC. The GPU and
DLA stages of the model execute within that same SoC. Deploying TuringPAI-Drive on automotive-
grade hardware presents signiﬁcant challenges in balancing inference latency, numerical accuracy,
and computational efﬁciency. This optimization reduces the GPU-side VLM preﬁll latency to 69.773
ms, bringing the VLM backbone below the 83.3 ms reference period corresponding to 12 Hz. At the
system level, TuringPAI-Drive generates trajectories at 12 Hz in on-vehicle operation.
To address these challenges, we deploy TuringPAI-Drive using the XPeng Native Runtime (XNRT),
an in-house inference runtime speciﬁcally designed for efﬁcient on-device AI deployment. Unlike
conventional deployment frameworks that primarily rely on general-purpose operator optimization,
XNRT provides a uniﬁed optimization framework across model architecture, numerical precision,
and target hardware. It is designed to be quantization-aware-training (QAT)-friendly and supports
model-speciﬁc, precision-aware, and hardware-aware optimizations. Through these capabilities,
XNRT enables aggressive computational acceleration while preserving numerical ﬁdelity, making
it possible to deploy TuringPAI-Drive on a single Orin-X SoC within the required real-time latency
budget.

The two INT8 conﬁgurations use QAT and SmoothQuant to mitigate quantization-induced numeri-
cal degradation, while differing in quantization granularity and execution precision. TensorRT FP32
and FP16 serve as additional precision baselines.
For the TensorRT FP32+INT8 conﬁguration, per-tensor INT8 quantization is applied to Linear and
Convolution operators, while the remaining operators primarily execute in FP32. The FP16 con-
ﬁguration exhibits substantial numerical degradation, as reﬂected by its low cosine similarity and
extremely large maximum absolute error. Therefore, FP32+INT8 mixed precision is adopted as the
TensorRT mixed-precision baseline.
In contrast, XNRT adopts a more ﬂexible mixed-precision execution strategy. It applies dynamic
per-token INT8 quantization to Linear and Convolution operators, while executing most remain-
ing operators in BF16. FP32 precision is retained for numerically sensitive operations, including
LayerNorm, RMSNorm, and operators involving transcendental functions such as Tanh. Beyond
quantization, XNRT further incorporates operator fusion and dimension-aware custom optimiza-
tions to reduce memory-access overhead and kernel scheduling costs. This combination of ﬁne-
grained quantization, mixed-precision execution, and hardware-speciﬁc operator optimization en-
ables XNRT to achieve substantially higher inference efﬁciency while maintaining high numerical
ﬁdelity. In particular, XNRT preserves mean and minimum cosine similarities of 0.998369 and
0.998106, respectively, relative to the PyTorch FP32 reference, substantially outperforming the Ten-
sorRT FP32+INT8 conﬁguration.

deviations. Its cosine mean and minimum are 0.678869 and 0.657518, respectively, and its worst-

In terms of inference efﬁciency, XNRT achieves a 4.93× speedup over TensorRT FP32, correspond-
ing to a 79.71% reduction in VLM preﬁll latency. Compared with TensorRT FP32+INT8 mixed
precision, XNRT delivers a further 3.69× speedup, reducing latency by 72.88%. These improve-
ments demonstrate the effectiveness of XNRT’s combined optimization strategy, particularly its
dynamic per-token quantization, BF16 execution for non-GEMM operators, operator fusion, and
hardware-aware kernel optimization.
The accuracy comparison further highlights the advantage of XNRT. Relative to TensorRT
FP32+INT8 mixed precision, XNRT improves cosine mean by 0.030832, reducing the cosine er-
ror (1 −cosine mean) to approximately 1/19.9 of that of the TensorRT conﬁguration. Meanwhile,
its MAE, RMSE, maximum absolute error, and relative L2 error are approximately 4.39×, 4.43×,
3.82×, and 4.43× lower, respectively. These results indicate that the latency reduction achieved by
XNRT does not come at the expense of substantial numerical accuracy loss. Instead, its ﬁne-grained
quantization and heterogeneous precision execution provide a more favorable balance between com-
putational efﬁciency and numerical ﬁdelity.
Deployment Signiﬁcance.
Taken together, XNRT establishes a substantially improved latency–
accuracy Pareto frontier for on-device VLA inference on a single Orin-X SoC. While TensorRT
FP32 provides the highest numerical accuracy, its 343.863 ms latency is approximately 4.93 times
that of XNRT and is far beyond the 12 FPS requirement. TensorRT FP32+INT8 mixed precision
reduces latency but suffers from considerable accuracy degradation. In contrast, XNRT achieves
both a sub-70 ms VLM preﬁll latency and cosine accuracy metrics above 0.99, outperforming the
TensorRT mixed-precision conﬁguration in both latency and numerical accuracy.
These results establish XNRT as a key enabling component for deploying large-scale VLA models
on resource-constrained automotive platforms. By jointly optimizing quantization granularity, nu-
merical precision, operator execution, and hardware-speciﬁc scheduling, XNRT makes it feasible
to run TuringPAI-Drive on a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC while meeting the real-time requirements
of XPeng’s Max intelligent vehicles. These vehicles use a two-SoC Orin-X computing platform,
whereas the model deployment and reported evaluation use one Orin-X SoC. Future work will fur-
ther investigate the generalization of these optimizations across different vehicle platforms, input
distributions, and VLA model scales.

Attribution Analysis
To complement the quantitative driving evaluation above, we conduct a qualitative analysis of the
visual evidence associated with the planned trajectory in HybridViT and HybridViT-MLQ, asking
whether HybridViT-MLQ’s efﬁcient, multi-scale aggregation of visual evidence retains the key vi-
sual cues associated with the planned trajectory. Attribution maps (or saliency maps) are a standard
post-hoc tool for explaining vision models [66–69], but have been less explored in driving-VLA
evaluation [70]. We apply Integrated Gradients, which attributes a model output to its input features
by integrating gradients along a straight-line path from a baseline input to the observed input [71].
Speciﬁcally, we compute pixel-level attributions with respect to the planned trajectory and visualize
them for all seven camera views at the last frame of the input sequence. Figure 6 presents two repre-
sentative scenes; for each scene, the rows show the original images, followed by the HybridViT and
HybridViT-MLQ attribution maps.
Across the cases we examined, HybridViT and HybridViT-MLQ assign attribution to broadly similar
objects at similar image locations and HybridViT-MLQ tends to produce more spatially concentrated
attribution around key objects, with weaker responses over background regions such as the sky,
buildings, and vegetation, although this pattern varies across camera views. In Figure 6(a), both
models highlight the car directly ahead, the cyclist on the right, and the pedestrian at the front left
near the crossing. In Figure 6(b), both highlight the trafﬁc signal, the rear wheel or taillights of
the lead SUV, while showing weaker responses in the darker surroundings. In the front ﬁsheye
view, HybridViT-MLQ also shows attribution around a front-left VRU that is less apparent in the
HybridViT maps; this may indicate an additional visual cue associated with the planned trajectory.
This is a qualitative difference in the attribution maps; further examples are provided in Appendix C.

TuringPAI-Embodied: Robotic Manipulation VLA
TuringPAI-Embodied combines the pretrained TuringPAI-VL backbone from Section 3.1 with a π0-
style ﬂow-matching action expert [9]. Each observation includes two 256 × 256 camera views and
an 80-dimensional canonical robot state. The action expert uses ten denoising steps to generate a
chunk of six consecutive actions in a canonical action representation. We pretrain the policy on
roughly 500 hours of manipulation data from multiple robot embodiments and then ﬁne-tune it with
supervision from LIBERO demonstrations. We use the resulting policy for the LIBERO evaluation
and the on-device timing measurements below.
TuringPAI-Embodied succeeded in 1,936 of 2,000 LIBERO [20] trials (96.8%), with 500 trials per
suite (Table 8). Per-suite success rates range from 93.2% on LIBERO-10 to 99.6% on Object. The
overall rate is close to the 96.85% OpenPI reports for its LIBERO-ﬁne-tuned π0.5 policy [72, 73].

Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we presented TuringPAI, a foundation for on-device Physical AI that connects gen-
eral vision-language understanding with real-world autonomous driving and robotic intelligence.
At its core, TuringPAI-VL combines HybridViT-MLQ with Physical-AI-oriented training to de-
velop general multimodal, spatial, geometric, and temporal understanding within a compact 0.65B-
parameter model. By integrating convolutional local aggregation with multi-scale latent queries,
HybridViT-MLQ substantially reduces the visual-token budget while preserving the spatial and tem-
poral evidence required by downstream physical tasks. Building on this foundation, we developed
two domain-speciﬁc VLA systems, TuringPAI-Drive and TuringPAI-Embodied, demonstrating the
transfer of shared vision-language capabilities to autonomous driving and robotic manipulation.
TuringPAI-Drive preserves trajectory prediction accuracy within 1.2% of the full-token Hybrid-
ViT baseline and generates trajectories at 12 Hz on a single NVIDIA Orin-X SoC in on-vehicle
operation. TuringPAI-Embodied achieves a 96.8% average success rate across the four LIBERO

edge-computing constraints.
Looking ahead, we see several opportunities to advance TuringPAI toward more general and
autonomous Physical AI. First, we aim to extend temporal modeling beyond short observation
windows toward long-horizon physical understanding, incorporating persistent temporal memory,
streaming representations, and action-conditioned future prediction to model evolving environments
and interaction dynamics. Second, we plan to strengthen the embodied foundation through large-
scale cross-embodiment and cross-task learning, incorporating richer proprioceptive and contact
information to improve generalization across robot platforms and interaction scenarios. Finally, we
aim to develop closed-loop Physical AI systems that integrate world models, outcome evaluation,
and failure-aware recovery, enabling policies to predict, act, evaluate, and adapt through sustained
real-world interaction. Together with continued advances in asynchronous inference and heteroge-
neous edge computing, these directions will move TuringPAI toward more capable, adaptive, and
reliable Physical AI systems.
