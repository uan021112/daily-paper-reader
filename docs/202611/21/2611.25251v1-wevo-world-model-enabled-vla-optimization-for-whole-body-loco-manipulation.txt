I. INTRODUCTION
Vision-language-action (VLA) models [1]–[5] couple
pretrained vision-language backbones with action heads
learned from demonstrations, whose scale and quality
strongly determine performance. Collecting such demon-
strations through teleoperation is particularly difficult for
high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) humanoids. Whole-body con-
trollers (WBCs) [6], [7] coordinate balance, locomotion, and
bimanual control but do not remove this data bottleneck.
Real-to-sim-to-real (R2S2R) systems based on 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS) [8]–[10] instead reconstruct deployment
scenes and generate whole-body trajectories without teleop-
eration. Policies trained on these synthetic demonstrations
can transfer to hardware, but residual appearance and dynam-
ics gaps still require learning from deployment experience.
Reinforcement learning can improve a behavior-cloned
(BC) policy under compounding errors and residual sim-to-
real mismatch, but VLA post-training requires long-horizon
rewards and optimization through diffusion or flow ac-
tion heads. Offline-to-online methods often update simple
MLP policies [11]–[13], limiting expressiveness through
their tractable action densities. Advantage-weighted methods
support richer policies [14]–[17], but often require human
DAgger interventions. RL-100 [18] differentiates the PPO

supplies policy initialization and physically grounded play
data; autonomous real robot rollouts align the video WM
with deployment observations, making it a more faithful
environment for policy improvement.
II. BACKGROUND
A. The LEGS Simulator
The LEGS simulator [8] is an R2S2R data engine for the
Unitree G1. It combines a 3DGS reconstruction of the static
scene with interactable object meshes recovered using SAM
3D [37]. MuJoCo [38] models robot–object contacts, while
the 3DGS scene provides background rendering. SONIC [6]
tracks scripted locomotion and manipulation primitives, pro-
ducing teleoperation-free trajectories with state, contact, and
action data for base-VLA training and WM adaptation.
B. Dense Progress Reward Models
SARM [39] estimates progress hierarchically: it first pre-
dicts the current semantic stage, then conditions a second
estimator on that stage to predict continuous within-stage
completion. Dataset-level stage-duration priors combine both
outputs into normalized task progress. SARM2 [40] gener-
alizes this hierarchy across tasks with a mixture-of-experts
stage estimator. Its rollout adaptation uses segment-level
labels of fast progress, slow progress, adjust, or mistake,
together with final task progress.
C. DreamDojo: A Whole-Body-Control World Model
DreamDojo [41] is an action-conditioned video WM built
on Cosmos [42]. Given a single seed frame and a sequence
of achieved 43-DoF joint states, it predicts future frames
in fixed-length chunks, ˆot+1:t+k = Wϕ(ot, ˆqt:t+k). Its post-
training on real Unitree G1 WBC trajectories provides
a whole-body prior, while autoregressive error accumula-
tion [43]–[45] motivates broad play data and bounded rollout
horizons.
D. Reinforcement Learning for Flow-Matching Policies

integrating a learned velocity field, but its implicit likelihood
complicates standard policy gradients. We consider three
alternatives. DICE-RL [23] searches modes of a frozen flow
prior πpre and learns a local residual correction:

III. METHOD
A. Real-to-Sim-to-Real Whole-Body VLA Initialization
For each task, we construct a LEGS simulator and train
a whole-body VLA with substantial hardware success, en-
abling teleoperation-free WM-adaptation rollouts.
1) Task Construction and Randomization:
Using the
robot-mounted ZED 2i camera [47], we reconstruct the
deployment room and objects in the LEGS simulator and
define task actions, success/failure criteria, and randomiza-
tion. Object position is sampled within a 0.20 m × 0.20 m
region with yaw in [−180◦, 180◦]; the base starts within a
0.30 m × 0.30 m region centered 1.5 m from the table with
yaw in [−30◦, 30◦]. Camera roll and pitch are independently
perturbed within ±15◦.
2) Base VLA Training: We collect 200 synthetic episodes
per task in under 30 minutes on 8 NVIDIA L40S GPUs, then
fine-tune a task-specific GR00T N1.7 policy [5] for 50,000
steps, freezing the vision-language backbone. Training takes
3.5 hours on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
3) Autonomous Real Robot Rollouts: The initial policy
runs autonomously until we construct a balanced retained
dataset of 20 real robot trajectories per task, comprising 10
successes and 10 failures. Because the base policies have SR
below 50%, collection can require more than 20 attempts;
excess failed trajectories are discarded and are not used
for training. Each retained trajectory contains synchronized
egocentric RGB, joint states, and VLA actions. The same
retained dataset is used for both task-specific WM adaptation
and the Real RL baseline, ensuring identical real robot
training data. Balancing the outcomes prevents the WM
adaptation set from being dominated by failures.
B. World Model Fine-Tuning
We
initialize
from
DreamDojo’s
released
2B G1 post-train checkpoint [41] and train in two
stages. LEGS simulator play data cover contact dynamics
and locomotion-induced viewpoint changes; each task then
uses 200 synthetic demonstrations and the 20 retained
real robot trajectories described above to combine broad
state-action coverage with deployment appearance.
1) Assets: We use over 100 household objects from open-
source 3D object datasets [48]–[51] and randomize their
mass and friction across episodes.
2) Manipulation Play Data: Each two-minute manipu-
lation episode samples 5–8 objects with randomized table
poses and one active arm; the other remains idle. We perturb
the nominal grasp target with independent uniform offsets of
±2, ±3, and ±2 cm and allow up to three grasp-and-place
attempts per object. All outcomes, including misses, slips,
collisions, and failed placements, are retained to supervise
contact dynamics.
3) Locomotion Play Data: Each locomotion episode sam-
ples an approach to a 0.50 m standoff followed by a ran-
dom 90◦turn, or an approach to 0.80 m followed by a
clockwise or counterclockwise semicircle. Forward speed
is 0.2–0.3 m/s, lateral velocity is zero, and SONIC tracks
yaw. These trajectories expose the WM to large egocentric
viewpoint changes caused by turning and sustained walking,
which are sparse in task-specific rollouts.
4) Play Data Mid-Training: The corpus contains 400 ma-
nipulation episodes (10 hours) and 100 locomotion episodes
(1 hour), totaling 500 episodes and 11 hours. Starting from
2B G1 post-train, we mid-train for 15,000 steps and
10 hours on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, producing the shared
checkpoint ϕmid.
5) Task-Specific Fine-Tuning: For each task τ, we fine-
tune ϕmid on 200 synthetic demonstrations and the same
20 retained real robot trajectories. Synthetic trajectories
cover randomized configurations and successful motions;
real trajectories provide deployment-camera successes and
failures that bridge the residual visual gap. Fine-tuning runs
for 30,000 steps and 17 hours per task, producing ϕτ for
policy rollout and optimization.

F. Closed-Loop Policy Improvement
1) Implementation
Details:
After
task-specific
fine-
tuning, we freeze the WM and optimize each task for 15
rounds. Each round generates 32 current-policy episodes
from real seed frames for up to 60 s. The progress model
labels each video and truncates successful episodes at com-
pletion (Sec. III-D), after which we perform 103 updates.
Policy, critic, target-network, and optimizer states persist
across rounds while the rollout batch is refreshed, yielding
480 episodes and 1.5 × 104 updates per task. We evaluate
every checkpoint on the real robot. Each run uses 8 NVIDIA
H100 GPUs with eight parallel workers, each hosting an
independent WM, WBC, and MuJoCo instance. A full run
takes about 12 hours: 75% for video generation, 20% for RL
updates, and the remainder for progress labeling and feature
caching.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
We ask three research questions in this section:
• Q1: How do simulated whole-body play data affect WM
fidelity and downstream policy learning?
• Q2: Why is a video WM a better policy-improvement
environment than the simulator?
• Q3: How should policies learn effectively from WM
rollouts?
A. Experimental Setup
1) Tasks: We evaluate three real robot tasks with increas-
ing locomotion–manipulation coordination (Fig. 5). In Pick-
and-Place Bottle (PNP), the robot walks to a table, grasps
a bottle with its left hand, and places it upright in a box.
Pick-Turn-Place Bottle (PTP) adds post-grasp locomotion:
the robot steps back, turns about 90◦, approaches a floor
basket, squats, and places the bottle inside. In Bimanual
Move Basket (Basket), it approaches a side platform, squats
to lift a basket bimanually, returns to the table, and places
it. The tasks span stationary manipulation, object transport,
and bimanual control.
2) World Model Evaluation: For each task, we compare
two WMs under a matched budget of 45,000 optimization
steps: the full model receives 15,000 steps of mid-training
on play data followed by 30,000 task-specific steps, while
the ablation skips play data and uses all 45,000 steps for
task-specific fine-tuning. Both use the same 200 synthetic
episodes and 20 retained real robot trajectories. A separate
held-out set contains 20 synthetic and 20 real episodes per
task, each split evenly between success and failure, and is
used only to report WM fidelity. We replay each recorded
action trajectory and compare the generated frames with
the temporally aligned ground truth, excluding terminal-
frame padding. We report PSNR, interaction PSNR (iPSNR),
SSIM, LPIPS, and FID. iPSNR is computed over the union
of robot-hand and manipulated-object masks obtained with
Grounding DINO [53] and SAM 2 [54]. The same frames
and masks are used for both models; higher PSNR, iPSNR,
and SSIM are better, while lower LPIPS and FID are better.
3) Policy Evaluation: For each policy-task pair, we report
success rate (SR) over 20 real robot trials. Table II reports the
final checkpoint from one training run per method and task,
evaluated over these 20 trials without checkpoint selection on
hardware results. Success requires the bottle to stand upright
in the tabletop box for Pick-and-Place Bottle, the bottle to be
placed in the floor basket for Pick-Turn-Place Bottle, or the
bimanually carried basket to be placed stably on the table for
Bimanual Move Basket. To track learning progress, we also

B. Q1: Effect of WM Mid-Training on Play Data
Table I shows a consistent benefit from play data: the

D. Q3: How Should Policies Learn from WM Rollouts?
Effective policy improvement requires learning from the
full rollout distribution. VLAW behavior-clones only suc-
cessful WM rollouts and achieves an average SR of 33.3%,
whereas DICE-RL learns from successful, failed, and sub-
optimal episodes and reaches 75.0%. Dense progress assigns
graded credit before task completion, allowing each rollout
to contribute to learning.
Dense reward is particularly important because WM roll-
out generation is computationally intensive. WMPO uses
sparse terminal rewards and achieves an average SR of
40.0%, utilizing only terminal outcome label from each
trajectory. The original paper’s ablation [29] further shows
substantially larger gains with 32 H100 GPUs than with 8,
the budget used here. Consistently, replacing dense progress
with sparse feedback in DICE-RL lowers mean success from
75.0% to 45.0%.
The rollout buffer must track the evolving policy distribu-
tion. In the Offline ablation, a static buffer of 480 trajectories
is generated once by the base behavior policy πBC and reused
under the same training-step budget. As the actor departs
from πBC, the growing policy–data distribution shift reduces
mean SR to 58.3%, compared with 75.0% when trajectories
are recollected under the current policy each round. The gains
are robust across policy-update rules: AWR and DICE-RL
achieve mean SRs of 71.7% and 75.0%, while ReinFlow
exhibits less stable optimization but still exceeds both BC
and Real RL in mean SR. Together, these results identify
full-distribution learning, dense rewards, and iterative online
rollout collection as key ingredients for effective WM-based
RL.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented WEVO, a teleoperation-free framework for
whole-body humanoid VLA post-training. Its central design
assigns complementary roles to two environments: the sim-
ulator supplies policy initialization and broad, physically
grounded experience, while the video WM incorporates
limited real robot deployment experience and supports policy
improvement. This separation matters empirically: direct
simulator RL reduces success on all three tasks, whereas
video WM RL raises mean SR from 23.3% to 75.0%,
compared with 48.3% for real robot RL. Dense progress
rewards and online rollout collection further enable effective
learning from generated trajectories.
Limitations include task-specific adaptation. Unitree G1-
specific interfaces preclude zero-shot transfer to other hu-
manoids. Future work should address cross-embodiment
transfer, long-horizon multi-view prediction, rollout effi-
ciency, and joint policy-WM adaptation.
