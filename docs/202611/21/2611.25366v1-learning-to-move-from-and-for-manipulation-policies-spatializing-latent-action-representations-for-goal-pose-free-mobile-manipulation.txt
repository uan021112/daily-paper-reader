Abstract
The success of manipulation policies is closely tied to the spa-
tial relationship between the robot and the target object, as
well as the task being performed, making the ability to adjust
the robot’s pose critical for successful execution. When the
robot starts away from the task, such adjustments require co-
ordinating navigation with local manipulation. Jointly train-
ing these capabilities incurs substantial computational and
data costs, while navigating toward a predefined goal pose
limits the flexibility needed to adapt to task-specific manipu-
lation requirements. To address these challenges, we propose
Learning to Move from and for Manipulation Policies (M4M),
a framework that learns manipulation-aware mobility di-
rectly from pretrained manipulation policies. By leveraging
the hidden representations of the policy’s action-generating
network as dense supervision, together with a limited num-
ber of manipulation execution outcomes, we learn a spa-
tially conditioned embedding that captures task-dependent
manipulation success across candidate robot poses. From
the robot’s current stereo observations, this representation
estimates manipulation success at unvisited poses without
rolling out the manipulation policy at each location. It is
further integrated into navigation policy training, while a
learned handoff policy decides when to transition from nav-
igation to manipulation, enabling the robot to move and
manipulate in a unified, task-aware manner. Across eight
articulated-object tasks in unseen RoboCasa kitchens, M4M
achieves 47.0% mean task success, compared with 26.5%
for the strongest baseline and 19.2% for direct manipulation
execution, and refrigerator-closing experiments show consis-
tent improvements across four frozen manipulation policies.

A robot that can close a refrigerator from a favorable pose
may still fail when it starts farther away. It must approach
the appliance, avoid obstacles, and establish a distance and
viewpoint from which its manipulation policy can complete
the interaction. The handle may otherwise appear in an unfa-
miliar part of the image, or the base may leave insufficient

Learning navigation and manipulation together from
demonstrations requires data covering both behaviors and
their coordination [42]. Reusing an existing manipulation
policy reduces what must be relearned, but leaves a central
question: how should the robot move, and when should it
hand off, before it has visited the candidate stances? Geo-
metric reachability and target proximity do not fully capture
a learned policy’s preferences [6]. Figure 3 illustrates this de-
pendence: four manipulation policies evaluated at the same
refrigerator poses exhibit different success landscapes.
We introduce M4M, which learns both decisions from
policy-derived success supervision. The policy’s latent ac-
tion representations at candidate stances are spatialized into
a success field that a student predicts from the current view,
so the robot can score stances before reaching them. A
compact controller reads this field, together with the target
and traversability maps, and decides at every step whether to
keep moving or hand off, without committing to a target base
pose. Goal-pose-free refers to this execution interface: can-
didate stances are scored, but no fixed navigation endpoint
is selected.
Executing the manipulation policy at every training can-
didate is expensive. Instead, candidate-view action repre-
sentations supply dense supervision, linked to success by
limited execution outcomes. Cross-view distillation transfers
this information to the current observation; privileged value
iteration supervises movement and handoff. Deployment
requires neither candidate-view rendering nor action-expert
queries at candidate stances. Figure 1 shows local approach
and handoff in unseen kitchens, evaluated at 0.5–2.5 m starts.
Our contributions are: (i) spatializing a frozen policy’s la-
tent action representations into a success field over unvisited
stances; (ii) distilling policy-derived movement and handoff
values into a controller that uses this field without explicit
goal-pose selection; and (iii) evaluation on eight tasks in
unseen kitchens, four-policy refrigerator experiments, abla-
tions, representation comparisons, and a shared-budget study.
2. Related Work
Language-guided object navigation.
Open-vocabulary
navigation grounds language in observations and maps to
reach a named object or spatial goal. VLMaps fuses visual–
language features into a spatial map [16], ConceptGraphs
builds open-vocabulary 3D scene graphs [12], and CoW
and VLFM combine target localization with exploration [10,
44]. LM-Nav composes pretrained language, vision, and
navigation models for instruction following [33]. These
approaches support finding and reaching targets; our success
field addresses the subsequent choice of a stance suited to a
particular manipulation policy. HomeRobot and OK-Robot
integrate navigation with grasping and placement [20, 43].

Mobile manipulation and stance selection.
Inverse reach-
ability maps select base poses from arm reachability and
manipulability [37]. N2M2 learns base motions that main-
tain manipulation feasibility [15], while ReLMoGen learns
base and arm subgoals executed by a motion generator [40].
MoManipVLA optimizes mobility and manipulation around
VLA-predicted waypoints [39], and MoTo selects docking
points using VLM-generated interaction keypoints and fea-
sibility objectives [38]. Where2Act, VoxPoser, and SayCan
score object interactions, spatial values, and skill feasibil-
ity [17, 18, 23]; our field evaluates a fixed policy’s success
at unvisited base stances. Closest to our setting, Mobi-π
compares rendered candidate views with initial images from
the policy’s training demonstrations [42], whereas N2M
learns pose preferences from rollouts [6]. We obtain dense
supervision from the frozen policy’s internal action states
and predict candidate-specific success from the current ob-
servation, without rendering or visiting each candidate at
deployment.
Foundation policies.
RT-1, RT-2, PaLM-E, OpenVLA, and
the π family connect visual and language knowledge with
robot control and embodied reasoning [2–5, 9, 19]; Octo
learns a generalist manipulation policy from diverse robot
trajectories [27]. SpatialVLA, Transporter Networks, CLI-
Port, PerAct, and Act3D incorporate spatial structure into
manipulation [11, 30, 35, 36, 46]. These systems address
the interaction itself; we retain the manipulation policy and
choose the stance from which it operates.
Which features predict success.
RRL, R3M, and VC-1
show that pretrained visual representations support control
and embodied tasks [22, 24, 34]. DINOv2 learns general-
purpose visual features [28], while CLIP and SigLIP align
images with language [31, 47]. RL Token builds a com-
pact interface to VLA features for action refinement with
online reinforcement learning [41].
Motivated by these
representation-learning approaches, we probe generic image
features and successive stages of a frozen policy. The action-
expert state ranks stances best across layouts (Sec. 4.4).
Cross-view distillation.
Knowledge distillation and
privileged-information learning transfer richer training-time
information to simpler deployment inputs [14, 21]; FitNets
and Attention Transfer supervise intermediate features or
attention patterns [32, 45]. Visual-policy distillation has
also transferred knowledge across camera configurations [1].
Our asymmetry is spatial: the teacher observes candidate
stance b directly, whereas the student observes pose a and
receives b as a query. Lift–Splat and Simple-BEV provide
geometry for relating the views [13, 29]. Feature matching
and success-score preservation transfer policy-specific in-
formation to candidate stances that need not be observed
online.

4. Experiments
We ask whether policy-feature distillation improves final
manipulation completion, which stage of the frozen policy
yields the most transferable stance ranking, and how the
improvement changes with the total rollout budget.

Figure 5 shows higher mean completion for our method on
all eight tasks. Equal-task macro success is 47.0%, com-
pared with 26.5% for N2M and 19.2% for direct execution.
Relative to N2M, the absolute gains range from 6.7 to 30.5
percentage points.
Task dependence and favorable initialization.
Task de-
pendence remains substantial. Refrigerator-drawer comple-

28.3% for N2M. Opening remains difficult: our success is
33.3% for the oven and 16.1% for the fridge, versus N2M’s
13.3% and 9.4%. The controller is transferred from refriger-
ator closing in all three cases. Target distance alone does not
specify a useful stance.
Dashed references in Fig. 5 report favorable-stance com-
pletion without navigation; lower rates on the two opening
tasks help contextualize their end-to-end results.
Matched one-shot stance selection.
With navigation re-
moved, our one-shot predictor reaches 33.3% macro success
versus N2M’s 26.5%, improving the mean on all eight tasks.
Refrigerator closing reaches 68.3±3.3% versus 51.7±3.3%.

4.4. Policy features as success supervision
We compare five representations using matched labels, the
same logistic-probe protocol, and PCA-64.
ImageNet
ResNet-18 supplies generic visual features. The other four
representations come from the same forward pass of frozen
π0.5, in computation order: SigLIP visual tokens before lan-
guage fusion, the fused image–instruction prefix before the
action expert, the final action-expert hidden state H before
action projection, and the decoded 50-step action chunk. Se-
lection regret is the empirical success gap between the best
available stance and the stance chosen by the probe.
On unseen layouts, regret falls from SigLIP to Prefix to
H at every label budget (Table 2). With 20% of the labels,
the means are 0.248, 0.177, and 0.148, respectively, versus
0.293 for ResNet-18. The Prefix lowers mean regret by
0.071 relative to SigLIP; H lowers it by a further 0.029 (16%
relative to Prefix). The H–Prefix gap remains 0.018 even
at full labels. Its mean regret with 5% of the labels (0.174)
is lower than that of every other representation at 100%,
although the gap to Prefix (0.176) is small.
Decoding the motion reverses the trend: at 100% of the
labels, Action (0.214) is close to SigLIP (0.210) and well
above H (0.158). The same reversal holds at every budget:
the probe on decoded actions ranks stances worse than the
probe on H. Within the training kitchen, Action leads at 5%,
H and Prefix are nearly tied at 20%, and H leads at 50% and
100% (Table S6); the consistent advantage appears when the
layout changes.

Our method has the highest mean success for all four poli-
cies. Gains over N2M range from 11.1 percentage points for
Diffusion Policy to 40.6 for π0. Direct success is similarly
low for GR00T N1.5 (4.4%) and Diffusion Policy (3.3%), yet
their success after repositioning differs substantially (55.6%
versus 23.3%). A policy’s initial-pose performance therefore
gives little indication of how much suitable repositioning
can recover.
5. Limitations
Evaluation covers simulated kitchens and 0.5–2.5 m starts;
predictors are task-specific and cross-policy tests cover re-
frigerator closing. Training uses privileged geometry and
rendered candidate views; deployment uses stereo observa-
tions and task instructions without test-scene reconstruction.
Real-world teacher supervision would require candidate-
view capture or scene reconstruction; robustness to render-
ing errors is untested. Probe, perception, and arrival-model
errors may limit transfer. Physical robots, longer-range navi-
gation, and moving obstacles remain untested.
6. Conclusion
M4M spatializes a frozen policy’s action-expert represen-
tations into a success field over unvisited stances. Dense
candidate-view supervision and limited execution outcomes
train a current-view predictor; a controller uses the field with
target and traversability maps to move and hand off without
selecting a fixed goal pose.
Across eight tasks in unseen RoboCasa kitchens, M4M
reaches 47.0% mean success, versus 26.5% for N2M and
19.2% for direct execution. Its one-shot variant reaches
33.3%, separating stance-prediction gains from closed-loop
execution. Refrigerator-closing gains extend across four
8


frozen policies, and the controller transfers unchanged to
seven other tasks. Representation and loss ablations support
policy-feature supervision.
Future work will test physical robots and improve uncer-
tainty handling, calibration, and transfer. Code and videos
are available on the project website: https://zsiyuan-
eng.github.io/M4M/.
