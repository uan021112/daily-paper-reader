Abstract
We investigate surface modeling of multi-transparent in-
terfaces to achieve accurate light transmission. The in-
terplay of errors in geometric structure, light paths, and
observation results during the reconstruction of transpar-
ent media with a high number of layers makes this a
challenging problem. Some existing techniques address
the reconstruction of transparent scenes by reducing the
number of layers or simplifying light paths; however, this
limits their direct applicability to high-order transparent
scenes. We propose GaussTrans, a compact framework
for light transport in high-order transparent scenes, con-
sisting of three components: Gaussian-Field Hybrid Sur-
faces, Transport-Coupled Refinement, and Primitive-Guided
Implicit Intersection. Gaussian-Field Hybrid Surfaces use
a hybrid approach combining Gauss surfaces and fields
to reconstruct different interfaces. Transport-Coupled Re-
finement reduces coupling errors by evaluating the entire
light path.
Primitive-Guided Implicit Intersection com-
bines primitive support regions to reduce errors in the
intersection of interfaces and rays.
In experiments on
datasets such as RefRef, GaussTrans achieves significantly
higher accuracy with relatively low spatial storage require-
ments.
1. Introduction
Reconstructing transparent objects has long been a challenge
in 3D scene reconstruction, and high-order transparent scene
reconstruction has found widespread applications in multi-
element optical systems [31], transparent objects with high
overlap angles, and experimental glassware [24]. However,
as the number of layers increases, the nature of this problem
fundamentally changes: errors in the position or normal of
upstream interfaces can alter subsequent intersection points
and incident directions, causing local approximation errors
to couple along the optical path. This poses challenges
for geometric accuracy and surface correction strategies.
Some reconstruction and rendering methods restrict light
paths to two interface interactions [5, 25] or model only
transparent horizontal planes [43], thereby limiting their
direct applicability to higher-order light paths.
Other
differentiable rendering frameworks support recursive or
multiple-interaction light propagation [19, 38, 47]. However,
tracking more interactions does not in itself automatically
eliminate geometric error coupling between different inter-
faces. In addition, some methods address transparent-object
imaging by separately modeling transmission, refraction,
and reflection components [29, 35]. However, extending

2. Related Work
2.1. Transparent Geometry Reconstruction
Transparent geometric reconstruction has undergone sig-
nificant development, primarily through the reconstruction
of refraction constraints by reconstructing transparent
interfaces. DRT [27] obtains ray–background mappings
from the encoded background and optimizes the mesh using
differentiable refraction tracing. NeTO [26] uses refractive
constraints that account for self-occlusion to fit an implicit
signed distance field. RCTrans [9] combines confidence
filtering to estimate ray–background correspondences in
natural scenes. Among photometric methods, Gao et al. [8]
estimate object contours from a reconstructed neural field,
then optimize the neural SDF using differentiable refractive
rendering supervised by image color. TNSR [6], proposed
by Deng et al., starts from an initial implicit surface and,
by taking derivatives of reflection and refraction traces,
propagates multi-view RGB errors to surface parameters for
refinement; its explicit tracing uses up to two refractions and
one reflection. ReNeuS [37] reconstructs internal objects
when the geometry of the transparent shell is known; NU-
NeRF [36] employs an outside-in reconstruction process.
DiffTrans [23] jointly estimates geometry, refractive index,
and absorption through recursive differentiable ray tracing.
RefracGS [33] jointly reconstructs a refractive water surface
represented by a neural height field and the scene beneath it.
In multi-interface scenes, increasing the number of interfaces
can raise the costs of these methods or render them inap-
plicable. Method design should therefore jointly consider
representation cost, applicability, and light-path accuracy.
2.2. Gaussian Surfaces and Ray Intersection
Weighted local fits and implicit surface blending have es-
tablished precedents in point-set and partition-of-unity sur-
faces [3, 13, 30]. There have been relevant studies on Gaus-
sian surfaces: 3DGS [20] uses volumetric primitives but
conflates appearance with geometry, while 2DGS [14] and
QGS [46] use planes and quadratic surfaces, respectively.
GOF [44] extracts opacity surfaces; GSrec [40] recovers sur-
faces from 3DGS using local structural hints. 3DGRT [28]
accelerates particle tracing. TSGS [24] is designed for first-
surface extraction, but complex multi-layer refraction re-
mains a limitation. TransparentGS [15] combines Gaussian
with a mesh proxy, assuming two refractions and at most
one total internal reflection. GLINT [29] decomposes the
interface, transmission, and reflection components under
the condition that refractive deflection is negligible. RT-
Splatting [35] combines surface and volumetric rendering
to model reflection and transmission. Local polynomial in-
tersections and support bounds also underlie earlier point-
set ray tracers [1, 2]. As a surface construction method,
GaussTrans should also include ray-intersection capabilities:
SuGaR [12] determines the sampling interval based on the
scale of the corresponding Gaussian along the line of sight;
IRGS [11] traces secondary rays along two-dimensional
Gaussians to query visibility and indirect incident radiance.
GRay [32] uses smaller Gaussians to reduce overlap be-
tween primitives and organizes spatial queries using oriented
bounding boxes. For continuous multi-interface refraction,
local surface representations or efficient primitive queries
alone are insufficient. Some methods still rely on limited
interactions or negligible refraction, leaving reliable intersec-
tion finding throughout multi-layer refractive propagation an
outstanding challenge.

gies tailored to different scene structures and interface ge-
ometries. SC-GS [17] predicts local rotation and translation
for each control point, then weights the transformations of
neighboring control points to drive Gaussian motion. Mango-
GS [16] associates Gaussians with sparse control nodes
based on position and features, and transfers the temporal
deformations of the nodes to the Gaussians. MoSca [22], on

3. Method
3.1. Overall Framework
Our work primarily focuses on multi-layer transparent
scenes. To balance generalization capability with the er-
ror coupling caused by multi-layer transparent interfaces, we
adopt the following prior constraints: Given rough interface
geometry, known medium properties, a calibrated camera,
and a background or environment model, GaussTrans first
fits Gaussian-weighted local Nth-order polynomial surface
patches (cubic in this paper) and constructs a field for each in-
terface. Subsequently, within a finite computational budget,
it considers the propagation of light rays across the interfaces
and predicts background landing positions or rendered im-
ages. The Transport-Coupled Refinement module provides
a refinement strategy for multi-layer interfaces; Primitive-
Guided Implicit Intersection, serving as the intersection mod-
ule, improves the robustness of light-path–interface intersec-
tions. Figure 2 summarizes the overall workflow of interface
construction, light transport, and geometric refinement.

3.3. Transport-Coupled Refinement

each layer, calculated independently, may no longer be con-

geometric states of other shells.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
We evaluate interface representation, geometric refinement,
and imaging on spherical shells, ellipsoidal shells, quartic
surface shells, and the RefRef [42] scene. Cubic surface
patches are used by default; the newly added refinement
and guided intersection tests cover 16 input sets and 1,024
pixels, respectively. Same-input controls share geometry,
cameras, media, and tracing budgets; capacity experiments
are matched based on geometric storage requirements. We
report geometric and optical path errors, RGB error ranges,
PSNR, SSIM [39], and VGG-LPIPS [45], while also quan-
tifying storage and runtime. Refinement parameters were
selected solely from training observations; evaluation used
held-out viewpoints. Other baselines retained their original
priors; timing comparisons were conducted under identical
execution conditions.

4.2. Surface Construction and Accuracy
We verify its accuracy in continuous multi-interface prop-
agation. First, we construct different surface representations
from the same input geometry. Since errors in preceding
intersection points and normals can affect subsequent
propagation, we evaluate the entire light path rather than
just local surface fits. Figure 4 uses 65 fixed rays from five
viewpoints, covering one to six layers of spherical and ellip-
soidal shells. With a setting of 400 cubic Gaussian functions
per interface, both GaussTrans and the QEM mesh [10]
stored with the same geometry successfully traced all
specified paths, with GaussTrans exhibiting a lower average
background landing point error. When the total capacity is
fixed, increasing the number of interfaces raises local normal
errors; maintaining the capacity per interface preserves
local accuracy. Therefore, multi-layer evaluation requires
simultaneous consideration of both continuous propagation
and the representation capacity of each interface.
We further validated these results on non-convex
geometries and final images. On the RefRef ring [42], under
comparable geometric storage, GaussTrans achieved lower
RGB error thresholds for total effective pixels, with fewer
hard failures across the entire image compared to Smooth
QEM; all hard failures were statistically preserved (Table 3,
Supplementary Figure S2). Across 100 test viewpoints of
the glass sphere, Table 2 compares TransparentGS [15],
TNSR [6], R3F [42], RT-Splatting [35], 3DGS [20], and
2DGS [14]. GaussTrans achieved the highest SSIM (0.9085)
and lowest LPIPS (0.0665) in the table, but R3F had a higher
PSNR, while TNSR and R3F had lower MAE in object
regions. This set of comparisons evaluates imaging quality,
while the ability to propagate continuously across multiple
interfaces is verified by the shell-and-ring experiments.
Memory compression is another benefit beyond the
aforementioned accuracy. On a six-layer rotating eccentric
ellipsoid, cubic Gaussian, quadratic B´ezier, and cubic B´ezier
methods [7]—each requiring approximately 861–863 KiB
of geometric memory—all completed 65 paths, with average
landing point errors of 0.001498, 0.003337, and 0.005653,
respectively. This indicates that GaussTrans still maintains
an advantage in average accuracy compared to these two
surface patch constructions. In a five-level comparison of
approximate storage matching with 100 to 1,600 Gaussian
elements per interface, both GaussTrans and Smooth QEM
completed 65 out of 65 paths, with the former reducing the
average landing point error by 95.1% to 98.9% (Figure 5).
The error curves in the figure are based on 44 rays processed
using 15 different configurations, while the method averages
are calculated using all 65 rays. Comparisons using the same
input mesh for the glass sphere also show that GaussTrans
achieves similar PSNR and lower LPIPS with approximately
1/25.8 of the geometric storage. External methods retain

4.3. Ablation Studies
Order of the surface. Under fixed centers, local frames,
and support domains and with 400 Gaussian primitives per
interface, surfaces of the second through eighth orders all
completed 65 out of 65 light paths (Table 1(a)); the linear
surface completed 61 out of 65, with the remaining four
paths undergoing total internal reflection. Compared to the
second-order fit, the third-order fit reduced the average land-
ing point error by 41.6%, with improvements in the mean
values across all five viewpoints and in 54 out of 65 light
rays. Higher-order fits trade increased storage and runtime
for accuracy, but the sixth- and eighth-order fits showed a
decline in performance.
Coordinated updates within primitives. By simultane-
ously updating the local coordinate system and surface coef-
ficients with matched center displacements and optimization
budgets, improvements were achieved in the background
landing-point errors in held-out views, as well as in geomet-
ric and light path consistency for both double-shell scenarios
(Table 1(b)), although isolated cases of deterioration still
occurred.
Cross-shell updates. The six-layer ellipsoidal shell uses
six control variables and ten synthetic background correspon-
dences. Independent corrections were selected separately
with the other shells at their initial geometries and then
combined; full updates utilized corrections already accepted
by other shells. Both approaches employed collaborative
refitting, 25 search evaluations, and a single final training
check. Compared to independent corrections, the full up-
date reduced the average landing point error across unseen
viewpoints by 27.4% and 36.0% on the two input sets, re-
spectively, with improvements observed for all 39 paired
rays in each set (Table 1(c)). Compared to the initialization,
the mean errors decreased by 29.7% and 33.3%, respectively;
updates involving only center shifting and smooth-normal
QEM updates resulted in deterioration.
Primitive-guided intersection finding. Under fixed fu-
sion field and intersection acceptance criteria, we compared
results with and without guidance. Among the 8 existing
hard-failure pixels in the six-shell scene, enabling guidance
reduced the number of hard failures from 8 to 2. In 41
grazing-ray perturbations, for the 33 cases with existing ob-
served intersection points, the number of recovered points
increased from 20 to 33. These two sets of directed diag-
nostics support the restorative effect of guidance on difficult
queries, though it comes with additional computational over-
head. Appendix S7 reports per-input and per-interface errors,
failures, and timings for the cross-shell ablation.
4.4. Complete Imaging Across Multiple Interfaces
Beyond evaluating the main transmission path, we further
traced the Fresnel reflection and transmission branches [31]
to verify whether the Gaussian interface can support com-
plete image generation. Figure S1 in the Supplementary
Materials shows 512×512 rendering results for the six-
shell scene, including an analytical reference, GaussTrans,
storage-matched QEM with smooth normals, and a dense
source mesh, all using the same light transport budget. This
experiment extends the evaluation from a single main path to
multi-branch color accumulation; however, the limited bud-
get still leaves a reflection tail: the average unresolved weight
of the foreground for the analytical reference, GaussTrans,
and dense source mesh is approximately 5.3%. The result-
ing images therefore retain unresolved contribution intervals
rather than representing converged transport.
4.5. Limitations
The accuracy of the GaussTrans interface depends on the
quality of the input geometry and local coverage. For thin-
walled structures, adjacent surfaces, or regions with rapidly
varying curvature, local surface fitting and Gaussian blending
may still result in positional and normal deviations, or even
introduce extra intersection points or omit valid ones. These
local errors may be amplified during continuous refraction,
limiting the applicability of the current method to complex
transparent structures.
Primitive-Guided Implicit Intersection uses local polyno-
mial roots to propose candidate positions; however, these
roots do not necessarily correspond to the zeros of the fusion
field and still require verification using actual field values
and support conditions. Nevertheless, the generation and
verification of candidates also increase computational over-
head. Therefore, this module is currently used to assist with
certain difficult queries.
5. Conclusion
We presented GaussTrans, compact Gaussian–MLS inter-
faces for high-order transport with same-field intersections
and normals. Given coarse geometry and known media,
Transport-Coupled Refinement reduces held-out path error
on perturbed inputs. At comparable geometry storage, one-
to-six-shell experiments reduce primary-transmission errors
versus evaluated meshes. On the nonconvex RefRef torus,
GaussTrans achieves lower RGB MAE bounds and fewer
hard failures than smooth QEM at comparable core storage.
These results characterize the accuracy–storage–runtime
trade-off.
