Bridging vision-language reasoning and learned control

Vision-language models give legged robots the common sense to hike kilometers autonomously in alpine and urban
terrain.

Autonomous navigation in diverse real-world environments remains one of the greatest challenges in robotics. It demands both robust
low-level motion control to handle diverse terrain and high-level reasoning to interpret environmental cues. We present ROAM (Robust
Open-world Autonomous Mobility), a system that enables legged robots to perform kilometer-scale navigation in complex alpine and urban
settings. ROAM combines the world knowledge of a Vision-Language Model (VLM), pre-trained on internet-scale data, with robust
locomotion and local planning policies learned in simulation via reinforcement learning (RL). The VLM provides common-sense reasoning
and semantic interpretation of the scene, assessing risks, interpreting signs, and producing semantic labels with associated navigation
costs. An open-vocabulary segmentation module converts these labels into a dense, real-time risk map, which conditions two pre-trained
RL policies responsible for agile locomotion and local navigation, effectively coupling high-level VLM reasoning with low-level physical
control. Real-world experiments showed the system’s ability to autonomously navigate a wide range of complex real-world environments.
The robot completes kilometer-long hikes with minimal intervention, interprets signs to avoid danger, and evaluates its environment to
identify safe areas on which to step. This work represents a step toward the reliable deployment of systems in unstructured environments.
However, it also underscores the challenges that remain in VLM reliability and spatial reasoning, indicating that foundational models are a
crucial component but not yet a complete solution for navigation in the wild.
INTRODUCTION
Enabling robots to navigate autonomously and safely across
diverse, unstructured environments — from indoor spaces and
urban areas to complex natural terrain — is a fundamental
challenge in robotics. Although recent advances in legged
robotics have demonstrated robust locomotion in the wild (1–
10), the ability to autonomously navigate these environments
remains an unresolved challenge.
Traditional navigation methods, which rely heavily on ge-
ometric traversability estimation, often fail in the presence
of non-geometric hazards such as tall grass, water, or mud.
Furthermore, these systems disregard implicit semantic norms,
such as staying on a designated trail, which are crucial for safe
and acceptable navigation in environments shared with people.
Although semantic segmentation-based approaches aim to pro-
vide high-level understanding, their reliance on fixed datasets
or label sets restricts their adaptability across the diverse and
varied conditions encountered in the real world.
A compelling hint to what these autonomous systems are
missing can be found by observing a human remotely operating
the same robot. Even without prior experience, a person can
successfully guide the robot through complex environments.
This indicates that the bottleneck is not the robot’s ability
to move, but its ability to decide where to move. Humans
accomplish this by applying common-sense reasoning: we
instinctively understand that a paved path is safe, that deep
puddles are a risk, and that one should follow a trail. This
intuitive, real-world understanding is the crucial component
that current navigation systems lack.
Recent breakthroughs in generative AI offer a promising
way to equip robots with this missing common-sense reason-
ing. Foundation models like Large Language Models (LLMs),
trained on internet-scale data, have shown a strong capac-
ity for high-level reasoning (11–16). Vision-Language Mod-
els (VLMs) have extended this capability into the visual do-
main (17–19). Meanwhile, self-supervised visual foundation
models have enabled powerful image understanding (20–22).
By learning from vast quantities of image and text data, these
models can interpret visual scenes, understand concepts such as
trail or puddle, and reason about their implications for naviga-
tion. This ability to perform common-sense reasoning directly

VLMs for robot navigation
The key challenge in applying VLMs to robotics is bridging
their high-level, common-sense reasoning with the low-level
commands required for physical control. An embodied agent
must ultimately translate multimodal sensory inputs, such as
images and proprioception, into joint-level commands (such as
target positions or torques) to produce movement.
The most direct approach is to have the VLM generate joint-
level actions. This can be attempted either by directly prompting
the model or by fine-tuning the pretrained VLM into a vision-
language-action (VLA) model, which adapts the architecture
with specialized action heads trained on embodied datasets. This
has been explored for tasks like manipulation (23–26). These
end-to-end models directly control the robot’s actuators from
image inputs. However, this approach faces hurdles: it typically
necessitates massive datasets of human demonstrations, which
are costly to acquire for diverse environments and difficult for
specific robot embodiments such as quadrupeds. Additionally,
the high-frequency control required for dynamic tasks, such as
locomotion, presents a challenge for the onboard deployment
of large models.
To improve data efficiency and reactivity, a common strat-
egy is to raise the level of abstraction for the robot’s action
space, making the task easier for the VLM and improving
generalization across different robot embodiments. These ab-
stract commands can then be converted into joint-level actions
through traditional controllers or other learning-based modules,
such as reinforcement learning policies. A central considera-
tion is identifying the level of abstraction that best supports
VLM-driven navigation across diverse environments. One level
of abstraction involves having the VLM or foundation model
directly generate continuous waypoints (27–30), or trajecto-
ries (31, 32). Although this leverages the model’s reasoning,
it requires consistently interpreting spatial information from
images and generating feasible actions from a high-dimensional
space. For example, projecting waypoints in the image space to
the Euclidean command space requires depth estimation and ge-
ometric post-processing. Transforming VLM outputs into robot
commands remains a challenge for current approaches (32, 33).
To further simplify the task, another line of research uses
the VLM as a high-level planner to select from a discrete set
of candidate actions. These candidates can be labeled target
positions (34–37), potential trajectories (38, 39), or abstract
instructions like landmarks (40–43). By narrowing the action
space, the VLM only needs to identify the best option, rather
than generate a precise command. However, these methods
often introduce high latency, as each step requires querying
the VLM. Although some works incorporate spatial maps for
indoor tasks (44–47), applying this paradigm to large, complex
outdoor settings remains an open challenge.
These VLM-centric strategies stand in contrast to traditional
outdoor navigation systems. For decades, these systems have
employed traversability estimation methods that use geometric
information (48–51), or leverage models trained on data from
human annotations (52, 53), predefined heuristics (54, 55), or
simulated data tailored to specific robot embodiments (56, 57).
These approaches usually estimate traversability in 2.5D
elevation maps consumed by planners such as model predictive
path integral (MPPI) control (58) for navigation. Expanding on
these geometric methods, recent work has also tackled discrete
terrain navigation, where systems must identify highly precise
footholds to traverse disjointed surfaces, gaps, or stepping
stones (59, 60).
Additionally, self-supervised learning techniques have been
explored, using various forms of supervision such as traction
estimates (61–63), collision feedback and bumpiness (64),
trajectory-based guidance (65–67), and human navigation
demonstrations (68). Although these approaches often build
spatial representations for real-time planning—thereby avoiding
the latency of continually querying large language models—they
lack the contextual adaptability that common-sense reasoning
provides. They tend to depend on fixed datasets, struggle to
generalize to unseen terrains, and require retraining whenever
the robot platform or operating conditions change.
This presents a trade-off: VLM-based methods offer rich
semantic understanding but face challenges in latency, data re-
quirements, or grounding for direct control, whereas traditional
methods are efficient but semantically brittle. This motivates
a hybrid approach that seeks to combine the strengths of both
paradigms. Although recent work has taken steps in this di-
rection (43), its reliance on scene-specific user input restricts
its use in large-scale autonomous navigation, since the LLM
prompt specifying where to stay and where to avoid must be
manually provided by the user for every new scene.
In this paper, we introduce a system for robust open-world
autonomous mobility (ROAM), a hybrid approach to investigate
the extent to which a VLM’s common-sense reasoning can
enable autonomous navigation in the wild (Movie 1).
The ROAM system
ROAM integrates a VLM for high-level world knowledge with
Reinforcement Learning (RL) policies for low-level control,
bridging abstract scene understanding with concrete physical
action. This allows the robot to interpret its environment, make
informed decisions, and execute those decisions robustly on
hardware. The overview of this system is shown in Fig. 1 and
Movie 1.
Our perception system uses a VLM to generate real-time,
semantically-aware navigation costs. We provide the VLM with
RGB images, robot information, desired output value ranges
(for example, a risk value from 0 to 1), and past prompts and
outputs as context. The model then outputs semantic labels for
objects in the scene, along with associated values, such as risk

for autonomous robot control. It processes RGB images and robot descriptions through a VLM to generate object labels enriched with risk, preferred area, and
steppable values. These labels are converted into semantic images using open-vocabulary semantic segmentation, which operates at a higher frequency than the
VLM analysis. The semantic images are then fused with elevation maps to create an environmental representation that reinforcement learning (RL) policies utilize for
fast and robust robot control.
or preference. For example, it may assign a low risk to a gravel
path, a moderate risk to the surrounding grass, and a high risk to
water. An open-vocabulary semantic segmentation module (69)
then uses these VLM-generated labels to produce a dense, real-
time risk image of the environment. This image is projected
onto the robot’s elevation map, providing the RL policies with
high-frequency spatial information (from 10 Hz to 50 Hz) for
responsive control. This abstraction enables RL policies to
operate on a processed representation of the environment rather
than raw sensor data, which simplifies the learning problem
and sim-to-real transfer.
To convert this semantic information into motion, we train
two distinct RL policies: one for low-level locomotion and
one for local navigation. The locomotion policy, an extension
of prior work (3, 7), is responsible for stable movement and
foothold selection, incorporating steppable area information
into its observation to avoid placing feet on unsafe terrain.
The navigation policy, building on RL-based methods (6),
handles local pathfinding, using the risk information from the
perception system to avoid hazardous regions while navigating
toward a goal. Training these policies in the real world would
be data-intensive and expose the robot to potentially damaging
situations. Therefore, we train both policies extensively in
simulation. Although simulation cannot perfectly capture real-
world complexity, we sidestep this challenge by training the
policies on abstract observations, such as height scans, which

This hybrid architecture allows ROAM to address the limita-
tions of previous approaches. By combining the common-sense
reasoning of VLMs with the robustness of RL policies trained
with domain randomization, our system eliminates the need for
exhaustive real-world datasets or comprehensive simulations,
enabling it to handle diverse and unstructured environments.
This semantic-spatial delegation, from semantic reasoning
through low-level control, differs from recent VLM-based
paradigms that require the foundation model to perform dense
spatial reasoning by selecting from action candidates, outputting
spatial waypoints, or generating low-level motor commands.
RESULTS
To evaluate the proposed ROAM system, we conducted ex-
periments across diverse environments—including mountains,
forests, urban areas, and indoor spaces—as shown in Fig. 2.
These experiments demonstrate that our method can generalize
effectively to different settings without requiring data specific
to those environments. The system assigns risks to different
terrains, interprets signage, and adjusts its behavior accordingly,
as illustrated in Fig. 2O and Movie 1.
Hiking in the Swiss Alps
To demonstrate the extent of autonomy we can achieve with our
method, we conducted two long-distance hiking experiments in
the Swiss Alps (Fig. 3 and Movie 1).
Zermatt
This experiment demonstrated our robot’s ability to navigate a
varied and challenging hiking trail in the mountains. The route
covered 10.1 km with a 1220 m elevation change, presenting
different scenarios along the way.
The route started at the Gornergrat station (Fig. 3A, Start),
located over 3000 m above sea level, and aimed to reach the
Furi cable car station 1220 m below (Fig. 3A, End). This route
presented several environmental challenges that required the
robot to adapt its risk analysis. The initial section consisted of
gravel and small rocks, requiring robust locomotion over uneven
terrain (Fig. 3A(i)). As the robot progressed, it encountered a
small lake, which required careful navigation along the water’s
edge without stepping into the lake (Fig. 3A(ii)). This section
highlighted the importance of semantic understanding from
RGB images. After the lake, the robot followed a gravel path
surrounded by rocks or partially high grass (Fig. 3A(iii)). Here,
semantic understanding helped to keep the robot on the safer
gravel path, avoiding grass areas that can potentially hide steep
slopes or rocks.
Steep slopes with an approximately 30%
gradient also challenged the locomotion system (Fig. 3A(iii)).
Near a station, the trail became more artificial, running along
the railroad tracks (Fig. 3A(iv)). After passing the mid-station,
the path changed to a grassy trail in the forest (Fig. 3A(v)). The
last part of the route changed to an urban environment with
asphalt ground near the cable car station (Fig. 3A(vi)). Here, the
system demonstrated its adaptability by adjusting labels, using
terms such as “road” or “paved_road” instead of “gravel_path,”
which helped segmentation.
Although the robot walked the majority of this route fully
autonomously, two major human interventions were necessary
to ensure safety due to the presence of construction zones
on the hiking trail.
In the first case, the fence in front of
the path leading to the construction zone was not correctly
segmented, even though the VLM identified a fence.
As
a result, the robot walked into it. The second intervention
became necessary when a deviation in the global navigation
path would have led the robot into hazardous construction
terrain. In this case, the segmentation model was unable to
adequately differentiate between the designated path and the
rough construction area, necessitating human correction to
prevent the robot from entering the dangerous zone. Both
interventions were necessary to maintain the robot’s safety
(fig. S7).
Despite these challenges, the robot completed most of the
hike autonomously without human assistance. The autonomy
rate in time was 96.7% throughout the entire mission. This
experiment demonstrated the effectiveness of our integrated
approach.
Seealpsee
In our second experiment, we tested our system on a trail around
the lake Seealpsee in Switzerland. This 2.38 km course pre-
sented a variety of challenges, including narrow paths adjacent
to the lake (Fig. 3B(i, iii, and iv)), pastures (Fig. 3B(i to iii and
v)), trees impairing Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)
signal (Fig. 3B(vi)) and the presence of animals such as cows
(Fig. 3B(iii and iv)). The complexity of the environment high-
lighted the limitations of purely geometry-based navigation
and showed the need for semantic understanding. There were
several sections near the lake where the global path deviated
from the actual local path and misdirected the robot into the
water (Fig. 3B(i)). In addition, the presence of pastures along
the path required adherence to semantic norms. Although it
is safe to cross them, it is undesirable to do so since they are
private property owned by farmers.
Our system successfully prevented the robot from entering
the lake or restricted pastures by using semantic information
from the environment, recognizing the boundaries of the lake
and the pastures, and adjusting risk and preference values
accordingly to stay on the designated path. We also observed
that the system’s output reflects the context: it only identified
“water” or “water_body” when the lake was visible, detected
increased risk from trees when entering wooded areas, and
adjusted risk values for areas with dense vegetation (fig. S2).
Although the robot’s navigation was largely autonomous, several
operator interventions were necessary to guarantee safety. One
such instance involved halting the robot as it approached the edge
of a lake. Another required intervention was at a turnstile, which
presented an impassable physical barrier for the robot. Despite
these exceptions, the system achieved a 97.5% autonomy rate
for the mission.

Contextual awareness and sign comprehension
We designed a controlled experiment consisting of several
challenges along a 1.2 km course at the Hönggerberg campus
in Zurich, Switzerland, to evaluate the behavior of our system
in the real world, on the real robot (Fig. 4 and Movie 1).
We compared the ROAM system with two baseline methods:
the Field Local Planner (49), which relies solely on geometric
information without explorative behavior, and Visual Navigation
Transformer (ViNT) (27), which uses goal images as navigation
commands. To obtain these goal images for ViNT, we first
teleoperate the robot through the course and then use images
sampled every 1.5 s as navigation goals.
We describe the
challenges and how our method performs in comparison to the
baselines in the following sections.
Robust pathfinding
This section evaluates the system’s robustness to deviations
between the planned path and the environment, introduced via
artificial offsets that mimic naturally occurring discrepancies
arising from inconsistent global path data and global localization
drift. The results, visualized in Fig. 4A, demonstrate that ROAM
seamlessly handles such discrepancies. For instance, when the
global path passed through a patch of bushes (Fig. 4A(i)), ROAM
identified an alternative route. This was achieved through its
local navigation policy and the semantic guidance derived
from the VLM’s segmentation. In contrast, the geometry-only
method (Fig. 4A(ii)) proceeded along the commanded path into
the bushes. The robot, unable to reconcile the global waypoint
with the high geometric cost of the terrain, became stuck and
required human intervention to continue the experiment. We
repeated the experiment in a grassy area, this time without
any geometric obstacles. Our method stayed on the gravel
path (Fig. 4A(iii)) thanks to the “preferred_area” estimation,
which favored the designated path and avoided the grass. The
geometry-only baseline blindly followed the global path and
traversed the grass due to its lack of semantic understanding
(Fig. 4A(iv)).
Semantic hazard avoidance
We demonstrate the common-sense reasoning capability of
our system by placing a mannequin on the path and reporting
the resulting behavior. Rather than representing an immediate
physical or geometric hazard, the mannequin serves to test
the VLM’s ability to contextually identify a human figure as a
high-risk obstacle. As shown in Fig. 4B, our method identified
the mannequin as an obstacle with the label “human.” The local
navigation policy used this semantic information and avoided
it. In contrast, the geometry-only method, lacking semantic
scene understanding, failed to recognize the mannequin as an
obstacle and stepped on its leg. The ViNT method struggled to
stay on the path, requiring multiple human interventions, and
did not change its behavior in the presence of the mannequin,
eventually stepping on it as well.
Contextual awareness
To assess the robot’s ability to interpret and respond to contextual
information, we placed construction signs along the path. Upon
encountering the first sign, which read, “DANGER - ROAD
WORK AHEAD IN 10 METERS - Do not walk on path,”
ROAM correctly interpreted the sign and adjusted its risk
assessments, marking the gravel path as high risk and the grass
as a preferred route. As a result, the robot correctly took a
detour through the grass. After navigating through the grass,
the robot encountered a second sign that read, “CAUTION -
END ROAD WORK - Merge Back to Path.” ROAM correctly
interpreted this sign, updating its evaluations and marking the
gravel path as preferred and low risk. The robot then returned
to the path and continued its journey (Fig. 4C).
The geometry-only baseline failed to interpret the signs and
continued to follow the path based on the global waypoints,
resulting in the robot entering the construction site. The ViNT
method also failed to read the signs and instead followed the
path based on the pre-captured target images. However, these
images did not include the construction site, and ViNT was
unable to adapt to this change in the environment. As a result, it
failed to avoid the barrier, collided with it, and deviated into tall
grass several times, requiring intervention. Ultimately, ViNT
was unable to recover and continued through the grass in the
wrong direction.
Validation of system components
To further validate our approach, we conducted several experi-
ments showcasing how ROAM applies common-sense reasoning
to locomotion and local navigation. We specifically demon-
strate how semantic information is used to step only in safe
areas, adjust path risk based on semantic understanding, and
further illustrate the exploration behavior.
Locomotion
To test ROAM’s ability to alter foot placement based on context,
we placed metal cans on the ground and added a warning sign
reading “WARNING: EXPLOSIVE CANS” (Fig. 5A(i)). The
VLM successfully interpreted the sign, assigning a high-risk
value to the cans and marking them as non-steppable. The
locomotion policy used the contextual information to avoid
stepping on the cans, selecting safe footholds based on the
steppable layer of the elevation map.
Next, we placed wooden pallets to create a path across the
grass (Fig. 5A(ii)). Without any signs, the VLM evaluated
both the pallet and the grass as low-risk areas, with the pallet
being slightly less risky. As a result, the robot traversed both
the pallet and the grass to reach the goal. When we placed a
“DO NOT STEP HERE” sign on the wooden pallet, the VLM
updated its evaluation, marking the pallet as high-risk and
non-steppable. The robot then adapted its behavior, choosing to
walk only on the grass to reach the goal. Conversely, when we
placed both “DO NOT STEP HERE” and “DANGER: MINE”
signs on the grass, the VLM marked the grass as high-risk
and non-steppable. In response, the robot stepped only on the
wooden pallet. This demonstrates how our system can adapt

Local navigation
We conducted a comparison of the paths taken by the robot
with and without semantic information. In the setting without
semantic information, all risk values were set to 0.0. Our local
navigation policy combines geometric data with a semantic risk
map to determine a path. This adjustment effectively disabled
the semantic input, ensuring the policy relied solely on geomet-
ric information for local navigation. We set a local navigation
goal about 200 m away and allowed the local navigation pol-
icy to reach this goal. With semantic information, the robot
preferred to walk on the road. In contrast, when no semantic
information was used, and only geometric information was
provided, the robot chose to move directly towards the goal by
walking on the grass (Fig. 5B(i)). In addition, when the robot
was near a railroad, our method prevented it from entering the
rail area. Without semantic information, the robot crossed the
rail and ended up in a dead-end area. These results indicate
that by using the VLM and semantic segmentation, our method
can produce more socially preferred paths compared to cutting
across grass and rails (Fig. 5B(ii)). Furthermore, we conducted
an experiment on a snow-covered path where the route was
surrounded by snow. Our method successfully identified the
snow-covered areas as less preferable and remained on the
asphalt path. In contrast, the geometric baseline did not adjust
its behavior in response to the presence of snow and traversed
the snow-covered area instead (Fig. 5B(iii)).
Exploration
In this experiment, we place barriers that block the intended
path, forcing the robot to explore alternative routes (Fig. 5C(i)).
ROAM recognizes the blockade and chooses an alternative path
(Fig. 5C(ii)). When the path deviates from the goal, the robot
cuts through the grass (Fig. 5C(iii)) and rejoins the main path
(Fig. 5C(iv)). This was possible because the grass area was
classified as not preferred, but not high risk.
Evaluating different levels of action abstraction
We evaluate our method by comparing different action spaces
for VLM-based navigation. The choice of action space presents
different levels of abstraction and different requirements for
spatial and embodiment understanding. We illustrate this in
Fig. 6A. Although any high-level command must ultimately
be translated into high-frequency joint-level actions (typically
50 Hz to 100 Hz), a key differentiator is where spatial reasoning
occurs.
Our proposed method, which outputs semantic labels and
values, delegates spatial processing to downstream modules.
This design frees the VLM from needing intrinsic spatial
understanding. Conversely, alternative approaches, such as
direct trajectory pixel coordinate generation, necessitate that the
VLM itself performs spatial reasoning to produce geometrically
correct outputs. More extreme methods, such as VLA models,
attempt to directly generate low-level motor commands. They
place a greater burden on the model by requiring not only spatial
reasoning but also a deep embodied understanding of the robot’s
kinematics and dynamics. Furthermore, these models often
require extensive fine-tuning and must meet the high-frequency
demands of dynamic locomotion.
In this initial investigation, we focus on methods that leverage
pre-trained VLMs in a zero-shot capacity, using prompting to
elicit navigational intelligence. We evaluate several distinct
action spaces, ranging from direct trajectory pixel coordinate
generation to the semantic risk-based planning used in ROAM.
Experimental setup for path generation
To provide a controlled comparison, we evaluate the ability of
a VLM to generate a safe and viable path to a designated goal
within a static image. For our method, ROAM, we replace the
full navigation policy with a standard A* path planner to ensure
the comparison focuses purely on the path generation in the
image space.
We make this methodological choice to decouple the
semantic-spatial perception quality from the robot’s physical
control dynamics. Deploying the trained RL navigation policy
is incompatible with a static image dataset, since it requires a
continuous stream of real-time proprioceptive states and a dy-
namic 2.5D elevation map. Furthermore, because the baseline
methods we evaluate against predict paths as pixel coordinates
within the 2D image space, an A* planner operating in the same
2D image space provides a direct proxy for comparison.
In this setup, the planner uses the semantic risk map as its
cost function. We benchmark this approach against several
baseline methods that use the VLM to generate a path more
directly.
We compared three types of methods. In our proposed
method, ROAM, the VLM is prompted to output a list of
semantic labels present in the scene (such as “pavement,” “tall
grass,” and “obstacle”) along with their associated navigation
risk (a value from 0 to 1). These text-based outputs then guide
an open-vocabulary segmentation model to generate a dense risk
map. The A* algorithm planned a least-cost path through this
map to the same goal coordinate used in the baseline methods.
In the direct trajectory pixel coordinate baseline, the VLM is
prompted with a natural language command (such as “Generate
a trajectory that follows the path”) or a goal coordinate and is
asked to output a sequence of pixel coordinates forming a path
(similar to that of Windecker et al. (32)).
In the label-based path selection baseline, we overlay discrete
labels onto the image and prompt the VLM to output a sequence
of these labels to form a path toward the goal. We explored
several labeling strategies, placing labels along straight lines
with ticks, simple trajectories, or distributing them in a grid.
To assess the impact of prior terrain knowledge, we tested two
variants for label placement: distributing them over the entire
image versus placing them only on navigable ground, which
was identified using the steppable map from our own ROAM
method.

Qualitative analysis
Fig. 6B presents a qualitative comparison of the paths generated
by each method on a representative scene. The results highlight
a notable challenge for current VLMs: a difficulty with precise
spatial reasoning. Methods relying on the direct output of
coordinates or selecting labels produced erratic or unfeasible
paths that fail to respect clear environmental boundaries. For
instance, the direct trajectory prompts often result in paths that
cut across unsafe terrain.
In contrast, the path generated via the ROAM pipeline is
visibly more reliable and coherent. By tasking the VLM with
semantic understanding, such as identifying path, grass, water,
and their risks, and offloading the spatial task to semantic
segmentation and geometric path planning by A*, our method
successfully leverages the VLM’s strengths in common-sense
reasoning. The VLM does not need to be spatially precise;
it only needs to correctly identify the concepts in the scene.
This allows the subsequent, spatially-aware planning module to
generate a safe and logical trajectory.
Quantitative analysis
To quantify these observations, we conducted an analysis across
27 outdoor and indoor scenes (see Statistical analysis). For each
image, we manually segmented “safe” and “preferred” regions
for traversal. We then measured the fraction of all trajectory
pixels, pooled over the scenes, that lay within the “safe” regions
and “preferred” regions. A trajectory was considered a “total
failure” if it entered an unsafe region, as this could correspond to
a mission-ending event for a real robot, whereas the “preferred”
percentage shows how much the trajectory follows the human
preference.
The results, summarized in Fig. 6C and Table 1, demonstrate
that the trajectories generated using the ROAM pipeline align
better with the human labeler’s intention, outperforming direct
action generation baselines. Our method consistently produces
paths that remain within safe regions, reflecting its robustness.
This suggests that for the current generation of foundation
models, abstracting the VLM’s role to a high-level semantic
advisor is a more effective strategy for navigation than tasking
it with generating concrete, low-level robot actions, which
requires spatial reasoning.
DISCUSSION
In this work, we have presented ROAM, an autonomous naviga-
tion system that bridges high-level common-sense reasoning
from VLMs with learned control policies to guide legged robots
across complex real-world environments. The core of our ap-
proach is a framework that translates the abstract, common-
sense reasoning of VLMs into actionable, low-level control.
Our approach aims to address the poor spatial awareness in
VLM-based robotics. Our method circumvents the inaccuracies
of directly querying VLMs for coordinates or commands by
instead using them for generating labels for open-vocabulary
semantic segmentation. This semantic information is then
projected onto a spatial map, which acts as a bridge to our RL
navigation and locomotion policies. This strategy distinguishes
our work from methods that rely solely on geometric data or
require costly, environment-specific retraining. As a result,
our system exhibits strong zero-shot generalization, navigating
effectively across various domains, including forest trails, rocky
inclines, and indoor spaces, without requiring fine-tuning.
The first advantage of our approach is the ability to apply
common sense, specifically interpreting semantic information
and environmental context without the need for hard-coded la-
bels or rules for every possible scenario. For example, the robot
recognized and avoided risky areas such as a lake, respected
private pastures, and used textual information on signage to
dynamically adjust traversal strategies. This common under-
standing is critical in situations where geometric information is
insufficient or misleading. The hiking experiments in the Swiss
Alps, which covered kilometer-scale distances, demonstrate
how our system handles different terrain variations.
The second advantage of our method is to fuse the high-
level, common-sense reasoning of VLMs, which can operate at
slower frame rates and tolerate larger processing delays, with the
reactive, low-latency control outputs of RL-based locomotion
and navigation policies. Unlike methods that struggle with
latency-sensitive tasks, our hierarchical integration ensures that
high-level semantic reasoning does not compromise rapid foot
placement and local navigation.
However, several limitations and areas for improvement re-
main. The quality of semantic interpretation depends on both
the accuracy of the large vision-language models and the relia-
bility of the open-vocabulary segmentation. In practice, these
models may occasionally misinterpret complex scenes, and the
segmentation may not match the semantic regions identified by
the VLM. For example, although the VLM might label an area
as “left_grass” or “nearby_rocks,” the segmentation process
does not accurately segment these corresponding regions.
Also, engineering solutions like the sign detector were added
to address the current VLM inference latency. As VLM speeds
improve, these temporary solutions should become obsolete.
A fundamental characteristic of ROAM is that its common-
sense reasoning is inherited from a pretrained VLM rather
than learned onboard. Consequently, the system’s ability to
interpret signs, recognize hazards, and apply contextual norms
is bounded by the capabilities of the underlying foundation
model. Although our field experiments relied on a commercial
cloud-hosted VLM, which introduces dependencies on network
connectivity, external service availability, and closed-source
model weights, ROAM is deliberately designed as a model-
agnostic framework.
The gap between commercial and open-weight VLMs is
closing and the performance of smaller open-weight VLMs
is improving rapidly. As detailed in our supplementary VLM
benchmark, current open-weight models already achieve highly
competitive performance on zero-shot terrain classification.
Although they currently trail leading commercial models in
tasks requiring complex contextual grounding, such as precisely
relating textual sign instructions to specific physical objects,
we expect this gap to diminish quickly. Once onboard open-
weight VLMs become capable enough to serve as the primary

Furthermore, our primary field experiments were conducted

visual degradation, such as heavy fog, heavy rain, night, or

advantage of ROAM’s hybrid architecture is its graceful degra-

policies also rely on active sensors (such as LiDAR and stereo

capability to interpret global maps sufficiently to provide reliable
route planning based solely on onboard imagery.
Another limitation is that our current semantic-spatial ab-
straction primarily captures visual hazards and lacks the granu-
larity to convey fine-grained material properties. Distinguishing
the subtle risk differences between thin ice, deep mud, or loose
gravel relies heavily on physical interaction rather than purely
visual semantics. Although ROAM currently mitigates this at
the control level—our RL locomotion policy is trained with
extensive domain randomization and is robust to such phys-
ical disturbances—the system’s high-level “common sense”
remains disconnected from actual physical contact. Current
typical VLMs operate purely on visual and textual inputs and do
not accept proprioceptive or tactile force information. Enabling
the next generation of foundational models to natively process
and reason over this type of rich, embodied physical interaction
data represents a crucial next step for the field.
In summary, this work takes a step toward unifying high-level
semantic understanding with agile, low-level control for legged
robots. However, substantial challenges remain. Although
VLMs demonstrate considerable potential, our findings suggest
that further advances are needed before they can enable robots
to navigate ubiquitously and autonomously in the complexities
of the diverse real world.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study design
The objective of this study was to evaluate whether the common-
sense reasoning of a pretrained VLM, combined with locomo-
tion and local navigation policies trained with RL, enables a
legged robot to navigate autonomously in unstructured real-
world environments. We conducted two long-distance hiking
deployments in the Swiss Alps (Zermatt, 10.1 km; Seealpsee,
2.38 km), in which we recorded the operator interventions, their
causes, and the autonomy rate. On a controlled 1.2 km course,
we compared ROAM with a geometry-only baseline (49) and
ViNT (27) under artificial path offsets, a mannequin on the
path, and construction signs. Further real-world experiments
isolated the effect of semantic information on locomotion, local
navigation, and exploration. In an image-based evaluation on
30 hand-labeled scenes, we compared VLM output abstractions
in terms of the fraction of the trajectory pixels that stayed
within safe and preferred regions. Supplementary experiments
benchmarked several VLMs, ablated the prompt, and tested the
reasoning of VLMs about stair height. Each hiking deployment
and each controlled-course run was performed once per method
(N = 1), and the mannequin avoidance experiment was repeated
10 times per condition (N = 10). Sample sizes were determined
by the available field time and the size of the labeled datasets
rather than by a power analysis. In the image-based evaluation,
the label-selection pipeline produced no output for 3 of the 30
scenes; these scenes were therefore excluded for all methods,
and all methods were compared on the remaining 27 scenes.
No field runs or mannequin trials were excluded.

Hardware configuration
We used the ANYmal D quadruped robot as our platform and
customized it with sensors to meet the specific requirements
of our mission. The robot is equipped with six RealSense
stereo cameras for close-range depth perception and a Velodyne
LiDAR sensor mounted on top for long-range depth measure-
ments. In addition, we integrated a front-mounted C1 camera
with a 198-degree field of view and high dynamic range for
improved visibility in varying lighting conditions. A default
wide-angle rear camera provides visual coverage of the rear.
For global localization, we equipped the robot with a Swift
Navigation Piksi Multi GNSS module using Real-Time Kine-
matic (RTK) corrections from the Swiss Federal Office of
Topography (swisstopo). To maintain internet connectivity
during field operations, we integrated a Teltonika RUTX11
router with a SIM modem to enable real-time data transfer and
processing. This connectivity supports communication with
external systems, including OpenAI’s API for the GPT-4 vision
model. To increase the robot’s computing capacity, especially
for processing camera data and generating elevation maps, we
added an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin module.
Software overview
To enable autonomous navigation, our system is composed of
multiple modules, ranging from low-level control to high-level
navigation. These components operate as independent nodes
within the Robot Operating System (ROS) and communicate
through various topics.
Each autonomous mission starts with the global graphical
user interface (GUI) module, where the user initiates the mission
by selecting coarse waypoints on a loaded satellite image. The
module then generates an interpolated path between these rough
waypoints using a server based on OpenRouteService. This
global path is represented in longitude-latitude-altitude (LLA)
coordinates and requires a transformation to the local robot
frame for further navigation. To achieve this transformation,
we estimate the robot’s pose by fusing data from the LiDAR
point cloud with GNSS measurements.
We sample local lookahead goals from the converted path,
which are fed into the local navigation policy.
The local
navigation module outputs the velocity command to reach the
given goal and sends it to the locomotion module, which controls
each actuator in the robot’s legs to perform the movement. In
parallel, a local elevation mapping module runs to continuously
fuse LiDAR and depth-camera point cloud data with semantic
information such as risk or steppable areas. These semantic
labels are generated by a separate semantic segmentation module
that queries the VLM for new labels to improve environmental
understanding. Details of each method are explained in the
following sections.
Environmental perception
Environmental perception is achieved through the fusion of
data from RGB cameras, depth cameras, and LiDAR sensors.
Vision-language model–based scene evaluation
To achieve a high level of semantic understanding of the environ-
ment, we used gpt-4-vision-preview, the latest model available
at the time of our experiments, which processes RGB images
captured by the robot’s front and rear cameras. Before process-
ing, the images are resized to 640 × 427 pixels. The inputs to
the VLM are the images and a text prompt that contains several
components: a task description, details about the robot’s capa-
bilities, guidelines for generating each output field, the desired
output format, and previously generated memory outputs and
estimates for context.
The prompt is divided into several sections. The “Task” sec-
tion provides the general instructions, explaining that the VLM
should analyze the image to inform the robot’s navigation. The
“Robot” section describes the robot’s capabilities as a quadruped
optimized for terrain navigation. It explains that the robot can
handle uneven surfaces, including stairs and gravel, but cannot
cross water or jump over large obstacles. The “Guidelines”
section specifies the required output constraints. For example,
the model must avoid using directional labels such as “left,”
“right,” or “ahead,” as well as similar or confusing labels at the
same time (such as “grass” and “grassy_area”). These measures
were implemented after observing that unconstrained output
sometimes resulted in undesirable or overly complex semantic
labels (such as “left_road” and “sunny_sky”). The guidelines
ensure that the model produces labels that can be directly seg-
mented by the open-vocabulary semantic segmentation system
without ambiguity.
We define specific output fields to capture different aspects
of semantic understanding. The risk field assigns each label
a numerical risk value ranging from 0.0 (safe) to 1.0 (risky).
High-risk categories include trees, people, cars, fences, walls,
and buildings. The steppable field indicates whether a surface
can be stepped on safely (0.0 for non-steppable to 1.0 for fully
steppable), regardless of its risk level. The preferred area field
rates the preference of the terrain for navigation from 0.0 (not
suitable) to 1.0 (highly suitable). For example, paths and trails
receive higher values than grass, gravel, rocks, or bushes.
A memory mechanism preserves the context over time. After
each analysis, the VLM outputs a memory text summarizing
the robot’s current situation. The last three memory outputs
are added to the prompt. In addition, the outputs of the last
three label values are averaged to stabilize labeling decisions
and serve as contextual input for subsequent queries. At the
end of the prompt, we provide a structured JSON format as the
output. The full prompt is shown in fig. S5.

al. (69). This network takes the labels from the VLM analysis,
augmented with an “other” category, and produces per-pixel
probabilities for each label. These probabilities are combined
with the label-specific values using the following formula:

Sign detection
Although our pipeline maintains semantic label updates every 10
seconds, important transient information—such as the presence
of signs—may be missed in this interval. To address this,
we run a YOLO detector (70) in parallel to detect signs in
the robot’s field of view. When a sign is detected, we pause
the robot and append the sign’s region into the VLM image
query, ensuring immediate attention to the sign’s content. This
approach improves the detection and interpretation of critical
signs, even with a high-field-of-view camera (198◦), which can
make the sign appear small. This allows the robot to correctly
interpret the sign and update its navigation strategy accordingly.
Semantic information integration with elevation mapping
To incorporate semantic information extracted from RGB im-
ages into the robot’s navigation and locomotion processes, we
integrate this data into a 2.5D elevation map. This map fuses
geometric measurements obtained from depth cameras and
LiDAR point clouds with the semantic fields—such as risk,
steppable, and preferred_area—provided by the VLM pipeline.
We adopt the framework developed by Miki et al. (71) and Erni
et al. (72), which is a mapping package that runs on the robot’s
GPU.
By assigning each semantic field to its own layer, the eleva-
tion map maintains a multi-dimensional, temporally consistent
representation of the environment. This approach effectively
preserves past data, as opposed to relying solely on the most
recent image input. As a result, the elevation map supports
both local navigation and locomotion planning: the navigation
module uses the elevation, risk, and preferred_area layers to
make geometrically and semantically informed path decisions,
whereas the locomotion module relies on the elevation and
steppable layers to determine optimal foot placement. In our
experiments, we used a 4 cm resolution over an 8 × 8 m area to
maintain enough detail and context for both local navigation
and locomotion.
Locomotion control
Our locomotion control framework builds on the work of Miki
et al. (3, 7), extending it with additional observational inputs
derived from theVLM. In simulation, we generate steppable and
non-steppable regions using Perlin noise patterns. Specifically,
we randomly sample a threshold and assign a value of 1.0
to areas above this threshold, and 0.0 to areas below it. The
corresponding steppable information is sampled in a circular
pattern identical to the height scans and then concatenated with
the height data. This combined exteroceptive observation is
provided to the neural network policy.
A penalty is applied whenever a foot makes contact with a
non-steppable region, guiding the policy to avoid stepping on the
hazardous areas while following the commanded velocity. More
concretely, we assign a negative reward of −𝛼when a foot steps
onto a non-steppable region, where 𝛼is a scaling coefficient
that determines the penalty’s relative importance within the
overall reward scheme. We set 𝛼= 0.1 in our implementation,
providing a moderate but effective incentive that encourages
the policy to maintain stable and efficient locomotion while
avoiding harmful foot placements.
To achieve a robust control policy suitable for real-world de-
ployment, we employ a teacher-student reinforcement learning
framework (2, 3, 7, 73). The training process consists of two
stages.
In the first stage, a teacher policy is trained in simulation
with privileged information not available on the real robot.
This includes perfect exteroception and friction coefficients
per foot. The policy receives height and steppable scans as
exteroceptive input, along with proprioceptive data such as
velocity commands, base height, roll and pitch commands,
base linear and angular velocities, base orientation (gravity
vector) and joint velocities and positions over time. The reward
function follows the structure of previous work (3, 7), combining
velocity-tracking rewards and penalties on joint velocities and
torques, augmented by the additional penalty for stepping on
non-steppable regions.
In the second stage, the trained teacher policy is distilled into
a student policy. We introduce a larger observation noise into the
exteroceptive inputs to make it robust in real world conditions,
following the noise model described in (3). However, we do not
add large noise to the steppable scan inputs, ensuring that the
policy remains sensitive to this crucial information and does
not learn to ignore it.
During deployment, the student policy receives exteroceptive
inputs from an elevation map, as described in the previous
section. By incorporating elevation and steppable layers, the
policy can make informed decisions about foot placement
in real-time. The use of steppable abstraction enables us to

Local navigation policy
Our local navigation control method builds upon the RL-based
approach by Lee et al. (6), extending it to integrate both seman-
tic segmentation and geometric measurements. This module
generates navigational commands (base velocity and yaw rate)
at 10 Hz.
The local navigation policy ensures efficient and safe navi-
gation by leveraging geometric and semantic information. The
policy also continuously adjusts its path based on real-time
feedback from the environment, enabling robust and reliable
performance even in dynamic and unpredictable conditions.
Incorporating semantic information
Building on the terrain elevation map input used by Lee et al. (6),
we incorporate additional semantic information, specifically risk
and preferred_area, obtained from the VLM module. The risk
information identifies areas that the robot must avoid, whereas
the preferred_area highlights favorable terrain for navigation.
For the risk information, an additional risk map is introduced
alongside the elevation map as input to the policy. During
training, high penalties are applied to paths that cross high-risk
areas, discouraging the robot from traversing them.
For the preferred area, we extend the human-avoidance strat-
egy proposed by Lee et al. (6), which introduced a virtual height
increment for human-detected areas to discourage traversal. We
generalize this idea by applying a virtual height increment to
any unpreferred areas detected by the VLMs. Specifically, when
the robot is within a preferred area, the height of surrounding
unpreferred areas is artificially increased, encouraging the robot
to remain on the preferred path. For instance, if the robot is
navigating a preferred walking path, the adjacent grass areas
are assigned a higher virtual height, making them less appeal-
ing for traversal. Conversely, when the robot is already in a
non-preferred area, no height increment is applied, ensuring
the robot can still navigate effectively from its current location.
Policy training
The local navigation policy is trained in a simulated environment
similar to Lee et al. (6), with a traversability graph generated
following probabilistic roadmap–based RL (PRM-RL) (74).
Risky areas, defined as unapproachable regions, are identified
by simulating robots with a pre-trained locomotion policy. Each
node in the navigation graph is labeled as either traversable or
not, and a negative reward of −1.0 is assigned when the closest
node to the robot’s foot is marked as untraversable.
During training, the robot starts on a randomly selected
obstacle-free node and follows a predefined sequence of nodes.
The path (sequence of nodes) is replaced with a global plan
during deployment. Navigation goals are determined using the
pure pursuit algorithm, selecting the next waypoint from the
locally transformed global path based on a fixed look-ahead
distance of 8 meters. This ensures the robot consistently moves
toward reachable, path-aligned goals in the environment.
Global path planning
Although the local navigation policy enables the robot to walk
to a given waypoint within its line of sight, following a long
path beyond the robot’s field of perception requires global
information. To address this, we implemented a global planner
that outputs waypoints for the local navigation policy.
The global planner utilizes satellite data in the WGS84
coordinate system, also known as the longitude-latitude-altitude
(LLA) coordinate system. The user selects a map section via
the corner coordinates, which is downloaded using the Web
Map Tiling Services of the Swiss Geo Portal (75). The satellite
image tiles, provided in the Cartesian LV03 coordinate system—
a locally used Swiss reference frame—are transformed to LLA
using the approximation given by swisstopo (76).
For global path planning, we developed a GUI that displays
the downloaded geo-referenced satellite image.
The robot
operator can set global goal points with arbitrary distances
by clicking on the map, delete points, and zoom in for easy
operation. To plan a path between the goal points, we created a
server based on OpenRouteService, which incorporates Swiss
hiking paths. The server takes the goal points as input and
outputs sequential waypoints in LLA coordinates in 2 m to 5 m
intervals. In order to enable the robot to follow these global
waypoints, they are continuously transformed into the local
robot frame.
Global and local pose estimation through holistic fusion
Accurate and precise global and local pose estimation allows
the robot to follow long-range and large-scale goals reliably.
To enable such an estimation, the optimization-based sensor
fusion framework Graph-MSF (77, 78) is developed further to
overcome the particular (practical) challenges of long-range
outdoor hiking, namely large distances potentially leading to
numerical issues, unreliable GNSS reception, drifting non-
global measurements, and environments poor in geometrical
features.
To provide accurate and reliable robot poses as
part of a map-based SLAM framework, particularly in cases
when the GNSS signal is lost, an improved version of the
degeneracy-aware X-ICP (79–81) Light Detection and Ranging

fuses Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) measurements together
with the globally accurate but noisy GNSS measurements,
the more consistent but drifting LiDAR-SLAM SE(3)-pose
measurements, and locally consistent TSIF (82)-based inertial-
kinematic odometry measurements. Graph-MSF uses a factor
graph formulation, where the state variables of the robot and
the relative context are estimated as the Maximum a Posteriori

SUPPLEMENTARY METHODS
Labels during experiments
In this section, we show how the VLM labels evolved over time
during the mission. In response to different scenes and contexts,
the VLM generated a variety of labels, each accompanied by
its associated value.
Zermatt
Fig. S1 illustrates these temporal changes, where the color
indicates the assigned risk value and the bars indicate the
presence of each label. For most of the mission, the gravel path
was identified as a low-risk label, but this eventually shifted
to labels such as paved_path, paved_road, and road (A to H
and I). Near the lake (B), labels such as puddle and water_body
appeared. In areas where fences appeared (D), a fence or fences
were detected. Similarly, when the path ran along a railroad
track, labels such as rail_track or train_track were generated
(F). To visualize the labels, we grouped similar labels into a
single row. To achieve this, we utilized a VLM to suggest
appropriate categories by providing all the labels from the
dataset. The categorized labels were then used to generate the
figure, ensuring a clearer representation of the data.
Seealpsee
Fig. S2 also illustrates the labels during the Seealpsee mission.
Throughout the majority of the mission, the traversable path
was consistently identified as gravel_path/hiking_trail with a
low-risk value (blue). This path was typically bordered by mid
to high risk environmental features such as grass/vegetation or
tree/trees. Where the path ran alongside the water, the label
water/water_body/lake was frequently detected (A, B, D, E).
Specific obstacles were also captured. For instance, at point
(D), the presence of cows on the trail triggered the detection
of animal/animals as a high-risk entity. Towards the end of
the trajectory, as the path approached the goal, labels such as
building/buildings appeared (H).
Navigation from indoor to outdoor environments
In this experiment, we aimed to demonstrate the versatility and
zero-shot adaptability of our navigation system across various
environments. The experimental course was designed to en-
compass a broad spectrum of settings, beginning indoors and
transitioning to an outdoor campus environment with paved
areas and buildings, before moving through a natural area with
forest and along paths bordered by grass fields. Throughout
these transitions, the system exhibited adaptability in risk assess-
ment (fig. S3). It seamlessly transitioned from indoor features,
such as floors and stairs, to outdoor elements, including paved
surfaces, grass, and buildings. As the environment shifted to
natural settings, the system further adapted, recognizing gravel
paths as preferable routes and trees as obstacles. Notably, the
system demonstrated a nuanced understanding by dynamically
adjusting risk values based on environmental variations, such
as the height of grass alongside paths. Additionally, the ex-
periment showed the system’s capability to manage dynamic
elements by accurately identifying and categorizing obstacles,
such as bicycles or benches, as potential obstacles. These find-
ings indicate the generalizability of our VLM-based navigation
approach.

both its ability to reach the goal and its rate of collisions with
the mannequin (fig. S4). We considered the trial successful

Evaluating different VLMs
The primary ROAM system architecture relies on a large,
cloud-based VLM (gpt-4-vision-preview, as described in
Materials and Methods), which, despite its powerful reasoning,
introduces practical considerations for deployment in the wild.
A dependency on stable internet connectivity and potential
network latency can be limitations in remote and unstructured
environments.
This motivated an evaluation to investigate
whether more compact or newer VLM alternatives could pro-
vide the necessary common-sense and contextual reasoning for
autonomous navigation. To this end, we conducted a quanti-
tative evaluation to assess the ability of a range of models to
perceive and assign risk.
For this evaluation, we created a benchmark comprising two
distinct challenges. The first, the TartanGround Dataset (85),
utilizes 372 simulated scenes to evaluate a model’s foundational
ability to identify common terrain. The second, the Sign Dataset,
uses 12 real-world images featuring instructional signs to assess
higher-level, contextual reasoning. We benchmarked several
models, including gpt-4o, various gemini and gemma models,
and qwen2.5-vl, measuring performance by calculating the
Micro Intersection over Union (IoU) between their generated
risk maps and our ground-truth labels.
Due to the rapid iteration and deprecation cycles of com-
mercial foundation models over the course of this research
and its subsequent review period, different model versions
were utilized across our evaluations. The original hardware
field deployments were conducted using gpt-4-vision-preview.
Following its deprecation by the provider, our comprehensive
VLM benchmarking (detailed in table S2) evaluated its direct
successor, gpt-4o, alongside gemini-2.0-flash and other open
weight models. Most recently, as these subsequent models were
also deprecated, our prompt ablation and robustness studies
were conducted using the currently available gemini-3.5-flash.
This necessary progression across multiple model generations
empirically demonstrates the model-agnostic nature of the
ROAM architecture, confirming that the system can seamlessly
integrate with and benefit from advancing foundation models
without requiring structural modifications to the underlying
control policies.
The results, shown in table S2, reveal a distinction in
model capabilities.
On the TartanGround dataset, most
models performed well, demonstrating a solid grasp of ba-
sic scene understanding.
Top contenders like gpt-4o and
gemini-2.0-flash achieved high IoU scores of 0.853 and
0.851, respectively, and the smaller gemma-3-27b model scored
a strong 0.836. This suggests that the ability to identify standard
environmental features is well-integrated across many modern
VLMs. However, on the more challenging Sign Dataset, which
requires associating a textual command with a physical object, a
bigger performance gap emerged. Here, gemini-2.0-flash
excelled with an IoU of 0.912, correctly interpreting the signs
and their context. In contrast, the performance of smaller mod-
els dropped sharply, with gemma-3-4b and qwen-2.5-vl-7b
dropping to 0.521 and 0.416. The reason for this failure became
apparent upon qualitative inspection: although smaller models
could often read the text of a sign, such as “DO NOT STEP,”
they failed to semantically ground this instruction to the correct
object in the scene, like the wooden pallet it was placed on.
This failure to bridge language with spatial context is a major
shortcoming.

The result is shown in table S3. It demonstrates that ROAM’s

the core premise of ROAM: dynamic, open-vocabulary gener-

unstructured real-world environments.

make this design choice concrete, we analyze how well current
VLMs can enforce an explicit, robot-specific height limit (such
as “stairs higher than 10 cm cannot be climbed”) from RGB
images alone.
Dataset
We collected 27 RGB images of staircases in diverse settings (in-
door and outdoor; varied lighting; concrete, carpeted, wooden,
and metal steps). The ground-truth riser heights were measured
manually and range from 13.0 cm to 18.0 cm. Each staircase
was photographed from multiple viewpoints and with differ-
ent camera fields of view, so that the same physical structure
appears under varying perspective and scale. Note that under
a 10 cm climbability limit every staircase in the dataset is im-
passable, so the correct decision is coarse, whereas under a
15 cm limit the dataset contains climbable stairs (13.0–14.5 cm),
boundary cases (15.5–16.0 cm), and obviously impassable stairs
(17.0–18.0 cm), requiring centimeter-level discrimination.
Prompt variants
We evaluated three model generations (Gemini 2.5, 3.0, and 3.5)
under three prompting conditions. We selected these models
based on their availability at the time of the evaluation. The orig-
inal condition uses the standard ROAM prompt, which instructs
the VLM to act as a navigation planner for the quadrupedal
robot and states that the robot can walk over stairs, assigning
them a low risk value depending on steepness; it contains no
height limit. The two constraint conditions append an explicit
rule to the robot description:
“You can walk over stairs or slopes, but they must
be lower than {10.0, 15.0} cm to be safe. If they are
higher than {10.0, 15.0} cm, they should be assigned
a high risk value (1.0) and unsteppable (0.0).”

Under the 10 cm rule, the newer models (Gemini 3.0 and 3.5)

high risk to all staircases well above the limit (17.0–18.0 cm),
whereas Gemini 2.5 assigned low risk to every staircase under
the 15 cm rule.
Direct stair-height estimation To isolate metric perception from
decision making, we simply asked the models to estimate the
riser height, without any other prompt. Fig. S15C plots them
against the ground truth. Gemini 2.5 produced estimates only
weakly correlated with the measured heights (mean absolute er-
ror ∼1.8 cm, with outliers exceeding 5 cm), whereas Gemini 3.0
and 3.5 track the ground truth substantially better (∼0.7 cm
mean absolute error). Nevertheless, errors of up to a few cen-
timeters remain, and estimates for the same staircase vary across
viewpoints and camera fields of view. Notably, even accurate-
looking estimates (such as 15.0 cm for a 15.5 cm staircase) can
fall on the wrong side of a nearby safety threshold.
Implications for ROAM First, the metric spatial understanding
of VLMs is improving rapidly across model generations: the
newest models produce better-correlated height estimates. Sec-
ond, centimeter-level discrimination near a safety threshold
remains unreliable: residual estimation errors of a few centime-
ters, their sensitivity to perspective and field of view, and the
tendency of estimates to drift toward a prompted threshold all
produce misclassifications precisely in the boundary regime.
Delegating this decision to the VLM alone would produce
occasional safety violations. This supports the architecture
of ROAM: the VLM provides semantic risk priors, whereas
the 2.5D elevation map and the learned policies enforce the
robot-specific physical limits. The learned locomotion policy
refuses untraversable stairs even when the VLM fails to flag
them, and when the VLM does assign high risk correctly, the
region is avoided earlier at the planning level.
