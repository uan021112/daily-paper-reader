ABSTRACT
A corridor, its mirror reflection, and its image on a display can look identical, yet
their physical surfaces lie at different depths. We test whether bounded camera
motions distinguish these explanations. Intervene3D takes an image and an initial
geometry estimate, predicts the view under each hypothesis and candidate mo-
tion, and selects a motion by maximin separability. It reports the mechanism and
first physical surface only if the available motions separate the hypotheses still
in contention. Otherwise it abstains. An adequacy test also rejects observations
that no hypothesis explains. On a matched synthetic benchmark with exact land-
marks, disabling abstention gives confident answers on 76.4% of unresolvable
scenes. With abstention, none receives a confident answer under exact or noisy
landmarks. At 100% accuracy on answered scenes, maximin selection answers
61.0% of scenes and summed selection 43.1%. The adequacy test lowers wrong
answers on strongly curved mirror scenes from 56.8% to 12.4%. Real display
experiments compare two hypotheses using one frozen depth-network estimate.
The second image supplies photometric evidence and is never passed through the
depth network. One executed motion reduces the pooled median surface error of
twelve networks by 31.5%. These networks have larger error inside the annotated
panel than outside on 33.4% of illusion-benchmark images. They place the first
physical surface behind the glass on 36.9% of Mirror3D frames.

INTRODUCTION
A corridor viewed through an open doorway, reflected in a planar mirror, displayed on a screen, or
seen through a window can produce the same image. The first physical surface along a camera ray
is the nearest surface that would stop a physical probe: corridor structure, mirror glass, the display
panel, or the window pane. A depth network may estimate the depicted corridor behind a display
even though the physical surface is the panel. When the images are identical, the initial image pro-
vides no evidence for choosing among these mechanisms. Camera motion can expose their different
light paths. Matched hypotheses produce the same initial image and differ only after motion. Fig-
ure 1 summarizes the method and shows, on one real stereo pair, the two hypothesis warps that are
compared. Several lines of work address mirrors, glass, multiple surfaces, and abstention. Multi-
layer formulations retain both a transparent interface and the scene behind that interface (Wen et al.,
2025; Wen & Deng, 2026; Min et al., 2026). Detectors localize mirrors and glass (Tan et al., 2021;
Na et al., 2026). Selective prediction with split conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005; Lei et al.,
2018) decides when a system should answer. These methods estimate surfaces and confidence from
a fixed viewpoint. This work asks whether any available viewpoint can identify the physical mech-
anism. Identifiability depends on the hypothesis family, the bounded action set, and a separability
threshold with units specified by the protocol.

the motion that maximizes the smallest separability among separable, contending hypothesis pairs.
Intervene3D-sum uses their summed separability. With two hypotheses, maximin and summed se-
lection coincide. Evidence that the selectors differ therefore comes from the synthetic benchmark.

tion set can separate the remaining alternatives, and applies an adequacy test where enabled. Table 1
states when each protocol performs these checks.
1. Action-set identifiability, intervention selection, and an adequacy test. We define separability
under camera motion and test whether every contending hypothesis pair can be separated by an
available action. A maximin selector chooses the motion. An adequacy test with a threshold
fixed in advance flags observations the family does not explain.
2. A benchmark with measured ambiguity. The three base mechanisms are pixel-identical at the
initial view to below 3 × 10−13 px. Scenes that no action resolves allow abstention to be scored.

3. Real camera motion. Registered frames of 53 real scenes supply executed camera motions for

4. Measurements across networks and scenes. Errors on depicted content are measured across
twelve checkpoints, and physical mirror surfaces are evaluated on 123 Mirror3D frames. One
illusion category uses a rebuilt stereo reference, validated under a fixed acceptance rule.

2.6
PROTOCOLS FOR REAL DATA
Both illusion-benchmark protocols use H = {HD, HE}. The calibrated stereo baseline supplies one
fixed viewpoint change, |A| = 1. Registered photographs of a static scene supply a larger action
set. Executed-motion separability is predicted before motion. Stereo separability uses the captured
second photograph. The depth network runs once on the initial image. Each hypothesis uses that
estimate to warp photographs, and the observed images provide the decision evidence. An image
is admissible only when at least 90% of the stereo reference disparity inside the region lies within
1 px of a plane fitted by random sample consensus. The criterion checks the flat-panel assumption
independently of the network estimate. Alignment first expresses the network output in inverse-
depth form ˆp, converting metric-depth outputs as required. Scale s and shift t in Equation 9 are
fitted by least squares to stereo reference disparity outside the annotated region, then applied inside
it:
dD = s ˆp + t.
(9)
Fit and evaluation regions are disjoint, so alignment cannot absorb the error being measured. The
same stereo reference aligns both the reported surface and the depth estimate used for comparison.
For the flat-panel hypothesis HE, the ring plane is fitted to the aligned estimate on a 24-pixel ring
outside the region and evaluated inside it. Fitting inside would inherit the depicted relief. The
ring locates panels flush with their surroundings, but can misplace a panel standing in front of the
background (Section 3.5).

RESULTS
A matched synthetic benchmark evaluates motion selection, abstention, and mechanisms outside
the family. Real data then evaluate the decision and the reported surface with twelve released depth
networks, under executed camera motion, in a hand-held RGB-D capture, and on stereo photographs.
Figure 3 summarizes failure rates (a), synthetic selection (b), and executed-motion surface errors (c).
3.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Synthetic variants of a base scene share the same initial rays and apparent depth. Their initial views
coincide to within 2.3 × 10−13 px, leaving a single-view classifier at chance. Marker points on the
operator’s rig provide a possible mirror cue after motion. They are resampled until their reflection
lies outside the initial view. A scene is labeled resolvable when some action separates its true
hypothesis from each alternative, with different actions allowed. Some scenes remain unresolvable
within the action set. Classifier splits follow base scenes. Causal explanation accuracy (CEA)
is the share of scenes with the correct mechanism, counting abstention as an error. Committed
accuracy is that share among answered scenes. The false physical certainty rate (FPCR) is the share
of unresolvable scenes answered with posterior above τ. Identifiability AUROC measures how
well the identifiability score ranks resolvable against unresolvable scenes. Overall abstention and
abstention on resolvable scenes are also reported. Synthetic values are means and sample standard
deviations over 11 seeds, with the benchmark redrawn per seed. Surface error is the median relative
error of the reported first physical surface against the stereo reference inside the annotated region.
It uses disparity for stereo photographs and depth for executed motions. An answer of HD retains
the network estimate. A network is fooled on an image when its median relative error inside the
annotated region exceeds its error outside. This operational label does not identify a mechanism
by itself. A region–checkpoint combination is one annotated region evaluated with one network
checkpoint. The action set includes translations of {0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30} m along each of three
axes in both directions, yaw and pitch rotations of {5◦, 10◦} in both directions, and the null action:
24 + 8 + 1 = 33 actions. Equation 3 uses λm = 1. Priors are uniform. The constants ϵ = 1.0 px,
β = 1.0, τ = 0.45, and λo = 4.0 px are not tuned. Exact landmarks define the exact-encoder
condition. The noisy encoder adds 0.6 px image-coordinate noise and 2% relative depth noise. On
real data, twelve published checkpoints are evaluated as released (Yang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2026;
Bochkovskiy et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2023; Piccinelli et al.,
2024; 2026). They span seven architecture families and a 38× range in parameter count. None is
trained or tuned on any evaluation set. A transparency-fine-tuned Depth Anything variant returns
metric depth that requires a depth prompt. Without that prompt its alignment fails, so the variant is
excluded from the twelve-checkpoint evaluation.

3.3
BEYOND THE HYPOTHESIS FAMILY
The first of two tests adds three primary out-of-family mechanisms: a spherical mirror, a cylindrical
display, and a water-like surface, plus a strongly curved mirror variant. Without the adequacy test,
maximin assigns 60–66% of the three primary mechanisms to a family member. None of those
answers is HD. The 2 px adequacy test lowers the share of wrongly answered scenes from 56.8%
to 12.4% for strongly curved mirrors. Over the three primary mechanisms, it lowers that share from
62.5% to 49.1%. It leaves in-family results unchanged with exact landmarks. For the noisy encoder,
it changes CEA to 0.385, committed accuracy to 1.000, and abstention to 62%. Water-like surfaces
are detected on only 4.2% of scenes because a family member, most often HR, fits within 2 px on
about 96%. A fifth hypothesis, a spherical mirror of known sag (curvature depth), answers curved-
mirror scenes at accuracy 1.000. A nominal sag gives wrong answers on 43% of those scenes. The
second test models glass as a dielectric interface that transmits the scene and weakly reflects the
operator, with normal-incidence Fresnel reflectance of 5.4–13.8%. Some action then separates the
direct hypothesis from its alternatives on 48.1% of direct scenes, and the transmission hypothesis
on 22.7% of transmission scenes. Neither class is resolvable under the basic transmission model.
Committed accuracy remains 1.000 across all four mechanisms. Mirrors become harder to resolve
because the pane now also reflects the operator: their resolvable share falls from 69.3% to 25.2%.
Varied marker contrast provides a cue because a pane hides dim reflected markers that a mirror
shows. The transmission gain vanishes with constant marker contrast.

3.4
EXECUTED CAMERA MOTION IN REAL SCENES
The executed-motion experiment uses illusion-benchmark scenes photographed from several posi-
tions. Of 906 ordered frame pairs over 53 scenes, 528 pass the fixed registration criteria, with median
reprojection error 0.80 px. Each of 135 annotated initial regions retains at least one accepted target
frame. Selected motions have median rotation 22.2◦and translation 0.204 m. Table 2 reports the re-
sults. Figure 3c shows that one executed motion lowers the pooled median surface error from 4.35%
to 2.98%. The relative reduction is 31.5%, and the median is lower on all twelve checkpoints. The
answer is worse than the network on 9.3% of the 1,620 region–checkpoint combinations. No com-
bination abstains with every accepted motion. Abstention is 0.8% within the 10◦/0.30 m envelope
of the synthetic action set (Table 2). Among the 663 contending combinations, mechanism accuracy
is 64.4%. Every wrong answer is HD on a physical panel. A post hoc analysis finds lower accuracy
with larger pose reprojection error, consistent with registration limiting the decision. Selection has
little effect here because maximin and summed selection coincide and almost every accepted motion
separates the two hypotheses. Five selection rules differ by at most 1.4 percentage points. These
data evaluate the decision and reported surface. Testing the advantage of maximin selection requires
three or more contending hypotheses. The hand-held mirror recording below supplies such a case.
Hand-held capture.
Figure 4 follows one region of each recording through the decision. On
the monitor, the network depth reproduces the depicted room, and the reported surface is the flat

3.5
REAL STEREO PHOTOGRAPHS
A calibrated stereo rig provides one fixed viewpoint change: a 63 mm baseline along the image rows.
Table 3 covers twelve checkpoints on 404 admissible photographs, giving 4,848 image–checkpoint
combinations. In the monitor-in-the-frame category, which shows a video frame on a screen, the
released reference disagrees with its stereo images by about 3 px inside the screen. This offset is
constant over time and also affects static bezel features. That category’s reference is rebuilt from
each stereo pair under a fixed acceptance rule. With that correction, the networks are fooled on
33.4% of images overall. Figure 3a gives the fooled share of each checkpoint. One stereo baseline
separates the two hypotheses on a minority of images. The system abstains on the rest. Table 3
shows that answers of HE have lower median surface error than the depth estimate in the first three
categories. Including answers of HD, the median error remains below the network’s for all twelve
checkpoints in each of those categories. The fourth category exposes the ring fit’s limitation. A
monitor filling the frame stands in front of the wall, so the ring plane lies behind the screen. The
system mostly answers HD, which is counted as the wrong mechanism because the region is a panel.
Its committed accuracy is therefore only 2.5%, although the median surface error of 7.6% is close
to the network’s 7.5%.

RELATED WORK
Prior work localizes mirrors and glass as scene elements (Yang et al., 2019; Mei et al., 2020; Tan
et al., 2021), models transparency through decomposed radiance (Na et al., 2026), and quantifies the
difficulty with dedicated benchmarks (Zama Ramirez et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2022).
Robustness studies carry the same concern to corrupted inputs (Kong et al., 2023), and a recent chal-
lenge evaluates current systems on high-resolution depth for these materials (Zama Ramirez et al.,
2025). Multi-layer formulations represent more than one surface along a ray (Wen et al., 2025; Wen
& Deng, 2026; Min et al., 2026). An illusion benchmark documents at scale that depth estimates can
follow depicted content (Yao et al., 2025). Detector and multi-layer outputs can supply priors over
the hypothesis family. This work tests whether the available observations determine the mechanism
behind an appearance. Classical analyses establish that image evidence alone can leave a family of
scenes indistinguishable (Kutulakos & Steger, 2008). Active and next-best-view perception choose
viewpoints using belief-weighted objectives (Yang & Waslander, 2022; Rainforth et al., 2024). In-
tervene3D asks whether any view within a bounded set permits a mechanism decision. It interprets
identifiability interventionally (Lippe et al., 2022). Deferral rules (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017; 2019)
and split conformal prediction (Vovk et al., 2005; Lei et al., 2018) control which predictions are re-
tained. The failure classifiers in Section 3.6 estimate when depth is unreliable. Intervene3D reports
a mechanism and first physical surface when its decision checks pass, or abstains.
5
CONCLUSION
Intervene3D combines action-set identifiability, motion selection, and explicit abstention to report
the first physical surface. On matched synthetic scenes, maximin selection answers more scenes
than summed selection at equal committed accuracy. The adequacy test rejects some mechanisms
outside the family. A known-sag mirror hypothesis permits correct answers on curved mirrors. Real
two-hypothesis experiments show that image evidence after motion can improve the reported surface
across twelve frozen depth networks.
Limitations.
The decision depends on the specified family, action set, and threshold. Most water-
like surfaces in Section 3.3 pass the adequacy test despite being outside the family. Real motions are
recovered from previously captured frames. The illusion experiments have two hypotheses. Mirrors
in video add a third but lack a cue separating the mirror from an opening. Scenes without panels or
mirrors are evaluated through the direct scenes of the synthetic benchmark. Stereo alignment uses
reference disparity outside the annotated region. The ring fit can misplace a panel in front of its
surroundings.

reference reaches 0.85. The statistic reaches 0.872. The rebuilt reference is sparse, valid on 30%
of the region pixels of the validation category, and never interpolated. Every error inside the region
uses valid pixels alone. Under it, 98 of 99 images remain admissible.

A.6
COMPARISON METHODS AND ABLATIONS
The synthetic comparisons in Tables 7 and 8 use the following methods. The classifiers use base-
scene-disjoint training and evaluation splits. The single-view classifier is a softmax classifier on
summary features of the initial landmarks, including position, depth, and visibility. Passive multi-
view is the same classifier with features that describe the response to one fixed, unchosen lateral
translation. The next-best-view proxy selects the largest predicted change from the initial geometry
under the maximum-prior hypothesis. It is a generic proxy and implements no published next-best-
view method. Random intervention chooses uniformly among allowed non-null actions. Largest-
translation chooses the allowed action with the greatest translation magnitude and breaks ties by
action order. Both then use the hypothesis-conditioned decision. The no-hypothesis-conditioning
ablation uses the direct transition for every hypothesis and retains its interface aperture for visibility.
The noisy encoder perturbs landmark coordinates by 0.6 px and depths by 2% relative Gaussian
noise. All other components are those of Intervene3D-sum. The no-abstention ablation disables all
abstention checks and forces Intervene3D-sum to return a mechanism. Forced maximin applies the
same change to the maximin selector.
On executed real motions, Table 13 also includes first frame, which chooses the earliest accepted
target frame. Largest-translation chooses the largest registered camera displacement. The other
real-data rules are maximin, summed, and random selection.

SECOND DATASET WITH ORDINAL SUPERVISION
The second dataset, LayeredDepth, provides ordinal relations between point pairs rather than dense
depth, together with a per-point layer index authored independently of the present work. The ordi-
nal form suits the question here, because a relation between two points holds under the scale and
shift ambiguity that a monocular predictor leaves open. Splitting the relations by layer index then
separates pairs that lie on a first visible surface from pairs that lie behind glass, with no quantity
of ours entering the split. DA-V2-Small and DepthPro answer the single-layer pairs well and are
less accurate on the multi-layer pairs, at 0.848 against 0.737 for DA-V2-Small and 0.843 against
0.669 for DepthPro (Table 18). The two networks agree closely on the surfaces they can see, at
first-surface accuracies of 0.847 and 0.842, but diverge on the surfaces behind an interface, where
the ambiguity under study arises.

C.7
MIRRORS IN REAL VIDEO
ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023) provides handheld video with registered poses and a laser-
scanned mesh. The mirror plane is taken from this mesh for 30 scenes and 1,433 mirror regions. The
regions are evaluated for all twelve checkpoints with the hypothesis set {HD, HE, HR}, which gives
17,196 region–checkpoint combinations. The operator’s reflection is not annotated, so no operator
cue is available, and without one a planar mirror and the opening it imitates predict the same images
under every motion. The protocol, written before any run, therefore predicted abstention on the pair
of HD and HR. The system abstains on 75.8% of combinations, and the identifiability of that pair
is zero in every abstention. It commits only where all hypotheses place the surface within 2%, so its
answers match the prediction there, at a median surface error of 0.036 for both. A surface is counted
as confidently wrong when it is committed and lies more than 10% from the mesh plane. In a post
hoc analysis, confidently wrong surfaces fall from 35.3% to 5.0% of combinations, entirely through
abstention. Table 19 shows that the share is lower for all twelve checkpoints, and it is also lower
in all 30 scenes. The experiment confirms the identifiability analysis on real mirrors. Without an
operator cue, no motion can separate these mirrors from the openings they imitate, and the system
declines these regions instead of committing to a wrong surface.

models stay at depth 3 with at least 40 samples per leaf, and nothing is tuned on the evaluation
set. Table 20 separates evaluation splits and category pools. With only the checkpoint held out, the

C.11
SELECTION UNDER RESTRICTED ACTION SETS
The hand-held mirror recording supplies 33 candidate motions per region. With all of them available,
maximin and summed selection choose the same motion on 318 of the 324 region–checkpoint com-
binations, because one motion separates every contending pair. The selection is therefore repeated
with the action set restricted to a random subset of k motions. For each k, 20 subsets are drawn per
region. Identifiability, selection, and the decision then use only the motions of the subset. The stored
separabilities and residuals of every candidate motion are reused, and the full action set reproduces
the counts of Section 3.4 exactly. Table 23 reports the share of correct decisions. Accuracy falls for
both rules as k shrinks, because fewer regions stay identifiable. Maximin is at least as accurate as
summed selection in all 160 combinations of subset size and draw: it is higher in 146 and equal in
14. The gap is largest at twelve motions, where it reaches 2.9 percentage points. The gain appears
where several hypothesis pairs contend, as the maximin criterion predicts. At twelve motions it
reaches 14.8 points for Metric3D-Large, where 27 of 27 regions have two or more contending pairs,
13.7 points for Metric3D-Small (26 regions), 3.5 for DA3-Large (19), 2.8 for DA3-Small (10), and
0.2 for DA3-Base (4). For the other seven checkpoints, at most two regions have more than one
contending pair, and the two rules select the same motions.

D.1
THRESHOLD SENSITIVITY
The separability threshold is swept from 0.25 to 3.0 px on the three-mechanism benchmark, with the
label and the decision recomputed at each value. Every reported quantity of Intervene3D-sum stays
unchanged, at 56.9% abstentions, a CEA of 0.431, a committed accuracy of 1.000, and an FPCR of
0.000. Only at 5.0 px does the abstention rate move, to 57.4%. Separability on this benchmark is
bimodal, so the seven thresholds tested between 0.25 and 3.0 px give identical aggregates. On real
data the two thresholds were fixed before the runs reported here, at 2.0 gray levels for one stereo
baseline and 1.0 px for executed motions.
D.2
SENSITIVITY TO THE OCCLUSION WEIGHT
The composite distance of equation 3 weights an occlusion term against a motion term, with λo =
4.0 throughout. The same eleven seeds are rerun at λo ∈{1, 2, 4, 8, 16}, a 16× range, with every
other setting fixed. Table 24 reports the outcome for Intervene3D-sum. Committed accuracy stays
at 1.000 at every weight, including on mirrors, and the weight moves only how many scenes are
answered. From λo = 2 upward the CEA stays between 0.430 and 0.436, and only at λo = 1, where
a single visibility change weighs no more than a pixel of motion, does it fall to 0.379.

D.4
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Visibility and general distance.
A landmark is visible under a hypothesis when it projects inside
the image and the ray to it passes through the finite interface aperture, and operator markers follow
the same rule. A landmark counted as visible under one mechanism and hidden under another
therefore contributes to Docclusion, and the term stays discrete, so a single unambiguous appearance
or disappearance carries the weight described in Section 2.3. The general implementation also
supports
Dgeneral = D + λgDgeometry + λfDfeature,
where Dgeometry is root-mean-square 3D distance between back-projected landmarks and Dfeature
is an appearance distance. Both weights are zero in every reported experiment, giving the two-term
distance in equation 3.
Landmarks and belief.
Each synthetic scene carries 40 content landmarks placed by jittered strat-
ified sampling over the inner 85% of the interface extent, in a random subset of 40 cells of a 7 × 7
grid, together with 3 operator markers. The camera measures 320 × 240 with fx = fy = 320.
Stratification ensures coverage of the region, and coverage determines whether a visibility change
becomes observable at all. The belief update uses β = 1.0 and a posterior floor of 10−6, which
stops one unlucky observation from eliminating a hypothesis permanently.
Computational cost.
On the four-mechanism benchmark the selector enumerates |A| = 33 ac-
tions against six hypothesis pairs. Predicting every consequence in closed form, with projection and
visibility, and evaluating identifiability takes a median of 46 ms per scene in NUMPY on one central
processing unit core, without graphics acceleration. On real photographs the depth forward pass
dominates the cost, at 462 ms per image end to end for the smallest checkpoint and 1140 ms for
the largest. The cost grows linearly in |A|, and the evaluations remain independent, which allows a
direct parallelization over available cores.

E.1
EXTENDING BEYOND PLANAR OPTICS
The four-member family stays planar in its interfaces, covering a flat panel, a planar mirror, and a
planar pane. Curved mirrors and non-planar transparent surfaces occur commonly, and the present
section states what changes to admit them and what remains.
Decision rule.
No part of the decision rule depends on planarity. Separability equation 2 holds
between the predicted consequences of any two hypotheses under any action, identifiability equa-
tion 4 maximizes that quantity over the action set, and the abstention rule equation 5 minimizes over
contending pairs against a separability threshold. A curved mirror enters as a further Hk carrying its
own transition W, and reaches the maximin selector equation 7 as one more pair without any change
to the criterion. The belief update equation 8 and the adequacy test behave the same way, since both
require a residual and nothing further. Appendix B.4 does exactly this with a spherical mirror.
Transition guarantee.
The present construction derives its value from closed-form transitions. A
planar mirror reflects exactly across a plane, and a plane-parallel pane forms a fixed apparent image
in the paraxial limit. Reference views therefore match to a largest deviation of order 10−13 px, and
any divergence after an action follows from the mechanism alone. A general curved surface admits
no exact transition, and two routes remain open, each weakening a different property.
The first route is a parametric surface, such as a spherical mirror of given sag. It keeps a closed-form
transition and therefore keeps the matched guarantee. Such a family extends in a controlled way,
although only to the surfaces that the parameterization covers and only with the right parameters.
With the true sag it commits correctly on curved mirrors, and with a nominal sag it commits wrongly
on 43% of them (Appendix B.4).
The second route is a learned transition. It covers arbitrary geometry but forfeits the property on
which the design depends. Once a separate fit produces W per mechanism, a difference between
two predictions may follow from a difference between fitted models instead of physical mechanisms,
which is exactly the confound that the shared projection removes. The matched-counterfactual guar-
antee would then hold only approximately, by an amount that is hard to bound.
The adequacy test addresses mechanisms that neither route models. A mechanism that no member
explains leaves a residual that the test can flag. A mechanism that some member explains within
the threshold under every action stays indistinguishable from that member, which no test on the
observations can change.
E.2
SCOPE AND LIMITATIONS
Five limits bound what the reported numbers establish. First, the synthetic identifiability AUROC
and the false physical certainty rate are contrasts rather than absolute scores. One computation pro-
duces both the resolvability label and the decision on the synthetic benchmark, so the AUROC of
1.000 and the FPCR of 0.000 in Table 7 stand only against the values that other methods reach on
identical scenes. The noisy-encoder ablation, which breaks that identity, keeps committed accu-
racy at 0.996 and the FPCR at 0.000. Second, identifiability is relative to the family, the action set
and ϵ. A mechanism outside the family is detected only when it leaves a residual above the ade-
quacy threshold, which the water-like surface of Appendix B.4 does on 4% of scenes. Third, the
illusion-benchmark motions come from frames captured for another purpose. On that benchmark
the hypothesis set holds two members, so maximin and the summed objective coincide. The hand-
held mirror recording supplies three contending hypotheses, and there maximin selection answers
correctly on 87.7%, against 55.6% for the largest motion and 33.3% for a random motion. With
the action set restricted, maximin is never less accurate than summed selection (Appendix C.11).
Fourth, the evidence for the decision on real data comes from an illusion benchmark whose panels
fool the predictors and from hand-held recordings of a monitor and a mirror. On mirrors without
an operator cue the decision abstains, as identifiability predicts (Appendix C.7). Fifth, the stereo
protocol fits the panel on a ring outside the region, which misplaces it where the panel stands in
front of its surroundings, and alignment uses the reference outside the region at test time, for the
answer and the predictor alike.

Two contemporaneous works appeared during the preparation of this paper. Nguyen et al. (2026)

E.3
REPRODUCIBILITY
All synthetic results are averaged over 11 seeds with the benchmark redrawn per seed, and the sam-
ple standard deviation is reported throughout. Splits of the synthetic benchmark follow base scenes,
which keeps the variants of a scene inside one partition. The learned failure classifier is reported
both with the checkpoint alone held out and with the held-out checkpoint’s scenes removed from
training as well (Appendix C.8). The protocols on real data, including their thresholds, the ring
width, the admissibility rule and the acceptance rule of the rebuilt reference, were written down
before the runs they govern, and analyses added after a result are labeled as such in the text. Every
depth checkpoint is used exactly as released, and nothing is trained or tuned on any evaluation set.
The output convention of each checkpoint is verified against reference stereo disparity before use,
because the attribute conventionally named predicted depth carries metric depth in some fam-
ilies and inverse depth in others, and invalid pixels of a prediction stay invalid rather than becoming
zero. One checkpoint, UniDepth-v2, runs without its optimized attention kernel on the available
hardware, and its numbers form a lower bound on the released model. Code will be released upon
acceptance.
E.4
USE OF GENERATIVE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
We developed the research problem, conceptual framework, hypothesis family, methodological ar-
chitecture, theoretical results, and benchmark design independently, before using any generative
artificial intelligence tool. Such tools then served only as a general-purpose assistant under our
direct supervision, for implementation and routine programming, plotting scripts, supplementary
controls, dataset cleaning, and refinement of text that we had already drafted. We reviewed every
assisted output before inclusion, verified all citations against the original sources, and obtained every
reported number from experiments that we ran with the released code.
