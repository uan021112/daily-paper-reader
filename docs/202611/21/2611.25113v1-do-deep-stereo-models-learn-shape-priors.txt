Abstract
Localizing object boundaries is important for embodied visual systems, since small
boundary errors can change the inferred geometry of physical surfaces. We study
whether deep stereo models produce boundaries consistent with simple shape
priors when local image and disparity cues are weak. We create a synthetic stereo
dataset consisting of objects drawn from a highly constrained family of shapes,
with randomized depth, slant, color and texture. Despite the simplicity of this
shape space, we find that several state-of-the-art stereo models retain systematic
boundary errors, even when finetuned or trained from scratch on the dataset. We
then introduce a simple generative stereo model that jointly predicts disparity and
a distance field representing object boundaries. The model improves boundary
localization and produces contours that adhere more closely to the underlying shape
space. These results expose limitations in the boundary predictions of current stereo
architectures when local evidence is weak, and motivate further study of whether
generative models can better combine regional grouping evidence with global
geometric structure.
1
Introduction
Localizing object boundaries is important for embodied visual systems that must interact with the
physical geometry of their surroundings. Errors near object boundaries can have consequences that
are disproportionate to their contribution to average error, because a small displacement changes
where one surface ends and another begins. In order to reliably localize boundaries, a vision system
must learn to group regions of uniform color, texture and disparity; and it must learn to combine
these with shape priors that enable completing boundaries that are ambiguous, such as in cases of
partial camouflage.
Deep stereo models are attractive for this because they promise to achieve it from being trained
end-to-end with a simple disparity loss. Given enough training data, these models ought to be able to
learn to group within-object regions and to localize crisp disparity boundaries between objects with
very high precision.
In this paper, we devise a controlled test of this ability using synthetic stereo images with heavily
constrained shapes (regular polygons) rendered using randomized depths, slant angles, colors and
textures. Because the space of possible shapes is so constrained, we expect high-capacity stereo
models trained on this distribution to be able to internalize its shape statistics sufficiently well to
precisely complete crisp boundaries, even when color, texture and disparity cues are weak or partially
missing. Instead, we find that a variety of state-of-the-art deep stereo models produce distorted
boundaries, even when finetuned or re-trained from scratch on the dataset, with errors concentrating
where local evidence is weak (Figs. 1 and 4). These errors are consistent with the models failing to
fully exploit the available shape regularities, although boundary accuracy alone cannot distinguish
this explanation from improved local contour prediction.
This motivates us to ask whether we can improve boundary recovery in these settings using a
generative formulation, where the model learns a distribution over plausible scene geometry instead

The architecture is deliberately simple, but it measurably improves boundary accuracy and produces
recovered contours that adhere more closely to the constrained shape space of our dataset.
The generative formulation also provides a natural representation of uncertainty when the scene

into an invalid averaged contour.
Together, our results suggest that more work needs to be done to achieve accurate physical perception,
and that generative models of scene geometry are worth considering as a means of representing both
local boundary evidence and global geometric structure.

Related work
Stereo and boundaries. Modern deep stereo models typically combine explicit correspondence
representations with learned spatial regularization and iterative refinement, often augmented by
monocular grouping priors and long-range spatial reasoning [Lipson et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2024,
Cheng et al., 2025, Jiang et al., 2025, Wen et al., 2025]. They have achieved increasingly strong
disparity accuracy and substantial cross-domain generalization. Although these models are generally
trained to predict disparity rather than explicitly represent object boundaries or shape, their learned
representations should in principle capture statistical regularities in scene geometry, including smooth
disparity within objects and abrupt changes at their boundaries. Our work asks how effectively they
exploit these regularities when the space of possible object shapes is deliberately made simple and
highly constrained.
Depth boundaries pose a particular challenge for stereo because the local evidence that identifies them
can be weak or missing. A model can achieve low average disparity error while shifting, rounding,
or blurring the boundaries that determine the recovered scene geometry. Prior work has addressed
this through explicit reasoning about occlusions and their boundaries, specialized cost aggregation
for textureless regions and disparity discontinuities, and losses designed to better preserve boundary
structure [Wang and Zickler, 2019, Liu et al., 2024, Xu and Zhang, 2020, Xu et al., 2024]. Our focus
is complementary: we ask how accurately learned stereo models localize and complete boundaries in
a setting where global shape regularities could compensate for weak local evidence.
Shape priors and perceptual organization. The ability to infer object shape from incomplete or
ambiguous local evidence has a long history in both human and computer vision. Humans readily

group spatially separated edge elements into coherent contours and complete object boundaries across
occlusion or camouflage, suggesting that local appearance cues are combined with more global
constraints on shape and contour organization [Palmer, 1999]. Deep visual models, however, do not
necessarily acquire the same biases from standard training objectives. Image classification networks
have been shown to rely disproportionately on local texture rather than global shape [Geirhos et al.,
2018, Baker et al., 2018], and more recent work on contour integration suggests that human-like use
of global contour structure emerges only in very large vision models [Lonnqvist et al., 2025]. Our
work asks whether similar limitations arise in stereo, where global shape is not itself the prediction
target but could, in principle, help resolve uncertain local evidence.
Generative models for geometric prediction. Generative models provide an alternative to determin-
istic regression by learning a distribution over plausible outputs conditioned on visual input. Diffusion
models have been applied to monocular depth, optical flow, and dense correspondence, demonstrating
both competitive geometric estimation and the ability to represent uncertainty or multiple plausible
solutions [Saxena et al., 2023, Nam et al., 2024, Ke et al., 2024, Zheng et al., 2025]. More broadly,
recent work suggests that generative image models can provide useful priors for downstream visual
understanding [Tang et al., 2023, Gabeur et al., 2026]. Our work explores these ideas for stereo, using
diffusion not primarily to improve average disparity accuracy, but to learn a distribution over plausible
scene shapes and to represent distinct interpretations when boundary geometry is ambiguous.

Task and dataset
Given a rectified stereo pair IL, IR ∈R3×H×W , we aim to estimate dense disparity d ∈RH×W
on the left-image grid. A scene point visible in both views at left coordinate (x, y) appears at
(x −d(x, y), y) in the right view, and larger disparity indicates smaller depth. Rectification restricts
the correspondence search to within each horizontal scanline.
Our focus is boundary localization and shape recovery when local evidence is weak. Weaknesses
occur when neighboring surfaces have similar color or texture, so are partially camouflaged; when
textureless regions provide little evidence for identifying left-right correspondences; and when
background pixels near occluding contours are visible in only one view, with no valid left-right match.
Our aim is to assess boundary recovery in conditions where local evidence is weak and learned global
shape regularities could be useful.
To create a controlled setting in which such regularities are simple and highly learnable, we construct
a procedural dataset of stereo images and true disparity maps using a heavily constrained set of object
shapes: regular planar polygons with degree up to eight. We randomize the objects’ depths, slants,
colors and textures as depicted in Fig. 2. This approach allows exact rasterization of images and
disparity near boundaries, avoiding the errors of sensor-derived data.
3


Each scene contains one or more objects drawn from the shape library. Objects appear with uniform
colors, procedural patterns, or textures from the Describable Textures Dataset [Cimpoi et al., 2014].
Backgrounds have zero disparity and are also textured or uniformly colored. As shown in Fig. 2,
some scenes are more challenging than others, depending on the distinctiveness of the objects’
appearance, their 3D slants, and the size of the depth separation between them. In the most extreme
case (bottom right), there are no local appearance or disparity cues for discerning large portions of
the objects’ boundaries, so in order to succeed in identifying the true boundaries everywhere, a model
must rely on nonlocal information about contour continuation and object shape to perform figural
scission [Kellman and Shipley, 1991], which is known to be ambiguous. (We say more about this in
Section 5.3.)
Associated with each stereo pair, the dataset includes ground truth disparity maps, visible segmentation
masks, amodal segmentation masks [Li and Malik, 2016, Zhu et al., 2017], and boundary maps
represented as unsigned distance fields. (For target continuous boundary points B ⊂R2 and discrete
image grid Ω⊂Z2 the rasterized unsigned distance field is u(p) = minq∈B∥p −q∥2 for p ∈Ω.)
The dataset includes all of these defined on both a cyclopean-image grid and the left-image grid; we
only use the left-image grids in this paper. The dataset generation code will be released alongside the
full training and inference pipeline at https://github.com/LINBOTANG123/HDIT-Stereo.
4
A simple stereo diffusion model
We contrast deterministic stereo models that produce per-pixel disparity estimates with a generative
framework that models a conditional distribution over scene geometry from which we can sample.
We adapt the HDiT diffusion model [Crowson et al., 2024] to stereo and introduce HDiT-Stereo, a
generative model for stereo disparity. Fig. 3 shows the pipeline.
Figure 3: Overview of HDiT-Stereo. Per-view image features and correspondence features from a
trainable photometric encoder and a frozen DINOv2 semantic encoder condition an HDiT denoiser.
From noise the model jointly generates disparity and an unsigned boundary distance field through
iterative denoising. Flame and snowflake symbols indicate trainable and frozen components.
HDiT-Stereo models the conditional distribution pθ(z | c(IL, IR)) over a scene representation z,
where c(IL, IR) denotes the conditioning features computed from the stereo pair. As shown in Fig. 3,
conditioning features include per-view image features and explicit correspondence features to capture
local matching evidence in addition to broader visual context. The photometric and semantic encoders
each include feature extraction and left–right correlation over a shared set of candidate disparities.
The photometric encoder uses a small CNN trained jointly with the model, while the semantic encoder
uses a frozen DINOv2 ViT [Oquab et al., 2024] to provide complementary contextual features.
We obtain a prediction by sampling z ∼pθ(z | c(IL, IR)). HDiT-Stereo jointly generates two
normalized fields on the left-image grid, z = ( ˜d, ˜u): disparity ˜d and the unsigned distance ˜u to
the nearest object boundary. Disparity represents surface geometry but encodes boundaries only
implicitly as discontinuities. In contrast, ˜u represents boundary geometry explicitly through its zero
level set, providing a direct target for learning contour location and shape.
When stereo evidence strongly constrains the geometry, the conditional distribution should concentrate
around a single interpretation. When the evidence is ambiguous such as the identical-appearance,
equal-disparity configurations illustrated in the bottom-right of Fig. 2, it can instead represent distinct
alternatives (Sec. 5.3). Variation across samples also enables uncertainty estimates (Sec. 5.2).
4


Training follows the EDM formulation [Karras et al., 2022]. We corrupt the target fields with Gaussian
noise at a randomly sampled scale σ and train the network to recover the clean fields. Per-view
image features and correspondence features are added to the HDiT tokens. At inference, we generate
disparity and boundary fields by iteratively denoising Gaussian noise conditioned on the stereo pair.

We compare against RAFT-Stereo [Lipson et al., 2021], Selective-IGEV [Wang et al., 2024], Mon-
Ster [Cheng et al., 2025], and DEFOM-Stereo [Jiang et al., 2025]. Each baseline is evaluated in
three distinct training regimes: zero-shot using the authors’ released weights from training on Scene-
Flow [Mayer et al., 2016]; fine-tuned weight using our training set; and trained from scratch using our
dataset. We also evaluate FoundationStereo [Wen et al., 2025] using its released pretrained weights to
test its zero-shot generalization to our dataset, since it is designed to transfer across domains without
task-specific fine-tuning.

To quantitatively evaluate our generative stereo model, we draw ten samples per scene and report two
statistics: the mean of each metric averaged across all samples, and the oracle best-1-of-10, which
selects the sample with lowest error using ground truth and is therefore unavailable at inference time.
We include the latter only to characterize the diversity of the learned distribution, and all practical
comparisons use the mean.
In addition to our full model in Fig. 3, we report a Base variant in Table 1 that is trained only with the
disparity objective and removes both the Shared Photometric and Shared Semantic encoders. This
5


isolates the contribution of the UDF prediction head and shared encoder conditioning beyond the
underlying generative HDiT. Additional ablations are reported in Appendix A2, with implementation
details in Appendix A1.
We use metrics that emphasize contour accuracy: Boundary EPE is mean absolute disparity error
within a 2-pixel band around ground-truth contours; Chamfer [Borgefors, 1988] is standard symmetric
mean nearest-neighbor contour distance; and Hausdorff-99 [Huttenlocher et al., 1993] is a variant of
Hausdorff distance that uses the 99th percentile of pooled bidirectional distances to reduce outlier
sensitivity. Alongside these boundary metrics, we also report standard global measures of EPE and
Bad-X, the percentage of pixels with disparity error above X pixels [Middlebury Stereo Evaluation].
The complete results are shown in Table 1. We find that our model is the most accurate on every
boundary metric. The improvement is not entirely attributed to an overall increase in model capacity
because our 23.4M trainable parameters closely match MonSter’s 23.0M. We find that the baselines’
zero-shot metrics are an order of magnitude worse than their performance in other training regimes,
suggesting relatively poor zero-shot transfer from SceneFlow to our dataset. We note that our model’s
improvement near boundaries does not come at the cost of degraded overall disparity accuracy. Our
model’s Bad-1 and Bad-2 scores also indicate that its residual error is concentrated in fewer pixels.

Recovered shape fidelity
Figure 4: Recovered foreground shapes. For each scene, the upper row shows disparity maps with
ground truth on the left; the lower row shows recovered foreground shapes overlaid on ground truth.
Dark regions indicate agreement, orange regions indicate disagreement, and solid black lines mark
ground-truth contours. Our model achieves the highest shape IoU.
Boundary error measures where a contour sits, but it does not measure which object a boundary
belongs to (i.e., figure-ground) or how closely it aligns with each object’s global shape. We evaluate
how closely each model’s recovered contours agree with the known object shapes using a test set of
two-object scenes. Given a model’s predicted disparity map, we segment the object that is nearest
(and therefore unoccluded) and evaluate its shape in two ways. First, we measure its agreement
(1−IoU) with the true object shape as projected into the left image. Second, we find the optimal
affine-plus-translation transformation that aligns it to the closest unprojected library shape and
compare its pixelwise distances to the boundary with those of the ideal polygon of its ground-truth
class. Their Euclidean difference defines distance to ideal.

5.2
Uncertainty
Since our model is generative, we can use multiple samples to produce uncertainty estimates, taking
the per-pixel standard deviation of disparity across 10 samples as uncertainty and the per-pixel
median as the point prediction, on a random 100-scene subset of the test set. We evaluate whether the
per-pixel uncertainty correlates with true disparity error at boundary and interior pixels (Fig. 5, left)
and whether it supports effective sparsification by discarding the least-confident pixels and measuring
the remaining error (Fig. 5, right).
We find that our model’s uncertainty is 5× higher at boundaries than in the interior (0.161 vs.
0.029 px). It correlates with true error (boundary r=0.63, interior r=0.55) and so concentrates in
locations that are hard to resolve rather than reflecting arbitrary sampling noise. The right of Fig. 5
shows that sparsification using the pixel-wise ranking by uncertainty closely tracks that using an
oracle-error ranking, with the most confident half of pixels cutting mean disparity error from 0.146
to 0.008 px against 0.005 px for the oracle. Thus, much of the pruning-based accuracy gains can be
obtained from the model’s own uncertainty, without access to ground truth.

sample standard deviation, binned separately for boundary and interior pixels across all test scenes.
Higher uncertainty indicates larger errors. Right: Mean error among pixels retained by predicted
uncertainty (orange) or ground-truth error (gray). Removing uncertain pixels substantially reduces
error without requiring ground truth for selection. Computed on a 100-scene test subset, and the

5.3
Figural scission, global shape, and bimodality
The bottom-right example in Fig. 2 shows a challenging case where neither the objects’ appearance
nor their disparity separation can be used to separate their overlap. Unlike the contour-fidelity
experiments in Sec. 5.1, the local disparity and appearance evidence is entirely insufficient to
determine the boundary assignment in these cases, and successful grouping relies critically on
nonlocal information about contour continuation and object shape.
The figure-ground ordering of the objects is ambiguous in these cases, and humans sometimes
perceive them as being bi-stable, with the perceived depth-order flipping spontaneously during
7


sustained viewing [Kellman and Shipley, 1991, Palmer, 1999]. Since our model is generative and
produces boundary maps (unsigned distance fields) in addition to disparity, it can mimic this ability
by learning to produce a bimodal output distribution over boundary maps. Our model does not always
achieve this, but Fig. 6 shows a successful example. We draw 100 samples (four shown in figure left)
using different noise seeds and embed the predicted distance fields in two dimensions using t-SNE.
The disparity samples are all the same as expected, and the boundary samples form two modes that
are concentrated near the two true unsigned distance fields. Appendix A3 provides a quantitative
analysis of how often our model successfully recovers both figure-ground interpretations in these
types of scenes.
Representing both alternatives naturally requires a multimodal output representation, and sampling
from our generative model provides one way to achieve this. A model that emits one answer per pixel
would need to choose only one of the boundary maps or an invalid average of the two; and a model
that only predicts disparity cannot mimic this effect.

Four sampled disparity and boundary predictions. Disparity remains consistent while the boundary
predictions capture both valid figure-ground interpretations. Right: t-SNE embedding of 100
boundary samples, forming two clusters near the two valid contour maps (orange and green stars).
Grey bands indicate the predicted zero-level boundary contours.
6
Discussion and limitations
Our controlled experiments show that strong stereo accuracy does not guarantee precise recovery
of simple object boundaries. Boundary distortions persist in state-of-the-art disparity models even
after in-domain training. HDiT-Stereo improves boundary localization and recovered shape fidelity
on this constrained distribution, and its generative boundary representation captures uncertainty and
alternative figure–ground interpretations.
These results motivate generative scene modeling as one approach to improving geometric coherence
in stereo, but they do not isolate whether the observed disparity improvements arise from stronger
global shape reasoning, explicit boundary supervision, or improved local contour prediction. Adding
explicit boundary supervision to existing stereo architectures would provide an especially useful
comparison.
Our experiments deliberately use a narrow synthetic distribution of regular polygons so that geo-
metric regularities are simple and precisely controlled, and all test scenes are drawn from the same
distribution as training. The results therefore establish differences in boundary recovery within this
distribution but do not show that they extend to richer object shapes or real-world stereo scenes.
Testing more complex and out-of-distribution geometry is an important direction for future work.
HDiT-Stereo also requires boundary-field supervision during training, and repeated sampling incurs
additional inference cost.

Appendix
A1
Implementation details
A1.1
HDiT-Stereo
Architecture.
The denoiser is an HDiT with 2 × 2 patches and three levels of depth [2, 2, 4] and
width [128, 256, 512], using shifted-window attention at the first two levels and global attention at
the third. Targets use a fixed 64 px normalization scale. The correspondence module searches a 64 px
disparity range and uses a frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14 for semantic features. Its soft-argmax disparity
receives an auxiliary loss with weight 0.2.
Encoders.
The denoiser is conditioned on the stereo pair through three encoders. The Per View
Encoder processes the left and right views independently with two 3 × 3 convolutional layers (128
channels, ReLU), followed by global average pooling. The two resulting 128-dimensional vectors
are concatenated, linearly projected, and added to the noise-level embedding to provide global
conditioning to every transformer block.
The Shared Photometric Encoder is a three-layer CNN (3 × 3 convolutions with 32, 64 and 64
channels, ReLU, total stride 2) applied with the same weights to both views. The two sets of L2-
normalized features are correlated between views to form a left-view cost volume over disparities
from 0 to 64 pixels in 2-pixel increments.
The Shared Semantic Encoder extracts frozen DINOv2 ViT-S/14 features from both views, adapts
them with a learnable projection, and correlates them between views to form a second, coarser cost
volume over the same disparity range. The two cost volumes are concatenated and aggregated into a
per-location disparity, which conditions the denoiser through a zero-initialized gate.
Training and Sampling
We train for 24 hours on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs with batch size 16
per device and bf16 precision. We use the EDM loss with soft-min-SNR weighting, σ ∈[0.01, 160],
AdamW with a constant learning rate of 5 × 10−4, and an inverse-power EMA.
We sample using EMA weights and DPM++(2M) SDE over 50 steps with a Karras noise sched-
ule [Karras et al., 2022]. We draw 10 samples per scene for quantitative evaluation.
A1.2
Other models
We use RAFT-Stereo, Selective-IGEV, and DEFOM-Stereo under the MIT license, and MonSter
and FoundationStereo in accordance with their non-commercial research restrictions. We train each
model for 24 hours on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs with batch size 16 per device and bf16 precision.
The total compute time for training all of these models is 24 GPU days. Including preliminary
work in setting up our training dataset and hyperparameter tuning HDiT-Stereo, the project required
approximately 50 GPU days.
A1.3
Inference Computational Cost
Table 3 compares the inference cost of HDiT-Stereo with the regression-based stereo baselines.
Because HDiT-Stereo performs iterative diffusion sampling with 50 denoising steps, it requires
substantially more computation at inference time than single-forward stereo networks. The Full
model requires approximately 3.18 TFLOPs per sample and 0.318 s per image in our evaluation
setting, compared with roughly 0.02–0.03 s for the regression baselines.
This increased inference cost is an important limitation of our approach. Our goal is not to improve
inference efficiency, but to study whether generative stereo models can better preserve ambiguous
boundary structure and represent multiple plausible solutions. The additional cost reflects the tradeoff
of iterative generative inference. Reducing the number of sampling steps, distilling the diffusion
model, or adopting faster generative samplers could improve efficiency in future work.

A3
Bimodality for ambiguous two-object scenes
We complement Sec. 5.3 and the successful example in Fig. 6 by measuring how often HDiT-Stereo
captures both plausible figure-ground interpretations for ambiguous two-object scenes. For each
ambiguous two-object scene, we draw 50 samples from our model and measure whether both
12


interpretations appear at least once. We quantify the amount of occlusion with the overlap fraction
h =
|M1 ∩M2|
min(|M1|, |M2|),
where M1 and M2 are the areas of the two objects in the absence of occlusions. This is the fraction
of the smaller object’s area that overlaps the other object, with h = 0 when the objects do not touch
and h = 1 when the smaller object lies entirely within the other. We use h instead of IoU because
IoU also depends on the objects’ size ratio: a small object lying entirely within a larger one has an
IoU no higher than their area ratio, even though it is fully covered.
Table 5 reports the percentage of scenes in which both figure-ground interpretations appear, grouped
by the overlap fraction h and two values of disparity offset ∆d between the two objects. Bimodality
occurs at best half of the time, and it trends lower for greater amounts of occlusion. When there is a
small non-zero disparity offset (∆d = 4) bimodality is reduced but not eliminated, reflecting that
disparity cues remain weak due to the uniform colors of the objects.

A5
Broader Impact
This work specifically focuses on evaluating various models on boundaries in synthetic scenes. While
we do not expect this specific application could have negative societal impacts, improvements to
scene recognition in general could have negative societal effects. In addition, HDiT-Stereo uncertainty
predictions should not be used in safety-critical applications, as they are population estimates rather
than analytical uncertainty values.
