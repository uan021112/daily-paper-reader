Abstract
Feed-forward models have been very successful in 3D re-
construction with ad hoc attention designs that relate all
view patches densely. However, the cost of this dense global
attention grows quadratically with the number of views,
preventing the model from applying to larger-scale recon-
struction settings. In this paper, we argue that most dense
attention computations are unnecessary from a classical
structure-from-motion perspective: 3D structures stem from
geometrically matching regions in 2D. We bring this prin-
ciple to learning-based feed-forward reconstruction with
our Sparse Geometric Attention Reconstruction (SGAR)
method. In SGAR, each patch token attends to only a small
set of patch tokens from other views, chosen by a lightweight

learned key selection module trained to predict cross-view
3D proximity of tokens. Thus, dense global attention is re-
placed by much more efficient geometric-similarity-guided
sparse attention. SGAR is a general method that can be ap-
plied to different feed-forward models by fine-tuning. In our
experiments, we show that SGAR is the fastest among both
state-of-the-art dense and sparse methods and the most ac-
curate sparse one. It runs up to 12× faster than dense atten-
tion at 1,000 views with stable pose accuracy where exist-
ing sparse methods fail, and shows the best performance in
reconstructing more complete geometries on the same time
budget.

1. Introduction
Multi-view scene reconstruction is a long-standing problem
in computer vision. Classical Structure-from-Motion (SfM)
solutions treat this as a geometric estimation problem and
solve it with a multi-stage pipeline built on projective ge-
ometry and robust optimization. Although effective, SfM
methods are based on complex pipelines and produce only
sparse reconstructions [34, 36–38, 46, 53]. Recent feed-
forward models such as VGGT [60] and DejaView [8] re-
place it by a single network that predicts cameras and ge-
ometry for all views in a single forward pass, matching
or even surpassing the earlier methods in a fraction of the
time when the views overlap substantially. At the core of
these methods is global attention: The tokens of all views
are processed jointly, so that every view is interpreted in
the context of every other. This is what makes these ap-
proaches so powerful, but also what makes them expensive
as the cost of global attention grows quadratically with the
total number of views. For example, in DejaView [8], the
time cost grows from a few seconds in 100 views to more
than 300 s in 1,000 views on one Nvidia A100 GPU. This
severely restricts the applicability of feed-forward models
to time-sensitive applications, such as robot navigation, and
to providing a fast initialization for optimization-based re-
construction pipelines.
However, much of this computation is redundant. As
shown in Fig. 2, global attention is highly sparse even in
densely trained models: When each query of DejaView
attends only to a small fraction of the oracle keys deter-
mined based on ground truth geometry, the model retains
most of the pose accuracy without any retraining. Many
recent works leverage this observation to accelerate VGGT-
style models, mostly without retraining. The strategies in-
clude merging similar tokens [11, 48], compressing each
view into a few descriptors [66], test-time training [20, 23],
or estimating the attention map at a coarse resolution and
keeping only its strongest blocks [16, 41, 52, 58]. These
methods infer the relevant keys from an approximation of
the attention itself, rather than from the geometric relations
between views on which reconstruction depends. As a con-
sequence, their accuracy degrades significantly compared
to dense methods, or they run out of memory on long se-
quences.
In this work, we revisit this problem from the perspective
of classical Structure-from-Motion, in which views are re-
lated through sparse 2D correspondences. We hypothesize
that, apart from a few tokens that anchor the global coor-
dinate frame, the keys a patch token requires are the tokens
that observe the same 3D surface region in other views. Key
selection then becomes a geometric-feature retrieval prob-
lem, which can be supervised directly with ground-truth ge-
ometry and solved without computing dense attention.
Building on this insight, we propose SGAR, which re-
places the dense global attention of feed-forward recon-
struction models with geometry-guided sparse attention
(Fig. 1). A lightweight projection, trained with a contrastive
loss on ground-truth geometry, maps patch tokens into an
embedding space in which tokens observing the same sur-
face lie close together. At inference, these embeddings are
clustered, and each patch token attends only to the patches
of its nearest clusters, together with a small set of tokens
that provide global context. Since the number of keys per
token grows much more slowly than the total number of
tokens, the cost of global attention becomes sub-quadratic
in the number of views. SGAR can be applied to different
dense attention models such as DejaView [8] and VGGT-
Ω[61] by fine-tuning pretrained weights and requires no
ground-truth geometry at inference.
Experiments on six datasets show that SGAR is the
fastest among state-of-the-art dense and sparse methods,
running up to 12× faster than dense models at 1,000 views
and keeping accuracy comparable to dense models in all
scenarios. Because of its high efficiency, we also show that
SGAR can handle many more images and cover much more
extended scenes for a given computational budget.
In summary, our contributions are as follows.
• We show that global attention in feed-forward reconstruc-
tion is highly sparse, and that the keys a query token re-
quires can be predicted by their geometric similarities.
• We propose SGAR, a geometry-guided sparse attention
mechanism whose keys are selected by a contrastively
learned embedding, obtained by fine-tuning a pretrained
dense model.
• We demonstrate that SGAR achieves the best trade-off
between efficiency and accuracy, scaling to 1,000 views
on a single GPU, and reconstructing the most complete
geometries at the same given time budget.
Code and models will be released.

2. Related Work
Feed-forward
3D
reconstruction.
Structure-from-
Motion pipelines [34, 36, 38, 46, 53] estimate poses and
structure in stages, from keypoint matching to bundle ad-
justment, and learned matchers [30, 44, 51] have made each
stage more robust. DUSt3R [63] and MASt3R [27] showed
that a network can instead predict aligned pointmaps
directly, but they operate on two images at a time and need
an optimization to merge the pairwise outputs. VGGT [60]
and the models that followed it [29, 61, 65, 70] read an
arbitrary number of images at once, interleaving attention
within each image and across all of them, and output
cameras,
depth and points without any optimization.
DejaView [8] reaches comparable accuracy with far fewer
parameters by applying one shared block recurrently.
Streaming models [59, 62] take a different route and ingest
the images one by one, updating a persistent memory. For
the models that process all views jointly, the cost of dense
global attention grows quadratically with the number of
views. However, we observe that dense attention is highly
redundant (Fig. 2), and SGAR therefore restricts attention
to sparse, geometrically similar tokens.
Sparse attention.
A growing body of work accelerates
VGGT-style models, most of it without retraining. Token
merging methods, following token merging for vision trans-
formers [7], reduce the tokens that enter global attention,
merging near-duplicate patches [48] or low-confidence ones
ranked by a distilled predictor [11]. FlashVGGT [66] com-
presses each view into a few descriptor tokens and turns
global attention into cross-attention to them. VGG-T3 [20]
and ZipMap [23] distill the key-value set into a fixed-size
network by test-time training. AVGGT [52] turns the early
global layers into frame attention and subsamples the keys
of the remaining ones on the patch grid, and Lite3R [74]
distills a sparse linear attention into the frozen backbone.
Block-Sparse [58] pools the keys into blocks and reads only
those that hold the attention mass, SAF3R [16] fits a sparse
pattern to each head, and Speed3R [41] pairs a compressed
branch with a sparse one that attends to the blocks it selects.
Most of these methods approximate dense attention at the
granularity of blocks or groups of tokens at the risk of over-
simplification. SGAR instead selects the exact keys that are
meaningful for every token individually from a geometric
point of view.
Restricting attention to a subset of keys is a long-
standing remedy in the broader literature too. Early meth-
ods fix the pattern in advance, with local windows and
strided or global tokens [5, 12, 31, 72], while content-based
methods group queries and keys by hashing [25] or clus-
tering [43], or retrieve the nearest keys from a large mem-
ory [68]. We investigate the problem in a different context
of geometric reconstruction and learn key selection by train-
ing geometric representations.
Learned geometric embeddings.
Finding correspon-
dences between images rests on learning geometric em-
beddings of points whose similarity reflects whether two
locations observe the same 3D point. Learned geometric
embeddings are trained with metric or contrastive objec-
tives on ground-truth correspondences [13]. Modern match-
ers reason over both images with attention, on sparse key-
points [30, 44, 55] or densely [19, 51].
MASt3R [27]
brings matching into feed-forward reconstruction with a
head trained by an InfoNCE loss [35], whose descriptors
are matched after the forward pass. Supervised contrastive
learning [24] extends InfoNCE to several positives per an-
chor, as when a patch overlaps several patches of another
view. SGAR uses the same idea as a supervision signal dur-
ing training: a multi-positive InfoNCE loss pulls together
the embeddings of corresponding patches and pushes away
all other patches. However, our learning objective is not
to find point correspondences, but to learn patch-level geo-
metric embeddings that are never matched explicitly. The
geometric similarities will be used to sparsify global atten-
tion with reconstruction as the final objective.
3. Method
We introduce SGAR, a sparse-attention reconstruction
scheme in which attention across views is restricted to
patches that correspond to the same 3D neighborhood. This
makes its cost sub-quadratic in the number of views as op-
posed to quadratic in dense attention. It rests on two things:
a learned key selection that clusters the tokens from differ-
ent views likely to be in 3D proximity, and a sparse global
attention mechanism in which every patch token attends in
full only to the patches of its own clusters and the 3 nearest
clusters, as well as to the rest of the scene through a few
global tokens and cluster summaries. SGAR can be readily
applied to any dense attention models such as DejaView [8]
and VGGT-Ω[61], by replacing its global attention with
our sparse ones and fine-tuning. This yields the complete
architecture of Fig. 3, where DejaView is used as an ex-
ample base architecture. We define the key selection pro-
cess in Sec. 3.1, describe our sparse attention mechanism in
Sec. 3.2, and present our training procedure in Sec. 3.3.

3.1. Learning key selection
Key selection aims to identify the relevant patches that will
restrict attention in Sec. 3.2. We first compute geometric
embeddings for every patch token with a learned projection
Wadj, where Wadjxi and Wadjxj are close in feature space
if and only if xi and xj belong to the same neighborhood
in 3D. We then consider these embeddings across all views,
and cluster them in feature space. By design, the resulting
clusters correspond to close 3D regions and will be used to
restrict attention computation in Sec. 3.2.

3.3. Training
We train SGAR by fine-tuning from a pretrained dense at-
tention model (e.g. DejaView, VGGT-Ω). The adjacency
projection and the temperature τ of Lcon are the only new
parameters. The model is trained with the following loss:
L = Lrecon + λconLcon,
(5)
where Lrecon are the dense model’s point-map, depth, ray
and camera losses on the ground truth, expressed in the
camera frame of the reference view after scale normalisa-
tion. The second term Lcon is the contrastive loss (2), with
λcon = 1.
Training Details.
We train on a mixture of 18 pub-
lic datasets that covers indoor and outdoor scenes, driv-
ing, aerial and object-centric captures, both real and syn-
thetic: ScanNet [14], Hypersim [42], Replica [50], Taskon-
omy [73], CA-1M [26], Aria Synthetic Environments [2],
MegaDepth [28], BlendedMVS [71], CO3D [40], ren-
derings of TRELLIS 3D assets [69], Mapillary Metropo-
lis [32], Virtual KITTI 2 [9], TartanAir and its V2
release [64],
MVS-Synth [22],
UnrealStereo4K [54],
Spring [33] and Kubric-4D [21, 57]. A sample of V views
is drawn only from scenes with at least V frames, without
repeated frames. In training runs, the view samples hold 2
to 32 views. The aspect ratio of images is drawn per batch
between 1:3 and 1:1, and receives color, blur, and zoom
augmentation. Since the dense model’s representation is to
be fine-tuned rather than relearned, the pretrained weights
are trained at a peak learning rate of 10−5 and the new pa-
rameters at 10−4, with AdamW. We train on 4 H100 GPUs
in bfloat16, with gradient checkpointing of every layer, and
evaluate the checkpoint after 50k steps.

5. Conclusion and Discussion
In this paper, we presented SGAR, a general method that
replaces the dense global attention of feed-forward recon-
struction models with geometry-guided sparse attention. A
learned projection embeds each patch such that patches ob-
8


serving the same surface lie close together, and our sparse
geometric attention then selects a small subset of relevant
keys for each query by clustering in this embedding space.
We applied SGAR to two state-of-the-art dense-attention
models and showed that it is the fastest model at every
sequence length, up to 12× faster than dense attention at
1,000 views, and the most accurate of the efficient alterna-
tives, with accuracy comparable to the original dense mod-
els. Despite the good performance, SGAR also comes with
limitations: sparsification still brings a small drop in accu-
racy relative to dense attention. Closing this gap is a promis-
ing direction for future work.
