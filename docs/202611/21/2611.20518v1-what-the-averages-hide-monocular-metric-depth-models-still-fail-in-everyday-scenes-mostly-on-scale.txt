What the Averages Hide: Monocular Metric Depth Models
Still Fail in Everyday Scenes, Mostly on Scale
Hualie Jiang1, Yujie Luo1,2,∗, Ziyang Song1, Ximeng Li1, Zhiqiang Lou1, Rui Xu1,†
1Antigravity Tech, Insta360
2Nanyang Technological University
Project page: antigravity-tech.github.io/DIMED
Abstract
Zero-shot monocular metric depth models are evaluated by computing metric and scale-
aligned metrics on each dataset and averaging them across datasets. Averages alone cannot
show how many images still have severely wrong metric depth. The gap between the two
scores shows how much error comes from scale, but not whether depth is underestimated
or overestimated, or how consistently; and without a per-image judgement of failure, they
cannot compare whether different models fail on the same images. We propose an evaluation
built on per-image results: the failure rate (metric δ1 < 0.5) as the criterion for whether the
task is solved; scale-aligned metrics for structure, and the bias, dispersion and absolute error
of the alignment scale factor for scale; and a cross-model comparison on the same images
that separates shared from model-specific failures. We apply it to 11 recent architectures
with and without ground-truth intrinsics (18 model evaluations) on the 7 everyday-scene
benchmarks (4,406 images) used by MoGe-2. Unless stated otherwise, rates are averaged over
the 7 datasets with equal weight. Metric depth estimation is not solved in everyday scenes,
and the remaining difficulty is mainly scale: the best of the 18 evaluations still has a failure
rate of 7.7%; 78.8% of failures have intact structure and are wrong only in scale, and after
an ideal scale correction from the ground truth 98.3% no longer fail (pooled over all failures
of the 18 evaluations). More than a third of the error of the top 6 models falls on images
that most models fail: with intrinsics, images failed by at least 6 architectures make up 7.3%
of the data, concentrated on HAMMER (4 of its 7 scenes) and DIODE; even when selected
only by architectures of other families, such images hold 34% to 40% of these models’ 1 −δ1
error, against 14% to 18% for as many random images from the same datasets. The scale
error varies with image content (in HAMMER pairs, adding objects reduces the scale error
of most models); ground-truth intrinsics mainly affect the scale, and in most families larger
models reduce both structure and scale errors, but the failures remain far from removed.
We recommend reporting failure rates, structure and scale separately, and results stratified
by how many models fail. We will release the evaluation code, the per-image results with
their shared-failure labels, and two diagnostic sets built from TUM RGB-D for studying how
image cues relate to the scale error.

Introduction
Recent zero-shot monocular metric depth models have advanced quickly (e.g. Hu et al., 2024; Bochkovskii
et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Piccinelli et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026): they are trained on large mixtures
of data, are not tuned to the test sets, and report high mean accuracy on several benchmarks. Their papers
also show qualitative results, many of them marking object sizes or point-to-point distances on the predicted
point cloud next to the ground truth (Yin et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2026;
Liu et al., 2026a); their limitation sections discuss fine structures, translucent surfaces, camera models and
similar issues, and when the ambiguity of scale is mentioned, it is usually attributed to out-of-distribution

scenes (e.g. Wang et al., 2025b). These models aim to generalise zero-shot to any image (Bochkovskii et al.,
2025) or to open-domain data (Wang et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2026a), a range that includes everyday indoor
and outdoor scenes; but neither the demonstrations nor the means show how many images of such scenes still
have severely wrong metric depth. We test this on such scenes (not specialised domains such as aerial or
endoscopic imagery, Appendix A). For the four models that the MoGe-2 paper evaluated under the same
protocol, our results essentially match its values (Appendix E), so we do not dispute the reported numbers
but reveal what the averages hide: which images fail, whether the error is in structure or scale, in which
direction the scale is off, and whether a failure is shared.
Current evaluations compute metric and scale-aligned metrics on each dataset and then average the scores or
ranks across datasets. This practice leaves three gaps. First, it looks at means, not failures. A mean
does not distinguish “every image is slightly off” from “most images are good and a few are severely wrong”,
yet depth is used one image at a time. With intrinsics, for example, MoGe-3 (Kong et al., 2026) reaches
nearly the same mean metric δ1 on KITTI (Geiger et al., 2013) and ETH3D (Schöps et al., 2017), 0.926
and 0.927, yet fails (metric δ1 < 0.5) on 0.3% and 5.5% of their images. Second, alignment discards
the scale. A metric score requires both structure and scale to be right; a scale-aligned score first aligns the
prediction to the ground truth with one global scale and then measures structure only. Papers often report
the two side by side, and their gap reflects the size of the scale error; per image, they also tell scale failures
from structure failures (Section 4.3). But the scale factor s∗found during alignment is discarded after use,
although it carries the direction of the scale error and whether it is consistent across images. The metric
and scale-aligned scores reported by current evaluations are unsigned, so neither tells underestimation from
overestimation of depth, nor a systematic scale bias (all images of a dataset off in the same direction by
similar amounts, fixed by one global factor) from an unstable scale (sometimes too small and sometimes
too large, not fixed by any global correction); the former is measured by the bias and the latter by the
dispersion (Section 3.3). Existing surveys and benchmarks do not measure scale separately either: they align
predictions affinely before computing metrics, which removes the scale error (Liu et al., 2026b; Ge et al.,
2024), or evaluate through downstream tasks, which cannot tell scale errors from structural ones (Li et al.,
2025). Third, current zero-shot metric depth evaluations report means and do not judge whether a single
image fails, so they cannot compare whether different models fail on the same images, nor tell whether a
model’s failures are its own or shared. A model paper need only report its own results; this can be seen
only by comparing several models on the same images, which is what an evaluation study can add. The two
differ in meaning: a model-specific failure shows that other models solve the same image, so the problem
lies mostly in that model; a shared failure is a difficulty common to most models and the one future models
most need to solve. These images can hold most of a model’s error on a dataset: 89% of the 1 −δ1 error
of MoGe-2 on HAMMER (Jung et al., 2023; Section 5.4).
Our evaluation is built on per-image results and answers the three gaps in turn (Section 3.3). Like MoGe-2,
we still average per dataset and then across datasets, but we first keep the metric δ1, the scale-aligned δ1 and
s∗of every image and derive all statistics from them. First, we judge whether the problem is solved by the
failure rate, the share of images whose metric depth is severely wrong. Second, we split the metric error into
structure, measured with scale-aligned metrics, and scale, measured with the bias, dispersion and absolute
error of s∗. The split is needed because the two errors call for different remedies. The absolute scale is harder
to judge from pixels alone than the structure: a photograph of a blank wall may show a wall 1 m away or 5
m away; the relative geometry of the wall (a plane and its orientation), which we call structure, also relies on
image cues, but not on objects of known size, and does not depend on the distance. When structure is right
and scale is wrong, in principle a single known length corrects all depths, whereas a structural error cannot
be corrected this way. Structure and scale are analysis dimensions applied at evaluation time, not properties
of the models, so models with and without an explicit decomposition (Section 3.1) are compared on the same
terms. Third, we compare 11 architectures on the same images, grade failures into shared, intermediate and
model-specific tiers by the number of architectures that fail, and split the mean error of each model by these
tiers. On top of this, we run two comparisons: how the scale error changes when only the image content
changes, and what ground-truth intrinsics and model size do to the scale and the failures (Sections 6, 7).

Contributions
1. An evaluation protocol that uses the failure rate as the main criterion and measures structure and
scale separately, with dedicated statistics for scale (the bias, dispersion and absolute error of s∗); it
suits any metric depth model, decomposed or not (Section 3.3), and addresses the first two gaps.
2. A per-image, cross-model analysis that grades failures into shared, intermediate and model-specific
tiers by the number of architectures failing on the same image, and splits the mean error of each
model by these tiers, separating the failures that lower the means of all models from those that decide
the ranking (Section 5). It addresses the third gap.
3. Public resources: the evaluation code; per-image shared-failure labels on the 7 benchmarks and the
per-image results of all model evaluations (the 18 of the main table and another 18 size evaluations
in Appendix I), so that a new model can split its error by shared failures directly against these labels;
and two diagnostic sets built from TUM RGB-D that vary structure, texture and distance under the
same camera and intrinsics (each sequence a separate recording) (Sections 5, 6, 8).

Related Work
In-domain depth estimation. Monocular depth estimation was first trained and tested on a single dataset
(Eigen et al., 2014; Laina et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019; Bhat et al., 2021), and the scale-invariant
error of Eigen et al. (2014), together with the Rel and δ1 metrics it adopted, is still in use; self-supervised
methods (Zhou et al., 2017; Godard et al., 2019; Spencer et al., 2022) recover depth only up to scale and are
evaluated after per-image median alignment. The metric scale of these models, where they have one, comes
from the camera and scene distribution of a single training set and usually does not transfer to other datasets.
Zero-shot relative depth estimation. To generalise across datasets, one line of work gives up metric scale
and predicts only relative depth. Chen et al. (2016) trained a depth network on relative-depth annotations of
random point pairs in web images. MiDaS (Ranftl et al., 2020) proposed scale- and shift-invariant losses,
trained on a mixture of datasets with different depth ranges, and evaluated zero-shot on unseen datasets; DPT
(Ranftl et al., 2021) replaced the backbone with a vision transformer. Depth Anything and Depth Anything
V2 (Yang et al., 2024a;b) scaled up training with large amounts of unlabelled and synthetic images; Marigold
(Ke et al., 2024; Pavlovic et al., 2026) fine-tuned a pretrained image diffusion model into an affine-invariant
depth estimator; MoGe (Wang et al., 2025a) predicts an affine-invariant point map and solves for the focal
length and shift from it. Their evaluation aligns predictions affinely to the ground truth and so assesses only
structure; they show that relative depth can be estimated accurately zero-shot across a wide range of scenes.
3


Zero-shot metric depth estimation. Recent work tries to recover both structure and scale zero-shot.
Most methods start from the camera: an object imaged near with a short focal length can look the same as
one imaged far with a long focal length, which a network that sees only the image cannot distinguish. Earlier
work fed camera parameters as extra input channels (CAM-Convs, Facil et al., 2019). Metric3D (Yin et al.,
2023) proposed the canonical camera space transformation, which maps all training data to one canonical
focal length, and Metric3D v2 (Hu et al., 2024) keeps this transformation, switches to a ViT backbone and
adds joint depth–normal optimisation. ZeroDepth (Guizilini et al., 2023) uses geometric embeddings derived
from the intrinsics as input. UniDepth and UniDepthV2 (Piccinelli et al., 2024; 2026) predict camera rays
and condition the depth prediction on them, and UniK3D (Piccinelli et al., 2025) extends this to other
camera models. Depth Pro (Bochkovskii et al., 2025) has a focal length prediction head. MoGe-2 (Wang
et al., 2025b) predicts an affine-invariant point map and a metric scale, and solves for the focal length and
shift afterwards. MoGe-3 (Kong et al., 2026) adds sparse volumetric refinement. MetricAnything (Yang
et al., 2026) scales up pretraining on noisy, heterogeneous data sources. FoundationGeo (Liu et al., 2026a)
predicts spatially varying per-pixel fields. DA3METRIC (Lin et al., 2026) is the metric branch of Depth
Anything 3, and DepthVLM (Yu et al., 2026) builds on a vision-language model.
Surveys and evaluations. The metric depth survey of Zhang et al. (2025) collects the results reported
in the original papers and notes that inconsistent datasets, training protocols and model settings make
fair comparison difficult. The survey of Liu et al. (2026b) evaluates many depth foundation models on 10
benchmarks under one protocol, but likewise aligns metric models affinely first, comparing only the depth
distribution and not scale. GeoBench (Ge et al., 2024) compares training recipes and fine-tuning strategies of
geometry estimation models in a unified codebase, restricts the evaluation to affine-invariant depth, and leaves
metric depth to future work. BenchDepth (Li et al., 2025) argues that alignment-based metrics introduce bias
and instead evaluates 8 models through 5 downstream tasks, including depth completion, stereo matching
and SLAM. RoboDepth (Kong et al., 2023) tests the robustness of 42 models under 18 image corruptions such
as weather and noise. The iBims-1 evaluation (Koch et al., 2018) proposed directed depth errors, the share of
pixels predicted too far or too near relative to a fixed reference plane (e.g. 3 m); they are per pixel and do not
separate structure from scale. The papers of the 11 evaluated models have not adopted such metrics. Van Dijk
and de Croon (2019) modify input images to analyse which depth cues a self-supervised model relies on, and
find that it relies mainly on the vertical position of objects rather than their apparent size. None of the papers
of the 11 evaluated models contains a metric dedicated to depth scale or reports per-image depth results or
failure rates (Depth Pro reports per-image success rates of its focal length estimate). In image classification,
Geirhos et al. (2020) measure error consistency, i.e. whether two models err on the same inputs, and Meding
et al. (2022) find that most ImageNet validation images are either classified correctly by all or all but one
model (46%) or by none or only one (11.5%, partly due to label problems), so model differences show only on
the remaining images. We bring this analysis to metric depth and add the direction and consistency of scale.

3.2
Benchmarks
Test data were chosen by three rules: the data are the original public release; the ground truth does not
depend on any evaluated model; and the data were not designed around the weaknesses of a particular model.
We therefore use, unchanged, the 7 real-world benchmarks with metric ground truth in the MoGe-2 evaluation
(its other 3 benchmarks, GSO, Sintel and Spring, are rendered and outside our scope): NYUv2 (Silberman
et al., 2012; 654 images), KITTI (Geiger et al., 2013; 652), ETH3D (Schöps et al., 2017; 454), iBims-1 (Koch
et al., 2018; 100), DDAD (Guizilini et al., 2020; 1,000), DIODE (Vasiljevic et al., 2019; 771) and HAMMER
(Jung et al., 2023; 775), 4,406 images in total. We did not select images, and we keep the preprocessing of
the MoGe evaluation loader (which reprojects every image to a view with the principal point at the centre).
Section 5 counts failures by recorded scene, taken from the dataset directory structure: 13 for ETH3D, 6 for
DIODE (3 indoor and 3 outdoor) and 7 for HAMMER; the other 4 datasets are not counted by scene. In 6 of
the 7 HAMMER scenes, a robot arm recorded the same trajectory twice, once with objects on the table and
once with an empty table (called “empty table” below); of the 775 images, 404 show objects and 371 an empty
table. The DIODE ground truth was captured with a FARO Focus S350 laser scanner (nominal range 0.6 to
350 m, error about ±1 mm); regions beyond the range (including the sky) and highly specular surfaces return
no depth, so the ground truth of outdoor images covers only part of the pixels (Vasiljevic et al., 2019). We
take the released ground truth of each benchmark as given and do not check it independently. The intrinsics
follow the MoGe benchmark. Appendix A lists datasets we considered but did not use (e.g. MetricScenes).

Decomposing the metric error. The error of monocular metric depth has two aspects: structure (whether
the relative geometry of the scene is correct) and scale (how much the scene is enlarged or shrunk as a whole).
Metric metrics contain both, so we measure them separately: structure with scale-aligned metrics, and scale
with statistics of the alignment scale s∗; Figure 1 contrasts the two for every model. The metric error can
also be split into a structure term and a scale term by decomposing the log error (Eigen et al., 2014); the
conclusions agree with ours, but its exact additive split holds only for the log error, its scale term µ2 carries
no sign, and it does not separate systematic bias from instability (Appendix B).
For each image, let the predicted depth be p and the ground truth on valid pixels be g:
• Metric Rel and δ1 compare p and g directly. For one image, Rel is the mean of |p −g|/g over its
valid pixels, and δ1 is the fraction of valid pixels with max(p/g, g/p) < 1.25.
• Structure, i.e. scale-aligned Rel and δ1, is computed after multiplying p by s∗, as in the scale-
invariant metrics of MoGe and related work, but with s∗estimated differently: s∗is the median of
g/p over the valid pixels, the L1-optimal scale for the per-pixel log error; it marks fewer intact images
as structure failures than the log mean or the ratio of medians of early self-supervised evaluation
(Zhou et al., 2017; Godard et al., 2019) (Appendix C). We do not use the MoGe protocol’s minimiser
of P|s p −g|/g on a 64×64 grid: it favours small s∗, and a few outliers can pull it close to 0 and
turn an image with intact structure into a structure failure (Appendix C). s∗does not maximise δ1,
so the scale-aligned δ1 is occasionally slightly below the metric δ1, by small amounts.
• Scale is the alignment scale s∗itself (also called the “scale factor” below; earlier evaluations compute
it for the scale-aligned metrics and then discard it), with s∗· p ≈g: s∗> 1 means the model
underestimates depth, and s∗< 1 that it overestimates depth. Over all s∗of a dataset or a model
evaluation, we report three statistics on ln s∗, so that s∗= 0.5 and s∗= 2 count as equal errors:
– Bias: mean(ln(1/s∗)). A positive value means depth is overestimated overall (predicted depth
larger than the ground truth) and a negative value underestimated, matching the sign of “estimate
minus truth” (opposite to the direction of s∗).
– Dispersion: std(ln s∗), computed within each dataset; larger means a less consistent scale
across the images of a dataset. The bias and dispersion of Table 3 are equally weighted averages
of within-dataset statistics and do not reflect differences in bias between datasets (Figure F.8).
– Absolute error: mean(|ln s∗|). Bias cannot replace it, as under- and overestimates cancel in
the mean.
• Failure: an image fails when its metric δ1 < 0.5, i.e. fewer than half of its valid pixels are within a
factor of 1.25 of the ground truth; the “failure rate” is the fraction of failed images. This criterion
targets uses that need metric scale; its sensitivity is given in Appendix G.
For a model whose structure is exactly right and whose only error is scale, the Rel at scale factor s∗is
|1/s∗−1|; we use this as the reference curve in Figure 2.

Main Results

The ranking by metric δ1 depends on the condition. With intrinsics, MoGe-2 (0.877), MoGe-3 (0.875),
MA-PointMap (0.875) and UniK3D (0.874) are within 0.005 of each other; without intrinsics MA-PointMap

Intervals of the failure rate and a per-image best reference. Resampling recorded scenes (2,000
times), the failure rate of MoGe-3 with K is 7.7% (95% interval 4.4% to 11.3%) and that of MoGe-2 with K
8.1% (5.0% to 11.4%). The differences between the top evaluations lie within these intervals, so “the best

4.2
Most Metric Failures Are Scale Failures
The four kinds of failure used in this section (metric failure, pure scale failure, flawed structure, structure
failure) and their thresholds are summarised in Table 2. Figure 1 shows the metric and scale-aligned δ1
and Rel of each model evaluation. Except for DepthVLM, the scale-aligned δ1 of every model evaluation
lies between 0.950 and 0.978, whatever its metric δ1, and the failure rates, 7.7% to 44.0% before alignment,
fall to 0.0% to 0.4% after it (2.7% for DepthVLM). The scale-aligned Rel (0.045 to 0.109) is 2.2 to 4.3
times smaller than the metric Rel (median 3.0).
Per image, most metric failures are scale failures. The 18 model evaluations give 79,308 “image ×
model evaluation” pairs, of which 15,085 are metric failures. After scale correction with s∗, which is computed
from all ground-truth pixels and is thus an upper bound for any practical correction, 98.3% of metric failures
no longer fail (98.8% with datasets weighted equally) and only 1.7% still fail. This share partly depends on
the criterion: a scale error above 25% fails the whole image, whereas a structural error counts as a failure
only if half of the pixels stay outside a factor of 1.25 after per-image alignment; we call scale-aligned δ1 <
0.5 a structure failure. For a finer split by structure (Table 4), we call the structure intact when the
scale-aligned δ1 is at least 0.9 and flawed otherwise. In 78.8% of metric failures the structure is intact and
the failure comes only from scale; we call these pure scale failures. Of the pure scale failures, 99.9% have
s∗outside [0.8, 1.25]; only 17 have a scale inside the range, all of them right at its edge. The remaining 21.2%
have flawed structure, and 94% of them also have s∗outside the range, i.e. a wrong scale, so 98.6% of all
metric failures have a wrong scale. The share of pure scale failures varies across models and datasets: only
17% for DepthVLM against 67% to 98% for the other 17 model evaluations, and only 52% on DDAD against
77% to 95% on the other 6 datasets. The log-error decomposition gives the same conclusion without any
threshold: on metric failures, 93% of the log error comes from the overall scale, and with failure judged by
δ2 or δ3, 99.7% and 99.9% of the failures no longer fail after scale correction (Appendix B).
Metric δ1 is determined mainly by whether the scale falls in a narrow band. Figure 2 plots the
per-image metric error against s∗; the dashed line is the value for a prediction with exactly right structure
and only a scale error. For such a prediction, δ1 equals 1 when s∗is between 0.8 and 1.25 and 0 otherwise;

There are 387 shared-failure images, almost all from HAMMER (218) and DIODE (157), and none from
KITTI. 90% come from 9 recorded scenes: 4 of the 7 HAMMER scenes (scene10, scene3, scene5, scene9)

failures occur in every scene. ETH3D and DDAD have many images failed by at least one architecture

Shared failures mostly point in one direction: on a shared-failure image, 92% of the failing architectures err
in the same direction on average. On HAMMER almost all overestimate depth (99.5%, with K), and 87%
of the failures have intact structure. On DIODE only 55% overestimate depth, the direction varies with
the scene, and only 65% have intact structure, so structural error plays a larger role.
The 16 images failed by all 11 architectures come from 5 scenes. The 10 HAMMER images are all empty-table
frames (Figure 4(a) to (c) shows one per scene): on each, the median scale-aligned δ1 over the 11 architectures

and (e)) but the architectures err in different directions; the other 4 have flawed structure to different degrees:
on two outdoor images most models get the relative distance between foreground trees and background
buildings wrong, another outdoor image has only a slight structural error (scale-aligned δ1 of 0.65 to 0.95
over the 11 architectures), and the indoor one is a large white wall (Appendix F, Figure F.9).

where the other architectures overestimate depth.

5.3
Model-Specific Failures Are Systematic Biases
Model-specific failures come mainly from 4 models. Table 6 lists, per architecture, on which kind of image
each failure falls. Of all failures, 27% are model-specific, of which 24 percentage points come from Depth Pro,
Metric3D-v2, DepthVLM and MA-DepthMap, and only 3 percentage points from the other 7 architectures
together. These 7 architectures have almost no failures of their own: 94% of their failures fall on images
failed by at least 3 architectures, and 58% to 79% on shared-failure images (44% for UniDepthV2).
The specific failures of these 4 models concentrate on one or two datasets, with a constant direction within
each: the 761 of Depth Pro on DDAD and ETH3D almost all underestimate depth (on ETH3D 91% have
intact structure, but on DDAD only 40% do, so Depth Pro’s DDAD failures combine a consistent scale
bias with structural error), the 298 of Metric3D-v2 on DIODE and the 189 of DepthVLM on HAMMER all
overestimate it, and the 108 of MA-DepthMap on HAMMER all underestimate it. They are thus systematic
scale biases rather than random errors, and cannot be found from the 7-dataset mean: Depth Pro has the
lowest mean metric δ1, but on NYUv2 and HAMMER it is on par with most models (Appendix F). The
exception is DDAD: only 8% of the 130 specific failures of DepthVLM and 40% of the 532 of Depth Pro
have intact structure; DDAD is in the training data of DepthVLM.
All 4 models use a canonical camera space (Table 1), but DA3METRIC, of the same class, has only 3%
specific failures, so we report this only as an observation and infer no causal relation.
5.4
How Shared Failures and Other Images Affect the Mean and Ranking
Readers use a mean for two things: to judge how accurate a model is overall, and to rank models. Table 7
splits the 7-benchmark mean error of each model into four parts by the k of the images (the three failure
tiers, and the shortfall of δ1 below 1 on images with k = 0); the parts sum to the error in Table 3.
Shared failures set the level of the error. Shared-failure images make up 7.3% of the data with datasets
weighted equally (8.8% pooled over all images, Table 5). They account for 38% to 46% of the 1 −δ1 error of
14


the top 6 models by metric δ1 (MoGe-2, MoGe-3, MA-PointMap, UniK3D, DA3METRIC, FoundationGeo)
and 27% to 34% of their Rel error, against 14% to 18% of the 1 −δ1 error for the same number of random
images drawn from the same datasets (2.3 to 3.2 times). On individual datasets the effect is stronger: 89%
of the 1 −δ1 error and 80% of the Rel of MoGe-2 on HAMMER come from these images, and 63% and
40% on DIODE. This is a circular estimate, since the shared-failure images are selected by these models’
own failures. A held-out test removes the circularity: for each model, we consider only the architectures
outside its own family, rank the images by how many of them fail, and take per dataset as many images as
there are original shared failures (rule in Appendix G); these images account for 34% to 40% of its 1 −δ1
error and overlap the original shared failures by 81% to 93%, well above the 14% to 18% of random images.
Counting by 6 families gives the same conclusion (Appendix G). Without them, the 1 −δ1 error of the
top 6 falls by 29% to 42% and their Rel by 19% to 29% (Table 7), so how low these means are is largely
set by images on which most models fail together.
The ranking is decided by images other than shared failures, but the effect of shared failures
on the ranking depends on the metric. Under metric δ1, removing the shared-failure images leaves
the ranking almost unchanged, with only two pairs of adjacent ranks swapped; the errors of the models
at the bottom come mainly from specific failures (Depth Pro, MA-DepthMap) or the intermediate tier
(UniDepthV2). Under metric Rel, 9 of the 11 models change rank: MA-DepthMap drops from 1st to 7th
and MoGe-2 rises from 5th to 1st. The reason is that δ1 is bounded: it costs a failed image at most 1, and
most models have a low δ1 on these images, which therefore cannot separate them; Rel is unbounded, so
an image with a scale off by a factor of two can outweigh many normal images; MA-DepthMap gets right
exactly the HAMMER images that the other models fail together (Section 5.2). Under δ1 this agrees with
Meding et al. (2022) on ImageNet: model differences show only on the images that not all models get right
or all get wrong; under Rel the shared-failure images are not failed by every model (MA-DepthMap gets
most HAMMER shared failures right), so they still separate models.
A model’s mean can thus be read as two parts: the part on shared-failure images lowers the means of all
models, and the part on the remaining images decides its gap to other models. We therefore recommend
reporting the shared-failure images alongside the mean, or the mean stratified by k, and, when ranking with
an unbounded metric such as Rel, stating whether the ranking depends on these images.

6.2
The Error Direction Mainly Follows the Sequence, Not the Model
Unlike the scenes of Section 5, TUM-Family and TUM-Ref share one camera and its intrinsics, so the error
direction can be compared across content. We use the 11 models with ground-truth intrinsics; the “family
average” averages over the frames of each sequence, then over the 8 sequences.
The error is in scale, not in structure. On TUM-Family, the family-average metric δ1 of the 11 models
is only 0.16 to 0.40, with failure rates of 60% to 84%; after scale correction, the scale-aligned δ1 of the 10
models other than DepthVLM (0.901) lies between 0.976 and 0.992. The failures here are thus almost all
pure scale failures: the models recover the structure of the board correctly and only place it at the wrong
distance. On TUM-Ref, the same 11 models reach a metric δ1 of 0.84 to 0.96, with biases between −0.12
and +0.02, all within the range that passes δ1 (±ln 1.25 ≈±0.22).
The error direction is set by the sequence, not by the model. We call a model direction-stable on
a sequence when the absolute value of its bias exceeds its dispersion, and direction-uncertain otherwise
(both computed over all frames of the sequence). Of the 88 cells of Figure 5, 70 are direction-stable. On the 4
sequences with printed texture, all 44 cells underestimate depth, 43 of them direction-stable (median biases
of −0.59 to −0.85); on the near sequence with structure and without texture, 10 of the 11 models stably
overestimate depth (median +0.44). The two NS-NT sequences show only a flat, plain surface whose image
contains no scale information; the bias there reflects the model’s prior and is not used to compare models.

6.3
Adding Objects (HAMMER)
Objects of known size are a classic cue to absolute scale, and the HAMMER pairs test it directly (Section 5.2).
We compare the absolute log scale error |ln s∗| of each model, taking the change as “with objects minus empty
table” (negative means the error shrinks), averaged over the 371 pairs. With only 6 scenes, we use an exact
scene-level sign-flip test (64 sign combinations); per-model bootstrap intervals are given in Appendix H.
Adding objects reduces the scale error of 10 of the 11 models (Figure 6a); the mean paired change of these
ten models is between −0.21 and −0.13. The change averaged over the 11 models is negative in 5 of the
6 scenes (scene-level sign-flip test, one-sided p = 0.03); individually, 5 models reach p ≤0.05 (Table H.1).
The empty-table images are dominated by bare walls and table tops, so “adding objects” also adds the
texture and structure that come with them. Solving the scale on the shared background pixels only (about
73%) gives almost the same change (Table H.1), so objects change the scale of the whole scene, not only
the depth of the objects. Failure rates fall accordingly: averaged over the 11 models on the 371 pairs, 30%
with objects against 49% without. With objects, the bias of every model decreases by 0.12 to 0.21. The
effect is largest where shared failures concentrate (scene5, scene3, scene10).
17


MA-DepthMap is the exception (+0.04): it underestimates depth on HAMMER (Section 5.2), and objects
make its depths smaller still (bias from −0.07 to −0.20), so its error grows. Objects thus act in the same
direction for all models; whether the error grows or shrinks depends on the original bias.
6.4
Adding a Printed Pattern (TUM)
A planar printed pattern differs from an object. It adds texture and thus more visual information than a
plain surface, and its text and graphics may carry some scale information; but a printed pattern can be of
any physical size, so it carries much weaker scale information than real objects and can even mislead the scale
estimate. The textured and untextured TUM-Family sequences share geometry and distance class and differ
in whether the surface is printed; they are separate recordings (Section 6.1), but can test whether current
models obtain scale from such content. In the group without structure, the texture is a conference poster laid
on the plane; in the group with structure, it comes from the coloured wrapping of the board and the posters
on the floor in front of it. The change is again taken as |ln s∗| “with pattern minus without pattern”.
There are four pairs (two geometries, each at near and far distance). On the sequences with a printed pattern,
the mean scale error of all 11 models is larger (Figure 6b). Here the change of each pair is the difference
of the means of |ln s∗| over all frames of the two sequences, and the “mean” of each model is the average
over the four pairs; 41 of the 44 “model × pair” cells are larger. The 3 cells that are smaller are all on the
near pair with structure: one each of DepthVLM, UniDepthV2 and UniK3D.
HAMMER pairs share a pose and TUM pairs are different recordings, so we compare only directions: objects
reduce the scale error and patterns increase it; more content alone does not give a better scale.
Printed patterns barely affect structure. On the same four pairs, the scale-aligned δ1 changes little: in
the two groups without structure, adding posters leaves structure unchanged or better (median +0.002 at
far and +0.057 at near distance); in the two groups with structure it gets slightly worse (medians −0.009
and −0.007), and apart from a drop of 0.12 for DepthVLM on the near pair, no drop exceeds 0.04. The
error caused by printed patterns is thus almost entirely in scale.
These posters were not optimised against any model, yet on the 4 textured sequences the 11 models almost all
stably underestimate depth (43 of 44 cells, Section 6.2), corresponding to underestimating depth by a factor
of about 1.8 to 2.3. The untextured sequences show no such consistent underestimation: on NS-NT-far all 11
models also underestimate depth, but with a median of only −0.20, and on the other 3 untextured sequences
most models overestimate depth. Prior work changes the local depth of monocular depth models with
optimised adversarial patches (Cheng et al., 2022); our results suggest that an unoptimised planar pattern
may already be associated with a shift in the global scale of metric models, while structure is unaffected
and scale-aligned metrics alone would not reveal it. Confirming causality requires comparing the same scene
from the same viewpoint with and without the pattern.

Ground-Truth Intrinsics and Model Size
Ground-truth intrinsics raise metric accuracy but do not improve structure. Supplying ground-
truth intrinsics raises the 7-benchmark mean metric δ1 of all 7 architectures that can be evaluated without
them, by +0.018 (MA-PointMap) to +0.135 (MoGe-3) (Figure 7a), but does not raise the scale-aligned δ1.
For the four models sharing the MoGe solver it drops (by 0.014 for MoGe-2, 0.018 for MoGe-3, 0.008 for
FoundationGeo and 0.002 for MA-PointMap), while the ray-input UniDepthV2 and UniK3D are unchanged to
three decimals. This is consistent with the design: in the post-hoc solve the intrinsics affect the ratios between
pixel depths only through the shift t, and with the focal length fixed, t must also satisfy the projection
constraints under the true focal length, so the structure may be fitted less well (Appendix D). The scale can
worsen too: on HAMMER, the post-hoc solve models have an overall bias near 0 (−0.05 to +0.10) without
intrinsics and about +0.2 with them. On single datasets the metric δ1 can even drop: on HAMMER for
MA-PointMap, FoundationGeo and also the ray-input UniK3D, and on NYUv2 for MA-PointMap (Tables F.1,
F.7). Ground-truth intrinsics are thus no upper bound on metric accuracy, especially for post-hoc solve models.

shared set without the family under test (Table I.4). On HAMMER, every size of these families, with or

For the canonical-camera and ray-input classes, the true focal length enters the scale conversion directly or
conditions the network, so the role of a wrong focal estimate can be tested: these models still fail heavily

and 91% for UniK3D, against 9% and 27% on the other images; Appendix G and Table I.4), so for them a
wrong focal estimate cannot explain the failures; whether limited focal coverage in training contributes is
not tested here. The test does not apply to the post-hoc solve class (including FoundationGeo): its scale
head acts after the solve, independently of the intrinsics (Appendix D).

Limitations and Reproducibility
Limitations
• Independence. The images of DIODE, HAMMER and ETH3D come from a limited number of recorded
scenes (6, 7 and 13), with overlapping viewpoints within a scene, so the images are not independent.
Section 6.3 has only 6 scenes and therefore uses a scene-level sign-flip test; the per-model intervals
(Appendix H) are themselves unstable; those of Appendix I treat each image as an independent
sample (Section 3.3) and are therefore too narrow; differences of a few hundredths between models
should not be read as a ranking. The shared failures of Section 5 come from 9 recorded scenes
(Section 5.2), so their share in the mean error is set by these scenes; the 11 architectures are not
independent either (Appendix G).
• Mechanism. This paper measures where scale errors occur and in which direction, not why models
err this way internally (e.g. on the printed-texture sequences, or MA-DepthMap on HAMMER).
Training data may contribute, but do not map simply onto HAMMER performance (Appendix E).
• Protocol. The thresholds for failure, intact structure and structure failure were not tuned on the results.
Appendix G varies them and Appendix B checks them with the threshold-free log-error decomposition
and with δ2 and δ3: the qualitative conclusions do not change, but the exact proportions do. The
motion thresholds of TUM-Family were compared only in terms of sample size. The benchmarks,
their intrinsics and the protocol all follow MoGe-2, which may favour the MoGe series.
• Training overlap. Five models were trained on DDAD (Section 3.1). Overlap is checked only at the
dataset level. Without DDAD the conclusions are unchanged (Appendix G).
• Ground truth. We take the released ground truth of each benchmark and of TUM as given, without
independent checks; the outdoor DIODE ground truth covers only part of the pixels (4% to 30% in
Appendix F), so the metrics there reflect only that region.
• Model size. Sizes are compared within families only (Appendix I).
Reproducibility. We will release the evaluation code, the model adapters (all models are used through
their public weights) and instructions for obtaining each third-party repository at the commits we used. The
two TUM-derived sets will be released as processed data under CC BY 4.0 (the licence of TUM RGB-D)
with the selection rules, source frames and intrinsics, and the results of all 36 model evaluations (including
the size evaluations of Appendix I) as per-image files, together with the shared-failure label of every image
(the number k of failing architectures and its tier).
9
Conclusion
Recent zero-shot metric depth models have not yet solved metric depth estimation in everyday scenes: the
top 4 model evaluations by metric δ1 still have failure rates of 7.7% to 9.8%; even taking the lowest failure
rate of any of the 18 evaluations on each dataset, DIODE and HAMMER reach 15.2% and 23.9%, whereas
the other five datasets are at most 0.2%; these failures are invisible in the means. The remaining difficulty
lies mainly in scale: 78.8% of metric failures have intact structure and are wrong only in scale, after scale
correction 98.3% no longer fail, flawed structure occurs mainly on the DDAD dataset, and structure failures
mainly in the DepthVLM model. The largest part of the failures (42%) is shared by most models: these
images cover only 7.3% of the data, concentrate on HAMMER (4 of its 7 scenes) and DIODE, still account
for 34% to 40% of the 1 −δ1 error of the top 6 models when selected by models of other families (against
14% to 18% for as many random images from the same datasets), and point in the same direction for most
models; on HAMMER they are mostly empty-table shots. In the HAMMER pairs, adding objects reduces
the scale error of most models, and the TUM sequences with printed patterns (separate recordings) have
larger scale errors; ground-truth intrinsics mainly affect the scale, and from the smallest to the largest size, 4
of the 5 families reduce both structure and scale errors; with intrinsics and the largest size, every architecture
still fails on at least 8.0% of images, and on 16.3% and 23.9% of DIODE and HAMMER (the two minima
come from the two public MoGe-2 checkpoints). For users: a reliable scale reference (such as a known length)
can largely correct most failures, and the metric scale should not be trusted on views without size cues, such
as an empty table or a blank wall. We recommend that future metric depth evaluations (points (i) to (iii)
20


answer the three gaps): (i) report the failure rate and its distribution over datasets besides the mean; (ii)
report metric metrics, scale-aligned metrics and the bias, dispersion and absolute error of s∗side by side;
(iii) evaluate several models on the same images and report the images that most models fail separately,
or stratify means by the number of failing models; and (iv) report ground-truth intrinsics as a separate
condition, not an upper bound on performance (Section 7).

A
Model and Data Selection
Model selection criteria. We evaluate recent state-of-the-art models that perform monocular metric
depth estimation across indoor and outdoor scenes with a single set of weights. This excludes four groups:
(i) metric models with separate indoor and outdoor weights, such as ZoeDepth (Bhat et al., 2023) and
Depth-Anything-V2-Metric (Yang et al., 2024b); (ii) models that output only relative (affine-invariant) depth,
such as MiDaS (Ranftl et al., 2020), Depth Anything v1 and v2 (Yang et al., 2024a;b), and Marigold v1 and
v2 (Ke et al., 2024; Pavlovic et al., 2026), which have no metric scale to evaluate; (iii) models that cannot
output a dense depth map, such as DepthLM (Cai et al., 2026), which answers the depth of one pixel per
generation and cannot be evaluated with our per-pixel protocol, so among vision-language models we use
DepthVLM, which outputs dense depth maps; and (iv) variants of MoGe-2 specialised for long-range scenes:
AerialMetric (Song et al., 2026), patched for aerial views, and WildMoGe (Xiangli et al., 2026), MoGe-2
fine-tuned on MetricScenes to recover the scale of distant landmarks. On the 7 benchmarks used here (same
protocol), its paper reports a metric δ1 of 0.734 without and 0.849 with intrinsics, against 0.768 and 0.874 for
MoGe-2 (its Table 1), so it does not improve on MoGe-2 in everyday scenes; we evaluate MoGe-2 directly.
Datasets considered but not used. MetricScenes (Xiangli et al., 2026), which includes a subset of
Stereo4D, is a training corpus: its partial depth comes from SfM/MVS with the scale recovered from
geo-tagged metadata or known stereo baselines, and the missing regions are filled by a Poisson completion
guided by MoGe-2, which also sets the scale of the foreground. Its completed ground truth thus depends on
MoGe-2, and WildMoGe, a fine-tuned MoGe-2, is trained on it. Datasets that target only transparent and
specular objects are specialised challenges, and further indoor datasets would add to an already large indoor
share. Aerial and medical scenes fall outside everyday scenes and their depth ranges lie far outside common
training data: the aerial dataset AerialMetric (Song et al., 2026) was captured at relative flight heights of 80
m and 120 m, with 70 to 300 m in its synthetic part; endoscopic data have working distances of millimetres
to centimetres, for example depths of about 15 to 246 mm in SCARED (Allan et al., 2021), and ground-truth
depth clipped to 0 to 100 mm in C3VD (Bobrow et al., 2023). Failures on these scenes would readily be
attributed to distribution shift rather than to scale recovery itself, so we do not use them.

Why the per-pixel median
26


1. Consistent with per-pixel metrics. δ1 and Rel are per-pixel errors. The per-pixel median and
the log mean both minimise the per-pixel log error directly, one in L1 and one in L2.
2. Robust to outlying pixels. The median has a breakdown point of 50%: as long as fewer than half
of the pixels are wrong, s∗is set by the others. The mean has a breakdown point of 0: a few pixels
with very large log errors can push e−µ away. So the log mean nearly equals the median on most
images, but on images with outlying pixels it can turn intact structure into a structure failure.
3. Uses pixel correspondence. The ratio of medians compares only the distributions of the two
depth maps. It uses no pixel correspondence and corresponds to no per-pixel objective: shuffling the
predicted pixels within the image leaves it unchanged. If the prediction preserves the depth order
and the log error varies monotonically with depth (e.g. p = c gk, a global compression or stretch
of the depth range), the two coincide exactly. They differ only when a local region is wrong: then
the medians of p and g fall on different pixels, and the ratio of medians folds the depth difference
between the two regions into s.
4. Symmetric in log. The per-pixel median, the log mean and the ratio of medians are symmetric in
p and g (swapping the two turns s into 1/s): overestimating by a factor of two and underestimating
by a factor of two give values of ln s that differ only in sign. The criterion max(p/g, g/p) < 1.25 of
δ1 and the statistics of ln s∗are symmetric in the same way, so the alignment scale should be too.
MoGe’s weighted median is not: points predicted too far (p/g large) get more weight and pull s
down, towards judging the depth overestimated. Solving at full resolution instead of on the 64×64
grid does not remove this tendency.

D
How Models Use the Focal Length
Table 1 groups the 11 architectures into four classes by how the intrinsics enter the model (Section 3.1). For
each class we describe three things: where the intrinsics enter the model, where the metric scale comes from,
and where the focal length comes from when no intrinsics are given. The descriptions below are based on the
papers and the inference code of each model; f and W denote the focal length and image width in pixels.
1. Canonical camera space, output conversion. The same scene captured with cameras of different
focal lengths shows objects at different sizes in the image while the depth is the same; a network that
sees only the image cannot distinguish “the object is near” from “the focal length is long”. Metric3D (Yin
et al., 2023) proposed the canonical camera space transformation (CSTM) to remove this ambiguity: during
training, all samples are converted to a canonical camera with focal length fc, so the network learns depth
in the canonical camera; at inference, the output is converted back using the real focal length. It offers
two variants: transforming the depth labels (CSTM label) or transforming the input image (CSTM image).
Earlier camera-aware methods such as CAM-Convs (Facil et al., 2019) feed camera parameters as extra input
29


channels, and Metric3D compares against this in an ablation. The four models of this class follow the idea of
CSTM label: the network does not receive the focal length, which appears only at the output:
• Metric3D-v2 (Hu et al., 2024): depth = network output × f/1000 (canonical focal length 1000 px).
• DA3METRIC (Lin et al., 2026): the paper states that it follows the canonical camera space
transformation of Metric3D-v2; at inference, depth = network output × f/300, where f is the mean
of fx and fy on the network input image.
• Depth Pro (Bochkovskii et al., 2025): the network predicts a canonical inverse depth C, and
inverse depth = C × W/f. The paper cites Yin et al. (2023) for the focal length conversion. It differs
from the previous two in two respects: the conversion acts on inverse depth, and the normalisation
uses the image width W rather than a fixed canonical focal length.
• MetricAnything-DepthMap (Yang et al., 2026): follows the formula of Depth Pro, inverse depth =
C × W/f, with the same canonical inverse depth and the same normalisation by the image width.
In this class, the metric scale is determined by the product of the network output and the focal length:
an error by some factor in the focal length gives an error by the same factor in depth. Depth Pro has a
field-of-view prediction head; without intrinsics, the predicted field of view gives f = 0.5W/ tan(FoV/2), so
intrinsics are optional for it. The other three lack a focal length source and need intrinsics.
2. Canonical camera space, input rescaling. DepthVLM (Yu et al., 2026) rescales the input image by a
factor fc/f (fc = 1000) so that the focal length of the rescaled image equals the canonical focal length. Depth
values do not change with image rescaling, so the network outputs metric depth directly on the rescaled
image, and the depth map is then resized back to the original size. This is the same transformation as CSTM
image in Metric3D. The focal length only determines the rescaling factor; without intrinsics no rescaling
is possible, so DepthVLM is evaluated only with intrinsics.
3. Post-hoc geometric solve (MoGe-2, MoGe-3, MA-PointMap, FoundationGeo). The network does not
receive the focal length and outputs a point map, one 3D point (x, y, z) per pixel. The point map differs from
the true point cloud by a global scale and a shift t along the optical axis. The focal length can be recovered
from the point map because pinhole projection requires every pixel to satisfy (u, v) = f · (x, y)/(z + t), where
(u, v) is the image coordinate of the pixel (with the image centre as origin and the half-diagonal as unit);
with the pixel positions known and the point map given by the network, f and t are constrained by this set
of equations. The four models use the same solver code (FoundationGeo and MA-PointMap reuse the MoGe
implementation): the point map is downsampled to 64×64 and P ∥f · (x, y)/(z + t) −(u, v)∥2 is minimised.
For a given t the optimal f has a closed form, and t is then solved with the Levenberg–Marquardt method.
Without intrinsics, f and t are solved jointly; with intrinsics, f is fixed to the true focal length and only
t is solved. The depth is then z + t, scaled by the scale head (below).
The metric scale does not come from this solve. MoGe-2, MoGe-3 and MA-PointMap use a separate scale
head that predicts a scalar from the global image feature (cls token), which is multiplied onto the depth
after the solve; FoundationGeo predicts a per-pixel scale field, likewise multiplied after the solve, and also
corrects the ray direction of each pixel by at most 3◦. In this class, the intrinsics therefore affect depth only
through the shift t: t changes the ratios between pixel depths in z + t as well as their overall size, but the
output of the scale head is not affected by the intrinsics. Before the solve, MoGe-3 refines the log depth
of the point map in 3 iterations, which do not involve the focal length.
4. Rays as network input (UniDepthV2, UniK3D). A ray is the direction from the camera centre through
each pixel. Given intrinsics K, the ray of pixel (u, v) is r = K−1[u, v, 1]⊤, normalised to a unit vector; both
models compute it this way. The rays are converted to polar and azimuthal angles, encoded as Fourier
features, and fed to the depth decoder as conditioning. The decoder predicts a distance ρ along each ray, the
3D point is ρ · r, and depth is its z component. Without intrinsics, the rays are predicted by the camera
head of the network: the camera head of UniDepthV2 predicts fx, fy, cx, cy and obtains the rays with the
same formula; that of UniK3D predicts the field of view and a set of spherical harmonic coefficients from
which it generates the rays, which can represent non-pinhole cameras.
In this class, the metric scale comes from ρ; the rays determine directions and influence the prediction of ρ
through the conditioning, rather than converting depth multiplicatively as in class 1.

Which elements of the intrinsics “with K” uses. The classes use different elements of the intrinsics, so
“with K” is not the same intervention for all of them: the four MoGe-family models use only fx (converted
from fov_x) and take the principal point at the image centre; UniDepthV2 and UniK3D use the full K;
Metric3D-v2, DA3METRIC, Depth Pro and MA-DepthMap use only the focal length; DepthVLM uses
the focal length only to set its resize factor. The MoGe evaluation loader first reprojects every image to
a view with the principal point at the image centre, so every model receives a centred principal point and
the models that use only the focal length lose no information.

Hard images shared by most models, per dataset. Tables F.1 to F.7 give only means. To show where
the difficulty of each dataset lies, Figures F.10 to F.13 select, in each dataset, the images failed by the most
architectures, together with the predictions of the 3 architectures with the highest mean metric δ1 on that
dataset (with intrinsics). The selection rule is as follows. We take 3 images per dataset; ETH3D and DIODE
contain both indoor and outdoor scenes, so we take 3 indoor and 3 outdoor images from each; KITTI has
no shared failure and only 1 image in the intermediate tier, so it contributes 1 image. Within each group,
images are taken from the shared failures (k ≥6, Section 5) in order of decreasing k and increasing mean
metric δ1 over the 11 architectures, at most one per scene, and filled from the intermediate tier (k from 3 to
5) when needed. Three adjustments were made. The HAMMER empty-table images contain no object that
reveals size and are already shown in Figure 4, so here we select only from the 404 images with objects. The
DIODE images failed by all 11 architectures come from indoor scene_00020 and outdoor scene_00022 and
are already shown in Figure 4 and Figure F.9, so here we select from the other scenes; only 2 indoor scenes
remain, so the third indoor image comes from another scan of one of them. The 3 shared-failure images
of DDAD are adjacent clips of the same open lot, all taken by CAMERA_06, so for DDAD we treat each
camera as a scene and take the other two images from the intermediate tier.
The 4 all-failed DIODE images with flawed structure. Of the 6 DIODE images failed by all 11
architectures (Section 5.2), 4 have flawed structure (Figure F.9), each in a different way. (a) is an indoor
scene dominated by a large textureless white wall, with a small structural error: the scale-aligned δ1 of
MoGe-2 is 0.71, and its predicted depth range (ratio of the 90th to the 10th percentile depth) is 7.5, close
to the ground-truth 9.1. (b) to (d) are outdoor scenes with buildings in the background and bare trees in
the foreground. Figure F.9 shows that MoGe-2 predicts the outlines of both the trees and the buildings
accurately; what it gets wrong is the relative distance between trees and buildings: it places the buildings
too close to the trees, compressing the depth range of the whole image, with ground-truth depth ranges of
9.6, 16.9 and 37.5 against only 3.3, 4.6 and 11.8 for MoGe-2. In (b) the ground truth covers only 16% of
the pixels, mostly on the building, so this compression matters little for δ1: the scale-aligned δ1 is 0.88 for
MoGe-2 and 0.65 to 0.95 over the 11 architectures, a slight structural error. In such scenes trees can be of any
size, there is no directly comparable reference between the foreground trees and the background buildings,
and the background of (d) is also strongly overexposed; but none of these image factors alone explains the
failure: Figure 4(d) and (e) also show trees in front of buildings without visible ground, yet MoGe-2 gets
their structure right, while Figure F.9(d) shows a fair amount of ground. The failure lies in the relative
distance between foreground and background. Scale alignment multiplies by a single global factor and cannot
compensate for such an error, so the scale-aligned δ1 of (c) and (d) is very low (0.13 and 0.34). Images on
which most models get the relative depth wrong do exist, but they are rare: structure failures make up only
1.7% of metric failures (Section 4.3). The images that all models fail are therefore hard for different reasons:
these DIODE images are hard in structure, while the HAMMER empty-table images have intact structure
and are hard because the image contains almost no object whose size reveals the distance.

Shared failures counted by family. The k of Section 5.1 treats the 11 architectures as independent
judgements, but some share code or authors. We merge them into 6 families: the MoGe family (MoGe-2,
MoGe-3, MA-PointMap, FoundationGeo, which share MoGe’s post-hoc solve; MA-PointMap is fine-tuned
from MoGe-2), the Uni family (UniDepthV2, UniK3D), the Depth Pro family (Depth Pro, MA-DepthMap;
the latter reuses the network and focal-length formula of the former, Yang et al., 2026), and one family
each for Metric3D-v2, DA3METRIC and DepthVLM. A family fails an image when more than half of its
members fail it, and an image is a shared failure when at least 4 families fail it. This gives 320 shared-failure
images (instead of 387): 221 on HAMMER, 206 of them among the original 218; 95 on DIODE, all among
the original 157; and 4 on the other 5 datasets. With datasets weighted equally, they make up 5.9% of the
images (instead of 7.3%) and account for 32% to 40% of the error of the top 6 models by metric δ1 (instead
of 38% to 46%). Splitting Depth Pro and MA-DepthMap into two families (7 in total) and still requiring
at least 4 failing families gives 461 shared-failure images (247 on HAMMER, 204 on DIODE), 8.7% of the
images and 38% to 48% of the error of the top 6 models. The HAMMER shared failures are thus stable
under every count; the number on DIODE depends on how architectures are counted and drops by about four
in ten with 6 families. Rebuilding the shared failures from the 7 architectures evaluated without intrinsics (at
least 4 failing) gives 198 HAMMER images, 179 of them among the 218 with K, and 231 DIODE images, of
which only 94 are among the 157 with K. The HAMMER shared failures are thus largely the same images
under both intrinsics conditions, whereas those on DIODE change markedly with the condition. Under the

families), and HAMMER 218, 190 and 206; the main results throughout use the first of these counts.
Held-out selection. The shared-failure images are selected by the 11 architectures’ own failures, so their
failure rates on these images are biased by the selection. We select images with only 5 architectures from
different families (MoGe-2, UniDepthV2, Metric3D-v2, Depth Pro, DepthVLM), requiring at least 4 to fail,

the 212 selected images and on 9% of the other 563; MoGe-3 on 84% and 4%; MA-PointMap on 85% and 5%;
FoundationGeo on 79% and 12%; UniK3D on 92% and 24% (each pair gives the failure rates on two groups of
images, not two parts of one whole). Only MA-DepthMap is reversed (6% and 34%): it is the only architecture
that underestimates depth on HAMMER (bias −0.14), so it happens to be right on the images where the
others overestimate (Section 5.2). DIODE yields 165 images: DA3METRIC 57% and 11%, MoGe-3 42%
and 8%, MA-PointMap 52% and 9%, FoundationGeo 53% and 14%, UniK3D 40% and 12%, MA-DepthMap
29% and 22%. Of the 6 architectures that took no part, only DA3METRIC belongs to a family outside the
selectors; it fails on 91% of the selected HAMMER images against 9% of the others, and 57% against 11% on
DIODE. The family-held-out version, in which each family is excluded from selecting its own test images, is
in Table I.4. Hard images selected by one set of models are thus much harder than the remaining images for
the models that took no part. The same held-out test applies to the error share: for each of the top 6 models,
we consider only the architectures outside its family (7 for the MoGe family, 9 or 10 for the others), rank the
images of each dataset by how many of them fail, breaking ties by their mean metric δ1 (lowest first), and take
as many images as there are original shared failures. Models from different families thus use different sets of

to 93%. On each dataset this amounts to “at least 3 to 7 of the other-family architectures fail”, a threshold
that varies with dataset and family; we fix the number rather than the threshold so that the error share can

the number but requires the share of failing other-family architectures to be at least 6/11 (the same share as
k ≥6 among 11, e.g. at least 4 of 7 other-family architectures); the images so selected account for 36% to

backgrounds of the two passes agree on all pairs, and the differences appear only on the objects and in a

Frame sampling and ground-truth check of TUM-Family. RGB and depth frames are paired when
their timestamps differ by less than 20 ms; a frame is kept when the camera has moved at least 0.10 m or
rotated at least 10◦since the last kept frame (the first frame is always kept), and when it has at least 500
valid depth pixels, giving 514 images (30 to 131 per sequence). These rules depend only on poses, sensor
metadata and depth coverage, and were fixed before looking at any model results. The motion thresholds are
loose because the far sequences are short: with 0.15 m or 15◦, the number of frames drops to about 345. Valid
depths range from 0.74 to 3.86 m (1st and 99th percentiles). The two camera distances differ only moderately:
by the within-sequence median of the per-frame median ground-truth depth, the near sequences are at 0.93
to 1.24 m and the far ones at 1.76 to 2.41 m, a factor of about 1.6 to 2.1. We use the official intrinsics of
the Freiburg 3 colour camera (fx = 535.4, fy = 539.2, cx = 320.1, cy = 247.6 pixels) and the official depth
scale factor of 5000. Attributing the scale error of a model to image content requires metrically accurate
ground-truth depth. We confirmed this with a model-independent test: for the 4 sequences with structure,
we reproject each frame into the next using the ground-truth poses and compare the reprojected depth with
the measured depth of the next frame. The error is smallest at a depth scale of 1.00 and clearly higher at

Model Size
This appendix tests separately what increasing model size does for scale recovery and for structure recovery.
Families with several public sizes are evaluated with the protocol of Section 3: Metric3D-v2 (ViT-S, ViT-L,
ViT-g), MoGe-2 (ViT-S, ViT-B, ViT-L), MoGe-3 (ViT-L, ViT-G), UniDepthV2 (ViT-S, ViT-B, ViT-L) and
UniK3D (ViT-S, ViT-B, ViT-L). Metric3D-v2 is evaluated only with intrinsics, and the other four families
both without and with intrinsics, for 25 model evaluations in total. Metric δ1 measures overall accuracy and

In δ1 points, the gain from size is mainly in scale. A “step” is a comparison between two adjacent
sizes of the same family under the same intrinsics condition (for example, MoGe-2 without intrinsics from
ViT-S to ViT-B); there are 16 steps (Figure I.1). The point estimate of each step is the difference between
the two corresponding rows of Table I.1 (7-benchmark averages).
To judge whether this difference is reliable or noise that might vanish with another set of images of the same
kind, we estimate 95% intervals with a paired bootstrap: for each dataset, we draw with replacement as
many images as the dataset contains, use the same drawn images for the larger and the smaller size, and
compute the difference on that dataset; the differences of the 7 datasets are averaged within one draw to
give a “combined difference”; repeating this 1,000 times gives 1,000 combined differences, whose 2.5th and
97.5th percentiles form the interval. Using the same drawn images for both sizes makes the interval reflect
only how the difference in relative performance between the two sizes changes with another set of images,
without the extra noise of each size drawing different images (Section 3.3).
The scale-aligned δ1 rises at every step, and none of the 95% intervals contains zero, but the gains are small,
at most 0.022 for one step. In 14 of the steps the metric δ1 rises together with the scale-aligned δ1, by
2.7 to 12.0 times the scale-aligned gain of the same step (median 4.9 times). Measured in δ1, the gain in
metric accuracy from larger models in these families therefore comes mostly from better scale estimation. For
MoGe-2 without intrinsics, from ViT-S to ViT-L, the scale-aligned δ1 rises from 0.946 to 0.971, the metric δ1
from 0.651 to 0.769, and the part removable by a global scale shrinks from 0.296 to 0.203.
Size does not improve scale monotonically. In 2 steps the metric δ1 actually drops: for MoGe-3 with
intrinsics from ViT-L to ViT-G by 0.009 (95% interval of the change [-0.017, -0.001]), and for Metric3D-v2
from ViT-L to ViT-g by 0.014 (interval [-0.021, -0.008]), while the scale-aligned δ1 of both steps still rises (by
50


+0.008 and +0.002). In these two families the largest size has better structure but not better scale. Even at
the largest sizes, the error removable by a global scale remains considerable (0.096 for MoGe-3 ViT-G with
intrinsics, 0.254 for Metric3D-v2 ViT-g), so making models larger does not remove the scale failures described
in Sections 4 and 5. In δ1 the effect of size on structure looks small because structure is already above 0.92
at ViT-S; in relative terms, structure errors shrink about as much as scale errors (Table I.5). Each family
has only two or three sizes, and we evaluated only the one set of weights released by the authors for each
size, so size itself cannot be separated from training settings that change with size.
