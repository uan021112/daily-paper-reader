Abstract 

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces can drive robotic arms, but they keep the user inside the fast sensorimotor loop: every approach, correction, and grasp must be neurally specified. Whether intracortical population activity can instead serve as a task-level condition for a learned embodied policy—without symbolic commands or hand-designed skill libraries—remains unknown. NI-ACT addresses this question with a hierarchical brain–embodied framework: a brief motor-cortical signal is read once as a persistent intent, and a visuomotor policy then closes the perception–action loop from vision and proprioception. In a counterfactual grasping task where the scene cannot specify the target, the neural intent alone determined which object the robot grasped; replacing the intent changed the behavior accordingly. Passing the full population latent as a dedicated intent token, rather than a one-hot decoded direction, raised grasp completion from 69% to 81% over 132 held-out scenes, whereas a continuous direction alone did not improve on a one-hot label. Where the signal was read also mattered: in one dual-array session, with the readout window matched, premotor (PMd) intent completed more grasps than primary motor (M1) intent (77% vs. 53%), and the conventional post-cue M1 readout reached 42%. Preparatory activity was itself sufficient: in a center-out task, intent read only from PMd before the go cue—before any movement—drove complete grasps, and in one recording it completed more grasps than the same animal’s post-cue readout. These results show that how a neural signal is represented, and where and when it is read, jointly determine how effectively it conveys movement goals to an embodied agent. 

Keywords: Brain–computer interface, motor cortex, premotor cortex, preparatory activity, neural population latent, shared autonomy, embodied policy, robotic grasping. 

# 1 Introduction 

Intracortical brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) have enabled people with paralysis to reach, grasp, and manipulate objects with robotic arms [1–3]. In these systems, the decoder translates neural activity into continuous kinematics—typically end-effector velocity—at every control step. This gives the user fine-grained authority, but it also fixes a division of labor: the brain must specify not only what to manipulate but how to move, and it must remain inside the robot’s fast sensorimotor

1arXiv:2611.19332v1 [cs.AI] 14 Nov 2026 loop for the entire task. Every approach, alignment, and contact correction is produced through the neural channel, so decoding noise accumulates directly into the trajectory. The cost is not merely bandwidth; it is that a task-level intention is continuously re-expressed as a movement-level command. Biological motor control is organized differently. Dorsal premotor (PMd) and primary motor (M1) cortex represent the goal of an upcoming reach before movement begins [4–6], and preparatory activity can occupy a population subspace largely separate from the execution subspace [7, 8]. When the reach goal is dissociated from the initial hand trajectory—for example, when a barrier forces an initial movement away from the eventual target—PMd carries goal information that M1 does not [4, 9]. This suggests that the cortex naturally exposes a low-dimensional, persistent goal signal, while much of the moment-to-moment control is delegated to downstream circuits. A BCI that reads this goal once, rather than re-decoding movement continuously, would mirror that hierarchy. What the lower level would then need is a controller that can close the fast loop from its own observations. Learned visuomotor policies offer an artificial counterpart of that lower level. Given camera images and joint state, action-chunking transformers and diffusion policies can locate an object, approach it, close a gripper, and correct motion from feedback [10, 11]. They are conditioned on a task specification—a language instruction, a target image, or a one-hot object label—and excel at the how once the what is fixed. Recent foundation models [12–14] share the same pattern: a high-level condition is consumed once, and a low-level policy closes the fast sensorimotor loop. The condition itself, however, remains symbolic. Whether a graded neural population state can serve as that condition—so that a brief neural signal commands a complete manipulation—has remained untested. Testing this idea requires care, because a learned policy can appear to follow neural commands while actually exploiting the scene. If the target object is visually identifiable, the policy may succeed even when the neural condition is uninformative or wrong. We therefore designed a counterfactual grasping task in which every layout is demonstrated once with each object as the target, so the scene alone predicts the goal only at chance. Substituting the neural activity with a trial recorded for a different goal then provides a causal test: if the signal drives behavior, substitution should collapse success and redirect the robot. We present NI-ACT, built around this logic (Fig. 1). A temporal convolutional encoder maps a brief window of motor-cortical activity to a persistent intent; the intent conditions an action-chunking policy that executes the grasp from vision and proprioception. The intent is read once per task, so the neural channel operates at the rate of decisions while the policy operates at the rate of control. Three comparisons carry the argument. First, we vary what is passed to the policy—a one-hot decoded direction, a continuous goal direction, or the full population latent—to ask how much of the neural representation the policy needs. Second, using simultaneous, anatomically labeled PMd and M1 arrays in a maze task that dissociates goal from movement, we ask which area carries the goal. Third, we ask when the intent can be read, by restricting the input to PMd alone or to activity before the go cue. In each case, the neural signal alone determines which object is grasped, substitution redirects the robot, and closed-loop performance diverges from what offline decoding accuracy would predict: a dense population latent conditions execution more reliably than a decoded symbol, and PMd—even before movement—carries the goal more strongly than M1. 2Figure 1: The NI-ACT framework. # 2 Related Work 

2.1 Neural Control and Goal Decoding 

Intracortical BCIs have demonstrated reach-and-grasp and up to ten degrees of freedom in people with tetraplegia [1–3], typically by decoding continuous kinematics with Kalman-type or recalibrated decoders [15, 16]. This preserves user authority at every step, but it also makes the brain specify the trajectory itself: every correction is produced through the neural channel, and decoding noise accumulates along the trajectory. A second line decodes intended goals rather than movement. Cognitive prosthetics have decoded reach targets from posterior parietal cortex [17, 18]; shared-control frameworks infer user goals and arbitrate between user and robot [19, 20]; intracortical and non-invasive systems combine neural commands with vision-guided assistance [21–24]; NOIR maps decoded intent to parameterized skills [25]; and AI copilots improve EEG-driven pick-and-place [26]. These systems show that neural signals can specify what to do rather than how to move, but they share a common bottleneck: neural activity is converted into a discrete symbol—an object label, a target direction, or a skill index—which discards the graded geometry of the population response and requires a hand-designed mapping from neural state to action. NI-ACT differs in that the neural population representation conditions a single learned policy directly, without an intermediate symbol or a skill library. 

2.2 Motor-Cortical Population Representations 

Two features of motor-cortical population dynamics motivate NI-ACT. First, the goal is available before movement: PMd represents candidate reaches before selection [4], preparatory states shape movement initiation [9], and preparation and execution occupy distinct population subspaces [7, 8]. Second, the goal is carried by a population state, not a single unit: latent-variable models recover 3structured single-trial dynamics [27–29], and brief windows decode well above chance [28]. These findings motivate passing a population representation rather than a single decoded class, and reading the goal before movement. What has not been tested is whether such a representation can condition a learned embodied policy—whether the population state can serve as the interface itself, rather than as the input to a decoder that produces a symbol. 

2.3 Learned Visuomotor Policies 

Action chunking [10], diffusion policies [11], and vision–language–action models [30] map observations and a task condition to action sequences, and recent foundation models [12–14] share a common pattern: a high-level condition is consumed once, and a low-level policy closes the fast sensorimotor loop. These policies are strong at the how , but the condition itself remains symbolic in all of them—a language instruction, a target image, or a one-hot label. NI-ACT replaces that condition with a cortical intent. This is not merely a change of input modality: a language string or a one-hot label is a symbolic, low-dimensional condition, whereas a population latent is a graded, high-dimensional state that carries the geometry of the neural response. Whether a learned policy can exploit that state is an empirical question that the interface comparison and the packaging controls in this paper are designed to answer. 

# 3 The NI-ACT Framework 

3.1 Hierarchical Formulation and Neural Encoder 

Let S ∈ RT ×N be a window of spike counts from N units in T bins of 20 ms, It the front and wrist camera images, and qt ∈ R9 the joint state of a Franka arm. NI-ACT computes (z, ˆv, ℓ ) = Eθ(S), u = G(z, ˆv, ℓ ), ˆAt:t+31 = πϕ(It, q t, u ), (1) where Eθ is the neural encoder, G the intent interface, and πϕ an embodied policy that predicts a chunk of 32 joint targets. The intent u is computed once before the episode and held fixed, while the policy replans from new observations: the neural channel operates at the rate of decisions, the policy at the rate of control (every 32 steps, ≈ 0.53 s). Frame-by-frame decoding noise is therefore confined to the intent and cannot accumulate along the trajectory; an error in the intent itself propagates through the episode, which is why intent accuracy is measured directly (Section 5.1). 

Eθ is a temporal convolutional network with three 1-D convolutional blocks (256 channels, kernel 5, dilations 1, 2, 4), followed by temporal mean and max pooling and a projection to z ∈ R128 ; two heads read out a unit direction ˆ v ∈ R2 and logits ℓ ∈ R7. It is trained on the training trials of each recording with 

LN = CE( ℓ, c ∗) + 0 .2∥ˆv − v∗∥22, (2) with 10% spike dropout, early stopping on validation trials, and the regression weight chosen on validation; it is then frozen. Freezing ensures that all interfaces read the same encoder for a given neural source, so differences between interfaces cannot be attributed to encoder training, and it matches the deployment scenario in which the decoder is calibrated separately from the robot policy. Neural recordings and robot demonstrations come from independent sources and share only the goal the interface transmits. Each robot demonstration is paired with a neural trial of the same target direction, drawn at random from that direction’s training trials, and the robot keeps its own images, states, and actions. Neural trials are split into training and held-out sets before pairing, so no neural trial is shared between training and evaluation (Fig. 3b). This decoupling is deliberate: 4the neural side supplies the intent, the robot side the execution, and the interface is the only channel between them. 

3.2 Intent Interfaces 

The interface G selects one of the three encoder outputs (Fig. 2), so the interfaces differ only in what they pass: Hard-7 is a one-hot vector of arg max ℓ, the conventional target-selection bottleneck; 

Soft-2D is the continuous goal direction ˆ v; Full-Latent is the full population latent z. They instantiate three assumptions—that goal classes are equidistant and unambiguous (Hard-7), that reach direction is the only task-relevant variable (Soft-2D), or that the policy can exploit the full population state (Full-Latent)—and the closed-loop comparison tests which holds. Full-Latent differs from the other two in information content, dimensionality, and injection pathway (Fig. 3a); two packaging controls (Section 5.3) separate these factors. All interfaces use identical demonstrations, train/test splits, policy architecture, and hyperparameters. 

3.3 Embodied Policy 

πϕ is an action-chunking transformer (ACT) [10]. One ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone, shared by the two cameras, encodes 224 × 224 front and wrist images into 49 spatial tokens per camera; a four-layer transformer encoder (width 512, eight heads) attends over the image tokens, the joint-state token, and, for Full-Latent, the intent token; a decoder predicts 32 future joint targets. The policy receives no object coordinates or target labels; it must find the intended object in the images. One policy is trained per condition by behavior cloning for 20,000 steps (batch 64, learning rate 10 −4, 10 −5 for the backbones), so that each interface is optimized for its own input format. For Full-Latent, the intent is stored separately from the joint state, has its own normalization statistics, and enters through a linear projection (128 → 512) as a dedicated token. We disable ACT’s conditional VAE: our task is deliberately multimodal at the scene level (the same image corresponds to three correct grasps), and with the VAE active the policy could resolve the ambiguity by sampling a latent mode, leaving the neural intent as only one of several sources of target information. With the VAE disabled, the only consistent source of target identity across the counterfactual demonstrations is u, which makes the neural signal’s contribution identifiable in closed-loop behavior. # 4 Experimental Paradigm 

4.1 Neural Datasets and Readout Windows 

To evaluate whether motor-cortical population activity can condition an embodied policy, we leveraged three intracortical recording datasets from non-human primates (Table 2). Monkey J (maze reaching) supplied the primary closed-loop experiments and the area comparison: MC_Maze (DANDI 000128) [28] for general goal decoding, and a dual-array recording (DANDI 000070) with anatomically labeled PMd and M1 arrays to dissociate the goal from the initial movement (79% of trials contained barriers or distractors, so the initial hand movement often differed from the eventual goal). Monkey M (center-out reaching) tested generalization across subjects and tasks, where goal and movement coincide. Monkey T (center-out reaching) analyzed the temporal accumulation of goal information during preparation. A learned policy can easily exploit visual shortcuts (e.g., “grasp the red cube”) and ignore the neural condition. We therefore designed a counterfactual task in Isaac Sim in which the scene alone is insufficient. Three colored cubes are placed at three of seven directions around the workspace center, with pairwise separation ≥ 90 ◦; because of this constraint, only seven distinct bearing triples exist, and variety is introduced through color assignment and spatial jitter. Each layout is demonstrated three times by a scripted pick-and-place controller, once with each cube as the target, so the scene alone predicts the target with probability exactly 1 /3 and only the neural signal can resolve the ambiguity. The training set comprises 207 successful demonstrations (69 layouts, 189,819 frames); we evaluate on two held-out sets collected after training, Test Set 1 (30 scenes from 10 layouts) and Test Set 2 (102 scenes from 34 new layouts). The test sets include targets whose scripted demonstrations failed, preventing a “teacher success” bias. The counterfactual design also enables a causal test. For a given scene, we replace the held-out neural trial with a trial from a different goal direction, keeping the scene, policy, and execution identical. If the signal drives behavior, substitution should collapse success and redirect the robot. Because only three of seven bearings hold a cube, a substituted direction is empty in a sizable share of scenes, where failure is guaranteed regardless of the signal; we therefore isolate and report the informative subset, the scenes in which the substituted direction was occupied by another cube. 

4.3 Closed-Loop Evaluation 

Each rollout starts from the home pose and runs for up to 1,400 control steps, with the policy replanning every 32 steps. Outcomes are classified into three mutually exclusive categories: intended grasp (the target cube rises by at least 10 cm), wrong-object grasp (a non-target cube rises instead), and no grasp (no cube is lifted). We additionally record target selection—the cube nearest the hand when the gripper closes—to separate selection errors from execution errors. Statistical comparisons on identical scenes and neural trials use exact McNemar tests with Wilson 95% confidence intervals; where multiple comparisons are reported, we state their number rather than applying a correction, except for the exploratory direction-wise contrasts, which are Holm-corrected. 

# 5 Results 

Offline decoding accuracy is necessary but not sufficient for closed-loop success: a representation can decode the goal correctly yet condition the policy poorly. The results below follow one chain. Section 5.1 establishes that the goal is available in the neural signal; Section 5.2 that it is sufficient to command a grasp and causally determines which object is grasped; Section 5.3 that how the goal is packaged determines whether execution succeeds; Sections 5.4 and 5.5 that where the goal is read determines how much of it the channel carries and how often the grasp is completed; and Section 5.6 that the result generalizes across animals and is available before movement. The more striking result is that offline accuracy does not predict closed-loop success. The four encoders were 77.0%, 90.1%, 97.2%, and 97.7% accurate and decoded the scene’s direction in 101, 120, 126, and 129 of 132 cases. The conventional and window-matched M1 encoders differed by 13.1 points offline, yet this translated into only a non-significant 14-scene gain in completion; a smaller 7.1-point offline gap between window-matched M1 and PMd translated into a 32-scene (24-point) gain. A decoder that is better at recovering the goal is not necessarily a better condition for the policy. The gap was not limited to scenes in which M1 chose the wrong target. Among scenes whose direction was decoded correctly, PMd intent completed 102 of 126 (81.0%) and conventional M1 intent 52 of 101 (51.5%); among the 114 scenes in which both PMd and window-matched M1 decoded the target direction, PMd intent completed 91 and M1 intent 63. Failed rollouts ran the full episode without reaching the cube (median closest approach 13.6 cm for PMd and 13.8 cm for M1), so the intent affects execution as well as object selection. Adding M1 to PMd did not degrade closed-loop performance and improved it numerically (108 versus 102), but the data establish sufficiency of PMd, not superiority of the combined array: PMd+M1 succeeded in 13 scenes that PMd missed and failed in 7 that PMd completed, which is not large enough to rule out random variation ( p = 0 .26). 14 Substituted intent shows that all four arms depend on the neural signal for object choice: success fell to 1 of 132 scenes for PMd and PMd+M1, to 3 of 132 for window-matched M1, and to 11 of 132 for conventional M1. 

5.6 The Signal Generalizes Across Animals and Is Available Before Movement 

Sections 5.4 and 5.5 established that PMd intent read around movement is the strongest signal. Two further questions matter for clinical use: does the framework generalize to other animals, and is preparatory activity—the pure intention before any movement begins—sufficient to drive the robot? 

Generalization across animals. Applying the same framework to a second animal (monkey M), the robot approached the intended cube in 30/30 and 29/30 held-out scenes across two sessions, and substituted intent again collapsed this performance (2–4 of 30), reproducing the causal result of Section 5.2. Absolute grasp completion varied between sessions (19/30 and 15/30); this likely reflects the downstream policy’s execution variance across recordings rather than a failure of intention transmission. 

Availability before movement. To test whether pure intention suffices, we restricted the encoder input either to PMd alone or exclusively to the 500 ms before the go cue, when the animal knows the target but has not moved. PMd alone drove 17 complete grasps, and the pre-movement intent drove 20; in both cases the robot approached the intended cube in 28 and 29 of 30 scenes, and substituted intent collapsed success (0/30 and 1/30). 6.1 Goal-Level Control and the Source of Intent 

These results point to an alternative division of labor for assistive BCIs: instead of the brain continuously steering the robot through a fast sensorimotor loop, it declares a task-level goal and leaves moment-to-moment correction to a learned policy. Because the signal is consumed once per task, frame-by-frame decoding noise cannot accumulate; the neural channel fixes the object, and the policy determines whether the grasp is completed. What the results do not establish is that this division of labor is preferable to continuous decoding in bandwidth or workload—that comparison requires a continuous-decoder baseline and a user in the loop. The source of the intent matters as much as the fact that it is read once. When goal and movement are dissociated, PMd encodes where the reach will end, whereas M1 follows the ongoing movement; in center-out reaching, where the two coincide, they decode the goal about equally well. This agrees with the view that PMd represents movement goals and M1 the movement being executed [4, 7], and it matters for interface design: an embodied policy needs the goal, not the trajectory. The window-matched control makes this a closed-loop result rather than an offline inference: PMd intent completed 102 grasps and M1 intent 70 ( p = 5 .6 × 10 −6), and the difference persisted among scenes in which both decoded the correct direction. For users who cannot produce overt movement, the preparatory results point to an interface that can act as soon as the intention is formed, without waiting for motor execution. 

6.2 Intent Packaging 

Packaging is a design choice with closed-loop consequences. A dense 128-D code conditioned execution more reliably than a one-hot label (107 vs. 91 of 132, p = 0 .005), and the ordering held across three independent draws although the margin varied. Because both interfaces selected the target correctly, the gain lies in execution once the goal is set; the packaging controls attribute it to dimensionality and format, not to trial-level geometry or the injection pathway. For interface design, once the goal is decoded, it should be passed as a dense code rather than as a label. Whether the benefit comes from the code’s dimensionality alone or from the relational structure between neighboring directions that the prototype preserves is left open; a random 128-D code per direction would separate the two. 

6.3 Limitations and Future Work 

Evidence boundaries. Three boundaries define what these results can and cannot claim. First, neural recordings and robot demonstrations come from different subjects and are aligned only through the goal direction: the study models the interface computationally, not with a user in the loop, and the neural channel never receives feedback about the robot’s state. Second, the intent is spatial only—the reach direction is transmitted, while object identity and grasp type are not—and the task has three cubes on seven bearings, one arm, a fixed home pose, and no occlusion; the effective geometric variety is smaller than the number of layouts suggests. Third, no continuous-decoder baseline was run, so the data establish that a goal-level signal is a sufficient destination for a learned policy and that the identity of the grasped object depends on it, but not that goal-level control is preferable to continuous decoding in bandwidth or workload. 

Translational challenges. The area comparison rests on one session of one animal, so it is the strongest claim here with the thinnest independent support; the most accurate readout also spans movement onset and therefore includes activity during the reach. Decoders degrade across 16 days: trained on three sessions and tested on the fourth, accuracy was 71.0–87.3% after per-session normalization, against 91.4–99.2% within a session, and adding 10 labeled trials per direction from the new day raised it to 82.9–90.8%, so a brief daily calibration would be required. The population latents of different animals and sessions are not aligned, so nothing here establishes that a policy trained on one recording would accept the intent of another. Each closed-loop policy was trained once, and the two packaging-control policies were evaluated only on the second test set. 

Future work. Three steps would convert this computational demonstration into an interface claim: (1) a continuous-decoder baseline, decoded from the same neural data and driving the same arm, to test whether goal-level control reduces user burden in success, time to completion, and neural bandwidth; (2) evaluation with participants, with intent allowed to change within an episode, to test the assumption that one read per task is enough; and (3) extending the intent to object identity and grasp type, together with transfer of the policy to a physical robot, to test whether the representation remains useful once perception is real. 

# 7 Conclusion 

NI-ACT connects motor-cortical population activity to an embodied visuomotor policy through a persistent goal signal. Three findings carry the argument. First, a brief population signal is sufficient to command a complete grasp, and substituting it redirects the robot: the neural channel, not the scene, fixes the object. Second, how the goal is packaged matters: a dense 128-D code conditioned execution more reliably than a one-hot label (107 versus 91 of 132 scenes, p = 0 .005), and packaging controls attribute the gain to dimensionality and format rather than to trial-specific geometry or the injection pathway. Third, where and when the goal is read matters: PMd carried the goal more strongly than M1 when movement was dissociated from it, and reading only PMd, or only activity before the go cue, was sufficient to command complete grasps. These results establish sufficiency, not superiority. No continuous-decoder baseline was run, the area comparison rests on one session of one animal, and no user was in the loop; whether goal-level control reduces bandwidth or workload relative to continuous decoding remains open. What the data do show is that a BCI need not be a continuous joystick for a robotic arm. By transmitting a persistent, population-level goal and delegating execution to an embodied policy, such an interface can restore a more natural division of labor, in which the brain decides what to do and the machine works out how to do it.
