FlowRow: Learning Navigation Behaviors in Agricultural Environments
with Long-Horizon Goal-Conditioned Flow Matching Policies
Utkarsh Bajpai
C´edric Pradalier
St´ephanie Aravecchia
Abstract— Autonomous monitoring and intervention in semi-
structured agricultural environments, such as orchards and
vineyards, require mobile robots to navigate to locations across
diverse field configurations. This entails performing navigation
behaviors including in-row driving, row turning and skipping,
and inter-row traversal. Recent learning-based approaches have
enabled robust in-row navigation from onboard perception, but
navigation across rows typically requires a priori knowledge of
field geometry to specify and tune row-transition maneuvers,
preventing arbitrary transitions between rows. We address this
challenge by framing navigation in these environments as a
goal-directed navigation task, where a long-horizon goal is
expressed relative to the robot and a planner generates local
paths based on the goal and the robot’s perceptual observations.
We present FlowRow, a flow-matching-based local planner
which models the distribution of diverse agricultural navigation
behaviors as SE(2) paths using the long-horizon goal and
depth perception. To enable systematic evaluation, we introduce
RowNavBench, a navigation benchmark for semi-structured
agricultural environments developed in Isaac Sim, comprising
900 episodes across grape vineyards, lemon orchards, and apple
orchards with varying row lengths, row and plant spacings,
plant positions, and weed growth. FlowRow achieves 87%
success, compared to 44% for the strongest generative behavior
cloning baseline retrained on the same demonstrations. We will
release our benchmark, data, and code upon acceptance.
I. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous mobile robots are increasingly deployed in
agricultural fields for precision intervention tasks such as
crop inspection, and harvesting, which require reliable nav-
igation to target locations. Such robots operate in semi-
structured environments such as orchards, vineyards, and
greenhouses, where crops are arranged in rows separated by
navigable corridors with headlands at their ends. Large fields
require long-horizon navigation, while crop rows constrain
how the robot can traverse the environment. A target location
may be spatially close to the robot yet lie in another row,
requiring the robot to first move away from it, transition
through a headland, and approach it from a different direc-
tion. The appropriate trajectory therefore depends not only
on the target location, but also on the surrounding row
structure. This gives rise to distinct navigation behaviors,
including in-row driving, headland turning, and row skipping,
as shown in Fig. 1 (a). Agricultural environments also vary
considerably in vegetation density and obstacle distribution.
Vegetation growth can narrow corridors or alter their appar-
ent structure, while rocks, weeds, and other obstacles can

In this work, we consider the problem of goal-directed
navigation in semi-structured agricultural environments.
Specifically, we consider a robot initialized in an unknown
row-crop field with headlands at the ends of the rows. The
robot has no prior map or knowledge of field geometry, such
as crop and row spacing, but can perceive its surroundings
through onboard perception. Given a relative goal position,
the robot must navigate to it while avoiding obstacles and
performing navigation behaviors, including in-row driving,
headland turning, row skipping, or combinations thereof.
Existing agricultural navigation systems often rely on
tracking pre-computed reference paths generated from known
field geometry [15]. While effective, these approaches can
degrade from localization errors under canopy, and environ-
mental variation. Recent perception-based methods reduce
dependence on localization by using onboard sensing for
localization-free in-row navigation [2], [18]. Such methods
estimate the row centerline for row following, while row
transitions are handled separately through rule-based maneu-
vers that assume known row geometry [4]. As a result, these
methods do not support navigation to arbitrary goal positions
and are limited to turning into the adjacent rows without

row skipping or traversing to non-adjacent rows. In contrast,
we unify these navigation behaviors through a single goal-
conditioned policy which performs local planning driven by
egocentric perception, as illustrated in Fig 1 (b).
The main contributions of this paper are:
• We introduce FlowRow, a flow-matching policy trained
entirely in simulation for long-horizon, goal-directed
local planning in unknown, semi-structured agricultural
environments. FlowRow requires no prior knowledge of
environment geometry and supports in-row navigation,
row turning, row skipping, and inter-row transitions.
• We introduce RowNavBench, a simulation benchmark
comprising 900 episodes across variations in row length,
row spacing, row curvature, and vegetation conditions.
We evaluate FlowRow on RowNavBench against depth-
based local planning baselines, demonstrating over 40%
higher success across the benchmark.
• We evaluate FlowRow under localization errors against
an oracle that tracks a pre-planned global reference
path, and show that local planning with FlowRow is
more robust to localization errors and results in fewer
navigation failures. We further validate FlowRow with
a real-world zero-shot deployment.
II. RELATED WORK
Autonomous navigation in agricultural fields has been
studied extensively [2], [15], [18]. Conventional navigation
systems require the robot to navigate to target locations by
following a global reference path defined as a sequence
of GNSS waypoints using prior knowledge of the field
geometry. This approach is effective when field structure is
known and localization remains reliable. However, GNSS
performance can degrade under dense canopy and in environ-
ments affected by multipath, introducing localization errors
that affect reference-path tracking.
A. Perception-based Navigation in Agricultural Fields
To

row-following, where the objective is to drive in-row without
navigating to a given goal position. Ahmadi et al. [2] demon-
strate visual-servoing-based row following with a straddler
robot by tracking crop lines from a top-down view over
small row crops. De Silva et al. [7] propose a learning-
based method for crop-line estimation from similar top-down
imagery. Sivakumar et al. [16], [18] instead demonstrate row-
following in maize fields from RGB images by predicting
crop-row keypoints and tracking the inferred row center with
controller. Similarly, Kim et al. [10] estimate the row center
from LiDAR range images and track it with a controller.
Headland turns are typically handled separately using state
machines or rule-based maneuvers with geometric priors. A
common strategy is to exit the current row, execute a 90◦
turn, advance by a distance determined from known field
geometry such as row spacing, and turn 90◦again before
resuming row following. Liu et al. [12] and Ahmadi et al. [2]
use such predefined maneuvers together with side-facing
cameras to determine alignment during the turn. Similarly,
Kim et al. [10] use intensity changes in LiDAR range images
to determine the robot’s alignment with the rows during the
maneuver. Ahmadi et al. [1] extend their visual-servoing ap-
proach to an omnidirectional straddler platform that performs
multi-row switching by counting visible crop rows in the
top-down view and driving across them. Prior perception-
based approaches have primarily focused on in-row driving,
with headland transitions handled through a combination of
onboard sensing and predefined maneuvers based on field-
geometry priors. To the best of our knowledge, prior work
has not demonstrated a local planner that jointly supports in-
row navigation, row skipping, and headland transitions from
egocentric observations without such priors.
B. Navigation with Imitation Learning
Imitation learning provides an alternative to designing
separate navigation behaviors by learning action-generation
policies from demonstrations. Diffusion [6] and flow match-
ing policies [11] are generative formulations for modeling
multimodal action distributions. Diffusion learns through
iterative denoising, while flow matching learns a continuous
vector field that transports samples from noise to the demon-
strated trajectory distribution. In navigation, NoMaD [20], a
diffusion policy, and FlowNav [8], a flow-matching policy,
navigate toward image-goals using previously acquired topo-
logical maps in structured indoor and outdoor environments.
NavDP [5] considers target-location navigation from RGB-
D observations, while SanD-Planner [21] encodes goals in
cartesian space and performs local planning from depth
observations by generating multiple diffusion-based trajec-
tory candidates as R3 splines. However, transferring such
policies to agricultural environments remains challenging.
CropFollow++ [16] shows that NoMaD can fail to identify
the crop corridor under canopy, while the same authors’
later diffusion-based study focuses only on agricultural row
turning [17] and reports difficulties with in-row control and
varied initial conditions. These results suggest that generative
navigation policies do not transfer trivially to row-crop
environments, and that learning an isolated turning behavior
does not address long-horizon goal-directed navigation. We
instead consider a single local planner that must infer and
compose in-row driving, headland turning, and row-skipping
behaviors from egocentric observations and a target location.
We therefore introduce FlowRow, a flow-matching policy
for long-horizon local planning in unknown agricultural
environments without explicit field-geometry priors.

IV. APPROACH
We present FlowRow, a goal-conditioned policy that maps
egocentric depth images and a body-frame goal to naviga-
tion behaviors for long-horizon navigation in agricultural
environments. In this section, we describe the key design
choices that enable the policy to generate these behaviors
from onboard observations. Our policy doesn’t assume a
map of the environment or prior knowledge of its geometry.
Instead, it acts as a local planner that generates a short-
horizon path conditioned on the long-horizon goal and the
robot’s observations. An overview of our policy architecture
is shown in Fig. 2. At each planning timestep t, the policy
receives the body-frame goal pb
g and maintains a history
of recent depth images Ht. These inputs are encoded and
fused by a transformer to produce a context representation
C. Conditioned on C, the flow-matching policy generates M
SE(2) B-Spline control poses. These control poses are used
to compute K candidate paths. A path τ ∗is selected from the
candidates and tracked by a pure-pursuit controller [14] at a
constant velocity. At the next timestep, the policy receives
a new depth observation, updates Ht and pb
g, and generates
a new set of candidate paths. This receding-horizon process
continues until the robot reaches the world-frame goal pw
g .
A. Long-Horizon Goal Conditioning
As a local planner, FlowRow predicts a short-horizon path
within the observable space of the depth image, while the
goal pw
g may lie tens of meters away. Depending on the
robot’s distance to the goal, it may lie within or beyond
this planning horizon. When beyond the horizon, the goal
must guide local planning over multiple planning steps. We
encode the goal in the robot’s body frame using its range
and bearing, and train the policy on both goals within and
beyond the planning horizon.

the prediction horizon, so FlowRow learns to generate the
appropriate local path rather than move greedily toward the
goal. For example, for a goal in another row, the policy can
generate continued in-row motion followed by a headland
turn instead of a direct lateral path through the crops. This
enables FlowRow to learn the field-constrained behaviors
required for long-horizon navigation.
B. Context Encoding
The goal and depth history form the conditioning input to
FlowRow. At each planning timestep, the policy maintains a
history of four depth observations in a history buffer, Ht =
[It−3, .., It]. We encode this history using the tokenized
depth encoder of SanD-Planner [21]. Each depth image is
processed by a shared ResNet-18 [9], truncated before its
final stage to preserve spatial information relevant to local
obstacle avoidance. The resulting feature maps are reduced to
an 8×12 spatial grid and projected to an embedding dimen-
sion of D = 256, producing 96 tokens per observation. We
augment these tokens with fixed two-dimensional sinusoidal
positional embeddings and learned frame-index embeddings
to capture spatial and temporal information. The goal pb
g
is encoded as φ(pb
g) as described in IV-A. The depth and
goal tokens are concatenated and processed by a four-layer
transformer [20], [21] to produce the context representation
C, used to condition the flow-matching model.
C. Action Representation
Given the context representation C, our flow-matching
policy generates actions. We represent the action to be
generated as a set of B-spline control poses that compactly
parameterize a short-horizon local path. Unlike position-
only control points used to parameterize B-splines [21], we
represent each control pose in SE(2), following related work
on Lie-group splines [13], [19]. This explicitly represents
both position and heading along the path, which is important
for our wheeled platform. To express the short-horizon path,
our policy emits M = 12 control poses Xj ∈SE(2),
j = 0, . . . , M −1, expressed relative to the current robot
body frame, each with position pj = (xj, yj)⊤∈R2 and
heading θj. We decode the control poses into a continuous
path using a cubic cumulative B-spline on SE(2) [13], [19].

V. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
We design our experimental evaluation to assess the effec-
tiveness, generalization, and robustness of FlowRow across
simulated and real-world agricultural environments with var-
ied field geometry. Specifically, our experiments address four
key aspects: (i) performance on RowNavBench compared to
existing local planning approaches, (ii) zero-shot deployment
of the simulation-trained policy in a real-world agricultural
environment, (iii) robustness to localization errors compared
to a controller tracking a pre-planned global reference path,
and (iv) the role of our goal representation, action repre-
sentation, and training supervision in enabling long-horizon
navigation through systematic ablations.

VI. CONCLUSION
In this work, we proposed FlowRow, a long-horizon goal-
conditioned flow matching policy for navigation in semi-
structured agricultural environments. We introduce Row-
NavBench, a benchmark for navigation in agricultural en-
vironments. We introduce a novel long-horizon goal condi-
tioning mechanism to navigate long horizon environments
with a local planner by training with goals beyond the
training horizon. We also introduced a novel action space
for flow matching policies as SE(2) spline control poses.
Our experimental results compare the performance of our
approach to state-of-the art generative imitation learning
baselines and show that our approach achieves 87% success
on this task. We further ablate our representation choices
and design decisions. We additionally show that FlowRow
is robust to localization errors. Finally, we show zero-
shot deployement of FlowRow in a real world agricultural
environment. Overall, our findings demonstrate how long-
horizon flow matching policies can be useful in agricultural
navigation. Future work will conduct extensive evaluation of
the sim2real generalization of this approach.
