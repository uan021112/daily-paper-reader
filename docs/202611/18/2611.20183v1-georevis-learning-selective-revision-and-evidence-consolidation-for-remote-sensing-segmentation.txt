# GeoReVis: Learning Selective Revision and Evidence Consolidation for Remote Sensing Segmentation 

## Ke An Liang Chen Yupei Wang *

## Beijing Institute of Technology 

## Abstract 

Recent methods try to inspect and revise masks to mitigate referential ambiguity and localization errors caused by com-plex spatial relations in language-guided remote sensing segmentation. However, these solutions often lead to Revi-sion Degradation : revising missing regions may introduce errors into previously correct ones. Even a globally better mask can omit correct regions found in inferior masks. We term this Evidence Fragmentation : correct target evidence is scattered across imperfect masks. All masks generated by the model at this time are suboptimal. We propose GeoReVis ,which unifies revision guided by execution feedback, Candi-date Selection, and Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation. It learns fine-grained revision from real actions that improve segmentation when executed and learns to select a better mask through quality comparisons between actual candi-dates, reducing the risk of degradation during revision. The selected whole mask can still discard useful regions. GeoRe-Vis therefore retains all candidates across rounds, partitions their spatial disagreements into composable regions, and jointly reconstructs regions conditioned on the full instruc-tion and context. This makes complementary and fragmen-tary evidence reusable regardless of its source candidate’s overall quality, yielding improved segmentation. We further build a data pipeline to generate fine-grained multi-round segmentation trajectories and derive supervision for revi-sion actions, candidate selection, and region composition from the model’s own outputs, extending training beyond whole-mask IoU supervision. We also propose ReVis-Eval ,a benchmark protocol evaluating multi-round revision and cross-round evidence consolidation accuracy. Experiments on ReVis-Eval and 5 benchmarks for remote sensing reason-ing and referring segmentation demonstrate the effectiveness of GeoReVis.

1arXiv:2611.20183v1 [cs.CV] 15 Nov 2026 1. Introduction 

Language-guided remote sensing segmentation identifies the pixels specified by instructions that refer to multiple targets at different granularities [12, 35]. A prevailing paradigm uses a vision-language model (VLM) to interpret the instruc-tion and generate segmentation prompts, which a segmen-tation decoder converts into masks [4, 31, 35, 40]. Remote sensing scenes contain densely arranged, similar-looking objects with interwoven structures and complex spatial rela-tions [19, 31, 39]. Even an accurately located prompt may therefore fail to delimit the intended target: the predicted mask can include surrounding background or closely associ-ated parts that fall outside the instruction’s scope [4, 40]. The existing single-pass paradigm is inherently disadvantaged in handling such errors. Recent methods address this problem by feeding pre-dicted masks back to the model for feedback revision [8, 34, 41, 42], or by comparing multiple predictions to select a bet-ter candidate mask [10]. These methods extend single-pass prediction through feedback and candidate selection. How-ever, these solutions raise two subsequent questions: Does revision necessarily improve the initial mask? Even if it does, is the best candidate mask the best segmentation the system can obtain? 

Revision can change pixels beyond its intended correction region. In Fig. 1 (a), revision recovers the tower rim but removes a larger correct region, lowering intersection over union (IoU) from 92.2% to 77.5%. We assess this Revision Degradation across all 1,158 LaSeRS samples using two feedback-based mod-els: GeoReVis-R and SocioReasoner ∗. GeoReVis-R is our revision-only variant of GeoReVis, while SocioReasoner ∗

uses Qwen3.5-2B and frozen SAM3, trained on LaSeRS with optical imagery and no digital maps. The revision improves more than 1% IoU on 38.7% and 24.9% samples. However, it decreases more than 1% IoU on 27.8% and 19.3% samples at the same time. These results reveal that Revision Degra-dation is prevalent, even when the mean change in IoU is positive (Supplementary Sec. A.1). Revision should not be adopted unconditionally. These observations lead to Insight 1: Revision may damage a correct region. 

Beyond Revision Degradation, we find that the IoU of the revised mask is nearly on par with that of the initial mask on 33.51% and 55.79% samples. After inspection, we discover that this does not mean the revision does nothing. Gains and losses often emerge simultaneously and offset one another. We further analyze this phenomenon in depth. For con-venience, we denote the mask with the highest IoU as the optimal candidate and the other masks as suboptimal candi-dates. In Fig. 1 (b), the optimal candidate correctly segments the downstream water and reaches 83.1% IoU, yet it drops the river adjoining the bridge, 15.2% of the target area, which a suboptimal candidate (66.9%) had captured. At this time, selecting either whole mask leaves useful target evidence unused. We perform examination on 1158 samples. Even assum-ing the optimal candidate can always be selected, the IoU remains far lower than that of the regional oracle that ac-commodates target evidence from all candidates (GeoReVis-R: 66.94 vs 75.39, SocioReasoner ∗: 63.90 vs 68.97). We term this dispersion of correct target evidence across imper-fect candidates Evidence Fragmentation (Supplementary Sec. A.2). Higher whole-mask quality does not imply that all useful evidence has been retained, yielding Insight 2: An optimal candidate mask can still omit useful evidence in suboptimal candidates. 

These insights motivate GeoReVis , a framework that learns Selective Revision and Evidence Consolidation for remote sensing segmentation. First, Feedback-Guided Re-vision learns fine-grained actions whose benefits have been verified through execution. Second, Candidate Selection 

compares the resulting candidate masks and retains a refer-ence mask to reduce Revision Degradation. Evidence Fragmentation, however, means that optimal mask selection can still discard useful target evidence. We therefore introduce Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation ,which retains all candidates, including rejected masks, and partitions their spatial disagreements into composable re-gions. Conditioned on the full instruction and image context, the model jointly determines which regions to combine, link-ing local decisions to the quality of the reconstructed mask. It then compares this reconstruction with the selected refer-ence mask to determine the next round’s input. Correct regions can thus contribute independently of their source candidate, converting evidence scattered across can-didates into a more correct segmentation. As shown in Fig. 1 (b), GeoReVis can effectively mitigate Revision Degradation and Evidence Fragmentation, significantly boosting segmen-tation accuracy to near the regional oracle. To support GeoReVis’ learning, we build an Execution-Driven Data Pipeline that generates fine-grained multi-round segmentation trajectories and derives supervision for revision actions, candidate selection, and region composition from the model’s own outputs. We label these outputs based on ground truth and segmenter evaluation, teaching each component which decisions improve segmentation based on its actual behavior. This extends training beyond coarse whole-mask IoU supervision. We also propose ReVis-Eval ,a benchmark protocol for measuring Revision Degradation and Evidence Fragmentation. ## 2. Related Work 

Language-guided remote sensing segmentation. Con-ventional task-specific segmenters such as RMSIN encode images and language separately [19]. RSRefSeg 2 and CROSS couple contrastive VLMs with SAM to strengthen spatial grounding [4, 23]. Generative VLMs enable implicit reasoning [15]: GeoPixel and SegEarth-R2 connect language reasoning to mask decoding [31, 35], while Think2Seg-RS learns spatial prompts for a frozen segmenter [40]. Beyond instruction-to-mask grounding, GeoReVis learns when to adopt corrections by supervising revision actions and candi-date selection with actual segmentation outcomes. 

Feedback revision and candidate selection in segmenta-tion. Generative VLMs in SegAgent and SAM-Veteran learn mask-guided corrective prompting [8, 42]. SocioRe-asoner revises spatial prompts from satellite and map ob-servations [34]. RSAgent uses rendered mask history for tool interaction and candidate selection [10], while GuideSeg verifies proposed regions before refinement [33]. FocalClick, a non-VLM interactive segmenter, preserves existing regions through local refinement and progressive merging [5]. GeoReVis extends Candidate Selection to Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation by partitioning spatial disagreements into regions, including rejected candidates’ evidence. Reconstructed-mask quality supervises joint com-position under the full instruction, decoupling local evidence reuse from candidate quality. 

## 3. GeoReVis 

GeoReVis separates mask revision, Candidate Selection, and region composition (Fig. 2). Selective Revision addresses Revision Degradation, while Cross-Mask Evidence Con-solidation recovers useful regions omitted by whole-mask selection. The Execution-Driven Data Pipeline supervises these decisions through their actual segmentation effects. Given image I and instruction q, Target Decomposition and Spatial Grounding prompt frozen SAM3 [3] to produce initial masks M0. Each target undergoes three rounds of revision, selection, and consolidation before the final masks are merged. All learned roles use Qwen3.5-2B [25] with role-specific LoRA adapters [11]. Actual executions supervise each decision. We first train Target Decomposition and Spatial Grounding on benchmark 4annotations to establish Initial Segmentation. The pipeline in Fig. 2 (right) generates training states through the same interfaces as inference. Teacher proposals, proposal-seeded search, and rules supply candidate actions. ## 4. ReVis-Eval 

ReVis-Eval is a benchmark protocol built on LaSeRS [35] with two evaluations. Revision Degradation Evaluation mea-sures feedback-driven improvement and preservation of cor-rect regions. Evidence Fragmentation Evaluation measures whether consolidating evidence across candidates improves on the optimal candidate. 

Revision Degradation Evaluation. We use the seg-mentation pipelines of GeoReVis, VisionReasoner ‡, and SocioReasoner ‡ without revision to generate one initial mask per model for each of the 1,158 LaSeRS samples. Filtering out masks with IoU above 0.95, which have limited room for improvement, leaves 3,201 initial masks. During evaluation, each method receives the same image, instruction, and initial mask for each of the 3,201 items and performs three revision rounds. After that, we report four metrics: 

(1) IoU Gain ( ∆IoU) : Mean IoU difference between final and initial masks. Higher ∆IoU indicates greater im-provement through revision. 

(2) Feedback Gain (FG) : Mean final-IoU difference be-tween paired runs with and without mask overlays at the same budget. Higher FG indicates more effective use of segmentation feedback. 

(3) Degradation Rate (DR) : Proportion of initial masks whose IoU drops by more than 0.01 after revision. Lower DR indicates less frequent Revision Degradation. 

(4) Correct-Pixel Preservation (CP) : Mean fraction of ini-tially correct target pixels retained in the final mask. Higher CP indicates better preservation of correct re-gions. 

Evidence Fragmentation Evaluation. We run GeoRe-Vis without evidence consolidation, SAM-Veteran ∗, and SocioReasoner ∗ for three revision rounds on 1,158 LaSeRS samples and collect their trajectories. We retain all candidate masks from each trajectory and take its final output as the reference mask B, yielding 3,474 banks with 4-10 masks each. During evaluation, tested methods receive the same im-age, instruction, and candidate bank, and produce a final mask without additional segmenter calls. Alongside final IoU, we report four metrics: 

(1) Best-Whole Gap (BWG) : Mean IoU difference be-tween the final mask and the candidate with the highest GT IoU. Positive BWG indicates improvement beyond optimal whole-mask selection. 

(2) BWG on High-Potential Banks (BWG P ): BWG av-eraged over 812 banks whose regional oracle exceeds the optimal candidate by more than 0.05 IoU. Higher 5BWG P indicates better use of available consolidation potential. 

(3) Historical Recall (HR) : Mean fraction of target pixels present in the bank but absent from B that are recovered in the final mask. Higher HR indicates more complete recovery of fragmented evidence. 

(4) Added Precision (AP) : Mean fraction of pixels added to B that belong to the target. Higher AP indicates more precise evidence recovery. Construction details and metric formulas appear in Supple-mentary Secs. D.2–D.3. 

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks and Datasets. We evaluate on five remote sensing segmentation benchmarks. LaSeRS [35] is the main benchmark, with 1,158 test queries covering nine dimen-sions across target granularity, multiplicity, reasoning, and instruction length. EarthReason [16] evaluates reasoning segmentation, while RRSIS-D [19], RefSegRS [39], and RISBench [7] evaluate referring segmentation. GeoReVis is trained separately on each benchmark’s corresponding train-ing split. ReVis-Eval uses held-out LaSeRS scenes to assess feedback revision and Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation under the controlled inputs specified in Sec. 4. 

Evaluation Metrics. Following the benchmark protocols and prior studies [24, 35], we use gIoU and cIoU as eval-uation metrics in all experiments. We report performance across nine key dimensions on LaSeRS [35]. ReVis-Eval additionally includes Revision Degradation Evaluation and Evidence Fragmentation Evaluation. Its metric definitions are given in Sec. 4. 

Baselines. As shown in Tables 1, 2, and 3, we conduct a comprehensive comparison with existing SOTA single-pass and feedback-revision segmentation models. All feedback-revision models are evaluated for three revision rounds. EarthReason comparisons focus on generative VLM-based reasoning segmenters. Referring benchmarks additionally in-clude task-specific models designed for explicit expressions. For fairness, we report published results on each benchmark when available. Otherwise, we retrain released implemen-tations on the same settings and mark these reproductions with ‡.To control for backbone and segmenter differences, we additionally train VisionReasoner, SAM-Veteran, and So-cioReasoner with Qwen3.5-2B and frozen SAM3, denoted by ∗. For SocioReasoner, we provide only the satellite image. Retraining settings and model-specific adaptations appear in Supplementary Sec. D.1. 

Implementation Details. We use Qwen3.5-2B and frozen SAM3 as a backbone for GeoReVis. We train rank-16 LoRA adapters for target decomposition, grounding, feedback as-sessment, revision, candidate selection, and evidence con-solidation. We use AdamW [22] with effective batch sizes of 16. Consolidation training uses four A6000 GPUs, with first-pass learning rates of 5 × 10 −6 and 3 × 10 −4 for the adapter and regional readout, respectively. At inference, GeoReVis performs three revision rounds, each with Candidate Selection and Evidence Consolidation. The readout architecture and training schedules appear in Supplementary Secs. B.4 and C.3. 

5.2. Main Results 

Results on LaSeRS. LaSeRS is the most challenging RS segmentation benchmark [35]. GeoReVis achieves 72.00% gIoU and 77.01% cIoU, leading all nine dimensions in Ta-ble 1. It improves gIoU over GeoReason, the SOTA single-pass segmentation model, by 7.04% and over SegEarth-R2, designed for multi-target and multi-granularity seg-mentation, by 14.80%. GeoReVis surpasses the backbone-matched VisionReasoner ∗ and feedback-revision model SocioReasoner ∗ by 8.06% and 8.87% gIoU, respectively. These results establish GeoReVis’s broad advantage across hierarchical granularity, target multiplicity, reasoning re-quirements, and linguistic variability in complex remote sensing scenes. 

Results on EarthReason. GeoReason emphasizes instruc-tion understanding and spatial grounding [9]. GeoReVis leads all four EarthReason metrics, averaging 79.36% (Ta-ble 2). These results demonstrate more accurate interpre-tation of diverse implicit instructions and segmentation of the intended targets. Despite annotation noise in execution-driven supervision, some predictions delineate targets even more accurately than GT masks (Supplementary Sec. E.7). 

Results on Referring Segmentation Benchmarks. Geo-ReVis also achieves the highest gIoU on all three referring benchmarks (Table 3). It surpasses UniGeoSeg on RRSIS-D by 1.10%, CROSS on RefSegRS by 1.21%, and RSRefSeg 2 on RISBench by 1.70%. Thus, GeoReVis effectively delin-eates explicitly specified targets as well as those described through complex implicit instructions. 

Overall. With only 2B parameters, GeoReVis leads all five benchmarks. Its advantage is largest on LaSeRS, which additionally stresses part-level delineation and multi-target composition [35]. These tasks expose the two problems identified in our insights: revision may damage a correct region, and an optimal candidate mask can still omit useful evidence in suboptimal candidates. 5.4. Ablation Studies 

Component Ablation. Table 6 shows complementary component gains. Verified Revision improves Initial Seg-mentation from 63.28% to 64.64% gIoU, whereas Unveri-fied Revision lowers it to 62.71%. Candidate Selection adds 1.94% gIoU and reduces Degradation Rate from 27.81% to 10.36%. The concept candidate and multi-round feed-back add 1.32% and 1.29% gIoU, respectively. These gains demonstrate the benefits of execution-verified supervision and selective adoption. Despite higher gIoU, multi-round feedback increases the Omitted-Evidence Rate from 35.92% to 38.69%. Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation lowers it to 27.46% and adds 2.81% gIoU and 3.58% cIoU. The gIoU gain reaches 4.46% on fragmentation-heavy queries (Supplementary Table 7), demonstrating effective evidence recovery. Our method also surpasses Independent Regions, which removes only cross-region aggregation, and general aggregation strategies (Sup-plementary Sec. E.3). 

Execution-Driven Data Pipeline. Our pipeline supervises revision actions, candidate selection, and region composi-tion from actual segmentation outcomes. Execution-verified labels improve single-round gIoU by 1.20% over unverified teacher supervision and 0.86% over synthetic pairs. Joint quality, region, and adoption supervision also benefits con-solidation (Supplementary Sec. E.2). The pipeline thus links each learned decision to its segmentation outcome. 

Revision Rounds and Computational Cost. GeoReVis reaches 68.13%, 70.26%, 72.00%, and 72.06% gIoU with one to four rounds, respectively. Two rounds outperform all Table 1 baselines at 3.98 seconds per query on four GPUs, versus 5.41 for SAM-Veteran and 6.68 for SegAgent. We use three rounds at 5.99 seconds per query, as a fourth adds little accuracy. Lightweight role adapters retain a 2B-scale VLM per call, while shared visual features reduce multi-stage overhead (Supplementary Sec. E.4). 

## 6. Conclusion 

GeoReVis addresses Revision Degradation and Evidence Fragmentation through Selective Revision and Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation. Results on five benchmarks and ReVis-Eval demonstrate superior segmentation, better preser-vation of correct regions, and recovery of evidence from suboptimal candidates.

A.1. Revision Degradation Despite Local Repair 

A revision changes both the target pixels recovered and the errors introduced elsewhere. To separate these effects, let M

and H denote the initial and revised masks, and let G be the target. The corrected pixel set is 

F = (( H \ M ) ∩ G) ∪ (( M \ H) \ G). (1) The newly erroneous pixel set is 

D = (( M \ H) ∩ G) ∪ (( H \ M ) \ G). (2) The two terms in F recover missing target pixels and remove background. Those in D remove correct target pixels and add background. Thus, repairing a visible error and improving the complete mask are different outcomes. Table 1 extends the main paper’s Fig. 1. GeoReVis-R is GeoReVis with revision only. SocioReasoner ∗ uses Qwen3.5-2B and frozen SAM3 trained on LaSeRS, with the optical image and instruction as input. Both are evaluated on all 1,158 LaSeRS samples. Revision Degradation, defined by an IoU decrease ex-ceeding 0.01, occurs in 27.81% and 19.34% of samples. Their positive mean gains therefore coexist with substantial per-sample damage. This is the empirical basis for Candi-date Selection: the executed mask, rather than the proposed correction alone, determines whether an update is useful. A near-stable revision has an absolute IoU change of at most 0.01. Among these samples, 31.44% for GeoReVis-R and 26.78% for SocioReasoner ∗ have both |F| and |D| 

exceeding 1% of |G|. A small net change can therefore conceal meaningful repair and damage. Mask feedback needs to be followed by an explicit comparison of what the execution changed.

## B. Complete Method and Mathematical Foun-dations 

B.1. Target Decomposition and Initial Segmenta-tion 

Given image I and complete instruction q, Target Decompo-sition produces target descriptions q1, . . . , q L. Each descrip-tion retains the relevant entity, relation, part, and collection constraints. Spatial Grounding converts ql into boxes and signed points, and frozen SAM3 [3] produces the correspond-ing initial mask. The full instruction remains visible to later roles, so a part-level edit is interpreted within the requested scene-level relationship. All learned roles use Qwen3.5-2B [25] with role-specific LoRA adapters [11]. The grounding adapter is merged into the revision backbone. Candidate Selection is trained on the original Qwen backbone and then adapted to multi-round ob-servations. Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation initializes from this multi-round selector. ## C. Execution-Driven Data Pipeline and Learn-ing 

C.1. Supervision Follows Actual Execution 

The pipeline labels the decisions that GeoReVis actually makes: which correction to execute, which candidate to se-lect, which regions to combine, and whether to adopt the reconstruction. It first constructs each prediction through the same interfaces used at inference. Training GT then evalu-ates the resulting masks. This makes supervision specific to both the model’s observed states and the available actions. 

Initial Segmentation establishes the target interface. 

Target Decomposition learns from 29,201 instruction records, while Spatial Grounding uses 38,320 demonstrations. These roles produce target descriptions and spatial prompts. Their outputs establish the starting masks for subsequent execution-driven supervision. 

Executed actions teach effective Feedback Revision. 

Teacher proposals, proposal-seeded search, and rules gener-ate spatial edits. Every proposal is executed through SAM3, with utility 

∆a = IoU( Ha, G ) − IoU( M, G ). (38) Programs with ∆a ≥ 0.01 and valid signed prompts super-vise the reviser. Verification connects the proposed operation to its actual segmentation effect. The verified pool contains 32,435 programs: 2,297 direct external proposals, 22,056 proposal-seeded search outcomes, and 8,082 rule-generated corrections. MiniMax-M3.1 sup-plies the external proposals during training. 

Proposal-seeded search refines executable spatial hy-potheses. Each source state contains the image, complete target context, current predicted mask, and training annota-tion. The teacher proposes up to 18 programs using replace ,

add , or erase . A program contains at most four indepen-dent boxes, four positive points, and two negative points in total, with at least one positive point per box. Coordi-nates are normalized to [0 , 1000] . Every program acts on the same original state, allowing its realized gain to be compared directly. After execution, the pool combines teacher proposals with available rule and geometric-scope candidates. Programs are deduplicated and ranked by executed IoU. The three highest-ranked programs with valid signed prompts seed one local search pass. For each seed, the ordered variants are: 16 1. Box extent. Contract each box edge by 0.5 image pixels, expand it by 1 pixel, or scale its width and height about the center by 0.95 or 1.05. Boxes are clipped to the image and adjusted to retain their positive points. 2. Editing scope. For an addition or replacement seed, swap 

add and replace while retaining its spatial prompts. 3. Background exclusion. For these seeds, identify up to two false-positive components in the executed mask. Each yields a separate variant with an interior negative point in the smallest seed box containing that point, sub-ject to the two-negative-point budget. Invalid or duplicate variants are removed. The first 18 unique variants are executed, and the local pass ends when this bud-get or the finite variant list is exhausted. The 22,056 proposal-seeded labels are those whose seed is an external teacher proposal. This procedure converts semantic proposals into spatial supervision verified by the actual segmentation de-coder. 

Bounded supplementation covers residual errors. 

Geometric-scope proposals group five to eight target com-ponents into two to four prompt regions, favoring balanced groups and tight boxes, with at most 18 programs. After local search, one residual pass tests at most 18 additional programs that jointly add missing components, erase excess components, or group larger target collections. This pass is skipped when the best valid candidate or initial mask already reaches 0.95 IoU. Search therefore ends after fixed candidate lists rather than recursively refining new variants. Candidate retention requires a gain of at least 0.01 and operation-consistent point signs. For addition and replace-ment, positive points lie on the target and negative points on background. For erasure, positive points identify unwanted current support and negative points lie outside it. Up to three distinct near-best alternatives per state are retained. Let Q0 and Qmax be the initial and best verified IoUs. Each retained candidate must satisfy both thresholds: 

IoU( Ha, G ) ≥ Qmax − 0.02 ,

IoU( Ha, G ) ≥ Q0 + 0 .8( Qmax − Q0). (39) The final training set uses one program per state. It prefers a direct teacher program within 0.005 IoU of the best re-tained externally supported program, otherwise choosing the highest-IoU externally supported variant. Rules supplement states without such a verified program, with their dataset share capped at 30%. Training annotations guide this offline search and labeling, while the reviser observes only the image, instruction, and current predicted mask. 

Actual candidates teach Candidate Selection. The se-lection pool contains 122,876 records from executed same-target pairs, cross-target contrasts, and replay. A pair’s pref-erence follows its measured segmentation quality. Full-mask and difference views are retained so the selector can learn both target consistency and the effect of the proposed change. Feedback Assessment uses 30,000 examples. The feedback-conditioned revision mix contains 18,000 exam-ples, and multi-round selection adds 2,220 executed pairs with 740 replay pairs from 384 training scenes. These records can share images because they supervise different states and decisions. Predicted feedback exposes the model to its observed errors, while replay maintains previously learned revision and selection behavior. 

Observed banks teach joint evidence use. For consoli-dation, the bank fixes region boundaries before GT assigns labels. Region labels use the exact optimum in Sec. B.5. The complete reconstruction receives IoU supervision, and reconstruction/reference pairs receive adoption targets. Ini-tial preference proposals are sampled deterministically from an actual donor mask, its union with the reference, or their intersection. These proposals are representable in the bank’s partition and are chosen independently of GT. GT evalu-ates the proposal afterward. This separates the source of candidate geometry from the source of its training label.

E.3. Candidate Mask and Fragmented Evidence In-tegration 

Table 9 tests the effectiveness and advantage of our approach to integrating candidate masks and fragmented evidence over general aggregation strategies. Whole-Mask Selection uses the candidate as a unit, while union and majority voting follow overlap rules. Membership Regions support finer decisions conditioned on the complete instruction. Independent Regions uses the same Shared Encoder, Membership Regions, and image and language context as the full model. It retains the same reference-mask initialization and Relative Adoption. Both variants use identical training samples, supervision labels, losses, and training steps. The sole change is removing the Area-Weighted Sum-mary ¯z from the regional readout in Eq. 20. Each region retains its contextualized feature zi but receives no pooled features from other regions. Full consolidation exceeds Whole-Mask Selection by 2.81% gIoU and 3.58% cIoU. Joint composition also im-proves over Independent Regions by 2.83% and 2.96%. These gains support the connection in Eq. 28: useful re-gional decisions should be judged by their combined effect on the complete mask. Retaining all rounds adds 0.56% gIoU over current-round evidence. Initializing from the learned selector adds 1.37% over random initialization, and Relative Adoption adds 0.99% over adopting every reconstruction. Together, these results support historical evidence retention, transfer of com-parative understanding, and selection after consolidation. 

E.4. Revision Rounds and Computational Cost 

Figure 2 and Tables 10–11 summarize accuracy and cost. One to three rounds improve gIoU from 68.13% to 70.26% and 72.00%, with corresponding cIoUs of 72.84%, 74.95%, and 77.01%. A fourth gives 72.06% gIoU. Three rounds capture nearly all of the observed gIoU gain. Two-round GeoReVis takes 3.98 seconds per query and exceeds all main-table baselines in gIoU. It is faster than SAM-Veteran at 5.41 seconds and SegAgent at 6.68 seconds. Three rounds add 1.74% gIoU for a total of 5.99 seconds. Consolidation contributes 0.74 seconds per query. 

Compact adapters specialize the execution roles. Each deployed Qwen3.5-2B instance contains 2.213B parameters, including its visual encoder. The seven rank-16 adapters contain 7.38M parameters each, totaling 51.67M. The re-gional readout adds 6.07M, including its frozen initializa-tion branch. Our implementation retains the original and grounding-merged backbone instances, giving 4.484B resi-dent VLM and readout parameters, excluding frozen SAM3. Roles reuse these instances through adapter switching, and each VLM call activates at most one adapter. Thus, role specialization preserves a compact per-call backbone com-pared with the 7B native backbones of SAM-Veteran and SegAgent [8, 42]. 

Shared computation limits multi-stage overhead. Can-didate Selection reads A/B preference logits directly. SAM3 caches image features across prompted decodes, while con-solidation shares visual encoding across all Membership Regions and reconstructs the mask without additional seg-menter calls. These mechanisms limit the cost of adding selection and evidence consolidation. Full GeoReVis uses 20.65 GB peak memory per GPU, below SAM-Veteran’s 24.68 GB and SegAgent’s 23.23 GB (Table 11).

## F. Limitations 

Unobserved target regions. The Execution-Driven Data Pipeline provides reliable supervision, Selective Feedback Revision acquires useful target evidence, and Cross-Mask Evidence Consolidation combines complementary regions. However, consolidation is restricted to the union of observed candidates (Sec. B.3). If a target region is absent from every candidate throughout Initial Segmentation and all revision rounds, GeoReVis cannot recover it through consolidation. 

Computational cost. Two-round GeoReVis outperforms all baselines in main Table 1 and runs faster than SAM-Veteran and SegAgent. The default three-round model im-proves accuracy further but increases inference time from 3.98 to 5.99 seconds per query (Tables 10–11). Its cost ex-ceeds SocioReasoner and SAM-Veteran, leaving room to improve the accuracy–efficiency trade-off. 

Saturation across revision rounds. GeoReVis improves segmentation through feedback revision and accumulated evidence, but gains become marginal beyond three rounds (Table 10). Annotation errors affect the quality of execution-driven supervision, while the model’s ability to discover and combine target evidence remains bounded. Repeating inference alone therefore does not provide unlimited im-provement.
