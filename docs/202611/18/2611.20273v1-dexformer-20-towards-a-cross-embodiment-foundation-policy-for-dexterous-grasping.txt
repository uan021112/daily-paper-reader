Abstract
Dexterous grasping policies are usually trained for one arm
paired with one hand. Every new pairing then needs its own
policy, and the number of policies grows with every arm
and hand added. We ask whether a single policy can con-
trol many arm–hand combinations and transfer to pairings
and hands it has never seen. This is hard because robots
differ in the number, layout, and dynamics of their joints,
so their observations and actions share no common inter-
face. We present DexFormer 2.0, a single reinforcement
learning policy for cross-embodiment dexterous grasping.
It maps every robot to a canonical joint interface with va-
lidity masks, encodes each joint’s morphology with atten-
tion biased by the kinematic tree, and uses a Transformer-
XL memory to infer embodiment-dependent dynamics from
recent interaction. Trained on 18 combinations of four arms
and six hands, it reaches 89% stable success on seen com-
binations and 51% on six held-out arm–hand pairs, out-
performing GRU and DexFormer 1.0 baselines. Without
fine-tuning, it also grasps with four new hand types and,
through retargeting, with parallel grippers. Ablations show

1. Introduction
Dexterous grasping policies are commonly developed for
a fixed robotic embodiment: one arm, one hand, and one
carefully matched action space. This specialization makes
policy development expensive as hardware diversifies. Re-
placing either the arm or the hand changes the kinematic
chain, reachable workspace, joint limits, control response,
and contact geometry; replacing both compounds these
changes. A straightforward solution is to train a separate
policy for every arm–hand pairing, but the number of poli-
cies grows with the Cartesian product of available arms and
hands, preventing knowledge sharing across related embod-
iments.
Learning one policy across heterogeneous arm–hand
systems is challenging for three reasons. First, different
hands expose different numbers and arrangements of ac-
tive joints, so their observations and actions do not naturally
share a fixed interface. Second, the same action value has a
different physical meaning under different joint axes, limits,
link placements, and kinematic depths. Third, morphology
alone is insufficient: closed-loop grasping also depends on
contacts and embodiment-specific dynamics that must be
inferred over time.
We present DexFormer 2.0, a morphology-aware recur-
rent policy for cross-embodiment dexterous grasping (Fig-
ure 1). We represent every robot through a canonical set of
29 joint slots—seven arm joints and up to 22 hand joints—
together with validity masks.
Each joint receives a 16-
dimensional morphology descriptor encoding its axis, at-
tachment position, joint and velocity limits, semantic role,
and kinematic depth. Our MorphNode encoder adds an at-
tention residual biased by the robot’s kinematic tree and
joint roles, and a Transformer-XL maintains a 16-step tem-
poral context over the fused features. A shared actor head
then produces a masked Gaussian policy over the canonical
action space, using the same network across all arm–hand
pairings.
We study a single policy over four robot arms and six
dexterous hands, forming 24 arm–hand combinations. We
randomly select 18 combinations for training and reserve
the remaining six for testing.
Evaluation on both train-
ing and held-out arm–hand pairs uses the same ten object
identities, isolating embodiment generalization from object-
identity generalization.
The policy reaches 89.01% sta-
ble success on the seen combinations and 51.18% on the
held-out pairs, and it keeps 48.85% on held-out pairs when
the objects are ten unseen YCB objects [4]. Without fine-
tuning, it also grasps with four hand types that never appear
in training, and its actions can be retargeted to four parallel
grippers. Our contributions are:
• We
introduce
a
joint
cross-embodiment
training
paradigm: one policy is trained on many arm–hand em-
bodiments together, through canonical joint slots and va-
lidity masks. The policy, without fine-tuning, transfers to
held-out pairs and to hand types absent from training, and
even to parallel grippers.
• We develop a topology-aware recurrent policy archi-
tecture that combines per-joint physical descriptors, a
gated spatial-attention residual, Transformer-XL mem-
ory, and a shared actor head. The architecture conditions
control on morphology without using a dedicated policy
or output head for each embodiment.
• We show that temporal memory, kinematic-tree bias,
and pairing coverage each matter for transfer: removing
memory or the tree bias roughly halves success on held-
out pairs, and training on 18 instead of 12 combinations
raises it from 5.65% to 51.18% at a matched interaction
budget.
2. Related Work
Dexterous grasping and sim-to-real transfer.
Rein-
forcement learning has enabled contact-rich dexterous con-
trol.
Andrychowicz et al. [1] showed that domain ran-
domization [22] can transfer simulated in-hand manipu-
lation policies to a physical hand, and later work ex-
tended this route to faster reorientation and rotation [9, 18].
For grasping, UniDexGrasp [28] and UniDexGrasp++ [24]
learn object-general policies through goal condition-
ing, geometry-aware curricula, and iterative generalist–
specialist learning, while UniGraspTransformer [26] dis-
tills object-specific experts into a shared Transformer pol-
icy. DexNDM [13] addresses the reality gap in in-hand rota-
tion using joint-wise learned dynamics. These studies target
object generalization and deployment for one embodiment;
we share a grasping policy when both the arm and hand
vary.
Generalist policies across embodiments.
Morphology-
agnostic control has a long line of work in simulated lo-
comotion.
Graph and modular policies share parame-
ters across limbs through message passing [10, 25], and
Transformer policies replace message passing with atten-
tion over joint tokens, optionally conditioned on morphol-
ogy [8, 12].
For manipulation, large imitation-learning
models pool demonstrations from many robots [7, 15, 16],
but they mostly cover parallel-jaw grippers. One Policy to
Run Them All [3] introduces the Unified Robot Morphol-
ogy Architecture (URMA), using morphology-aware en-
coding and decoding to share a locomotion policy across

Building on these lines of research, DexFormer 2.0 stud-
ies joint arm-and-hand embodiment variation: one grasping
policy is shared across the Cartesian product of four arms
(Flexiv, Franka, KUKA, and xArm7) and six hands (Revo3,
Sharpa, LEAP, Allegro, RAPID, and Shadow), yielding
4 × 6 = 24 arm–hand combinations.
3. Method
We train one policy that controls different arm–hand com-
binations in joint space and generalizes to held-out arm–
hand pairs and to entirely new hand types, as shown in Fig-
ure 2. Each robot is mapped to shared joint slots (Sec. 3.1),
each described by its joint’s physical properties (Sec. 3.2).
MorphNode encodes them into a spatial code (Sec. 3.3), a
Transformer-XL adds memory of recent steps alongside a
task encoder (Sec. 3.4), and a shared actor head outputs
masked joint actions (Sec. 3.5). Training uses PPO on a
mixture of embodiments (Sec. 3.6).
3.1. Problem Formulation
Let e = (a, h) denote an embodiment formed by arm a ∈A
and hand h ∈H. We seek one stochastic policy πθ(ut |
o≤t, me) that controls all embodiments in A × H. The
observation ot contains the robot joint state, object pose and
target position, fingertip states and contact forces, and a 64-
point object point cloud. The morphology description me
is derived from the robot model and joint limits. The action
ut specifies relative joint targets for both the arm and hand.
All embodiments are mapped to a canonical interface
with Na = 7 arm slots and Nh = 22 hand slots. A binary
mask ve ∈{0, 1}Na+Nh marks physically active joints.

Padding slots have zero observations, descriptors, action
means, and policy-loss contributions, so inactive joints af-
fect neither execution nor learning.
3.2. Joint-Level Morphology Representation
For each canonical joint i, we construct a descriptor mi ∈
R16. It contains the joint axis expressed in its parent-link
frame (3 dimensions), joint attachment position (3), the cen-
ter and half-range of its position limits (2), velocity limit
(1), a semantic one-hot role for arm or finger identity (6),
and normalized kinematic depth (1). Unlike a categorical
robot identifier, the descriptor exposes local physical struc-
ture that can be shared with held-out pairs.
Each joint combines its descriptor with six state features:
position, velocity, previous action, and a three-dimensional
joint location. This forms a 22-dimensional feature xi,t,
accompanied by the validity mask. The mount pose, i.e.,
the relative wrist-to-palm pose, contributes seven additional
values. Kinematic ancestry is derived from the robot model
and used to compute joint-to-joint tree distances.
3.3. MorphNode Spatial Encoder
MorphNode combines a body MLP path with a topology-
aware MorphAttention residual. In the body path, masked
joint features and validity bits are flattened separately for
the arm and hand and encoded by an arm encoder, a hand
encoder, and a mount encoder for the mount pose. Each
output is adapted to 128 dimensions, and a fusion MLP
produces the body feature bt. The arm, hand, and mount
encoders are MLPs with layer widths 128–96, 64–96, and
64–64, and the fusion MLP has two 128-unit layers.
The MorphAttention residual projects the 29 joint fea-
tures to 128-dimensional tokens and appends five context
tokens: object state, goal state, contacts, remaining task ob-
servations, and mount pose. Two spatial attention layers,
each with four heads, update these 34 tokens. For joint to-
kens i, j, the attention logits include learned tree-distance
and joint-role biases, in the spirit of structural encodings
for graph Transformers [29]:

3.5. Actor and Value Heads
The actor head, a single MLP, maps the 256-dimensional
context to the canonical action means:

(5)
where fθ has widths 256 →256 →256 →29 and is shared

The actor is a diagonal Gaussian with learnable, state-
independent standard deviations bounded to [0.05, 1.0].
Sampled actions are clipped to [−5, 5], scaled by 0.05 for
arm joints and 0.08 for hand joints, and applied as relative
joint-position targets. The critic uses the same architecture
with a scalar value head, is used only in training, and shares
no weights or memory with the actor.
3.6. Training Objective
We train the shared actor–critic with PPO [20] over a mix-
ture of embodiments. Each rollout contains environments
with different arm–hand pairings, so every update aggre-
gates gradients across morphologies. The policy runs at
60 Hz in a 240 Hz simulation with 64-step rollouts. Re-
wards encourage fingertip reaching, thumb-and-finger con-
tact, and target tracking once in contact, with a success
bonus and action penalties.
A curriculum raises gravity
from zero to full, and physical parameters are randomized
(Supp. Sec. A). Invalid canonical slots stay masked in obser-
vation normalization, action sampling, and the policy loss.

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Embodiments.
Four arms (Flexiv, Franka, KUKA, and
xArm7) and six hands (Revo3, Sharpa, LEAP, Allegro,
RAPID, and Shadow) form 24 embodiments (Figure 3a). A
fixed split uses 18 combinations for training and holds out
Franka–LEAP, Franka–Sharpa, KUKA–Allegro, KUKA–
Shadow, xArm7–RAPID, and Flexiv–Revo3.
Objects.
Training uses ten primitive configurations from
five shape families at fixed nominal size: three cuboids, one
sphere, two capsules, two cones, and two cylinders. For
object generalization, we evaluate ten YCB objects [4] ex-
cluded from training: apple, lemon, peach, pear, orange,
plum, tomato soup can, tuna can, potted meat can, and
gelatin box (Figure 4).
Metric.
Our primary metric is the stable success rate
(SR), the percentage of evaluation episodes that succeed.
An episode succeeds if it completes all 240 control steps,
keeps the object position error below 5 cm throughout the
final 30 steps (0.5 s), and has no non-timeout termination.
All episodes remain in the denominator, and split-level re-
sults average the per-embodiment rates equally.

4.4. Effect of Training Embodiment Coverage
We vary the number of training arm–hand combinations
from 6 to 12 to 18 while keeping all four arm types and
all six hand types in every training set. The architecture,
optimizer, rollout length, environment count, and check-
point iteration are unchanged, so the interaction budget
is matched and fewer training embodiments receive more
samples each.
Table 3 reports transfer to the same six held-out pairs
in every run, which fixes the evaluation set while training
coverage changes. Across all 24 embodiments, SR rises
from 14.21% to 32.22% to 79.55%. On the common held-
out set, SR increases from 0.07% to 5.65% to 51.18% as
training coverage grows from 6 to 12 to 18 combinations.
The 18-combination policy gains 45.53 percentage points
over the 12-combination policy on this fixed test set. All
arm and hand types are present even in the smallest training
set, so the gain comes from pairing coverage.

4.5. Transfer to Unseen Hand and Gripper Types
The held-out pairs in Figure 6 use arm and hand types en-
countered during training. To test transfer to new hands,
we pair each of the four training arms with four previously
unseen right hands. Each hand fills the canonical slots by
joint role (Supp. Sec. A), supplies its morphology descrip-
tors to the same frozen policy, and is evaluated on the ten
seen objects.
Wuji 1 has the highest SR on all four arms (Table 4),
with a mean of 27.18%. Tesollo achieves 11.78% on av-
erage and performs best on xArm7 (20.70%). XHAND1
achieves 16.78% on average, with 26.50% on Flexiv and
27.70% on xArm7 but below 7% on Franka and KUKA.
Revo2 achieves 14.07% on average and performs best on
Flexiv (26.60%). Across the four hands, the policy achieves
17.45% mean SR (2,792 of 16,000 episodes).
Parallel grippers.
The gripper columns of Table 4 ask
whether the same frozen policy can drive a gripper with
a single opening degree of freedom. Mapping the thumb
and index commands directly to the jaws yields one sta-
ble grasp in 16,000 episodes. We therefore let the policy
observe and command a virtual LEAP hand [21] attached
to the real flange. Its arm actions are executed unchanged,
and the jaw opening follows the distance between the vir-
tual thumb and index fingertips (Supp. Sec. C). With this
fixed map, mean SR ranges from 7.55% for UMI to 16.62%
for the Franka Hand. Because the policy observes a LEAP
hand here, this shows that its actions can be retargeted to a
gripper, not zero-shot transfer to gripper morphology.
4.6. Robustness to Hand-Joint Faults

4.7. Real-World Experiments
We deploy on a physical Flexiv arm with two hands, Revo3
and LEAP (Figure 7). The simulation policy observes ob-

depth noise, object size, and initial states (Supp. Sec. H),

world fine-tuning. Once the predicted object position stays
within 5 cm of the target for four consecutive new frames, a
scripted routine holds the grasp, lifts and carries the object
to a basket, and releases it. A trial succeeds if the object

5. Conclusion
We presented DexFormer 2.0, a single reinforcement learn-
ing policy that controls heterogeneous arm–hand systems
through a canonical joint interface, per-joint morphol-
ogy descriptors, topology-biased attention, and a short
Transformer-XL memory. Trained on 18 of 24 arm–hand
combinations, it reaches 89.01% stable success on seen and
51.18% on held-out arm–hand pairs, and 48.85% when the
objects are also unseen. Memory, the kinematic tree bias,
and pairing coverage are each necessary.
Without fine-
tuning, the policy also grasps with four unseen hand types,
tolerates unseen joint faults, and drives parallel grippers

through retargeting. On a real Flexiv arm, distilled depth-
based students succeed in 68% (Revo3, held-out pair) and
92% (LEAP) of trials without real-world fine-tuning.
