Abstract. This paper presents a depth-aware path planning and colli-sion avoidance framework for the safe navigation of robotic nursing assis-tants in cluttered environments with low-height obstacles. Conventional LiDAR-based navigation systems, while accurate for mapping and local-ization, fail to detect transparent or low-height obstacles, whereas vision-based systems are prone to noise and occlusion. The proposed framework integrates RGB-D depth sensing, rule-based obstacle avoidance, and the NavfnROS global path planner to drive the robot from an initial point to a goal point. The proposed approach is implemented on the Husky A200 robot, equipped with a Kinova Gen3 arm, to form a complete setup for a robotic nursing assistant. This system features a multi-layer architec-ture for global planning, perception fusion, and real-time control in the ROS-Gazebo environment. Experimental evaluations in a simulated clut-tered environment demonstrate up to 40% higher success rate and 35% improvement in minimum clearance compared with single-sensor base-lines. Furthermore, real-world experimental results validate the proposed approach as a computationally efficient and safety-aware navigation so-lution for autonomous healthcare robots. The code is available at https: //github.com/Peeyush331098/Depth-based-obstacle-avoidance.git

Unlike structured industrial settings, hospitals are unstructured, dynamic, and highly cluttered, with narrow corridors, movable beds, trolleys, and the constant presence of humans. Among the sensing modalities investigated for autonomous navigation, sys-tems based on LiDAR are the state of the art because of their capacity to provide reliable 3D mapping, high-precision range measurements, and steady performance across a range of lighting conditions. Because of these benefits, Li-DAR is an essential part of autonomous wheeled systems and hospital-grade SLAM frameworks [5,6]. For example, hospital service robots have effectively used 2D LiDAR navigation frameworks for waypoint-based path tracking and corridor following, while 3D LiDAR extensions improve spatial awareness in semi-structured interior spaces [7]. However, safety in patient-care scenarios may be jeopardized because LiDAR-only systems frequently miss challenging features such as translucent partitions, low-height obstacles, or partially occluded objects [18]. LiDAR technology also has inherent limitations, such as a limited vertical field of view, low point density, absence of semantic or color information, and difficulty in recognizing translucent or reflective surfaces (such as glass doors or polished floors) [15]. These limitations hamper reliable perception in reflective or visually complex healthcare environments. To overcome such limitations, depth cameras have emerged as a powerful complementary sensing approach [16]. By providing dense pixel-wise depth maps integrated with visual cues, depth cameras enable enhanced perception of low-lying, irregular, and specular obstacles often missed by LiDAR alone. Therefore, depth imaging constitutes a promising pathway toward perception-aware and context-adaptive navigation, facilitating safer and more efficient path planning [14] for robotic nursing assistants in cluttered and dynamic hospital environ-ments. Recent studies demonstrate that exploiting the contextual richness of depth imagery significantly enhances both obstacle perception and path replan-ning under dynamic conditions [18,6]. Moreover, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been applied to inter-pret fused sensor data for adaptive, human-aware navigation [7,11]. Vision-based obstacle avoidance algorithms have been proposed in [1,13,2,21]. In [1], the authors used YOLOv5 for real-time object detection and dis-tance estimation, which is valid only for known obstacles. In real-world scenarios, there are obstacles that are not recognized by such object detection algorithms. Despite these advances, achieving real-time fusion, maintaining computational efficiency, and ensuring safe human–robot interaction remain open challenges. ## 2 Related Work 

Conventional mobile robot navigation frameworks, such as the ROS Navigation Stack, use a global planner (e.g., NavfnROS) to generate paths and a LiDAR-driven local planner (e.g., DWAPlannerROS) to avoid local obstacles [22]. Li-DAR sensors work well in controlled settings, but they frequently miss low-height or transparent obstacles, making them risky to use in areas where people are present. Recent studies have investigated vision-based obstacle avoidance [19] and RGB-D camera-based navigation [8], which offer richer spatial information but suffer from occlusion and depth noise. For dynamic indoor situations, hy-brid approaches that combine LiDAR and RGB-D perception with global plan-ning have demonstrated promise [3]. The majority of current research, however, focuses on neural models or computationally demanding sensor fusion that is difficult to run in real time. In contrast, the proposed Navfn-Reflex framework balances perception accuracy, computing efficiency, and safety for healthcare-oriented mobile robots by achieving quick, rule-based obstacle avoidance through the use of a five-zone depth partitioning and automatic replanning. 

## 3 Proposed Approach 

The proposed depth-based navigation methodology integrates multiple sensing and planning components to achieve robust, safe, and adaptive navigation for robots in dynamic indoor environments, such as hospitals. The framework uses RGB-D (depth) sensing with a global path planner, enabling the system to perceive complex surroundings, avoid obstacles, and dynamically replan when necessary. The methodology consists of the following interconnected stages. First, a map generation process constructs an occupancy grid of the environment using LiDAR-based SLAM. This map forms the foundation for path planning, which 4 P. Thakur et al. 

combines a global planner for long-term route generation with a local layer for real-time obstacle handling. Sensor processing describes how depth camera data are analyzed and combined into a unified risk signal, and the design choices that make this processing robust are then outlined. The depth-based navigation algo-rithm integrates all modules into a single operational procedure, and finally the overall workflow describes the hierarchical interaction among the global plan-ning, perception, and local control layers. Together, these components form a depth-based navigation framework that ensures the robot can navigate autonomously and safely in complex environments populated by humans with dynamic obstacles. 

3.1 Map generation 

The Husky A200 mobile robot constructs a two-dimensional occupancy grid map using a UST-10 LiDAR sensor. LiDAR scans are fused with odometry and IMU data through a SLAM framework. Recent work proposes jointly optimiz-ing robot poses and occupancy map values in one formulation, yielding higher map fidelity and trajectory accuracy [17]. As the robot moves, each new scan is aligned to previous data to incrementally build a consistent global map of free, occupied, and unknown regions. The resulting occupancy grid is saved us-ing the map_saver utility and subsequently used by AMCL for pose estimation during autonomous navigation. 3.2 Path planning 

Using the generated occupancy map, the navigation framework adopts a hy-brid planning architecture integrating global and local layers. The global plan-ner (e.g., NavfnROS ) computes an optimal route over the occupancy grid, while the local planner uses real-time sensor (RGB-D/LiDAR) feedback to handle dynamic obstacles. A corridor-detection mechanism enables safe traversal in narrow passages, and when the global path is obstructed, the system triggers local avoidance and dynamic replanning. global and local optimization have been shown in recent studies to significantly improve trajectory smoothness, collision rates, and responsiveness in changing environments [20]. This depth-aware, adaptive planning framework enables safe, efficient, and human-aware navigation in complex indoor corridors. 

3.3 Sensor processing 

In depth camera processing , a bottom region of interest (ROI), covering the lower 

ρ fraction of the depth frame, focuses on obstacles along the robot’s path. The ROI is divided into five zones ( far-left to far-right ), ignoring invalid or floor-level pixels. Each zone’s mean and 5th-percentile depths yield a stable proximity measure, and the minimum distance dmin over all zones indicates the nearest obstacle. If dmin < δ s, where δs is the safe distance, a reflex controller issues one of three avoidance actions— turn left , turn right , or stop —temporarily overriding the nominal path follower. In the fusion and decision layer , the navigation node combines global plan-ning ( NavfnROS ) with local reflex control through a weighted risk model: 

R = wGσG + wRσR, (1) where σG and σR denote global path deviation and local proximity risk, respec-tively, and wG + wR = 1 . When R > R th , where Rth is the risk threshold, the system executes a short reflex action, pauses forward motion, and republishes the last goal for automatic replanning. This unified strategy ensures smooth, collision-free navigation and quick recovery in dynamic or cluttered environ-ments. 

3.4 Design choices and robustness 

To ensure dependable performance in the presence of sensor uncertainty, a num-ber of design improvements were integrated into the depth-based perception framework. (a) Percentile-based depth estimation provides consistent range mea-surements by reducing noise from hot pixels, specular reflections, and surface discontinuities. (b) For efficient obstacle detection in confined spaces, adaptive spatial weighting dynamically adjusts the contribution of image regions, giv-ing near-field areas more weight. (c) To ensure temporal consistency across the perception pipeline, temporal validation and time gating reject outdated or asyn-chronous frames using timestamp verification. (d) Temporal fusion with short-term prediction smooths successive depth frames and maintains spatial continu-ity during short sensor dropouts. Together, these mechanisms provide a robust, uncertainty-tolerant depth per-ception architecture that enables consistent and reliable navigation in complex indoor environments. 6 P. Thakur et al.

## 4 Simulation and Real-World Experiments 

4.1 Simulation setup, results and discussion 

Simulation studies were performed in the Gazebo environment using the Husky A200 mobile robot, equipped with a 2D LiDAR for localization and an Intel RealSense D435 depth camera for perception. To simulate realistic healthcare situations, a hospital-like indoor environment was designed with furniture, ob-stacles, and narrow passageways. The proposed depth-based Navfn path follower with reflex safety layer was implemented in ROS, where the depth-based reflex module ensured that local obstacles were avoided while NavfnROS generated global routes. For real-time reflex actions ( turn , stop , or move forward ), the system employed a five-zone depth analysis. When motion stagnation was detected, it triggered automatic replanning. ## 5 Conclusion 

This work presented a hybrid depth-based Navfn path follower with reflex safety layer for safe and reliable navigation of robotic nursing assistants in cluttered indoor environments. The proposed system integrates the Navfn global planner with a five-zone RealSense depth reflex layer, enabling adaptive and perception-driven obstacle avoidance in real time. A built-in stuck detection and automatic replanning mechanism enhances autonomy by allowing the robot to recover from blocked or dynamic situations without human intervention. Compared with single-sensor or LiDAR-only baselines, experimental evalua-tions in ROS-Gazebo using the Husky A200 platform showed notable gains in navigation reliability, with up to 40% higher success rates and 35% greater ob-stacle clearance. The modular ROS design of the architecture enables efficient performance and simple deployment on real hardware. Future work will focus on validation in real healthcare settings and on extend-ing the system with learning-based decision modules, semantic perception, and human-intent prediction to provide socially compliant, context-aware navigation for assistive robotic applications.
