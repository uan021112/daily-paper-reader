# Déjà-Events: Multi-view 3D Geometry with Event Cameras via Sparsity-Aware Adaptive Gating 

Arda Cinar Demirtas 1, Pablo Varela Bernal 2, Marc Pollefeys 1 and Sunghwan Hong 1

> 1

ETH Zürich 2TU Delft 

Abstract — Event cameras keep capturing structure where conventional cameras blur or saturate, which makes them attractive for 3D perception in fast or poorly lit scenes. Event datasets with dense 3D ground truth, however, are far smaller and less diverse than the image collections behind feed-forward 3D reconstruction models. We present Déjà-Events, an event-only model that predicts depth, camera pose and pointmaps from multiple views in a single forward pass. Rather than relying on scarce 3D labels, we distil it from Déjà View, a feed-forward RGB model that refines its tokens by repeatedly applying one shared transformer block. On paired RGB and event recordings, the event student learns to reproduce the frozen teacher’s final tokens, which requires no depth or pose ground truth and trains only about 1% of its parameters. Because events are dense at edges and moving texture but nearly absent in textureless regions, we further introduce sparsity-aware adaptive gating, which adapts the shared block’s gates to events and adjusts them per patch by event density, so that accuracy holds as refinement steps are added. Trained only on simulated data, Déjà-Events achieves the lowest pointmap error among the event-based methods we evaluate, on EventScape and zero-shot on the real MVSEC and DSEC datasets. On DSEC it halves the error of the strongest baseline (0.15 vs. 0.30 AbsRel) with about a tenth of its parameters. It also has the lowest depth error on EventScape and DSEC and the lowest trajectory error on EventScape. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Robots that move fast or operate in poor lighting need the 3D geometry of their surroundings, and their own mo-tion through it, with little delay, yet these are exactly the conditions in which frame cameras blur, saturate or under-expose [1], [2]. Event cameras avoid much of this: each pixel asynchronously reports brightness changes as they happen, giving microsecond temporal resolution and a much higher dynamic range than conventional cameras [3], [4]. These make events an attractive modality for 3D reconstruction in such scenes [5]. Feed-forward reconstruction models predict camera poses, depth and 3D pointmaps from a set of images in a single for-ward pass [6], [7], [8]. They are trained on large and diverse image collections with 3D annotations [6], [8], whereas event datasets with dense 3D ground truth are much smaller and less diverse [1], [9], [5]. We therefore distil geometry from an RGB model instead: a frozen RGB teacher sees RGB frames, and an event student learns to reproduce what the teacher computes from events paired with those frames alone, transferring the teacher’s priors to events [1], [5]. Because the student inherits those priors but not the sensor, it can keep predicting structure where the teacher’s frames saturate (Fig. 1).   

> Fig. 1. Qualitative results on PEOD [10]. At a tunnel exit the RGB frame is saturated across most of the road and the vehicles ahead, while the event stream retains structure. In the blown-out area the RGB teacher predicts a flat, ground-plane-like depth, while the event student keeps the vehicles and the tunnel structure. Neither model was trained on PEOD.

Earlier methods that distil geometry into events used monocular depth models [1], [9] or VGGT [5] as the teacher. Ours is Déjà View [8] (DVLT), which refines the patch tokens of all images by reusing one shared transformer block K times, matching much larger feed-forward models with a fraction of their parameters. Because K is set at inference time, the same model can also trade accuracy for compute [8]. In a well-exposed RGB frame almost every patch car-ries information, whereas events fire only where brightness changes, mostly at edges and moving texture, leaving texture-less areas nearly empty [4], [1], [9]. In DVLT, each step’s update is scaled by a gate that depends only on the step and is thus identical for every patch. We expect this to suit events poorly, as sparse patches may contribute little early in the refinement and benefit from context once nearby dense regions are resolved. We therefore introduce sparsity-aware adaptive gating (SAAG), which fine-tunes this gate for events and adds a small per-patch correction conditioned on event density and the current step. a) Feed-forward 3D reconstruction: Feed-forward re-construction models regress cameras and 3D structure di-rectly from images. DUSt3R [11] and MASt3R [12] predict pointmaps for pairs of images, and DUSt3R fuses larger sets through a global alignment optimisation, whereas VGGT [6], 

π3 [13], MapAnything [7] and Depth Anything 3 [14] pro-cess all views jointly and recover cameras, depth and points in one forward pass through a deep stack of transformer layers. DVLT [8] instead applies a single shared block for a number of steps chosen at inference. 

b) Geometry from events: Early event-based methods recovered structure geometrically, for example by a space sweep along a known camera trajectory in EMVS [15]. Learned depth estimators either reconstruct intensity images from events with E2VID [16] and apply a frame-based depth network to them [4], or regress depth directly, as E2Depth [4] does with a recurrent convolutional network and EReFormer [17] with a recurrent vision transformer. Their depth maps can be combined with camera poses from event visual odometry such as DEVO [18] to form pointmaps. Other models predict pointmaps directly by adding events to the feed-forward models above, while keeping RGB as an input: EAG3R [19] adds events to handle dynamic and low-light scenes, and Interp3R [20] uses them to interpolate a frame-based model’s pointmaps between frames. A con-current work, Event3R [2] instead reconstructs from events alone, training a pairwise backbone initialised from DUSt3R with ground-truth and self-supervised losses, and fusing more than two views by global optimisation. EvGGS [21] also works from events alone, but needs known camera poses to place the 3D Gaussians it predicts. 

c) Distillation from RGB foundation models: Another line of work, motivated partly by the scarcity of event recordings with dense 3D labels, distils RGB foundation models which requires only paired frames and events. Depth AnyEvent [1] supervises an event student with the depth that a monocular RGB model predicts on aligned frames. EventDAM [9] also distils a monocular model; it scores the event sparsity of each patch and uses this score to mix RGB and event features into auxiliary depth targets, to weight feature distillation, and to enforce consistency across sparsity levels. EventVGGT [5] distils VGGT, aligning the features of an event student with the teacher’s alongside a depth loss on randomly mixed RGB and event features and a temporal-consistency loss. 

d) Input-adaptive computation: Event-specific net-works exploit sparsity for efficiency by recomputing ac-tivations only where new events arrive, as AsyNet [22] does with sparse convolutions and AEGNN [23] on an event graph. Adaptive computation methods instead learn how much computation to spend: Adaptive Computation Time [24] and PonderNet [25] learn how many recurrent steps to take, Universal Transformers [26] apply one shared block recurrently under such a halting rule, and Mixture-of-Depths [27] learns which tokens may skip a layer. Among the distilled models above, only EventDAM [9] makes event sparsity explicit, and it does so in the training objective; REALM [28] and EventVGGT [5] align features without conditioning on it. We instead let sparsity steer the refinement itself, by conditioning the shared block’s gates on the event density of each patch, which adds no loss term and only a few thousand parameters. III. B. Feature distillation 

We distil the teacher into the student on sets of S consec-utive views, each consisting of an RGB frame and the events of its time window. In EventScape, on which we distil, the frame and the event camera share a viewpoint and a pixel grid, so the n-th token of the student covers the same part of the scene as the n-th token of the teacher. The student is moreover initialised from the teacher and adapted only by LoRA, so it starts inside the teacher’s token space; the two can therefore be compared directly in latent space (Sec. III-B.2). 

1) Event tokenisation: For each frame, the events in its time window are accumulated into a voxel grid of five time bins, in which every event adds its polarity, +1 for brightening and −1 for darkening, to its pixel, split linearly between the two nearest bins [33]. Each bin is then divided by its Euclidean norm over the image, which makes the grid invariant to how many events the window happens to contain. The input stem turns this grid into a three-channel image that DINOv2 can read: at each pixel the three channels hold the accumulated positive polarity, the accumulated absolute polarity and the accumulated negative polarity, summed over the bins, and each channel is divided by its maximum over the frame. This image is normalised with a per-channel scale and shift that start at the ImageNet normalisation values and are learned, so that they can adapt to events. 

2) Distillation loss: We train the student to reproduce the teacher’s final tokens zK rather than its predictions. All three heads read zK and are frozen, so a student whose tokens match the teacher’s obtains cameras, rays and depth from the same decoders, and a single loss covers all three outputs. Because DVLT is trained with K drawn from Beta(2 , 1) 

rescaled to [8 , 16] [8], its outputs are usable at any K in that range, and we therefore treat the teacher’s zK as a target at each such K. We draw one K from this distribution per iteration, run both models for K steps and match their final tokens of every view, including the camera and register tokens,

C. Sparsity-aware adaptive gating 

During distillation the gates of Eq. (2) keep the values learned on RGB and, as they depend only on the step, apply the same scales to every patch. This suits event images poorly: a few patches are dense with events while large regions are nearly empty (Sec. I), so the scale that suits one patch at a given step may not suit another. Sparsity-aware adaptive gating (SAAG) adapts the gates to events in two ways. First, it fine-tunes the gate MLPs of both attention blocks together with the adapters, recalibrating the step schedule for events. Second, it makes the gates depend on the event density σn ∈ [0 , 1] of each patch n, the average absolute polarity in the patch relative to the most active patch in the frame, computed once from the input and fixed across steps. A. Teacher and baselines 

The teacher is the frozen DVLT model [8]. It sees RGB frames rather than events, so we report it in italics as a reference and not as a competitor. Our event student, Déjà-Events, is distilled on the EventScape training split without any depth or pose ground truth, for 20,000 steps on clips of 2 to 18 views, with LoRA rank 16 and 1.6 M trained parameters, using AdamW with a cosine schedule from peak learning rate 3 × 10 −4 (a tenth of that for adapters on pretrained weights) and weight decay 0.05. We compare against three kinds of event-based methods, which also form the groups of Table III. The depth-only networks are E2Depth [4] and EReFormer [17], retrained on EventScape, and DepthAnyEvent and its recurrent variant DepthAnyEvent-R [1], abbreviated DAE and DAE-R, which build on Depth Anything V2 [35] and whose released check-points were trained on EventScape. EventVGGT [5] predicts depth and pose with two separately trained checkpoints: 

Option A takes depth from the depth checkpoint and pose and intrinsics from the pose checkpoint, and Option B takes all three from the pose checkpoint, making it a single model. We run both released checkpoints with the authors’ inference code and event representation, unmodified. For visual odometry we use DEVO [18], and pairing its poses with each depth network gives modular pipelines, which are lifted to 3D with the ground-truth intrinsics, whereas Déjà-Events, its teacher and EventVGGT use the intrinsics they predict. 

B. Datasets 

EventScape [36] is simulated in CARLA [37] with a 

512 ×256 event camera and dense depth and poses; we distil on Town01 to Town03 and test on the 116 sequences of its released Town05 test set, capping depth at 200 m. MVSEC [38] records driving with a 346 ×260 DAVIS346, and we use four of its outdoor drives, one by day and three at night, with the lidar depth capped at 80 m. DSEC [39] records driving with a 640 ×480 event camera; since its test split has no public ground truth, we follow [40], [1] and use ten validation sequences held out from its training split, with lidar-based depth that DSEC limits to 50 m and pseudo ground-truth poses that we obtain by lidar odometry [41]. 

C. Evaluation protocol 

Each test sequence is cut into non-overlapping 18-frame windows, which every method processes at its own input res-olution; EReFormer’s positional embeddings are hardcoded for a 224 ×224 input, so on DSEC its predictions and the ground truth are centre-cropped to 16% of the frame, and its numbers there are not strictly comparable with the rest. Because DAE and DAE-R predict depth only up to scale and shift, depth is aligned with a median-based scale and shift. Pose and pointmaps are aligned with Sim(3), and ground-truth points are expressed in the window’s first camera. None of the baselines were trained on MVSEC or DSEC. Depth is measured by the absolute relative error (AbsRel) and ∆1, the fraction of pixels within a factor of 1.25 of ground truth. Pose is measured by the absolute trajectory error (ATE) in meters and AUC 20 , the area under the accuracy curve, up to 20 ◦, of the larger of each frame pair’s rotation and translation-direction errors [42]. For pointmaps, we follow DVLT [8] and report AbsRel, the point error relative to the ground-truth distance, and Inl@25, the fraction of points within 25% relative error, which is the 3D analogue of ∆1.V. B. Pose 

On EventScape, Déjà-Events’s trajectory error is a little over half that of DEVO and a small fraction of EventVGGT’s (Table II). On the real sensors, DEVO has the lowest translation errors, with the student second. D. Distillation on MVSEC day 1 

Table IV reports results on MVSEC night1, where low illumination degrades the teacher’s RGB input. It also adds Déjà-Events distilled for one more epoch on a daytime drive, which leaves night1 held out, with the same feature distillation and no ground truth. At night, Déjà-Events already has less than half its teacher’s trajectory error and a more accurate reconstruction, while the teacher keeps the lower relative depth error. The distillation on day 1 transfers to night: relative depth error falls below the teacher’s and pose accuracy reaches the teacher’s level, while the trajectory error is unchanged. The reconstruction, however, becomes less accurate than the zero-shot model’s. We attribute this to the camera intrinsics through which the pointmap is formed: the extra epoch pulls the predicted focal length towards the teacher’s, 2.8 times the true value, whereas the zero-shot model overestimates it by only 1.6 times.

Accurate and cheap Event 3D reconstruction: Déjà-Events gives the most accurate event-only reconstruction we measure on all three datasets, and it does so from a model an order of magnitude smaller than its closest competitor: 118 M parameters against EventVGGT’s 1.16 B, at less than half its compute and 40% of its memory (Table V). Only 1.6 M of those parameters are trained, and none of that training uses depth or pose ground truth, because the geometric prior and the decoders are inherited from the RGB teacher and only the encoding of events has to be learned.

It is widest on DSEC, whose colour frames stay well exposed even in its darkest sequences, and smallest on MVSEC, whose low-resolution greyscale frames come from three night drives and one daylight drive; there the student already reconstructs the scene more accurately than its teacher, and distillation on the day sequence brings its night depth error below the teacher’s as well (Table IV). Because the student is trained to reproduce its teacher, it can overtake it only where the teacher’s input fails, and driving data rarely leaves the regime its frame cameras handle. Future work should therefore evaluate on agile flight, where motion blur degrades the frames, to test whether events overtake them there. 

Bounded by the teacher’s camera: How much the predicted camera costs can be measured by projecting the same depth and poses through the ground-truth calibration. On MVSEC this lowers the teacher’s pointmap error by 18% and the student’s by 7%, leaving the two within 0.013 of each other, and it turns the student distilled on day 1 from the least into the most accurate reconstruction we obtain on that sensor, which confirms that its regression is one of intrinsics rather than of depth. On DSEC the same substitution removes about a sixth of the student’s error and leaves the teacher well ahead, so the gap there is depth, not the camera. Intrinsics are nonetheless the cheapest of these errors to remove, because a deployed camera is calibrated: fitting the camera head of an already distilled student to that calibration would repair its weakest output while depth and pose remain label-free. 

Timing is the next axis: An event camera reports each brightness change as it happens, so events record both structure a blurred frame loses and when, inside the interval, that structure moved. The first is what the student already exploits: blur and saturation degrade the teacher’s input while the event stream keeps its edges, which is why it overtakes its teacher where frames fail. The second it does not use. A frame integrates the interval into one image, so the teacher’s tokens cannot represent when anything happened inside it, and our stem discards the same information by summing over the bins (Sec. III-B.1), which leaves fast motion smeared across the summed image. Reading the bins would sharpen that and, more usefully, give geometry between frames rather than only at them. Doing so from events alone needs targets that vary within the interval, not a temporal encoder by itself. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented Déjà-Events, an event-only feed-forward model that predicts depth, camera pose and pointmaps in a single pass. It is distilled on paired RGB and event recordings from a frozen RGB teacher, DVLT, which refines its tokens by reusing one shared block, so it needs no depth or pose ground truth and trains about 1% of its parameters. Distilled only in simulation, it has the lowest pointmap error of the event-based methods we evaluate on EventScape and, zero-shot, on the real MVSEC and DSEC sensors, and the lowest depth error on EventScape and DSEC, at about a tenth of the strongest baseline’s parameters. Sparsity-aware adaptive gating conditions the shared block’s gates on where events fire; on DSEC it alone does not degrade as inference steps grow past the trained range, leaving recursion depth a usable compute knob.
