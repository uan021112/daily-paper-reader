# SplatFlow3D: Learning Scene Flow via 3D Motion Feature Splatting 

Bo Wang, Zhenping Sun, Yang Yu, Jian Li, Member, IEEE ,Li Liu, Senior Member, IEEE , and Dewen Hu, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Considering the motion continuity and stability of 3D objects over short inter-frame intervals, introducing multi-frame settings in scene flow estimation can potentially address occlusion. In this paper, we propose a two-frame backbone, SimpleFlow3D, which integrates dense information from the image plane and sparse information from the depth dimension in a decoupling-followed-by-coupling manner by hierarchically constructing the 3D motion feature. SimpleFlow3D achieves SOTA results with minimal parameters. We also propose aweight-sharing multi-frame framework to splat the previous 3D motion feature to the current estimation process. The framework unifies the first and non-first frame inference by constructing pseudo inputs. After combining the proposed framework and backbone, the newly born multi-frame method SplatFlow3D further advances the state of the art. It achieves lower errors than the two-frame backbone, with fewer parameters than current methods. On both KITTI and Spring, SplatFlow3D ranked 1st over all published and submitted methods. Experiments show that SplatFlow3D improves occlusion handling, non-rigid motion estimation, and generalization to real-world scenes. Code is available at https://github.com/wwsource/SplatFlow3D. 

Index Terms —Multi-frame Settings, Scene Flow Estimation, Occlusion, Non-rigid Motion, Splatting Transformation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Accurately estimating the 3D motion of objects plays a critical role in understanding complex scene structures and behavioral patterns, and therefore finds extensive applications such as human motion estimation [1], [2], object tracking [3], [4], and moving object segmentation [5]. As a fundamental and challenging problem in 3D computer vision, scene flow estimation (SFE) aims to estimate the pixel-wise/point-wise 3D motion between consecutive stereo, RGB-D, or point cloud data, thereby providing more challenging dense and fine-grained 3D motion estimation results. Existing methods employ dense feature point matching: using a deep neural network to extract the higher-order feature representations of pixels [6]–[8] or LiDAR points [9], [10]

Feature similarity is only applicable to non-occluded regions between frames, while occlusion is widespread in real-world scenarios; hence, SFE faces a stern test. To address this issue, most methods mainly focus on introducing rigid motion priors through explicit [8], [11] or implicit [7] approaches. These priors allow occluded regions to enjoy a unified and orderly 3D transformation as part of the entire rigid body. However, powerful semantic representation abilities are required, which can lead to an increase in model parameters and significant data requirements. Considering the motion continuity and stability of 3D ob-jects over short inter-frame intervals, introducing multi-frame settings can also address occlusion. However, this comes with certain risks. The inference of each frame requires processing information from multiple frames, which significantly in-creases the computational load. In addition, there is a potential conflict between the priors provided by historical frames and the judgment of the current frame, leading to unstable, or even worse, estimation results. In our research, we aim to design a simple yet effective multi-frame scene flow method, SplatFlow3D, which requires the ability to effectively address occlusion and non-rigid motion. To this end, we propose two key components: a two-frame backbone and a multi-frame framework. First, we propose a novel iterative two-frame backbone, SimpleFlow3D. The network integrates dense information from the image plane and sparse information from the depth dimension in a decoupling-followed-by-coupling manner by hierarchically constructing the 3D motion feature. Specifically, we construct the appearance correlation feature based on RGB images and the depth correlation feature based on depth maps, respectively. While characterizing the distribution of correlations, these two features can be used to indirectly regress the plane displacement and directly calculate the depth change. We propose a novel convolutional layer that uniformly encodes the two features with optical flow and depth change to generate a 3D motion feature, which can serve as a carrier to simultaneously store the 3D motion estimate and confidence. The entire network iteratively encodes 3D motion features and outputs motion residuals to update predictions. Second, we propose a weight-sharing multi-frame frame-work for the proposed scene flow backbone. Inspired by the recent optical flow method SplatFlow [12], we introduce the splatting transformation, i.e., a differentiable forward warping operation, to unidirectionally transmit the previous frame’s 3D motion feature to each iteration of the current frame. This significantly reduces the number of network parameters and further improves network performance by accelerating convergence. We combine this framework with the proposed backbone to realize a new multi-frame scene flow method, SplatFlow3D. In benchmark experiments, the proposed two-frame Sim-pleFlow3D achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) results with min-imal parameters. Furthermore, the proposed multi-frame Splat-Flow3D improves on these SOTA results, with fewer parame-ters than current methods. On FlyingThings3D [13], compared with the best published RGB-D input-based method, Splat-Flow3D reduces the 3D error from 0.064 to 0.036, with a 43.8% error reduction. A. Two-frame Scene Flow Estimation Methods 

Traditional approaches for estimating two-frame scene flow usually leverage the variational technique, which can be solved as an energy minimization process [14], [16], [17]. For instance, Wang et al . [17] develop an over-parameterized scene flow representation that models both rotational and translational motion components, coupled with a two-stage optimization framework to estimate two-frame scene flow. The emergence of deep learning has enabled the direct extraction of feature representations and patterns from data, leading to remarkable progress in scene flow estimation [6]–[8], [18]– [21]. Existing methods generally rely on feature similarities. For example, FlowNet3D [9] operates directly on point clouds with PointNet++ [22], with a flow embedding computed in one layer to capture the correlation between two point clouds, which is then propagated through finer layers to estimate the scene flow. The cutting-edge CamLiFlow [21] consists of 2D [23] and 3D [24] branches with multiple bidirectional connections in specific layers. Both branches adopt a pyramid structure, which refines the optical flow and scene flow from the two frames’ feature similarity tensors in a coarse-to-fine way. Some variations in the principle of feature similarity exist. For example, OpticalExp [6] uses optical expansion to recover the pixel’s motion in depth between frames. By combining the initial depth value of the first frame, it can estimate the final depth value of the second frame, indirectly estimating the scene flow per pixel. However, the calculation of optical expansion comes from accurately estimating optical flow [25], which still relies on feature similarity. To address occlusion, existing methods introduce rigid mo-tion priors. Most recent work [8], [11], [18], [20] introduces explicit rigid motion priors to the network through additional object detection or segmentation. For example, RigidMask [8] assumes that the scene is made up of a background and several dynamic rigid bodies. These instances are segmented through a dedicated network, combined with rough initial scene flow estimation, and independently parameterized by 3D rigid transformations. Although it performs well on driving datasets like KITTI [14], it requires pixel-level ground truth annotation to train another moving rigid body segmentation network. Unlike the above methods, RAFT-3D [7] implicitly incor-porates rigid motion priors in its network. It uses semantic information to classify each pixel into a soft group of rigid objects and solves the optimized 3D rigid transformation of the group through a differentiable Gauss-Newton algorithm. The combination of GRU and Gauss-Newton iterations, together with the large number of pixels involved in the solution of each objective, determines an enormous theoretical computational cost. Thanks to customized CUDA operators, it enjoys a high degree of computational optimization. It is noted that this high degree of customization also implies that the maintenance and improvement of the method may pose some challenges. Compared with existing methods, our approach dose not use rigid motion priors, which can be highly constrained by scene-specific information and semantic details. Instead, we have developed a carefully designed network that can more effectively utilize multi-frame information that is more universal in nature. This allows for greater computational efficiency and improved performance across a wider range of scenes and circumstances. 

B. Multi-frame Scene Flow Estimation Methods 

Multi-frame information can provide richer visual and mo-tion priors, and is widely used in depth estimation [26], [27], optical flow estimation [12], and scene flow estimation. For ex-ample, Feng et al . [26] propose a context-aware transformer to refine the matching costs generated from multiple frames, and Wu et al . [27] extract depth features from consecutive frames and compute the 3D cost volume. Multi-frame information is used here to improve the accuracy of depth estimation. Traditional multi-frame SFE methods [28]–[32] also use the energy minimization framework to explicitly model multi-frame scene flow. Built upon the piece-wise rigidity as-sumption, Vogel et al . [28] ensure a temporally consistent piece-wise planar segmentation. While following a similar conceptual framework, Golyanik et al . [29] employ RGB-D data as opposed to stereo sequences. A. Preliminaries 

Our method follows the recurrent correlation-based pipeline of RAFT [36]. This pipeline has three standard operations. We use them as they are. We describe them below. 

Correlation pyramid construction. RGB data are encoded into image features by a convolutional network consisting of six residual blocks and three downsampling layers. Given the image features F it and F it+1 of frames t and t + 1 , the per-pixel correlation volume is computed by the dot product of the two feature maps. The last two dimensions of this volume are average-pooled with kernels of size 1, 2, 4, and 8. This gives a four-layer correlation pyramid Yt.IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON CIRCUITS AND SYSTEMS FOR VIDEO TECHNOLOGY 4

Correlation sampling. At the nth iteration, the (n − 1) th optical flow provides a target position for each pixel. Around this position, each layer of Yt is bilinearly sampled on a grid of size (2 × r + 1) 2, where r is the local neighborhood radius. The sampled values of all layers are concatenated into the appearance correlation feature. 

GRU-based update. The appearance correlation feature and the current state are fed into a GRU unit. The GRU unit uses convolutional layers to update the hidden state feature, and then regresses a residual. The residual updates the motion fields. In our method, the motion fields are the optical flow and the depth change. 

B. SimpleFlow3D 

The inference process of the proposed two-frame backbone SimpleFlow3D is equivalent to the Pt→t+1 process of remov-ing the previous frame’s information input (blue arrow, Fig. 1a). SimpleFlow3D takes the RGB-D data of frames t and t + 1 

as input. The RGB data are encoded into image features by the encoder described in Section III-A. The correlation pyramid of frame t is built by the standard construction in Section III-A. The correlation pyramid and the depth maps of frames t and 

t + 1 are input to a scene flow iteration module to output the iteratively updated optical flow and depth change. Then, the network uses convex upsampling to generate the full-resolution results, which can be further converted into the scene flow of frame t with camera intrinsics. Data enter the scene flow iteration module (Fig. 1b) n

times iteratively. Taking the nth time as an example, the network generates the appearance correlation feature and depth correlation feature, respectively. The appearance correlation feature is obtained by the standard sampling in Section III-A. The construction process of the depth correlation feature is as follows: (1) We use the ( n − 1)th optical flow to perform 

(2 × r + 1) 2 grid-based bilinear sampling on the depth map of frame t + 1 , and sequentially concatenate these sampled depth values to obtain the depth sampling map. (2) We sum the ( n − 1)th depth change and the depth map of frame 

t to generate the depth prediction map. (3) We compute inverse depths for both the depth sampling map and depth prediction map, respectively, then subtract them to obtain the depth correlation feature. Subsequently, a convolutional network jointly encodes the optical flow, depth change, and two correlation features to generate a 3D motion feature. Fig. 2 shows the 3D motion feature encoding process. With the 3D motion feature as input, the GRU unit performs the standard update in Section III-A. The above describes the proposed decoupling-followed-by-coupling integration approach for image plane and depth dimension data, which first constructs the appearance and depth correlation features separately and then encodes them into the 3D motion feature uniformly. In our final model, we follow GMA [37] to globally aggregate the 3D motion feature using self-similarity, to obtain the spatially aggregated 3D motion feature as additional input to the GRU unit. C. SplatFlow3D 

Building upon SimpleFlow3D, we introduce the proposed multi-frame SplatFlow3D, which uses a weight-sharing multi-frame framework to unify the first and non-first frame infer-ence. If t > 1, the multi-frame framework will establish temporal connections between the estimation process Pt−1→t of the previous frame and the estimation process Pt→t+1 of the current frame for the proposed two-frame backbone SimpleFlow3D, and use the Splatting transformation to construct the aligned 3D motion feature from the previous frame as additional temporal information input for the current frame t. If t = 1 , the multi-frame framework will construct a pseudo-aligned 3D motion feature as additional temporal information input for the current frame t. The network employs shared weights to process the same-sized additional inputs constructed for both scenarios, thus achieving unified inference. 

1) Non-first Frame Inference: As illustrated in Fig. 1a, we employ the Splatting transformation to establish a bridge for transferring historical information from Pt−1→t to Pt→t+1 .When performing the non-first frame inference, we splat the 3D motion feature of frame t − 1 to the coordinate system of frame t to obtain the aligned 3D motion feature, which is then fed into the scene flow iteration module’s all iterations, as additional input to the GRU unit. All inputs of the GRU unit will be concatenated into a new input feature for updating the hidden state feature. In the process of splatting, the source feature will publish its value to the neighboring integer pixels of its target sub-pixel, with the 2D motion direction provided by the optical flow. The value published to the same integer pixel will be IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON CIRCUITS AND SYSTEMS FOR VIDEO TECHNOLOGY 5

weighted and summed in a specific normalization to generate a complete target feature map. Specifically, we use Softmax Splatting as our splatting type, i.e., we use an exponential value of the source-to-target similarity to calculate normalized weights in our SplatFlow3D. The mathematical expression of the normalized weight is as follows, 

ni = exp (Zi) · wi

As a crucial component, the differentiable splatting transformation strongly supports the proposed multi-frame framework, and further efficiently addresses the challenges of occlusion and multi-frame computational burden in scene flow estimation. 

2) First Frame Inference: If frame t is the initial frame of the entire video, the aforementioned operations cannot be directly executed. Given the absence of historical frames, it would be na¨ ıve to use another dedicated two-frame network for the first frame inference, such as the approach of Splat-Flow [12]. However, the two independent networks lead to redundancy in the model structure, and they increase learning difficulty. Based on the above considerations, the proposed weight-sharing multi-frame framework uses only one GRU unit to handle all scenarios. For the first frame inference, we construct a pseudo-aligned 3D motion feature to match the input types and quantities of the non-first frame inference, as shown in Fig. 1b. Although the input composition of the GRU unit remains identical in both inference scenarios, the network needs to adaptively distinguish between these two cases. This distinction is crucial because the pseudo-aligned 3D motion feature not only fails to provide valuable motion priors but also may introduce noise interference, thereby affecting the estimation accuracy of both the first frame and subsequent frames. To address this, we employ a zero-initialized tensor with dimensions identical to the aligned 3D motion feature for constructing the pseudo-aligned 3D motion feature. This approach ensures that the pseudo-aligned 3D motion features constructed for the first frame of all videos are identical, which reduces the network’s difficulty in differentiating and learning this specific scenario. Through this approach, the network employs exactly the same architecture and weights when performing the first and non-first frame inference. 

D. Training and Inference 

The difference between multi-frame settings and two-frame settings lies in that multi-frame settings require input infor-mation from more than two frames to estimate the scene flow of the current frame. Our method additionally embeds information from the previous frame, i.e., we use three frames in multi-frame settings. Whenever the next frame of data is acquired, the network calculates the scene flow from the current frame to the next frame, and constructs the aligned 3D motion feature for embedding into the scene flow estimation of the next frame. We only need to train on short sequence data composed of just three frames to achieve inference on long sequence data composed of any number of frames. Specifically, we utilize the data of three consecutive frames as a training unit, with the optical flows and inverse depth changes of the first two frames serving as supervision for our network. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 1) Datasets: The primary datasets for scene flow tasks are FlyingThings3D [13] (hereinafter Things), KITTI2015 [14] (hereinafter KITTI), and Spring [15]. Our method requires three adjacent frames to be merged into a single input data unit for network training. Consistent with the approach used in SplatFlow [12], we adjust Things [13] and KITTI [14]. Regarding Things [13], considering only the left camera, we can collect a training dataset containing 71,648 data units. We use the middle three frames of each forward testing sequence to form a multi-frame testing dataset. The clean pass and final pass each consist of 437 data units. For KITTI [14], we use the multi-view extension of the standard KITTI 2015 scene flow dataset. 

2) Implementation Details: We use PyTorch to implement our method. We use the AdamW [38] as an optimizer and the One-cycle [39] as the learning rate adjustment strategy. We evaluate our method after 12 (for training) and 24 (for testing) GRU iterations. We use the NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU for training and testing. 

3) Training Processes: The training processes of our Sim-pleFlow3D and SplatFlow3D both include C+T and K-finetune. In the C+T process, we first load and freeze pre-training weights and separately train unique sub-modules. Then, we release all frozen parameters and fine-tune all of them. The batch size is 8, there are 100K training steps, and the learning rate is 1.25 × 10 −4. The pretraining weights of SimpleFlow3D come from GMA, and those of SplatFlow3D come from SimpleFlow3D. In addition, we can also train all weights of SplatFlow3D from scratch with 400k training steps and a batch size of 8, to achieve the same performance. In the K-finetune process, we fine-tune the model obtained from the C+T process on a KITTI data-enhanced hybrid dataset composed of Things [13] and KITTI [14]. C. Ablation Studies 

We conduct a series of ablation experiments. We start from GMA [37] as the baseline, which iteratively regresses the residual flows of the image plane, and gradually replace its components with our proposed components. All data come from the C+T training process. 

1) Depth Processing: Optical flow estimation methods [36], [46], [47] only need to process dense data from 2D planes. The sparse nature of the depth dimension determines that scene flow estimation is not a straightforward extension of optical flow estimation from 2D to 3D. We design two straightforward depth processing methods from a 2D perspec-tive. Among them, “Decoupling” utilizes the optical flow of GMA to index the depth map of the next frame, thereby calculating the depth change. Inspired by GMA, “Coupling” encodes RGB-D data uniformly and iteratively regresses the residual flows of the XY Z dimensions. Unlike the two baselines, we first generate the appearance correlation feature and the single-channel depth correlation feature, which together serve as the 3D motion confidence. We then encode them, along with the 3D motion estimate, uni-formly into the 3D motion feature in a decoupling-followed-by-coupling manner. We refer to this baseline as “Decou-pling+Coupling”. It can be seen from TABLE IV that “Decoupling” exhibits significantly higher 3D error in EPE 3D . This is because directly using optical flow for indexing is only effective for non-occluded regions. However, after directly copying GMA, “Coupling” still does not achieve satisfactory results on the 2D and 3D dataset metrics. This indicates that SFE differs from OFE, and it cannot simply apply OFE techniques to handle the additional depth dimension. Finally, we can see that “Decoupling+Coupling” shows significant advantages over the other two baselines across all 2D and 3D dataset metrics, which fully demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method. Furthermore, combined with Section IV-C3, we can derive an important insight, that is, a feature, which stores both 3D motion estimate and confidence, can significantly improve the regression accuracy of scene flow in both two-frame and multi-frame settings. 

2) Depth Correlation Feature Channel: In SimpleFlow3D, we construct the depth correlation feature (denoted as Cd)solely based on the depth data before encoding the unified 3D motion feature. Based on the different sampling radii rd

of Cd, we design the “Single” and “Multiple” baselines with radii of 0 and 4, respectively. It can be seen from TABLE IV that “Multiple”, i.e., SimpleFlow3D, is better than “Single” on all dataset metrics, at a similar parameter cost. The performance improvement indicates that large-scale sampling can enhance the positioning accuracy of both the image plane and depth dimension. In summary, we propose a novel multi-frame scene flow estimation network named SplatFlow3D, which splats the previous 3D motion feature to the current estimation process. Our method achieves SOTA accuracy, remarkable occlusion handling, non-rigid motion estimation, and real-world scene generalization abilities while maintaining high efficiency in the parameter cost.
