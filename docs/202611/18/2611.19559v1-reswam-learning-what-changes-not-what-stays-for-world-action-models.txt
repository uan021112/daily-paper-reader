## ABSTRACT 

World-action models (WAMs) augment vision-language-action policies (VLAs) with future prediction so that the policy can anticipate how its actions will affect the physical world. Existing WAMs supervise complete future observations. However, observations typically change little over an action chunk, so the future loss is dominated by copying static background content rather than learning task-relevant robot and object motion, limiting policy learning. Moreover, existing WAMs rely on large video generation models or separate world models, incurring heavy training and inference compute that hinders closed-loop deployment. To improve policy performance, we propose visual residual learning , supervising only the feature change from the current frame to the future frame in a frozen DINOv3 space. Compared with complete future features, this residual learning target yields a higher feature rank with richer and more diverse representations of scene changes, and spatially concentrates on the robot arm and manipulated objects rather than the background. To further improve computational efficiency, we present ResWAM, a residual world-action model with 2.3B parameters in total, including a lightweight 0.5B dual-branch DiT that jointly trains an action branch and a residual branch via conditional flow matching. Without robot pretraining ( i.e. , pretraining on external robot action trajectories beyond the benchmark demonstrations), ResWAM achieves top average success rates on LIBERO-Plus (81.1%), RoboTwin 2.0 (93.8%), and RoboDojo (9.40% success, 13.41 score) at the lowest training (2.7–4.0 TFLOPs) and inference compute (1.0–1.4 TFLOPs), and leads real-robot Open, Put, and Stack (80.0% average, +10 .0% over π0.5) at 92.7 ms latency. As frontier AI models advance rapidly, physical intelligence is emerging as a key frontier for perceiving, reasoning about, and interacting with the physical world (Seed, 2026; Fan et al., 2026c). Current approaches broadly follow two paradigms: vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world-action models (WAMs), shown in Fig. 1(a) . VLAs adapt pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) to predict actions from visual and language inputs (Black et al., 2024; 2025; Fan et al., 2026b; Kim et al., 2025), but train only on action labels without modeling how actions change the physical world. WAMs add future visual prediction alongside actions so the policy accounts for the physical consequences of its commands (Bi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2026). Existing WAMs typically realize this by predicting complete future frames or features via a video generation model or a separate world model ( Fig. 1(b) ). However, this standard paradigm suffers from two fundamental limitations in supervision target and 

model architecture .First, in terms of supervision target , supervising complete future observations weakens policy learning because consecutive frames over an action chunk change very little. For instance, on LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), current and future visual features share an average cosine similarity of 0.96; consequently, the future loss is largely satisfied by copying static background tokens rather than capturing how the robot and objects move in response to actions, diluting the auxiliary supervision signal for control. To mitigate this issue, recent studies have explored compact representations of temporal changes. Yet, these approaches still rely on indirect modeling or additional architectural complexity, such as latent-action encoding and future-feature reconstruction (Zhang et al., 2026a), action inference from latent transitions (Qiu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b), or delta tokenization coupled with video generation (Yan et al., 2026). Second, in terms of model architecture , existing WAMs incur high training and inference cost from large video generation models or separate world models (Fig. 1(b)). Video-generation WAMs (Bi et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2026) fine-tune large video diffusion models with costly 3D video VAEs, while external-world-model WAMs (Chen et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026) require separate pretrained world models or multi-stage teachers. The above limitations (static-background dominance in supervision and heavy backbone cost in architecture) motivate our hypothesis: 

(H) Supervising a lightweight DiT with visual residuals in WAMs can achieve improved performance while reducing computational costs. 

To approach (H) , we introduce visual residual learning , which shifts WAM supervision from predicting what the future world looks like to predicting how the world changes . Given visual tokens Zt and Zt+H extracted from the current frame and the frame H steps ahead using a frozen DINOv3 (Siméoni et al., 2025) encoder, respectively, we construct the visual residual target as ∆t = Zt+H − Zt, capturing temporal changes while suppressing static background content. We identify two key advantages of this supervision strategy, as illustrated in Fig. 1(c) . First , visual residuals exhibit higher feature rank than future representations, suggesting richer and more diverse information ( Sec. 4.2 ). Second ,visualizing the residuals in image space reveals that they concentrate on the robot and manipulated objects rather than static backgrounds, highlighting action-relevant scene changes ( Sec. 4.3 ). Empirically, supervising ∆t improves average success rate by +2 .9% over directly predicting Zt+H at identical compute on RoboTwin 2.0 ( Fig. 7 ). On the architectural side, we design ResWAM (Fig. 1(b)) to improve computational efficiency. Instead of using a large video generation model or a separate world model, ResWAM has only 2.3B parameters in total, including a lightweight 0.5B dual-branch DiT that jointly denoises actions and frozen DINOv3 visual residuals via conditional flow matching, without pixel reconstruction or future decoding. Across all evaluated benchmarks, ResWAM achieves the lowest training compute (2.7–4.0 TFLOPs), inference compute (1.0–1.4 TFLOPs), and inference latency (92.2–99.7 ms). 

Fig. 1(d) highlights the favorable success-latency trade-off of ResWAM on LIBERO-Plus and its strong performance on RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo. We summarize our contributions below. 

❶ Supervision-wise , to strengthen the supervision signal for higher performance, we propose visual residual learning. We show that this signal exhibits higher feature rank than full future features and concentrates on the robot and manipulated objects rather than static backgrounds, providing a more focused and expressive supervision signal. 

❷ Architecture-wise , to improve computational efficiency, we design ResWAM, a residual world-action model with 2.3B parameters in total, including a lightweight 0.5B dual-branch DiT that jointly denoises actions and frozen DINOv3 visual residuals via conditional flow matching in place of video generation models or separate world models. 

❸ Experiment-wise , without robot pretraining, ResWAM achieves the highest average success rates on LIBERO-Plus (81.1%), RoboTwin 2.0 (93.8%), and RoboDojo (9.40% success, 13.41 score) at the lowest training (2.7–4.0 TFLOPs) 2and inference compute (1.0–1.4 TFLOPs), and leads real-world FR3 tasks (80.0% average, +10 .0% over π0.5) at 92.7 ms latency. 

## 2 Related Work 

Generalist robot policies. Generalist policies combine visual-language priors with robot demonstrations to transfer across tasks and embodiments. RT-2 and OpenVLA cast discretized controls as language tokens (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). For continuous control, Octo uses diffusion over action chunks (Ghosh et al., 2024), π0 and 

π0.5 introduce flow-matching action experts (Black et al., 2024; 2025), and OpenVLA-OFT performs parallel action regression (Fan et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2025). Video-based pretraining further broadens supervision by extracting latent actions before robot adaptation, as in LAPA and UniVLA (Bu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2024). These policies predict only actions or latent actions, whereas ResWAM also predicts visual residuals to supervise world change. 

World models for robot control. Classical visual foresight rolls out action-conditioned video for goal compari-son (Finn & Levine, 2017). Video-generative WAMs produce visual subgoals, interleave video and action generation, or jointly model video latents and actions (Bi et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b; Lv et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026); Mantis and Fast-WAM instead use visual or video prediction primarily during training (Yang et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026). Feature-space methods predict latent futures: DINO-WM and FLARE forecast or align future embeddings (Zheng et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), while others learn future transitions, semantic or motion features, or latent subgoals (Chen et al., 2026a; Lin et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026; Won et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). These methods use complete future frames, video latents, or future features as their learning target, whereas ResWAM replaces the complete-future learning target with a current-to-future visual residual. 

Temporal change modeling in world models. Recent methods model representations of temporal changes between current and future states rather than the complete future. RLA-WM encodes frame-to-frame feature changes into latent actions with an autoencoder and decodes them back into future tokens (Zhang et al., 2026a); Delta-JEPA decodes actions from latent displacements (Zhang et al., 2026b); and AD-WM regularizes latent state transitions through action recovery for planning (Qiu et al., 2026). DeltaWAM jointly predicts visual deltas and actions while retaining a dense anchor and a pretrained video generator (Yan et al., 2026). However, these methods still require compressing temporal changes into latent actions and decoding them back to future features, recovering actions from latent displacements for planning, or coupling delta tokenizers with full-frame video generators; ResWAM instead directly supervises frozen DINOv3 visual residuals alongside actions with a lightweight dual-branch DiT end to end.

## 4 Rethinking World Supervision through Visual Residuals 

4.1 From future states to visual residuals 

Existing WAMs typically supervise world prediction by reconstructing complete future observations or latent represen-tations. Specifically, given demonstration trajectories, a visual encoder E maps each observation into N visual patch tokens, each of dimension D. Let Zt = E(ot) ∈ RN ×D denote the current visual representation, where the j-th row 3zt,j ∈ RD corresponds to patch j. Conventional WAMs use the full future representation as the supervision target for 

ˆZt+H , given by 

Zt+H = E(ot+H ) ∈ RN ×D . (1) However, over a relatively short horizon of H steps, much of the scene remains unchanged, making the future representation Zt+H largely redundant with the current representation Zt and causing the future loss to be dominated by copying static background tokens. This motivates a natural question: Can we isolate and directly learn the changes between current and future observations, rather than predicting the entire future representation? To this end, we define the visual token residual as the difference between future and current visual representations: 

∆t = Zt+H − Zt ∈ RN ×D , (2) where each row δt,j = zt+H,j − zt,j captures the feature change at patch j. From a saliency perspective, the residual suppresses redundant static scene content while emphasizing temporal changes, providing a more focused supervision signal for action-induced visual changes. Before incorporating ∆t into our policy architecture, we assess its suitability as a learning target from two comple-mentary perspectives: (i) feature diversity, quantified by the rank of the feature matrix, which measures the number of linearly independent feature directions within a frame (Sec. 4.2); and (ii) spatial saliency, measured by the concentration of residual norms across image regions (Sec. 4.3).

Building on the VLM output, we introduce two coupled branches: an action branch (i.e. , “Action DiT” in Fig. 4) predicting action chunks ˆAt

Unlike conventional WAM architectures (Fig. 1(b)) that either rely on large video generation backbones (such as Wan (Bi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2026) and Cosmos (Kim et al., 2026)) and 3D video VAEs to reconstruct future pixels or maintain a separate world model alongside a VLA (Chen et al., 2026a), ResWAM attaches a lightweight 0.5B dual-branch DiT directly to a pretrained VLM and a frozen DINOv3 encoder, totaling only 2.3B parameters and keeping both training and inference compute lightweight.

## 6 Experiments 

6.1 Experimental setup Model and training setup. ResWAM combines RynnBrain-1.1-2B (Li et al., 2026a) as the VLM backbone, a frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 (Siméoni et al., 2025) encoder ( 2 × 2-pooled to N = 64 tokens), and a 0.5B dual-branch DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023), trained directly on downstream demonstrations with AdamW ( App. C ) without robot pretraining. 

Benchmarks and evaluation protocols. We train and evaluate one multi-task policy per benchmark: (1) on 

LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), we train across all four standard suites ( Spatial , Object , Goal , Long ) and evaluate across all 10,030 perturbed episodes of LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2025) without exposure to perturbations during training; (2) on RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025), we train on clean and domain-randomized demonstrations across all 50 bimanual tasks and evaluate 100 trials per task under both clean and randomized conditions; (3) on RoboDojo (Chen et al., 2026b), we train on the 35-task training set and evaluate across all 42 tasks (2,100 episodes) spanning five capability dimensions; and (4) in real-world manipulation, we train and evaluate over 30 trials per task on a Franka Research 3 (FR3) arm following DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) across three tasks ( Fig. 5 ): Open (opening the top drawer, placing a red eraser inside, and closing it) for multi-stage articulated manipulation, Put (placing a cucumber onto a plate) for elongated-object grasping and placement, and Stack (stacking three blocks) for sub-centimeter alignment.

Table 4 shows that ResWAM achieves the best success rate on all three tasks (Fig. 5) with the lowest training compute (2.8 TFLOPs), inference compute (1.0 TFLOPs), and latency (92.7 ms), reaching 90.0% on Open, 96.7% on Put, and 53.3% on Stack, with an 80.0% average that exceeds π0.5 (70.0%, 5.0 TFLOPs, 120.4 ms) by 10.0 percentage points. Inspecting failure modes across baselines clarifies this gain. First, action-only VLAs ( π0.5 at 70.0% and GR00T-N1.6 at 53.3%, 245.5 ms) suffer from open-loop drift in multi-stage and contact-rich execution despite robot pretraining: lacking explicit supervision on how actions alter the scene, slight handle-grasp slips in Open or millimeter-level misalignments when placing the second and third blocks in Stack go uncorrected, toppling the tower or leaving the drawer ajar. Second, among WAMs, Fast-WAM (50.0%, 12.0 TFLOPs train, 143.6 ms) expends capacity reconstructing full future video frames and overfits to static tabletop appearance and lighting noise under limited real-world demonstrations, causing imprecise grasps, while LaWAM (68.9%, 198.8 ms) predicts complete future latents dominated by static scene content and reaches only 36.7% on Stack (−16 .6 points behind ResWAM). By supervising only the residual, ResWAM filters out static background variations and forces the policy to anticipate fine-grained gripper-object displacements, enabling accurate handle engagement, stable three-block stacking, and reliable drawer closure without external robot pretraining.

10 7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we addressed both policy performance and computational efficiency in world-action models. To improve policy performance, we proposed visual residual learning in the space of a frozen DINOv3 encoder. Compared with complete future features, learning visual residuals achieves a higher feature rank across diverse directions of scene change and concentrates spatially on the moving robot arm and manipulated objects rather than static backgrounds, providing a richer and more focused supervision signal. To improve computational efficiency, we designed ResWAM, a residual world-action model with 2.3B parameters in total, including a lightweight 0.5B dual-branch DiT that jointly denoises actions and frozen DINOv3 visual residuals via conditional flow matching without relying on video generation models or separate world models. Extensive experiments on LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin 2.0, and RoboDojo show that without robot pretraining, ResWAM achieves the highest average success rates (81.1%, 93.8%, and 9.40%) alongside the lowest training compute (2.7–4.0 TFLOPs), inference compute (1.0–1.4 TFLOPs), and inference latencies (92.2, 99.4, and 99.7 ms) among all compared VLAs and WAMs. Real-robot evaluations further confirm this advantage: ResWAM leads every compared method on Open, Put, and Stack at 1.0 TFLOPs and 92.7 ms latency, with an 80.0% average success rate that exceeds π0.5 by 10.0 percentage points.

## A Rank of the Residual and of the Future 

For each window (t, t + H) on LIBERO ( H = 8 ) and RoboTwin 2.0 ( H = 32 ), both ranks of Eq. (3) are computed on the token-centered matrix JX , where X ∈ { Zt+H , ∆t} denotes either the future tokens or the residual. Centering prevents a component shared by all tokens from counting as a direction; with N = 64 layer-normalized DINOv3 ViT-B/16 (Siméoni et al., 2025) tokens of dimension D = 768 , both ranks are at most 63 .

## B Projecting the Residual onto the Image 

To visualize the residual in image space (Fig. 3), we extract layer-normalized patch tokens on the 16 × 16 grid from the frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 (Siméoni et al., 2025) encoder and compute the per-patch residual δt,j = zt+H,j − zt,j . For the saliency map , we compute the ℓ2 norm ∥δt,j ∥2 at each patch, bilinearly upsample it to the image resolution, and blend it with the future observation ot+H using the jet colormap. For the PCA-RGB map , we project centered residual tokens onto the top three principal eigenvectors of the residual covariance, normalize each component to [0 , 1] as an RGB channel, and upsample to the image resolution by nearest-neighbor interpolation. 

## C Training and Implementation Details 

Optimization. Across benchmarks, we train ResWAM using AdamW with (β1, β 2) = (0 .9, 0.95) , weight decay 

10 −8, and BF16 mixed precision with fully sharded data parallelism (FSDP). The learning rate warms up linearly over 1,500 steps and then follows a cosine decay schedule from 10 −4 to 10 −5 for the action and residual expert and from 

10 −5 to 10 −6 for the VLM backbone. All simulation benchmarks use a global batch size of 256. 

LIBERO-Plus setup. On LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023), we train a single multi-task model on all four suites for 100k steps and evaluate it zero-shot across all 10,030 perturbed episodes of LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2025) with horizon 

H = 8 and loss weight λw = 0 .1. The action is a 7-D end-effector delta with a gripper command, normalized to [−1, 1] 

using the per-dimension 1st and 99th percentiles. At test time, the policy predicts 8 actions and executes the full chunk before replanning. 

RoboTwin 2.0 setup. On RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025), we train a single multi-task model on the combined clean and domain-randomized demonstrations released with Fast-WAM (Yuan et al., 2026) with horizon H = 32 and loss weight λw = 0 .1. The action is the 14-D absolute joint position normalized by its mean and standard deviation, with binary grippers. At test time, the policy predicts 32 actions and executes 16 steps before replanning. 

RoboDojo setup. On RoboDojo (Chen et al., 2026b), we train a single multi-task model on the official 35-task simulation training set with horizon H = 32 and loss weight λw = 0 .1. The action is the 14-D absolute joint position normalized by its mean and standard deviation. At test time, the policy predicts 32 actions and executes 10 steps before replanning across all 42 tasks (54 evaluation configurations, 2,100 episodes). 

Real-world setup. For the real-world experiments, following the DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) hardware setup, we use a Franka Research 3 (FR3) arm equipped with a Franka Hand on three tabletop manipulation tasks: Open (“Open the top drawer, put the red eraser inside, and close the drawer.” ), Put (“Put the cucumber on the plate.” ), and Stack 

(“Stack the three blocks into a tower.” ). We train a single multi-task model for 30k steps with a global batch size of 32, horizon H = 32 , and loss weight λw = 0 .1, using the 8-D end-effector state (3-D position, 4-D quaternion, and 1-D gripper width). 

## D Computational Efficiency 

Training and inference FLOPs protocol. The per-sample training ( Train ) and per-chunk inference ( Infer. ) TFLOPs in Tables 1 to 4 are counted directly on the official operator graphs at batch size 1 in BF16 under the camera views, state dimensions, and action horizons of each benchmark. Infer. counts all active inference modules per predicted action chunk over 10 denoising steps. Train counts algorithmic single-sample compute, comprising one forward pass (including any online frozen encoders or video VAEs) and one backward pass without activation checkpointing. 

Inference latency protocol. All latencies in Tables 1 to 4 were measured by us on one NVIDIA RTX A6000. Each model runs its official inference code in its default precision with batch size 1 and 10 denoising steps under the camera views, state dimensions, and action horizons of each benchmark. The timed region starts from preprocessed inputs on the GPU and ends with the predicted actions on the GPU, so CPU-side image resizing, tokenization, and normalization are excluded. Each model is warmed up for 20 iterations and then timed over 1,000 iterations (100–200 iterations for multi-second video WAMs), measuring wall-clock time with a device synchronization ( torch.cuda.synchronize or JAX block_until_ready ) before and after each call. The warmup excludes any compilation time.
