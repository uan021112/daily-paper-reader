# EasyNav: A Modular Architecture for Real-Time Navigation Across Heterogeneous Robot Environments

Abstract — Most robot navigation stacks implicitly bind map-ping, localization, planning, and control to specific represen-tations and data flows, yielding architectures hard to extend, compare experimentally, or reason about end-to-end timing. This is critical in heterogeneous scenarios, where multiple spa-tial representations, sensing modalities, and coordinate frames coexist and evolve at different rates. This paper presents a navigation architecture centered on an explicit, shared naviga-tion state that serves as the sole interaction interface between modular components. Constraining mapping, localization, plan-ning, and control to operate over this shared state enables in-terchangeable, representation-agnostic pipelines under explicit compatibility and timing contracts. We realize this architecture as EasyNav, an open source ROS 2 framework, and validate it through real-robot experiments, including indoor navigation with laser sensing and large-scale indoor-outdoor navigation with GPS and 3D LiDAR across multi-level routes. Compared with Nav2 under matched conditions, EasyNav reduces reaction latency from 377 to 33 ms indoors and from 71 to 8 ms outdoors, while also using less CPU and memory. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous robot navigation has matured into a core capability of modern robotic systems, yet the architectural principles underlying most navigation stacks have evolved far more slowly than the sensing, mapping, and learning techniques they integrate. Contemporary frameworks [1], built on ROS and its successor ROS 2 [2], [3], typically in-tertwine environment representations, localization, planning, and control through implicit data flows, ad hoc interfaces, and callback execution order. This coupling hampers sys-tematic comparison, complicates reuse across robots and domains, particularly when navigation spans indoor and outdoor environments or combines metric, semantic, and topological information, and, most critically, leaves end-to-end latency and time/frame consistency as emergent prop-erties of scheduling rather than design objectives, making reactivity and determinism hard to guarantee. This paper presents a navigation architecture organized around an explicit navigation state that captures, in a unified and temporally consistent form, all information required by mapping, localization, planning, and control. By enforcing interaction exclusively through this shared state rather than through direct dependencies, navigation components remain modular and interchangeable under well-defined compat-ibility and timing constraints, elevating time and frame

Long before ROS, layered robot control architectures such as LAAS’s three-tier decision/execution/functional de-sign [4] and JPL’s CLARAty [5] already pursued modularity, reusability, and interoperability across heterogeneous robots and missions through explicit layer boundaries and shared functional interfaces. CARMEN [6] took the opposite stance, deliberately avoiding a strict architectural standard in favor of common conventions to lower the barrier of entry for new algorithms. EasyNav shares both goals, grounding them in a single, formally declared state contract rather than layer boundaries or convention alone. The idea of coordinating independent modules through a shared data store dates back to classical blackboard ar-chitectures [7], where independent knowledge sources read and write incrementally to a common workspace without direct knowledge of each other. Robotics adopted this pattern early: Fawkes [8] organizes plugins around a time-stamped blackboard with well-defined read and write interfaces, used as a convenience mechanism with no declared, checkable manifest of a plugin’s full data dependencies. More recently, behavior-tree engines such as BT.CPP, used by Nav2 [1], expose typed, per-node ports that explicitly declare local data dependencies [9]; these ports, however, do not constitute a navigation-wide contract over validity time, coordinate frame, freshness, or execution ordering. EasyNav instead makes the shared state the sole interaction interface and attaches freshness and frame-consistency obligations to every entry, so compatibility between two plugins becomes an explicit, checkable property rather than an assumption. Composing several environment representations within one pipeline is not new either: Nav2’s layered costmaps [10] already let independent plugins, such as a static map, obstacle inflation, or custom layers, contribute sequentially to a single two-dimensional costmap consumed by the planner. EasyNav generalizes this along two axes: representations are not lim-ited to a single costmap type, since planar costmaps, multi-level surfaces, and volumetric point clouds may coexist and feed different consumers, and timestamp/frame propagation is enforced by the architecture rather than left to each layer’s implementation; a similar single-representation commitment appears in single-scene motion planning [11] and single-map terrain navigation [12], none designed for heterogeneous representations under a shared timing discipline. On the execution side, the Orocos Real-Time Toolkit [13] pioneered explicit separation of real-time and non-real-time components communicating through ports, a goal EasyNav shares but applies specifically to navigation, coupling it with the time-stamped state above. A growing body of work targets the default ROS 2 executor’s lack of explicit ordering and resulting priority inversions and starvation, proposing chain-aware schedulers [14] and refined response-time anal-yses [15]. The proposed architecture organizes navigation around a strict separation of concerns and explicit control of data flow, execution, and time: functional roles interact only through well-defined interfaces and shared state (Figure 2), enabling modularity, reusability, and principled reasoning about timing and consistency. 

A. System Organization and Execution Model 

The pipeline decomposes into a small set of functional subsystems, sensing, environment representation, state es-timation, planning, and control, each interacting with the rest exclusively through the shared navigation state, with no direct dependencies between them and no assumptions on intermediate representations (e.g., planners need not output purely geometric paths). Not every role is required in every deployment; trivial or placeholder components may stand in when unneeded, letting the same structure span from purely reactive motion to global navigation. 

B. Modular Components and Representation Independence 

A central goal is to decouple navigation functionality from environment representation: environment models are interchangeable components that may coexist, cooperat-ing sequentially or serving different consumers (e.g., pla-nar maps, multi-level surfaces, volumetric models), with representation-specific processing, such as obstacle integra-tion or safety margins, encapsulated within them so it never leaks into localization, planning, or control, which instead operate over abstract navigation data and remain interchange-able across sensing setups as long as they adhere to the navigation state interface. 

C. Navigation State as an Explicit Coordination Mechanism 

At the core lies an explicit navigation state S, accessed exclusively by navigation components and organized as a set of uniquely keyed entries e = ( k, v, t, f ): a string key k,a value v of arbitrary type, its generation timestamp t, and the coordinate frame f it refers to when applicable. Let K

denote the set of all keys that may appear in S across the navigation pipeline, i.e., the union of the keys declared by every component. Each component independently declares the keys it requires, R ⊆ K, and produces, W ⊆ K, and may only read R and write W . A pipeline is well formed only if every required key is produced by some active component, turning compatibility into an explicit, inspectable require-ment rather than an implicit assumption. The reference implementation documents this contract through normalized per-plugin specification sheets (Section V); automatically checking it at assembly time is left as future tooling. Because entries are uniquely keyed rather than overwrit-ten, S lets components cooperate or compete over multiple coexisting estimates without ambiguity, e.g., different repre-sentations for prediction versus correction, or planning versus collision checking, enabling systematic reasoning about data validity, consistency, and timing. 

D. Explicitly Ordered Execution and Reactive Scheduling 

Components are associated with nominal execution rates that bound computational activity, skipping computation when insufficient time has elapsed without affecting pipeline consistency. Upstream components can also explicitly signal new information, temporarily overriding nominal rates down-stream to improve end-to-end reaction time; responsiveness thus becomes an explicit scheduling property rather than a side effect of asynchronous execution, avoiding reliance on implicit callback timing or middleware-specific scheduling that obscures latency. 

E. Time and Frame Consistency as a Design Principle 

The architecture treats temporal consistency as a funda-mental concern rather than an implementation detail: naviga-tion decisions are only meaningful when the validity times of the data and transformations they combine are explicit and satisfy the temporal-consistency policy required by the consumer; ignoring this leads to systematic geometric errors and compromises determinism, particularly in dynamic envi-ronments or systems with non trivial kinematics. Concretely, this guarantee holds per entry, via the timestamp t attached to every value and explicit freshness checking by consumers, not via atomic cross-key snapshots or an assumption of simultaneity: two entries read together, e.g., pose and path, may originate from different write cycles, each still carrying the timestamp needed to reason about its own validity. All navigation data carries a validity timestamp, inherited from the physical instant it refers to, distinct from the wall-clock instant a component computes and emits its output. Estimates inherit the validity timestamp of their sensory or odometric sources even if computed later, and trajectories and control commands are tagged with the validity timestamp of their most recent contributing input, so freshness reasoning reflects when data is valid for, not when it was processed; their separate emission time, when a command is actually published, is what end-to-end latency measurements use (Section V). Coordinate transformations obey the same constraint: they are queried using the timestamp of the data they apply to, and substituting the current system time for correct temporal labeling is an architectural error except when explicitly justi-fied, since it introduces delay and geometrically inconsistent results. Transformations are therefore managed through a dedicated, centrally accessible store guaranteeing immediate availability once produced, avoiding asynchronous delivery that could introduce hidden latency. Internally generated transformations are inserted directly into this store, so subsequent components access them de-terministically within the same cycle, while external trans-formations go through a time-aware, non-blocking ingestion mechanism that preserves ordering. Transformation availabil-ity thus becomes an explicit architectural property rather than an emergent side effect of middleware scheduling, a foundation for predictable, robust navigation behavior often absent from existing stacks. IV. R EFERENCE IMPLEMENTATION 

This section demonstrates that the architecture of Sec-tion III can be realized within a realistic robotic system, 

EasyNav , under practical constraints such as real-time exe-cution, transformation handling, and integration with existing robotic software ecosystems, without introducing additional architectural concepts. Although the architecture itself is middleware agnostic, the reference implementation is re-alized in ROS 2 to demonstrate feasibility under realistic conditions and facilitate reproducibility. 

A. Components and plugins 

EasyNav is a single monolithic process built around a ROS 2 Lifecycle Node, the System Node , which internally contains five ROS 2 Lifecycle Nodes (Figure 3) that load and manage the plugins implementing each navigation stage and activate their processing cycles at configured rates. The specific plugins, their execution rates, and their parameters are all specified through a single configuration file, enabling reproducible deployment and explicit control over system behavior without code changes. The functional role of each node is summarized below:  

> •

Sensors Node. This node is the single entry point through which sensory information enters the navigation state; plugins elsewhere may still create auxiliary sub-scriptions for monitoring, but these do not participate in inter-component navigation data flow. This supports, for example, outdoor setups where a navigable-surfaces representation feeds localization’s prediction stage and the planner, while a volumetric representation feeds the correction stage, and a route plugin that biases a costmap-based planner toward predefined waypoint graphs while still avoiding obstacles (Figure 1b). > •

Localizer Node. This node instantiates a localization plugin that estimates robot position from odometry, perceptual data, and one or more environment represen-tations, explicitly supporting less conventional outputs too, e.g., pose sets with probabilities, probabilistic grids, or topological regions; the only requirement is that the chosen planner or controller can consume whichever output the localizer produces.  

> •

Planner Node. This node hosts a planning plugin that computes a route to a goal. While the common case is a sequence of poses, the architecture does not constrain the output format: alternative representations, e.g., way-points annotated with timestamps or velocities, enable advanced behaviors such as social-navigation speed adaptation to avoid interfering with nearby people.  

> •

Controller Node. This node instantiates a control plugin that generates velocity commands, using both stamped and non-stamped twist representations, the standard motion-command interfaces under ROS 2. To implement the navigation state defined by the proposed architecture, a thread-safe blackboard mechanism has been developed. This blackboard allows arbitrary data types to be stored and retrieved using string-based keys, enabling flexible information exchange between navigation compo-nents. Throughout the remainder of this paper, including the algorithms below, this blackboard object is referred to by its implementation name, NavState .Special care avoids continuous dynamic memory alloca-tion, incompatible with real-time execution: allocation occurs only when a new entry is created, and subsequent accesses update data via copy operations. Perceptual data, updated at high frequency, is the most critical case; since a given sen-sor’s data typically has constant size throughout execution, data structures optimized for this pattern prevent performance degradation. The blackboard is owned by the System Node and a reference to it is passed to each internal node whenever its processing cycle is executed. This design ensures a single shared navigation state while avoiding unnecessary data duplication and preserving deterministic access patterns. Individual reads and writes are serialized through a sin-gle internal mutex, giving atomic per-entry access without atomic cross-key snapshots, consistent with Section III-E. For entries written from a different thread than the one reading them, e.g., path, goals, or pose across the real-time/non-real-time boundary, a safe-copy accessor avoids exposing a concurrently overwritten value to the reader; components that both write and read the same key from one thread use a plain reference accessor instead, avoiding an unnecessary copy. 

B. Managing Time and Geometry 

In ROS 2, the transformation system maintains geometric relationships between reference frames over time, following REP 105 2, strictly enforced in our implementation: updates (TFs) are published on /tf and /tf static and received through a buffer fed by a listener, which resolves queries between arbitrary frames by interpolating between the closest 

> 2https://www.ros.org/reps/rep-0105.html

available samples, waiting up to a configurable timeout, or erroring when the request would require extrapolating into the future. This standard mechanism is incompatible with our execution model, where transformations may be produced and consumed within the same system cycle: waiting for a transformation to be published and then processed by a listener callback is not acceptable. For this reason, our architecture employs a dedicated real-time transformation buffer, the RTTFBuffer : it is fed by a transformation listener running in its own real-time thread, dedicated exclusively to external transformations; all internally produced transformations are inserted directly into it at creation time, in addition to being published through a broadcaster; and it is globally accessible to all nodes and plugins through a singleton interface. The system further adheres to the temporal labeling criteria of Section III-E and never waits for a transformation to become available at a given timestamp, since doing so would introduce uncertainty and compromise execution predictability. There are two situations in which a required transforma-tion may not be available at the exact time it is queried:  

> •

A sensory measurement from sensor s, whose associ-ated coordinate frame is denoted Fs, typically point-based data such as 2D or 3D range scans, is acquired at time ti, but the transformation from the robot base frame to Fs is not available until time tj , while the transformation is required at time tk, with ti < t k < t j .In this case, a perception buffer is used, and the most recent valid measurement whose timestamp is less than 

tk is selected. Given that the transformation listener operates in real time, this situation only occurs under exceptional circumstances.  

> •

The maps manager needs, for a perception acquired at time tp, the transformation from Fs to the map frame. This transformation is produced by localization from its own, potentially earlier, perception at time tℓ ≤ tp,since localization executes far more frequently, in the real-time thread, than the maps manager, which runs in the non-real-time thread; the maps manager accepts the transformation computed at tℓ instead of waiting for one matching tp exactly. This is an explicit bounded-staleness policy, not an unquantified approximation: the temporal error tp − tℓ is bounded by localization’s execution period in the real-time thread (e.g., 20 ms at its nominal 50 Hz rate), since a fresher transformation becomes available every cycle; the maps manager, up-dated at a lower rate, simply consumes the most recent one. 

C. Real-Time Execution Model 

As discussed, the framework executes as a single monolithic process launched from a single configuration file, avoiding complex interdependencies between multiple launcher files and simplifying deployment, debugging, and reproducibility. Within this process, two threads execute the navigation cycles, one real-time and one non-real-time; the RTTF-Buffer’s transformation listener runs in the separate dedicated real-time thread described in Section IV-B. The navigation-cycle real-time thread is configured to use FIFO scheduling with a high priority, typically around 80, and runs under the PREEMPT RT fully preemptible kernel configuration, natively available in mainline Linux without requiring an out-of-tree patch. Neither thread delegates its execution cycle or frequency control to the ROS 2 executor’s timer dispatch: each thread governs its own wall-clock rate loop directly, invoking the corresponding system cycle every iteration and spinning its callback group only for a bounded duration. This avoids the default ROS 2 executor, which imposes no explicit callback execution order and provides no built-in triggering control, and can result in priority inversion and starvation of ready callbacks under high load or tightly constrained scheduling conditions 3. In such situations, the resulting be-havior can negatively affect the intended execution frequen-cies. At startup, the shared NavState and RTTFBuffer are created before the real-time and non-real-time threads are spawned, each running its own loop that invokes the system’s real-time or non-real-time cycle, detailed in Algorithm 1, while the system is active; on shutdown, both threads are signaled to stop and joined before the navigation system itself is shut down. Instead of timer-driven execution, the system explicitly manages execution cycles and degrades gracefully when nominal rates cannot be sustained, favoring predictable be-havior over implicit scheduling; limiting the thread count further improves resource-usage determinism, an important property when navigation coexists with other time-critical processes. The System Node propagates execution to each compo-nent’s update() or update rt() method, both returning immediately once their configured frequency has not yet been reached. The real-time cycle additionally lets upstream components trigger execution ahead of their nominal rate: new sensor data (line 2) or a newly updated robot pose from localization (line 3) each set a trigger flag that propagates downstream, temporarily overriding nominal frequencies to improve reaction time to dynamic events, e.g., an obstacle’s sudden appearance, while preserving a single, explicitly ordered flow, as Algorithm 1 illustrates. A scan mode bridge node is interposed between the LiDAR and the navigation stack, forwarding measurements unchanged during normal operation and, at a predefined point, switching to a blocked mode that replaces LiDAR ranges with a fixed blocked value, providing an identical controlled trigger to both frameworks. Twenty independent executions are performed per framework and platform; in each, the robot navigates toward a predefined goal for a fixed period before the blocking event is triggered. EasyNav reacts markedly faster than Nav2 in this setting, with a mean latency of 32.97 ms versus 377.19 ms for indoor experiment and 7.86 ms versus 70.68 ms for outdoor experiment (Table III).

Regarding computational efficiency, EasyNav reaches the same indoor patrol objective with lower CPU and memory demand than Nav2, exhibiting denser velocity-command publication and longer trajectories. Notably, this indoor com-parison already favors Nav2: reaching a viable configuration on the resource-constrained Raspberry Pi 4 required trim-ming Nav2 down to the nodes used by the evaluated pipeline (Section V-A), reducing its footprint relative to a default deployment, yet EasyNav still exhibits substantially lower CPU and memory usage. The outdoor evaluation extends the comparison to a different robot, sensing stack, and adaptation direction, confirming the framework’s reliability across platforms. Both platforms use the same costmap-based representation (Table II); heterogeneous representa-tions coexisting in a single pipeline are instead demonstrated qualitatively via the NavMap integration (Section IV-A), with quantitative characterization left to future work. Regarding reaction latency, both experiments show that EasyNav achieves substantially lower reaction latency than Nav2 in the controlled perceptual-blocking experiment, con-sistent with the perception-triggered execution mechanism, in which newly received sensory data explicitly activates the ordered real-time execution path instead of waiting for the next controller-driven update. In the same indoor configu-ration, the measured real-time cycle frequency (49.99 Hz) closely matches both its nominal rate and the observed 

cmd vel publication frequency (49.41 Hz), consistent with commands being published at the cycle frequency while controller recomputation remains independently gated. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a navigation architecture organized around an explicit, time-stamped navigation state as the sole interaction interface between mapping, localization, plan-ning, and control, validated through EasyNav, a fully open source ROS 2 realization deployed on real indoor and indoor-outdoor platforms. Under a matched comparison against Nav2, EasyNav consistently uses less CPU and memory while sustaining higher controller-update frequencies and maintaining the configured command-publication rates, and reacts to a controlled perceptual-blocking event with both a lower mean and a lower spread in reaction latency, consistent with a perception-triggered, explicitly ordered execution path rather than implicit, timer-driven scheduling. These results support the claim that treating navigation state, timing, and frame consistency as explicit architectural properties is com-patible with practical real-time performance, without trading away the modularity and representation independence central to the proposed architecture. Two limitations follow directly from the scope of this evaluation: the individual contribu-tion of the perception-triggered execution mechanism is not yet isolated through targeted ablations, and the coexistence of heterogeneous representations, demonstrated qualitatively through the NavMap integration, is not yet characterized quantitatively; both are left as future work.
