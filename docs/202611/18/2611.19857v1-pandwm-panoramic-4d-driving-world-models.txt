Abstract
Driving world models usually generate videos from six
pinhole cameras with separate optical centres and small
overlaps, and must learn to keep these views consistent.
We present PanDWM, which stitches them into one 360◦
panoramic band. Camera boundaries then fall inside one
image, so keeping the views consistent means keeping the
image continuous, and each pixel still records its cam-
era ray.
Conditioned on layouts, a causal student gen-
erates the band window by window in four steps; it is
distilled from a causal teacher and trained on its own
rollouts.
A feed-forward decoder lifts each frame along
these rays into 3D Gaussians, and static Gaussians from
neighbouring frames complete them into 4D scenes. On
nuScenes, scored in one stitched 360◦view, PanDWM has
lower FID and FVD than the multi-camera baselines at 8–
46× their speed, and keeps its 50-step teacher’s FID while
lowering FVD by 22–25%. Code: https://github.
com/AIGeeksGroup/PanDWM. Website: https://
aigeeksgroup.github.io/PanDWM.
1. Introduction
Driving world models predict future surround-view video
from scene layouts and past frames, and serve as control-
lable simulators for autonomous driving. Their predictions
must stay consistent across cameras and over long rollouts.
Simulating trajectories that leave the recorded one also re-
quires rendering the predicted scene from new viewpoints.
Existing layout-conditioned generators [5, 15, 53, 76]
represent the surround view as six perspective videos, one
per camera.
This representation cuts the scene at every
camera boundary. The cameras have distinct optical cen-
tres and overlap only marginally, and content that crosses a
boundary is split between two separately generated images.
Cross-view consistency then has to be enforced by atten-
tion between views, by conditioning some views on others,
or by concatenating the views into one canvas. Methods
that lift generated video into 3D or 4D scenes [19, 65, 80]
reconstruct from these per-camera videos and inherit their
seams.
We propose PanDWM, a driving world model that
generates the surround view as a single 360◦panorama
(Fig. 1). Instead of aligning six separately generated views,
PanDWM places every camera boundary inside one image,

where cross-camera consistency becomes spatial continu-

The network processes the panorama as two halves rather
than six camera views, which also lowers the cost.
Be-
cause camera images sample their periphery more densely,
six views that are nowhere coarser than the panorama need
6240 tokens per frame against 4608, and the same network
denoises and decodes the halves 1.17× faster (supplemen-
tary Sec. 2.5). Long videos are usually produced clip by
clip [5, 33, 57], at tens of denoiser evaluations per clip and
with a gap between the recorded history seen in training
and the generated history seen at inference [24]. PanDWM
instead generates the panorama causally, one window at a
time, with a four-step student trained on self-generated his-
tory. A feed-forward decoder lifts each generated frame
along the calibrated rays into 3D Gaussians for novel-view
rendering. 4D Gaussians add ego-aligned static Gaussians
from neighbouring frames, filling regions that the current
frame does not observe along new trajectories.
Our contributions are:
• Panoramic driving world modeling. We generate the
surround view as one 360◦panorama, which turns cross-
camera consistency into spatial continuity at a lower cost
than six views. In the 360◦view, PanDWM has lower
FID and FVD than multi-camera baselines.
• A four-step causal panoramic generator.
A block-
causal student trained on its own rollouts runs 8–46×
faster than the baselines and matches the FID of its 50-
step teacher with 4 instead of 100 denoiser evaluations.
• Feed-forward 3D and 4D Gaussian lifting. A decoder
lifts generated latents along the source-camera rays into
pixel-aligned Gaussians in one pass, and static Gaussians
from neighbouring frames complete them into 4D scenes
with the lowest depth error among camera-only methods.
2. Related work
Driving video generation and world models.
Magic-
Drive [14] introduced layout control for street-view im-
ages, conditioning on 3D boxes, road maps and text.
Multi-camera video generators carry these conditions to
six views [5, 15, 39, 48, 53, 55] and keep the views con-
sistent through factorized cross-camera attention [53] or
by placing the perspective images side by side on one
canvas [76].
Others also predict semantics and metric
depth [26] or bring occupancy and LiDAR into the gener-
ation [20, 25]. Longer videos come from conditioning on
past frames [17, 21, 33, 57, 73], and recent models distil
the generator to a few denoising steps to lower the cost of
such rollouts [9, 31, 72]. The panoramas of Panacea and
OmniNWM are sets of surround cameras of this kind. In
contrast, PanDWM generates the rig as one panorama in
which every camera boundary lies inside the image.
Panoramic generation. Panoramic video models build on
perspective video diffusion. They adapt pretrained models
to equirectangular distortion and the wrap-around seam [45,
56], outpaint perspective clips to the full sphere [32,
41], or generate perspective views jointly before stitching
them [58]. DynamicScaler [30] slides its denoising win-
dow over canvases of arbitrary size, and PanoWorld [27]
follows camera trajectories in aerial scenes. Each frame in
these models has a single projection centre, and no cam-
era rig or driving layout is involved. In driving, panora-
mas have served BEV mapping [12, 52] and controllable
street-view images [42], and QuaDreamer [54] generates
panoramic video for quadruped robots. None of them gen-
erates panoramic driving video from scene layouts.
Few-step autoregressive video diffusion. Autoregressive
video diffusion assigns noise levels per token [4] or lets
noise grow across temporal chunks for streaming [37].
Few-step causal generators are distilled from a slower
teacher, by distribution matching distillation (DMD) [67,
68] from a bidirectional teacher in CausVid [69] or by
consistency distillation [40] from an autoregressive teacher
in Causal Forcing++ [77].
A student trained only on
recorded context drifts once it conditions on its own out-
puts. Self Forcing [24] therefore rolls the student out dur-
ing training, CausalDrive [61] combines this with DMD
for real-time front-view driving, and Self-Forcing++ [11]
and LongLive [64] extend generation to minutes. Adver-
sarial losses further sharpen few-step samples [29, 38, 67].
PanDWM brings self-rollout distillation with an adversar-
ial loss on rollouts to layout-conditioned panoramic driving
video.
From video to 3D and 4D scenes. Per-scene optimiza-
tion fits Gaussians to recorded driving videos [7, 62], to
generated videos [16, 79], or to recordings augmented with
generated views [35, 75]. FreeVS [46] instead synthesizes
novel trajectories with conditional video diffusion and no
explicit geometry.
Feed-forward methods predict Gaus-
sians in one pass from pixel-aligned features [3, 6, 60],
surround-view inputs [43], pixel–voxel representations [51]
or volumes [34]. Driving-specific models extend VGGT
to 4D [70], enforce photometric and geometric consistency
across views [49] or aggregate frames through Gaussian ve-
locities [63]. Some generative world models output such
scenes directly.
WorldSplat [80] decodes multi-view la-
tents into pixel-aligned Gaussians and accumulates static
geometry, DiST-4D [19] generates metric depth with the
video, and X-Scene [65] and Drive2Gauss [78] lift gen-
erated scenes into 3D or 4D Gaussians.
Reconstructed
panoramas [66, 71] are equirectangular images with one
centre each. In contrast, our decoder lifts panoramic latents
along the rays of the six source cameras.

3. Method
PanDWM takes recorded panoramic history, 3D box and
HD-map layouts and a text prompt, and predicts future
panoramic video that can be lifted into renderable Gaus-
sian scenes (Fig. 2).
The panoramic band places every
camera boundary inside one image (Sec. 3.1). A block-
causal diffusion model generates the band window by win-
dow (Sec. 3.2), a four-step student makes this generation
fast and robust to its own history (Sec. 3.3), and a feed-
forward decoder lifts each frame into 3D and 4D Gaussians
(Sec. 3.4).
3.1. Panoramic band representation
Six separately generated views have to agree at every cam-
era boundary. We avoid such boundaries by generating one
360◦× 33.3◦panoramic band per frame. We stitch the six
nuScenes images onto one azimuth–elevation grid with the
projection of OneBEV [52], which gives every pixel a single
source camera, and keep only the central 33.3◦of elevation.
The band is split into two overlapping halves of 2304×256,
which a multi-view video backbone processes as two views
per frame. Both cuts lie inside single cameras, the blended
overlap in the front camera and the wrap-around in the rear
one. Every camera boundary therefore lies within one half,
and consistency across it becomes spatial continuity within
a single view.
3.2. Causal panoramic video diffusion
Backbone and conditioning. Each half is encoded by the
Stable Diffusion 3.5 VAE. The denoiser is the diffusion
transformer of Stable Diffusion 3 (SD3) [13] with UniM-
LVG’s cross-view and temporal extensions [5], initialized
from UniMLVG weights. To let the halves exchange infor-
mation along the full 360◦row at low cost, we use row-wise
cross-half attention, in which each token row attends to the
same row of both halves. Temporal attention runs over the
window separately for each half. Class-coloured 3D box
wireframes and HD-map outlines are stitched into the band
like the images and enter through image-adapter residuals,
while CLIP and T5 embeddings of a short text prompt enter
SD3’s joint attention. Fourier-encoded rays of a virtual pin-
hole camera facing each half’s centre condition the cross-
view and temporal blocks [5] and identify each half and its
heading.
Causality and teacher training. Autoregressive rollout re-
quires that no frame sees the future. Each window contains
3 clean history frames and 16 target frames, and a frame-
block causal mask [64] lets each frame attend only to itself
and earlier frames. The backbone has no temporal convolu-
tions, and the mask alone determines causality. The history
is excluded from the loss, and all target frames share one
noise level in rectified-flow training [13]. The teacher sam-
ples with 50 guided Euler steps, and the last 3 output frames
3


of each window become the history of the next.
3.3. Four-step student trained on its own history
Fifty guided steps cost 100 denoiser evaluations per win-
dow, and a model trained on recorded history meets its own
imperfect outputs as history at inference. We address both
with a four-step student trained on self-generated history.
Initialization and sampling. The causal student fθ pre-
dicts clean latents and is initialized from the teacher by
causal consistency distillation [40, 77].

The stu-

at σ ∈{1, 0.9, 0.75, 0.5}, predicting a clean latent and re-
noising it between steps.
Training on self-generated history.
Starting from 3
recorded frames, the student rolls out windows without gra-
dients, and the final 3 generated frames become the history
of the training window [24, 61]. Gradients flow through a
randomly selected denoising exit step of that window, and
the history latents stay clean. DMD [67, 68] matches the
student’s output distribution to the teacher’s. It uses the
frozen guided teacher and a fake-score network trained on
student samples, both conditioned on the student’s history.
Adversarial supervision. DMD pulls the student toward
its teacher rather than toward recorded video. To anchor the
student to recordings, we add spatial and temporal discrim-
inators on intermediate fake-score features. Spatial heads
score pooled tokens, and temporal heads score consecutive-
frame differences. A hinge loss compares generated win-
dows with recordings at the same time steps and noise level,
including the first window after recorded history.

normalized by its energy, and ρe of the high-frequency en-

3.4. Feed-forward Gaussian lifting
Decoder and geometry. Adjacent pixels of the band can
come from cameras with different optical centres. We there-
fore place each Gaussian on its own source-camera ray.
We adapt the pixel-aligned Gaussian decoder of World-
Splat [80] to the band and train it from scratch. The decoder
takes the panoramic latents and Pl¨ucker ray maps of the cal-
ibrated source-camera rays. Cross-half attention and con-
volutional upsampling then predict one Gaussian per pixel,
with colour, opacity, scale, rotation, range dp and offset ∆p.
With c(p) the source camera of pixel p, the Gaussian centre

dition the video model, these rays keep the distinct source-

Supervision and depth relabelling.
The Gaussians are
rendered into the six nuScenes cameras, and the renders are
differentiably stitched back into the band for panoramic L1,
LPIPS and auxiliary depth losses [80]. Part of the 3D de-
coder’s training uses student-generated latents, with their
VAE decodings as targets and no depth loss, so that the de-
coder also learns to lift predicted content. To improve the
predicted ranges, each Gaussian is moved along its ray to
the range that Depth Anything 3 [28] estimates from the
input frames, jointly over the clip for 4D Gaussians. Pix-
els that Depth Anything 3 labels as sky or leaves without
a range keep dp. The decoder is fine-tuned with this rela-
belling so that its other attributes fit the new geometry.
4D Gaussians. A single frame leaves regions unobserved
along new trajectories.
The conditioning 3D boxes sep-
arate dynamic from static Gaussians.
The 4D decoder
starts from the 3D one and is trained on multiple frames,
whose static Gaussians are aligned by ego motion and com-
bined with the target frame’s dynamic Gaussians. Leave-
one-out reconstruction encourages complementary geome-
try, and an opacity coverage term encourages complete ren-
ders. At render time, aligned static Gaussians from neigh-
bouring frames fill pixels that the current frame leaves un-
covered, and the current frame keeps its dynamic content.
For recorded input, only earlier frames are used.
4. Experiments
4.1. Setups
Data. We train on the 700 training scenes of nuScenes [1]
and evaluate on its 150 validation scenes. Annotated 3D
boxes and HD maps are rendered into panoramic layout im-

of each validation scene, following the evaluation configu-
ration of UniMLVG [5] in the OpenDWM repository [39],
and serves as the benchmark for comparison with other
methods.

scenes that allow a 19.5 s rollout. Test-S takes three clips
from each, starting 5, 10 and 15 s in (249 clips), and Test-
L scores windows 3–12 of a 19.5 s rollout from each scene
start. Both test sets are disjoint in time from Val-150. Abla-
tions use the first 3.5 s of 78 validation scenes (Dev-78), and
checkpoints were selected on Dev-78 and on long rollouts
of 12 of the 83 scenes. Val-150, Test-S and Dev-78 clips

Metrics. Video quality is measured with FID [22] and I3D-
based [2] FVD [44], where FVD-1 and FVD-2 cover the
first and second 16-frame windows. The flicker ratio com-
pares frame-to-frame changes of generated and recorded
video on static scenes, with 1 as the reference, and the
sharpness ratio compares their detail.
Controllability is
measured by object recall from Faster R-CNN [36] and
road IoU from Mask2Former [8], and efficiency by denoiser
evaluations (NFE) and time per frame. All methods are
compared with equal sample counts and identical valid re-
gions, alongside real-vs-real reference distances [10] and
temporal measures [18]. For our method we report means
and standard deviations over three seeds, with 95% scene-
level jackknife intervals.
Evaluation spaces and baselines. We run UniMLVG [5],
the SD3.5-based OpenDWM model [39], DreamForge [33],
DiST-4D [19] and OmniNWM [26] with official weights
and sampling settings (reproduction checks in supplemen-
tary Sec. 3).
All of them predict from recorded history
and are scored on the same 32 target frames of each Val-
150 clip, with 12 Hz outputs matched to these frames by
LiDAR timestamp. DiST-4D and OmniNWM take a sin-
gle recorded frame as history, and OmniNWM receives no
boxes or map.
Quality is evaluated in the 4114 × 256
panoramic band that our model generates, into which base-
line outputs are stitched with the OneBEV [52] mapping.
For a second evaluation, our band is mapped back into the
six real camera views at 256 × 448. Gaussian baselines are
DrivingForward [43], Omni-Scene [51], ReconDrive [70]
and MCSplat [49].
Implementation details. We train in four stages on 8 GPUs
with AdamW, with learning rates of 2×10−6 for the student,
4×10−7 for the fake score and 3×10−5 for the discrimina-
tor heads, and an EMA decay of 0.99; the stages and other
settings are in supplementary Sec. 1.4.

4.2. Comparison with driving video models
Short-term prediction. PanDWM has a lower FID, FVD-1
and FVD-2 in the band than every baseline and generates 8
to 46 times faster, at 1.96 s per frame (Tab. 1 and Fig. 4). It
also has higher object recall and road IoU than DreamForge,
DiST-4D and OmniNWM. Generating the band natively
avoids the resampling loss of six-view outputs. Recorded
views at 256 × 448 already score 17.9 FID once stitched
into the band, whereas mapping the band into the six cam-
eras costs 0.63 FID on recorded data. In the six cameras,
PanDWM also has the lowest FID (5.34). At camera bound-
aries it adds no seams beyond those of recorded video, un-
like four of the five baselines (supplementary Sec. 5). Con-
fidence intervals and the full six-camera evaluation are in
supplementary Sec. 2.1.
Front-view world models.
We also compare the front
view of our panoramas with HorizonDrive [72], Epona [73],
Vista [17] and GEM [21] on 149 of the Val-150 clips (Fig. 5;
settings in supplementary Sec. 3). The data loader used
for these runs excludes scene-0018, in which one frame
is 50.5 ms off, beyond its 50 ms synchronization tolerance
(Val-150 uses 51 ms). At 2.5 s, PanDWM keeps the marked
objects in place, whereas the front-view models lose or dis-
place them.
Multi-camera comparison. Fig. 6 compares our panora-
mas with those of UniMLVG and OmniNWM [26], whose
six views are stitched with the OneBEV mapping of Tab. 1.
Across camera boundaries, PanDWM reproduces the ap-
pearance of the recorded band.

5. Conclusion
We presented PanDWM, a driving world model that gen-
erates the surround view as a single 360◦panoramic band.
A four-step block-causal student, trained on self-generated
history, generates the band window by window, and a feed-
forward decoder lifts the generated latents along calibrated
source-camera rays into 3D Gaussians, which static Gaus-
sians from neighbouring frames complete into 4D scenes.
Limitations and Future Work. (1) Each window sees only
the last three generated frames, and Test-L FVD grows from
86.6 (windows 3–5) to 126.6 (windows 9–11).
Renders
of the accumulated static Gaussians could serve as longer
memory. (2) 4D completion covers only static content, leav-
ing unseen parts of moving objects empty; aligning them
with the 3D boxes is left for future work.

1. Implementation details
1.1. Panoramic preprocessing and conditions
Drawing on OneBEV [52], the six nuScenes images are
mapped to a 9600 × 900 canvas spanning 360◦in az-
imuth and ±25◦in elevation.
Keeping rows [150, 750)
gives a 9600 × 600 band with 33.3◦vertical coverage. The
halves use columns [0, 5376) and [4224, 9600), each span-
ning 201.6◦. Their 1152-column overlap (43.2◦) lies inside
the front-camera region. Each half is resized to 2304×256.
For display and evaluation, removing the right half’s 494
overlapping columns gives a 4114 × 256 band, with a 128-
column raised-cosine cross-fade replacing the hard cut. The
rear seam wraps from column 9600 to column 0 within the
rear-camera region, without overlap or blending.
The two layout inputs are class-coloured 3D box wire-
frames and HD map outlines. Map elements comprise driv-
able areas, lanes, pedestrian crossings, walkways, stop lines
and car parks within 65 m. The condition panoramas come
from the same release as the RGB panoramas; our renderer
for these conditions draws each camera with its calibration
and stitches the results with the RGB mapping, so that cor-
responding condition and RGB pixels share a source cam-
era.
1.2. Video architecture and causal teacher
The SD3.5 VAE produces a 32×288 latent with 16 channels
for each half. A patch size of 2 gives 16 × 144 = 2304 to-
kens. The denoiser uses the 24-layer SD3 transformer [13],
extended and initialized from UniMLVG [5]. Cross-view
and temporal self-attention blocks follow layers 1, 5, 9, 13,
17 and 21, with learned scalar gates blending their outputs
into the main stream. Row-wise cross-view attention ex-
changes same-row tokens between both halves, while tem-
poral attention operates over all window tokens of each half.
Concatenated box and map images enter an image adapter
whose residuals are added to the first six transformer blocks.
CLIP and T5 encode text for SD3’s joint attention. Each
half’s virtual pinhole camera faces the half’s centre; its
Fourier-encoded ray map is added to the cross-view and
temporal block inputs. These conditioning rays differ from
the calibrated source-camera rays used for Gaussian place-
ment.
Windows contain 19 frames at 10 Hz, with 3 clean his-
tory frames excluded from the loss and 16 targets sharing a
noise level σ. Rectified-flow training [13] uses

the released tests; all 55 test cases passed.

1.5. Gaussian decoder and supervision
Drawing on WorldSplat’s pixel-aligned head [80], the de-
coder is trained from scratch for paired panoramic latents
and Pl¨ucker ray maps. It uses a convolution to 512 chan-
nels, cross-view attention over both halves before and after
a convolutional middle block, and four decoder blocks that
upsample by 8× to each half’s 2304 × 256 grid. Each pixel
predicts colour, opacity, scale, rotation, metric range and
positional offset. Range d ∈[0.5, 400] m uses inverse-depth
parameterization. The source-camera origin and calibrated
unit ray determine its placement, with a learned offset; both
halves’ Gaussian sets are concatenated without removing
overlap predictions.
Rendering-based supervision follows WorldSplat [80].
We render into the six nuScenes cameras using gsplat’s anti-
aliased mode, then differentiably stitch the renders with the
data mapping. L1 and LPIPS supervise colour, with a weak
depth term clipped at 100 m. The 3D stages use precom-
puted dense per-camera depth maps as targets; the later 4D
stages, including the final fine-tuning, use Depth Anything
3 [28] estimates computed during training on the recorded
panoramas. In the last 1000 steps of the 3D stages, out-
puts of the consistency-distilled four-step student replace
the recorded latents with probability 0.5 for 500 steps and
0.75 for 500 steps; the 4D stages use recorded latents only.
For student latents, the VAE decoding of the same latent
supplies the target and depth supervision is disabled.
1.6. 4D Gaussian training and completion
In training, Gaussians whose centres fall inside a 3D box of
the eight movable classes (car, truck, bus, trailer, construc-
tion vehicle, pedestrian, motorcycle and bicycle) enlarged
2


by 0.3 m are dynamic; others are static. At render time,
completion and depth fusion use every annotated box, so
objects such as barriers and traffic cones also keep only the
Gaussians of frame t; the static depth metrics exclude only
the eight movable classes. The 4D decoder starts from the
3D decoder and trains on three recorded frames per clip.
Besides individual-frame reconstruction, their static Gaus-
sians are transformed into the target frame using ego motion
and rendered with the target frame’s dynamic Gaussians. A
leave-one-out term renders the target from the other frames’
static Gaussians. Coverage supervision penalizes 1 −α on
valid pixels, preventing transparent Gaussians from match-
ing dark regions against a black background.
At render
time, the static Gaussians of frames t −20, t −15, t −10,
t −5, t + 5 and t + 10 that have opacity at least 0.3 and lie
within 40 m horizontally of their own frame’s ego position
fill pixels not covered by frame t. This offline completion
uses both preceding and following frames; dynamic content
comes only from frame t.
1.7. Gaussian evaluation settings and render refine-
ment
Depth relabelling and fine-tuning. In Tabs. 3 and 4, each
Gaussian is moved along its pixel ray to the range that
Depth Anything 3 [28] estimates from the input frames,
the recorded panoramas for reconstruction and the VAE-
decoded generated frames for generated scenes; pixels la-
belled sky or without an estimate below 99 m keep the de-
coder’s range. For 3D Gaussians the estimate uses the frame
alone. For 4D Gaussians it is computed jointly over the
clip, and the depth of each static pixel is fused with the
static points of frames t ± 2, ±4, ±6 and ±8 moved into
the same camera by ego motion: candidates within 10%
of the pixel’s own depth are accepted and the median is
taken. For reconstruction, only recorded frames up to the
input frame are loaded, so the estimate and the fusion use
earlier frames only, and the 4D scene adds the static Gaus-
sians of the frames 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 s before the input;
no target frame and no later frame is used, and a history
frame that coincides with a target is left out for that tar-
get. The learned offset keeps only its component across the
ray, softly bounded to one decoder pixel. The decoder is
fine-tuned for 2000 steps with the same relabelling inside
training and an LPIPS weight of 0.5 on the frame, compos-
ite and leave-one-out renders. With the decoder used before
this fine-tuning, on 78 development clips, the per-pixel rela-
belling gives 4D reconstruction PSNR 18.70, static AbsRel
0.269 and δ1 0.678 at 224×400, and shifted-view FID 19.9
and 46.3 at 0 and ±4 m; keeping the decoder’s own ranges
and correcting only one DA3 scale per frame gives 18.91,
0.330, 0.557, 18.8 and 59.6. The relabelling therefore im-
proves depth and large shifts at the cost of consistency near
the recorded trajectory (FVD-1 at 0 m 107.5 against 69.5).

2.5. Inference cost at matched angular resolution
The panorama has 11.4 and 7.7 pixels per degree horizon-
tally and vertically, against about 7 and 6.5 for front and
side camera views at 256 × 448, so it carries 1.71 times
as many tokens per frame (4608 against 2688). To sep-
arate the representation from this resolution, we time the
same four-step student on the two halves and on six camera
views at three further sizes (Tab. S4): 304×736 matches the
panorama’s average angular resolution, 320×832 makes the
views nowhere coarser than the panorama, and 448×800 is
the output size of DreamForge, DiST-4D and OmniNWM.
All six views share one size, so the wider rear camera stays
coarser than the panorama in every configuration. The re-
sized views are upsampled from 256 × 448, so only their
cost is measured; all runs use one H100, the same weights,
clips and seeds, and the time scope of Tab. 1, denoising plus
decoding. At the average angular resolution, six views cost
as much as the two halves (0.479 against 0.495 s per frame).
Six views that are nowhere coarser need 6240 tokens and
take 1.17 times as long, and six 448 × 800 views take 1.66
times as long: camera images sample their periphery more
densely and must cover extra elevation at their centres to
reach ±16.7◦at their edges.

from frame t. Unlike the evaluation, which removes frame
t’s Gaussians at depth discontinuities and those larger than
1 m, the figure keeps all of them, since the removal outlines
objects with holes in views without translation. The pixels
that then remain uncovered (0.6%, 3.4% and 0.04% in the
reference, forward and turned views) are filled by push-pull
interpolation, which the generated-scene evaluation applies

5. Seams at camera boundaries
To measure seams, we average the absolute horizontal lu-
minance difference between neighbouring columns of the
stitched band over rows, the 32 scored frames and the 150
Val-150 clips. At a column b, the seam ratio sums this pro-
file over columns b −2 to b + 2, subtracts four times its
mean over the columns 4 to 10 away from b on both sides,
and divides by that mean; it is close to 1 without a seam and
to the step size for a one-column step. Recorded panora-
mas already have seams at the six camera boundaries, from
exposure and parallax differences (ratio 10.28), and stitch-
ing 256 × 448 camera views adds more (14.95). We there-
fore report each method’s excess over recorded views at its
own resolution, stitched in the same way, with 95% inter-
vals from 2000 scene-bootstrap resamples.
