Abstract
Joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) world mod-
els predict future embeddings instead of pixels and plan in
latent space. Two challenges remain: 2D images leave the
metric geometry that manipulation acts on implicit and en-
tangled with appearance, and what these models imagine
stays hidden, as they are mostly judged in simulation by
short-horizon latent planning. We present JEPA4R, a JEPA
world model that observes single-depth-camera point clouds,
predicts and plans in their latent space, and acts through a
policy head on its frozen latents; we call this 4D point plan-
ning. A decoder on the frozen world model shows what it
imagines as 4D point clouds, with a mean point error within
1.1 cm over 200 steps. Under the policy-learning protocols of
OGBench and CortexBench, JEPA4R leads pixel JEPA mod-
els on OGBench Cube-Single, Cube-Double, Cube-Triple
and Scene, and ranks first on MetaWorld and Adroit Relo-
cate with 98.4% and 88.0%, above the runner-up, R3M, at
96.0% and 65.3%. Trained only in a digital twin, JEPA4R
and its policy heads run zero-shot on the real arm with both
a gripper and a dexterous hand. Code & Website.
1. Introduction
World models built on joint-embedding predictive architec-
tures (JEPAs) predict how a scene’s latent changes under
an action and plan toward the latent of a goal [4, 79, 110].
However, current JEPA world models have two significant
challenges in robot manipulation, which hinges on metric
3D geometry:
First, most JEPA world models read 2D input. Point-
cloud JEPAs mostly pretrain encoders without actions [27,
36, 75, 115] or forecast LiDAR occupancy [112], so metric
geometry rarely reaches the model that predicts and acts.
An image entangles metric geometry with appearance and
perspective, so a pixel model must infer depth, whereas
a point cloud from the same camera records visible sur-
faces at their metric positions (Fig. 3). Even web-scale
2D foundation features show limited 3D awareness [18].
LeWM [50], trained from pixels without pretraining, has its
lowest success on OGBench Cube [61], where it trails DINO-
WM [110] on frozen DINOv2 features (74% vs. 86%) [50].
Its authors relate this gap partly to the 3D nature of the
scene [50].
Second, what these models imagine stays hidden. JEPA

latents are trained to be predictable, not reconstructive. Ex-
isting tools only decode them to images [50, 110] or audit
them indirectly [50, 84], so the imagined 3D scene stays
out of sight, which hampers debugging and real-robot safety
checks. Scores do not reveal it either. JEPA world mod-
els are mostly scored in simulation by short-horizon goal
reaching [50, 79, 110], where a sampling-based planner
such as the cross-entropy method [15] searches a few ac-
tion blocks for the predicted latent closest to a goal latent.
Such search tests the predictor over short, in-distribution
horizons, not how well the latents serve the policies robots
learn. For example, LeWM latents that plan well trans-
fer poorly to policy learning [45], and the model is eval-
uated only in simulation [50]. Frozen-encoder imitation
on established suites such as CortexBench [51] asks in-
stead how well a representation lets a policy learn a skill
from a few demonstrations, which matters when robot
data are scarce. However, JEPA world models rarely face
it.
To address these two challenges, we propose JEPA4R,
a JEPA world model designed to see metric geometry and
to show what it imagines, so that a robot’s predictions can
be checked before it acts. It reads a single-camera point
cloud through a parameter-matched encoder (Fig. 2). Ev-
ery point cloud is paired with an image from the same
eye-to-hand camera, so the 2D JEPAs are trained or eval-
uated on the same trajectories and viewpoint. To evaluate
how JEPA4R performs in robot policy learning, we train a
policy head on the frozen latents under established proto-
cols. Specifically, we use goal-conditioned policy learning
on OGBench [61] and imitation on CortexBench, where
JEPA4R is ranked among published representations and
2D JEPAs evaluated on the same data. JEPA4R predicts,
plans and acts in the 4D point latent space of its encoder,
and we call this 4D point planning. An imagined rollout
is the latent sequence that the predictor generates autore-
gressively from context frames and actions.
A decoder
attached to the frozen world model shows this imagina-
tion by turning every imagined rollout into a decoded 4D
rollout (Fig. 1a).
All world models train in simulation.
The RM75-B twin, a digital twin of the RealMan RM75-
B arm, lets JEPA4R’s twin-trained world models and pol-
icy heads run zero-shot on the real arm. Notably, we eval-
uate policy learning on manipulation suites with colored
point clouds from a pipeline that also runs on the real arm,
while previous work [58] evaluates planning alone with xyz-
only raycast point clouds and finds the two modalities on
par.
JEPA4R leads the 2D JEPAs on OGBench Cube and
Scene and ranks first on CortexBench, above every published
representation (Tabs. 1 and 2, Fig. 4). Its decoder recovers
the observed scene from encoded and predicted latents alike,
so even long imagined rollouts stay close to the observed
point clouds (Tab. 3, Fig. 7). On the real arm, JEPA4R’s
twin-trained policies run with both a parallel gripper and a
dexterous hand.
In general, our paper can be summarized in the following
three points:
• 4D point planning: we propose JEPA4R, which predicts,
plans and acts in the latent space of realistic single-depth-
camera point clouds, to bring the metric 3D geometry that
2D inputs leave implicit into JEPA world models.
• Latent-space reconstruction: we propose a decoder at-
tached to the frozen world model that turns every imagined
rollout into 4D point clouds, to show what JEPA world
models imagine, which their latents keep hidden. The de-
coded imagination keeps a mean point error within 1.1 cm
over 200 environment steps, so it can be inspected for
debugging and real-robot safety checks.
• Experiments on benchmarks and in the real world: we
learn policies on OGBench and CortexBench in a paired
2D/3D evaluation, where JEPA4R’s point-cloud features
rank first on CortexBench, above every published repre-
sentation and 2D JEPA. JEPA4R transfers zero-shot to the
real arm with both a gripper and a dexterous hand, after
training only in a digital twin.
2. Related work
JEPA and latent world models.
JEPA world models ex-
tend joint-embedding prediction [3, 5, 8, 20, 53, 54, 91]
to action-conditioned dynamics and regress latents, unlike
world models that generate observations [7, 22] or predict
rewards [24].
DINO-WM [110] and V-JEPA 2-AC [4]
learn predictors on frozen foundation features, whereas
PLDM [79] and LeWM [50] train encoder and predictor end
to end. Follow-up work analyzes planning success [2, 81],
generalization beyond expert rollouts [57], counterfactual
action evaluation [80] and behavioral monitoring [84], and
adds objectives for action sensitivity, physical-state ground-
ing [96, 104, 106], object-level masking [56] and policy
transfer [45], but all these models observe images, whose
features show limited 3D awareness [18]. JEPA world mod-
els are mostly scored by planning success [49, 50, 79, 110],
and PSG-JEPA [96] adds policy learning, whereas pre-
trained robot representations [31, 48, 55, 72, 77, 95, 97]
are compared by frozen-feature imitation, chiefly in Cor-
texBench [51] on MetaWorld [99] and Adroit [73], with
further studies varying learner, data and test conditions [11,
14, 16, 23, 28, 60, 76, 78, 83].
We learn policies on
OGBench [61] and CortexBench with paired 2D/3D data
and compare them with published representations and 2D
JEPAs.
3D world models.
Most point-cloud JEPAs pretrain en-
coders without actions [27, 36, 75, 115] or forecast LiDAR
occupancy [112] instead of driving a planner. Other 3D


world models forecast explicit particles [41, 90], points [29,
30, 65, 105], Gaussians [47], depth-augmented video [107],
structured latents [42] or occupancy [109], and 3DPWM [65]
and PointWorld [30] plan with such forecasts on real arms
rather than in a latent space. Such explicit forecasts make
every prediction visible at the cost of generating each ele-
ment of the next scene, whereas JEPA world models pre-
dict in a latent space and have so far left the imagined
scene unseen. On the policy side, point-cloud encoders
range from PointNet [66, 67] to Point Transformer V3 [93]
and Sonata [94], 3D policies condition diffusion heads
on such encoders [32, 102, 103], and controlled studies
favor point clouds and 3D-aware encoders over 2D in-
puts [21, 25, 43, 68, 113, 114], but such policies lack a world
model. Decoders on frozen features map self-supervised
representations [10, 108] and the predicted latents of JEPA
world models [50, 110] to images, and recent work lifts
video world-model rollouts into 3D through generated pix-
els [33, 87]. Feed-forward networks regress dense point
maps from images [37, 85, 86], PAGE-4D [111] extends
VGGT to dynamic scenes, and point-set decoders generate
or complete clouds [1, 19, 98, 100, 101]. Closest to ours,
Oberweger et al. [58] find LeWM on idealized raycast point
clouds statistically on par with its pixel version on its own
benchmarks. JEPA4R instead reads colored point clouds
from one fixed depth camera. Its policy head acts on the
frozen latents of a world model that can also imagine the
scene, and a decoder adapted from PAGE-4D turns these
latents directly into point clouds.
Sim-to-real with 3D observations.
Depth and point-cloud
observations narrow the gap between simulation and real-
ity: policies trained on them in simulation transfer to real
hands and arms [12, 46, 69], point-cloud policies run on
real arms and humanoids [102, 103], and point-cloud mod-
els resist changes in appearance and viewpoint [64, 102].
Simulators are also matched to the deployment scene. Ri-
alTo [82] scans a digital twin and robustifies point-cloud
policies in it, ACDC [13] generates digital cousins from one
image for zero-shot transfer, and Gaussian splatting renders
photorealistic RGB for sim-to-real [40, 70]. World models
are either learned directly on physical robots [92] or trained
in simulation and transferred to legged robots [35, 38] and
arms [65, 89]. Among JEPA world models, V-JEPA 2-AC [4]
plans on real arms with a predictor trained on real robot
video, whereas DINO-WM [110] and LeWM [50] are eval-
uated in simulation. JEPA4R is a point-cloud JEPA world
model trained in a twin of its deployment arm.
3. Preliminaries
Following LeWM [50], we train world models on offline
trajectories of observations ot and actions, cut into clips
of four frames spaced five environment steps apart. The
action block at concatenates the five actions between con-
secutive frames. An encoder with a projector, fθ, maps each
observation to a latent zt = fθ(ot) ∈R192. The pixel en-
coder is a ViT-tiny [17] trained from scratch on 224 × 224
images. The predictor gϕ, a six-block transformer condi-
tioned on action embeddings through AdaLN-zero [62], at-
tends causally over at most three past latents; within a clip,
ˆzt+1 = gϕ(z1:t, a1:t). With teacher forcing on a clip o1:4,

The model acts by planning: the cross-entropy method

of a goal observation. We follow this planning setting in
the supplementary material (Sec. S1) and evaluate every
world model through a policy head on its frozen latents
(Sec. 4.3).
4. Method: JEPA4R
Manipulation acts on metric geometry, and a robot should
see what its world model expects before it moves. JEPA4R
therefore observes the scene as a point cloud from one depth
camera, predicts and plans in the latent space of these point
clouds, acts through a policy head on its frozen latents, and
decodes what it imagines into 4D point clouds (Fig. 2).
4.1. Point clouds from one depth camera
JEPA4R observes the scene through one fixed depth cam-
era (Fig. 3), as in a deployed cell. It is the eye-to-hand
camera that renders each benchmark’s pixels, treated as a
time-of-flight sensor. Each depth image is lifted to world-
frame points and cropped to a workspace box. The ren-
derer’s segmentation removes the table, floor, static fix-
tures and invisible goal markers and labels the task ob-
jects, which on Adroit Relocate include the visible goal
marker.
Merging points within 5 mm voxels keeps sur-
faces near the camera from dominating. We then sample
N = 1,200 points.
Each point stores xyz and rgb, so
ot = Pt ∈RN×6, normalized by a fixed affine map shared
with the real arm.

4.3. Acting through a policy head
A world model helps a robot through the actions its la-
tents support, so JEPA4R acts through a policy head πω
on its frozen latents (Fig. 2, top).
The head reads the
latents of recent frames, plus proprioception or a goal
latent zg = fθ(og) where the task provides them, and
outputs the next action or action block.
We train πω
while fθ stays frozen and report the success rate of the
trained policy (Sec. S2).
The head’s success thus mea-
sures what the latents make available to control, and
JEPA4R and every baseline we evaluate share the same
heads.
Fed with imagined latents, a latent-only head
also lets JEPA4R look ahead: gϕ rolls the head’s action
blocks forward, and decoding the imagined rollout with Dψ
(Sec. 4.4) previews the policy in 4D point clouds before it
acts.
4.4. Latent-space reconstruction
To show what the world model imagines, a decoder Dψ maps
every frozen latent zt, observed or imagined, to its frame’s
1,200-point cloud in one pass (Fig. 2, bottom; Fig. 7). We
train Dψ post hoc on the frozen world model, because a
training-time reconstruction loss does not help the control of
JEPA world models [50].
Expanding one 192-d vector into 1,200 metric points
needs a strong geometric prior, so the decoder builds on
PAGE-4D [111], a feed-forward reconstruction model for
dynamic scenes. PAGE-4D combines a DINOv2 ViT-L [59],
24 alternating frame and global attention layers and a DPT
point-map head [74]. A set generator turns zt into PAGE-
4D’s input tokens: 256 learnable queries pass through three
residual MLP blocks that zt modulates through FiLM [63],
and the resulting 16 × 16 grid of 1,024-d tokens enters
PAGE-4D after its patch embedding. A learnable affine
map moves the 224×224 point map into the target frame, an
rgb branch colors it, and 1,200 pixels form ˆPt. The decoder

5. Experiments
5.1. Benchmarks and Metrics
OGBench.
We use the Cube (Single, Double, Triple,
Quadruple), Scene and Puzzle (3 × 3, 4 × 4, 4 × 5) environ-
ments of OGBench [61] (Tab. 1, Fig. 6). Cube and Scene
are 3D manipulation tasks, and Puzzle comes in three board
sizes. The metric is the success rate on each environment’s
five official evaluation goals.
CortexBench.
We use five MetaWorld [99] tasks (As-
sembly, Bin-pick, Button, Drawer and Hammer) and the
Adroit [73] dexterous-hand task Relocate (Tab. 2). The met-
ric is the success rate, taken as the best of 20 evaluations.
On MetaWorld, the published representations of the VC-1
study [51] use the same metric.

the point clouds (Sec. 4.2) and LeWM, PLDM and LeJEPA
on the paired images, while VISReg and V-JEPA 2.1 use the
authors’ released weights (Sec. S2.4). We then train a policy
on the frozen latents and report its success rate. All methods
use the same policies and evaluation episodes. The JEPA4R
world model trains with AdamW for 10 epochs, with a learn-
ing rate of 5×10−5, a batch size of 128 and bf16 precision.
All training runs on one NVIDIA RTX 5090 (32 GB) with a
16-vCPU Intel Xeon Gold 6459C and 90 GB of RAM, using
PyTorch 2.8 and CUDA 12.8. For more details, see Sec. S2.
5.3. Point Clouds Outperform Pixels
OGBench.
Cube and Scene are 3D manipulation tasks, and
JEPA4R outperforms every other method on Cube-Single,
Cube-Double, Cube-Triple and Scene (Tab. 1), with 78.4%
on Cube-Single and 64.8% on Scene. On Cube-Quadruple,
every encoder, JEPA4R included, reaches 0.0. Its goals
ask the arm to move, stack or cycle four cubes, up to four
pick-and-place steps each, and the policy must learn them
from random play data [61]. This long-horizon policy is
hard to learn from any representation: all six offline goal-
conditioned RL methods of OGBench, GCIQL included,
also reach 0 on it even with ground-truth states [61]. We
therefore leave Cube-Quadruple out of Tab. 1. On Puz-
zle, most methods come close to full success on 3 × 3
and 4 × 4, JEPA4R with 100.0% and 95.6%. On 4 × 5,
JEPA4R reaches 68.0%, above every other method except
LeJEPA.

5.5. Ablation Study
Decoder backbone.
We replace PAGE-4D in the decoder
with other feed-forward 3D reconstruction models, keep
the set generator, the affine map, the training data and
both training stages unchanged, and evaluate again on OG-
Bench Cube (Tab. 4a).
PAGE-4D and VGGT give the
same per-frame error (0.79 cm), and Depth Anything 3
reaches 0.75 cm. The gap stays as small on imagined roll-
outs: at environment step 200, PAGE-4D reaches 0.89 cm,
VGGT 0.88 cm and Depth Anything 3 0.85 cm. Although
PAGE-4D extends VGGT to dynamic scenes, the two de-
code rollouts equally well. Across the three backbones,
each error varies by at most 0.11 cm, so the geometry in
the frozen latents does not depend on one particular de-
coder.
Point-cloud encoder.
We replace JEPA4R’s PointNet with
other point-cloud encoders of the same parameter count,
keep the rest of the world model, the training data and the
policy head unchanged, and compare on OGBench Scene
(Tab. 4b). PointNet reaches the highest success (64.8), so we
keep it as JEPA4R’s default encoder.
Temporal window.
By default, the world model reads
three context frames spaced five environment steps apart.
We change the number of context frames to two and four,
with the policy head unchanged, and compare on OGBench
Scene (Tab. 4c). With two frames, success is 63.2, and from
three frames on it no longer changes (64.8 with three and
with four), so the default of three frames is enough.
Training hyperparameters.
JEPA4R trains with a learn-
ing rate of 5×10−5 and a batch size of 128. We halve and
double the learning rate to 2.5×10−5 and 10−4, and set the
batch size to 64 and 256 while keeping the learning rate
and the number of epochs, on OGBench Scene (Tab. 4d).
JEPA4R stays between 57.6 and 68.5 in every setting. Chang-
ing the learning rate costs at most 2.9 points, whereas the
batch size matters more, with 57.6 at 64 and 68.5 at 256.

6. Limitation and Future Work
This paper focuses on JEPA world models and policy learn-
ing from single-camera point clouds. A general world model
shared across scenes and robots relies on large-scale, di-
verse 3D robot data, which remains scarce and is beyond
the scope of this paper. How to collect high-quality data
for JEPA world models, and whether a general recipe ex-
ists, remain open questions and are the focus of our next
steps.
7. Conclusion
Most JEPA world models have seen scenes in 2D, been
judged in simulation by short-horizon latent planning and
hidden what they imagine. JEPA4R predicts, plans and acts
in the latent space of single-depth-camera point clouds, and
on 3D manipulation tasks it leads the 2D JEPAs by a large
margin. It ranks first on MetaWorld and Adroit Relocate
with 98.4% and 88.0% success, above the runner-up, R3M,
at 96.0% and 65.3%. Its decoder turns every imagined rollout
into 4D point clouds, with a mean point error within 1.1 cm
of the observed points over 200 environment steps. Beyond
simulation, JEPA4R completes closed-loop deployment on
a real robot arm with both a gripper and a dexterous hand,
after training only in a digital twin. Because manipulation
hinges on metric 3D geometry, these results suggest that
3D observations may become the default input of embodied
JEPA world models. We hope JEPA4R brings these models
closer to robots that plan in 4D point space and check what
they imagine before they act.

S2. Experimental settings
S2.1. Paired data
Each world-model dataset is collected in simulation and
rendered by one fixed camera into paired images and point
clouds that match row by row (Sec. 4.1). On Cube, episodes
mix low-noise expert, high-noise expert, expert-then-random
and random control in proportions 0.2, 0.4, 0.2 and 0.2; Puz-
zle follows the noisy recipe of OGBench [61]; on Scene,
MetaWorld and Adroit Relocate, they mix noisy expert con-
trol, expert control interrupted by random actions, and ran-
dom actions in proportions 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. Cube has 10,000
episodes per setting, of 200, 500, 1,000 and 600 steps for
one to four cubes; Puzzle follows OGBench’s official scale,
with 1,000 episodes on 3 × 3 and 4 × 4 and 3,000 on 4 × 5,
of 1,001 steps each; Scene, MetaWorld (per task) and Adroit
Relocate have 10,000 episodes of 200 steps.

S2.2. World models
JEPA4R and the LeWM baseline are identical except for the
encoder: a PointNet on point clouds for the former and a
ViT-tiny/14 on images for the latter. Both are trained with
Eq. (1) for 10 epochs (AdamW, learning rate 5×10−5, batch
size 128), one pair per dataset.
S2.3. Policy learning and evaluation
Every encoder is frozen, and every benchmark reports suc-
cess rate. Cube uses GCIQL [61] and Puzzle uses decoupled
Q-chunking (DQC) [39], both offline goal-conditioned re-
inforcement learning on OGBench’s official play data for
106 steps: besides the latent, the input carries the robot state
on Cube and the button lights on Puzzle, the goal gives the
target state of the objects or the lights, and evaluation runs
50 episodes on each of the five official tasks, with Puzzle
reporting the best of its ten evaluations during training. Meta-
World and Adroit Relocate use the official imitation protocol
of CortexBench [51], and Scene reuses the same code on its
five evaluation tasks: behavior cloning for 100 epochs on 25
demonstrations per task (100 on Relocate), scoring the best
of 20 evaluations.
S2.4. Baselines
PLDM [79] and LeJEPA [5] are trained on our image data
for 10 epochs with their own recipes; VISReg [91] and V-
JEPA 2.1 [54] use the authors’ released weights and are
evaluated by us. All four encoders are frozen, with the
same policy learning and evaluation as JEPA4R. PLDM and
LeJEPA use a ViT-tiny/14 and VISReg and V-JEPA 2.1 a
ViT-B/16; on Cube, PLDM uses an Impala encoder and
V-JEPA 2.1 a ViT-L/16.

S4. Discussion
What the results mean.
The results argue for three
changes to the JEPA route. First, 3D-native input: an image
entangles metric geometry with appearance and perspective,
so a pixel model must infer depth, whereas a point cloud
records visible surfaces at their metric positions; the decod-
ing results in Sec. 5.4 also show that frozen point-cloud
latents retain this metric geometry, from which the policy
head can read where the objects and the gripper are in space.
Second, evaluation through the policies robots learn, rather
than through short-horizon goal reaching alone: latents that
plan well can transfer poorly to policy learning [45], so
evaluating by planning alone does not show whether a repre-
sentation serves policy learning. Third, decoded latents as
a standard diagnostic: frozen latents decode back to point
clouds, so imagined futures can be compared directly with
observations.
Outlook.
The approach is promising in several directions.
On the 3D representation that JEPA4R learns, a policy can
be trained from little data and deployed on the real arm,
which suits robotic settings where real data are expensive
and hard to collect at scale; the world model and the policy
head, trained only in a digital twin, run zero-shot on the
real arm, so embodied world models can be trained mainly
in digital twins rather than on large amounts of real robot
video. The JEPA recipe carries over to point clouds with
the predictor and the objective unchanged, the same upgrade
applies to other JEPA world models, and 3D observations
may become the default input of embodied JEPA world
models. The decoder shows that, while training stays free of
reconstruction, frozen latents and entire imagined rollouts
can still be decoded into metrically accurate 4D point clouds,
so a robot can check the expected outcome of an action
sequence before executing it; building on this, planning can
move from approaching a goal latent to operating in 4D
point space, with goals given directly as point clouds and
every imagined step checkable in metric space. Finally,
combining paired 2D/3D data with policy learning offers a
way to compare world-model representations beyond short-
horizon planning.
