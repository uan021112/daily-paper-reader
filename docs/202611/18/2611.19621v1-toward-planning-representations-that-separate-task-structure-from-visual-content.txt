# Toward Planning Representations That Separate Task Structure from Visual Content 

Aarav Srivastava, Vishwas Sathish, Rajesh P. N. Rao 

Paul G. Allen School of Computer Science & Engineering University of Washington, Seattle, United States 

{aaravs07, vsathish, rao}@cs.washington.edu 

Abstract: Effective planning requires representations that preserve task-relevant geometry and object identity while ignoring visual variation that does not alter the task. Existing evaluations of visual generalization often change several fac-tors simultaneously, making the source of a failure difficult to identify. We in-troduce a controlled evaluation of object-goal navigation (ObjectNav), the task of reaching an instance of a named object category, in procedurally generated ProcTHOR houses. Training and evaluation houses share floor plans, object lo-cations, goals, and start states, while visual factors are changed individually or in combination. Across model-free reinforcement learning (RL), model-based RL, and agents using frozen pretrained visual representations, our results show that appearance changes impair navigation, but their effects differ sharply by fac-tor. Surface-material changes account for most of the average change in success, whereas clutter and lighting have little effect. The magnitude of a global image-statistics shift does not reliably predict performance: changing only the goal ob-ject’s appearance can substantially impair an end-to-end agent despite a small image-level change. We found that a model-based agent that plans over frozen features from the pretrained DINOv2 image encoder achieves the highest success (0.83) under the full shift, but remains sensitive to surface appearance. Our exper-iments reveal an important requirement for planning representations: they must separate invariant spatial task structure from appearance. 

Keywords: World models, state abstraction, visual generalization, embodied nav-igation 

## 1 Introduction 

A representation used for planning should assign similar states to scenes that afford the same actions and outcomes. In object-goal navigation (ObjectNav) [1], an agent must find and reach an instance of a named object category, such as a bed. An agent trained to reach the bed in one house should still reach it after a room is repainted if the navigable space, object positions, goal, and start state are unchanged. Failure under that change shows that the agent’s behavior depends on visual information that the navigation problem does not require. Visual distribution shifts can degrade reinforcement learning (RL) policies trained from pixels [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Existing benchmarks commonly change several properties together, including layout, objects, lighting, camera conditions, and appearance. They establish whether an agent generalizes, but usually cannot determine which changed property caused a failure or whether separate failures combine. For planning, this distinction matters. A latent state may support planning in the training environment while still binding an action-relevant object or a route to the particular textures, meshes, or illumination seen during training.

What are the properties that underlie current failures in visual generalization in planning and RL? We address this question with a controlled evaluation of RL navigation agents in ProcTHOR [10], a collection of procedurally generated houses from the AI2-THOR simulator [11]. For each training house, the evaluation houses preserve the reachable floor area, object positions, goal instances, and evaluation start poses. They differ in appearance, and an object swap also changes that object’s mesh; Figure 1 shows one house and its variants. We independently vary surface materials, lighting, sky, non-goal object appearance, goal-object appearance, and clutter, then evaluate both isolated changes and cumulative combinations. The setup turns visual generalization into a diagnosis problem: it identifies which visual variables each agent’s behavior depends on. We evaluate model-free agents trained with proximal policy optimization (PPO) [12], PPO with vi-sual augmentation, PPO policies on frozen pretrained encoders, two model-based agents that learn a world model, DreamerV3 [13] and TD-MPC2 [14], and TD-MPC2 with frozen DINOv2 fea-tures [15]. The results identify two types of sensitivity. Across agents, changes to walls, floors, and ceilings produce the largest average change in success. End-to-end PPO also depends strongly on the goal object’s particular appearance, even though changing that object affects relatively few pix-els. Global image-shift magnitude therefore only partly characterizes the difficulty of a perturbation. Frozen features from pretrained DINOv2 reduce sensitivity to lighting, sky, and object appearance. TD-MPC2 trained on these features reaches the highest success, although surface-material changes still reduce it. Together, these results highlight the importance of separating invariant spatial task structure from appearance for visual generalization in embodied planning. We contribute (i) a controlled ObjectNav evaluation whose changed houses are verified in the simu-lator to keep the floor area, goal, and start poses of the training house; (ii) a comparison of model-free, model-based, and frozen-encoder agents on it, including one frozen feature under both kinds of learner; and (iii) the findings above. We propose no new representation-learning method. 

## 2 Related Work 

Visual generalization in RL. Procedural generation evaluates generalization to unseen levels [2]; Kirk et al. [3] survey zero-shot generalization in deep RL. The Distracting Control Suite, SODA, and RL-ViGen vary visual factors such as backgrounds, color, lighting, camera configuration, and layout [4, 5, 6]. These benchmarks measure performance under a changed test distribution. Our evaluation instead keeps the navigation problem fixed and isolates the contribution of each appear-ance factor. Controlled perturbation benchmarks exist in imitation-learning manipulation: Factor World [16], The Colosseum [17], and KitchenShift [18] vary factors such as lighting, textures, and camera pose, and Gao et al. [19] study composition of observed factor values. We apply this de-2sign to RL navigation, where no value of a changed factor is seen in training, compare model-free, model-based, and frozen-encoder agents, and test whether separately measured effects combine. 

Augmentation and pretrained representations. Domain randomization and image augmenta-tion expose policies to selected nuisance variation during training [20, 7, 8, 9]. Frozen visual en-coders pretrained on large image or video corpora are also used in imitation learning and model-free RL [21, 22, 23], although well-tuned learning-from-scratch baselines can be competitive [24]. Burns et al. [25] relate performance under visual shift in manipulation to segmentation properties of visual encoders. Results for frozen features in model-based RL differ across settings: Schneider et al. [26] report limited benefits, while Jones et al. [27] report benefits under severe visual shift. We place one frozen DINOv2 feature under a model-free and a model-based learner. 

Abstractions for planning. Task-informed objectives can direct a latent state to retain information needed for control while discarding nuisance detail [28, 29]. DreamerV3 reconstructs observations, whereas TD-MPC2 learns latent dynamics, reward, and value without an observation decoder. Com-paring them under controlled shifts tests whether these modeling choices are sufficient to remove appearance dependence. Our result does not isolate a single training objective as its cause. It identi-fies the visual variables that an abstraction for planning must handle. 

## 3 Controlled ObjectNav Evaluation 

This section defines the task, the appearance interventions, and the measurements. 

3.1 Task and invariants 

We use object-goal navigation [1] in AI2-THOR [11] with ProcTHOR-10k houses [10]. The agent observes a 128 × 128 RGB image with a 90 ◦ field of view and selects one of five actions: move forward by 0.25 m, turn left or right by 30 ◦, or tilt the camera up or down by 30 ◦. An episode succeeds when the goal object is visible and within 1.5 m in straight-line distance. Episodes end after 200 steps. The reward is +10 on success, −0.01 per step, and +1 per meter of reduction in straight-line distance to the goal. We select five houses, P0–P4, with 128–681 reachable floor positions on a 0.25 m grid. Each house has a fixed goal-object type and location. Twenty percent of reachable positions are held out from training. We draw 25 position-and-heading start poses once from the held-out set and use exactly those poses in every evaluation house. This controls the task distribution as well as the geometry. 

3.2 Appearance interventions 

Starting from a training house A, we construct variants that change one of six factors: surface materi-als for walls, floors, and ceilings; lighting; sky; non-goal object appearance; goal-object appearance; or clutter. Object changes replace an object with a different asset of the same type. The goal asset is changed only when a replacement with the same footprint exists. Clutter consists of two to eight small objects placed on existing surfaces. We use two complementary evaluations. The reordered cumulative ladder starts with clutter (R1), adds lighting and sky (R2), adds object appearance including the goal (R3), and then adds surface materials (R4). The order follows the average effect measured in single-factor trials, from smallest to largest. The original ladder begins with surfaces, lighting, and sky (L1), adds object appearance (L2), then adds clutter (L3). Each ladder combines the same per-factor fields. A factor therefore has the same implementation wherever it appears, and R4 and L3 are the same house. Each constructed house must satisfy three checks in the simulator: it has the same reachable posi-tions as A, each evaluation start pose remains reachable, and it contains the same number of goal in-stances. We run all three checks on three independent simulator loads (one load for the goal-control houses of Appendix E) as a guard against simulator nondeterminism. 3.3 Evaluation and image measurements 

Agents are evaluated without further training, from the same start poses before and after each in-tervention. Tables give success as the mean ± standard deviation over the five training seeds, each seed averaged over houses; figures give 95% confidence intervals from a stratified bootstrap that resamples the seeds within each house [31]. Declared significance tests between agent types appear in Appendix H. Five DreamerV3 runs did not learn the training house (success below 0.5); analyses that compare a change with house A exclude them, while raw success rates keep every run. For each changed house, we pool pixels from the 25 start views into one 256-bin histogram per color channel and measure its Wasserstein-1 (earth mover’s) distance from A in gray levels (0–255). We also measure the mean per-pixel difference between aligned views. Neither depends on the agent. 

## 4 Agents and Training 

All agents train for 300k environment steps in house A, with five seeds in each of five houses. PPO [12] uses the convolutional encoder of Mnih et al. [32], trained end to end through Stable-Baselines3 [33]. PPO+Aug applies brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue jitter during training. PPO+I-JEPA, PPO+MAE, and PPO+DINOv2 train only a small policy and value head on a frozen vision transformer (ViT) pretrained by I-JEPA [34], MAE [35], or DINOv2 [15]. DreamerV3 learns a recurrent latent world model and reconstructs 64 × 64 observations [13]. TD-MPC2 learns latent dynamics, reward, and value without an observation decoder, then plans with model predictive path integral (MPPI) control from a three-frame stack [14]. TD-MPC2+DINOv2 instead receives the frozen DINOv2 feature of one frame, as PPO+DINOv2 does, through its multi-layer perceptron (MLP) state encoder. Appendix B gives all settings. ## 5 Results 

Each effect of a change below compares an agent with itself in house A and in the changed house (same weights, same start poses), so differences between agent types in input, encoder, or capacity cannot create it; they matter only when agent types are compared, mainly in Section 5.4. 

5.1 Accumulated appearance changes reduce navigation success 

Figure 2 gives the first result. Agents that perform well in the training house can lose substantial success when the navigation problem is unchanged and only appearance changes. All agent types are nearly unaffected by clutter. After lighting, sky, object, and surface changes accumulate, success ranges from 0.25 for PPO+MAE to 0.83 for TD-MPC2+DINOv2. 1 PPO declines from 0.94 in A to 0.29 in R4. TD-MPC2+DINOv2 remains near its training-house rate through R3. The pooled curve hides meaningful house-to-house variation. In P3, every agent type reaches at most 0.50 success in the full-shift house, and TD-MPC2+DINOv2 varies from 0.38 to 0.50 across three evaluation passes. In P2, PPO retains 0.79 success in the full-shift house. The benchmark therefore identifies a general pattern, not a property of every house. 

5.2 Surface materials and goal appearance expose different failures 

The cumulative ladder establishes that appearance matters. The single-factor evaluation identifies where that dependence comes from (Table 1 and Figure 3). Across agent types, changing surfaces lowers mean success from 0.94 to 0.66. Lighting, sky, non-goal objects, and clutter leave mean success between 0.89 and 0.94. Surface materials are the lowest-success single-factor condition for every agent except PPO, where changing only the goal object produces a comparable result. The surface effect is uneven across houses: averaged over agent types, the repaint alone lowers success from 0.96 to 0.87 in P0 and from 0.90 to 0.32 in P3 (Table 10; each house has one repaint). That exception separates two kinds of failure. PPO reaches 0.58 when only the goal appearance changes, against 0.94 in its training house. Changing only surfaces yields 0.59. The frozen-encoder and world-model agents keep at least 0.82 success under the goal-object condition, while PPO+Aug, also trained end to end, falls to 0.78 (Table 9). Swapping the goal asset on top of every other L2 change gives the same pattern (Table 11): PPO falls from 0.37 to 0.17 and PPO+Aug from 0.38 to 0.23, while the others change by at most 0.09. 5.3 Global image-shift magnitude does not uniquely identify vulnerable factors 

If agents depended mainly on low-level image statistics, larger changes in those statistics would correspond to lower success. Figure 4 shows the color distributions behind this measure for each single change in one house. Across all changes, the drop in success averaged over agent types has a within-house rank correlation of 0.73 with color-histogram distance (Appendix F). That association is largely induced by surface repainting, which produces the lowest success and most of the largest color shifts: 8–71 gray levels, against at most 15 for changes that leave the surfaces alone. Within each kind, the correlation is 0.37 among changes without repainting and 0.11 among repaints. The goal-object control provides a sharper counterexample. In the four houses with a swappable goal, replacing the goal appearance shifts the color distribution by about one gray level but changes PPO success from 0.94 to 0.58. New lighting shifts the distribution by roughly five gray levels and leaves PPO success at 0.95. Mean per-pixel difference has a higher all-condition correlation, 0.86, because it retains some information about where the change occurs. 5.4 Frozen DINOv2 features improve success under shift, most under TD-MPC2 

On the original ladder (Table 2), visual augmentation does not yield a reliable improvement at the 300k-step budget. PPO and PPO+Aug both reach 0.52 success at L1. At L2, augmentation reaches 0.35 versus 0.29 for PPO, and the 95% interval for the difference between the two agents’ drops from house A includes zero (6 points, from −2 to 14). At 150k steps, augmentation improves success at L2, but its advantage decreases after both agents receive more training (Appendix G). The frozen encoders differ. PPO+DINOv2 reaches 0.72, 0.65, and 0.66 at L1–L3, while PPO+I-JEPA and PPO+MAE reach at most 0.47 and 0.35 at L1. Pretraining data and objective therefore affect which appearance variables the policy depends on; frozen features are not interchangeable. TD-MPC2 trained on the same frozen DINOv2 feature scores higher still: 0.88, 0.82, and 0.80 along L1–L3, above both TD-MPC2 with its learned encoder and PPO+DINOv2. Its single-surface success is 0.89 compared with 0.72 for PPO+DINOv2. These comparisons do not isolate plan-ning: PPO+DINOv2 and TD-MPC2+DINOv2 share the single-frame feature but differ in learning algorithm and trainable size, and TD-MPC2 and TD-MPC2+DINOv2 differ in encoder, image res-olution, and frame history. The agent also remains below its 0.99 training-house success, so the combination reduces rather than removes dependence on surface appearance. 

## 6 Discussion 

We first state what the evaluation identifies and then what it implies for future methods. 

6.1 What the controlled evaluation identifies 

The experiments distinguish performance under visual shift from the variables that generate it. Every agent in this study retains some dependence on surface appearance, and an end-to-end policy also depends on the goal asset. Frozen DINOv2 features remove much of the sensitivity to lighting, sky, and object appearance, and TD-MPC2 trained on them reaches higher success still. For that agent, the remaining failure concentrates on surfaces rather than clutter or object appearance. This finding bears on prior results for pretrained features in model-based RL: Schneider et al. [26] find that frozen representations need not improve sample efficiency or out-of-distribution perfor-mance, while Jones et al. [27] find benefits under severe visual shift. Here the same DINOv2 feature improves both a model-free policy and TD-MPC2. Three untested differences could account for the contrast with Schneider et al. [26]: their factors vary during training, their simulated scenes differ from our indoor imagery, and their frozen representation enters through a single linear layer. 76.2 Requirements for future approaches Learning where to look. Every agent here encodes the whole frame, so a repainted wall filling much of the view enters the state with the goal. Two results point to selective input: the goal swap changes few pixels yet lowers PPO’s success from 0.94 to 0.58, and surfaces, which fill most of the view, give the lowest single-factor success for every agent type except PPO. Foveated primate vision moves a small high-resolution region to where the task needs information [36, 37]; glimpse policies, patch attention, and active predictive coding learn such selection [38, 39, 40, 41]. We hypothesize (untested) that learning where to look on strong pretrained features can close the surface gap. 

Counterfactual visual pairs. A state encoder can be trained to agree across paired views that preserve geometry, goal location, and action outcomes while changing surfaces, illumination, or asset appearance, as our house pairs do. The consistency objective should be conditioned on the task, to keep the goal identity planning requires; task-informed objectives are a start [28, 29]. 

Objects, geometry, and materials. The goal-object result calls for object identity that persists across assets. The surface result calls for spatial state that represents free space, boundaries, and navigable layout independently of texture. Object-centric features, task-conditioned attention, se-mantic maps, or latent cognitive maps can provide candidate state variables [25, 42]. 

Evaluating the next model. These are design directions, not methods evaluated here. The bench-mark supplies a direct test: an approach should retain success when the spatial task is unchanged, identify the goal across assets, and continue to perform when factors are composed. A world model should also be judged by whether its planned states and values agree across these interventions. 

## 7 Limitations 

We study one navigation problem in simulation, in five houses, each with a fixed goal; our conclu-sions do not extend to ObjectNav with varied goals or unseen layouts. Agents may partly memorize the goal’s location, and surfaces may cue their own position, so the surface effect may mix lost position cues with appearance the task does not require. Five houses show substantial variation that we do not explain, and each has one repaint. We measure success, not internal states: a drop shows that behavior depends on the changed factor, not where in the agent, and unchanged success does not show an unchanged state; comparing latent states across houses at the same poses is the next measurement. Object swaps change the mesh as well as the texture. The reordered ladder was chosen from single-factor results on the same agents. Agent types use each method’s own settings and differ in image resolution, frame input, trainable size, and pretraining (Appendix B); only PPO versus PPO+Aug and PPO+I-JEPA versus PPO+MAE differ in one respect, so other comparisons between agent types are not ablations. DreamerV3 is likely undertrained at 300k steps: five of its 25 runs did not learn house A (nine at 150k; Appendix G). Seven of 200 agents were retrained after saved models were lost, and world-model evaluation is stochastic (Appendix C). The reward includes dense straight-line-distance shaping, and success needs no explicit stop action. 

## 8 Conclusion 

Our results obtained from a fixed vision-based navigation task reveal that the behavior of current embodied agents still depends on appearance. Surface materials account for most of the average reductions in success rate. Goal-object control reveals an additional asset-specific failure in end-to-end PPO. Global color-shift magnitude does not determine which intervention harms an agent. Frozen DINOv2 features, and TD-MPC2 trained on them, increased navigation success under these changes, but surface appearance remains a failure mode. Our work offers a new benchmark for future abstraction-learning methods for embodied planning, evaluating whether they preserve scene geometry information and goal identity while discarding appearance the task does not require. 8Acknowledgments

## B Agent settings 

Table 4 lists the training settings of every agent type. Each agent type uses its method’s own settings, so the types differ in several respects besides the one each is named for. Image resolution: the PPO agents receive 128 ×128 images, resized to 224 ×224 for the frozen encoders, while DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2 receive 64 ×64 images and TD-MPC2+DINOv2 receives the PPO+DINOv2 feature. Frame input: TD-MPC2 stacks three frames, DreamerV3 carries a recurrent state, and the other agents receive one frame. Trainable size: the frozen-encoder PPO agents train a head of 0.17M (I-JEPA, MAE) or 0.21M (DINOv2) parameters, while end-to-end PPO trains a 4.8M-parameter encoder and head. Pretraining: I-JEPA and MAE use the same ViT-H/14 architecture and differ in pretraining objective, while DINOv2 uses a larger ViT-g/14 pretrained on a larger image collection. PPO versus PPO+Aug (augmentation) and PPO+I-JEPA versus PPO+MAE (pretraining objective) are the only pairs that differ in a single respect.

## E Goal-object control 

Table 11 compares, for each agent type, success in L2noT, which applies every L2 change except the goal asset, with success in L2, where the goal asset changes too. Each agent is compared with itself in one evaluation pass. In P2, whose goal cannot be swapped, L2noT and L2 are the same house; there the difference is exactly zero for every run of the PPO-based agents and at most 0.04 in magnitude, averaged over seeds, for the world models, whose evaluation is stochastic. 

## F Image measurements 

Figure 4 in Section 5.3 shows the pixel distributions behind the color-shift measure for one house, and Table 12 gives the measure for every house. Table 13 separates the channels for lighting and sky and compares the measure with the mean per-pixel difference, which can never be smaller. Table 14 and Figure 6 relate it to the drop in success over all 69 changed houses (five houses, 14 changes each, without the missing goal-only house in P2), including the combinations used by the ladders. Lighting and sky shift the color distribution by similar, small amounts, 5.6 and 5.4 gray levels on average across houses (lighting 3.0–8.1, sky 1.6–9.3; Table 12), yet their effects differ (Table 9). The new sky lowers PPO’s success from 0.94 to 0.75 and lowers PPO+Aug, PPO+MAE, and TD-MPC2 by 0.06–0.07; the new lighting leaves PPO almost unchanged but lowers PPO+MAE from 0.92 to 0.84. The two changes differ in kind (Table 13). New lighting acts like a change of brightness: its mean per-pixel difference equals its histogram distance in every house. The sky also changes the light inside the room, but alters its color balance: in every house its three channels shift less evenly than under new lighting (in P0, red by 17.5 gray levels and blue by 2.9), and its per-pixel difference exceeds its histogram distance, because some pixel values rise while others fall.
