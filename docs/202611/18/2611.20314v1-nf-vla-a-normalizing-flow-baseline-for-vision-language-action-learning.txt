Abstract: Autoregressive normalizing flows (NFs) provide a tractable density for learning and sampling continuous robot actions. We show that a single maximum-likelihood objective can train vision–language–action (VLA) policies competi-tive with leading methods on manipulation benchmarks. We present NF-VLA, which combines conditional autoregressive normalizing flows with score-based action refinement. Its deep–shallow architecture concentrates visual, language, and proprioceptive prefix conditioning in a high-capacity deep block, while un-conditioned shallow blocks provide a shared invertible action transformation. The vision–language backbone and flow are jointly trained by exact maximum likeli-hood on Gaussian-smoothed action chunks. The same learned density supplies a single score-based correction at inference, refining generated actions to account for training-time smoothing without a separately trained denoiser. Trained on LIBERO from a pretrained vision–language backbone without robot pretraining, NF-VLA achieves 98.35% success across the four standard suites and 72.6% on LIBERO-Plus using the same checkpoint without further fine-tuning. Ablations show that deep-only prefix conditioning and score-based refinement both improve closed-loop success. Together, these results establish carefully structured autore-gressive flows as a competitive approach to VLA control, with one learned density supporting policy training, action generation, and refinement. 

# 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models connect pretrained visual and linguistic representations to robot control, allowing policies to generate actions from images and language instructions [1, 2]. A central design choice is the action model: it determines how observations condition the action 

10th Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL 2026), Austin TX, USA. arXiv:2611.20314v1 [cs.RO] 15 Nov 2026 distribution and how that distribution produces executable commands. Existing approaches include discrete action prediction, continuous regression, diffusion, and flow matching [2, 3, 4, 5]. Normalizing flows (NFs) offer a formulation in which an invertible transformation defines both a continuous density and a sampling procedure. Their tractable change-of-variables likelihood allows direct maximum-likelihood training [6, 7]. TARFlow [8] and the STARFlow family [9, 10, 11] demonstrate the potential of Transformer autoregressive flows for visual and multimodal generation, with STARFlow introducing a deep–shallow architecture for scaling latent image generation. These developments motivate a study of conditioning and sampling in autoregressive-flow VLA policies: where and how to inject multimodal context, and whether the learned density’s score can improve actions after sampling. Recent work establishes normalizing flows as capable robot policies. NinA [12] introduces coupling-based action decoders for VLAs, emphasizing efficient generation, while RoMAN-Flow [13] develops autoregressive-flow policies for offline reinforcement learning and one-step policy distillation. Building on this progress, we focus on the manipulation performance obtain-able from likelihood-based imitation learning itself. The central design question is how to organize multimodal conditioning and sample refinement within a single continuous-density model. We present NF-VLA , a VLA policy that combines conditional autoregressive flows with score-based action refinement. Building on the deep–shallow architecture of STARFlow [9], we con-centrate visual, language, and proprioceptive conditioning in a high-capacity deep block, while shallow blocks provide a shared invertible transformation between action coordinates and inter-mediate latents. The vision–language backbone and action model are trained jointly with a single exact maximum-likelihood objective on Gaussian-smoothed actions. At inference, the same learned density supports flow inversion and a single Tweedie denoising correction computed from its own score [14, 8]. Learning, sampling, and refinement therefore share one density model, without a separately trained denoiser or an additional training objective. Starting from a pretrained vision–language backbone, NF-VLA is trained jointly on the full LIBERO dataset without robot pretraining or reinforcement-learning post-training. It achieves 98.35% suc-cess across the four standard LIBERO suites [15], including 97.8% on LIBERO-Long. The same checkpoint achieves 72.6% on LIBERO-Plus [16] without further fine-tuning, demonstrating strong aggregate performance under benchmark perturbations. These results place NF-VLA competitively alongside leading VLA policies in the benchmark comparisons. Ablations support the two design choices: concentrating prefix conditioning in the deep block improves average LIBERO success by 1.20 percentage points over all-block prefix conditioning, and a single score-based refinement step improves success by 1.90 percentage points using the same trained checkpoint. Our contributions are threefold: • We present NF-VLA , an autoregressive-flow VLA baseline trained end to end with a single likelihood objective and using the learned density for both sampling and score-based action correction. • We evaluate conditioning placement and mechanism, finding that deep-only prefix condi-tioning achieves higher average success than all-block conditioning in our tested configu-rations. We additionally quantify the control benefit of self-score correction and report the inference costs of the evaluated designs. • We evaluate the resulting policy on LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus, reporting strong standard-task performance alongside a perturbation-dependent robustness profile. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Vision-Language-Action Models 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models adapt pretrained vision–language representations to language-conditioned robot control. RT-2 [1] represents robot actions as text tokens, allowing 2vision–language models to learn control alongside web-based vision–language tasks. OpenVLA [2] develops an open-source VLA with discretized action prediction and demonstrates adaptation to new manipulation tasks. Subsequent work explores alternatives to token-by-token action decoding: OpenVLA-OFT [3] combines parallel decoding and action chunking with continuous-action regres-sion, showing that action representation and training objectives are consequential design choices. Generative action models provide another approach to continuous control. Diffusion Policy [4] models action sequences through conditional denoising diffusion and executes them with receding-horizon control. Within VLAs, π0 [5] couples a pretrained vision–language model with a flow-matching action expert, while SmolVLA [17] develops a compact architecture with a flow-matching decoder. 

2.2 Normalizing Flows 

NFs model continuous densities through invertible transformations with tractable Jacobian determi-nants. Real NVP [6] uses affine coupling layers, while inverse autoregressive flow [18] and masked autoregressive flow [7] exploit autoregressive structure to obtain triangular Jacobians. More re-cently, TARFlow [8] combines affine autoregressive transformations with Transformers for image generation, and the STARFlow family [9, 10, 11] develops latent-space flows with deep–shallow architectures for visual and multimodal generation. Flow matching [19] is also connected to NFs: it trains continuous-time flows by regressing vector fields along probability paths. Recently in robotics, Ghugare and Eysenbach [20] investigate their use in behavior cloning and rein-forcement learning. Within VLAs, NinA [12] replaces FLOWER’s action decoder with Real NVP-style coupling flows [6], evaluating MLP and Transformer conditioners and emphasizing inference efficiency. RoMAN-Flow [13] uses autoregressive flows with context supplied to every flow block, followed by advantage-weighted offline policy optimization and one-step distillation. SERNF [21] combines normalizing-flow policies with action-chunked critics for off-policy fine-tuning on real robots. Our focus is the performance of the likelihood-trained VLA policy itself: we concentrate direct conditioning in the deep flow block and reuse its action density for inference-time refinement, retaining a single maximum-likelihood training objective.

# 4 NF-VLA 

NF-VLA combines a pretrained vision–language backbone with a conditional NF over continuous action chunks (Figure 1). Visual, language, and proprioceptive features condition a deep autore-gressive block, while shallow flow blocks transform between action latents and normalized actions. We train the model end to end by exact maximum likelihood on Gaussian-smoothed actions and generate actions by flow inversion followed by a single score-based denoising step.

4.2 Context-conditioned autoregressive flow 

NF-VLA adopts the TARFlow parameterization [8] with the deep–shallow architecture of STARFlow [9]. The head comprises K affine flow blocks with alternating autoregressive order-ing: in the forward pass, the normalized action first goes through unconditioned shallow blocks, then maps to the latent through a deep block conditioned with Ct. The reverse pass therefore be-gins with the conditioned deep block and proceeds through the unconditioned shallow blocks. We evaluated whether exact maximum-likelihood training supports competitive manipulation per-formance on LIBERO and how this performance changes under the perturbations in LIBERO-Plus. Both evaluations used the same NF-VLA checkpoint without additional fine-tuning on perturbed tasks. 

5.1 Implementation details Observations and actions. We initialized the vision–language backbone from SmolVLM-500M-Instruct [22] and fine-tuned it jointly with the action model. Each input image was resized to 384 ×

384 and encoded into 36 visual tokens. The 72 visual tokens and 50 instruction-token positions produced 122 features of width 960. Instructions were right-padded, with padded positions retained in the backbone computation. After projection and prepending the proprioception token, the flow context had shape 123 × 1024 . We used action chunks with H = 10 and da = 7 , comprising three translational increments, three rotational increments, and one gripper command per step. Actions and proprioception were normalized over each dimension using training-set statistics. 

Flow configuration. The action head contained six affine autoregressive blocks: five shallow blocks with two Transformer layers each and one conditioned deep block with 14 layers. All blocks used width 1024 and 16 attention heads. One-dimensional rotary position embeddings [23] used a frequency base of 10 ,000 . In the deep block, context and action tokens occupied positions 0–122 and 123–132, respectively; shallow blocks used positions 0–9 in their processing order. Gaussian smoothing used σ = 0 .05 in normalized action space. 

Training and inference. We trained the policy with the full LIBERO dataset on 4×B200 GPUs with a global batch size of 256, starting from the pretrained VLM without robot pretraining. At in-ference, we sampled at temperature 1.0, applied one Tweedie correction step and then unnormalized the actions. Flow inversion cached attention keys and values and required K(H −1) = 54 sequential token evaluations, in addition to context-prefix processing and the subsequent score computation. 

5.2 Evaluation setup LIBERO. We evaluated the Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites of LIBERO [15], each con-taining ten tasks. For each task, we ran 50 episodes, using each provided initial state once, for 500 episodes per suite and 2,000 episodes overall. 

LIBERO-Plus. We tested the policy’s zero-shot generalization on 10,030 perturbed task instances spanning camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting, background tex-tures, sensor noise, and object layouts [16]. 

5.3 Performance on LIBERO 

NF-VLA achieved 98.35% success on LIBERO, completing 1,967 of 2,000 episodes (Table 1). Among the listed models, NF-VLA achieved the highest overall score, 1.5 percentage points above 

π0.5’s reported 96.85%. It also achieved the highest scores on Object, Goal, and Long, including 97.8% on Long compared with π0.5’s 92.4%, while scoring 1.4 points lower on Spatial. These results support the viability of the likelihood-trained action model across the evaluated tasks, including longer action sequences. Of the 33 failed episodes, 11 occurred in two drawer-related tasks.

# 6 Ablations 

We examined the contributions of score-based denoising, conditioning depth, and conditioning mechanism (Figure 2). The full NF-VLA configuration from Table 1 used VLM features as prefix context in the deep block, left the shallow blocks unconditioned, and applied score-based denoising after generation. The conditioning variants retained this denoising step. 

Score-based denoising. Removing the denoising step from the trained NF-VLA checkpoint re-duced average success by 1.90 percentage points. The largest decrease occurred on Goal, where success fell from 98.4% to 94.4%. This suggests the score-based denoising rectifies the action gen-eration from the learned Gaussian-smoothed density and improves downstream control performance. 

Conditioning depth. Extending VLM prefix conditioning to the shallow blocks and retraining re-duced average success by 1.20 percentage points relative to the full model. Spatial success increased slightly, but performance declined on the other three suites, with the largest decrease on Long, from 97.8% to 94.0%. Thus, conditioning the deep block alone achieved higher overall success than dis-tributing prefix context across all flow blocks in this evaluation. This result is consistent with the design rationale of concentrating direct conditioning in the high-capacity deep block (Section 4.2). 8Figure 2: Effect of NF-VLA design choices on LIBERO success. Bars show success rates for the baseline and three ablation variants across the four suites; diamonds show suite averages rounded to one decimal place. Conditioning mechanism. Replacing prefix conditioning with adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN) [34] in both deep and shallow blocks yielded 92.85% average success. Compared with prefix conditioning in all blocks, AdaLN reduced average success by 4.30 percentage points and performed worse on every suite. This comparison holds conditioning depth fixed and supports pre-fix conditioning over AdaLN for the evaluated action model. 

Inference efficiency. We benchmarked the three conditioning configurations in FP32 with BF16 autocast on a single RTX 4080 SUPER GPU, measuring NF sampling and the Tweedie step sep-arately (Table 3). Relative to AdaLN, prefix conditioning reduced NF sampling latency by 30.3% for the baseline and 25.9% when applied to all blocks. Both prefix variants were faster in our im-plementation despite attending to more context tokens, as AdaLN modulation introduced additional computation. # 7 Conclusion 

We studied conditioning and sampling in autoregressive-flow VLA policies through NF-VLA, a baseline trained end to end with a single exact maximum-likelihood objective on Gaussian-smoothed actions. The learned density supports both action sampling and score-based correction. In the eval-uated configurations, restricting prefix conditioning to the deep flow block achieved higher average success than conditioning all blocks, and prefix conditioning outperformed AdaLN at the same all-block placement. A single Tweedie correction improved closed-loop success using the model’s own score, without additional training or changes to the objective. Together with the reported inference costs, these results establish the practical value of conditioning placement and self-score correction within this policy family. NF-VLA achieved strong task performance with a perturbation-dependent robustness profile. These findings support autoregressive NFs as a basis for studying how multi-modal context and learned action densities can be used for robot control.
