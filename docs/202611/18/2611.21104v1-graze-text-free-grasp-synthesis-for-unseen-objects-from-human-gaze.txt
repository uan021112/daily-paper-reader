Abstract
Text-conditioned hand–object interaction (HOI) generation
provides intuitive semantic control, but language is an in-
direct way to specify where an interaction should occur on
an object. Naming the part to grasp requires object-specific
words, and naming the approach side requires a reference
frame that a prompt lacks. We propose GRAZE (GRAsp
from gaZE), which generates hand–object grasp motion
from recorded gaze instead of object and part names. Its
Geometry-Aware Gaze Encoding (GeoGaze) maps gaze
rays to cues on object geometry, and cross-attention con-
ditions a diffusion Transformer on these cues. We also in-
troduce HOT3D-Grasp, a grasp-centered benchmark with
object-disjoint folds built on HOT3D recordings. On un-
seen objects, GRAZE grasps the target part more often than
the strongest text-conditioned baseline (49.3% vs. 43.6%),
achieves the highest grasp success (66.3% vs. 46.6%),
and transfers without fine-tuning to GazeHOI (45.3% vs.
33.8%). Gaze conveys the approach side: when gaze is ro-
tated around the object, the approach turns with it in 59.1%
of cases, against 20.5% for a text model retrained with clock
words. On the same backbone, a text prompt names parts
better, but gaze adds the approach side. Given both, GRAZE

1. Introduction
Generating realistic hand–object interaction (HOI) motion
is essential for animating hands in virtual and mixed reality.
Recent generative models synthesize HOI motion from a
text prompt [5, 8, 10, 18, 24, 33, 38, 44]. Earlier methods
needed the wrist or object trajectory as input [49], whereas
a prompt only has to describe what the hand should do, such
as “grab the handle of the mug.”
Yet language is not always the most direct control signal
for grasping. Text conveys semantic intention, such as the
object category and the action, but grasping also requires
spatial intention: which object to approach and where to
contact it. Text-conditioned methods such as Text2HOI in-
fer contact from the prompt and the object geometry, so the
user has no direct way to specify the grasp location [5].
Words fall short in three cases.
First, these models ob-
serve objects only as geometry, so an appearance word can-
not separate similarly shaped objects (Fig. 1a). Second, a
part name such as a dumbbell’s “head” may refer to two
symmetric instances, and “left” or “right” cannot pick one
because these words depend on an unstated viewpoint: the
same head is on the left from one side of the table and on
the right from the other (Fig. 1b). Third, the same part name
(e.g., handle) denotes differently shaped regions on differ-
ent objects, so the prompt does not identify the intended
grasp location (Fig. 1c).
These cases concern where to grasp. Text also leaves the
approach side unspecified. This side matters in real scenes:
if the handle of a mug faces away from the person, the hand
must reach around the mug or first turn it, so a plausible
grasp motion depends on how the object is rotated relative
to the person. The object can lie in any pose relative to the
person, but text-conditioned methods never observe where
the person stands. Adding a direction word does not help: a
user’s “3 o’clock” has no reference frame in the scene, and
even a text model retrained with object-frame clock words
turns its approach to match a changed word no more often
than a random direction (25%, Figs. 1d and 5b).
These limitations motivate an input that needs no object
or part names and carries spatial information that words ex-
press poorly, such as where the person is relative to the ob-
ject. Human gaze provides such an input: people typically
look toward an intended object or region before and during
interaction [1, 3, 20, 30, 32]. Gaze does not define a com-
plete hand trajectory, but it offers two spatial cues for grasp
generation: where the ray meets the object marks the in-
tended part, and where the ray starts, at the eye, tells which
side of the object faces the person. Gaze is also cheap to
obtain: the eye tracker of an egocentric headset records it
without annotation.
In this paper, we propose GRAZE (GRAsp from gaZE),
a gaze-guided framework for generating grasp-based HOI
motion toward the object region the person looks at. Given
the object’s geometry, its initial pose, and the first 30% of
a recorded gaze sequence, GRAZE synthesizes one grasp-
and-lift motion offline. Our encoder GeoGaze maps each
gaze ray onto fixed Basis Point Set (BPS) points [35], using
ray closeness for local proximity and displacement align-
ment for global direction. Cross-attention passes these cues
to a diffusion Transformer. MANO fitting [37] and a contact
refinement then turn the predicted hand vertices into grasps.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate text-
free, gaze-guided grasp-based HOI generation, in which
recorded gaze replaces object and part names as the con-
dition. On unseen objects, GRAZE grasps the target part
more often than the strongest text baseline, especially on
handles, which the text baselines rarely grasp even when
the handle is named (Figs. 1c and 5a). Gaze also conveys
the approach side, which text leaves open: when the gaze
is rotated around the object, the approach of GRAZE turns
with it in 59.1% of the cases, against 20.5% for a text model
retrained with clock words. With one distractor, a multi-
object GRAZE picks the gazed object more often than a
text baseline (47.4% vs. 15.3%, supplementary Tab. S4).
Second, we propose Geometry-Aware Gaze Encoding
(GeoGaze), whose displacement-alignment term encodes
the approach side.
Third, we construct HOT3D-Grasp,
a grasp-centered benchmark that extends HOT3D [2] with
part annotations and object-disjoint three-fold splits for un-
seen objects.
2. Related work
HOI
motion
and

Recent
text-
conditioned full-body human–object interaction methods
jointly generate human and object motion, guided ei-
ther by contact, as in HOI-Diff [33], CG-HOI [12], and
CHOIS [23], or by relational constraints, as in THOR [44].
Hand–object methods emphasize articulated grasps and
local contact [9, 19, 25, 40, 45, 50, 51]. Text2HOI pre-
dicts a text-conditioned surface contact map to guide diffu-
sion and hand refinement [5]. DiffH2O [10] uses BPS ob-
ject features [35] and separate diffusion models for grasp-
ing and manipulation. LatentHOI improves unseen-object
generalization by generating global motion and grasp codes
with diffusion.
A GraspVAE decodes the grasp codes
into articulated hands [24]. Text2Grasp studies object-part-
conditioned static grasps [6]. LatentAct instead conditions
on an explicit 3D contact point [34]. OpenHOI grounds
open-vocabulary affordance regions with a 3D multimodal
large language model (LLM) and conditions diffusion on
these regions [54].
Language provides semantic control but specifies the
grasp region only indirectly, and even affordance grounding

language nor a contact point tells where the person is rela-
tive to the object, and this position decides from which side
the hand reaches it. GRAZE instead grounds recorded gaze
rays on object geometry. These rays start at the person’s eye
and convey both the grasp region and the approach side.
Gaze-conditioned
motion
generation.
Most
gaze-
conditioned methods forecast future full-body motion
from past motion and gaze. The MoGaze dataset shows
that gaze helps predict pick-and-place intent [21], and
GazeMotion uses gaze to forecast body motion [17].
GIMO predicts future motion from past motion, 3D gaze,
and the scene, exchanging information between gaze and
motion branches [55], and GazeMoDiff fuses gaze and
observed motion with graph attention, then conditions
diffusion through cross-attention [46].
These methods
predict body motion and model neither hand articulation
nor hand–object contact.
Closest to our setting, GHO-Diffusion synthesizes hand–
object interaction from gaze: it represents each frame’s
gaze by the inverse-distance-weighted feature of the nearest
object point, aggregates these features with self-attention,
and generates object motion and then hand motion [42].
GRAZE instead encodes gaze as ray closeness and displace-
ment alignment over fixed BPS correspondences, and con-
ditions the motion on these cues with cross-attention. The
concurrent Gaze2HOI [28] introduces the EgoAIM bench-
mark and forecasts the next second of egocentric interaction
on HOT3D-Aria from observed video, gaze, and motion
history. GRAZE instead synthesizes the full grasp-and-lift
motion from 3D gaze rays over object geometry, without
text or motion history.

objects.

Gaze window.
Gaze leads the hand: people fixate the ob-
ject, often where they will grasp it, before the hand ar-
rives [20, 30]. The gaze that reveals the grasp intention
therefore falls in the approach before contact. In HOT3D-
Grasp, the hand first comes within 2 cm of the object at
a median of 35% of the valid frames, and 88% of the
first-30% gaze frames precede this point (supplementary
Sec. S1.1). The remaining frames carry a zero gaze token,
in training and at inference alike. For a model trained on
all-frame gaze, giving only the first 30% at test time keeps
grasp success and costs 2.1 part-accuracy points (supple-

refinement where the hand should touch the object.

4.2. Experimental setup
Baselines.
We
compare
GRAZE
with
four
text-
conditioned HOI generation methods:
Text2HOI [5],
DiffH2O [10], LatentHOI [24], and OpenHOI [54]. We
train all baselines on each fold with their authors’ settings,
including their refinement (Text2HOI and OpenHOI train
a separate refiner). For OpenHOI, as in its authors’ recipe
for a new dataset, we train the contact estimator and the
generator and use the released affordance-tuned multimodal
LLM. DiffH2O and LatentHOI do not take the clip length
as a condition, so we set their output length to the clip
length (supplementary Sec. S1.3).
GHO-Diffusion [42]
releases only a data toolkit, so we evaluate GHO-style gaze
features on our backbone instead (Sec. 4.4, supplementary
Tab. S9).
The text baselines receive object geometry and a prompt
naming the target object and part.
GRAZE receives the
same geometry and gaze instead. All methods use the same
test clips and are evaluated on the annotated active hand.
The inputs differ in one more way: GRAZE and the same-
backbone controls of Sec. 4.4 are also given the object’s
first-frame pose, which the baselines do not use. This pose
serves only to transform the gaze into the object frame and
to place the generated motion in the scene. Supplementary
Tables S6 and S8 also apply our refinement, with nearest-
point displacements, to the baselines.
Metrics.
Grasp success: the Grasp Success Rate (GSR),
our primary metric, counts a grasp as successful when the
active hand touches the object (within 5 mm) in each of the
final five valid frames and, at the final frame, penetrates
it by at most 10 mm and has lifted it by at least 5 mm.
Part grounding:
Part Contact Precision (PCP), adapted
from the contact precision of NL2Contact [53], is the frac-
tion of object points touched in the final frame that lie on
the target part. Part Accuracy (Part Acc.), adapted from
Text2Grasp [6], is one when the target part holds the most
touched points.
Both are zero without contact.
Physi-
cal plausibility: Interpenetration Depth (ID) measures how
deep the hand enters the object [10, 24], averaged over the
frames in which it penetrates. The final-contact rate (Con-
tact) is the proportion of samples that meet the contact con-
dition of GSR. For grasp stability, we fix the final hand
and simulate the object under gravity [15, 19]. Sim is the
proportion of samples whose object moves less than 2 cm,
and SimD is the mean object displacement (supplementary
Sec. S4). Table 1 averages ten samples per test clip, and dif-
ferences are tested with clip-level bootstrap 95% confidence
intervals (CIs, n.s. if the interval includes zero).
Implementation details.
Clips are padded to F = 100
frames. Each object is represented by N = 1,024 points
and K = 1,024 basis points (rnorm = 0.85), and each
hand by M = 100 MANO vertices. The 8-layer diffu-
sion Transformer [43] (D = 512) uses a 1,000-step co-
sine diffusion schedule [31], sampled with all 1,000 steps,
and is trained with AdamW [27] for 4,000 epochs, with a
10% gaze-condition dropout for training stability. The ray-
closeness bandwidth is σ = 0.15 (supplementary Fig. S4).

Which part.
Gaze raises Part Acc. by 5.6 and PCP by
6.5 points over no condition and is not significantly dif-
ferent from the ground-truth part label. The gain is clear-
est on handles: a handle sticks out to one side, so a gaze
toward that side indicates it (Part Acc. 26.1% vs. at most
18.0% without gaze, Fig. 5a). Caps, rims, tops, and lids are
harder: people also look at the top of an upright object in
84% of body grasps, so such gaze does not single out these
parts. On these parts the text prompt is stronger (34.7% vs.
19.6%), and over all parts it is slightly ahead (52.2% vs.
49.3%, supplementary Sec. S5.2).
Which side.
Gaze also conveys the approach side, which
the prompt and the part label omit. We rotate the recorded
gaze around the object by 90◦, 180◦, and 270◦, as if the per-
son looked from another side. The final wrist of GRAZE
turns with the rotation (within 45◦) for 59.1% of the rotated
inputs, whereas LatentHOI retrained with clock words fol-
lows a changed word in only 20.5% (chance: 25%, Fig. 5b,
supplementary Sec. S5.2 and Fig. S5). The eye position
alone carries much of the benefit: a model given only
the eye-to-centroid ray (Head-only, supplementary Tab. S9)
beats no condition (GSR +4.1, Part Acc. +4.3). GRAZE
leads it in GSR (+3.2) but not in Part Acc. (+1.3, n.s.).
The gaze direction matters on the 11% of clips whose gaze
deviates from this ray by at least 10◦: there, GRAZE ex-
ceeds Head-only by 19 GSR points. The raw ray and a
GHO-style feature [42] follow the rotation less often than
GRAZE (50.8% and 53.7% vs. 59.1%).
Which object.
A multi-object variant of GRAZE receives
the gaze and every candidate object. With one or two dis-
tractors, it contacts only the unseen target in 47.4% and
34.6% of the scenes, against 15.3% and 4.0% for Text2HOI
extended to several objects and given the text prompt (sup-
plementary Sec. S2.3 and Tab. S4).
Gaze and text.
When a prompt is also available, the two
inputs complement each other (Tab. 1 and supplementary
Tab. S9). Adding gaze to the prompt keeps its GSR and
Part Acc. (n.s.) and lets the approach follow a rotated gaze
in 57.4% of the rotated inputs (gaze alone: 59.1%, n.s.),
which a prompt alone cannot do. Adding the prompt to gaze
raises PCP from 50.1% to 52.3%, the highest of all methods,
and Part Acc. from 49.3% to 51.6% (n.s.).
4.5. Robustness to gaze noise
Real eye tracking is imperfect, so we corrupt the recorded
gaze at test time, without retraining. We either add ran-
dom angular noise to every gaze direction (RMS error of
5◦or 10◦), or drop each gaze frame with probability 0.2 or
0.5, which zeroes its gaze token. In every case, GSR and
Part Acc. change by at most 1.1 points (n.s., supplementary
Tab. S11).
5. Conclusion
We presented GRAZE, which generates grasp-and-lift mo-
tion from the first 30% of the recorded gaze instead of object
and part names, together with HOT3D-Grasp, a benchmark
with object-disjoint folds.
On unseen objects, GRAZE
grasped the target part more often than the strongest text
baseline and achieved the highest grasp success. Without
fine-tuning, it also achieved the highest grasp success on
the GazeHOI dataset. Unlike the text models, it turned its
approach with rotated gaze, and a multi-object variant se-
lected the gazed object among distractors more often.
Gaze also has limits. People do not always look at the
part they grasp, e.g., at the top of an object grasped by its
8


body. Adding a text prompt to gaze raised PCP, while gaze
still set the approach side. Future work will fuse gaze and
language more tightly when a prompt is available, generate
motion online from a live eye tracker, and extend to gaze-
driven pick-and-place in whole scenes.

S1. HOT3D-Grasp benchmark construction
and evaluation protocols
Figure S1 summarizes the object and grasp-part coverage of

S1.1. Clip construction
HOT3D-Grasp is derived from long HOT3D [2] scenario
recordings with synchronized hand, object, and gaze anno-
tations. We manually segment each grasp from the onset of
the hand approach until shortly after the object leaves the
table (final lift: median 11.1 cm, at least 5 cm in 74% of the
clips). Each clip retains the available left- and right-hand
MANO [37] motion, the target-object pose and geometry,
and the frame-level gaze ray. We use a fixed input length of
F = 100 frames, as clips have at most 92 frames (37 on av-
erage). Figure 3 illustrates this temporal segmentation with
an example clip cut from one HOT3D recording, together
with the gaze input of its first 30% of frames. This window
mostly covers the approach: in the ground truth, the hand
first comes within 2 cm of the object after a median of 35%
of the valid frames, and 88% of the first-30% gaze frames
precede it.
Three annotators verify the boundaries of each clip, as-
sign its object and active hand, name a grasp part when the
dominant contact region forms a semantically distinguish-
able object part, and segment that part directly on the object
surface.

DiffH2O and LatentHOI are originally formulated with
separate grasping and manipulation stages. For DiffH2O,
we train the authors’ full model in its one-stage configura-
tion with short text prompts (its “simple annotations”) on
each fold and refer to this model as DiffH2O throughout.
For LatentHOI, we train its complete model with the re-
leased code on each fold. Neither method takes the clip
length as an explicit condition: DiffH2O generates a clip-
length motion with its official zero mask, and the fixed
96-frame output of LatentHOI is resampled in time to the
clip length, so that each is judged on the end state of its
own motion.
We introduce no further adaptation of ei-
ther two-stage method. In contrast, Text2HOI has no ex-
plicit grasp–manipulation stage separation, so we train its
full model directly on our benchmark. We convert the final
motions from all methods to a common hand–object repre-
sentation and compute all reported comparison metrics with
the same evaluator, thresholds, frame masks, and object
meshes. OpenHOI [54] adds an object affordance map, pre-
dicted by a multimodal large language model (LLM) from
the prompt, to the Text2HOI contact estimator and gener-
ator. We compute the affordance maps with the released
affordance-tuned multimodal LLM and, following the au-
thors’ recipe for a new HOI dataset, train the contact esti-
mator and the generator on each fold. The maps separate
parts on only 4 of the 21 objects whose clips grasp two or
more different parts. On the other 17 objects they barely
depend on the part name (median correlation 0.99 between
maps for different part names). On most objects, OpenHOI
thus behaves like Text2HOI with a weak part cue, consistent
with its lower scores in Tab. 1 of the main paper. To rule out
a reproduction error, we follow the released configuration
and train the generator for as many epochs as the Text2HOI
generator on which it is built. On every fold it reaches a
training loss similar to that of Text2HOI, so its lower scores
do not come from failed training.

S1.4. Zero-shot GazeHOI protocol
We convert GazeHOI [42] to the format of HOT3D-Grasp
and give the same converted data to every method. Each clip
runs from 12 frames before the first hand–object contact to
11 frames after the lift onset (at most 99 frames, median 45).
The hand parameters are converted to our MANO layer, and
the scene and the object meshes are rotated so that their
world-up direction matches that of HOT3D. The evaluation
set contains 939 clips of 36 objects from 194 recordings:
every clip of both GazeHOI splits in which the recorded
gaze ray comes within 1 cm of the object, i.e., the 94 clips
of 28 objects of the test split and 845 clips of the training
split. No evaluated model is trained on GazeHOI, so both
splits are unseen by every method. Each method generates
one sample per clip. GazeHOI has no part annotation, and
its prompts name the hand but neither the object nor a part
(e.g., “Grab this with right hand.”), so every text baseline
receives this prompt (GRAZE receives only the gaze and
the same hand flag) and Part Acc. and PCP are not reported.
The three object-fold models of Tab. 1 are applied without
fine-tuning, and we report the mean over the three models.
GRAZE uses the gaze of the first 30% of the frames, as on
HOT3D. Adding a part word to this prompt does not help
the strongest baseline: on the 94 test-split clips, with four
samples per clip, LatentHOI reaches a GSR of 30.5% with
the original prompt, 29.9% with “Grab body of this with
...” (n.s.), and 23.0% with “Grab handle of this with ...”
(significantly lower).
S2. Additional method details
S2.1. Frames, masking, and diffusion
All inputs and outputs are expressed in the initial object
frame (Sec. 3.1 of the main paper), and padded frames and
absent hands are masked in attention and in the loss. The
denoiser predicts the clean state bx0 from xt = √¯αtx0 +
√1 −¯αtϵ, where ¯αt is the cumulative noise schedule at dif-
fusion step t and ϵ ∼N(0, I), with one reconstruction loss
for the hands and the object.
S2.2. Geometry-aware gaze features
For every valid frame and BPS index, GeoGaze retains two
separate values: ray closeness (a Gaussian of the distance
from the nearest object point qk of the basis point to the
forward gaze ray) and displacement alignment (the cosine
between the gaze direction and the BPS displacement ϕk,
rescaled to [0, 1]). Closeness uses the gaze origin and di-
rection and remains nonzero when a noisy ray passes near,
rather than through, a small object. Alignment uses the di-
rection only and remains defined without an intersection.
It encodes the viewing direction in the object frame, not
where the ray lands. The two K-dimensional fields of a
frame are concatenated before the gaze projection, so each
frame contributes one gaze token in which the closeness and
alignment entries keep separate projection weights.
Let σ = 0.15 denote the fixed bandwidth in the nor-
malized object coordinate system. Since objects are scaled
to rnorm = 0.85, σ corresponds to about 17.6% of the
maximum object radius.
For an object point qk at dis-
tance ρ from the forward gaze ray, its relative contribution is
w(ρ) = exp(−ρ2/(2σ2)). Figure S4 visualizes the close-
ness field at three bandwidths. Two geometric quantities,
measured on the 912 clips independently of any model out-
put, motivate the chosen value. First, the gaze ray often
passes near rather than through the object: in 33% of clips
its median distance to the nearest object point before con-
tact exceeds 0.05. A field whose maximum falls below 0.1
carries almost no signal. This happens for 23% of clips
at σ = 0.05 but only 8% at σ = 0.15. Second, the field
should stay part-scale: the median part radius of multi-part
objects is 0.38, and at σ = 0.15 the highlighted width (full
width at half maximum, FWHM, 2.35σ) is 46% of a typ-
ical part diameter, whereas at σ = 0.30 it reaches 92%
and spans nearly a whole part. We selected this value as
a fixed locality–robustness trade-off and use it for every ob-
ject, split, and ablation. It is not changed at test time.

S2.3. Multi-object slots
Multi-object target selection.
Each test scene of the
three object folds places the recorded target, an object un-
seen in training, together with one distractor (two-object
setting) or two distractors (three-object setting), drawn from
seen and unseen objects. Each setting has one scene per clip
(912 scenes). Target Selection Accuracy (TSA) is the pro-
portion of scenes in which the hand contacts the target and
no distractor. Distractor Contact Rate (DCR) is the propor-
tion in which it contacts any distractor, so TSA+DCR≤100
(contact as defined for multi-object evaluation in Sec. S4:
at least two active-hand keypoints within 2 cm). Held is the
proportion of scenes whose hand still holds the target in the
final five frames.
For Q candidates, the object encoder and GeoGaze share
weights across candidates. Candidate-specific gaze and ob-
ject features remain in their own slots, while the global ob-
ject condition is obtained by mean pooling.
Two-object
training pairs the target with a context object present in the
same HOT3D recording and randomly permutes their slot
order. The context slot retains its recorded trajectory, and
reconstruction losses supervise every object slot. There is
no explicit collision loss or target-index embedding. The
hand streams attend to all candidate gaze features, so the
model must infer which candidate is the target from where
the gaze falls relative to each candidate’s geometry. The
same parameters are used for every slot, allowing zero-shot
three-candidate evaluation without new learned parameters.
Text2HOI∗adaptation.
The original object input be-
comes slot 1, and an identically encoded candidate is con-
catenated as slot 2. Each slot receives its own PointNet ge-
ometry, scale, centroid, trajectory token, and slot-aligned
contact-map embedding.
The target slot receives the
prompt-derived contact map and the context slot a zero map.
As with GRAZE, the two-slot model is evaluated with a
third slot that reuses the slot weights, without fine-tuning.

S3. Implementation and reproducibility
Because the network predicts vertices rather than MANO
parameters, we fit MANO pose, translation, and shape to
the M = 100 predicted vertices of each hand using ver-
tex matching with weak pose, shape, and temporal regular-
ization. We then apply a deterministic contact refinement.
Neither stage is learned, and neither uses ground-truth hand
pose or ground-truth contact.
MANO fitting uses 80 Adam iterations with learning rate
0.05, pose and shape regularization 10−4, and pose- and
translation-temporal regularization 10−3. The contact re-
finement then optimizes the fitted mesh for 300 AdamW
16


steps at learning rate 5 × 10−4 with the BimArt [52] pene-
tration, displacement, and acceleration terms weighted 10,
100, and 1,000. A frame counts as in contact when the pre-
dicted hand comes within 2 cm of the object. The refine-
ment runs from the first predicted contact to the end of the
valid sequence with a five-frame transition and uses the last
ten valid frames when no contact is predicted. All GRAZE
variants use these fixed values.
All baselines are trained from scratch on the corre-
sponding HOT3D-Grasp split (OpenHOI keeps its released
affordance-tuned multimodal LLM), using the optimiza-
tion budgets of their original configurations where released.
Text2HOI uses 30,000 generator, 10,000 contact-estimator,
and 3,000 refiner iterations at batch size 64. DiffH2O uses
400,000 iterations, and LatentHOI uses 15 epochs for its
grasp VAE followed by 1,250 epochs for latent diffusion.
The OpenHOI generator is trained for as many epochs as
that of Text2HOI. We change the data interface and use the
one-stage configuration of DiffH2O (Sec. S1.3), but do not
shorten a baseline merely to match our training time.
The diffusion Transformer has 8 layers with latent di-
mension D = 512, feed-forward dimension 1,024, 4 at-
tention heads, and dropout 0.1. For training stability, we
apply a 10% gaze-condition dropout, which replaces the
whole gaze condition of a sample with the zero gaze to-
ken. The model is trained with AdamW [27] (learning rate
10−4, weight decay 10−2, batch size 64) under a 1,000-step
cosine diffusion schedule [31], and sampling uses all 1,000
steps. Object points are normalized to rnorm = 0.85 within
the unit-ball BPS domain (K = 1,024).
The GRAZE model is trained for 4,000 epochs on each
fold, and the final checkpoint is used without validation-
based selection. The model has 23.9M trainable parame-
ters, and training on one fold takes 14 to 16 hours on one
NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GB).
The software environment is PyTorch 1.13.0 with CUDA
11.6 and PyTorch3D 0.7.2.
S4. Metric definitions and aggregation
We use the annotated active hand(s), ignore padding and
absent hands, and average each metric over the samples
of a fold and then over the three folds.
Table S5 gives
the compact protocol.
Differences between methods are
tested with clip-level bootstrap 95% confidence intervals
(1,000–10,000 resamples), paired on clips wherever the out-
puts of two methods can be matched to the same clip.
In the main comparison of Tab. 1, the samples of every
baseline are matched to the same clips as ours (same ob-
ject, clip, and length), so all tests are paired. When ob-
jects instead of clips are resampled within each fold, the

[−4.4, 16.2]) or PCP (+7.6, [−1.5, 17.2]), whereas over

Penetration and contact-based success.
A valid frame i
of a sample is in contact when at least one vertex of the an-
notated active hand(s) lies within 5 mm of its nearest object-

than an exact point-to-triangle distance. The penetration
depth ∆i is the depth of the deepest active-hand vertex in-
side the object, with ∆i = 0 when no vertex is inside. ID
averages ∆i over the penetrating frames. Depths no greater
than 2 mm are treated as zero, and samples without a pen-
etrating frame are omitted. A prediction that never touches
the object therefore has no penetration to count, so ID is
read together with the contact-based metrics.
The same
ID is reported on GazeHOI (Tab. 2 of the main paper and
Tab. S8).
Let W be the final five valid frames, i⋆the final valid
frame, and Li the displacement of the object bottom from its
initial-frame height along the HOT3D world-up direction u
(the lowest object vertex projected on u). A sample passes
Contact when every frame of W is in contact, and
GSR = Contact 1[∆i⋆≤10 mm] 1[Li⋆≥5 mm]. (S1)
A sequence with fewer than five valid frames fails Contact
and therefore GSR.
Simulation
stability
(Sim,
SimD).
We
follow
the
simulation-displacement protocol of ObMan [15], reused
by GraspTTA [19], and port its reference PyBullet [11] set-
tings unchanged. Each sample is simulated once at its fi-
nal valid frame, the frame at which GSR is evaluated, with
the hand and object in the evaluator’s world frame. The
final MANO hand mesh is a static, massless concave tri-
angle mesh. The object is a dynamic body built from a V-
HACD [29] convex decomposition of its mesh, with a mass
of 1 kg and its center of mass at the mean of the mesh ver-
tices. Gravity of 9.8 m/s2 acts against the HOT3D world-
up direction. The simulation runs for 35 steps of 1/240 s
(0.146 s), and no table or other support is present. SimD
is the distance between the initial and final object center of
mass, averaged over all samples. Sim is the proportion of
samples displaced by less than 2 cm. An object that touches
no hand falls freely, about 10.7 cm in this duration, and is
kept in both averages. ObMan notes that balanced pene-
tration forces can also hold an object, so Sim and SimD are
read together with ID. Among the samples in contact whose
final-frame penetration depth is at most 10 mm, GRAZE
holds the object in 80.8% of the cases, against at most
70.4% for the baselines, whereas above 10 mm LatentHOI
is slightly more stable (67.8% vs. 63.1%).
Run on the
recorded motions, the same protocol gives Sim 73.9% and
SimD 2.04 cm over the three object folds, which serves as a
reference for the generated grasps.

Part metrics.
PCP maps each final contacting hand vertex
to its nearest object point, deduplicates these points, and
measures the fraction of them on the annotated target part.
Part Accuracy is one when the target part has the largest
such contact count and zero otherwise. A sample with no
final contact receives zero for both part metrics. Table S12
also reports both metrics without the one-part objects and
as macro averages.
Multi-object target-contact selectivity.
For multi-object
evaluation, an object is counted as contacted when at least
two active-hand keypoints lie within 2 cm in any valid
frame. TSA measures target-contact selectivity rather than
stable grasping. The 2 cm threshold compensates for sparse
keypoint and mesh discretization and is applied identically
to all methods.

Clock words and rotated inputs.
Clock words are de-
fined in the frame of each object, so that they need no world
pose. The up axis is the signed mesh axis (±x, ±y, or ±z)
that points closest to the world-up direction in most clips
of the object. Twelve o’clock is the positive direction of
the next mesh axis in the cyclic order x →y →z →x,
and 3 o’clock lies 90◦clockwise from it when seen from
above. The recorded direction of a clip is the azimuth of
the active-hand center at the first contact frame around this
up axis, assigned to the nearest of the four clock positions.
LatentHOI and our backbone with the text prompt are re-
trained on every object fold with “, approaching from N
o’clock” appended to each training prompt, where N is the
recorded direction. Clips in which both hands act are ex-
cluded. Follow is computed on the remaining 848 single-
hand clips of the three object folds for every method, with
four samples per clip. At test time, every clip receives the
recorded word and the three words rotated by 90◦, 180◦,
and 270◦. For GRAZE, the recorded gaze rays are instead
rotated by the same angles about the up axis of the object
through its first-frame centroid. An output follows the ro-
tation when its final-wrist azimuth, relative to the output

turned by the rotation angle to within ±45◦. Fig. 5b of the
main paper further splits the remaining outputs: one that
stayed within 45◦of the output for the unrotated input did
not turn, and every other one turned elsewhere. Classifier-

not make our backbone follow the clock word: it follows

More detailed prompts for the text baseline.
The
prompts of the text baselines are short, which could put
them at a disadvantage against a per-frame gaze input.
DiffH2O [10] shows that more detailed text descriptions
give finer control over the generated interaction.
Its de-
scriptions detail the sequence of actions. Our task, how-

added detail does not help: on the three object folds (own
pipeline, as in Tab. 1 of the main paper), GSR is unchanged
(46.6% vs. 46.6%, 95% CI of the difference [−2.1, 2.1]),
and Part Acc. and PCP fall (41.6% vs. 43.6% and 40.1% vs.
42.5%).

Scope.
GRAZE is trained on single-object grasp-and-lift
clips from nine HOT3D subjects (912 clips, 31 objects) and
is tested outside HOT3D only zero-shot on GazeHOI. Mo-
tions beyond grasping are not covered. Wrong-part grasps
remain the main failure mode (Fig. S6), and the generator,
trained from motion data without a physics simulator, can
still form contact through penetration, which ID and Sim
measure. Clip boundaries and part labels are manual anno-
tations, and the study is offline, without a user evaluation.
Gaze quality and accessibility.
The evaluation uses
recorded gaze from HOT3D and GazeHOI and does not
cover gaze-tracking latency or a user in a closed loop with
the generator. Gaze should also be treated as an optional in-
terface rather than a universal replacement for language or
other accessibility-oriented controls.
