Abstract
Autoregressive vision-language-action (VLA) models predict discrete action tokens with the next-token
objective of their vision-language backbone, so the action tokenizer defines the interface between the
policy and the robot. In bimanual manipulation, the two arms share a repertoire of movements yet
coordinate through complementary roles, and an action representation should capture both. However,
most existing tokenizers encode both arms as a single concatenated action vector, in which motion
patterns common to the two arms are not shared and cross-arm dependencies remain entangled
within each token. The policy must therefore learn both aspects implicitly. We propose Bimanual
Coordination Action Tokenization (BiCAT), which shares one motion representation across arms while
conditioning each arm’s tokens on its partner through symmetric cross-arm attention and a joint
decoder. The policy then predicts all tokens of each arm in parallel, generating the left arm first and
the right arm conditioned on it. Across ten RoboTwin 2.0 tasks and six real-world tasks on three
embodiments, BiCAT achieves the highest success among the compared tokenizers. In simulation, it
improves mean success by 17.6 percentage points over the strongest of eight baselines, with the largest
gains on tasks that require close coordination between the arms, while requiring only 40.5 ms per
action chunk. These results suggest that the action tokenizer is a natural place to model bimanual
coordination.

Introduction
Vision-language-action (VLA) models extend pretrained vision-language models to robot control (Zitkovich
et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025b,a; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026). A
common design keeps the autoregressive next-token prediction of the backbone: an action tokenizer discretizes
continuous action chunks into tokens, which the policy generates either one token at a time (Brohan et al.,
2023; Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Pertsch et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025) or in blocks of tokens
predicted in parallel (Liu et al., 2026a; Jawaid and Xiang, 2026; Dong et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). The
action tokenizer thus determines what the policy learns to predict and how errors in those predictions affect
the resulting robot motion (Pertsch et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a; Dong et al., 2026). Existing tokenizers
have explored frequency-domain compression (Pertsch et al., 2025), learned temporal abstraction (Mete et al.,
2024), vector quantization (van den Oord et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2025), and coarse-to-fine
coding (Liu et al., 2026a; Jawaid and Xiang, 2026), but they treat an action chunk as a flat vector and do not
model how its dimensions are organized across the arms of the robot.
This limitation is especially consequential in bimanual manipulation, where successful execution depends on
coordinated motion across both arms. Tasks such as handing over objects, folding fabric, and carrying large
objects often require two arms (Smith et al., 2012; Krebs and Asfour, 2022), and VLAs are increasingly being
applied to these tasks (Liu et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025b; NVIDIA et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). However,
most general-purpose action tokenizers concatenate both arms’ actions into a single vector, a formulation we
call joint tokenization. Although this formulation can capture cross-arm dependencies, it leaves the structure
of bimanual coordination to be learned implicitly.
Human bimanual manipulation illustrates two useful aspects of this structure: the arms share a repertoire of
movements, yet adapt their actions to complementary roles (Guiard, 1987). During a handover, for example,
one hand presents and releases the object while the other reaches to receive and secure it. For robots with
matching arm kinematics, this suggests an action representation that captures both reusable motion patterns
across arms and task-dependent coordination between them. Joint tokenization does not explicitly distinguish
these two aspects, leaving the policy to learn both shared motion structure and cross-arm dependencies from
the concatenated representation.
Making this structure explicit raises a design question. One option, which we call per-arm tokenization, splits
the action by arm and tokenizes each arm separately, as in M2Tok (Xu et al., 2026), leaving coordination to
the policy. Per-arm tokenization yields arm-indexed tokens and lets both arms reuse one motion representation,
but it removes partner context from each arm’s codes; in our experiments, such a tokenizer performs no
better than the strongest joint tokenizer (Sec. 4.2). This paper therefore asks: once the arms are tokenized as
separate streams, how should the streams interact to yield a better interface for bimanual policies?
Our key insight is that arm-indexed tokens need not be arm-independent: each arm’s tokens can be computed
with access to its partner while the two streams remain separate. We build on this insight in Bimanual
Coordination Action Tokenization (BiCAT), a per-arm tokenizer that restores partner context. Its design follows
one principle: share the motion representation across arms while conditioning discretization on the partner
arm. For the shared motion patterns, BiCAT exploits the mirror-image mounting of the arms: it maps the right
arm into the left arm’s convention, so that mirrored motions share one canonical trajectory, and encodes both
arms with one shared causal temporal encoder. For the cross-arm coordination, symmetric cross-arm attention
adds partner context to each stream before shared finite scalar quantization (FSQ) (Mentzer et al., 2024),
and a joint decoder reconstructs both trajectories from temporally paired codes. Trained by reconstruction
alone, BiCAT produces separate tokens for each arm, which the VLA predicts in two successive blocks: the
tokens of each arm are generated in parallel, and the right arm is conditioned on the left.
We compare BiCAT with eight baselines from seven tokenizer families under a common VLA training protocol.
On ten RoboTwin 2.0 tasks (Chen et al., 2026), BiCAT improves mean success by 17.6 percentage points over
the strongest baseline, with a gain that grows from 1.5 points on single-arm tasks to 21.3 on tightly coupled
ones, and on six real-world tasks across three embodiments it succeeds in 113 of 150 trials, compared with 74
for OAT (Liu et al., 2026a). Two-block prediction keeps policy latency at least 5.6× lower than token-by-token
decoding. Ablations show that the gain requires both the tokenizer and the prediction schedule: removing
cross-arm interaction or predicting both arms in one block leaves BiCAT no better than the strongest baseline.

Our contributions are threefold:
• BiCAT, a per-arm action tokenizer that shares one motion representation across mirrored arms and exchanges
partner context on both sides of the discrete bottleneck.
• Improved bimanual VLA control over eight baselines from seven tokenizer families in simulation, and over
FAST and OAT on three real embodiments.
• An analysis of per-arm tokenization showing that cross-arm interaction inside the tokenizer and arm-wise
block prediction in the policy are jointly required for the gain over joint tokenization.
2
Related Work
2.1
Vision-Language-Action Models
VLAs build on vision-language models pretrained on internet-scale image and text data (Beyer et al., 2024;
Steiner et al., 2024) and are fine-tuned on large robot datasets (Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2024;
Khazatsky et al., 2024) for control (Driess et al., 2023; Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al.,
2025b; Lee et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026), inheriting the instruction following and generalization of their
backbones (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2025a). Existing VLAs differ mainly in how
they output actions. Continuous approaches regress actions from the backbone or attach a generative action
expert, such as a diffusion or flow-matching model (Kim et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025b,a; NVIDIA et al., 2025;
Shukor et al., 2025). Discrete approaches tokenize actions and keep the autoregressive next-token objective of
the backbone (Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Pertsch et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025;
Goyal et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026b). Discrete prediction can incur higher inference latency (Pertsch et al.,
2025; Liu et al., 2026b), but it trains the backbone with the same next-token objective as its pretraining, which
better preserves its pretrained knowledge than propagating a continuous action-expert loss into it (Driess et al.,
2025). Both the latency and the learnability of discrete prediction depend on the action tokenizer (Pertsch
et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026a; Dong et al., 2026), which makes it a central design choice for VLAs; we study it
in the bimanual setting.
2.2
Action Tokenization
Early discrete-action VLAs such as RT-1, RT-2, and OpenVLA (Brohan et al., 2023; Zitkovich et al., 2023;
Kim et al., 2024) discretize each action dimension into bins and predict the resulting symbols autoregressively,
which yields long sequences as the action dimension and horizon grow. FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025) shortens
them with a discrete cosine transform (DCT) and byte-pair encoding (BPE), at the cost of variable-length
sequences that can decode into invalid actions (Liu et al., 2026a). Learned tokenizers instead compress
action chunks into fixed sets of latent symbols: VQ-BeT (Lee et al., 2024b) and VQ-VLA (Wang et al.,
2025) use vector quantization, QueST (Mete et al., 2024) combines causal temporal encoding with finite
scalar quantization (Mentzer et al., 2024), and OAT (Liu et al., 2026a), NAC (Jawaid and Xiang, 2026), and
CATok (Zhang et al., 2026) order their tokens from coarse to fine.
Beyond compression and reconstruction fidelity, recent work has examined how action representations affect
downstream policy learning. ActionCodec (Dong et al., 2026) investigates tokenizer properties associated
with VLA optimization, while X-Tokenizer (Kang et al., 2026) and ActionPiece (Lian et al., 2026) incorporate
vision-language alignment and relational supervision between action chunks, respectively. When applied to
bimanual robots, most existing discrete tokenizers operate on the concatenated action vector of both arms,
the joint tokenization of Sec. 1. M2Tok (Xu et al., 2026) expands quantization capacity through multiple
codebooks and tokenizes each arm separately, but does not model cross-arm interaction within the tokenizer,
leaving its role in discrete action representations largely unexplored.
2.3

and ALOHA Unleashed (Zhao et al., 2024) learn bimanual skills through action-chunk prediction, RDT-1B (Liu
et al., 2025) develops a large-scale diffusion policy for bimanual manipulation, and general-purpose VLAs

3
Method
BiCAT tokenizes a bimanual action chunk into two arm-indexed token streams that share one motion
representation and are computed with access to each other (Fig. 2). Sec. 3.1 introduces action tokenization
in the bimanual setting, Sec. 3.2 describes the tokenizer, whose training is summarized in Algorithm 1, and
Sec. 3.3 describes how a VLA predicts its tokens.

3.3
Policy Training
After tokenizer training, we freeze BiCAT and use its tokens to supervise a pretrained VLM backbone with
parameters θV: PaliGemma 2-3B (Steiner et al., 2024) in simulation and Molmo2-ER (Fang et al., 2026) on
real robots (Sec. 4). The observation o forms the input prefix. We augment the vocabulary with K action
symbols and an input-only mask token (Chang et al., 2022).

Semi-autoregressive action prediction.
Following semi-autoregressive decoding (Wang et al., 2018), the
policy instantiates Eq. (1) with the two arm streams as successive blocks:
pθV(y | o) = pθV(yL | o)pθV(yR | o, yL).
(10)
The M = 8 tokens within each arm block are predicted in parallel, and the right-arm block is conditioned
on the complete left-arm block. During training, the input suffix ([MASK]M, yL) predicts (yL, yR) under
block-causal attention. This lets the right block use the complete ground-truth left block without exposing its
own targets. Both blocks receive equally weighted cross-entropy supervision LCE in one forward pass. At
inference, two sequential block predictions generate the action tokens, and the frozen decoder reconstructs the
continuous chunk. We use greedy decoding restricted to action symbols.
4
Experiments
We evaluate BiCAT in simulation (Secs. 4.1–4.2) and on real robots (Sec. 4.3). Our experiments assess both
downstream policy performance and, through controlled ablations, the impact of the key design choices of
BiCAT.
4.1
Simulation Setup
Benchmark and protocol.
RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2026) comprises 50 bimanual manipulation tasks.
Tokenizers and policies are trained on all 2,500 demonstrations from all 50 tasks. Each learned tokenizer is
pretrained with its own objective and then frozen, and a PaliGemma 2-3B backbone (Steiner et al., 2024) is
fully fine-tuned to predict its action tokens from a language instruction, the discretized robot state, and three
RGB views. Policies predict 32-step chunks and execute the first 16 before replanning. As many of these
tasks need only one arm, we evaluate on ten representative tasks, selected before training to cover three levels
of inter-arm coupling, inspired by the coordination taxonomy of Sa et al. (2026): two single-arm tasks, four
loosely coupled tasks where both arms act largely independently, and four tightly coupled tasks where each
arm’s motion depends on its partner (Appendix A). We report success over 100 rollouts per task and the
ten-task mean (settings in Appendix B).
Baselines.
We compare BiCAT with Bin (Kim et al., 2024), FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025), QueST (Mete et al.,
2024), OAT (Liu et al., 2026a) with token-wise (OATsing) and five-block (OATpow2) prediction, M2Tok (Xu
et al., 2026), NAC (Jawaid and Xiang, 2026), and ActionCodec (Dong et al., 2026); Table 1 lists their token
budgets, vocabularies, sizes, prediction schedules, and latencies. To keep the comparison controlled, all
learned tokenizers use a vocabulary of 1920 or 2048 codes, 16 tokens per chunk except NAC (24, following
its multi-scale design), and between 7.4M and 11.9M parameters, with BiCAT the smallest. All tokenizers
are fitted or trained on the RoboTwin training data; FAST is refitted rather than taken from its released
universal tokenizer, and M2Tok uses its released implementation (Appendix B.3). All methods share the data,
backbone, observations, and optimization settings and differ only in the tokenizer and its prediction schedule.

two-block prediction cost at inference time?

Q2: What explains the gains?
Figure 3 relates mean success to six properties of each tokenizer and its trained
policy. Consistent with ActionCodec (Dong et al., 2026), reconstruction fidelity does not predict control
(ρ16 = −0.13): Bin and FAST reconstruct actions most accurately yet perform worst. Token accuracy is also
uninformative (ρ9 = −0.42), as it is not comparable across vocabularies. Success instead tracks reliance on
visual input (ρ16 = 0.72), consistency across replanning (ρ16 = −0.80), and smoothness at chunk boundaries
(ρ16 = −0.78), and less closely tolerance to token errors (ρ16 = −0.51); BiCAT has the smallest boundary jump
among the nine tokenizers. As eight of the 16 points are BiCAT or its variants, we read these correlations as
descriptive rather than causal.
Q2: Which components contribute?
Table 3 ablates the design principle and the prediction schedule. Raw
coordinates sets s = 1, so the two arms share features for identical joint values rather than for mirrored
motions, and a motion such as reaching inward is encoded differently for each arm; Arm-specific modules
gives each arm its own modules and normalization bounds. These variants lower success to 58.7% and
56.9%; sharing aligns the latents of the two arms (left–right MMD of 0.088 for BiCAT, versus 0.193 and
0.286; Appendix D). Removing the pre-quantization cross-attention (Decoder-only coupling), joint decoding
(Encoder-only coupling), or both (Independent arms, a controlled per-arm variant) lowers success to 51.7%,
56.0%, and 50.0%. The two interactions are complementary: each adds far more when the other is present
(17.6 versus 6.0 points for cross-attention, 13.3 versus 1.7 for joint decoding), and removing them weakens
error tolerance and visual grounding. Predicting both arms in one block (Single-block prediction) lowers
success to 51.1%, so neither partner-aware codes without the arm-wise schedule nor the schedule without
partner-aware codes exceeds NAC. Reversing the block order (Right-first prediction) lowers success to 54.2%,
so the order also matters; we use left-first throughout.

Q3: How does the vocabulary affect control?

Q3: Where does BiCAT underperform?
The improvement is not uniform. BiCAT reaches 88% on Handover

Handover Mic, four tokenizer ablations with the same block order reach 92–96% (Appendix C), so this gap
is not inherent to arm-wise tokens or two-block prediction.

Q4: What does two-block prediction cost?
Table 1 reports per-chunk latency on one H200 GPU with batch
size 1. Encoding and decoding take under 5 ms for every tokenizer, so latency is set by the prediction schedule.
With two blocks, BiCAT takes 40.5 ms, comparable to M2Tok and NAC, which also predict two blocks, and
5.6× to over 11× faster than tokenizers decoded token by token. Only ActionCodec, which predicts a single
block, is faster (26.8 ms), but Single-block prediction costs BiCAT 18.2 points of success, so the second block is
worth its extra decoding step.

Data and training.
We collect 100 teleoperated demonstrations per task (105 for Pen). For each embodiment,
one tokenizer is trained on all of its demonstrations and frozen, and one VLA policy with a Molmo2-ER
backbone (Fang et al., 2026) is trained on its tokens for all tasks of that embodiment. Within an embodiment,
all methods use exactly the same data and training configuration, including training steps, learning rate,
warmup, and batch size (Appendix F.1), and the final checkpoint is evaluated.
Baselines and evaluation.
We compare BiCAT with one representative tokenizer from each of the two main
families: FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025), the widely adopted compression-based tokenizer, and OATsing (Liu
et al., 2026a), a learned tokenizer with ordered tokens and token-wise prediction. All policies run on one
NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU and are evaluated for 25 trials per task, 150 per method. A trial succeeds only if
every stage of the task is completed, and the initial object placements are identical across methods. Inference
latency is measured on YAM, from receiving an observation to outputting an action chunk, over all inference
10


calls. The robot waits during inference, so latency lengthens an episode but does not change the observations
on which chunks are predicted (Appendix F.2).
Results.
As shown in Table 5, BiCAT succeeds in 113 of 150 trials, compared with 74 for OATsing and 25 for
FAST, leads on all six tasks, and has the lowest inference latency, 4.7× lower than FAST. Consistent with
simulation, the advantage persists across physical bimanual embodiments.
5
Conclusion & Limitations
We introduced BiCAT, a bimanual action tokenizer for autoregressive VLAs that combines a shared motion
representation across mirrored arms with partner-conditioned discretization, while preserving separate token
streams for each arm. The policy predicts these streams in two successive blocks, requiring only two decoding
steps per action chunk. Across simulation and three real-world bimanual embodiments, BiCAT achieves the
highest success rates among the evaluated tokenizers, with larger gains on tasks requiring tighter coordination.
Ablations show that both cross-arm interaction within the tokenizer and blockwise prediction in the policy are
necessary for these gains over joint tokenization, highlighting action tokenization as an effective way to model
bimanual coordination. However, BiCAT currently operates in joint space: its fixed per-joint sign mapping
does not directly extend to end-effector actions, which require different transformations for positions and
orientations. Each tokenizer is also trained for a single embodiment and assigns equal, fixed token budgets to
both arms. Extending BiCAT to end-effector actions, cross-embodiment transfer, and adaptive token allocation
remains future work.

A.1
Evaluation Protocol
Each method is evaluated with 100 rollouts per task using different random seeds. We use RoboTwin’s
task-specific success checks and report both per-task success and the unweighted mean across the ten tasks.
Policies predict 32-step action chunks, execute the first 16 steps, and then replan from a new observation. All
ten evaluation tasks belong to the 50-task collection used for training.
B
Implementation Details
This section complements Sec. 4.1 with the simulation training details of BiCAT and the baselines.

B.1
Training Data and Inputs
The 5% validation split is drawn at the episode level with a fixed seed, giving 2,375 training and 125 validation
episodes, and is shared by tokenizer and policy training. Action chunks of 32 steps are sampled within episode
boundaries; each step has 14 dimensions, six joint values and one gripper value per arm. The three RGB
views come from the head and the two wrist cameras, and the robot state is encoded as a text prefix with 256
bins per dimension. All policies use the paligemma2-3b-mix-224 checkpoint.

B.3
Tokenizer Configurations
Table 1 in the main paper lists the token count, vocabulary size, and prediction schedule of each tokenizer.
All learned tokenizers use a hidden width of 256 unless noted and are trained on action reconstruction.
• Bin (Kim et al., 2024): each normalized action value is mapped to one of 256 uniform bins.
• FAST (Pertsch et al., 2025): a discrete cosine transform, coefficient quantization, and byte-pair encoding,
fitted on the RoboTwin training split rather than taken from the released universal tokenizer; it yields 26.8
tokens per chunk on average (range 9–80).
• QueST (Mete et al., 2024): causal temporal convolutions followed by a six-layer causal Transformer encoder,
with FSQ levels [8, 8, 6, 5].
• OAT (Liu et al., 2026a): learned register tokens trained with nested prefix dropout over {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}
registers, with FSQ levels [8, 8, 6, 5]; the two variants differ only in their register and prediction block
schedules.
• M2Tok (Xu et al., 2026): the released implementation with width 120. A shared convolutional VQ-VAE
encodes each arm independently into two latent frames, each quantized by four cosine-similarity codebooks
of 512 entries, and reconstructs each arm from its own tokens.
• NAC (Jawaid and Xiang, 2026): a SEANet encoder over the time-major flattened chunk and two-level
residual FSQ with levels [8, 8, 4, 4], a coarse level of 8 tokens and a fine level of 16; its Vocos decoder is
trained with reconstruction, adversarial, and feature-matching losses.
• ActionCodec (Dong et al., 2026): a six-layer Perceiver-style encoder and decoder, with 16 latent vectors
quantized by a 2048-entry VQ codebook updated by exponential moving average.
16


• BiCAT: the architecture of Sec. 3, with one shared causal self-attention layer and one symmetric cross-arm
attention layer in the encoder, FSQ levels [8, 8, 6, 5], and a six-layer joint decoder.

D
Shared Arm Representations
Figure 6 visualizes the pre-quantization latents of BiCAT, Raw coordinates, and Arm-specific modules. We
encode 10,000 action chunks from the ten evaluation tasks, average each arm’s eight pre-quantization latent
vectors, and embed the left- and right-arm averages of each model jointly with t-SNE. With all modules
shared, the latents of the two arms overlap. With Arm-specific modules, the two arms occupy largely separate
regions, so the shared FSQ grid assigns the same token IDs to latents that are distributed differently for the
two arms.
We quantify the overlap with the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) between the left- and right-arm averages
in the original 4-D latent space, using an RBF kernel whose bandwidth is the median pairwise squared
distance. The MMD is 0.088 for BiCAT, 0.193 for Raw coordinates, and 0.286 for Arm-specific modules,
and the ordering is unchanged after pooled per-dimension standardization. Under Raw coordinates, the two
arms remain visually mixed in the t-SNE embedding, yet their MMD is more than twice that of BiCAT, so
canonicalization aligns the arms beyond what the shared encoder alone achieves. These distances describe
arm-marginal distributions: they are consistent with, but do not directly measure, a token ID denoting the
same motion for both arms, and they do not by themselves measure coordination or task success.

17


Policy.
The real-world policies use Molmo2-ER (Fang et al., 2026) as the vision-language backbone, which is
fully fine-tuned together with the vision encoder and the modality bridge. The policy observes the language
instruction, the joint state of both arms, and three RGB views from the current time step: a top camera and
the two wrist cameras. On Bimanual YAM and Unitree G1 the top view has a resolution of 360 × 640 and the
wrist views 480 × 640; on Bimanual SO-101 all three views are 480 × 640. The policy predicts 32-step action
chunks as in simulation, but on the real robots it executes all 32 steps of each chunk before replanning, rather
than the first 16.
Optimization.
Tokenizer pretraining uses the settings of Table 7 except for the number of steps (Table 8).
Policy training uses the same peak learning rate and cosine decay as in simulation, with a global batch
size of 128, BF16 mixed precision, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Within an embodiment, the training
configuration is identical across methods: BiCAT and OATsing are trained for the same number of tokenizer
steps, FAST is fitted on the same demonstrations, and all three policies share the data, observations, number
of policy steps, learning rate, warmup, and batch size. They differ only in the tokenizer and its prediction
schedule.
F.2
Evaluation Protocol
Each method is evaluated for 25 trials per task. A trial counts as a success only if the robot completes every
stage of the task without error; on Block, for example, the block must be placed in the box and the box
handed over. Partial completion counts as a failure. During deployment, the robot waits while the policy
predicts the next action chunk and executes the chunk once inference has finished, so differences in inference
latency lengthen the episode but do not change the observation on which each chunk is predicted. The initial
object placements are identical across methods for each trial, and each trial is limited to 120 s, after which
it counts as a failure. Inference latency is measured from receiving an observation to outputting an action
chunk, over all inference calls during the YAM evaluation, and is reported as mean ± standard deviation.
