Corresponding Author Transformer-based 3D reconstruction models such as VGGT and π3 predict cameras, depth and point maps directly from many input views. Causal attention models such as StreamVGGT extend them to online video but cache all past tokens, so their total compute grows quadratically with the stream length. Recurrent models such as CUT3R and TTT3R keep a fixed-size state at constant cost, but they progressively forget early geometry. We present LinearVGGT, a hybrid model distilled from StreamVGGT, which uses Mamba2 layers for efficient sequence modeling in three of every four global layers. The remaining layers use register-guided sparse attention to retrieve a few relevant past frames, compensating for the limited exact long-range recall of Mamba2. Our distillation needs only 9% of the GPU hours and 31% of the training frames that StreamVGGT uses to learn from VGGT. LinearVGGT keeps an almost constant latency of 27 ms per frame over 1,024 frames and is on average 12 × faster than StreamVGGT. It outperforms its teacher and is competitive with the state-of-the-art streaming model LingBot-Map, while running 2.5× faster and using 2.7× less memory.

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in transformer-based 3D visual geometry reconstruction have demonstrated strong performance in recovering scenes’ 3D geometry attributes (e.g., camera parameters, point maps, depth maps) from multiple views of scenes (Wang et al., 2025a; 2026c;b). However, their reliance on dense global attention incurs quadratic computational complexity in the number of visual tokens and substantial memory overhead, limiting their scalability to long image sequences and applications to streaming video inputs like SLAM (Maggio et al., 2025). These limitations have motivated a growing body of work aimed at improving the computational and memory efficiency of these models. Token-merging based methods like FastVGGT, GoToHunt etc recognize the redundant attention pattern in global attention and only select the most important tokens for computation. Despite being easy to implement and training free, these methods still struggle with quadratic computational complexity of global attention. Recurrent models such as CUT3R (Wang et al., 2025b) and TTT3R (Chen et al., 2026b) keep a fixed-size state at a constant cost per frame, but this state forgets earlier frames as the stream grows (Chen et al., 2026b). Furthermore, StreamVGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026) and LingBot-Map (Chen et al., 2026a) have explored using causal attention architectures or its more efficient variants to solve this. Nevertheless, the nature of causal attention determines that it still suffers from prohibitively long inference time when increasing the number of processed frames. This tradeoff raises our research question: Can a streaming visual geometry transformer run at a nearly constant cost per frame without losing the accuracy of causal attention? 

Linear-time sequence models such as Mamba2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) and Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025) suggest an answer. They carry the history in a fixed-size state, so each new input costs the same, and hybrid language models (Lieber et al., 2024; Kimi Team, 2025) recover the recall of attention with a few attention layers among them. Although the great efficiency makes it appealing to adapt these cutting-edge architectures, applying them to 3D reconstruction domain is non-trivial, we recognize two primary challenges: First, the adaptation to 3D reconstruction domains, especially performance on long sequences. We observe significant performance drop of selective state space models under long sequences inputs, which counteracts the efficiency gain of these linear models. Second, the new modeling architecture will trigger training from scratch, which requires extensive GPU time and resources ( Table 3.) To address these challenges, we propose LinearVGGT, a hybrid streaming visual geometry transformer whose Mamba2 layers carry a compressed summary of the stream at a constant cost. To counteract the decay of this summary on long streams, we interleave the Mamba2 layers with periodic attention layers in a 3:1 ratio. These attention layers fill in what the summary misses: direct interactions with the key tokens of the past frames most relevant to the new frame. Full attention in these layers would bring the growing cost back, yet most of its interactions are redundant. Prior analyses of VGGT show that its global attention can be sparsified with little loss of accuracy (Wang et al., 2026a; Sun et al., 2026). Our preliminary experiments agree: a new frame loses little accuracy when it reads only the first frame and a few past frames (Section 3.4). The open question is which frames and tokens to read. Register tokens offer a cheap answer: in vision transformers they collect global information about their image (Darcet et al., 2023), and VGGT-Ω already uses them to exchange scene information across frames (Wang et al., 2026b). We therefore propose register-guided sparse attention, a hierarchical design that selects frames first and tokens second. At the frame level, the registers of a new frame rank all past frames by attending only to their registers, and the new frame attends to the first frame, which anchors the world coordinates, and to the top-ranked frames. At the token level, a lightweight indexer keeps in each past frame only the patch tokens that the frame itself attends to most. To avoid the training cost discussed above, we distill LinearVGGT from StreamVGGT with a three-stage procedure inspired by MOHAWK (Bick et al., 2024). the first two stages align each new layer with the corresponding attention layer, first through its attention matrix and then through its output. The third stage trains the student end to end against both the predictions of the teacher and ground-truth depth, cameras and point maps, first updating the new layers and then all layers except the image encoder. The whole procedure takes 59 hours on one GPU and 1.2M training frames. This is 9% of the GPU hours and 31% of the frames that StreamVGGT needs to learn from VGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026), and less than 0.5% of the GPU hours used to train VGGT or LingBot-Map. Despite this small budget, LinearVGGT has lower accuracy and completeness errors than its teacher in all four multi-view reconstruction settings, with up to 49% lower accuracy error. It also outperforms the recurrent CUT3R and TTT3R. Additionally, we observe that streaming inference is also limited by the overhead of launching GPU kernels. We therefore optimize inference by capturing the fixed-shape part of each frame as CUDA graphs and fusing its small kernels. With these optimizations, LinearVGGT keeps its latency of average 27ms per frame, on average 12 times faster than StreamVGGT and also faster than CUT3R and TTT3R. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We propose LinearVGGT, a streaming visual geometry transformer that interleaves Mamba2 layers with register-guided sparse attention, so that its cost stays nearly constant while it keeps the accuracy of causal attention; • We introduce a three-stage distillation for visual geometry transformers that turns a pretrained model into an accurate linear-time model with a small fraction of the data and compute that training such a model usually requires; • Extensive experiments show that LinearVGGT reaches state-of-the-art efficiency in streaming recon-struction while its accuracy remains competitive with that of state-of-the-art models. 

2 Method 

Figure 2 gives an overview of LinearVGGT. It keeps the image encoder, the frame attention layers and the prediction heads of StreamVGGT and changes only the layers that connect frames: three of every four become Mamba2 layers (Section 2.2), and every fourth becomes a register-guided sparse attention layer (Section 2.3). We obtain the model by distillation from StreamVGGT (Section 2.4) and accelerate its streaming inference (Section 2.5). 

2.1 Preliminaries: Streaming 3D Reconstruction Problem setting. A streaming model receives RGB frames I0, I 1, . . . one at a time, without camera parameters. For each frame t, it predicts the camera gt, the depth map Dt and the point map Xt in the coordinate frame of I0, from the frames I0, . . . , I t alone and before frame t+1 arrives. The model must therefore carry what it needs from the past in some memory, and the cost of reading this memory decides how long a stream it can process. 

Causal Global Attention StreamVGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026), the model we build on, keeps the whole past as this memory. Following VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a), it encodes each frame into P patch tokens and adds one camera token and R register tokens, which gives N = P + R + 1 tokens per frame. An aggregator of L blocks then alternates frame attention, among the tokens of one frame, with global attention, across frames, and two heads decode the camera token and the patch tokens. In streaming mode, the global attention is causal over frames and caches the keys and values of every past frame. For one head of a global layer, the tokens of frame t attend to all cached frames:

2.3 Register-guided hierarchical sparse attention 

A geometry model needs more from the past than a decaying summary. The first frame defines the world coordinates of every prediction, and a frame that revisits a place must align with the earlier views of it. We therefore follow hybrid language models (Lieber et al., 2024; Kimi Team, 2025) and interleave the Mamba2 layers with attention layers in a 3:1 ratio, which keep the weights of the corresponding 4Linear-time Online Visual Geometry Transformer with Hybrid Architecture

Token indexing. A selected frame still brings N tokens, many of which cover textureless or repeated regions. When frame s ≥ 1 enters the cache of a sparse layer, a token indexer therefore keeps its camera and register tokens and only the ⌈ρP ⌉ patch tokens that receive the most attention from the tokens of frame s itself, summed over heads and queries. In contrast to VGGT-Ω (Wang et al., 2026b), whose register attention lets only registers exchange information across frames, our registers only select the frames, and all tokens of the new frame then attend to the selected tokens. 

2.4 Three-Stage Distillation 

Training a new architecture from scratch requires the compute and data of a large-scale pretraining run, most of which would go into relearning what StreamVGGT already knows. Since only the cross-frame layers of LinearVGGT are new, we keep the pretrained weights and teach these layers to behave like the attention they replace. Inspired by MOHAWK (Bick et al., 2024), we transfer the teacher into the student in three stages that move from the operator to the output. Equation (2.3) provides the starting point: we initialize the maps that produce x, b and c with the value, key and query projections of the teacher layer and copy its output projection, and the sparse layers start as exact copies of their teacher layers.

A student trained only to imitate inherits the errors of its teacher, and the ground-truth term lets it correct them. We first update the new layers alone and then all layers except the image encoder, so that the frame attention layers and the heads adapt to the new cross-frame layers. 

2.5 Efficient streaming inference 

Streaming inference is bound by kernel launches rather than by computation (Section 3.4), and a CUDA graph removes this overhead by replaying a recorded sequence of kernels with a single launch, provided that every tensor keeps its shape. In LinearVGGT, the fixed-size states of the Mamba2 layers make this hold for almost the whole frame, so we capture it as seven CUDA graphs, split at the six sparse attention layers, and run only these layers and the camera head, whose inputs grow with the stream, kernel by kernel. We also cache the positional embeddings and the normalized, rotated keys of past frames, and fuse sequences of small operations into single kernels. 

3 Experiment 

Our experiments answer three questions: Q1: How accurate is LinearVGGT compared with state-of-the-art streaming models, including its attention-based teacher? Q2: Does its cost per frame stay constant as the stream grows, and what does it cost to train? Q3: Which parts of the design are responsible? 

3.1 Setup Training. We train on 14 datasets that span indoor, outdoor, object-centric and synthetic scenes, including ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017), CO3Dv2 (Reizenstein et al., 2021), MegaDepth (Li & Snavely, 2018) and Waymo (Sun et al., 2020). The supplementary material lists all datasets, the hardware and the remaining training settings. 

Baselines. We compare with the streaming models StreamVGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026), OVGGT (Lu et al., 2026), CUT3R (Wang et al., 2025b), TTT3R (Chen et al., 2026b), LingBot-Map (Chen et al., 2026a) and ZipMap (Jin et al., 2026) in its streaming mode. As a reference, we also list offline models that see all frames at once: VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a), π3 (Wang et al., 2026c), FastVGGT (Shen et al., 2026) and GoToHunt (Zheng et al., 2026). We run every baseline with its official code and weights in the same evaluation pipeline. 

Benchmarks and metrics. For multi-view reconstruction, we follow the protocol of CUT3R and StreamVGGT on 7-Scenes (Shotton et al., 2013) and NRGBD (Azinovic et al., 2022) and report ac-curacy (Acc ↓), completeness (Comp ↓) and normal consistency (NC ↑). With sparse views , we take one keyframe every 200 frames on 7-Scenes and every 500 frames on NRGBD, which gives 2 to 5 frames per sequence; with dense views , one every 20 and 50 frames, which gives 8 to 50 frames. 3.2 3D Reconstruction 

We first evaluate scene-level reconstruction from sparse views, which tests each model on image collections with little overlap. As Table 1 shows, among the online methods, LinearVGGT outperforms its teacher StreamVGGT and the recurrent CUT3R and TTT3R in accuracy and completeness on both datasets, and it halves the accuracy error of StreamVGGT on 7-Scenes. Only LingBot-Map is more accurate, while LinearVGGT runs 2.5 times faster than it and 12 times faster than StreamVGGT. Compared with the offline methods, which see all frames at once, LinearVGGT is more accurate on 7-Scenes and on par with VGGT on NRGBD, at a higher throughput than any of them. With sparse inputs, LinearVGGT thus combines an accuracy competitive with state-of-the-art online and offline models with the highest throughput in the comparison. Sparse views show how a model handles little overlap, but not how it behaves as a stream grows. We therefore also evaluate dense streams: we take every second frame of each sequence and reconstruct the first N of them, for increasing N (Figure 3). The error of LinearVGGT also grows with the length of the stream, from 0.014 at 50 frames to 0.046 at 400, but far more slowly than that of CUT3R and streaming ZipMap, which reach 0.129 and 0.159, and at a rate similar to TTT3R and StreamVGGT. Only LingBot-Map stays flat over the whole range. LinearVGGT models long sequences less well than it, but is the fastest method in the comparison: over 256 frames it runs 2.5 times faster than LingBot-Map, 1.8 times faster than CUT3R and TTT3R, and 12 times faster than StreamVGGT. 3.4 Analysis Inference efficiency. The latency of LinearVGGT stays between 25 and 28 ms per frame over a stream of 1,024 frames (Figure 1b). The latency of StreamVGGT grows from 68 ms at frame 8 to 586 ms at frame 256, and the model runs out of memory after 445 frames on a 96 GB GPU. The recurrent CUT3R and TTT3R also run at a constant latency, of about 45 ms, and LingBot-Map takes 59 to 71 ms, so LinearVGGT is the fastest of the online models at every stream length. Its memory grows slowly: the caches of the sparse attention layers hold 6.3 GiB after 1,024 frames, about 6 MiB per frame, against 98 MiB per frame for the cache of StreamVGGT. 

Inference optimization. Figure 5 shows where the time of a frame goes in eager execution. In every module except the prediction heads, the host spends several times longer on launching GPU kernels than the GPU spends on computing them: a frame launches about 4,200 kernels, and the GPU is busy for only 35% of the time. The gap is largest in the Mamba2 layers, whose computation takes 2 ms of the 22 ms they occupy. Launches dominate because a fixed-size state keeps the work of each frame small and constant. In StreamVGGT, attention over the growing cache takes over instead: the same engine lowers its latency from 62 to 40 ms at frame 8, but only from 584 to 574 ms at frame 256. We therefore capture the fixed-shape work of each frame as CUDA graphs, cache the repeated computation and fuse small kernels (Section 2.5). These optimizations lower the latency of a frame from 78 to 27 ms. 

Training efficiency. Table 3 compares the training cost of LinearVGGT with that of related models. Our distillation takes 59 GPU hours and 1.2M training frames, which is 9% of the GPU hours and 31% of the frames that StreamVGGT needs to learn from VGGT. Redundancy of attention. Register-guided sparse attention rests on the premise that a new frame does not need to attend to the whole history. Figure 6 examines this premise on the global attention of StreamVGGT, for clips of 16 frames. In the first and last layers, the attention of a new frame is close to uniform over the past, so it averages the history rather than retrieves from it. In the middle layers, the attention concentrates: in layer 14, a fifth of the past tokens holds 90% of it. In neither case does a new frame need all tokens of all past frames. The trained model agrees. Letting its sparse layers read all past frames with all their tokens does not improve accuracy: with dense views, the mean error stays level on NRGBD (0.045 against 0.048) and rises on 7-Scenes from 0.037 to 0.062. It only raises the cost: after 1,024 frames, a frame takes 335 ms instead of 25 - 28 ms. A few selected frames thus suffice, at a fraction of the cost of full attention. 

3.5 Ablation Study 

We remove or replace one component of LinearVGGT at a time and report the accuracy with dense views (Table 4). We use dense views because, with sparse views, the anchor and the selected frames already cover the whole input. 

Hybrid layout. A student with Mamba2 layers in all global layers has an accuracy error five times as high on 7-Scenes (0.185 against 0.037) and four times as high on NRGBD (0.197 against 0.048) as a student that keeps attention in every fourth layer. A recurrent state alone thus does not carry what the reconstruction needs from the past, which confirms the need for the sparse attention layers. 

Anchor and frame routing. Without the anchor, the accuracy error rises from 0.037 to 0.049 on 7-Scenes and from 0.048 to 0.055 on NRGBD, although the model reads the same number of frames. The first frame defines the coordinate frame of all predictions, and the routing alone does not select it reliably. For the remaining frames, register attention beats both alternatives: the most recent frames raise the error to 0.046 and 0.054, and random frames to 0.079 and 0.050. Which past frames are read thus matters. 

Token indexer. The purpose of the indexer is to save memory. Without it, every cached frame keeps all its tokens, and the caches of the sparse layers hold 24.5 GiB after 1,024 frames; the indexer lowers this to 6.3 GiB. Accuracy and completeness stay at the same level, so the tokens it removes are not needed. 4 Related Works 

Transformer-based 3D Reconstruction Models Recent transformer-based 3D reconstruction models like VGGT, VGGT-Ω and π3 have demonstrated that dense 3D geometry features can be predicted by transformers. However, these methods rely on dense global attention inference and this already become the bottleneck of 3D visual geometry transformers. While existing works use methods like sparse attention mechanisms or token -merging to reduce the cost of redundant global attention, their computational costs still increase quadratically with the number of images, hindering their applications to scenarios under large numbers of input images. 

Efficient Online 3D visual geometry transformers Robots (Cadena et al., 2016), AR/VR head-sets (Klein & Murray, 2007; Engel et al., 2023; Maggio et al., 2025) and autonomous vehicles (Bresson et al., 2017; Zuo et al., 2026) need the camera pose and the geometry of each frame as the frame arrives. An offline model must process all frames again for each new frame, which is too slow for this setting. StreamVGGT (Zhuo et al., 2026) makes the global attention of VGGT causal and caches the keys and values of all past frames, so that a new frame reads the full history without recomputation. As a result, the cache and the cost of each frame grow with the stream, and the total cost grows quadratically. InfiniteVGGT (Yuan et al., 2026) and XStreamVGGT (Su et al., 2026) prune this cache without retraining, and LongStream (Cheng et al., 2026) refreshes it periodically. LingBot-Map (Chen et al., 2026a) limits this context by position: a new frame attends to a few anchor frames, a window of recent frames and a trajectory memory of six tokens per past frame. Recurrent models take the opposite approach. CUT3R (Wang et al., 2025b) reads from and writes to a fixed-size scene state at a constant cost per frame, and TTT3R (Chen et al., 2026b) treats this state update as test-time training. A fixed-size state, however, forgets earlier frames as the stream grows (Chen et al., 2026b). Linear Complexity Sequence Models Linear attention (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) writes causal attention as a recurrence over a fixed-size state, so each step has a constant cost and memory. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) and Mamba2 (Dao & Gu, 2024) add a selective, data-dependent decay to this state, and Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025) and Kimi Delta Attention (Kimi Team, 2025) combine such a decay with the delta rule (Schlag et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024). These models train in parallel with chunkwise kernels (Yang & Zhang, 2024) and run as recurrences at inference, so their total cost grows only linearly with the sequence length. Their fixed-size state, however, recalls specific past tokens less exactly than attention (Arora et al., 2024; Jelassi et al., 2024) and often fails on sequences longer than those seen in training (Ben-Kish et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Ruiz & Gu, 2025), so hybrid models such as Jamba (Lieber et al., 2024) and Kimi Linear (Kimi Team, 2025) keep a few attention layers. In addition, a new architecture must be trained from scratch, which is costly for a large pretrained model such as VGGT. Distillation avoids this cost by transferring the knowledge of a pretrained transformer into a linear-time model. MOHAWK (Bick et al., 2024) first matches the mixing matrices, then the hidden states of each block, and finally distills the full model, and Mamba in the Llama (Wang et al., 2024) initializes Mamba layers from attention weights and keeps a quarter of the attention layers. These methods target language models. Inspired by MOHAWK, we propose a distillation method for visual geometry transformers. It initializes the new layers from the attention weights of StreamVGGT, follows the three stages of MOHAWK, and adds ground-truth supervision to the last stage, so that the student can correct the errors of its teacher. 11 Linear-time Online Visual Geometry Transformer with Hybrid Architecture 

5 Conclusion 

We presented LinearVGGT, a streaming visual geometry transformer that runs at a nearly constant cost per frame. Its Mamba2 layers summarize the stream in a state of fixed size, and its register-guided sparse attention layers restore what this summary forgets by reading a few relevant past frames. Distilled from StreamVGGT with 9% of the GPU hours that StreamVGGT itself required, LinearVGGT processes a 1,024-frame stream at 25 to 28 ms per frame and outperforms its teacher in 3D reconstruction and video depth estimation. These results suggest that a streaming visual geometry transformer does not need exact attention to its whole past: a compressed summary, together with direct access to a few frames chosen by their content, is enough. We hope that our distillation makes it cheap to explore further recurrent designs for long streams.

A.1 Architecture Mamba2 layers. Each of the 18 recurrent layers is an unmodified Mamba2 block (Dao & Gu, 2024) whose shape follows the teacher attention it replaces: width 1024, 16 heads of dimension d = 64 , a state of size n = 64 per head, no expansion, and a causal convolution with a kernel of 4 tokens. The block projects each token ui with one linear map into a gate zi, the input xi, the vectors bi and ci of every head, and one step logit δi per head. The query–key normalization and the rotary embedding of the teacher have no counterpart in the block and are dropped. 

Token-causal scan. The teacher is causal over frames: a token attends to all tokens of its own frame, including those after it in raster order. The scan of a recurrent layer is causal over tokens, so a token cannot read the later tokens of its frame in this layer. The frame attention layers of the same block, which we keep, see all tokens of a frame at once and supply this context.

The distillation of a 1.28B-parameter model thus reduces to the regression of one T × T matrix per head and layer. These problems are independent across layers once every layer receives the input of its teacher layer, which is the setting of Stage 1. The sparse attention layers are the trivial case: they compute the teacher matrix itself on the selected frames and need no matrix stage. 

Why the matrix stage is not enough. Two facts keep Stage 1 from finishing the distillation. First, 

M = A cannot be reached. The Mamba2 matrix is a rank-n matrix with n = 64 under a decay mask and is causal over tokens, while the attention matrix is unconstrained and causal over frames, so a residual ∥M − A∥F > 0 remains in every layer. Second, we keep the official Mamba2 block, in which a SiLU follows the convolution on X and an RMSNorm precedes the output projection (Equation (A.1)); MOHAWK removes both from its student. With them, X = V and Equation (A.6) hold only approximately, even at initialization. Stage 2 therefore trains each layer on its output, which includes these two parts, and Stage 3 trains all layers together, because the teacher inputs of the first two stages hide how the residual errors of 24 layers accumulate. 

A.4 Losses Stage 1. The set Ω contains 96 query tokens at even spacing over the 8N tokens of a clip, and the loss compares the corresponding rows of the mixing matrix and of the teacher attention in all 16 heads. The teacher rows are the softmax of Equation (2.1) under the causal mask over frames. A teacher row therefore has mass on the later tokens of its own frame, which the token-causal mixing matrix cannot reproduce; this part of the target sets a floor for L1 and is recovered by the frame attention layers. The output projection and the RMSNorm of Equation (A.1) do not enter the mixing matrix and stay fixed in this stage.

CUDA graphs. In eager execution, a frame launches more than 4,000 GPU kernels, and the Python and dispatch work between them takes most of the 77.2 ms. FastStreamer writes the computation of one frame as a single program and cuts it at the six sparse attention cores, whose key and value caches grow with the stream. Each of the seven segments between the cuts has fixed tensor shapes and is captured once per resolution as a CUDA graph, with the states and convolution buffers of the Mamba2 layers in static buffers that the graph updates in place. The first segment holds the encoder and the layers before the first sparse layer, and the last one ends with the DPT head. The sparse attention cores and the camera head, which has a growing cache of its own, run eagerly between the graphs. This step alone brings the latency from 77.2 to 34.8 ms without any change of the outputs. 22 Linear-time Online Visual Geometry Transformer with Hybrid Architecture 

Exact optimizations. Five further changes remove repeated work and leave every output bit unchanged. (i) The linear and convolution weights of the aggregator are stored in bf16, the precision to which autocast converts them on every call. (ii) The positional embeddings of the encoder and of the DPT head are computed once per resolution and not once per frame. (iii) The rotary tables are gathered once per resolution and not in every attention call. (iv) Only the intermediate features that the heads read are assembled. (v) The sparse layers cache their keys after query–key normalization and rotary embedding, so a frame normalizes and rotates only its own keys and not those of all selected frames. 

Fused kernels. Two Triton kernels then replace chains of small kernels: one computes the residual addition, LayerScale and the following layer normalization, and one the query–key normalization with the rotary embedding. They halve the number of kernels of the remaining program and bring the latency to 26.3 ms. Their layer normalization statistics differ from those of PyTorch at the level of fp32 rounding, so the outputs are close to the eager ones but not bitwise identical. On a 200-frame TUM sequence, the median relative difference per frame is 1.5 × 10 −3 for depth, 4.0 × 10 −3 for point maps and 4.5 × 10 −4

for the camera encoding. For comparison, a switch between two attention backends of PyTorch in the eager model changes the same outputs by 1.4 × 10 −3, 4.1 × 10 −3 and 4.7 × 10 −4, so the fused kernels stay within the numerical noise of the eager path. 

B Evaluation Details 

3D reconstruction. We use the 18 test sequences of 7-Scenes and the 9 scenes of NRGBD with the split and the code of CUT3R and StreamVGGT. With sparse views , the input is one keyframe every 200 frames on 7-Scenes and every 500 frames on NRGBD; with dense views , one every 20 and 50 frames, which gives 8 to 50 views per sequence. For the long streams of Section 3.2, we take every second frame of a 7-Scenes sequence and keep the first N of them. The predicted point maps of all views are cropped to their central 224 ×224 pixels, aligned to the ground truth with a similarity transform from the pixel correspondences (Umeyama, 1991) and refined with ICP at a distance threshold of 0.1 m. Accuracy is the distance from a predicted point to its nearest ground-truth point, completeness the distance from a ground-truth point to its nearest predicted point, and normal consistency the agreement between the normals of matched points. For every sequence, we take the mean and the median of each quantity over its points, and the tables report the average of both over the sequences (Mean and Med.). 

Video depth. We follow CUT3R on Sintel, Bonn and KITTI and feed each sequence as one stream. The predicted depth of a sequence is aligned to the ground truth with one scale factor, as in Table 2, or with a scale and a shift, as in Table 9. 

Camera pose. We follow CUT3R on Sintel (14 sequences), on the 8 dynamic sequences of TUM (Sturm et al., 2012) and on 100 test scenes of ScanNet, the latter two with the first 90 frames at a stride of 3. The predicted trajectory is aligned to the ground truth with a similarity transform, and we report the absolute trajectory error (ATE) and the relative pose errors in translation and rotation. 

Efficiency measurement. We time all methods on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU with batch size 1 and 518 ×392 frames. The latency of a frame covers the full step from the image to the outputs of all heads and is measured with CUDA events; memory is the peak allocated GPU memory.

The routing of Equation (2.4) ranks the past frames with the register tokens alone. We compare it with an oracle that ranks them by the attention mass that all tokens of the new frame give to all cached tokens of a past frame, and that selects the k = 3 frames with the most mass. Setup. We run StreamVGGT in streaming mode on clips of 16 frames from BlendedMVS and CO3Dv2 and record the attention of the last frame in all 16 heads of every global layer. G Limitations 

Our distillation transfers the causal attention of StreamVGGT to Mamba2 layers at a small fraction of the original training cost, but it does not fully remove the drift of a recurrent state on long streams. Sparse attention recovers part of the lost history, yet the Mamba2 state still compresses hundreds of frames into a fixed size, and we train on clips far shorter than the streams we test on. Camera pose is also less accurate than some state-of-the-art methods. We therefore see the training recipe itself as the path forward: because a new architecture can be distilled in 9% of the GPU hours that StreamVGGT needs, future work can iterate quickly over recurrent designs, memory mechanisms and longer training clips, and arrive at models that stay accurate over long streams. 31
