Abstract 

In-Hand Object Scanning (IHOS) using commodity monocular smartphone cameras holds immense po-tential to democratize 3D digitization for non-expert users, accelerating applications in digital archiving, augmented reality, and custom fabrication. Yet, IHOS remains largely understudied due to a lack of re-alistic datasets and systematic benchmarks. To bridge this gap, we introduce Toys R Us , a high-quality dataset featuring diverse figurines captured in 4K UHD video using standard RGB smartphone cameras. Analyzing monocular IHOS from first principles, we examine structure-from-motion (SfM) for camera pose estimation, which is key to IHOS. We identify two failure modes: small baselines between consec-utive frames, and sparse feature matches across long-range frames exacerbated by dynamic hand occlu-sions. Guided by these insights, we propose a robust strategy that operates SfM on video sub-sequences sampled at sufficient angular intervals, and iteratively stitches the corresponding sub-trajectories into a unified global trajectory. Finally, for high-fidelity surface reconstruction, we introduce occlusion-aware 2D Gaussian Splatting which incorporates opacity rendering and explicitly mask hand regions in loss computation. Quantitative and qualitative evaluations demonstrate that our framework significantly outperforms existing approaches, including neural implicit representations and 3D foundation models, in both geometric accuracy and photometric fidelity. a video of an object toyed with by hand

# 1 Introduction 

The widespread availability of smartphones offers an accessible avenue for democratizing 3D capture via In-Hand Object Scanning (IHOS), where a user “ toys ”1 with an object in front of a handheld camera (Fig. 1). Given an unconstrained video of a toyed object, IHOS methods aim to reconstruct a complete, colored 3D surface model. This capability enables diverse applications, such as digitization of cultural heritage [53] and

1arXiv:2611.33824v1 [cs.CV] 25 Nov 2026 artworks [9], custom digital fabrication and 3D printing [44, 4, 7, 6, 2, 3], and personal item retrieval in assistive robots and AR/VR wearables [71, 41, 51, 42, 50]. IHOS has drawn growing attention [24, 63, 20, 72, 26]; however, existing research remains constrained by synthetic or overly simplified capture setups and limited object variety. For example, most works rely on repurposed datasets like HOPE [56] and HO3D [19], which are originally curated for robotic manipulation and 6DoF pose estimation. These datasets contain low-resolution videos of common household items with simple geometries, such as milk cartons and pitchers. Consequently, they fail to satisfy the increasing demand for high-fidelity digitization and fabrication of complex, customized objects, particularly in an era where modern smartphones routinely capture 4K UHD videos. Using such datasets ultimately bottleneck methodology progress in IHOS. To address these limitations, we introduce a novel benchmark dataset called 

Toys R Us , which features 4K UHD videos of diverse figurines with complex, intricate geometries. The key to IHOS lies in accurately estimating per-frame camera poses, which serve as a fundamental prerequisite for surface reconstruction methods such as Marching Cubes [35], Poisson surface reconstruction [28], signed distance function (SDF) learning [39, 37, 60], and Gaussian Splatting [30, 23]. However, dynamic hand occlusions (Fig. 1) severely complicate pose estimation [20, 54, 26], causing traditional pipelines such as Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [47] to fail. To address this issue, some approaches [24, 25, 54] constrain firm grasp and jointly estimate the hand and object pose. However, maintaining static grip permanently occludes certain surface regions and leave them unable to be reliably reconstructed. Alternatively, recent works [26, 12] use generative AI agents to produce 3D object proxies and use them to assist in camera pose estimation and surface reconstruction. While effective for common objects, generating proxies fail on complex or customized items (Fig. 6), significantly limiting their generalizability. With the new dataset Toys R Us , we investigate IHOS from first principles by examining SfM for camera pose estimation. We identify two failure modes: small baselines between consecutive frames [40, 58, 8, 5], and insufficient feature matches between long-range frames. We address these issues with a robust iterative strategy, termed CamStich , that performs SfM on video subsequences sampled at adequate angular intervals, and then stitches the corresponding sub-trajectories into a coherent global trajectory. We further introduce occlusion-aware 2D Gaussian Splatting ( OA-2DGS ) to handle severe dynamic hand occlusions in colored surface reconstruction. The developed methods lead to a simple pipeline for IHOS and constitute our technical insights and novelty. We extensively evaluate our IHOS pipeline against existing approaches that exploit SDF learning for neural implicit surface reconstruction, and 3D foundation models. Experiments demonstrate that ours significantly outperforms these approaches in terms of both geometric accuracy and photometric fidelity. To summarize, we make three key contributions: 1. We release a new dataset, Toys R Us , featuring cellphone-captured 4K UHD videos of diverse figurines with complex geometries and fostering IHOS research. 2. We rigorously analyze SfM from first principles on the 4K UHD videos, revealing two issues caused by small baselines between consecutive frames and insufficient feature matches between long-range frames. 3. We address the above issues with an iterative SfM and develop occlusion-aware 2D Gaussian Splatting for surface reconstruction. Our two-step method significantly outperforms existing methods for IHOS. 

# 2 Related Works 

Camera pose estimation for IHOS is a crucial step and can intuitively be addressed by SfM. However, prior work points out that SfM fails under severe dynamic hand occlusions [20, 54, 72]. To mitigate this issue, some approaches leverage depth sensors [63, 25] to enhance keypoint matching, which limits their applicability to monocular setups. Others [24, 10, 22, 68, 15, 54] require a firm grasp and estimate hand pose to facilitate camera tracking; but static grasps permanently occlude certain surface regions, preventing reliable reconstruction. Recent methods [26, 12] employ generative AI agents to produce 3D proxies to aid pose estimation, yet they fail on customized objects with complex geometries. From first principles, we re-examine SfM for camera pose estimation on IHOS videos and identify its failure modes. We address these 2issues by introducing an iterative SfM strategy, demonstrating its effectiveness in achieving robust camera pose estimation even under severe dynamic hand occlusions. 

Colored surface reconstruction for IHOS , given estimated camera poses, can be achieved by a conventional pipeline that first produces sparse 3D points triangulated from image features, then generates dense point clouds using Multi-View Stereo (MVS) [48], and lastly extracts surfaces via Martching Cubes [35] or screened Poisson surface reconstruction [27]. However, sparse 3D points often lack sufficient structural information for reliable reconstruction. To address this, recent methods learn neural implicit functions to represent surfaces as the zero-level set of a signed distance function (SDF) [72, 24, 63]. These methods include IDR [66], NeuS [60], VolSDF [67], UNISURF [39], and PET-NeuS [61]. While effective, optimizing an implicit function per object is time-consuming. Alternatively, 2D and 3D Gaussian Splatting (GS) [30, 17, 23] represent 3D scenes with Gaussian primitives and utilize explicit ray-splat intersection to achieve high-quality, real-time surface reconstruction. Yet, GS methods require accurate and dense view-dependent camera poses that are difficult to obtain from IHOS videos, leaving them unexploited for this task. Additionally, feed-forward modelsl like ForeHOI [12] train on large-scale synthetic datasets for efficient mesh prediction but generalize poorly to novel, complex geometries. To overcome these limitations, we present occlusion-aware 2DGS (OA-2DGS) by exploiting a simple loss term to handle dynamic occlusions in 2DGS, achieving high computational efficiency and high-quality surface reconstruction. 

Datasets for IHOS research . Existing IHOS research largely repurposes datasets like HOPE [56] and HO3D [19], which were originally designed for robotic manipulation and object-hand pose estimation. Consequently, methods developed on these datasets are biased toward tracking interaction dynamics [10, 22, 68, 15, 54] rather than recovering colored surfaces, a task demanding high geometric accuracy and photometric fidelity. As summarized in Table 1, legacy datasets exhibit severe limitations: they feature low-resolution video instead of HD video, generic rather than customized objects, constrained firm grasps instead of natural, user-friendly toying manipulation, and static camera setups rather than convenient hand-held captures. Such characteristics fail to meet contemporary standards and the growing need for customized 3D capture and fabrication. To bridge this gap and advance IHOS research, we introduce the Toys R Us 

dataset as a core contribution. 

Structure from Motion (SfM) reconstructs 3D geometry and camera poses from multi-view im-ages [47, 48] through feature extraction [36], feature matching [21], relative camera pose estimation [49], outlier filtering [16], and bundle adjustment [55]. Toolboxes like COLMAP [47, 48] integrate these compo-nents to support diverse 3D tasks, while individual modules have been further advanced by learned feature extractors (e.g., SuperPoint [13]) and matchers (e.g., SuperGlue [46] and LightGlue [33]). Beyond the mod-ular step-wise SfM pipeline, recent 3D foundation models directly produce 3D reconstructions from 2D images, including VGGT [59], MASt3R-SfM [14], and MapAnything [29]. Crucially, training these models relies on massive camera-pose annotations typically generated by COLMAP as pseudo ground truth. Yet, 3D foundation models have not been explored for IHOS, and we observe that they perform poorly in this setting. Consequently, we re-examine SfM for camera pose estimation in IHOS videos featuring severe dy-namic hand occlusions, identify its failure modes, and propose targeted solutions that constitute our core technical novelty.

## 3.3 Evaluation Protocol 

Metrics. We assess methods using two types of metrics: 

• Geometric metrics evaluate the geometric accuracy [38] by comparing the reconstructed surface with the ground truth. We adopt the Chamfer Distance, Accuracy (the ratio of points sampled from the recon-structed surface that are true positives), Completeness (the ratio of points sampled from the ground-truth surface recalled by the reconstruction), and F1 score (the harmonic mean of Accuracy and Completeness). 

• Photometric metrics focus on the reconstructed appearance. For a given camera pose, we render images from the ground-truth and the reconstructed colored surface, respectively, and compare them using the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), the structural similarity index measure (SSIM) [62] and the LPIPS distance [70].

# 4 First-Principles Methodology Development 

We approach IHOS from first principles by re-examining SfM for camera pose estimation: why it struggles on IHOS videos ( §4.1), and how to make it work ( §4.2 and §4.3). We further present occlusion-aware 2DGS to reconstruct colored surface ( §4.4). Fig. 4 illustrates our final method. 

## 4.1 Revisiting SfM Applied to IHOS Videos 

Existing works [26, 20] point out the brittleness of SfM when applied to IHOS videos due to severe hand occlusions and dynamic object movements relative to the background scene. A straightforward idea to address these issues is masking hand-associated pixels and background in each frame, focusing only on the target object. Today’s vision foundation models make these easily achievable. We leverage the egocentric hand segmentation model [69] and SAM2 [43] to detect, segment and track hands and foreground object, and derive backgrounds in all video frames. We execute SfM over the target object of the video frames with COLMAP. Toward an in-depth analysis, we have extensively tuned hyperparameters with COLMAP and observe a dilemma (Fig. 3) related to the minimum triangulation angle when adding frames: (1) a small minimum angle allows SfM to exploit large amounts of frames but produces erroneous depth estimates and eventually yields camera trajectory drift (Fig. 3A), and (2) a large angle produces insufficient amounts of feature matches and thus makes SfM terminate early without exploiting enough frames (Fig. 3B). 

## 4.2 SfM on Subsequences of Video Frames 

Motivated by the above dilemma, we develop an iterative variant of SfM, described as below. 

An iterative execution of SfM. Setting a proper minimum triangulation angle mitigates camera trajectory drift (Fig. 3A) but SfM terminates when insufficient feature matches are found between the exploited and remaining frames (Fig. 3B). However, the remaining frames can still support another execution of SfM. Hence, we propose to continually execute SfM over remaining frames. Denote the set of remaining frames at iteration k as Uk; U1 is the initial set of all frames. Let Ek and Pk denote the sets of exploited frames and their corresponding camera poses estimated by SfM, respectively. The iterative SfM repeats the procedure: Pk, Ek, Uk+1 = SfM( Uk), where Uk = Ek ∪ U k+1 . The iteration terminates when no remaining frames can be exploited by SfM. After each execution, SfM yields a sub-trajectory of camera poses and a set of 3D points (Fig. 5 and Fig. 4), leaving a problem of stitching them into a global trajectory. Remarks. The above two variants of SfM have respective advantages. The iterative version requires a pre-defined minimum triangulation angle, allowing SfM to iteratively make use of all frames. In contrast, the parallel version allows parallel processing and hence accelerated procession compared to the iterative one. However, it relies on a proper sampling interval as another hyperparameter, setting which depends on the object motion speed. For example, if a 30-second video at 60 fps (1,800 frames) captures two full rotations (720 ◦), sampling at every 5 frames amounts to an angular displacement of ∼2◦ between consecutive sampled frames. This is a good triangulation angle for SfM in the COLMAP toolbox. In fact, we find that our final method Castoags is robust to different sampling intervals (refer to Suppl. §B). Hence, we apply the parallel variant of SfM with sampling interval 5 in this work.

## 4.4 Occlusion-Aware Surface Reconstruction 

The recovered global trajectory and point cloud enables surface reconstruction with various methods such as the classic methods Delaunay [32] and Poisson [27]. Beyond these traditional methods, we propose occlusion-aware 2D Gaussian Splatting (OA-2DGS) for surface reconstruction. Note that 2DGS [23] excels in surface reconstruction for static scenes but does not handle dynamic occlusions (caused by hand in IHOS). It uses 7a reconstruction loss term between a frame I and its rendered frame ˆI. We enhance it by adding a loss term to handle dynamic hand occlusions. Recall that, for each frame I, we derive binary masks for hand, foreground object, and background (Fig. 4). For this frame, we define its target opacity A as the foreground mask, i.e., setting the foreground to 1 and elsewhere to 0. We design an L1 term between A and the 2DGS rendered opacity ˆA. Within this term, we further apply a per-pixel weight W to ignore any reconstructions on the hand regions. The weight W is the complement of the hand mask, with hand regions set to 0 and elsewhere to 1. Collectively, our designed term is ∥W ⊙ ( ˆA − A)∥1, which focuses on reconstructing the foreground object without being distracted by the background and hand regions and does not compute opacity predictions on hand regions. Below is our final loss OA-2DGS:

Lc removes the effect of dynamic hand, helps learning opacity pertaining to the foreground object, and enables the follow-up Marching Cubes to produce clean and smooth surface. To train 2DGS, we use Lc along with standard regularizers, including the distance between the ray-splat intersections and splats’ normal with the gradients of the depth maps. We name this method as Occlusion-Aware 2D Gaussian Splatting ( OA-2DGS ).

## 5.1 Benchmarking Results 

Table 2 and Figures 6 and 7 list numeric metrics and visual comparisons of all the methods. Our Castoags significantly outperforms the compared methods. Somewhat surprisingly, methods based on 3D foundation models (e.g., VGGT and MAtCha) achieve poor performance, an observation aligning with [12] which reports that such models fail to provide a good SfM initialization in face of dynamic occlusions. Dynhor and ForeHOI, specifically designed for IHOS, struggle on Toys R Us . Their failures are rooted in a common limitation: both propose producing 3D priors using generative AI agents which do not generalize to custom, novel objects as featured in Toys R Us .Notably, our Castoags takes only 0.5 hours for surface reconstruction per figurine (Table 2), whereas the runner-up method Color-NeuS takes 12 hours and Dynhor [26] takes 9 hours. Moreover, methods based on 3D foundation models are much faster owing to their feed-forward mechanisms: for camera pose estimation, MapAnything and VGGT takes seconds and MASt3R-SfM takes 48 minutes; they further take 0.2 hours to reconstruct surfaces using OA-2DGS. MAtCha takes 0.3 hours to directly output reconstructed surfaces, and ForeHOI is even faster that outputs surfaces in 0.1 hours. However, these methods yield poor quality of reconstructed surfaces, since they have not learned similar complex geometries as the custom figurines and hence have limited generalizability.

We advance the research of In-Hand Object Scanning (IHOS) by first introducing a new dataset Toys R Us ,featuring 4K UHD videos of diverse figurines that have complex geometries. Since the key challenge for IHOS is accurately estimating camera poses in face of severe dynamic hand occlusions, we rigorously analyze the well-established Structure-from-Motion (SfM) technique and explain its failure on IHOS videos: small baselines between consecutive frames, and insufficient feature matches between long-range frames exacer-bated by the hand occlusions. To address these issues, we propose a simple strategy, called CamStitch, which operates SfM on subsequences of frames sampled at appropriate temporal intervals and stitch the resulting sub-trajectories of estimated camera poses into a global one. With reliable camera pose trajectory produced by CamStitch, we further develop occlusion-aware 2D Gaussian Splatting (OA-2DGS) for colored surface re-construction. OA-2DGS adopts an opacity loss term, focuses on foreground regions in the reconstruction loss term, and explicitly masks hand regions in loss computation. Combining CamStitch and OA-2DGS achieves significantly better surface reconstruction results than existing IHOS approaches w.r.t both geometric and photometric metrics.

Datasets like HOPE [56] only release a limited subset of sparsely sampled frames (e.g., ∼300) rather than the full continuous video. This inherent data sparsity causes a significant gap compared to data collection in real-world scenarios. Consequently, when methods repurpose such datasets for the IHOS task (e.g., Dynhor [26]), they are forced to utilize 3D proxy generation to compensate for the lack of sufficient video frames and object information. However, this proxy-based reliance ultimately fails to reconstruct the authentic geometric shapes of uncommon objects. Notably, our Toys R Us is explicitly tailored for IHOS. Failure Case Analysis. Our method also encounters challenges, as the Toys R Us dataset contains di-verse customized objects across various materials, colors, and geometries. We visualize several representative failure cases in Fig. 14 to illustrate these limitations and present future directions. In practice, smooth sur-faces and transparent materials frequently co-occur, causing specular reflections under changing illumination, where highlight spots (indicated by red arrows in Fig. 14) obscure the true colors of the objects. Single-color textures lack trackable features, hindering feature detection and matching in CamStitch. # F Quality Examination via 3D Printing 

While our proposed evaluation protocol does not require physical fabrication to assess surface reconstruction quality, we perform 3D printing to validate the practical utility of our IHOS method for custom fabrication. Fig. 15 illustrates the 3D printing workflow. Below, we detail specific steps involved in our commodity desktop 3D printing pipeline. We also include a video named printing.mp4 in the supplementary materials that records the printing process. 

Full color printing. We fabricate the objects using a Bambu Lab multi-color printer equipped with the Automatic Material System (AMS). To support Bambu Studio slicer, we save the reconstructed colored mesh in .stl format, a universal file format for 3D printing. Remarks. Executing this workflow yields several insights. First, the reconstructed surfaces provide a solid foundation for fabrication, yet additional manual intervention is required to make them print-ready. For example, we manually repair specific mesh regions to ensure watertight geometry necessary for successful 3D printing. Second, achieving finer color segmentation is difficult. Manually selecting detailed color regions necessitates frequent filament exchanges. This results in significant material waste due to purging, nozzle clearance, and refilling cycles.
