Abstract 

Fine-grained visual understanding requires multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to recognize small visual entities and understand their relationships. Scene graphs offer a natural way to represent this information, orga-nizing visual entities as nodes and their relationships as edges. However, we identify two challenges: existing scene graph benchmarks provide insufficient coverage of fine-grained entities and the relationships among them, and ex-isting methods for fine-grained visual understanding have yet to effectively leverage such graph-structured informa-tion in MLLMs. To address these challenges, we intro-duce Fine-Grained Multimodal Scene Graph Benchmark (FG-GraphBench) and FiGRA. FG-GraphBench is a bench-mark comprising three complementary tasks: fine-grained visual question answering (FG-QA), image captioning (FG-Caption), and image description generation (FG-Description). We develop a two-stage construction pipeline that discovers and grounds fine-grained entities and estab-lishes their relations to construct the fine-grained multi-modal scene graphs (MSGs) in FG-GraphBench. Building on these MSGs, we propose FiGRA, a Fi ne-grained Graph 

Representation Augmentation method for MLLMs. FiGRA adopts a two-stage training strategy. It encodes multimodal entity and relation features as continuous graph tokens, and integrates them into the MLLM. Experiments show that Fi-GRA significantly outperforms strong baselines across three tasks of FG-GraphBench. Further analyses confirm the effectiveness of FG-GraphBench and highlight the impor-tance of preserving fine-grained entity and relation infor-mation for visual understanding. 

1. Introduction 

Recent advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have led to remarkable progress in a wide range

of vision-language tasks [6, 27, 28, 43]. However, many real-world tasks require fine-grained visual understanding. Current MLLMs often struggle to recognize small objects and understand the relationships among them [1, 26, 41, 47, 56]. As a result, they may overlook visual details and pro-duce hallucinations [24]. A promising way to address these limitations is to in-troduce explicit structural priors into visual understanding. Scene graphs organize visual entities as nodes and their re-lationships as edges [16, 21, 48]. Their nodes provide di-rect object-level cues for recognizing fine-grained objects, while their edges provide explicit relation-level cues that MLLMs would otherwise need to infer from image [42]. However, realizing these benefits in MLLMs presents two key challenges. Challenge 1: How can we obtain scene graphs that are sufficiently fine-grained to pro-vide the object-level and relation-level cues needed for fine-grained visual understanding? Existing scene-graph-based benchmarks were largely developed for conventional scene understanding and typically emphasize relatively salient objects, while fine-grained entities and their rela-tionships remain insufficiently covered [16, 21]. Moreover, these benchmarks are not specifically designed to evaluate fine-grained visual understanding [11, 37, 58]. 

Challenge 2: How can we effectively incorporate such fine-grained graph information into MLLMs so that it en-hances both fine-grained object perception and relation understanding? Several recent approaches to fine-grained visual understanding improve access to local visual evi-dence through visual cropping [18, 56], hierarchical zoom-ing [41], patch-wise image decomposition [35], or high-resolution encoding [15, 26]. While these strategies can en-hance the perception of small or local visual details, they generally treat fine-grained information as visual regions or patches rather than explicitly modeling the structured de-pendencies among entities. Conversely, prior graph-based approaches have explored structured representations for vi-sual reasoning [42, 46], but were not designed to support fine-grained visual understanding in MLLMs. 1arXiv:2611.25408v1 [cs.LG] 19 Nov 2026 To address Challenge 1 , we introduce FG-GraphBench, a benchmark for fine-grained visual understanding with fine-grained MSGs. FG-GraphBench comprises three com-plementary tasks: FG-QA for fine-grained visual ques-tion answering, FG-Caption for fine-grained image caption-ing, and FG-Description for fine-grained image descrip-tion generation. Together, these tasks assess MLLMs’ fine-grained entity recognition and relational reasoning capabil-ities. In particular, FG-QA places greater emphasis on rela-tional reasoning, whereas FG-Caption and FG-Description require models to recognize fine-grained entities and cap-ture their relationships in generated text. We construct FG-GraphBench through a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 enriches the source entity set through vocabulary-based grounding to localize source entities and detect additional instances, fol-lowed by two rounds of discovery using a vision-language model (VLM) to identify new fine-grained entities, with two-stage filtering to remove unreliable entities. Stage 2 predicts holistic visual relations and constructs dependency relations using a dependency candidate tree, integrating the resulting relations with the enriched entity set to form the final fine-grained MSGs. To address Challenge 2 , we propose FiGRA , a Fi ne-grained Graph Representation Augmentation method for MLLMs. It encodes MSGs into continuous graph tokens and injects them into the MLLM alongside visual inputs. FiGRA first performs multimodal graph encoding to ob-tain entity and relation representations, then projects them into continuous graph tokens and integrates them into the MLLM. It adopts a two-stage training strategy, consisting of a pre-training stage with a frozen MLLM and a super-vised fine-tuning stage for downstream tasks. In summary, our key contributions are as follows: (1) New Perspective. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to explore fine-grained MSGs as a unified struc-tural representation for enhancing fine-grained visual un-derstanding in MLLMs. (2) New Benchmark and Data Construction Pipeline. We develop a two-stage pipeline to construct fine-grained MSGs and build FG-GraphBench, comprising FG-QA, FG-Caption, and FG-Description. (3) New Approach. We propose FiGRA, which encodes mul-timodal entity and relation features into continuous graph tokens and integrates them into MLLMs through two-stage training. (4) SOTA Performance. FiGRA achieves the best results on the primary evaluation metrics among evaluated baselines across all three tasks. 

2. Related works 

Fine-Grained Visual Understanding in MLLMs. Re-cent work improves fine-grained visual understanding in MLLMs through high-resolution image encoding and adap-tive visual search, enabling models to access local vi-sual details more effectively [13, 25, 41, 56]. Beyond vi-sual perception, region-level understanding has been ex-plored through referring, grounding, segmentation, and open-world visual recognition [12, 23, 40, 44, 50, 54]. For image description, existing methods enhance local visual details or generate region-level captions at different granu-larities [15, 26, 35]. 

Scene Graphs for Visual Understanding. Scene graphs represent objects, attributes, and their relations [16, 21]. Existing fine-grained scene graph generation meth-ods distinguish semantically similar predicates and expand predicate vocabularies [30, 51, 52]. There are also stud-ies that address challenges in relation learning and annota-tion quality in scene graph generation [19, 32, 57]. Beyond scene graph generation, scene graphs have also been used for explicit visual reasoning [42, 46, 55]. 

3. Data construction pipeline and FG-GraphBench 

3.1. Benchmark overview 

Existing scene graph benchmarks provide insufficient an-notations of fine-grained objects and their relationships. To address this limitation, we develop a unified data construc-tion pipeline for discovering fine-grained objects and con-structing their relationships, and use this pipeline to build FG-GraphBench, a fine-grained multimodal scene-graph benchmark. As illustrated in Fig. 1, our construction pipeline consists of two stages: Stage 1 discovers and verifies fine-grained objects, while Stage 2 constructs their relationships. 

3.2. Stage 1: fine-grained entity detection and veri-fication 

Stage 1 constructs a unified entity set that covers both small independent objects and fine-grained entities asso-ciated with larger objects. We begin with a scene graph, which provides source entities (with bounding boxes) and annotated relations. Complete implementation details are provided in Sec. B of Supplementary Material. 

Initial vocabulary-based grounding. We first retain source entities and use their bounding boxes with SAM [20] to obtain pixel-level masks. We then query Ground-ingDINO [29] with source entity names to detect additional instances matching these names, and segment the resulting regions with SAM. 

Two Round VLM Fine-grained Entity Discovery. In-spired by Ramanathan et al. [39] and Pan et al. [33], we con-sider fine-grained visual entities to manifest in two forms: standalone entities and object-dependent entities. The for-mer refer to independent fine-grained entities in the scene. The latter are visually associated with larger entities via de-pendency relations (e.g., part of and attached to). This dis-tinction motivates a two-round discovery strategy designed 2Images

In the first round, the VLM (Qwen3.7-Plus) receives the image and a simplified source scene graph, selects source entities as visual anchors, and searches around them for fine-grained components. In the second round, it additionally takes the first-round discoveries as context and rescans the full image for standalone entities. The VLM outputs are subsequently used for grounding with GroundingDINO and segmentation with SAM, followed by geometric and semantic verification before inclusion in the final entity set. 

Two-stage filtering. We merge candidates from vocabulary-based grounding and two-round VLM discov-ery. We apply two filtering stages. First, we apply geomet-ric filtering to merge overlapping candidates with the same name and remove spatially inconsistent candidate, while preserving the original source entities. Second, we use Qwen2-VL and LLaVA for semantic filtering. For highly overlapping candidates with different names, the models se-lect the name that best matches the region. We also exam-ine candidates with low grounding confidence to determine whether the current name matches the corresponding mask. If the two models do not both confirm the current name, a new name is generated. We then run GroundingDINO and SAM again and keep the new name only when the resulting mask is consistent with the original region (see Sec. B.3). 

3.3. Stage 2: Relation construction 

Stage 1 expands the source entity set with newly discovered fine-grained entities, and Stage 2 infers relations involving them. These include general visual relations (e.g., next to 

and riding ) that capture relationships among entities in the broader scene, and dependency relations (e.g., a button on 

a shirt) that characterize how fine-grained entities are as-sociated with larger objects. We therefore construct rela-tions through two pathways: Holistic Relation Construc-tion and Dependency Relation Construction with the De-pendency Candidate Tree . See Sec. C for details. 

3.3.1. Holistic relation construction 

ROBIN [34] is a multimodal language model trained for dense scene graph generation, capable of predicting visual relationships among specified entities. We employ ROBIN to construct relations across all entities. Given the image and all grounded entity regions, ROBIN predicts their re-lations in the context of the full scene. Since relations be-tween original entities are already provided by the source annotations, we retain only ROBIN predictions involving at 3least one newly discovered entity. 

3.3.2. Dependency relation construction with the depen-dency candidate tree Dependency Candidate Tree Construction. We first use Depth Anything V2 [49] to estimate a dense depth map for each image. For each target entity, we identify potential parent entities whose masks substantially cover the target and exhibit consistent local depths across the target’s mask boundary. We score each candidate connection using mask coverage, local depth consistency, and an area-ratio penalty that discourages excessively large parents. We then add a virtual root with zero-weight connections to all entities and apply the Chu–Liu/Edmonds algorithm [5, 9] to obtain a maximum spanning arborescence. Removing the virtual root yields the Dependency Candidate Tree , in which en-tities without a suitable parent remain independent. 

Dependency Relation Prediction. For each edge in the Dependency Candidate Tree involving at least one newly discovered entity, we provide Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct [43] with the image and the names of the two connected entities. The model predicts a dependency relation (e.g., has, con-tains) to form a directed relation triplet. These dependency relations are integrated with the original relations and holis-tic relation predictions to construct the final fine-grained MSG (see Sec. C.2). 

3.4. FG-GraphBench 

Using the above pipeline, we construct FG-GraphBench with three datasets for complementary tasks. Built from GQA dataset [16], FG-QA evaluates fine-grained relational question answering. Built from Action Genome [17], FG-Description evaluates detailed image description. Built from MS COCO Captions dataset [4], FG-Caption evalu-ates concise image caption generation with fine-grained de-tails. Each sample in FG-GraphBench consists of an image, its corresponding MSG, a question, and the associated text response. We use Qwen3.7-Plus to construct fine-grained questions and corresponding responses (see Sec. A.7). Within FG-QA, we categorize questions into four types: 

queryRel for identifying an entity through its relation to an-other entity, verifyRel for determining whether a specified relation holds, existRel for asking whether an entity satis-fying given relational constraints exists, and chooseRel for selecting the correct relation from candidate options. Ta-ble 1 summarizes statistics for FG-GraphBench. We further conduct a data quality check to assess the reliability of the constructed MSGs. The results demon-strate high annotation accuracy, achieving 88.0%–90.4% entity accuracy and 83.4%–86.0% relation accuracy across the three datasets (see Sec. A.6). More detailed information about the datasets is provided in Sec. A.

5. Experiments 

In this section, we conduct various experiments to verify the effectiveness of FiGRA and FG-GraphBench, focusing on following questions: Q1 : How does FiGRA perform across the three fine-grained visual understanding tasks? Q2 : Do FiGRA’s modules and two-stage training strategy contribute to its performance? Q3 : Does FG-GraphBench supply use-ful fine-grained visual information? Q4 : How do different methods of incorporating graph information into MLLMs affect model performance and computational cost? 

5.1. Experimental settings 

Baselines. We compare FiGRA with two groups of base-lines: (1) Open-source MLLMs : including LLaVA-1.5-7B [28], Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct [43], ALLaVA-3B [2], Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct [10], and InternVL3.5-8B [45]. (2) Specialized Methods for Fine-Grained Vi-sual Understanding : including fine-grained visual question answering (VQA) methods (ViCrop [18], Zoom Eye [41], SEAL [47], and PinPoint [22]), and fine-grained image captioning methods (Patch Matters [35], IT [36], Synthe-sized [7], SC-Captioner [53], and ShareGPT4V-7B [3]). We fine-tune open-source MLLMs with LoRA and adapt spe-cialized methods following their respective protocols. Im-plementation details are provided in Sec. E. 

Metrics. For FG-Description and FG-Caption, we report BLEU scores, CIDEr, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and CLIP-Score. We use Entity-Acc and CAPTURE [7] to evalu-ate fine-grained visual content in generated descriptions. Entity-Acc measures scene graph entity coverage as the pro-portion of unique entity names in each graph appearing in the generated description. CAPTURE evaluates the con-sistency of objects, attributes, and relations extracted from generated descriptions and reference captions. For FG-QA, we report overall and per-type accuracy, WUPS [31], and Macro-F1. 

5.2. Overall performance 

To answer Q1 , we compare FiGRA with open-source MLLMs and specialized methods on all three FG-GraphBench tasks, with results reported in Tables 2 to 4. As shown in Tables 2 and 4, FiGRA achieves consis-tently strong performance across detailed image descrip-tion generation and image captioning tasks. These results demonstrate FiGRA’s ability to capture fine-grained visual details in both concise captions and detailed descriptions. As shown in Table 3, FiGRA achieves 59.59% over-all accuracy on FG-QA. It outperforms the strongest base-line (PinPoint (Qwen)) by 9.32 percentage points. It also achieves the highest accuracy across all four question types. We attribute these gains to question-relevant subgraph re-trieval and graph encoding, which provide the MLLM with relevant entities and their visual and semantic relationships. We also provide a qualitative case study comparing Fi-GRA with LLaVA-1.5-7B on FG-GraphBench in Sec. F. 

5.3. FiGRA ablation study 

To answer Q2 , we evaluate four key components of FiGRA. Results are reported in Table 5. 

(1) w/o Shortest-Path Prediction. We remove shortest-path prediction from pre-training while retaining feature and topology reconstruction. Performance drops across all three benchmarks. This confirms that shortest-path super-vision provides relational signals beyond feature and topol-ogy reconstruction. (2) w/o Pre-training. We remove pre-training stage and train randomly initialized graph mod-ules only during supervised fine-tuning. Performance drops across all three benchmarks. This confirms the impor-tance of pre-training for learning effective entity and re-lation representations. (3) w/o Subgraph Retrieval. We replace question-relevant subgraph retrieval with the com-plete MSG for FG-QA and FG-Caption. FG-QA accuracy drops from 59.59% to 53.09%. This highlights the impor-tance of selecting relevant entities and relations for question answering. (4) w/o Graph Encoder. We remove the Graph Encoder and directly apply graph-to-sequence transforma-tion to the fused entity and relation features. Performance drops across all three benchmarks. This confirms the impor-tance of graph message passing for incorporating relational context into entity representations.

Computational Cost. We report average input tokens, training time per epoch, and inference time. All methods use the same graph retrieval strategy. As shown in Figure 4 and Table 6, Graph Lineariza-tion causes only marginal performance drops but incurs the highest computational costs. This suggests that MLLMs can effectively interpret graph information in text form, but this representation achieves slightly lower performance while requiring more computational resources. In con-trast, Compressed Graph Token achieves the lowest com-putational costs but suffers substantial performance degra-dation, particularly on FG-QA, suggesting that mean pool-ing discards fine-grained entity and relation information. These results reveal a trade-off between computational effi-ciency and fine-grained visual understanding. Our method achieves a better balance between the two. 

6. Conclusion 

To address the limited coverage of fine-grained entities and their relationships in existing scene graph benchmarks, in this paper, we introduced FG-GraphBench, a benchmark for fine-grained visual understanding across three comple-mentary tasks with fine-grained MSGs. We developed a two-stage data construction pipeline to construct the fine-grained MSGs in FG-GraphBench by discovering fine-grained visual entities and establishing semantic and de-pendency relations among them. To address the challenge 8of effectively incorporating fine-grained graph information into MLLMs, we proposed FiGRA, which integrates mul-timodal graph representations into MLLMs through contin-uous graph tokens and a two-stage training strategy. Ex-periments show that FiGRA outperforms existing meth-ods across all three fine-grained visual understanding tasks. Further analyses highlight the importance of explicitly mod-eling fine-grained entities and their relationships in MLLMs for fine-grained visual understanding.

A. Dataset Information 

A.1. Dataset Scale 

Action Genome (AG) [17]. Action Genome is a large-scale video dataset built on Charades to represent human-object activities as spatio-temporal scene graphs. Its annotations localize the acting person and the objects involved in each action with bounding boxes, and describe their interactions through attention, spatial, and contact relations. The annotation protocol uniformly samples five frames from each action interval, providing frame-level graphs that capture how interactions evolve over time. For our FG-Description, we select one representative frame per video, corresponding to the frame with the largest number of visible annotated objects. The scene graph annotations associated with these frames serve as the original scene graphs. 

MS COCO Captions [4]. MS COCO Captions is an image-captioning benchmark built on the MS COCO image collec-tion, which was designed to include multiple objects in natural scene contexts. The caption dataset itself does not contain scene-graph annotations. Therefore, for FG-Caption, we associate each COCO image with its overlapping GQA image and reuse the corresponding GQA scene graph, including objects, bounding boxes, and relations. 

GQA [16]. GQA is a compositional visual question answering dataset built upon Visual Genome scene graphs covering approximately 113K real-world images [21]. Each graph represents visible objects as nodes, with bounding boxes and typically one to three attributes, and connects them through semantic relations describing actions, spatial configurations, and comparisons. For FG-QA, we exclude GQA images that overlap with MS COCO Captions and retain only the scene graphs associated with the remaining images as our original scene graphs. A.7. Question Construction 

FG-QA question construction. Inspired by the relational question templates in GQA [16], we generate four types of questions from graph relations: queryRel asks for an entity connected to another entity by a specified relation, verifyRel verifies whether a specified relation holds, existRel checks whether an entity satisfying given relational constraints exists, and chooseRel selects the correct relation from two candidate spatial relations. The first three types can involve multi-hop entity references, whereas chooseRel focuses on direct relations between two entities. We construct entity references from graph paths and filter candidates based on path validity, reasoning depth, and the relative mask areas of the involved entities. Negative examples for verifyRel and existRel are generated by modifying entities or relations to produce statements unsupported by the graph. For each image, we first sample a question type using weights of 0.60, 0.15, 0.15, and 0.10 for queryRel, verifyRel, existRel, and chooseRel, respectively. We then retain up to five candidates of the selected type, prioritizing longer reasoning paths and higher mask area scores. Finally, we use Qwen3.7-Plus to revise questions that are grammatically incorrect or unnatural, while preserving their original meaning. For questions that require an entity or relation as the answer, the VLM also proposes alternative answers with equivalent meanings. The ground-truth answer remains determined by the verified graph structure rather than generated by the VLM. 

FG-Description question construction. For FG-Description, we formulate question construction as a detailed image description task. We provide the Qwen3.7-Plus with the image. We generate three complementary descriptions for each sample using different instructions. The first produces a faithful overall account, the second emphasizes visible fine-grained details, and the third focuses on spatial layout, background, and scene context. All variants are required to form a single fluent English paragraph, balance the main subjects with informative details, and describe only observable content. 

FG-Caption question construction. FG-Caption is constructed by refining the five human-written COCO captions asso-ciated with each image. The original captions and the image are provided jointly to the VLM (Specifically, we use Qwen3.7-Plus). Each source caption is rewritten independently while preserving its main meaning. The VLM is encouraged to add visually supported information about objects, attributes, materials, clothing, body parts, actions, background elements, and spatial relations. 

B. Detailed Implementation of Stage 1 

B.1. Initial Vocabulary-based Grounding 

The main paper describes how the source vocabulary initializes grounding. Here we give the implementation details. For every source entity, its annotated bounding box is clipped to the image boundary and used as a box prompt for SAM. We use the ViT-H variant of SAM and request a single mask for each box. We use GroundingDINO with the Swin-T OGC checkpoint [29] to localize each distinct source entity name in an image, with box and text thresholds of 0.30 and 0.25 , respectively. The resulting boxes are segmented with SAM ViT-H, and the masks are stored separately from the source masks to preserve the original annotations.

We therefore need to select a single consistent parent assignment while maximizing the overall quality of the selected connections. At the same time, some entities may not have a suitable parent and should remain independent. To deal with such problems, we introduce a virtual root and connect it to every entity with a directed edge of weight zero. We then apply the Chu–Liu/Edmonds algorithm [5, 9] to obtain a maximum-weight spanning arborescence rooted at the virtual node. This selects at most one parent for each entity while maximizing the total score of the selected edges. Because the virtual-root edges have zero weight, an entity is attached to the virtual root when none of its candidate parents provides a positive score. Finally, we remove the virtual root and its incident edges, leaving a forest that we refer to as the Dependency Candidate Tree. 

Dependency Relation Prediction. The Dependency Candidate Tree determines which entity pairs are considered for dependency relation prediction. For each selected tree edge j → i, we first examine the provenance of the two entities. If both entities originate from the source scene graph, we skip relation prediction for this pair because their relations are already provided by the source annotations. We therefore apply dependency relation prediction only to edges involving at least one newly discovered entity. For each retained edge, we use Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct [43] to infer the dependency relation between the two entities. The model receives the image together with the names of entities j and i, where j and i are specified as the candidate parent and child in the Dependency Candidate Tree, respectively. The model is instructed to predict a single dependency predicate for the entity pair. The predictions are combined with the relations retained from the source scene graph in the subsequent relation integration step.

E. Baselines 

Open-source MLLMs. Our open-source multimodal large language model baselines consist of LLaVA-1.5-7B [28], Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct [43], ALLaVA-3B [2], Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct [10], and InternVL3.5-8B [45]. We freeze the vision encoder and the base language model, and apply LoRA only to the attention projection layers of the language model. All models are trained using autoregressive cross-entropy loss on the ground-truth responses. 

E.1. FG-QA Baselines 

ViCrop [18]. ViCrop is a training-free approach for fine-grained visual question answering. It uses attention maps or input gradients from a frozen MLLM to estimate image region importance, selects a crop by maximizing the importance score within sliding windows at multiple scales, and adds the resized crop to the original image for a second prediction. We use ViCrop as a training-free baseline without fine-tuning the backbone MLLM. 

PinPoint [22]. PinPoint trains a region selector using annotated image regions as supervision. The original method uses bounding boxes to annotate answer regions and supporting evidence, with an encompassing region covering both. The selector is trained on the training split of each dataset and evaluated on its test split. To adapt PinPoint to FG-QA, we construct encompass boxes from our scene graphs. For each question, the encompass box is the smallest rectangle enclosing the bounding boxes of the entities mentioned in the question and the entity corresponding to the answer. We use these encompass boxes to supervise the training of separate PinPoint selectors with LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct as backbones, and evaluate both models on the FG-QA test split. 

SEAL [47]. SEAL is a multimodal visual question answering framework that combines a VQA LLM with a Visual Search Model (VSM). We use it without additional fine-tuning. The VQA model first identifies visual details needed to answer the question but missing from the full image. The VSM then recursively searches the image for these targets and returns the corresponding crops and their locations to the VQA model, which uses this additional visual information to produce the answer.

E.2. FG-Description and FG-Caption Baselines 

SC-Captioner [53]. SC-Captioner is an image-captioning model based on Qwen2-VL. Its training proceeds in two stages. The first freezes the base model and trains a LoRA adapter to generate reference captions. This adapter is subsequently merged into the base model. The second stage trains the model to correct its own captions through reinforcement learning. Given a fixed correction instruction, the model revises an initial caption. Both captions and the reference are parsed into objects, attributes, and relations. The reward favors revisions that remove unsupported facts or add reference-consistent ones. It penalizes revisions that delete correct information, introduce inconsistencies, or leave the caption unchanged. KL regularization against the frozen SFT model stabilizes the process. We use the revised caption as the final output. 

ShareGPT4V-7B [3]. ShareGPT4V-7B is a multimodal large language model built upon the LLaVA-1.5 architecture, consisting of a CLIP-Large vision encoder, a two-layer MLP projector, and a Vicuna-v1.5-7B language model. It leverages high-quality image captions from ShareGPT4V to improve vision-language alignment and detailed visual understanding. In our experiments, we use the ShareGPT4V-7B as an image captioning baseline and evaluate it both without additional fine-tuning and after applying LoRA to the language model’s attention projection layers. 

IT [36]. IT is a training-free image textualization framework that combines a pretrained MLLM with vision expert models to generate detailed image descriptions. It first generates a holistic reference description and extracts object entities from it. Vision expert models are then employed to detect hallucinated objects and obtain fine-grained visual information, including object attributes, spatial positions, depths, and sizes. Finally, an LLM uses the reference description and the extracted visual information to generate a refined caption. In our implementation, we use a pretrained LLaVA model as the backbone MLLM for IT and adopt the training-free setting. 

Patch Matters [35]. Patch Matters is a training-free captioning method that combines global and regional visual obser-13 vations. It first constructs a global view and multiple candidate image patches, and uses a pretrained multimodal language model to describe the complete image and the selected regions. The global caption, regional captions, and spatial information of the patches are subsequently combined and provided to a language model, which produces the final detailed caption. We adopt the training-free setting and do not fine-tune the model on FG-Caption. 

Synthesized [7]. Synthesized is a five-stage detailed caption construction pipeline. It first uses a pretrained LVLM to generate an overall image caption, then applies SAM to identify salient regions and generates local captions for them using the same LVLM. A phrase-level hallucination filtering stage uses the Factual parser to extract visual elements and OWLv2 to remove unsupported phrases. Finally, an LLM merges the filtered overall and local captions into a coherent, detailed description. We run the complete pipeline at inference time, without fine-tuning its component models.
