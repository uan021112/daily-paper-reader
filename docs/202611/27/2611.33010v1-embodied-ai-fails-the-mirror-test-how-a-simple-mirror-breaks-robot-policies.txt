## Abstract 

Can a simple mirror expose the limitations of today’s robotic manipulation policies? Inspired by the mirror self-recognition test in animal cognition, we investigate whether robot policies can reliably manipulate physical objects in scenes containing their reflections. We intro-duce RoboDojo-Mirror, a benchmark built on RoboDojo that preserves task objectives, success criteria, and pol-icy checkpoints while comparing a mirror-free baseline with single-mirror, dual-mirror, and mirrored-tabletop con-ditions. Across five policies spanning Vision-Language-Action models, World Action Models, and agentic poli-cies, every mirror condition reduces aggregate success rates: averaged over the three conditions, the drop ranges from 10.8 to 56.7 percentage points across policies, and it reaches 62.5 points on the mirrored tabletop. Be-cause a mirror is also a physical obstacle, we compare it against geometrically matched non-reflective panels; 50– 88% of mirror-specific failures occur without recorded robot–panel contact, so direct collisions alone do not ex-plain the degradation. Trajectory and contact analyses in-stead reveal recurring behaviors consistent with phantom targeting of reflected objects and spatial mislocalization of

Targeted diagnostics further characterize grasp-ing near reflections, policy-dependent pushing progress, and physical-target interaction under main-camera occlu-sion. Real-robot experiments with π0.5 and FastWAM also show lower overall success rates under the upright-mirror and mirrored-tabletop conditions. These findings reveal a shared vulnerability to reflective environments across di-verse robot policies and motivate reflection-aware evalua-tion of embodied intelligence. 

## 1. Introduction 

Can an ordinary mirror reveal limitations in how robots con-nect visual observations to physical actions? The classi-cal mirror self-recognition test examines whether animals can use reflections to guide actions toward their own bod-ies [10]. Inspired by this behavioral probe, we ask whether robot policies can reliably locate and manipulate physical objects when their observations also contain reflections of those objects. Mirrors create a structured discrepancy between visual appearance and actionable space. Reflections preserve rec-ognizable features and change with physical objects, yet their apparent locations contain no additional manipulable targets. They can also adjoin real objects in images, form-ing composite contours, or reveal otherwise occluded tar-gets. These properties enable tests of two complementary capabilities: maintaining reliable manipulation in the pres-ence of reflections, and using reflected observations to act on occluded targets. Recent advances in generalist robot policies have ex-panded the range of manipulation tasks that can be per-formed from visual and language inputs, yet whether these policies possess the two capabilities above remains unclear. Vision-Language-Action models directly generate actions from multimodal observations, while World Action Mod-els jointly model actions and future observations [2, 29]. Agentic policies provide another approach by selecting and invoking robot tools through repeated observation and rea-soning [15]. However, strong performance under stan-dard evaluation conditions does not establish robustness to changes in visual evidence. Controlled perturbation bench-marks have demonstrated substantial performance degrada-tion under variations in viewpoints, lighting, backgrounds, and other conditions [7]. Reflections are not covered by these perturbations: rather than altering appearance, they duplicate task-relevant content at locations with a known geometric relationship to the physical scene. Existing mirror-based evaluations have begun to ex-amine embodied agents’ self-centric perception and interaction, including tasks inspired by mirror self-recognition [12]. We investigate a different operational set-ting: generalist policies performing external-object manipu-lation under controlled mirror configurations. The question posed above breaks down into three parts: how reflections affect task completion, whether observed degradation ex-tends beyond the effects of an inserted physical panel, and what execution behaviors accompany these changes. We introduce RoboDojo-Mirror, an evaluation bench-mark built on RoboDojo [3]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, it preserves the original manipulation objectives and success criteria while evaluating policies under four conditions: no mirror, a single mirror, dual mirrors, and a mirrored table-top. On a shared set of eight tasks, we pair layouts and pol-icy seeds across conditions to measure changes relative to each policy’s own mirror-free baseline. For selected poli-cies, geometrically matched non-reflective panels provide additional controls for the effects of inserting physical struc-tures. We further design three diagnostic scenarios, namely grasping on a mirrored tabletop, pushing an object con-nected to its reflection, and acting with reflections as the main visual cue, combining task outcomes with trajectory and contact observations. This protocol enables researchers to assess reflection robustness using existing policies and task evaluators, and to characterize failures beyond aggre-gate success rates. Across five policies spanning Vision-Language-Action models, World Action Models, and agentic policies, all three mirror conditions reduce aggregate success rates, with average drops of 10.8–56.7 percentage points across poli-cies. Matched panel comparisons show that 50–88% of mirror-specific failures occur without recorded robot–panel contact, so direct collisions alone do not explain the degra-dation. The accompanying behaviors are consistent with phantom targeting of reflected objects and spatial mislocal-ization of real ones. The three diagnostics further show more frequent empty grasps, pushing progress that varies with policy and contour, and limited physical-target con-tact even with mirror-related hints. Real-robot experiments with π0.5 and FastWAM show a similar overall degradation trend. Together, these results reveal a shared vulnerability to reflections across diverse robot policies. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Robotic Manipulation Benchmarks and Visual Robustness 

Robotic manipulation benchmarks provide complementary tools for evaluating policy capabilities. LIBERO stud-ies knowledge transfer in lifelong robot learning [20]. RoboTwin 2.0 combines scalable data generation with structured domain randomization for bimanual manipula-tion [4], while RoboDojo provides a unified simulation and real-world evaluation framework for generalist manipula-tion policies [3]. Recent benchmarks place greater empha-sis on robustness beyond nominal task success. LIBERO-2Plus introduces controlled perturbations to object layouts, camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language, light-ing, backgrounds, and sensor observations [7]. LIBERO-VPro evaluates closed-loop visual robustness by perturbing the evidence available during execution, including obser-vation degradation, camera staleness, and cross-view con-sistency [17]. LIBERO-Pro2.0 further covers static and runtime shifts across environmental, observational, execu-tion, robot-state, and language dimensions [35]. RoboPRO complements success-based evaluation with collision met-rics under clutter and controlled perturbations [18]. Build-ing on these evaluation principles, our work focuses on mirror-augmented manipulation, where reflections intro-duce structured visual ambiguity alongside physical scene changes. We evaluate task outcomes and contact behavior, and use geometrically matched non-reflective panels to ex-amine performance differences beyond the presence of the panel itself. 

2.2. Mirror Perception and Spatial Reasoning 

Mirrors challenge visual understanding because reflected objects can resemble directly observed objects while occu-pying different physical locations. Early mirror-detection methods identify reflective regions through contextual con-trasts [28], while video-based approaches exploit spatial and temporal correspondences [19]. These methods ad-dress the localization of mirror regions rather than the ac-tion consequences of reflected observations. Mirror3D fur-ther estimates mirror geometry to refine unreliable depth measurements on reflective surfaces [26]. More recently, MIRA-Bench evaluates reflection-aware perception, spatial reasoning, and scene-level decisions in real RGB-D mir-ror scenes, exposing difficulties in reconciling reflected ap-pearance with depth measurements [30]. MirrorQA ex-amines subject-centered left–right reasoning in mirror con-texts [21]. Related diagnostic benchmarks, including Hal-lusionBench [11] and IllusionVQA [25], investigate visual illusions and failures of image-grounded reasoning. Un-like these perception-focused studies, we evaluate task out-comes and contact behavior during closed-loop manipula-tion under reflections. 

2.3. Mirror-Mediated Robotic Interaction 

Robotic mirror research spans geometry and sensorimo-tor learning. Hart and Scassellati infer mirror viewpoints from learned sensorimotor relationships [14]. Marchand and Chaumette develop visual servoing for robot-mounted mirrors [22], while Lanillos et al. combine active inference and sensorimotor prediction for mirror self-recognition and self/other distinction [16]. Yoshioka et al. exploit mirror re-flections to observe occluded regions, while UMI incorpo-rates side mirrors for manipulation and shows that corrected mirror views can outperform uncorrected ones [6, 31]. Re-cent embodied evaluations extend mirror-based tests to mul-timodal agents. MirrorBench studies self-centric intelli-gence through simulated mirror interaction [12], while Zil-iotto et al. evaluate whether agents can infer hidden self-attributes from reflections and use this evidence to guide actions [36]. Beyond self-centric tasks, Being-M0.7 evalu-ates mirror-assisted grasping of a directly occluded toy in a real-world humanoid task [33]. Our work studies reflection robustness across multiple generalist policies and manipu-lation tasks under controlled mirror configurations. 

## 3. Benchmark Design and Evaluation Protocol 

Built on RoboDojo [3], RoboDojo-Mirror evaluates pol-icy robustness to reflections while preserving the original task objectives and success criteria. Fig. 2 summarizes the paired evaluation protocol and controls. 

3.1. Policy and Task Selection 

We evaluate five policies spanning Vision-Language-Action models, World Action Models, and agentic poli-cies: WALL-OSS-1.0-Lite (WALL-OSS) [27], VPP2 [13], Awomo-0.5 [1], Rex-M1 Preview [9], and GPT-6-Astra (Astra) [23, 34]. This selection covers different approaches to generating robot actions from visual observations. For open-weight policies, we use official checkpoints and inference configurations. Within each policy, we hold the checkpoint, observation interface, action interface, and inference settings fixed across scene conditions. For Astra, the available camera streams, robot-state interface, motion tools, and inference budget are likewise held fixed across conditions. We select a shared eight-task set T that balances diverse manipulation requirements with strong overall RoboDojo leaderboard performance. All policies are evaluated on T

under four conditions, using paired comparisons against their own mirror-free baselines. The tasks are listed in the supplementary material.

3.3. Evaluation Protocol and Metrics 

Paired Evaluation. For each policy and selected task, we run separate closed-loop episodes with matched layouts and policy seeds across scene conditions. The main evaluation uses five layouts and three policy seeds per task for WALL-OSS, VPP2, Awomo-0.5, and Rex-M1 Preview. Astra uses five layouts with one rollout per layout. 

Termination and budgets. We retain the original task success criteria and task-specific termination rules. An episode ends when the task terminates or its execution bud-get is exhausted. Panel contact is recorded but is not an additional termination condition. For Astra, the execution budget also includes a limit on model calls. Diagnostic ex-periments report their own layouts, seeds, and budgets when these differ from the main evaluation. ## 4. Performance under Mirror Conditions 

Following Section 3, we report overall performance, mirror–panel comparisons, targeted behavioral diagnostics, and real-world validation. Trajectory, video, and contact analyses characterize three observed behaviors: collision, phantom targeting, and spatial mislocalization. 

Collision. The robot contacts the mirror during execu-tion, potentially disrupting manipulation. We distinguish such contact events from task failures, as contact does not necessarily cause failure. 

Phantom targeting. The robot directs manipulation ac-tions toward a reflected object rather than its physical coun-terpart, suggesting that the reflection is treated as an action-able target. 

Spatial mislocalization. The robot acts at an incorrect location relative to the physical target, resulting in missed grasps or ineffective manipulation without clear evidence of phantom targeting. 

4.1. Overall Performance 

Table 1 reports task scores and success rates aggregated over the task set, with each policy compared against its own mirror-free baseline. All five policies achieve mirror-free success rates of 60.8–72.5%, and every mirror configuration reduces their aggregate success rates. The average paired SR drops range from 10.8 to 56.7 percentage points across policies. With a mirrored tabletop, VPP2 drops from 72.5% to 27.5%, while Rex-M1 Preview drops from 66.7% to 4.2%. Task scores also decrease under all mirror configurations. Overall, dual mirrors tend to cause greater performance degradation than single mirrors, with VPP2 as an excep-tion. WALL-OSS and Awomo-0.5 reach their lowest suc-cess rates under dual mirrors, whereas VPP2, Rex-M1 Pre-view, and Astra perform worst with a mirrored tabletop. Astra shows little aggregate change under a single mir-ror (65.0% to 62.5%), but its SR decreases to 52.5% under dual mirrors and 47.5% with a mirrored tabletop. Despite the highest recorded robot–mirror contact rates under sin-gle and dual mirrors (55.0% and 85.0%), Astra exhibits rel-atively small SR drops, suggesting that contact occurrence alone does not explain performance degradation. Since execution budgets differ across policies, these ag-gregates should not be interpreted as a controlled cross-policy ranking. Furthermore, task outcomes alone cannot separate visual interference from physical obstruction, mo-tivating the mirror–panel comparisons in the next subsec-tion.

4.4. Real-World Validation 

To examine whether the performance degradation observed in simulation extends to physical manipulation, we evaluate robot policies under real-world mirror conditions. We evaluate π0.5 [24] and FastWAM [32] on a Franka robot across four tasks: produce placement, block stack-ing, bracket flipping, and cylinder insertion. Each task uses no-mirror, upright-mirror, and mirrored-tabletop conditions (Fig. 6). The upright condition uses a single vertical mir-ror, matching the simulated single-mirror configuration in type. Five matched initial layouts with two repetitions each yield 10 trials per task, policy, and condition. Checkpoints, instructions, camera configurations, and execution budgets remain fixed across conditions. Further experimental details are provided in the supplementary material. Table 6 shows lower overall SR under both mirror con-ditions. For π0.5, SR decreases from 77.5% without mir-rors to 52.5% with an upright mirror and 27.5% with a mir-rored tabletop; FastWAM decreases from 60.0% to 30.0% and 10.0%, respectively. Both policies achieve 80.0% on block stacking without mirrors but 0.0% with a mirrored tabletop. These results show an overall degradation trend consistent with simulation.

E.3. Visual Patch and Collision Controls 

We further evaluate VPP2 at one fixed initial state per con-tour using five matched policy seeds. The visual interven-tion adds a 70 × 18 mm matte patch at the object–reflection junction. The patch has no collider. We compare the unmodified mirror and patched mir-ror under two collision settings: robot–mirror collisions enabled, and robot–mirror collisions selectively filtered. Object–mirror collisions remain enabled in both. Within each contour, the checkpoint, task instruction, camera con-figuration, and initial state are held fixed across the com-pared conditions. The experiment comprises 3×5×2×2 = 60 executions. Table 21 shows that the patch does not improve sep-aration success when robot–mirror collisions are enabled. Cross-like and Π-like configurations remain at 0/5 un-der both visual conditions, while the I-like configuration changes from 5/5 without the patch to 3/5 with it. The change from 0/5 to 5/5 in the cross-like and Π-like configurations suggests that the physical mirror constrained VPP2’s access to effective pushing motions. One possible explanation is that VPP2 continued to generate motions that were obstructed by the mirror. Filtering robot–mirror colli-sions allowed such motions to proceed, making the physical target easier to reach. The filtered results therefore charac-terize performance with this physical constraint removed. The reduction in control steps with the patch may reflect changes in contour continuity, local reflected content, or vi-sual salience.

## G. Real-World Experimental Details 

G.1. Robot, Cameras, and Policy Configurations 

We use a Franka FR3 manipulator equipped with a parallel-jaw gripper. Visual observations are provided by two Intel RealSense D435i cameras: a fixed external camera over-looking the workspace and a wrist-mounted camera. Both cameras capture RGB images at 640 × 480 resolution and 30 Hz. Depth, infrared, and inertial streams are disabled in the camera configuration. The policy input includes the two RGB views and an eight-dimensional proprioceptive state: seven measured joint positions in radians and the total gripper width in me-tres. The deployed action interface contains seven absolute joint-position targets and a binary gripper command, where 0 denotes opening and 1 denotes grasping. Commands are executed through the ROS 2 joint-impedance control stack. We use Franka-specific checkpoints for both π0.5 and FastWAM. The π0.5 deployment uses a LoRA-adapted mul-titask checkpoint at training step 31,927, with a 32-step pre-diction horizon and a 15 Hz action grid. FastWAM uses the multitask checkpoint at training step 30,000, with a 64-step prediction horizon and a 30 Hz action grid. Each checkpoint is paired with its saved normalization statistics. For π0.5, camera images are processed at 224 × 224 res-olution. For FastWAM, the client forwards the separate native-resolution images to the server, which applies the checkpoint’s training-compatible image preprocessing.
