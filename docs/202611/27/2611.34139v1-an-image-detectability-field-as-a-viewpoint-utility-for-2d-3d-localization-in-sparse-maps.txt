# An Image Detectability Field as a Viewpoint Utility for 2D–3D Localization in Sparse Maps 

Han Sun ∗, Jun Li and Christy Jie Liang 

Faculty of Engineering and Information Technology, University of Technology Sydney, Ultimo NSW 2007, Australia 

{han.sun@student, jun.li@, jie.liang@}.uts.edu.au 

Abstract —In 2D–3D localization, a camera estimates its pose by matching an image to a 3D map. A camera held in a hand or mounted on a robot can often choose its viewpoint before it takes the image. In this work, we ask whether an image detectability field is a useful viewpoint utility when the planner reads only the geometry of the map. The field can be queried at any 3D point near a surface. From the local geometry around the point, it predicts how likely a 2D image detector is to fire at the projection of the point. We use the trained field as it is, without fine-tuning it for view selection. A fixed SuperPoint and EPnP-RANSAC localizer then estimates the pose from the chosen view. We answer in two settings, each with a test registered before the runs. On synthetic ShapeNet objects with sparse point maps, summing the field over the visible landmarks beats counting them by +2 .0 points of localization success rate (object-clustered 95% interval [+0 .7, +3 .2] ). It also beats summing Harris-3D ranks by 

+2 .2 [+0 .8, +3 .5] . On real indoor scenes from SceneFun3D, our adaptation of the learned view scorer LWL falls below counting on the test rooms. We therefore add the field to a learned per-landmark planner. On the test rooms with sparse maps whose landmarks the field also selects, the planner with the field beats the same planner without it by +1 .8 points. It beats the planner with Harris-3D or curvature in its place by +1 .0 and +0 .8 points. All three room-clustered intervals lie above zero. In both settings, the field helps in sparse maps and is on par with Harris-3D in dense maps. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In 2D–3D localization, a camera estimates its pose by matching the keypoints of an image to the points of a 3D map. The map can be a model of a scene, which a camera uses to relocalize after it has lost track. The map can also be the model of a known object, which a camera uses to estimate the pose of that object. A camera held in a hand or mounted on a robot can often turn or move a short distance before it takes the image. It can therefore choose the viewpoint from which to take the image (Figs. 1, 2). Prior work learns to score such candidate viewpoints on maps built by structure from motion [1], [2], [3]. The viewpoint is chosen before the image is taken, so the planner cannot use the content of the image and has to rely on the map. Such a map usually holds more than geometry. In a structure-from-motion map, each point also carries a colour and the descriptors of the keypoints from which it was triangulated. In this work, the planner reads only the geometry of the map. This geometry is a point set and the depth needed to decide which points a view sees. It contains no

Once the image is taken, the localizer matches it against the descriptors of a reference image (Secs. III-B, III-C3). The same fixed localizer serves every planner, so a difference in success comes only from the chosen viewpoint. With geometry alone, the planner has to predict where a 2D image detector will find matchable keypoints without seeing the texture. In a structure-from-motion map, the landmarks are triangulated detections, so where they lie already shows where the texture is. Points sampled from a model or a scan spread uniformly over the surface and carry no such signal (Sec. V-B4). DetField [4] is an image detectability field that provides this signal from geometry. From the local geometry around a point, it predicts where the 2D image detector SuperPoint [5] fires on images of the surface. On sparse maps, the landmarks that it selects localize better than those selected by the classical 3D saliency score Harris-3D [6], as reported in [4]. We use the trained field as it is, without fine-tuning it for view selection. We ask whether the field helps choose the viewpoint beyond counting landmarks and other geometric cues. We also compare it with Harris-3D used in its place. We answer this question in two settings. The first setting uses synthetic ShapeNet objects [7] rendered as geometry-only images, and the planner chooses among twelve viewpoints spread around a known object. Here the planner uses the field directly as its utility, and the score of a candidate is the sum of the field over its visible landmarks. The second setting uses real indoor scenes from SceneFun3D [8], with a dense and a sparse map tier. In each decision, the candidates are four to eight real frames from the same part of a room. Every candidate is localized in advance, and the planners only rank the stored outcomes. We retrain the learned view scorer LWL [2] on the outcomes of our localizer. On the test rooms, it falls below counting the visible landmarks. We therefore build a planner from geometry alone that beats counting. Like the learned scorer of Hanlon et al. [1], it describes each visible landmark by geometric features, such as its distance and viewing angle. A classifier predicts from these features whether the landmark will be correctly matched, and the planner sums the predictions. We add the field to this planner and compare it with variants that add Harris-3D or the nine curvature invariants that the field takes as input instead. A. Viewpoint selection for visual localization 

Hanlon et al. [1] select the viewpoint at a given location for localization in a structure-from-motion landmark map. They compare heuristics based on Fisher information, landmark count and viewing angle with a learned scorer. LWL [2] learns a viewpoint scorer from the landmarks visible in a view, supervised by localizing simulated images against the map. Among published learned view scorers, LWL is the closest to our setting, because it reads only map geometry at planning time. ActLoc [3] encodes the landmarks of the map and the mapping camera poses, and predicts localization accuracy over viewing directions. Each landmark in ActLoc also carries its colour. Our planner reads no colour, so we leave ActLoc out of the comparison. Two recent works also model localization quality for viewpoint planning. LIDIA [9] adds the direction of the light source to a geometric model of localization quality and compares it with the Fisher information field (FIF) [10], [11]. PIVOT [12] optimizes the viewing direction on the sphere to maximize the visibility of features. These methods weight landmarks by geometry, appearance or illumination. The image detectability field weights landmarks by where a 2D image detector is predicted to fire. 

B. Perception-aware planning and localizability 

Davison and Murray [13] used active vision for simultane-ous localization and map building. Perception-aware planners score future views by how much they help pose estimation. The scores come from the photometric texture and the ge-ometry of the scene [14], from the Fisher information of the landmarks [10], [11] or from the uncertainty of the scene and the camera [15]. Feature-selection methods choose the visual cues or features that most improve pose estimation [16], [17]. Map-quality measures score a query pose from the structure of the landmark map [18]. Most of these scores measure information or uncertainty. The image detectability field instead predicts from geometry alone where a 2D image detector fires. 

C. Detectability, matchability and mesh saliency 

Hartmann et al. [19] learn to predict from the descriptor of a keypoint whether it will be matched. MeshLoc [20] localizes against dense meshes. Its results remain competitive even when it renders the raw geometry without colour or tex-ture. In graphics, mesh saliency and best-view measures rate viewpoints from curvature or from the visible polygons [21], [22], [23]. These measures are designed for human viewing or shape understanding. DetField [4] is instead trained to predict where SuperPoint fires on images of a surface. For rooms it is trained on real images, and for objects on rendered geometry-only images (Sec. IV). At query time, it reads only the local geometry and needs no descriptor or image. In this work, we use the trained field as it is for viewpoint selection. 

D. Active object recognition and pose estimation 

Next-best-view methods for object recognition and pose estimation choose the next view to reduce the uncertainty of the class or the pose of the object. They plan views for recognition from depth images [24], predict the uncertainty reduction of each view with a Hough forest [25] or accumulate pose hypotheses over RGB-D views [26]. These methods choose each view from the observations made so far. Our object planner sees no image. It chooses a single view before any observation, from the geometry of the object map alone. III. A. Setting 

The task is to choose a viewpoint for localization against a prior 3D map (Fig. 1). Before the image is taken, a planner chooses among a few candidate camera poses, and the image taken at the chosen pose gets one localization attempt. We study this task in two experiments. The map is a known synthetic object in Experiment 1 and a scanned real room in Experiment 2 (Secs. III-B, III-C). The planner reads only the geometry of the map. This geometry is a point set P and the depth needed to decide which points a view sees. The depth is rendered from the model of the object or measured in the mapping frames F of the room. The planner reads no image, colour or descriptor, including the candidate images. The localizer is a fixed 2D–3D pipeline that matches the candidate image against map points described by a reference image. We restrict only the input of the planner, because we ask how much of the localization success can be predicted from geometry alone. A decision d offers a set of candidate poses Cd for one localization attempt. The set has twelve or twenty candidates in the object setting and four to eight in the indoor setting. 3) Localizer: We use the object pipeline of [4] unchanged. The pipeline chooses its landmarks among the sparse points by a score at each point. The two landmark selections of our results differ only in this score. For the field landmarks , the score is the single-pass value of DetField at the sparse point (Sec. IV-A). For the Harris landmarks , the score is Harris-3D [6] on the sparse points, with 16 nearest neighbours at 

s = 0 .02 and 8 at s = 0 .04 . These values come from [4], which chose the best value for each voxel side on the same objects. The sparse points are ranked by the score and thinned by 3D non-maximum suppression with a radius of 0.05. The top K ∈ { 16 , 32 , 64 } points over the whole object become the landmarks, so the landmark sets are nested in K.For a pair of reference and candidate views, the landmarks visible in the reference view receive the SuperPoint [5] descriptors of the reference image at their projections. A landmark is visible if its depth agrees with the rendered depth within 0.03. SuperPoint detects at most 1024 keypoints in the candidate image, with a 2D suppression radius of 4 px and a score threshold of 0.005. Each keypoint is matched to its nearest landmark descriptor with a ratio test at 0.9. EPnP [27] inside RANSAC [28] then estimates the pose with a reprojection threshold of 4 px, confidence 0.999 and at most 2000 iterations. A pose needs at least four inliers. An attempt succeeds if the rotation error is below 10 ◦ and the camera-centre error is below 0.05. 

4) Planner: The planner reads the sparse map, the land-marks and the values at the sparse points that its utility needs, such as the field value or the Harris-3D rank. The CAD model is known in this task, so the planner also reads depth rendered from the model for the reference view and the candidate. It never sees the images. With this depth, the planner applies the same visibility test as the localizer, so its visibility is exact. In rooms, the planner has to estimate visibility from the map (Sec. III-C4). The planner-visible landmarks V(c)

of a candidate c are the top K landmarks that are visible in both the reference view and c. The scalar utilities (Sec. IV-B) are computed from V(c), except co-vis , which ignores the landmarks.

θ are pooled. For a paired difference between two planners, we resample objects with replacement 20,000 times (seed 0). In each resample, we take the mean difference over the decisions of the drawn objects. The 95% interval spans the middle 95% of these means. We cluster by object because the decisions of one object share its geometry. A comparison passes if the lower end of its 95% interval is above zero. Intervals clustered by category are a secondary readout. We committed the setting, the utilities, the analysis script and the criterion before running the test, and ran it only once. The registered criterion uses field landmarks, with s

and θ pooled. It requires field-sum to exceed count , Harris-matched and permuted-field (Sec. IV-B). The results with Harris landmarks are reported with the same criterion. The comparison with Harris-sum and the results at single values of s are reported without a decision rule.

Each object has twenty reference directions, so each decision offers twenty candidates. The budgets are K ∈ { 32 , 64 , 128 }, and the 3D suppression radius is 0.02. Harris-3D uses its standard 32 nearest neighbours. The localizer and the success threshold are otherwise the same. Its criterion is the same as for sparse objects and uses field landmarks (Sec. C. Experiment 2: indoor relocalization 1) Data and maps: Experiment 2 studies relocalization in real indoor scenes. The candidates of a decision are real video frames near an anchor frame, and the planner chooses which of them to take (Sec. III-C2). For each room, SceneFun3D [8] provides a laser scan and handheld videos with camera poses, colour frames and depth maps. We use one video per room and downscale its images by a factor of two. We reconstruct a mesh from the laser scan with screened Poisson surface reconstruction [29] at octree depth 11. The normals of the scan points are oriented towards the cameras that see them, and the 3% of vertices with the lowest reconstruction density are removed. A crop is the part of the mesh that a cluster of nearby frames sees. The frames of a cluster have camera poses within 15 ◦ and 0.5 m of a seed frame. Each crop gives two map tiers. The dense map is a point cloud sampled from the crop mesh. The sparse map keeps one random point of the dense map per voxel of side δ ∈{50 , 100 } mm, as in the sparse maps of DetField [4]. The sparse tier is the registered main setting (Sec. III-D). On sparse maps, the landmarks that DetField selects localize better than those selected by Harris-3D [4]. Voxel subsampling also makes the density nearly uniform, so the density of the map carries no information on texture. The planner then has to obtain this information from geometry. The depth of the mapping frames is the high-resolution depth of the dataset. We treat it as an ideal depth sensor. It only decides which map points a mapping frame sees.

Frames that see little of the crop would only add trivial failures. Crops with fewer than 20 usable frames are skipped. Decision generation uses the dense geometry in both tiers. 3) Localizer: We use the room pipeline of [4] unchanged. It follows the object pipeline of Experiment 1 (Sec. III-B3) with three differences. First, the landmarks are chosen in the reference frame. Among the landmark positions visible in r(c), the pipeline ranks the positions by the landmark score, applies 3D non-maximum suppression and keeps the top K. A position is visible if its depth agrees with the depth of r(c) within 5 cm. The suppression radius is 0.02 in the normalized coordinates of the crop. We write LK (r(c)) for these landmarks . Second, the candidate image gives at most 4096 SuperPoint keypoints with a score above 0.002, and RANSAC uses a reprojection threshold of 8 px. Third, an attempt succeeds if the rotation and camera-centre errors with respect to the dataset pose are below 5◦ and 10 cm. The other steps and parameters are the same as for objects. Table I lists the indoor values. The landmark budget is K ∈ { 64 , 80 , 96 }. Success depends on K, and no single budget is canonical. We therefore av-erage the results over the three budgets within each decision (Sec. III-C5). The two landmark selections differ in the landmark score. For the main field landmarks , the score is the field, ranked as described in Sec. IV-A. These landmarks form the sparse localization system of DetField itself, which a view planner on a sparse map would work with. At δ = 100 mm, the landmark positions include twelve off-sample points per sparse point, since the field can be queried anywhere. These points lie within 0.75 δ of the sparse point in its tangent plane and within ±0.25 δ along its normal. At 50 mm, the landmark positions are the sparse points. For the Harris landmarks ,the score is Harris-3D [6] on the sparse points with eight nearest neighbours, so the field under test does not choose the landmarks. These results are reported separately. The dense tier uses Harris landmarks only, for the same reason. 

4) Planner-side visibility: The planner has no depth image of the candidate. It estimates from the map alone which of the landmarks LK (r(c)) the candidate c sees. In the dense tier, it renders a z-buffer of the dense points. In the sparse tier, it splats each sparse point as a square of the projected size of its voxel (supplementary material, Sec. S3). The landmarks that the planner finds visible form V(c) ⊆ L K (r(c)) . All utilities (Sec. IV) are computed from V(c), the pose of c and the map. They are computed for each K, δ and landmark selection. 

5) Endpoint and statistics: The endpoint is the binary suc-cess y of one localization attempt. The supplementary material (Sec. S4) reports two other endpoints. For each decision and planner, Eq. (1) is computed for each pair of K and δ and averaged over the six pairs. In the dense tier, it is averaged over the three budgets. The paired differences and their 95% intervals are computed as on objects (Sec. III-B5), with rooms in place of objects as clusters. We cluster by room because the decisions of one room share its geometry. 

D. Splits and registration 

We split each dataset once and at random into a training set (70%), a validation set (10%) and a test set (20%). ShapeNet is split by object within each category, over all objects of the 55 categories of ShapeNetCore [7]. SceneFun3D is split by room, over all of its rooms. DetField and the learned planners use the same split, so neither of them sees a test object or a test room before the final run. We make every design choice of the planners on the validation set. Before the run on the test set, we committed a registration. It fixes the commands, the training data, the hyperparameters and seeds, the landmark selection, the endpoint, the aggregation, the interval and the hypotheses (Sec. IV-E). It also lists the quantities that are reported without a decision rule. The test set is run only once, so the reported results are not chosen among several runs. IV. M ETHOD 

A. The image detectability field 

DetField [4] predicts the image detectability f (x | P ) ∈

[0 , 1] at any query point x near the surface that the map P

samples. Image detectability is the response that a 2D image detector is expected to produce at the surface point nearest to x, aggregated over the views that see this point. The input of DetField is geometry only. Each map point carries nine curvature invariants. They are the squared Frobenius norm and the squared determinant of the Hessian of a local quadric fit, and the surface variation, each at 16, 32 and 64 neighbours and rank-normalized within the map. An encoder reads the map. A decoder evaluates f at the query point from the encoder features and the invariants of the nearest map point. DetField was trained in [4] to predict where SuperPoint fires. For rooms, SuperPoint runs on real photographs of the scanned scene. For objects, it runs on rendered grey images that encode the surface normal and the depth. Colour enters only through the training target on real photographs. It is not an input of the field. In the sparse rooms, we use the weights that [4] trained for sparse maps, and in the dense rooms its weights for dense maps. On objects, we use its object weights for both the sparse and the dense maps. We take all weights from [4] without further training. On objects, we query f at the map points, as in the object pipeline of [4]. We read the field in two ways. Every planner below uses the single-pass value f (x). To rank the field landmarks in rooms (Sec. III-C3), we follow [4] and average the within-crop ranks of f over four copies of the map. The copies are the original map and three fixed random rotations of it, so that the ranking depends less on the orientation of the map. On objects, the landmarks are ranked by the single-pass value, as in the object pipeline of [4].

C. Per-landmark learned planner 

We use this planner only in rooms (Experiment 2). Counting treats all visible landmarks alike, but whether a landmark is matched depends on how the candidate sees it. Following the per-landmark features of Hanlon et al. [1], we learn the probability that a visible landmark yields a correct match. A candidate is scored by the sum of these probabilities,

The label zℓ is 1 if a keypoint of the candidate image was matched to ℓ and lies within 8 px of the projection of ℓ under the true pose. The localizer uses the same threshold for inliers. The geometric features ϕG use only the map, the reference frame and the planned pose. For each landmark, they describe how c sees the landmark, how the view from c relates to the view from r(c), the angles between the surface normal and the two viewing rays, and how many landmarks and map points lie around it. The supplementary material (Sec. S3) lists all 13 features. Hanlon et al. describe each landmark by its viewing range over all mapping images. Our features relate each landmark to the reference image only, because the localizer matches only against that image. 

Arms. Five planners share ϕG and the classifier (Fig. 3). They differ only in the input added to ϕG. G adds nothing. 

G+F adds the within-crop rank of f (ℓ). G+H adds ρH (ℓ).

G+C9 adds the nine invariants of the nearest map point. 

G+F+C9 adds the field rank and the invariants together. G+H 

tests whether Harris-3D adds as much as the field. G+C9 tests whether the field adds anything beyond its own input, since a classifier could learn from the invariants directly. 

Training. The classifier is a histogram gradient-boosted tree ensemble, and the supplementary material (Sec. S3) gives its hyperparameters. We train one model per arm and landmark selection on the training rooms. The models use only the records with K = 96 , because the landmark sets are nested in K and smaller budgets would repeat the same landmarks. At most 4 × 10 5 records are sampled (seed 0). The models are applied unchanged to all K and δ, and the same models serve the validation run and the test run. The dense tier uses the same recipe. 

Why this base. The registered plan was to add the field to LWL [2], which is the closest published learned view scorer (Sec. II). Our adaptation of LWL did not exceed count on the validation set in either tier. A rule written before that run then moved the hypotheses to the per-landmark planner. This planner was the strongest geometry-only planner on the validation set. 

D. Published baselines 

We adapt two published planners to our protocol. LWL [2] bins the landmarks on an image grid and feeds per-bin statistics of their coordinates to an MLP. We retrain it on the outcomes of our localizer. FIF [10], [11] scores a view by the Fisher information of the landmark bearings. For both, we choose the variant on the validation set, and the supplementary material (Sec. S3) gives the details. The heuristics of Hanlon et al. [1] are among the scalar planners ( count , angle ).

Scope of the claim. We state the advantage over Harris-3D only for landmarks that the field selects. With Harris landmarks, the results do not follow the same pattern (supple-mentary material, Secs. S1, S4). In sparse rooms with Harris landmarks, the field arm does not beat the Harris-3D arm. The advantage therefore belongs to the landmark selection of DetField and the planner together. We have one explanation for the difference between sparse and dense maps, which we have not tested. On dense geometry, Harris-3D and the curvature invariants are computed from many neighbours and already carry most of the information that the field computes from the same invariants. On sparse samples, these per-point estimates are coarser, while the encoder of the field reads the map around the point. 

Limitations. The object experiment is synthetic. Its images are geometry-only images rendered from the model, and the object weights of the field were trained on the same kind of image. Its planner also sees exact visibility from the model. The experiment therefore shows what the field can do in a clean setting, and Experiment 2 tests the field on real captures. Indoors, the mapping frames see the map through the high-resolution depth of the dataset, which acts as an ideal depth sensor. This depth only decides which map points a mapping frame sees, and the planner sees no depth of the candidate. All evidence on real images comes from one dataset, SceneFun3D. On two scenes from HM3D [30], a descriptive check also finds that adding the field to G raises the success (supplementary material, Sec. S7). Each decision offers at most eight candidates, because the candidates are real frames. Prior protocols render far more candidates [1], [2]. In return, real frames carry no rendering gap. Every result is an offline replay of recorded captures. The planner ranks stored outcomes and never moves a camera, so we do not test closed-loop behaviour. The replay scores all planners on the same outcomes. The localizer was fixed by the registration, and the results hold for this localizer. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We asked whether an image detectability field is a useful viewpoint utility for a planner that reads only the geometry of the map. We answered in two settings, each with a test reg-istered before the runs. On synthetic sparse objects, summing the field over the visible landmarks raised the localization success rate by +2 .0 points over counting them and by +2 .2

points over summing Harris-3D ranks. On dense objects, it was on par with both. On the test rooms with sparse maps, the field also selects the landmarks. Adding the field to a learned per-landmark planner raised the success rate by +1 .8 points over the same planner without it. It also beat the planner with Harris-3D or curvature in its place by +1 .0 and +0 .8 points. This gain over Harris-3D depends on the landmarks that the field selects. On dense maps, the field added about as much as Harris-3D and curvature. In both settings, the value of the field lies in sparse maps. The diagnosis in Sec. VI suggests that a larger gain needs a view-dependent signal, for instance a field conditioned on the viewing direction and the distance. Planner-side visibility. The planner has no depth image of the candidate, so it estimates from the map which landmarks 

c sees. A landmark is visible if it projects into the image and lies at most a tolerance behind the nearest map point in its cell. In the dense tier, the planner renders a z-buffer of the dense points with cells of 4 px and a tolerance of 5 cm. A sparse point drawn as a single pixel would hide nothing behind it. In the sparse tier, the planner therefore splats each point as a square of the projected size of its voxel. The square is at most seven cells wide, and the tolerance is δ. The planner-visible landmarks V(c) ⊆ L K (r(c)) are the selected landmarks that pass this test.

Features of the per-landmark planner. For each landmark 

ℓ ∈ V (c), ϕG holds 13 features. Five features describe how c

sees the landmark. They are its depth, its angle to the optical axis, its two normalized image coordinates and its distance from the image centre. Three features relate this view to the reference view. They are the distance of the landmark to the reference camera, the angle between the viewing rays from c

and from r(c), and the log ratio of the two distances. Two features give the absolute cosine between the surface normal and each of the two viewing rays. The last three features count the other landmarks of V(c) within 32 px in the image of c, the map points within 2δ of the landmark, and the landmarks in 

V(c). In the dense tier, the radius of the second count is 2 cm. In the sparse tier, δ is a further input, so one model serves both voxel sizes. The normals there come from a principal component analysis of the 16 nearest sparse points. 

Classifier. The per-landmark planner is the histogram gradient-boosted tree classifier of scikit-learn [17]. It uses 300 iterations, a learning rate of 0.05, at most 31 leaves, at least 200 samples per leaf and an L2 penalty of 1.0. It needs no feature rescaling. We set these hyperparameters in a preliminary probe and did not tune them afterwards. 

Published baselines. Our LWL [10] bins the landmarks on a 30 × 30 image grid. The per-bin means of their 2D, depth or 3D coordinates feed an MLP with two hidden layers of 300 units and dropout 0.5. The MLP is trained with a weighted binary cross-entropy on the outcomes of our localizer, with the noise augmentation of the original. We choose the input type, the learning rate and the epoch on the validation set, over two landmark families. One family is the landmarks of the localizer, and the other is all planner-visible map points. The chosen setting is trained with three seeds, and the test uses the mean of their probabilities. FIF [18], [19] scores a view by the Fisher information of the landmark bearings. We summarize this information by its log-determinant, smallest eigenvalue or trace, with exact or quadratic visibility, and choose one variant on the validation set. 

Differences from prior protocols. We follow the single-step view selection of Hanlon et al. [5] and LWL [10], with four differences (Table SI). First, their maps hold structure-from-motion points triangulated from image features, so the density of the landmarks encodes texture. ActLoc [12] also gives its planner the colour of each landmark. The map of our planner holds scan points and the depth of the mapping frames, without descriptors or colour. Second, their candidates are rendered views without roll. Our candidates are four to eight real frames with roll. Real frames avoid the gap between rendered and real images, but they give far fewer candidates per decision. Third, LWL obtains its labels by localizing with SIFT [8] and COLMAP [11], and Hanlon et al. localize with hloc [6]. Our localizer matches SuperPoint [7] keypoints against the descriptors of one reference image. Fourth, we use the single threshold of 5◦ and 10 cm. LWL reports four thresholds from 0.05 m and 0.4◦ upwards, and Hanlon et al. report six from 0.025 m and 1◦ upwards. The evaluation code of LWL divides the success rate by the success rate of the oracle, and the table in the LWL paper appears to use this normalization. We report both the raw success and the success relative to the oracle (Table IV). Absolute numbers are therefore not comparable across papers, and only the orderings of planners can be compared. S4.
