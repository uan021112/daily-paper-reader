ABSTRACT
Vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit strong semantic understanding but remain
limited in their ability to reason about metric 3D space in the physical world.
Existing VLMs typically infer 3D scene structure from one or more images without
known camera calibration or cross-view geometric constraints. This makes metric
3D inference inherently ambiguous and leaves it unclear whether their spatial
predictions are grounded in visual evidence or driven primarily by semantic priors.
In contrast, calibrated stereo provides explicit cross-view geometry that resolves
this ambiguity and offers a more reliable foundation for metric 3D perception and
geometric generalization. We investigate how VLMs can learn transferable 3D
representations directly from calibrated binocular observations. To this end, we
introduce StereoVLM, the first geometry-aware binocular VLM for metric 3D
spatial reasoning. StereoVLM follows a hierarchical learning paradigm spanning
from cross-view correspondence and disparity estimation to calibration-conditioned
metric depth perception and semantic spatial reasoning. We further find that
disparity supervision generalizes substantially better than direct binocular depth
supervision on unseen environments. To evaluate these capabilities, we introduce
Stereo3D-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark across three levels, from point-
level geometry to object-level spatial reasoning. StereoVLM-8B achieves 95.17%
depth accuracy (δ1) and a 66.98 average object-level score on Stereo3D-Bench.
Intervention results further indicate that the model’s performance on Stereo3D-
Bench benefits from valid cross-view geometry and cannot be explained solely by
additional visual tokens or semantic priors. Hierarchical geometric supervision
also yields average gains of 3.54 points across five spatial benchmarks and 4.3
percentage points in RoboCasa manipulation success.

INTRODUCTION
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in understanding and
reasoning about visual content in the digital world (Alayrac et al., 2022). Building upon these
advances, recent efforts have begun to extend such capabilities beyond the digital realm into the
physical world, positioning VLMs as a key foundation for embodied AI (Driess et al., 2023; Kim et al.,
2024; Brohan et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). However, extending VLMs from the
digital to the physical world requires more than semantic understanding: it demands geometrically
grounded spatial intelligence for reasoning and acting in 3D environments.
To extend VLMs beyond semantic understanding, recent studies have explored various ways to
enhance their spatial reasoning capabilities. SpatialVLM (Chen et al., 2024) learns qualitative
and quantitative spatial concepts from large-scale spatial question-answering data, while Spatial-
RGPT (Cheng et al., 2024) augments region-level representations with monocular depth cues for
object-centric reasoning. 3D-LLM (Hong et al., 2023), in contrast, grounds language models in
reconstructed 3D scene representations. Despite these advances, spatial benchmarks such as VSI-
Bench reveal persistent limitations in current models’ ability to construct and reason about 3D space
from visual observations (Yang et al., 2025). Moreover, the geometric information available to these
models is typically inferred from monocular observations or supplied through estimated depth and
reconstructed 3D representations, making it difficult to determine whether their spatial predictions
are grounded in geometric evidence directly available from visual observations or primarily reflect
learned priors—a concern reinforced by evidence-absent evaluations showing that VLMs can exploit
scene- and category-level priors (Zhang et al., 2026).
Calibrated stereo provides explicit geometric constraints for metric 3D perception: cross-view
correspondences determine disparity, which can be converted into metric depth using known camera
intrinsics and baseline. Yet, providing binocular observations alone does not guarantee that a VLM
will acquire geometric representations that generalize beyond the training distribution. Existing
stereo methods primarily focus on geometric estimation, while VLMs typically treat paired images as
generic visual inputs without explicitly learning the geometric relations between them. This raises
a central question: How can a VLM learn transferable representations from binocular geometry
that support metric 3D perception and high-level spatial reasoning? To investigate this question,
we introduce StereoVLM, a geometry-aware binocular VLM designed to learn transferable 3D
representations directly from calibrated stereo observations.
To connect binocular geometry with spatial understanding, we organize the training supervision into a
hierarchy of tasks spanning pixel-level correspondence, disparity estimation, calibration-conditioned
metric depth, and object-level spatial reasoning. This hierarchy progressively connects cross-view
matching to image-space geometric measurement and then to metric-scale estimation, before extend-
ing the learned geometry to language-referred objects and task-relevant 3D grounding. Importantly,
we find that disparity provides a substantially more generalizable supervision signal than direct
binocular depth on unseen environments, motivating its role as an intermediate geometric representa-
tion between cross-view correspondence and metric depth. We further establish Stereo3D-Bench to
evaluate this progression across point-level geometry, object-level geometry, and object-level spatial
reasoning. Beyond conventional task accuracy, controlled right-view interventions and geometric
out-of-distribution evaluation test whether model predictions genuinely depend on valid cross-view
geometry. Compared with matched training without stereo instructions, hierarchical geometric super-
vision raises the five-benchmark spatial-reasoning average by 3.54 points and RoboCasa (Nasiriany
et al., 2024) manipulation success by 4.3 percentage points.
Our contributions are:
• We introduce StereoVLM, the first geometry-aware binocular VLM for metric 3D spatial
reasoning, with hierarchical supervision that connects cross-view correspondence and
disparity to calibration-conditioned metric depth and language-grounded spatial reasoning,
yielding transferable 3D spatial representations.
• We establish Stereo3D-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark spanning point-level geometry,
object-level geometry, and object-level spatial reasoning, together with a verifiable evaluation
protocol that uses controlled input interventions and geometric out-of-distribution evaluation
to test whether predictions genuinely rely on binocular geometry.

• We demonstrate that disparity provides a more generalizable geometric supervision signal
than direct binocular depth on unseen environments, while hierarchical geometric supervi-
sion benefits broader spatial reasoning and downstream embodied control.

RELATED WORK
Spatial reasoning in vision-language models
Existing studies have enhanced spatial reasoning in
VLMs through spatial instruction tuning (Chen et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2026), depth-aware visual
representations (Cheng et al., 2024), and 3D grounding (Hong et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b).
Meanwhile, benchmarks such as VSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2025) reveal that current multimodal
models still struggle with metric 3D understanding and spatial reasoning. Recent works explore
metric depth prediction in VLMs, including queried-point prediction in DepthLM (Yeh et al., 2026)
and dense prediction in DepthVLM (Yu et al., 2026). VLM3 (Cai et al., 2026) covers metric depth,
cross-image correspondence, camera pose estimation, and object-level 3D understanding through
separate task-specific training. Joint learning across low-level geometry and high-level semantic
spatial understanding remains less explored.
Stereo vision and embodied intelligence
Stereo vision recovers scene geometry by establishing
cross-view correspondence and converting disparity into depth using camera calibration. Modern
stereo methods, including RAFT-Stereo (Lipson et al., 2021), IGEV-Stereo (Xu et al., 2023), Founda-
tionStereo (Wen et al., 2025) and S2M2 (Min et al., 2025), employ correlation or geometry volumes
to achieve accurate and generalizable disparity estimation. Recent work has begun incorporating
binocular sensing into embodied systems. StereoVLA (Deng et al., 2025) fuses stereo-geometric and
monocular-semantic features and adds auxiliary depth and camera-parameter objectives for VLA
manipulation, while StereoPolicy (Han et al., 2026) uses cross-view attention to adapt diffusion poli-
cies and pretrained VLA policies without explicit 3D reconstruction. EATR-Stereo (Wu et al., 2026)
further routes paired-view evidence according to proprioceptive state for long-horizon humanoid
control, and VLBiMan (Zhou & Jia, 2026) combines vision-language grounding with calibrated
stereo reconstruction in a modular one-shot bimanual system. These methods demonstrate the value
of binocular cues, with a primary focus on geometric estimation or embodied action. Our work
bridges embodied stereo sensing and VLM spatial reasoning through a stereo VLM jointly trained on
cross-view correspondence, disparity estimation, calibration-conditioned metric depth perception,
and high-level semantic spatial reasoning.

STEREO3D-BENCH
Stereo3D-Bench evaluates whether binocular evidence supporting metric geometry also transfers
to language-grounded understanding of objects and task-relevant 3D structure. Its main split draws
on four data sources—DROID, Xperience-10M, FSD, and RoboCasa—and covers all eight tasks.
An out-of-domain (OOD) split uses independently collected in-house data to evaluate point-level
correspondence, disparity, and depth. Figure 2 illustrates the benchmark construction and task
hierarchy. Controlled right-view interventions further test whether predictions depend on valid
cross-view correspondence rather than merely on the presence of a second image. The supplementary
material details data and question construction (section A), split and leakage controls (section B), and
the evaluation protocol (section C).
3.1
TASK HIERARCHY
Stereo3D-Bench comprises eight tasks across three levels: three point-level geometry tasks, three
object-level geometry tasks, and two object-level spatial reasoning tasks. Each query is posed as
a short language question on an ordered stereo pair. Depending on the task, the model returns a
coordinate, a scalar, a 2D box, or an oriented 3D box with an object label.

location in the right image, its horizontal disparity, or its metric depth along the left-camera optical
axis. These tasks assess the model’s ability to establish cross-view correspondence, estimate binocular
disparity, and recover metric scene geometry from stereo observations.

3.2
DATA SOURCES AND VQA CONSTRUCTION
The main split combines real-world data from DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) and Xperience-
10M (Ropedia, 2026) with synthetic data from FSD (Wen et al., 2025) and RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al.,
2024). For the synthetic sources, FSD provides rendered ground-truth disparity, while RoboCasa
provides ground-truth disparity and depth directly from the simulator. FSD lacks physical metric
calibration and is therefore evaluated only for correspondence and disparity.
For the real-world sources, DROID provides aligned sensor depth, while Xperience-10M provides
its officially released metric stereo-depth annotations. The five object-level tasks are evaluated on
DROID and Xperience-10M.
Evaluation data.
The main split uses held-out samples from the four sources above. Training and
evaluation data are separated before annotation generation. DROID is split by scene (laboratory,
robot, and collection date), and Xperience-10M by recording UUID; all images and derived questions
from held-out groups are excluded from training. FSD evaluation frames are excluded from training,
while RoboCasa reserves five of its 24 task identities for evaluation and excludes all associated
samples from training. To further assess geometric generalization to unseen scenes, we independently
collected an in-house OOD test set covering point-level correspondence, disparity, and depth. Results
are reported separately. Split details are provided in section B.

3.3
BINOCULAR CONTROLS
A correct prediction does not by itself demonstrate that the model uses binocular evidence. We
evaluate each query under five input conditions while holding the checkpoint, question, calibration,
and decoding procedure fixed. Correct stereo (Il, Ir) defines the primary benchmark score; the other
four conditions are diagnostic controls. Single left supplies only Il, whereas zero right supplies
(Il, 0), where 0 is a zero-valued image with the same dimensions as Ir. These controls test whether
any improvement requires visual evidence from the right image rather than merely a second image
slot. Duplicated left supplies (Il, Il), removing the binocular displacement while preserving the same

We encode these targets as separate question-answer pairs and jointly optimize the model across
all three question types. Correspondence supervision emphasizes cross-view localization, disparity
supervision quantifies image-space displacement, and depth supervision requires interpreting that
displacement under the supplied calibration. Deriving all three targets from a common geometric
reference preserves their geometric relationship during target construction, while joint training
encourages the model to learn both cross-view matching and its connection to metric scale.
Language-grounded object supervision.
Point-level supervision captures local stereo geometry.
To associate this geometry with semantic entities and their spatial extent, we introduce object-
level supervision conditioned on linguistic references. Object correspondence, disparity, and depth
link the referred object to its cross-view location and geometric measurements, while oriented 3D
grounding supervises its position, dimensions, and orientation in the rectified-left camera frame.
Task-target grounding further requires the model to identify and localize the object specified by
a manipulation instruction. Together, these objectives extend supervision from local geometric
estimation to language-conditioned object localization and task-relevant 3D grounding.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Model and training setup.
We initialize StereoVLM-2B and StereoVLM-8B from the correspond-
ing Qwen3-VL-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) checkpoints. Unless otherwise specified, our final models
are instruction-tuned for 3,000 steps with all parameters updated, using an initial learning rate of
1×10−5, a warmup ratio of 0.03, and an effective global batch size of 64. Stereo images are processed
at a resolution of 800 × 320. Stereo instructions account for 25% of the sampled training mixture,
with 15% assigned to point-level geometry and 10% to object-level supervision. The remaining 75%
comprises general visual-language, grounding, spatial-reasoning, tracking, and robotics instructions.
Full data and optimization details are provided in section D.1.
Evaluation.
We evaluate StereoVLM on Stereo3D-Bench for binocular geometry and object-level
spatial understanding, on five external benchmarks (Omni3D (Brazil et al., 2023), DA-2K (Yang et al.,
2024), OmniSpatial (Jia et al., 2026), MindCube (Wang et al., 2025a), and VSI-Bench (Yang et al.,
2025)) for spatial-reasoning transfer, and on RoboCasa for manipulation performance. We further
assess geometric generalization on the independently collected in-house OOD split of Stereo3D-
Bench, focusing on point-level correspondence, disparity, and depth. Evaluation splits are described
in section B. Settings specific to each experiment are provided in the corresponding subsections.

depth δ1 from 75.96% to 95.17%. StereoVLM-2B also surpasses GPT-6 Astra on all three tasks,
reaching 94.51% in depth δ1. The remaining general-purpose VLMs exhibit substantially weaker
stereo geometric performance, whereas the depth-specialized baselines receive only the left image and
do not exploit binocular evidence in this evaluation. Compared with the single-view DepthVLM-4B
reference, StereoVLM-8B improves depth δ1 by 58.06 percentage points. Together, these results
show consistent improvements in image-space geometry and metric-depth estimation across both
model scales.

Across the five object-level tasks, StereoVLM-2B and

and task targets, complementing the gains in point-level geometry. We also outperform cascaded
pipelines in accuracy and efficiency (section D.8).

TRANSFER BEYOND STEREO3D-BENCH
We test spatial reasoning and embodied control using matched 2B models trained with and without
stereo instructions. Both share the same initialization, optimization schedule, and training budget.
Removing stereo instructions preserves the relative sampling proportions of the remaining categories.
General spatial reasoning.
We evaluate general spatial reasoning on three monocular benchmarks
(Omni3D, DA-2K, and OmniSpatial) and two video benchmarks (MindCube and VSI-Bench). Table 6
shows that adding stereo instructions improves all five benchmarks, increasing the overall average
from 47.94 to 51.48, a gain of 3.54 percentage points. The largest gain occurs on Omni3D at 8.21
points, while the video benchmarks MindCube and VSI-Bench improve by 3.71 and 0.92 points,
respectively. With shared spatial supervision, correct stereo and single-left training yield similar
averages (50.23 and 50.21). Adding correspondence and disparity supervision to correct stereo raises
the average to 51.48 (section D.6.1).
Embodied control.
We further evaluate whether the learned representation benefits downstream
robotic control. We initialize the same GR00T policy (Bjorck et al., 2025) architecture from the
pretrained Qwen3-VL-2B model and representation models trained with and without stereo instruc-
tions. Each policy is trained for 60k steps using 50 demonstrations per task and evaluated on 24
RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024) tasks with 50 rollouts per task. To isolate the benefit of stereo
supervision from exposure to RoboCasa data during representation training, we exclude RoboCasa
from the representation-training mixture for this embodied-control experiment.
Table 7 shows that stereo instruction tuning increases manipulation success from 38.1% to 42.4%, a
gain of 4.3 percentage points over general instruction tuning alone. It also exceeds the pretrained
Qwen3-VL-2B initialization at 34.9% by 7.5 points. All policies share the same stereo-input setting
and downstream training protocol. Further controls support the benefits of geometric supervision and
valid binocular observations for manipulation (section D.6.2).
6
CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
We present StereoVLM, a geometry-aware binocular vision-language model for metric 3D spatial
reasoning, trained with hierarchical stereo supervision from cross-view correspondence and disparity
to calibration-conditioned metric depth and semantic spatial reasoning. We show that disparity
supervision provides substantially stronger metric-depth generalization to unseen environments than
direct binocular depth supervision, while controlled interventions verify that StereoVLM benefits
from valid cross-view geometry. Hierarchical geometric supervision further benefits broader spatial
reasoning and downstream embodied control. The current study is limited by its focus on controlled
benchmarks and simulated kitchen tasks, as well as its reliance on calibrated and synchronized
cameras; future work should address the simulation-to-real gap and improve robustness to challenging
visual conditions and diverse robot platforms.

A
DATA CONSTRUCTION
Training data and Stereo3D-Bench share the same stereo representation, question schemas, and
coordinate conventions, but intentionally use different geometric-reference pathways. Training super-
vision uses FoundationStereo predictions, whereas Stereo3D-Bench is scored against source-native
geometric references. This separation prevents benchmark performance from directly measuring
reproduction of the stereo estimator used to construct the training data. The following subsections
describe the shared representation, the training and evaluation reference pathways, the semantic
annotations, and question generation. Dataset splitting and leakage control are described in section B.
A.1
UNIFIED STEREO DATA REPRESENTATION
Every source sample is converted into an ordered, rectified stereo pair at a resolution of 800 × 320.
During preprocessing, camera intrinsics are standardized across calibrated stereo datasets. We retain
the rectified camera metadata, including the horizontal focal length fx, stereo baseline B, and camera
extrinsics when provided by the source. Disparity is expressed in rectified-left pixel coordinates, and
physical depth is represented by the left-camera optical-axis distance in meters.
Training and evaluation pairs use the same representation but differ in reference provenance. Foun-
dationStereo (Wen et al., 2025) provides dense disparity supervision for the training mixture. In
Stereo3D-Bench, DROID uses its aligned sensor depth, Xperience-10M uses its officially released
stereo-depth annotation, RoboCasa uses simulator geometry, and FSD uses its source-native disparity
reference. Because FSD does not provide physical metric calibration, it contributes correspondence
and disparity questions but not metric-depth questions. Figure 5 compares the source-native references
with the outputs of S2M2 and FoundationStereo.

A.4
QUESTION GENERATION AND QUALITY CONTROL
A question is formed from the references above and is posed on the ordered stereo pair. A point-level
question lists one or more pixels in the left image, and the answer returns one value per pixel in the
same order. Correspondence is a right-image coordinate, disparity is expressed on a normalized scale
whose image width is 1000, and depth is in meters. The focal length and baseline are written into
depth questions. An object-level question refers to an object by language. It asks for the object’s
right-image box, its disparity, its depth, its oriented 3D box in the left-camera frame, or the label and
3D box of the object to manipulate.
Both training and Stereo3D-Bench apply the same automatic filters. Part of the proposed object list is
removed. A 3D box is kept only when the box projected back onto the image has sufficient overlap
with the object mask. Stereo3D-Bench then receives a manual review, and questions that remain low
quality are removed. Training questions do not receive this review.

Training data and Stereo3D-Bench follow the data-construction procedure in section A, while their

Object-level semantics.
Object correspondence is measured by the IoU between the predicted and
reference right-image boxes, averaged over all correspondence questions. An invalid box contributes
an IoU of zero. Object disparity uses Acc@2px, the fraction of questions satisfying | ˆd −d| ≤2
pixels after conversion from the width-1000 normalized scale. Object depth uses the δ1 definition
above. Oriented 3D grounding uses the volumetric IoU between the predicted and reference oriented
boxes in the rectified-left camera frame, averaged over all questions, with invalid boxes assigned zero
IoU. Task-target grounding is correct only when the predicted target label is correct and its oriented
3D-box IoU is at least 0.25. The semantic average is the unweighted mean of these five task scores
after each is expressed as a percentage.
Invalid outputs.
A prediction is invalid if it is missing, cannot be parsed into the required answer
schema, contains non-finite values, gives a non-positive depth, or specifies an invalid 3D box. For a
multi-point answer, parsed entries are scored individually and missing entries are marked invalid;
entries beyond the requested number are ignored. Invalid semantic predictions receive zero IoU or
count as failures for Acc@2px, δ1, and task-target grounding. Invalid point-level predictions are
excluded from MAE and AbsRel but remain in the coverage denominator; invalid depth predictions
also remain in the δ1 denominator.
Controlled right-view inputs.
For each query, the checkpoint, left image, question, calibration, and
decoding configuration are held fixed. Correct stereo (Il, Ir) provides the primary benchmark score.
Four diagnostic conditions alter the availability or validity of the right view. Single-left input removes
the second image, (Il). Duplicated-left input places the left image in both slots, (Il, Il). Zero-right
input replaces the right view with an equally sized black image, (Il, 0). Shuffled-right input replaces
the correct right image with a right image from another benchmark example, (Il, I′

D.8
COMPARISON WITH MODULAR STEREO–VLM PIPELINES
We evaluate whether a general-purpose VLM equipped with an external stereo estimator can achieve
comparable object-level spatial reasoning to StereoVLM. We construct two modular baselines using
FoundationStereo (Wen et al., 2025) and a frozen Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), and
compare their semantic accuracy and end-to-end inference latency with StereoVLM-2B.
Baseline construction.
The two baselines differ in how stereo geometry is provided to the VLM.
The localization-based pipeline first queries the VLM to localize the referred object in the left
image. It then aggregates FoundationStereo-predicted disparities within the localized region using a
fixed central-window median and disparity-based filtering. The selected pixels are back-projected
into 3D, and a PCA-based heuristic estimates an approximate oriented bounding box of the visible
surface. These geometric measurements are then provided to the VLM to generate the final answer.
The direct-input pipeline instead provides the stereo RGB pair, a predicted depth visualization, an
8 × 20 disparity/depth grid, and camera calibration to the VLM in a single call. Neither baseline
receives ground-truth answers, bounding boxes, or masks at inference time. StereoVLM-2B directly
answers each question from the stereo RGB pair and the task prompt, including the applicable camera
calibration, in one VLM call.
