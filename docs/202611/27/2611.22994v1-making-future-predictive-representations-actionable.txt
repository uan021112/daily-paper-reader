Abstract
Recent world action models (WAMs) drop future predic-
tion at inference, assuming that representations learned
through auxiliary future supervision still benefit action gen-
eration. However, our layerwise analysis of Fast-WAM re-
veals a depth-dependent mismatch: while probes decode
future state transitions more accurately from deeper visual
layers, the gradients and visual focuses of the future and
action objectives increasingly diverge with depth, leaving
much of this predictive information unused by the action
pathway. To address this issue, we introduce RelayWAM,
which adds an explicit transition space between the video
and action backbones. Conditioned on the task instruction,
robot state, and current action estimate, recurrent transition
layers extract step-relevant predictive cues across depth and
relay them directly into the action attention, without gener-
ating future frames at inference. Experiments show that Re-
layWAM substantially amplifies the benefit of future super-
vision, translating into stronger generalization under distri-
bution shifts in both simulation and real-world robot tasks.
On LIBERO-Plus, RelayWAM achieves 72.03% mean suc-
cess, compared to 56.42% without future supervision and
51.50% for Fast-WAM.
1. Introduction
World action models (WAMs) bring future prediction into
action learning. Built on large-scale pretrained video mod-
els, they jointly learn to generate actions and to predict
future observations [4, 18, 20, 27, 29, 30, 35, 51, 62, 63,
65, 72, 79]. Recent work goes further: future-prediction
supervision improves policy performance even when the
future-generation branch is removed at inference, and fu-
ture prediction serves only as an auxiliary training objec-
tive [32, 36, 55, 70, 76]. The intuition is straightforward. A
model that can predict “what happens next” should encode
object dynamics and future state changes, and this should
help action generation generalize.
This intuition, however, rests on an implicit assumption.
In Fast-WAM [70] and similar models, future supervision
can improve action generation through the shared represen-
tation. This design assumes that the action pathway actually
uses the information that future supervision writes into this
representation. Existing methods are validated mainly by
success rates in simulation and on real robots. These results
show that future supervision helps, but not how the repre-
sentations it shapes are used. This leaves an open question:
Question.
What representations does future supervision
shape, and are they actually used by action generation?
To answer this question, we analyze future-supervised
WAMs from two sides, writing and reading. On the writing
side, we introduce training-focus attribution, which reveals
the visual content that each objective actually relies on. We
find that the two objectives are only partly aligned. Fu-
ture information is indeed written into deeper layers, while
the gradients of the two objectives become less aligned and
their training-focus maps gradually drift apart. On the read-
ing side, we erase this information from the features read by
the action pathway. The resulting action change is close to
that caused by erasing random directions of the same rank.
These findings reveal a discrepancy between the informa-
tion shaped by future supervision and the information uti-
lized by action generation.
These findings motivate us to build RelayWAM, which
introduces an explicit interface to bridge the two pathways.
It introduces a transition space between the intermediate-
to-deep layers of the Video DiT and the Action DiT. Since
the future information required for action generation varies
across action steps and execution states, we condition tran-

Contributions. Our contributions are as follows:
• We characterize how future supervision shapes shared
representations and how the resulting information re-
lates to action generation, providing a representation-
level analysis of future-supervised WAMs.
• We introduce training-focus attribution, together with lin-
ear probes and causal erasure, to investigate the writing
and reading behaviors of future-supervised representa-
tions.
• We build RelayWAM, which adds an explicit interface
that converts future information into a form usable for ac-
tion. It makes future supervision substantially more ef-
fective and achieves strong generalization in simulation
and on real robots.
2. Related Work
Vision-language-action policies.
VLA policies [5–7, 17,
26, 28, 39, 46, 47, 53, 60, 61, 68, 74, 75, 80, 81] generate
actions from the current language instruction and visual ob-
servation. They are supervised mainly by robot trajectories,
whose scale and diversity are far below internet-scale data,
and policies trained only to predict actions tend to rely on
the current visual appearance rather than on how the en-
vironment will evolve under interaction. WAMs address
this by using pretrained video models as world models and
adding future-prediction objectives that capture object dy-
namics and state transitions, which improves action gener-
ation and cross-task generalization [13, 23, 57, 77].
World action models.
WAMs differ in how future infor-
mation enters action generation. Explicit WAMs first gen-
erate future observations and then decode actions through
an inverse dynamics model or an action head [3, 10, 13, 16,
33, 35, 44, 65]. Implicit WAMs avoid pixel-level genera-

3. Analysis
While attention weights show where tokens look, they do
not measure how much each visual feature actually con-
tributes to a training objective. To capture this objective-
dependent reliance, we introduce training-focus attribution,
which quantifies the contribution of visual features to each
objective through their activations and gradients.
We analyze two models: A1, Fast-WAM jointly trained
with action and future objectives, Laction + λfutureLfuture, and
A0, an otherwise identical model trained with the action ob-
jective alone, Laction. Details of all analyses are provided in
the supplementary material.

Beyond Fast-WAM, we further apply the same analysis

predictive information into deep visual features, but
along directions the action pathway barely reads. We
term this the representation–utilization gap. The bottle-
neck lies not in the predictive capacity of the backbone,
but in the missing conversion of future information into
a form usable for action.
4. RelayWAM
4.1. Overview
To bridge the representation–utilization gap identified in
Sec. 3, RelayWAM introduces an explicit interface that
extracts step-relevant future cues from the visual back-
bone and converts them into action-usable representations
(Fig. 4). Specifically, transition layers attached to interme-
diate and deep layers of the Video DiT extract predictive
visual cues conditioned on the task instruction and robot
state, accumulate them across depth into transition tokens
aligned with the action steps, and inject these tokens di-
rectly into the native action attention, while keeping future
frames purely as a training signal.
Backbone.
RelayWAM builds on an FFI WAM [70] in
which a pretrained Video DiT [45, 57] and an Action DiT,
each with L layers (L = 30), are coupled as a Mixture-
of-Transformers (MoT) [34]. Given the current observation
ot, the language instruction c, and the proprioceptive state
st ∈RDs, the Action DiT generates an action chunk of H
steps via flow matching [37, 41] with flow time τ ∈[0, 1].

4.3. Training and inference
RelayWAM retains the exact training objective Laction +
λfutureLfuture of FFI WAMs without introducing any auxil-
iary loss on the transition layers: the feature conversion is
learned end-to-end from Laction, whose gradients also prop-
agate back into Xv
l through the cross-attention readout, en-
abling both objectives to jointly shape the visual backbone.
At inference, RelayWAM generates no future frames. The
Video DiT runs only once on the current observation, and
its visual keys/values and the conditioning tokens C are
cached across all denoising steps. Since the transition to-
kens depend on the current action estimate and flow time,
only the lightweight transition layers are rerun at each de-
noising step, recomputing Tl from Tl0−1 = 0 and the re-
sulting transition keys/values ( ˆKl, ˆVl).

5.3. Ablation study
To disentangle the contributions of future supervision and
the transition space, we construct a 2 × 2 controlled com-
parison: A0 is the Base architecture trained with action su-
pervision alone, A1 adds future supervision, B0 adds the
transition space without future supervision, and B1 is the
full RelayWAM. All variants share the same backbone, ac-
tion objective, training data, and optimization configuration.
As shown in Table 4 and Figure 6, the transition space
amplifies the benefit of future supervision. On LIBERO-
Plus, future supervision brings only a modest gain on the
Base architecture (+5.2 points), but roughly triples this
gain when paired with the transition space (+15.6 points).
LIBERO-PRO shows the same pattern, while in-distribution
LIBERO scores are near saturation for A1, B0, and B1.
Representation-level probing helps explain this synergy.
Reusing the layer-20 probe from Section 3.1, we find that
future supervision raises future predictability more with the
transition space than without it, suggesting that the cross-
attention readout makes the visual features more responsive
to future supervision rather than merely preserving them.
Moreover, actions are already highly decodable from the
transition tokens at the entrance of the transition space in
RelayWAM, whereas in B0 action decodability builds up
only gradually with depth. Together, these results suggest

5.4. Real-world results
We further compare RelayWAM against Fast-WAM [70]
and DM0.5 [12], a strong VLA baseline built upon large-
scale embodied pretraining, with all models trained on the
exact same 80 real-world demonstrations.
As shown in
Figure 5, RelayWAM consistently outperforms Fast-WAM
across every evaluation setting, and its performance ad-
vantage widens progressively as environmental perturba-
tions intensify from the Fixed setting to unseen OOD place-
ments. The Light setting separates the two FFI models most
dramatically: under colored ambient illumination, Fast-
WAM’s success rate drops sharply to 15%, whereas Re-
layWAM maintains a 70% success rate—nearly matching
the embodied-pretrained DM0.5 (75%) without utilizing
any large-scale embodied pretraining. Since RelayWAM
and Fast-WAM share the exact same backbone and dif-
fer solely by the 34.96M transition layers, this substantial
real-world robustness gain directly stems from converting
future-predictive features into representations that the ac-
tion pathway can readily read, echoing the strong lighting
robustness observed on LIBERO-Plus.

6. Conclusion
We uncover a representation–utilization gap in future-free
inference World Action Models: while future supervision
writes rich predictive information into deep visual layers,
it lies along directions largely orthogonal to those read by
the action pathway. To bridge this gap, we propose Relay-
WAM, whose recurrent transition space extracts task- and
state-conditioned predictive cues and injects them into na-
tive action attention, outperforming even models that de-
code future frames at inference. Advancing world action
models requires not only accurate future prediction, but also
explicit, action-aligned interfaces that let the action path-
way read it.
