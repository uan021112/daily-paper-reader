Abstract
Looped multi-view reconstruction refines scene features by
repeatedly applying a shared transformer block. Making
these updates cheaper requires knowing how an approxima-
tion at one step affects the rest of the refinement. We study
this with paired interventions in D´ej`a View and stacked re-
construction transformers, separating the error an approxi-
mation injects, how that error propagates, and how it affects
the output. In D´ej`a View, approximation errors are attenu-
ated more strongly than matched random perturbations un-
der a scale-adjusted state-error measure, and a local Jaco-
bian decomposition separates the contribution of channel
gating from the response to spatial error structure. Spa-
tial approximations made early benefit from subsequent re-
finement, whereas cross-step reuse depends on which quan-
tity is cached and how reuse steps are spaced. At equal
final norm, approximation errors change the predicted ro-
tations 1.55 times as much as random errors. These find-
ings guide D´ej`a Vite, which reduces spatial resolution and
pools keys and values early in refinement, then interleaves
attention-output reuse with full attention, and is adapted
through lightweight self-distillation. Across five reconstruc-
tion benchmarks, the balanced configuration runs 1.63×
faster than the official 16-step D´ej`a View model with a 1.86-
point reduction in average pose AUC@3◦. At matched la-
tency, it exceeds the interpolated curve of a trained baseline
using full refinement blocks by 4.73 points in AUC@3◦and
improves the corresponding geometry metrics.
1. Introduction
Feed-forward reconstruction models such as VGGT [30],
π3 [32], and Depth Anything 3 [14] recover camera poses
and dense geometry from image collections with a deep
stack of transformer layers. D´ej`a View [3] replaces the stack
with a loop: a single shared block refines the scene features
repeatedly, and the number of iterations sets the inference
budget. Spending less then means running fewer iterations,
each at full cost. The loop permits a finer choice (Fig. 1 (c)),
because an individual iteration can itself be made cheaper,
so computation can be distributed unevenly along the re-
finement.
The tools for cheaper iterations already exist.
Token
merging [2, 23] runs a transformer on a coarser grid with
fewer tokens. AVGGT [25] keeps every query but lets it at-
tend to a subsampled set of keys and values. Diffusion mod-
els reuse attention outputs from an earlier denoising step in-
stead of recomputing them [15, 18, 22]. What these works
do not provide is a rule for a loop. Stacked models place
approximations by the role of each layer, and every step of
a loop has the same weights. Diffusion caching judges a
reuse by how much the features changed since the last step,
which does not say what the following steps do with the
error.
The cost of an approximate step is not settled at the step
itself. Its error enters a state that the remaining iterations
keep refining, so the cost depends on how those iterations
transform the error and on how much of it reaches the pre-
dicted cameras and geometry. We measure this directly. We
run the model twice on the same input, once exactly and
once with a single step approximated, and track the differ-
ence between the two runs through the remaining iterations
and the prediction heads.
Our empirical diagnosis reveals why layer-wise accel-
eration does not transfer directly to looped models. In a
stacked model, each layer has its own weights and its own
job, and accelerations choose what to approximate from that
job. A loop gives no such guide: every step runs the same
block, and removing any single step barely changes the re-
sult. What differs between steps is how much refinement
follows them. In a 16-step loop, an error introduced at step
2 passes through fourteen more updates, and one introduced
at step 14 through two. Two findings show how this decides

where each approximation goes. First, later iterations ab-
sorb approximation errors. A coarse-grid step at iteration 7
costs 21.9 points of AUC@3◦one step later but 2.5 points at
the output, whereas the same step at iteration 12 still costs
10.3 points at the output (Fig. S1 in the appendix). The
loop shrinks these errors more than random perturbations of
equal magnitude, which we trace to its channel gates: they
scale down the channels that carry the error (Sec. A.4). Spa-
tial approximations therefore belong early. Second, reuse
errors compound. A reuse step reads an attention output
computed from an earlier state, so each reuse inherits the
drift of those before it: with five reuse steps in a schedule,
the error that the last one makes at its own step is 11.1 times
that of the same reuse applied alone. Reuse is therefore kept
sparse and separated by exact steps.
Building on these empirical findings, we propose D´ej`a
Vite, which runs D´ej`a View with the following schedule:
coarse-grid steps first, KV-pooled steps next, and exact
steps afterwards, the last of which alternates with reuse
of the global attention output.
Low-rank self-distillation
adapts the model to this schedule, and one set of adapted
weights serves several operating points that trade accuracy
for speed. On five benchmarks, our balanced configura-
tion runs 1.63× faster than the official 16-step D´ej`a View
model at a cost of 1.86 points in average AUC@3◦. At
equal latency, it exceeds a trained baseline that keeps full
blocks and lowers the iteration count by 4.73 points and im-
proves the geometry metrics. With operators and training
held fixed, our placement outperforms uniform placement
by 2.62 points.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We identify the steps of D´ej`a View as interchangeable
refinements with a stationary attention pattern, a prop-
erty enabled by weight sharing and absent from stacked
models. Later steps also absorb errors from approximate
operations more effectively than random perturbations of
equal magnitude.
• We characterize when approximation and reuse are
worthwhile: spatial approximations are cheapest early, an
error’s output cost depends on its direction, and reuse cost
is governed by error compounding, which in turn depends
on the cached quantity.
• D´ej`a Vite, our acceleration method, runs 1.63× faster
than the 16-step model and exceeds a trained full-block
baseline by 4.73 points in AUC@3◦at equal latency.

Our position. D´ej`a View can save computation only by

instead makes individual iterations cheaper and places each
approximation by how its error propagates.

3.2. Loop Steps Are Interchangeable Refinements
Before approximating steps, we ask what a step does. Three
properties separate the loop from stacked models (Tab. 1).
No step is critical. We skip one update at a time and record
the worst step of each scene. In D´ej`a View the worst skip
costs a median of 0.18 points of AUC@30◦and at most
1.32 points. Every scene of every stacked model contains a
layer whose removal costs more than this maximum, with
medians of 48.8, 54.8, and 82.2 points for VGGT, π3, and
DA3. The cross-view read of a single step is just as dispens-
able. Replacing the other views’ keys and values with those
of another scene costs a median of 1.9 points at the worst
step of D´ej`a View, against 15.9, 11.9, and 7.5 points in the
stacked models.
Attention stops changing. From 27% of the loop onward,
the global attention patterns of consecutive steps are nearly
identical in all 65 scenes. Over the trajectory the relative
update size falls from 0.76 to 0.07, and consecutive updates
align in direction, with cosine rising from 0.17 to 0.98. No
stacked model reaches a stationary pattern in any scene.

Late steps thus repeat one operation with a shrinking step
size, which is what makes a late attention output reusable.
Weight sharing is the cause. We fine-tune two eight-step
models from the D´ej`a View block with the same data, loss,
and schedule, one with tied and one with untied weights.
The tied model stays flat: skipping any step costs 3 to 4
points of AUC@3◦. The untied model specializes its last
block, whose removal costs 18 to 19 points across two
seeds, and it is less accurate, with AUC@3◦of 46.2 and

ing the time conditioning and training with a fixed number
of steps.
Since steps are interchangeable refinements, an approxi-
mation is placed by its position along the trajectory, with no
layer role to consult. The remaining subsections study how
that position matters. Section A.2 gives the protocols and
per-step results.
3.3. Later Iterations Absorb Approximation Errors
A single approximation costs less at the output than at the
step where it is applied (Fig. S1). A coarse-grid step at iter-
ation 7 costs 21.9 points of AUC@3◦one step later and 2.5
points at the output. At iteration 2 all three operations end
with no measurable loss, and at iteration 12 the coarse-grid
step still costs 10.3 points.
The state shows the same behavior (Tab. S3). D´ej`a View
reduces the scale-adjusted error in all 12 operator–position
conditions. It retains 0.29–0.85 of the error at the output,
and each fraction is below that of a random perturbation of
the same magnitude. The difference comes from the chan-
nel gates. Along the path through the gates alone, a KV-
pooling error injected after step 12 has a median gain of
0.469, against 0.767 for random directions, with the same
ordering in all 65 scenes (Fig. S2). Approximation errors
carry their energy in channels that the gates scale down,
whereas random perturbations spread it over all channels.
The attenuation is a contraction by the gates (Sec. A.4).
VGGT and DA3 show the opposite preference. VGGT
retains more of the approximation error than of the random
perturbation in five of six conditions, and DA3 in all six.
Their absolute retained fractions exceed one in 17 of 18
conditions (Sec. A.3).
The output error depends on the direction of the remain-
ing state error as well as its size. When final residuals are
rescaled to equal norm and decoded, the residual of an ap-
proximation changes the predicted rotations 1.55 times as
much as a random residual, and more in 62 of 65 scenes
(Tab. S5 and Sec. A.5). Decoded pose and geometry are
therefore our criterion for comparing placements and sched-
ules.
3.4. Spatial Approximations Belong Early
Since later iterations absorb errors, spatial approximations
are cheapest early (Fig. S3 and Sec. A.6). With four coarse-
grid steps, the early layout gives a rotation deviation of
0.30◦, against 1.69◦for uniform and 1.78◦for reversed
placement, and it wins on all 30 held-out scenes. With four
KV-pooling steps the deviations are 0.17◦and 0.32◦, with
wins on 29 of 30 scenes. Reuse behaves differently. Moving
it from late to early steps increases the deviation by 0.41◦,
and late placement is preferred in 60 of 65 scenes, because
its immediate error is smaller late in the trajectory.
Section 5.3 confirms this placement after adaptation,
and Sec. A.12 reports its transfer across budgets and view
counts. A predictor fitted on these measurements ranks the
cost of held-out operators and budgets (Sec. A.11).
3.5. Reuse Errors Compound Across Steps
A reuse step depends on an earlier state, so repeated reuse
interacts. With five reuse steps at {6, 8, 10, 12, 14}, the lo-
cal error of the last output reuse is 11.1× that of the same
reuse applied alone. The factor is 7.0× for values and 7.5×
for key–value pairs, but 1.6× for keys and 2.0× for at-
tention probabilities (Fig. S4 and Sec. A.7). Output reuse
should therefore be spread out.
On 30 held-out scenes,
spreading five output-reuse steps lowers the rotation devi-
ation from 0.710◦to 0.374◦and wins on 25 scenes. The
stability of the cached quantity predicts the local error of a
single reuse, with Spearman correlation 0.95–1.00. The fi-
nal cost is set by how these local errors compound along the
trajectory, so we select reuse layouts by evaluating complete
schedules.
4. Accelerating Looped Reconstruction
Our method, D´ej`a Vite, has three parts, each answering one
question. The approximate operations (Sec. 4.1) decide how
a step is made cheaper. The compute schedule (Sec. 4.2) de-
cides when each operation runs, using the findings of Sec. 3.
Adaptation (Sec. 4.3) recovers the accuracy that the approx-
imations cost and lets one set of weights serve several speed
settings. We keep the notation of Eq. (1): zt is the state be-
fore step t, K is the number of steps, and τt is the relative
time of step t.
4.1. Approximate Operations
A step of the loop spends its time in two places: every layer
scales with the number of tokens, and global attention ad-
ditionally scales with the number of query–key pairs across
all views. The three operations in Fig. 2 remove these costs
at different granularity. Coarse-grid processing shrinks the
token set of the whole block, KV pooling shrinks only the
key set of global attention, and cross-step reuse skips global
attention altogether.

4.2. From Findings to a Compute Schedule
The operations above say how to save computation. The
schedule says when, and this is where the analysis enters:
the same operation costs little accuracy at one step and
much at another, so the order of the steps determines the
trade-off. We write a schedule as a string with one letter
per step, read from the first step to the last. C, P, F, and
R denote a coarse-grid step, a KV-pooled step, a full step,
and a reuse step. The three operating points use the sched-
ules C2P2F9RFR (K = 16), C2P4F5RFR (K = 14),
and C6P4FRFR (K = 14) for the quality, balanced, and
fast settings. A superscript repeats a step, so C2P4F5RFR
runs two coarse steps, four KV-pooled steps, five full steps,
and then a reuse step, a full step, and a reuse step. The
string length equals K: every step advances the loop index
t, including reuse steps. Spatial approximation occupies the
early trajectory, followed by full-resolution refinement. In-
terleaving late reuse with full attention refreshes the cache
between reuse steps. This arrangement follows the posi-
tion and composition findings in Secs. 3.4 and 3.5; operator
counts set the three operating budgets. Matched-adaptation
schedule comparisons in Sec. 5.3 test the resulting alloca-
tion against uniform, reversed, stability-based, and random
layouts.
4.3. Adaptation and Budget Control
The pretrained block was trained to run exact steps, so the
scheduled approximations cost accuracy when applied di-
rectly. Adaptation closes this gap with two requirements.
The correction must be able to differ along the trajectory,
because the early steps are coarse and the late steps reuse at-
tention. It must also be defined for any number of steps, so
that one set of weights serves every schedule. We meet both
by placing a small number of low-rank corrections, which
we call anchors, at fixed relative times and interpolating be-
tween them.
We use nine sets of rank-16 LoRA anchors [7], applied to
the query–key–value and output projections and both feed-
forward projections in the frame and global attention sub-
blocks. Let W ∈Rdout×din be one of these pretrained pro-
jection matrices, shared by all steps, with input width din
and output width dout. Anchor j ∈{0, . . . , 8} stores a low-

Finally, a learned multistep update improves accuracy at
low step counts. Each step applies a per-channel weighted
combination of its own update and the two previous ones,
as multistep solvers do for differential equations (Eq. (21)
in Sec. B.1). The weights follow the same anchor construc-

relative times j/6, j ∈{0, . . . , 6}, linearly interpolated at
τt. A run of coarse steps and its update history stay on the
coarse grid, which equals applying Eq. (3) at every step, so
the history is cleared at each change of resolution.

5.4. Comparison and Limitations
In the dataset average, the balanced tier has higher pose
AUC and lower latency than VGGT, FastVGGT, AVGGT,
and π3 (Tab. S15).
Individual datasets differ.
VGGT
reaches 96.48 on DTU against our 83.42, and π3 has bet-
ter geometry on ScanNet++ (Tab. S14). On 300-view Scan-
Net++ inputs, the quality, balanced, and fast tiers reduce
FLOPs by 31.8%, 52.7%, and 75.3% (Tab. 3). Peak mem-

The accuracy cost is concentrated. Against the 16-step
model, the balanced tier wins, ties, and loses on 41, 13, and
99 of the 153 scenes, and it loses on 12 of the 13 ETH3D
scenes. Scenes with low covisibility lose more, 2.50 points
against 1.15 for high covisibility (Sec. B.6).
6. Conclusion
Our interventions show that the steps of D´ej`a View are inter-
changeable refinements and that later steps absorb approxi-
mation errors. The loop shrinks these errors in all 12 tested
conditions, and more than random perturbations of equal
magnitude, because its channel gates scale down the chan-
8


nels that carry them. At the output, the direction of the re-
maining error sets its cost: at equal norm it changes the pre-
dicted rotations 1.55 times as much as a random error. Spa-
tial approximations are cheapest early in the loop, and reuse
is cheapest when its steps are spaced apart. Guided by these
findings, D´ej`a Vite combines coarse-grid processing, key–
value pooling, and attention-output reuse with lightweight
self-distillation. Across five benchmarks, the balanced con-
figuration runs 1.63× faster than the 16-step model with
a 1.86-point reduction in average AUC@3◦, and at equal
latency it exceeds a trained full-block baseline by 4.73
points.

A. Interventions in the Reconstruction Loop
We follow each approximation from the step where it enters
the loop to the pose and geometry decoded at the output.
The paired runs in Sec. A.1 provide a common reference
for the single-step and schedule comparisons.
A.1. Paired trajectories and error measurements
We compare the released checkpoints of D´ej`a View,
VGGT [30], π3 [32], and DA3 [14] through paired inter-
ventions.
Analysis data. Our analysis uses 65 scenes, with 13 each
from DTU, ETH3D, 7-Scenes, ScanNet++, and nuScenes,
with at most 32 input views per scene; scenes with fewer
views use all of them. We use K = 16 for D´ej`a View.
Within each dataset, even-indexed scenes are used to derive
placement rules and odd-indexed scenes to evaluate their
predictions. Comparisons are paired by scene, and uncer-
tainty in output deviations is summarized with hierarchical
bootstrap confidence intervals.
Paired interventions. For each scene, we run an unmod-
ified reference trajectory and a perturbed trajectory with
identical images and view ordering. At a selected step u,
we replace one operation and then resume the original com-
putation for the remaining steps. Interventions include to-
ken pooling, restricting cross-view attention, and skipping
an update. In D´ej`a View, we additionally probe cross-step
reuse of keys, values, attention probabilities, and attention
outputs. We vary the intervention position while keeping its
type and strength fixed. Multiple-intervention experiments
further vary the placement and spacing of a fixed number
of approximate steps. KV subsampling follows the second
step of AVGGT [25]: keys and values are subsampled on a
2 × 2 grid, the first frame and the diagonal of the attention
matrix are kept, and one mean key–value token is added.

untie the loop weights to test whether late updates acquire a
specialized role.
This section gives the protocols behind Sec. 3.2. The
skip, cross-view ablation, and stationarity measurements
use the released checkpoints on the 65 analysis scenes with
at most 32 views. A unit is one loop step in D´ej`a View and
one pair of a frame-attention block and a global-attention
block in the stacked models. Each intervention is applied to
a single unit, and every other unit runs exactly.
Skip. Skipping a unit leaves the state unchanged at that unit.
In D´ej`a View the loop index still advances, so the following
steps receive their original time inputs. For each scene we
skip every unit in turn, record the drop in AUC@30◦against
ground truth, and keep the largest drop over units. Table S1
reports the median of this worst-unit drop over scenes, its
maximum, and the number of scenes in which it exceeds 5
points. The largest worst-unit drop of D´ej`a View over all
65 scenes is 1.32 points, and every scene of every stacked
model has a unit whose skip costs more than that.
Cross-view ablation. This intervention removes what the
other views contribute at one unit and keeps the activation
statistics. For the queries of a view, the keys and values
of all other views are replaced by those of a donor scene
recorded at the same unit, and the keys and values of the
query’s own view are kept. The donor is another scene of
the same dataset with the same token grid and at least as
many views when one is available. Using a scene as its
own donor reproduces exact attention with zero error, which
verifies the implementation. The statistic is the same worst-
unit drop in AUC@30◦as for the skip.

Tied and untied weights. All variants start from the re-
leased D´ej`a View block and run eight steps. The tied model
shares one block across the eight steps. The untied model
holds eight copies of the block, one per step, which are iden-
tical at initialization and give the same output as the tied
model before training. Both are trained by distillation from
the released model with the same data, loss, and schedule:
20,000 steps at a learning rate of 10−4, then 60,000 steps at
5 × 10−5. We train the untied model with two seeds, and
evaluate on 32 scenes.
Table S2 reports the AUC@3◦drop from skipping each
step after 80,000 training steps. The tied model stays flat
at 3 to 4 points. The untied model develops a specialized
last block, whose skip costs 19 and 18 points in the two
seeds. This specialization grows with training: the last-
block cost is 15 and 12 points after 20,000 steps, 17 and 17
after 50,000, and 19 and 18 after 80,000. Swapping the last
two blocks of the untied model costs 12 points and repeat-
ing its last block costs 10 to 11 points, while both operations
change the tied model by at most 1 point. The updates of
the untied last block also lose their alignment with the pre-
ceding update, with a cosine of −0.05 and −0.02 against
0.78 for the tied model. The untied model has eight times
the block parameters and is less accurate, with AUC@3◦of
46.2 and 47.7 against 53.2.
Time conditioning and partial weight sharing. Two fur-
ther controls isolate weight sharing. A tied model trained
without time conditioning reaches 52.8 and loses 4 points
for every skipped step. A tied model trained with a random
number of steps, as in the original recipe, loses 2 to 3 points
per skipped step after 20,000 training steps, against 3 to 5
for a fixed number of steps. Giving only the last step its own
block reproduces the specialization of the untied model in
that block, with a skip cost of 17 points in both seeds, while
the seven shared steps keep a flat cost of 5 to 7 points. The
interchangeable steps therefore follow from weight sharing,
and a free block becomes a specialized output stage.

A.7. Spacing repeated attention reuse
We compare the error at the last reuse step with the error of
an isolated reuse at that same step. This comparison mea-
sures the contribution of cache history. A second compari-
son fixes the number of reuse steps and measures the effect
of spacing on the final poses (Fig. S4).
Cross-step reuse couples the current approximation to
earlier states. To characterize this dependence, we com-
pare five cached quantities: keys, values, key–value pairs,
attention probabilities, and projected attention outputs. At-
tention probabilities specify the weights applied to values;
caching them still allows the values to be recomputed from
the current state.
For the five-reuse layout at steps {6, 8, 10, 12, 14}, the
local error of output reuse at the final reuse step reaches
11.1× its isolated-reuse counterpart.
The corresponding
amplification is 7.0× for value reuse and 7.5× for joint
key–value reuse.
Reusing only keys or attention proba-
bilities gives factors of 1.6× and 2.0×, respectively. Re-
peated approximations therefore exhibit different interac-
tions across the cached quantities.
The distinction predicts different placement preferences.
On 30 held-out scenes with a budget of five reuse steps,
spreading output reuse across the trajectory reduces rota-
tion deviation from 0.710◦for the late concentrated layout
to 0.374◦, winning on 25 scenes. The spread-out place-
ment also wins on 27 scenes for value reuse and 22 for key–
value reuse. For attention-probability reuse, the concen-
trated layout wins on 22 scenes. The preferred reuse layout
thus varies with the cached quantity in these comparisons.
Across the tested cache quantities, the reported Spearman
correlations between stability and single-reuse local error
are 0.95–1.00. The spacing comparison evaluates the accu-
mulated effect directly on the final poses.
A.8. Precision and perturbation magnitude
We repeat the propagation experiments in FP32 using the
same scenes, intervention plans, and sampled perturbations
as the bfloat16 runs. Conditions are grouped by their me-

we summarize the ratios of FP32 to bfloat16 condition-level

deviation, while changed inputs can induce different round-

Across scales 0.25, 0.5, and 1, the maximum-to-

A.11. Predicting the cost of unseen operators and
layouts
The prediction target is the final rotation deviation from the
clean reconstruction. We evaluate operator rankings at indi-
vidual steps and layout selection under a fixed budget sepa-
rately.
The predictor is multiplicative. For an approximation ap-
plied at step u, let ϵu be its local error, the relative error
of the global-attention output at that step; for a coarse-grid
step it is the state deviation right after the step. The cost
c is the rotation deviation of the final prediction from the
clean prediction. The predicted cost is ϵu exp(h(u)), where
the position profile h(u) is the median of log(c/ϵu) over
the discovery scenes and over five perturbations applied at
step u: KV pooling, the coarse grid, output reuse, AVGGT-
style key subsampling, and random noise. The profile rises
from −2.1 at the first step to −0.8 at the last. The three re-
duced predictors keep only exp(h(u)), only ϵu, or only the
change of the global-attention output between consecutive
steps. For each held-out approximation type we take the
median predicted and measured cost over the 30 test scenes
at each of the 16 steps, and report the Spearman correlation
across steps. For a budget of several approximate steps, the
prediction sums the per-step predictions, using the median
discovery ϵu, over 12 candidate layouts; regret is the mea-
sured cost of the predicted best layout relative to the mea-
sured best.
We fit a cost predictor on the 35 even-indexed scenes and
evaluate it on the 30 odd-indexed scenes. Across three held-
out approximation types, the predictor achieves rank cor-
relations of 0.84–0.89 with measured cost (Tab. S6). The
useful predictors vary with the operation: position alone
matches the full predictor for pooling on half the heads,
while local error alone gives the highest correlation for
attention-probability reuse.

approximate steps, the predictor selects the early-prefix lay-

A.13. Interaction between successive interventions
We combine an initial pooling or noise intervention with
windows of one, two, or four modified updates. For final
deviation E, we summarize the interaction between an ini-
tial intervention A and a window B as
I(A, B) = E(A + B) −E(A) −E(B),
(19)
where A + B denotes their joint application.
Most re-
ported interactions for freezing values are nonpositive. The
strongest positive interaction among the tested value-cache
conditions occurs when values are cached for four steps af-
ter pooling at step 8: I = 0.127, with a 95% confidence in-
terval of [0.110, 0.161]. The corresponding four-step value-
freezing condition gives −0.033 [−0.062, −0.012]. Interac-
tions depend on the intervention, its position, and the way
cached quantities are maintained. We use these operator-
specific responses to characterize reuse composition.

Distillation data and sampling. The distillation images are
the subset described in Sec. 4.3, about 3% of the images of
the training splits of the D´ej`a View training datasets. Each
optimizer step uses a single scene, drawn from a dataset
chosen with equal probability, and its number of views is
drawn from 4 to 24. The budget K of the step is drawn
uniformly from the set of budgets of the checkpoint. The
tier checkpoint instead draws one of the three schedules of
Sec. 4.2 uniformly at every step, so one set of anchors serves
the quality, balanced, and fast settings. One scene of each
dataset is held out from training and used to monitor the
distillation loss. The student runs its schedule during train-
ing, including the reuse steps, and gradients pass through
all steps.
Multistep update. Let Dt = fθ(zt; τt, τt+1) −zt be the
update of the block at step t, computed with the operator of
20


that step. The state advances as
zt+1 = zt + Dt + c1(τt) ⊙Dt−1 + c2(τt) ⊙Dt−2, (21)
where c1, c2 ∈RD are per-channel weights and ⊙is the
channel-wise product. Each weight is stored at seven an-
chors and linearly interpolated at τt. The weights are ini-
tialized to zero, so training starts from the plain loop. Terms
whose update does not exist are dropped: the first step uses
no history, and the history is cleared at each change between
the coarse and the full grid. Successive coarse steps retain
their update history on the coarse grid. The channel gates
act inside fθ and are unchanged.
Learned KV pooling. In a KV-pooling step, each pooled
key and value is a weighted mean of the tokens of its 2 × 2
window. Head h holds a score vector uh and a scalar bh.
The weights of a window are the softmax of the scores
⟨ki, uh⟩of its keys ki, and the same weights pool the val-
ues. The bias added to the attention logit of a pooled key is
log n + bh, where n is the number of tokens in its window.
Both uh and bh start at zero, which gives mean pooling with
bias log n, so training starts from the unlearned operator.
The camera and register tokens stay unpooled and receive
no bias.
Attention-output cache. The cached tensor At of Eq. (5) is
the output of the output projection of global attention, taken
before LayerScale and before the time-conditioned gate. A
reuse step applies its own LayerScale and its own gate to the
cached tensor, so the time conditioning of the step is kept.
A reuse step needs a full-resolution attention output from
the step before it. Every schedule therefore places a reuse
step directly after a full or a KV-pooled step, and never after
a coarse-grid step or another reuse step.
Parameter accounting. The adapted model stores approx-
imately 120.6M parameters, comprising 117.1M base pa-
rameters, 3,538,944 parameters in nine LoRA anchor sets,
10,752 multistep weights (two weights per channel at seven
anchors, for 768 channels), and the per-head scores and bi-
ases of learned KV pooling. Parameter counts refer to this
compact representation. At inference, precomputed dense
projections materialize the step-specific updates, and their
additional storage is accounted for in the runtime memory
footprint.
Timing protocol. We measure latency on an exclusively al-
located A100-SXM4-80GB GPU. Each measurement uses
two warmup runs followed by five timed runs, with CUDA
synchronization before and after each forward pass; we re-
port the median. Timing covers the encoder, recurrent up-
dates, prediction heads, cache accesses, and schedule tran-
sitions. Image loading, preprocessing, and evaluation align-
ment are outside the timed region. Step-specific merged
projections are precomputed before timing; their resident
storage contributes to inference memory usage. Compila-
tion with torch.compile is disabled. D´ej`a View and the
plain baseline are timed with the original implementation of
the block, and our schedules with our optimized implemen-
tation.

Controls. The uniform control spreads the steps of each op-

such layouts, train an adapter for 2,000 steps on each, and
keep the layout with the lowest distillation loss on held-out
training scenes, averaged over the budgets; this layout is
then trained with the full recipe. No benchmark image en-
ters the stability ranking or the selection. In all controls a
reuse step directly follows a full or a KV-pooled step, as
required by the cache (Sec. B.1).

B.6. Scene sensitivity, latency and memory
The results in Sec. 5.4 use two different units of aggrega-
tion. The benchmark summary weights datasets equally.
The win, tie and loss counts below give one vote to each
scene.
Scene-level outcomes. Against the official 16-step model,
the quality, balanced, and fast tiers obtain 49/21/83,
41/13/99, and 24/8/121 wins/ties/losses in AUC@3◦across
153 scenes, using an absolute difference below 0.2 points
to define a tie. The balanced tier’s scene-weighted mean
difference is −1.61 points. Its losses are particularly pro-
nounced on ETH3D, where it loses on 12 of 13 scenes.
Camera trajectories on ETH3D. Figure S5 shows what
these losses look like. We run each model on all views of
an ETH3D scene and align the predicted camera centers to
the ground truth with a similarity transform. Across the 13
scenes, the position error is 0.4–2.8% of the scene extent
for D´ej`a View, 0.5–3.0% for the quality tier, and 0.4–3.4%
for the balanced tier. The losses of these two tiers are a
small, uniform increase of the camera error, and the trajec-
tories remain intact. The fast tier stays within 1.2–4.1% on
12 scenes and breaks on meadow, where several of the 15
cameras are misplaced.
Scene characteristics. Grouping scenes by within-dataset
all-view covisibility tertiles yields balanced-tier AUC dif-
ferences of −2.50, −1.19, and −1.15 points for the low,
middle, and high groups, respectively.
Each group con-
tains 51 scenes. For the fast tier, the corresponding dif-
ferences are −7.25, −4.52, and −4.61. Lower covisibil-
ity is associated with larger losses in these measurements.
For example, the fast tier reduces AUC from 72.7 to 51.5
on ScanNet++ scene 25f3b7a318, which lies in the low-
covisibility group.
Scaling with the number of views. Table S16 times each
stage on ScanNet++ inputs of 24 to 300 views. The encoder
and the prediction heads cost the same for all configura-
tions, and the loop dominates as the view count grows: it
takes 68% of the D´ej`a View latency at 24 views and 93% at
300 views. The speedup of every tier therefore grows with
the view count, from 1.21×, 1.38×, and 1.69× at 24 views

For the benchmark settings in Tab. 3, all three tiers use

Where the additional memory goes. Table S17 measures
peak memory in a fresh process for each configuration, with
eight-frame decoder chunks. At 300 views, the balanced
tier with all step weights precomputed peaks at 12.61 GiB
against 8.49 GiB for D´ej`a View, an increase of 4.12 GiB.
Merging the weights when each step starts reduces the peak

the multistep update history and the cached attention output

Together, per-step merging and early release save 1.86
GiB at 300 views and preserve bit-identical outputs, leaving

comparison. Latency is nearly unchanged: 11.85 seconds

weights.
At 24 views, both per-step and lean execution

weights. Applying the low-rank adapters at run time with-

C.3. State and pose deviations at native perturba-
tion magnitude
State attenuation and task deviation. We compare KV-
pooling errors with eight random directions matched to the
error norm of each token, using the original 16-step model
on 65 scenes with at most 32 views. At step 12, the struc-
tured perturbation leaves a smaller final state deviation than
all eight random controls in 62 scenes. Its rotation deviation
exceeds the random-control median in 50 scenes; both prop-
erties hold simultaneously in 47 scenes. The median scene-
wise state-deviation ratio is 0.75, while the median rotation-
deviation ratio is 1.26, with an interquartile range of 1.01–
1.56. At step 7, structured perturbations leave smaller state
deviations in all 65 scenes, but exceed the random-control
median rotation deviation in only five. The median rotation
ratio is 0.59, with an interquartile range of 0.46–0.75.
The task measurements also vary with position.
At
step 12, structured perturbations reduce AUC@3◦against
ground truth by 1.91 points, compared with 1.51 points for
the random controls, computed by taking the median across
directions within each scene and then averaging across
scenes. At step 7, the corresponding reductions are 0.59 and
2.52 points. A smaller final state deviation can therefore ac-
company either a smaller or a larger task change, depending
on the intervention.
C.4. Task recovery in the adapted model
The adapted 14-step model also exhibits position-dependent
task costs.
KV pooling at step 2 ends with a median
AUC@3◦change of −0.1 points, compared with −0.8 for
step 7 and −4.1 for step 12. Reuse at these positions ends at
−0.4, −1.4, and −3.5 points, respectively. These analysis
scenes and view counts differ from the official benchmark
protocol used for the budget curves.
C.5. Additional pooling in an adapted schedule
Sensitivity within an adapted schedule. The evaluated
adapted 16-step configuration uses coarse-grid updates at
steps 1–8, KV pooling at steps 9–12, and exact, reuse,
exact, and reuse operations at steps 13–16.
Reapplying
reuse at steps 14 and 16 leaves the configured computa-
tion unchanged; these steps therefore provide no additional
reuse intervention.
The remaining exact global-attention
evaluations are particularly sensitive: replacing them with
pooling at steps 13 and 15 reduces AUC@3◦by 8.94 and
6.30 points, respectively; replacing them with reuse reduces
AUC by 3.52 and 4.62 points.
This schedule exhibits a
concentration of sensitivity at its remaining exact attention
steps.

C.7. Repeated failures on paired ScanNet++ inputs
The paired view-count study in Tab. S8 identifies a subset of
ScanNet++ scenes with large pooling-induced AUC losses
in the adapted model. These failures recur with 100-view
and 151–300-view inputs. Added early pooling overlaps the
adapted model’s coarse-grid phase, making the existing op-
erator schedule part of the intervention context. Scene-level
task metrics and the operations already assigned to each step
are therefore relevant when transferring a placement rule to
an adapted model.
