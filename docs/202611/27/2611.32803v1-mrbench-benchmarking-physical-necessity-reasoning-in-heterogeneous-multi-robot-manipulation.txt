Abstract
Recent advances in multi-robot manipulation have shown
how robots can collaborate to accomplish complex tasks.
However, successful coordination does not reveal which
robots are actually needed. The same task may require dif-
ferent teams as robot capabilities change, resources become
unavailable, or objects replace robotic support.
We ar-
gue that reliable multi-robot manipulation requires physical
necessity reasoning: understanding what a task demands,
which resources can fulfill those demands, and which are
truly necessary. We introduce MRBench, a benchmark for
physical necessity reasoning in heterogeneous multi-robot
manipulation. It comprises eight tasks with distinct concur-
rency and reach constraints, each evaluated under five con-
figurations that change the required resources while pre-
serving the task goal. Our evaluation reveals that existing
coordination agents perform well with reference teams but
struggle when robot embodiments change or passive objects
can replace robotic support. To address this gap, we pro-
pose MRScissors, which separates task requirements from
measured physical capabilities and identifies minimal fea-
sible teams through constrained search. Across two model
backbones, MRScissors achieves 85.4% and 91.2% team-
selection accuracy, outperforming the strongest baselines
by 10.4 and 9.5 points, respectively. Together, these find-
ings expose a critical gap between coordinating robots and
understanding their physical necessity.

1. Introduction
“Plurality is never to be posited without neces-
sity.” — William of Ockham
Recent advances in foundation-model agents have enabled
robots to coordinate manipulation tasks through task de-

composition, collaborative planning, and shared execu-
tion [4, 6, 28, 29, 36]. Yet successful coordination does
not reveal which robots are actually needed. In practical
workcells, robots may become unavailable, be replaced by
different models, or have their roles taken over by simple
fixtures. This raises a question beyond coordination: Which
physical capabilities does a task truly require, and which
robots or objects are necessary to provide them?
The answer depends on the task’s physical constraints
rather than robot count alone. Some tasks require several
independent actions at once, while others span locations be-
yond a single arm’s reach (Fig. 1A). Crucially, required
physical roles do not always require additional robots.
As shown in Fig. 1B, the same tomato-flume task needs
three arms under its reference configuration, becomes in-
feasible when a key arm is removed, but can be completed
with two when a different arm reaches farther or a suitable
chute replaces the missing support. The task goal remains
unchanged, yet its minimal feasible team changes. This mo-
tivates physical necessity reasoning: determining what a
task demands, what the available resources can provide, and
which combination is minimally sufficient.
Classical multi-robot task allocation studies capability-
based team formation [18, 30], while recent embodied
agents extend robot selection and coordination to heteroge-
neous teams [4, 27, 29, 56]. However, existing benchmarks
largely evaluate coordination ability with fixed teams or se-
lection from available robots [36, 42]. They do not sys-
tematically test whether agents can infer task requirements,
assess physical capabilities, and recognize when passive
objects can replace robotic roles. A successful execution
proves that a team is sufficient, not that it is necessary.
To investigate this gap, we introduce MRBench, a
benchmark for physical necessity reasoning in heteroge-
neous multi-robot manipulation. MRBench comprises eight
tasks spanning two sources of multi-arm necessity: Con-
currency and Reach constraints (Fig. 2). Each task is eval-
uated under five configurations—exact, surplus, shortfall,
swapped, and borrow—that preserve the goal while chang-
ing the available physical resources. Ground-truth minimal
teams are computed from geometric constraints and exact
search, with feasible solutions validated in Isaac Sim [39].
This design tests whether agents can distinguish necessary,
redundant, missing, and replaceable robotic support.
Our evaluation reveals a striking gap: agents that coor-
dinate reference teams successfully often struggle when
physical resources change. Existing coordination meth-
ods achieve high accuracy under reference configurations,
yet degrade substantially when robot embodiments are
swapped or passive objects can replace robotic roles. To
address this gap, we propose MRScissors, which separates
task requirements from measured physical capabilities. A
team-independent task representation identifies the required

2. Related Work
Benchmarks for multi-robot tasks.
Benchmarks have
grown from one arm [34] to two [5, 19, 25, 40, 59] and sev-
eral, with tasks built around coordination: arms that share a
table (RoCoBench [36]), compositional constraints (Robo-
Factory [42]), humanoid teams (CoHuB [41]) and a hu-
man partner (PARTNR [2]); other suites vary scenes, in-
structions, models or harnesses on a fixed task [7, 12, 14,
15, 54, 60]. Their tightly coupled tasks, which no single
robot can finish alone for lack of hands or of reach, come
2


with the team, so a successful run shows that the team suf-
fices, not that each arm is needed. VIKI-Bench [27] and
GroundAct [52] do ask which robots a task needs, but have
no infeasible task; to our knowledge, no benchmark asks for
the fewest arms of a tightly coupled task and accepts that no
team exists. MRBench does both (Tab. 1): five configura-
tions per task change the answer under one instruction, as in
contrast sets [17], and 14 of its 40 versions have no feasible
team, like the unanswerable questions of SQuAD 2.0 [43].
Planners and agents for multi-robot tasks.
Most multi-
robot manipulation work plans for a team that the task sup-
plies [10, 11, 13, 16, 20, 22, 31, 32, 47–49, 51, 58], often
on tightly coupled tasks, and work that does choose arms
starts from full models or stated requirements [3, 18, 21,
23, 30, 37, 38, 46, 53]. Foundation-model agents instead
decide at run time what each robot does: RoCo [36] lets
arm agents talk until they agree on subtasks and waypoints,
HMAS-2 [6] has a central model propose actions that each
robot checks, and SMART-LLM [29] forms coalitions from
skills and may leave robots unassigned or decline to plan;
others coordinate by messages, shared programs, or plan-
ners and verifiers [4, 9, 26, 28, 33, 35, 45, 50, 55–57], and
single agents learn to ask for help or refuse [1, 8, 24, 44].
These works score success, steps, tokens or utilization, but
not how many arms a task needs or how many model ses-
sions deciding takes. MRScissors addresses both by split-
ting the decision: a model blind to the arms writes the jobs
the task needs, code measures what every arm can do, and
an exact search returns the fewest arms with a certificate of
why each is needed, all in about four sessions.
3. MRBench
MRBench asks which arms, or which passive objects in
their place, a task truly needs, when the goal stays fixed
and the minimal feasible team changes. Eight tasks (Fig. 2)
in five configurations give 40 versions and 480 scenes.

3.2. Task design
Concurrency and Reach.
The two conditions of Eq. (2)
give two suites of four tasks. The Concurrency tasks need
three hands at once, one more than a two-handed robot has:
two bodies held while a third hand works (Con-01, Con-02),
one held while two work (Con-03), or three held together
(Con-04). In the Reach tasks no two arms act at once, but
no fixed arm reaches every place: bodies pass between arms
by being set down, or each arm works where only it reaches
(Fig. 2). We leave out loads too heavy for one arm: privi-
leged grasps do not model grip or payload.
Five configurations.
They change only a task’s doers;
each but the reference defeats a shortcut that skips measur-
ing. (i) The reference exact needs all three arms. (ii) surplus
adds a redundant Franka at N; it defeats staffing by presence.
(iii) shortfall removes a needed arm, so no team is feasi-
ble; the answer must name the job without a doer; it defeats
presumed feasibility. (iv) swapped trades one arm for an-
other embodiment, which can leave every place staffed yet
the task infeasible, or let two arms do it; it defeats roles
by place. (v) borrow swaps a needed arm for a prop that
can do its job and a distractor that cannot, alike but for one
measured property; it defeats substitution by appearance.

Design rules.
The instruction gives only the goal: no arm,
role, step or tool. Easy and hard levels differ in one parame-
ter; success is judged by state predicates, not by a method’s
claims; physics is tuned on ten other tasks and a prototype.
3.3. Benchmark construction
Workcell and scenes.
One table has three fixed places,
west (W) and east (E) 1.4 m apart with south (S) between,
and a fourth, north (N), for the surplus arm; each takes
a Franka, a PiPER or an SO-101. We simulate in Isaac
Sim 4.5 with Isaac Lab 2.1 [39]; grasps are privileged but
refused on a body wider than the open fingers unless a sec-
ond hand presses its far side. Each version has 12 scenes
(two levels, six seeds): the seed picks the room, recolors
the task’s objects and adds three everyday items.
Ground truth is computed.
The ground-truth (gold) an-
swer of a scene comes from IK with margins (Eq. (1)) and
an exact search for the smallest feasible teams (Eq. (3)). In
Isaac Sim the scripted references succeed in all 312 feasible
scenes and the distractors fail as designed in all 96 borrow
scenes; the solver proves infeasibility, and every two-arm
attempt in Isaac reports the job left undone.
Audits.
Each claim that an arm is necessary holds under
IK with margins or, where IK cannot decide, in Isaac. Fixed
rules, which never measure, do well per version but not per
task: the best (count the arms, take the larger object, call
any team with an SO-101 infeasible) gets 25 of the 96 task-
scenes right, so task-level accuracy is the headline.
3.4. Protocol and metrics
The protocol removes four conveniences that multi-robot
benchmarks offer and real workcells do not. (i) No team is
given: a method gets the goal, each arm’s model, place and
orientation, and the objects, but no role, skill list or reach.
(ii) Feasibility is not promised: 14 of the 40 versions are in-
feasible, and “infeasible: these jobs cannot all have a doer”
is then the answer. (iii) Names do not decide: a prop and
its distractor look alike, and masses are given only within a
factor of 0.75–1.3, so fitness must be measured. (iv) Fail-
ure is not proof: a failed run’s failure report names the jobs
whose signals fired, and only such a signal, not a missed
grasp, counts against a smaller team; reading any failure as
a need for more arms is a fifth shortcut, failure as proof.

4. Baselines
Prior methods answer neighboring questions: how a given
team cooperates [6, 36], or which robots get which sub-
tasks [4, 29]. We adapt each by one rule, change what a
method takes in and gives out, never how it reasons: all
get one output form, passive candidates and the same tools,
split by role, and differ in who decides, where they learn
what an arm can do, and what they revise after failure.
References and a solver.
Oracle-script replays the gold
team’s scripted reference as the ceiling for SR; Oracle-team
hands the gold team to the executor, so it fails only in execu-
tion. In LLM + solver, as in LLM+P [33] and FLEET [45],
the model translates the scene into jobs, groups and doers
(Sec. 3.1), and exact search returns the fewest arms or “in-
feasible”, so its errors are translation errors.
Prior methods, adapted.
We adapt one method per way
of organizing a team: a fixed pipeline (SMART-LLM), per-
arm dialogue (RoCo) and a central proposal with arm ve-
toes (HMAS-2). EMOS [4] allocates centrally from robot
resumes, Tool-RoCo [56] activates arm agents centrally or
lets them join on their own, and CoEnv [28] coordinates
a given team, so each falls under one of these. SMART-
LLM-style [29] runs its three stages, decomposition, coali-
tion formation and allocation, as three sessions and keeps
its released instruction to use fewest robots; coalitions are
formed by querying the arms, and a subtask without one
makes the task infeasible. RoCo-style [36] gives each arm
its own model, which sees the fixed cameras and its own
wrist camera, queries only its own kinematics and may de-
cline to take part; the arms talk in turn, up to three rounds,
until all agree on a plan, which may declare the task infea-
sible. HMAS-2-style [6] has a central model, which sees the
scene, not the arms’ kinematics, propose team, idle arms,
objects and feasibility; each arm accepts or vetoes its part
with its own can reach, for up to three rounds.
Fair comparison.
Every method hands its answer to one
shared executor, after CaP-X [15]: a fresh session of the
same model receives the whole answer (the arms used and
idle, the objects, the steps and the constraints the method
names), writes a program for the declared arms, checks
it with check program and runs it with try in sim.
Oracle-team hands it the gold team and objects without
steps, and a feasible scene answered as infeasible counts
as failed. Under the equal budget, every method receives
the same failure report after a failed run and may change
the team. On each backbone all methods share information,
tools, output form, budget and tuning effort.

Measured capability table.
Code first makes the graph
concrete: a body the prover judges too wide for one hand
becomes two acts in one group, and a carry is checked
where the gripped part arrives. It then fills the table ˆc of
Eq. (1) from tool results alone: who can for every arm
at every site, meet for every pair of arms on a carry, and
measure for every bound of every object. A reach cell
reads can, cannot or, when the arm falls short by at most
2 cm (the tool–simulator gap), near; an object passes only
if all its bounds hold. Each cell keeps its evidence.
Exact search and certificate.
On ˆJ, ˆG and ˆc, code solves
Eq. (3) with the search of LLM + solver (Sec. 4): it tries
subsets of arms from one arm up and returns every smallest
team in which each job has a doer and the jobs of a group
distinct doers, using an object only where hands fall short.
The two methods thus differ only in where the graph and
the table come from. The answer is a certificate: what each
arm’s removal leaves undone; each object used or rejected,
with its measurement; the jobs that cannot all have a doer
when no team exists; and the near cells counted as reached.
Stand-in objects.
Only when the search finds no team
does MRScissors look for more objects: the prover, still
blind, is told which jobs lack a doer and asked what on the
table could take them over. It may add objects, each with its
bounds and placing job, but may change no other job; the
new objects are measured and the search reruns.
Review with unequal burdens.
The graph is the one part
not measured, so it is reviewed. Code flips each element
(drops a job, takes it out of its group, moves a site, drops
a bound) and marks those whose flip changes the answer.
Two reviewer sessions, blind to each other, read the graph,
the certificate and the marks; one looks for what was written
easier than the task, the other for what was written harder.
Each edit must cite a tool call of its own session; code ap-
plies it and redraws the certificate. An edit that changes
nothing is dropped, one after which the task needs more
arms or has no team is kept, and any other must quote the
words of the goal that allow it and pass an arbiter session.
An episode takes about four sessions (Tab. 2): prover, two
reviewers and, when needed, stand-in pass and arbiter.

MRScissors names the right team most often on both

6.3. From the right team to task success
Right teams carry different solutions.
The executor car-
ries out a method’s whole answer (Sec. 4), so SR and MSS-
SR score its solution as well as its team. MRScissors’ steps,

other methods the model writes them. On all 312 feasible
episodes, MRScissors succeeds with a right team (MSS-SR)

24.0% and 24.0–27.4%.
Oracle-team, handed the gold
team, reaches 58.7% and 60.3% (Tab. 3). With a right team,
MRScissors’ own episodes succeed in 55.9% and 58.8%,

7. Conclusion
Before arms can cooperate, an agent must decide how many
a task needs, and that decision must rest on measurement.
MRBench turns it into a test: eight tasks on heteroge-
neous arms, five configurations each that change the mini-
mal feasible team under one goal, and audited gold answers.
MRScissors answers it from measured evidence: a solver
choosing over a measured table names the right team in
85.4% and 91.2% of episodes on two backbones, against at
most 75.0% and 81.7% for the other methods. Yet MRScis-
sors gets all five configurations right in at most 59.4% of
the task-scenes and misses at least a third of the borrow
episodes: what can stand in for an arm is the open problem.

A. Appendix
A.1. Task cards
Each card gives the instruction, the arms at W/S/E, the pa-
rameter that sets the two levels, why three arms are needed,
the scripted reference with its duration in Isaac Sim at the
easy / hard level (seed 0; a single figure when both levels
take the same time), the success predicate, checked on the
objects’ states, and what the necessity audit checks. The
instruction is given as in the default scene: in the varied
scenes it names the task’s objects by the colors the seed
gives them, and Rea-01 and Rea-04 name their level’s num-
ber of fruit and wipe time. Instructions state the goal, and
three also bound the process: Rea-02’s tomato must roll, not
be carried, and success asks it to reach the pot within 5 s of
leaving the shelf; Rea-03’s line must be whole when it is set
off; Rea-04’s sponge must stay on each spill for the wipe
time. The gold answers are smallest under these bounds.
Figures 4 and 5 show each reference in a varied scene.
Con-01, spring doors.
“Take the orange box out of the
cabinet and set it on the green mat, with both doors closed.”
Three Frankas; box width 23 / 26 cm. A cabinet stands
across the middle of the table, its doorway facing S. In
front, two spring doors as high as the box open inward un-
der a fixed ceiling; each is worked by a low lever outside it,
a bar beside the side wall with a knob. The box is wider than
one door’s way and deeper and taller than the slot over the
ceiling, so no turning gets it out: both doors must be held
open while a third hand draws the box out by its T-handle,
whose post runs in a narrow slot in the ceiling. Reference
(35.0 s): W and E each swing their lever round and hold
their door at 88°; S draws the box out under the ceiling,
takes it again from above, holds it up while the doors close,
and sets it on the mat. Success. The box has left the cabinet
and rests on the mat with both doors closed, for 1 s and to
the end of the run. Audit. The ceiling and the T-handle keep
every link of the retriever clear of the doors (IK: at least
1.4 cm at 81–88°). With W+S alone, the east door stays
shut and blocks the box. Grasps refuse the box’s 23–26 cm
face.
Con-02, hinged leaves.
“Pin the blue and the orange leaf
together through all their slots with the grey pin, so that they
hold each other level between their posts.” Three Frankas;
slot clearance 6 / 3 mm. Two leaves hinged on posts west
and east meet in the air as a clevis inside a glass guard; their
slots line up only while both are held level, since, let go,
the west leaf hangs down and the east one springs up. Each
leaf enters the guard through a slot in its side wall, and only
the arm at its end reaches its bar; the pin goes in through
a small opening in the south wall. Reference (25.2 s): W
and E turn their leaves level and hold them; S slides the pin
through the opening and the three slots; pinned, the leaves
hold each other level. Success. The pin runs through all
three slots and both leaves are level within 3°, for 1 s. Audit.
Hinges and guard leave no two-arm route, such as pinning
the parts on the table and lifting them (IK scans: no other
arm reaches a leaf; one arm holding both comes no nearer
than 17 cm). Geometry rules out setting the pin, a flat key,
in one leaf’s slot and using it as a lever on the other, so this
route is excluded without a run.
Con-03, spring lid and basketball.
“Take the orange
ball out of the box with the spring lid and set it in the
cradle north of the box.”
Three Frankas; lid spring 0.3
/ 0.6 N m/rad. A long box with slick, lipped inner walls

Isolation.
Every session is started without access to the
benchmark’s code: either through a command-line agent
with all of its own tools switched off, or, for the main ta-
ble, through the model’s API with our own loop. In both,
the only tools are the environment’s, and the context holds
only the method’s prompts, the tools and their replies.
An episode, step by step.
In Rea-01 the agent receives
only the goal and the briefing. It looks at the table and lo-
cates the apple, the crate, the partition and the pads; it writes
the jobs (take the apple at the start, carry it past the partition,
put it in the crate); it asks who reaches each site, finding that
only W reaches the start, only E the crate, and that the par-
tition leaves no place both end arms reach; and it submits
W+S+E with a reason for each arm. The shared execu-
tor then writes a program in phases for the declared arms,
check program checks its paths, and try in sim runs
it; after a failed run the executor may write a new program,
up to three runs, but nothing goes back to the method. The
scored team is the declared arms plus any arm that moved.

Why adapt.
SMART-LLM can leave robots out and re-
fuses in prose tasks whose skills are missing, but it does not
score infeasibility. Its released prompt already asks for the
fewest robots. RoCo’s WAIT pauses an arm only for a step,
and its broken-gripper example concerns the rest of the task.
EMOS tests reach quantitatively and can leave robots unas-
signed, but reports no feasibility slack. HMAS-2 checks
each assigned action but has no objective over the whole
team. VIKI-R scores selected sets and plans, not physical
task success, and its box-support example does not show
that an arm can be left out. Two UR5e arms share a task in
RoCo, HMAS-2 runs three or six arms of one kind, and one
VIKI-R example activates two Panda arms; EMOS teams
robots of four different types. RoCo, EMOS and VIKI-R
revise a plan only from checks made before anything is ex-
ecuted: validation feedback, each robot’s check against its
resume, and a plan checker. HMAS-2 also re-plans when
an executed action fails, as when a box slips from a gripper.
Our adaptations give them the common outputs and passive
candidates of MRBench.
Revision after a failed run.
Under the equal budget, ev-
ery row receives the same failure report and may change
its team. SMART-LLM-style puts the last plan and the re-
port into the prompt of its first stage, decomposition, and
reruns all three stages. RoCo-style sends the report to all
arms as an environment message and opens a new round of
dialogue; as in the first, the latest proposal is submitted once
all agree or after three rounds. HMAS-2-style sends the re-
port to the central model and to every arm and opens a new
round of proposal and veto; a plan still vetoed after three
rounds is submitted as it stands. LLM + solver’s search
takes arms, passive objects and transfers between arms. Af-
ter a failed run a new session receives the last plan and the
report, writes the job graph again, and the solver solves
again. In every row, a failure of execution alone may be
repaired by changing only the program.

A.7. Prompts
Each model session opens with a prompt as its system mes-
sage: a line naming the session’s role, the shared part where
the prompt includes {COMMON}, and the method’s own in-
structions. We give key passages of the shared part and of
each method’s own instructions, verbatim; [...] marks
omitted text, a bracketed label names the session and is not
sent to the model, and the full prompts will be released with
the code. Braced fields are filled before the call: {ARM}
and {ARMS} with an arm’s name and the team’s, {SIDE}
with one of a reviewer’s two sides. Images reach a session
only through look, and the tools are those of Tab. 8. The
prompts call passive objects things.
Shared part.
It states the question and the form of the
answer, the same for every method.
Shared part ({COMMON})
## The question
A team of robot arms is mounted round one table,
with things on it and a goal. The question is how
big a team the goal needs:
- which arms are needed -- the fewest that can do
the whole task -- and which can stay idle;
- whether a thing on the table can stand in for an
arm [...];
- or that the task cannot be done with what is here,
and what is missing.
## The answer: `submit_team_plan`
- `feasible`; `arms_used` (the fewest arms that do
it; empty if it cannot be done); `idle` (every
other arm, each with why);
- `things_used` (each with the job it does and, if
it takes over an arm's job, which); [...]
- `missing` (if it cannot be done: which job has no
doer, and what kind of arm or thing would do it);
- `steps` (who does what, and what happens at the
same time); `key_constraints` (what forces the
number of arms, with numbers).
[...]
Baselines.
SMART-LLM-style runs its three stages as
three sessions, each receiving the output of the stages be-
fore it. In RoCo-style each arm speaks in a session of its
own, in the order W, S, E, N of the arms present, for at most
three rounds, and receives the dialogue so far and the latest
proposal. In HMAS-2-style the central planner receives the
plan board, its earlier proposals with the arms’ answers, and
each arm checks the new proposal in a session of its own,
for at most three rounds. LLM + solver is one session.
Baselines: own instructions
[SMART-LLM-style / decomposition]
Look at the scene (`briefing`, `look`, `locate`, `
measure`) and break the goal into subtasks. [...]
Do not assign arms yet.
[SMART-LLM-style / coalition formation]
For each subtask, find who can do it:
- **arms**: query each arm about the subtask's
skills -- `can_reach` at its place, pointing the
way it needs (read the margin), `read_arm` for the
fingers' opening, `heft` where a mass decides it;
- **things** on the table that could do it instead
of an arm [...]: `measure` them and say why each
would or would not do.
A subtask that no arm and no thing can do gets an
empty coalition.
[SMART-LLM-style / task allocation]
Allocate each subtask to a member of its coalition,
using the minimum number of robots, so that
subtasks that must happen at the same time get
different doers and every subtask's order is kept.
[...] If some subtask's coalition is empty, the
task cannot be done: say which subtask, and what
kind of arm or thing would do it.
[RoCo-style / each arm]
You are the {ARM} arm, one of the robot arms
({ARMS}) mounted round one table, each run by its
own copy of you [...]. You talk with the other
arms to agree on a team plan; nothing moves until
all of you agree on one.
[...]
You can query only your own joints (`read_arm`) and
your own kinematics (`can_reach`); ask the others
about theirs in your message.
[...]
- `data.taking_part`: whether you take part (you may
choose not to);
[...]
The dialogue ends when every arm agrees with one
proposal.
[HMAS-2-style / central planner]
You propose the whole plan; each arm checks its own
part with its own kinematics and accepts or vetoes
it. You do not query the arms' kinematics yourself
[...] If the plan board shows vetoes, revise the
plan to answer them.
[HMAS-2-style / each arm]
Check your own part with your own tools -- `
can_reach` at the places it asks you to work (read
the margin), `read_arm`, [...] -- or, if it leaves
you idle, whether that is right. Then `respond`:
accept or veto (`data.accept`), the reason, and
your evidence (the tool results).
[LLM + solver]
You translate the task into a job graph; a solver

MRScissors.
The prover receives the briefing and the
scene graph without the arms and, of the hands, only the
widest opening; it has no reach tool, and its camera pictures
show the whole table, arms included. The stand-in pass re-
ceives the job graph and the jobs without a doer; an object
it adds comes with the job of placing it, and the jobs al-
ready written stay as they are. The two reviewers share one
prompt, with {SIDE} filled by one of two sides, and receive
the goal, the job graph, the certificate and the flip-tested el-
ements; the arbiter receives the goal, the job graph and the
edits in dispute.
MRScissors: own instructions
[prover]
You model a task on a table as a **job graph**: what
the goal asks to be done, where, and what must
happen at the same time. Robot arms will do it,
but you are not told which arms there are, where
they stand or what they reach -- that is measured
afterwards, by code, for every arm at every place
you name. [...]
- **jobs** -- what must be done, and where a hand
(or a thing standing in for one) must be to do it.
[...]
- **group** -- jobs with the same group name are
done at the same time, each by its own hand. [...]
- **things** -- a body on the table that is not what
the goal names, which the plan puts in place so
that the task works [...] list every candidate
under `things` with the sizes that decide, and
code measures each and keeps the one that fits.
[...]
Write every job the goal needs; one the goal does
not need is a mistake too.
[stand-in pass]
Your one question now: **is there a body on the
table that would do any of these jobs once put in
place?** [...] The jobs themselves stay as you
wrote them: you cannot change, drop or add a job
here. [...]
[reviewer]
Every cell (who reaches where, who meets whom) is a
tool result. [...] What may be wrong is what the
model wrote: a job missing or not needed, a place
written where it is not, what is or is not done at
once, who may do a job, a thing's spec. [...]
{SIDE}
[...]
Each edit needs `evidence`: the tool result it rests
on, **from your own calls in this session**; an
edit whose evidence names no tool you called is
refused. An edit that makes the task easier also
needs `quote`: the words of the goal it rests on,
copied exactly.
[...]
Code applies each edit, measures what it names and
draws the answer again. An edit that changes
nothing is dropped. One that makes the task harder
for the arms is kept on its evidence. One that
makes it easier is not kept on its own: it goes,
with its quote and evidence, to an arbiter, who
keeps it only if the goal and the scene bear it
out.
[{SIDE}: too easy]
Look for what the graph leaves out or writes easier
than the task is:
- a job the goal needs that is missing [...];
- jobs that must be done at once and are not in one
group. [...]
[{SIDE}: too hard]
Look for what the graph asks that the goal does not:
- a job the goal does not need;
- jobs in one group that need not be done at once
(one could be done and let go before the other);
[...]
- a job no hand can do that a thing on the table
would do once put in place [...]
[arbiter]
For each unit of edits, decide one thing: does the
goal, read as written, and the scene, as the tools
show it, bear the edit out? Keep it only if they
do. If you cannot tell, refuse it: the model as
written stands.
