Abstract
Pretrained latent diffusion models are effective priors for
transfer learning, from geometry estimation to physics sim-
ulation. Existing approaches fine-tune the diffusion back-
bone but freeze the encoder of the VAE to preserve the
latent distribution. However, a frozen encoder may miss
salient details in non-RGB inputs and is limited to three
channels. We introduce Diffusion Prior Anchoring (DPA),
a regularizer applied during VAE fine-tuning that uses a
score-distillation update to steer latents toward the high-
density manifold on which the pretrained diffusion model
denoises accurately.
We validate DPA across three im-
age and video models (FLUX 3, LTX-Video, Wan2.2) and
four tasks: monocular depth estimation, MRI reconstruc-
tion, wind-flow simulation, and surface normal estimation,
where DPA lowers the prediction error by 7.5% relative
to the frozen VAE, 4.9% to decoder-only fine-tuning, and
4.0% to unanchored VAE fine-tuning. Its gain grows the
further the target data lies from the RGB training distribu-
tion, from 3.3% on monocular depth estimation to 7.2% on
complex-valued MRI. DPA also applies to modified VAEs,
including pruned and multi-channel variants. With a four-
channel VAE that encodes depth and surface normals, it
lowers depth estimation error by 9.3%.
1. Introduction
Latent diffusion models pretrained on large-scale image
and video collections provide powerful generative priors
for transfer learning to downstream tasks.
Fine-tuned
on datasets far smaller than their pretraining corpora,
they accurately estimate monocular depth and surface nor-
mals [22, 29, 46], simulate interactive environments in real
time [23, 60], and serve as fast surrogates for physics sim-
ulations [47]. In each case, transfer learning exploits the
structural prior that the diffusion backbone has learned in
the latent space of a separately trained VAE [51].
However, this paradigm rests on the assumption that the
pretrained VAE accurately reconstructs the target domain.
In practice, this confines adaptation to three-channel inputs
and outputs that resemble natural RGB images or videos.
In many domains of interest this assumption does not hold.
Geometric maps, complex-valued MRI scans, and physical
velocity fields share the spatial and temporal layout of nat-
ural images and videos, but fall outside the RGB pretrain-
ing distribution. Consequently, a frozen VAE reconstructs
their pseudo-RGB encoding poorly. Modalities with more
than three channels, such as joint depth and surface normals,
cannot be encoded at all.
To unlock latent diffusion models for such domains one
should adapt the VAE alongside the diffusion backbone. In

current practice only the VAE decoder is fine-tuned, while
the encoder is kept frozen [46, 47, 60] to preserve the ex-
pected latent distribution for the pretrained diffusion model.
However, a decoder cannot recover information that the
frozen encoder has not learned to preserve. Jointly updat-
ing both the encoder and the decoder might seem the obvi-
ous solution, except that fine-tuning the VAE solely for re-
construction is unconstrained with respect to the diffusion
model. Latents thus drift away from the manifold on which
the diffusion backbone was trained to denoise.
We propose Diffusion Prior Anchoring (DPA), which
regularizes VAE fine-tuning directly with the frozen dif-
fusion model, via score distillation sampling (SDS) [48].
At each training step, an encoded latent is perturbed with
noise, denoised by the frozen backbone, and pulled toward
its clean estimate (Fig. 1, left). This steers the encoder to-
ward latents that the pretrained prior recognizes and thus
can denoise accurately, while the reconstruction loss pre-
serves task-specific fidelity. DPA needs only forward passes
of the diffusion model and adds no inference cost.
We validate DPA across three image and video founda-
tion models (FLUX 3, LTX-Video, Wan2.2) and four di-
verse tasks (Sec. 4): surface normal estimation, monocular
depth estimation, accelerated MRI reconstruction, and CFD
urban wind-flow simulation. DPA further extends to multi-
modal prediction, where a four-channel VAE encodes depth
and surface normals in a single shared latent, and to VAEs
pruned by up to 80% of their parameters.
Across all tasks and backbones, diffusion models trained
on DPA-anchored latents outperform unanchored baselines,
and the gains are largest where they are most needed,
i.e., where the frozen VAE reconstructs the target domain
poorly. Systematic ablations show that anchoring is most
beneficial when the diffusion backbone is fine-tuned with
limited capacity, when multiple modalities share one latent,
and when the backbone serves as an unconditional genera-
tive prior for posterior sampling. Finally, we show that our
setup is competitive with specialized state-of-the-art depth
and surface normal estimators when trained with more data
and pixel-space supervision.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We introduce Diffusion Prior Anchoring (DPA), a
backpropagation-free regularizer that aligns the VAE with
the frozen diffusion prior, at zero inference cost.
• We systematically characterize when prior anchoring
helps most and show that DPA improves monocular depth
estimation, surface normal estimation, MRI reconstruc-
tion, and wind-flow simulation across three image and
video backbones (FLUX 3, LTX-Video, Wan2.2).
• We show that DPA supports changes to the VAE archi-
tecture, such as changing the number of input and output
channels and parameter pruning, while retaining the gain
of anchoring.
2. Related work
Repurposing latent diffusion models.
Latent diffusion
models for images [15, 51, 69] and videos [4, 20, 62, 63]
learn generative priors over the compressed latent space
of a pretrained VAE. Fine-tuned on modest amounts of
task-specific data, these foundation models become strong
dense predictors for monocular depth and surface nor-
mals [17, 22, 29, 30, 42, 46, 72] as well as material prop-
erties [31, 35, 39, 76]. Beyond static scene understanding,
pretrained image and video generators have been extended
into world models that simulate complex environments for
autonomous driving [18] and robotics [28, 43], game en-
gines that respond to user actions in real time [23, 38, 60],
and neural surrogates for physics simulators [47].
VAE adaptation for new modalities.
Existing ap-
proaches that repurpose latent diffusion models predomi-
nantly keep the pretrained VAE frozen [17, 18, 22, 29, 76].
This is natural for world models and game engines, which
synthesize RGB frames within the VAE’s pretraining dis-
tribution.
In contrast, dense predictors must cast non-
RGB targets into three-channel pseudo-images compatible
with the frozen encoder, e.g., by replicating single-channel
depth maps across RGB channels or mapping surface nor-
mals to color space [17, 22, 29, 72].
Multi-modal tar-
gets must be encoded into separate latents or predicted se-
quentially [17, 31, 35, 39, 76].
Even end-to-end pixel-
space fine-tuning of the diffusion backbone has kept the
VAE fixed [42]. Where the frozen VAE reconstructs tar-
get modalities poorly, recent methods fine-tune the decoder,
but keep the encoder frozen to preserve the latent distribu-
tion [46, 47, 60], with Marigold V2 [46] further backprop-
agating decoded pixel-space losses through the updated de-
coder into the diffusion model. In contrast, DPA enables
channel expansion and pruning of both encoder and de-
coder, without disrupting the pretrained diffusion prior.
Latent space regularization and alignment.
Several re-
cent works regularize the VAE latent space so that a diffu-
sion model fits it more easily. REPA [74] aligns the hidden
states of a DiT with features of a pretrained vision founda-
tion model, VA-VAE [71] applies this alignment to the VAE
latents, and REPA-E [37] uses it for joint end-to-end train-
ing of a VAE and a DiT from scratch. Other regularizers
enforce spatial [36] or scale equivariance [55]. These meth-
ods shape the latent space before or during the training of
a new diffusion model. Moreover, the alignment methods
rely on vision encoders that are themselves trained on natu-
ral RGB images and thus face the same domain gap as the
frozen VAE on non-RGB data. In contrast, DPA adapts the
VAE to an already pretrained diffusion model and uses this
frozen model itself as the regularizer.
2


Diffusion models as priors.
Score distillation sampling
(SDS) [48] and Score Jacobian Chaining [64] optimize 3D
representations by perturbing rendered views with noise and
descending along the denoising residual of a frozen text-to-
image diffusion model. Subsequent variants refine the dis-
tillation target or noise schedule [25, 26, 67], and related
score-matching gradients have been used to train one-step
generators [73] and personalized text-to-image adapters [1].
In inverse problems, diffusion posterior sampling [8] and
regularization by denoising (RED) [14, 41, 50] combine a
measurement consistency term with a denoiser or diffusion
model that acts as the prior. DPA shares this structure, pair-
ing the VAE reconstruction loss as a data fidelity term with
the SDS gradient of a frozen diffusion prior, but applies it to
regularize an encoder network over a dataset rather than op-
timizing individual reconstructions at test time. While dif-
fusion models have also been trained from scratch jointly
with VAEs as learned latent priors [24, 59, 68], DPA repur-
poses an existing pretrained diffusion transformer to regu-
larize VAE adaptation with forward passes alone.

3.1. Diffusion Prior Anchoring
When the VAE is fine-tuned to a new domain, it is typically
trained on LVAE, which combines a domain-space recon-
struction loss with KL regularization toward N(0, I). Cru-
cially, LVAE is agnostic to the pretrained diffusion model:
besides the (typically weak) KL term, the encoder is free
to map inputs to latent configurations that minimize recon-
struction error, regardless of whether the frozen model ϕ
can accurately denoise them.

tuning to the diffusion model by augmenting the training
objective with an anchoring regularizer A,

Perturbing z into zλ maps adapted latents that have

tents in regions that ϕ can accurately denoise.
To anchor latents to the marginal prior pϕ rather than
a specific condition, we query ϕ unconditionally. For the
image-editing and first-frame-conditioned video backbones
in our experiments, this corresponds to passing an empty
text prompt and omitting the reference image or first frame.
3.2. Noise floor and sampling schedule
Instantiating the anchor in Eq. (2) requires addressing latent
variations below the finest sampled noise level and specify-
ing how λ is sampled and weighted.
Noise floor.
The anchor samples log-SNR levels up to
λmax, corresponding to a minimum noise-to-signal ratio
σ0
= e−λmax/2.
Per-coordinate variations in z much
smaller than σ0 are drowned out by noise at every sampled
level and remain invisible to A. Without further constraint,
the encoder can store high-frequency task details in sub-σ0
variations, which lowers LVAE without increasing A. To
prevent this, we impose a latent noise floor [24] during VAE
training: the decoder reconstructs from ˜z + σ0ϵ0, where ˜z
is a sample from the encoder’s posterior and ϵ0 ∼N(0, I),
corrupting sub-σ0 variations before decoding so they can-
not reliably reduce reconstruction error. The floor is applied
only while the VAE is trained, so the DiT is trained on la-
tents without the noise floor.

4. Experiments
We first evaluate DPA on surface normal estimation against
alternative VAE adaptation strategies (Sec. 4.2), and use this
setting to ablate the anchor (Sec. 4.3) and analyze how it
changes the latent space (Sec. 4.4). We then modify the
VAE architecture itself, expanding its input and output lay-
ers so that a single shared latent encodes both depth and
surface normals (Sec. 4.5) and pruning it for faster encoding
and decoding (Sec. 4.6), and compare DPA models trained
at a larger scale with state-of-the-art methods (Sec. 4.7).
Finally, we evaluate whether DPA’s benefits transfer to
non-RGB inverse problems and video foundation models
through 12-fold accelerated MRI reconstruction (Sec. 4.8)
and CFD urban wind-flow simulation (Sec. 4.9).
4.1. Setup
Every experiment follows a two-stage pipeline: we first
adapt the VAE to the target domain and then fine-tune the
DiT from its pretrained weights on the resulting latents.
With identical data, reconstruction losses, and step bud-
gets across both stages, we compare four VAE variants:
(i) Frozen, the unmodified pretrained VAE, (ii) Decoder-
only, which fine-tunes D with E frozen, (iii) Control, which
fine-tunes E and D on LVAE without the anchor or noise
floor, and (iv) DPA, which fine-tunes E and D on LDPA.
Round-trip reconstruction (Rec.) evaluates D(E(x)) with-
out the noise floor across all variants, and DiT loss measures
the flow-matching velocity loss of the frozen, unconditional
pretrained DiT on the adapted latents prior to DiT fine-
tuning. Unless stated otherwise, DPA uses [λmin, λmax] =
[−8, 4] (σ0 = e−2 ≈0.135) and β = 1, and each config-
uration is trained once. Full training budgets and hyperpa-
rameters for all tasks are provided in the supplement.

4.4. Latent space analysis
Besides lowering the DiT loss, DPA shrinks the latents
(standard deviation 0.55 against 0.85 for the control) and
moves them further from those of the frozen encoder (rela-
tive distance 1.00 against 0.69, Tab. 2). Latents of smaller
scale are easier to denoise, but scale explains only part of
the lower DiT loss. Rescaled to the standard deviation of
the DPA latents, the control latents reach a DiT loss of
0.117, three times the 0.036 of DPA. In the latents of Fig. 2
(third row), DPA encodes flat surfaces such as the walls and
the floor as nearly uniform regions, whereas the latents of
the frozen and control VAEs vary at fine scales there, and
each DiT generates latents like those of its VAE. The frozen
VAE’s decoder (fourth row) shows that the control latents
remain close to the frozen VAE’s encoding, which treats the
normal map as an RGB image. For a normal map this RGB
encoding is arbitrary, and DPA changes it into one that suits
the DiT better, with a DiT loss of 0.036 instead of 0.190
(Tab. 2) and a lower prediction error (Tab. 1). To verify that
DPA’s gain stems from aligning latents with the diffusion
prior rather than simpler confounding factors, we evaluate
five modifications to the control VAE (latent rescaling, an
L2 penalty toward the frozen latents, matched FLOPs, an
angular loss, and VA-VAE alignment [71]). None improves
upon the control (see supplement).

4.5. VAE channel expansion for multi-modal data
Many domain-specific tasks in physics, medical imaging,
or 3D vision involve multi-modal data with more than three
channels. We study the effect of DPA on such tasks through
joint monocular depth and surface normal estimation. Ini-
tializing from the pretrained RGB kernels, we expand the
first and last layers of the VAE to four channels and fine-
tune it on concatenated depth and normal maps with and
without DPA. We then train a single DiT on this shared la-
tent and compare it against separate single-modality models
for depth and normals.
As shown in Tab. 4, DPA improves single-modality
depth estimation, though by a smaller relative margin than
on normals (3.3% vs. 4.0% in Tab. 1), likely because the
frozen VAE already reconstructs depth maps far better than
normal maps (Fig. 1). The multi-modal model is less accu-
rate on depth than the depth-only model, but slightly more
accurate on normals than the normals-only model, for both
the control and DPA. Interestingly, the gain of DPA over the
control is larger in the multi-modal model than with single
modalities, 0.61 vs. 0.19 on depth and 0.40◦vs. 0.34◦on
normals (differences are computed before rounding). This
again shows that DPA helps most where the data lies far
from the training distribution of the pretrained VAE. Tiling
two latents of the frozen VAE, an alternative way of han-
dling multiple modalities, matches the four-channel DPA
model on depth (6.01 vs. 6.02) but is 1.73◦less accurate on
normals and needs twice as many latent tokens (see supple-
ment).

4.10. Training cost
The anchor adds one forward pass of the frozen DiT per
VAE training step, which increases the VAE training time
by 39% for FLUX 3 and by 4% for LTX-Video, where the
video VAE step dominates the cost. To test whether this
additional compute is responsible for the gain of DPA, we
train a FLOPs-matched control on surface normals (1.9×
the VAE steps). It lowers the round-trip error from 1.54◦
to 1.42◦but not the prediction error (15.71◦vs. 14.98◦for
DPA), so the gain stems from the anchor (see supplement).
5. Conclusion
Adapting a latent diffusion model to a new modality usu-
ally keeps the VAE encoder frozen to preserve compatibil-
ity with the pretraining prior. On out-of-distribution inputs,
however, a frozen encoder loses salient detail and restricts
adaptation to modalities that can be mapped onto three RGB
channels. Diffusion Prior Anchoring (DPA) removes this
bottleneck by using the frozen diffusion model itself to reg-
ularize VAE training via score distillation. It needs only
forward passes of the frozen DiT, adds no cost at inference,
and supports adapted, channel-expanded, and pruned VAEs
across image and video backbones.
DPA improved downstream accuracy across every
modality we tested, with gains that correlate with the recon-
struction error of the frozen VAE (Fig. 1). Where the pre-
trained VAE already reconstructs inputs well, as in monoc-
ular depth estimation, the improvement is modest at 3.3%,
whereas domains with large round-trip error see substan-
tially larger gains, reaching 7.2% on MRI. Within a given
task, anchoring helps most when the model relies strongly
on the pretrained prior, such as under low-rank fine-tuning
or unconditional sampling, while its benefit narrows when
full fine-tuning has enough capacity to absorb latent drift.
Our results also raise open questions for future work.
First, although SDS is known to be mode-seeking, replacing
it in DPA with objectives that address this limitation [14, 67]
did not improve downstream accuracy in our supplementary
experiments. Combining SDS with the VAE reconstruction
loss may already prevent mode collapse, yet a more system-
atic study of score-distillation variants in this setting could
still yield stronger anchors. Second, the unconditional prior
proved a better anchor than one conditioned on the input
image, even though conditioning matches the downstream
setup more closely. Understanding this behavior, together
with anchoring to priors that already model the target task,
is a promising direction for future research.

Supplementary Material
This supplement follows the structure of the main paper.
Section A defines the gains and round-trip errors of Fig. 1,
Sec. B details the anchor of Sec. 3, and Secs. C to K give the
training details and additional results of Secs. 4.2 to 4.10.
Depth and normals are evaluated with the code and proto-
cols of Marigold V2 [46], including its RANSAC alignment
in log space for depth, so that Tab. 5 compares with its pub-
lished numbers. For surface normals, the indoor error is the
mean angular error over iBims-1, NYUv2, and ScanNet, the
three benchmarks of Tab. 1.
A. Gains across tasks
Figure 1 plots the relative gain of DPA over the control in
each task against the round-trip error of the frozen VAE in
that task. The gain is the relative reduction of the error met-
ric of the task, namely AbsRel for depth with one VAE per
modality (Tab. 4, 3.3%), the mean angular error for nor-
mals (Tab. 1, 4.0%), the test VRMSE for wind flows with
LTX-Video (Tab. 7, 5.2%), and for MRI the reduction of
the RMSE of the magnitude image implied by the PSNR
gain of 0.65 dB (Tab. 6, 7.2%). The round-trip error is a
normalized RMSE, so that it compares across modalities.
Per sample and target channel, the mean squared error over
the valid pixels is divided by the variance of the target, the
result is averaged over channels, and its square root is aver-
aged over samples. Channels without variance, such as the
zero channel of MRI, are skipped. We measure the round-
trip error on the encoder means, decoded without the noise
floor, in the target space of each task, on 200 Hypersim val-
idation frames for depth and normals, 100 validation slices
for MRI, and 100 of the 1,000 test simulations of WinDiNet
for wind flows. The four-channel VAE has no frozen coun-
terpart, so Fig. 1 shows its depth gain (9.3%, Tab. 4) without
a round-trip error.

Pretraining distribution.
The pretraining distribution π

with the shift s = 6.93 for FLUX 3.

With w = 1/σλ (Sec. 3.2), a draw
from π gives the velocity residual ˆvϕ−v = (ˆϵϕ−ϵ)/αλ with
target v = ϵ −z, which is the residual of the velocity loss

gradient through the target alone. The uniform component
covers the low noise levels that π rarely draws. With the
anchor averaged over the latent elements, β = 1 gives an
anchor gradient of 10–16% of the norm of the reconstruc-
tion gradient on the pretrained FLUX 3 VAE (median 15%,
8 batches of 16 Hypersim frames). Most of the anchor gra-

The log-SNR range [λmin, λmax] = [−8, 4]

C. Surface normal estimation
Training.
The VAE is fine-tuned for one epoch (about
2,500 steps) at batch size 16 and learning rate 10−4, with 50
warm-up steps, gradient clipping at 10, the L1 reconstruc-
tion loss, and the KL weight 10−6. FLUX 3 Image Klein
Base is then fine-tuned for two epochs (about 5,000 steps)
at batch size 16 and learning rate 10−4, decayed linearly to
10−5, with a fixed prompt (“Turn this photo into a surface
normal map.”) and the text path frozen. Both stages use the
normal maps at 576 × 768.

Design choices.
Table 11 lists variants of DPA beyond

π, the mixture of noise levels increases the error by 0.29◦.

adjustment of FLAIR [14], which replaces part of the noise

fewer directions, fewer principal components would hold
most of their variance, and its curve in Fig. 7b would rise
faster than that of the control. Instead, the two curves nearly
coincide: the DPA and control latents both need 19 of the
32 components for 90% of their variance (the image latents
23), and they have about the same effective dimensional-
ity (participation ratio 9.3 and 8.7). DPA therefore keeps
the dimensionality of the latents and reduces their variation

Training.
The reconstruction loss is the mean of the
losses of the modalities, so that the weights of the KL term
and the anchor keep their meaning for every C. The anchor
is off for the first 5% of the steps and reaches β = 1 linearly
over the next 10%, so that the new layers learn the target
before the anchor regularizes the latents. The four-channel
VAEs weight the losses of depth and normals with Grad-
Norm [7] (asymmetry 1.5), which balances the gradients of
the modalities on the input of the last layer of the decoder,
with weights of mean one. The four-channel VAEs and the
VAEs for one modality are fine-tuned for 10,000 steps on
the 21,239 Hypersim frames shared by the depth and nor-
mal splits of Marigold [31] at 576 × 768, with 2,398 frames
of other scenes for validation. Log depth is normalized by
its per-image percentiles, and normals are given in camera
space [31]. The diffusion model follows the normals recipe
of Sec. C with the multi-channel latent as its target. Depth
errors are means over four sampling seeds.
Tiling.
An alternative to channel expansion keeps the
frozen RGB VAE and encodes depth (replicated to three
channels) and normals into two latents, which a single
DiT predicts side by side as one latent of double width
(Marigold [31] concatenates such latents along channels in-
stead). Trained and evaluated as in Tab. 4, tiling matches
the four-channel DPA model on depth (AbsRel ×100 6.01
vs. 6.02), but it is 1.73◦less accurate on normals (16.78◦
vs. 15.04◦) and needs twice as many latent tokens.
G. VAE pruning
Procedure.
Following the depth pruning of diffusion
transformers [16], we prune whole blocks, namely the resid-
ual blocks that keep the channel width and the attention
layers of the mid blocks of the encoder and the decoder,
and replace each removed block by the identity. The blocks
are removed greedily, each time the one whose removal in-
creases the round-trip error on training data least per re-
moved parameter, and the prefixes of this sequence are the
pruned VAEs. For the FLUX 3 VAE (84.0M parameters),
the round-trip error is the masked L1 error on the normal
maps of 16 Hypersim training images, and the prefixes we
use remove 22.5%, 50.6%, 71.8%, and 80.1% of the param-
eters (4, 9, 16, and 19 blocks, L1 error 0.070, 0.142, 0.226,
and 0.385 against 0.041 unpruned). For the Wan2.2 VAE
(704.7M parameters), it is the VRMSE of (u, v) on 8 win-
dows of 33 frames from 8 training simulations, and the pre-
fixes remove 27.2%, 51.9%, and 74.7% of the parameters
(4, 11, and 16 blocks, VRMSE 0.51, 0.87, and 3.56 against
0.27). Encoding and decoding times are measured for four
768 × 768 images, or one clip of 113 frames at 256 × 256,
in bf16 on one RTX 6000 Ada.
Results.
Each pruned VAE is fine-tuned without (control)
and with DPA, followed by the DiT, as for the unpruned
VAEs (Tab. 14). On normals, the DPA VAEs with 71.8%
and 80.1% of the parameters removed are as accurate as
the unpruned DPA VAE (14.95◦and 15.00◦vs. 14.98◦) and
encode and decode 1.6× and 1.8× faster. The speed-up is
smaller than the parameter reduction because most of the
removed parameters belong to blocks at low resolution. On
wind flows, pruning raises the test VRMSE of the control
from 0.450 to 0.482, 0.477, and 0.493, and DPA lowers it
by 6.8%, 4.4%, and 3.7% (4.9% unpruned). With 74.7%
of its parameters removed, the Wan2.2 VAE encodes and
decodes a clip 1.5× faster.

Training.
The control and DPA VAEs are fine-tuned for
1,000 steps at batch size 16 and learning rate 10−4 (50
warm-up steps, gradient clipping at 10, L1 loss, KL weight
10−6), DPA with β = 0.1 and the noise floor. FLUX 3 Im-

for 2,200 steps at batch size 16 on the latents of each VAE,
including the frozen one, with a fixed prompt that describes
a knee MRI slice.
Posterior sampling.
At every step, the sampler decodes
the clean estimate ˆx of the diffusion model and makes it
consistent with the measurements y = M ⊙F(Sx), with
the sensitivities S of the 8 coils provided with the dataset,
the 2D Fourier transform F, and a variable-density Poisson-
disc mask M with a fully sampled 24 × 24 center. Five
conjugate-gradient steps from ˆx on ∥M ⊙F(Sx) −y∥2 +
γ∥x −ˆx∥2 give the consistent image, which is re-encoded
and re-noised to the next level of the noise schedule of
FLUX 3 with the noise predicted by the diffusion model
instead of fresh noise. After the last step, the measured k-
space is restored exactly, and four samples are averaged.
The measurements are divided by 1.175 times the 99.9th
percentile of the zero-filled magnitude, so that the scal-
ing does not use the target.
γ and the number of steps
({2, 5, 10, 25, 50}) are tuned on 18 validation slices (posi-
tions 40, 70, ..., 280 of the two validation volumes).

The evaluation code of SMRD [45] uses the maximum of

J. Urban wind-flow simulation
Setup.
WinDiNet conditions LTX-Video on a first frame
that encodes the building footprint and the inlet, and on the
inlet speed and the domain size, and generates the veloc-
ity (u, v) for 112 time steps at 256 × 256.
Each frame
stores (u, v), divided by 30 m/s, and the building mask in
its three channels, so the video VAEs need no changes to
their input and output layers. Our VAEs keep the physics
loss on the decoded flow with which WinDiNet fine-tunes
its decoder. We use LTX-Video 2B (version 0.9.6-dev) and
Wan2.2 (5B).
Training.
The LTX-Video VAEs are fine-tuned on 5,010
training simulations for ten epochs at batch size 32 and
learning rate 10−5 decayed to 10−6 (50 warm-up steps, gra-
dient clipping at 10, KL weight 10−6), DPA with β = 10−2
and the noise levels and noise floor of the image experi-
ments. The diffusion model is then fully fine-tuned on all
10,000 training simulations at batch size 64 (WinDiNet: 16)
and learning rate 10−5, on a cosine schedule over 10,000
steps stopped at step 3,900.
For Wan2.2, the VAEs are
trained for five epochs on random windows of 33 frames,
and the diffusion model on a cosine schedule over 3,600
steps, stopped after nine epochs (about 1,400 steps). Roll-
outs use two sampler steps, and Tab. 7 reports the mean
over the last three checkpoints of each diffusion model, one
epoch apart.
Inverse design.
For inverse design, the layouts proposed
by a surrogate must improve the simulated flow. We test
this in the morph mode of [47], in which 15 buildings, each
split into 2 × 2 blocks held together by a cohesion penalty,
are moved by gradient descent through the surrogate to re-
duce the share of pedestrian-level wind speeds above 15 m/s
(danger) and 5 m/s (discomfort) and below 1 m/s (stagna-
tion) in a target region, or to keep the speeds within a tar-
get band. We optimize the five problems of [47] and 16
new ones (8 random layouts, 2 inlets each) through each
LTX-Video surrogate of Tab. 7, with two sampler steps and
200 Adam steps (400 for two inlets), and simulate the ini-
tial and the optimized layouts with the CFD solver. On the
simulated flow, the layouts optimized through DPA lower
the objective by 27.2% on average, those of WinDiNet by
26.0%, and those of the control by 25.7%.
K. Training cost
On NVIDIA GH200 GPUs, the VAE stage takes 2.1 GPU-
