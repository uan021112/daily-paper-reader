Abstract
Generating realistic 3D assets remains constrained by classi-
cal representations: surface meshes and compact physically
based materials struggle to express objects whose appear-
ance arises from fine geometry or complex light transport,
while existing Gaussian-splatting generators typically bake
fixed illumination into appearance. We introduce VideoRNG,
a two-stage method that generates complete, relightable
3D assets from a text prompt or a single image without
prescribing a mesh or analytic material model. A video
generator fine-tuned from a pretrained video diffusion back-
bone first produces a structured RGBA turntable sequence
under a known pose, camera, and illumination. A feed-
forward large reconstruction model initialized from a pre-
trained video diffusion backbone then maps the video-VAE
latent frames of selected turntable frames directly to millions
of anisotropic Gaussians with learned neural appearance
features. A shared directional decoder and stochastic Gaus-
sian ray tracer turn this representation, which we call ray-
traced Relightable Neural Gaussians (rtRNG), into radiance
under novel viewpoints and illumination. To preserve high-
frequency appearance while learning geometry, we introduce
depth-anchored appearance training, and we make million-
Gaussian reconstruction practical through foreground token
selection and cost-aware distributed sampling. VideoRNG
generates complex assets such as fur, feathers, and spider
webs that can be interactively rendered, relit, and integrated
into scenes.

1. Introduction
Creating complex, realistic 3D objects is essential to pop-
ulating convincing virtual worlds, but shape modeling and
material authoring remain labor-intensive and require sub-
stantial expertise, which limits the scale and diversity of
virtual environments that can be built within a given bud-
get. Generative models may offer a way forward: recent
text- and image-conditioned models can rapidly synthesize
high-quality 3D assets [16, 35, 36]. Yet their output remains
limited by the representations they produce.
Existing systems generally produce either surface meshes
with physically based rendering (PBR) materials or Gaussian
radiance fields that bake specific illumination into appear-
ance. PBR materials typically combine diffuse and micro-
facet specular lobes controlled by textures such as base color,
roughness, and metallicity [18, 31]. This representation is
compact, interpretable, and well supported by existing tools,
but a poor fit for objects whose appearance arises from fine
geometry or complex light transport, such as fur, feathers,
moss, dense foliage, and spider webs. In contrast, image
and video models need not explain their output through a
prescribed geometric primitive or analytic reflectance model,
and can readily depict such visual complexity.
Two lines of work suggest a route beyond this limita-
tion. Gaussian representations can describe geometry too
awkward for conventional surface meshes [9, 19], and can
be predicted directly from sparse images [45], but their
stored appearance is commonly entangled with fixed illumi-
nation. Neural appearance models instead learn complex,
relightable material response without committing to an ana-
lytic BRDF [20, 34]. However, these methods assume that
the underlying mesh geometry is known. Relightable Neural
Gaussians (RNG) combine the two ideas by attaching neural
directional appearance to Gaussian geometry for relight-
ing [4]. These methods reconstruct a supplied object rather
than generate one from a text or image prompt; VideoNeu-
Mat [42] demonstrates that a generative video prior can sup-
ply observations for neural-material extraction, but considers
planar material samples rather than complete 3D objects.
This raises a natural question: can we extract complete,
relightable 3D assets—including geometry and materials
outside the practical reach of classical representations—from
the rich outputs of a generative video model?
We introduce VideoRNG, a two-stage method for gen-
erating relightable neural 3D assets from a text prompt or
a single image (Figure 2). The first is a video generator
fine-tuned to produce an RGBA turntable sequence under a
prescribed object-pose, camera, and illumination protocol.
The second is a feed-forward large reconstruction model
(LRM) initialized from a pretrained video backbone and
fine-tuned for reconstruction; it maps the video-VAE latent
frames of selected turntable frames directly to millions of
anisotropic Gaussians in a single forward pass. Each Gaus-
sian stores geometry and a learned appearance feature rather
than a fixed color or PBR parameter set. A shared neural
decoder conditions that feature on the incoming light and
outgoing view directions to predict radiance. Forward ren-
dering and differentiable training use stochastic Gaussian
ray tracing to account for visibility and shadows [28, 39].
We call this combination of RNG’s neural directional appear-
ance and stochastic Gaussian ray tracing rtRNG: ray-traced
Relightable Neural Gaussians. Its appearance feature space
and decoder are learned jointly with the reconstructor from
rendered images, without requiring a dataset of Gaussian
assets as supervision. After training, the shared decoder
can be distilled once into a smaller network used by every
asset. The assets can be interactively ray-traced from novel
cameras, relit, and inserted into a scene with full global il-
lumination (Figure 1); compared with traditional Gaussian
splatting, ray tracing has multiple benefits, including clean
scene insertion and arbitrary shadow computation under any
lighting.
Moving from VideoNeuMat’s planar material samples
to complete 3D objects introduces substantial reconstruc-
tion challenges. We emit one Gaussian along the camera
ray of every foreground pixel, allowing the model to pre-
serve fine and disconnected structures without first reducing
them to a mesh. To make this high-resolution representation
tractable, we remove background tokens before the trans-
former and group similarly sized objects during distributed
training to avoid GPU idling due to load imbalance in a
multi-GPU setting. We further observe that joint geometry
and appearance learning produces soft appearance, motivat-
ing a fixed spatial reference for learning fine material detail.
Our depth-anchored objective places Gaussian centers at
ground truth depths during training while supervising the
depth predictor for inference. Together, these choices let
VideoRNG represent objects such as furry coats, fuzzy dan-
delions, and delicate spider webs while retaining view- and
light-dependent appearance. Our contributions are:
• We introduce VideoRNG, a two-stage pipeline that ex-
tracts complete relightable 3D assets from text- or image-
conditioned generative video, without constraining their
geometry to a mesh or their appearance to a prescribed
PBR material model (Section 3).
• We initialize a foreground-sparse LRM from a pretrained
generative video DiT and fine-tune all of its parameters to
directly predict Gaussian geometry and neural directional
appearance at 10242 resolution. Our efficient training
scheme supports variable-length, million-Gaussian out-
puts, and learns the neural appearance basis from rendered
images without requiring a dataset of Gaussian assets as
supervision (Section 3.2; Supp. Sec. C.5).
• We propose depth-anchored appearance learning: during
training, we place Gaussian centers at ground truth depths
for rendering and supervise predicted depths with a sepa-

2. Related Work
Text- and image-conditioned 3D generation. DreamFu-
sion [23] optimizes a 3D representation via score distilla-
tion from a 2D image prior, while MVDream [26] provides
a multiview-consistent prior. Feed-forward systems pair
generated sparse views with large reconstruction models
such as LRM, Instant3D, InstantMesh, LGM, GRM, and
GS-LRM [6, 14, 29, 38, 40, 45]. More recent generators,
including TRELLIS, TRELLIS.2, and DiffSplat, directly pro-
duce structured 3D latents or Gaussian assets [16, 35, 36].
LSRM [15] reconstructs meshes from posed views, with an
inverse-rendering extension that predicts BRDF materials.
These methods improve speed and fidelity across several
output representations, but none provides learned relightable
neural appearance for objects with complex light transport.
Video and multiview diffusion as 3D priors. Multi-
view diffusion models generate consistent observations for
optimization or feed-forward reconstruction [14, 26], and
SV3D adapts latent video diffusion to orbital novel-view
synthesis and 3D generation from one image [30]. Gen3R
couples a pretrained video diffusion model with a VGGT-
derived geometry latent space to jointly generate images,
point clouds, depth, and cameras [7]. Diff4Splat pairs a
video diffusion model with a latent dynamic reconstruction
model to predict explicit deformable Gaussian fields for 4D
scene generation [22]. VideoNeuMat extracts planar neu-
ral materials from generated video [42]. VideoRNG uses
video diffusion both to generate structured observations and
to initialize a separate reconstruction backbone, which is
fine-tuned with its denoising head replaced by a Gaussian
prediction head to produce an explicit, renderable asset; this
extends VideoNeuMat’s planar materials to complete static
objects with relightable neural appearance.
Neural appearance representations and generation.
Neural materials replace restricted analytic reflectance mod-
els with learned directional appearance, supporting ef-
fects such as parallax, self-shadowing, and layered scat-
tering [11, 41, 44]; RNA and 8DNA extend them to com-
plete relightable assets with global light transport but assume
known surface geometry [20, 34]. Generative Neural Mate-
rials synthesizes planar neural materials from image or text
conditioning in a shared feature basis [25]. Concurrent work
GenerativeShells [1], with partially overlapping authors, gen-
erates relightable neural appearance within a user-provided
bounding mesh and supports animation through mesh defor-
mation, whereas VideoRNG generates complete relightable
Gaussian assets, including geometry and appearance, from
text or image prompts. VideoRNG learns its appearance
basis jointly with a geometry-predicting LRM from rendered
images, without a dataset of pre-existing rtRNG assets.
Relightable Gaussian assets and generators. RNG at-
taches neural directional appearance to Gaussian geometry,
relighting objects with hard surfaces and soft boundaries
without an analytic shading model, but fits each object in-
dependently [4]. Large Material Gaussian Model and Luce
generate relightable Gaussians by predicting explicit PBR
quantities such as albedo, roughness, metallicity, and nor-
mals [27, 43]. RelitLRM generates relit Gaussian appear-
ance for a target environment map rather than relightable
assets [47]. The concurrent work F-RNG has similar goals
to ours but different methods: it reconstructs neural Gaus-
sian assets with frozen relighting priors and shades blended
features using rasterization [5]. The above works do not
currently ship code or models for comparison.
Gaussian rendering. 3D Gaussian Splatting rasterizes
anisotropic Gaussians efficiently [9], 3D Gaussian Ray Trac-
ing adds primary and secondary rays [19], and sorting-free
stochastic ray tracing makes transparent Gaussian clouds
practical inside conventional path tracers [28], with a dif-
ferentiable extension that supports reconstruction and re-
lightable per-Gaussian shading with traced visibility [39].
rtRNG builds on these renderers for interactive rendering,
shadow rays, and integration with conventional geometry.

Latent observations. We select 29 uniformly spaced
frames from the turntable, starting at the front view, and
encode them with the causal video VAE (temporal compres-

camera rays used to position the emitted Gaussians, and
alpha-derived foreground masks select transformer tokens
and emitted Gaussians (details in Supp. Sec. C.1).
We also support RGB video on a black background and
six independently encoded axis-aligned orthographic views,
with the same architecture and depth-anchored objective.
RGBA video is most robust overall, but supplementary ma-

3.2. Stage 2: Reconstructing rtRNG Assets
Output representation. An asset comprises N ray-traced
Relightable Neural Gaussians (rtRNG), {gi}, each with a
position µi, an anisotropic scale si ∈R3, a rotation qi,
an opacity αi, and a neural appearance latent zi ∈R32.

Rendering cost grows with Gaussian count. We group
assets into four buckets by foreground token number and
sample each distributed batch from one bucket, reducing
cost variation across GPUs while preserving each asset’s
marginal sampling probability. Supp. Sec. C.5 gives the
procedure and training-cost measurements.
3.3. Training: Depth-Anchored Learning
During LRM training, Gaussians from all eight latent ob-
servations are rendered jointly under point lights at camera
poses distinct from those of the input turntable. Section 3.2
places every Gaussian on its pixel ray at a depth predicted by
the network (Eq. 3). The reconstructor learns from rendered
images of synthetic assets under different cameras and point
lights than the input frames, so the natural training strategy
is to optimize predicted depth jointly with all other attributes
through the rendering loss. When that gradient flows through
predicted depth, however, reducing image error can move
Gaussian centers as well as change appearance. We observed
blurry appearance under such joint training, including when

3.4. Rendering rtRNG Assets
A shared decoder fψ(zi, ωin, ωout) predicts directional ap-
pearance at render time. It is a nine-layer MLP with hidden
width 512, conditioned on the appearance feature and the
incoming light and outgoing view directions. (For final ren-
dering, we use per-asset distillation into a four-layer MLP
of width 64.) Both directions are defined in the Gaussian’s
local coordinate frame, before applying its rotation qi; world-
space directions are mapped into this frame by R(qi)⊤. The
directional input comprises the two in-plane components of
each direction, Fourier-encoded with six frequencies; the
in-plane components of their half vector, encoded with one
frequency; and the two components along the Gaussian’s
third local axis. The rotation that orients the Gaussian also
fixes the frame in which its appearance is expressed, so the
encoding depends only on the light and view directions rela-
tive to the Gaussian, not on any world axis, and anisotropic
reflectance can be represented. We adopt the two-output
shading formulation (”shadow hint”) of RNA [20]: the de-

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental Setup
Data and training. We train the video generator and the
large reconstruction model (LRM) on approximately 22K
textured assets curated from TexVerse [48]. We first discard
assets with overly simple PBR materials, then keep those
whose captions describe materials, preferring complex ma-
terials and single objects rather than full scenes. For each
asset, we render an 81-frame RGBA turntable at 10242 un-
der a fixed environment light, following Section 3.1. This
set is used to train our video generator and provides the in-
put observations to the LRM. To supervise the LRM, we
also render 128 views per asset under different camera/point
lights than the 81-frame trajectory, with ground truth depth
and masks. This set contains 64 frames with fixed and struc-
tured camera/light trajectories along with 64 per-asset ran-
dom camera/light positions, with the aim of providing more
robustness to the LRM supervision. Reconstruction bench-
marks evaluate validation objects under held-out views and
lights; text-generation benchmarks evaluate assets synthe-
sized from prompts.
The two models are trained separately. We fine-tune the
video generator on the rendered turntable clips with its flow-
matching objective for three epochs. The LRM is initialized
from the pretrained 1.3B video DiT and fine-tuned end to end
with the depth-anchored objective (Section 3.3), following a
coarse-to-fine schedule from 2562 to 10242 with AdamW at
a constant learning rate of 2 × 10−5. Supp. Sec. C.2 reports
the step counts, compute setup, and input augmentation for
both models.
Baselines. We use each method’s supported conditioning.
Due to different designs, we cannot simply match the number
of views provided to the methods. In Figure 5, VideoRNG
receives generated multi-view observations; Pixal3D [13] re-
ceives four; TRELLIS.2 [35] and TRELLIS [36] receive
the generated front view; and DiffSplat [16], TRELLIS
text-xlarge, and LGM with MVDream [26, 29] receive text
prompts. All outputs are rendered with the same ortho-
graphic cameras. Supp. Secs. A and B give the broader
text-generation and ground truth reconstruction protocols.
We relight TRELLIS.2 meshes with PBR materials in
Blender Cycles; TRELLIS image-large and text-xlarge,
LGM, and DiffSplat use Gaussians with baked appearance.
In the ground truth benchmark, Hunyuan3D-2mv + Paint
produces a textured mesh, relit with its texture as albedo,
and LSRM, Pixal3D [13] and ReLi3D [2] predict meshes
with PBR materials, which we use for relighting. VideoRNG
predicts Gaussians with neural appearance features: under
a new environment map, its shared decoder evaluates re-
sponses to sampled incident-light directions, combined with
ray-traced visibility as in Eq. 7.

5. Conclusion
VideoRNG turns text or image prompts into relightable
rtRNG assets through video generation and feed-forward
reconstruction. Shared neural appearance captures fine struc-
tures for relighting and scene integration. Depth anchoring
improves PSNR over joint depth supervision (29.22 versus
25.39 dB).

C.3. Depth-Anchored Appearance Ablation
We train three depth-training variants on the full training
split at 2562: rendering loss only, which jointly optimizes
predicted depths and the other Gaussian attributes without
a depth loss; joint training with depth supervision (Eq. 4);
and depth anchoring (Eq. 5). The two depth-supervised
objectives use the same depth-loss term, but supply predicted
depths or ground truth depths, respectively, to the training
renderer. All models are evaluated using predicted depths,
with each asset rendered from a novel viewpoint under a
novel lighting condition. Table 1 reports full-frame PSNR,
SSIM, and VGG LPIPS; Figure 3 shows two representative
examples.
Rendering-loss-only training reaches 24.49 dB PSNR,
and adding depth supervision raises this to 25.39 dB. Depth
anchoring reaches 29.22 dB, a further gain of 3.83 dB over
joint training with depth supervision, with better SSIM and
LPIPS as well. Figure 3 shows the corresponding reconstruc-
tions. This comparison supports using ground truth Gaussian
centers as the spatial reference for appearance learning dur-
ing training, beyond adding depth supervision alone.
C.4. Shading Rule: Transmittance Blend versus Bi-
nary Selection
Equation 7 blends the two decoder outputs with the traced
transmittance Tiℓ. The shadow-hint formulation of RNA [20]
instead selects one output per light sample by thresholding
the visibility at 0.5, so only the selected output receives gra-
dient. We compare the two rules in a controlled fitting study
on 50 assets, fitting each model to these same assets and
evaluating full-frame PSNR on validation views of four of
them, with everything else fixed: the same data order, initial-
ization, optimizer, and step budget, on 32 GPUs with one
sample per GPU. The comparison uses a reduced decoder of
four layers and width 64, the size of our distilled per-asset
decoders, trained from scratch so that decoder capacity is
the bottleneck.
Transmittance blending outperforms binary selection
at all seven evaluated checkpoints, reaching 30.62 versus
29.66 dB at 15K steps, a gain of 0.96 dB. Two properties
explain the direction: the blend is the exact expectation of
the direct term under fractional occlusion, and both outputs
receive gradient wherever 0 < Tiℓ< 1, which on a represen-
tative asset carries about 21% of the opacity mass, whereas
the selection leaves one output without gradient on each side
of the threshold.
C.5. Training: Efficient Foreground Sampling
Foreground sparsity makes the number of tokens and Gaus-
sians a property of the object: across our training set the
emitted Gaussian count N per object ranges from about
0.45 M (10th percentile) to 8.4 M (maximum) at 10242, with
a mean of 1.8 M, and the cost of a training step—dominated
by ray tracing and shading—grows roughly linearly with N.
With data-parallel training on G GPUs and one object per
GPU, every step waits for the largest of G independently
drawn objects, so the expected step time is governed by the
expected maximum of N rather than its mean; for our train-
ing set the expected maximum over 64 draws is 3.3× the
mean (Table S3).
We reduce this waiting without changing each object’s
18


sampling probability. Objects are sorted by N (available
before training from the foreground masks) and split into K
equal-size buckets. At each step, all GPUs use a common
bucket sampled with probability proportional to its size, and
each GPU independently samples an object uniformly within
that bucket. The marginal probability of every object is
unchanged, so this is still uniform sampling of the training
set; only the joint distribution across ranks is altered, so that
the G objects processed together have similar cost.
Measured throughput.
We use K=4. Table S3 quantifies
both ingredients. Foreground selection reduces the work
per object from 8.4 M Gaussians (one per pixel of the eight
10242 views) to 1.8 M on average, but with one object per
GPU every step waits for the largest of 64 independently
drawn objects, whose expectation is 6.1 M Gaussians for
our size distribution. Bucketed sampling (K=4) lowers this
expected maximum to 3.1 M without changing the marginal
sampling distribution. On 64 GPUs, it achieves 1.7× the
measured training throughput of foreground selection alone.
Together with the foreground-only output head, this is what
makes training the reconstructor at 10242 with millions of
Gaussians per object practical.

C.7. Appearance Decoder Distillation
Teacher and student.
The full appearance decoder is a
nine-layer MLP with leaky-ReLU of negative slope 0.01 af-

We compare the feed-forward reconstruction with direct per-
object optimization against the same generated turntable
(81 RGBA frames). Three representations are fitted with
the same schedule against all 81 frames at 10242: 3000
Adam iterations, each on a batch of eight frames drawn

C.10. Limitations
The video model sometimes produces inconsistent content
across frames, which can lead to ghosting artifacts in re-
constructed assets. Accurately reproducing sharp specular
effects also remains challenging, as reported in prior neural
appearance models [11, 20].

D. LLM-Driven Prompt Generation Pipeline
We used an AI assistant to generate candidate object descriptions for text-conditioned generation. The task prompt requested
independently written descriptions of approximately 40–80 words, each describing a single, plausible real-world object with
distinctive overall geometry and inherent geometric or material detail. It encouraged diversity across natural and manufactured
subjects, including fibrous, thin, perforated, layered, and optically complex structures, while discouraging template-based
expansion and near-duplicate subjects. The instructions excluded camera, lighting, animation, background, and rendering-style
directions, since the observation protocol is prescribed by the fine-tuned generator. They also favored structures visible from
lateral viewpoints, consistent with the horizontal turntable protocol.
Original prompt supplied to the AI assistant. The following instructions were used to request candidate descriptions; the
requested subject properties do not themselves establish reconstruction capabilities.
Create 100 prompts for 3D object generation. Each prompt should describe one interesting, realistic
object with distinctive overall geometry and rich, inherent medium-scale and fine-scale geometric or
material detail. Prefer subjects with intrinsic optical complexity, such as translucency, subsurface
scattering, layered fibers, sheen, iridescence, glossy or wet coatings, or mixed opaque and
transmissive regions, as well as other features that are difficult to represent practically with
traditional 3D meshes and compact analytic material models.
1. Write each prompt individually.
Independently conceive and write every prompt. Do not use code, scripts, template filling, synonym
substitution, or combinatorial expansion to generate or multiply prompt content. Do not pad the
collection by repeating the same description with a different name, species, color, or material.
2. Describe only the object itself.
This is because the video model consuming the prompt is finetuned to produce a turntable of only one
object. Describe its identity, overall form, proportions, component arrangement, intrinsic coloration,
materials, and visible geometric details. Each subject must be one coherent object. A functional
assembly with multiple connected parts counts as one object.
Do not describe scenery, backgrounds, ground planes, surrounding props, or added display stands.
Integral components, including an object's base, frame, or supports, are allowed if appropriate.
Avoid written text, logos, labels, and legible symbols unless they are essential to the identity or
function of the object, since they introduce a separate text-rendering challenge.
3. Exclude all camera, lighting, and animation instructions. This is because the video model consuming
the prompt is finetuned to produce a turntable of a static object under pre-defined pose, camera and
light conditions, orthogonal to the prompt.
Do not mention cameras, viewpoints, framing, lenses, camera movement, trajectories, turntables, or shot
sequences.
Do not describe lighting setups, light sources, cast shadows, moving lights, or changes in lighting and
reflections. Intrinsic material properties, such as roughness, metallicity, or translucency, are
allowed.
Do not describe actions, behaviors, movement, animation, deformation, or changes over time. Use neutral
descriptions of static anatomy, shape, and component arrangement. Distinguish fixed geometry from an
action: a helical shell or downward-curving leaf is a shape, not a movement.
A static posture or fixed articulation may be specified when needed to define an animal, plant, vehicle,
tool, or mechanical object clearly, but do not describe an action or motion.
When describing optical complexity, state the intrinsic material property itself rather than a lighting
setup or how the object changes under moving illumination.
These restrictions are instructions for writing the prompts. Do not repeat them inside the generated
prompts, even as negative phrases such as "no camera movement" or "no lighting changes."
4. Choose believable real-world subjects.
Select recognizable natural objects or human-made artifacts. Their anatomy, proportions, materials,
thicknesses, connections, and construction must be plausible and internally consistent. Avoid
fantastical hybrids, impossible structures, arbitrarily attached parts, and invented embellishments
intended solely to increase complexity.
5. Make geometric richness inherent to the subject.
Choose objects whose actual anatomy, material structure, or functional construction already provides
interesting geometry. Describe specific features and where they occur. Phrases such as "highly
detailed," "intricate texture," are fine but should not be relied upon to create complexity.
Prioritize distinctive medium-scale structures and finer surface relief that can plausibly remain
visible at the scale of the complete object. Do not rely on hidden internal mechanisms or invisible
microscopic features to justify complexity.
22


Using more detail than typical for the given object is fine, but do not artificially cover every
surface with detail. Preserve naturally smooth regions. Reject objects whose overall geometry is simple
and whose apparent complexity comes only from printed patterns, color variation, or added decoration.
Simple geometric components are acceptable when their arrangement forms a genuinely rich overall
structure, especially when combined with material complexity.
Prefer subjects whose defining structures are visible across lateral viewpoints. Avoid relying on
geometry that is visible only from directly above, directly below, or inside the object, since the
primary observation protocol is a horizontal turntable.
6. Maximize meaningful diversity across the collection.
Cover a broad range of natural and manufactured subjects, such as terrestrial and marine animals,
plants, minerals, foods, textiles, tools, vehicles, instruments, toys, and mechanical devices.
Vary silhouettes, proportions, materials, component arrangements, and types of geometric detail.
Related subjects are acceptable when they contribute substantially different geometry. Avoid
near-duplicates and excessive concentration in a few categories.
Balance the collection across different representation challenges, including:
- fibrous, hairy, fuzzy, or feathery structures;
- thin, perforated, filamentary, or disconnected geometry;
- branching, layered, folded, frilled, or foliated forms;
- porous, cellular, woven, knitted, braided, or interlocking structures;
- translucent, subsurface-scattering, or mixed opaque and transmissive materials;
- anisotropic, iridescent, pearlescent, glossy, wet, or otherwise strongly view-dependent surfaces; and
- complex but plausible assemblies of multiple materials and connected components.
Individual prompts may exercise several of these challenges. Do not satisfy a category through
superficial wording: the relevant structure or material must be inherent to the chosen subject and
described concretely.
7. Keep the output concrete and self-contained.
Write each prompt as a concise standalone paragraph of approximately 40-80 words, beginning with a
clear object identity. Focus on its defining structures instead of an exhaustive inventory of minor
details. Do not add photographic, cinematic, rendering-style, or quality tags.
Before responding, check that each prompt describes only the object, contains no camera or lighting
instructions or temporal events, and contributes meaningful structural variety.
Output exactly 100 prompts, one unnumbered prompt per line, with no bullets, category headings,
explanations, blank lines, or additional text.
