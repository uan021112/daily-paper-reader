Abstract
The appearance of a scene is determined by the albedo,
roughness, metallicity and normal of every point on its sur-
face, and by the light that reaches it, its irradiance. Recov-
ering these intrinsics from images is ambiguous, and meth-
ods that predict them per pixel give the same surface point a
different decomposition in every view. We introduce PointX,
a simple feed-forward model that predicts intrinsics per 3D
surface point instead. A multi-view transformer encodes a
set of unposed images, and for any queried surface point,
PointX pools the point’s tokens across the views in which
it appears and decodes the point’s intrinsics once. Each
point of a 3D reconstruction of the scene, such as a point
cloud or a mesh, thus receives a single prediction, which is
exactly consistent across views by construction and enables
view-consistent relighting and editing. The same model also
predicts per-view maps, from one image or many, by query-
ing the point seen at every pixel. These maps agree across
views up to the precision of the estimated geometry and cor-

1. Introduction
Inverse rendering decomposes an image into the inputs of
a renderer: the albedo, normal, roughness and metallic-
ity of every surface point, and the light that reaches it,
its irradiance. With these intrinsic properties [2], a cap-
tured scene can be relit, its materials edited, or new ob-
jects inserted into it. However, the decomposition is am-
biguous, since the same pixel color has several valid ex-
planations: a wall that appears red may be white under
red light or red under white light.
While recent meth-
ods [6, 25, 36, 40, 52, 67, 72, 103, 104] achieve accurate
single-view predictions, a scene is usually captured in sev-
eral images, or a video. The same surface point appears
in several of them, yet per-image methods resolve the am-
biguity in each image independently, so the point receives

a different decomposition in every view. Applications us-
ing these intrinsics across multiple views then inherit this
disagreement: a relit scene flickers from view to view, an
edit made in one image does not carry over to the next, and
materials texturing a reconstruction show seams.
One line of work addresses this inconsistency by attach-
ing the intrinsics to a 3D representation of the scene, fitted
per scene [23, 30, 56, 65, 106, 111] or predicted by a net-
work [35, 53, 73, 109], and rendering them into every view,
so that all views show the same underlying intrinsics. How-
ever, every view then inherits the errors of this represen-
tation, and per-scene fits take minutes to hours. A second
line of work drops the 3D representation. Multi-view net-
works [14, 54, 98] compute the features of each view from
all images through cross-view attention, and video models
do the same across frames [20, 55, 85, 104]. Their pre-
dictions are more consistent than those of single-view net-
works, but each view is still decoded independently, so cor-
responding pixels of different views can still disagree. A
third line of work keeps the per-view predictions and rec-
onciles them afterwards, through a multi-view consensus at
test time [11] or a per-scene optimization [41], which has
to resolve the disagreement of separately predicted views.
Thus far, consistency has either required an explicit 3D rep-
resentation, been encouraged through attention and training,
or been enforced after the predictions are made.
Our key insight is to predict intrinsic properties per sur-
face point rather than per pixel. We introduce PointX, a
feed-forward model that takes one or several unposed im-
ages of a static scene and predicts the intrinsics of a query
surface point using evidence from all views in which it ap-
pears. A multi-view transformer, initialized from a feed-
forward reconstruction model [90], first computes a feature
map for each image. Given a query point in 3D or pixel
space, we then find its correspondences across the input im-
ages and sample the feature maps at these locations. A 3D
point is projected into each image with cameras and depth,
estimated by a frozen reconstruction model [90] or taken
from any other source, and a pixel can also be matched
across the images with a dense matcher [66]. We pool these
features into a single representation of the surface point,
weighting each view by the confidence that the point is vis-
ible in it, and small MLP heads decode the point’s intrinsic
properties from it only once. The resulting prediction be-
longs to the point rather than to a view, making it consis-
tent by construction. The same model can predict proper-
ties directly for the points of a reconstruction, without hav-
ing to fuse separate per-view predictions, or produce per-
view maps by querying every pixel, which then agree across
views as far as their correspondences do. PointX also ben-
efits directly from more accurate geometry and correspon-
dences, such as those of a dense matcher, without retrain-
ing. In Fig. 1, PointX predicts the intrinsic properties of the
points of a laser-scanned scene [74], so that a light placed
once or a material edited once produces consistent results
across the input views.
We evaluate PointX in three experiments. First, with the
geometry of the scene given, we query the surface points
of rendered scenes [70, 117] directly: PointX is competi-
tive with or more accurate than baselines whose per-view
predictions are averaged at each point, and has no disagree-
ment between views to resolve. Second, we measure the
consistency of per-view maps at exact correspondences on
laser-scanned real scenes [74], where PointX is the most
consistent. Third, we measure prediction accuracy on ren-
dered scenes and real photographs [96]: PointX is compet-
itive with the state of the art from a single image, and be-
comes more accurate with every added image. Ablations
show that pooling the features of all views into a single
representation per point brings this consistency at no cost
in accuracy: decoding every pixel from its own features is
equally accurate, but far less consistent.
In summary, our contributions are: (1) a formulation that
predicts intrinsic properties once per surface point rather
than per pixel, and is consistent across views by construc-
tion; (2) PointX, a feed-forward model that implements it
from one or several unposed images, and serves both per-
point and per-view prediction; and (3) an evaluation of in-
trinsics on surface points, and of their consistency at exact
correspondences on real scenes.
2. Related Work
Intrinsic decomposition and inverse rendering.
Intrin-
sic images [2, 46] separate an image into reflectance and
shading.
Early methods relied on physical assumptions
and hand-designed priors [1, 24, 28, 86, 95]; later ones
learned priors from human judgments [3, 64, 116], ren-
dered scenes [49, 70, 117] and time-lapse video [50]. In-
verse rendering widens the output to materials and light-
ing for whole indoor scenes [52, 75, 94, 117, 118]. Gen-
erative models have recently raised single-image accu-
racy [6, 8, 15, 36, 40, 67, 72, 85, 103], since they can re-
solve the ambiguity with a plausible sample. All of these
methods predict single-view, per-pixel intrinsics.
Consistency across views and time.
The disagreement
between views has been addressed in three ways.
In-

flectance along correspondences with a penalty, solved per
sequence [4, 16, 22, 42–44, 63, 100]; later work used
such sequences as a training signal for single-image net-
works [48, 50, 102]. Reconstruction-based inverse render-
ing attaches materials and lighting to a 3D representation,
optimized per scene [5, 23, 29, 30, 41, 56, 65, 81, 97, 99,
106, 107, 111, 112] or predicted by a network [35, 53, 73,

Feed-forward reconstruction, matching and tracking.
Feed-forward reconstruction models [34, 37, 47, 57, 61,
82, 89, 90, 92, 93] predict depth and cameras for a set of
images or a video with a transformer that attends across
views. We use one as our multi-view transformer, and a
frozen copy to estimate the geometry that conditions it.
Dense matchers [17–19, 58, 66, 84, 87, 113] estimate a
correspondence and its certainty for every pixel of an im-
age pair without recovering 3D geometry, and point track-
ers [12, 13, 26, 32, 33, 45, 79] follow a pixel’s surface point
through a video. In our framework, a track is the pixel at
which one surface point is visible in each view, obtained by
projecting a 3D point with this geometry or by matching a
pixel across the images, which is more precise and what our
per-view maps rely on.
Pooling the observations of a point.
Pooling per-view
features at a 3D point is a staple of generalizable view syn-
thesis and reconstruction: pixelNeRF [101] averages them,
IBRNet [91] and NeuRay [59] weight them by learned vis-
ibility, MVSNeRF [7] fuses them in a cost volume, trans-
formers attend along epipolar lines [83] or over all view to-
kens without an explicit point [27, 31, 71, 108], and GenRe-
con [73] conditions a generative mesh model on their statis-
tics. Our pooling follows these works: a visibility-weighted
mean of the tokens of a surface point, decoded into intrin-
sic properties. A second line lifts per-view features or la-
bels into a 3D field and consolidates their disagreements
there [21, 22, 38, 39, 62, 76, 88, 114, 115], at the cost of a
per-scene optimization. We pool the tokens before decod-
ing, so there are no per-view predictions to consolidate.
3. Method
We introduce PointX, illustrated in Fig. 2, a feed-forward
transformer that takes a set of images of a scene and pre-
dicts the intrinsic properties of any queried surface point.
Section 3.1 states the problem and how a surface point is
located in the images, Sec. 3.2 describes the architecture,
and Sec. 3.3 the training setup.

Geometry.
A 3D point is only meaningful relative to the
geometry of the scene. We assume a camera and a depth
map Dv for every view v. They can be given, for example
by a scan or a multi-view reconstruction, or, for unposed
images, estimated from the images themselves by a recon-
struction model. Unprojecting Dv yields the point map Xv,
the 3D point observed at each pixel of view v.
Tracks.
To predict the properties of p, we first locate it
in the input images. The track of p is the set of locations
xv at which it appears in the views, which we obtain by
projecting p with the camera of each view v. Projection
returns a location even where p is occluded, so each location
xv carries a confidence cv ∈[0, 1] that p is visible in view v.

Pixel queries.
A pixel query is a special case of Eq. (1): a

Querying every pixel of an image yields its intrinsic maps,
while querying the points of a point cloud or the texels of a

of Eq. (2), still computed with the point maps and cameras

3.2. Architecture
PointX consists of three parts (Fig. 2): a multi-view trans-
former turns every image into a map of tokens, a pooling
step reads one token per view along the track of the query
and averages them into a single token, and MLP heads de-
code the intrinsic properties of the point from this token.
Input geometry.
We run VGGT-Ω[90] (frozen) once on
the input images to estimate a camera and a depth map with
per-pixel confidence for every view. This geometry is an
input of our multi-view transformer, but we first change
the frame in which it is expressed: VGGT-Ωexpresses its
camera poses relative to the first view, whereas our model
should treat all views symmetrically. We therefore express
all geometry in a common frame, the mean-frame: its origin
is the mean of the camera centers, its forward axis the nor-
malized mean of the viewing directions, its horizontal axis
the mean of the cameras’ horizontal axes made orthogonal
to the forward axis, and its vertical axis the cross product of
the two. No view is privileged in this frame, and for a sin-
gle view it coincides with the camera frame. The predicted
normal n is expressed in the mean-frame as well.
Multi-view transformer.
The transformer computes one
feature map per view from all images jointly, so that
the features of a view combine evidence from all views.
We use the transformer of VGGT-Ω[90], initialized from
its released weights and fine-tuned, which alternates self-
attention within each view (frame attention) and across all
views (global attention) [89]. Since a normal is decoded
from a token pooled over several views, the features of ev-
ery view must encode the pose of its camera in the mean-
frame, which the image alone cannot provide. We therefore
give every pixel of a view ten geometry channels, all ex-
pressed in the mean-frame: its viewing ray in 6D Pl¨ucker
coordinates [80], the 3D point that VGGT-Ωestimates at
that pixel, and the scalar log-confidence of this estimate. An
additional, zero-initialized patch embedding embeds these
channels, and its output is added to the patch embedding
of the image, so that the pretrained features are unchanged
at initialization. Further, VGGT-Ωmarks its first view with
separate camera and register tokens. We drop the camera to-
kens and use the same register tokens for every view, mak-
ing our transformer, unlike the frozen VGGT-Ωthat esti-
mates its input geometry, equivariant to the order of views.
Token map.
The transformer outputs feature maps at
1/16 of the image resolution, too coarse for per-pixel de-
tail, which a DPT decoder [68] upsamples to the full image
resolution (see Sec. A.1). The token map tv of view v then
holds one token per pixel, built from two sources. The first
is a linear projection of the upsampled feature at that pixel,
which carries the multi-view evidence of the transformer.
Inspired by D4RT [105], we add a linear projection of the
9×9 RGB window of Iv around the pixel. Both projections
are shared by all views.

Heads.
Each intrinsic is decoded from z by an individual
two-layer MLP, followed by a sigmoid for albedo, rough-
ness and metallicity, a softplus for irradiance which is pre-
dicted directly as log(1+e), and a unit-length normalization
for the normal. To obtain the normal map of a view, we ro-
tate n from the mean-frame into that view’s camera frame.
Consistency.
Nothing in Eq. (3) refers to the view from
which a point is queried. With a track obtained by projec-
tion, the locations, the weights and hence z depend only on
the point, the geometry and the images. A surface point
thus receives a single prediction from whichever view it is
queried: its disagreement across views is zero by construc-
tion, without a consistency loss or fusion of per-view pre-
dictions. Per-view maps are consistent up to a small dis-
agreement, because corresponding pixels of two views do
not query exactly the same point. Even with exact geom-
etry, the two pixels unproject to neighboring points, since
each view samples the surface on its own pixel grid. Errors
in the estimated depth and cameras move them further apart
and tilt the normals. With a matcher, the two tracks also
differ slightly, as each is matched from its own view.

4. Experiments
We compare PointX with recent state-of-the-art methods in
two settings. In Sec. 4.1, the geometry of the scene is given
and we query its surface points directly. In Secs. 4.2 and 4.3,
only the images are given: we query every pixel of every
image and evaluate the consistency and the accuracy of the
resulting per-view maps. Finally, we present a set of key
ablations in Sec. 4.4 and discuss applications in Sec. 4.5.
Baselines.
We compare with single-view methods [40,
67, 103], video models [20, 55, 85], the multi-view
models MVInverse [98],
MVID and MVID-Ω[14],
and GeoID [11], a test-time multi-view consensus on
RGB↔X [103].
All use their released weights, and the
video and multi-view models process several images jointly.
Methods that need posed images [73] or per-scene opti-
mization [41] are not part of the main comparison. We eval-
uate GenRecon [73] and Intrinsic Image Fusion [41], on the
task of Sec. 4.1 in Sec. C.2. We omit methods that target
single objects [54] or have no released model [35].
Datasets and metrics.
Every dataset is evaluated on sets
of up to eight views of a scene. Hypersim [70] and Inte-
riorVerse [117] are rendered and come with ground-truth
intrinsics and depth, MAW [96] provides measured albedo
for real indoor photographs, and ETH3D [74] provides real
photographs with laser-scanned geometry. For accuracy, we
report the PSNR of the albedo, as predicted and after fit-
ting a per-channel scale to the ground truth (PSNRs); the
PSNRs of the irradiance, which is defined only up to the
exposure of an image; the mean angular error of the nor-
mals; and the RMSE of roughness and metallicity. The dis-
agreement between two views is measured at corresponding
points: we report the mean angle between their normals and,
for the other properties, the RMSE between corresponding
points divided by the RMSE between randomly paired ones
(RMSEr, see appendix), so that a constant map is not re-
warded. An RMSEr of 0 means that the views agree, and an
RMSEr of 1 that they agree no better than at random.

4.2. Consistency of Per-View Maps
The geometry of a scene is often unknown, and many ap-
plications expect one map per image. We hence measure
next whether a method’s per-view maps agree at ground-
truth correspondences.
Protocol.
We apply the reprojection protocol of MVIn-
verse [98] to the real photographs of ETH3D, whose laser
scans give exact correspondences. For every pair of views,
the pixels of one view are projected into the other with the
ground-truth depth and kept where the depth of the other
view confirms that they are visible. The maps of the two
views are compared at these corresponding pixels, with the
normals rotated into the world frame by the ground-truth
cameras. Additional results are in the appendix.
Results.
Table 2 shows that PointX is the most consistent
on all five properties with the tracks of a dense matcher,
RoMa-Ω. In Fig. 4, the marked surface point looks differ-
ent in the two views, and the baselines assign it a different
albedo in each view, whereas PointX’s predictions look the
most similar and have the lowest difference. The video and
multi-view baselines process the views jointly but still de-
code each view separately, which does not ensure consis-
tency. In PointX, corresponding pixels are decoded from
tokens pooled along nearly identical tracks, so their predic-
tions can differ only as much as their tracks do (Sec. 3.2).
Its consistency therefore improves with the precision of the
tracks, without retraining. With tracks projected through
the coarser geometry of VGGT-Ω, it already leads on irra-
diance, normals and roughness. The matcher extends the
lead to all four properties. When ground-truth geometry is
available, its exact tracks make PointX highly consistent,
with by far the lowest disagreement on every property.

Input geometry.
Without the geometry channels, we ob-
serve in Tab. 4a that every property becomes less accurate
and less consistent. The normals degrade the most: they are
expressed in the mean-frame, so the model must infer the
orientation of each camera from the images alone, and any
error in it tilts all the normals of that view (see Sec. C.5).
Global attention.
With global attention restricted to each
view, the views interact only through pooling. In Tab. 4a,
this variant remains as accurate from one image, but im-
proves far less with more images, and its maps disagree
more.
Encoding the views jointly thus turns additional
views into accuracy gains (Fig. 5) and aligns the tokens of
a surface point across views, which pooling alone does not.

Confidence.
The confidence of Eq. (2) down-weights the
views in which a point is occluded. As observed in Tab. 4b,
this improves accuracy with every source of tracks. The
gain is largest with projected tracks, where nothing else re-
jects the occluded views, and smaller with RoMa-Ω, whose
own confidence already rejects most of them.
Runtime.
On an A100 GPU, PointX predicts all maps of
eight 512 × 336 images in 1.6 s with ground-truth tracks,
2.0 s with VGGT-Ωtracks and 5.8 s with RoMa-Ωtracks.
4.5. Applications
Since PointX predicts one set of intrinsics per surface point,
its predictions attach directly to a 3D representation of the
scene, with no per-view maps to fuse. In Fig. 1, we query
every point of the laser scan of an ETH3D scene once to ob-
tain an intrinsic point cloud. A new light or an albedo edit
applied in 3D then changes every photograph consistently,
since each surface point is modified once (see Sec. D).
In Fig. 6, we instead query one point per texel of a mesh,
which bakes the predictions into its textures. The result is a
standard asset for physically based rendering: a path tracer
renders it under a new light with the predicted materials,
and an inserted chrome ball reflects the textured scene.

5. Conclusion
We introduce PointX, a simple feed-forward model that pre-
dicts the intrinsic properties of any queried surface point
from one or more images of a scene. Our core insight is that
intrinsics belong to the surface points of a scene, not to the
pixels of an image. PointX therefore pools the evidence of
all views in which a point is visible into a single token and
decodes its intrinsics once, making each point’s prediction
consistent across views by construction. Its per-view maps
achieve state-of-the-art consistency on real scenes, and their
accuracy, competitive from a single image, improves with
every added view to lead on most metrics. PointX also pre-
dicts the intrinsics of point clouds and meshes, for view-
consistent relighting, editing and object insertion.

PointX: Multi-View Consistent Intrinsics by Querying Surface Points
Appendix
A. Model and Training Details
We complement Sec. 3 with further details on the architecture, the training procedure and the training data, in the notation of
the main paper.
A.1. Architecture
Transformer and decoder.
The transformer is initialized from the released 1B-parameter checkpoint of VGGT-Ω[90]:
a DINOv3 ViT-L/16 patch encoder [77] followed by 24 frame-attention and 24 global-attention blocks of width 1024 in
alternation. The DPT decoder [68] follows the dense head of VGGT-Ωwithout its output layer and is trained from scratch.
It fuses the tokens of four transformer blocks into a feature map at 1/4 of the image resolution, and two further upsampling
stages bring it to the full resolution, each with a skip connection from the first two convolution blocks of an ImageNet-
pretrained VGG19 [78]. The decoder feature has 128 channels and the tokens have 256 dimensions.
Tracks.
For the tracks of RoMa-Ω[66], we use its fast setting and take its predicted overlap probability as the confidence
of the matcher.
Heads and model size.
Each head is a two-layer MLP with a hidden width of 1024 and a GELU between its two layers;
its output passes through the activation given in Sec. 3.2. In total, PointX has 999M trainable parameters: 911M in the
transformer, 87M in the decoder and 1.3M in the heads. The frozen VGGT-Ωthat estimates the input geometry and RoMa-Ω
are not counted.
A.2. Training
Training sets.
Each GPU holds 24 images per step, as ⌊24/N⌋sets of N views, with N drawn from 2 to 24; with probability
0.1, a step instead holds 24 unrelated photographs, each a set of a single view. A multi-view set consists of frames of one
camera trajectory: either a run of frames at a random stride, or a random frame together with frames that are close to it in
camera pose.
Augmentation.
Images are resized and cropped to a width of 512 px and to a random aspect ratio between 0.33 and 1 that
is shared by all images of a step, and each set is mirrored horizontally with probability 0.5. With probability 0.5, the input
images of a set are degraded by a random combination of blur, downsampling, noise and JPEG compression, while their
labels are left unchanged.
Queries.
Each step draws 16,384 query pixels per GPU, all in one randomly chosen view of each set. Half of them form
the 16 × 16 windows of the gradient loss. We draw 10% of the queries on metallic surfaces, 27% near depth discontinuities
and the rest uniformly. The source of the tracks is drawn uniformly among the three sources of Sec. 3.3 at every step; where
a dataset provides no ground-truth cameras or depth, the geometry of VGGT-Ωtakes the place of the ground truth.
Losses.
The losses of the five properties are summed with equal weights. The gradient loss is an L1 loss on the finite
differences of prediction and label within each window, at four scales that each subsample the window by two. Metallic
surfaces are rare, so within the metallicity loss, the queries on them are up-weighted by their inverse frequency, capped at 2.
In addition to the queries, the pixels of every view on a grid of stride 4 are decoded from their own token alone and supervised
with the same losses, with weight 0.5. Each loss is applied only where its label is valid: the dataset must provide the property
(Tab. 5) and define it at the pixel, which excludes, e.g., the normal of the sky and the irradiance of saturated pixels.
15


Irradiance exposure.
Where frames are exposed individually, the irradiance label of a surface point differs between two
views by the ratio of their exposures. We estimate this ratio as the median ratio of the labels over the surface points that both
views observe, which we find with the ground-truth geometry, solve for the exposure of every view of a set by least squares,
and divide its labels by it. The common exposure of a set remains arbitrary: we fit one scale between prediction and label per
set in closed form before the L1 loss, and penalize its logarithm with weight 0.1. Where the views cannot be related, as for
single photographs, the scale is fitted per image.
Optimization.
We use AdamW [60] (β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.05), bfloat16 mixed precision, gradient clipping at
an L2 norm of 10, and an exponential moving average of the parameters with decay 0.999, which is the model we evaluate.
PointX is trained with the losses above for 70k steps, with 5k warm-up steps to a learning rate of 1×10−4 and a cosine decay
to 10−5. Training takes approximately 5 days to complete on 8 A100 80GB GPUs.

B. Evaluation Details
Test sets.
On Hypersim [70], we use the 46 scenes of the official test split with up to two camera trajectories each, and
form 69 sets of eight views: a random frame of a trajectory and the seven frames closest to it in camera pose. On Interi-
orVerse [117], we form sets of up to eight views in the same way, one for each of 105 random scenes of the official test
split (773 images). On MAW [96], we use all 874 photographs of its 38 scenes, in sets of up to eight in capture order. On
ETH3D [74], we use the 13 scenes with laser scans of the high-resolution multi-view benchmark, in 50 sets of up to eight
overlapping views (395 images). The photographs of MAW and ETH3D are downsampled to a width of 1024 px.
Baselines.
We run every baseline with its released code and weights and follow its conventions for the color space of its
inputs and outputs. The video models [20, 55, 85] receive the views of a set as the frames of one clip; for the single-image
results of Tab. 3, every method processes each image separately. MVID [14] predicts the normals of a set in the camera frame
of its first view. We rotate them into the frame of each view with the ground-truth cameras. All predictions are resized to the
resolution of the ground truth before they are scored.
Accuracy.
Albedo errors are computed in linear RGB, and LPIPS on gamma-encoded images. For PSNRs, we fit one scale
per color channel to the ground truth by least squares; the irradiance is then compared after gamma encoding. All metrics are
computed over the pixels with a valid label.

C.2. Additional Baselines
In Sec. 4, we omit methods that need posed images or per-scene optimization. Since the task of Sec. 4.1 provides the
cameras and the geometry of every scene, we evaluate two of them on it: Intrinsic Image Fusion [41] and GenRecon [73].
Table 7 shows that PointX is considerably more accurate than both on every property of InteriorVerse. On Hypersim, it is
more accurate than GenRecon, while Intrinsic Image Fusion builds on RGB↔X [103], which was trained on the test scenes.
Figure 10 shows two scenes.
Setup.
Intrinsic Image Fusion draws samples of RGB↔X for every view, fuses them into one material field on a mesh of
the scene, and refines this field by inverse path tracing. We give it the ground-truth cameras, the ground-truth depth maps as
its mesh, and the instance segmentation it requires, from SAM 2 [69] masks that we link across views with the ground-truth
depth. GenRecon generates a mesh with material textures from posed images and sparse scene points, for which we use the
ground-truth cameras and points of the ground-truth depth maps. The result of each method is rendered into the views of a
set and scored like the maps of the other baselines. Intrinsic Image Fusion optimizes a set for more than one hour on an A100
GPU and GenRecon takes about ten minutes, whereas PointX takes seconds (Sec. 4.4).
Intrinsic Image Fusion.
The fusion makes the samples of RGB↔X about as consistent as the ground-truth maps (Tab. 1),
but it does not correct them: its accuracy remains that of RGB↔X. The inverse path tracing explains the images by the light
that the surfaces of the mesh exchange. Its authors evaluate it on scenes covered by about 200 views, whereas our sets hold
eight views of a part of a scene, so much of this light comes from surfaces that no view shows. The optimization compensates
for the missing light with a brighter albedo, which saturates on Hypersim. On InteriorVerse, it improves the metallicity but
degrades the roughness and the PSNRs of the albedo. On one of the scenes of its authors, its released pipeline raises the
albedo PSNR of the fusion from 15.0 to 22.6 dB with all 202 views, and not at all with eight of them.
GenRecon.
GenRecon is a generative reconstruction model, and its authors evaluate its geometry only. Its materials are
sampled from a 3D generative prior conditioned on the images: they look plausible but are far from the true ones. Its mesh
also deviates from the true surface, in particular on Hypersim, where a set often shows only a part of a large room, so the
renders of this one mesh still disagree at the true surface points.

D. Relighting and Editing
The point cloud of Fig. 1 holds one PointX query per point of the laser scan.
Relighting.
Let L be the product of the predicted albedo and irradiance of a point, i.e., its diffuse radiance under the
original illumination. We dim it and add the radiance Lnew of the point under new point lights, computed with a Cook–
Torrance model [10] from its predicted albedo, roughness, metallicity and normal: L′ = kL + Lnew, with k < 1. Each
photograph is then multiplied, in linear RGB, by L′/L at the pixels to which the points project.
Albedo editing.
We scale the albedo of the points of an object and multiply each photograph by the same factor, in linear
RGB, at the pixels to which these points project.
E. Limitations and Future Work
Geometry and tracks.
PointX locates a surface point through the geometry or the matches it is given, so their errors carry
over to its predictions. With the geometry estimated by VGGT-Ω[90], its per-view maps are less consistent than with the
tracks of RoMa-Ω[66] or with ground-truth geometry (Tab. 2), and an error in an estimated camera tilts the normals of its
view. RoMa-Ωin turn matches every pair of views, so its cost grows quadratically with their number. Since the tracks are an
input, PointX benefits from any progress in reconstruction and matching without retraining.
Pooling.
The weights of Eq. (3) have no learned parameters and depend only on the visibility of a point. They make the
prediction of a point independent of the view, but not smooth along a surface: neighboring points that are visible in different
sets of views are decoded from different sets of tokens, which can leave a small step between their predictions, e.g., where
the frame of another image ends. A confidence that also decreases towards the border of an image is a simple extension that
we leave for future work.
Albedo and illumination.
On Hypersim, PointX does not reach the most accurate baseline on albedo and irradiance
(Tabs. 1 and 3). Part of this gap is the overall scale of the albedo, i.e., the difference between PSNR and PSNRs, and it
remains when more views are added. Views of a scene under the same illumination add evidence about its surfaces, but not
about how their brightness divides into albedo and irradiance. PointX also assumes a static scene whose illumination does
not change between the images. Images captured under varying illumination could provide the missing evidence and are an
interesting direction.
Training data.
All multi-view supervision comes from rendered scenes: the real photographs are single images with
pseudo-labels, and they carry no roughness or metallicity (Tab. 5). On real scenes, we evaluate the accuracy of the albedo and
the consistency of all five properties, but not the accuracy of the other properties. Real multi-view captures with measured
materials would benefit both training and evaluation.
Number of views.
PointX encodes all images of a scene in one forward pass and is trained with up to 24 views. Its
consistency holds by construction within such a set of images. Extending the pooling across sets, for long videos or large
scenes, is left for future work.
