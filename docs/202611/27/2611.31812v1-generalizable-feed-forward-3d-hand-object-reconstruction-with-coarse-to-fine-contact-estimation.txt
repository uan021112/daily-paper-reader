Abstract
Reconstructing the 3D poses of hands and objects from a
single image, together with the contact between them, is
fundamental to understanding manipulation. To be practical,
the reconstruction must generalize to unseen objects, and the
contact must be localized precisely. Yet joint feed-forward
estimators often rely on predefined object sets, while contact
is typically predicted from image features alone, without
the explicit geometry needed to localize it. To address both
limitations, we present HORCRUX, a unified feed-forward
transformer with coarse-to-fine contact estimation.
For
unseen objects, a frozen foundation model encodes the
object from a given or image-reconstructed mesh rather
than from object-specific knowledge. A shared transformer
then reasons jointly over the object and one or two hands
to keep them aligned. For precise contact, coarse per-joint
predictions from latent features are resolved over finer hand
regions using the reconstructed geometry. Across seven
benchmarks, HORCRUX achieves state-of-the-art object and
hand pose accuracy among joint estimators and outperforms
a dedicated contact estimator in fine-grained contact. These
results suggest that contact is best estimated jointly with
reconstruction, whose geometry directly localizes it.
1. Introduction
Understanding how hands interact with objects is central
to robot learning from human video [52, 57], augmented
reality [4], and interaction analysis [54]. This requires recov-
ering the 3D poses and relative arrangement of the hands and
object from a monocular image [22, 51, 62]. Beyond pose,
contact reveals where the hands touch the object and thus
how it is held [73]. In practice, these applications involve
everyday objects and depend on exactly how each is grasped.
The reconstruction must therefore generalize to objects un-
seen in training, and the contact must be localized precisely
on the hand surface. Yet both remain challenging, as models
have no prior knowledge of an unseen object, and contact
predicted from image features alone is often imprecise.
Existing methods address these requirements only in part.
Joint feed-forward estimators [34, 35, 67] reason about the
hand and object together but rely on knowledge of predefined
objects, so they generalize poorly to unseen ones. Compos-
ing per-entity foundation models [7, 38, 49] handles unseen
objects given their meshes, yet estimating each pose indepen-
dently can misalign the hand and object. Meanwhile, recon-
struction methods mainly use contact as an auxiliary signal
(e.g., for supervision [5] or to refine hand pose [63, 73] and
object shape [26]), while dedicated estimators [28, 29, 42]

predict it without reconstruction. Even those predicting
dense contact [28, 29] rely on the image alone, without the
explicit hand-object geometry needed to localize it precisely
or to keep it consistent with the reconstruction. As a result,
no existing feed-forward model handles unseen objects while
also localizing contact precisely on the hand surface.
Achieving both means combining what existing methods
offer only separately. To generalize to unseen objects with-
out misalignment, a model should encode the object from its
geometry rather than from object-specific knowledge, while
still reasoning about the hand and object together. Precise
contact similarly calls for two complementary cues. Latent
features indicate which joints are likely in contact but lack
the explicit surface geometry needed to resolve which side
of a finger touches the object. Contact inferred from re-
constructed meshes captures this geometry but inherits their
pose errors. A model that reconstructs the hand and object
has access to both cues, and combining them can yield finer
and more accurate contact.
Building on these insights, we propose HORCRUX, a uni-
fied feed-forward transformer that performs Hand-Object
Reconstruction with Contact estimation and Robust gener-
alization to Unseen objects. It rests on two components:
(i) joint reasoning over foundation features for unseen
objects and (ii) coarse-to-fine contact estimation for pre-
cise contact. The former encodes the object with a frozen
object-pose foundation model [7], whose features capture its
geometry from a given or image-reconstructed mesh [58, 59].
A shared transformer then reasons jointly over these features
and those of each hand to predict the hand and object poses,
whether one or two hands are present. The latter in turn
draws on the resulting reconstruction rather than the image
alone. A coarse head first predicts per-joint contact from the
latent features of this transformer, which keeps it informa-
tive even under imprecise reconstruction. A fine head then
resolves it over finer hand regions [29] using distances to the
reconstructed object, separating the palm side of each finger
from its back. Contact is thereby localized precisely with-
out a separate estimator. Figure 1 summarizes the outputs
of HORCRUX.
Our key contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose HORCRUX, a unified feed-forward trans-
former that reasons jointly over frozen foundation fea-
tures, generalizing to unseen objects with given or image-
reconstructed meshes and handling one or two hands.
• We introduce coarse-to-fine contact estimation, which
predicts per-joint contact from latent features and resolves
it over finer hand regions using the reconstructed geometry,
without a separate estimator.
• Across seven benchmarks, HORCRUX achieves state-of-
the-art object and hand pose accuracy among joint es-
timators. It also outperforms a dedicated estimator in
fine-grained contact.
2. Related Work
Joint hand-object reconstruction.
Feed-forward meth-
ods reconstruct the hand and object jointly from a single
image [1, 5, 15, 21, 23, 35, 60, 72, 79], often coupling them
for consistency through shared features or signed distance
fields [9, 10, 34, 51, 62, 75]. Yet their generalization is lim-
ited, as many handle only predefined object sets and rely
on the appearance of their training objects [34, 35, 67]. For
unseen objects, some learn to reconstruct the shape from the
image [9, 10, 75] or use semantic and point-cloud cues [71].
HORCRUX instead takes the shape as input (i.e., a given or
image-reconstructed mesh [58, 59]) and encodes it with a
frozen object-pose foundation model [7], relying on geome-
try rather than on the appearance of training objects.
Per-entity foundation models.
Foundation models gen-
eralize broadly, but each addresses the hand or the object
alone. Hand regressors recover MANO [53] hands from
in-the-wild images [49, 50] or egocentric videos [78]. Zero-
shot object-pose estimators handle unseen objects given their
meshes [3, 7, 32, 43, 46], typically by comparing rendered
views with the image, so their internal features encode the
object geometry. To handle both, recent systems build on
pretrained visual backbones [14, 45, 56, 66] for hand-held
object reconstruction [77] or combine per-entity estimates
through per-image test-time optimization [38]. However,
independently estimated poses can misalign the hand and
object, and the object pose degrades under hand occlusion.
HORCRUX uses the features of foundation models rather
than their outputs, training a shared transformer to reason
jointly over them and predict the hand and object poses.
Hand-object contact estimation.
Reconstruction methods
mainly use contact as an auxiliary signal for supervision [5,
22], test-time refinement [19, 63], and evaluation [16]. Some
further predict it to refine hand pose [73], human-object
reconstruction [41, 69], and object shape [26]. As an out-
put, contact comes from one of two cues. Geometry-based
methods derive it from the reconstruction, as in hand-object
distance fields [10, 30, 51] or contact maps for interaction
recognition [11], but inherit its errors. Image-based methods
instead predict it directly, ranging from coarse per-hand con-
tact states [42, 54] to dense contact on human bodies [61] and
hand surfaces [28]. Recent work [29] reasons over finer hand
regions by prompting multimodal models (e.g., GPT [44]
and Claude [2]). However, these methods rely on image
features alone and are decoupled from reconstruction, so
their contact is often imprecise without explicit geometry
and may contradict the recovered poses. HORCRUX instead
combines both cues within reconstruction, predicting coarse
per-joint contact from latent features and resolving it over
finer hand regions using the reconstructed geometry.

3. Method
We present HORCRUX, a unified feed-forward transformer
that reconstructs the hands and the manipulated object from
a monocular image and estimates their fine-grained contact.
After defining the problem (Section 3.1), we describe how
a shared transformer reasons jointly over frozen foundation
features to predict the poses and coarse contact (Section 3.2).
We then detail how coarse-to-fine contact estimation resolves
it using the resulting reconstruction (Section 3.3) and how the
model is trained (Section 3.4). Figure 2 shows the pipeline.

3.3. Coarse-to-Fine Contact Estimation
The coarse contact bch of Eqn. (5) is predicted from latent
tokens rather than measured on the reconstructed meshes,
so it does not inherit their pose errors. At the joint level,
however, it cannot determine which side of a finger touches
the object (Figure 3a), and neither can coarser per-hand
contact states [42, 54]. We therefore resolve bch over the
P = 103 finer hand regions [29] (e.g., the palmar and dorsal
sides of each finger segment), giving ech ∈[0, 1]P . Whereas
prior work prompts multimodal models (e.g., GPT [44] and
Claude [2]) to predict region contact from the image alone,
we localize it with the reconstructed geometry that latent
tokens lack. Yet geometry alone inherits the pose errors that
latent tokens avoid. We thus combine the coarse contact with
region distances from the reconstruction (Figure 3b), so that
the reconstruction itself becomes evidence for contact.

3.4. Training
We train HORCRUX with frozen encoders in two stages that
follow its coarse-to-fine design. The first stage trains the
projections, the shared transformer, and the pose and coarse
contact heads. The second stage then trains the fine head on
top of the frozen model, as region distances are informative
only once the reconstructed hand and object roughly align.
Freezing the model also keeps the fine head from altering
the poses. Each sample supervises only the hands and object
it contains, so one- and two-hand samples from different
datasets jointly train a single model.

Implementation details.
HORCRUX is trained using
AdamW [39] with a cosine-decayed learning rate of 10−4
and a batch size of 256 on NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPUs.
The foundation encoders [7, 49, 56] remain frozen, and hand
crops are augmented with random scaling and translation.
Each model is selected by early stopping on its validation
split. HORCRUX‡ is a single model trained on the union of
all training splits, excluding every test frame. Further details
are in Supp. B. The code will be released upon publication.
Baselines.
We compare HORCRUX against four groups of
methods: (i) hand-pose estimators [8, 47, 70, 81]; (ii) zero-
shot object-pose estimators given the object mesh [7, 32,
43, 46]; (iii) joint hand-object estimators [34, 35, 67]; and
(iv) bimanual hand-object estimators [5, 16], which we re-
train on each two-hand dataset. Hand-pose estimators are
paired with MegaPose [32] and object-pose estimators with
H2ONet [70], the strongest counterparts, and marked with †.
Evaluation protocol.
For the hand, we report mean joint
and vertex position errors (MJE, VPE) and their Procrustes-
aligned variants (PA-MJE, PA-VPE). For the object, we
report the AUC of ADD-0.5D [67], the percentage of frames
whose average point distance (ADD) lies within half the
object diameter. For the joint pose, simulation displacement
(SD) is the distance the object moves under simulated gravity
with the hand fixed, and intersection volume (IV) is the
volume shared by the hand and object meshes [22]. Contact
deviation (CDev) [16] averages the predicted hand-object
distance over the GT contact vertices. The full protocol and
metric definitions are in Supp. B.
4.2. Hand-Object Reconstruction
One-hand interaction.

4.4. Generalization to Unseen Objects
Cross-dataset transfer.
We apply each DexYCB-s0 model
to OakInk, HO3D, and HOI4D without retraining and
swap the source and target datasets of the two-hand mod-
els. As shown in Table 5, the strongest joint estimator
SemiHand [35] is within 1.94 AUC of HORCRUX on HO3D,
whose objects also appear in DexYCB. On the unseen ob-
jects of OakInk and HOI4D, HORCRUX reaches an object
AUC of 93.29 and 67.68 with a lower hand error, exceeding
SemiHand by 52.79 and 52.11. The lower IV of the baselines
there stems from objects left far from the hand, as their CDev
of at least 136.21 shows. Across the two-hand datasets in
Table 2, the bimanual baselines likewise reach at most 13.41
AUC, whereas HORCRUX attains 22.56 and 29.63. The mar-
gin thus grows with object novelty, as the baselines rely on
the appearance of their training objects. HORCRUX instead
keeps the object in the grasp by posing it relative to the hand
and recovers its rotation from the geometry encoded by a
frozen object-pose foundation model. Retrained without this
encoding, the model still keeps the object in the grasp, but
its rotation error rises sharply (see Supp. C).

5. Conclusion
Further analyses.
Beyond the main experiments, the
supplementary material provides additional analyses of
HORCRUX.
First, we evaluate robustness to occlusion,
grouping the test frames by the fraction of the hand or object
silhouette hidden by the other. Second, we report the object
pose accuracy under stricter thresholds. Third, we ablate the
frozen hand and object features by removing each and retrain-
ing the model to isolate its contribution. Finally, we provide
further qualitative results on one-hand and two-hand inter-
actions, contact estimation, and approximate object meshes.
Comprehensive results are available in Supp. A and C.
Closing remarks.
We presented HORCRUX, a unified feed-
forward transformer that reconstructs one or two hands with
an unseen object and localizes their contact precisely, which
existing methods achieve only in part. Joint reasoning over
frozen foundation features encodes the object from its ge-
ometry rather than object-specific knowledge and keeps it
aligned with the hands. The model thus extends to unseen
objects, even with image-reconstructed meshes. Coarse-to-
fine contact estimation then draws on this reconstruction
rather than the image alone, resolving per-joint contact from
latent features over finer hand regions to localize it on the
hand surface. Across seven benchmarks, HORCRUX outper-
forms prior joint estimators in object and hand pose, and
its contact is more accurate than that of a dedicated esti-
mator. These findings suggest that foundation features let
hand-object reconstruction generalize to unseen objects, and
that this reconstruction in turn localizes contact precisely.

B. Experimental Details
This section extends the experimental setup of the main paper with additional details of HORCRUX. We first describe the
implementation details in Supp. B.1. We then present the loss terms in Supp. B.2. Next, we summarize the datasets with their
splits and training filters in Supp. B.3. Finally, we detail the evaluation protocol and metrics in Supp. B.4.
B.1. Implementation Details
Architecture.
The DINOv3 encoder Eimg is a frozen ViT-B/16 [56] applied to I at 224 × 224, and its scene tokens serve
only as context for the other tokens. The hand encoder Eh of Eqn. (2) combines the same DINOv3 with a frozen hand-pose
backbone [49] on the crop Ih at 256 × 256, which also covers the held object [67]. The DINOv3 features capture the hand
together with the held object, whereas the hand-pose backbone adds a strong prior on hand appearance. The backbone features
are resampled onto the 16 × 16 DINOv3 grid and concatenated channel-wise, and ψh projects them with LayerNorm-linear
layers [55]. Each left hand is mirrored into a right hand by diag(−1, 1, 1) so that both hands share ψh and the hand head,
and its predictions are mirrored back. A learned side embedding still distinguishes the two hands. For the object, the frozen
object-pose foundation model Eo [7] runs once per frame on I and Vo. The projection ψo of Eqn. (3) adds an MLP embedding
of this feature to a learnable initial token z0
o. The output layer of this MLP is zero-initialized, so training starts from z0
o alone.
The transformer F comprises L = 12 global self-attention blocks of width 768 and four register tokens [14].
The hand and object heads are two-layer MLPs with hidden widths of 768 and 256, respectively. The hand head predicts
the MANO [53] pose θh as 6D joint rotations [80] and the shape βh. For the translation th, it predicts a weak-perspective
camera (sh, xh, yh) [49], which the intrinsics K lift to

Training.
The first stage minimizes Eqn. (9) and gradually adds the physical terms of Eqn. (20) after a warm-up. Early

would over-trust the region distances. At every epoch, we thus shift the object in each training reconstruction by the offset
error of Eqn. (8) on a random validation image and recompute the region distances so that they carry test-like errors. Both
stages use AdamW [39] with a cosine-decayed learning rate of 10−4, a weight decay of 0.05, and a batch size of 256. All
results use an exponential moving average of the first-stage weights with a decay of 0.999. When one- and two-hand samples
share a batch, the outputs and losses of a missing hand are discarded.

B.3. Datasets
For one-hand interactions, we use the official s0 and s3 splits of DexYCB [6], the official HO3Dv2 partition [20], and the
rigid-object categories of HOI4D [37]. We also use the official split0 of OakInk [74], which holds out one camera view of each
sequence. For two-hand interactions, we use the official H2O split [31] and a sequence-level split of OakInk2 [76]. A closure
filter keeps the one-hand training frames in which at least four hand vertices lie within 5 mm of the object and their contacts
are in force closure [36]. This removes frames in which the hand approaches, releases, or unstably holds the object. Two
visibility filters then require at least four of the 21 joints and 90% of the object vertices to project into the image. HORCRUX‡
is trained once with the same architecture and recipe on the union of all training splits, excluding every test frame. One- and
two-hand samples from different datasets thus jointly train this single model.

C.2. Robustness to Occlusion
In a grasp, the hand and object hide each other’s image evidence. Following Wang et al. [67], we bin the test frames by
the fraction of the hand or object hidden by the other. The DexYCB-s0 models are scored on DexYCB and on OakInk
without retraining. Table 11 shows that every method degrades toward the most occluded hand bin on DexYCB. HORCRUX
nevertheless leads every baseline in every bin, most clearly in the middle bin with a PA-MJE of 5.18 against 6.00. On OakInk
the hand errors show no occlusion trend and HORCRUX stays below every baseline. Occlusion harms the object far more. On
DexYCB the AUC of the joint estimators falls from between 78.70 and 86.96 in the first bin to between 18.22 and 25.44 in the
last, whereas HORCRUX keeps 93.51 and 72.65. On OakInk HORCRUX stays above 90, whereas the joint estimators reach
only 23.49 to 41.35 as they fail on the unseen objects. Only HORCRUX thus remains accurate under heavy object occlusion.

C.3. Ablation of Foundation Features
We measure the contribution of the two frozen foundation features [7, 49] by removing each from the DexYCB-s0 model
and retraining the first stage with all else unchanged. Each variant is evaluated in domain and on the transfer targets without
retraining. As shown in Table 13, removing the object-pose feature of the given mesh lowers the average object AUC from
85.65 to 77.00. The ablated model still places the object at the grasping hand but largely loses its rotation, as its median
rotation error rises from at most 27◦to at least 50◦on every dataset. On OakInk the closest-point ADD-S hides this loss
with 94.08 against 93.29, but the plain ADD of Table 10 reveals it with a drop from 56.40 to 45.97. The MJE changes by at
most 1.23, so this feature serves the object alone. Removing the hand-pose backbone instead raises the average MJE from
18.00 to 30.71. The increase is larger on every target than in domain, reaching 21.87 on HO3D against 7.21 on DexYCB. The
object AUC also drops by 2.73 to 4.47 points, as the object is placed relative to the predicted hand. Since HORCRUX uses
only the backbone features of the hand-pose model and learns the hand pose on the joint task, these features mainly provide
a transferable prior on hand appearance. The object-pose feature thus recovers the object rotation, whereas the hand-pose
backbone drives the hand accuracy and its transfer across datasets.

D. Limitations and Future Work
Limitations.
While HORCRUX generalizes well to unseen objects, several limitations warrant further investigation. First,
HORCRUX encodes the object from its geometry and thus requires an object mesh as input. Second, its accuracy may drop on
objects and scenes far from the training data (e.g., articulated, deformable, or unusually shaped objects). Third, HORCRUX
inherits the fixed input resolution and pretraining biases of its frozen foundation encoders. Addressing these limitations would
further broaden the applicability of HORCRUX to hand-object interactions in the wild.
Future work.
HORCRUX opens up several promising avenues for future research. First, estimating the object shape jointly
with the poses would remove the need for an input mesh. Second, initializing a physics simulator with the reconstructed hands,
object, and fine contact could yield stable and penetration-free grasps that in turn provide physical supervision for training.
Third, extending HORCRUX from single images to videos would resolve single-frame depth and occlusion ambiguities and
reveal how contact forms and releases over time. Progress in these directions would bring HORCRUX closer to mesh-free,
physically plausible, and temporally consistent reconstruction of everyday hand-object interactions.
