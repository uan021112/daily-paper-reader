## Abstract 

Accurate 3D surface reconstruction from unposed images remains a fundamental challenge in computer vision. Recent feed-forward pointmap methods eliminate the need for camera poses but inherently produce discrete, sparse representations. In this paper, we present a generalizable pipeline that directly recovers continuous, high-fidelity 3D surfaces from unposed images by seamlessly integrating pointmap-and ray-based representations. Our approach first extracts explicit geometric priors using a pre-trained pointmap model. A query-view-centric selection module then identifies geometrically relevant reference views via a robust surfel voting mecha-nism. Next, a cross-attention regressor bridges target query rays with reference fea-tures to estimate a continuous coarse surface. Lastly, a raylet-based module aggre-gates local 3D features to carve out high-frequency details and analytical normals. Trained as a single foundation model on 9 diverse datasets, our method demon-strates exceptional zero-shot generalization on 6 unseen datasets, clearly surpassing state-of-the-art baselines in geometric accuracy and detail preservation. Code and data are available at: https://vlar-group.github.io/FoundSurface.html 

## 1 Introduction 

Accurate 3D surface reconstruction from multiple images plays a key role in emerging applications such as embodied AI and augmented reality. In recent years, significant progress has been made in the field of 3D reconstruction through the development of various 3D representation techniques [82]. These include coordinate-based methods such as radiance fields (NeRF) [56], and point-based methods like 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [43], and their variants [29, 12]. While these approaches achieve impressive results in novel view synthesis, they usually rely on highly accurate camera poses, which are often unavailable in practice, and typically struggle to recover precise 3D surfaces.

Furthermore, they generally require time-consuming per-scene optimization rather than functioning as generalizable, feed-forward models [99]. Recently, the pioneering feedforward method DUSt3R [81] has enabled precise 3D geometry re-construction by directly predicting pixel-aligned pointmaps from unposed image pairs. Subsequent methods [101] have extended this pointmap representation to handle multiple views [78, 92], to improve computational efficiency [65, 52], etc .. Leveraging powerful Transformer architectures and large-scale training datasets, these approaches achieve excellent accuracy in recovering surface points from image collections. However, the reconstructed pointmaps are inherently discrete and sparse, lacking the continuous and fine-grained geometric details required for many downstream applications, such as robot manipulation and scene editing. Another recent line of work focuses on ray-based representations such as RayDF [53] and RayletDF [84]. Their key innovation is learning a ray-surface distance field for any query light ray conditioned on visual inputs, thus enabling the recovery of continuous and high-fidelity surfaces. However, they still rely on accurate camera poses or sparse point clouds as inputs to reconstruct detailed surfaces. In this paper, we present a generalizable, feed-forward 3D surface reconstruction pipeline that takes only a set of unposed images as input and directly recovers continuous, high-fidelity 3D surfaces. We achieve this by seamlessly integrating pointmap- and ray-based representations. In particular, as illustrated in Figure 2, our pipeline comprises four key components: 1) a pre-trained pointmap-based model such as VGGT [78], which extracts visual tokens and generates pixel-aligned pointmaps along with estimated camera poses; 2) a query-view-centric selection module designed to identify geometrically relevant reference views; 3) a coarse ray-surface distance regressor utilizing cross-attention layers; and 4) a raylet-based surface module for refining surface details and normals. Among the four modules of our pipeline, the core is the second and third modules, which seamlessly integrate pointmap- and ray-based representations, effectively estimating continuous surfaces for any query views conditioned on the recovered pointmaps from unposed input images. In contrast, directly applying RayletDF to VGGT pointmaps can lead to inaccurate raylet initialization, resulting in multi-surface artifacts and missing regions. Our pipeline is based on large Transformer and standard neural layers with sufficient capacity, without relying on domain specific assumptions. Therefore, it is rather suitable to consume various indoor and outdoor datasets. We train a single found ation 3D surface model on a mixture of 9 public datasets with depth annotations, including BlendMVS [94], WildRGB [86], ScanNet [21], HyperSim [63], MVS-Synth [37], ARKitScenes [4], S3DIS [1], SceneNN [33], and GTASfM [79]. Our model is termed FoundSurface and Figure 1 shows qualitative results. Our contributions are: • We introduce a large feed-forward model to recover continuous 3D scene surfaces from an arbitrary number of images without needing poses. • We design a query-view-centric selection module together with a cross attention based fusion module to seamlessly integrate pointmap- and ray-based 3D representations. • We train a single foundation model and demonstrate superior 3D surface reconstruction accuracy across multiple datasets, clearly surpassing existing baselines. 

## 2 Related Work 

To recover 3D surfaces from images, classical SfM [59] and SLAM [9] systems extract sparse point clouds that can be smoothed into continuous surfaces, though they often fail to capture fine details. 2Early deep learning methods utilized explicit 3D representations such as voxel grids [17, 90, 91], point clouds [24], octrees [72], and meshes [41]. Nevertheless, the quality of these discrete structures is inherently bottlenecked by memory footprints and resolution constraints. Comprehensive surveys of these methods are available in [5, 32]. 

Implicit Representations for Surface Reconstruction : To bypass the resolution limits of explicit 3D formats, implicit representations use simple MLPs to parameterize continuous 3D shapes [ ? ]. Techniques such as occupancy fields (OF) [15, 55], un/signed distance fields (U/SDF) [16, 60, 75, 22], vector fields (VF) [93, 61], and NeRF [56, 73] operate by mapping 3D coordinates to local geometric or visual properties. While these approaches and their variants [82] yield highly accurate surface reconstructions and 2D renderings, they rely on dense 3D spatial sampling and exhaustive network evaluations per view and often require images with accurate poses as input. In this paper, our FoundSurface learns a feedforward model from unposed images, directly regressing surface points for any query viewing rays in single pass. 

Point-based Representations for Surface Reconstruction : A recent breakthrough is 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [43], which models environments as a set of 3D Gaussians with optimizable positions, covariances, and colors. Supported by a custom tile-based rasterizer, 3DGS delivers real-time 2D view synthesis. Nevertheless, because it inherently lacks tight geometric constraints, 3DGS falls short in extracting fine surface details and generating highly accurate depth maps. While a multitude of recent works [13, 25, 85, 31, 34, 18, 89, 48, 35, 98, 87, 58, 97, 67, 11, 102, 30] have attempted to enforce surface alignment and improve depth quality, they generally require time-consuming per-scene optimization and images with accurate poses as input. Another breakthrough is DUSt3R [81] which demonstrates 3D geometry can be represented by pixel-aligned pointmaps and recovered from unposed image pairs in a single forward pass. A series of succeeding methods [101, 47, 8, 23, 14, 78, 80, 92, 38, 100, 50, 88, 39, 104, 46, 26, 49] extend it from different perspectives, including handling more views , modeling videos, processing faster, etc .. Although these methods achieve impressive results, they are limited to recovering discrete and sparse point clouds, falling short of producing continuous, high-fidelity surfaces. 

Ray-based Representations for Surface Reconstruction : Another line of work models 3D shapes through ray-based neural functions. By taking individual rays as input, these models directly regress either RGB values [51, 27, 57, 69, 10] or explicit surface points [28, 2, 53, 84, 45, 70, 40, 71, 96]. Despite their impressive rendering efficiency and surface accuracy, most of these methods require posed images as input and struggle to generalize across diverse 3D scenes. In this paper, we leverage the robust surface representation capabilities of RayDF [53] and RayletDF [84] and seamlessly integrate them with pointmap-based representations. This synergistic approach enables feedforward 3D surface reconstruction directly from sets of images. 

## 3 FoundSurface 

FoundSurface recovers continuous high-fidelity 3D scene surfaces through a coarse-to-fine, discrete-to-continuous progression. Given a set of unposed and uncalibrated multi-view images I = {Ii ∈

RH×W ×3 | i = 1 , ..., K }, we first extract an explicit geometric prior using a pre-trained pointmap-based model elaborated in Section 3.1. Next, we filter out redundant information by selecting geometrically relevant views for any target query in Section 3.2. We then transition from 3D points to rays by regressing an initial, continuous coarse surface, which resolves global geometry and occlusions detailed in Section 3.3. Finally, we refine this coarse surface using a raylet-based module that aggregates high-resolution local 3D features to recover fine-grained details, yielding the final high-fidelity surfaces, discussed in Section 3.4.

3.2 Query-View-Centric Selection Module 

As shown in the leftmost block of Figure 3, when reconstructing the geometry of a novel query view, which is defined explicitly by a given target camera pose Ttarget and Ctarget , intuitively, not all input views are helpful. Views that do not observe the target surfaces will only introduce noise and computational overhead. Therefore, instead of globally querying features of all input views, we design a selection module to identify only the overlapping, geometrically relevant reference views by a surfel-based voting mechanism, ensuring the selected views provide reliable geometric coverage. Specifically, we first establish a canonical geometric reference for this novel query view. We project all available input pointmaps onto the camera plane defined by the target query pose, and apply a standard depth buffer (z-buffer) to render a dense target pseudo-depth map, Dtarget . We then uniformly divide Dtarget into non-overlapping patches, conceptualized as target surfels. To evaluate a candidate view j out of all input views, we similarly divide its pointmap Pj into candidate surfels and project them onto the target view. For each pixel (x, y ) within a projected candidate surfel, we compare its depth value Djx,y against the canonical target depth Dtarget x,y . The pixel casts a valid match vote according to an indicator function: 

vx,y = I   Dtarget x,y − Djx,y < δ depth

By aggregating these surfel-level results, we compute the percentage of matched surfels for the entire candidate view j, yielding a robust, coverage-aware overlap score. Finally, we rank all input views based on this surfel overlap score and retain only the top-k candidates. This geometric filtering eliminates irrelevant regions and provides our subsequent attention modules with a highly curated and effective query candidate set: {Tj }Kj=1 , {Cj }Kj=1 , {Pj }Kj=1 , {Fj }Kj=1 .More implementation details are provided in Appendix A.1.

3.4 Raylet-based Surface Module 

To elevate our coarse surface to a high-fidelity continuous representation, we must compensate for the missing local geometric details. We achieve this by refining the coarse raylets {ln}Nn=1 through the aggregation of high-resolution local 3D features. We first voxelize the initial pointmaps {Pj }Kj=1 and index the corresponding visual features {Fj }Kj=1 

obtained in Section 3.3. We then utilize a 3D sparse convolution network (SPConv) [19] to process these indexed features, constructing a robust 3D local feature volume. As shown in the rightmost block of Figure 3, the sparse convolution network produces a set of spatial anchors and their associated local features, denoted as A = {am}Mm=1 . Here, the anchor points are generated through sparse convolution over the voxelized 3D representation, rather than being directly sampled from the original VGGT pointmaps. Each anchor is associated with a learned local geometric feature, which is subsequently used to refine nearby raylets. 3.5 Training 

We employ a decoupled two-stage training strategy to train our core modules. 

Stage 1: Coarse Ray-Surface Distance Regression. In this stage, our goal is to establish an accurate global geometric framework. We train the coarse ray-surface distance regressor to predict the continuous coarse depth map Dtarget . Supervision is provided by the aligned ground truth depth 

Dgt , using a loss function that combines a pixel-wise ℓ1 distance with a spatial gradient loss to enforce surface consistency: 

ℓcoarse = |Dgt − Dtarget | + ∥∇ Dgt − ∇ Dtarget ∥ (9) 

Stage 2: Raylet-based Surface Refinement. Once the coarse geometry is reasonably established, we freeze or isolate the first-stage predictions and focus entirely on training the raylet-based surface module. To capture high-frequency geometric details without destabilizing the learned global structure, we predict a residual distance map ∆D. During this stage, we apply a stop-gradient operation to the coarse predictions, formulating the fine-stage loss as: 

ℓf ine = Dgt −  sg (Dtarget ) + ∆ D (10) where sg (·) denotes the stop-gradient operator. Here, the global residual map ∆D is formed by aggregating the local raylet residuals corresponding to each pixel, i.e., ∆D = {∆dn}Nn=1 , where 

∆dn is computed following the local feature aggregation mechanism detailed in Section 3.4. This two-stage training paradigm effectively decouples global topology localization from local surface refinement, thereby enabling high-fidelity 3D reconstructions. More details are in Appendix A.4. 

## 4 Experiments 

Training Datasets : We train our model on a mixture of 9 datasets, including the training splits of ScanNet [21], GTASfM [79] and ARKitScenes [4], together with the full datasets of Blend-6MVS [94], WildRGB [86], HyperSim [63], MVS-Synth [37], S3DIS [1] and SceneNN [33] . For evaluation, we use ScanNet++ [95], 7Scenes [66], MultiScan [54], Mushroom [62], Replica [68], and Tanks&Temples [44]. Detailed dataset splits are provided in Appendix A.17. 

Baselines : We compare our approach against five representative baselines: 1) VGGT Projection [78], evaluated via the direct projection of its pointmaps; 2) PriorDA [83], a state-of-the-art depth completion model applied to VGGT pointmaps; 3) CUT3R [80], a feed-forward model utilizing state tokens for novel query views; 4) FLARE [100], the latest feed-forward Gaussian splatting model; and 5) RayletDF [84], a state-of-the-art generalizable ray-based method. 

Metrics : We evaluate surface reconstruction by comparing the predicted ray–surface distance against the ground-truth distance. We report the absolute distance error ( ADE , in meters), together with four commonly used metrics [74]: root mean squared error ( RMSE ), absolute relative distance ( Abs Rel ), squared relative distance ( Sq Rel ), and the Threshold accuracy ( δ ≤ 1.25 ) which measures the percentage of predictions that fall within the specified threshold of the ground truth. For normal evaluation, we follow the metrics used in normal estimation [3]. We report both the mean and median angular errors on normal. Additionally, we evaluate performance by measuring the percentage of pixels with an angular error below thresholds of 11.25°, 22.5°, and 30.0°. Detailed computation of all metrics is in Appendix A.7&A.8. 

4.1 Evaluation of Out-of-domain Reconstruction 

In this setting, our model takes 50 unposed input views and predicts ray–surface distances for query rays. For each scene, we uniformly sample 50 images from the test RGB sequence as input views. We further ablate the number of input views in Tables 3& 4. We evaluate on ScanNet++, 7Scenes, MultiScan, MuSHRoom, and Tanks&Temples. These datasets are excluded from both the VGGT and our training datasets, thereby forming a strictly out-of-domain evaluation setting. For VGGT Projection [78], we directly project the pointmaps predicted by VGGT into the query view using Open3D [103]. We further combine VGGT projection with PriorDA [83], a state-of-the-art depth completion model, to fill missing regions after projection. For the feed-forward reconstruction model CUT3R [80], we first forward all input views and then query the scene state token to predict query-view point coordinates. For the feed-forward Gaussian splatting model 

FLARE [100], we render query-view depth maps from the predicted 3D Gaussians. For the ray-based method RayletDF [84], which is originally optimized for Gaussians and point clouds, we retrain it on VGGT-predicted pointmaps from our training datasets for a fair comparison. Standard deviation results are provided in Appendix A.6. 

Results & Analysis: Table 1 and Figure 4 report quantitative and qualitative comparisons on ray-surface distance estimation for all query rays of test images. To assess the contribution of our Raylet-based refinement module, we also report the performance of our coarse ray-surface distance regressor in Table 1, denoted as w/o refine .Our method achieves the best ADE across all out-of-domain datasets, outperforming all baselines by a large margin. For example, on ScanNet++, our ADE is 0.132m compared with 0.198m for PriorDA. These results demonstrate the strong generalization ability of FoundSurface on unseen datasets. Compared with the state-token-based model CUT3R, our method preserves finer geometric details, as illustrated in Figure 4. Removing the proposed refinement module substantially degrades reconstruction accuracy, confirming its effectiveness. More results are provided in Appendix A.13.

4.3 Influence of the Number of Input Views 

We evaluate the effect of input-view density by varying the number of input views from 5 to 100. Its advantage is particularly pronounced given sparse views, demonstrating stronger robustness under limited observations. Additional qualitative results are provided in Appendix A.11. 

4.4 Evaluation of Single View Reconstruction 

In this section, we evaluate single-view reconstruction. For each test sequence, we sample one image as the input view and use its two adjacent frames as query views for evaluation. Quantitative results are reported in Table 5. Our method still achieves the best performance across all datasets. CUT3R obtains the second-best results on most datasets, likely benefiting from its training design for unseen-view inpainting. Additional qualitative results are provided in Appendix A.12.

## 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced a generalizable, feed-forward model that reconstructs continuous, high-fidelity 3D surfaces from unposed image collections. By seamlessly integrating pointmap-and ray-based representations through a query-view-centric selection module and a raylet-based refinement network, our method bridges the gap between discrete geometric priors and continuous surface generation. Extensive cross-dataset evaluations demonstrate that our approach achieves superior zero-shot generalization and geometric accuracy compared to existing baselines. 

Limitations : Our model has a large size due to the reliance on a pre-trained pointmap backbone. A.1 Details of Query-View-Centric Selection 

For reference view selection, we first project the N point coordinate frames to the query camera plane using the extrinsics and intrinsics predicted by VGGT, resulting in N × H × W projected images. A pseudo-depth map for the query view is then generated by taking the minimum positive value across these projections. Lastly, both the projected images and the pseudo-depth map are divided into 8×8 non-overlapping patches. The candidate view evaluation uses thresholds of δdepth =0.05 and 

τvote =0.3. For all experimental evaluations, the number of selected reference views is fixed at 2.

The Raylet refinement network frefine consists of three modules: (1) a dimensionality reduction network that contains two linear layers and a ReLU layer. (2) a Raylet prediction network which is an 8-layer MLP network. (3) a sparse convolution network to aggregate local features in 3D space. Considering that the pointmaps estimated by VGGT are defined within a normalized coordinate space, we set the voxelization resolution to 0.025 . This configuration inherently facilitates an adaptive voxelization strategy, where the effective resolution scales dynamically according to the scene’s spatial extent. Structure of Raylet prediction network and sparse convolution network is available in Section A.18.

A.4 Details of Training 

The VGGT prediction and ground truth are not located in the same coordinate system; thus, we need to align novel view camera pose for supervision. Though the VGGT is expected to predict at a canonical normalized scale during training stage, in inference, it does not perfectly align with it. Directly training the network to predict novel views on data normalized by VGGT preprocessing pipeline would force the network to compensate for the coordinate system mismatch. Thus, we re-calculate the transformation from VGGT prediction to ground truth data during training. Specifically, we first filter out the points whose confidence scores are within the top 85% . Then least-square fit algorithm [7] is utilized to compute the scale, rotation and translation between the VGGT prediction to ground truth data, forming a transformation matrix. Subsequently, we use the transformation matrix to align the ground truth data for supervision. Our models are trained using the AdamW optimizer and a OneCycle learning rate schedule. Specif-ically, the coarse regressor is trained for 200 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4, while the refinement network is trained for 100 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4. In each epoch, we sample 4,200 data points for training. The model is trained on 4 A800 GPUs for about 72 hours. 17 A.5 Computation Cost Analysis

A.17 Details of Datasets A.17.1 ScanNet++ 

We adopt the official test splits for novel view synthesis but exclude scene “1ada7a0617” due to corruptions in its COLMAP data . For each scene, we uniformly sample every 5th frame from the video sequence for training, and reserve every 10th frame in the test set for testing. We use the iPhone sequences with COLMAP registered poses and render mesh scans to obtain high-resolution depth maps. All the images are resized to 960 ×720. 

A.17.2 ScanNet 

We use the official training and test splits of ScanNetV2 dataset, with 1201 training scenes and 100 test scenes. Similar to ScanNet++, we also load every 5th frame from the trajectories for training, and then reserve every 10th frame in test set for evaluation. 

A.17.3 ARKitScenes 

We use the training split of DUSt3R. For training views, we sample every 10th RGB image from the “vga-wide" dataset set and unsample the depth to 640 ×480 as in DUSt3R. 

A.17.4 BlendMVS 

All the images of 507 scenes in raw dataset are used in our training dataset. For convenience of sampling during training, we calculate the overlap between each image pair and employ greedy algorithm to rank images in every scenes in video frame sequence order. 

A.17.5 HyperSim 

We use the “cam_00" trajectory in each scene and all the images are used for training. Similar to BlendMVS images are ranked in video frame sequence order for training convenience. 

A.17.6 WildRGBD 

Given the extensive scale of the dataset, we select the first ten scenes from each category to construct our training set. Following the preprocessing pipeline in DUSt3R, all images are cropped and resized to a fixed 4:3 aspect ratio to maintain consistency across samples. 

A.17.7 MVS-Synth 

The official raw dataset, featuring a resolution of 960 ×540, is employed. All images within the sequences are incorporated into our training framework without further downsampling. 24 A.17.8 S3DIS 

Our training set comprises all areas except for Area 5 and every 10th frame is reserved for validation. Since the raw data is unordered, we employ a custom ranking algorithm to establish a coherent sequence for training. 

A.17.9 SceneNN 

All scenes in the dataset are utilized for training. To reduce temporal redundancy while maintaining scene coverage, we sample frames from each trajectory at a stride of 10. 

A.17.10 GTASfM 

We adhere to the official training split of GTASfM. All images within the designated split are used to construct our training dataset. 

A.17.11 Multiscan 

We use official test split of Multiscan, loading every 50th image from trajectory and then reserve every 10th frame in test set for evaluation. 

A.17.12 Replica 

We use the pre-processed Replica dataset provided by DN-Splatter [74], which contains 8 scenes with 1200 ×680 resolution images. Each scene consists of 64 training images and 16 testing images. 

A.17.13 7Scenes 

Since the original ground truth is imperfect, the same as [77], we adopt COLMAP GT [6] provided that is obtained via global optimization across all sequences.For evaluation, we use the 18-scene split, consistent with the setup in Spann3R [76]. 

A.17.14 MuSHRoom 

We employ the official MuSHRoom split, using the long-sequence RGB-D data captured by Kinect sensors. Every 10th frame is sampled for testing. 

A.17.15 Tanks&Temples 

Data is sourced from the RMVDB [64] variant, which provides high-quality processed depth maps. For evaluation purposes, we reserve every 10th frame of the sequences as the test set. 

A.18 Details of Sparse Convolution and Raylet Distance Network 

We simply employ the SparseConv architecture as our Feature extractor. Particularly, we use the existing U-Net implementation of SPconv PyTorch package https://github.com/traveller59/ spconv . As illustrated in Figure 11, the encoder and decoder have 8 layers respectively, which produce 512-dimensional features. Block means a sequential application of SparseConvolution in encoder or SparseInverseConvolution in decoder, GroupNorm and ReLU activation. Our Raylet distance network architecture consists of: 1) an initial MLP layer with ReLU that projects input features to 512 dimensions; 2) a block of 8 successive dense layers (512 neurons, ReLU); and 3) a final linear output layer that predicts the raylet distance.
