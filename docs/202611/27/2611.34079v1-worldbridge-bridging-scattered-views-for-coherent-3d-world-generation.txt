Abstract
Generating coherent and explorable 3D worlds from
scattered images with little or no visual overlap is a fun-
damental and long-standing challenge in computer vision,
which existing geometry-aware methods fail to address.
Meanwhile, existing image- and video-based methods may
generate unseen regions but produce inconsistent geometry
across views. To bridge this gap, we present WorldBridge,
a framework for coherent and controllable 3D world gen-
eration under scattered images with little or no visual over-
lap. WorldBridge encodes scattered references into a global

1. Introduction
Creating explorable 3D worlds from images [29, 66, 97]
has been a long-standing challenge in computer vision, with
broad applications in robotics [43], AR/VR [37], and video
games [62]. In practical scenarios, users may wish to cre-
ate a 3D world from only a few reference images, which
can depict different parts of a scene, exhibit substantially
different visual appearances, and share little or no visual
overlap. Such sparse observations leave large portions of
a scene unobserved, making its geometry and appearance
underconstrained [55, 79]. Rather than treating such ambi-
guity solely as a reconstruction challenge, we view it as an
opportunity for controllable world generation, where users
can specify how scattered references are spatially arranged
while allowing unobserved regions to be plausibly synthe-
sized. Yet, achieving this goal requires integrating scattered
visual references, user-specified constraints, and generated
content into a spatially coherent 3D world.
Existing methods have made considerable progress
in 3D world generation.
Image- and video-based ap-
proaches [8, 19, 98, 102] use powerful generative models to
synthesize realistic views under camera control. However,
these approaches may produce inconsistent 3D geometry
across the generated views.
Geometry-aware generative
methods [20, 27, 34, 52] incorporate geometric informa-
tion to improve the consistency of generated appearance and
scene structure. Other approaches [61, 101] maintain geo-
metric memories to preserve information from previously
observed or generated views. Despite these advances, exist-
ing methods still struggle when the reference images have
little or no visual overlap. Geometric information from iso-
lated observations provides limited guidance for connect-
ing distant regions, while generative models may introduce
content that does not faithfully follow the input references.
Consequently, maintaining both global scene coherence and
reference fidelity remains difficult, particularly under flexi-
ble camera and text conditioning.
In this work, we present WorldBridge, a generative
framework for creating coherent and controllable 3D worlds
from scattered reference images with little or no visual over-
lap. We aim to preserve the observed content while plau-
sibly generating the unseen regions under flexible camera
and text conditioning. We coordinate shared global scene
context with information from individual reference images.
The global context ensures the consistent generation of ge-
ometry and appearance across different views. The individ-
ual references provide constraints to preserve the observed
content. This allows us to generate plausible 3D worlds
while maintaining consistency with the observations.
Specifically, WorldBridge encodes the posed reference
images into a shared global scene representation. A camera-
conditioned scene reader uses this representation to guide
the generation of each target view.
In parallel, a sepa-
rate reference encoder preserves information from individ-
ual reference images. Our temporal and spatial reference
attention mechanism further integrates temporal references
along the target camera trajectory and spatial references
outside the trajectory. This mechanism allows both types
of references to be used without assigning artificial tempo-
ral positions to spatial references. A plug-and-play latent-
reference consistency strategy further improves reference
fidelity through reprojection-guided test-time optimization.
Together, these designs enable us to generate both RGB im-
ages and 3D geometry for the requested camera viewpoints.
Fig. 1 illustrates the capabilities of WorldBridge under
different camera and text conditioning. First, WorldBridge
can generate a plausible 3D scene from scattered views with
little overlap; see Fig. 1(a). As illuminated in Fig. 1(b), our
method can also generate different spatial layouts from the
same reference images by changing the camera poses. As
Fig. 1(c) shows, we further allow users to control unseen
regions through text prompts while preserving the observed
content. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 1(d), our method gen-
eralizes well to references with markedly different visual
styles, generating coherent 3D worlds even from combina-
tions of real-world photographs and stylized images.
To comprehensively verify our effectiveness around
3D world generation with scattered references, we intro-
duce the WorldBridge benchmark suite. Existing bench-
marks [48, 103] focus on evaluating generation on over-
lapping reference views, while our benchmark provides up
to six scattered references with little or no visual over-
lap. Specifically, WorldBridge-Short evaluates RGB syn-
thesis from two low-overlap references in short real-world
sequences of small scenes.
WorldBridge-Long evaluates
visual quality, geometric consistency, camera control, and
reference fidelity using multiple low-overlap references in
large real-world scenes. WorldBridge-Agent extends these
evaluations to synthetic scenes, assessing the integration of
both temporal references along the target trajectory and spa-
tial references outside it. Extensive experiments on existing
novel-view-synthesis benchmarks and our proposed World-
Bridge benchmark demonstrate superior view fidelity, ge-
ometric accuracy, camera controllability, consistency, and
generation quality, compared with previous approaches.
To summarize, our contributions are listed as follows:
• We introduce WorldBridge, a framework for coherent
and controllable 3D world generation from scattered im-
ages with little or no visual overlap. It preserves obser-
vations while plausibly generating unseen regions for 3D
worlds through flexible camera and text conditioning.
• We propose a coordinated generation strategy that com-
bines global scene context with reference-specific infor-
mation.
Global-context-grounded generation provides
shared guidance across views, while temporal and spatial

2. Related Work
Novel View Synthesis and 3D Reconstruction.
Novel
view synthesis (NVS) generates images from unseen view-
points using reference observations.
Optimization-based
methods [1, 36, 53, 54] reconstruct implicit or explicit 3D
representations from posed images for photorealistic ren-
dering. Sparse-view methods [13, 33, 55, 70, 90, 104] use
regularization to improve reconstruction from limited ob-
servations. Feed-forward methods [3, 4, 7, 41, 67, 71, 74–
76, 82, 92, 95] further leverage learned multi-view and ge-
ometric priors to recover 3D representations from sparse or
unposed images, with motion and structure priors also sup-
porting robust monocular depth estimation under adverse
conditions [86]. Generative methods synthesize plausible
content beyond the observed regions, drawing on image and
video diffusion priors for novel view synthesis [6, 8, 19,
45, 60, 61, 79, 80, 98, 101, 102] and learned geometric pri-
ors for 3D shape completion [87]. Recent geometry-aware
methods [20, 21, 27, 34, 35, 52, 77, 83] incorporate geo-
metric representations or model appearance and geometry
jointly to improve 3D consistency, with related cross-modal
alignment challenges arising in joint audio–video genera-
tion [84]. A concurrent work [40] leverages camera-queried
implicit representations for end-to-end novel view synthe-
sis. In contrast, our work focuses on the more unconstrained
setting of bridging scattered on- and off-trajectory refer-
ences with little or no visual overlap to generate coherent
3D worlds.
3D Scene Generation. Research on 3D content spans spa-
tiotemporal reasoning over dynamic point clouds [100] and
the generation of diverse, explorable environments. Early
methods [9, 46, 91] use learned scene representations and
compositional priors to generate large-scale environments.
Other methods [10, 44, 65, 99] leverage pretrained image
generators for text- and image-conditioned scene synthe-
sis.
Recent approaches [16, 32, 39, 51, 69, 78, 96, 97]
support scene expansion and interactive exploration, with
multi-agent workflows also used for scientific video un-
derstanding [85] and for spatially consistent video gener-
ation across clips using shared 3D layouts [64]. Camera-
controlled methods [12, 29, 63, 66] further enable explo-
ration along specified camera trajectories. While these ap-
proaches advance controllable world generation, integrat-
ing multiple scattered observations is not their primary fo-
cus.
WorldBridge instead generates coherent 3D worlds
from multiple posed references with little or no overlap, in-
cluding references outside the target camera trajectory. It
coordinates shared scene context with individual reference
information to guide generation while preserving observed
content.
3. Proposed Method
3.1. Preliminaries

3.3. Global-context-grounded Generation
When scattered reference views share little or no visual
overlap, cross-view feature correspondences break down
completely. Relying solely on fine-grained reference latents
leaves the diffusion transformer blind to the global spatial
layout–it lacks a unified structural representation to infer
how disconnected regions connect across unobserved blind
spots. To resolve the inherent tension between global struc-
tural coherence and local observation fidelity, we decouple
scene conditioning into two complementary pathways: a
global context path that infers a coarse, scene-wide spatial
topology, and a local reference path that encodes each ref-
erence’s appearance independently.

3.4. Temporal and Spatial Reference Attention
As shown in Fig. 3, WorldBridge integrates temporal refer-
ences RT, spatial references RS, and generated target views
G through joint attention. Temporal references have known
positions in the target sequence, while spatial references
have camera poses but no assigned sequence positions. To
avoid introducing artificial temporal relationships, we ap-
ply selective temporal positional encoding and use learnable
role embeddings for spatial references.
During training, temporal references are sampled from
the target frames, while spatial references are sampled from
other frames of the same video. We also randomly treat
some temporal references as spatial references, retaining
their camera poses but omitting their temporal positions.
Specifically, temporal RoPE is applied only when both
tokens in an attention pair have known sequence positions:
mij = 1[i ∈G ∪RT ∧j ∈G ∪RT].

For pairs involving spatial references (mij = 0), temporal
RoPE is omitted. Spatial RoPE and the SCoPE [93] camera
encoding based on Pl¨ucker rays are retained for all pairs.
This allows WorldBridge to integrate temporal and spatial
references without imposing artificial temporal positions.
3.5. Latent-Reference Consistency
To further preserve observed content, inspired by [88], we
propose a plug-and-play strategy to refine the predicted la-
tents during inference. For each reference, we estimate a
metric-scale 3D point map using MoGe-3 [38] and project
its RGB points into each target camera Pj. This produces
a pseudo-image eIj and valid mask Mj. Unreliable depth
boundaries are discarded, and a depth buffer resolves pro-
jection conflicts.

masked MSE between bIj and eIj, averaged over target views

4. Experiments
4.1. WorldBridge Benchmark
Existing evaluation benchmarks do not fully capture the
challenges of preserving scattered observations while gen-
erating coherent scenes along extended camera trajectories,
especially when references have little or even no overlap
and lie outside the target path. To address this gap, we intro-
duce the WorldBridge benchmark suite (Fig. 4), comprising
three complementary settings that assess low-overlap view
bridging, long-range multi-reference generation, and the in-
tegration of off-trajectory observations. We also provide a
reference overlap comparison on the right of (Fig. 4) be-
tween ours and public benchmarks to show the low-overlap
world generation setting of our benchmark.

WorldBridge-Short
evaluates view bridging between
two low-overlap references.
It contains 100 clips of 5–
15 seconds from RealEstate10K [103], DL3DV [48], and
SpatialVID [72], using the first and last frames as refer-
ences. We first screen candidate clips using Qwen3.5 [58]
and RoMa v2 [15], then use ViPE [28] and DepthAnything
v3 [47] to estimate camera poses and depth. Each clip pro-
vides at most six intermediate target views, each with at
least 50% of its pixels estimated to be visible in one or both
references. This setting primarily assesses RGB fidelity in
reference-supported regions.
WorldBridge-Long
evaluates
long-range
generation
from multiple references, containing 20 indoor and outdoor
videos of 20–60 seconds from SpatialVID [72], each with
six references and 50 non-reference target views. The ref-
erences cover complementary regions along the trajectory,
with low overlap encouraged between non-adjacent views.
We use the same annotation pipeline as Short and evaluate
RGB and reference fidelity, geometry accuracy, camera
control, cross-view consistency, and generation quality.
WorldBridge-Agent
evaluates generation from refer-
ences on and off the target trajectory. It comprises 20 syn-
thetic indoor and outdoor scenes created through an agent-
assisted workflow: an agent designs a scene blockout and
camera trajectory, which provide structural guides for ref-
erence image generation. Each scene has two six-reference
settings sharing the same target trajectory, with references
either on or off that trajectory. We use the same evalua-
tion categories as Long, except RGB fidelity and geometry
accuracy, since captured target images and ground-truth ge-
ometry are unavailable. Additional benchmark details are
provided in the supplementary material.
4.2. Experimental Settings
Implementation Details.
We implement our pipeline
based on GAE [52], which provides a good geometry-
appearance space for scene generation. The scene encoder
and scene reader are initialized from LagerNVS [67] and
tuned; the reference and text encoders are DepthAnything
v3 [47] and Qwen3-0.6B [89] and kept frozen; the pipeline
has 0.94B trainable parameters in total. We train our model
on a collection of video clips from RealEstate10K [103],
DL3DV [48], and SpatialVID [72], which contains about
22k video clips at 672×378 and is separate from the evalu-
ation benchmarks. The model is trained on 32 GPUs for 10
epochs using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 64,
and a learning rate decaying from 5 × 10−5 to 5 × 10−6.
During training, we use a mixing sample strategy to sample
temporal and spatial references from the video clips. More

6. Conclusion
We present WorldBridge, which generates coherent 3D
worlds from scattered images with little or no overlap.
Global scene context guides each view, while temporal
and spatial reference attention preserves observed details.
Latent-reference consistency improves reference fidelity at
inference.
Our proposed benchmark suite assesses low-
overlap view bridging, long-range generation, and the use
of off-trajectory references. Results on diverse benchmarks
show the effectiveness of our pipeline.
Limitations. Although our method generates high-quality
3D scenes, it may lack fine-detailed and structural geome-
try. When target views have little visual overlap with the
spatial references, these references provide limited guid-
ance, which may reduce generation quality. Further discus-
sion is provided in the supplementary material.

A. Implementation Details
A.1. Training Configuration
We implement WorldBridge based on GAE [52], which pro-
vides a good geometry-appearance space for scene gener-
ation. The scene encoder and scene reader are initialized
from LagerNVS [67] and tuned; the reference and text en-
coders are DepthAnything v3 [47] and Qwen3-0.6B [89]
and kept frozen; the RGB and geometry decoder are kept
frozen; the pipeline has 0.94B trainable parameters in to-
tal. We train our model on a collection of video clips from
RealEstate10K [103], DL3DV [48], and SpatialVID [72],
which contains about 22k video clips at a 1280 × 720 reso-
lution and is separate from the evaluation benchmarks. The
training data is labeled by ViPE [28] and DepthAnything
v3 [47] for metric camera pose and depth, and Qwen3.5-
27B [58] for caption. The diffusion transformer is trained
with a resolution of 672 × 378, while the global context
supervision is at a resolution of 512 × 288. The model is
trained on 32 GPUs for 10 epochs, using the Adam opti-
mizer with a batch size of 64, and a learning rate decaying
from 5 × 10−5 to 5 × 10−6.
A.2. Training Strategy
Target-view sampling.
At each training step, we draw the
target sequence length N uniformly from {21, . . . , 41}. We
use two sampling modes to expose the model to both wide
camera motion and dense local trajectories. With probabil-
ity 0.8, we partition the full clip into N equal-duration bins
and select one valid frame uniformly from each bin. With
probability 0.2, we sample N consecutive frames, using a
randomly selected valid start and stride when a constant-
stride sequence is available, and an irregular sequence of
valid frames within the same duration range otherwise. In
both modes, we order the target frames chronologically and
use their sequence indices 0, . . . , N −1 for temporal RoPE.
Reference-view sampling.
Temporal references are se-
lected from the sampled target sequence, while spatial ref-
erences are drawn uniformly without replacement from the
same clip’s full pool of valid frames after excluding the
sampled target frame indices. Visual overlap between spa-
tial references and targets can vary across samples.
We
sample up to 6 references during training, while it can in-
fer with more references. The temporal-only configurations
use the first frame (15%), both endpoints (15%), or three
to six target frames (10%). The spatial-only configuration
uses one to six spatial references (35%). The mixed config-
urations use the first frame and one to five spatial references
(10%), or both endpoints and one to four spatial references
(10%). The remaining 5% of the configuration distribution
uses no image references. Within each configuration, any
variable reference count is sampled uniformly over its spec-
ified range. A separate 5% joint dropout of reference, cam-
era, and text conditioning provides the unconditional branch
for classifier-free guidance.
Temporal position dropout.
With probability 0.5, we
disable temporal RoPE for temporal references and treat
them as spatial references. Target-to-target attention retains
temporal RoPE. This exposes the model to the same ref-
erence images both with and without temporal positions,
complementing training with spatial references, whose in-
teractions always omit temporal RoPE.
A.3. Inference Setting
For the benchmark results reported in the main paper, we
generate each target sequence in a single sampling run with
50 denoising steps, a classifier-free guidance scale of 2, a
seed of 42, and an empty text prompt. Let N be the num-
ber of requested target views. When N ≤41, we insert
41−N auxiliary cameras into the target trajectory and gen-
erate all 41 views jointly. We evaluate the outputs at the
original target cameras. When N > 41 (e.g., N = 56
for WorldBridge-Long and WorldBridge-Agent (On), and
N = 50 for WorldBridge-Agent (Off)), we extend the tem-
poral RoPE indices from 0, . . . , 40 to 0, . . . , N −1 and gen-
erate all N views. For longer trajectories, WorldBridge also
supports segment-wise generation. The final K generated
frames of each segment serve as temporal context for gen-
erating the next segment.
A.4. Latent-reference Consistency Configuration
For the implementation of Latent-reference Consistency, we
estimate each reference’s metric depth once with MoGe-
3 [38] using the supplied camera field of view, and a single
refinement step in each denoising step. Using the supplied
cameras, we forward-project the reference RGB into each
target view and retain valid projected pixels. The gradi-
ent direction is RMS-normalized, with a relative step size
of 0.01. For view i, let di ∈[0, 1] be its distance to the
nearest reference (if the reference is a spatial reference,
it should be the target view that is nearest to the spatial
reference), normalized by the maximum over views.

B. WorldBridge Benchmark Details
Our WorldBridge benchmark suite comprises 100 short
real-world video clips, 20 long real-world video clips, and
20 agentic-authored worlds.
Each authored world con-
tributes two paired evaluation cases, On and Off, for a total
of 40 cases. We will release our benchmark as well as our
model upon acceptance.
Real-video curation and annotation.
The curation pro-
tocol uses two stages. First, Qwen3.5-27B [58] separately
reviews a chronological sequence of sampled RGB frames
and the candidate reference images. The first review checks
shot continuity, distracting overlays, visual quality, and in-
dependently moving objects. The second checks whether
the references provide complementary observations of a co-
herent scene. RoMa v2 [15] measures matching support
for the single Short pair or all 15 Long pairs. The proto-
col advances candidates to reconstruction when both visual
checks and the matching criteria pass.
In the second stage, ViPE [28] estimates per-frame
camera pose and intrinsics along the retained clip. DA3
Nested [47] uses the estimated cameras and intrinsics to pre-
dict the depth maps. One positive scale per clip rescales the
ViPE [28] translations to the metric scale of the predicted
depth. We check frame correspondence, valid depth cov-
erage, abrupt pose changes, scale consistency across win-
dows, and agreement between overlapping depth estimates.
We review RGB, depth, and trajectory overlays and hold
clips with unresolved issues. Captions describe the scene
and camera motion.
B.1. WorldBridge-Short
The 100 clips come from RealEstate10K [103] (29 clips),
DL3DV [48] (37 clips), and SpatialVID [72] (34 clips).
All clips’ durations are 5–15 seconds, and the first and
last frames provide two references. The retained clips in-
clude 36 indoor, 20 outdoor, 23 indoor-to-outdoor, and 21
outdoor-to-indoor paths.
We back-project candidate target pixels with the esti-
mated target depth, transform them to each reference cam-
era, and check image bounds and agreement with the ref-
erence depth. We take the union of the two reference sup-
port masks. Eligible intermediate frames have at least 50%
estimated reference support. Viewpoint diversity and near-
duplicate checks reduce repeated views, and we retain at
most six targets per clip. This yields 549 target views across
100 clips, with two to six views per clip.
B.2. WorldBridge-Long
The 20 retained long clips comprise 10 indoor and 10 out-
door scenes. The evaluated spans range from 20 to 60.0
seconds. Six reference frames follow one continuous tra-
jectory and cover complementary regions. We place ten
evenly spaced interior timestamps in each of the five ref-
erence intervals and use the nearest retained source frames,
excluding the references. This gives 50 evaluation views per
clip. For labeling the long clips, we sample native frames at
approximately 15 fps while preserving the exact six refer-
ence frames and their timestamps. We estimate dense depth
in overlapping windows and apply a single translation scale
across the clip.
B.3. WorldBridge-Agent
World construction.
The 20 agent-authored worlds com-
prise 10 indoor and 10 outdoor environments. Indoor ex-
amples include offices, libraries, exhibition spaces, and res-
idences. Outdoor examples include streets, markets, gar-
dens, and coastal trails. We use agent-assisted geometric
blockouts to specify the layout, traversable regions, and
camera paths. We render structural guides at the reference
cameras and use them to generate images with a shared
scene description and appearance cues. We compare the
generated views against the guides and other references,
checking openings, major structures, object identity, and
plausible sightlines. Off-path camera placement also checks
clearance, reachability, and visibility in the blockout. In our
construction, the agent is GPT-6 Astra [56], and the image
generation model is GPT-Image [57].
Paired On and Off conditions.
Both conditions share the
same authored world and target trajectory. On-trajectory
provides six reference images at poses on the trajectory,
with known temporal positions. Off-trajectory provides six
separately generated references at poses outside the target
path, with spatial camera parameters and no assigned times-
tamps. We select 50 common target cameras by taking the
nearest authored poses to ten evenly spaced interior times in
each interval between successive On references. The nom-
inal trajectories span 50–58 seconds. The authored camera
parameters specify the intended views, and the structural
guides constrain reference generation.

RGB fidelity.
We compare generated and ground-truth

E. Discussion
E.1. Limitations and Failure Cases
WorldBridge can generate convincing RGB views coupled
with dense point maps, while its 3D geometry is not per-
fectly fine-grained. One possible reason is the bottleneck
of view-based 3D representation, rather than the 3D native
representation. For example, in the office and street exam-
ples in Fig. 13, the desktops and building fronts establish
the overall layout, but chair frames and the edges of walls
break up into sparse or uneven points. In the future, it is
possible to include the 3D native representations [14, 22–
26, 30, 31, 50], e.g., structured 3D latent [81] for better
structured or fine-grained geometry generation. Also, we
may incorporate agent procedure [5, 17, 42] to further en-
hance the quality of the results.
A second limitation arises when a target camera sees lit-
tle of what the spatial reference depicts. Fig. 16 keeps one
reference fixed per scene and varies the target trajectory.
The percentages measure the fraction of the target image
visible from the reference camera, averaged along the tra-
jectory. At 80% coverage, the fountain surround and loco-
motive front closely resemble the references. At 20%, the
turquoise mosaic remains recognizable, but the surround-
ings at its base change. The locomotive is still visible, yet
the proportions of its front vary across the sequence. As
more of the target view falls outside the reference, fewer
observed surfaces constrain how the subject fits into its sur-
roundings. In the future, we can consider more data aug-
mentation and synthesis techniques to provide training data
that includes spatial references with low visibility from the
target camera trajectories.
E.2. How the Global Branch Helps Generation
Fig. 17 compares the scene reader’s RGB prediction with
the local-only and full models (local+global) at the same
target camera. The reader queries the shared scene tokens
with the target camera rays. Although its RGB prediction
is coarse, it already places the white house to the left of
the road in the first row and the bed against the left wall in
the fourth. Broad boundaries in the visualized reader fea-
tures also follow the road and the walls. These observations
suggest that the reader supplies a coarse spatial layout to
the requested viewpoint, which provides layout priors for
view-specific generation.
The indoor examples show why this layout matters when
both references contain plausible but different objects. In
the fourth row, the local-only model reproduces the dining
set from reference A at a camera facing the bed in refer-
ence B. In the fifth row, it places a bed beyond a doorway
where the target shows a hallway. With global context, the
bed appears in the first of these views and the hallway stays
open in the second. These comparisons suggest that global
conditioning helps determine which observed content be-
longs at each camera, while the diffusion transformer can
use local reference detail to render features such as the pat-
terned bedding.
