Abstract
Large image-to-3D generators recover detailed heads from
a single photograph but treat every image as a new object.
For several images of one person, they hallucinate unseen
regions independently without using information from the
other images. We present Head Anything, which generates
detailed head meshes from a few images with changing ex-
pressions and viewpoints. Instead of training a multi-image
prior, we turn a pretrained single-image generator with a
native 3D latent space into a joint generator that denoises
all images together.
Since global attention between im-
ages is hard to learn from scarce 3D head data, lightweight
correspondence-attention adapters let every 3D token at-
tend only to its corresponding points in the other images.
We obtain correspondences by fitting a neural parametric
head model to the generator’s own single-image outputs,
requiring no ground truth at test time. Training on both
ground-truth and fitted correspondences makes the adapters
robust to fitting errors. Each mesh stays pixel-aligned with
its own image and borrows the unseen regions from all oth-
ers. On head scans, Head Anything lowers the head Cham-
fer distance of the best image-to-3D generator by 32% and
its cross-frame inconsistency by 47%, and its meshes im-
prove face tracking. Code and models will be released.
1. Introduction
Telepresence, games, film production and virtual try-on
need the 3D geometry of a person’s head, including the hair,
the ears and the back of the head, at a level of detail that to-
day requires a multi-view capture studio [17, 26]. Most im-

ages of people, however, come from casual videos or photo
collections, such as those in Fig. 1, and depict the head from
varying viewpoints and with different expressions. Recov-
ering complete, detailed and mutually consistent meshes
from such images would make studio-quality head geome-
try available from everyday data, with every mesh following
its own image and benefiting from what the others reveal.
This problem is underconstrained. Each image shows
only part of the head, and since expression and pose change,
multi-view reconstruction does not apply. A strong learned
prior is indispensable, but high-quality head scans exist for
only a few hundred people [11, 39], far fewer than the hun-
dreds of thousands of objects used to train general image-
to-3D generators [8]. A prior over multiple images is even
harder to learn, since the same point on the head appears at
a different position in each image as expressions and poses
change, and global attention across images cannot reliably
learn these correspondences from so few identities.
Existing methods work around this lack of 3D data with
different trade-offs.
Parametric head models [3, 11, 21]
and their trackers [12, 13, 28, 49] naturally combine many
frames, but their geometry is coarse and misses hair and fine
detail. Feed-forward methods predict per-pixel geometry
and correspondences [19], but only for the visible surface.
Large image-to-3D generators [20, 22, 36, 41–43] produce
complete, detailed meshes, but from one image at a time,
and their multi-image variants assume a static object. Video
and multi-view diffusion models share information along
optical flow [7, 10], epipolar lines [15, 37] or a morphable
head model [6, 35], but only between 2D images.
We introduce Head Anything for multi-image 3D head
generation by adapting a pretrained single-image generator
with a native 3D latent space to jointly generate 3D heads
from multiple images of the same person. As Fig. 2 shows,
it jointly denoises all 3D latents using lightweight adapters
letting each 3D token attend only to corresponding points
in the other images.
Unlike global attention, correspon-
dence attention knows where to look from the first training
step. The correspondences are deformable and bootstrapped
from the generator itself by fitting a neural parametric head
model [11, 12] to its single-image outputs.
This elimi-
nates ground-truth requirements at test time, while training
on both ground-truth and fitted correspondences makes the
adapters robust to fitting errors. Routing attention through
these correspondences within a 3D latent generator distin-
guishes our method from image-space correspondence at-
tention and from simply combining a generator with a para-
metric fit. Experiments show our meshes are more accurate
than all baselines, far more consistent across frames than
image-to-3D generators’ meshes, and improve a parametric
face tracker’s performance. Joint generation yields greater
accuracy and consistency than frame-by-frame generation
using our backbone, Pixal3D [20], fine-tuned on head scans.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We introduce a joint multi-image head generator that cou-
ples the sampling processes of a pretrained single-image
3D generator, so that every mesh follows its own image
and benefits from all others.
• We propose a novel correspondence attention mechanism
in which every 3D token attends to its corresponding
points in the other images, given by a neural parametric
head model fitted to the generator’s own outputs and made
robust by mixed ground-truth and fitted correspondences.
• We demonstrate that joint generation produces more ac-
curate 3D heads than all baselines, far more consistent
unseen regions than image-to-3D generators, and meshes
that improve parametric face tracking.
2. Related Work
Single-image 3D generation. 3D generators trained on
object collections [8] either lift novel-view and multi-view
diffusion models to 3D [24, 33] or generate directly in na-
tive 3D latent spaces.
TRELLIS [42] denoises a sparse
voxel structure and then structured latents on the occupied
voxels. TRELLIS.2 [43] makes these latents compact at
high resolution.
TripoSG [22] and Hunyuan3D-2.1 [36]
use rectified flows [23, 25] over vector-set latents, and
Direct3D-S2 [41] scales sparse attention to high-resolution
volumes. Pixal3D [20] back-projects image features into
the voxel grid, so that the generated geometry is aligned
with the input pixels and preserves fine details. All of these
methods generate one object from one image, and their
multi-image modes assume one rigid object.
3D head reconstruction. Morphable models [3] such as
FLAME [21] form the basis of most monocular face re-
construction and tracking methods [9, 13, 28, 49]. They
provide a consistent topology, but their geometry is coarse
and covers neither hair nor accessories. NPHM [11] models
identity and expression of complete heads with neural fields
learned from scans, and MonoNPHM [12] adds appearance
and fits it to monocular videos, with detail bounded by its
latent space. Feed-forward methods regress per-frame ge-
ometry and correspondences from images [19], but only for
the visible surface. Capture setups [17, 26] and the avatars
built on them [29] need calibrated camera rigs. We use a
parametric head model only as a source of dense correspon-
dences, while the geometry comes from the generator.
Correspondence-guided multi-image generation. Video
diffusion models attend between frames at the same spa-
tial location [4, 40], while video editing methods follow
correspondences. FLATTEN restricts attention to patches
on the same optical-flow path [7], and TokenFlow prop-
agates diffusion features through nearest-neighbour corre-
spondences [10]. Multi-view diffusion models attend along
epipolar lines [15, 37], and for heads, Morphable Diffu-

3. Method
Given n images {If}n
f=1 of a person’s head, we generate
one detailed mesh Mf per image, pixel-aligned with If and
completed from Ig̸=f in regions unseen in If. We fine-tune
a single-image generator on head scans (Secs. 3.1 and 3.2)
and couple n of its sampling processes (Sec. 3.3) through
correspondence attention (Sec. 3.4), routed by correspon-
dences bootstrapped from its own outputs (Sec. 3.5).
3.1. Preliminaries
We build on Pixal3D [20], which generates a mesh in
two stages. A sparse-structure flow (S1) denoises a dense
163×8 latent, which a dense VAE decoder maps to occu-
pied voxels on a 643 grid. A structured-latent flow (S2)
then denoises latents at these voxels at 5123 resolution, fol-
lowed by cascaded refinement to 10243 and mesh extrac-
tion with a sparse VAE decoder.
Both flows use diffu-
sion transformers [27] with N=30 blocks. They receive
DINOv3 [34] features through cross-attention and projec-
tion injection, which back-projects patch features onto grid
tokens to align the output with the input pixels. Each flow
is trained with the rectified-flow objective [23, 25]

3.2. A Single-Image Head Prior
Object-level generators miss the specific shape of a face and
often fail on hair, so we fine-tune Pixal3D on photorealis-
tic renders of the NPHM head scans [11]. The resulting
Pixal3D-FT is a strong single-image head prior, but it gen-
erates every frame on its own, so the frames do not benefit
from each other. It also hallucinates the back of the head
and the hair independently for every frame.

3.4. Correspondence Attention
Different frames observe different regions of the head, so
the tokens of a region that one image does not show can
take their shape from the frames that do. Since the head
turns and the face deforms between frames, the same voxel
holds different points of the head in different frames, and a
token can only profit from another frame if it knows where
its corresponding point lies. Global attention to all tokens of
other frames would have to learn from a few hundred iden-
tities where to attend among tens of thousands of tokens,
which it cannot do reliably (Sec. 4.4). We thus restrict at-
tention to corresponding positions, as Fig. 3 illustrates, so
the adapters only learn what to take from each frame.

3.5. Bootstrapped Correspondences
At test time, no scan is available. As shown in Fig. 2 (top
right), we bootstrap correspondences by fitting the head
model to Pixal3D-FT’s per-frame outputs, whose detailed,
pixel-aligned meshes provide better targets than pixels.
FLAME fitting. Fitting a parametric model to a generated
mesh requires a good initial scale and pose. Since the mesh
lacks template topology, we obtain dense FLAME [21]
correspondences from the image.
Pixel3DMM [13] fits
FLAME to each image. We then project the rasterised tem-
plate coordinates at face pixels onto the single-frame mesh,
pairing mesh points with template coordinates. We jointly
fit a similarity (sf, Rf, tf) and the FLAME shape, expres-
sion, and jaw parameters to these pairs using a robust point
loss. The similarity places every mesh in a canonical head
frame with a consistent scale and serves as Af. For real
images and our FaceVerse renders, Af combines per-frame
head pose and scale, estimated from Face Anything [19],
with a single FLAME fit to the first frame. This ensures
consistent placement across all frames.

4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation Details
For Pixal3D-FT, we fine-tune Pixal3D on 10,180 scans of
the 477 NPHM identities outside our test set, each rendered
from two viewpoints. S1, S2 and the cascade are trained
for 8k, 8k and 12k steps, respectively, with learning rates
of 10−5, 10−5 and 5·10−6 and batch sizes of 16, 16 and 4.
For joint generation, the adapters follow every third block
of both flows, with width 512, 8 heads and K=4 keys per
frame in S2, which amounts to 55 M parameters per stage.
We train them with the backbone on groups of 2 to 8 scans
from the 459 of these 477 identities with at least four scans.
Each stage is trained for 4k steps with two groups per step in
S1 and one in S2 and learning rates of 10−4 for the adapters
and 10−5 for the backbone. Both trainings use AdamW, 200
warm-up steps and a condition dropout of 0.1 for classifier-
free guidance. On NVIDIA A100 GPUs, the fine-tuning
uses two GPUs per stage, and the adapter training takes
about 7 h for S1 and 6 h for S2 on one GPU. For eight im-
ages, our method takes about 2.3 times as long as generating
them one by one with Pixal3D-FT.

5. Limitations and Future Work
Despite its strong performance, our method is specialised
for human heads because it derives correspondences from a
parametric head model. It therefore does not directly gen-
eralise to full human bodies or generic objects. Extending
correspondence-routed joint generation to these settings, for
example through body models or category-level correspon-
dences, is a promising direction. The underlying paramet-
ric fit becomes less reliable under heavy occlusion or when
only a small portion of the face is visible.
Our method
also assumes that head geometry remains consistent across
images, apart from expression changes. Photo collections
taken at different times, with changes in hairstyle or acces-
sories, therefore fall outside its scope. Finally, its reliance
on a computationally expensive diffusion-based image-to-
mesh generator makes real-time applications infeasible.
6. Conclusion
Head Anything turns a pretrained single-image 3D genera-
tor into a consistent joint generator for any number of im-
ages of a person. Each 3D token attends to corresponding
points in other frames. We obtain these correspondences
by fitting a parametric head model to the generator’s out-
puts, requiring no ground truth at test time. Training on both
ground-truth and fitted correspondences makes the adapters
robust to fitting errors. Each mesh follows its own image,
while regions unseen in that image are completed using in-
formation from all images. On scan benchmarks and real
captures, Head Anything improves accuracy over generator
and parametric baselines. It also improves consistency over
image-to-3D generators, while its meshes improve paramet-
ric face tracking. More broadly, correspondence-routed at-
tention lets a single-image 3D generator exploit several im-
ages without training a multi-image prior from scratch.

C. Additional Implementation Details
C.1. Base Model and Sampling
We build on the public code and the released weights of
Pixal3D [20], which follows the two-stage design of TREL-
LIS.2 [43] with DINOv3 [34] conditioning, and sample all
stages with its released sampler settings. The noise of ev-
ery stage is seeded per frame, so a frame receives the same
noise in the single-image pass and in every joint group. The
cascade from resolution 5123 to 10243 and the decoding run
per frame on the jointly generated S2 latent.
C.2. Single-Image Fine-Tuning
Pixal3D-FT is Pixal3D fine-tuned in all three geometry
stages with Eq. (1) and no architectural change. It is fine-
tuned on renders of the scans, and our adapters are trained
on the same renders. We rasterise each scan with its cap-
tured vertex colours from random viewpoints with varying
fields of view. We match foreground colour statistics to real
video frames and apply slight Gaussian blur, sensor noise
and a JPEG round trip. Finally, we crop around the head
as in the real-image pipeline. Among several variants of
this renderer, this one was closest to real photographs in
DINOv3 feature statistics.
C.3. Adapters and Joint Training
Architecture. The adapters follow every third block of
both flows, starting with the third block. Each adapter uses
LayerNorm, width-512 query, key and value projections
with 8 heads, the key embeddings of Eq. (5), and a learned
null key-value pair. A zero-initialised projection maps back
to the backbone width, with its output scaled by a gate on
the timestep embedding. The key embeddings combine a
depth MLP, used in both stages, with a validity embedding
in S1 and an offset MLP in S2, and every stage has 55 M
adapter parameters. The depth features di and dj are the
coordinates of the query and the key token along the view-
ing axis of the view-aligned latent grid of their own frame.
Training. Registration groups and fitted groups are drawn
with equal probability. All frames of a group share the flow
time, which S1 draws from a logit-normal distribution. The
backbone is fine-tuned together with the adapters, with a
learning rate ten times lower than that of the adapters.
C.4. Correspondences
Registration correspondences. For registration groups,
φf→g in Eq. (4) uses the 8 nearest registration vertices with
inverse-distance weights. Af is the known similarity trans-
form from the scan to the render’s view-aligned latent grid.
In S1, a correspondence is valid if Φf→g(p) lies inside the
latent grid; in S2, a key token is valid if it lies within two
tokens of Φf→g(p). Invalid keys are masked, and the null
key is always available.

Relation of the two kinds of correspondences. Both in-
stances of Eq. (3) map a point of the head in one frame
to the same point in another frame, but they are built dif-
ferently. Registration maps interpolate the displacements
of corresponding vertices, so they are exact on the scan
surface and extrapolate off the surface by inverse-distance
weighting. Fitted maps evaluate MonoNPHM’s learned de-
formation field, which is smooth and defined everywhere,
but inherit errors from the FLAME and MonoNPHM fits.
Their placement Af comes from the FLAME fit rather than
the scan. Training on registrations alone would leave a gap
between training and test-time correspondences, which the
fitted pool closes. The GT-only variant in Tab. 4 and the
fitted-only variant in Tab. S4 evaluate the two extremes.
C.5. Clean-Up
The pipeline’s standard mesh clean-up closes small holes,
cuts the decoded surface along non-manifold edges and re-
moves connected components below an area threshold. Re-
moving pieces disconnected from the head by these cuts
opens holes, most visibly around the mouth. Our clean-up
applies the same repairs but removes floating components
only after re-welding the cut, then fills small loops. Above
the head region’s lower boundary, it closes each remain-
ing boundary loop of perimeter 10–250 mm with a flat ring
patch over the hole border. For real images, the minimum
perimeter is 3 mm. This removes most holes and leaves the
geometry metrics essentially unchanged (Tab. S1). We ap-
ply this clean-up to both Head Anything and Pixal3D-FT
meshes. It is not a contribution of this work but makes the
meshes usable as hole-free head surfaces.
C.6. Real-Image Pipeline
For a sequence of real images, as for the FaceVerse ren-
ders, all frames are cropped with one square crop around
the matte of the first frame, enlarged by 10%. This crop
defines one virtual camera in which every frame is gener-
ated. Its intrinsics come from capture calibration, the Face-
Verse render camera or, for in-the-wild images, the focal
length predicted by Face Anything [19]. Face Anything pre-
dicts metric depth and canonical coordinates for each frame,
from which we obtain its rigid head motion relative to this
camera. Scaled ray-ICP against the predicted depth then
estimates the metric scale of each single-frame mesh. On
NPHM, every frame is generated in its own render camera,
and Af comes from per-frame FLAME fits.
D. Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocol
D.1. Datasets
NPHM. We use the 10 held-out NPHM [11] identities 088,
127, 195, 243, 291, 339, 387, 436, 482 and 535, which are
not used in training, each with eight expressions. They are
rendered with the photorealistic renderer of Sec. C.2 from
random viewpoints with a yaw within ±30◦, a pitch be-
tween −8◦and 20◦and a field of view between 10◦and
70◦. Each group of eight frames is generated jointly, and all
80 frames are scored.
FaceVerse. We randomly select 10 FaceVerse [39] iden-
tities with artifact-free scans, each with eight expressions.
Every scan is rendered from a random viewpoint with a
yaw within ±30◦and a pitch between −8◦and 20◦, with
one camera distance per identity and a field of view at
which the head fits into the image in all eight frames. Our
pipeline generates all eight images in one virtual camera
(Sec. C.6), so the change of viewpoint appears as head mo-
tion. Each ground-truth scan is cut 20 mm below the chin.
Since the scans are not metric, a single dataset-wide scale
factor, shared by all methods, sets the median outer eye-
corner width to 90 mm.
NeRSemble and AVA-256. We use the 10 NeRSem-
ble [17] identities 139, 170, 171, 175, 176, 177, 178,
192, 497 and 510, none of which is an NPHM training or
test identity, and the 10 AVA-256 [26] identities AAN112,
ADL311, AEY864, AJR151, ANX726, CDR970, DOT682,
FXN596, KDA058 and NRE683. For each identity, two
synchronised cameras about 20◦to 33◦to the left and right
of the face observe four timestamps: a near-neutral face,
a smile, an open mouth and one further expression, where
a cheek expression replaces the smile on AVA-256, which
has no smile sequence. The two cameras are chosen by a
fixed rule among those that see the whole head at all four
timestamps, and NeRSemble timestamps in which the head
leaves one of the two views are not used. Since the meth-
ods assume one intrinsic matrix per sequence, the AVA-256
images of the two cameras are resampled into one virtual
pinhole camera per identity with the real camera rotations
and centres. For evaluation, we hold out all other cameras
of an identity, seven on NeRSemble and seven or eight on
AVA-256, and reconstruct the multi-view stereo points of

each timestamp with COLMAP [31, 32] from the calibrated
cameras.
Inputs. All methods receive the same RGBA crops, with
BiRefNet [47] mattes in the wild and the dataset mattes on
the captures. We binarise mattes for every method because
soft edges cause some generators to produce flat cards.
Parametric trackers receive the same crops composited on
white, as their pipelines require. The image-to-3D genera-
tors are run with their official code and default settings on
every image.
D.2. Alignment and Metrics
Scan benchmarks. Every prediction is aligned to its scan
using the same similarity search [2, 38]. We run scaled
trimmed ICP from 72 starting orientations, using a two-
sided residual to prevent scale collapse. We then run face
ICP from each resulting pose, followed by 12 perturbed
restarts from the best poses. We keep the pose with the low-
est face residual and finally align the face rigidly, so that
the metrics compare shapes rather than placements. The
face and lips regions are defined by a FLAME fit of each
scan, and the head region by a plane 2 cm below the chin of
that fit. To measure cross-frame consistency, we move each
aligned prediction’s non-face points into every other frame
of the same person using the inverse-distance-weighted dis-
placement of the 8 nearest correspondence vertices. Corre-
spondences come from NPHM registrations or FLAME fits
to FaceVerse scans. We measure the mean distance to each
target frame’s aligned non-face surface and average over all
target frames.
Captures. For silhouette IoU, we fit a per-frame similar-
ity transform to masks from the alignment cameras, includ-
ing the input cameras, by maximising a differentiable soft-
silhouette IoU from 144 starts. We then measure IoU in
held-out cameras within a head region shared by all meth-
ods. For the Chamfer distance, every mesh is aligned to
the stereo points of its timestamp with the similarity search
above, and only the prediction vertices within 10 mm of
a camera-visible stereo point are used.
The cross-view
consistency is the head Chamfer distance between the two
meshes generated from the two synchronised images of one
timestamp, each placed by its own alignment to the stereo
points.
