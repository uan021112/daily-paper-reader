## Abstract 

Large-scale robotic simulation requires diverse deformable objects with executable physical proper-ties and realistic interaction behaviors. However, existing 3D asset collections primarily emphasize visual diversity, while scalable physical construction and verification remain challenging. We introduce RFW-S, a large-scale dataset of 83,365 simulation-verified assets spanning 485 everyday categories across five structural families: soft solids, cloth sheets, shells, garments, and bags. We develop an automated pipeline integrating reference-guided 3D generation, structure-aware physical modeling, and LLM-assisted parameter estimation and calibration. To ensure physical reliability, we introduce an independent simulation verification framework that evaluates object behaviors under interactions distinct from those used for parameter calibration. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of independent verification and characterize deformable responses under different physical configurations. We further evaluate robotic grasping in terms of both task success and object integrity, and demonstrate the physical realism of our simulated objects through sim-to-real replay of fragile-object manipulation. RFW-S provides a scalable foundation for physically grounded simulation and embodied robotic learning.

Robots operating in everyday environments must interact with objects whose shape changes under contact: a sponge compresses during grasping, a towel drapes over a surface, and a bag folds as it is lifted. These responses affect how contact is established, whether a grasp succeeds, and whether an object remains intact. Simulation offers a way to study such interactions across object types and physical conditions, but doing so requires a diverse collection of deformable assets with executable, tested physical behavior. Existing resources provide important parts of this foundation. Large 3D collections offer broad visual diversity [1], while clothing datasets and deformable simulation environments provide specialized geometry and interaction settings [2, 3]. Recent systems further connect asset construction to physics: ManiTwin verifies rigid-object grasps, PhysX-Omni generates assets across physical types, and Agentic Real2Sim reconstructs recorded interaction episodes [4–6]. For everyday deformables, the remaining challenge is to combine broad semantic coverage with physical configurations that can be reused and assessed beyond the interactions used to construct them. This challenge extends beyond assigning material parameters to a generated mesh. Deformation depends jointly on geometry, structural constraints, material controls, and contact. A filled soft object needs interior material, an open bag needs a preserved cavity and opening, and a garment needs a surface representation that permits folding. An unsuitable representation can prevent the intended response even when its material parameters are adjusted. Moreover, a configuration that runs without numerical failure may still deform incorrectly, and one that satisfies a calibration interaction may fail under a different stimulus. Scaling asset generation, therefore, requires both structure-aware physical construction and behavioral evaluation that is separate from candidate selection. We introduce RoboFlyWheel-Soft (RFW-S) , a resource of 83,365 simulation-verified assets spanning 485 everyday categories across five structural families: soft solids, cloth sheets, shells, garments, and bags (Figure 1). We construct a category-balanced pool of 100,000 assets through reference-guided generation, adapt their geometry to compatible simulation representations, and use a multimodal language model to estimate effective physical parameters. Simulation feedback guides bounded corrections to supported controls. Each asset links its executed configuration to recorded behavioral evidence. The central design is to separate calibration from final verification. Once calibration selects a candidate, we freeze its geometry, representation, parameters, and numerical settings, then evaluate it with a distinct interaction. A separately scheduled runtime and behavioral contract check evaluates execution, finite state, and response criteria under a drop probe. In total, 83,365 assets pass both physical verification and the runtime and behavioral contract check, spanning all 485 everyday object categories. We evaluate RFW-S through controlled physical tests, robotic grasping, and sim-to-real experiments. Our results show that material tuning alone cannot replace structural adaptation, and that calibration success does not guarantee valid behavior under a different interaction, motivating independent verification. Grasping experiments expose the gap between task completion and object integrity. Finally, replaying simulated gripper closure commands on a real robot reproduces both breakage and intact transfer, supporting the physical realism of our fragile-object simulation. Our contributions are threefold: • A large-scale dataset of everyday deformable objects. RFW-S provides 83,365 simulation-verified assets spanning 485 categories and five structural families, with executable physical configurations and associated behavioral evidence. • A scalable pipeline for physically verified asset construction. We combine structure-aware mod-eling, language-model-assisted parameter estimation, and simulation-guided calibration with independent interaction tests that screen for failures beyond calibration. • Physical and downstream validation. Controlled studies demonstrate the importance of structural adaptation and independent verification. Robotic grasping evaluates task success alongside object integrity, while sim-to-real replay reproduces both breakage and intact transfer, supporting the realism of fragile-object simulation. 22 Related Work

## 4 From Generation to Verified Simulation 

Construction separates representation choice, effective parameterization, candidate selection, and verification. Calibration observations can influence the candidate that is selected; the independent probe evaluates that selection after its geometry, parameters, and numerical settings are frozen. The separately scheduled RC outcome is joined by configuration identity when forming the usable subset. 

4.1 Coverage-aware generation and structural adaptation 

A canonical category and appearance specification condition Qwen-Image-2.0 reference generation [31]. References guide TRELLIS.2 geometry generation [32] and family-specific construction. Thin sheets can be procedural; structured objects retain their generated three-dimensional form. Geometry retries remain assigned to the same category and selected reference, maintaining the intended allocation before filtering (Figure 5). Structure determines the representation: filled objects require interior material, bags need meaningful openings, and garments need surfaces that can fold. We use filled-volume, sheet, cloth-garment, shell-garment, bag-shell, reinforced-shell, and sealed-membrane profiles. Profiles determine compatible solvers and constraints; a filled beanbag can use MPM despite its semantic “bag” label. Volume construction uses interior filling and surface reconstruction. Surface adaptation removes small components and duplicated vertices while preserving openings. Connectivity, finite vertices, boundary edges, and repairs accompany the asset. Semantic family guides this choice but does not uniquely determine a solver. An open fabric bag and a filled beanbag can require different representations despite sharing an everyday category family. The record therefore retains the proposed route and the mode actually executed. Representation diagnosis precedes interpretation of a parameter change: a control can only affect behavior when the selected solver and adapter implement it. Garment adaptation addresses a recurrent failure: strong rest-curvature support can preserve a generated upright pose even with softer material. The flexible cloth profile reduces this support while retaining stretch resistance and self-collision. 4.2 Effective parameters and calibration feedback 

Given geometry x and representation r, Qwen (API identifier qwen3.8-max ) estimates θ0 from reference images and semantic cues. The adapter maps it to executable controls, θeff = Ar(θ0). A calibration probe 

Sr(x, θ eff ; ucal ) measures contact, finite state, deformation, recovery, and residual motion where applicable (Figure 6). Diagnostics distinguish parameter mismatch, absent contact, numerical failure, and unsupported response. The same model proposes bounded corrections from structured text summaries of calibration. Iteration stops at acceptance or the candidate budget; Appendix G.1 gives the model settings. Effective controls matter: a proposed viscosity field that is not passed to the MPM material leaves the response unchanged. Local diagnostics instead identify active controls, including yield stress for supported elastoplastic representations. Observation duration also matters: a short window can confound slow settling with persistent motion. The records therefore retain the executed parameter map, time step, substeps, and observation duration (Appendix C.2).

5.4 Task completion and a deformation-gradient-based integrity criterion 

A complementary grasp–lift–hold evaluation contains 1,000 qualified instances drawn from seven base geome-tries. Fixed command, slip feedback, a local force prior, and a privileged reference each evaluate the same 660 calibration and 340 held-out instances at seed zero. Base geometries are shared across the split, which reserves instances and conditions. The reference selects one outcome from five closure trials using privileged damage readouts. The protocol comprises 3,000 single-controller trials and 5,000 reference trials, yielding 4,000 reported controller–instance outcomes. The proxy score D ∈ [0 , 1] maps contact-time deformation-gradient statistics to configured onset/failure bands. Integrity is 1 − D, the damage flag is D ≥ 0.25 , and intact success requires task completion with D < 0.25 .The score is computed after simulation from ∥F − I∥F , which responds to rotation as well as deformation. The minimum observed score is 0.2653, so the chosen 0.25 threshold flags every outcome. Continuous score distributions describe variation in the proxy response. Controllers act through commanded closure; their nominal force settings are mapped to closure depth (Appendix F). 

Sim-to-real replay of fragile-object manipulation. We replay the simulated gripper closure commands on a physical Piper arm to test whether simulated fragile-object outcomes carry over to the real robot (Figure 10). The simulation predicts a clear separation between two grasps of the same tofu block: a crushing grasp breaks the tofu during lifting, while a non-damaging grasp delivers it to the receiving plate intact. The real robot reproduces both outcomes. Under the crushing grasp the real tofu fractures into fragments that fall onto the plate, and under the non-damaging grasp it is placed without visible breakage. A grasp that crushes the object in simulation thus also crushes the real object. The consistent sim-to-real replay indicates that the material response of our simulated assets is physically accurate: the effective parameters and representation recorded for the asset reproduce the boundary between damaging and non-damaging closure observed on the physical robot. This makes the simulated failure modes informative for real manipulation, complementing the task and integrity measures above. Appendix F.1 describes the replay setup and simulation settings. 13 6 Discussion and Limitations 

6.1 Coverage and configuration-specific usability 

RFW-S connects semantic coverage to an executable configuration and behavioral record for each evaluated asset. Retaining all 485 categories preserves semantic breadth, while unequal retained counts identify categories with sparse usable coverage. Targeted representation inspections diagnose selected failures. Audits of semantic correctness, geometric diversity, and near-duplicate rates would characterize additional aspects of dataset quality. Verification results apply to the recorded solver, representation, parameter configuration, and probes. Effective simulation parameters control those executions. Paired physical-object measurements would provide an external reference for response fidelity and inferred scale and material values. 

6.2 Feedback and behavioral criteria 

Independent testing exposes mismatches after calibration acceptance; production traces document 326 accepted corrections. A controlled study of correction efficacy would pair update policies on the same initial failures, retain trace prefixes across budgets, and compare independently tested outcomes and evaluation cost. The present expanded study evaluates shared initial candidates across separately executed runs. Expert judgments inform representation-dependent behavioral bands. The study uses one rating per clip and summarizes rating distributions and diagnostic examples. Multiple ratings and a separate validation set would quantify the reliability of judgments and the agreement between criteria and perceived plausibility. Historical settling labels are identified separately in Appendix H. 

6.3 Task completion and the integrity readout 

The grasping application reports task completion together with a specified integrity criterion. Every reported outcome exceeds the proxy threshold, limiting comparisons based on binary intact success. The deformation-gradient readout also responds to rigid rotation. A rotation-invariant strain measure and calibration against physical damage observations would strengthen its physical interpretation. Such a change would require recomputing the scores and outcomes. The sim-to-real replay complements this proxy with a physical observation: the gripper closure that crushes tofu in simulation crushes the real tofu, and the non-damaging closure transfers it intact in both domains. This agreement supports the physical realism of the simulated assets for fragile manipulation. Paired force and deformation measurements on the physical robot would further quantify response fidelity across materials. The instance split shares seven base geometries, and the reference selects among five trials using privileged readouts. Broader geometry coverage, additional seeds, and physical response measurements would extend the application study. Reusable configurations and verification records also support future interaction and policy-learning experiments under additional stimuli and solver settings. 

## 7 Conclusion 

RFW-S connects 100,000 generated assets in 485 everyday deformable categories to structure-compatible representations, effective parameters, and recorded behavioral evidence. Physical verification and the separately scheduled RC check jointly retain 83,365 assets and all categories. Independent testing exposes calibration-accepted response mismatches, while configuration comparisons and a grasping application distinguish deformation, task completion, and a specified integrity criterion. The resulting resource supports reproducible deformable-object studies with explicit configuration and evaluation scope. F.1 Sim-to-real replay protocol 

Figure 10 pairs two MPM simulations with two physical-robot recordings of the same tofu grasp–transfer task. The gripper closure commands of each simulated grasp are replayed on a physical Piper arm. The simulation uses a dual-arm Piper scene with the right arm active and the left arm stationary; the figure shows the manipulation area. An oblique orthographic camera approximates the recording viewpoint, and representative frames are selected by action stage. Robot frames are cropped and proportionally resized, without mirroring or synthesized intermediate frames. Both simulated cases use 6,460 MPM particles and identical material settings: Young’s modulus 28 kPa, Poisson ratio 0.32, density 950 kg/m 3, yield stress 5 kPa, and a damage extension that reduces shear and tensile strength over an accumulated-plastic-strain interval of 0.15–0.45. The two cases differ only in the commanded gripper motion. In the crushing case the gripper holds the block at the pickup plate, closes further, lifts, and translates; local damage develops before lifting, the block loses support and falls, and the impact adds deformation. In the non-damaging case the gripper lifts and transfers the block with moderate closure. The contrast between breakage and intact transfer therefore follows from the commanded closure rather than from different material settings, which is the quantity replayed on the robot. Finger-mesh signed distance fields support particle contact projection and friction, and the receiving surface is approximated by a plane. Reconstructed particle surfaces are excluded from the finger interiors for rendering. The MPM damage model is separate from the post-hoc proxy in Equation 5; the replay outcomes are reported in Section 5.4.

## I Future Work 

A natural extension of RFW-S is toward hybrid articulated–deformable objects, where rigid kinematic structures are coupled with soft components such as fabric covers, straps, cushions, seals, membranes, or compliant connectors. Extending the current structure-aware construction and independent verification procedure to such mixed systems could bridge deformable-object simulation with articulated reconstruction, part-level kinematics, and physically grounded interaction [5, 33–39]. At the scene level, RFW-S assets could serve as physically grounded building blocks for generative simulation. Future systems could automatically compose editable and executable worlds with scene layouts, lighting, articulated and deformable objects, and task-relevant interactions, supporting scalable data generation as well as controlled construction of rare or challenging scenarios [40–48]. Beyond explicit simulation, the physical configurations and executed interaction traces in RFW-S could support 4D and physics-aware video generation. Coupling simulator states with generative video renderers could provide controllable dynamics under object motion, camera trajectories, illumination, and external interactions, while video-to-4D reconstruction could close the loop by recovering structured, simulation-ready worlds from real or generated observations [48–52]. 33 Finally, RFW-S could support broader embodied benchmarks and policy learning beyond the grasp–lift–hold protocol studied here, including long-horizon deformable manipulation, bimanual and high-DoF dexterity, VLA training, safety-aware interaction, and sim-to-real evaluation. Its physical verification and integrity records could enable evaluation criteria that jointly consider task completion, object preservation, and physical or semantic safety [3, 14, 53–61]. 34
