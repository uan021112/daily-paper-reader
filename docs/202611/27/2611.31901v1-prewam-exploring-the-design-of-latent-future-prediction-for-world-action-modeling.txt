## Abstract 

World-action models combine action generation with future prediction, allowing visual transitions to serve as supervi-sion for policy learning. Our controlled study of prediction spaces shows that spatially structured semantic features sup-port better overall generalization under visual perturbations and make motion information more readily recoverable from learned predictive representations. Building on these obser-vations, we introduce PreWAM, a world-action model that or-ganizes future supervision around state changes in a predic-tion space defined by pretrained DINO features. Specifically, Residual World Prediction jointly learns source-relative changes and their resulting future states to emphasize dy-namic information while preserving future-state semantics. Temporal-Scale Prediction conditions world queries on tem-poral distance to capture both short-term local motion and longer-horizon state evolution. These predictive objectives jointly shape a shared vision-language backbone with ac-tion supervision during pretraining and downstream policy learning. Experiments across the LIBERO benchmark family, SimplerEnv, RoboTwin 2.0, and real-world robotic manip-ulation demonstrate strong manipulation performance and improved generalization and robustness under diverse distri-bution shifts. 

## 1. Introduction 

Robot demonstrations specify what action to take, while the accompanying observations reveal how the scene changes as that action unfolds. World-action modeling uses both signals, combining action learning with prediction of fu-ture observations. Latent prediction makes this combination particularly attractive: a visual encoder supplies dense su-

pervision from videos, and a policy can learn to anticipate transitions without reconstructing every pixel [20, 29]. Making future supervision useful for control involves two design dimensions: the representation of future observations and the formulation of their prediction. Future frames mix persistent content, manipulation-induced changes, and ap-pearance variations, whose relative emphasis depends on the prediction space. Image self-supervision, video reconstruc-tion, and predictive video learning impart different biases toward spatial correspondence, appearance detail, and tem-poral structure. We investigate how these representational biases affect the information a policy learns from future supervision and its ability to act beyond the training distribu-tion. Our controlled study reveals that prediction spaces with similar standard-task performance differ in their robustness to visual changes. Spatial semantic supervision provides the strongest overall generalization, while reconstruction super-vision retains advantages in some categories. Displacement readout provides complementary evidence: motion informa-tion remains more accessible under perturbations in policies trained with spatial semantic targets. The second dimension concerns how prediction targets are organized over time. Supervising only the future state leaves the change from the source implicit, while a fixed prediction interval restricts supervision to a single temporal scale. We introduce Residual World Prediction (RWP) and 

Temporal-Scale Prediction (TSP) to make both relationships explicit. RWP predicts changes relative to a source state and jointly supervises these changes and the future states they reconstruct. TSP conditions world queries on temporal distance, providing supervision over several horizons within the same clip. We instantiate this design in PreWAM, where world pre-diction and action learning jointly shape a shared vision-language backbone. Pretraining combines robot demonstra-tions, which provide both objectives, with human videos 1arXiv:2611.31901v1 [cs.RO] 24 Nov 2026 that supply additional predictive supervision. Downstream posttraining continues joint prediction and action learning on the target manipulation data. At deployment, only the policy backbone and action expert are required. We evaluate PreWAM on standard manipulation tasks and a range of distribution shifts. It achieves 98.1% success on LIBERO, 79.1% on LIBERO-Plus, and 58.7% on LIBERO-Pro. LIBERO-Pro2.0 further tests robustness to static and runtime disturbances. SimplerEnv and RoboTwin 2.0 further extend the evaluation to real-to-sim transfer and multitask bimanual manipulation. Three tasks on a physical Piper plat-form further evaluate single-arm and bimanual manipulation. Our contributions are: • Controlled comparisons reveal that prediction spaces sup-porting similar in-distribution performance can yield sub-stantially different policy generalization, with spatial se-mantic supervision providing stronger overall robustness to visual perturbations. • We introduce PreWAM, a world-action model that orga-nizes future supervision around state changes across tem-poral horizons, allowing predictive learning to shape the shared representations used for action generation. • Experiments demonstrate strong generalization across the LIBERO benchmark family and effective manipulation in SimplerEnv and RoboTwin 2.0, complemented by real-world evaluation of single-arm and bimanual tasks on a Piper platform. 

## 2. Related Work 

Vision-Language-Action Models. Vision-language-action models transfer the perceptual and linguistic knowledge of pretrained multimodal backbones to robot control. Action-token prediction provides a direct extension of vision-language modeling to robot demonstrations [15], while dedi-cated continuous-action experts generate action sequences from multimodal representations [23, 25]. These methods learn to map observations and language instructions to ac-tions from robot demonstrations, making the quality of mul-timodal representations an important foundation for policy learning. 

Representations for Prediction and Control. Visual pretraining objectives emphasize different aspects of an ob-servation. Reconstruction preserves appearance information, image self-supervision learns dense semantic features, and predictive video learning captures temporal regularities in representation space [1, 27, 30]. When these representations become prediction targets, their properties also influence the information emphasized during policy training. Feature-space world models use pretrained visual representations for future-state prediction and action-conditioned planning without reconstructing pixels [3]. 

World Modeling for Policy Learning. World-action models couple visual prediction with robot control, but differ in how predictive information reaches the policy. Video-based architectures can connect world and action experts through shared latent interfaces, allowing joint supervision to shape the visual features used for action generation [10]. Prediction can also operate directly in a pretrained feature space without reconstructing future images. Such methods predict future features or jointly model current and future representations to provide auxiliary supervision for action learning [20, 29], and can also learn from videos without action annotations [29]. PreWAM explicitly models changes relative to source states and organizes predictive supervision across multiple temporal horizons. 

## 3. World Representations for Policy Generaliza-tion 

World representations define the visual content that future prediction brings into policy learning. How does the content emphasized by these representations affect a policy when observations depart from familiar scenes? We approach this question through policy evaluation under perturbations and motion readout from the learned predictive representations. We compare spatial semantic features from DINOv3 [27], reconstruction latents from the Wan 2.1 video VAE [30], and predictive video features from V-JEPA 2 [1]. The com-parison uses direct future-state prediction within a common Qwen-based policy framework and training data, with each frozen encoder supplying its own visual prediction targets. Downstream training uses the same optimization settings across prediction spaces, with a global batch size of 256 and 36,000 updates. 

3.1. Generalization across Prediction Spaces 

Similar task success in familiar scenes masks differences in the effects of predictive supervision. The three policies fall within a 0.20-point range on LIBERO, yet separate more clearly under the seven perturbation categories of LIBERO-Plus (Figure 1). Spatial semantic supervision provides the strongest aggregate performance, while the relative strengths of the other spaces depend on the perturbation. The category results help characterize these distinctions. Spatial semantic supervision has its clearest advantages over reconstruction supervision under camera, background, and lighting changes. Such changes can substantially alter an image while preserving the intended interaction, making correspondence across appearances useful for control. This pattern is consistent with the benefit of targets that preserve spatial scene content without emphasizing every appearance detail. Reconstruction supervision remains competitive: it leads on the Robot and Language categories and closely matches spatial semantic supervision under noise. Its Robot performance suggests that retaining fine visual configura-tions can also be useful when the robot’s appearance changes. 2Figure 1. World representations and policy generalization. 3.2. Motion Information in Policy Representations 

Displacement readout from world queries. We first exam-ine whether the world queries supplied to the world pre-dictor contain motion information. We mean-pool their final-layer VLM features and fit a linear ridge probe to three-dimensional end-effector displacement. The probe is fitted on clean observations and held fixed across pertur-bations. We evaluate on held-out episodes using five-fold cross-validation grouped by episode. Across the three predic-tion spaces, mean readout errors range from 16.80 to 18.45 mm, well below the 55.10 mm baseline that always predicts the training-fold mean displacement, indicating that world queries encode recoverable motion information. Readout errors are close on clean observations but di-verge under camera shifts and noise. Spatial semantic and reconstruction supervision both outperform predictive video supervision in these conditions, with spatial semantic su-pervision yielding the lowest error, also under background changes. These results show that world queries learned with spatial semantic supervision retain more readily accessible motion information under these visual perturbations. 

Action-query attention. Figure 1 visualizes attention from the action queries that condition the action expert in a task that places cheese on a plate. With spatial semantic supervision, these queries focus on manipulation-relevant regions in both external and wrist views. Reconstruction and predictive video supervision show less consistent focus across views, with more diffuse responses or attention away from the manipulation region in one view. This example illustrates the task-relevant visual focus of action queries learned with spatial semantic supervision. The study above shows that the prediction space affects policy generalization, the accessibility of motion information in world queries, and the visual attention of action queries. We next investigate how to organize prediction targets within this space, explicitly modeling source-relative changes and temporal distance through Residual World Prediction and Temporal-Scale Prediction.

4.3. Temporal-Scale Prediction 

RWP describes what changes relative to a source state; TSP organizes these changes across temporal horizons. Shorter horizons capture local robot and object motion, while longer horizons capture the spatial configurations that emerge as these motions accumulate. TSP supervises transitions over short, medium, and long horizons among four paired states sampled across the training clip, teaching the shared repre-sentation both local motion and longer-range state evolution. Horizons measure relative distance between sampled states, with physical duration determined by the sampling interval. All query groups are processed together in a single VLM forward pass conditioned on the current observations and instruction. 4.4. Training and Inference 

Action learning and world prediction jointly train the shared backbone, with the available supervision determined by the data source. 

Pretraining. We initialize the backbone from a pre-trained VLM and learn the world predictor and action expert jointly. Pretraining alternates robot demonstrations with hu-man videos. Robot demonstrations supply both action and visual transition targets, giving the joint objective 

Lrobot = Lact + λwm Lwm . (10) Human videos provide the same visual transition targets without robot-action labels and are optimized with Lwm 

alone. We set λwm = 0 .1 and β = 0 .5.

Posttraining. The downstream policy inherits the back-bone, predictor, action expert, and learned queries from pretraining. It is then trained on demonstrations from the target manipulation setting with Lrobot . The target encoder remains frozen in both stages. 

Inference. Current observations and the instruction pass through the VLM. Conditioned on the embodied latent ac-tions and available proprioception, the action expert inte-grates its learned velocity field from noise to an action chunk. World queries remain in the backbone input, preserving the token context used during training. The frozen target encoder and the world predictor are not executed. 

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Experimental Setup 

Training settings. PreWAM uses Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [2], a 16-layer transformer action expert, and frozen DI-NOv3 ViT-L/16 targets. Pretraining alternates DROID robot demonstrations [13] with Something-Something V2 human videos [12] for 6,250 cycles, each comprising one robot up-date and one video update. The global batch size is 1,024 per update. We then posttrain separate policies on LIBERO demonstrations and on the Bridge/RT-1 mixture for Sim-plerEnv, both with a global batch size of 256. For LIBERO and SimplerEnv, downstream training retains both action and world objectives and runs for 36,000 updates. The represen-tation comparisons and ablations use the same downstream batch size, update count, and optimization settings. For RoboTwin 2.0, we posttrain one policy on 50 tasks using 50 clean and 500 randomized demonstrations per task, with a global batch size of 256 for 100,000 updates. The pol-icy predicts and executes 32-step action chunks with four flow-matching inference steps; LIBERO and SimplerEnv use seven-step chunks. The real-world setup and evaluation protocol are described in Sec. 5.4. 

Benchmarks and protocols. LIBERO tests four suites of language-conditioned manipulation [21]. Its robustness extensions evaluate the learned policy without adaptation: LIBERO-Plus introduces seven categories of distribution shift [11], and LIBERO-Pro changes objects, positions, se-mantics, tasks, and environments [38]. LIBERO-Pro2.0 further separates static changes from interventions triggered during execution [39]. SimplerEnv provides real-to-sim eval-uation of policies trained on real robot demonstrations [18]. We use its Visual Matching setting for Google and WidowX robots, which aligns simulated scenes with the corresponding real environments. RoboTwin 2.0 tests bimanual manipula-tion under clean and randomized conditions [8]. We evaluate 100 episodes per task in each setting. 5.2. Benchmark Results 

LIBERO Suite. PreWAM maintains strong performance on standard manipulation tasks and generalizes to distribu-tion shifts without additional adaptation. It achieves 98.1% on LIBERO, close to Fast-WAM and Motus, but reaches 79.1% on LIBERO-Plus, exceeding them by 27.6 and 34.4 percentage points (Table 1). This contrast shows that similar performance on standard tasks can conceal substantial differ-ences in robustness. PreWAM achieves the highest success rates in the Robot, Language, and Light categories, with strengths spanning several types of variation. On LIBERO-Pro, PreWAM achieves the highest average success rate of 58.7%, leading the Spatial and Object suites (Table 2). LIBERO-Pro2.0 further evaluates static perturba-tions and interventions during execution. PreWAM achieves 64.0% and 70.1% in these settings, with drops of 28.1 and 22.0 percentage points from its unperturbed performance (Table 3). Its Runtime drop is the second smallest after π0.5,while its Static drop is also among the smallest, showing strong retention of manipulation performance under both types of disturbance. 

SimplerEnv. PreWAM also performs well in real-to-sim evaluation, achieving the highest average success rates of 78.9% on Google Robot and 69.8% on WidowX (Table 4). On Google Robot, it leads on Drawer and Place, reaching 75.0% and 75.9%. On WidowX, it achieves 100.0% on Egg-plant while maintaining strong performance on Spoon and Block. These results extend the effectiveness of PreWAM to policies trained on real robot demonstrations and evaluated in matched simulation environments. 

RoboTwin 2.0. PreWAM scales to multitask bimanual manipulation with a single policy across 50 tasks. It achieves 92.72% on Clean and 91.98% on Randomized, averaging 92.35% (Table 5). Its Clean performance is comparable to LingBot-VA and LaWAM, while its Randomized suc-cess rate is the highest among the compared methods. The 0.74-point gap between the two settings indicates consistent performance under scene randomization in this multitask bimanual benchmark.

5.4. Real-World Evaluation 

Setup and protocol. We evaluate PreWAM on a dual-arm Piper platform with two 6-DoF arms, parallel grippers, a top-view RGB camera, and two wrist cameras. Three tasks cover single-arm pick-and-place, precise bimanual placement, and coordinated bimanual manipulation (Fig. 4). Basket requires one arm to place a block in a basket; stage-1 success requires grasping and lifting the block off the table. Stack requires the two arms to position a yellow block and align and stack a red block on top; stage-1 success requires positioning the yellow block. Cup requires the left arm to stabilize a cup while the right arm places a red block inside; stage-1 success requires stabilizing the cup with the left arm while grasping and lifting the block with the right. We collect 50 demonstrations per task at 20 Hz and fine-tune PreWAM, π0 [5], and π0.5 [25] separately for each task. All three models undergo 4,000 task-specific fine-tuning updates. Each model is evaluated over 20 full-task trials per task, recording both stage-1 and full-task success with all 20 trials as the denominator. All policies use three current RGB views and 10 sampling steps. Actions are executed at 20 Hz with a nominal replanning interval of 16 control steps. The next chunk is prefetched eight steps in advance while the current chunk continues to execute. 

Results. As shown in Fig. 4, PreWAM achieves 90.0% mean stage-1 success, compared with 71.7% for π0 and 80.0% for π0.5. The largest stage-1 gain occurs on Stack ,where PreWAM reaches 80%, compared with 55% for both baselines. PreWAM completes Basket in all 20 trials and achieves 30% and 55% full-task success on Stack and Cup ,respectively. These results show that PreWAM reliably com-pletes preparatory operations such as grasping and position-ing, while subsequent stacking and placement remain chal-lenges for completing the bimanual tasks. Mean full-task success is 61.7%, compared with 36.7% for π0 and 68.3% for π0.5.

Inference efficiency. In real-world deployment, mean policy-call latency on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 is 463 ms for PreWAM, 501 ms for π0, and 884 ms for π0.5.

## 6. Conclusion 

Our controlled study links prediction spaces to policy gen-eralization: spatial semantic supervision provides stronger overall robustness and more accessible motion information under camera shifts and noise. PreWAM combines source-relative residual prediction with temporal-scale conditioning to shape shared policy representations during pretraining and posttraining, while retaining direct action generation at deployment. Evaluations on LIBERO benchmarks, Sim-plerEnv, and RoboTwin 2.0 support this design across tasks and embodiments. Real-world evaluations on three Piper tasks further assess single-arm and bimanual manipulation through stage-level success, full-task completion, and infer-ence latency.
