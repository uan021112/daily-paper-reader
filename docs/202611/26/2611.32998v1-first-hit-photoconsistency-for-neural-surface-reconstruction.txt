Abstract
Recent neural implicit and Gaussian splatting approaches
have achieved impressive results in multi-view surface re-
construction. However, jointly optimizing geometry and ap-
pearance introduces ambiguities. A parametric appearance
model can compensate for geometric errors, reproducing the
input images accurately without an accurate surface. We
demonstrate accurate neural implicit surface reconstruction
using only multi-view patch photoconsistency with geometry-
based visibility, without volumetric rendering or parametric
appearance models. For each reference ray, sphere trac-
ing computes the first surface intersection. Its position and
normal define a local patch whose photoconsistency is eval-
uated across visible neighboring views using colors sampled
directly from the input images. For efficient sphere trac-
ing, we use a 1-Lipschitz multi-layer perceptron. We use
a single photoconsistency loss, without foreground masks
or auxiliary geometric regularization. Experiments show
that our method achieves the lowest mean Chamfer distance
on BlendedMVS (BMVS) among compared methods and
matches the geometric accuracy of leading reconstruction
methods on DTU.
1. Introduction
Reconstructing a surface from multiple calibrated images
is a fundamental problem in 3D vision. Multi-view stereo
(MVS) has traditionally recovered geometry through image
matching and geometric consistency [11, 33]. Neural im-
plicit representations have subsequently become a standard

tool, providing continuous, resolution-independent repre-

distance or occupancy fields through differentiable surface
rendering [29, 44]. Later approaches, including NeuS [38]
and VolSDF [45], adopted volumetric rendering, converting
the implicit representation into a density and accumulat-
ing colors along camera rays. More recently, 3D Gaussian
splatting [20] has enabled efficient rendering using explicit
Gaussian primitives. Geometry-oriented variants, including
2DGS [15], PGSR [4] and AmbiSuR [22] aim to improve
surface reconstruction while retaining opacity-based com-
positing along camera rays.
In these methods, photometric supervision is applied to ren-
dered images rather than directly to the reconstructed surface,
potentially allowing geometric inaccuracies to persist (Fig. 2,
top). Moreover, parametric appearance models, such as color
MLPs or spherical harmonics, can compensate for geomet-
ric errors by adjusting the predicted appearance. Methods
such as NeuralWarp [6] and Geo-NeuS [10] address these
limitations by introducing explicit multi-view patch photo-
consistency alongside volumetric rendering, improving geo-
metric accuracy. This raises the question of whether patch
photoconsistency alone can provide sufficient supervision
to optimize a continuous neural surface, without volumetric
rendering or a parametric appearance model.
To investigate this question, we directly optimize a contin-
uous neural surface through multi-view patch photoconsis-
tency, building on the principle of photometric agreement
between corresponding image regions [8, 11, 37]. Specif-
ically, we represent the surface as the zero level set of a
1-Lipschitz MLP and use sphere tracing [14] to compute
the first surface intersection along each camera ray (Fig. 2,
bottom). At each intersection, the surface position and nor-
mal define a local tangent-plane patch whose photoconsis-
tency is evaluated across neighboring views, with visibility
explicitly determined from the evolving surface geometry.
Colors are sampled directly from the input images through
differentiable interpolation, without a parametric appearance
network. Since patch photoconsistency relies on sufficiently
accurate initial correspondences, existing methods start from
a sparse set of matched keypoints [11] or refine an existing
reconstruction [6, 37]. Similarly, we initialize the implicit
field from a coarse geometric proxy obtained by classical
MVS from the same input images, then refine its zero level
set using only multi-view patch photoconsistency.
Our method achieves the lowest mean Chamfer distance on
BMVS and matches the geometric accuracy of leading re-
construction methods on DTU.
The main contributions of this paper are:
1. A geometry-only optimization formulation of neural sur-
face reconstruction based solely on multi-view patch pho-
toconsistency, without volumetric rendering, a parametric
appearance model or auxiliary geometric regularization.
2. A differentiable first-hit optimization framework that ex-

2. Related Work
Multi-view stereo and variational refinement. Classical
MVS recovers geometry by enforcing photometric consis-
tency across calibrated views together with explicit visibility
reasoning, whether by expanding and filtering patches from
matched seeds [11] or estimating per-view depth maps and
fusing geometrically consistent estimates [12, 26, 33, 41].
Variational approaches optimize surfaces directly through
multi-view photoconsistency, evolving level sets on volu-
metric grids [8, 30] or refining triangular meshes through
photometric reprojection error [7, 37], typically with smooth-
ness regularization. Like these methods, we optimize the
surface directly through multi-view photoconsistency, but
represent it as a continuous neural field rather than a grid or
mesh, explicitly determine visibility using first-hit intersec-
tions and require no auxiliary geometric regularization.
Neural surface reconstruction. Neural implicit methods
represent geometry as the level set of a signed distance or oc-
cupancy field and optimize it by comparing rendered colors
with observed images, typically using a parametric appear-
ance model. Colors are evaluated either at the first surface
intersection [29, 44] or, following NeRF [28], integrated
along camera rays [38, 45]. Later work improves geomet-
ric detail and representation capacity [23]. To address the
geometric ambiguity of photometric rendering, several meth-
ods introduce additional constraints. MVSDF incorporates
stereo-derived depth and feature consistency [46], while Neu-
ralWarp and Geo-NeuS combine volumetric rendering with
patch photoconsistency [6, 10]. Geometry-oriented Gaus-
sian splatting methods [4, 15, 22] similarly add geometric
constraints to opacity-based rendering.
Differentiable surface rendering. Optimizing implicit sur-
faces through ray intersections requires both differentiat-
ing the intersection points and computing them reliably.
Differentiable surface rendering methods propagate gradi-
ents through these intersections using implicit differentia-
tion [29, 44] or approximate sphere-tracing gradients [24].
Other approaches address visibility discontinuities through
reparameterization [3, 36]. Sphere tracing [14] finds the first
surface intersection along a ray using steps bounded by the
distance to the surface. Eikonal regularization encourages
SDF behavior [13] but does not guarantee such bounds, and
its optimization can exhibit instabilities [42].
Lipschitz neural networks. A 1-Lipschitz field guaran-
tees that sphere-tracing steps do not exceed the distance to
the surface. Expressive 1-Lipschitz architectures include
GroupSort [2], which uses norm-preserving activations, and
convex potential layers (CPL) [27, 31]. Lenain et al. [21]
fit 1-Lipschitz neural fields to range scans using first-hit
depth regression and Coiffier and Béthune [5] fit such fields
to point clouds or triangle soups. While we also use a 1-
Lipschitz architecture, we address surface reconstruction
from calibrated RGB images through multi-view patch pho-
toconsistency with explicit cross-view visibility.

refined with two safeguarded Newton steps along the ray.

3.4. 1-Lipschitz architecture
To ensure convergence of sphere tracing, we constrain fθ
to be 1-Lipschitz. We adopt a backbone Φθ : RD →RD
interleaving Convex Potential Layers (CPL) and MaxMin
activations [2, 27, 31], where D is the even dimension of the
hidden layers. The resulting MLP is parameterized as
fθ(x) = v⊤Φθ(Ax) + β,
(9)
where A ∈RD×3 satisfies A⊤A = I3, v = ev/∥ev∥2 and
β ∈R. Since each component is 1-Lipschitz, so is fθ.
Further architectural details are provided in Appendix A.5.

3.6. Initialization
Patch photoconsistency requires sufficiently accurate initial
correspondences. Like NeuralWarp [6], which introduces
patch warping only after an initial VolSDF [45] fitting phase,
we initialize fθ from a coarse geometric proxy. Starting from
an enclosing sphere, we carve away free space using depth
maps from the classical MVS method ACMMP [41], then
fit fθ to the SDF of the resulting volume (Appendix A.2).
4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation details
Unless stated otherwise, we use an MLP with 8 layers of
width 256, Convex Potential Layers, MaxMin activations
and positional encoding with L = 6 (Appendix A.5). We
optimize for 300k steps using Adam with cosine learning
rate decay from 10−3 to 10−4, sampling 16,384 rays per
batch, with 90% drawn uniformly from pixels whose rays
intersect the initial surface and 10% from the remaining pix-
els. For each reference view, we select K = 6 source views
and compute normalized correlation on grayscale intensities
sampled over 5 × 5 tangent patches, with ρ = 1. Further
implementation details appear in Appendix A.1.
Baselines.
We compare the proposed method with neu-
ral implicit surface methods IDR [44], NeuS [38], Neu-
ralWarp [6], ProbeSDF [35] and Geo-NeuS [10], classical
MVS methods COLMAP [33], ACMMP [41] and PMVS
[11], and Gaussian-splatting surface reconstruction meth-
ods 2DGS [15], PGSR [4], and AmbiSuR [22]. IDR, NeuS
and ProbeSDF use foreground masks during fitting, while
AmbiSuR uses pretrained depth priors. For classical MVS
baselines, we apply Poisson surface reconstruction [19] with
density trimming at threshold 7, following [10]. We report
Neuralangelo [23] separately in Appendix A.6, as our runs
with its released implementation yielded substantially worse
results than those reported in the original paper (1.71 vs.
0.61 mm).
4.2. BMVS
To complement DTU’s fixed camera trajectory (Sec. 4.3),
we evaluate on BMVS [43], whose object-centric scenes pro-
vide approximately 360◦view coverage, testing our method
under more varied visibility configurations. We evaluate on
8 scenes used in prior neural surface reconstruction bench-
marks, with 31 to 143 calibrated images at 768 × 576 res-
olution. Unlike DTU, BMVS uses reference meshes re-
constructed from images, without independently measured
geometry or reported accuracy [43]. We follow the BMVS
protocol used by ProbeSDF [35]. Neural implicit meshes are
extracted using marching cubes at resolution 5123 [25]. The
metric is the bidirectional unsigned point-to-surface distance
between the predicted mesh and the reference mesh. Dis-
tances larger than 0.025 in normalized scene coordinates are
discarded before averaging, and scores are reported ×103.

4.3. DTU
DTU [1, 17] provides calibrated multi-view images with
structured light ground truth, for which Jensen et al. [17] re-
port a standard deviation of 0.14 mm, measured on a sphere
of known geometry. We evaluate the standard 15 scans,
each containing 49 to 64 images at 1600 × 1200 resolution.
We reran baselines using the official implementations and
recovered their published results under their original evalu-
ation protocols, except for Geo-NeuS (0.60 mm versus the
published 0.51 mm), consistent with independent reruns re-
porting 0.59–0.62 mm [18, 32]. For both rerun columns, all
meshes undergo the same silhouette crop using masks dilated
by 12 pixels [6]. Neural implicit meshes are extracted using
marching cubes at resolution 5123 [38, 44], while Gaussian
splatting methods retain their mesh extraction procedures.
We sample the meshes, transform the samples to DTU coor-
dinates, and compute bidirectional Chamfer distance CD1
(mm) against the official ground truth (Appendix A.4).

cavities. Detailed results are given in Table 10.
5.2. Visibility and depth localization
To isolate the effect of visibility filtering, we fix the recon-
structed surface and estimate depth along reference rays by
minimizing the patch photoconsistency loss, with and with-
out source-view occlusion filtering. We compare these esti-
mates against depths derived from DTU ground-truth point
clouds. Partially occluded rays are those whose ground-truth
surface points are visible from at least two and occluded
from at least two of the six selected source views. Across
15 DTU scans, filtering reduces mean absolute depth error
from 2.81 to 2.13 mm on 83k partially occluded rays, com-
pared with 1.19 to 1.15 mm on 148k reliable foreground rays.
Improvements across all 15 scenes indicate that visibility
filtering primarily benefits depth localization under partial
occlusion (Table 13).
5.3. Patch photoconsistency vs. volume rendering
To assess the effect of replacing volumetric rendering with
direct patch photoconsistency, we compare our formulation
with NeuS, which uses volumetric rendering without explicit
multi-view matching. We initialize NeuS from the same
coarse surface, retaining its architecture, rendering loss and
eikonal regularization, but without foreground masks. With
matched initialization and geometry architecture, our for-
mulation outperforms NeuS on 14 of 15 DTU scans (0.60
vs. 0.75 mm), while achieving the same mean accuracy as
Geo-NeuS (0.60 mm), which combines volumetric rendering
with patch photoconsistency.

5.4. Computational cost
Sphere tracing makes the forward pass costly due to iterative
network evaluations and visibility queries, whereas implicit
differentiation keeps the backward pass inexpensive. This
yields a runtime of 5 h 10 min per DTU scene on an H100
for our optimized implementation (Appendix B.3), including
10 min for initialization, comparable to NeuS (4 h) and Geo-
NeuS (5 h) and faster than Neuralangelo (7 h), but slower
than Gaussian splatting methods (10–20 min) (Table 8).
6. Conclusion
We have demonstrated that accurate neural surface recon-
struction does not require joint geometry and appearance
optimization. By optimizing first-hit surface intersections
through multi-view patch photoconsistency, our formula-
tion avoids the coupling between geometry and appearance
inherent in rendering approaches while achieving competi-
tive or superior geometric accuracy. These results suggest
that direct surface optimization is an effective alternative
to volumetric rendering with appearance models for neural
surface reconstruction. As an extension, photometric bundle
8


adjustment [7], alternating between camera pose and sur-
face optimization, reduces mean BMVS CD1 from 2.022 to
1.896 (6.2%), improving all eight scenes (Appendix C.4).
Similar pose refinement would probably also benefit other
reconstruction methods. This formulation also has limita-
tions. Photoconsistency constrains only surface regions with
valid intersections and multi-view correspondences, so the
method depends on initialization, although similar results
from ACMMP and COLMAP initializations show that it
does not rely on a particular MVS method. Omitting geo-
metric regularization isolates what photoconsistency alone
can constrain, at the cost of local roughness in some recon-
structions. Normalized correlation also discards absolute
intensity and contrast, motivating richer photometric match-
ing objectives. Finally, extending the approach to larger,
unbounded scenes remains future work.

B.3. Efficient sphere tracing
(i) After each iteration, rays that have converged or left
the valid depth interval are excluded from subsequent
network evaluations.
(ii) The final non-grazing intersection is differentiated
implicitly using Eq 7, rather than backpropagating
through all tracing iterations.
(iii) Following DIST [24], sphere tracing starts at the in-
tersection between the camera ray and the enclosing
sphere Ω0, preventing useless evaluations of fθ in
empty space.
B.4. Computational cost
Normal differentiation.
Our implementation stops gra-
dients through the tangent plane normals, avoiding second-
order derivatives. Differentiating through the normals in-
creases iteration time by 20% and GPU memory by 140%,
while worsening mean CD1 on five DTU scans (0.525 vs.
0.478 mm).

B.5. Sphere tracing reliability
For each model, we uniformly sample image rays until 100k
yield a sphere-tracing intersection (M = 128). Using 192-
bit Arb interval arithmetic, we certify the absence of earlier
roots and enclose the first intersection within 10−7r0, where
r0 is the radius of the enclosing sphere Ω0. On rays with
certified intersections, we evaluate first-hit accuracy and
overshoots with a tolerance of 10−3r0. Gradient statistics
use the maximum sampled ∥∇xfθ∥2 along each ray.
