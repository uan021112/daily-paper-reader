Abstract
Understanding how objects move in dynamic scenes re-
quires recovering their 3D motion from visual observations
and predicting their positions at unobserved times. Exist-
ing methods either optimize scene-specific velocity fields or
impose predefined physical and kinematic assumptions. We
instead study learning a continuous-time dynamics opera-
tor across scenes, mapping multi-view visual observations
to 3D object kinematics. We introduce Adaptive Pixel Flows
(AdaPixFlow), which learns this operator from multi-view
RGB-D observations without per-scene optimization or pre-
defined motion models.
The method fuses observations
from all cameras into a shared voxel grid in world co-
ordinates, from which the network predicts a continuous-
time velocity field. Because the operator is trained across
scenes, it learns motion priors that carry over to unseen
scenes.
The field can then be integrated from observed
states to predict object positions between observations and
beyond the observed sequence.
On held-out PhysInOne
scenes, AdaPixFlow improves F1 over the strongest com-
peting method by 2.5% for in-between prediction and 15%
for future prediction. Compared with per-scene velocity-
field methods, the improvements are 30% and 85%, respec-
tively. Applied zero-shot to Spring-Gaus, it improves future-
prediction F1 by 12%.
1. Introduction
Predicting how objects move, such as a swinging pendulum,
a spinning panel, or an object dropping into liquid, is a key
task in animation, simulation, mixed reality, and robotics
[1, 4, 10, 19, 21, 32, 51]. Dynamic reconstruction methods
recover such motion from video by fitting a time-varying
3D representation to each scene. This can include deformed
canonical space, factorized spacetime volume, or trajecto-
arXiv:2611.26433v1  [cs.CV]  19 Nov 2026


ries of 3D Gaussians [3, 12, 20, 31, 35, 39, 44, 47, 53].
Feed-forward 3D trackers [49] and 4D reconstruction mod-
els [55] infer time-varying geometry and correspondences
across scenes without per-scene fitting. These methods can
be queried at any time within the observed sequence, but
they do not model the dynamics that would predict how the
scene evolves after it.
A complementary line of work focuses on explicitly
modeling scene dynamics. Some methods represent motion
through learned velocity fields [22, 24, 30] or per-particle
dynamics [23]. Others recover material parameters from
visual observations to drive differentiable physical simula-
tors [16, 25, 58], or specify these parameters manually [50].
While these approaches provide a more explicit account
of temporal evolution, they are often optimized indepen-
dently for each scene and rely on a prescribed model of the
underlying dynamics. Recent video-based dynamics mod-
els [45, 56, 57] learn dynamics that transfer across scenes
or object instances, but remain tied to specific material or
object categories, or rely on pretrained world-model repre-
sentations.
A different modeling perspective is to treat 3D dy-
namics as a direct parameterization of visual observa-
tions, rather than scene-specific or material-specific mod-
els. In this paradigm, one can learn the representation space
for a continuous-time dynamics operator that can general-
ize across physical systems and scenes.
Inspired by the
operator-learning philosophy [26, 29, 43], we seek a repre-
sentation of such an operator that admits zero-shot evalua-
tion on previously unseen scenes, without material-specific
modeling.
Concretely,
we
consider
kinematics
through
a
continuous-time 3D velocity field that describes the
evolution of object positions. Learning such a field directly
from video presents a fundamental challenge: the motion
between and after observations may be underdetermined by
the observations themselves, because visual observations
may be entangled with viewpoint and occlusion or be
sparsely sampled.
Thus, the field must draw on priors
learned across scenes. Addressing the entanglement with
viewpoint and occlusion further requires a representation
in which scene geometry and motion can be expressed
independently of the viewing configuration.
Our key insight is to learn the dynamics operator on a
volumetric fusion of the observations. This expresses scene
states in one world coordinate frame shared by all cam-
eras, rather than in the image coordinates of each view.
We draw from the longstanding use of volumetric fusion
to combine multiple views into a common representation,
from depth fusion [9, 33] to perception across arbitrary
camera rigs [38]. With calibrated multi-view RGB-D ob-
servations providing the geometric grounding, we describe
scene evolution through the 3D motion of back-projected
visual observations, which we term Adaptive Pixel Flows
(AdaPixFlow). As illustrated in Figure 2, AdaPixFlow pa-
rameterizes this motion using an observation-conditioned
continuous-time 3D velocity field, along which the back-
projected object points are evolved through differentiable
advection. This enables continuous-time prediction of ob-
ject positions both between observations and beyond the ob-
served sequence. Figure 1 shows predictions on a held-out
scene.
Our contributions are:
• We introduce a continuous-time dynamics operator that,
trained once across scenes, predicts the 3D motion of ob-
jects in unseen scenes from sparse multi-view RGB-D ob-
servations.
• We formulate the dynamics on a fused voxel grid, in
which the back-projected object pixels are advected along
a predicted velocity field, and train it through the advec-
tion without velocity labels or ground-truth point corre-
spondences.
• We evaluate AdaPixFlow on held-out PhysInOne [59]
scenes and test its zero-shot generalization on Spring-
Gaus [58], PhysGaia [17], and Kubric-4D [13, 42].
Across these benchmarks, we compare against per-scene
dynamics models and adapted 4D reconstruction and
tracking methods. On PhysInOne, AdaPixFlow improves
F1 over the strongest competing method by 2.5% for in-
between prediction and 15% for future prediction, with
gains of 30% and 85% over per-scene velocity-field meth-
ods.
Without additional training, it improves future-
prediction F1 on Spring-Gaus by 12% and in-between
prediction F1 on PhysGaia by 5%, while achieving com-
petitive performance on Kubric-4D, including against
methods trained on that benchmark.
2. Related Work
Dynamic reconstruction and tracking.
Neural radi-
ance fields and 3D Gaussian splatting have enabled dy-
namic scene representations based on canonical-space de-
formations, factorized spacetime representations, and time-
dependent Gaussian trajectories [3, 11, 31, 36, 39, 54].
Subsequent work recovers 4D geometry and motion from
monocular video [20, 44], while feed-forward methods ex-
tend reconstruction and tracking to unseen scenes [14, 49,
55]. More recently, Trace Anything [28] and Uni4R [52]
have introduced continuous-time 3D point queries. Trace
Anything predicts, for each pixel, a cubic B-spline trajec-
tory with a fixed number of control points over the normal-
ized time span of its input frames, which may be sparse or
unordered. It forecasts by continuing a trajectory along its
tangent rather than with a learned dynamics model. Uni4R
predicts per-pixel 3D velocities from features interpolated
between two consecutive frames and integrates them over
that interval.
Under this frame-pair formulation, every
query needs the frame after it, so Uni4R does not predict
beyond the last input frame. AdaPixFlow instead learns a
continuous-time dynamics operator in a shared 3D space,
enabling motion prediction both between sparse observa-
tions and beyond the observed sequence.
Learned dynamics models. Another line of work learns


predictive dynamics from visual observations using latent
particles, RGB-D particle grids, or object slots [46, 48, 56,
57]. Related approaches model temporal evolution through
continuous-time neural dynamics [6, 37] or learned oper-
ators [5, 26]. Several of these models operate on explicit
3D particle or Gaussian states [46, 56, 57] and typically ad-
vance them in discrete simulation steps. AdaPixFlow com-
bines a shared metric 3D state, fused from all calibrated
views, with a continuous-time dynamics operator that can
be queried at any time between and after observations.
Physical dynamics from video. Physical dynamics can
also be recovered from video using physics-informed ve-
locity fields [22] or differentiable simulators of continuum
mechanics and spring-mass systems [2, 16, 25, 58]. Such
methods either estimate simulator parameters from obser-
vations or take them as given [50]. Other approaches esti-
mate per-Gaussian velocities [23, 24, 30]. These methods
typically optimize scene-specific representations under pre-
scribed dynamics formulations. In contrast, AdaPixFlow
learns a continuous-time dynamics operator across scenes
and infers a 3D velocity field for a new scene directly from
its observations, without scene-specific optimization or a
predefined dynamics model.

3.2. Learning a Continuous-time Dynamics Opera-
tor
For a prediction interval beginning at observation k0, we
construct the context c = (zk0, zkp, zk1, e, s), for, kp =
max(k0−s, 0), and k1 = k0+s, where s is the observation
spacing and e ∈{0, 1} indicates whether the succeeding
observation is available. Thus, c ∈C = Z3 × {0, 1} × N.
In in-between prediction, both bounding observations are
available and e = 1. In future prediction, k0 = k is the
last observed step, kp = k −2, and s = 2. The succeeding
observation is unavailable, so we set zk1 = 0 and e = 0.
We learn an operator Gθ that maps this context to a time-
dependent 3D velocity field:
Gθ : C →V,
c 7→vθ(·, · | c),
where V = {v : Ω× [0, H] →R3} with velocities in vox-
els per time step, and H is the longest horizon the operator
predicts. We use H = 12 ≥max(s, J): in-between pre-
diction integrates over t ∈[0, s] and future prediction over
t ∈[0, J], with t measured from k0.
We implement Gθ as a 3D U-Net [7, 41] trained across
scenes. In a single forward pass, the network outputs the
velocity fields at all H+1 integer times t = 0, . . . , H, the
node fields V0, . . . , VH of Figure 2. Velocities at intermedi-
ate times are linearly interpolated between consecutive node
fields (Appendix B). The inputs are the three fused states, e,
and s, so time enters only as the offset t from k0, never as
an absolute timestamp. The output layer is zero-initialized,
so the initial velocity field is zero.

when predicting between observations and beyond the ob-

generalize to unseen scenes and datasets without per-scene

4.2. Motion Underdetermined by the Observations
We examine whether the advantage of AdaPixFlow depends
on motion complexity. Using ground-truth states, we divide
target times into linear and non-linear subsets according to
whether an oracle linear interpolation achieves an F1 of at
least 90. For each in-between target, the oracle has access
to the exact object states at both ends of the observation
gap and selects the best of three interpolation rules: cen-
troid translation, rigid motion with interpolated rotation, or
straight-line point trajectories. For future prediction, we use
constant-velocity extrapolation from the last two observed
states and evaluate targets 4–10 steps ahead. F1 is pooled
over all targets in each subset.
On linear targets, AdaPixFlow matches or outperforms
the strongest trackers on both PhysInOne (Tab. 9) and the
physics benchmarks (Tab. 2). Its advantage is clearer on
non-linear motion: it achieves the highest F1 on non-linear
in-between targets across PhysInOne, Spring-Gaus, and
PhysGaia, and on future targets 4–10 steps ahead in PhysI-
nOne and Spring-Gaus. This is consistent with how the
trackers are applied to unobserved times: we interpolate and
extrapolate their tracks linearly in time (Sec. E.1).
4.3. Generalization to Unseen Scenes and Datasets
On held-out PhysInOne scenes (Tab. 3), prediction accu-
racy decreases as the observation spacing increases, while
remaining high on the Val scenes despite their differences
from the training distribution. On the 15 Test scenes, in-
between F1 decreases from 90.9 to 78.7 as the spacing in-
creases from 2 to 8 steps. The 8 Val scenes achieve com-
parable or higher scores (90.8–99.9), despite having back-
grounds and a phenomenon (PlasticineFall) absent from
training. Future prediction is less accurate on Val than on
Test (55.8 vs. 68.3), as the Val forecast windows include
ground contacts.
We further evaluate AdaPixFlow without retraining on
Spring-Gaus [58], PhysGaia [17], and Kubric-4D [13, 42].
These datasets differ from PhysInOne in object materials,
camera configurations, and scene scale.
Kubric-4D pro-
vides depth and masks. For Spring-Gaus and PhysGaia, we
render both from the provided particle data. Across scenes
evaluated by all methods (Tab. 10), AdaPixFlow achieves
the highest accuracy on Spring-Gaus in both tasks and on
PhysGaia in-between. On Kubric-4D, it ranks second to
STv2, which was trained on Kubric data.
Finally, AdaPixFlow retains much of its performance
with estimated rather than dataset-provided depth and
masks.
Using depth from Depth Anything 3 [27] and
masks from SAM 2.1 [40], it achieves F1 scores of 78.5
in-between and 53.3 in future prediction on the comparison
scenes. FreeGave, given the same masks, scores 66.5 and
30.0 (Sec. A.5).
5. Conclusion
AdaPixFlow demonstrates that a shared continuous-time
dynamics operator can predict 3D object motion across
scenes from sparse multi-view observations. By combining
calibrated geometric reconstruction with a learned velocity
field, it predicts states at unobserved times without fitting
a separate model to each scene. Experiments on held-out
PhysInOne scenes and external physics benchmarks show
competitive or improved prediction accuracy over recon-
struction, tracking, and per-scene dynamics baselines, with
the largest gains for non-linear motion and longer forecasts.
These results suggest that learning motion in a shared 3D
space provides a useful alternative to interpolating recon-
structed trajectories.
Future work. The current operator models kinematics.
Conditioning it on contact and force models would extend
it to interactions such as collisions and bounces. Extend-
ing the forecast horizon through training on chained pre-
dictions is another direction. Finally, applying AdaPixFlow
to real multi-view captures will require handling errors in
estimated depth and segmentation.

A.2. Held-out Scenes
Figure 6 adds the Linear reference to Tab. 3, and Figure 7
shows eight held-out test scenes at the middle of the gap

Linear blends the two observations per voxel, which
fades objects instead of moving them. At the middle of a
gap, a voxel occupied in only one observation keeps half its
occupancy and falls below the threshold, so Linear recovers
only 29% of the ground-truth object voxels at s=2.
Accuracy varies across tiers. Tier B2 drops to 63.7 at
s=8 and to 32.0 in future prediction, and two of its three
scenes combine a spinning object with a collision.
The test scenes share their phenomena, and 14 of the 15
share a background, with training scenes. The Val scenes
are therefore our strongest test of generalization: their back-
grounds never appear in training, and neither does Plas-
ticineFall, one of their two phenomena. Both phenomena
are falling deformable objects, and we leave other unseen
phenomena and real captures to future work. Finally, the
window of this evaluation is capped at 128 voxels, which
removes 22–56% of the ground-truth object in DropInLiq-
uid, CrankPush, and PanelSpin.

Training.
We build the training targets by fusing all
cameras of a training scene, 12, or 15 for special materials,
and use them only as unordered object points per step. The
network never sees a fusion from fewer views or from esti-
mated depth during training, so such test inputs differ from
the training distribution. We draw source points from the
initial observation for forward targets and, when the next
observation is given, from that observation for backward
targets. Advected points outside the crop are left out of
the prediction-to-target term of the Chamfer distance, and
we do not supervise color. We found the redraw of train-
ing windows necessary: without it, a preliminary run on
the same scenes collapsed to predicting no motion by step
3,000.
Design choices. Our design follows from preliminary
experiments with earlier models. Blending fused states per
voxel, or interpolating values at fixed positions, cross-faded
objects instead of moving them. Even a continuous-time
operator trained on spline paths in a fused feature space
reached only the IoU of per-voxel blending (0.79). Rolling
out from the first observation compounded errors, reach-
ing an IoU of 0.34 over 22 intervals, although each interval
alone reached 1.00 from its true start. We therefore move
points, start each prediction from an observation, and con-
dition the field on both neighboring observations.

E.1. Adapting 4D Reconstruction and Tracking
Methods
None of SoM [44], MoSca [20], STv2 [49], and D4RT [55]
predicts unobserved time steps, so we adapt them as listed in
Tab. 17. D4RT releases no weights, so every D4RT number
comes from OpenD4RT, an unofficial reproduction.
For transparency, we note four departures from this pro-
tocol. First, we registered the stride-2 candidates of STv2


after its stride-4 target scores existed. With stride 4 every-
where, its means are 87.2 and 50.0 instead of 87.8 and 50.7.
Second, the candidate set of OpenD4RT for its first scene,
ElasticFall in-between, grew after we had seen target scores.
Third, MoSca ran out of memory on three scene–task pairs
and used the pre-registered fallback settings for short clips.
Finally, selecting candidates on the observed steps does not
always pick the better candidate on the targets. On Elastic-
Fall future, two unselected candidates score 57.5 and 35.8,
above AdaPixFlow.
E.2. Uni4R’s Formulation under Sparse Observa-
tions
Uni4R [52] releases no code, so we test its formulation with
a reduced reimplementation rather than the method itself.
Following its Sec. 3, we treat every pixel of an input frame
as an anchor point. We interpolate features along a straight
line between consecutive input frames, decode a per-pixel
3D velocity from them and the query time, and integrate it
with Euler steps of 0.1 from the preceding frame. We train
with its integral-consistency and flow-matching losses. Our
model is smaller than Uni4R. It uses a DINOv2 ViT-S/14
encoder [34], has 54 M parameters in total, and is trained
for 4.5 h on one GPU on 118 Kubric-4D training scenes,
whose rigid object poses give the ground-truth 3D position
of every pixel at every frame. Like the other adapted meth-
ods, it reads the depth of the observed views, from which
we obtain its anchor points.
We train it twice with the same budget, once on consecu-
tive frames only, as Uni4R does, and once on inputs spaced
1 to 8 steps apart, as AdaPixFlow does. We evaluate both
on the Kubric-4D scenes of Appendix F. Each training view
is a separate input, and we unite the points of all views and
score them as in Tab. 14, averaging over the scenes with
scored targets (five at s=4 and six otherwise).
When trained on consecutive frames, the model barely
moves the points between sparse observations. It improves
on holding the preceding observation, from which it inte-
grates, by 6.4 F1 points at a spacing of 4 and by 2.8 at a
spacing of 8, compared with 12.8 and 9.2 when trained on
sparse spacings (Tab. 6). AdaPixFlow integrates from the
nearer observation and improves on holding it by 8.7 and
8.9 points. Beyond the last frame, where we extend Uni4R’s
feature interpolation past the last input, neither variant im-
proves on holding the last observation by more than 0.4
points, whereas AdaPixFlow improves on it by 12.4. Since
our reimplementation is much smaller than Uni4R, these
numbers describe how its formulation responds to sparse
sampling, not Uni4R’s own accuracy.
