# VLM-guided Grounding Expert for Generalised Referring Expression Comprehension 

Spirit AI Agent Team *

Spirit AI, Beijing, China 

Abstract 

Generalised referring expression comprehension (GREC) aims to identify and localise the zero, one or multiple ob-jects referred to by a language expression. In robotic ma-nipulation, grounding also needs to remain accurate when requests for the same objects are paraphrased or embed-ded in additional context in robotics. However, grounding expert models are sensitive to instruction variation, while generative vision–language models (VLMs) struggle with complete target recovery and rejection of absent targets. These complementary limitations motivate combining the contextual representations of VLMs with the direct spatial prediction capabilities of grounding experts. We therefore introduce VLM-guided Grounding Expert (VGE), which combines a frozen VLM with a trainable grounding expert through layerwise joint attention and text-conditioned in-stance scoring. These interfaces leverage multimodal un-derstanding of the VLM to guide object-query refinement and candidate selection while retaining direct bounding-box prediction. Across three datasets, VGE achieves the highest averaged F1 score among the evaluated methods on all five test splits, with differences of at most 1.2 percent-age points across instruction conditions within each split. On the dataset with robotic scenes, VGE also achieves the highest F1 across instruction variants and no-target, single-target and multiple-target cases. VGE provides a 5.3–

13 .0× inference speedup over the baseline with the same VLM base model. These results support combining VLM context with specialised spatial prediction to address both grounding challenges, with particularly strong performance in robotic scenes. Code will be released upon publication.

Generalised referring expression comprehension (GREC) addresses the former by extending conventional single-target grounding to expressions referring to zero, one or multiple objects [9, 10]. Instruction variation arises when users express the same manipulation request through dif-ferent action descriptions and object references [13]. In human–robot interaction, requests also appear in longer contexts that include explanations or incidental remarks, adding irrelevant information without necessarily changing the intended targets [24]. Despite progress in visual grounding, the joint chal-lenges of varying numbers of targets and instruction vari-ation remain insufficiently explored. Grounding expert models such as Grounding DINO [20] learn image–text alignment with dedicated box-prediction modules [20], while general vision–language models (VLMs), such as Qwen3.5 [25] and Gemma4 [27], generate object locations in text based on their multimodal understanding. Our diag-nostic evaluation identifies distinct limitations in grounding experts and VLMs. Grounding experts remain sensitive to instruction variation, while VLMs struggle to localise all matching objects and recognise when no valid target ex-ists. These complementary failure modes motivate combin-ing the relative robustness of VLMs to instruction variation with the spatial prediction capabilities of grounding experts. In this work, we introduce the VLM-guided Ground-ing Expert (VGE), which combines a frozen VLM with a trainable grounding expert to condition object localisation and target selection based on the multimodal understand-ing of the VLM. Since grounding requires both refining candidate representations and determining which instances match the instruction, we introduce VLM guidance at both stages. Specifically, VGE incorporates the VLM’s contex-tual representations into the grounding expert through two interfaces. Layerwise joint attention conditions the expert’s object-query refinement on VLM context, allowing instruc-tion information to guide spatial prediction within the ex-pert. Text-conditioned instance scoring complements this process by assessing each candidate’s relevance to the in-struction. By using the VLM as a frozen representation provider rather than an autoregressive coordinate genera-tor, VGE retains direct prediction of the grounding expert, maintaining high prediction efficiency. Together, these in-terfaces use instruction context to guide both box prediction and the selection of zero, one, or multiple targets, preserv-ing native visual processing and direct box prediction. We make three contributions. • We conduct a diagnostic evaluation of grounding experts and VLMs under instruction variation and varying num-bers of targets, identifying distinct limitations in instruc-tion robustness and object localisation and selection. • We propose VGE, connecting a frozen VLM to a trainable expert via layerwise joint attention and text-conditioned instance scoring. These interfaces guide target localisa-tion and selection using multimodal context. • Across three datasets, VGE achieves the highest averaged F1 among the evaluated methods on all five test splits. On RoboRefIt, it also leads across all evaluated cases while providing a 5.3–13 .0× inference speedup over Qwen3.5-9B across the three datasets. Training only the ground-ing expert and interfaces provides a competitive solution compared with additional VLM parameter updates. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Visual Grounding 

Visual grounding establishes correspondences between natural-language descriptions and regions in an image. A central task is referring expression comprehension (REC), which aims to identify and localise the object specified by an expression. Language-conditioned detectors can be used for this task. Grounding DINO integrates grounded pre-training, language-guided query selection and cross-modal fusion to locate objects described by category names or referring expressions [20]. YOLO-World learns region– text alignment through contrastive learning [3], while LLMDet introduces language-model supervision during de-tector training and removes the language model at infer-ence [8]. GREC extends REC to expressions referring to zero, one or multiple objects [9]. RECANTFormer ad-dresses varying target counts with an encoder-only trans-former and Hungarian matching between predicted and ground-truth boxes [10]. However, our evaluation shows that these methods are sensitive to added context even when the intended targets are unchanged. Generative VLMs perform visual grounding through multimodal instruction following. Shikra represents spa-tial coordinates as numerical text [2], while Qwen3.5 can generate bounding-box coordinates from image–text in-puts [25]. This formulation allows the language model to use multimodal context when interpreting a request and producing spatial outputs. However, language understand-ing and grounding accuracy need to be assessed separately. Ref-Adv shows that strong conventional REC performance can coexist with difficulties resolving expressions that re-quire distinguishing targets from challenging visual distrac-tors [5]. Autoregressive coordinate generation also incurs sequential decoding overhead. VGent reports increasing in-ference time with the number of output boxes for the eval-uated generative VLMs [12]. ECG Mirage also shows that strong predictive performance can coexist with limited use of visual evidence [17]. These findings motivate combining VLM-based instruction understanding with dedicated local-isation modules that predict boxes directly. 

2.2. Integrating VLMs with Localisation Modules 

Existing VLM baselines connect multimodal representa-tions to spatial predictions through generated tokens, atten-tion maps or task-specific modules. In segmentation, LISA passes the hidden representation of a generated segmenta-tion token to a mask decoder [14]. F-LMM instead converts attention maps from a frozen multimodal model into masks and refines them with a SAM-based module [30]. VGE also keeps the VLM frozen, but provides fine-grained mul-timodal context with layerwise joint attention and object-query refinement with instance scoring. 2Other methods extract regions of interest (RoI) first and input them into VLMs. Groma encodes RoI as vi-sual tokens for region-level understanding and grounded responses [23]. ROD-MLLM associates detected regions with region-anchor tokens that the language model gen-erates to identify referred objects, supporting expressions with no matching target or multiple matching targets [31]. These methods incorporate region-specific representations into language-model processing, yet they still rely on the VLM’s linguistic capabilities. In contrast, VGE enables spatial understanding by leveraging both the VLM’s mul-timodal understanding and specialised grounding experts.

3.2. VGE Architecture 

VGE instantiates the grounding model F by combining a frozen VLM with a trainable grounding expert, as illus-trated in Figure 2. Given an image I and an instruction P ,both models process the inputs using their respective pre-processing pipelines. The VLM provides contextual repre-sentations of the image and instruction, while the ground-ing expert performs object localisation and target selec-tion. We use Grounding DINO [20] as the expert, retain-ing its visual backbone, text encoder, fusion encoder and detection decoder. We introduce two trainable interfaces to incorporate VLM context into complementary stages of grounding: object-query refinement and candidate scoring. Layerwise joint attention guides the former, while text-conditioned instance scoring adjusts the latter. The expert’s box-prediction head produces candidate bounding boxes, and confidence filtering followed by non-maximum sup-pression (NMS) yields the final predictions bB, which may contain zero, one, or multiple boxes. We train the com-plete grounding expert and both interfaces while keeping the VLM frozen, using its context to guide grounding with-out VLM-generated coordinates.

3.4. Text-conditioned Instance Scoring

3.5. Training Objective 

We train the grounding expert and both interfaces while keeping the VLM frozen, allowing the expert to learn from fixed multimodal representations without updating the VLM parameters. Supervision is applied only to the ex-pert’s final-layer predictions. Hungarian matching assigns ground-truth boxes in B∗(I, P ) to predicted candidates [1], and the training objective is 

L = Lfocal + λ1Lℓ1 + λ2LGIoU , (8) where focal loss Lfocal supervises the corrected instance logits si [18], while Lℓ1 and LGIoU supervise matched bounding boxes [26]. The coefficients λ1 and λ2 balance the regression terms. Unmatched queries receive negative classification targets. For no-target samples ( M = 0 ), all queries are negative, and both regression terms are zero. Supplementary Sec. S3.1 provides further training details. 

4. Results 

We evaluate VGE through five research questions. • RQ1: How do instruction variation and varying numbers of targets affect grounding experts and VLMs? • RQ2: How does VGE compare with baselines across in-struction variants and varying numbers of targets? • RQ3: Can training only the expert and interfaces match configurations that also adapt the VLM? • RQ4: How does VGE compare with grounding experts and VLMs in inference latency? • RQ5: How do layerwise joint attention and text-conditioned instance scoring contribute? 44.1. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate on gRefCOCO [9, 19] and Ref-ZOM [11] for general scenes, and RoboRefIt [22] for robotic scenes. We retain the original images, target annotations and test splits. Where no validation split is available, we reserve 1,200 proportionally sampled training references for validation. Each evaluation reference comprises an original instruction, a generated paraphrase and a context-augmented variant, all referring to the same annotated targets in the same im-age. Supplementary Sec. S2 describes the data splits and instruction-generation procedure. Our main VGE configuration combines a frozen Qwen3.5-9B [25] with Grounding DINO-base and both proposed interfaces. All task-specific training uses only original instructions. RQ1 evaluates Qwen3.5-4B/9B, Grounding DINO-base and LLMDet-base [8] without task-specific parameter updates. RQ2 compares VGE with Grounding DINO, LLMDet, RECANTFormer, and both zero-shot and LoRA-adapted versions of Qwen3.5-9B and Groma. For models that use confidence filtering, thresholds are calibrated on original validation instructions and held fixed across test splits and instruction variants within each dataset. Supplementary Sec. S3 provides model configura-tions and training and inference details. Following GREC [9], we use an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 to determine valid box matches. We implement greedy one-to-one matching between predicted and ground-truth boxes. Exact-set accuracy requires every target to be matched without additional predictions. We also report mean sample F1, which averages per-reference F1 scores to capture partial recovery. For no-target references, valid empty predictions score one and non-empty predic-tions score zero [9], so mean sample F1 equals no-target accuracy. Malformed or truncated outputs score zero and remain included in all comparisons for VLM baselines. Fol-lowing prior work [33], we report standard deviations esti-mated from 1,000 bootstrap resamples. We resample refer-ences with pairing across methods and instruction variants, using predictions from a single evaluation run with seed 0.

4.3. RQ2: Grounding Performance 

We evaluate whether VGE improves grounding across in-struction variants and varying numbers of targets on gRef-COCO, RoboRefIt and Ref-ZOM. We compare VGE with task-trained grounding experts, RECANTFormer, and zero-shot and LoRA-adapted Qwen3.5-9B and Groma. All task-specific training uses each dataset’s original instructions. As shown in Table 1(a), VGE achieves the highest context-augmented F1 across all five test splits, with a dif-ference of at most 1.2 percentage points between instruc-tion conditions within each split. Grounding DINO and LLMDet remain sensitive to added context even after the task-specific training. Qwen3.5-9B with LoRA performs better on original and paraphrased instructions on gRef-COCO Test B and Ref-ZOM, but struggles under context augmentation. On Ref-ZOM, for example, LoRA achieves 90.7% F1 on original instructions and 81.7% with added context, whereas VGE achieves 86.1% under both condi-tions. These results indicate that VGE maintains competi-tive grounding performance under added context, support-ing its use in settings where robotics manipulation instruc-tions are embedded in broader conversational context [24]. As shown in Table 1(b), the breakdown by number of targets shows the greatest improvements on robotic scenes. VGE achieves the highest F1 in all three target groups on both RoboRefIt splits. For multiple-target references, it reaches 96.0% and 81.3% on Test A and Test B, compared with 90.7% and 76.3% for LoRA-adapted Qwen3.5-9B. 5. Conclusion 

We introduced VGE, which integrates a frozen VLM with a trainable grounding expert through two interfaces. VGE uses multimodal context to guide object-query refinement and target selection while predicting bounding boxes di-rectly. Across three datasets, it achieves the highest context-augmented F1 among the evaluated methods, with at most 1.2 percentage points of variation across instruction condi-tions within each split. On RoboRefIt, it also leads across no-target, single-target and multiple-target cases, support-ing its effectiveness under both instruction variation and varying numbers of targets. VGE achieves these results with a 5.3–13 .0× inference speedup over the evaluated VLM baseline. A limitation of VGE is that freezing the VLM can leave room for further gains through task-specific adap-tation. Nevertheless, results across two datasets support training only the grounding expert and interfaces as a com-petitive default that avoids VLM parameter updates. S3. Implementation Details 

S3.1. Interface Implementation and Training 

VGE pairs the grounding expert’s six decoder layers, in depth order, with the VLM’s last six full-attention layers, excluding linear-attention and sliding-window-attention layers. At each paired VLM layer, keys are extracted af-ter native key normalisation and positional transformations, and values are taken from the native value projection. The trainable mappings f ℓK and f ℓV concatenate features across VLM heads, project them to the expert’s attention dimen-sion and reshape them into expert heads. Joint attention includes all non-padding image, text and special-token po-sitions. The adapted VLM keys and values share a single attention softmax and the expert’s native output projection with the expert keys and values. Each decoder layer uses an independent, unconstrained scalar gate αℓ, initialised to zero. The gated combination is applied after output projec-tion and replaces the native self-attention output, retaining the subsequent residual, normalisation and cross-attention operations. For text-conditioned instance scoring, HP contains fea-tures from the final VLM block before output normali-sation at non-padding text positions, including template and system tokens where present. We apply layer nor-malisation followed by the linear mapping fH , which projects these features to 256 dimensions with a bias and no activation. Each layer-normalised final object query qi attends to the projected features through a train-able nn.MultiheadAttention module with eight 32-dimensional heads, biased projections and zero dropout. Concatenating qi with the resulting context ci gives a 512-dimensional vector. The linear mapping fs converts this vector into a scalar correction to the native matching logit. Its weights and bias are initialised to zero, preserving the expert’s native scores at initialisation. The main configuration uses the public Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B and 

IDEA-Research/grounding-dino-base check-points. We freeze the VLM and train the complete grounding expert and both interfaces on the original in-structions using AdamW [21]. Peak learning rates are 10 −4

for the expert and 5 × 10 −4 for the interface parameters. S1 We use weight decay 0.01, β = (0 .9, 0.999) and ϵ = 10 −8.Training uses a global batch size of 32. S3.2. Input Preprocessing 

The grounding expert and VLM process the same image and instruction using their respective preprocessing pipelines. For Grounding DINO, RGB images are resized while pre-serving the aspect ratio, with a target shorter edge of 800 pixels and a maximum longer edge of 1,333 pixels. Images are then padded within each batch. Pixel values are rescaled by 1/255 and normalised using mean (0 .485 , 0.456 , 0.406) 

and standard deviation (0 .229 , 0.224 , 0.225) . Qwen uses its native image processor with minimum and maximum pixel counts of 65,536 and 1,048,576, respectively. We apply no random flips, crops or colour augmentation. The grounding expert accepts up to 512 text tokens, with longer sequences truncated from the left. The VLM input is limited to 8,192 tokens, including image and template to-kens. The main configuration uses no system prompt and disables thinking mode. Training uses original instructions, while evaluation uses the original, paraphrased or context-augmented instruction specified by the evaluation condi-tion. Assistant target outputs are excluded from the inputs used to compute expert predictions. 

S3.3. Baseline Configurations and Inference 

We evaluate Grounding DINO-base and LLMDet-base us-ing both public checkpoints and task-specific fine-tuning. Grounding DINO and the Hugging Face implementation of LLMDet score candidates using the maximum token-matching probability. The native LLMDet implementa-tion used for task-specific fine-tuning instead averages these probabilities over the referring expression. Each detector configuration is calibrated independently on original vali-dation instructions. The Qwen LoRA baseline uses coordinate-generation cross-entropy with all-linear adapters of rank 8, a scaling factor of 16, zero dropout and a learning rate of 5 × 10 −5.The base VLM parameters remain frozen. VGE’s adapta-tion variants use the same LoRA settings. Expert-gradient updates the adapters through the expert loss, whereas CE-adapted uses coordinate-generation cross-entropy with weight 1.0 and blocks expert-loss gradients into the VLM. Adapted-frozen keeps a previously trained LoRA adapter fixed during expert training. The grounding expert and both interfaces remain trainable in all VGE configurations. Confidence thresholds are selected to maximise mean sample F1 on original validation instructions and remain fixed across test splits, instruction variants and target counts within each dataset. VGE and task-trained Grounding DINO search thresholds range from 0.05 to 0.95 in incre-ments of 0.05, while native LLMDet uses a predefined grid between 0.01 and 0.90. The selected thresholds on gRe-fCOCO, RoboRefIt and Ref-ZOM are (0 .40 , 0.25 , 0.30) 

for VGE, (0 .45 , 0.35 , 0.30) for Grounding DINO and 

(0 .40 , 0.25 , 0.30) for LLMDet. Candidates meeting the threshold undergo hard non-maximum suppression at IoU 0.5, with at most 900 output boxes. Zero-shot Qwen and its LoRA baseline generate up to 1,024 tokens and share the same JSON parser. Outputs con-tain XYXY bounding boxes with coordinates normalised to 

[0 , 1000] , or [] when no target is present. The parser ac-cepts a single box, a bbox 2d object or a list of such ob-jects, optionally enclosed in a Markdown code fence. Ev-ery box must contain finite coordinates satisfying 0 ≤ x1 <x2 ≤ 1000 and 0 ≤ y1 < y 2 ≤ 1000 . Malformed out-puts, invalid boxes and generations truncated by the token limit are treated as failed predictions. Valid boxes are con-verted to pixel coordinates without further filtering or non-maximum suppression. 

S3.4. Evaluation Protocol 

All methods are evaluated on the same references within each test split. Failed outputs remain included and receive a score of zero. We evaluate original, paraphrased and context-augmented instructions separately. Combined re-sults weight the three instruction conditions equally, and re-sults by target count use the corresponding no-target, single-target or multiple-target references. Predicted and ground-truth boxes are matched greedily at an IoU threshold of 0.5. At each step, we select the re-maining pair with the highest IoU and remove both boxes from further matching. Matched pairs are true positives (T P ), unmatched predictions are false positives ( F P ), and unmatched targets are false negatives ( F N ). For references with at least one predicted or ground-truth box, sample F1 is

2T P + F P + F N . (S1) A valid empty prediction receives a score of one when the ground-truth set is empty. Malformed or truncated outputs receive zero regardless of the target count. On no-target references, mean sample F1 equals no-target accuracy. Reported standard deviations are calculated from 1,000 paired bootstrap resamples of reference IDs using seed 0. Each configura-tion is trained once with seed 0, so these standard devia-tions describe reference-level resampling variability rather than variation across training runs. 

S4. VLM and Grounding-Expert Choices 

In this experiment, we examine how VLM and grounding expert choices affect the grounding performance of VGE. Figure S1(a) compares frozen Qwen3.5-4B, Qwen3.5-9B and Gemma4-12B with Grounding DINO-base fixed. Panel (b) compares Grounding DINO-tiny and Grounding DINO-base with frozen Qwen3.5-9B fixed. For each con-figuration, we train the grounding expert and both interfaces on original instructions using the same training budget and learning rates. Each VLM retains its native visual prepro-cessing. We report mean sample F1 averaged equally over the three instruction conditions. VLM choice has a comparatively small effect across the four splits. Replacing Qwen3.5-9B with Qwen3.5-4B changes F1 by at most 2.09 percentage points, while re-placing it with Gemma4-12B changes F1 by at most 0.64 percentage points. These results show that VGE maintains similar performance with either of the two tested VLM fam-ilies after training the expert and interfaces. The smaller Qwen3.5-4B also retains much of the performance of the main configuration. Grounding-expert choice has a larger and domain-dependent effect. Relative to Grounding DINO-base, Grounding DINO-tiny improves F1 by 2.30–3.01 percent-age points on gRefCOCO but reduces it by 9.00–10.87 points on RoboRefIt. The larger expert therefore performs better on robotic scenes but is not uniformly preferable across the evaluated datasets.
