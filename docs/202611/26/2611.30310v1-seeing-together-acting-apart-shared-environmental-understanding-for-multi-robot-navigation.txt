Abstract 

Multi-robot embodied systems are typically designed to collaborate by coordinating actions, sharing tra-jectories, or learning joint policies. We ask a complementary question: can collaboration emerge before decision-making, by first building a shared understanding of the environment? We introduce Shared Envi-ronmental Understanding , a policy-agnostic representation that aggregates partial egocentric observations from multiple robots into a shared latent world model. This shifts collaboration from the policy space to the representation space, enabling decentralized decision-making with shared environmental context. To this end, we propose Shared Environment Encoding and Retrieval (SEER), a framework that organizes multi-robot observations into a shared memory, retrieves relevant context via spatio-temporal similarity, and encodes it using a predictive latent world model. The resulting representations are injected into downstream navigation agents as tokens. Experimental results demonstrate that SEER captures action-relevant environ-mental structure and consistently improves navigation performance across both specialized VLN agents and general vision-language model agents, while preserving single-agent capabilities. 

1 Introduction 

Multi-robot embodied systems are commonly expected to collaborate by coordinating their behaviors: agents may communicate, share experience, divide tasks, avoid conflicts, or jointly decide what to do next. This question naturally extends classic embodied navigation and vision-and-language navigation settings, where an agent must ground observations and instructions into actions in realistic environments [1, 2]. It has also motivated a large body of work on multi-robot coordination, communication, task allocation, and multi-agent decision making [3–6], as well as recent embodied multi-agent systems that share semantic maps, messages, frontier assignments, action plans, or generated rollouts for cooperative navigation and planning [7–9]. However, most existing approaches frame collaboration at the level of agent-centric traces, such as sharing actions, trajectories, maps, or intermediate decisions. While effective, these methods tightly couple collaboration with policy design and communication protocols. This raises a fundamental question: can collaboration emerge from shared perception before shared decision-making? In other words, instead of exchanging what agents do , can they first share what they understand about the environment? In realistic settings, different robots naturally observe complementary parts of the same environment from diverse viewpoints and at different times. Aggregating these partial observations can yield a richer representation of the environment than any single agent can obtain alone. Crucially, such a representation can serve as a policy-agnostic interface: it can be shared across agents while allowing each agent to make decisions independently using its own policy. We therefore propose to shift collaboration from policy space to representation space. We introduce Shared Environmental Understanding , a policy-agnostic representation that aggregates multi-robot observations into a shared latent description of the environment. This shared representation provides a common environmental context, while allowing each agent to act independently with its own policy. 2 Related Works 

Multi-Robot Coordination. Multi-robot coordination covers task allocation, communication, distributed planning, heterogeneous cooperation, distributed optimization, coordinated exploration, mapping, and aerial/multi-UAV swarms [3, 4, 10–13]. Learning-based approaches study inter-agent communication and decision making through differentiable communication and MARL [5, 6, 14–16], while recent embodied systems extend coordination to cooperative visual-semantic navigation, ambiguous instruction resolution, heterogeneous collaboration, LLM/VLM-driven planning, and embodied multi-agent benchmarks [7– 9, 17–20]. Related ideas also appear in cooperative driving/perception, city-scale simulation, and multi-view-consistent generation for urban or driving worlds [21–30]. World Models for Embodied Agents. 

Embodied world modeling builds on large-scale datasets, simulators, and 3D language-grounding bench-marks, including Matterport3D, ScanNet, Habitat, HM3D-Sem, EmbodiedScan, EmbodiedQA, ScanRefer, ReferIt3D, ScanQA, SQA3D, OpenEQA, 3D-LLM, and SIG3D [31–42]. World models have evolved from latent dynamics and imagination-based control [43–46] to interactive/generative prediction of future observations, occupancy, and navigation rollouts [47–51]; representation-predictive methods such as V-JEPA and V-JEPA 2 further highlight latent-feature prediction over pixel reconstruction [52, 53]. Recent embodied models also encode explicit 3D/4D structure or navigation geometry and connect perception with language and action [54–61]. Vision-and-Language Navigation. VLN grounds natural-language instructions into sequential navigation actions. Early work established standard benchmarks and agents, including speaker-follower training, cross-modal matching, self-monitoring, regretful navigation, and back-translation [1, 62–66]. The field later expanded to multilingual, dialog, object-centric, continuous, and outdoor settings [67–72], and recent agents use large-scale pretraining, transformers, map-centric or history-aware representations, future-view prediction, VLM/LLM reasoning, map-guided planning, causal learning, aerial VLN, long-horizon and streaming settings, physical deployment, semantic-map memory, and world-model-based navigation [2, 73–93]. 3.3 Integration with Downstream Navigation 

SEER is designed as an environmental understanding layer. After SEER-World learns a shared latent world representation zSEU, it from multi-robot observations, we use it to condition downstream navigation agents without replacing their original decision process. This design allows each robot to retain its own policy while receiving additional shared environmental context. Let hit denote the local navigation state of robot i, including its current observation, visual-language history, and model-specific memory. A downstream navigation agent predicts actions from its local state and instruction ait ∼ πi(a | hit, xi), while our SEER-augmented agent additionally conditions on SEER tokens as ait ∼ πi(a | hit, xi, Uit). Uit denotes the SEER conditioning tokens provided to robot i. SEER therefore changes what the agent knows about the shared environment, but does not change the agent’s action space, controller, or decision interface. In our implementation, Uit is constructed by querying the frozen SEER-World model with the retrieved multi-robot context and converting the resulting latent predictions into continuous soft tokens. These tokens are inserted immediately before downstream action prediction, acting as world-conditioning prompts. We use the same interface for both Qwen 3.5 4B [101] and StreamVLN [83], demonstrating that SEER can serve as a reusable upstream representation for different types of navigation agents. 4.3 Representation-Level Validation: Action-Relevant Latent Dynamics 

We introduce a representation-level inverse dynamics probe to test whether SEER encodes action-relevant environmental structure. Since the latent state is not directly interpretable, we freeze the learned forward latent dynamics and, for each transition, enumerate candidate actions. The action whose predicted next latent is closest to the true next latent is selected. High recovery accuracy therefore indicates that the latent space preserves how different navigation actions transform the world state, rather than merely encoding static appearance. Table 3 shows that the probe is non-trivial: V-JEPA2 achieves reasonable accuracy, indicating that generic predictive video features capture motion cues, but still lags behind SEER. Ours w/o Retrieval 

( replaced by uniform random sampling ) already performs well, suggesting that SEER-World learns discriminative latent dynamics via multi-view prediction. SEER Retrieval further improves both single-and dual-agent settings, isolating the benefit of shared retrieval: relevant observations from the anony-mous SEER Pool provide cleaner cross-robot context than the world model alone. Importantly, LIDA is a held-out counterfactual action recovery test across diverse spatial configurations, not a direct training-loss measure, making high scores unlikely to stem only from action bias, visual repetition, or retrieval leakage.

5 Conclusion 

This paper argues for a shift in how multi-robot navigation should be understood. The essence of collaboration is not merely that multiple robots move at the same time, avoid each other, or exchange action histories; it is that each robot can make the world more legible for the others. By formulating multi-robot VLN around shared environmental understanding, we move the problem from surface-level coordination to a deeper question of collective scene comprehension. Complementary embodied experience can be transformed into shared knowledge that improves navigation beyond isolated single-agent perception. The real-robot study further shows that this idea is not confined to benchmark artifacts. We believe this representation-centric view offers a cleaner, more scalable, and more general foundation for embodied multi-agent systems.

Episode definition. Each paired episode combines two original R2R episodes, assigned to robots A and B. Each robot retains its own instruction, start state, goal, and reference trajectory. Training and evaluation splits. Training pairs are sampled within scenes from the R2R train split. Duplicate pairs are removed with A/B swaps treated as equivalent, and identical or reversed-identical paths are filtered out. Evaluation pairs are constructed from R2R val-unseen and grouped into four configurations: 

Different scenes. The robots navigate in different scenes, scene A̸ = scene B. This configuration measures navigation when the shared pool contains observations from unrelated environments. 

Same scene without intersection. The robots share a scene, but their effective paths do not intersect. This configuration provides scene-level context with spatially separated routes. 

Same scene with intersection. The robots share a scene and their effective paths intersect. This configuration provides opportunities to reuse observations of common local regions. 

Overlapped trajectories. The effective paths are identical, reversed-identical, or related by a continuous sub-trajectory. Paired episodes have different instruction texts. This configuration corresponds to the same/sub-trajectory setting and provides highly overlapping environmental context. 

Geometric checks and deduplication. Path intersection is computed between polyline segments on the horizontal XZ plane. A projected intersection is accepted only when the corresponding vertical separation satisfies |yA − yB| ≤ ϵfloor , which separates crossings on different floors. We remove duplicate episode pairs and repeated geometric path pairs; identical and reversed-identical paths are canonicalized and filtered when they are outside the target category. Sampling uses a fixed random seed, and path coordinates are rounded before canonicalization to reduce floating-point instability. Pair construction preserves the R2R train/val-unseen split.

> A.5 Experimental Setup

Simulation and agents. Single-robot navigation is evaluated on the original R2R-CE and RxR-CE Val-Unseen episodes in Habitat [33]. Multi-robot navigation uses the four paired R2R val-unseen configurations in Appendix A.4. We integrate SEER with StreamVLN [83] and Qwen3.5-4B [101], using the soft-token interface in Appendix A.2. 

Retrieval settings. All main experiments use a FIFO pool of 1,024 observation records and a total context budget of K = 8 observations. Retrieval uses weighted sampling without replacement, with weights renormalized over the remaining records after each draw. The temporal and spatial coefficients are γT = γS = 1, and the weight-floor parameter is ϵ = 0.1. These settings are shared across both down-stream agents and all four paired-agent configurations. When cross-robot observations are unavailable, recent self-history supplies the remaining context. 

Navigation metrics. Navigation Error (NE) is the final geodesic distance to the goal. Success Rate (SR) measures the fraction of episodes ending within 3 m of the goal, while Oracle Success Rate (OSR) uses the closest point along the executed trajectory. Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) additionally accounts for path efficiency, and normalized Dynamic Time Warping (nDTW) measures similarity to the reference trajectory. For paired episodes, ISR averages individual goal-reaching outcomes, and CSR counts episodes in which both robots succeed, as defined in the main text. 

Sharing baselines. Table 4 compares no sharing, history sharing, action sharing, observation sharing, spatial-description sharing, and SEER using Qwen3.5-4B. The spatial-description baseline uses InternVL3-8B [119] as an additional expert. These comparisons evaluate different shared inputs within the same downstream navigation interface.  

> A.6 Real-World Deployment

Platforms and server. We deploy SEER on a wheeled AgileX LiMO Pro and a quadruped Unitree Go2 in indoor corridors and rooms. LiMO Pro is equipped with LiDAR and an Intel RealSense D435 camera [120]; Go2 provides an onboard wide-angle camera. SEER consumes egocentric RGB observations from both platforms. A remote workstation with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 runs the shared pool, retrieval, SEER-World, and downstream navigation models. Robot clients handle image capture and physical action execution, as shown in Figure 8. 

Observation and action exchange. At each decision step, the client sends a JPEG-compressed RGB image in a multipart HTTP POST request. The server updates the pool, retrieves context, constructs SEER tokens, and evaluates the downstream agent for that robot. Both StreamVLN and Qwen3.5-4B use the discrete action interface {FORWARD , L EFT , R IGHT , S TOP }, encoded as {1, 2, 3, 0 }. The server returns a JSON action list, which the client executes sequentially before requesting the next prediction. SSH is used to start or stop clients and reset episodes.

Single-agent navigation. Table 1 reports both downstream agents with and without SEER on R2R-CE and RxR-CE. On R2R-CE, SR increases from 56.9% to 58.1% for StreamVLN and from 40.3% to 44.9% for Qwen3.5-4B. On RxR-CE, SR increases from 52.9% to 54.3% and from 39.7% to 42.4%, respectively. The corresponding SPL results also improve. These results show that shared-world conditioning remains useful when the context is formed from a single robot’s observations. 

Paired-agent navigation. Table 2 reports higher ISR and CSR with SEER in all four configurations. For example, in the same-scene intersecting setting, StreamVLN’s CSR increases from 31.2% to 35.9%, while Qwen3.5-4B’s CSR increases from 18.1% to 23.9%. The corresponding gains are 4.7 and 5.8 percentage points. Different-scene pairs provide a control with unrelated cross-robot observations, while intersecting and overlapped routes provide shared local evidence. ISR summarizes individual success across robots, and CSR captures the joint outcome of each pair.
