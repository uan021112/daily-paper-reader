Abstract
Shape decomposition represents 3D objects as a compact
set of geometric primitives for interpretable modeling and
simulation. However, existing decomposition methods are
trained primarily for geometric fidelity, without explicit con-
straints on physical plausibility, and can therefore produce
disconnected parts or physically unstable reconstructions.
We present PhysDec, an agentic framework that draws on
a pretrained vision-language model’s semantic understand-
ing and commonsense reasoning to predict material-aware
primitive decompositions from a few images. Within this
framework, PhysDec-Loop is an agentic pipeline that rea-
sons about part structure and materials, using a reference 3D
point cloud for geometric feedback and physical verifiers to
guide iterative correction. Through self-evolution, we further
develop another agentic pipeline, PhysDec-Evolved, which
learns from prior corrections to predict primitive decomposi-
tions from images without point-cloud feedback or a correc-
tion loop at inference. Experiments show that PhysDec-Loop
improves geometric fidelity, physical plausibility and visual
quality over geometry-focused baselines, while PhysDec-
Evolved approaches its performance at substantially lower
inference cost.
1. Introduction
Shape decomposition represents a complex object with a
small set of interpretable geometric parts, enabling compact
storage, editing and simulation. Recent learned methods pre-
dict these representations from images or 3D observations
using cuboids, superquadrics and autoregressive primitive
representations [10, 31, 46, 58, 61]. However, their training
objectives emphasize geometric fidelity without explicitly

enforcing physical plausibility. As shown in Fig. 1, their
predictions can resemble the target shape yet contain discon-
nected parts or become unstable in simulation.
A part-based representation should explain both an ob-
ject’s shape and its physical behavior [24]. Parts must con-
nect and provide the support needed to keep the object stable,
while their geometry and materials determine mass distri-
bution. For example, a chair leg may closely match the
observed surface yet fail to support the seat if their con-
nection is missing. Therefore, we need to consider part
structure, material properties and physical stability together
with geometric fidelity.
Pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) provide se-
mantic understanding and commonsense knowledge for rea-
soning about these coupled structural and material require-
ments. Recent work has applied these capabilities to 3D
scene creation and image-based reconstruction, using code
generation, execution and visual feedback [13, 14, 60]. Such
workflows can incorporate explicit task constraints through
instructions and tools, then revise their outputs in response
to execution feedback [35]. These capabilities motivate us
to couple VLM reasoning with geometric tools and physical
verifiers, so that primitive decomposition is guided by both
geometric fidelity and physical plausibility.
We present PhysDec, an agentic framework for phys-
ically grounded shape decomposition from images. We
equip a pretrained VLM with tools for generating and ren-
dering material-aware primitive decompositions. An agentic
pipeline, PhysDec-Loop, uses a reference 3D point cloud
to measure geometric errors and physical verifiers to assess
physical plausibility. Guided by this feedback, the agent iter-
atively revises part structure and material choices to satisfy
geometric and physical constraints.
Further, we develop another agentic pipeline, PhysDec-
Evolved, from PhysDec-Loop to reuse its correction experi-
ence. We first run PhysDec-Loop on training objects with
known 3D shapes, collecting verified revisions and success-
ful decompositions. Self-evolution uses this experience to
improve the instructions [1] and build a library of exam-
ples and reusable insights [66]. PhysDec-Evolved predicts
a decomposition directly from images, without point-cloud
feedback or an online correction loop.
Experiments show that PhysDec-Loop improves geomet-
ric fidelity, physical plausibility and visual quality over
geometry-focused baselines. Self-evolution eliminates re-
peated correction, substantially reducing inference cost while
retaining quality comparable to PhysDec-Loop. Our contri-
butions are:
• PhysDec-Loop, an agentic pipeline that couples VLM
reasoning with geometric tools and physical verifiers to
jointly refine part structure and materials, yielding faithful
decompositions whose parts remain connected and stable
under simulation.
• PhysDec-Evolved, which combines prompts iteratively
evolved using verifier feedback with a retrieval library
built from PhysDec-Loop’s verified refinements. This self-
evolution process removes online correction and reduces
inference cost without updating VLM weights.
• Experiments showing that our method improves geomet-
ric fidelity, physical plausibility and visual quality over
geometry-focused baselines, with applications to scene
reconstruction and robot manipulation.
2. Related Work
Shape decomposition and programs.
Cuboid and su-
perquadric decompositions provide compact geometric rep-
resentations [3, 4, 31, 46]. Hierarchical prediction [32] and
DPA-Net [61] extend decomposition to single and sparse-
view images. EMS, Marching-Primitives and Light-SQ im-
prove geometric fitting [22, 23, 51]; SweepNet and Super-
Frusta expand primitive expressiveness [12, 67]; SuperDec
and SuperFlex learn compact decompositions [10, 43]. Kob-
sik et al. [18] use masks from pretrained visual models to
guide fitting. Complementing these geometric representa-
tions, CSGNet and ShapeAssembly encode Boolean com-
position and attachment relationships [15, 37], while Prim-
itiveAnything learns autoregressive assembly from human-
crafted decompositions [58]. ShapeMOD discovers reusable
macros and ShapeLib designs semantic modeling functions
with LLMs [16, 17]. Physical Primitive Decomposition
jointly estimates geometric and physical attributes [24],
while Gen2Physics recovers material-based mesh compo-
nents with assigned densities [7]. SNAP3D uses simulation
feedback to refine connections between generated parts [45].
PhysDec brings geometric and physical verification into a
shared refinement loop for material-aware primitive decom-
position.
Agentic 3D modeling and experience reuse.
SceneCraft
and Holodeck combine language-based planning with code
generation or asset layout optimization [14, 56]. VIGA,
SEIG, aDSL and AgentSTAR use executable representations
and revision for reconstruction, structured modeling and
tracking [13, 28, 50, 60], with related benchmarks cover-
ing scene authoring and dynamic inverse graphics [38, 59].
HARMONY combines hierarchical agentic reasoning with
point-cloud-guided geometric refinement for monocular
scene reconstruction [42]. Physical requirements extend
this modeling problem beyond appearance: Make It Stand
optimizes balance [34], PhyScene constrains scene lay-
outs [55], and SceneSmith, Articulate-Anything, CoDim-
Recon and WorldAgent target simulation-ready scenes and
assets [20, 33, 48, 53]. PhysAg3D and DiagGen use phys-
ical tools and simulation feedback to refine interactive and
deformable assets [6, 9]. BrickWright and BrickBench ad-
dress buildability [19, 35], while FunCAD-Harness tests

mechanism functionality [65].
Self-Refine and Reflexion use feedback and reflection [27,
39]; ExpeL and Voyager retain transferable experience [49,
66]. GEPA evolves prompts and Agentic Context Engineer-
ing maintains structured playbooks [1, 64]. Text2Sim re-
trieves distilled simulation expertise during execution-based
repair [54]. SceneEvolve evolves a 3D reconstruction har-
ness with fixed model weights, while PhySGA distills phys-
ical trial-and-error into reusable skills [26, 57]. PhysDec
reuses corrections verified by geometric and physical checks
as improved prompts, examples and insights. This experi-
ence enables direct primitive prediction at inference with
fixed VLM weights.
3. Method
Given one to four images, PhysDec predicts a compact set of
primitives, each with geometry, pose and material attributes.
PhysDec-Loop revises proposals using a reference 3D point
cloud for geometric feedback, together with physical checks
and visual review. We run this loop on training objects and
reuse its correction experience to improve prompts and build
a retrieval library. The resulting PhysDec-Evolved uses these
prompts and retrieved experience to predict decompositions
directly from images, without online verification or correc-
tion.

3.2. The self-correction loop
PhysDec-Loop follows four stages (Fig. 4). Analyse iden-
tifies the object’s category, support type, symmetry, pro-
portions and parts with their materials. Propose outputs a
primitive decomposition in JSON format. Verify evaluates
the proposal using geometric checks, physical checks and
a separate VLM review (Sec. 3.3). Critique examines the
images and verifier outputs to identify errors and provide
revision guidance. Propose then uses this feedback to gener-
ate an updated decomposition, which is verified again. We
repeat this cycle for up to T=3 correction rounds, with op-
tional early stopping (see supplementary material for full
prompts and example feedback).

3.3. Verifiers & Critique
Verification combines geometric measurements, physical
checks and visual review to assess each proposal. We mea-
sure Chamfer distance between sampled primitive surfaces
and the reference 3D point cloud, and render reference points
colored by their distance to the predicted surface to expose
missing regions and local shape errors. Physical checks fol-
low the support type inferred during Analyse (Fig. 5), since
objects that stand, rest on a surface, attach to a wall or hang
require different tests. With material-dependent part masses,
MuJoCo [44] tests stability in standing or resting poses and
balance under suspension, recording motion and tilt to diag-
nose unstable or unsupported parts. Wall-mounted objects
are checked geometrically for a rear contact face. Suspen-
sion also provides a balance test for airborne and floating
objects.
A separate VLM compares proposal renders with the in-
put images, assessing proportions, part placement and com-
pleteness, individual part shape, object identity, structural
coherence and material plausibility. It provides an overall
visual score together with criterion-level explanations that
identify the relevant parts and views (see supplementary
material for the scoring rubric). Critique combines this re-
view with geometric errors, contact and support diagnostics,
rendered error maps and simulation snapshots to identify
failures and provide part-specific revision guidance to Pro-
pose.
3.4. Self-evolution
Self-evolution converts experience from PhysDec-Loop into
reusable guidance for PhysDec-Evolved (Fig. 6).
Both
use the same VLM and primitive representation; PhysDec-
Evolved replaces per-object correction with evolved prompts
and a retrieval library. To construct this guidance offline,
we run PhysDec-Loop on training objects with reference
geometry and record the decompositions and feedback from
successive rounds, keeping VLM weights fixed. When a
revision improves Sr, we retain the before-and-after pair and
describe the changes that led to the improvement.
The library stores this experience in three forms. Exem-
plars are physically valid decompositions with good geomet-
ric fit, providing concrete examples of how to decompose an
object. Insights summarize successful corrections as general
advice: when a change is useful, what to change and why it

Alongside the library, we improve the instructions that
guide part structure and primitive construction. We use
GEPA [1] to repeat a simple process: predict decomposi-
tions for training objects, inspect their verifier feedback, and
ask the VLM to rewrite the instructions to address recurring
errors. Each candidate prompt produces decompositions
without online correction. We evaluate these predictions
using the same scoring rule and verifiers as PhysDec-Loop
(Secs. 3.2 and 3.3), and retain changes that improve perfor-
mance on validation objects. The coordinate frame, primitive
definitions, material menu and output schema remain fixed
(see supplementary material for prompt blocks before and
after evolution).
At deployment, PhysDec-Evolved uses Analyse to iden-
tify the object’s parts and category in one VLM call. We
filter exemplars by this category and use DINOv2 [30] image
similarity to retrieve a relevant example, together with appli-
cable insights and material priors. Propose then predicts the
decomposition in a second VLM call using the improved in-
structions and retrieved context. The Verify–Critique loop
is omitted: no point-cloud feedback, proposal rendering or
simulation is required at deployment.
4. Experiments
We evaluate PhysDec against baseline methods on ShapeNet
objects (Secs. 4.2 and 4.3), partial observations from ASE
(Sec. 4.4) and Replica scene scans (Sec. 4.5), and test robot
manipulation of the results (Sec. 4.6). We also conduct
ablation studies to assess the contribution of each component,
and a user study to validate the VLM score (Sec. 4.7).
4.1. Setup
Comparisons and metrics.
We compare against SQ [31],
EMS [22], Marching-Primitives [23], SuperDec [10] and
SuperFlex [43]. We report Chamfer-L1 (×100), primitive
count, physical-check pass rate and VLM visual score (0–
100). For a fairer geometric comparison with baselines
that use point clouds or SDFs, we refine the scales and
poses of parts predicted from images against reference point
clouds before Chamfer evaluation (see supplementary mate-
rial for details). Physical validity requires the decomposed
object to remain connected and pass its support-specific
check (Sec. 3.3). Claude Opus 5.5 performs Analyse, Pro-
pose and Critique in PhysDec-Loop, Analyse and Pro-
pose in PhysDec-Evolved, and prompt rewriting during self-
evolution. Claude Sonnet 5.5 provides the VLM review
in Verify and the reported visual scores; GPT-6.1 Sol pro-
vides an additional independent visual score in Tab. 1. Mu-
JoCo [44] performs the physical simulations.
4.2. PhysDec-Loop
We evaluate on 585 ShapeNet [5] objects, 45 from each of
the 13 categories used by SuperDec and SuperFlex [10, 43].
We use the data preprocessed by Occupancy Networks [29].
Our agent receives four 137×137 RGB images and their
camera parameters from this dataset. The baselines use
the corresponding ground-truth 3D point clouds as input
(converted to an SDF for Marching-Primitives [23]). Each
object’s support type is labelled once by a VLM from its
four views; every method is checked against the same labels.

4.6. Robot manipulation
We evaluate pick-and-place manipulation with a KUKA LBR
iiwa 14 and an Allegro hand in MuJoCo [62], using scenes
from Sec. 4.5 and ShapeNet objects from Sec. 4.2. All
methods use the same superquadric grasp planner [47], with
matched object masses. PhysDec supports successful scene
manipulation (Fig. 12), while baseline failures include dis-
connected parts, missing grasps and collisions. On a ground

5. Conclusion
PhysDec combines VLM reasoning with geometric tools
and physical verifiers for physically grounded shape de-
composition from a few images. PhysDec-Loop improves
geometric fidelity, physical plausibility and visual quality,
while PhysDec-Evolved reuses verified corrections through
evolved prompts and retrieval to approach this quality with
substantially fewer model calls and without updating VLM
weights. Experiments span complete objects, partial obser-
vations and scanned scenes.
Limitations.
PhysDec currently focuses on objects com-
posed of rigid parts, without modeling joint motion or ma-
terial deformation. Our physical checks assess connectivity
and stability under prescribed support conditions, includ-
ing standing, wall attachment and suspension; passing these
checks does not establish robustness to arbitrary external
forces or interactions. We primarily use closed-source VLMs.
Although the open-source Qwen configuration maintains
high physical validity, it trails these models in geometric
fidelity and visual quality (Tab. 5). Extending the repre-
sentation to articulated and deformable objects, broadening
physical tests, and improving open-source models for this
task are promising directions for future work.

Prompt S1. System prompt, shared by Analyse, Propose and Critique. The extended primitives are on, as in all reported runs.
You decompose objects into a small set of superquadric primitives, the representation used by SuperDec (Fedele et al., ICCV
2025). You see one photo or rendering of an object and infer its full 3D shape as primitives.
# Canonical object frame
All coordinates are in the object's own frame, never the camera's.
- +Z is UP (the direction the object's top faces).
- +X is FRONT (the direction the object faces; for a chair, where a sitter's
knees point; for a car, the windshield side; for a sofa, the open side).
- +Y is the object's LEFT. Right-handed: X cross Y = Z.
# Scale and placement
Work at roughly unit scale: centre the object near the origin and let its
longest dimension span about 1.0, so coordinates land in [-0.5, 0.5]. Only the
RELATIVE proportions of your parts matter -- the pipeline recentres your output
and rescales it into a sphere of radius 0.5 (SuperDec's normalisation) before
rendering, so a uniformly-too-small or off-centre answer costs you nothing.
Getting one part's size wrong RELATIVE to the others does cost you.
12


# A superquadric
Each primitive has 11 numbers:
- "size": [sx, sy, sz] -- SEMI-axes, i.e. HALF-extents along the primitive's own
local X/Y/Z before rotation. A plank 0.40 long, 0.30 wide and 0.05 thick is
size [0.20, 0.15, 0.025]. Halve every dimension you measure. This is the most
common mistake by a wide margin.
- "shape": [e1, e2] -- roundness exponents in [0.1, 1.9]. Both are defined
about the primitive's OWN LOCAL AXES, before rotation.
e2 shapes the cross-section in the primitive's local XY plane:
0.1 square, 0.5 rounded-square, 1.0 circular, >1.2 pinched/concave.
e1 shapes the profile along the primitive's local Z:
0.1 flat top and bottom with straight sides, 0.5 softly rounded edges,
1.0 elliptical, >1.2 tapering to a point at each end.
So: [0.1, 0.1] cuboid; [0.1, 1.0] cylinder; [1.0, 1.0] ellipsoid;
[0.3, 0.3] rounded box; [1.4, 1.0] a spindle / double cone.
Prefer exponents near 0.1-0.3 for man-made flat-sided parts.
Watch which exponent owns the outline you care about. For a flat blade --
a tail fin, a lamp's upright, a chair's splat -- the shape you actually see
is the one in the chord-by-height plane, and that is governed by e1, not e2.
A fin at [0.15, 0.6] keeps full chord all the way to the tip; the same fin at
[0.9, 0.6] tapers to about a third of its chord. Changing e2 there only
thickens or thins the aerofoil section and does nothing to the profile.
- "rotation_deg": [rx, ry, rz] -- intrinsic X-Y-Z Euler angles in degrees,
right-handed, applied about the primitive's own centre before translating it.
Worked examples, so you get the signs right:
a chair backrest reclined 15 deg away from the sitter is [0, -15, 0];
a wing swept 25 deg aft on the object's LEFT (+Y) side is [0, 0, 25];
a tube whose axis must run along the object's X (a fuselage, an axle, a
horizontal rail) is [0, 90, 0] TOGETHER WITH size [r, r, half_length].
That last case matters, and it is the easiest way to wreck an answer. A tube
is always built along its local Z, so its length always goes in size[2], and
the rotation is what swings that axis onto the object's X. Do not ALSO put
the length in size[0]: [0.48, 0.06, 0.06] with ry=90 is a long part rotated
until it points straight DOWN, which is how a fuselage ends up standing on
its nose. Write [0.06, 0.06, 0.48] with ry=90 instead. The same goes for
ry=-90 and for rx=+-90 about the object's Y.
Leave [0, 0, 0] only when the part's local axes really do line up with the
object's, which is true of most boxy parts.
- "position": [x, y, z] -- the primitive's CENTRE in the object frame.
- "name": a short semantic label, e.g. "seat", "front_left_leg", "left_wing".
# Shape families (optional "kind")
Barr defines four superquadric families. Omit "kind" and you get the default,
which is the right answer for the large majority of parts. Reach for another
family only when the default genuinely cannot express the part.
- "kind": "ellipsoid" (default). Everything above applies to it. Solid, convex,
no hole. Use it unless one of the cases below is clearly true.
- "kind": "toroid" -- adds "hole": a number from 1 to 8. A ring, a tube open
end to end, a hollow shell seen through. USE THIS WHEN THE PART HAS A HOLE
YOU COULD PUT YOUR HAND THROUGH: a boat hull's open cockpit, a mug or basket
interior, a wheel rim, a handle, an armrest loop, the open frame of a chair
back. hole = 1 pinches the opening shut, 2 gives an opening about a third of
the width, 4 makes the wall thin and the opening wide. The hole always runs
along the primitive's local Z, so rotate it the same way you would a tube.
- "kind": "hyperboloid1" -- adds "waist": 0.05 to 0.95, the middle radius as a
fraction of the end radius. Narrow in the middle, flared at both ends, along
local Z. Use for a shape whose sides curve INWARD: a table pedestal, a wine
glass stem, an hourglass, a spool, a waisted vase. waist 0.8 is a slight
pinch, 0.3 is a pronounced hourglass. Note an ellipsoid with e1 > 1.2 tapers
to points at the ends, which is the opposite shape; do not confuse them.
- "kind": "hyperboloid2" -- also takes "waist". Two separate flared caps facing
away from each other. This is almost never a single object part. Do not use
it unless the part really is two disjoint opposed shells.
Two rules that do not change between families. "size" is always the half-extent
of the part's bounding box, so a ring 0.40 across and 0.10 tall is still
size [0.20, 0.20, 0.05]. And e1/e2 still control roundness, so a toroid with
e2 = 0.2 is a rectangular ring rather than a round one.
13


Do not reach for an exotic family to win detail. A hole that you could not put
a finger through is not a hole, it is a dent, and a plain ellipsoid describes
it better and more cheaply.
# Tapering, bending, tubes and rings (optional)
Four extras for parts a plain superquadric fits badly. Leave them out and the
part is exactly as before.
- "taper": [t1, t2], each from -0.9 to 0.9. The part narrows or widens
linearly along its "deform_axis" (local z unless you say otherwise). At the
+end of that axis the other two half-extents are multiplied by (1 + t), at
the -end by (1 - t); "size" is the size at the middle. t1 acts on the first
of the other two local axes and t2 on the second, in x, y, z order -- for the
default z axis, t1 on x and t2 on y. A lamp shade 0.32 across at the bottom
and 0.20 at the top is size [0.13, 0.13, h] with taper [-0.23, -0.23]. A leg
that thins toward the floor has a POSITIVE taper, because its wide end is
the +z end. Use it for shades, tapered legs, a car's nose, a boat's bow, a
backrest that widens upward.
- "bend_deg": 0 to 180, with "bend_dir_deg". The deform axis curves into a
circular arc that turns through bend_deg over the part's whole length, and
both ends swing toward the side bend_dir_deg names: an angle in the plane of
the other two local axes, from the first toward the second (for the default
z axis, 0 is toward local +x and 90 toward local +y). Use it for a curved
bench seat or backrest, a bowed arm, a crescent, a banana-shaped part.
- "deform_axis": "x", "y" or "z" (default "z"): the local axis taper and bend
act along. Use the part's long axis, so a long horizontal seat with size
[0.10, 0.50, 0.03] that curves around a sitter has "deform_axis": "y".
- A tube: "kind": "tube" with "spine", four points [x, y, z] in the object
frame -- the start, two control points and the end of a smooth curve (a
cubic Bezier: it passes through the first and last points and is pulled
toward the middle two; four points on a straight line make a straight
tube). "profile": [a, b], the half-thickness of its cross-section (a
measured upward, or along x for an upright tube; b across), and
"roundness" from 0.1 to 1.9 (1.0 round, 0.1 square). An optional "taper":
[t] scales the cross-section by (1 + t) at the end and (1 - t) at the
start. A tube takes no "size", "shape", "rotation_deg" or "position". Use
it for a curved or bent slender part -- a lamp's arm, a bent-tube chair
frame, a handle, a curved rail or armrest, a cable -- as ONE primitive
instead of a chain of short ones, so it cannot come apart at the joints.
For a straight slender part a normal primitive is still the simpler answer.
- "repeat": {"count": n, "center": [x, y]} on any part, tubes included: n
identical copies spaced evenly around the vertical axis through (x, y) in
the object frame (default [0, 0]). Write copy 1 exactly where it sits; copy
k is copy 1 turned by (k - 1) x 360/n degrees about that axis. The whole ring
counts as ONE part toward the budget. Use it for parts repeated around a
centre -- the arms and candles of a chandelier, the five legs of an office
chair's base, the blades of a ceiling fan, the feet of a pedestal -- and
count them in the photos rather than writing fewer to save parts. Copies
that are not evenly spaced, or not around a vertical axis, are written out
one by one as usual.
# Symmetry
Most objects are bilaterally symmetric about the XZ plane. To mirror a part
from the object's left to its right:
negate position y; negate rotation rx and rz; KEEP rotation ry; keep size and
shape identical.
Negating y alone is wrong for any rotated part -- it would turn a wing swept
aft into one swept forward.

# Objective
[block propose.construction starts]
Reproduce the object's geometry with AS FEW primitives as possible; SuperDec
optimises exactly this accuracy/compactness trade-off. Concretely:
- One primitive per functional part. Do not tile a flat surface with several
boxes when one flatter box will do.
- Do not stack primitives redundantly on the same volume. Interpenetration
AT JOINTS is expected and not penalised: a wing root belongs inside the
fuselage, a leg's top belongs inside the seat. Overlap two primitives only
where the real parts actually meet.
- DO represent parts that are hidden or occluded in the photo. You are
reconstructing the object, not the visible pixels: a chair photographed from
the front still has four legs. But do not add parts the object does not have
-- an airborne aircraft with the gear up has no landing gear.
- Do not fight a taper or a curve with exponents -- raising e1 rounds off both
ends and narrows the waist. Give a tapered part a "taper" and a curved one a
"bend_deg", or make a curved slender part a tube.
- Where a moulded shell flows continuously from one part into another (a seat
into its back, a fuselage into its tail cone) split it at the point of
maximum curvature, the visual "corner", and let the two primitives
interpenetrate through the fillet.
[block propose.construction ends]
# View conventions for the renders you will be shown
Renders are coloured per primitive on a white background. The canonical three
are orthographic; "match" is perspective. These directions are exact -- reason
from them rather than from intuition, and note that "front" shows you a
mirror-image left/right because you are facing the object.
- "front": camera on +X, looking back toward the origin. Screen-right is +Y,
the object's LEFT. Screen-up is +Z.
- "left": camera on +Y, looking toward the origin; you see the object's left
flank. Screen-right is -X, the object's BACK -- so the object faces
screen-LEFT. Screen-up is +Z.
- "top": camera above on +Z, looking down. Screen-right is -Y, the object's
15


RIGHT. Screen-up is +X, so the object faces screen-UP.
- "three-quarter": azimuth 35 deg, elevation 20 deg. A front-left-above view:
the front faces slightly screen-left, the object's left side toward
screen-right.
- "match": posed to match the input photo using your camera estimate.

F. Chamfer distance and decomposition quality
Limitations of Chamfer distance.
Chamfer distance (CD) measures surface proximity, but does not fully capture the quality
of a primitive decomposition. Small parts can be important for recognizing or supporting an object while contributing little to
its average surface error; CD also does not directly assess connectivity, stability or material-dependent mass distributions.
Prior work shows that CD can overlook local point-density mismatches [52], ignores surface normals [21], and can miss fine
geometric details that matter to human perception [11, 25]. When used as a mesh reconstruction loss, CD can also lead to
33


clustered vertices and twisted faces [63]. These observations motivate evaluating CD alongside visual quality and physical
plausibility.
We compare PhysDec-Loop with SQ, EMS, Marching-Primitives, SuperDec and SuperFlex on the 585 objects in Table 1.
Across these five baselines, 573 of the 2,925 object–method comparisons have a lower baseline CD, covering 249 objects.
Despite the lower CD, Claude Sonnet 5.5 prefers our decomposition in 524 of these pairs (91%), with GPT-6.1 Sol agreeing in
432. Figure S5 illustrates these disagreements using the reported CDs: reference-assisted values for PhysDec-Loop and the
baselines’ original evaluation values. We select examples where SuperDec, SuperFlex or Marching-Primitives has at least 10%
lower CD.
Optimization as an optional agent tool.
When lower CD is a priority, we can provide reference-based optimization as
an additional tool for PhysDec-Loop. The tool aligns a copy of the current decomposition to the reference point cloud and
optimizes its part scales and poses. It returns the suggested changes, the six parts with the largest residual errors and up to
three reference regions that remain uncovered. Critique uses this feedback to guide the next Propose step, allowing the
agent to revise the decomposition under the existing geometric and physical checks. The fitted copy is used for diagnosis; the
agent produces the revised primitives. The initial proposal, other verifiers, round budget and selection rule remain unchanged.
Optimization adds 7.6–20.8 s per round on an Apple M2 chip using eight CPU cores, so we keep this tool optional rather than
include it in the main pipeline.
Chamfer evaluation protocol.
The ShapeNet baselines take ground-truth point clouds as input (converted to a reference
SDF for Marching-Primitives), whereas our VLM proposes primitive parameters from images. Even a geometrically exact
decomposition can receive an elevated CD from a small mismatch in global scale, rotation or translation. For our predictions,
we estimate a global similarity alignment and fit each part’s scale, rotation and translation, keeping its primitive family, shape
parameters and material assignment fixed. The fit minimizes symmetric Chamfer-L1 between 8192 sampled surface points
and 4096 reference points using Adam for 300 steps, with an initial learning rate of 0.01 and cosine decay. Final evaluation
samples 4096 surface points and reports the mean of the two directed nearest-neighbor distances, multiplied by 100. Table S9
reports CD after alignment, per-axis scaling and part refinement; refinements that change the physical-check outcome are
rolled back. These values measure reference-assisted geometric fit.

MuJoCo uses gravity of 9.81 m/s2, a 2 ms time step, and sliding, torsional and rolling friction coefficients of 1, 0.02 and

orientation. Wall contact is evaluated geometrically: surface points within 1% of the bounding-box diagonal of the rearmost
surface must cover at least 3% of the projected outline. The suspension test runs for 5 s and checks lateral balance and
disconnected parts; its allowable center-of-mass offset is 2% of the span. For airborne and floating objects, this is a balance
proxy rather than a simulation of flight or buoyancy.
Materials and mass.
PhysDec uses the effective density associated with each predicted material and construction form;
methods without material predictions use a uniform density of 1000 kg/m3. The material choices are inferred without
ground-truth material or mass annotations. The resulting mass distributions are modeling assumptions for the physical checks,
rather than measurements of the objects’ true masses. The lamp example in the main paper holds the geometry fixed and
changes the density assignment to illustrate its effect on stability.
Primitive count.
For PhysDec, #Prim. counts entries in the primitive program: a complete radial array counts once, although
its copies are expanded for rendering and simulation. This count measures program compactness, rather than the number of
collision pieces or equal geometric complexity across different primitive vocabularies.
Self-evolution.
In Table 2 of the main paper, Base and PhysDec-Evolved predictions use no correction loop. Base and
PhysDec-Evolved each require two model calls per prediction; PhysDec-Loop call counts also include VLM review. For
the ShapeNet and unseen-category comparisons, calls count only the run producing the reported result. The comparison
evaluates the combined effect of evolved instructions and retrieved experience. The seed used to initialize prompt evolution
already includes structure rules and retrieval, as detailed in Tab. S3; it differs from the original-prompt Base predictor.
Consequently, the Base-to-Evolved improvement does not isolate automatic prompt rewriting from these other changes. Call
counts describe prediction-time model invocations, excluding offline construction of the library and prompt evolution; they are
not measurements of wall-clock time or token cost.
Reference component counts.
The connectivity penalty in Eq. (2) assumes a single connected object. During offline
self-evolution, the evaluator instead penalizes max(0, cr −cref), where cref is stored in the training and validation reference
labels as pieces_ref. These counts are initialized from the ground-truth surface point clouds: we voxelize at 1% of the
bounding-box diagonal, dilate by one voxel to bridge small gaps, and count connected regions containing at least 1% of the
points, with a minimum count of one. This accommodates samples containing multiple separate objects, such as a monitor
with detached speakers. Missing reference counts default to one, and these labels are used only for offline evaluation, not as
inputs to PhysDec-Evolved at deployment. PhysDec-Loop also supports an explicit object count in scene context when a crop
contains multiple objects; ordinary single-object runs use one.

Setup.
We randomly sample 65 objects, five per category, from the 390 test objects with available baseline predictions at the
time of the study. Each object is reconstructed by SuperDec, SuperFlex and PhysDec-Loop, yielding 195 results with the
VLM scores used in Table 1. Five participants rate all results on a five-point scale from 1 (bad) to 5 (excellent).
Participants compare each reconstruction with the four input images using the same rendered views as the VLM judges.
Methods are anonymized and randomly ordered for each object, with identical rendering styles and no VLM scores shown.
Instructions cover the six geometric and structural criteria in Tab. S8; material assignments are not shown to participants but
account for 1/13 of the VLM rubric’s total weight.
Evaluation scope and participant information.
The questionnaire collects ratings of 3D reconstruction quality to compare
methods and assess agreement with VLM scores. Its purpose is to evaluate the reconstructions, rather than participants’
personal characteristics or experiences. Participants were informed that their ratings would be used in this CVPR submission.

Both VLM judges correlate strongly with mean human ratings (Spearman ρ = 0.81) and match human preferences

gives an average ρ = 0.86 and 94.9% pairwise agreement. Thus, the VLM scores broadly reflect human judgments, although

We evaluate pick-and-place manipulation in MuJoCo using a KUKA LBR iiwa 14 with an Allegro hand [62]. We compare
with SuperDec and SuperFlex using the same grasp planner on each method’s superquadrics [47], with object masses matched
across methods. The robot lifts, carries and places each object; success requires moving all its parts and leaving it stable,
without disturbing surrounding objects in scenes. A reconstruction that falls apart or topples when released has already failed,
but the robot still plans on its pieces where they lie.
