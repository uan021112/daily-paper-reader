Improving Zero-shot 3D Semantic Mapping in Offroad with Geometric
Priors
Eerik Alamikkotervo1, Risto Ojala1
Abstract— Semantic 3D mapping is a powerful representation
that enables platform agnostic mobile robot navigation in chal-
lenging offroad conditions. Current vision foundation models
can accurately predict semantics zero-shot on structured scenes
but performance is compromised in natural environments. On
the other hand, 3D point clouds encode valuable information for
discriminating semantics in scenarios where vision foundation
models fail. In this work, we propose fitting a sphericity-
elevation distribution for each semantic class to act as geometric
anchor. Only the points with the most confident vision founda-
tion model predictions are considered when fitting to produce
high quality geometric prior distributions. The geometric prior
is fused with the vision foundation model prediction to produce
the final semantic mapping. The proposed prior improves SAM3
baseline by +6.3 in mIoU and +8.9 in accuracy on the RELLIS-
3D dataset, and by +1.7 in mIoU and +2.1 in accuracy on the
WildScenes dataset. The source code will be made public upon
paper acceptance.
I. INTRODUCTION
Semantic understanding of the environment is crucial for
autonomous navigation in offroad conditions. Most offroad
research concentrates on traversability estimation that re-
duces the rich geometry and semantic information to single
dimension [1], [2]. These methods commonly classify any
elevated 3D points as non-traversable and cannot accurately
reason about the passability of different types of vegetation.
Semantic 3D perception would provide more flexible foun-
dation for offroad navigation that can be easily adapted to
different scenarios and platforms.
While the current foundation models for general purpose
segmentation [3], [4] achieve impressive accuracy on struc-
tured environments their performance is significantly lower
on unstructured natural scenes. Separating different kinds
of vegetation and terrain from images is one of the major
challenges as there are no clear visual boundaries. At the
same time correct classification of different vegetation and
terrain types is critical for succesful navigation decisions as
they cover significant portion of natural scenes and have
different traversability properties.
Different terrain and vegetation types typically have dis-
tinctive geometric features that can help image based founda-
tion models discriminate between them. We propose utilizing
elevation and sphericity computed from lidar point clouds for
this task. Elevation separates the terrain from vegetation and
obstacles above it. Sphericity discriminates between different
terrain and vegetation types based on their local shape. We
fit an elevation-sphericity histogram for each semantic class

II. RELATED WORK
A. Vision foundation models for zero-shot semantic segmen-
tation
Historically, segmentation models have been trained on
task- and domain-specific datasets to achieve reliable perfor-
mance within that domain. The current trend is to pretrain
large vision models on massive, diverse datasets using self-
supervised, weakly supervised, or multimodal objectives,
and then adapt them to a variety of downstream tasks and
domains with minimal additional training. These models are
referred to as vision foundation models. Thanks to extensive
pre-training many vision foundation models can be used
in zero-shot manner, meaning they require no training in
the target domain to perform inference. Variety of vision
foundation models have been proposed in recent years. DINO
[7] and EVA [8] are popular examples of feature extraction
arXiv:2611.24696v1  [cs.CV]  18 Nov 2026


backbones that can be fine tuned for variety of downstream
tasks. SAM [9] and Grounded SAM [10] are designed for
the segmentation task. Large vision language models like
Gemini [11] and Qwen VL [12] support image inputs but
they are trained for higher level image understanding and
reasoning not pixel level segmentation task.
Another recent trend has been the deveploment of open
vocabulary models to allow segmenting images with arbitary
prompts. SAM3 [3] added support to query masks with
short open vocabulary concenpts. DINO.txt [13] aligns dense
DINO features with text prompt encodings to to allow open
vocabulary segmentation. OpenSeg [4] employs a separate
mask proposal network that generates class agnostic masks
proposals that are then classified based on the feature simi-
larity with the encoded text prompts.
B. Zero-shot segmentation in 3D
Traditional supervised 3D segmentation networks are
trained to produce semantic labels from the pointcloud
with manually labeled dataset. For zero-shot methods a
common approach is to predict features or segmentation
masks from the image with vision foundation models and lift
them to 3D with known calibration [14], [15]. Image based
foundation models provide better zero-shot open vocabulary
performance thanks to better training data availability, most
importantly image-caption pairs. Image foundation models
can also serve as supervision to distill 3D prediction models
[16], [17] enabling zero-shot open-vocabulary segmentation
from 3D inputs alone, while also allowing image and 3D
modalities to be fused for improved performance. However,
the performance gains are limited as the 3D model relies
on the image model for supervision leading to correlated
outputs.
C. Zero-shot segmentation in offroad
Offroad conditions pose spesific challenges to zero-shot
segmentation in both 3D and image modalities. Images lack
easily identifiable salient objects and most foundation model
training data is from structured scenes which leads to de-
creased performance in offroad environments. In 3D, points
don’t necessarily form dense easily identifiable clusters as
in the common 3D segmentation benchmarks like ScanNet
[18] and Matterport3D [19]. Instead the point density is
singificantly lower and shapes more irregular.
Very limited research has been conducted to solve zero-
shot semantic segmentation in offroad conditions. Instead,
most works concentrate on estimating just the traversability
in a self-supervised manner [1], [2]. However, such ap-
proaches can’t effectively handle scenarios where the plat-
form needs to navigate over variety of terrain, obstacles and
vegetation with different passability. 3D semantic maps are
also inheritly platform agnostic unlike most self-supervised
traversability estimation methods. Traversability is property
tied to a specific platform while 3D semantic mapping is an
objective description of the environment.
Best of our knowledge OTAS [20] is the only work aiming
to specifically address the issue of zero-shot semantic seg-
mentation in offroad conditions. They demonstrate that dense
CLIP [21] features commonly used for open vocabulary
segmentation are noisy in the offroad scenario. To solve this
problem they extract dense DINOv2 features that are visually
more robust and cluster them to form mask proposals. These
mask proposals are then classified using their average CLIP
feature. In the 3D case the point coordinates are concatenated
with the features before clustering to encourage spatial
coherency.
D. Geometric features in offroad robotics
Geometric features have been long used for evaluating
the environment properties in offroad conditions. Common
features are step-height, roughness and slope [22]. These
features work well in solid terrain, but not when passing
through vegetation is required. High vegetation typically
has high roughness and step height leading to classification
as obstacle. Utilization of linearity, planarity and sphericity
features is one proposed solution to this problem [23], [24].
Vegetation typically has high sphericity, whereas terrain and
structures exhibit high planarity, and thin obstacles high
linearity.
E. Research Gap
We improve zero-shot 3D semantic mapping by utilizing
geometric priors based on elevation and sphericity extracted
from lidar point clouds. Refrence elevation-sphericity his-
togram is fitted for each class utilizing the points with
most confident vision foundation model predictions. The
fitting process is lightweight and the histogram describes
the geometrical separation of different classes in human
understandable form. In existing works, OTAS utilizes 3D
coordinates to improve spatial segmentation coherency but
ignores other cues available from geometry [20]. On the
other hand, distilling 3D networks with image based founda-
tion model supervision requires environment specific neural
network training that is time and computation heavy. [16],
[17].
III. METHODS
Given a synced lidar scan-image sequence our method
generates a semantic pointcloud map of the sequence. The
method is divided to four parts. First confidence score
maps are generated for each class using an open vocabulary
vision foundation model (Section III-A). Then sphericity
and elevation are computed for the 3D point cloud map
accumulated from the posed scans (Section III-B). Then
sphericity-elevation histogram is fitted using the points with
most confident vision foundation model predictions (Section
III-C). Finally, the geometric prior is queried from the fitted
histograms and fused with the vision foundation model
confidence maps to produce the final segmentation output
(Section III-D).

E. Validation
We evaluate the proposed method using three zero-shot,
open-vocabulary image segmentation models: DINOv2.txt,
SAM 3, and OpenSeg. Given a text prompt, SAM 3 outputs
a set of mask proposals, each paired with a mask score
in the range [0, 1] representing the model’s confidence in
that mask, as well as a pixel-wise score in [0, 1] indicating
the confidence that each pixel belongs to that mask. We
define the final per-pixel confidence score as the product
of the global mask score and the pixel-wise mask score.
DINOv2.txt employs a ViT-L/14 backbone with registers
alongside its corresponding vision head and text encoder.
OpenSeg uses an EfficientNet-B7 backbone paired with CLIP
ViT-L/14@336px text embeddings, following OpenScene
[16]. For both DINOv2.txt and OpenSeg, we scale the raw
cosine similarities between the text prompt embeddings and
visual embeddings by a temperature of T = 100 before ap-
plying a softmax function, consistent with the CLIP training
recipe.All models operate at their respective recommended
input resolutions: SAM 3 inputs are stretched directly to
1008 × 1008 px, OpenSeg inputs are resized to a 640 px
long side while preserving the aspect ratio, with the short
side padded to 640 px and DINOv2.txt inputs are resized to
a 224 px short side while preserving the aspect ratio.
Additionally, we compare against OTAS, which is specif-
ically designed for zero-shot 3D semantic segmentation in
unstructured environments. For each feature patch, we assign
the median depth of all LiDAR scan points projecting into
that patch. Following the authors’ recommended configura-
tion, we use the spatial variant of OTAS with 24 clusters, 24
components, a DINOv2 ViT-S/14 backbone with registers,
and a 224 px input resolution, setting the voxel size to 0.2 m
to match our method’s resolution. The reconstructed map is
queried with every dataset class prompt, and each 3D point
is assigned the class yielding the highest similarity score.
To keep GPU memory consumption manageable, the map is
processed in chunks of 500 samples.
F. Data
We evaluate our method using the WildScenes [6] and
Rellis3D [5] datasets. Both include image-lidar scan pairs
with known calibration and ground truth for pointcloud
semantics. We only consider the lidar scan points that are
inside the camera field of view as all evaluated methods
require image data in addition to the 3D points. Lidar points
further than 25 m are filtered out as alignment errors increase
and sensor resolution decreases with longer distances.
WildScenes is collected on Australian forest trails sur-
rounded by high tree canopies. We exclude ambigous classes:
other-terrain, other-object and unlabelled. We also exclude
sky and water as these labels are not present in the 3D data.
RELLIS3D is collected on rural fields and trails in Texas
dominated by grass, bushes and smaller trees. We exclude
ambigous classes void and object. We exclude sky as it is
not present in the 3D data and person and vehicle as they
are dynamic. We also exclude building and dirt as no points
belonging to these classes are retained with the camera fov
filtering with 25 m max distance used here. The poses were
computed with kiss-icp [25].
IV. RESULTS
Accuracy, mIoU and class wise IoU are reported for each
model on Rellis3D dataset (Table I) and WildScenes dataset
(Table II). The geometry prior improves performance for
all benchmarked zero-shot segmentation models. SAM3 is
the best performing baseline model achieving 28.1 mIoU
and 73.9 accuracy on Rellis3d and 47.9 mIoU and 80.5
accuracy on WildScenes. With the geometry prior SAM3
achieves +6.3 mIoU and +8.9 accuracy increase on Rellis3d
and +1.7 mIoU and +2.1 accuracy increase on WildScenes.
On Rellis3d the classes with highest gains are Bush (+44.2),
Grass (+8.8), Puddle (+5.2) and Water (+28.7) and on
WildScenes Bush (+3.8), Dirt (+7.9) and Grass (+9.2). OTAS
often confuses similar classes, like dirt and gravel or tree-
foliage and tree-trunk and achieves slightly lower accuracy
than the DINOv2.txt and OpenSeg baselines. Qualitative
examples for OTAS, SAM3 and SAM3 with geometric priors
are presented in Tables III and IV.
For further analysis, we ablate different smoothing fac-
tors and the performance of only sphericity or elevation
based prior histograms (Table V). With both datasets the
joint elevation-sphericity prior outperforms either component
alone. Optimal smoothing factor depends on the quality of
the image predictions as it controls how much weight the
geometry is given over the image predictions. Smoothing
factor 0.25 was found to provide the best compromise across
both datasets and is used with all reported results here. In
most cases, any choice of the smoothing factor provided
significant improvements over the baseline.
V. DISCUSSION
This paper introduced a method to improve zero-shot seg-
mentation in offroad scenes with geometric priors computed
from the 3D data. The geometric priors increased perfor-
mance especially on natural classes like bush, grass, dirt,
water and puddle while performance on structured classes
was very little affected. Our explanation for this behavior is
two-fold. First, the zero-shot vision foundation models are
more prone to make mistakes on natural classes due to the
limited training data from these domains, leaving more room
for improvement. Second, the elevation-sphericity histogram
has better separation between natural classes than between

While deep learning models can extract more complex
features from 3D pointclouds, the proposed approach has
a few main advantages over them. Deep learning models
require training on each new environment while the dis-
tribution fitting process is much faster and requires less
computation. Additionally, the proposed approach is more
transparent: the geometric separation between classes can
be directly evaluated from the fitted histograms. Finally,
for many natural classes elevation and sphericity provide
sufficient separation when fused with the vision foundation
model predictions as the 3D shapes are simpler compared to
cluttered indoor scenes.
The performance of our method is limited by class sepa-
rability within the histogram; consequently, classes sharing
similar elevation and sphericity values cannot be distin-
guished. Furthermore, our approach relies on the vision
foundation model to provide confident detections for each
class in order to fit these distributions. Any bias or error
on the vision foundation models detections affects the fitted
histogram and consequentially the computed priors. In future
work, more geometric features could be added to achieve
more discriminative prior distributions or alternatively pre-
trained point-encoders could be used to extract more complex
point features. Another future challenge is calibrating the
vision foundation model and geometric prior confidences for
improved fusion and uncertainty estimation.
While histograms can capture arbitrary distribution shapes,
they also introduce significant limitations. Specifically, a
query bin’s prior depends solely on its own value, without
accounting for neighboring bins. Consequently, an empty bin
adjacent to high-density regions is treated identically to an
empty bin in a sparse region. Future work could address this
limitation by applying spatial smoothing to the histograms.
Parameterized distributions, such as 2D Gaussians are also
a potential solution but they also restrict the shape of the
distribution.
