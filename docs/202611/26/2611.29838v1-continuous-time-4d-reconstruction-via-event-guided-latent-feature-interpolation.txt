Abstract
Recent geometric foundation models can successfully convert a video sequence into
frames of registered point clouds, but cannot predict moments beyond the observed tem-
poral resolution. We propose a continuous-time 4D reconstruction method that extracts
3D structure and camera poses at any time instant, with additional guidance from event-
camera measurements. While event cameras provide evidence at microsecond resolution
to continuously fill the temporal gap, integrating them into existing RGB-based founda-
tion models remains challenging. We formulate the problem as interpolation in the latent
feature space and augment event-based losses in the image space as an auxiliary task to
boost accuracy. Operating in the latent space leverages the powerful geometric priors
of foundation models, while the event loss provides physically grounded guidance. To
effectively address the lack of paired training data, we further propose a self-supervised
training framework that uses pseudo-ground-truth labels of estimated geometry and sim-
ulated events generated from high-frame-rate RGB videos. Through extensive experi-
ments, we demonstrate that the proposed pipeline outperforms baselines that temporally
interpolate either the input RGB videos or the estimated geometry, and that it generalizes
to unseen real-world scenarios. Controlled experiments with synthetic corruptions show
that it remains robust under motion-blur and low-light conditions.

Such dynamic structural knowledge, combined with rich semantic context, enables image

We propose using a stream of RGB video, paired with event-camera measurements, for
continuous-time 4D reconstruction, enabling recovery of 3D geometry and camera motion
at arbitrary timestamps. Event cameras asynchronously record local brightness changes with
microsecond-level temporal resolution [9], providing precise evidence of visual changes be-
tween frames. However, paired event-RGB data with ground-truth geometry is scarce to
train a foundation model from scratch. A plausible solution is to integrate asynchronous
event measurements into the strong geometric priors of RGB-based models.
We frame the continuous-time 4D reconstruction problem as temporal interpolation: re-
constructing the geometry and camera motion at an interpolated timestamp, given two adja-
cent RGB frames and the event stream between them. If we aim to incorporate event input
without altering the foundation model, we can either interpolate the input image or the 3D
output. Input-space interpolation synthesizes intermediate RGB frames using event-based
video frame interpolation [5, 34, 35] and feeds them into a frame-based geometric foundation
model. Because the interpolation is optimized for image quality, the results exhibit geomet-
ric artifacts around occlusions, motion boundaries, and blurred regions, leading to errors in
depth and pose. At the other end, output-space interpolation [14] interpolates the recon-
structed pointmaps using event measurements. However, the decoded output is agnostic to
rich internal representations, which are useful for modeling subtle inter-frame dynamics,
such as multi-view correspondence and contextual scene cues.
In contrast, we propose latent-space interpolation, which performs continuous-time in-
terpolation in the internal latent feature space of the geometric foundation model. The con-
ventional model operates frame-wise: the encoder converts each frame into a latent repre-
sentation, and the decoder extracts geometric information, such as point maps, depth, and
camera pose, for that frame. Given two adjacent RGB frames and an event stream between
them, our framework converts the frame-wise features into a continuous feature field volume

that can be sampled at any timestamp and decoded into geometry, illustrated in Figure 1.
The latent interpolation further incorporates event evidence to match temporal information
in image space. In parallel with our 4D reconstruction pipeline, we attach a color interpola-
tion branch that predicts continuous-time color information at arbitrary timestamps, whose
appearance change should match the measurements in event streams. The event formulation
naturally handles images with challenging illumination or fast motion, given the sensor’s
measurement characteristics. Most importantly, the appearance constraint serves as a proxy
task, providing useful temporal constraints that guide underlying geometric changes.
While we build on existing foundation models, we still need to train the proposed frame-
work using high-frequency ground-truth depth and pose information acquired from an RGB
video paired with an event camera, which is rarely available. We circumvent the problem by
using pseudo-ground-truth labels obtained from high-frame-rate videos. Temporally dense
RGB frames can simulate realistic events and provide estimated depth and camera pose us-
ing geometric foundation models. Empirically, the trained model successfully operates on
both real-world and synthetic datasets, yielding more accurate 4D reconstruction than both
input-space and output-space interpolation baselines. We further analyze its robustness to
motion blur and low light, a direct consequence of incorporating event measurements.
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
• We propose event-guided latent feature interpolation, a framework for continuous-time
4D reconstruction that interpolates within the feature space of a pretrained geometric
foundation model, fully leveraging its learned geometric prior to decode depth and
camera pose at arbitrary timestamps.
• We introduce a proxy task of continuous appearance interpolation and event simula-
tion, which simulates events from the predicted continuous color changes and matches
them to the observed event stream, regularizing geometry interpolation with an addi-
tional temporal constraint.
• We propose a self-supervised training framework built on high-frame-rate RGB datasets
that simulates realistic events and distills pseudo-ground-truth depth and pose from a
pretrained foundation model, addressing the scarcity of event datasets with ground-
truth geometry while transferring effectively to real-world event streams.
• We demonstrate through experiments on real-world datasets and synthetic motion-blur
and low-light corruptions that our latent-space interpolation yields more accurate 4D
reconstruction than RGB-interpolation and pointmap-interpolation baselines across di-
verse scenarios.

Related Works
3D/4D Geometric Foundation Model.
Beyond the classical SfM/MVS pipeline, recent
geometric foundation models learn to predict scene geometry directly from images via large-
scale training. DUSt3R [38] regresses per-pixel 3D points from an image pair in a shared
coordinate frame, implicitly recovering relative camera poses and showing strong general-
ization across diverse scenes. Building on this representation, MASt3R [17] adds dense
feature matching for accurate two-view correspondence, VGGT [36] scales to many views
with a feed-forward transformer that jointly predicts depth, pose, and tracks, and MapA-
nything [16] unifies varied inputs such as single images, multi-view collections, and posed

sequences within a single framework. Extending pointmap-based models from static to dy-
namic scenes, MonST3R [42] finetunes DUSt3R on dynamic datasets and CUT3R [37] pro-
cesses streaming video with a recurrent persistent state. Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [20], which
we adopt as a backbone, jointly predicts depth and camera rays under a unified depth-ray
representation on a plain transformer, achieving state-of-the-art results on both static 3D and
dynamic 4D reconstruction. Beyond per-frame reconstruction, recent works directly output
continuous-time 4D representations [18, 24, 41], but rely solely on sparse RGB frames with-
out any direct measurement within the blind interval between them. A parallel line of work
injects event streams into geometric foundation models [27, 39], yet they treat events as a
per-frame robustness cue and do not exploit their microsecond-scale temporal resolution for
continuous-time reconstruction.
Event-guided Continuous-time Interpolation.
Since event cameras provide direct ob-
servations within the unknown interval between RGB frames, many works have leveraged
them for continuous-time interpolation. Event-based video frame interpolation (EVFI) treats
the event stream as motion guidance to synthesize intermediate RGB frames between two
frames. TimeLens [34] pioneered this direction by combining event-driven motion esti-
mation with frame-based warping in a unified network. TimeLens++ [35] extends it with
parametric motion trajectories, and more recently, VDM-EVFI [5] adapts pretrained video
diffusion models to event-conditioned interpolation. However, feeding such synthesized in-
termediate frames directly into a frame-based geometric model amplifies their visual arti-
facts around occlusions and motion boundaries, leading to downstream depth and pose er-
rors. Closest to ours, Interp3R [14] instead interpolates 3D geometry rather than color, using
events to predict pointmaps at arbitrary times between two frames. However, Interp3R oper-
ates on the model’s already-decoded pointmaps, after the rich latent representation has been
collapsed into a 3D output, discarding intermediate cues such as multi-view correspondence
and contextual scene information. In contrast, we interpolate in the latent feature space be-
tween the encoder and decoder of a pretrained geometric foundation model, preserving its
learned geometric prior while allowing events to guide fine-grained temporal changes.
3
Method
3.1
Overview
Given a set of RGB frames {I0,...,IN} and the event stream E recorded across them, our goal
is to estimate depth and camera pose at an arbitrary query timestamp t within any inter-frame
interval. We build on Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [20], an RGB-based feed-forward geomet-
ric foundation model, which we use as a frozen reconstruction backbone. Its transformer
encoder jointly maps all input frames to per-frame latent feature tokens, {F0,...,FN} =
Enc(I0,...,IN), which carry rich geometric and contextual information. A DPT [30] de-
coder then maps each latent feature to a depth map and a ray map, (Di,Ri) = Dec(Fi). The
camera pose is recovered from the per-pixel rays Ri, while the depth Di together with the
rays yields a per-frame point map Xi.
We perform temporal interpolation directly in the latent token space between the encoder
and decoder, where rich geometric information is still preserved. To recover the geometry
within each blind interval, we perform interpolation independently for each adjacent frame
pair. Given two adjacent RGB frames (Is,Ie), their features (Fs,Fe), and the event stream E

3.2
Event-Guided Feature Interpolation Network
Our network takes {Is,Ie,Fs,Fe,E} as input and predicts the interpolated feature F(t) at a
query time t, which is then converted into depth and camera pose by the frozen decoder.
Here Is,Ie ∈R3×H×W are the RGB endpoints, E is the event stream over the interval, and
Fs,Fe are the corresponding DA3 features F ∈RS×Hf ×Wf ×C, where S is the number of scales,
Hf and Wf are the height and width of the feature grid, and C is the channel dimension. The
S = 4 scales are the multi-level feature maps extracted from different ViT [8] layers and
consumed by the frozen DPT [30] decoder. Since each feature map may carry different
semantic and geometric information, we train four separate Geometry Feature Fusion and
INR modules in Fig. 2, while sharing a single Context Feature Extraction module across
scales. In the following, we describe the processing of a single scale for clarity. As illustrated

in Fig. 2, the network has three major modules: (i) the Context Feature Extraction module
turns the two frames and the events between them into a temporal-context feature Fctx; (ii) the
Geometry Feature Fusion module aggregates the endpoint features Fs,Fe under this context
into K discrete control-point features Fcp; and (iii) the INR (Implicit Neural Representation)
module samples and refines them into the continuous feature F(t).
Context feature extraction.
We fuse the two adjacent frames and the events between them
into a temporal context feature that carries the inter-frame motion. The key design is to treat
the K-bin event voxel as a K-frame video, so that the temporal change within the inter-
val is processed as a sequence. Concretely, given events {(xk,yk,tk, pk)} over the interval
[ts,te] with polarities pk ∈{−1,+1}, we accumulate them into the voxel grid with bilinear
interpolation in time as in [47]. Modality-specific stems separately encode the two RGB
frames and each event bin. The RGB features are blended across the K steps by time-based
weights wi = i/(K −1), and the two modalities are then fused into a single context sequence
Fctx ∈RK×Hf ×Wf ×C. We then use a recurrent encoder from the video restoration literature,
RVRT-style propagation [19], to sequentially aggregate temporal cues across the K control
points of Fctx.
Geometry feature fusion.
Conditioned on this temporal context, we aggregate the relevant
geometric information from the two endpoint features Fs,Fe. Using the context feature Fctx
as the query and the endpoint features as keys and values, we cross-attend to Fs and Fe
separately and blend the two retrievals with a content-aware gate α initialized to the temporal
prior t. Because the endpoints describe only the two boundary timestamps, we further add
Fctx back as a residual, supplying event-driven cues about the intermediate moments of the
interval. The two operations combine into the control-point features Fcp ∈RK×Hf ×Wf ×C, one
per control point,
Fcp = (1−α)⊙CA(Fctx,Fs)+α ⊙CA(Fctx,Fe)+Fctx,
(1)
where the cross-attention block CA(·,·) takes the first argument as the query and the second
as the key/value.
Continuous output representation.
To obtain a continuous feature at any query time t,
we perform INR-based refinement over the K control-point features Fcp. We first sample
the two control points adjacent to t and blend them into the feature at t, then encode t with
a frequency embedding [26] and refine the feature through FiLM-modulated [28] layers,
where an MLP maps the time embedding to per-layer affine parameters.
For stable training, and inspired by zero-initialized residual learning [43], we formulate
the INR output as a residual over a linear-interpolation baseline of the endpoint features.
Specifically, the baseline is defined as FLerp(t) = (1 −t)Fs +t Fe, and the output projection
of the INR is zero-initialized so that training starts exactly from this baseline:
F(t) = FINR(Fcp,t)+FLerp(t).
(2)
Here, FINR(Fcp,t) samples Fcp at t and refines it through the FiLM layers. The frozen decoder
maps F(t) to a depth map and a ray map, (D(t),R(t)) = Dec(F(t)), and the camera pose is
recovered from the ray map.

3.3
Appearance-based Proxy Task
Joint appearance-geometry estimation.
The appearance proxy is motivated by the intu-
ition that temporal appearance changes carry cues about the underlying geometric changes,
making continuous color prediction a natural regularizer of the latent feature trajectory. We
thus attach a continuous color interpolation branch alongside the geometry branch, predict-
ing continuous-time color C(t) at the same query timestamps as depth and camera pose. The
color branch mirrors the depth branch, sharing the context feature Fctx but running its own
Color Feature Extraction, Color INR and Color Decoder modules, as depicted in Fig. 2. The
two branches are coupled both through this shared context and through gated cross-modal
injection between their INR heads. At each INR layer, one branch adds the other’s query-
time feature through a learned gate, initialized near zero so the branches start decoupled and
learn to couple over training. We supervise C(t) with a color reconstruction loss combining
Charbonnier, MS-SSIM, and LPIPS terms,
Lcolor = LCharb +λSSIM LMS-SSIM +λLPIPS LLPIPS.

3.4
Self-Supervised Training from High-Frame-Rate Video
Unlike the RGB domain, building a large-scale training set for event-guided 4D reconstruc-
tion is hard, since real RGB-event datasets with ground-truth depth and camera pose are
scarce and limited in scene diversity. To obtain a large and diverse training set, we propose
a self-supervised training pipeline based on high-frame-rate video data and self-distillation.
High-frame-rate RGB video is relatively abundant and already used to train color interpola-
tion networks [4, 6, 31, 33]. From these dense frames we synthesize both the input events

and the ground-truth geometry. As shown in Fig. 3, we first simulate realistic events from the
dense frames with an event simulator [10]. We then obtain pseudo ground-truth depth and
camera pose by distilling the frozen DA3 teacher, i.e., running it on the dense frames to get
frame-wise depth and camera pose. Pairing the simulated events with this pseudo-ground-
truth lets us train across diverse scenes without any real-world event data.
4
Experimental Results
4.1
Experimental Setup
Implementation details.
Our network is built on top of Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [20],
specifically the DA3-LARGE-1.1 model. The number of temporal control points is set to
K = 20, and Clow and Chigh are set to 0.15 and 0.35. We train with the AdamW [22] optimizer
for 90 epochs at a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a weight decay of 5 × 10−3. The loss
weights are set as λray = λcolor = λevent = λo = 1.0, λd = 0.1, λSSIM = 0.2, and λLPIPS =
0.1. The predicted color at query time t is parameterized as a residual over the linear blend
(1 −t)Is +t Ie of the endpoint frames. We scale the color loss by 50× at pixels where an
event is observed, to focus more on regions where the appearance actually changes.
Baselines.
We compare against interpolation methods at both ends of the geometry pipeline,
input-space and output-space. As input-space interpolation baselines, which feed event-
based RGB interpolation outputs into a frame-based geometric foundation model, we use
TimeLens [34], a representative event-based continuous-time RGB interpolator, and VDM-
EVFI [5], a more recent diffusion-based interpolator. Their synthesized intermediate frames
are passed to the frozen DA3 backbone for depth and camera pose prediction.
As the
output-space interpolation baseline, we use Interp3R [14], which interpolates pointmaps
from Align3R [23], initializes its decoder from Align3R, and is trained only on PointOdyssey [46]
Since Interp3R is not open-sourced, we reimplement it for a fair comparison. Our reimple-
mentation is built on DA3, takes the DA3 point cloud as input, initializes its decoder from
DA3, and is trained on the same datasets and for the same number of epochs as our model.
We additionally include an Ours w/o events variant that shares all components with our full
model but removes the event-based encoder in the context feature extraction module in Sec-
tion 3.2, performing latent feature interpolation from RGB inputs alone. All other training
settings are kept identical to the full model.
Datasets.
We evaluate on the real-world event dataset DSEC [11] and the synthetic dataset
Sim4D [25]. DSEC is an RGB-event driving dataset providing ground-truth depth, cap-
tured with a Prophesee Gen3.1 event camera synchronized with RGB cameras and a LiDAR.
For ground-truth camera poses, we follow ES-PTAM [12] and use LiDAR-inertial odom-
etry as reference. For evaluation, we take the first 200 frames of the zurich_city_04_a-d
sequences. Sim4D [25] is a Blender-based synthetic dataset of moving objects and cameras,
providing accurate ground-truth depth and camera pose at a minimum of 120 FPS. From its
high-FPS frames, we synthesize events [10], motion blur, and low-light images [2, 3, 21]
for the robustness analysis in Section 4.3. We use the curtain, modular_vehicle, rhino, mer-
cedes, and wave_toy scenes. For both DSEC and Sim4D, we sample frame pairs at query
intervals of 2, 4, 8, and 16 frames, following previous works [5, 14, 34]. An interval of
T corresponds to T −1 intermediate frames between the endpoints Is and Ie. For training,

4.6
Ablation study
Table 4 reports the ablation study on depth and pose interpolation. For efficiency, each
ablated model is trained for 60 epochs on 50% of the training data and evaluated on Sim4D-
Sharp. Removing the event input causes the largest drop in depth accuracy, showing that
the event stream plays a crucial role in temporal interpolation. The appearance-based proxy
loss and the INR-based refinement also each lead to clear reductions in depth error. We
further analyze the effect of our self-supervised training framework by varying the training
data. Here, synthetic GT denotes training only on PointOdyssey, while pseudo GT denotes
training only on the data generated by our self-supervised framework. When the model is
trained only on synthetic data, both depth and pose accuracy drop substantially. This is
likely due to the inaccurate event simulation from low-FPS RGB images and the domain
gap of synthetic data. In contrast, training only on our pseudo-GT data from high-FPS real-
world RGB videos performs almost as well as using both sources together. This shows that
our self-supervised training framework plays a key role in producing accurate interpolation.
5
Conclusion
We present an event-guided latent feature interpolation framework for continuous-time 4D
reconstruction that predicts depth and camera pose at arbitrary timestamps between RGB
frames. Unlike prior input-space and output-space approaches that interpolate either RGB
frames or pointmaps, our method builds a continuous latent feature field directly within
the latent space of a pretrained geometric foundation model. This design fully leverages
its learned geometric prior while letting events guide fine-grained temporal changes. To
regularize the feature interpolation, we introduce an appearance-based proxy task that jointly
predicts continuous color and matches simulated events from it against the observed event
stream. We further propose a self-supervised training framework to overcome the scarcity
of event datasets with ground-truth geometry. Built on high-frame-rate RGB videos, this
framework simulates realistic events and distills pseudo-ground-truth depth and camera pose
from a foundation model teacher. From extensive evaluation and analysis on real-world and
synthetic corruptions, we demonstrate that our method outperforms RGB-interpolation and
pointmap-interpolation baselines and remains robust where these baselines break down. Our
ablation study further highlights the effectiveness of the appearance-based proxy task and
the self-supervised training framework.

A
Additional Qualitative Comparison
We additionally conduct qualitative comparison against two baselines that are not shown in
the main paper: VDM-EVFI [5] and Ours w/o events. VDM-EVFI [5] is an event-based
RGB interpolation model built on top of a diffusion backbone. Ours w/o events is an ablated
variant of our model that does not use the event stream as input. It learns latent feature
interpolation solely from the two endpoint RGB frames. For a direct comparison with other
baselines, TimeLens [34] and Interp3R [14], we report qualitative results on the same scenes
as Fig. 4.
In Fig. 7, VDM-EVFI exhibits the same limitation as TimeLens. Because its pipeline is
two-stage, first RGB interpolation and then geometry estimation, errors accumulate across
the two stages. As a result, the pillar disappears in the DSEC [11] scene and the player’s head
is lost in the SportsSloMo [4] scene. Interestingly, on Sim4D [25], VDM-EVFI yields no-
ticeably sharper and cleaner reconstructions than TimeLens. Being a diffusion-based model,
however, it tends to hallucinate fine details: the rhino’s nose and legs are sharp but inaccurate
when compared to GT.
The results of Ours w/o events highlight the importance of the event input and show
that our full model actively exploits it. When the model relies only on the two sparse RGB
keyframes to perform feature interpolation, it misplaces the pillar in DSEC and the boxer’s
fist in SportsSloMo. Our full model, which uses temporal cues from events, accurately
locates objects at intermediate timestamps. Naturally, our method also yields more accurate
boundary reconstruction, as visible in the Sim4D result.
B
Additional Qualitative Robustness Analysis
In Fig. 8, we further analyze color and depth interpolation quality under corrupted inputs.
We compare our method against two event-based RGB interpolation baselines. For each,
we show the interpolated color frame at t = 0.5 paired with the corresponding reconstructed
point cloud under sharp, motion-blur, and low-light inputs. Under motion-blur, our method
successfully reconstructs sharp intermediate frames, whereas both TimeLens and VDM-
EVFI produce blurred interpolations that lead to noise in the point cloud reconstruction.
Under low-light (dark) inputs, both baselines fail to separate the foreground from the back-
ground. When the RGB input is heavily corrupted, interpolating the RGB first and then
estimating geometry suffers more from error accumulation. Although our training data does
not contain low-light scenes, our method generalizes naturally to this regime by actively
using event information.

Interval-wise Quantitative Analysis on Sim4D
In the main paper, we conduct an interval-wise quantitative comparison on the DSEC dataset
(Table 1), but report only average values for Sim4D (Table 2). In this section, we pro-
vide an interval-wise quantitative analysis on the Sim4D dataset for all three input types:
sharp, motion-blur, and low-light. For Sim4D-Sharp (Table 5), our method and the baselines
achieve comparable depth errors at small intervals, but our method demonstrates superior
performance than the baselines as the interval grows. For Sim4D-Blur (Table 6) and Sim4D-
Dark (Table 7), our method already outperforms the baselines by a large margin even at
small intervals. On these corrupted inputs, our method achieves the best performance across
all depth metrics at every interval. In terms of global pose error (ATE), our method is worse
than the image-space baselines on sharp images. We attribute this to the simple scene struc-
ture of Sim4D. However, this gap narrows on Sim4D-Blur and reverses on Sim4D-Dark,
where our method performs best.
