Abstract 

Open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS) enables flexible, text-driven mapping of remote sensing (RS) im-agery beyond predefined categories. Despite advances in segmentation foundation models, RS-OVSS remains con-strained by the scarcity of large-scale, semantically diverse datasets with accurate pixel-level annotations. Meanwhile, existing RS datasets contain abundant scene- and object-level annotations that offer valuable semantic information but lack precise pixel-wise supervision. To exploit these complementary sources, we propose HS-OVSeg , a unified training framework that integrates heterogeneous scene-, object-, and pixel-level supervision for RS-OVSS. Specif-ically, we generate pixel-wise pseudo-labels using SAM3, conditioned on scene-level class names and object-level class names with bounding boxes. Combined with ground-truth semantic masks, these pseudo-labels enable joint train-ing across all three annotation granularities through LoRA-based fine-tuning of SAM3. Our unified training spans 20 RS datasets, including 3 scene classification, 4 object detection, and 13 semantic segmentation datasets, with 1,000 images from the 7 classification and detection datasets manually annotated for pixel-level evaluation. HS-OVSeg substan-tially outperforms existing RS-OVSS models across both the 20 training-source and 5 held-out datasets, demonstrating strong multi-granularity segmentation capabilities under heterogeneous supervision. The code will be available at 

https://github.com/zhu-xlab/HS-OVSeg .

1. Introduction 

Remote sensing (RS) imagery captures rich semantics at multiple levels, from pixel-level land-cover categories (e.g., vegetation) and object-level entities (e.g., cars) to scene-level concepts (e.g., airports). Diverse RS applications require recognition beyond predefined categories, which conven-tional semantic segmentation models struggle to accommo-1arXiv:2611.32518v1 [cs.CV] 24 Nov 2026 date. Open-vocabulary semantic segmentation (OVSS) ad-dresses this limitation through flexible, text-driven seman-tic mapping [8, 17]. Advances in vision-language models (VLMs), such as CLIP [28], dense vision-language seg-mentation methods [8, 39, 55], and segmentation founda-tion models such as SAM3 [5] have further advanced open-vocabulary recognition and pixel-level localization. RS-OVSS has also advanced through RS-aware vision-language representations and segmentation [18, 50], training-free dense prediction [20, 27, 44, 49], and foundation model-based segmentation [21]. However, progress remains con-strained by the scarcity of large-scale, semantically diverse datasets with accurate pixel-level annotations. Existing RS segmentation datasets primarily cover land-use/land-cover categories, with limited representation of diverse object- and scene-level concepts. Obtaining comprehensive pixel-level annotations across these semantic levels is prohibitively ex-pensive. An opportunity lies in the heterogeneous annotations available across RS datasets. As illustrated in Fig. 2, scene labels indicate overall semantic concepts, object annotations provide class names and bounding boxes, and pixel annota-tions offer precise semantic masks. Coarser annotations of-ten cover diverse scene and object concepts underrepresented in segmentation datasets. However, their complementary se-mantic coverage comes with different spatial supervision granularities, making it challenging to integrate them into a unified RS-OVSS learning objective. To address this challenge, we propose HS-OVSeg , a uni-fied framework that jointly exploits scene-, object-, and pixel-level annotations for RS-OVSS. The key idea is to unify different spatial supervision granularities into a com-mon pixel-level learning signal while leveraging comple-mentary semantic coverage. Specifically, scene-level labels provide semantic cues for pixel-level pseudo-label genera-tion, while object-level annotations offer geometric guidance through bounding boxes. These pseudo-labels are combined with ground-truth (GT) semantic masks under a shared ob-jective for concept presence and pixel-level segmentation. Built upon SAM3, HS-OVSeg optimizes shared low-rank adapters [14] across all three annotation levels while keeping pretrained weights frozen. At inference, the adapted model segments pixel-, object-, and scene-level concepts using only images and category names, as illustrated in Fig. 2. We train HS-OVSeg on 20 RS datasets, comprising 3 scene classification, 4 object detection, and 13 semantic seg-mentation datasets, with 1,000 images from the 7 classifica-tion and detection datasets manually annotated for pixel-level evaluation. HS-OVSeg substantially outperforms existing RS-OVSS models across these datasets and demonstrates strong generalization on 5 held-out datasets. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose HS-OVSeg , a unified segmentation frame-work that jointly learns from semantically complementary scene-, object-, and pixel-level annotations despite their different spatial granularities, extending RS-OVSS train-ing beyond pixel-annotated datasets. • We develop an annotation-guided pseudo-labeling strat-egy that uses semantic cues, geometric guidance, and pre-trained spatial priors to convert coarse annotations into a common pixel-level supervision signal for joint learning. • HS-OVSeg substantially outperforms existing RS-OVSS methods across 20 training-source datasets spanning all three annotation granularities and demonstrates strong cross-dataset generalization on 5 held-out datasets. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Open-Vocabulary Semantic Segmentation 

OVSS extends semantic segmentation beyond predefined categories by leveraging VLMs, particularly CLIP [28]. Ex-isting methods generally follow training-based and training-free paradigms. Training-based approaches improve dense visual-semantic alignment through mask-aware adaptation in OVSeg [23], lightweight adapters in SAN [48], cost aggre-gation in CAT-Seg [8], and embedding balancing in EBSeg [32]. Training-free approaches exploit pretrained representa-tions through dense label extraction in MaskCLIP [55], atten-tion and feature refinement in SCLIP [39], ClearCLIP [16], and NACLIP [13], vision foundation model-guided attention in ProxyCLIP [17], and patch correlation reconstruction in CorrCLIP [52]. GEM [3] further explores zero-shot visual grounding, while Trident [35] integrates multiple founda-tion models for training-free segmentation. Recently, SAM3 [5] has advanced concept-conditioned segmentation through text prompts. In RS, RemoteCLIP [24], GeoRSCLIP [54], and SkySense-O [57] have advanced open-vocabulary visual understanding and semantic localization. For RS-OVSS, GSNet [50] combines RS-specific and general vision-language features, while RSKT-Seg [18] introduces efficient cost aggregation and RS knowledge transfer. Training-free approaches, including SegEarth-OV [20], RSCLIP [44], LD-Seg [49], and ReAttnCLIP [27], enhance dense semantic prediction through feature, attention, and spatial refinement. More recently, SegEarth-OV3 [21] explores SAM3-based segmentation through dual-head prediction fusion and cate-gory filtering. 

2.2. Multi-Task and Heterogeneous Supervision 

Weakly supervised approaches have explored learning seg-mentation from image-text supervision, such as text-guided region grouping in GroupViT [47] and region-word align-ment in OpenSeg [12]. Unlike existing approaches that focus on multi-task uni-fication or limited annotation types, HS-OVSeg jointly ex-ploits scene-, object-, and pixel-level supervision under a unified RS-OVSS objective. By integrating these hetero-geneous annotations, HS-OVSeg extends training beyond conventional pixel-annotated datasets, enabling broader se-mantic coverage across diverse scene and object concepts. 

3. HS-OVSeg 

As shown in Fig. 3, HS-OVSeg adopts a teacher–student framework to unify heterogeneous scene-, object-, and pixel-level supervision for RS-OVSS. The frozen pretrained SAM3 serves as the teacher, transforming scene- and object-level an-notations into pixel-level pseudo-labels. The student adapts SAM3 through low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [14], jointly learning from these pseudo-labels and GT semantic masks under a unified supervision objective. 

3.1. Preliminaries of SAM3 for HS-OVSeg 

We introduce only the SAM3 [5] components used in HS-OVSeg. Its Perception Encoder (PE) encodes the input im-age and category text, while a multimodal fusion encoder produces class-conditioned visual features; a query-based decoder predicts class-matching scores, bounding boxes, and instance masks; a presence head estimates whether the queried class occurs in the image, and a semantic head pro-duces its dense binary segmentation map; for object-level pseudo-label generation, a geometry encoder incorporates bounding boxes as spatial prompts. Quality of pseudo-labels. 5. Conclusion 

We presented HS-OVSeg, a unified multi-granularity frame-work for RS open-vocabulary semantic segmentation in-tegrating scene-, object-, and pixel-level supervision. By generating pixel-level pseudo-labels from coarse annotations using SAM3 and optimizing shared low-rank adapters, HS-OVSeg enables learning across annotation granularities. Ex-periments on 20 training-source datasets demonstrate sub-stantial improvements over existing methods, particularly for scene- and object-level segmentation, while maintaining strong pixel-level performance. Evaluation on five held-out datasets further demonstrates cross-dataset generalization. These results highlight the potential of heterogeneous super-vision for multi-granularity RS segmentation. 6. Experimental Setup 

6.1. Training Sources 

Heterogeneous training sources. We use 20 RS datasets: three scene-classification, four object-detection, and 13 semantic-segmentation sources. Figure 4 summarizes their sample counts, categories, and supervision granularities. The prepared splits contain 47,180 scene-labeled, 116,575 box-labeled, and 479,712 pixel-labeled samples, totaling 643,467 images or tiles. These counts represent prepared training en-tries, not unique full-resolution images or repeated sampling draws. Scene and detection sources provide only image-level and box-level annotations for adaptation; manually anno-tated semantic masks are reserved for testing. The scene sources are AID [45], NWPU-RESISC45 [6], and Pattern-Net [56]. The detection sources are DIOR-R [7, 19], DOTA-v2 [10], FAIR1M-2.0 [36], and xView [15]. The pixel-labeled sources are DeepGlobe-LandCover [9], FLAIR-HUB [11], Five-Billion-Pixels [38], FloodNet [29], GID-15 [37], Global-LoveDA [40–42], ISPRS-Potsdam [31], LandCover.ai [2], OpenEarthMap [46], RescueNet [30], SkyScapes [1], UAVid [25], and iSAID [51]. Global-LoveDA combines the EarthVQA and EarthVL-GLOBAL semantic subsets released in EarthVLSet, rather than the original LoveDA split. We retain the release names FLAIR-HUB, DOTA-v2, and FAIR1M-2.0 to distinguish dataset versions. Prepared sources may use subsets of the offi-cial collections: DOTA-v2 contains 2,248 tiles from 109 source images, while GID-15 and Five-Billion-Pixels share some source imagery. Thus, training counts do not represent unique original images. FLAIR-HUB uses 15 active CoSIA classes, and SkyScapes uses 19 classes from its Dense vari-ant. Background and excluded-class policies follow the released evaluation catalogs rather than each benchmark’s original protocol.

6.3. Training and Model Selection 

Optimization and data exposure. We target 30 epochs with AdamW, learning rate 10 −4, weight decay 0.05, poly-nomial decay power 0.9, and LoRA rank 8 at update scale 1.0. Four A100 GPUs each process four BF16 images. Each joint epoch samples 100,000 images at a Scene:Object:Pixel ratio of 1 : 2 : 4 , uniformly across datasets within each level, with up to 12 prompts per image. We validate every three epochs and at the final epoch, without using test masks for checkpoint selection. Augmentation applies random scaling in [0 .75 , 1.5] , a 518 × 518 crop, flips, and right-angle rota-tions, with corresponding mask and box transforms. Source, ablation, and OOD evaluations use 518 × 518 windows at stride 388. Overlapping categorical predictions use center-weighted voting with a separable Hann window floored at 0.05 per axis. Small images are enlarged to the minimum 12 window size, and predictions are restored to the GT grid. 

Ablation exposure and completed budgets. Single-level runs sample 14,288 Scene, 28,576 Object, or 57,136 Pixel images per epoch, approximately matching the correspond-ing exposure in joint training. Scene + Object uses 42,864 samples per epoch at a 1 : 2 ratio; Scene + Pixel uses 71,424 at approximately 1 : 4 ; and Object + Pixel uses 85,712 at ap-proximately 2 : 4 . Pairwise controls preserve per-level expo-sure. Unless explicitly ablated, all adapted variants share the HS-OVSeg architecture, preprocessing, and teacher-target construction. Comparisons use single runs; completed bud-gets are detailed below. 

Spatial augmentation. Scale factors are sampled from the nominal [0 .75 , 1.5] range, subject to a minimum supporting a 518 × 518 crop. Labels undergo identical transforms, with nearest-neighbor interpolation for categorical masks. At inference, small images are enlarged while preserving aspect ratio, and predictions are mapped back to the original grid. 

Model selection and completed training budgets. We set a 30-epoch budget and select checkpoints by mean val-idation mIoU across the 13 pixel-labeled datasets. Official validation splits are used when available; otherwise, ran-domly held-out training subsets serve as validation data and are excluded from adaptation. Test masks are used only for final evaluation. 

6.4. Evaluation Protocol 

All quantitative comparisons use single-label decoding: each pixel takes the highest-scoring fused-logit category, or back-ground if the maximum is negative and background is de-fined. Presence probabilities do not reweight these scores. Independent prompt-wise masks instead threshold sigmoid probabilities at 0.5; their IoUs are not directly comparable to single-label results. Direct pseudo-label evaluation in Sec. 4.2 uses binary category masks consistent with training targets. 

Valid pixels and averaging. Each dataset catalog defines queryable, background, excluded, and ignore categories. We accumulate dataset-level confusion counts before computing mIoU, averaging queryable classes with nonzero prediction– GT union. Background is excluded from the class mean, although valid background pixels may yield false positives; ignore pixels are excluded. Level means cover 3 Scene, 4 Object, or 13 Pixel datasets. The overall source mean directly averages all 20 datasets, while the five-dataset held-out mean is computed separately. Neither is an average of per-image IoUs.

11. Implementation Details of HS-OVSeg 

11.1. Model Adaptation 

Eligible linear and convolutional weights receive low-rank updates; biases and one-dimensional normalization parame-ters remain frozen. Each tensor has independent factors with effective rank min(8 , d out , d in ) and update scale one, with-out rank division. An adapter receives gradients only from objectives along its computation path. Presence and fused segmentation losses update their respective paths, including both prediction branches and shared decoder components. No instance-matching loss is used. Only the frozen teacher receives geometry prompts. The trainable student fusion head propagates segmentation gradients through both inputs. 

11.2. Online Pseudo-label Generation 

The teacher uses the frozen pretrained weights with LoRA disabled and stop-gradient; it receives no exponential-moving-average updates. Teacher and student share aug-mented images and category text, while the object teacher also receives annotated boxes. Pseudo-labels are regenerated for each sampled view, not cached offline. Semantic logits are resized bilinearly before sigmoid; object query masks undergo sigmoid and maximum aggregation before resizing, as in Eq. (12). GT masks retain discrete labels after spatial transforms. 11.3. Category and Instance Sampling 

Each image uses up to 12 prompts from its dataset vocab-ulary. Positives come from scene labels, object categories retained after augmentation, or valid semantic masks. If pos-itives exceed the budget, a seed-dependent subset is chosen; remaining slots use annotation-absent categories without a fixed positive-to-negative ratio. Thus, per-image CE does not use a global cross-dataset category catalog. Object batches retain at most 36 boxes, first distribut-ing them across sampled image–category groups and then filling the remaining budget. Categories excluded by in-stance sampling are omitted from prompts rather than treated as negatives; omitted boxes for retained categories are ex-cluded from pixel supervision. Negative sampling priori-tizes categories seen in other images from the same dataset and batch, using at most half the negative budget rounded upward when available. Remaining negatives come from annotation-absent categories, which may still be visually present if annotations are incomplete.
