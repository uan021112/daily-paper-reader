Reconstructing dynamic scenes from RGB video requires reasoning about both 3D geometry
and motion across viewpoints, yet existing methods either split reconstruction and tracking into
fragmented pipelines or face prohibitive dense spatiotemporal decoding costs. We introduce MoGe-
4D, a feed-forward framework that unifies geometry and motion prediction via ordered queries of
paired allocentric and egocentric dynamic point maps. Each query maps source pixels to a target
time simultaneously in both camera frames, resolving relative camera pose in closed form while
flexibly serving depth, dynamic registration, and dense 3D tracking. For efficient dense querying,
MoGe-4D decouples scene modeling from query decoding: a high-capacity context encoder caches
coarse global spatiotemporal features once, while a lightweight query encoder rapidly decodes
dense point maps at flexible resolutions. Furthermore, as supervising dense 4D queries demands
continuous 3D correspondences whose raw trajectory storage is prohibitive, we introduce RigIDTrack,
a synthetic data pipeline and dataset that compresses surface dynamics into rigidity-regularized
affine bodies, recovering exact arbitrary-frame 3D trajectories with ∼15% storage overhead over
RGB-D video. Across diverse benchmarks, MoGe-4D achieves state-of-the-art dense 3D tracking,
matches dedicated 3D foundation models in reconstruction, and runs substantially faster than prior
4D baselines. Project Page: https://microsoft.github.io/MoGe-4D

1 Introduction
Reconstructing a dynamic world from RGB video requires reasoning about 3D scene structure
and motion across changing viewpoints. While feed-forward 3D foundation models [32, 59, 65, 66]
excel at static multiview reconstruction, extending them to dynamic 4D scenes has largely relied
on appending motion-specific branches or separate tracking modules [11, 34, 36, 53], which
fragments geometry and motion into decoupled tasks. Alternatively, D4RT [79] unifies both tasks
by formulating 4D prediction as spatiotemporal queries over an encoded scene representation.
Yet because its decoder operates point by point, scaling to dense prediction across space and
time incurs prohibitive computational costs. This tension exposes a central challenge for 4D
foundation models: retaining the generality of query-based prediction while enabling efficient,
dense evaluation over video sequences.
Toward a practical and scalable 4D foundation model, we pursue three complementary design
goals. First, unification: because 3D geometry and motion both reduce to locating scene points
across space and time, a single prediction interface can learn and service both coherently without
fragmented task-specific branches. Second, flexibility: reconstruction aggregates observations
across views, whereas tracking follows surface points over time; the framework should therefore
support arbitrary source–target query combinations at task-adaptive output resolutions. Third,
efficiency: as video analysis requires hundreds of dense spatiotemporal queries, computation and
memory must scale tractably with sequence length and query count.
We introduce MoGe-4D, a feed-forward framework that realizes these goals through a unified
query interface and a dual-encoder architecture (Fig. 2). Given an unposed video, an ordered
source–target frame query predicts paired allocentric and egocentric point maps, locating source-
frame pixels at the target time in the source and target camera coordinate systems, respectively
(Fig. 2, left). Pairing both maps within a query directly captures dynamic geometry and relative
camera pose, while varying source–target query combinations covers video depth estimation,
multiview dynamic registration, and dense 3D tracking (Fig. 2, middle). To keep dense evaluation
tractable, we decouple scene modeling from query decoding via a context–query dual encoder
(Fig. 2, right): a high-capacity context encoder runs once at coarse resolution to cache global
spatiotemporal features, while a lightweight query encoder re-encodes source images at flexible
resolutions to decode dense point maps. This design amortizes global video computation across
queries and decouples output resolution from the context backbone at high throughput.
Supervising dense 4D prediction requires continuous 3D correspondences across arbitrary
frames alongside surface geometry, posing a severe storage bottleneck for synthetic data pipelines.
Exploiting the local coherence of surface motion even under non-rigid deformation, we introduce
RigIDTrack, a synthetic data pipeline and data format that clusters dense surface elements into
2


rigidity-regularized affine bodies, compressing millions of dynamic mesh faces into a few thousand
shared transformation trajectories. This format recovers exact 3D trajectories for any pixel across
arbitrary times and camera frames with only ∼15% storage overhead beyond standard RGB-D
video, establishing a scalable foundation for dense 4D pretraining.
Across diverse multiview reconstruction and 3D tracking benchmarks, MoGe-4D outperforms
existing tracking methods on all aggregate metrics, reducing world-space relative point error
by 28%–44% across model variants. At the same time, its unified predictions match dedicated
state-of-the-art 3D reconstruction models such as π3, DA3, and VGGT-Ωwhile surpassing prior
unified 4D methods. Meanwhile, our dual-encoder design substantially accelerates dense 4D
reconstruction and tracking over existing baselines.
2 Related Work
2.1 Feed-forward 3D reconstruction
Feed-forward 4D models build on advances in 3D reconstruction.
Classical methods esti-
mate geometry and cameras via iterative optimization [47, 73], which DROID-SLAM [56] and
MegaSaM [31] augment with learned correspondence, depth, or flow priors, while monocular
models [24, 63, 70, 72, 74] scale learned priors for direct single-image geometry prediction. For
multiview reconstruction, DUSt3R [65] directly regresses pixel-aligned point maps from unposed
image pairs, with extensions improving matching [29], scaling to multiple views [10, 14, 54, 71],
maintaining persistent state [28, 58, 62], accepting geometric inputs [25], or handling dynamic
scenes [5, 80]. VGGT [59] further scales joint camera and geometry prediction, followed by π3,
Depth Anything 3, and VGGT-Ωwith reference-free point maps, depth–ray prediction, and larger-
scale training [32, 60, 66]. Most related in architecture, DAGE [38] pairs π3 with MoGe-2 [64] in
a dual-stream design for high-resolution 3D geometry.
2.2 Dynamic reconstruction and tracking
Optimization-based methods fit scene-specific dynamic NeRFs [30, 41, 42, 45], 3D Gaussians [35, 67],
or low-rank motion bases [61] using depth and track priors.
For feed-forward 3D tracking,
SpatialTracker [68, 69] and TAPIP3D [78] track queried points via correspondence and geometric
reasoning, whereas Trace Anything [33] regresses continuous-time B-spline trajectories. Joint 4D
models extend point-map regression to motion: DPM [52] and St4RTrack [11] predict pairwise
dynamic point maps, Flow4R [46] predicts points and scene flow, V-DPM [53] extends VGGT to
video point maps, 4RC [36] and Any4D [23] decode geometry and allocentric motion from shared
video features, and Track4World [34] uses 3D correlation for dense flow.
2.3 Unified 4D representations
Table 1 compares feed-forward 4D prediction paradigms across input scope, motion representation,
and query granularity. Pairwise point-map methods [11, 52] operate on two-view inputs without
sequence-level context, coupling distinct tasks across two source images (cross-time motion for one
view and cross-camera geometry for the other) in a single pass with separate decoders and inter-
map redundancy. Decoupled flow-and-geometry methods [23, 34, 36, 46] separate static geometry
from scene flow, leveraging flow inductive biases at the cost of a coordinate trade-off: allocentric
flow removes camera motion yet suffers from relative-motion ambiguity without static references,
whereas egocentric flow entangles camera and object motion. V-DPM [53] scales dynamic point
maps to sequences, but anchors all predictions to a single privileged frame, accumulating coordinate
drift away from the reference while requiring repeated evaluation of a heavy conditional decoder
across target times. As a conceptual turning point, D4RT [79] formalizes a unified spatiotemporal
query space, yet its unconstrained query axes and point-wise granularity make dense per-frame
decoding computationally expensive.
MoGe-4D instead adopts a concise unified point map query interface (Table 1, bottom), offering:
(i) Both allocentric and egocentric dynamics: combining the advantages of world-referenced motion
and observer-relative coordinates. (ii) Shared intra-query computation: because both maps in a

3.2 Architecture
MoGe-4D pairs a high-capacity context encoder with a lightweight query encoder (Fig. 3). The
context encoder processes the input sequence once, while the query encoder re-encodes each source
image conditioned on the cached source and target context:

source-aligned query features for pair (i, j).
Context encoder. To relate observations across viewpoints and time, the context encoder applies
a high-capacity ViT with cross-frame attention over the full sequence, concentrating over 90%
of the model parameters in this stage while operating on a coarse spatial token grid to curb the
quadratic cost of global attention on long videos. Multi-layer spatial features from each frame are
computed once and cached, amortizing scene-wide spatiotemporal reasoning across all subsequent
reconstruction and tracking queries. To explore different pretrained representations for this role,
we train three model variants initialized from DINOv2, V-JEPA 2.1, and VGGT-Ω.
Feature bridges. Feature bridges project selected context-layer features into the representation
spaces of corresponding query blocks via LayerNorm and a linear layer, and are initialized with a
standalone feature-alignment stage before end-to-end training to bridge both encoders’ pretrained
representations.
Query encoder. For each query (i, j), a pretrained ViT-Base query encoder re-encodes source
image i to recover fine spatial detail omitted by the coarse context grid. At conditioned blocks,
bridged context features from frames i and j supply additional keys and values alongside the
query tokens, fusing local visual detail with global scene context while keeping tokens aligned
with the source image grid. Because this conditioning requires no spatial grid matching between
streams, query token budgets can be adjusted freely at inference to trade off spatial detail and
speed without recomputing the cached context.
Decoders. From the source-aligned features Zi→j, a shared convolutional neck branches into
separate heads that decode the paired point maps ( bP allo
i→j, bP ego
i→j), surface tangents, point-wise
uncertainty, and masks for visibility, sky, and dynamic regions. Meanwhile, lightweight MLP heads
operate directly on context tokens to predict metric scale and pairwise covisibility. Architectural
specifications and auxiliary output definitions are detailed in Appendix A.1.

3.3 Training
We train MoGe-4D end to end on a mixture of synthetic and real, dynamic and static multiview
datasets, sampling O(T) source–target queries per clip to keep decoding costs manageable (full
loss definitions and schedules in Appendices A.2 and A.3). After aligning the affine-invariant
predictions to ground truth in each camera frame via ROE scale–shift alignment [63], we supervise
the model with three groups of objectives (using inverse-distance-weighted L1 errors for both
geometry and motion):
• Geometry losses. A global point-map loss supervises overall scene layout, while a multi-scale
local point-map loss [26] fits local translations to supervise regional shape, complemented by
an edge loss on neighboring-point differences for surface continuity.
• Motion losses. To further reinforce temporal coherence, we penalize errors on point-map
differences between adjacent sampled target times for each source frame, fitting translation
offsets to absorb bulk displacement and camera-origin residuals. Applied at two granularities,
a global motion loss constrains scene-wide dynamics, while a local motion loss isolates
intra-region rotation, articulation, and deformation.
• Auxiliary losses. Dedicated objectives supervise surface tangents, point-wise uncertainty,
visibility/sky/dynamic masks, metric scale, and pairwise covisibility.
Training curriculum. Training begins with shorter clips and smaller query-token budgets on
dynamic tracking datasets to establish cross-time prediction at low cost, then progressively scales
both budgets while expanding to the full mixture of dynamic and static multiview data.

4 RigIDTrack
Training dense 4D models on synthetic data requires persistent cross-frame correspondences
through object motion, camera motion, and occlusion. Yet storing explicit trajectories for every
pixel or mesh face is prohibitively expensive and locally redundant, as nearby surfaces move
coherently even on articulated or deforming objects. We introduce RigIDTrack, a synthetic data
pipeline and representation format that compresses dense surface motion into rigidity-regularized
affine bodies, i.e., surface clusters sharing transformation trajectories (Fig. 5). This compact
format supports dense 4D supervision across arbitrary source pixels, target times, and camera
frames (see Appendix B for full pipeline and algorithm details).
Dynamic scene generation. We procedurally compose indoor and outdoor environments in Blender
from diverse 3D scene and object assets, combining skeletal animation, non-rigid deformation,
and rigid-body physics. Independently moving cameras follow smooth, collision-aware paths that
alternate between free exploration and tracking nearby objects, producing varied object dynamics,
viewpoint changes, and occlusions.
Affine-body motion compression. To compress surface motion, we first fit rigidity-regularized
per-face affine trajectories to capture local stretch and shear beyond pure rigid motion. We
then cluster faces with consistent relative motion under a prescribed error tolerance and refit a
shared transformation trajectory for each body (with static scene geometry grouped into a single
background body). This formulation operates directly on observed mesh motion without requiring
predefined rigid parts, skeletons, or skinning weights, naturally accommodating rigid, articulated,
and general deforming assets alike. In practice, it compresses millions of dynamic faces into a few
thousand trajectories with sub-pixel level reprojection error (Fig. 6).
Dense tracking ground truth recovery. For each rendered frame t, we store the RGB image,
depth Dt(u), intrinsics Kt, world-to-camera extrinsics Et, pixelwise body ID Bt(u), and per-body
world affine transforms {Ab,t}. Unlike mesh-based formats that require per-pixel triangle IDs and
barycentric interpolation on full meshes [18], associating each pixel with a single body ID enables
direct coordinate transport without storing or querying mesh geometry. For a valid source pixel u
belonging to body b = Bi(u), its 3D position at target time j in camera frame k is recovered as

5.1 3D Tracking
Setup and baselines. We evaluate 32-frame clips (5 fps) seeded from the first frame across six
synthetic and real-world benchmarks: DynamicReplica [22], PointOdyssey [81], Syn4D [18], Aria
Digital Twin (ADT) [40], Panoptic Studio (PStudio) [20], and the held-out test split of RigIDTrack.
Trajectories are evaluated in both camera and world space (after sequence-level scale and Sim(3)
alignment, respectively) over all valid points and the visible subset, using relative end-point error
(RelEPE, normalized by ground-truth camera distance), AUC25, and occlusion accuracy (OA).
Baselines include St4RTrack [11], Open-D4RT1, Any4D [23], Trace Anything [33], V-DPM [53],
Track4World [34], and 4RC [36].
Results. All three MoGe-4D variants outperform prior methods across every averaged tracking
metric (Table 2(a)). The V-JEPA 2.1 variant performs best overall, reflecting the benefit of
spatiotemporal video pretraining for motion prediction. Compared with the strongest tracking
baselines (V-DPM and 4RC), it reduces all-point RelEPE by 47.5% in camera space (0.084 vs.
0.160) and 44.2% in world space (0.077 vs. 0.138), while raising world-space AUC from 72.65% to
78.89%.

Setup and baselines. We evaluate up to 32 unposed frames per clip (subsampled to keep ≥50%
adjacent-view overlap) across six benchmarks: 7-Scenes [50], Bonn [39], ETH3D [49], KITTI [15],
Sintel [3], and ScanNet [6]. We measure scale-/affine-aligned depth, camera pose error (ATE,

trained from DINOv2 initialization under our exact data, batch size, and step budget.

5.4 Ablation Study
Dual-encoder capacity allocation. In Figure 9(a), enlarging the context encoder substantially
reduces both reconstruction and tracking errors, whereas enlarging the query encoder from ViT-B
to ViT-L brings only minor gains at ∼2.5× the per-query latency. Thus, global spatiotemporal
reasoning in the context encoder governs overall representational capacity, allowing a lightweight
ViT-B query encoder to decode dense predictions accurately at low per-query cost.
Feature-bridge alignment and query attention. In Table 4, randomly initializing the feature
bridges instead of pre-aligning them degrades all metrics, dropping global-point AUC from 71.17%
to 62.65%. Pre-aligning the bridge projections helps the query encoder leverage pretrained context
features effectively during end-to-end training. Removing the query stream’s own keys and values
in conditioned blocks (w/o self KV, leaving pure cross-attention) also causes a modest drop across
both tasks, supporting the retention of intra-frame self-attention alongside context conditioning.
Point-map pose recovery vs. direct regression. Replacing closed-form point-map pose recovery
with direct regression from a context camera token (trained with the camera loss of π3 [66])
substantially degrades pose accuracy: ATE increases from 0.057 to 0.125, and global-point AUC
drops from 71.17% to 54.11% (Table 4). The gap is especially pronounced under wide baselines,
indicating that solving poses from confidence-weighted 3D correspondences can be more reliable
than direct token regression in challenging, long-range scenarios.
Inference token budgets. As per-frame token budgets vary in Figures 9(b–d), error reductions
saturate around 500 context tokens, 900 map-query tokens, and 250 pose-query tokens; we adopt
these as our default settings for a favorable accuracy–speed trade-off. Increasing map-query tokens
beyond 900 barely changes aggregate metrics but progressively sharpens fine geometric boundaries
(Figure 10), enabling test-time adjustment between inference speed and spatial detail without
re-running the heavy encoder.

6 Limitations and Discussions
While MoGe-4D generalizes well across diverse dynamic scenes, we discuss its current boundaries
and directions for future improvement below.
Object boundaries and small structures. Sharp depth and motion discontinuities at object
boundaries, as well as very thin structures, remain challenging. Finite query-token budgets and
convolutional decoding can smooth abrupt 3D transitions across neighboring pixels, causing
flying-point artifacts near edges or losing geometry on few-pixel structures. Confidence and edge
filtering suppress these outliers, while higher-resolution or adaptive decoding could better preserve
detail.
Domain-specific adaptation. MoGe-4D targets general scene-level geometry and motion perception,
whereas specialized domains such as 3D facial capture and articulated hand tracking demand finer
domain-specific precision beyond our current scope. Adapting the model with targeted real-world
supervision and synthetic data via agentic 3D modeling (Appendix B.1) is a promising direction.
Sequence-length extrapolation. Although trained on clips of at most 32 frames, our three variants
extrapolate differently to longer sequences (Appendix C.5). The DINOv2 variant generalizes most
robustly beyond 500 frames, benefiting from strong intra-frame features and permutation-invariant
alternating attention without temporal positional encodings. VGGT-Ωshares this alternating
attention but extrapolates slightly less well, likely due to its privileged reference-frame embedding,
whereas V-JEPA 2.1 degrades beyond 100 frames even with YaRN extrapolation [43] due to its
13


reliance on temporal RoPE. More broadly, because full global attention scales quadratically with
sequence length, feed-forward inference over hundreds of frames serves primarily as a generalization
probe rather than a practical deployment mode. Pairing our query interface with streamable or
recurrent context encoders is a natural direction for long-horizon 4D perception.
7 Conclusion
We presented MoGe-4D, a feed-forward framework that unifies dense 3D geometry and motion
prediction by querying paired allocentric and egocentric point maps. By decoupling sequence-wide
spatiotemporal reasoning in a coarse context encoder from on-demand decoding in a lightweight
query encoder, our model reuses global scene representations across arbitrary query combinations
and output resolutions. To make training at this dense spatiotemporal granularity practical,
we developed RigIDTrack, a synthetic data pipeline and compact representation that compress
surface dynamics into rigidity-regularized affine bodies for arbitrary-view trajectory supervision.
Together, these designs achieve state-of-the-art dense 3D tracking, reconstruction accuracy on
par with dedicated 3D models, and substantially faster 4D inference, demonstrating that dense
spatiotemporal point-map querying provides an effective and scalable foundation for dynamic 4D
scene perception.

A MoGe-4D Implementation Details
A.1 Architecture and Initialization
Context backbone variants. We instantiate the context encoder with DINOv2 ViT-g/14, V-JEPA
2.1 ViT-G/16, or the VGGT-Ωbackbone, containing approximately 1.14B, 1.01B, and 0.91B
parameters, respectively (excluding the bridges, query encoder, and prediction heads; for VGGT-Ω,
we retain its image encoder and multiview aggregator without its task heads). Each variant
outputs multi-layer per-frame spatial features after cross-frame interaction, which are cached and
reused across all source–target queries. The DINOv2 variant omits frame positional encoding
to support unordered image sets, whereas the V-JEPA 2.1 variant applies 3D rotary positional
embeddings [51] for temporally ordered video.
Query backbone and conditioning. All three variants share a DINOv2 ViT-Base query encoder
with 12 transformer blocks, a feature width of 768, and 12 attention heads. Eight of the 12 blocks
receive bridged context keys and values alongside the query stream’s own keys and values (six
blocks take both source and target context; two take source context only), while the remaining
four blocks apply standard self-attention.
Cross-backbone bridges. Because the context and query encoders have different feature dimensions
and intermediate representations, each bridge applies LayerNorm followed by a linear projection
from a selected context layer to the corresponding query-backbone layer. We pretrain the bridges
via standalone feature alignment on in-the-wild images and videos for 2,000 steps with a batch
size of 8, minimizing L2 and cosine distance between projected context features and target
query-backbone features. At each conditioned query block, the bridged context features are then
projected into the additional keys and values described above.
Dense prediction heads. A shared convolutional neck decodes the spatial query features into the
paired point maps ( bPi→j,i, bPi→j,j) and auxiliary maps. For each coordinate system, a dedicated
tangent head predicts scale-invariant horizontal and vertical surface tangents bTi→j,k ∈RHi×Wi×2×3,

B RigIDTrack Data Pipeline Details
Sections B.1–B.3 detail our synthetic RigIDTrack dataset, including procedural dynamic scene
generation, affine-body motion compression and rendering, and dataset statistics. Beyond synthetic
rendering, Section B.4 presents an annotation pipeline that builds RigIDTrack representations
from existing dynamic RGB-D videos by lifting 2D tracks and discovering motion-consistent affine
bodies. Section B.5 compares RigIDTrack with related dynamic 3D tracking datasets.
B.1 Procedural Dynamic Scene Generation
Built with Blender, the generator procedurally samples indoor and outdoor environments and
populates them with humans, animated objects, static props, and vehicles drawn from Objaverse [7],
Objaverse-XL [8], and licensed commercial assets, randomizing their categories, counts, scales,
and placements. Smooth, collision-aware camera trajectories are simulated with varying speeds
and spatial ranges, guiding object placement toward visible regions while rejecting intersecting
bounding boxes. Objects can have native skeletal or non-rigid animation and undergo rigid-body
physics simulation with collision proxies and turbulence-driven impulses. Camera orientation
alternates between free exploration and gazing at nearby visible objects.
Limitations and agentic scene synthesis. While rule-based procedural generation scales effectively,
the resulting appearance and motion distributions remain constrained by hand-crafted placement
and simulation rules.
Given recent advances in multimodal large language model (MLLM)
agents for 3D modeling, agentic workflows offer a more scalable and higher-quality alternative for
synthesizing diverse dynamic scenes. Crucially, our affine-body compression (Sec. B.2) operates
directly on animated meshes, thus it is agnostic to upstream scene authoring and can directly
process agent-generated scenes.

Annotation pipeline. The pipeline runs overlapping sliding windows to incrementally discover
affine bodies from lifted point trajectories, maintaining registered bodies and an active subset for
motion grouping.
1) Trajectory lifting. We use AllTracker [17], a 2D Dense Tracking model, to track pixels from
a reference frame within the window, and the corresponding per-frame depths and camera
parameters lift these 2D tracks into world-space 3D trajectories.
2) Adaptive patch extraction. The trajectory map is subdivided into image-space patches until
each patch’s trajectories can be fitted by a shared affine motion within the error tolerance,
discarding patches that are too small or insufficiently supported. These patches replace
mesh faces as the motion primitives in the subsequent grouping step.
3) Motion grouping and body association. The patches and active bodies are jointly merged
using the motion-grouping algorithm (Algorithm 1), extended to accommodate missing or
invalid trajectory observations. Motion consistency is evaluated over valid shared frames with
sufficient temporal overlap. Patches sharing motion with a registered body inherit its identity
and extend its lifetime; otherwise, they form new bodies, grouping mutually consistent
patches together. A body that receives no new patches is retired when its remaining overlap
expires in the next window.
4) Transformation fitting and ID-map rendering. We incrementally refit body transformations
across overlapping windows and generate body-ID maps by projecting and rasterizing the
valid, body-labeled surface geometry.
Streaming and long-range tracking. The sliding-window pipeline processes videos incrementally,
with total processing time growing linearly with video length. Body identities can persist across
multiple tracker windows, extending tracking beyond the lifespan of individual 2D tracks. Tracking
at the body level also supports partial occlusion: when a point’s 2D track or depth observation
is lost, its position can still be inferred from the shared body transformation estimated from
sufficiently supported visible parts. By discovering shared motion among patches, the algorithm
can thus maintain correspondences beyond those directly provided by the 2D tracker and depth.
Input requirements and conservative labeling. The algorithm depends on the quality of RGB-D
observations, camera parameters, and 2D tracking. In particular, high-quality, temporally stable
depth is important, as depth jitter can prevent reliable motion fitting and association. We therefore
adopt a conservative tracking principle: rather than seeking to recover every body or connect every
long-range correspondence, we require accepted patch fits and body associations to satisfy the
prescribed error tolerance on valid observations. Unreliable regions are left unmerged or discarded,
prioritizing the reliability of retained annotations over coverage.

C.5 Long-Horizon Trajectory Evaluation on Oxford-Spires
Figure 15 reports the complete long-horizon camera trajectory results across all 13 evaluated
500-frame sequences from the Oxford-Spires dataset [55], comparing all three MoGe-4D variants
(DINOv2, V-JEPA 2.1, and VGGT-Ω) alongside VGGT-Ω, Depth Anything 3, π3, LingBot-Map,
and MapAnything. Each method processes the full 500-frame sequence in a single feed-forward
pass without sliding windows. Consistent with the analysis in Section 6, our permutation-invariant
DINOv2 variant generalizes most reliably to 500-frame sequences while running substantially
faster than competing feed-forward reconstruction baselines.
