## Abstract 

Visual world models predict future visual states con-ditioned on actions, but recursive errors in entity poses and spatial relations limit their long-horizon prediction for robot planning and control. We ask whether a sepa-rately predicted relational state can correct this drift with-out restructuring the visual model. This is challenging be-cause graph nodes and visual states share neither elements nor a feature space. We propose Joint Graph–Visual Dy-namics Correction (JointGV), a plug-in based on a cross-granularity dynamics hypothesis: despite their different representations, graph-node and visual-state changes de-scribe the same physical transition. JointGV rolls out a task-conditioned scene graph alongside the visual world model, couples their predicted changes, and combines the resulting graph-motion context with graph-predicted node positions to compute a bounded residual. Graph-learning and correction gradients do not propagate into the orig-inal visual dynamics. We attach JointGV to DINO-WM (spatial tokens) and DreamerV3 (a global latent state), evaluating both on DMC Cheetah Run and Walker Walk across three seeds and extending the evaluation to contact-rich manipulation with DMC Finger Turn Hard. Across 32-step rollouts, JointGV improves structural and visual-prediction accuracy, reducing joint-position error by 11– 28%. For spatial tokens, learned graph–visual change cou-pling gives lower error than broadcasting the same mean graph-motion context to every token, particularly when non-adjacent parts appear close together in the image. On real-robot inverted-pendulum sequences, JointGV reduces DINO-WM’s 32-step pole-angle error from 12 .56 ◦ to 6.69 ◦

and improves DreamerV3 and LeWorldModel as well. In simulated inverted-pendulum control, an actor trained with JointGV-corrected rollouts completes 46 of 50 episodes, compared with none using the original rollouts.

## 1. Introduction 

Visual world models predict how a robot and its environ-ment change under an action. Robot planning evaluates multi-step action sequences, making recursive rollout nec-essary [7, 27, 34]. Reusing each predicted state as the next input, however, accumulates errors in entity poses and spa-tial relations. Figure 1 shows this structural drift in open-loop predictions initialized from RGB observations of a physical inverted-pendulum robot: the predicted pole pro-gressively deviates and blurs, while its angle error increases with rollout horizon across three visual world models. Existing approaches reduce long-horizon error by chang-ing the native predictive representation [29, 33], using aux-iliary trajectory supervision [31], learning residuals within the original latent pathway [9, 21], or replacing the visual state with object- or graph-centric dynamics [4, 11]. These approaches modify the visual representation, derive correc-tion from the same latent pathway, or require a separately trained structured world model. They do not address how an independently predicted relational rollout can correct an existing visual state when their elements and feature spaces do not correspond. Our key hypothesis is that, although graph nodes and visual states differ in granularity and content, their action-induced changes describe the same physical transi-tion. We propose Joint Graph–Visual Dynamics Correc-tion (JointGV), which rolls out a task-conditioned scene graph alongside an existing visual world model and couples their predicted changes in a shared space. Graph-learning and correction gradients do not propagate into the original visual encoder or dynamics. Our contributions are: (1) a cross-granularity graph– visual correction interface for predictive states without shared elements or feature spaces; (2) a plug-in architec-ture that uses a separately predicted relational rollout while preserving the original visual dynamics; and (3) three-seed evaluation across four DMC locomotion pairings and a contact-rich DMC manipulation task, real-robot prediction, simulated control, and analyses identifying when learned graph–visual coupling is most useful. 

## 2. Related Work 

Visual world models for robot learning. Visual world models learn action-conditioned states for planning and control. DayDreamer and DreamerV3 learn behaviors in latent imagination [7, 27]. DINO-WM predicts DINOv2 patch features, IRASim predicts robot–object videos, and LeWorldModel predicts image embeddings [13, 15, 34, 35]. 

Object-centric and graph-structured world models. 

Graph networks model physical systems through entity states and relational message passing [12, 17, 18]. C-SWM, G-SWM, SlotFormer, and DAFT-RL build object interac-tions into the predictive state [4, 8, 11, 28], while recent methods extend structured prediction to multimodal, recur-rent, and evolving graphs [1, 5, 19, 26]. These methods use the structured state as part of the world model itself. JointGV instead preserves an existing visual state and uses an independently predicted graph rollout as a correction source. 

Structure and residual correction of world models. 

GRWM and GeoWorld alter the geometry of the native pre-dictive representation [29, 33]. 4D-WAM aligns spatially corresponding video and trajectory-field changes as auxil-iary training supervision [31]. ReDRAW and Sandwich-Residuals instead learn residuals within the original latent pathway to adapt to dynamics or distribution shift [9, 21]. Structure-guided video prediction generates frames from predicted poses or keypoints [14, 24], and PhyDNet sep-arates physical dynamics from residual factors [10]; these methods train the visual predictor around the structured state. JointGV addresses a different interface: it couples an independently predicted scene-graph rollout with visual states that have no shared indexing or feature space, then corrects the visual prediction without propagating correc-tion gradients into the original visual model.

3.4. Graph–Visual Correction Interface 

The interface is defined on predicted changes rather than absolute features. Graph nodes and visual states retain dif-ferent information, but the changes induced by the same ac-tion isolate the physical transition represented by both pre-dictions. The graph rollout then supplies two complemen-tary cues: node changes describe how task-relevant entities evolve, while predicted node positions provide their spatial reference. This separation allows the same correction prin-ciple to operate on both spatial tokens and a global latent state. JointGV operationalizes the cross-granularity dynamics hypothesis by coupling projected graph-node and visual-state changes. The two representations need not share el-ements, cardinality, or a feature space, and the interface is not tied to a particular coupling operator. We use Sinkhorn optimal transport for spatial tokens and normalized atten-tion pooling for a global latent state.

3.5. Training Objective 

JointGV trains graph observation, graph dynamics, graph– visual alignment, and bounded correction while retaining the original visual-model objective. The uncorrected visual prediction is detached before alignment and correction, so these losses do not update the original visual encoder or dynamics. During recursive correction training, the visual model and graph dynamics are evaluated without gradients, and only the correction module is updated. The complete objective and recursive losses are provided in Sec. S4.

4.2. Long-Horizon Prediction with DINO-WM 

Table 1 and Fig. 3 show that JointGV reduces joint-position error across all reported horizons, improves the latent and decoded-image metrics, and better preserves articulated shape during rollout. Longer self-fed visual training improves DINO-WM at later horizons but worsens its shorter-horizon joint-position error. JointGV improves both the Kv = 1 and Kv = 8 

backbones, showing that its gain is not explained by longer visual training alone. 

Contact-rich manipulation. On DMC Finger Turn Hard, JointGV reduces 32-step joint-position error by 27.6% and RGB MSE by 51.8%, while better preserving the finger–spinner configuration over the rollout (Table 3 and Fig. S7). Additional metrics are provided in Sec. S6. 

4.3. Evidence for Graph–Visual Change Coupling 

Table 1 first separates graph-conditioned correction from residual capacity and additional supervision. The visual-only residual changes the 32-step joint-position error only from 0.898 to 0.893 pixels, whereas JointGV reduces it to 

0.791 pixels. Across three seeds, the corresponding reduc-tions are 4.3% ± 4.3% and 16 .0% ± 3.7% . Supplying the residual with the same node annotations, but no predicted graph, reaches only 0.883 pixels. The larger gain therefore comes from conditioning the correction on the predicted graph rollout, rather than from residual capacity or matched node supervision alone. Mean graph-motion broadcasting improves the matched visual backbone, showing that the predicted graph rollout already provides a useful correction signal without token-specific routing. Cosine and OT coupling further reduce the 32-step error to 0.793 and 0.791 pixels, respectively. Their similar accuracy shows that the correction interface is not tied to one operator. The additional gain over broadcasting is largest when non-adjacent parts approach in image space, where assigning differentiated graph motion becomes more important. An independently trained heatmap-based key-point network also measures a 13 .6% reduction and finds improvement on 95 .4% of the test windows. Complete paired comparisons and confidence intervals, and stratified analyses are provided in Secs. S6 and S6.8.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion 

JointGV improves long-horizon prediction across spatial-token and global-latent visual models, DMC articulated locomotion,contact-rich DMC manipulation, and real-robot sequences. These results support the central cross-granularity hypothesis: graph nodes and visual states with-out shared elements can still be coupled through changes in-duced by the same action. The stronger coupling advantage when non-adjacent parts approach further identifies where learned graph–visual correspondence is most useful. The coupling analysis clarifies the role of graph–visual alignment. Cosine and OT coupling obtain similar overall accuracy, indicating that the correction interface is not tied to a particular operator. Both outperform broadcasting the same mean graph motion to every visual token, and this dif-ference grows when non-adjacent parts approach in image space. The benefit therefore comes from associating visual changes with differentiated graph motion, particularly when the projected structure becomes ambiguous. The visual-only and supervision-matched residual con-trols show that the gain is not explained by correction capac-ity or additional node annotations. Longer visual training also does not reproduce JointGV’s short- and long-horizon 8improvements, while propagating correction gradients into the original visual dynamics degrades their native predic-tion. JointGV therefore uses the graph rollout as an exter-nal, gradient-isolated correction source rather than another branch of the original latent pathway. JointGV adds computation and currently assumes super-vised task-defined node roles. Its correction also depends on graph observation and prediction quality; overlapping links can cause node-localization errors. Automatic role discov-ery, confidence-aware correction, broader visual models, and stable physical closed-loop deployment remain future work. The current hardware system exhibits non-negligible control-command delay at high speed, preventing reliable six-second physical balancing despite the improved visual predictions. Overall, JointGV provides a plug-in inter-face through which separately predicted rela-tional dynamics correct long-horizon visual roll-outs without restructuring the original visual world model.

## S5. Implementation and Evaluation Details 

S5.1. Implementation Settings 

The primary DMC runs were trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 D v2 GPU with 24 GB of memory; ad-ditional seeds were trained on RTX 4090 and 22 GB RTX 2080 Ti GPUs using the same model and optimization set-tings. All DMC tasks use eight graph slots of dimension 

dg = 64 , three graph message-passing layers, and a graph-history window of Hg = 4 . Graph dynamics and correction use K = Kc = 8 . Node-feature increments and visual corrections are bounded by cn = 0 .25 and cmax = 1 .0.For spatial-token alignment, dc = 32 , the entropic regu-larization coefficient is ϵ = 0 .1, Sinkhorn runs for 20 iter-ations, and σpos = 0 .15 . The alignment-loss weight starts at 0.1 and is adjusted every 50 minibatches using an ex-ponential moving average of gradient norms. DINO-WM and DreamerV3 use a learning rate of 10 −4 and batch size 16. DINO-WM uses AdamW with 16-frame sequences; DreamerV3 uses Adam with 64-frame sequences. DINOv2-S/14 and RelTR inputs are resized to 224 × 224 pixels. We first verify the DreamerV3 implementation using its online actor–critic protocol; the retained Walker Walk agent achieves an evaluation return of 737 .8 ± 15 .2. We then freeze this policy and use it to collect a fixed dataset for the prediction experiments. In each data split, 80% of the episodes are collected with this policy and 20% use uni-formly random actions to broaden the motion coverage. The DreamerV3 backbone and JointGV correction reported in this paper are trained offline from scratch on these fixed tra-jectories; the online checkpoint is used only for data collec-tion. This protocol ensures that the original and corrected predictions are trained and evaluated on identical observa-tions and actions, without confounding the comparison with online exploration, actor learning, or changes in the replay distribution. 

S5.2. Rollout Protocol 

All compared methods receive the same observed frames and recorded actions. A rollout begins with three preced-ing RGB frames and the rollout-origin frame, followed by 32 recursive predictions. DINO-WM uses three visual-history frames and JointGV uses four graph-history frames. DreamerV3 filters the four observed context frames through its recurrent state-space-model posterior. No future RGB observation, graph, or simulator state is supplied after pre-diction begins. The Cheetah Run and Walker Walk test sets each contain 20 episodes and 14 fixed windows per episode, giving 280 matched windows per task. Stored RGB images are 64 ×

64 pixels. Predictions within an experiment use the same decoder for visualization and image-based evaluation. 

S5.3. Metric Computation 

At horizon H, each metric is averaged over prediction steps 1 through H within a window and then across windows. RGB MSE compares a decoded prediction with its target image. SSIM uses an 11 × 11 Gaussian window with stan-dard deviation 1.5. LPIPS uses its AlexNet feature net-work. DINOv2-token MSE compares predicted tokens with frozen-encoder tokens extracted from the observed future image. Joint-position error measures articulated structure. The 3same frozen RGB observer extracts normalized two-dimensional positions from every predicted RGB frame; distances to target positions are averaged over visible nodes and converted to pixels. This evaluation observer was trained separately from JointGV’s internal graph observer and does not access JointGV’s graph features, predicted po-sitions, or relations. Applying it to observed DMC frames gives measurement floors of 0.19 pixels for Cheetah Run and 1.46 pixels for Walker Walk. Graph-only errors use graph-predicted positions directly and therefore have a dif-ferent measurement path. 

S5.4. Computational Cost 

The DINO-WM backbone contains 22.7M parameters, of which 0.326M are trainable. JointGV adds 64.4M parame-ters through the graph branch and its RelTR-based observer, of which 0.531M are trainable. JointGV also increases roll-out time because the graph observer, recursive graph dy-namics, and correction are evaluated alongside the visual backbone. The graph observer is evaluated once on the con-text frames, while recursive graph dynamics and correction account for most of the additional cost.

For DINO-WM on Cheetah Run, we repeat JointGV and the visual-only residual control with three independently trained seeds. These intervals measure test-window sampling variation, whereas the standard deviation across seeds measures training variation. The visual-only residual also improves two of the three runs, but its reduction is smaller and more variable; its seed-0 confidence interval includes zero. S6.8. Stratified Analysis 

We analyze when graph–visual coupling provides the largest additional benefit. For each DINO-WM test win-dow, we compute two quantities over rollout steps 1–32: (i) the minimum ground-truth image distance between non-adjacent nodes, which measures how closely separate artic-ulated parts approach in the image, and (ii) the mean error of the graph-predicted node positions. The 280 windows of each task are divided into four equal-sized groups according to each quantity. Table S5 reports paired differences in average 32-step joint-position error. For Cheetah Run, we compare mean graph-motion broadcasting with OT change coupling to iso-late the additional benefit of token-specific graph–visual coupling. For Walker Walk, we compare JointGV with its original visual backbone to examine how structural ambi-guity and graph-prediction quality affect the complete cor-rection. Confidence intervals use 2,000 paired bootstrap re-samples over windows. Because windows from the same episode are not independent, these window-level intervals are optimistic. On Cheetah Run, the additional gain from OT coupling over mean broadcasting is concentrated in windows where non-adjacent nodes approach in image space. The differ-ence decreases from 0.038 pixels in the closest quarter to 

0.001 pixels in the farthest quarter, with Spearman corre-lation ρ = −0.22 and a 95% interval of [−0.33 , −0.10] .Its relation to graph-position error is weak ( ρ = −0.06 ,

[−0.17 , 0.06] ), indicating that this coupling advantage is as-sociated more strongly with projected structural ambiguity than with overall node-position accuracy. On Walker Walk, JointGV also provides its largest re-duction when non-adjacent nodes are closest. Its improve-ment decreases as graph-predicted position error grows: the paired difference changes from −4.69 pixels in the most accurate quarter to −1.02 pixels in the least accurate quar-ter. The correlation between graph-position error and the JointGV-minus-original difference is ρ = 0 .67 , with a 95% interval of [0 .59 , 0.73] . This result identifies inaccurate graph prediction, particularly under severe limb overlap, as a remaining failure mode.

Figure S2 shows that both configurations remain more accurate than their corresponding backbones through σ =0.05 . S7.2. MuJoCo Inverted-Pendulum Control and Hardware Status 

We test whether the prediction improvement can support control in a MuJoCo model of the RM65-B inverted-pendulum system. Both actors use the same action con-straints and a shared reward predictor trained on observed simulator RGB frames. The task reward decreases with ab-solute pole angle from vertical, and an episode terminates when the pole reaches the horizontal. The actors differ in the visual predictions used during imagined training: one uses the original DINO-WM prediction, while the other uses JointGV-corrected predictions. Figure S3 shows one representative rollout, and Table 4 of the main paper reports the aggregate results. Across 50 unseen simulator initial states, the JointGV actor completes the six-second horizon in 46 trials, compared with none for the original-model actor. Its mean time before termination increases from 2.28 to 5.86 seconds. The shared reward pre-dictor also has lower validation error on JointGV-corrected rollouts, although this is only a diagnostic; balancing is measured by executing each actor in the simulator. For DINO-WM, JointGV also reduces the average 32-step pole-angle-change error from 3.088 ◦ to 1.457 ◦ and ob-tains the lower error on 82 .1% of the matched windows, indicating more accurate prediction of the pole’s tilt trend. We also tested the actors on the physical RM65-B sys-tem. The earlier seven paired trials used preliminary check-points; both actors reached the angle-triggered stop, al-though JointGV produced a longer recorded control win-dow in six pairs. The representative trial shown in Fig. 6 starts from comparable pole angles: after 1.27 seconds, the original-rollout actor reaches −83 .3◦, whereas the JointGV-rollout actor remains at −2.7◦. This demonstrates a short-duration physical-control advantage, but not stable balanc-ing. High-speed operation introduces non-negligible com-mand delay, and we have not yet obtained a controller that consistently maintains the physical pole for six seconds.
