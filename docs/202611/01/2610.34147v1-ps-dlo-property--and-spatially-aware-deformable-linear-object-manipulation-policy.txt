# PS-DLO: Property- and Spatially Aware Deformable Linear Object Manipulation Policy 

Mengdi Li 1,∗, Boxian Yao 2,∗,†, Xiyu Zhu 3,∗, Yifan Yang 2, Yunfan Lou 2, Xuan Han 2, Tao Liu 4, and Zhibo Pang 2B

Abstract —Deformable linear objects (DLO) pose a coupled perception and control challenge: their slender geometry makes precise localization difficult, while their deformations depend on object-specific physical properties. To address this challenge, we propose PS-DLO, a property- and spatially aware DLO manipulation policy. First, contrastive image–text learning maps the initial observation to categorical descriptions of DLO prop-erties, providing explicit physical and structural priors for action generation. Second, SAM3 extracts mask-guided features from future observations, which are distilled into the policy’s action expert to encode task-relevant DLO regions and their subsequent configurations. We evaluate PS-DLO on nine real-world tasks across single-arm and bimanual platforms, training a shared multi-task policy for each embodiment. PS-DLO achieves an average success rate of 68.9%, exceeding the strongest baseline by 20.0 percentage points. In generalization experiments, PS-DLO achieves higher success rates than the baseline in six of seven visual-shift conditions on butterfly-knot untying. These results demonstrate that combining object-property priors with future spatial supervision improves multi-task DLO manipulation and robustness to visual variation. 

Index Terms —Deformable linear object manipulation, vision-language-action models, visual grounding, physical-semantic con-ditioning, multi-task learning.

Variations in these properties can require different grasping and motion strategies even for the same task goal. Reliable DLO manipulation therefore requires policies that accurately localize relevant object regions while adapting their actions to the physical properties of the manipulated object. Previous DLO research has demonstrated the value of geometric modeling and property-conditioned control within specialized manipulation pipelines [2], [3]. Vision-Language-Action models (VLA) offer a promising route toward shared multi-task policies by combining pretrained multi-modal rep-resentations with robot action learning [4], [5]. However, adapting these models through action supervision alone leaves the use of spatial and physical cues largely implicit. Demon-strated actions specify the desired behavior but do not directly supervise which object regions and property cues should be preserved in the representations used to generate that behavior. Although such information may be available in visual ob-servations, its consistent use across DLO configurations and instances is not explicitly encouraged. This raises a central question: How can we explicitly guide a shared VLA policy to attend to interaction-relevant regions while incorporating object-dependent physical information into action generation? A. DLO Manipulation 

Existing DLO manipulation methods use dense descriptors and goal-conditioned Transporter Networks for object cor-respondence and rearrangement [7], [8]. Other approaches exploit deformation models and feedback control [2], [9], tactile motion primitives [10], or cable tracing and crossing classification [11], [12] to support task-specific manipula-tion [13]. Recent work considers property adaptation and broader task coverage. Li and Choi condition insertion on DLO flexibility estimated after a predefined grasping interaction [3], while Hierarchical DLO Routing combines VLM-based plan-ning with learned and predefined skills [14]. DLO-Lab and WireCraft provide simulation environments spanning multiple DLO tasks [15], [16], with DLO-Lab also demonstrating joint multi-task VLA training. Building on these directions, our work investigates mask-guided representation supervision and categorical physical-semantic conditioning for real-world multi-task DLO manipulation. 

B. Representation Learning for VLA Policies 

VLA models adapt pretrained vision-language representa-tions for robotic control [17], [18], with different interfaces for incorporating proprioception. π0 projects continuous states into action-expert tokens, whereas π0.5 discretizes them into its textual input prefix [5], [19]. Beyond input conditioning, SAM3D-guided alignment and AffordVLA introduce object-level geometry and task-conditioned affordances through vi-sual representation supervision [20], [21]. Predictive representations further connect visual learning with the consequences of robot actions. Mask World Model forecasts future semantic masks and uses mask-centric fea-tures for control, reporting improved robustness to appearance changes [22]. Efficient-WAM reduces the cost of future visual prediction through video-expert distillation while retaining the prediction branch during deployment [23]. FLARE incorpo-rates future information through alignment between intermedi-ate policy features and action-aware future embeddings, with-out computing target embeddings at inference [24]. Building on these advances, our work combines SAM3-selected next-frame supervision within the action expert with CLIP-derived DLO property descriptions supplied through the existing VLA text-conditioning interface [25]. B. Framework Overview 

In DLO manipulation, interaction regions are difficult to identify, and differences in object properties affect action selection. We use physical-semantic conditioning and local vi-sual supervision to support action learning. As shown in Fig. 2, PS-DLO builds on a flow-matching VLA model backbone [5], incorporates the extracted property prompt through the existing language input pathway, and adds learnable DLO queries and a representation alignment head to the action expert. Train-ing consists of three stages: cross-modal adaptation, teacher pretraining, and joint training. 

Stage 1: Cross-modal adaptation. We construct an ex-tractor based on contrastive image-text learning [25] to align representations of initial main-view images and DLO-property prompts. The extractor is trained with the InfoNCE contrastive loss Lcontrast . This stage grounds property descriptions in DLO observations, enabling the extractor to provide object-specific conditioning for action learning. After adaptation, we freeze the extractor throughout teacher pretraining and joint training. 

Stage 2: Teacher pretraining. A frozen SAM3 model extracts visual features and a DLO mask from the next-frame main-view image using a concept prompt. Mask-guided patch selection retains the relevant regions. The task prompt and the property prompt are encoded by the SAM3 text encoder and passed to the Teacher Encoder together with the selected visual features. A fusion module and a Q-former produce teacher tokens, which condition an auxiliary action Diffusion Trans-former (DiT) through cross-attention. The auxiliary action DiT also receives the proprioceptive state and noisy action chunk. We jointly optimize the Teacher Encoder and auxiliary action DiT using a flow-matching loss Laux .

Stage 3: Joint training. The TA encoder is initialized from the pretrained Teacher Encoder’s parameters. During joint training, the TA encoder and auxiliary action DiT continue to receive flow-matching supervision, while the Teacher Encoder is updated only by EMA. The student policy learns to generate action chunks using a flow-matching loss on demonstration actions. Its intermediate DLO-query representations are pro-jected through the alignment head, and their mean cosine distance to the stop-gradient Teacher query representations serves as a future constraint on action learning.

E XPERIMENTS 

A. Experiment Setup 

We conduct experiments on two robot embodiments: a dual-arm PiPER and a single-arm Franka Research 3. For each embodiment, we train a shared policy across all of its tasks. We define five task categories around bending stiffness, topology, extensibility, length, and thickness. PiPER performs three cate-gories: bending stiffness, topology, and extensibility. Bending stiffness involves cable straightening and rope straightening, while topology covers single-knot, bow-tie, butterfly-knot, and figure-eight-knot untying. Extensibility is evaluated through elastic-rope hooking. Franka Research 3 performs the remain-ing two categories: length (long rope wrapping) and thickness (thick-rope threading and lifting), as shown in Fig. 3. In addi-tion to the five task-defining property dimensions, we construct a property-prompt dataset that describes each demonstration using six categorical attributes—the number of DLO, bending stiffness, topology, extensibility, length, and thickness—for property extraction and policy conditioning. 

B. Data and Evaluation Protocol 

Each demonstration contains multi-view RGB observations, proprioceptive state, action sequences, and the corresponding task and property prompts. After cleaning, the dataset contains 500 demonstrations: 100 for straightening (50 cable and 50 rope), 100 for knot untying (25 each for single-knot, bow-tie, butterfly, and figure-eight knots), and 100 each for elastic-rope hooking, long-rope wrapping, and thick-rope threading followed by lifting. For a fair comparison, all baseline models use exactly the same training data as PS-DLO and are fully fine-tuned for 10,000 optimization steps. During training, performance is monitored on the validation set, and the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is selected for real-robot evaluation. C. Main Results and Analysis 

Attribute recognition. We evaluate the DLO property extractor on a held-out test set constructed from 50 separately collected trajectories that were excluded from model training. From the first three seconds of each trajectory, we randomly sample 10 frames within each one-second interval, yielding 1,500 candidate frames. After manually removing visually duplicate frames, we obtain 500 test images used exclusively for evaluation. A prediction is considered correct only if all predicted attributes match the ground-truth annotation. The extractor achieves an accuracy of 99 .4% on this test set. This metric measures the recognition of categorical DLO properties rather than the accuracy of continuous physical parameter estimates.

180 trials per method. Overall performance is calculated as the macro-average of task success rates. D. Ablation Study 

Using π0.5 as the baseline, we organize the ablation exper-iments into three groups: property fusion strategies, SAM3-based spatial supervision, and teacher-target updates. Table II reports the success rate, peak training memory per GPU, and peak inference memory of each configuration. 

Property fusion strategy. We compare prefix conditioning, suffix conditioning, and joint text conditioning for incor-porating DLO property information. Joint text conditioning combines the task instruction, discretized robot state, and DLO property description within the policy’s text input. The three variants achieve success rates of 50 .0% , 56 .1% , and 49 .4% ,respectively, compared with 48 .9% for the baseline. Suffix conditioning performs best among the variants using prop-erty conditioning alone, improving success by 7.2 percentage points. These results indicate that the effectiveness of property conditioning depends on its integration into the policy. 

SAM3-based spatial supervision. The future-frame SAM3 configuration achieves a success rate of 57 .2% , exceeding the baseline by 8.3 percentage points. Combining joint text conditioning with SAM3-based supervision from the current observation achieves 61 .1% , compared with 49 .4% for joint text conditioning alone, an improvement of 11 .7 percentage points. These comparisons support the usefulness of mask-guided representation supervision in the evaluated configura-tions. Teacher-target update strategy. Using a fixed pretrained Teacher Encoder to provide alignment targets yields a success rate of 63 .3% . In the full method, the TA encoder and auxiliary action DiT are optimized through auxiliary action supervision, while the Teacher Encoder is updated through an exponential moving average of the TA encoder’s parameters. 70 .0% , respectively. Under combined color and lighting, color and background, and lighting and background changes, PS-DLO achieves 65 .0% , 40 .0% , and 45 .0% , exceeding π0.5 by 

20 .0, 15 .0, and 15 .0 percentage points, respectively. When all three changes are applied simultaneously, PS-DLO succeeds in 2 of 20 trials (10 .0% ), while π0.5 succeeds in none. These results suggest improved robustness across the evaluated conditions on this task, although the low success rates under the three-factor condition reveal a limitation under combined visual changes. B. Interpretation of Generalization Results 

PS-DLO outperforms π0.5 in six of the seven visual-shift conditions on butterfly-knot untying (Table III), with gains of 15.0–20.0 percentage points under pairwise combinations of color, lighting, and background changes. One possible expla-nation is that SAM3-based spatial supervision encourages the policy to retain DLO-relevant features despite changes in scene appearance. This interpretation is consistent with prior work showing that object-centric representations can improve ma-nipulation generalization under visual changes [32]. However, the advantage narrows under more challenging conditions: when all three factors change, PS-DLO succeeds in only 2 of 20 trials. Higher success rates also do not imply smaller performance drops; under the background-only change, PS-DLO falls from 90.0% to 70.0%, while π0.5 remains at 70.0%. These results show that PS-DLO retains a performance advantage under most tested shifts, but remains sensitive to visual variation. 

C. Limitations on Elastic-Rope Hooking 

Elastic-rope hooking is the only task where PS-DLO does not achieve the highest success rate, reaching 20.0% com-pared with DreamZero’s 30.0% (Table I). Beyond rope– hook positioning, this task requires handling elastic recoil and maintaining hook engagement after release. DreamZero jointly predicts future video and actions [30], which may help anticipate these dynamic changes. PS-DLO instead uses future observations for representation supervision during training, while its episode-level property description provides a static prior. This performance gap motivates extending spatial super-vision toward explicit prediction of deformation and contact transitions, although the current comparison does not isolate the effect of future prediction. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented PS-DLO, which combines object-property conditioning with future spatial representation distillation through an action-supervised TA and an EMA-updated teacher. Across nine real-world DLO manipulation tasks, PS-DLO achieves an average success rate of 68 .9% , exceeding the best baseline by 20 .0 percentage points. It also achieves higher suc-cess rates under most tested visual shifts, although elastic-rope hooking and combined visual changes remain challenging.
