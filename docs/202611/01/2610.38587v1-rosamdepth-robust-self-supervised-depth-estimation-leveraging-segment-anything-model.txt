## Abstract 

Robust depth estimation aims to maintain high-quality depths across diverse conditions. However, most existing methods estimate depth without taking into account the object-level information. As a result, the predicted depth may easily deviate within objects and become blurred un-der adverse conditions. To overcome this weakness, we propose RoSAMDepth, a novel framework that can as-sist robust self-supervised depth estimation in leveraging rich and diverse object-level priors from the Segment Any-thing Model (SAM). We focus on incorporating object-level information across three key aspects: a segment-guided representation contrasting method that injects object-level awareness into the feature representation space; an adap-tive regional outlier masking strategy combined with a re-gional Gaussian likelihood loss that enforces regional depth smoothness; and an object-level reliability estimation strat-egy that mitigates the influence of unreliable supervision. Extensive experiments across multiple datasets and diverse weather conditions demonstrate that our method produces sharper, more accurate depth predictions, consistently out-performing state-of-the-art methods. Our code is available at https://github.com/xagao/RoSAMDepth. 

## 1. Introduction 

Monocular depth estimation is a fundamental task in com-puter vision, crucial for downstream applications, such as autonomous driving, robotics, and AR/VR. Supervised methods [1, 5, 15, 17, 26] achieve high accuracy but rely on expensive ground-truth (GT) depths for training. To alleviate this dependency, self-supervised methods lever-age geometrical constraints from stereo pairs or monocu-lar videos to learn depth estimation without explicit annota-tions [2, 7, 8, 11, 22, 36, 40, 43].

Despite their success under standard daytime conditions, self-supervised methods often degrade in challenging con-ditions such as nighttime or adverse weather [7, 21, 28, 37], where the photometric consistency breaks. Early works ad-dress a specific condition (e.g., nighttime), using adversarial learning or feature disentanglement for domain representa-tions [18, 30, 32]. Recent methods [7, 21, 28, 37] focus on unified robustness across diverse degradations by synthe-sizing paired data with teacher–student learning or further adapting synthetic-trained teachers to real degraded data. However, existing methods typically treat degraded regions uniformly, without considering the semantic or object-level differences that significantly influence visual perception and depth estimation. As shown in Fig. 1, ig-noring object-level priors can lead to blurred depth bound-aries and unstable predictions under complex environments or diverse conditions. To better estimate depth across di-verse real data, it is important to understand the object spa-tial relationships and assess the reliability of depth cues. Meanwhile, the predicted depth should also be smooth only within the same continuous object region, while allowing sharp depth boundaries near object edges. Thus, incorpo-1arXiv:2610.38587v1 [cs.CV] 28 Oct 2026 rating object-level information is key to refining robust self-supervised depth estimation under adverse conditions. Indeed, prior methods exploit semantic segmentation to incorporate object information for depth estimation under standard conditions [12, 19]. However, semantic segmenta-tion is inherently limited, as it cannot distinguish instances within the same class, which may have drastically differ-ent depths. It is also constrained by a limited set of pre-defined classes, often failing to recognize novel objects in the open world. Moreover, segmentation models trained on specific domains or weather conditions generalize poorly across diverse scenarios. In comparison, the Segment Any-thing Model (SAM) [14] offers a better option. SAM is a general-purpose image segmentation model pre-trained on massive and diverse datasets. It separates individual in-stances and demonstrates strong zero-shot robustness to un-seen objects and various weather conditions [14, 25, 29]. Building upon these advantages, we propose to integrate SAM for additional object-level information to enhance robust self-supervised depth estimation. Our framework jointly improves feature representation and depth learn-ing. Specifically, for representation learning, we propose segment-guided representation contrasting (SRC) that ex-ploits SAM masks for implicitly embedding object-level structural awareness into the feature space. Furthermore, to improve supervision for depth learning, we identify key limitations in the conventional smoothness loss and the pixel-level pseudo-label quality indicator. In response, we introduce two strategies: an adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) strategy paired with a regional Gaussian likelihood loss to rectify inconsistent depth outliers; and an object-level reliability estimation (ORE) strategy that mitigates the influence of unreliable supervision signals. Our method significantly outperforms previous methods in diverse conditions through different real-world datasets [3, 20]. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to effec-tively integrate SAM into robust self-supervised depth es-timations, using only pre-generated SAM masks during the training as object-level priors. • We propose three novel designs to take full advantage of SAM’s object-level priors: segment-guided represen-tation contrasting (SRC), adaptive regional outlier mask-ing (AROM) strategy combined with a regional Gaussian constraint, and object-level reliability estimation (ORE) strategy. These components provide efficient guidance to train the model and lead to improved depth. • Extensive experiments on the nuScenes [3] and Oxford Robotcar [20] benchmarks demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art approaches, de-livering sharper and more accurate depth under diverse real-world conditions. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Self-supervised Monocular Depth Estimation 

Self-supervised monocular depth estimation methods use multi-view images for training and learn to estimate the depth from a single input image with photometric recon-struction consistency as supervision. SfM-Learner [43] firstly introduces an unsupervised paradigm that relies on photometric consistency, formulating depth estimation as a view synthesis task. Monodepth2 [8] introduces the per-pixel photometric loss with an auto-mask and a full-resolution sampling for training the model to reduce the im-pact of moving objects. Several methods [4, 9, 12, 19] use extra semantic information to improve performance, and the frameworks explored in [16, 38] jointly learn depth, cam-era pose, and optical flow. Additionally, the multi-frame monocular depth estimation is handled in [10, 36], which predicts more accurate depths by taking two frames of a monocular video as the input. 

2.2. Depth Estimation under Adverse Conditions 

Robust depth estimation under adverse conditions, such as rain, night, and fog, remains challenging and actively stud-ied. Early approaches focus on nighttime. DeFeat-Net [30] jointly learns depth and cross-domain features, ADIDS [18] utilizes a generative adversarial network (GAN) to gener-ate paired nighttime images and splits features into private and invariant sub-spaces, and ADFA [32] employs a GAN-based discriminator with adversarial training to align night-time and daytime features. Later methods like RNW [35] and STEPS [41] integrate image enhancement to improve depth quality, and WSGD [33] combines denoising with a lighting change decoder to predict per-pixel changes. More recent methods extend beyond nighttime to tackle multiple conditions by exploiting generative techniques such as GANs and diffusion models [27, 39, 42, 44]. Md4all [7], Robust-Depth [28], and WeatherDepth [34] enhance ro-bustness through GAN-based weather augmentations inte-grated into teacher-student frameworks. Complementary to these approaches, DM-MDE [31] incorporates stable diffu-sion priors, and Syn2RealDepth [37] proposes an auxiliary space and a real adaptation stage. Our key insight is that the fidelity of synthetic-to-real adaptation can be significantly improved with explicit object-level guidance. We opera-tionalize this by incorporating cues from SAM, which en-hance depth estimation in two ways: 1) enabling the model learning object-aware perception, and 2) providing object-level priors for supervision signals refinement.

Robust Teacher Depth We build upon the synthetic-to-real training paradigm introduced in [37], which consists of a synthetic adaptation stage followed by a real adaptation stage. Our improvements focus on the real adaptation stage, where the model encounters the challenges of real-world data. Following [37], we use the depth network trained on the synthetic stage as a fixed teacher network, denoted as 

Φt in our framework. For clarity, the initial synthetic adap-tation stage and the pose network training, which remain unchanged from [37], are omitted from Fig. 2. 

SAM Mask Generation We leverage SAM to generate object-level information without any fine-tuning. For each input image It, we run SAM in its ‘segment-everything’ mode to produce a set of K binary masks, M raw sam ∈{0, 1}K×H×W . To obtain a partition of non-overlapping object-level segments, we apply a post-processing step: each pixel is assigned to the mask with the largest area among all candidate masks in M raw sam that contain it. This generates the final masks Msam ∈ { 0, 1}N ×H×W , where 

N is the number of non-overlapping object-level regions and Msam,i ∈ { 0, 1}H×W denotes the i-th region. All SAM masks are pre-generated before training, ensuring that 3SAM is only used offline. Additional details are provided in the supplementary material. 

3.2. Object-Aware Representation Learning 

To inject object-level awareness into the depth network, we introduce segment-guided representation contrasting (SRC). Our SRC transfers segmentation priors from SAM through a regional prototype-based contrastive approach. It aims to encourage the depth network to infer geometric co-herence, ensuring that pixels within the same segment are assigned structurally consistent depths. Specifically, SRC exploits SAM from a clustering perspective, contrasting the features of the depth network according to the given seg-ments of the SAM. A key design choice of SRC is to perform the contrasting at the feature level rather than imposing direct constraints on the final depth. This is because SAM tends to over-segment a single object into multiple parts, and enforcing depth discontinuities at every segment boundary could lead to erroneous supervision. Instead, by transferring segmen-tation priors into the depth decoder’s feature space, SRC allows the network to implicitly learn object-aware repre-sentations without explicitly altering the depths. 3.3. Depth Learning with Object-Level Priors 

To further enhance the quality of predicted depth, we leverage object-level priors to improve depth learning sig-nals. First, we propose to achieve object-aware smooth-ness by combining an adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) strategy with a regional Gaussian likelihood loss. This combination suppresses intra-region outliers and main-tains regional smoothness. Second, we develop an object-level reliability estimation (ORE) strategy for pseudo-label reweighting, which assesses depth reliability at the object level rather than the pixel level. These components together enforce region-consistent supervision and substantially im-prove the depth quality in diverse conditions. 

Object-aware Smoothness. This adaptation of the supervi-sion signal comes from the motivation that object segmen-tation can be used to formulate a more precise smoothness constraint, thereby better regularizing the depth map. We begin by discussing the shortcomings of the conventional smoothness loss and then show how we mitigate those lim-itations with the help of SAM. 

Limitations of previous smoothness losses. Most exist-ing methods define smoothness loss using first-order depth derivatives [7, 8, 36, 43]. Given mean-normalized inverse depth d∗ = d/d , the conventional smoothness loss is:          

> Lsm =|∂xd∗|e−| ∂xI|+|∂yd∗|e−| ∂yI|,(3)

in which e−| ∂I | serves as the edge-aware weights to avoid penalties across object boundaries, where depth is not nec-essarily continuous. Such weights are usually derived from the image edges, which often coincide with object bound-aries [8, 43]. However, the inherent limitations of the boundary-aware smoothness loss prevent it from leveraging more accurate object boundaries. One example is illustrated in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3b, the depth estimation fails around people, resulting in blurred depth and erroneously predicting part of the back-ground building as a continuous foreground. In this case, the gradient of the boundary-aware smoothness loss mostly 4arises near the depth boundary (Fig. 3c), since the loss mainly focus on where the image is smooth ( e−| ∂I | is large) and the depth exhibits disconunity ( |∂d ∗| is large). How-ever, in regions where the depth is incorrectly estimated but locally smooth (e.g., mispredicted background between the object boundary and the erroneous depth boundary), the loss incorrectly assumes depth continuity ( |∂d ∗| is small) and fails to respond, resulting in weak gradients confined to a limited neighborhood around the depth boundary. 

Regional smoothness based on segment . To address this limitation, we extend the smoothness formulation from ob-ject boundaries to object regions. Intuitively, the inaccu-rate depth values in Fig. 3b can be understood as regional outliers within the same object (background building). Object-Level Reliability. A widely adopted strategy for evaluating the reliability of pseudo-labels is to compute the pixel-wise photometric error [21, 28], assuming that low lo-cal errors correspond to trustworthy predictions. However, this local metric is misaligned with the nature of depth reli-ability, which typically exhibits strong object-level spatial correlations. As illustrated in Fig. 4b and d, scaling the depth of a car by different factors results in only localized deviations of the indicator (photometric error in Eq. (8)), while the interior region remains falsely reliable. This is be-cause pixels within the same object often share similar ap-pearances, leading to chance alignments even when the ob-ject’s depth is mispredicted. Consequently, pixel-level eval-uation identifies only scattered unreliable pixels and fails to capture object-level unreliability. Furthermore, when selecting depths corresponding to the lowest indicator values as in [21, 28], many depths on in-correctly scaled factors are still considered trustworthy, pro-ducing incorrectly selected depths (see Fig. 4c). These find-ings reveal the inherent insufficiency of pixel-level indica-5tors and motivate us to shift the focus from individual pixel evaluation to object-level reliability estimation. We define this as the object-level reliability estimation (ORE) strategy, which aims to assess the reliability of pseudo-labels within each object region for more effective supervision signals. ## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Implement Details 

Both our teacher and student depth networks adopt the same architecture [36] as in [37]. In our implementation, we use the pretrained teacher model provided by [37], which is trained on synthetic data, without any fine-tuning. The student network Φs is initialized from the teacher Φt and trained for 10 epochs following [37]. The model is imple-mented in Pytorch [24] and optimized using Adam [13] with a batch size of 10. The learning rate is initialized to 8 × e−5

and decayed by a factor of 0.5 every 5 epochs. For the Seg-ment Anything Model [14], we use the off-the-shelf default ViT-H pretrained model. Additional implementation details are provided in the supplementary material. 

4.2. Dataset and Evaluation Metric 

We evaluate our method on the nuScenes [3] and Oxford Robotcar [20] datasets and follow the same training data schedules as [37]. NuScenes [3] is a comprehensive bench-mark with real driving data under daytime, nighttime, and rainy conditions. In our experiments, we utilize 21,476 im-ages for training, including 15,129 daytime, 3,796 rainy, and 2,551 nighttime frames. The evaluation set consists of 6,019 consecutive frame pairs: 4,449 daytime, 1,088 rainy, and 602 nighttime. We report results in both single-frame and multi-frame settings, and all metrics are computed for ground truth within 0.1–80 m. For the Oxford RobotCar [20] dataset, following WSGD [33], we use 17,790 daytime and 19,162 nighttime images for training, and 1,411 single images for testing (702 daytime images and 709 nighttime images). Evaluation is performed under the single-frame setting, with metrics computed within 0.1–50 m. We employ commonly adopted metrics [7, 8, 31, 35, 37] for depth estimation, including absolute relative error (Ab-sRel), root mean square error (RMSE), squared relative er-ror (SqRel), and threshold accuracy ( δ1).

## 6. Conclusion 

This work presents RoSAMDepth, a novel robust self-supervised monocular depth estimation framework that leverages object-level priors from SAM during training. Our method integrates three key components: segment-guided representation contrasting (SRC) for structural awareness, adaptive regional outlier masking (AROM) with regional Gaussian likelihood loss for consistent depth, and object-level reliability estimation (ORE) for reliability-aware learning. ## A. Methods Details 

A.1. SAM Mask Generation 

SAM often produces a hierarchical set of masks for a sin-gle object, detecting not only the entire object but also its constituent parts (e.g., wheels, doors, windows, lights, and even bumpers for a car, as shown in Fig. S1). While de-tailed, this hierarchy introduces redundancy and potential noise for tasks requiring object-level information. To ad-dress this, we adopt a simple yet effective strategy. We as-sume that in overlapping masks, the mask with the largest spatial extent represents the object instance, while smaller nested masks represent its parts. Formally, let M raw sam ∈ { 0, 1}K×H×W denote the set of 

K binary masks produced by SAM for an input image I,where M raw sam,i ∈ { 0, 1}H×W is the i-th mask. Specifically, we iterate through masks with an area below a threshold (set to 10 5 pixels, approx. 200 × 500 ). If a small mask is signif-icantly covered by a larger mask (overlap ratio > 0.75 ), it is considered a sub-part and is merged into the larger parent mask. As shown in Fig. S1, the resulting unified segmenta-tions serve as robust object-level priors. These object-level priors ensure that the network achieves consistent and accu-rate depth predictions across the entire object instance. 

A.2. Training Framework 

We build upon the Syn2Real-Depth framework [37] to in-vestigate how object-level priors from SAM [14] can en-hance robust self-supervised depth estimation in diverse real-world scenes. Syn2Real-Depth serves as an ideal base-line due to its real adaptation stage designed for diverse real data. The framework employs ManyDepth [36] as the depth backbone, originally defining three domain-specific mod-els: Φday (trained on real daytime data), Φsyn (trained on diverse synthetic data), and Φreal (trained on diverse real data). The pose network follows the standard architecture described in [8, 36]. Since our contribution focuses on inte-grating SAM-based guidance into the real-world adaptation stage, we simplify the notation to better reflect our train-ing paradigm. Specifically, we designate Φsyn as the initial teacher Φt, and Φreal as the student Φs, which is fine-tuned on real data with object-level priors.
