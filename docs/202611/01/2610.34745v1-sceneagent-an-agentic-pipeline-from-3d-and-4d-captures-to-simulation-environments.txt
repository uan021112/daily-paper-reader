SceneAgent: An Agentic Pipeline from 3D and 4D
Captures to Simulation Environments
Luke Hollis1∗, Tianxing Fan1∗, Heng Yang1
1Computational Robotics Group, School of Engineering and Applied Sciences, Harvard University
∗Equal contribution, lhollis@g.harvard.edu, tianxing_fan@seas.harvard.edu, hankyang@seas.harvard.edu
Visual captures provide detailed scene appearance, but converting them into interactive simulation environ-
ments requires object reconstruction, physical modeling, and correction of intermediate errors. We present
SceneAgent, an agentic pipeline that coordinates these steps through simulator-based visual inspection and
repair. The pipeline processes 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), photogrammetry, LiDAR captures, AI-generated
3DGS scenes, and 4D videos of dynamic scenes, and combines object segmentation, physical parameter
estimation, part decomposition, articulation, and deformable simulation. To support policy learning, SceneAgent
generates diverse object variants (“digital sisters”) and scripted demonstrations. On a cluttered bin scene
from GraspClutter6D, SceneAgent raises F1 at 1 cm for every coding agent with scorable outputs both with
and without its skills, and its reconstructions are better than SimFoundry [47]: all agent configurations exceed
SimFoundry’s F1 score, with a best of 60.75% versus 6.9%. On three dynamic scenes from 4DCodeBench [51],
SceneAgent raises the mean dynamic IoU of Gemini 4 Argon from 0.86% to 99.09%. We further demonstrate
diverse simulated interactions and preliminary policy transfer to a real robot.

1 Introduction
Generalist robot policies [5, 6, 23, 37, 38, 40] learn from large teleoperated datasets to perform a wide range of
manipulation tasks specified by language instructions. Collecting these datasets is labor-intensive and can take months
or years. Similarly, evaluating the resulting policies in a target environment requires physical robot trials, which are
time-consuming, difficult to reproduce, and costly to scale [2].
Simulation offers a way to scale both data collection and evaluation. Reconstructed environments can generate
demonstrations [59, 63, 67], support policies that transfer to physical robots [27, 45, 68], and provide estimates of
real-world policy performance [19, 30, 31, 47, 69]. In particular, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [22] reconstructs
photorealistic scene appearance from images, enabling simulators to render observations grounded in a captured
workspace [11, 21, 45].
Visual reconstruction alone, however, does not provide the object geometry, physical parameters, or articulation
needed for interaction. Real-to-sim systems recover these components by combining segmentation, mesh generation,
pose alignment, and physical modeling [12, 19, 47, 68]. SimFoundry, for example, supports articulated objects and
estimates their physical parameters [47]. A remaining challenge is to coordinate these components across diverse
inputs and correct errors that arise between stages. An incorrect mask, scale, or transform can produce an unusable
object even when the individual models return plausible outputs.
We present SceneAgent, an agentic pipeline that converts 3D and 4D captures into interactive simulation environments
(Figure 2). Agents execute reconstruction and physical modeling tools, inspect rendered outputs in the target simulator,
and repair visible errors before the scene is used downstream. The resulting environments contain segmented objects
with estimated physical parameters, decomposed parts, and articulation or soft-body behavior where needed. For 4D
captures, which are videos of dynamic scenes, agents also fit simulated dynamics such as liquid flow, deformation, and
destruction mechanics to the observed scene (Section 3.6). SceneAgent also generates digital sisters—related object
variants—and combines captured objects with artist-authored environments to expand the range of training scenes.
We evaluate reconstruction quality on a cluttered bin scene from GraspClutter6D [3] and a Messy Kitchen dataset from
CollectData [8] using coding agents with and without SceneAgent, and compare with SimFoundry. Here, a harness is
the software environment in which a coding agent runs, and skills are the reusable instructions and tools that guide
its conversion workflow. With SceneAgent, F1 at 1 cm improves for every harness with scorable paired outputs, and
reconstruction quality is much better than SimFoundry’s (Figure 5). On three dynamic scenes from 4DCodeBench [51],

In summary, our contributions are:
• An agentic scene-conversion workflow that integrates reconstruction, physical parameter estimation, part decompo-
sition, and articulation with simulator-based visual inspection and repair, and that extends from 3D captures to 4D
captures by fitting simulated dynamics to the motion observed in a video.
• Scene augmentation through digital sisters and hybrid environments that combine captured objects with artist-
authored assets, together with an automated research loop that turns failures of a policy on the real robot into
simulated training data.
• A quantitative study of reconstruction quality across coding-agent harnesses on static captures and on dynamic
scenes from 4DCodeBench, complemented by qualitative examples of articulated, deformable, and contact-rich
interactions and preliminary real-world policy transfer.

Other work addresses factors that determine simulation fidelity: joint refinement of geometry and calibration [35],

Demonstration generation and scene variation.
Digital reconstructions can expand limited demonstrations into larger
training datasets. Real2Render2Real [67] synthesizes demonstrations from object scans and a human video using
kinematic rendering. RoboSplat [63] varies object pose, appearance, and embodiment while adapting demonstrated
trajectories, and ExoGS [59] replays exoskeleton demonstrations through editable Gaussian-splat assets. ManiS-
plat [14] reconstructs a scene from robot video to synthesize additional trajectories. TwinRL [62] uses a reconstructed
environment to expand fine-tuning data and identify configurations for human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning. Our
demonstration workflow samples layouts, settles objects under physics, executes a scripted expert using simulator
state, and retains successful episodes for policy fine-tuning.
Physical properties, articulation, and deformation.

observed interactions. ReconPhys [57] estimates deformable dynamics from video, and 4DCodeBench [51] evaluates
agents that reconstruct dynamic scenes from video as executable simulation and rendering programs, the setting
of our 4D extension. Complementary methods address cable simulation [64], articulated reconstruction [33], and

3 SceneAgent
SceneAgent converts a capture into an interactive simulation environment through three stages: agent-guided
reconstruction and repair, scene augmentation, and demonstration generation. We first describe the agent workflow,
then the representations and conversion tools it coordinates, and finally the downstream training procedure. Section 3.6
extends the workflow from static 3D captures to videos of dynamic scenes.

at transitions between models, where file conventions, coordinate frames, and segmentation errors can affect the

The orchestrator agent first plans the full conversion and processing steps from the input state, then the ingest agents
prepare the input data and build the 3dgs, and finally the runner agent executes the processing tools with actor-critic
visual review of each step. Alignment and orientation skills adjust object transforms and the background’s placement in
the simulator. A packager assembles the scene, and an inspector reviews the exported result in Isaac Lab. Object-,
scene-, and task-variation skills then generate digital sisters and alternative layouts.
The workflow supports both scene-level conversion and inspection of individual objects. Agents can examine close-up
renders to identify incomplete segmentation, missing geometry, or incorrect placement; Figure 21 shows examples of
these detailed views. This iterative correction concerns the current conversion.
Each agent collaborates with the hardness and passes state through files in the scene directory. Following the
orchestrator agent, they execute the conversion plan following a manifest for each step then a ledger of accepted or
rejected outputs. Then per converted object in the scene, the reviewer agent inspects the object registration overlays,
eight isolated views (six axis-aligned and two oblique), and finally two views of the object in the fully assembled scene.
If a problem is discovered, such as an alignment, articulation, or decomposition issue, the agent retries by proposing a
corrected pose or other fix and reviews again.
The alignment residuals in Figure 7 come from a trimmed similarity ICP, seeded with the SAM3D pose, that aligns
each mesh to the scene. We use the normalized median and 90th-percentile point distances, coverage, and silhouette
agreement, and accept a candidate only when all four pass fixed thresholds. Procedures from each conversion are
saved to files that later conversions can read.
The agents also maintain SceneAgent’s skills and tools and are instructed to research methods that may improve their
conversion results, and if they identify a potential solution, request to evaluate it, and then modify their skills. The
candidate suggested change to the tools and the current baseline are then run on a set of static 3D, dynamic 4D, and

3.2 Input Preparation and Scene Representation
For RGB image sequences, the pipeline uses COLMAP [49] to estimate camera poses and Nerfstudio’s splatfacto
big [55, 65] to train a 3DGS reconstruction. Agents can inspect and correct capture-processing issues, including
per-image color calibration [60]. The pipeline also accepts existing 3DGS files, photogrammetry assets, and LiDAR
captures, as well as generated scenes from models such as World Labs Marble [61], NVIDIA Lyra [4], and Tencent
HY-World [16]. Videos of dynamic scenes follow the 4D pathway of Section 3.6.
For the Gaussian-splat pathway, we represent the capture as {gi}N
i=1, with gi = (µi, Σi, αi, hi) denoting its center,
covariance, opacity, and spherical-harmonic color coefficients. Calibrated cameras c provide image projections πc and
camera-frame depths zc. The output is a scene S = (Gbg, {ok}K
k=1), containing a completed background splat and K
objects. Each object is represented as ok = (Pk, Mk, Tk, θk, Jk), where Pk is its set of Gaussians, Mk its mesh, Tk
its rotation, translation, and uniform scale, θk its physical properties, and Jk its joints, which may be empty.
3.3 Object Reconstruction and Background Completion
We associate semantic features with the Gaussians using image masks and embeddings, following the general
approach of LangSplat [43]. SAM [24] produces object masks on every tenth input view by default; across our scenes
this stride ranges from 1 to 10. OpenCLIP ViT-B-16 [18, 46] embeds each masked crop into a 512-dimensional vector,
and DINOv2 ViT-S/14 [39] gives each object a visual feature vector. Both are inputs to the physics prior (Section 3.4);
neither is used to decide which Gaussians belong to an object. Masks from different views are grouped into one object

For each object, we combine semantic features, predictions from a per-Gaussian physics model, and a vision-language
model (VLM) to estimate material and physical properties. These include friction, rigidity, mass, and density. The
implementation uses 10 material classes, 6 body types, and 8 collision primitives, and records the resulting properties
in the exported scene.

The physics prior is a multilayer perceptron whose inputs are DINOv2 and OpenCLIP features and 16 geometric
quantities. It has output heads for material class, body type, and collision primitive, and it regresses nine numerical
properties. It is trained on object records from GSO, YCB, and ABO, and its targets are labels written by a VLM, not
physical measurements. The VLM audit decides the material, and we treat the numerical values as approximate priors.
The model regresses mass and density as logarithms. Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, and the rendering material
parameters are looked up from the material class. Rigidity is a unitless score between 0 and 1. It enters the simulation
only through deformability, and an object becomes a soft body when its deformability is at least 0.65.
The ten material classes are plastic, metal, wood, fabric, paper and cardboard, rubber and foam, glass and ceramic,
food and organic matter, liquid and granular matter, and unknown. The six body types are dynamic rigid, static rigid,
kinematic, soft deformable, cloth shell, and fluid or granular. The eight collision primitives are box, sphere, cylinder,
capsule, convex hull, triangle mesh, tetrahedral mesh, and cloth mesh. The Isaac Lab export uses mass, friction,
restitution, body type, and collision approximation, and it stores the remaining properties as USD metadata. The
recorded material review is automatic. It is signed by a coding agent, not a person.
PartSAM [73] decomposes each reconstructed object into parts. A VLM inspects these parts for missing geometry,
such as the tip of a marker hidden beneath its cap. When completion is needed, CubePart [70] generates the missing
region, which is appended to the mesh. Articulate Anything [25] then proposes joints and motion ranges in URDF
format from image or text inputs. Agents review the resulting articulation in simulation. Figure 8 follows this process
from a marker image to a simulated writing interaction.

3.5 Fracture Simulation
We also explore material failure during manipulation, including paper tearing and ceramic fracture. Figure 12 shows
a Dexmate Vega with two Sharpa Wave hands tearing parchment from a roll and placing it over a bowl, alongside
examples of jars shattering after impact. The parchment uses an orthotropic thin-sheet finite-element model in NVIDIA
Warp [32], with irreversible cohesive fracture along interior edges. The tear path emerges as interfaces fail. In the
illustrated rollout, 49 interfaces fail progressively, leaving a detached sheet that covers 95% of the bowl opening
after the hands withdraw. The strength, fracture energy, and friction parameters are currently engineering estimates;
prediction from captures and validation against measured break forces remain future work.
Coverage is the fraction of points on a 71 by 71 grid over the bowl opening that lie beneath the detached sheet,
counting a point only where the sheet is no lower than 12 mm below the rim, and we report the lowest value over
the frames after the hands withdraw. The ceramic examples use a separate model. Each scanned vessel is divided
into connected patches of its original surface triangles, an inner wall 4.5 mm thick is inferred for each patch, and
neighboring patches are joined by bonds in NVIDIA Blast that break irreversibly once the stress in the wall exceeds the
material strength. Cracks therefore follow the boundaries between patches, and the fragments are native PhysX rigid
bodies.
The material values are engineering assumptions that a scan cannot measure, with a tensile strength of 6 MPa, a
Young’s modulus of 6 GPa, a fracture energy of 14 J/m2, and a density of 1850 kg/m3 for every vessel. Neither model
is part of the automatic scene export. Each example is authored as its own Isaac Lab scene, the parchment in the
kitchen scene and the vessels from their Polycam captures, which a preparation script converts to USD and divides
into fragments. All examples use scripted controllers, and none uses teleoperation or a learned policy. The robot
hands tear and place the parchment, a hand holding a cloth wipes one vessel and pushes it off its pedestal, and the
other vessels are dropped without a robot.

input video in side-by-side and overlay views at early, middle, and late frames. Depending on the discrepancy, it revises
the camera, the static geometry, the initial states, or the physical parameters and compares the views again. The final
scene is produced by a single build script that does not read the input video or any external assets, so its frames,
meshes, and trajectories can be regenerated from scratch and checked for format, frame count, and resolution.
Unlike the priors used for static captures (Section 3.4), the initial states and physical parameters of a 4D capture
are constrained by the observed motion, and the agent adjusts them until the simulation matches the video. The
fitted parameters reproduce the observed motion, but they are not necessarily unique and have not been tested
on other interactions. The solvers are of the same types as those in our deformable, fluid, and fracture examples
(Figures 11–13).

3.7 Scene Export and Editing
The converted scene is packaged in Universal Scene Description (USD) format [41]. Our experiments use Isaac
Lab [34]; the pipeline also targets MuJoCo [56] and Unreal Engine [10]. An interactive Three.js viewer (Figure 14)
supports robot placement, background cropping, asset insertion, and manual correction. Users can add converted
objects or assets from repositories such as NVIDIA Omniverse [36] before using the scene for a task.
SceneAgent exports each scene as OpenUSD. We worked primarily in Isaac Lab, and we also tested and verified the
exported scenes in MuJoCo and Unreal Engine. OpenUSD’s physics schema describes rigid bodies, mass, collision
shapes, joints, and physics materials with static and dynamic friction, restitution, and density, so a simulator that reads
the schema can load these properties from the file. Our export marks each movable object as a rigid body with its
mass and collision geometry, and stores its friction, restitution, density, and other estimated properties as attributes on
the same object.
The schema does not cover deformable bodies, fracture, or fluids and gels. Isaac Lab adds these through its PhysX
extensions and through NVIDIA Warp, Newton, and Blast, which our soft-body, tearing, fracture, and fluid examples
use. MuJoCo and Unreal Engine have their own deformable and destruction models, so these behaviors do not carry
over through the USD file and would need to be set up in each engine.
3.8 Digital Twins and Sisters
The reconstructed scene provides a digital twin of the captured workspace. To introduce variation, we generate
related instances of its objects, termed digital sisters, and compose scenes with randomized object placement and
lighting. These variations aim to expose a policy to a broader range of appearances and configurations during training.
Figure 15 shows seven reconstructed objects and their generated variants.
We generate digital sisters in two ways. The first perturbs the twin itself without calling a model. Bounded deformations
change the shape of the object’s own mesh, and each result is checked for changes in extent and volume and for
whether the object can still be grasped. A procedural texture of noise, hue rotation, mottling, and speckle is baked into
the vertex colors. The second uses Meshy. For new object instances, a reasoning model (Gemini 2.5 Flash) divides
the object into functional components and proposes variations of each, and an image model (Gemini 3 Pro Image)
edits the object image one component at a time. Each edit is then checked for real-world plausibility and fit with the
scene, and Meshy’s image-to-3D model lifts each accepted image to a textured mesh. For appearance alone, Meshy’s
retexture model (Meshy-6) repaints the original mesh with its geometry and UVs unchanged.
The edit prompt asks the image model to change one named component by the proposed variation, keep the result
realistic and ordinary, leave the other components unchanged, and match the scene. The retexture prompt asks for the
same object with a small change in surface detail, such as fine grain or light wear, and rules out new components,
holes, and changes in shape.
3.9 Hybrid Scenes from Captures and Digital Art
SceneAgent can also convert an individual captured object and place it in an artist-authored environment. This allows
a task-specific asset, such as a device component or server-rack part, to be incorporated into an existing scene without
recapturing the entire workspace. The same object conversion, physical modeling, and inspection tools apply to these
hybrid environments.
The examples span grippers and dexterous hands. Figure 16 shows Franka Emika Panda arms with Universal
Manipulation Interface (UMI) grippers [7], while Figure 17 shows Sharpa hands on Panda arms and a Dexmate
mobile manipulator. These examples illustrate the range of environments and interactions that can be assembled; the
policy-transfer study in Section 4.4 focuses on pick-and-place tasks.

4.1 Reconstruction Quality Across Agent Harnesses
Setup and reference.
Each harness converted the same scene from the MessyKitchens benchmark [1], once with
SceneAgent skills and once without them. We score the delivered objects against the benchmark’s ground-truth
meshes, which come from 3D scans of the real objects and are independent of every conversion run, including ours.
We use these meshes as the benchmark provides them, without postprocessing or manual correction. Figure 5 lists
the model and reasoning setting of each harness. Every run receives the same task prompt and the same version of
the SceneAgent skills, and no person intervened during the runs.
Each delivered mesh is placed using its saved transform. A single rigid transform derived from a shared capture frame
aligns the run to the reference; object scales are preserved, and no object is individually refit or repaired for scoring.
We sample 100,000 points per object with three sampling seeds, average metrics across seeds, and weight objects
equally. These seeds vary metric sampling, not the scene-conversion run.
We also evaluate on scene hard/000091 of GraspClutter6D [3], in which 20 YCB objects are stacked and occlude one
another in a bin. The input is 13 RGB views at 1920×1080. We score the delivered objects against the dataset’s

trained model to generalize in the real-world deployment, we employ the following variations between episodes when
generating successful demonstration episodes: object positions drawn randomly in a fixed bounding box with the size
of 42.9 × 9.8 (cm), yaws of the object orientation sampled randomly in [0, 2π), target-object size scale with ±10%,
base position of the Franka arm offset in a 2 cm-radius disc, exterior-camera pitch varies in ±2 degrees. In the layouts
of tasks during evaluation, we only employ randomization in the object positions.
All policies in the latest round are LoRA fine-tunes of the same pretrained π0.5 (DROID) checkpoint, which serves as
the baseline initialization. Fine-tuning applies LoRA (rank 16, α = 16 on the Gemma-2B backbone; rank 32, α = 32
on the 300M action expert) while the base transformer weights stay frozen; the SigLIP vision encoder and the small
action/state/time projection heads train at full rank. Every policy trains for 20k steps at batch size 32 on a single H200,
with each training job takes roughly 6.5 hours. And during evaluation, we run checkpoint 19999 of each policy on
the tasks. We train all the policies in a simulation-only manner: all demonstrations are collected in the simulation
environment and no real world demonstration is used.
We first present a comprehensive examination of the effect of scaling the number of simulated episodes on the
real-world performance. Table 2 shows the success rates of different policies completing the pick-and-place task.
We use 200 layouts in evaluation in simulation and use 50 layouts in real-world evaluation. Success rates in both
simulation and real-world do not improve indefinitely with the scaling of demonstration dataset. The real-world success
rate drops significantly after scaling past 40 demonstration episodes, indicating that excessive demonstrations alone
may create severe overfitting. Figure 22 further shows the different failure modes of policies in real-world deployments.
Now we present the effect of utilizing meta-learning strategies on the real-world performance. In order to achieve
different meta-learning strategies, we curate three simulated dataset with different purposes. Dataset 1 consists of 2k
demonstrations of the eggplant pick-and-place task with all the variations within episodes. Dataset 2 consists of 6k
tasks. It is aimed at training a policy that can do three pick-and-place tasks: pick and place an eggplant, a purple cube
and a cucumber. Among those 6k demonstration episodes, 2k are dedicated to each object respectively. Dataset 3
consists of 2k demonstration episodes of the eggplant pick-and-place task. But these 2k demonstration episodes do
not have full variation and randomization. Among these 2k demonstrations in dataset 3, only the positions of the object
are varied. With the three aforementioned curated datasets, we train three polices using dataset 1, dataset 1 + dataset
2, dataset 1 + dataset 2 + dataset 3, respectively. Table 3 shows that performance improves significantly when
we deploy the meta learning strategy of training on dataset 1 to 3 in sequence. Figure 23 presents three real-world
deployment episodes of policy 3 alongside their simulated counterparts and wrist-camera observations.

5 Limitations and Future Work
Conversion cost and verification.
Scene conversion requires repeated VLM calls and an external GPU workstation.
Processing all objects in a detailed environment remains slow and error-prone. Visual inspection can identify geometric
inconsistencies, but it cannot establish the accuracy of hidden geometry or physical parameters.
Background
completion and generated object variants also rely on predictions of content absent from the capture.
Physical and visual fidelity.
Estimated mass, friction, rigidity, and articulation parameters have not yet been calibrated
against measured robot interactions. The fracture examples additionally use engineering estimates. Captured Gaussian
splats retain the lighting of the original scene, so relighting and compositing mesh objects can introduce inconsistent
shadows and reflections. Future work should validate physical parameters against measurements and assess their
effect on contact-rich manipulation, including deformation, tearing, and adhesive contact.
Evaluation scope.
The reconstruction study uses one kitchen scene and one cluttered bin scene, each converted
once per configuration. Broader evaluation with more scenes and repeated conversion runs is needed to characterize
generalization and variability. The 4DCodeBench study covers three synthetic scenes, and real-world 4D videos,
which lack 3D ground truth, remain to be evaluated. The real-world study currently focuses on pick-and-place. Object
variants, placement, and lighting were randomized together, so their individual effects have not been isolated. Paired
real and simulated trials are also needed to assess the predictive value of the environments for policy evaluation.
Further automation.
Future directions include isolating the effects of scene and physical-parameter randomization,
extending the viewer to inspect recorded trajectories, and moving capture processing and policy adaptation onto robot
hardware so that data can remain on the device.

6 Conclusion
We presented SceneAgent, an agentic pipeline for converting 3D and 4D captures into interactive simulation environ-
ments. Its central workflow coordinates reconstruction and physical modeling tools with simulator-based inspection
and repair, and for videos of dynamic scenes it fits simulated dynamics to the observed motion. Digital sisters, hybrid
scenes, and an automated research loop, which turns real-robot failures into simulated demonstrations, extend the
converted environments for policy training and evaluation.
On a cluttered bin scene from GraspClutter6D, SceneAgent raises F1 at 1 cm for every harness with scorable paired
outputs, and its best F1 score is 60.75%, compared with 6.9% for SimFoundry. On three dynamic scenes from
4DCodeBench, SceneAgent raises the mean dynamic IoU of Gemini 4 Argon from 0.86% to 99.09%. Qualitative
examples demonstrate a range of simulated interactions and preliminary real-world deployment after policy fine-tuning.
Quantitative policy evaluation and validation of estimated physical parameters remain ongoing. The code, processed
scenes, and example converted objects will be released with the paper.
