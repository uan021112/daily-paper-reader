Abstract — Robot manipulation in everyday environments inherently involves substantial tool usage. Seemingly simple household tasks, such as cooking pan-fried tofu, require forceful cutting, delicate brushing, and precise placement using differ-ent tools under contact-rich interaction. Thus, the ability to autonomously swap and use task-appropriate tools is important for tool-centric manipulation. We introduce SwapBot, a modu-lar, tool-centric end effector system featuring low-cost adapters that quickly convert common household tools into robot end effectors. Rather than pursuing a single universal gripper, SwapBot adopts a unified design principle that combines a fixed robot-side coupling interface with low-cost, tool-side adapters, enabling compatibility with diverse tools—including articulated ones—and multiple robot platforms. Each adapter integrates compact tactile sensors, supports fast autonomous swapping, and interfaces with an intuitive teleoperation system for efficient demonstration collection. User studies show that, during teleoperation, SwapBot reduces task completion time and operator workload. Using collected demonstrations on the robot, we train imitation learning policies to successfully perform tool-centric tasks like cooking tofu, stirring salad, flipping meat. Our ablation study shows that using tactile-augmented demonstrations improves policy performance across contact-rich skills, increasing success rates by 15–25 percentage points over RGB-only policies. These results highlight modular, tactile-equipped tools as a practical and extensible pathway arXiv:2610.40646v1 [cs.RO] 29 Oct 2026 toward robust and capable robot manipulation. I. I NTRODUCTION 

Robotic manipulation is advancing toward increasingly human-level capabilities; however, seemingly simple tasks, such as cooking pan-fried tofu, remain challenging for robots to perform. Performed with different tools under contact-rich interaction, these tasks require precise but forceful control (cutting), delicate interaction with deformable objects (brush-ing), and dexterous motion (grasping and placing). Such 

tool-centric manipulation is challenging under the current end-effector design, which falls into two broad categories. First, anthropomorphic hands mimic human dexterity [1], [2], but their mechanical complexity imposes a high cost in maintenance, sensing, and control, hindering scalable data collection and policy learning. Second, parallel jaw grippers ,though practical and demonstrating impressive versatility [3], [4], [5], struggle to perform tasks requiring nuanced physical interactions, such as cutting or spreading. These capability vs controllability tradeoffs create significant barriers to more capable and scalable tool-centric robot manipulation. Humans accomplish complex manipulation tasks by using tools, e.g., knives, brushes, or screwdrivers, as extensions of their bodies. Robotists have explored tool use using dexter-ous hands [6], [7] or parallel grippers [8], [9]. However, ex-isting methods either inherit the complexity of manipulating anthropomorphic hands, struggling to maintain tool stability under load, or, critically, lack tactile sensing [10] at the tool-object interface, hindering effective control and learning for contact-rich tasks. To balance robot manipulators’ capability and control-lability, we propose to equip robots with the ability to swap modular tools through a tool changer. Industrial tool changers, such as those used in CNC machining or on UR5 robots, demonstrate the practicality of extending robotic capabilities through automatic tool exchange. However, these costly systems are typically customized for specific robots and cannot easily accommodate actively articulated tools - e.g., tongs and scissors. Moreover, they lack integration with tactile sensing, limiting their ability to support contact-rich manipulation tasks. Instead, our approach turns accessi-ble household items into robot tools and equips each tool with compact, custom tactile sensors, enabling robots to both select the appropriate tool for each subtask and sense contact at the tool–object interface. Drawing inspiration from surgical robots that interchange end-effectors such as scissors, cauterizers, and forceps, our system thus enables scalable household manipulation through tool diversity, not anatomical mimicry .We present SwapBot, a modular, low-cost, tool-centric manipulation system with integrated tactile sensors , as shown in Fig. 1. It features: (1)a unified adaptation inter-face for diverse tools and robots, (2) fast and autonomous tool swapping protocol, (3) integrated tactile sensing, and (4) intuitive teleoperation for demonstration data collection. Our system employs inexpensive, 3D-printed interfaces ($10 each) to quickly adapt common household tools . We apply this modular system to three robot platforms: Franka [11], UR5 [12], and Tycho [13] and to a variety of household tools - including articulated tools that require actuation, such as tongs and chopsticks. Robots equipped with our system can use our designed coupling interface and protocol 

to autonomously switch the tool within 10 seconds . Our teleoperation interface combines tool-matched leader devices with real-time visualized tactile feedback, providing opera-tors with an intuitive interface for contact-rich manipulation. Furthermore, we show that employing tactile sensing sig-nificantly boosts the performance of the autonomous policy learned from human demonstrations. To assess the ease of teleoperation with SwapBot, we conduct human studies with 12 participants of varying tele-operation skill levels (beginner, intermediate, and expert), focusing on two representative tasks: brushing and cutting. Participants report less subjective workload of control and higher success rates compared to the use of a generic teleoperation tool. SwapBot also enables the teleoperated completion of tasks previously found challenging or impos-sible for simple grippers, reducing task completion time and offering more satisfying user experiences. In robotic experi-ments, we further demonstrate the autonomous execution of a challenging long-horizon task: cooking pan-fried tofu. Using the demonstration data that our system collected, we train imitation learning policies and show that tactile feedback significantly improves policy performance, increasing task success rates by 25 absolute percentage points, from 35% to 

60% . We additionally provide qualitative results (e.g., stirring salad and flipping meat) in the supplementary videos. In summary, our contributions include: 1) A unified, low-cost, modular tool-centric end-effector system that supports rapid autonomous tool swapping and the use of diverse household tools, including articulated tools. 2) Integrated compact tactile sensing that improves both teleoperation performance and imitation-learning success in contact-rich task. 3) An intuitive teleoperation interface for collecting demonstrations , improving operator precision and en-abling high-quality data collection. 4) Empirical evaluation via user studies and policy learn-ing: quantitative tofu-cooking results and qualitative demonstrations of stirring salad and flipping meat, show-ing improved user experience and policy performance. II. T HE SWAP BOT SYSTEM 

The SwapBot system contains four main components: modular and interchangeable end-effectors (Section II-A), compact tactile sensing (Section II-B), the teleoperation interface (Section II-C), and policy learning from demon-strations (Section II-D). Our plug-and-play design supports low-cost prototyping with a variety of daily-use tools while supporting tactile sensing, large-scale demonstration collec-tion, and downstream policy learning. A. Modular End-Effectors: Tool Adapters and Coupling 

Design Goals. Industrial robotic coupling is usually tai-lored to specific tools and robots and often costs hundreds or even thousands of dollars. In contrast, we aim to develop a modular tool coupling system that is accessible and low cost (tool-side modules under $10), rapid swapping of tools, robot-agnostic, enabling compatibility with common manip-ulators, and flexible for both passive and articulated tools. To achieve these goals, we design our tool-swapping adapter and coupling system to enable a fully accessible fabrication workflow and straightforward assembly using basic hand tools, such as an Allen wrench. As shown in Figure 2 (left), we 3D-print tool adapters and inserts to enable the quick instrumentation and mounting of common household tools. The adapter both attaches the tool securely and houses the tactile sensors stably. For tools with moderate handle sizes, the same adapter can be reused, while soft silicone fills the remaining gap to accommodate varia-tions in handle shape and size. Only substantially thinner tools require a smaller 3D-printed adapter, while the robot-side coupling interface remains unchanged. Though many everyday tools include their own actuation mechanisms, equipping each with dedicated motors for automatic control would be complex and expensive. Instead, we shift all power and control functionalities to the robot side, ensuring a simpler and lower-cost tool implementation. We design compact, tool-specific piezoresistive tactile sensors that provide task-relevant contact information for teleoperation and policy learning. The piezoresistive tactile sensors consist of a three-layer structure, shown in Fig. 2 (center), where a force-sensitive material layer (Velostat) is sandwiched between top and bottom electrodes (conductive fabric) for signal transmis-sion. These sensors [14] convert real-time normal force pressure information into an electrical signal via the drop of resistance. The electrodes are made from copper-nickel conductive fabric, laser-cut to precise dimensions for optimal fit considering the sensitivity they need for tasks. To accom-modate the limited surface area of tools, we implement the sensors in a matrix layout, where sensing nodes are formed at the intersections of orthogonal electrode rows and columns. This configuration enables an n × n sensor array using only 2n electrodes. Our approach is low-cost, relies on only off-the-shelf materials, and supports easy customization through digital fabrication. We show the design and placement of tactile sensors for each tool in Fig. 2 (center). For tools with larger surfaces, we place sensors on both the handle and the blade or tip (e.g., tongs, spoons, knives). For smaller or thinner tools, such as chopsticks, brushes, and forks, it is impractical to mount sensors directly on the blade or tip. In these cases, we instrument only the handle, providing indirect measurements of tool–object interaction. This strategy ensures that tactile information is captured where feasible while keeping the modules compact and lightweight. For blade or tip instru-mentation, we mount the fabricated tactile sensors directly onto the tool surface. To accommodate handles of varying sizes, instead of attaching sensors directly, we install them between the TPU layer and the PLA adapter cover and fill the gap between the handle and TPU shell with soft silicone. This approach allows the sensors to comply with different handle shapes, stabilizes the installation process, and ensures consistent reflection of sensor readings during interaction. 

C. Teleoperation Interface: High-Quality Demonstration Collection 

We design the teleoperation interface to maximize operator intuitiveness and data quality, both of which are critical for downstream policy learning. Our interface maps the intuitive leader device’s 6-DoF pose and actuation commands directly to the corresponding tool on the robot side. By using similar tools on both sides, e.g., chopsticks to control chopsticks or a knife to control a knife, operators interact with familiar household tools, making control feel natural. This reduces the re-targeting effort common in teleoperation and lets op-erators focus on task execution rather than compensating for mismatched dynamics. For each leader tool, we estimate the 6-DoF pose using an Intel RealSense T265 tracking camera, and use the result to drive the robot during teleoperation. For articulated tools, we infer the actuation command from the relative motion measured by two IMUs, providing accurate correspondence between human and robot tool motion. During demonstration data collection, all data streams are synchronized at 20 Hz, including multi-view RGB images, tactile signals, robot joint states, actions, and task labels. This ensures that multimodal inputs remain temporally aligned for learning. We also provide visual overlays that display the live contact forces and tactile maps to help operators anticipate and refine contact-rich interactions. As we will show in Fig. 6, tactile information is visualized on-screen to indicate contact information. This immediate feedback provides operators with additional information about contact, improving both control precision and the quality of collected demonstrations. To evaluate the interface, we conduct controlled human studies to measure task completion time, success rate, and the subjective workload (see Sec. III-B). Integrated tactile feedback emphasizes subtle events, such as first contact or changes in load distribution, which are often ambiguous in vision alone. Our experiments aim to answer the following questions. 

(1) Characterization of tactile sensing: How well do our compact, tool-specific tactile sensors perform in terms of sensitivity and detection range, and does our design achieve the right balance for integration into diverse tools? (2) Intuitive teleoperation: Does our tool-centric teleoperation interface provide a natural, user-friendly experience that improves demonstration efficiency and data quality compared to standard baselines? (3) Policy learning with tactile feedback: Does integrating tactile sensing into imitation learning policies improve performance on complex, long-horizon tasks? 

A. Characterizing Tool-Specific Tactile Sensing 

Directly applying the tactile sensors without considering their size and sensitivity can lead to saturated and inconsis-tent signals during data streaming. To ensure smooth and reproducible sensing, we characterize the performance of our resistive tactile sensors made with electrodes of varying widths. Signals were recorded under controlled pressure using a force gauge, from which we extracted both sensitivity and detection range. These measurements provide valuable insights into how the electrode design impacts sensing qual-ity and guides our choice of parameters. As we show in Figure 2 (right), electrode size has a direct impact on both sensitivity and signal quality. Larger electrodes provide higher sensitivity, with identical forces producing stronger responses due to increased overlap be-tween the conductive layers. However, these larger electrodes also generate non-smooth signals with sudden jumps under load, and can saturate the 10-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC), making them less reliable for continuous feedback. Smaller electrodes, by contrast, sacrifice sensitivity but pro-duce smoother, more stable signals with a wider measurable range. To balance sensitivity, detection range, and signal fidelity within ADC limits, we select a 2 mm electrode width, which provides sufficient sensitivity while maintaining smooth, stable responses.

To evaluate the interface, we conduct a controlled human study with 12 participants spanning three teleoperation skill levels (beginner, intermediate, and expert), with four partici-pants in each group. Each participant performs 10 trials per task–condition pair, terminating upon success or a 60-s timeout. C. Policy Learning with Tactile Feedback for Long-Horizon Tasks 

We then evaluate whether tactile sensing improves imita-tion learning for complex, long-horizon manipulation. As a testbed, we use the challenging real-world task of preparing pan-fried tofu for quantitative experiments. The tofu is fragile and easily breaks if mishandled, requiring both gentle and dexterous manipulation. The robot must (1) gently brush sauce onto the tofu, (2) transport it to a pot using tongs for frying, and (3) return it to the cutting board and slice it into two pieces without damage. A trial is successful if the tofu is placed back on the board and cut cleanly into two pieces. Additionally, we provide qualitative demonstrations of other tool-mediated behaviors (e.g., stirring salad and flipping meat) in supplementary videos. We used our teleoperation interface to collect 100 demon-strations for each stage, i.e., brushing, transporting to the pot, and cutting. The reverse transport back to the board was not evaluated quantitatively since it mirrors the forward motion, but we collected the same number of demonstrations to train the policy for completeness and composition. We then used these demonstrations to train tool-specific imitation learning policies under different input modalities. As baselines, we compared against policies trained without tactile sensing. We also performed ablations using: tactile only, top-down RGB + tactile, and egocentric T265 RGB + tactile. For all policies, we used a CNN feature extractor concatenated with proprioceptive state and tactile signals (when available) as inputs to the policy network. We evaluated each tool-specific policy across 20 trials in a 20 cm × 20 cm workspace. Policies trained with tactile sensing achieved consistently higher success rates and were robust to small tofu shape changes and variations on initial position. RGB-only policies, in contrast, frequently failed due to partial observability of the tool–object interaction—especially at the tong tips where contact onset and incipient slip are often occluded in RGB— leading to poor contact regulation, slipping, or damage. Egocentric RGB + tactile performed poorly, reaching at most 40% success even with 100 demonstrations and staying near zero in tong picking and knife cutting tasks. Top-down RGB + tactile achieved moderate success (up to 50% in brushing and cutting), but it lagged significantly behind our full RGBs + tactile method. Though the top-down view offers reliable localization of the tofu on the cutting board, it lacks local visual detail for fine control. Conversely, the egocentric T265 view provides local information but suffers from its angled viewpoint (greater than 45°), which reduces localization accuracy com-pared to the near-vertical top-down camera. Our full RGBs + tactile policy achieved up to 85 % success in brushing, 70 % in tong picking, and 90 % in knife cutting, clearly outperforming all baselines (Tab. II). A. Tool Manipulation 

To enable robots to understand and manipulate tools, prior work has explored task-oriented grasping and functional affordances through self-supervised learning [16], [17]. How-ever, these works mainly focus on how to utilize a single tool’s different parts, i.e., how the tool should be grasped. [8], [9] further demonstrate long-horizon manipulation involving diverse tools through planning and learning. In contrast, our work focuses on an integrated hardware and learning system that converts everyday household tools into interchangeable, tactile-instrumented end effectors, combines tool-matched teleoperation with tactile feedback for demonstration collec-tion, and learns visuotactile policies for contact-rich manip-ulation. Moreover, previous works [16], [18] try to learn the synergy between different tool grasping points and tasks using self-supervised learning methods. The learned latent representations could provide a structured and condensed understanding of tool objects. However, our method does not rely on tool-affordance representations; instead, it uses a mechanically simple custom tool changer to directly focus on the interaction and manipulation parts of the task. 

B. Tool Design for Robot Tool Usage 

Several works aim to extend the range of tasks a single robot can perform by improving tool design and switching mechanisms. Industrial robots such as the UR5 employ dedicated tool changers that provide robust and precise switching performance. However, these systems are typically tailored to specific robots and tools, are expensive, and can-not easily accommodate actively articulated tools. Previous works have explored interchangeable surgical instruments [19] and mechanically actuated tools compatible with par-allel grippers [20]. In contrast, SwapBot provides a unified, low-cost system that supports both passive and articulated household tools, ensures secure coupling and reliable torque transmission for forceful, contact-rich manipulation, and integrates tactile sensing with tool-matched teleoperation for demonstration collection and downstream policy learning.

These systems provide high-resolution tactile feedback and have been demonstrated for a variety of robotic tasks, including fine-grained manipulation [22], material identification [23], and pose estimation [24]. To extend tactile coverage over larger areas of a robot’s body, capacitive and resistive tactile skins are often used [25]. These skins typically use a three-layer stack of orthogonal electrodes with a dielectric/resistive layer, where electrode intersections act as pressure-sensing nodes. This scalable design supports seamless integration onto customized robotic components, such as grippers [26], arms [27], and full-body surfaces [28], enabling broader and more versatile tactile sensing in complex environments. Our system adopts scalable tactile sensor designs and customizes them to diverse tools via digital fabrication. Tactile data has also been integrated into policy training for a variety of manipulation tasks, often showing substantial performance gains. For example, sensors mounted on parallel grippers [29], [30] provide valuable contact information that improves grasp stability and task success. However, such approaches are typically limited to specific gripper designs. In contrast, SwapBot enables more flexible measurement at the tool–object interface. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented SwapBot, a modular, tool-centric manip-ulation system with integrated tactile sensing that im-proves demonstration collection and policy learning for long-horizon tasks. Tactile feedback proved crucial for both tele-operation quality and imitation learning robustness, espe-cially with limited data. However, the current system has limitations: tactile sensors remain low resolution and prone to wear, sensor placement is fixed by human intuition, and pol-icy learning depends on predefined task segmentation. Future work will explore more durable, higher-resolution sensing, data-driven strategies for sensor placement, and autonomous policy sequencing without manual subgoal definitions. We will also open-source the system to promote reproducibility and community adoption.
