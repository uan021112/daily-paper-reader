# ThinkLocoManip: A Whole-Body Control Agentic Harness for Legged Loco-Manipulation 

Nuo Chen 1, Shizeng Zhang 1, Yujie Yang 1, Jinghong Wang 1, Zhenyang Qu 1, Xiao-Ming Wu 2, Yunfei Guo 3,Zhongwen Lu 1, Haojie Liu 1, Chunpeng Lu 1, Qiao Sun 1, Xueyi Zhang 1, Jiangwei Zhong 1, B

Abstract — In this paper, we propose ThinkLocoManip, an agentic harness for legged loco-manipulation designed to tackle complex, long-horizon tasks in large-scale environments. Exist-ing approaches typically follow either direct action generation or a plan-then-act paradigm, both of which can struggle with the generalization and error-recovery capabilities required for extended task execution. In contrast, ThinkLocoManip adopts an interleaved reasoning-and-acting paradigm, allowing the agent to continuously reason about its current state, execute actions, observe their outcomes, and adapt its strategy accord-ingly. Building on this paradigm, our agentic harness orches-trates a suite of specialized tools for perception, navigation, manipulation, and locomotion , enabling flexible composition of heterogeneous capabilities. Unlike existing robotic agentic harnesses, ThinkLocoManip explicitly incorporates locomotion as a tool domain for agent reasoning and execution. We further design a set of skills that explicitly account for both tool usage within each functional domain and coordination across domains, supporting effective execution of complex behaviors over long horizons. To tackle task failure during long-horizon interactions, we develop keyframe thinking mechanism to preserve critical execution context and support efficient error recovery. Extensive real-world experiments demonstrate that ThinkLocoManip substantially outperforms representative methods based on both direct action generation and plan-then-act paradigms across challenging long-horizon legged loco-manipulation tasks. Notably, ThinkLocoManip achieves a success rate of 77%, more than twice that of the strongest plan-then-act baseline (37.1%). Our experiments further validate the effectiveness of interleaved reasoning and acting, tool orchestration, and coordinated skill execution.

II. RELATED WORKS 

A. Loco-Manipulation Policy 

Achieving general-purpose loco-manipulation remains a fundamental challenge for legged robots equipped with ma-nipulators. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into optimization-based control and learning-based methods. Traditional optimization-based approaches [1]–[3] typically formulate loco-manipulation as a whole-body optimization problem and employ methods such as model predictive control (MPC) to jointly coordinate legged locomotion and arm manipulation. Learning-based approaches, in contrast, learn loco-manipulation capabilities from data and generally follow two paradigms. The first is direct action generation , where reinforcement learning (RL) is commonly used to first train low-level whole-body controllers that maintain balance and stabilize the robot during motion [4]–[8]. Building on these stabilized whole-body controllers, subsequent methods [9]– [11] further learn goal-conditioned loco-manipulation poli-cies that coordinate locomotion and manipulation. To further improve manipulation capabilities, recent approaches [12], [13] incorporate imitation learning or vision-language-action models (VLAs) to learn manipulation policies from human demonstrations. Despite their effectiveness, these methods can struggle to generalize to diverse real-world environments and long-horizon tasks due to limited training-data diversity and the sim-to-real gap. The second learning-based paradigm is plan-then-act [14]–[17], which decomposes loco-manipulation into reusable primitive tools and employs large language models (LLMs) or vision-language models (VLMs) to compose them into high-level plans for complex tasks. Such modularization improves compositionality and enables more flexible task execution. However, because reasoning and execution are typically separated, these approaches can become brittle when observations invalidate the initial plan or execution errors occur, limiting their ability to recover during long-horizon interactions. In contrast, ThinkLocoManip introduces an agentic har-ness for legged loco-manipulation based on an interleaved reasoning-and-acting paradigm. Rather than committing to a fixed policy or a pre-generated plan, the agent contin-uously reasons about its current state, invokes appropriate tools, observes execution outcomes, and adapts its strategy accordingly. This design improves generalization to diverse environments while enabling robust recovery from execution errors and external interference. B. Agentic Harness for Robotics 

Leveraging LLMs’ semantic understanding and reasoning capabilities, agentic harnesses have emerged as a promis-ing paradigm for robotic manipulation [18], [19]. Early works [16], [20]–[22] leverage LLMs to decompose high-level instructions into sequential sub-tasks and synthesize Python programs that invoke predefined action primitives. However, these methods typically follow an open-loop plan-then-act paradigm, in which the LLM generates the complete executable program upfront based on the task instruction. Consequently, they cannot adapt to intermediate execution outcomes, making them vulnerable to execution errors and environmental uncertainties. To improve the robustness and generalizability of robotic task execution, subsequent methods [23]–[27] incorporate agentic harnesses to support task decomposition, closed-loop interaction, execution monitoring, and error recovery. Despite their success in tabletop manipulation, extending these ap-proaches to legged loco-manipulation remains challenging, as long-horizon tasks in large-scale and unstructured envi-ronments require effective coordination between locomotion and manipulation. In this work, we introduce ThinkLocoManip , an agentic harness for legged loco-manipulation that equips the agent with specialized tools for perception, navigation, manipula-tion, and locomotion . Unlike existing robotic agentic har-nesses, ThinkLocoManip explicitly incorporates locomotion 

as a first-class tool domain, allowing the agent to reason about and invoke locomotion capabilities alongside other tools during task execution. By coordinating these hetero-geneous capabilities, ThinkLocoManip enables robust long-horizon task execution in complex environments. III. Navigational Tools for Localization and Path Planning. 

The robot needs to autonomously navigate through the environment to reach the target position of each sub-task. We design two approaches to enable robot navigation: a map-based way and a target-based way. Map-based Nav: 

Based on an pre-built LiDAR-based robocentric 3D occu-pancy grid map [29], the robot can quickly localize itself [30] and annotate waypoints in the map. The agent then matches the location information in the task description to the corresponding waypoint names and finally reaches the task destination through path planning [31]. Target-based Nav: 

Unlike map-based nav, target-based nav does not need pre-built map to plan the path. It directly computes the relative angle and distance between the robot base and the target pose, and then plans a straight-line trajectory toward the target. We design a two-stage approaching method, in which the robot first adjusts its body orientation toward the target object and then moves straight toward it. 

Manipulative Tools for Diverse Physical Interaction. After reaching the task location, the agent selects and combines appropriate manipulation tools from the primitive tool set according to the specific sub-task requirements. For grasping task, we employ EconomicGrasp [32], a lightweight and high-performance 6-dof grasp pose detection method [33], [34], to generate a suitable grasp pose for the selected object. To provide the robot with more diverse physical interaction capabilities, we further design a set of manipulation tools, including lifting up, putting down, pushing and pulling, enabling the agent to perform more complex tasks such as opening a chest or cabinet. However, it is still hard for robot to accomplish challenging loco-manipulation purely depending on manipulative tools without locomotion tools. 

Locomotion Tools for Whole-Body Control. Unlike ex-isting robotic agentic harnesses that primarily orchestrate perception, navigation, and manipulation capabilities, we explicitly expose locomotion as a first-class tool domain, allowing the agent to reason about and actively control the robot’s whole-body motion during task execution. To enable stable movement and task-dependent body adjustment, we provide locomotion tools for both body velocity and pose control. Velocity Control: We adopt HIM [35] to train a robust reinforcement learning policy for locomotion velocity control. The agent invokes this tool by specifying the de-sired planar velocity and yaw rate, which are tracked by the low-level locomotion policy while maintaining stable legged motion. This allows the agent to continuously adjust the robot’s position and orientation, such as approaching a manipulation target, aligning the body with the target, or repositioning the robot when the current configuration is unsuitable for subsequent manipulation. Pose Control: We develop a matrix-based formulation to compute the leg joint angles required to achieve a desired body posture. C. ThinkLocoManip-Skill for Long-Horizon Tasks 

Although the agent is given so many kinds of primitive tools, it is still challenging to pick suitable tools and compose them together in a rational order. The agent should be capable of decomposing a task into a sequence of sub-tasks, coordinating whole-body posture throughout long-horizon loco-manipulation, and recovering promptly from execution failures. Toward this end, we introduce ThinkLocoManip-Skill , defines both the workflow for composing primitive tools within each skill and the behavioral constraints for coordinating different skills. The description specifies the functionality of each skill briefly, enabling the agent to efficiently match appropriate skills to corresponding sub-tasks. To ensure that each skill is invoked under appropriate conditions, we define the pre-rule of each skill to specify when it should be triggered. Then the agent follows the predefined workflow of skills and executes each tool sequentially. To ensure safe and stable execution throughout the entire skill process, we set five basic skills for long-horizon loco-manipulation: Sub-task Planning is responsible for decomposing the task instruction into asequence of sub-tasks. After each sub-task is completed, the agent verifies whether the execution was successful. If a failure is detected, the agent replans the execution order of the remaining sub-tasks. Path Navigation guides the robot to the target position by selecting appropriate navigation tools based on the requirements of each sub-task. For indoor nav-igation, the agent invokes map-based navigation to reach a pre-labeled waypoint. For sub-tasks that require approaching a target in either indoor or in-the-wild environments, the agent first detects the target’s spatial coordinates and then invokes target-based navigation to approach it. We set the distance between the robot head and the target object position to 10 cm. Object Searching handles cases in which the target object is not visible given the robot’s current orientation. It instructs the agent to invoke perception tools to detect the target object. If the target is not detected, the agent commands the robot to rotate 45 ◦ clockwise until the object is detected or the search attmpts reaches a upper limit.or the maximum number of search attempts is reached. Error Recovery enables the agent to analyze the causes of execution failures based on task execution logs and replan sub-tasks accordingly. We design Keyframe Thinking mechanism to efficiently record important task execution logs, helping the agent identify execution failures and adapt its behavior. The detail is shown in Section III-D. D. Keyframe Thinking for Error Recovery 

During long-horizon loco-manipulation, execution failures can invalidate the planned sub-task sequence and inter-rupt task progress. To address this challenge, we intro-duce Keyframe Thinking, an error-recovery mechanism that enables the agent to analyze task execution logs, identify potential causes of failure, and replan the sub-task sequence accordingly. We define a keyframe as a time instant at which the robot makes physical contact with an object during loco-manipulation task execution. Then the agent logs the RGB image, robot states, current sub-task and primitive tool’s name into a file. Rather than recording every frame during task execution, we log information only at keyframes, preserving essential task context while reducing the agent’s reasoning time. After each sub-task execution, the resulting state is recorded and provided to the agent alongside the keyframe information to determine whether the task has been successfully completed. IV. EXPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

In the main setup, we conduct five diverse long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks in real-world indoor environ-ments to systematically evaluate our method. These tasks require the robot to autonomously execute multiple stages of navigation, whole-body adjustment, and manipulation over extended task horizons. All experiments are performed on the Lenovo BOT MX, a wheeled quadruped robot equipped with an Agilex PiPER 6-axis robotic arm mounted at the front of the robot, as shown in Fig. 4. We use GPT-5.6 Sol as the LLM backbone of our agent. At the beginning of each task, the agent is provided only with a natural-language task instruction and must autonomously control the robot throughout the entire task execution. According to task complexity, we categorize the five tasks into three simple tasks and two challenging tasks. 

a) Simple Tasks: (1) Table Grasping : The robot is required to navigate to a table, grasp a specified object, and place it at a designated position. We evaluate the task using tables of two different heights, requiring the agent to adapt the robot’s whole-body configuration to different manipulation heights. (2) Ground Grasping : The target object is placed on the ground. The agent must first locate and approach the object, and then adjust the robot’s body posture to reach a feasible configuration for grasping. (3) Button Pressing : The robot is required to navigate to a switchgear cabinet, identify and press the button of the specified color, and subsequently navigate back. 

b) Challenging Tasks: (1) Chest Manipulation : The robot is required to navigate to the chest, grasp its handle and pull it open, retrieve the target object from inside, and finally place the object on the table. This task requires the agent to coordinate multiple sequential navigation and manipulation behaviors over a longer execution horizon. (2) 

Cabinet Manipulation : Similar to Chest Manipulation , the robot must navigate to the cabinet, grasp its handle and pull the cabinet open, and then retrieve the target object from inside. The task requires coordinated whole-body adjustment and manipulation across multiple stages. We compare three method as baselines: (1) ACT [36], a visormotor policy for robotics manipulation. E. Robustness to External Interference 

We also conduct experiments on three indoor tasks to evaluate the robot’s robustness to external interference. We manually reset the task environment after each successful trial, such as replacing the grasped objects or reclosing the chest after successful opening. The results are shown in Table V. The Plan-then-Act method fails to recover because its task plan is fixed after generation and cannot be updated in response to execution failures. We also observe that although ACT and π0.5 tend to resume task execution after environment resets, such as moving the gripper close to the object, they still fail to complete the tasks successfully, demonstrating their sensitivity to external interference. Our method achieves the best performance, which can be at-tributed to the design of the Error Recovery skill and the Keyframe Thinking mechanism. This enables the agent to accurately diagnose failure causes and promptly adapt its behavior to recover from execution failures. V. CONCLUSION In this work, we present ThinkLocoManip , an agentic harness for complex, long-horizon legged loco-manipulation in large-scale real-world environments. Rather than directly generating actions or committing to a fixed plan before exe-cution, ThinkLocoManip adopts an interleaved reasoning-and-acting paradigm, allowing the agent to continuously reason about the current state, invoke appropriate tools, ob-serve execution outcomes, and adapt its strategy accordingly. Building on this paradigm, we organize specialized primitive tools for perception, navigation, manipulation, and locomo-tion, and introduce ThinkLocoManip-Skill to structure tool usage and cross-domain coordination. We further develop 

Keyframe Thinking to preserve critical execution context and support efficient error recovery during long-horizon interactions. Extensive real-world experiments demonstrate that ThinkLocoManip substantially outperforms represen-tative direct action generation and plan-then-act methods, achieving an overall success rate of 77.0% , compared with 37.1% for the strongest baseline. Additional ablations, external-interference experiments, and in-the-wild evalua-tions further validate the importance of whole-body control and Keyframe Thinking, as well as the robustness and gener-alization capability of the overall framework. We hope Thin-kLocoManip provides a step toward more general-purpose robotic agents that can flexibly compose heterogeneous ca-pabilities, adapt to intermediate execution outcomes, and robustly accomplish long-horizon loco-manipulation tasks in complex real-world environments.
