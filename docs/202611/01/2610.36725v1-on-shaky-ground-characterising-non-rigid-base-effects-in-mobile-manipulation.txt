# Abstract 

Mobile robot manipulators extend contact-task capability beyond the workspace of fixed robot installations, but their bases must also sus-tain the forces generated by the task. On lightweight platforms, base motion and support compliance can change the force and motion re-alised at the workpiece. How these non-rigid base effects manifest during sustained contact remains insufficiently characterised. This pa-per investigates non-rigid base effects in mo-bile robot manipulators through a sanding case study combining exploratory simulation with experiments on two lightweight, unactuated wheeled testbeds. Joint measurements of con-tact force, tool motion, and base vibration show that sustained contact coexisted with substan-tial task error: under low base–ground resis-tance, only 54–70% of a 100 N command was realised, with tool-centre-point errors reaching 100 mm. Increasing base–ground resistance during contact improved force realisation and arrested further tool displacement, while base vibration did not vary consistently with force-tracking error in the tested conditions. These observations illustrate how non-rigid base be-haviour can affect contact-task execution and highlight the value of examining force, mo-tion, and vibration together when characteris-ing lightweight mobile manipulation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Fixed-base industrial manipulators are widely used for contact-rich processes such as sanding, polishing, and assembly. Their rigid bases enable accurate motion and force control, but limit operation to a fixed workspace. Figure 1 illustrates these effects. Normal and tangen-tial forces at the sanding tool are transmitted through the manipulator to the base. If the horizontal reaction exceeds the available traction, the base can slip. If the resultant wrench moves the zero-moment point outside the support polygon, the system can lose static stability. Compliance in the tyres, suspension, or ground surface can also introduce oscillation of the base and manipu-lator. These behaviours are collectively referred to as 

non-rigid base effects . They affect contact-task perfor-mance because base motion contributes to tool centre point (TCP) error [Zhao et al. , 2021], while base vibra-tion and deformation can disturb the contact force. Un-der force control, the arm commands tool advance rela-tive to its base, whereas the contact force depends on the tool–workpiece compression. Base motion away from the workpiece under the contact reaction can therefore ab-sorb part of the commanded advance and contribute to a sustained force shortfall and to undesired tool motion relative to the workpiece, even while contact is main-tained. Existing work has examined mobile-manipulator sta-bility, wheel slip, machining accuracy, compliance, and contact-force control, but there is limited experimen-tal characterisation of how base motion manifests si-multaneously in realised contact force and tool motion relative to the workpiece during sustained contact on lightweight mobile manipulators. This paper addresses this gap through a sanding case study combining ex-ploratory simulation with experiments on two instru-mented lightweight wheeled testbeds. Their unactuated bases serve as mechanical surrogates for mobile robot bases (Section 4.1). The study addresses three ques-tions: (i) What non-rigid base effects arise under con-tact forces? (ii) How do base–environment coupling, arm posture, tool excitation, and support compliance affect force realisation, tool motion, and base vibration? (iii) Do the principal response patterns persist across differ-ent lightweight platforms? The contributions are: 

• An experimental characterisation of non-rigid base effects in force-controlled sanding, showing that sus-tained contact coexisted with force shortfall and TCP error, and that increasing base–ground resis-tance during contact improved force realisation and arrested further tool displacement; 

• An assessment of how these effects vary with com-manded force, arm posture, tool excitation, and support compliance on two lightweight mobile plat-forms, with base-vibration measurements providing a complementary view of the dynamic response. These measurements identify force and motion responses that future active compensation strategies on mobile robots would need to address. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Robotic Surface Finishing 

Robotic grinding and sanding require simultaneous path tracking and regulation of the tool–workpiece interac-tion. Process studies relate abrasive properties, tool speed, feed rate, and normal force to material removal and surface quality [Torrance, 1990; Luo et al. , 2014; Zhang et al. , 2006; Li et al. , 2024]. Reviews of mobile robotic machining and grinding identify limited struc-tural stiffness, motion accuracy, and process vibration as persistent limitations [Tao et al. , 2019; Li et al. , 2026]. Previous studies have addressed grinding vibration through feed-speed and force control, and through pos-ture selection [Dimo et al. , 2001], force-controlled end-effectors for flexible workpieces [Wang et al. , 2021], and tool-holder compliance design [Asada and Goldfine, 1985]. These approaches mainly address vibration and compliance associated with the manipulator, tool, or workpiece, while the influence of a mobile base and its interaction with the ground has received less attention. 

2.2 Mobile Machining and Base Stability 

Mobile robotic machining extends the workspace avail-able for large aircraft, ships, rail vehicles, and other large-scale structures [Tao et al. , 2019; Li et al. , 2026]. Existing systems commonly use heavy automated guided vehicles, rail-mounted bases, external metrology, or com-pliant grinding heads to recover machining accuracy [M¨ oller et al. , 2017; Zhao et al. , 2021; 2023; Yang et al. ,2024]. These systems typically rely either on heavy mo-bile platforms or on specially designed, structured envi-ronments to provide sufficient stability, reducing the mo-bility and ease of deployment that motivate lightweight mobile manipulators. The tip-over stability of mobile manipulators has a substantial theoretical foundation. Papadopoulos and Rey [1996] introduced the force–angle stability margin for systems subject to external and inertial loads. Gar-nier et al. [2018] analysed the dynamic tip-over stability of a mobile manipulator for naval-manufacturing tasks and showed that joint speed, joint acceleration, and structure height affect its stability. More recent work derives individual wheel support forces for direct moni-toring of contact loss [Petrovi´ c and Mattila, 2022], while experiments with a UR5 on a mobile base show that high, cantilevered payloads can destabilise the platform during navigation [Bostelman and Hong, 2017]. Hence, force effects on the mobile base are worth investigating. Wheel slip is often excluded by the pure-rolling as-sumption. Galicki [2023] treated wheel-slip reaction forces and unstructured end-effector forces as distur-bances in finite-time task-space control of mobile ma-nipulators. Such slip must therefore be accounted for when lightweight robot platforms are adopted for con-tact tasks. 

2.3 Base Compliance and Physical Interaction 

Wang and Carmichael [2024] experimentally varied mobile-base rigidity during physical human–robot col-laboration and found that base compliance affects task accuracy and human interaction. Their results show that the mechanical properties of the mobile base and its con-tact with the ground can directly influence physical in-teraction. Gao et al. [2026] investigated dynamic base–arm cou-pling in mobile robot–environment interaction and de-veloped a motion/force controller to compensate for the resulting disturbances. Their wall-cleaning experiments considered force-controlled interaction while the mobile base remained in motion. Together, these studies show that base compliance and base motion can significantly affect physical interaction. However, experimental characterisation of how base mo-tion manifests jointly in realised contact force and tool motion relative to the workpiece remains limited for sus-tained force-controlled contact on lightweight platforms intended to remain stationary. The present study exam-ines this setting by measuring contact force, tool motion, and base vibration together. 

# 3 Exploratory Simulation 

The simulation is used as an exploratory tool to identify potential failure modes, examine how they vary with dif-ferent system and task parameters, and inform the phys-ical experiments (see Section 4). 

3.1 Environment and Control 

The simulation uses MuJoCo [Todorov et al. , 2012] with a UR10 manipulator on a differential-drive base facing a rigid vertical workpiece, as shown in Figure 2a. The wheels and base structure are modelled as rigid, so the simulation captures traction and stability limits but does not include compliance at the wheel–ground contacts. The end-effector carries a rotating sanding tool with a compliant pad and a virtual force/torque sensor. The pad is modelled using multiple MuJoCo contact points with compliant contact parameters, providing an ap-proximate distributed contact over the sanding surface. A hybrid force–position controller regulates the normal contact force while controlling tangential motion [Sicil-iano and Villani, 1999]. Contact force, wheel friction, arm posture, mass distribution, and tool excitation are varied to examine the resulting failure modes. 

3.2 Observed Failure Modes 

Heuristic parameter sweeps produced three repeatable behaviours (Figure 2). First, contact excitation pro-duced oscillation of the tool and base around the tar-get pose. The oscillation increased with tool–workpiece friction, commanded normal force, and arm extension, while tool speed changed the dominant excitation fre-quency. These observations led to the use of separate force-variation and base-vibration metrics in the physi-cal experiments. Second, base slip occurred when the tangential forces required at the wheel–ground contacts exceeded the available traction. Slip became more likely with larger contact loads, lower wheel–ground friction, and postures that reduced the load on the driven wheels. This also motivated measuring tool motion and base response sep-arately, since tool-path error alone cannot distinguish base motion from manipulator tracking error. Third, the simulated robot lost stability when the combined gravitational and contact wrench moved the zero-moment point outside the support polygon. Ex-tended postures and unfavourable mass distributions re-duced the stability margin, identifying loss of stability as another potential failure mode under contact load. These observations are qualitative. The simulated contact parameters were not identified from the physical platforms, and the rigid-wheel model does not represent compliance in the tyres, platform, or supporting surface. Such model mismatch is a source of the simulation-to-reality gap discussed in robotics [Zhao et al. , 2020]. The simulation is therefore used to identify plausible failure modes and qualitative trends rather than to predict the hardware results quantitatively.

4.1 Platforms 

The cart carries a UR10 on an aluminium frame and deck with four lockable wheels, on a concrete floor (Figure 3a). The trolley carries a UR5 on a lighter plywood deck with four lockable wheels (Figure 3b). A foam layer be-neath the trolley provides a repeatable compliant sup-port that stands in for compliant tyres, suspension, or soft ground, the non-rigid components motivating the base-compliance study of Wang and Carmichael [2024]. 

4.2 Experimental Setup 

Both platforms use a wall-mounted panel and the same wrist-mounted RYOBI ROS300-S random-orbital sander (300 W, 125 mm pad), operated at its lowest and highest dial settings, referred to as low and high tool speed. 1 The base–ground resistance is set low by leaving the wheels free and high by locking them. Locking the wheels in-creases the resistance to base motion but does not rigidly fix the platform to the ground; compliance in the wheels, deck, and support remains. A wrist-mounted Axia80-M20 six-axis force/torque sensor (ATI Industrial Automation; effective resolution 0.1 N and 0.005 N m) measures contact force. A deck-mounted Orientus inertial measurement unit (Advanced Navigation; ±4 g range) records base acceleration at 1000 Hz, with no additional software filtering applied to the recorded data. An Intel RealSense D435 camera on a fixed tripod tracks a tool-mounted ArUco marker to measure tool motion (Figure 3c).

4.3 Force Controller 

A task-space admittance controller converts normal-force error into end-effector velocity while maintaining tangential position. Cartesian commands are resolved to joint velocities using a quadratic programme [Havi-land et al. , 2022]. 

4.4 Task and Trials 

The controller regulates normal force against the wall while holding a fixed tangential target relative to the base. Normal end-effector motion is commanded relative to the base, so motion of the base relative to the wall changes the resulting tool–workpiece displacement. The TCP error is estimated from the tool-mounted marker, tracked by the fixed camera, as its displacement from a reference position (Section 4.5). It is therefore measured relative to the workpiece, whereas the tangential target is held relative to the base; base motion thus enters the TCP error even when the arm tracks its own command. The trials are organised into three experiments, sum-marised in Table 2. Experiment 1, on the cart, varies the base–ground resistance, arm posture, and commanded force in single-condition trials (T1–T4). Experiment 2, also on the cart, switches from low to high resistance part-way through contact at two tool speeds (T5, T6), providing a within-trial comparison of the two resistance conditions while tracking TCP error with the camera. Experiment 3 examines whether the same effects are ob-served on a lighter and more compliant platform. The trolley is lighter than the cart and is placed on foam to introduce additional support compliance. T7 and T8 re-peat the low- and high-resistance conditions at high tool speed. A separate hard-floor and foam comparison at low resistance, high tool speed, and a 20 N command examines the effect of support compliance. 4.5 Evaluation Metrics 

The realised force ratio ρF = ¯F /F ∗ is the mean normal force ¯F divided by the commanded force F ∗. Force error 

eF is the root-mean-square deviation from the command. Force variation σF is the standard deviation of the force after subtracting a least-squares linear fit over the eval-uation interval, while the slope of that fit is reported separately as force creep. All metrics are computed over steady contact, excluding the transients at contact onset, at the resistance switch, and at retraction; for T5 and T6, the low- and high-resistance phases are evaluated separately. Base vibration app is the peak-to-peak acceleration normal to the deck over 1 s intervals, summarised by its median and maximum. Base vibration is compared within each experiment. TCP error eTCP is the magni-tude of the tool-marker displacement over the evaluation interval (Section 4.4); for the high-resistance phases of T5 and T6 it is therefore the displacement added after the switch, whereas the figures show the displacement accumulated from the start of the record.

5.1 Force Shortfall and TCP Motion at Low Resistance 

At a 100 N command, all low-resistance trials realised only 54–70% of the commanded force (Table 2). The shortfall persisted through steady contact. In T1, force rose to about 50 N and then crept upwards at 0.4 N s −1,giving a realised force of 58.8 N and a force error of 41.5 N (Figure 4a). Force variation was 2.6 N, indicating that the principal departure from the command was a sustained force shortfall rather than large fluctuations around the target. On the second platform, T7 realised 54% of the com-mand and accumulated 96 mm of TCP error (Figure 6a). The error initially grew at 2.9 mm s −1 before slow-ing, while force continued to rise, with a force creep of 0.8 N s −1.Experiment 2 shows the same combination within sin-gle trials (Figure 5). In the low-resistance phases of T5 and T6, the realised force ratios were 0.58 and 0.70, while TCP error accumulated to 98 and 103 mm at 2.8– 3.1 mm s −1 with the tangential target fixed relative to the base. Sustained contact with relatively low short-term force variation, 3.3 and 3.5 N, thus coexisted with continued tool motion across the workpiece, until the resistance was increased part-way through contact. 

5.2 Response to Increased Base Resistance 

The within-trial switches in Experiment 2 provide the most direct comparison of the two resistance conditions. In T5 and T6, resistance was increased at 45 s and 39 s, respectively, while the force command remained at 100 N. TCP-error growth was arrested within about 1 s, with only about 1 mm of additional displacement over each high-resistance evaluation interval. Force rose to-wards the command over about 5 s, giving realised force ratios of 0.88 and 1.01. Force error fell from 41.6 to 14.1 N in T5 and from 30.6 to 7.7 N in T6, reductions of 66% and 75%. Force variation increased from 3.3 to 6.9 N and from 3.5 to 6.1 N, respectively, and force creep was 0.75–0.77 N s −1.The substantially lower force error indicates that overall force tracking improved at both tool speeds. The single-condition comparisons showed the same di-rection of change. From T1 to T3, the realised force ratio increased from 0.59 to 0.94 and force error de-creased from 41.5 to 6.9 N (Figure 4c). From T7 to T8, the realised force ratio increased from 0.54 to 0.94 and force error decreased from 47.2 to 9.9 N; T8 also showed about 1 mm of TCP error (Figure 6b). The same direc-tion of change was therefore observed in the within-trial switches, in the separate trials, and on both platforms. 

5.3 Dependence on Posture and Commanded Force 

Changing from the standard to the low posture reduced the force shortfall at low resistance. From T1 to T2, the realised force ratio rose from 0.59 to 0.68 and force error fell from 41.5 to 32.1 N (Figure 4a,b). Force still crept upwards at 0.3 N s −1 in T2 and remained well below the 100 N command. The posture change altered the response without removing the sustained force shortfall. The commanded-force comparison showed a larger change. T4 differed from T2 only in the commanded force, reduced from 100 to 25 N. At 25 N, the realised force was 26.3 N, force error was 1.9 N, and force varia-tion was 1.3 N, with negligible force creep (Figure 4d). # 6 Discussion 

The central observation is that sustained contact did not guarantee that the commanded force was realised or that the intended tool position relative to the workpiece was maintained. Under the 100 N command at low resis-tance, contact was maintained throughout while the re-alised force remained well below the command and the tool moved about 100 mm across the workpiece. The within-trial resistance changes provide the clearest link between base–ground resistance and contact execution: with the force command unchanged, TCP-error growth was arrested and realised force increased. The same di-rection of change was observed on the second platform. The simultaneous force shortfall and TCP motion, and their response to increased resistance, are consistent with base motion absorbing part of the commanded tool ad-vance, as outlined in the Introduction. Lowering the arm changes the contact-force moment about the wheel con-tacts, while reducing the commanded load changes the reaction that the support must sustain. The posture and load comparisons show how these task conditions affect the expression of non-rigid base effects. Vibration characterises another part of the response. Tool excitation is evident even out of contact, and the hard-floor and foam comparison shows the influence of support conditions. The reduction in vibration when the pad was loaded in Experiment 3 is consistent with con-tact changing how excitation is transmitted through the tool, arm, base, and support. The T1 end-of-contact bursts also show that vibration can evolve substantially within one resistance condition. The improved force tracking at high resistance, despite increased vibration, illustrates how these measurements describe different as-pects of contact. The three measurements consequently serve comple-mentary purposes. Force quantifies how closely the con-tact load follows the command; the tool marker reveals continued tool motion relative to the workpiece even when the contact force appears to have plateaued; and base vibration describes the response to tool excitation and support conditions. Their joint measurement makes the consequences of non-rigid base behaviour visible be-yond a single tracking metric. The simulation supplies a qualitative framework for base motion, oscillation, and loss of stability, while the hardware comparisons reveal compliance-related responses that are not represented by the rigid-wheel model. The scope of these conclusions is bounded by the case-series design. Each condition was run once, with the principal force and motion responses observed on two platforms. The measured tool motion includes contribu-tions from manipulator tracking error, arm deflection, and base motion. Realised force and arm configuration also changed across the resistance conditions, so their contributions to the observed vibration cannot be sep-arated from the effect of increased base–ground resis-tance. All contact trials used the operating sander, leav-ing tool and controller contributions to force variation unresolved. Surface finish and material removal were not assessed, so vibration amplitude is not established as a measure of process quality. The two platforms and sup-port conditions cover only a limited range of lightweight mobile manipulator configurations. 

# 7 Conclusion and Future Work 

This sanding case study characterised non-rigid base ef-fects during contact tasks on two lightweight wheeled manipulation testbeds. At low base–ground resistance, a 100 N command produced persistent force shortfall, with only 54–70% realised and TCP errors of about 100 mm in the camera-tracked trials. In the within-trial com-parisons, increasing resistance during contact arrested further TCP-error growth within about 1 s and raised the average realised force to 88–101% of the command during the high-resistance phases. Separate low- and high-resistance trials on the second platform showed the same direction of change. The magnitude of the force shortfall depended on posture and commanded force: the low posture reduced it, while a 25 N command was closely tracked at low resistance. Base vibration showed a distinct dependence on tool excitation, support, and contact loading. The exploratory simulation provided a qualitative framework for base motion, oscillation, and stability, while the hardware exposed compliance-related responses not represented by the rigid-wheel model. To-gether, these results characterise non-rigid base effects through force realisation, tool motion, and dynamic re-sponse. Future work should examine commanded force, pos-ture, and support conditions independently, repeat the comparisons across a wider range of platforms, and re-late the measured dynamic response to surface finish and material removal. Experiments on actuated mobile robots should also evaluate coordinated wheel-motor and arm commands to compensate for measured base mo-tion while maintaining contact force. Their effectiveness would need to be assessed against available wheel trac-tion and base stability. These studies would help define feasible contact conditions for mobile manipulators op-erating with non-rigid base–environment support.
