## Abstract 

Feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting has recently emerged as a promising alter-native to per-scene optimization, enabling fast novel view synthesis by predicting Gaussian primitives directly from input images. However, it quickly faces a bot-tleneck of rendering efficiency while scaling from sparse to dense multi-view inputs, which offer more geometric constraints but produce a rapidly growing set of candidate Gaussians. Existing solutions cannot reliably distinguish redun-dant primitives, leading to many redundant primitives from repeatedly observed regions and increased rendering cost without much gain in visual quality. We propose BudSplat, a compact feed-forward 3DGS framework that learns to keep the most valuable Gaussians under a limited primitive count. BudSplat has two novel designs. The first is a detail-aware importance score that is extracted from multi-level visual tokens, providing an explicit cue for regions where pruning is likely to harm perceptual quality. The score can be injected into voxel fusion to preserve high-value local contributors during multi-view aggregation. The second is quality-aware Gaussian pruning that selects a compact set of primitives by jointly considering rendering contribution and detail importance. The quality-aware prun-ing enables local quota redistribution that helps avoid over-allocating primitives to large redundant regions. Experiments on RE10K, DL3DV, and ACID show that BudSplat achieves a favorable trade-off among rendering quality and efficiency. 

## 1 Introduction 

Novel view synthesis aims to recover a scene representation from a set of input images and render photorealistic images from unseen viewpoints. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become a highly effective representation for this task due to its explicit Gaussian primitives and efficient splatting-based renderer, offering an attractive balance between reconstruction quality and rendering speed [Kerbl et al., 2023]. However, 3DGS relies heavily on per-scene optimization that necessitates a separate reconstruction and optimization process for each new scene, limiting its applicability to large-scale data processing, interactive applications, and practical deployment. Feed-forward 3DGS, which predicts Gaussian primitives directly from input images in a single network pass, has recently been investigated to amortize the cost of per-scene optimization. Different

feed-forward designs have been explored, including pixel-aligned Gaussian prediction, cost-volume-based geometry reasoning, transformer-based large reconstruction models, voxel-aligned prediction, graph-based Gaussian aggregation, and unposed feed-forward reconstruction [Charatan et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2024a, Ziwen et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025b, Zhang et al., 2024b, Jiang et al., 2025]. More general feed-forward world models also begin to support 3D Gaussians as one of their output representations [Liu et al., 2025b]. Along the above development, feed-forward 3DGS has also been moving from sparse and constrained inputs toward larger numbers of views, broader scene coverage, and weaker assumptions on camera poses. This trend exposes an underexplored bottleneck – as the number of input views increases, the main challenge gradually shifts from reconstruction ambiguity to rendering efficiency. Denser input views provide stronger geometric constraints and better scene coverage, but these benefits come at a cost of a much larger number of candidate primitives. Typical feed-forward pipelines generate Gaussians densely from pixels, image tokens, or volumetric features, and then suppress the generated Gaussians via voxelization, pooling, or other aggregation mechanisms. Although the suppression removes some redundant primitives, the final Gaussian set can still grow rapidly with the increase of input views. Since the cost of splatting is closely related to the number of visible Gaussians, such growth increases rendering pressure and weakens one of the key advantages of 3DGS as an explicit representation. A straightforward solution is to prune the predicted Gaussians, but simple pruning based on opacity or confidence is suboptimal as different Gaussians carry different rendering values. In multi-view reconstruction, regions that are observed many times often have high confidence, but they tend to produce highly redundant Gaussian primitives. In contrast, thin structures, occlusion boundaries, and high-frequency textures usually occupy only a small spatial extent, yet their reconstruction strongly affects perceptual quality of rendered images. If pruning is simply driven by opacity, confidence, or a global top-k rule, the retained Gaussian set can be easily dominated by large, stable, and visually simple regions while those fine but important details are either averaged out during fusion or removed during pruning. This suggests that compact feed-forward 3DGS requires not only fewer Gaussians, but also reliable criteria for deciding which Gaussians are worth keeping. We address this issue from the perspective of rendering-budget-constrained representation learning. Instead of retaining as many predicted primitives as possible, the model should preserve the subset of Gaussians that contributes most to rendering quality under a bounded primitive count. To this end, we introduce BudSplat, a compact feed-forward 3DGS framework that propagates detail-aware importance through Gaussian generation, fusion, and pruning. BudSplat positions compactness as one part of feed-forward prediction rather than a separate post-processing step. It identifies local regions where Gaussian removal is likely to hurt perceptual quality, such as boundaries, thin geometry, and high-frequency texture. This enables importance-preserving voxel fusion that prevents valuable local contributors from being diluted by redundant observations in smooth regions, as well as quality-aware Gaussian pruning that allows selecting a compact set of primitives for rendering. On top of that, BudSplat introduces a local quota redistribution strategy that further prevents large smooth regions from monopolizing the retained primitives, ensuring sufficient representation of detail-rich regions even under a small Gaussian budget. Hence, BudSplat redefines the role of pruning in feed-forward 3DGS: pruning is not generic Gaussian reduction after dense generation but a learned allocation mechanism that decides where the limited rendered primitives should be spent. It strikes a good balance among visual fidelity, representation compactness, and rendering efficiency as the number of input views increases. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify the primitive growth problem in feed-forward 3DGS and study Gaussian prediction from the perspective of rendering-budget-constrained representation learning. • We propose a detail-aware compact 3DGS framework that integrates residual token-based importance estimation, importance-preserving voxel fusion, and quality-aware Gaussian pruning with local quota redistribution, enabling the model to retain primitives that are more valuable for rendering. • Experiments on RealEstate10K, DL3DV, and ACID demonstrate that our method achieves a better trade-off between visual fidelity and rendering efficiency under challenging low-overlap evaluation settings. 22 Related Work 

Feed-forward and generalizable 3D Gaussian Splatting. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) rep-resents scenes with explicit anisotropic Gaussian primitives and enables efficient differentiable rendering, but it still requires per-scene optimization [Kerbl et al., 2023]. Feed-forward 3DGS amor-tizes this cost by predicting Gaussians directly from input images. Early methods such as pixelSplat and MVSplat generate pixel-aligned Gaussians from sparse posed views, with MVSplat further using cost-volume reasoning [Charatan et al., 2024, Chen et al., 2024]. Later works improve geometry, hier-archy, or alignment with depth priors, hierarchical Gaussians, and voxel-aligned prediction [Xu et al., 2025a, Liu et al., 2025a, Tang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025b]. Other methods scale the paradigm through transformer-based reconstruction, long-sequence modeling, iterative refinement, whole-scene reconstruction, or unposed inputs [Zhang et al., 2024a, Ziwen et al., 2025, Kang et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2024, 2025c, Ye et al., 2024, Xu et al., 2025b, Jiang et al., 2025]. WorldMirror further studies general feed-forward world reconstruction with 3D Gaussian outputs [Liu et al., 2025b]. Despite these advances, most methods still rely on dense per-view, pixel-aligned, or voxel-aligned primitive prediction, leaving the growth of rendered Gaussians under large-view inputs insufficiently addressed. 

Compactness, fusion, and redundancy reduction. Many works compress optimized 3DGS scenes after reconstruction by pruning low-significance Gaussians, learning masks, reducing per-point storage, modeling inter-primitive redundancy, or combining pruning with quantization and distilla-tion [Fan et al., 2024, Girish et al., 2024, Lee et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2024, Ali et al., 2024]. These methods demonstrate substantial redundancy in 3DGS, but mainly operate as post-reconstruction compression. In feed-forward 3DGS, FreeSplat, FreeSplat++, GGN, MuSASplat, and AnySplat reduce multi-view redundancy through feature fusion, Gaussian pooling, or differentiable voxeliza-tion [Wang et al., 2024, 2025c, Zhang et al., 2024b, Xu et al., 2026, Jiang et al., 2025]. These approaches reduce duplicated observations or improve cross-view aggregation, but they are not formulated as rendering-budget allocation methods and do not explicitly preserve local perceptual details during fusion. Our method differs from the two kinds above by treating compactness as rendering-budget allocation during feed-forward prediction, unifying detail-aware token importance, importance-preserving voxel fusion, and Gaussian-level pruning. 

## 3 Method 

Given a set of multi-view input images, our goal is to predict a compact 3D Gaussian representation that can be rendered efficiently while preserving feed-forward reconstruction quality. We build on an AnySplat-style pipeline, where a visual geometry backbone predicts dense 3D points and image-aligned features, and these dense candidates are fused into Gaussian primitives for rendering. This design is effective for unconstrained reconstruction, but becomes less scalable as the number of input views increases. More views provide stronger geometric constraints, yet also generate many redundant candidates from repeatedly observed smooth regions. In contrast, visually important regions such as thin structures, occlusion boundaries, and high-frequency textures often occupy only a small fraction of the scene. Our method makes primitive generation and pruning aware of rendering value. We first estimate patch-level detail importance from multi-layer VGGT tokens, then preserve this signal during voxel fusion, and finally select a compact Gaussian set according to a quality-aware ranking criterion. The model therefore does not simply keep the most confident primitives, but allocates the retained Gaussians to regions that are more likely to affect novel-view quality. Figure 1 illustrates the overall pipeline. Given multi-view RGB images, the VGGT backbone first predicts dense geometry, confidence cues, and multi-layer visual tokens. The dense geometry and image-aligned features provide an initial pool of Gaussian candidates, while the retained multi-layer tokens are passed to our detail-aware decomposition module. This module reconstructs common low-rank token components with a down-up projection structure, uses the residual as detail cues, and predicts a patch-level detail score with a lightweight detail head. Finally, the fused Gaussians are ranked by a quality-aware pruning criterion that combines opacity, detail importance, and support-based redundancy correction, followed by patch-level quota redistribution. 3.3 Importance-Preserving Voxel Fusion 

The detail signal is useful only if it survives the transition from dense candidates to fused Gaussians. In the baseline pipeline, candidates from multiple views are grouped by spatial voxelization. This removes duplicates, but a confidence-dominated fusion rule can dilute rare local structures. For example, if a voxel contains many smooth-surface candidates and only a few boundary candidates, averaging mainly by confidence may suppress the boundary contribution. We therefore make voxel fusion detail-aware. 3.4 Quality-Aware Gaussian Pruning 

Voxel fusion reduces many duplicates, but it does not fully control rendering cost. With many input views, the fused Gaussian set can still contain many primitives from repeatedly observed regions. Rendering all of them is expensive, while naive global pruning may remove small but important details. These fixed VGGT token cells serve as allocation bins for already-fused Gaussians: cross-view merging is handled by the preceding 3D voxel fusion. We first assign each patch a base quota proportional to its candidate count, and then distribute the remaining slots according to patch detail strength and remaining capacity. The quotas sum to the same global budget K, and Gaussians within each patch are selected by the same ranking score rm. This turns pruning from pure global competition into locally balanced allocation, giving detail-rich patches a better chance to retain their most valuable primitives. ## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate our method on large-view-count feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting, focusing on both novel-view synthesis quality and rendering efficiency under limited Gaussian budgets. Our experiments compare the quality-efficiency trade-off against representative feed-forward 3DGS baselines and analyze the contribution of the proposed detail-aware fusion and pruning modules. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Datasets. We train our model on a mixture of RealEstate10K (RE10K) [Zhou et al., 2018] and DL3DV [Ling et al., 2024], and evaluate it on RE10K, DL3DV, and ACID [Liu et al., 2021]. RE10K mainly contains forward-facing indoor and outdoor videos with smooth camera trajectories. DL3DV contains more diverse scenes and larger view changes, making it suitable for evaluating many-view reconstruction. ACID is used as an out-of-domain benchmark to test cross-dataset generalization. Unless otherwise specified, all methods are evaluated on the same context-target splits for each dataset. 

Evaluation protocol. We build on the evaluation protocol of NoPoSplat [Ye et al., 2024] to construct a low-overlap, large-baseline stress test. Closely spaced inputs can let a compact Gaussian set overfit a narrow camera trajectory, so we evaluate on target views with large viewpoint changes and low visual overlap. For RE10K and ACID, we start from the original NoPoSplat Ye et al. [2024] test-view candidates and keep view pairs whose overlap is below 30% . We then sample intermediate target views along the selected camera segment for novel-view evaluation. For DL3DV, we compute the view overlap between the two endpoint camera poses of each candidate trajectory segment and retain segments whose endpoint overlap is below 30% . All methods are evaluated on the same context and target views. This common stress-test protocol changes target-view difficulty, so its absolute scores are not directly comparable to published standard-benchmark values. We evaluate image quality with PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS [Zhang et al., 2018]. In addition, we report the number of Gaussians used for rendering, denoted as #GS, since our goal is to reduce rendering cost while preserving reconstruction quality. We evaluate all methods under different numbers of input views, including 16, 20, 24, 32, 48, and 72 views. 

Baselines. We compare with representative large-view-count feed-forward 3DGS methods, includ-ing GGN [Zhang et al., 2024b], AnySplat [Jiang et al., 2025], WorldMirror [Liu et al., 2025b], and VolSplat [Wang et al., 2025b]. GGN models relations among Gaussian groups and performs Gaussian pooling, AnySplat uses differentiable voxelization to merge dense primitives, and WorldMirror provides a general feed-forward world reconstruction framework with 3D Gaussian outputs. We also include VolSplat, which predicts voxel-aligned Gaussians with known camera poses. Since VolSplat assumes posed inputs while our method and the other baselines operate without camera poses, this comparison is not strictly fair and should be viewed as a pose-aware reference. For each method, we use the official implementation and recommended configuration whenever available. All methods are evaluated using the same context and target views, and all rendered images are evaluated at 448 × 224 resolution for a fair comparison. 

Implementation details. All experiments are conducted on 8 NVIDIA H100 GPUs with mixed-precision training. We initialize the model from the official VGGT pretrained weights and train on a mixture of RE10K and DL3DV with sampling weights 0.7 and 0.3, respectively. The training batch size is 1 per GPU. We use AdamW with learning rate 2.0 × 10 −4, a warm-up of 180 steps, and train for 8K steps. 4.3 Ablation Study 

We conduct an ablation study to verify the contribution of the main design modules. All variants are evaluated under the same Gaussian budget and the same low-overlap evaluation protocol of 24-view setting on RE10K, so the comparison focuses on reconstruction quality rather than differences in Gaussian count. We ablate four components: detail-token importance estimation, importance-preserving voxel fusion, quality-aware Gaussian pruning, and patch-level quota redistribution. Table 2 reports the results. The budgeted baseline uses the same Gaussian budget as our full model and selects primitives by opacity-only pruning after AnySplat-style voxel fusion. Removing the detail-token branch disables the explicit local detail prior used by both fusion and pruning. Removing importance-preserving voxel fusion falls back to confidence-dominated voxel aggregation. Removing quality-aware pruning replaces our ranking score with opacity-only selection, while removing patch quota redistribution uses global top-K selection without local budget balancing. The results show that all proposed components contribute to the final performance. Compared with the budgeted baseline, adding the proposed modules substantially improves all metrics. Each removal from the full model leads to a performance drop, confirming that detail-aware importance, detail-preserving fusion, quality-aware ranking, and local quota redistribution are complementary. In 9particular, removing the detail-token branch causes the largest degradation, indicating that explicit local detail estimation is important for Gaussian prediction under a strict rendering budget. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented BudSplat, a compact feed-forward 3DGS framework for large-view-count novel view synthesis under a rendering budget. Instead of treating Gaussian reduction as post-hoc compression, BudSplat learns to allocate primitives according to rendering value. It extracts detail-aware importance from multi-level visual tokens, preserves this signal during voxel fusion, and selects fused Gaussians with quality-aware pruning and local quota redistribution. Experiments on RE10K, DL3DV, and ACID show that BudSplat achieves a favorable trade-off among rendering quality, Gaussian compactness, and efficiency. A limitation is that BudSplat reduces redundancy after the VGGT geometry backbone, but does not optimize the backbone memory itself. Thus, very dense input sequences may still be limited by VGGT computation before Gaussian pruning is applied. BudSplat is complementary to memory-efficient VGGT variants, and future work may combine backbone-level optimization with budget-aware Gaussian allocation. The method also depends on the reliability of predicted geometry and confidence cues, and extremely small budgets can still remove details in highly complex regions.

B.1 Residual Token Decomposition 

BudSplat estimates local detail importance from residual token components. The actual module is a learned non-linear down-up projection with detail supervision. The following idealized result explains why separating a compact common component from a residual component is a reasonable design. 

Proposition 1 (Residual energy under an idealized low-rank projection) Let x ∈ RC be a zero-mean token with covariance Σ. Consider an idealized rank-r orthogonal projection P that recon-structs x by P x. The projection minimizing the reconstruction error E∥x − P x∥22 is the projection onto the top-r eigenspace of Σ. The residual x − P x contains the token components outside this common low-dimensional subspace. 

Proof 1 For an orthogonal projector P , the reconstruction error is 

E∥x − P x∥22 = tr( Σ) − tr( P Σ).

Minimizing the error is therefore equivalent to maximizing tr( P Σ) over all rank-r orthogonal projectors. By the Rayleigh–Ritz theorem [Ritz, 1909], the maximizer is the projector onto the eigenspace spanned by the top r eigenvectors of Σ. Hence the residual removes the best rank-r

common component under this idealized objective. 

Implication. Our learned down-up projection module is not restricted to be an orthogonal PCA projector, so the proposition should not be interpreted as an exact characterization of the trained network. It provides an idealized justification: reconstructing common token components with a compact low-rank module encourages the residual branch to emphasize information not explained by dominant subspace structure, which is a useful cue for boundaries, thin geometry, and high-frequency variations.

B.6 Summary of Theoretical Support 

The results above support the design of BudSplat in four ways. First, residual token decomposition is motivated by an idealized low-rank projection view, where residual components capture information outside common token subspaces. Second, the fusion softmax provably increases the relative influence of contributors with higher detail or support priors and preserves above-average feature responses in relevant directions. Third, quality-aware pruning is the exact optimizer of a separable fixed-budget surrogate, and the support term acts as a principled redundancy correction. Fourth, patch-level quota redistribution and hybrid detail supervision are motivated by standard resource-allocation and variance-reduction arguments. These statements do not prove that BudSplat must outperform baselines on any dataset. They show that the proposed modules are internally consistent with well-defined surrogate objectives, while the final quality-efficiency gains are established empirically.

## D Visualization of Gaussian Density Allocation 

To better understand how BudSplat allocates the Gaussian budget, we visualize both the target-view projected Gaussian density and the corresponding RGB rendering before and after pruning. Directly plotting retained and discarded Gaussians in 3D is often difficult to interpret, since large-view-count feed-forward reconstruction can produce millions of primitives. We therefore use a target-view density rendering diagnostic. For a fixed target camera, all visible Gaussians are rendered with the same small opacity and the same color, so pixels covered by more Gaussians receive larger accumulated values. This diagnostic approximates the projected Gaussian density in the target view. It is used only for analysis; normal RGB rendering still uses the original Gaussian opacity, color, covariance, and the standard rendering pipeline. Figure 3 shows the result. The top row visualizes the projected density before pruning, after pruning, the removed density, and the retention ratio. Let Dpre and Dpost denote the density maps before and after pruning. We compute the removed density as max( Dpre − Dpost , 0) , and the retention ratio as Dpost /(Dpre + ϵ). The removed-density map highlights where the pruned Gaussians originally contributed, while the retention-ratio map indicates which regions preserve more projected Gaussian support. The density maps show that BudSplat does not remove Gaussians uniformly across the image. Before pruning, broad low-frequency regions contain high projected density, which is expected because these regions are repeatedly observed by many input views and can generate many overlapping Gaussians. After pruning, the density becomes more concentrated around visually informative structures. The removed-density map indicates that many discarded Gaussians come from smooth or highly overlapped areas, where multiple primitives provide similar low-frequency information. In contrast, the retention-ratio map shows stronger preservation around edges, object boundaries, and texture-rich regions, suggesting that the compact budget is preferentially allocated to locations that are more likely to affect novel-view quality. The bottom row provides an additional sanity check using normal RGB rendering. Unlike the density diagnostic, these RGB images are rendered with the original Gaussian attributes and the standard renderer. The before-pruning and after-pruning renderings remain visually close, indicating that a large fraction of the removed Gaussian density is redundant for final image formation. At the same time, the absolute difference map is not uniformly distributed over the image. Most differences appear around high-frequency details, such as edges, boundaries, thin structures, and texture transitions. This is expected: smooth regions can tolerate stronger Gaussian reduction because nearby primitives often provide similar color and geometry support, whereas small changes around high-frequency structures are more visible in RGB space. 17 This visualization is consistent with the design of BudSplat. The detail-aware token branch estimates where primitive removal is more likely to affect perceptual quality. Importance-preserving voxel fusion propagates this signal before aggregation, reducing the chance that rare but important local contributors are diluted by many redundant smooth-region observations. Quality-aware pruning and local quota redistribution then reduce unnecessary density in easy regions while retaining more support near detail-sensitive structures. Therefore, pruning behaves as budget reallocation rather than uniform Gaussian deletion: it removes many redundant primitives from low-frequency regions while preserving the primitives that are more important for rendering local details. This analysis is qualitative and target-view dependent, but it provides an intuitive explanation for the quality-efficiency trade-off observed in the main experiments. BudSplat can substantially reduce the number of rendered Gaussians without causing large global changes in RGB rendering. The remaining differences are mainly concentrated in high-frequency regions, which are precisely the regions where compact Gaussian allocation is most challenging and where detail-aware budget control is most needed.

Inherited and added components. BudSplat retains the AnySplat-style reconstruction pipeline: the pretrained VGGT geometry backbone, dense point/confidence and image-aligned feature prediction, spatial voxel grouping, Gaussian parameter decoding, and rasterizer. Our additions operate along this existing flow. The residual-token branch supplies detail scores before fusion, detail-aware weights preserve their influence inside the inherited voxels, and the learned gate, quality-aware ranking, and patch-level quotas produce the budgeted representation. Introducing importance before aggregation is essential to this design because post-hoc ranking alone cannot recover a local contributor that has already been diluted during fusion. 

Fixed hyperparameters and scope. All reported datasets and view counts use λc = 1 .0, λd = 1 .5,

λt = 0 .5, τf = 1 .0, η = 0 .5, β = 1 .0, and γ = 0 .15 . We keep these coefficients fixed across scenes, datasets, view counts, trajectories, and keep ratios, so the reported comparisons use a common allocation rule. Within this rule, the pre-fusion contributor prior tj supplies a weak local consistency cue, whereas the post-fusion count sm = |V m| supplies a mild logarithmic correction for support already merged into one Gaussian. These stage-specific roles explain why a highly supported boundary can still be retained through its opacity and detail score, rather than being rejected solely on support. The evidence is limited to the evaluated distributions since a dedicated sparse 360-degree benchmark has not been tested. Fusion also remains dependent on the predicted geometry, since weighted averaging cannot completely separate distinct surfaces assigned to the same voxel. 18 Patch allocation and distributed training. This assignment partitions an already-fused representation and it does not instantiate a separate Gaussian for every overlapping input patch. Training memory and efficiency scope. To locate the remaining memory bottleneck, we measure synchronized peak allocated memory for a complete training step and for a VGGT-only forward-backward pass with the same inputs and mixed precision. At 24 views, these peaks are approximately 42 GB and 33 GB per GPU, respectively; the backbone-only peak is about 79% of the full-pipeline peak. Because the peaks are measured separately, their 9 GB difference is not an additive estimate of BudSplat’s new modules: it also includes Gaussian decoding, voxel grouping, rasterization, and backward buffers. These measurements place the timing results in context. BudSplat leaves the VGGT memory bottleneck in place, but the representation produced after this one-off computation is compact and can be reused for many novel-view renderings. 19
