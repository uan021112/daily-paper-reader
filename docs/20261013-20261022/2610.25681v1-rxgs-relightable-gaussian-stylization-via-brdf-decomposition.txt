Abstract 

Reference-based 3D scene stylization transfers the appearance of a reference image onto a reconstructed 3D scene while preserving scene geometry. Recent methods perform this transfer by optimizing the appearance representation of neural scene models including Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). These methods optimize an RGB appearance representation, in which a single per-point colour combines the effects of material reflectance, environment illumination, together with the shading it produces, and the view-ing direction. When stylization modifies this combined colour, the illumination effects present during stylization are baked into the stored appearance, so the stylized scene cannot be rendered under different illumination. To address this limitation, we present RxGS, a relightable 3D scene stylization framework built on a Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF)-aware 3D Gaussian representation. Rather than modifying a per-point RGB colour, RxGS opti-mizes only the diffuse albedo field recovered by inverse rendering, while leaving roughness, metallicity, surface normals, and the estimated environment lighting unchanged. As a result, the stylized scene keeps all the components a physically-based renderer needs other than the edited albedo, and can therefore still be relit. RxGS optimizes the albedo field using a nearest-neighbour feature matching loss together with a spatial smoothness prior over Gaussian centres. # 1 Introduction 

Reference-based 3D scene stylization transfers the appearance of an artistic reference image onto a reconstructed 3D scene while keeping the rendered result geometrically consistent across viewpoints. Recent neural rendering methods, in particular Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [1] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2], produce high-quality novel views and have prompted a growing body of 3D stylization work. These methods stylize a scene by modifying the RGB appearance representation that the neural scene model has learned. In many applications a scene must be stylized once and then rendered under several different lighting conditions without repeating the stylization. This requires the stylized scene to keep a representation that can be relit after the appearance transfer, a requirement the RGB representation does not meet, as the next paragraph argues. An RGB appearance representation stores, at each point, one colour that already combines material reflectance, the environment illumination together with the shading 2it produces, and view-dependent effects. Because these factors are not stored sepa-rately, a stylization process that edits this colour necessarily edits the illumination contribution together with the intrinsic material. The illumination present during styl-ization is thereby written into the stored appearance: when the scene is later rendered under a different illumination, the stored colour does not change, so no new shading or shadows appear. As illustrated in Fig. 1, stylization applied to an RGB appear-ance representation fixes the artistic appearance and the illumination contribution together, which prevents the result from responding to novel lighting. To remove this restriction we build on recent inverse rendering methods for 3D Gaussian representations. Such methods [3] recover separate scene attributes, namely diffuse albedo, material properties, and environment illumination, and so support relightable rendering and material editing. These separated representations have not previously been used for reference-based 3D scene stylization. We present RxGS, a relightable 3D scene stylization framework built on a Bidirec-tional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF)-aware Gaussian scene representa-tion. Given a reconstructed scene, RxGS first applies inverse rendering decomposition to recover, per Gaussian, the diffuse albedo ai, the roughness ri, the metallicity mi, the surface normal ni, and one global environment lighting L. Stylization then optimizes only the field of diffuse albedos {ai} and leaves {ri, m i, ni} and L and all geometry unchanged. Relightability follows from this bookkeeping rather than from any claim that the albedo alone fixes the rendered colour. A physically-based renderer consumes the geometry, the per-Gaussian material attributes {ai, r i, m i, ni}, and the environ-ment lighting L. RxGS changes only the albedo coefficients {a⋆i } and holds every other input fixed, so all non-albedo inputs the renderer needs remain intact, and the same renderer can be re-run under any chosen L to produce a relit image of the stylized scene. To optimize the albedo field, RxGS uses a nearest-neighbour feature matching (NNFM) loss in the VGG feature space together with a spatial smoothness term over Gaussian centres. We evaluate RxGS on synthetic and real-captured scenes, measuring cross-view geometric consistency with the Geometry Consistency Cost (GCC) defined in Section 4. RxGS attains the lowest GCC on every evaluated NeRF-Synthetic scene and remains competitive on real-captured scenes, while keeping a relightable scene representation after stylization. Our contributions are the following. 

• We present RxGS, a relightable 3D scene stylization framework based on a BRDF-aware Gaussian scene representation. 

• We formulate stylization as optimization of the diffuse albedo field alone in a logit parameterization, regularized by a smoothness term on a k-nearest-neighbour graph over Gaussian centres, so that artistic appearance transfer leaves the material and illumination parameters required for relighting unchanged. 

• We evaluate on synthetic and real-captured datasets, reporting cross-view geometric consistency (GCC) that is competitive with prior stylization methods together with relightable stylization that prior methods do not provide. 32 Related work 

## 2.1 3D Scene Representation and Rendering 

A 3D scene representation encodes scene geometry and appearance in a compact, learn-able form that supports downstream tasks such as novel view synthesis, scene editing, relighting, and scene understanding. NeRF [1] introduced an implicit neural represen-tation that parameterizes volumetric density and radiance with multilayer perceptrons, achieving novel view synthesis from multi-view observations. NeRF requires dense volumetric sampling during both training and rendering, which is computationally expensive. To reduce this cost, later work introduced voxel grids, tensor factorizations, and multi-resolution encodings, including DVGO [4], Plenoxels [5], TensoRF [6], and Instant-NGP [7]. As scene editing and stylization tasks developed, several methods adopted surface-based representations that give a more explicit geometric correspon-dence than a purely volumetric field, including VolSDF [8], NeuS [9], NeuMesh [10], and NeRF2Mesh [11]. Among explicit representations, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2] represents a scene with a set of anisotropic Gaussian primitives. Unlike volumetric methods that rely on dense ray sampling, 3DGS renders through differentiable Gaussian rasteri-zation, which is fast enough for real-time rendering while still reconstructing scene geometry and appearance. Following 3DGS, many extensions adapt the representa-tion to downstream tasks, including scene editing, relighting, and inverse rendering. The inverse rendering line, which recovers separate material and lighting attributes for each Gaussian, is the one RxGS builds upon, and we review it next. 

## 2.2 Inverse Rendering with 3D Gaussian Representations 

Inverse rendering recovers physically meaningful scene properties, namely geometry, material attributes, and illumination, from image observations. Several methods built this decomposition into neural rendering frameworks. NeRFactor [12] factorizes scene appearance into geometry, reflectance, visibility, and illumination for relightable ren-dering. PhySG [13] uses spherical Gaussian representations of the BRDF and of the illumination to put physically-based shading into neural rendering. TensorIR [14] intro-duces a tensor-based inverse rendering framework that makes scene decomposition and relightable rendering more efficient. After 3DGS, inverse rendering was extended to Gaussian representations by attaching material, normal, and illumination attributes to the Gaussian primitives. GaussianShader [15] adds a shading function to Gaussian rendering and estimates geometry-aware normals to improve reflective surfaces. Relightable 3D Gaussian [16] attaches BRDF attributes and incident lighting to each Gaussian and renders through differentiable physically-based rendering. GS-IR [3] formulates inverse rendering on a BRDF-aware Gaussian representation by jointly estimating scene geometry, material properties, and environment illumination from multi-view observations; RxGS takes the decomposition produced by GS-IR as its input. 42.3 3D Scene Stylization 

Neural style transfer was first developed for images [17–20], where the goal is to synthesize an image that keeps the scene content of one input while matching the appearance of a reference style image. As 3D scene representations matured, styliza-tion was extended from 2D images to 3D scenes. Before neural scene representations, 3D stylization operated on explicit representations such as meshes and point clouds [21–23], manipulating surface textures, geometric attributes, or rendered appearance directly. Neural scene representations let stylization act on a learned appearance represen-tation, so that style is propagated consistently across viewpoints. Early NeRF-based methods modify the appearance field of a reconstructed scene while keeping the geom-etry fixed [24–29]. ARF [25] optimizes the appearance field of a NeRF with a neural style transfer objective, giving multi-view consistent stylization, and ARF-plus [30] improves stylization quality and training stability through stronger appearance rep-resentations and optimization strategies. Ref-NPR [31] improves controllability by transferring appearance from reference images through geometry-aware feature corre-spondence, which enables localized stylization. LAE-NeRF [32] lets a user mark target regions in a neural scene representation and propagates the edit through neighbouring volumetric regions. SNeRF [27] alternates between image-space stylization and scene reconstruction, transferring artistic appearance into the neural scene representation across iterations. Because 3DGS associates appearance attributes directly with individual primitives, it makes appearance manipulation explicit. StylizedGS [33] supports region-specific style transfer through semantic masks. ReGS [34] adapts 3DGS to reference-based stylization with a texture-guided mechanism that adjusts local Gaussian arrangements to reproduce texture detail from the reference. StyleSplat segments individual objects in a Gaussian scene and fine-tunes the selected Gaussians for object-level styliza-tion. Further methods match segmented regions across views before transfer to keep the style consistent over the whole scene [35], geometry-aware texture transfer [36], diffusion-based stylization and distillation [37], and feed-forward or optimization-free stylization of Gaussian splats [38, 39]. All of these methods, whether NeRF-based or Gaussian-based, edit a per-point RGB appearance, and so produce a stylized scene that stores one fixed colour per point and cannot be relit. A smaller line of work stylizes a decomposed appearance representation rather than RGB colour. TANGO [40] performs text-driven stylization by editing BRDF attributes on a relightable mesh, which allows appearance editing under varying illumination. RxGS differs from this mesh-based, text-driven setting: it performs reference-image stylization directly on a BRDF-aware Gaussian representation, editing only the diffuse albedo field of the Gaussians and keeping the rest of the decomposition fixed.

## 3.1 Gaussian Representation 

3.1.1 3D Gaussian Splatting 

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2] represents a scene with a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives placed in 3D space. Each Gaussian is parameterized by a centre 

xi ∈ R3, a scaling vector si ∈ R3, a rotation quaternion qi ∈ R4, an opacity αi ∈ [0 , 1], and appearance-related attributes. The scaling vector and rotation quaternion together define the Gaussian covariance. The trainable parameters of the i-th Gaussian are 

Gi = {xi, si, qi, α i, ϕ i}, (1) where ϕi denotes the appearance representation of the Gaussian. Because the Gaussian primitives are defined in 3D space, the appearance attributes of a Gaussian are shared by every viewpoint that observes the same spatial region. This gives a fixed correspondence between scene geometry and appearance during rendering, so a change to a Gaussian’s appearance attributes affects all views of that region in the same way, which is useful for keeping a stylization consistent across viewpoints. 63.1.2 BRDF-aware Gaussian Representation 

GS-IR [3] refines the 3DGS appearance attribute ϕi into a BRDF-aware decomposition consisting of diffuse albedo, roughness, metallicity, and surface normal, while jointly estimating a global environment lighting L, represented as an environment map, that is, a function on the unit sphere of incident directions giving the incoming radiance from each direction. Specifically, 

ϕi = {ai, r i, m i, ni}, (2) where ai ∈ [0 , 1] 3 is the diffuse albedo of the i-th Gaussian, ri ∈ [0 , 1] is the roughness, 

mi ∈ [0 , 1] is the metallicity, and ni ∈ R3 is the surface normal. In an RGB appearance representation a single per-point colour already combines material reflectance, the illumination together with the shading it produces, and view-dependent effects. The decomposition above instead stores the albedo ai as a separate per-Gaussian coefficient, alongside ri, mi, ni, and the global lighting L. Although the diffuse albedo is represented separately, it still influences the colour of specular reflec-tions under the metallic-roughness BRDF when metallicity is non-zero. Therefore, modifying the albedo changes both the diffuse appearance and the tint of specular highlights. However, the remaining BRDF attributes, including roughness, metallicity, surface normals, and environment lighting, remain unchanged throughout stylization. Consequently, the scene retains the complete set of rendering parameters required for relightable rendering.

3.2.3 Style Loss 

Following ARF [25], we use the nearest-neighbour feature matching (NNFM) loss as the style objective. Given a rendered image ˆIk and the reference style image J, the style loss is Lnnfm ( ˆIk, J ). It extracts feature maps from a pre-trained VGG-16 network for both images, and for each feature vector of ˆIk it finds the most similar feature vector of J by cosine similarity, then penalizes the cosine distance to that match. Following ARF, the features are taken from the conv3 block of the VGG-16 network. To control the trade-off between content preservation and stylization strength, RxGS optionally incorporates a content loss. The stylization objective is 

Lstyle = Lnnfm + λcLcontent , (10) where λc weights the content term. Unless stated otherwise we set λc = 0, so stylization is driven by the NNFM objective alone.

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate RxGS from two angles: cross-view geometric consistency and relightable stylization. We first compare RxGS with existing reference-based 3D scene stylization methods using a geometric consistency metric, which measures how well scene struc-ture is preserved across viewpoints after stylization. Because the existing methods do not support relighting, we then evaluate the relightable stylization of RxGS by ren-dering stylized scenes under different illumination conditions and examining how the appearance responds. 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. 

We use NeRF-Synthetic [1] and NeRF-LLFF [41] for the geometric consistency com-parison, and add TensorIR [14] and Mip-NeRF 360 [42] for the relightable stylization experiments. NeRF-Synthetic [1] is a synthetic multi-view dataset of eight object-centric scenes, each with 100 training images and 200 test images rendered at 800 ×800, with camera poses and foreground masks. NeRF-LLFF [41] is a real-world forward-facing multi-view dataset captured by handheld cameras with calibrated poses; it contains more complex geometry, occlusions, and appearance variation than NeRF-Synthetic, and we downsample the input images by a factor of four, following prior work [5, 11, 31]. TensorIR [14] is a multi-view dataset for inverse rendering and relight-ing, with diverse materials and illumination and with ground-truth geometry, material, and lighting; we use its Lego and Hotdog scenes for relightable stylization. Mip-NeRF 360 [42] is a real-world multi-view dataset of unbounded indoor and outdoor scenes captured over 360-degree viewpoints, with larger extents and more complex structure than NeRF-Synthetic and LLFF; we use its Garden , Kitchen , and Bicycle scenes. 

Implementation Details. 

We take the BRDF-aware Gaussian representation reconstructed by GS-IR [3] as the input to RxGS. Stylization optimizes the diffuse albedo while keeping the estimated geometry, roughness, metallicity, surface normals, and environment lighting fixed. Fol-lowing ARF [25], we use the NNFM loss with a pre-trained VGG-16 network and take features from the conv3 block. Comparison Methods. 

We compare RxGS with ARF [25], Ref-NPR [31], and StylizedGS [33]. Evaluation Protocol. 

To reduce bias toward a particular artistic appearance, we evaluate each scene with three different reference styles, and for the geometric consistency comparison we aver-age the reported results over the three styles. For relighting, NeRF-Synthetic and LLFF scenes are rendered under several illumination conditions by rotating the esti-mated environment map while keeping all stylization parameters fixed. We additionally evaluate relightable stylization on TensorIR and Mip-NeRF 360 scenes to examine the behaviour of stylized scenes under varying illumination across different scene types. 

Metric. 

To measure cross-view geometric consistency we use the Geometry Consistency Cost (GCC), a per-pixel optical-flow warping error used in earlier video and 3D stylization work [31, 43]. Given two stylized renderings ˆIk and ˆIk−1 from nearby viewpoints, we estimate the optical flow from ˆIk−1 to ˆIk with the pre-trained RAFT model [44], warp ˆIk−1 to the viewpoint of ˆIk using this flow, and compute the mean per-pixel error between ˆIk and the warped frame over the pixels that the warp marks as visible. ## 4.3 Relightable Stylized Rendering 

Fig. 5 shows relighting results on stylized LLFF scenes under different illumination. As the illumination changes, the rendered appearance shows the corresponding shading while keeping the stylized texture and colour distribution. In the Flower scene, rotating the dominant light source moves the shadows across the petals: under the 90-degree rotated lighting, shadows appear on the petals that face away from the incoming light. The Horns scene behaves similarly, with shadow locations shifting as the lighting direction changes; when the illumination rotates toward the right of the scene, shadows appear on the left of the horns, and under back-facing illumination larger shadowed regions appear in the foreground and the overall appearance is darker. The stylized scene therefore remains responsive to illumination changes after stylization, producing different shading under different lighting while preserving the stylized texture and scene structure. Fig. 6 shows relighting results on stylized Mip-NeRF 360 scenes under four envi-ronment maps. The stylized appearance stays the same, but the four maps produce distinct illumination-dependent appearance. The City environment gives brighter ren-derings with stronger overall illumination, the Fireplace environment adds warmer colour tones, the Forest environment adds a green tint, and the Sunset environ-ment gives a softer appearance with more moderate lighting transitions. These effects 14 appear across the Bicycle , Garden , and Kitchen scenes: the stylized texture and structural detail stay fixed while the rendered appearance follows the illumination of each environment map. RxGS therefore keeps a relightable scene representation after stylization, so the stylized scene responds to different lighting environments without re-running the stylization. 

## 4.4 Ablation Study 

4.4.1 Effect of the Style Supervision Strategy 

We compare two supervision strategies. The first renders the stylized albedo with-out illumination, so style supervision acts in the albedo domain. The second renders the stylized albedo under randomly sampled Lambertian illumination, which adds illumination-dependent shading during optimization. The comparison uses eight NeRF-Synthetic scenes and two reference styles, giv-ing 16 scene-style pairs. For one optimization run we define its loss reduction as (Linit − L final )/Linit , where Linit and Lfinal are the stylization loss Lstyle at the first and the last iteration. Direct albedo supervision gives a lower final stylization loss than randomized-illumination supervision on all 16 pairs. Averaged over the 16 pairs, the loss reduction is 32 .2% for direct albedo supervision and 20 .1% for randomized-illumination supervision. We attribute this gap to the extra appearance variation that randomized illumi-nation introduces. Because the lighting direction changes during optimization, the rendered appearance contains illumination-dependent shading that is unrelated to the target style. The style supervision signal is then less stable and feature matching in the VGG space is harder. Direct albedo supervision removes the illumination contribu-tion, so the optimization works only on the intrinsic appearance transfer. We therefore use direct albedo supervision in all experiments unless stated otherwise. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We presented RxGS, a relightable 3D scene stylization framework built on a BRDF-aware Gaussian scene representation. RxGS optimizes only the diffuse albedo field and leaves the roughness, metallicity, surface normals, and environment lighting fixed, so artistic appearance transfer does not alter the material or illumination parameters and the stylized scene keeps a representation that can be relit. RxGS preserves cross-view geometric consistency and produces illumination-dependent appearance under novel lighting.
