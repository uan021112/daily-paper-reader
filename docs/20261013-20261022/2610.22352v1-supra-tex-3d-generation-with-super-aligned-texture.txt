Abstract
Single-image 3D generation aims to dress a given 3D mesh with relightable materials while faithfully preserving
the visual details of a reference image. Existing methods either introduce artifacts when fusing inconsistent
multi-view predictions or struggle to preserve fine details through implicit image-feature conditioning. To
resolve these challenges, we introduce SUPRA-Tex, built on the principle of decoupling visible-material estima-
tion, surface alignment, and observation-guided 3D completion. This division of labor allows the 3D generator,
trained on limited 3D data, to focus its capacity on preserving visible evidence and completing unobserved
surfaces. Specifically, we first train an MLLM-based image editing model to predict lighting-separated PBR
maps in the reference view. We then project these maps onto the visible surface and encode them with the
pretrained material VAE encoder into native observation latents that share the generation target’s latent space.
An interleaved flow transformer holds these latents as fixed, read-only anchors while alternating unobserved-
region and full-surface updates to generate complete material latents. This design achieves super-alignment
with the reference, delivering What You See Is What You Get fidelity on visible regions and coherent comple-
tion on unobserved surfaces. Trained solely on public datasets, SUPRA-Tex achieves SOTA performance in
both geometry-conditioned texturing and end-to-end asset generation, matching or even exceeding leading
commercial systems on in-the-wild images. Project page: supra-3d.github.io/supratex.

1 Introduction
For applications such as product digitization and embodied AI, generating relightable 3D assets from a single reference
image requires faithful preservation of the reference appearance. We refer to this requirement as super-alignment:
accurately reproducing fine-grained text, logos, and patterns at their corresponding visible surface locations, while
completing unobserved regions with materials consistent with the visible appearance.
Two prominent approaches to appearance generation are multi-view synthesis and native 3D generation. Multi-view
pipelines leverage geometry-conditioned 2D generative models to synthesize appearance across surrounding viewpoints,
then project and fuse the resulting images onto the mesh (Tencent Hunyuan3D Team, 2025a). However, cross-view
discrepancies can introduce seams and ghosting, while blending conflicting predictions blurs sharp reference details (Lai
et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026).
Native 3D generation circumvents view-fusion artifacts by synthesizing appearance directly on 3D representations (Lai
et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2026), yet preserving fine reference details remains
challenging. For example, TRELLIS.2 employs a 3D material VAE to compress surface PBR properties into material
latents, which are generated under geometry and image conditioning before being decoded into surface materials. Its
image cross-attention provides no explicit correspondence between image details and 3D surface locations, leaving the
generator to learn where those details belong. To improve spatial alignment, recent work lifts high-resolution image
features into 3D through camera projection (Li et al., 2026a). Yet even with explicit spatial correspondence, these
conditions remain visual features rather than material observations. Consequently, the 3D generator must simultaneously
shoulder the responsibilities of inferring PBR properties from visual features, reconstructing fine reference details at
their corresponding surface locations, and synthesizing materials for unobserved regions.
We argue that these responsibilities should not fall on a single 3D generator. Given the inherent scarcity of 3D training
data, requiring the 3D model to learn these heterogeneous tasks jointly increases its learning burden. Instead, we offload
visible-material estimation to 2D generative priors and establish explicit surface alignment through camera projection,
separating both from the 3D generative task. Our key insight is to translate visible reference evidence into native material
observations before 3D generation, then organize full-surface completion around this grounded evidence. Rather than
forcing the 3D model to infer physical materials from abstract visual features, this formulation enables the generator to
anchor directly on established material evidence, preserving reference details while focusing more of its capacity on
completing unobserved surfaces.
Based on this principle, we introduce SUPRA-Tex, a framework for generating relightable 3D assets with super-aligned
textures. First, we leverage strong 2D priors by training an MLLM-based image editing model to jointly predict
lighting-separated PBR maps in the reference view in a single forward pass, while preserving fine visual details. Next,
we project these maps onto valid visible surface locations and encode the resulting partial materials with the pretrained
material VAE encoder into native observation latents. Sharing the target latent space and directly decodable by the
pretrained material decoder, these latents provide spatially aligned material content rather than abstract visual features
that the 3D backbone must translate into PBR properties. We also encode the spatial extent of these observations to
guide completion.
To guide full-surface material generation, we design an interleaved flow transformer that reads the native observation
latents as fixed, read-only reference memory at every block. Its blocks alternate between updating unobserved latent
cells and the full surface, allocating more updates to cells without observations while retaining full-surface interactions
for coherent completion. Since observations are indexed by 3D surface coordinates, the same interface incorporates
additional views, including 2D-generated back views, and interactive 2D edits without retraining the 3D backbone.
Through this division of labor, SUPRA-Tex delivers What You See Is What You Get fidelity in the reference view while
completing the asset with globally coherent, relightable materials, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
Using only public datasets for training, SUPRA-Tex achieves SOTA results in both geometry-conditioned texturing and
end-to-end asset generation. With synthetic back-view conditioning, SUPRA-Tex achieves reference fidelity and visual
quality on real photographs comparable to or better than those of the leading commercial systems in our comparisons.
Code and checkpoints are released on our project page to facilitate future research: supra-3d.github.io/supratex.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• Our key insight is to establish surface-aligned material observations before 3D generation, allowing the generator
to focus on preserving reference details and completing unobserved surfaces. Built on this insight, SUPRA-Tex is
trained solely on public datasets and, with synthetic back-view conditioning, achieves reference fidelity and visual
quality comparable to or better than those of leading commercial systems in our comparisons.
• We present native observation encoding, which projects reference-view PBR estimates onto valid surface locations
and encodes partial materials in the target latent space, simplifying learning to preserve reference appearance.
• We propose an interleaved flow transformer that uses fixed, read-only observation anchors and alternates unobserved-
cell and full-surface updates for reference-faithful, coherent material completion.

2 Related Work
Multi-view texture generation. Most texturing pipelines synthesize appearance in 2D views and fuse the predictions
onto the mesh. Early approaches paint the mesh view-by-view with depth-guided diffusion and inpaint unobserved UV
regions (Richardson et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024a). TexFusion aggregates view-wise denoising
predictions in a shared latent texture map (Cao et al., 2023). Subsequent works improve cross-view coherence by
synchronizing multi-view denoising (Liu et al., 2024b; Cheng et al., 2025; Bensadoun et al., 2024) or generating
views jointly via geometry-conditioned diffusion (Huang et al., 2025b; Shao et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2026; Han et al.,
2026; Zhang et al., 2026), a recipe also adopted by large image-to-3D systems (Zhang et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2025b;
Tencent Hunyuan3D Team, 2025b). Reference-guided methods also explicitly target appearance preservation: EASI-Tex
combines mesh-edge guidance with image features for single-image texturing (Perla et al., 2024), while AlignTex aligns
multi-view artwork with the mesh before projection and texture refinement (Zhang et al., 2025b). Optimization-based
approaches such as DreamMat distill geometry- and light-aware diffusion priors into PBR materials (Zhang et al.,
2024b). Paint-it instead optimizes convolutionally parameterized PBR texture maps with score distillation (Youwang
et al., 2024). To produce relightable assets, multi-view generation is extended to PBR channels using lighting-aware
priors, material latent representations, or alternating view-space baking (Deng et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025a; He
et al., 2025a; Yeo et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2025; Tencent Hunyuan3D Team, 2025a; Lai et al., 2025; Bao et al., 2026;
Wu et al., 2026b), often aided by single-image intrinsic delighting (Kocsis et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024b; Ke et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2024; He et al., 2025b; Careaga & Aksoy, 2024). Nevertheless, residual inconsistencies between
synthesized views can blur or ghost sharp reference details during projection and blending. In contrast, SUPRA-Tex
completes materials in native 3D space, using projected observations as conditioning anchors rather than directly fusing
synthesized views into the final texture.
Native texture generation. Driven by the rise of large-scale 3D pretraining, native texture generation has emerged
as an alternative paradigm. Existing approaches explore diffusion over point, octree, and UV representations (Yu
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Yu et al., 2024) and PBR prediction with octree-aligned Gaussians (Xiong et al., 2025).
Latent texture generators, such as NaTex, LaFiTe, and TEXTRIX, learn structured latent spaces for surface colors or
attributes (Lai et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026), while native 3D foundation models jointly represent
geometry and appearance (Chen et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025; 2026). Beyond global conditioning, partial observations
and spatially aligned features have also been explored. At scene scale, WorldGrow (Li et al., 2026b) extends TRELLIS
with context-conditioned 3D block inpainting for progressive scene generation. TEXGen back-projects partial UV
maps (Yu et al., 2024), NaTex encodes initial textures for refinement (Lai et al., 2026), and TEXTRIX encodes projected
appearance with its pretrained attribute VAE in the generation target’s latent space (Zeng et al., 2026). For spatial feature
alignment, camera projection techniques originally developed for implicit reconstruction (Saito et al., 2019; Yu et al.,
2021a) have been adapted to 3D generative models, such as CUPID’s pose-aligned features and Pixal3D’s camera-frame
back-projected multi-scale features (Huang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). Even with spatially aligned image features, the
3D generator must still infer material properties, preserve reference details, and complete unobserved surfaces.
3 Method
Given a reference image I, a mesh M, and its camera projection π, SUPRA-Tex generates full-surface, relightable PBR
materials. Figure 2 summarizes the three stages: reference-view material estimation, surface-aligned native observation
encoding, and observation-guided 3D completion. We first introduce the material representation and upstream geometry
model, then describe the three stages, training objective, and inference procedure. Appendices A and B provide
implementation and training details; Appendix G.1 describes additional-view conditioning and texture editing.
3.1 Preliminaries
Surface PBR and material latents. We represent surface appearance through physically based rendering (PBR)
properties over the mesh M. At any surface location p ∈M, these properties comprise albedo a ∈R3, metallic
m ∈[0, 1], and roughness r ∈[0, 1]. We build on the O-Voxel representation of TRELLIS.2 (Xiang et al., 2026),
which additionally includes opacity α ∈[0, 1], giving the material tuple T(p) = (a(p), m(p), r(p), α(p)). Separate
sparse VAEs compress 3D geometry and surface materials into geometry latents G = {gs}s∈S and material latents
Z = {zs}s∈S over active coordinates S. For sparse surface attributes P, the pretrained material VAE encoder Emat
yields Z; conditioned on G, the material generation stage models PBR materials directly in the native 3D space by
generating Z, which the decoder Dmat restores onto the mesh surface.
Geometry foundation and camera projection. Our framework takes a mesh M and camera projection π, either
provided as ground truth or predicted from the reference image. We use the open-source TRELLIS.2-based Pixal3D
variant (Li et al., 2026a; Xiang et al., 2026), denoted Pixal3D⋆, which supports geometry and material generation. Its
geometry module predicts the reference-aligned mesh for end-to-end generation.

3.2 Image-to-Material Translation
To reduce the learning burden on the 3D generator, we separate reference-view material estimation from 3D surface
completion. Specifically, we train an MLLM-based image editing model F2D to infer reference-view albedo and
metallic-roughness (MR) maps directly from the input photograph:
bT2D = F2D(I) = (ba, bm, br) ,
(1)
where ba denotes the albedo map, and bm and br denote the metallic and roughness maps. To reuse the 3-channel pretrained
image VAE, we pack bm and br with a constant channel into a 3-channel map, which also fulfills the input format expected
by the pretrained material encoder.
Joint alignment and one-step inference. To coordinate channel predictions while retaining pretrained image priors,
F2D adapts a multimodal DiT backbone (Super Intelligence Team, 2026) via factorized modality-axis joint attention,
jointly predicting albedo, metallic–roughness, and auxiliary normal maps. This attention exchanges features across
output modalities at corresponding spatial locations. We train the 2D material estimator on RGB images paired with
ground-truth material and normal maps rendered from Objaverse, using a latent flow-matching objective at the fixed
maximum-noise endpoint σ = 1.0. At inference, a single DiT forward pass at this endpoint (1.2 s on one H800) estimates
clean material latents, which the VAE decodes into 1024 × 1024 material maps without iterative denoising.
This design has two advantages. First, it uses 2D image priors to disentangle illumination from intrinsic materials at full
reference resolution. Second, it shields the downstream 3D generator from abstract visual features, letting the 3D model
use physical PBR observations to focus on reference preservation and unobserved-surface completion.

3.4 Observation-Guided Completion
We generate complete material latents via flow matching over the mesh M, predicting a velocity field at time t from
the current noisy material latents Zt, geometry latents G, and native observation representation Co (Lipman et al.,
2023). Native observation latents provide spatially aligned material evidence for reconstructing visible appearance. The
generator must learn to use this evidence while completing unobserved surfaces coherently. Pixal3D⋆injects projected
image features through additive residuals and updates all surface tokens at every layer. We introduce an interleaved
flow transformer that retains observations as fixed, read-only memory and alternates unobserved-cell and full-surface
updates. This schedule assigns more hidden-state updates to unobserved cells while allowing visible materials to be
refined during full-surface generation.

3.5 Training and Inference
Full-surface training objective. The generation stream X synthesizes material latents over the surface S. Training
observations are constructed from ground-truth materials of public 3D assets using visible-surface observation encoding,
with complete material latents as targets. With Zgt the normalized ground-truth latents and ϵ ∼N(0, I),
Zt = (1 −t)Zgt + [σmin + (1 −σmin)t] ϵ,
v∗= (1 −σmin)ϵ −Zgt,

where σmin is a small residual noise scale, t = 1 corresponds to pure noise, and t = 0 to material latents with residual
noise σminϵ. The loss is
LFM = E[MSE(vθ(Zt, t; O, G), v∗)] ,

averaged over all valid tokens and channels, including observed coordinates. The observation representation remains
fixed and is not noised along the flow path; it serves as conditioning input to the generator. The generation stream is
supervised against complete-material targets over the entire surface, learning to reconstruct visible appearance from the
fixed observations and to complete unobserved regions.
Inference. Given dataset-provided or predicted geometry and cameras, we encode the mesh into G and S and the
filtered observations into anchors O (Sec. 3.3). Starting from Z1 ∼N(0, I) over S, we integrate the learned velocity
field with Euler steps, then de-normalize and decode the final latents with Dmat into full-surface PBR attributes for
export. Optional KV caching reuses the observation stream’s per-layer keys and values across sampling steps; Table 6

4 Experiments
The supplement covers datasets, implementation, baseline configurations, evaluation protocols, and additional analyses.
Datasets. Our 3D material generator is trained solely on publicly available 3D datasets. The training sources are
summarized in Appendix B.1. We evaluate on two datasets with no overlap with the training set: DTC-Tex, comprising
100 texture-rich product scans selected from the Digital Twin Catalog (Dong et al., 2025), and Complex-Tex, comprising
110 Sketchfab (Sketchfab, n.d.) meshes with denser inscriptions, finer patterns, and more varied shapes.
Implementation details. The 2D material estimator adapts FireRed-Image-Edit-1.1 (Super Intelligence Team, 2026) at
1024 × 1024 resolution. The 3D generator uses 60 Transformer blocks, with self-attention initialized from TRELLIS.2
and the remaining components initialized randomly; the pretrained material encoder and decoder remain frozen. We
train the generator with a global batch size of 576 using AdamW on 64 GPUs. Inference uses 25 Euler steps without
classifier-free guidance.

compares ten baselines spanning native 3D generation and dedicated texturing methods. PBR methods are additionally

4.2 End-to-End Single-Image-to-3D Generation
Setting and baselines. Each method generates both geometry and appearance end-to-end from a single reference image,
without access to ground-truth geometry or cameras during generation.
We compare with TRELLIS.2, Pixal3D⋆, Hunyuan3D 2.1, Step1X-3D, UniLat3D, and CUPID (Xiang et al., 2026; Li
et al., 2026a; Tencent Hunyuan3D Team, 2025a; Li et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026). SUPRA-Tex and
Pixal3D⋆share geometry predicted by the latter, with separate appearance pipelines. Other baselines generate their own
geometry. For scoring, outputs are brought into a common coordinate frame, with GT similarity alignment applied to
methods marked † in Table 3; this alignment plays no role in generation.
Results. Table 3 shows that SUPRA-Tex leads across all 9 metrics on both datasets. Relative to Pixal3D⋆, four-view
PSNR improves by 1.77 dB on DTC-Tex and 2.49 dB on Complex-Tex, foreground PSNR by 0.89 dB and 1.60 dB, and
LPIPS decreases by 64.8% and 58.6%, respectively. Higher reference-view CLIP-I and lower 16-view distribution
distances further indicate stronger input consistency and multi-view appearance quality. Since Pixal3D⋆and SUPRA-Tex
share predicted geometry, these results show that our appearance pipeline retains its advantage on predicted surfaces,
complementing the given-geometry results in Table 1.

4.4 Ablation and Analysis
We evaluate the observation-injection architecture and material content under the given-geometry protocol of Sec. 4.1.
The architecture variants share training data, initialization protocol, optimization settings, training budget, and checkpoint-
selection rule. All comparisons use the same geometry, cameras, valid observation support, and sampling seed.
How observations are used. Table 5 compares three conditioning designs with the same predicted PBR observations.
Residual injection adds encoded observations through layerwise residuals. Joint-update attention updates observation
states in selected layers and all generation states in every layer. In contrast, SUPRA-Tex keeps observations read-only
and alternates unobserved-cell and full-surface updates (Sec. 3.4). It improves shaded PSNRfg over residual injection by
0.87 dB on DTC-Tex and 0.29 dB on Complex-Tex, and matches joint-update attention in shaded fidelity while improving
albedo PSNRfg by 0.15 and 0.16 dB with lower MR MAE. Thus, the design preserves or improves reconstruction quality
without spending computation on observation-state updates or full-surface queries at every block. The complete design
also samples 1.20× and 1.18× faster than joint-update attention on the two datasets (Sec. 4.5).
Effectiveness of 2D material estimation. To test whether the FireRed-based 2D material estimator supplies useful
physical material estimates, we replace its predicted metallic and roughness with fixed values (M = 0, R = 0.5) or
dataset means, keeping predicted albedo and the 3D generator unchanged (Table 5). Relative to fixed and dataset-mean
MR, predicted MR improves shaded PSNRfg by 0.44 and 0.26 dB on DTC-Tex, and 0.69 and 0.57 dB on Complex-
Tex, respectively, with lower MR MAE on both datasets. These gains show that the 2D estimates provide useful
material information beyond albedo alone. Replacing all predicted PBR channels with ground truth (Oracle PBR)
further improves shaded PSNRfg by 2.31/1.12 dB and albedo PSNRfg by 2.62/2.51 dB on DTC-Tex/Complex-Tex.
The remaining gap indicates that improving 2D material estimation can further improve 3D texture quality without
changing the completion model. Using Qwen-Image-2.1 (Qwen Team, 2026) (Ours‡ in Table 1) narrows this oracle gap
to 0.98/0.47 dB in shaded PSNRfg and 1.03/2.36 dB in albedo PSNRfg. The same completion model therefore works
with a different 2D prior, and a stronger prior improves 3D texture quality toward the oracle.
Partial and complete material encoding. Sharing the material encoder does not imply that a partial observation yields
the corresponding slice of a complete-material latent. We isolate this distinction using GT geometry, cameras, and
PBR values on Complex-Tex, bypassing both 2D prediction and flow generation. For each object, we encode complete
materials and the filtered visible subset with the same frozen encoder using its posterior mean, then compare features
at identical observed coordinates. We measure the RMS difference over coordinates and channels after dividing each

4.5 Runtime and Memory
Table 6 compares Joint-update attention with SUPRA-Tex on all 210 given-geometry cases, using one H800 GPU per
request. Both receive identical geometry and observation latents and use 25 Euler steps, seed 42, FP32 parameters,
BF16 autocast, and FlashAttention 3 without classifier-free guidance. We report the mean of per-object median times
from three runs after two warmups. These runtime measurements use the 10,000-step Joint-update attention checkpoint
and the 30,000-step SUPRA-Tex checkpoint, separately from the matched-training quality ablations in Table 5; this
timing comparison does not isolate interleaving alone.
SUPRA-Tex achieves sampling speedups of 1.20× on DTC-Tex and 1.18× on Complex-Tex. Observation KV caching
adds 1.067× and 1.064× speedup, including cache construction, with identical latent outputs at every Euler step. Mean
peak allocation rises by 1.94 and 1.81 GiB, respectively. The interleaved schedule also reduces query-token evaluations
by 17.54% and 16.91% versus querying every cell at each block; these are not total FLOP reductions.
Stage sums exclude loading and file I/O. Geometry and observation preparation are timed once; estimation, sampling,
and decoding use warm measurements. Peak memory covers only the resident denoiser and sampling tensors.
5 Limitations
Figure 6 shows how imperfect geometry, uncertain materials, and missing observations limit asset quality.
Large image–geometry discrepancies. In end-to-end generation, SUPRA-Tex uses geometry predicted by Pixal3D⋆.
Large discrepancies in shape or pose between the reference image and the mesh can place projected material observations
on incorrect surface locations. Correspondence filtering removes some unreliable observations, but cannot correct
the underlying geometry, which remains fixed during material generation. Severe discrepancies can therefore prevent
faithful texture alignment. Improving upstream geometry and camera alignment is therefore an important direction, but
we do not evaluate corrected-geometry controls here.
Hallucinated geometry on unseen surfaces. Upstream geometry generators such as Pixal3D⋆can introduce implausible
unseen structures even when the visible shape matches the reference. These structures may deviate from shapes learned
by the material generator, hindering coherent texture completion. Since SUPRA-Tex conditions on fixed geometry, it
cannot remove these structures; their influence on texture generation can reduce final asset quality.

Inconsistent completion across visibility boundaries. Completing unseen surfaces is particularly challenging when
text or patterns extend beyond the visible region. For example, lettering that wraps around a can may be only partially
visible in the input. The model may preserve the visible fragment yet fail to continue its strokes or layout coherently
onto the hidden side, producing inconsistent text or patterns across the visibility boundary.
Errors in 2D material estimation. The 2D material estimator can misinterpret material properties, for example by
assigning high metallic values to a non-metallic surface. These estimates condition the 3D generator, which may retain
the error in the completed asset and produce implausible reflections under rendering or relighting. Accurate transfer of
reference details thus does not guarantee correct material decomposition.
Failure cases. Figure 6 illustrates these limitations. Reference fidelity depends on usable image–surface correspondence
and reliable material observations, while fine detail on unseen surfaces must be generated. The plate shows a printed
motif reconstructed as protruding geometry, and the sign retains spurious structures after texture refinement. The owl
separates recognizable appearance details from an ambiguous reflectance estimate; the teapot separates sharp observed
patterns from less precise unseen completion. These are qualitative cases, without corrected-geometry controls or
measured unseen-surface ground truth. A back view may help the last case; we report no paired two-view result.
6 Conclusion
We presented SUPRA-Tex, a framework for single-image 3D generation with super-aligned textures through observation-
guided material completion. By separating visible-material estimation and surface alignment from 3D generation
and using native observation latents as fixed, read-only anchors, SUPRA-Tex preserves fine reference details while
generating coherent, relightable materials across the full surface. Trained solely on public data, it achieves state-of-
the-art results among the evaluated methods in geometry-conditioned texturing and end-to-end asset generation. With
synthetic back-view conditioning, it is also competitive with commercial systems in human evaluations on the mixed
real-and-synthetic comparison set. These results demonstrate the value of spatially aligned, directly usable material
observations for reconciling reference fidelity with full-surface generation.

A Additional Method Details
A.1 Geometry and Surface Observations
Geometry foundation and camera projection. Our framework operates on an underlying mesh M and camera
projection π, provided as ground-truth inputs or estimated from the reference image. For end-to-end inference, we
follow the default MoGe-2-based camera-estimation pipeline of Pixal3D⋆, whose geometry module generates the mesh.
For the 3D position xs of latent coordinate s, the model samples image features through camera projection:
fs = Sample(Φ(I), π(xs)) ,
(11)
where Φ(I) denotes a 2D feature map. In the geometry refinement stages, Pixal3D⋆projects both the original and
spatially upsampled image features and concatenates the sampled features. Combined with global image conditioning,
these features align the predicted mesh M and camera projection π with the reference view, serving as the geometric
input for our material generation.
Surface observations and latent coordinates. The full surface defines the active latent-coordinate set S. Valid visible
surface voxels form the partial PBR input Po, which is encoded before any latent-level conditioning is constructed.
The resulting coordinates So identify cells with observations, but a cell in So can also contain unseen surface voxels;
U = S \ So therefore denotes cells without any observation, not all unseen surface. Coverage ρs is the observed fraction
of the surface voxels actually present in a cell, and repeated image samples of one voxel do not increase it. Boundary
distance ds uses the six-neighbor graph of active latent coordinates and is −1 if no unobserved cell is reachable.
Inference-time boundary filtering. At inference, we additionally apply foreground-mask erosion and local depth-
discontinuity filtering to reduce texture contamination from unreliable correspondences. Foreground erosion removes
mixed pixels along the silhouette, and depth-discontinuity filtering rejects correspondences whose neighboring visible
depths differ by more than a threshold, where a small projection error would move the observation onto a different
surface. All material channels share the retained observation support, from which local material encoding, coverage, and
boundary distance are computed.

B Training Data and Optimization
B.1 Training Data
We train our models using publicly available 3D datasets. The 3D generator uses assets from TexVerse (Zhang et al.,
2025a), Objaverse-XL (Deitke et al., 2023), ABO (Collins et al., 2022), HSSD (Khanna et al., 2024), and open
synthetic 3D collections, together with human scans from 4D-Dress (Wang et al., 2024), Human4DiT (Shao et al.,
2024), THuman2.1 (Yu et al., 2021b), and X-Humans (Shen et al., 2023). These assets provide geometry and material
supervision for observation-guided full-surface completion. The 2D material estimator is trained on paired RGB
images, material maps, and normal maps rendered from Objaverse assets. All datasets are used in accordance with their
respective licenses for non-commercial academic research.
B.2 Training F2D
We initialize F2D from FireRed-Image-Edit-1.1 adapted to individual PBR and normal prediction tasks, and jointly
fine-tune it on aligned RGB images and ground-truth material and normal maps rendered from publicly available 3D
assets. Both the task-specific adaptation and joint fine-tuning use these rendered data. Modality-axis attention is inserted
at Transformer blocks 29, 39, 49, and 59, with rank 256 and four attention heads. We optimize these adapters and
backbone blocks 29–49 using a latent flow-matching objective and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), with learning
rates of 2 × 10−4 and 5 × 10−7, respectively. The VAE and text encoder remain frozen.
By folding the batch dimension, the model processes albedo, metallic-roughness, and an auxiliary normal stream in
parallel, inserting lightweight cross-attention adapters at specific transformer layers to exchange features across modality
axes at identical spatial coordinates. We train F2D with a flow-matching objective anchored at the fixed maximum-noise
endpoint σ = 1.0 using an outlier-masked MSE loss. Elementwise latent-flow residuals with absolute magnitude greater
than 50 contribute zero loss but remain in the averaging denominator. Losses are averaged equally across the albedo,
metallic-roughness, and normal streams. At inference, we use a single prediction at this endpoint to estimate the clean
material latents, which the image VAE decodes into 1024 × 1024 PBR maps.
B.3 3D Generator Training
Preprocessing for training. We build training observations from ground-truth PBR materials of public 3D assets using
the visible-surface observation encoding pipeline. Foreground validity and first-hit visibility determine the observed
surface voxels supplied to the sparse material encoder. We follow Pixal3D (Li et al., 2026a) for asset preprocessing
and reference-view sampling. During training, we drop the entire observation condition with probability 0.03 while
retaining the geometry condition.
Optimization. We use random seed 42 throughout our training and evaluation pipelines. The material encoder and
decoder are frozen. Generator self-attention is initialized from TRELLIS.2 and the remaining components randomly.

C Evaluation Protocols
C.1 Evaluation Datasets
DTC-Tex comprises 100 objects from the Digital Twin Catalog (Dong et al., 2025). We score the 2,399 catalog assets
for surface-texture complexity from six views with Qwen3.8-27B, discard near-duplicates and broken meshes, and retain
the 100 highest-scoring objects. Each object is evaluated from a front-view reference, together with its ground-truth
mesh, PBR maps, and a 16-view camera rig. Typical cases are household products with printed labels or decorative
motifs, such as canned goods, decorated ceramics, and books.
Complex-Tex complements this set with 110 Sketchfab (Sketchfab, n.d.) meshes that carry denser inscriptions and finer
patterns on more varied shapes. This subset includes branded packaging and vehicles covered by logos and fine print, as
well as buildings and characters with more irregular shapes. A method receives only the designated reference image, or
that image together with the mesh when geometry is given. Figure 8 shows examples from both datasets.
Both DTC-Tex and Complex-Tex underwent strict duplicate checks during data collection to ensure no overlap with the
training assets. We will release both evaluation benchmarks.
C.2 Rendering and Metrics
Protocol. Both settings evaluate the PBR asset each method exports, rendered under a shared protocol rather than
compared in UV space, because latent decoding or remeshing changes the face layout. Predicted and ground-truth assets
are rendered with the same cameras and forward lighting at 1024 × 1024 and downsampled to 512 × 512 with Lanczos
interpolation; methods whose output lives in another coordinate frame are brought into the evaluation frame by their
own transform or, where marked †, by a similarity transform to the ground-truth mesh. Per-view metrics are PSNR,
SSIM (Wang et al., 2004), and VGG-LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) over the full image with a black background, so they
penalize errors in silhouette as well as appearance, and CLIP-I, the cosine similarity of CLIP ViT-L/14 features (Radford
et al., 2021) between corresponding views. We additionally report PSNRfg on both shaded RGB and albedo, the same
PSNR restricted to the ground-truth object mask, so that large black background regions do not dominate the score.
21


SUPRA-Tex
Distribution metrics compare multi-view image sets within each dataset: FDDINOv3 uses 1024-dimensional DINOv3
ViT-L/16 CLS features (Siméoni et al., 2025). In end-to-end evaluation, FID (Heusel et al., 2017) uses Inception-v3
features, while FDCLIP and CMMD (Jayasumana et al., 2024) provide additional distribution measures. For methods
that output PBR, we report mean absolute error (MAE) of metallic and roughness over the intersection of the predicted
and ground-truth object masks. Per-view metrics are averaged over views per object, then over valid objects. We report
results separately on DTC-Tex and Complex-Tex. In the given-geometry setting, the mesh and camera are provided as
ground truth; end-to-end generation predicts geometry, aligned to GT by a similarity transform only for scoring.
Rendering cameras and alignment. For given-geometry evaluation, we replay the 16 camera poses and front-light
settings stored in each ground-truth asset’s transforms.json; the designated input camera defines the reference view,
and the other 15 cameras define novel views. Ground-truth and predicted assets use identical cameras and lighting,
rendered with Blender 3.0.1 and Filmic color management. Where geometric alignment is required, we use iterative
closest point (ICP) and silhouette-based refinement to estimate a similarity transform to the ground-truth geometry
before rendering. This alignment is used only for evaluation.
DINOv3 Fréchet distance for given-geometry texturing. For Table 1, we extract the 1024-dimensional final-layer CLS
token of DINOv3 ViT-L/16 without additional ℓ2 normalization. We alpha-composite each shaded rendering onto black,
resize its shorter side to 256 pixels using bilinear interpolation, center-crop to 224 × 224, and apply ImageNet mean
and standard-deviation normalization. These features are computed in FP32 with TF32 disabled using Transformers
4.57.3. We compute the Fréchet distance between the feature means and unbiased covariances of the generated and
corresponding ground-truth image sets, using float64 statistics. This is one set-level score per method and dataset, rather
than an average of object-level distances.
C.3 Open-Source Baselines
Given-geometry comparison. Each object provides a ground-truth mesh and a forward-lit reference image from a
specified view; every method textures the ground-truth mesh from this image through its own interface, and we render
16 views comprising the reference view and 15 novel views. We compare against the texturing stages of the native
3D generators TRELLIS.2 and Pixal3D⋆and of the pipelines Hunyuan3D 2.1 and Step1X-3D (Xiang et al., 2026; Li
et al., 2026a; Tencent Hunyuan3D Team, 2025a; Li et al., 2025a), and against the dedicated texturing methods LumiTex,
MVPainter, MV-Adapter, TEXGen, UniTEX, and Ink3D (Bao et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025b; Yu
et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2026; Han et al., 2026). TRELLIS.2, Pixal3D⋆, Hunyuan3D 2.1, LumiTex, and MVPainter
output PBR materials and are evaluated on shaded renderings, albedo, metallic, and roughness; the remaining methods
output RGB textures and are evaluated on shaded renderings only, with unavailable channels marked “–”. We use the
official implementations and released checkpoints for all baselines, providing the reference camera to every method that
supports camera conditioning.
End-to-end comparison. When geometry is also predicted, reference fidelity depends on geometry reconstruction,
surface correspondence, and material generation together. This is the setting the observation interface is most exposed
to: the anchors now land on a predicted mesh through a predicted camera, so any geometry or correspondence error
propagates into the materials. Each method takes the reference image alone. Pixal3D⋆and SUPRA-Tex share the
same predicted geometry and apply their respective appearance-generation pipelines; the other baselines use their own
geometry-generation pipelines. SUPRA-Tex textures this predicted mesh with the pipeline of Sec. 3.5. We compare with
TRELLIS.2, Pixal3D⋆, Hunyuan3D 2.1, Step1X-3D, UniLat3D, and CUPID (Xiang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Tencent
Hunyuan3D Team, 2025a; Li et al., 2025a; Wu et al., 2026a; Huang et al., 2026), covering native latent generation,
staged geometry-then-texture pipelines, and joint object–pose modeling. Since output material attributes differ across
these systems, we compare complete assets through rendered appearance only. For scoring, outputs are brought into
a common coordinate frame using their coordinate transforms or, for methods marked †, similarity alignment to the
ground truth; this alignment plays no role in generation.
Reconstruction metrics take the reference view as anchor and compare four rendered views against the ground truth: the
reference direction, its two sides, and the back. The side and back views thus test the completed surface, not only the
reference view. CLIP-I compares the reference-view rendering with the input image. FDDINOv3, FDCLIP, FID, and
CMMD compare the set of 16 rendered views of generated assets with the corresponding ground-truth set within each
dataset. Reconstruction and CLIP-I measure fidelity to a specific object; the distribution metrics measure the visual
quality of the generated population.
Baselines. We use the authors’ released checkpoints and implementations for the open-source baselines, following
their inference recipes with method-specific sampling, resolution, and texture-export settings. Integration includes
input-format conversion and repairs to mesh preprocessing, UV construction, and export. For given-geometry texturing,
each method receives the designated reference image and GT mesh with its original materials removed, using the
method’s texturing interface; the reference camera is supplied where the interface supports it. No GT material maps or
additional GT images are provided. For image-to-3D generation, each method receives the same single reference image.
Pixal3D⋆and SUPRA-Tex share predicted geometry, as specified in Sec. 4.2.

E.3 Observed-Support Encoding versus Zero Filling
As discussed in Sec. 3.3, zero-padding unobserved regions assigns the missing values concrete material semantics,
such as black albedo. In the zero-filled formulation, we observed a pronounced dark bias on unseen surfaces. Figure 9
illustrates this behavior: the back of the calculator becomes nearly black and retains spurious front-side characters, while
the dish develops dark mottling on its red side and white underside. With observed-support-only encoding, SUPRA-Tex
instead produces a green calculator back and preserves the dish’s red-and-white color regions.

Porcelain-plate example. Figure 14 demonstrates single-view generation, two-view fusion, and image-space material
