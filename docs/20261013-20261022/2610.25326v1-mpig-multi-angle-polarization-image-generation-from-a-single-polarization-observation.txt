MPIG: Multi-angle Polarization Image Generation
from a Single Polarization Observation
Jue Wang, Ruilin Huang, Liquan Shen, Zhen Tian, Xia Hua
Abstract—Polarization imaging provides informative cues for
material analysis, scene understanding, and visual perception.
However, acquiring complete polarization observations typically
requires specialized hardware and multi-angle measurements,
thereby increasing system complexity and limiting practical
deployment. In this work, we address the problem of predicting
the missing 45◦, 90◦, and 135◦polarization views from a
single 0◦observation by leveraging a learned scene prior. To
this end, we propose MPIG (Multi-angle Polarization Image
Generation), a Swin Transformer-based framework that cou-
ples cross-angle feature interaction with residual decoding to
predict angle-dependent intensity variations while preserving
shared scene structure. Specifically, measured Stokes differences
and polarization-strength-aware supervision guide the recovery
of polarization modulation, orientation, and magnitude, while
low-rank factorization further reduces the decoding cost. We
also construct HighPol, a large-scale real-world polarization
dataset comprising 5,474 groups of high-resolution four-angle
polarization images collected from diverse indoor and outdoor
scenes. Experimental results demonstrate improved image and
polarization accuracy, achieving 29.729 dB PSNR, 0.9505 SSIM,
and 21.514◦AoLP MAngE while requiring 85.86 M parameters
and 1.126 T FLOPs. Furthermore, downstream validation on
glass and semantic-segmentation tasks suggests that the gener-
ated polarization images can preserve task-relevant information,
demonstrating the effectiveness of MPIG and the practical utility
of predicted polarization observations for downstream vision
applications.
Index Terms—Polarization imaging, deep learning, image gen-
eration.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Polarization Imaging Systems and Applications
Polarization Applications. Polarization imaging captures sur-
face and material cues that complement conventional intensity
measurements. These cues facilitate a wide range of tasks,
including surface normal estimation [20], [21], reflection sep-
aration [22], material recognition [5], and transparent object
analysis [13], [23]. Specifically, surface reconstruction meth-
ods relate polarization responses to object geometry, whereas
transparent-object methods exploit polarization-dependent re-
flections to distinguish glass from its surroundings [24]. Such
information is particularly valuable when appearance alone
provides weak boundaries or ambiguous material cues.
To support these applications, several datasets have been
developed. For instance, PolaRGB [12] provides paired RGB
and polarization images from real scenes, RGBP-Glass [14]
focuses on transparent object segmentation, and PDAVIS [25]
records polarization information with high temporal resolution
using an event camera. Their diverse acquisition formats
and annotations reflect the requirements of image restoration,
object segmentation, and dynamic-scene analysis, respectively.
However, these resources are generally tailored to specific
tasks and offer limited coverage in terms of scene types,
materials, and polarization strengths. Greater scene diversity is
therefore essential for studying polarization across both indoor
and outdoor environments.
Polarization Acquisition. As illustrated in Fig. 1, rotating-
polarizer systems [10] combine a conventional camera with
sequential analyzer measurements to estimate Stokes param-
eters. Although such systems are inexpensive, scene motion
between exposures may introduce misalignment. In contrast,
division-of-focal-plane (DoFP) cameras [11] integrate micro-
polarizers at the pixel level, thereby capturing multiple ana-
lyzer orientations in a single exposure. This design improves
temporal alignment and supports dynamic scenes, but it relies
on dedicated hardware and spatial multiplexing. Since each
orientation samples only a subset of the sensor grid, inter-
polation is required to reconstruct complete analyzer images,
and the resulting reduced spatial resolution and pixel crosstalk
can degrade polarization estimates. Consequently, the two
acquisition strategies embody a trade-off between hardware
cost and spatial sampling on the one hand, and temporal
consistency on the other.
B. Ill-posed Reconstruction and Image Generation
Learned Reconstruction and Image Generation. Recovering
high-dimensional scene information from incomplete obser-
vations is inherently ill-posed, as exemplified by HDR [26],
light-field [27], hyperspectral [28], and 3D recovery through
scattering media [29]. To alleviate this ill-posedness, learned
priors capture statistical regularities that constrain the missing
information. For instance, Wu et al. [30] employ network
priors to handle blur and motion, whereas Vadathya et al. [27]
integrate learned priors with structured reconstruction to re-
cover light fields from coded observations. These approaches
explicitly link scene statistics with the underlying image
formation process.
Image generation architectures offer complementary means
of representing such priors. U-Net [31] combines an encoder–
decoder hierarchy with multi-scale feature fusion to preserve
spatial detail. GANs [32], [33], including Pix2Pix [34], adopt
adversarial training to learn image translation and promote
realistic outputs. Swin Transformer [19] aggregates local con-
text via shifted-window attention and constructs hierarchical
scene representations. Diffusion models [35] generate images
through iterative denoising; for example, SR3 [36] applies


3
conditional diffusion to super-resolution, and Kim et al. [37]
combine diffusion features with GAN inversion for multimodal
face generation. Unlike direct image regression, adversarial
training assesses the resemblance between generated and real
images, whereas denoising progressively transforms a noisy
representation into an image conditioned on the observation.
Across these architectures, perceptual realism and pixel fidelity
emphasize different aspects of image quality, while the ob-
servation model ultimately determines which scene quantities
must be inferred.
Polarization Prediction and Generation. Recent methods
have extended learned image prediction to polarization es-
timation from conventional RGB inputs. Lin et al. [16]
compare restoration and generative architectures for RGB-
to-polarization estimation, including an estimator based on a
pretrained masked autoencoder (MAE), thereby investigating
how appearance representations transfer to polarization pre-
diction. PolarAnything [17] employs image-conditioned latent
diffusion to predict DoLP and a periodic AoLP representation.
Representing the angular quantity periodically accounts for
the equivalence of orientations separated by 180◦, and the
predicted polarization quantities are subsequently used to
synthesize analyzer views. In a different formulation, Gen-
Polar [18] estimates channel-wise linear Stokes components,
thereby retaining polarization variation across RGB channels,
and further distills a diffusion teacher into a one-step gener-
ator to reduce the number of inference steps. These studies
differ in their output parameterization and generation process:
DoLP/AoLP describe polarization magnitude and orientation,
whereas Stokes components express intensity and signed mod-
ulation. Both representations provide a basis for synthesizing
images at specified analyzer angles and connect the estimated
polarization state to observable intensity variations.

As shown in Fig. 2, a shared Swin-style hierarchical en-

embedding, window attention, and patch merging. At an input

spatial resolution. Shifted-window attention and patch merging

IV. DATA PREPARATION AND IMPLEMENTATION
DETAILS
In this section, we first provide a detailed introduction to our
dataset in Section IV-A, and then present the implementation
details in Section IV-B.
A. Data Acquisition and Scene Coverage
To support the development and evaluation of polarization
image generation in real-world indoor and outdoor environ-
ments, we construct HighPol, a large-scale RGB-polarization
dataset that exhibits rich polarization variations. Data were
captured using a LUCID TRI050S1-QC polarization camera,
whose micro-polarizer array samples four analyzer orienta-
tions in a single exposure. The camera was operated through
ArenaView with a fixed exposure time of 47,178.6 µs. Us-
ing the software’s polarization-view mode, four-angle RGB
images were exported at a resolution of 1224×1024 and sub-
sequently resized to 512×512 for the generation experiments.
The dataset covers diverse scenes and illumination conditions,
including both natural and artificial lighting.
The HighPol dataset covers a broad range of indoor and
outdoor scenes with diverse material reflection properties. As
illustrated in Fig. 3(a), outdoor scenes include glass curtain
walls, metallic vehicles, and water surfaces, whereas indoor
scenes contain transparent plastics, ceramics, and tabletop
materials. To enrich the distribution of polarization patterns,
scenes were collected across different times of day (morning,
noon, and evening) and weather conditions (sunny, cloudy,
and rainy), leading to substantial variation in both polarization
intensity and spectral appearance. In total, HighPol consists of
5,474 four-angle polarization image groups, corresponding to
21,896 high-resolution images.
Table I compares the proposed dataset with existing public
polarization datasets. HighPol complements these resources
with varied scene and material coverage at high spatial reso-
lution. Specifically, scene diversity is measured by the variety
of scene categories and polarization-sensitive materials, while
polarization strength coverage is reflected by the richness
of the DoLP distribution. These characteristics make the
dataset suitable for both polarization image generation and
polarization-aware perception. Fig. 3(b) shows representative
examples with diverse polarization patterns and realistic re-
flection properties.

B. Implementation Details
Experimental settings. We combine 5,474 four-angle image
groups from HighPol with 3,526 groups from PolarVis to
construct a 9,000-group benchmark at 512 × 512 resolution.
Samples are organized into clusters using contiguous source-
index blocks and visual similarity computed from pretrained
image features and image-level comparisons. Each cluster is
assigned intact to a single partition, yielding 7,500 training,
750 validation, and 750 test samples. MPIG is initialized
from a public ImageNet-pretrained encoder and trained for
80 epochs using AdamW with a batch size of 14 and seed 42
on an A800 80 GB GPU. The encoder and decoder learning
rates are initialized to 10−5 and 10−4, respectively, with a
five-epoch warmup followed by cosine decay. During epochs
1–4, the base image–Stokes objective is blended with an
initialization objective using weights e/5 and 1−e/5, respec-
tively. The initialization objective combines residual and image
reconstruction with Stokes-difference, intensity-sum residual,
modulation-magnitude, and direction supervision. The vector,
direction, and magnitude auxiliary losses use fixed coefficients
from epoch 1 onward. From epoch 5, the initialization objec-
tive is removed and training follows Eq. (28).
Comparison settings. All baselines use the same data split
and receive I0 as input. Pix2Pix [34] and the Swin gen-
erator [19] are trained for 100 epochs. For the conditional
DDPM [35], we adopt a U-Net with lossless 2×2 pixel pack-
ing, 1,000 diffusion timesteps, velocity prediction, and 50-step
DDIM sampling. It is trained for 120 epochs, and the epoch-
100 EMA checkpoint is selected for evaluation. RGB2Pol-
MAE [16] is trained for 150,000 updates with 224×224 crops
and uses tiled inference. PolarAnything [17] is fine-tuned for
80,000 updates and evaluated using the selected 40,000-update
checkpoint with 20 sampling steps. GenPolar [18] is trained
for 100 teacher epochs and 30 student-distillation epochs, with
the selected teacher and student checkpoints at epochs 60
and 30, respectively; its student performs one-step inference.
Baseline checkpoints are selected by validation image PSNR.
PolarAnything and GenPolar are initialized from their public
pretrained weights. To adapt the three polarization methods
from RGB input to I0, MAE and GenPolar predict RGB
Stokes components, whereas PolarAnything predicts a polar-
ization state shared across color channels. These predictions
are then combined with I0 to synthesize the missing views.
Evaluation settings. For evaluation, predictions are clipped to
[0, 1] and saved as 8-bit RGB images. Both predictions and
references are then read as grayscale images using OpenCV
and rescaled to [0, 1] for image and polarization assessment.
Image PSNR and SSIM are computed for each predicted view
and subsequently averaged over the three target angles and
all 750 test groups. DoLP is computed with S0 = J0 + J90
and a denominator stabilizer of 10−8. AoLP MAngE averages
min(|bϕ −ϕ|, π −|bϕ −ϕ|) over all pixels and test groups. For
either prediction or reference, pixels with S1 = S2 = 0 are
assigned the numerical value ϕ = 0 and remain included in
the average. We report DoLP PSNR/SSIM/MAbsE and AoLP
MAngE. The DoLP quality metrics use values clipped to [0, 1],
whereas the DoLP> 1 ratio is computed before this DoLP
clipping. Both are derived from the same saved 8-bit analyzer
images. Physical diagnostics include the mean intensity-sum
residual EΣ = ⟨|J0 + J90 −J45 −J135|⟩and the percentage
of pixels with DoLP> 1, denoted r>1. The parameter count
includes all instantiated networks, including unused pretrained
modules in PolarAnything and GenPolar. FLOPs cover all
three 512 × 512 outputs, including tiling and sampling, with
two FLOPs per multiply–accumulate.

I0 combined with the MPIG-generated 45◦, 90◦, and 135◦
views (MPIG), and four identical copies of I0 (Copy-I0).
Across all conditions, the intensity branch consistently re-
ceives the same measured I0. We distinguish between frozen-
input substitution, which replaces the polarization inputs of
a fixed downstream model, and input-specific training, which
trains the same architecture separately under each input condi-
tion. Throughout the downstream experiments, MPIG is held
fixed.
Frozen-input substitution on ZJU-RGB-P. Following the

VI. CONCLUSION
In this work, we presented MPIG for predicting three miss-
ing analyzer views from a single 0◦observation through shared
encoding, cross-angle residual decoding, and polarization-
aware supervision. We further introduced HighPol, a large-
scale dataset comprising 5,474 high-resolution four-angle im-
age groups collected from diverse indoor and outdoor scenes.
On the combined HighPol/PolarVis test set, MPIG achieves
the best overall image and polarization quality among the
evaluated methods, while requiring fewer parameters and
FLOPs than RGB2Pol-MAE. Comparisons with the Copy-
I0 baseline and downstream segmentation experiments further
demonstrate the utility of the predicted angle-dependent vari-
ations.
Despite these advances, polarization estimation remains
challenging under strong reflections, severe intensity varia-
tions, and complex material responses. In addition, MPIG
processes individual images and does not explicitly model
temporal consistency. Future work will therefore focus on
addressing these challenging conditions and extending the
framework to temporally coherent and efficient video-based
polarization reconstruction.
