Abstract. The photometric stereo model allows the construction of 3D virtual replicas of real objects from images acquired under different light conditions. The assumptions of this model are strongly restrictive and are seldom fully satisfied in applications. Indeed, the surface of the observed objects may deviate from ideal conditions, and the geometry of the scene is often only an approximation of the one assumed by the model. Additionally, real-world surfaces may present reflection phenomena, which introduce distortions in reconstructions. In this paper, we present a revised version of the Matlab package ps3d [12], developed to implement the photometric stereo model under various conditions. The new package contains various extensions, the most notable one is the presence of an alternative model to address the presence of a reflective object surface. In addition, it implements particular metrics to analyze the compliance of a dataset to model assumptions, and it is compatible with GNU Octave. The tool allows users to test the model under different observation configurations, also in presence of reflection, using either synthetic or experimental data. 

1. Introduction 

The problem of reconstructing a three-dimensional replica of a given object, starting from easily ob-tainable data, has been studied over years through several different techniques. For example, normal integration [4, 9, 10, 46] estimates the shape of an object from its normal map, while reflectance trans-formation imaging (RTI) [16, 33, 43] constructs a 2D interactive model that derives all possible lighting of the target on the basis of a large amount of images with different light sources. Several deep-learning methods have also been developed for this purpose; see, e.g., [23, 35, 58]. 

Shape from shading (SfS) [3, 17, 21, 29, 51, 53, 59] techniques address the problem of creating a 3D digital model of a surface from a collection of images. Among the best known approaches for SfS are multiview [18, 26, 31] and photometric stereo (PS) [55, 56]. In the first one, the lighting of the scene is fixed and the observer, i.e., the camera, moves through it collecting a sequence of pictures. On the contrary, PS considers a fixed camera pointing at an object of interest, and each image correspond to a different light source. In this paper we focus on this approach. A PS dataset is composed of images collected under different lighting conditions by a fixed camera. The standard model, also known as Lambert’s law , assumes that the observed light intensity is independent of the point of view, and depends exclusively on the relative position between the observed object and the light source. Such property of a surface is called Lambertianity . Another important assumption is that the light source is at infinite distance from the observed scene, so that all light rays are parallel to a known direction. For simplicity, we consider the camera positioned at a large distance from the scene,

which corresponds to orthographic projection. If needed, perspective parameters can be determined by camera calibration techniques. The PS problem has been handled in various settings [1, 28, 47]. In [30], Kozera proposed a formulation that leads to a Hamilton-Jacobi problem, later numerically analyzed in [38, 39], for which at least two images are needed. An alternative formulation, described for example in [12], requires at least three images with different lighting, and determines the solution of the PS problem in two steps. Initially, the normal vector field is estimated from data, then these vectors are used to solve a Poisson problem to determine an approximation of the shape of the object. PS has been employed in many research areas, from medical studies [11, 52] to archaeology [25, 40, 42]. The basic assumption of known light positioning is very limiting, since in real-world applications it is not always possible to measure the exact light location in the scene during the acquisition of the images. Hayakawa [20] showed that, if at least six differently lighted images are available, then the lights position can be estimated from the dataset. This procedure has been used in [12] to create a practical algorithm which determines lighting directions from data, solving at the same time the so-called bas-relief 

ambiguity [2]. Although the method produces accurate results when applied to synthetic datasets, numerical tests show that the computation with experimental data often suffers of some issues. In some cases, the model returns inaccurate reconstruction, or the algorithm may even break down before determining a solution. These issues arise from “non-ideal” dataset, due to unmet model assumptions. To handle such cases, in [13] two procedures for dataset pre-processing have been proposed, based on numerical indicators of ideality. These methods lead to a reduction of a dataset that may improve the solution quality, allowing to determine reasonable reconstructions. Another important issue of the standard PS model is that real objects rarely behave as Lambertian surfaces, and reflect (part of) the light in the direction of the observer, making the observed intensity depend on the point of view. This results in distorted data information. The non-Lambertian case has been extensively studied: some learning-based method are described in [27, 36], while in [24] a statistical approach, based on solving a regression problem, is used to estimate the normal vector field. Differently, in [57], starting from the observation that non-Lambertian surfaces are subject of shadows and reflection, a solution method based on low-rank matrix factorization affected by sparse errors has been proposed. A pre-processing technique to treat the same condition has been studied in [48]. In this paper, we present a new method to treat the reflection issue. The problem is re-formulated as a minimization problem and solved using the Majorization-Minimization algorithm [22]. The model is defined under the assumption of known lighting, and here we present preliminary results obtained from the application to synthetic datasets. Since an implementation of PS related algorithms is not trivial, we present in this work a revised version of the Matlab package ps3d from [12]. It extends the original software by including all the configurations described above and improving some routines. The user has the possibility to test the algorithms using both synthetic and experimental datasets, through easily modifiable demonstration scripts. The new package has been renamed to ps3dtools to avoid confusion with the name of one of its functions. Several implementations of the photometric stereo model are publicly available. Some are based on classical Lambertian approaches, such as the Python implementation of Woodham’s algorithm provided in [54] and the Matlab implementation [50], which follows the formulation of Hayakawa. More recently, LINO-UniPS [34] tackles the photometric stereo setting, where lighting conditions are arbitrary and unknown, by means of learning-based methods designed to estimate surface normals without explicit knowledge of light directions. However, none of these tools provides an approach to assess the conformity of a dataset with the model assumptions, nor do they include an explicit model for reflective surfaces. The package ps3dtools presented in this paper addresses all these aspects. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the PS model in detail, while non-ideality in data is treated in Section 3, and reflection phenomena in Section 4. Section 5 illustrates the ps3dtools package, showing some numerical experiments that can be performed with it. Finally, Section 6 summarizes considerations related to the paper content. 2. The photometric stereo model 

In this section, we briefly recap the main features of the PS mathematical model. An extensive description can be found in [12]. The basic configuration considers an object placed at the origin of a reference system in R3, and a camera positioned so that its optical axis coincides with the z-axis and the lens is directed towards the target. We assume the point of view at infinite distance, allowing for the use of orthographic projection. The images used by the method are obtained moving a light source, theoretically at infinite distance from the object, to different positions in the upper half-space. For each position, a picture is collected. Images are assumed to have resolution (r + 2) × (s + 2) . Each of them has sides of length A and B,where h = A/ (r + 1) is the size of a pixel. Assuming square pixels, we set B = ( s + 1) h. Therefore, each picture defines a domain Ω = [ −A/ 2, A/ 2] × [−B/ 2, B/ 2] . When processing experimental data, that not necessarily verify the ideality assumptions of PS, the computational procedure may fail. Indeed, light sources positioned too close to the target, or the pres-ence of non Lambertian surfaces, may introduce distortion in the data and result in strongly inaccurate reconstructions. Moreover, when Hayakawa’s method is implemented to estimate the light positions, the computation may even breakdown because of the above issues. The breakdown is caused by the fact that, when the matrix M contains non-ideal data, the symmetric matrix G, solution of (3), may not be numerically positive definite, preventing the computation of its Cholesky factor. This kind of problems may happen in many experimental scenarios when data is collected on field. In particular, the requirement of positioning light sources at a large distance from the target may be very limiting in real-world applications. This was observed, e.g., in [13], where the aim was to reconstruct archaeological engravings found in 

Domus de Janas sites. Such prehistoric remains are funerary hypogea formed by very small rooms carved into stone, with partially decorated walls. Due to the structure of the sites, the light sources have to be positioned quite close to the target, failing to meet one of the model assumptions. Moreover, rock surfaces deviate from Lambertianity, due to their composition, so that the basic mathematical model is only an approximation of reality. In [13], it was observed that processing experimental datasets, typically composed by 10–20 images with different lighting, lead to distorted 3D reconstructions. At the same time, removing specific images from the dataset may have the effect of dramatically improving the accuracy of the computed solution. Such “non-ideal” images to be removed were initially chosen by chance, as there is no visual characteristic that identifies them. To help practitioners to reduce the number of images and produce a more effective dataset, two numerical indicators for “data ideality” were developed in [13]. Such procedures iteratively remove an image from a dataset, and check the remaining data for consistency. This results in a list of images to be excluded, that the user may use to improve results. The first indicator checks for the positivity of the matrix G in (3), and selects the configuration that corresponds to the largest positive value of the minimal eigenvalue of G.The second one starts from an alternative formulation of Hayakawa’s procedure, proposed for the first time in [13]. It can be shown that solving (3) is equivalent to finding an upper triangular matrix R such that (4) || Rzt∥ = 1 , t = 1 , . . . , q. 

This produces a nonlinear least-squares problem, which is solved by a Gauss–Newton algorithm proposed in [44]. It turns out that in the presence of distorted data, the Jacobian of the problem becomes rank-deficient at convergence. The second ideality indicator selects the configuration which ensures that the last singular value of the Jacobian is nonzero. The two indicators have been implemented in ps3dtools toolbox. The ps3dtools package 

An important aspect of this paper is the practical implementation of the models discussed, which bridges theory and real-world applications. Here, we describe the ps3dtools package, built for this purpose. It implements the photometric stereo model, enabling the reconstruction of the shape of objects from multiple images, both grayscale or RGB. Here we propose a revised version of the software first presented in [12]. The toolbox is developed in the Matlab environment [37], but it is compatible with GNU Octave [19], an open source package with 10 A. BUCCINI, E. CRABU, F. PES, G. RODRIGUEZ

• ps3dstep1 computes the normal vector field by leveraging normligths , that estimates the normal vector field N and the light directions L, starting from the initial estimation obtained from the SVD of M , and rotatelights , that returns the final estimates of the matrices N and L, and the rotation matrix Q;

• ps3dstep2 determines the 3D coordinates of the observed object, depending on Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions. Some parts of the computation are included in a set of Auxiliary functions ; see Table 2. Such group also includes various data visualization routines, such as plotlights and plotlights3d , to represent light directions, showdset , to display a dataset, and writeply , which stores a computed surface in a 

.ply file. Such format is suitable to drive 3D printers and to visualize the reconstructed surface on a 3D viewer/editor like, e.g., MeshLab [41]. Unfortunately, writeply cannot be used in Octave. 5.2. Ideality indicators. This pair of functions implements the computation of the ideality indicators described in [13]. They extract from a dataset a subset of images that meet the specific condition considered by the corresponding indicator. The first indicator of ideality ( psideal1 ) tries to ensure the positiveness of the matrix G in (3), while the second one ( psideal2 ) aims at keeping the Jacobian of (4) full rank; see Section 3. 5.3. Reflection model. The function refl implements the model introduced in Section 4 to process data affected by reflection phenomena. It detects reflection points in data and estimates the normal vector field. Type I and Type II errors for the reflection matrix E are computed by the typeerrors 

function. 5.4. Data generation. A data generation script is useful for the user to make experiments under specific chosen conditions. The function datagen , by fixing certain parameters, creates a dataset that reflects desired observation conditions. Such function is strictly related to the psdatasynth package, described in [15]. It is, in fact, a simplified version of the psdatagen script from that package. If psdatagen is installed, datagen invokes some of its functions to generate a set of synthetic pictures. The data directory contains a set of functions that supports the user in creating a dataset in the format required by the package, from a set of acquired images. The findoffset script is used to detect the offset for aligning a dataset. Such process is motivated by small displacements characterizing experimentally acquired images, and often enhances the definition of the reconstructed surface. The functions createdataset and createdatasetrgb can be used to create a dataset from a collection of pictures. Two conversion routines may be helpful to speed up data processing. We mention the fact that digital cameras usually produce compressed images, which may introduce distortion in 3D reconstructions. Modern cameras allow to store images in “RAW” format, avoiding any processing by the camera software. Such “raw” images are memory consuming, since they are not compressed, and are often stored using a proprietary format which is not supported by common software packages. To convert them to a suitable format (e.g., PNG) a specific computer program is required. We advise the use of the open source application Raw Therapee [49] for this task. 6. Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented a revised and extended version of the Matlab package ps3d , designed to support the photometric stereo model for the 3D reconstruction of real objects from images acquired under varying illumination conditions. The new release addresses several limitations of the original package. In particular, we introduce an alternative model to handle reflective surface phenomena, which represent one of the most common sources of distortion in real-world acquisitions. Furthermore, the package now includes dedicated indicators to quantitatively assess the compliance of a given dataset with the PS model assumptions. Compatibility with GNU Octave has also been ensured. The tool supports both synthetic and experimental data, and it is completed by some demo tests. REFERENCES 15
