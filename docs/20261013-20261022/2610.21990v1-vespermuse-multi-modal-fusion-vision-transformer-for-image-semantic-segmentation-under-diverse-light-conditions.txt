Abstract
Semantic segmentation is critical for the reliable navigation and scene understanding of autonomous
systems, yet its performance degrades severely in low-light environments due to noise, artifacts,
colour distortion, and the loss of structural details. To address this challenge, this work introduces
VesperMUSE, a unified multi-modal framework designed to achieve robust image semantic segmen-
tation across diverse illumination conditions. The architecture addresses the light deficiency prob-
lem on multiple levels: proposing a novel physics-informed synthetic low-light adaptation algorithm,
approaching Low-Light Image Enhancement (LLIE) through a thermal-guided paradigm, and finally
aiming to recover the intrinsic scene details for semantic segmentation through a multi-modal fusion
approach. Following enhancement, the framework dynamically integrates the primary RGB features
with auxiliary thermal representations and surface normal maps highlighting geometric discontinu-
ities and material boundaries. At the core of this integration is a highly scalable, modality-agnostic
self-attention mechanism that extracts both global context and fine local features from an arbitrary
number of auxiliary inputs. By combining and projecting these hierarchical multi-modal represen-
tations into a unified component, the attention module allows the network to selectively attend to
relevant cross-modal cues without requiring any architectural modifications when different sensor
configurations are used. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that VesperMUSE successfully recovers
missing visual cues, providing a flexible, state-of-the-art (SOTA) solution for machine perception in
extreme and unstructured real-world environments.

Introduction

Semantic Segmentation. The performance and goal-achievement capability of autonomous or
Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) systems, which have become a preferred choice for navigating
dangerous environments in recent years, rely strongly on scene understanding. High-level Computer
Vision (CV) tasks such as pixel-level semantic segmentation [1, 2] or instance-level object detection
[3] have emerged as powerful tools for mapping sensor observations into information and cues that
guide decision-making. The main challenge remains robust perception under varying conditions
[4, 5]. Semantic segmentation achieves scene understanding by assigning each pixel in an image
to a specific class, with classes defined during training. In this way, the entire scene is parsed, and
objects can be more easily identified by their shape or distinct attributes. Due to its highly reliable
approach, the applications of semantic segmentation extend beyond outdoor scene parsing to do-
mains such as medicine [6], indoor environments [7], and even more challenging missions such as
survivor localisation [8] in hazy or poorly lit conditions.
Usually, high-level CV tasks perform best under near-ideal conditions: consistent illumination that
ensures accurate colour balance, without haze, smoke, noise, or other degradations. However, real-
world environments rarely provide such conditions, therefore, robust systems must be capable of
handling non-ideal sensor inputs [9, 10]. In this study, the focus is on improving low-light semantic
segmentation and understanding the steps required to recover lost detail, as well as how to compen-
sate for features that cannot be restored [11].
Low-Light Conditions. Light-deficient environments occur as frequently as daylight scenes, mak-
ing them not merely an edge case of CV systems but a domain that requires rigorous treatment
[12]. What makes low-light images particularly challenging is that limited illumination introduces
additional issues beyond the obvious reduced exposure, such as global and local structure loss,
noise, artifacts, and prominent colour distortions [13, 14]. There are multiple approaches to im-
proving semantic segmentation performance under such conditions, with the main categories being:
enhancement-based methods [15] (where a network is applied prior to segmentation to enhance the
images), knowledge distillation [16] (where a student network trained on low-light images is con-
strained to match the internal representations of a teacher network trained on well-lit data), single-
network approaches (where the feature space is analyzed using frequency-domain techniques [17]
with a greater emphasis being placed on edge information), and multi-modal fusion approaches
[18, 19], in which the limited information in low-light images is compensated by incorporating addi-
tional representations.
Thermal Imagery. An expected observation is the reliability of thermal (infrared) sensing in low-
13


light environments, as such representations do not depend on illumination but rather on heat irradi-
ation, making them largely light-agnostic [20] and capable of preserving details often lost in low-
light imagery, such as clear structural boundaries and reduced artifacts [21]. However, this modality
presents several limitations, including poor separation of objects or surfaces with similar tempera-
tures [22], background interference when ambient temperatures approximate those of objects [23],
and the absence of colour information, which remains an essential cue for accurate scene parsing
and object identification [24]. Despite these constraints, the practical acquisition of thermal data
has become increasingly accessible. The commercial miniaturization of Long-Wave Infrared micro-
bolometers has rendered these sensors both cost-effective and easy to integrate into real-world au-
tonomous systems. In the scope of this study, the thermal modality is technically leveraged to en-
hance low-light RGB data by providing a high Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) in environments where
visible-spectrum sensors succumb to photon scarcity. Specifically for semantic segmentation, ther-
mal data provides an invariant heat-signature prior very helpful for class-specific identification (e.g.,
distinguishing biological entities from inanimate structures).
Improving Segmentation Performance Through Enhancement. VesperMUSE adopts a pre-
segmentation thermal-guided enhancement approach, where low-light images are first enhanced
using a model trained separately from the semantic segmentation network. This design provides
greater modularity, allowing the framework to be extended to other degradations, like haze or blur,
by simply replacing the enhancement module. Another advantage is that the training data can be de-
coupled, removing the need for low-light, well-lit, and semantic segmentation annotations to coexist
within the same dataset. Since the enhancement network is trained independently, its predictions
achieve a high degree of visual quality, as the module is optimized to approximate normal lighting
conditions. In contrast, jointly trained (one-stage) networks may produce intermediate-enhanced
outputs represented in latent space which is not suitable for direct human evaluation. This aspect is
particularly important for the present work, as the low-light enhanced RGB map is further used as
input for additional representations.

Further improvements. In the context of VesperMUSE, to aid the semantic parsing process, LLIE
is conducted on the low-light images with thermal being brought as an extra representation. The in-
frared imagery is later employed [9] for the segmentation training as well, since it provides relevant
cues for both tasks. To make the current approach more robust, several questions need answers in
order to move forward: How to integrate more structural information if thermal maps do not provide
all that is needed? How will the information be fused in such a way that if specific scenarios might
require more modalities, such as Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) [29], or event cameras, the
network will structurally support that without architecture modifications?
The answer to the first question lies in the use of surface normal maps derived from monocular
depth estimation. Specifically, DepthAnything V2 [30] is employed to extract monocular depth
15


maps from the RGB input (which may be the original RGB or synthetic low-light adapted and en-
hanced depending on the configuration and objective). These monocular depth maps provide addi-
tional structural information which might be missing from the RGB and thermal maps by leverag-
ing the pre-trained representations. Surface normals are then obtained by normalising these maps
through gradient extraction.
The main innovation introduced by the core component of the VesperMUSE, its semantic segmenta-
tion module, lies in the attention mechanism employed during the encoding stage. The underlying
concept is that each modality is analyzed both globally and at the level of fine local features, thereby
providing a comprehensive representation. To capture the contribution of each modality, hierarchical
feature maps are fused and treated as inputs to the main attention components. Given that the atten-
tion mechanism is conceptually designed for one primary modality and k ∈N auxiliary modalities,
it can be readily configured to support an arbitrary number of modalities with varying channel con-
figurations (e.g., RGB with three channels or single-channel thermal maps). This flexibility makes
the model highly adaptable to diverse scenarios and imaging conditions where the availability of
different modalities is different than the current analysed scenario, thereby addressing the second
question proposed before.
Data availability and adaptation. One important aspect to take into account is the limited avail-
ability of data for conducting such a study. While multi-modal semantic segmentation datasets pro-
vide a high degree of reliability, and a wide range of datasets serve as effective benchmarks for
LLIE, no available dataset integrates well-lit and low-light counterparts RGB, thermal imagery,
structural information, and annotated semantic maps simultaneously. Given that semantic segmen-
tation annotations are expensive to obtain, the most appropriate approach is to utilize datasets that
include well-lit RGB images, thermal/structural data, and semantic maps.
Consequently, a novel synthetic low-light adaptation pipeline is proposed as part of the Vesper-
MUSE framework. The algorithm leverages local image structure and global lighting information,
generating degradations that capture natural exposure variations, noise characteristics, and colour
distortions. A dedicated analysis provides a quantitative evaluation of the physical and perceptual
quality of the synthesized images to show the generated images’ conformity with real-world data.
This integration of both local and real-world illumination information ensures that the generated
images faithfully reproduce the deficiencies of natural low-light environments.

To summarize, the contributions of VesperMUSE are:
• Unified framework: VesperMUSE enables improved low-light semantic segmentation while
preserving strong performance in well-lit settings.
• Thermal-guided LLIE: A highly computational-efficient enhancement module tailored to
optimize downstream semantic segmentation.
• Modality-agnostic fusion: A scalable Fusion Vision Transformer supporting an arbitrary num-
ber of input modalities.
• Comprehensive evaluation: Consistent competitive performance with SOTA results across
multiple benchmarks.

2.1 Synthetic Low-Light Image Adaptation
The development of LLIE models has been historically hampered by the limited dataset availability
and the quality of light-deficient representations. Early efforts such as the SID dataset [31] focused
on capturing short-exposure raw images along with long-exposure counterparts. This was a good
representation of how a LLIE dataset should look like, however it was limited in terms of the format
it used: RAW. The consumer and surveillance hardware operate much more easily using conven-
tional formats such as PNG or JPG, which also provide more efficient computational processing.
Moreover, the conversion of RAW images into other encodings fails to replicate the natural noise
and illumination patterns captured in the low-light conditions, the quality of the resulting data hin-
dering the supervised learning process of LLIE models.
More recently, the problem with data limitation was fixed with the appearance of paired real-world
datasets such as LOL [32], LOL-v2 real [33], SDSD-in/out [34], and LOL-Blur [35]. Another field
of concern, however, lies in the reliance of synthetic low-light adaptation methods. Widely-used
current approaches, such as the work of Yang et al. through their LOL-v2 synthetic dataset [33] have
provided a very solid foundation for how synthetic low-light images should be constructed: follow-
ing a real-world dataset illumination distribution extracted from multiple datasets and delivering the
result into an easy-to-use encoding format.
2.2 Low-Light Image Enhancement
Classical Methods. LLIE has been firstly addressed using plain-enhancement [36] which focused
mainly on histogram equalisation [37] and gamma correction [38, 39]. These methods amplify the
brightness and contrast of underexposed images through pixel intensity transformations. Because
of the complex and uneven way illumination interacts with different areas from the image, these
models struggled to model the light projection process, resulting in perceptually inconsistent outputs
containing over-enhanced regions and amplified noise.
The later introduced Retinex theory [40] aims to decompose the image in two components: re-
flectance, which represents the intrinsic scene information and illuminance, focusing on lighting
variations. To better account for the illumination effects on the local and global structures, LLIE-
methods have employed the theory as the underlying assumption behind the image architecture
[41, 42, 43]. These models focused on estimating and refining the illumination map and recon-
19


structing the image in a later stage with the more balanced and denoised reflectance map. This
component-separation approach lead to more visually appealing and plausible results than the tradi-
tional enhancement techniques which rely heavily on hand-crafted priors and assumptions, making
them hard to generalize across multiple datasets.
CNN-based Methods. Besides the improvements brought in language applications or high level
CV tasks, the advancement of Deep Learning (DL) has also transformed the field of image enhance-
ment becoming the dominant paradigm [14, 44, 45]. One of the earliest DL models on the scene of
LLIE was LLNet [46] which proved that supervised feature learning can improve brightness and
contrast while also recovering lost details. Generative models, like EnGAN [15], focused on creat-
ing a direct mapping from low-light images to their enhanced counterparts by employing an end-
to-end framework. These methods further improved upon traditional hand-crafted heuristic-based
algorithms through hierarchical feature extraction.
The Retinex theory [40] is furthermore employed as an assumption, leveraging the ability of DL
frameworks to learn feature decomposition. While such models, benefit from both physical priors
and data-driven learning, they often require multi-stage training pipelines through subnetwork spe-
cialisation [42] on tasks such as: decomposition, illumination/reflectance enhancement, and recon-
struction. As a result, the computational and training complexity of this kind of models are high. To
solve this, one-stage Retinex-inspired Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) have also been pro-
posed in order to increase the computational efficiency, but they struggle with noise mitigation and
artifacts, resulting in an unsatisfactory enhancement process.
Another issue that CNN-based models exhibit is the limited receptive field of convolutional layers,
hence reducing their ability to capture long-range spatial dependencies constructing the global con-
textual relationship across distant image regions. As light’s interaction with the scene is not linear,
hence why histogram-matching models fail in the LLIE domain, this limitation restricts the models’
capacity of capturing diverse scene structures and mapping the relationships between them.
ViT-based Methods. To overcome the inherent locality of convolutional operators, ViT-based
models [27, 47] have dominated the SOTA models charts for LLIE in the past years. The success
achieved in machine translation by the Transformer architecture [48], has also spread to CV and
more specifically to image enhancement. The principal particularity that ViTs possess is the self-
attention module which manages to capture long-range dependencies which guide the enhancement
process towards a complex resolution of the LLIE challenge.
20


Several architectures have marked the evolution of using ViTs [27] in LLIE. For example, UFormer
[49] adapts the U-shaped [26] encoder-decoder paradigm, replacing the standard convolutions found
in U-Net [26] with Transformer blocks while preserving level-wise skip connections. Retinexformer
[50] incorporates illumination representations into the self-attention mechanism to model non-local
interactions across regions with varying lighting conditions. More recent work, such as ISALux
[51] or ModalFormer [52], successfully manage to integrate semantic and structure priors into the
self-attention module aiming to compensate the light-deficiency induced detail loss with visual or
feature-based information represented through different modalities.
Despite their strong performance, Transformer-based methods face notable computational chal-
lenges. The self-attention mechanism incurs substantial computational complexity due to its reliance
on the spatial dimensions of the feature maps. To mitigate this limitation, several methods, such as
Restormer [53], DepthLux [54], employ different strategies: computing the attention map channel
wise considering the depth of the feature map as the number of channels, contracting the feature
space before the self-attention module and then expanding it back to its original dimensions, or re-
placing convolutions with their more efficient variation, depthwise separable convolutions.
RGB-Thermal Fusion for LLIE. RGB-Thermal fusion has recently been explored to enhance ro-
bustness in extreme low-light conditions by leveraging the illumination-invariant nature of thermal
imagery. In particular, some studies [55] propose a thermal-aware ViT architecture, where thermal
features are explicitly embedded into the transformer pipeline to guide global feature modeling un-
der severe illumination degradation. Complementarily, Jha et al. focus on the interaction between
illumination cues derived from the low-light RGB image and the corresponding thermal map, de-
signing a mechanism that aligns and integrates these complementary representations for more stable
enhancement [56]. These works demonstrate that modeling cross-modal dependencies, especially
between illumination and thermal structure, can significantly improve detail recovery and perceptual
consistency in challenging low-light scenarios.
2.3 Modality Fusion for Image Semantic Segmentation
Multi-modal semantic segmentation has emerged as an effective strategy for improving scene under-
standing and feature richness limitations under real-world conditions where RGB might be insuffi-
cient [57]. Complementary sensing modalities such as thermal (infrared) or depth maps have been
included as inputs to improve the information representation of a specific scene [9]. Depth maps
21


provide geometric and distance cues that can help disambiguate low-contrast regions and cluttered
backgrounds [58], while thermal images capture thermic distributions, offering a visual representa-
tion which is not subject to change under diverse light patterns or other deficiencies often noticed in
nature like smoke or haze [20]. Semantic segmentation is not the only high-level CV task that has
been benefiting from adding multi-modality in learning, with object detection, tracking, autonomous
driving perception systems [59] also improving performance by leveraging multiple representations
of the same scenes.
In the case of RGB-Depth semantic segmentation, early methods focused on fusing modalities at the
input or feature level with some approaches directly concatenating RGB and depth maps or fusing
them by summation [60, 61]. In time, models have evolved to a two-stream architecture [4], where
independent networks extract features separately before integrating them in the decoding process.
The recent focus has been placed on feature fusion approach which separates the fusion logic from
the backbone and employing modules like Separation-and-Aggregation Gate [62] or Attention Com-
plementary Modules [63] to provide parallel modality streams at each hierarchical level.
A significant focal point of recent work is discriminative treatment of modalities [64]. This repre-
sents a change from the initial models which treated all network layers equally. More recent frame-
works like LASNet [65] argue for level-specific treatment. This involves designing distinct models
for different hierarchies: enhancing shallow spatial details (thermal-focused) and deep semantic
features (RGB-focused) separately. Methods frequently integrate channel weighted selection and
spatial-attention mechanisms to mitigate the inherent discrepancies (originating from distinct under-
lying data distributions) between modalities [44].
The inherent limitations of CNNs, stemming from their constrained receptive fields, are also evident
in modality fusion models. The Transformer architecture [27] has emerged as a compelling solution
to this issue [66]. Driven by the capacity of the self-attention mechanism to model complex rela-
tionships across different contexts, current research is shifting from dedicated dual-modality models
toward unified architectures. While earlier methods perform well on specific pairings like RGB-
Depth or RGB-Thermal [67], unified frameworks capable of integrating multiple concurrent data
streams offer a much richer understanding of scene context, semantics, and structure. Consequently,
these architectures yield superior performance on advanced scene understanding tasks.

3.1 Framework Design and Motivation
The framework behind VesperMUSE is based on three main components each having a dedicated
role in the focus of this study: finding solutions for improving semantic segmentation under both
low-light and well illuminated scenes.
VesperMUSE can be run both in low-light or normal light configuration. If the latter is activated,
then the framework starts from Stage 3.
Stage 1: Low-Light Synthetic Adaptation. The first step is obtaining the synthetic low-light imag-
ing which will be processed using the algorithm presented in Section 3.2.1. To link this framework
as closely as possible to real world scenarios, the reasoning was physics-informed.
Stage 2: Low-Light Image Enhancement. After the low-light maps are processed they serve as
inputs to the thermal-guided LLIE module described in Section 3.3, along with the thermal imaging.
The LLIE model is trained under a supervised approach trying to predict the original RGB map
coming from the two focus datasets: PST900 [8] and CART [25]. The training and test splits are
based on the ones used for the semantic segmentation stage. The result of the training process is
the best checkpoint in terms of Peak-Signal-to-Noise-Ratio (PSNR), each dataset having its own
selected checkpoint.
Stage 3: Obtaining the Surface Normal maps. The RGB image (LLIE or not) serves as the in-
put to the DepthAnything V2 [30] model, the resulting map being a monocular depth estimation.
This representation is then used to produce surface normals by taking the gradients of it and thereby
aiming to normalise the structural imagery.
Stage 4: Fusion-based Semantic Segmentation. The three inputs corresponding to the same
scene: RGB, thermal, and surface normal are then passed to the Fusion Vision Transformer which
processes each of them using a dedicated pre-trained backbone and then fuses them in a modality-
agnostic manner. After the features have been fused, a decoding process commences processing
the output of the framework, the segmented map. Likewise LLIE, each dataset has its own trained
checkpoint selected on the mIoU performance.

limited in terms of: input data format (relying on the RAW format), noise modeling (linear noise

3.2.2 Auxiliary modalities
VesperMUSE’s segmentation module works with three modalities: the main modality (well-lit RGB
or LLIE map obtained with the algorithm presented in Section 3.2.1 and the thermal-guided en-
hancement model described in Section 3.3), and two auxiliary modalities: thermal and surface nor-
mal derived from the monocular depth map obtained from the main RGB modality.
Thermal. As previously discussed, the thermal modality provides enhanced robustness under vary-
ing illumination conditions. Furthermore, its effectiveness is largely attributed to the intrinsic phys-
ical properties it captures, namely the thermal inertia and emissivity of different materials and sur-
faces. These characteristics offer complementary information to the Fusion Vision Transformer,
beyond what is available in the visible spectrum.
In the PST900 dataset [8], which depicts a subterranean environment with minimal heat sources,
thermal imaging acts as a reliable structural cue, enabling the detection of humans even in the pres-
ence of obscurants. Conversely, in the CART dataset [25], the thermal modality facilitates the dis-
crimination of diverse textures commonly encountered in natural outdoor environments.
Surface Normal. To better extract the context of the scene, a monocular depth map is extracted to
highlight the structural and material differences between objects or surfaces. The noise and colour
distortions, attenuated but still present in the enhanced RGB images and the thermal consistency
of nearby objects in thermal maps might misguide the network in distinguishing edges and sepa-
ration of entities. The first order gradients of the depth map are taken and turned into unit normal
vectors to isolate and emphasize the geometric discontinuities where objects might protrude from
their backgrounds. Furthermore, the scale-invariant representation of the geometric features pro-
vides the robustness needed in terms of the relative position of the objects to the camera, enabling
a informative features extraction for close objects, as found in the PST900 dataset [8], and distant
objects as in the CART dataset [25].

3.4.1 Fusion Encoder Block
The Fusion Encoder Block represents the unitary component of the encoding stage of the Fusion Vi-
sion Transformer. Its role is to coherently combine the features originating from multiple modalities
and to assess the unique information each one of them contains and how this information interacts
with the other sets of specific data from the other modalities.
To achieve this, the Fusion Encoder Block employs 2 modules: the Fusion Self-Attention Module
which combines and selects the most relevant features based on the current context and a FFN which
further refines the fused maps. The modules interact through skip connection which aid the gradient
flow and normalisation layers that provide data distribution unity across the samples.
The principal advantage of the proposed Fusion Encoder Block lies in its structural scalability to
an arbitrary number of modalities, k ∈N. The only requirement is the designation of one modality
as the primary modality, while the remaining k modalities are treated as auxiliary. Consequently,
the functionality of the Fusion Encoder Block can be formally generalized at an abstract level to
accommodate any number of modalities, and can be expressed as follows:

3.4.2 Fusion Self-Attention Module
The main constituent of Vision Transformers is the self-attention module which captures relevant
features from the images, highlighting the prevalent information from the visual space. However, its

effectiveness represents a compromise in terms of computational complexity with the standard vi-

proposed Fusion Self-Attention Module aims to achieve a both global and local feature extraction,
which allows the model to adapt the latent representations considering a wider range of information.

3.4.5 Segmentation Head
To map the high-dimensional fused representations extracted by the Fusion Vision Transformer into
the distinct probability logits necessary for each class, a CNN is utilized.
Within the VesperMUSE architecture, the Segmentation Head utilizes a CNN to map the extracted
feature representations into class logits, employing Batch-Normalisation (BN) to stabilize the acti-
vations. To act as a stochastic regularizer, a Dropout layer is strategically inserted between the two
convolutional layers. By randomly zeroing hidden units during training, this layer prevents feature
co-adaptation and forces the network to learn robust, distributed representations. Consequently, it
mitigates overfitting to high-dimensional noise within the dense feature maps and ensures that the
final pixel-wise classification generalizes effectively across diverse spatial contexts.
Let cls denote the number of target semantic classes, W1 and W2 represent the weight matrices of
the first and second convolutional layers respectively, and ϕ denote the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)
activation function. Given an input feature map Fin, the functionality of this Segmentation Head can

issue by introducing a modulating factor that reduces the importance of well-classified samples
and emphasizes misclassified or uncertain predictions. Additionally, class-dependent weights are
incorporated to further compensate for dataset imbalance.

3.4.7 Fusion Vision Transformer Training
The model is trained for 40K iterations for the PST900 dataset [8] and 80K iterations for the CART
dataset [25] using the AdamW optimizer [71] implemented in PyTorch [72], with a base learning
46


rate of 3 × 10−4, weight decay of 0.05, and momentum coefficients (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999). Gradient
clipping with a maximum norm of 0.1 is applied to stabilize optimization.
Parameter-wise Learning Rates. Differential learning rates are employed for backbone and task-
specific components. The ResNet backbones [28] are optimized with a learning rate scaled by 0.1
relative to the base rate, while newly introduced fusion and prediction layers are trained with the
full base learning rate. This strategy enables stable fine-tuning of pretrained representations while
allowing faster convergence of randomly initialized layers.
Learning Rate Scheduling. A cosine annealing schedule is employed with Tmax = 10K for the
PST900 dataset [8] and Tmax = 20K for CART [25]. Tmax controls the duration (in iterations) over
which the learning rate gradually decreases from the initial value η0 to the minimum value ηmin. The
minimum learning rate is ηmin = 1 × 10−7. A short warm-up phase is used. Given that η0 denotes the

4.5.1 The effect of LLIE on Semantic Segmentation performance
The LLIE process conducted before the segmentation in the low-light configuration of VesperMUSE
is essential for recovering lost detail and removing the noise. In this section the effect of the en-
hancement will be thoroughly studied to determine what improvement it brings in the semantic
segmentation process and if some classes can benefit more from LLIE.
Figures 4.8 and 4.10 show that the RGB configuration of VesperMUSE dominates in terms of perfor-
mance which is absolutely expected. The main focus point of this study is to see if under a tri-modal
input setting with no prior enhancement for the RGB, the performance is decreasing.
For the PST900 dataset [8] the overall dataset mIoU improvement is strongly influenced by the large

performance differences on the Fire-Ext. and Survivor classes, while the Hand-Drill and Backpack
classes show a slight performance decline. The differences, however, have a considerable offset in
the magnitude. While for the Hand-Drill and Backpack classes it can be considered just a model
or training particularity, it is not the same case for Fire-Ext. and Survivor. A good candidate expla-
nation for this would be the common features shared by the two groups of classes. Survivor and
Fire-Ext are represented using brighter colours and a larger surface covered by their instances, while

distributed more evenly, opposed to the artificial light and the cave environment of PST900 [8], man-

and enhancement process throughout. The only issue that persists even after the enhancement pro-
cess has been applied is the absence of the Person class among the predictions which can be again
attributed, as in the PST900 [8] case, to the small surface covered by noise across its instances.

4.5.2 Modality choices and their rank
The inputs of VesperMUSE can be configured in a large number of ways. Although the standard and
recommended configuration is composed of three inputs: RGB, thermal, and surface normals, the
model can be run using any number of modalities as inputs with very little changes as the Fusion
Self-Attention was specifically designed to structurally support any number of modalities.
The only constraint, however, is that one modality has to be declared as the main modality while
the others are auxiliaries. The main modality is used as the main source of information in the en-
coding stage and also has a larger backbone responsible for extracting the features from it. In all
the configurations presented until now, the main modality was the RGB (normal or synthetically
low-light adapted and then enhanced), but the purpose of this ablation study is to show how the
model behaves under different number of inputs and what are the effects of modifying the rank of
the modalities.
Table 4.6 presents different input configurations and provides evidence for why RGB was chosen as
a primary modality even when running under a LLIE configuration and the enhanced images present
noise and colour distortions, while the thermal is left unaffected. Furthermore, LLIE RGB is shown
to also validate the primary modality advantage when interchangeably switched with the surface
normal representations.
Thermal and Surface Normals as Auxiliaries. A first observation, as shown in Figure 1.1, is that
both modalities bring an important contribution if compared to the segmentation network using
65


only the LLIE RGB as input. Their impact is very similar when individually employed, with an
average of ≈3% performance deficit when the surface normal is not used and an average of ≈3.5%
performance deficit considering both datasets. The combined benefit is observed mainly on the
PST900 [8] dataset with an average improvement of ≈5% for both modalities, confirming again the
fact that the CART dataset [25] provides more informativeness in its RGB maps than PST900 [8]
because of the different light conditions.
The relevance of the RGB modality is again confirmed when comparing the two configurations
where LLIE RGB and surface normal representations are switched so that each one is alternatively a
main modality and an auxiliary. The results on both datasets show that there is an average of ≈7.0%
performance deficit when the surface normal map is used as a main modality.
Thermal as Main Modality. Given that this ablation study is conducted under low-light config-
uration, it would be expected for the thermal modality provide more information about the over-
all composition of the image, as it is not affected by noise, exposure and loss of global and local
structures. However, a configuration with thermal used as a main modality, with surface normals,
reflectance and illumination as auxiliaries shows a large performance decline compared to the other
configurations where LLIE RGB is used as the main modality.
The reflectance and illuminance maps represent a decomposition of the LLIE RGB image conducted
under the assumption of the Retinex Theory [40] which suggests that visual information can be sepa-
rated into two core components: reflectance responsible for the colour information and illuminance
encoding the intensity of the pixels.
The decomposition can be done using various ways, from classical to neural networks which are
trained specifically for accurate representation of both components. However, for the purpose of
this experiment, a simpler approach was used, where the illumination is considered as the maximum
value across the channels and reflectance as the original image divided by the illumination, both
filtered using, a Gaussian Kernel.

4.5.3 Architectural motivation
Impact of the Fusion Strategy. To motivate the design of the proposed Fusion Encoder Block, it is
compared against a baseline strategy that relies on naive concatenation. In this baseline, multi-modal
features are simply concatenated along the channel dimension prior to passing through a standard
Transformer block. As shown in Table 4.7, the proposed cross-attention fusion approach yields a
substantial performance improvement, outperforming the concatenation baseline by ≈4% mIoU.
This performance gap highlights the limitations of early concatenation. While concatenation aggre-
gates multi-modal data into a unified tensor, it forces the subsequent layers to implicitly learn the
cross-modal alignments from scratch without any structured guidance. In contrast, the proposed
fusion mechanism explicitly models the spatial and semantic correlations between the primary
and auxiliary modalities. By utilizing cross-attention, the network dynamically queries the auxil-
iary streams to enhance the primary features, ensuring that the model selectively attends to relevant
multi-modal cues rather than treating all concatenated channels with equal initial weight.
Replacing Decoders with Convolutions. As detailed in Section 3.4.4, the core feature refinement
component within the VesperMUSE decoder is a lightweight, attention-based Transformer block.
This module is specifically designed to refine the multi-scale fused features, utilizing self-attention
to dynamically weigh and prioritize spatial information that is most relevant to the current semantic
context. To validate this design choice, an ablation study was conducted where these Transformer
blocks were replaced with standard pointwise convolutions, effectively removing the global context-
aggregation capabilities of the attention mechanism while maintaining a similar channel dimension-
ality.
67


Table 4.7, shows that reverting to a purely convolutional decoder results in a measurable perfor-
mance degradation, dropping the mIoU from 0.7818 to 0.7351 on PST900 [8], and from 0.5957 to
0.5320 on CART [25]. While the convolutional substitution slightly reduces the total parameter
count (from 180M to 179M), it increases the computational overhead (from 321G to 326G FLOPs).
More importantly, the drop in segmentation accuracy confirms that the spatially-aware feature re-
finement provided by the Transformer block is crucial. The self-attention mechanism allows the
network to effectively resolve local ambiguities by leveraging long-range dependencies, a capability
that simple pointwise convolutions lack.

5.1 Known Limitations
Synthetic Low-Light Adaptation Algorithm Strong Degradation. In the current configuration,
the main issue that is persistent within the low-light synthetic pipeline is that a small sample of the
low-light samples are excessively degraded so that the LLIE module cannot recover lost informa-
tion. The source of this issue remains unknown, however it is suspected that the issue comes from
increasing the variability of illumination in the low-light conditions. As a result, a very few number
of samples from the final light distribution are affected by this issue. Figure 5.1 presents a part of
the affected samples with some managing to capture the overall structure of the image keeping some
of the objects observable while others represent a totally noise and artifacts-covered representation
which cannot be correctly evaluated by the segmentation module.
A solution to this problem would be either removing the affected samples or further tuning the syn-
thetic low-light adaptation algorithm to diagnose problematic scenarios and increase the illumina-
tion threshold while keeping the noise levels lower than for other samples.
LLIE module Output Colour Imbalance. A comparative analysis of the global intensity dis-
tributions (Figure 5.2) reveals that the LLIE module introduces luminance-dependent chromatic
aberrations via non-linear channel uncoupling. Specifically, in the shadow and mid-tone regimes
(0 ≤I ≤150), the model disproportionately attenuates the blue channel relative to the source
distribution, inducing an unnatural warm colour cast. Conversely, in the extreme highlight regime
70


(I →255), the output exhibits anomalous green-channel clipping, with saturation peaks signifi-
cantly exceeding both the original image limits and concurrent RGB channels. This divergent colour
distortion fundamentally disrupts the intrinsic RGB ratios across the dynamic range, highlighting
a deficiency in colour constancy and necessitating the integration of cross-channel coupled tone
mapping.
Since the current loss function configuration does not have a colour loss integrated, this would
strongly benefit the reduction of channel-based colour discrepancies between the well-lit images
and the predicted maps from the LLIE module. In this case, the loss function would receive a new
term, namely a colour-focused loss.

5.2 Future Work
The subsequent development of VesperMUSE should primarily target both performance enhance-
ment and the reduction of computational complexity. At present, further improvements in perfor-
mance can be facilitated by adopting more efficient backbone architectures, potentially surpassing
those derived from the ResNet family. Additionally, the utilization of modality-specific backbones
for thermal and structural inputs represents a promising direction, as these are expected to outper-
form the current convolutional adaptation strategy employed within the model.
Furthermore, it is worthwhile to investigate alternative representations of image information, such as
operating in different colour spaces or explicitly decomposing the input into low- and high-frequency
components within the LLIE module. Such approaches may enable a more principled treatment of
light irradiation, which has been shown to be an effective strategy in LLIE tasks.
5.3 Final Thoughts
The proposed framework is intended to establish a foundation for future advancements in seman-
tic segmentation under challenging and highly variable illumination conditions. Despite recent
progress, the field continues to be constrained by the limited availability of diverse and represen-
tative datasets, which significantly hinders the development of robust and generalizable methods.
The scenarios explored in this study reflect realistic and often critical environments, including safety-
sensitive missions where reliability is paramount. In such contexts, the deployment of models that
are not only performant but also transparent with respect to their limitations becomes essential. This
is particularly relevant for applications such as survivor identification, where the consequences of
failure are profound. Therefore, advancing DL-based approaches in this domain is not solely a mat-
ter of improving accuracy, but also of fostering trust, robustness, and accountability in systems de-
signed to operate under uncertainty.
