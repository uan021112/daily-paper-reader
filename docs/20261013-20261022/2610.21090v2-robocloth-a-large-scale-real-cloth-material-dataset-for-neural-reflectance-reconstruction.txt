## Abstract 

We present RoboCloth, a dataset of 500 real cloth materi-als captured under flexible, calibrated camera and lighting configurations, where both are robotically controlled during acquisition. Each material is imaged in approximately 580 high-dynamic range frames spanning a wide view-light hemi-sphere; camera poses are refined via structure-from-motion and aligned to the robot frame, yielding per-material sparse surface geometry and structured per-point reflectance obser-vations ready for neural material training. We demonstrate the value of this data through a two-stage neural reconstruc-tion pipeline: Stage 1 trains a shared MLP decoder on our corpus to learn cloth material priors; Stage 2 then freezes the decoder and fits a dense 2048 ×2048 latent texture to each target material. Evaluating on held-out materials from our own dataset and on the independent UBO2014 benchmark, we show that a decoder trained on our captures outperforms decoders trained on existing measured reflectance datasets and analytic PBR fitting, transferring to unseen acquisition

## 1. Introduction 

Cloth is challenging to render faithfully, as real fabrics ex-hibit spatially varying reflectance driven by fiber microstruc-ture, weave geometry, surface roughness, and anisotropic scattering — properties that interact nonlinearly with view-ing and illumination direction and vary continuously across the surface. Recovering dense, shader-ready material repre-sentations from real observations is a prerequisite for photo-realistic cloth rendering in physically based pipelines, with direct applications in virtual try-on, digital fashion, and vi-sual effects. Recent work has shown that learning a shared 

neural BRDF decoder across many materials enables gen-eralizable reflectance estimation without committing to any analytical BRDF parameterization [25, 28]. In this paradigm, a decoder maps compact per-point latent codes to reflectance values queryable by a path tracer [24, Ch. 14]; given a new 1arXiv:2610.21090v2 [cs.CV] 19 Oct 2026 material, a dense latent grid is optimized against sparse observations with the decoder frozen, producing spatially varying reflectance maps that plug directly into a physically based rendering pipeline. The decoder amortizes reflectance estimation across materials: a prior trained on sufficiently diverse and well-covered captures can generalize to unseen fabrics and recover dense representations from limited mea-surements, a capability that neither single material overfitting nor purely analytical models can offer. The limiting factor is training data. Learning a gener-alizing decoder for cloth requires captures that simultane-ously satisfy three conditions: real measured appearance to avoid real-to-synth domain gaps, diversity in the captured materials to support generalization, and dense+flexible view-light coverage—directions sampled freely across both hemi-spheres rather than from a preset fixed hardware layout—to enable reliable reflectance-geometry disentanglement. Ex-isting resources do not combine real cloth diversity with flexible view-light coverage at scale; see Table 1. Most large-scale datasets of real materials are not multi-view: each surface is observed from only one or two camera posi-tions [18, 48]. Multi-view alternatives are either not spatially varying [5, 19], or cover fewer than a hundred materials [41]. The closest resource to ours, UBOFAB19 [21], provides 378 real fabrics with multi-view, spatially varying measurements, but its view and light directions are dictated by a preset fixed hardware layout rather than sampled freely across both hemispheres, which bounds the angular coverage of the view-light domain. The cross-dataset results in Section 5.3 show the performance drop caused by this insufficient view-light coverage. To fulfill these requirements, we present RoboCloth, a system designed to capture a dataset of 500 materials. Our system comprises two robot arms carrying an HDR camera and a calibrated LED area light, together with a motorized turntable, yielding ≈ 580 HDR images per material; see Fig-ure 1. View and light directions can be sampled freely across both hemispheres, rather than being constrained to a fixed set of hardware-defined configurations. While approximate light and camera positions are controlled by the robot API, we recover accurate poses and sparse surface geometry via COLMAP [31]. Our dataset is not merely a larger image collection; it directly targets a downstream task: recovering dense, shader-ready material representations from real cloth captures for a path-traced pipeline. To this end, we train a neural BRDF de-coder on our data and evaluate it by fitting a dense per-pixel material map (a latent texture) to each held-out material, measuring reconstruction accuracy and appearance under path-traced rendering, and comparing against classical PBR fitting [2] and decoders trained on existing material datasets. A controlled ablation over dataset scale quantifies its contri-bution to downstream generalization.

## 2. Related Work 

We group prior work into four categories: measured re-flectance and material datasets, inverse-rendering and relight-ing datasets, neural material representations, and calibrated capture systems. 

Measured reflectance and material datasets. Classical BRDF databases such as MERL [19] (100 isotropic mate-rials) provide dense per-sample reflectance but no spatial variation across the surface. Denser angular collections are either not spatially varying or fixed to specialized hard-ware: the UTIA anisotropic BRDF database [5] (150 ma-terials, 96 fabrics) stores one spatially-averaged BRDF per material rather than a per-point SVBRDF. Although its ac-quisition setup uses actuated camera and light arms, their motion is constrained to fixed-radius goniometric trajecto-ries rather than arbitrary Cartesian poses, and all materi-als share the same uniform 81 ×81 directional grid. The UBO2014 BTF database [41] (84 materials, 22,801 images each) captures per-point appearance only under a fixed dome. Among cloth-specific resources, UBOFAB19 [21] provides 378 fabric materials acquired with a commercial X-Rite 2TAC7 scanner under a preset fixed hardware layout, provid-ing at most 768 calibrated HDR measurements per material and Ward anisotropic SVBRDF fits in AxF format. Tang et al. [33] capture woven-fabric BSDFs from a reflection-transmission photo pair. UMat [29] provides 2,000 real tex-tiles for single-image high-resolution material capture, while OpenSVBRDF [18] scales to 1,000 near-planar SVBRDFs using a 16,384-LED rig and only two camera views ( ≈193 

raw HDR images, ≈15 min per sample), but is not cloth-specific. Real-SVBRDF [1] covers 80 real materials across 462 capture sets with only 12 images per set. Across these measured resources, none targets a large cloth corpus with flexible view-light coverage (see Kavoosighafi et al. [11] for a broader survey). Moreover, none of these works compares against other datasets by evaluating how well a prior trained on its data generalizes to new materials. On the synthetic side, MatSynth [38] (4,069 PBR materials) offers strong pre-training but does not replace real, calibrated cloth captures. 

Inverse rendering and relighting datasets. Several datasets provide real object-level multi-illumination bench-marks useful for evaluating inverse rendering and relight-ing [13, 16, 23, 37, 49]. But these target object-centric 

capture, and are neither designed for learning cloth mate-rial priors, nor for evaluating dense shader-ready material reconstruction from near-planar samples. 

Neural material representations. NeuMIP [14] repre-sents materials as multiscale neural feature textures and has become a standard backbone for learned material models, with subsequent work extending neural reflectance repre-sentations through hierarchical architectures, efficient neu-ral sampling, real-time appearance models, and spherically distributed primitives [4, 42, 44–46]. Unified neural BTF encoding [28], building on neural BTF compression [26], trains a decoder shared across materials and supports encod-ing previously unseen materials. DeepBRDF [9] learns a latent space of measured BRDFs, and neural BRDF repre-sentations with importance sampling [32] make learned re-flectance directly usable in path tracers. HyperBRDF [7] and differentiable rendering pipelines [3, 15] show that recon-struction quality is tightly coupled to training data diversity and angular coverage. PhotoMat [48] trains a strong gener-ator from 12,000 single-flash real photos, underscoring the value of real captured data. Generative Neural Materials [25], diffusion-based generators, and single-image capture meth-ods [29, 39, 40, 47] all depend on large, diverse, and realistic training corpora. Despite this progress, real captured datasets with cloth-specific diversity and flexible view-light coverage remain limited. 

Calibrated material capture systems. Capture systems trade off hardware, coverage, and throughput: dense domes [41] and LED arrays [18] give rich angular sam-pling but need dedicated, non-portable hardware. Robot-based gonio-reflectometers such as ARGon3 [8] automate dense angular measurements of individually mounted sam-ples. Closest to our design is the BTF scanner of Rainer et al. [27], which moves a camera and an LED light on two arms around a static sample and acquires a dense 40 ×40 

view-light scan in about 11 hours per sample; its measure-ments were not publicly released. Commercial scanners [21] provide high-quality fabric measurements under a preset fixed hardware layout. Lightweight rigs [1] reduce hard-ware cost but yield sparse per-sample observations. Our robotic system—two robot arms carrying one HDR camera and one calibrated LED area light, together with a motorized turntable—enables practical cloth acquisition with hundreds of calibrated images per sample under flexible view-light variation, without commitment to a fixed hardware geometry or analytical BRDF model. 

## 3. Dataset 

RoboCloth is a dataset of 500 real cloth materials, each cap-tured under approximately 580 calibrated camera-light con-figurations using a robotic acquisition rig. For each material we release 16-bit linear HDR images, robot-frame-aligned sparse geometry, a structured per-point observation tensor, and the calibration metadata required to reproduce our ren-dering pipeline. The dataset is split into training ( ≈400 

materials) and test ( ≈100 ) partitions and is hosted on the Hugging Face Hub under CC BY 4.0 . Full construction details and ablations are provided in Section 9. 

3.1. Acquisition 

As illustrated in Figure 1, our robotic rig consists of two UFACTORY XARM 6 robot arms (one carrying the cam-era, one carrying an LED light source) and a ferromagnetic turntable inside a matte-black enclosure. The turntable pro-vides a third controlled degree of freedom: rotating the sample re-orients it beneath the fixed camera-light pair, so each arm configuration is reused across several distinct sample-relative azimuths, effectively tripling the diversity of camera-light configurations reachable without moving the arms. Each cloth sample is fixed flat by corner magnets (stan-dardizing the flattening procedure across operators), and a ChArUco fiducial [6, 30] mounted beside the sample serves for both offline calibration and per-session drift checks. The sample width and length are recorded at mounting time and later used to crop the valid material region from the sparse point cloud. Each material is acquired in a single auto-mated session lasting approximately 20 minutes. At each of the ≈580 steps, the system records one raw Bayer HDR frame together with the robot-logged camera pose, light pose, and turntable angle, giving a densely paired image-and-metadata stream with no manual annotation. Each pixel then contributes a per-point radiance observation: In Section 3.2 we describe how to place surface points, cameras, and lights 3in a common metric world frame, and in Section 3.3 we calibrate the observed radiance to physical units.

## 4. Neural Reconstruction Pipeline 

We evaluate our dataset through a two-stage neural recon-struction pipeline, as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3. Stage 1 trains a shared MLP decoder on the full training corpus to learn a material prior. Stage 2 freezes that decoder and fits a dense per-material latent texture to each unseen cloth. This freeze-and-fit scheme is similar to Generative Neural Materials [25], which trains a shared decoder on synthetic materials and fits per-material latent textures against the frozen decoder to build training data for generative mod-eling. The decoder architecture, renderer, and the Stage 2 training step (losses, optimizer, and training schedule) are held fixed across all comparisons. 

4.1. Differentiable Renderer 

Both stages share the same differentiable single-bounce [10] path tracer, adopting the PyTorch differentiable path-tracer implementation from FIPT [43]. Indirect illumination is omitted, as the sample sits in a matte-black enclosure with no secondary radiant sources within the camera’s dynamic range. ## 5. Experiments 

To measure the value of different training corpora for Stage 1, we use a controlled experimental protocol: the decoder ar-chitecture, renderer, and Stage 2 procedure are held fixed across all comparisons, so differences in reconstruction qual-ity are directly attributable to the training data. We target two key questions: Does training on our captured dataset produce a stronger material prior than training on existing reflectance datasets? Does the learned prior transfer to unseen cloth materials from an independent capture sys-tem? The complete implementation of both stages is pub-licly available at https://github.com/theialab/ robocloth , so that every experiment reported below can be reproduced. 

5.1. Datasets and Baselines 

We compare three Stage 1 training sources, all consumed through the same auto-decoder loop: RoboCloth (our real robotic captures), Bonn [21] (the UBOFAB19 fab-ric SVBRDFs), and MERL [19] (real, measured isotropic BRDFs). Per-source statistics and loading details are in Sec-tion 10.1 and Table 5. 

Baseline (PBR). As a non-neural reference we fit spatially-varying Disney principled BRDF [2] texture maps with the same renderer and optimizer as Stage 2. The model sums an anisotropic GGX specular lobe, a Burley diffuse lobe, and a sheen lobe; the optimized parameters are 2048 ×2048 textures of base color, ambient occlusion, roughness, metallic, IOR, anisotropy (strength and rotation), specular tint, sheen, and sheen tint, plus a normal map, at the same resolution as the neural latent grid. The full BRDF and its component definitions are given in Section 11. 

5.2. Implementation Details 

The decoder architecture, the direction encoding, the per-point and dense representations, the two-stage optimizers, and the full training and fitting schedules are reported in Sec-tion 12. The complete code for both stages is publicly re-leased, so that all reported experiments can be reproduced and fine-grained technical questions can be answered directly from the implementation.

## 6. Conclusion 

We presented RoboCloth, a dataset of 500 real cloth ma-terials captured under flexible, calibrated camera and light coverage using a robotic acquisition system. Our end-to-end pipeline produces per-material training records — aligned HDR images, sparse surface geometry, and structured ob-servation tensors that feed directly into neural decoder train-ing without manual annotation. Under a controlled two-stage protocol in which only the Stage 1 training data varies, a decoder trained on our captures learns a stronger cloth prior than decoders trained on existing measured reflectance datasets, recovering more faithful held-out appearance and transferring competitively to the UBO2014 benchmark. 

Limitations. The dataset is cloth-specific: the acquisition protocol and evaluation are tailored to near-planar fabric samples and do not generalize to three-dimensional objects or non-textile materials. Acquisition throughput is modest: each material takes ≈20 minutes and needs a human opera-tor to mount and change the sample between sessions. Thus, although in practice we have ≈5000 more cloth samples, throughput is currently limited by the most difficult resource to scale: the graduate student. We are therefore considering a different capture modality for faster acquisition; throughput could also clearly be scaled by adding more robot arms. No-tably, the dataset-size ablation (Figure 5 in Section 8) shows that transfer PSNR still improves at the full corpus: learning has not saturated, and we stopped at 500 materials simply as a round number, given the slow capture. Since a mate-rial prior is available from previous scans, an active-capture agent could select the next-best view-light configuration for each new sample, replacing the ≈580 uniformly scheduled frames with a small adaptive set and directly addressing the throughput bottleneck. Finally, this work performs SVBRDF inversion for planar materials. How to integrate the resulting material priors effectively within a scene-level 

3D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction [12] remains an open research direction.

## 7. Ablation: Grazing-Angle Supervision 

Our captured observations do not cover near-grazing inci-dence ( cos θi ≈ 0), leaving the decoder unconstrained there. The grazing-decay loss of (3) constrains the shape of the contribution fr cos θi to fall off smoothly to zero as θi → 90 ◦,without forcing the raw BRDF fr itself to vanish. We com-pare it against two simpler pseudo-observation losses and a no-grazing baseline. ZeroExact adds a pseudo-observation that pins the raw BRDF to zero at exactly the grazing angle (θi = 90 ◦). NearZeroBRDF pins the raw BRDF to zero over a thin band just below grazing ( cos θi ∈ [0 .005 , 0.03] ). No grazing applies no such supervision. To visualize the learned reflectance we use the Stage-1 BRDF-lobe plot: we select one learned point latent, freeze the decoder, fix the outgoing direction ωo (one colored curve per θo ∈ { 15 , 30 , 45 , 60 }◦), and sweep the incident elevation 

θi from 0◦ (surface normal) to ±90 ◦ (grazing) at a fixed azimuth; the polar radius is the contribution fr cos θi and the dashed line marks the specular direction. Figure 4 shows four representative surface points. Only our loss ((3)) yields lobes that taper smoothly to zero at grazing. ZeroExact fixes only the single, measure-zero point θi = 90 ◦ and leaves its entire neighborhood free, so the contribution balloons outward toward grazing with no smooth transition; forcing fr itself to zero is also unphysical, since the raw BRDF is generally non-zero near grazing — it is the product fr cos θi that must vanish. NearZeroBRDF 

pins fr to zero across a thin near-grazing band but cannot shape the transition between the last supervised observation and the grazing region, leaving non-physical lobes and spikes near ±90 ◦. The no-grazing baseline is unconstrained and shows the same inflation. 

## 8. Ablation: Dataset Size 

We ablate the size of the Stage-1 training corpus to test whether our dataset provides a sufficiently diverse distribu-tion that the learned prior keeps improving as more materials are added. We draw random subsets of 100 , 300 , and 500 

cloth materials and apply the same 0.8/0.2 split to each, yielding 80 , 240 , and 400 training materials respectively (denoted RoboCloth-100/300/500). Every subset trains a decoder for the same number of Stage-1 iterations ( 4.8 × 10 5

steps) under the identical architecture and loss, and is then evaluated with the Stage-2 UBO2014 transfer protocol of Section 5.3: we freeze the decoder and optimize only the per-point latent and the per-channel scale β on the same 12 

UBO2014 materials for 60 epochs. Figure 5 and Table 4 report the mean UBO2014 PSNR over all 12 transfer materials. Transfer PSNR increases monotonically with the training-set size ( 36 .28 → 36 .57 →

36 .76 dB), and the per-material numbers improve almost everywhere: the decoder consistently benefits from more of our data, indicating that the corpus carries genuine ap-pearance diversity rather than redundant samples. The gain also flattens toward the full set ( +0 .29 dB from 80 to 240 

materials versus +0 .19 dB from 240 to 400 ), suggesting that our full corpus is approaching saturation for this transfer task.

9.4. Radiometric Calibration Details 

Demosaicing and quantum-efficiency correction. The Spinnaker sensor outputs a 14-bit Bayer mosaic in RGGB pattern, stored left-aligned in a 16-bit PNG. Both the raw mosaic and the debayered HDR PNG are re-tained on disk. 

Camera factor calibration. The camera factor κ converts pixel values to physical radiance under the linear sensor model. It is measured once per exposure cohort by imaging a near-Lambertian white reference card while sweeping the LED through a fixed polar grid ( linear hemi routine). κ. For our Dataset Nov11 captures, three exposure co-horts {20000 , 20000 , 8000 } μs give factors (κ1, κ 2, κ 3) sat-isfying κ3 = κ2 · (8000 /20000) to within the fit residual.

## 12. Implementation Details 

Full hyperparameters for Stage 1 and Stage 2 are reported in Table 6. This section gives the full implementation. 

Architecture and optimization. The decoder is a four-layer MLP (width 256 , LeakyReLU (0.2), skip connection at layer 2, softplus output); incident and outgoing directions use real spherical harmonics of degree L = 3 and the latent dimension is d = 24 . Stage 1 jointly trains the shared decoder and the per-point parameter tables for all ≈ 400 

materials with 8-bit Adam; Stage 2 freezes the decoder and fits each held-out material with full-precision Adam. Owing to compute and memory limits, every material is trained and fit on a fixed random 10% subsample of its surface points, applied identically to all sources so the per-material budget is equal. In every Stage-2 fit we additionally optimize a per-channel scale β that aligns the frozen decoder to the target capture’s color space and radiometric calibration. 

Per-point optimizable parameters. In Stage 1, each sparse surface point owns three directly optimizable quanti-ties stored as requires grad=True tensors: the latent code zm,j , the surface normal nm,j , and the tangent tm,j .These are updated by the same Adam step as θ, with the learning rate for (n, t) matching that of the latent tables (2 × 10 −3). The Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization is applied in the forward pass and is fully differentiable, so gradients flow back into the unconstrained (n, t) parameters without any projected-gradient step. 

8-bit Adam in Stage 1. Storing the complete per-point latent tables for ∼400 materials with Vm ∈ [10 3, 5 × 10 3]

points each requires keeping ∼400 × 5000 × 24 × 4 bytes ≈

192 GB of optimizer state in full precision. We use 

bitsandbytes 8-bit Adam to reduce this by 4×, fitting all optimizer states on a single node without offloading. 

Round-robin material sampling. At each Stage 1 iter-ation, the active material set is rotated by a deterministic round-robin so that every material is touched within a fixed window of iterations. Within the active set, (point, view) pairs are sampled without replacement until the batch of 

5 × 10 5 rays is filled. 

Validation and checkpointing. At validation time, emitter samples are accumulated in chunks of Schunk = 2 to bound peak memory. We checkpoint every 10 4 steps and select the checkpoint at which the Bonn validation loss plateaus (≈ 4.8 × 10 5 steps) as the Stage 2 initialization for all cross-dataset comparisons.
