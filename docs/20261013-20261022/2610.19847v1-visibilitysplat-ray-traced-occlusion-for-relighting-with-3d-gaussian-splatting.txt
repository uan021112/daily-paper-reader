ABSTRACT
We address inverse rendering with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by introducing explicit visibility modeling for
relighting as well as material and light decomposition. Our approach augments the standard rasterized 3DGS
pipeline with a sparse visibility oracle that estimates transmittance-weighted occlusion for Gaussian primitives
from sampled lighting directions. This oracle is implemented using a point-based ray tracer accelerated by a
bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) and queried sparsely within the inverse-rendering objectives, while the original
3DGS loop is retained for training efficiency, interactive visualization, and tooling compatibility. Shading follows
a physically based rendering (PBR) formulation with a principled microfacet bidirectional reflectance distribution
function (BRDF) and low-resolution high-dynamic-range (HDR) environment maps. We evaluate our method on
the TensoIR synthetic dataset against baselines based on neural radiance fields (NeRFs), tensor representations,
and 3DGS. The results show competitive performance and improvements over several earlier baselines for novel-
view synthesis (NVS), albedo reconstruction, and relighting. Ablations indicate that explicit visibility modeling
is an important contributor to relighting and material-illumination disentanglement, while pure PBR supervision
can favor appearance fitting in NVS. Overall, the findings suggest that sparse, point-based visibility queries effec-
tively complement rasterized splatting for inverse rendering while preserving the practical advantages of the 3DGS
ecosystem.

INTRODUCTION
Inverse rendering (IR) seeks to recover geometry,
reflectance, and illumination from images by inverting
the image formation process.
Neural radiance fields
(NeRFs) [25] enable high-fidelity novel-view synthesis
(NVS) and have been extended toward relightable
decompositions under unknown illumination (e.g.,
NeRFactor [41] and TensoIR [18]), but their implicit
volumetric formulation is costly to train and render.
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [19] instead optimizes
explicit point primitives via differentiable rasterization,
enabling real-time rendering and practical tooling, and
has recently been adopted for IR (e.g., 3D Gaussian
Splatting for Inverse Rendering (GS-IR) [22] and
Gaussian Inverse Rendering (GIR) [30]).

RELATED WORK
2.1
Radiance Fields
The original NeRF method [25] introduced neural volu-
metric representations for NVS, with subsequent work
improving anti-aliasing and efficiency via scale-aware
integration and more explicit encodings [2–4, 7,12, 13,
27]. 3DGS [19] further shifts computation to explicit
anisotropic Gaussian primitives rendered by a custom
rasterizer, enabling real-time performance. Geometry-
oriented variants [16,37] and BVH-based Gaussian ray
tracing [26] broaden the representation toward editing
and secondary-ray effects.
2.2
Inverse Rendering
Neural fields for inverse rendering:
IR aims to
recover geometry, materials, and illumination from
images and is intrinsically ill-posed. Early NeRF-based
methods reduced ambiguity with simplifying assump-
tions: PhySG [39] represents bidirectional reflectance
distribution functions (BRDFs) and lighting using
spherical Gaussians (SGs) but omits complex visibility
and indirect transport. NeRFactor [41] factorizes shape
and reflectance under unknown lighting.
InvRen-
der [42] derives spatially varying indirect illumination
directly from the learned radiance field to improve
albedo recovery.
Ref-NeRF [31] improves specular
handling via a reflected-radiance parameterization that
also yields better normals. To raise physical fidelity
without losing efficiency, TensoIR [18] builds on
tensor-factorized fields to support on-the-fly visibility
queries and second-bounce effects during reconstruc-
tion.
Complementary surface-aligned fields stabilize
geometry and normals for IR pipelines [32, 35]. More
recent work targets illumination modeling itself:
ENVIDR [21] learns a neural environment for shiny
scenes. NeRF-as-Emitter [23] replaces the distant-light
assumption with a near-field light NeRF, improv-
ing accuracy under spatially varying illumination,
and NeILF++ [38] jointly learns incident-light and
outgoing-radiance fields with inter-reflections to better
disentangle material and lighting.
Gaussian splatting for inverse rendering: Adopting
3DGS for IR leverages real-time forward rendering but
introduces new challenges: splats lack native surface
normals, and forward splatting does not natively model
occlusion or multi-bounce light. GS-IR [22] addresses
these by regularizing per-Gaussian normals and baking
visibility for secondary effects while recovering geom-
etry, spatially varying bidirectional reflectance distribu-
tion functions (SVBRDFs), and environment lighting
under unknown illumination. Subsequent work deep-
ens physical correctness: GaussianShader [17] esti-
mates robust normals and per-splat shading for reflec-
tive scenes. DeferredGS [33] decouples geometry from
lighting and performs deferred shading for editable re-
lighting and Ref-Gaussian [34] targets glossy reflec-
tions with a specialized deferred pipeline. Beyond per-
fect mirrors, SSS-GS [10] introduces subsurface scat-
tering to a 3DGS framework using an explicit surface
plus an implicit scattering component. To improve gen-
eral relighting, RNG [11] conditions radiance on view
and light directions with shadow cues and depth re-
finement, while BiGS [24] unifies surface and volu-
metric materials via bidirectional spherical harmonics
(SH), supporting near- and distant-light setups. Finally,
GI-GS [8] explicitly decomposes direct/indirect com-
ponents and uses lightweight path tracing on a G-buffer
for multi-bounce global illumination, bringing 3DGS-
based IR closer to physically faithful relighting at in-

Physically Based Rendering
We follow a physically based rendering (PBR) formula-

NeRFactor [41], and GS-IR [22]. Image formation is
expressed by the surface rendering equation with geo-

BRDF, while geometry, reflectance, and illumination
are represented by differentiable fields and optimized
from multi-view images. Formally,

In practice, illumination is computed by evaluating the

geometry, visibility, and indirect effects are estimated.
For example, TensoIR introduces efficient online visi-
bility and secondary shading, NeRFactor learns a vis-
ibility field, and GS-IR attaches BRDF parameters to
Gaussians with an occlusion approximation.
3.3
Environmental Lighting
Accurate recovery of incident illumination is essential
for robust IR. We model distant lighting as a high-
dynamic-range (HDR) spherical environment map pa-
rameterized in equirectangular coordinates [9]. Unlike
SGs or mixtures of SGs, this image-based representa-
tion preserves high-frequency angular detail and thus
supports sharp cast shadows without requiring many
analytic lobes.
Each HDR environment-map texel stores radiance L,
and we optimize these values directly during training
with solid-angle compensation to account for the non-
uniform texel area of equirectangular maps.
Because reflectance acts as an angular low-pass filter,
the recoverable illumination bandwidth is limited by
the object’s BRDF lobes [29]. Accordingly, we use a
compact environment-map parameterization, with con-
crete settings summarized in the implementation details
paragraph.

3.5
Point-Based Ray Tracing
In our pipeline, the ray tracer is not used as a replace-
ment renderer. It serves as a sparse visibility oracle for
evaluating transmittance along sampled light directions.
The 3DGS rasterizer is a highly optimized, tile-based
renderer: for each screen-space tile it collects all pro-
jected splats overlapping the tile, sorts them in front-
to-back order, and evaluates color and opacity via el-
liptical weighted average (EWA) splatting [43] in that
order. This design delivers excellent throughput for pri-
mary views but is tied to camera projection and ordered
blending per tile.
A point-based ray tracer allows us to evaluate Gaus-
sian splatting from any point in space in any direction.

multiplication commutes, the hits need not be depth-

in arbitrary order while maintaining correctness for T.

should match the early-out used by the rasterizer to
keep consistency between visibility estimated by ray
tracing and the screen-space renderer. To make the ray
tracer a drop-in companion to standard 3DGS (so we
can reuse pretrained point clouds and the existing ras-

EWA-splatting formulation as the rasterizer. For a ray

by the BVH for that ray into the center pixel of this vir-
tual camera. This yields consistent blending with the
original tile-based renderer while allowing us to drive
the evaluation from rays rather than from a full image
plane.

4.1
Dataset & Metrics
We report experimental results on the synthetic Ten-
soIR dataset [18] for IR of single objects. It contains
four objects, each with reference images rendered under
ten different lighting conditions, along with albedo and
normal maps. We evaluate normal-estimation quality
using mean angular error (MAE). For relighting, NVS,
and albedo reconstruction, we report peak signal-to-
noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity index (SSIM),
and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS)
[40]. Some results of our method for each model can
be seen in Fig. 5.
Following prior work, we compensate for the inherent
albedo-shading scale ambiguity by scaling each pre-
dicted albedo with a single global RGB factor that min-
imizes MSE to the ground truth [1,14,18,20,22,30,41].
We do not apply color correction [5], all reported and
visualized synthetic albedo results therefore use this
scale normalization unless stated otherwise.
To visualize the HDR environment maps, we follow
TensoIR [18] and apply gamma tonemapping to convert
HDR radiance to low-dynamic-range (LDR) values for
display.
NVS results are rendered using PBR with the estimated
material parameters and environment lighting.

We compare our method with previous inverse render-
ing methods, NeRFactor [41], InvRender [42], NVD-
iffrec [28], GS-IR [22], TensoIR [18] and IRGS [15]
on the TensoIR synthetic dataset. All comparisons are
performed using multi-view images captured under un-

Our method is competitive with prior 3DGS-based IR
approaches and improves over several earlier baselines
across NVS, albedo reconstruction, and relighting.
However, IRGS [15] achieves the best overall results
on this benchmark.
These results position Visibil-
itySplat as a direct explicit-visibility baseline that
remains competitive with specialized 3DGS-based
IR methods while retaining the standard rasterized
3DGS pipeline for training and visualization.
We
attribute our performance gains over earlier baselines
primarily to the explicit visibility term: on masked,
single-object scenes the dominant ambiguity is often
cast-shadow visibility and mutual occlusion under
direct environment lighting, while multi-bounce trans-
port is comparatively weak. In this regime, sparse but
physically grounded transmittance queries already pro-
vide a large part of the benefit for disentangling albedo
and lighting.
Compared to IRGS, our lightweight
separation between rasterized splatting for efficient
training/preview and sparse ray-based visibility queries
offers a favorable practicality-accuracy trade-off, but
still leaves room for improvement in terms of absolute
reconstruction quality.


Our main source of error is imperfect surface recon-
struction rather than instability of the occlusion ray
tracer. In particular, inaccurate normals lead to visi-
bly curved shadow boundaries and local relighting ar-
tifacts (see Fig. 4b). This interpretation is consistent
with our higher mean angular error compared to com-
peting methods. Despite these geometric limitations,
our relighting quality in PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS re-
mains competitive with state-of-the-art approaches.
At the measured 800×800 evaluation resolution on an
NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, final PBR relighting takes
about 0.83 s/image, so the current implementation is
not real-time.
Forward relighting time is dominated
by explicit visibility queries (about 55%) and discrete
BRDF/environment-map integration (about 44%), the
remaining time is spent on standard 3D Gaussian splat-
ting and minor overhead. Thus, real-time deployment
would require optimizing both the visibility pass and
the PBR integration.
4.3
Ablation Study
We conduct an ablation study on the components of our
method to validate their effectiveness. We report PSNR,
SSIM, and LPIPS for NVS, albedo reconstruction, and
relighting on the Lego model from the TensoIR Syn-
thetic dataset (Tab.
3), including a PBR-only base-
line without visibility and without opacity-mask loss,
as well as single-component removals.
PBR-only baseline. We disable both the visibility ray
tracer and the opacity-mask loss and optimize with PBR
supervision alone. Interestingly, this baseline yields the
highest NVS PSNR (and lowest LPIPS), though not the
highest SSIM. We attribute this behavior to stronger
appearance fitting, where albedo absorbs more illumi-
nation information and therefore reproduces the train-
ing lighting more easily. However, this entanglement
hurts decomposition: albedo reconstruction and relight-
ing quality both degrade compared to the full model.
Visibility. To isolate the visibility term, we compare
the without visibility variant (with opacity-mask loss re-
tained) against the full model. Reintroducing explicit
visibility improves relighting metrics and yields cleaner
shadow behavior, indicating better separation of mate-
rial and illumination.
Opacity mask loss. As described in the optimization
section, this term mainly acts through improved geo-
metric support for ray-based visibility queries. Com-
paring without opacity loss with the full model shows
lower relighting quality. Removing the opacity-mask
loss also decreases albedo PSNR, while albedo SSIM
and LPIPS remain nearly unchanged.
This suggests
that coarse albedo structure is preserved but radiometric
consistency degrades. Better-defined occluding geome-
try from opacity regularization allows visibility rays to
produce cleaner shadow estimates, which in turn stabi-
lizes relighting.
5
CONCLUSION
We present VisibilitySplat,
an IR approach that
augments 3DGS with a sparse visibility oracle for
transmittance-weighted occlusion. Implemented using
BVH-accelerated point-based ray tracing, the oracle
complements the standard rasterized 3DGS training
and visualization loop while injecting explicit visibility
cues for material-illumination disentanglement.
On
a synthetic benchmark,
we improve over several
baselines in PSNR for NVS, albedo reconstruction, and
relighting, while remaining competitive in SSIM and
LPIPS. Ablations suggest that explicit visibility queries
primarily improve relighting and decomposition qual-
ity, even when a PBR-only variant can score higher on
NVS appearance metrics.
Limitations.
Our evaluation targets single, masked,
static objects, behavior in cluttered or dynamic scenes,
or under imperfect segmentation, is not assessed. The
illumination model accounts for direct lighting only. In-
direct transport (interreflection, subsurface, participat-
ing media) and very high-frequency environment light-
ing are not modeled. Finally, experiments are restricted
to synthetic data, robustness to real captures, complex
materials (e.g., glass, hair, translucency), and sensor
noise remains untested.
Future work. Future extensions include multi-object
and full-scene evaluation, incorporation of indirect
lighting, tighter coupling between splatting and visibil-
ity (e.g., differentiable or cached variants), improved
geometric robustness, and investigations toward real-
time deployment and comprehensive testing on real
datasets.
Code availability. We will release the full implementa-
tion and supporting resources upon publication of this
paper.
