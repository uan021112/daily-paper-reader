Abstract —Modern robotic photography requires understand-ing photographic intent, planning camera motions, and pro-ducing aesthetically pleasing images in complex environments. We present RoboPhoto-VLA, a unified Vision-Language-Action framework that jointly models perception, reasoning, and cam-era control for autonomous robotic photography. RoboPhoto-VLA incorporates PhotoCoT for structured action planning and Aesthetic Direct Preference Optimization (ADPO) to align camera behaviors with human aesthetic preferences. To facilitate training and evaluation, we construct OpenPhotography, a robotic photography dataset with language-guided demonstrations. Ex-tensive real-world experiments demonstrate that RoboPhoto-VLA produces photographs better aligned with human aesthetic preferences while generalizing effectively to unseen subjects, viewpoints, poses, and complex photographic instructions. 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Robotic photography is emerging as an important em-bodied AI application in creative production, event record-ing, and commercial media, where robots are expected to autonomously execute camera movements according to high-level user instructions [1]–[3]. Unlike conventional robotic manipulation [4]–[6], whose objective is successful task execu-tion, robotic photography requires balancing two complemen-tary objectives: accurately understanding the semantic intent of the user while producing photographs that satisfy human aesthetic preferences. Achieving this objective demands not only precise camera control, but also reasoning about ab-stract photographic concepts such as composition, viewpoint, framing, and subject interaction. Developing robots capable of jointly perceiving scenes, interpreting photographic intent, and generating aesthetically pleasing camera behaviors therefore remains a fundamental challenge [7]–[9]. Existing robotic photography systems have explored reference-guided camera control, semantic event photography, and interactive computational photography [10]–[12]. How-ever, these methods are typically built upon modular pipelines that decouple perception, language understanding, and camera control [13]–[15], limiting their ability to reason over abstract photographic intent and adapt to open-ended instructions, unseen subjects, and dynamic shooting environments. Recent Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models address this limita-tion by unifying perception, reasoning, and action within a single policy [16]–[21]. II. RELATED WORKS 

Recent Vision–Language Models (VLMs) have achieved remarkable progress in multimodal understanding by combin-ing pretrained vision encoders with large language models, enabling strong performance on visual reasoning, captioning, and question answering tasks [22]–[27]. Building upon these advances, Vision–Language–Action (VLA) models integrate perception, language, and robot control into a unified policy, allowing pretrained VLMs to directly predict robot actions for embodied manipulation and long-horizon planning [18], [28]– [33]. Representative models such as RT-2-X and OpenVLA demonstrate impressive generalization across robotic manipu-lation tasks but are primarily designed for task execution rather than photography-oriented reasoning and aesthetic decision-making. Robotic photography has traditionally focused on camera control, viewpoint selection, framing, and target track-ing through rule-based or modular pipelines [10]–[12]. Al-though recent systems incorporate language understanding and scene perception, they generally lack unified reasoning over visual observations and abstract photographic intent, as well as explicit optimization toward human aesthetic prefer-ences. In contrast, our work formulates robotic photography as an aesthetic-aware Vision–Language–Action problem. Built upon a unified VLA framework, RoboPhoto-VLA introduces photography-oriented reasoning and preference optimization to bridge abstract photographic intent with executable camera actions, enabling aesthetically aligned robotic photography. III. METHOD 

We formulate robotic photography as an embodied vision– language–action (VLA) learning problem. Given an RGB observation I, a natural-language photography instruction x,and a camera action trajectory A = {a1, · · · , a T }, the ob-jective is to learn a policy πθ (A|I, x ), that not only exe-cutes feasible camera motions but also produces photographs aligned with human aesthetic preferences. To this end, we propose RoboPhoto-VLA, which extends a general-purpose VLA policy with photography-oriented reasoning and prefer-ence optimization. The overall framework consists of three components: an OpenVLA backbone for end-to-end policy learning, PhotoCoT for structured photographic reasoning, and Aesthetical Direct Preference Optimization (ADPO) for aesthetic alignment. 

A. OpenVLA-based Policy 

We adopt OpenVLA-7B as the backbone policy due to its strong embodied reasoning capability. Following OpenVLA, the policy consists of a dual visual encoder, a lightweight projector, and a large language model (LLM). Given an observation image, visual features are extracted by SigLIP and DINOv2, zv = fv (I), while the language instruction is encoded as zl = fl(x). The projected visual tokens and language tokens are concatenated and processed by the LLM to obtain a joint representation h = fLLM ([ zv , z l]) , from which the policy autoregressively predicts the camera trajectory 

A ∼ πθ (A|I, x ). Following OpenVLA, each continuous action dimension is independently discretized into 256 bins according to the 1% and 99% quantiles of the training distribution and represented as language tokens, allowing robot control to be optimized using the standard next-token prediction objec-tive. Unlike OpenVLA, RoboPhoto-VLA explicitly introduces photography-oriented reasoning and aesthetic preference learn-ing, enabling the policy to optimize photographic quality rather than only task completion. 

B. Photography-Oriented Chain-of-Thought 

General-purpose VLA models directly predict actions from observations, making it difficult to interpret abstract photo-graphic instructions. We therefore introduce PhotoCoT , which decomposes policy learning into photographic grounding and camera planning. Given an observation and instruction, Photo-CoT first generates a grounding representation g = fg (I, x ) = 

{o, v, c, s }, where o, v, c, and s denote the target subject, viewpoint, composition, and scene constraints, respectively. In-stead of directly producing actions, the policy first predicts an intermediate reasoning sequence r = fr (g) = {r1, · · · , r K },which explicitly describes the multi-step camera adjustment process. The final camera trajectory is then generated as 

A = fa(r).

C. Aesthetic Direct Preference Optimization 

Although supervised fine-tuning enables reliable camera control, it does not explicitly optimize photographic aesthetics. We therefore formulate aesthetic alignment as a trajectory-level preference learning problem. For each observation– instruction pair (I, x ), multiple candidate camera trajectories are executed to capture corresponding images. These images are evaluated using a multimodal aesthetic assessment model, and the trajectories with higher and lower aesthetic scores are used to construct preference pairs (y+, y −). We initialize preference optimization from the PhotoCoT-supervised policy. The reference policy πref is a frozen copy of this policy, while

LADPO = −E [w log σ(∆)] . (5) Finally, RoboPhoto-VLA is trained using a two-stage paradigm. We first perform supervised fine-tuning with Photo-CoT reasoning traces to learn photography-oriented grounding and camera planning. The resulting policy is subsequently op-timized using ADPO to align camera trajectories with human aesthetic preferences. The overall objective is

A. Experimental Setup 

All experiments are conducted on a UR-series robotic arm equipped with an Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D camera. The balancing coefficient λ is set to 0.1 based on validation performance, balancing camera-control capability and aesthetic alignment. OpenPhotography contains 600 demonstrations covering 42 instructions, diverse subjects, viewpoints, camera heights, and human poses. Each demonstration includes an RGB observation, an instruction, a manually verified Qwen3-VL-generated PhotoCoT trace [34], a camera trajectory, and the resulting photograph. The demonstrations are split into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 6:2:2. For OOD evaluation, samples are further divided into in-distribution and OOD subsets at a ratio of 7:3, with all OOD samples excluded from training and preference optimization. Cross-subject evaluation separately considers unseen single-person and multi-person scenes; cross-view evaluation uses 45 ◦, 90 ◦,and 135 ◦ viewpoints; and cross-posture evaluation considers unseen human poses. For preference learning, multiple SFT-policy trajectories are executed and their photographs are scored by a multimodal aesthetic assessment model [35]. After removing failed or ambiguous comparisons, 200 trajectory-level preference pairs are retained, with the higher- and lower-scoring trajectories labeled y+ and y−, respectively. We compare RoboPhoto-VLA with three representative general-purpose VLA models (RT-2-X, π0, and OpenVLA) and two representative robotic photography systems (ANSEL and Snapbot). The VLA baselines are fine-tuned on OpenPho-tography using their official implementations, while ANSEL and Snapbot are evaluated using their original photography pipelines. Following prior work, we report three complemen-tary metrics. Quality Score (QS) [36] measures overall image quality, including sharpness, exposure, and framing. Aesthetic Score (AS) [37] evaluates photographic composition and visual attractiveness using a pretrained aesthetic assessment model. Instruction–Image Alignment (IIA) [38] computes CLIP sim-ilarity between the generated image and the corresponding language instruction. All reported numbers are averaged over the testing set, and higher values indicate better performance. 

B. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods 

Table I reports the comparison with representative VLA models and robotic photography systems. RoboPhoto-VLA consistently achieves the best performance across all cross-subject, cross-view, and cross-posture settings. Compared with general-purpose VLAs (RT-2-X, π0, and OpenVLA), our method significantly improves both aesthetic quality (AS) and instruction alignment (IIA), demonstrating the importance of photography-oriented reasoning and preference alignment. Compared with traditional photobots (ANSEL and Snapbot), RoboPhoto-VLA further produces higher-quality images while preserving accurate instruction following. The largest gains are observed in multi-person scenes and challenging viewpoints, where structured PhotoCoT reasoning and ADPO jointly en-able robust subject grounding, multi-step camera planning, and aesthetically consistent image capture. C. Cross-Domain Generalization 

We further evaluate out-of-distribution generalization on unseen subjects, viewpoints, and human poses. As shown in Table I, existing VLA models exhibit noticeable performance degradation under all distribution shifts, particularly for un-seen identities and viewpoints. In contrast, RoboPhoto-VLA consistently maintains high QS, AS, and IIA scores across all settings. The results indicate that PhotoCoT learns trans-ferable photographic reasoning beyond subject appearance, while ADPO improves the robustness of camera planning and aesthetic composition under diverse real-world scenarios.

Combining PhotoCoT and ADPO consistently achieves the best overall performance. V. CONCLUSION 

We present RoboPhoto-VLA, a unified vision–language– action framework for autonomous robotic photography that bridges high-level photographic intent and low-level camera control. By combining PhotoCoT with aesthetic preference optimization via ADPO, our framework enables robots to rea-son about composition, plan coherent camera trajectories, and capture images aligned with human visual preferences within a unified policy. Extensive real-world experiments across di-verse subjects, viewpoints, and poses demonstrate consistent improvements over general-purpose VLA models and existing robotic photography systems. We also introduce OpenPhotog-raphy, an open benchmark for advancing research in embodied robotic photography. We hope this work establishes robotic photography as a valuable testbed for embodied intelligence and advances research on aesthetic reasoning, human–robot creative collaboration, and preference-aware action learning.
