## Abstract 

Generated videos often lose geometric consistency under camera motion: structures warp under viewpoint change, parallax becomes inconsistent, and smooth camera movement may collapse into shot-like transitions. We argue that this failure is largely a data problem. In web-scale video corpora, most visible motion comes from dynamic objects captured by static or weakly moving cameras, leaving only sparse supervision for viewpoint-induced appearance changes. To address this, we propose Video Geometry Corrector ( VGC ), a post-hoc video-to-video model trained on synthetic paired supervision. Starting from real videos with diverse camera motion and pose annotations, we construct VG-Pairs with a synthetic paired-supervision data generation pipeline. Each video clip is treated as a geometry-consistent target and paired with a distorted counterpart synthesized by a pretrained image-to-video inpainting model using trajectory-adaptive keyframes. VGC learns to map the distorted input to the geometry-consistent target without modifying the source generator, and uses optical flow as an auxiliary cue to help disentangle camera motion from object motion. Experiments on pose-conditioned and prompt-driven benchmarks show that VGC improves geometric consistency across multiple source generators, and scaling to a larger backbone further extends the framework toward more general video refinement.

## 1 Introduction 

Recent video generation models can synthesize visually compelling clips from text, images, or video conditions [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. However, their apparent ability to behave as world simulators often breaks down once the camera moves. Rigid structures may warp under viewpoint changes, parallax may become inconsistent, scene layout may drift across frames, and continuous camera motion may collapse into shot-like transitions. These failures are especially severe under large viewpoint changes, showing that current models often generate plausible frame sequences but fail to maintain a stable 3D scene layout. We argue that camera-motion-induced geometric distortion is largely a data problem. In web-scale video corpora, most visible motion comes from dynamic objects captured by static or weakly moving cameras, leaving only sparse supervision for viewpoint-induced appearance changes. As a result, camera motion and object motion are easily entangled: when asked to generate a moving-camera shot, a model may explain the visual change by deforming scene structure rather than maintaining a stable 3D layout. To address this problem, existing works have largely followed two directions. The first makes the generator more geometry-aware by introducing 3D supervision or spatial priors during training [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. However, as foundation models evolve rapidly and are often accessible only through checkpoints or inference APIs, modifying the source model is not always feasible. The second direction applies post-hoc video refinement [7, 14, 15]. These methods primarily target pixel-level restoration, such as super-resolution, denoising, and temporal smoothing, thereby improving appearance without explicitly correcting the underlying geometric distortion. Post-hoc geometry correction, which requires reasoning about 3D structure under camera motion, remains comparatively underexplored. The key bottleneck is the training data. Training a geometry corrector requires paired supervision: a geometry-distorted video and a geometry-consistent target that follows the same camera trajectory. Such pairs are nearly impossible to collect in the real world. To address this challenge, we construct the VG-Pairs dataset using a paired-supervision data generation pipeline. Starting from real videos with diverse camera motion and annotated poses, we synthesize distorted counterparts using Wan 2.1-Fun-InP, a pretrained image-to-video inpainting model based on Wan [4]. Instead of conditioning only on the first and last frames, we adopt a trajectory-adaptive keyframe strategy that inserts additional keyframes at abrupt pose changes, enabling stable generation of shorter subsegments that are later stitched together. This process produces paired videos with identical semantics and similar camera motion, but different geometric fidelity. Built on VG-Pairs , we propose Video Geometry Corrector ( VGC ), a video-to-video diffusion model that transforms geometry-distorted videos into structurally coherent outputs. VGC restores stable scene geometry without regenerating videos from scratch, preserving scene content and object appearance. To further disentangle camera motion from object motion, we incorporate optical flow as an auxiliary structural cue. Optical flow separates globally coherent camera-induced motion from local object dynamics, helping the model determine what to correct and what to preserve. Although our main focus is correcting geometry in static scenes under camera motion, scaling VGC 

to larger-capacity models naturally extends its capability to dynamic videos containing human and object motion, moving toward general video refinement. This flexibility distinguishes VGC from prior geometry-focused approaches [16, 10], which typically focus on static environments. The main contributions of this work are: • We identify a critical gap in post-hoc video refinement and attribute camera-induced geometric distortions to insufficient supervision for viewpoint-induced appearance changes. • We address the paired-data bottleneck with VG-Pairs , a synthetic paired-supervision dataset built from real videos with camera motion and their trajectory-aware geometric distortions. • We propose VGC , a post-hoc video-to-video geometry corrector augmented with optical flow cues, demonstrating strong structural restoration, cross-generator transferability, and scalability to dynamic videos. 

## 2 Related Work 

Geometry-consistent video generation. Recent video diffusion models such as Sora [1], CogVideoX [2], HunyuanVideo [3], and Wan [4] achieve strong visual quality, yet maintaining 2geometric consistency under camera motion remains challenging. Most existing approaches address this issue at the generator or training level. Some methods introduce explicit geometric supervision during training. GeoVideo [9] incorporates depth prediction and cross-frame consistency losses. Geometry Forcing [10] aligns intermediate features with pretrained 3D foundation models [17], and Epipolar Geometry Improves Video Generation Models [11] introduces epipolar constraints during fine-tuning. Other approaches improve geometry through post-training alignment or reward opti-mization. VideoGPA [16] derives preference signals from reprojection consistency, VideoREPA [18] transfers physical reasoning from video foundation models, and VGGRPO [19] extends geometry-aware rewards into latent space for dynamic scenes. A third direction incorporates explicit 3D structure into the generation pipeline. World-consistent Video Diffusion [12] maintains a point-cloud memory for spatial consistency, while GEN3C [13] uses geometric priors for camera-controlled video generation. In contrast, VGC frames geometry correction as a post-hoc video-to-video task, avoiding modification of the source generator and enabling transfer across model families. 

Video refinement and enhancement. A separate line of work improves generated or degraded videos through post-processing. Upscale-A-Video [7], SeedVR [14], and SeedVR2 [20] focus on restoration and super-resolution, progressing from multi-step diffusion upscaling to single-step arbitrary-resolution enhancement. Meanwhile, several works [8, 21] introduce new datasets to refine high-resolution video generation. VEnhancer [15] jointly improves spatial and temporal quality using a space-time controller on pretrained diffusion priors. Enhance-A-Video [22] enhances temporal consistency in DiT-based models without retraining. ReVision [23] refines coarse videos using 3D object-centric representations and physical motion priors. Despite improving visual quality, temporal smoothness, and motion plausibility, these methods largely overlook post-hoc correction of scene-level geometric consistency under camera motion. VGC differs by targeting geometry correction, using paired supervision constructed from real videos to repair structural inconsistency while preserving scene content and motion. 

## 3 Method 

Our goal is to restore geometric consistency in generated videos under camera motion. We attribute these failures to insufficient supervision for viewpoint-induced appearance change in existing video data. Instead of modifying each video generation model to learn stronger geometric priors, we formulate geometry correction as a post-hoc video-to-video task and train a separate corrector with paired supervision. This decouples geometry correction from video generator design, making our method applicable across different model families. Our framework consists of two stages. First, we build VG-Pairs by synthesizing paired distorted inputs and geometry-consistent targets from real videos with diverse camera motion and pose annotations. Second, we train a video-to-video diffusion model, VGC , on these pairs to correct geometry. Optical flow extracted from the distorted input serves as an auxiliary cue for disentangling camera motion from scene dynamics. 

3.1 Paired Supervision from Videos with Camera Motion 

The central design choice is to use videos with diverse camera motion not merely as additional training data for a generator, but as a source of correction pairs for a video-to-video task. Hence, we construct VG-Pairs , a dataset of geometry-distorted videos paired with geometry-consistent supervision, using the following data generation pipeline. Let x = ( x1, . . . , x T ) denote a real video clip with camera poses p = ( p1, . . . , p T ), where each pose is represented as pi = ( ti, qi) with camera position ti ∈ R3 and rotation qi ∈ S3. We treat x as the geometry-consistent target and synthesize a distorted counterpart, y, using Wan 2.1-Fun-InP, a pretrained image-to-video inpainting model based on Wan [4]. Given a start frame and an end frame, this model generates the intermediate video that connects them. To make y useful for supervision, it should preserve the scene identity and approximately follow the camera trajectory of x while still exhibiting geometric errors. We therefore select a trajectory-adaptive set of keyframes K = {k1, . . . , k m+1 } with 1 = k1 < · · · < k m+1 = T , and generate one video segment between each consecutive keyframe pair. 3.2 Motion Disentanglement with Optical Flow 

Geometry distortions under camera motion arise in part because current video training data contains rich human and object motion, but relatively sparse supervision for viewpoint-induced appearance change. As a result, video models can confuse camera motion with scene dynamics, and may explain viewpoint change by deforming scene structure instead of preserving a stable 3D layout. Geometry correction is therefore not only a restoration problem, but also a motion disentanglement problem. Optical flow provides a natural auxiliary representation for this purpose. Unlike RGB frames, which entangle appearance and motion, optical flow explicitly represents motion through its location, direction, and magnitude. Camera motion tends to induce a globally coherent motion field, whereas object motion appears as local deviations from that field. We therefore compute forward optical flow between consecutive frames of the distorted input video and use it as an additional conditioning signal for the correction model. Because the flow is computed from the distorted input rather than the target, the same signal is available during both training and inference. Optical flow does not supervise the model directly; instead, it provides motion structure that helps distinguish what should be corrected from what should be preserved.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Implementation details. We construct VG-Pairs from two publicly available datasets, DL3DV [24] and SpatialVID [25]. We select 9K videos from DL3DV, reserve the first 1K for evaluation, and filter 63K videos from SpatialVID that contain large camera motion. To construct paired supervision, we use Wan 2.1-Fun-InP to synthesize geometry-distorted videos from trajectory-adaptive keyframes. Given a start frame and an end frame, Wan 2.1-Fun-InP generates the intermediate video connecting them. For clips with complex camera motion, we apply this model independently to multiple keyframe-bounded subsegments and stitch the generated segments together to form the distorted training input. Because Wan’s VAE temporally compresses the video, keyframes are selected so that each generated subsegment has a frame count compatible with the temporal compression schedule. We use Wan 2.1-1.3B [4] as the backbone of VGC . The model is trained for 10K steps on 32 H100 GPUs with AdamW, using a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5, weight decay 0.01 , and a global batch size of 64. Unless otherwise specified, all training videos contain 81 frames at a resolution of 832 × 480 .Optical flow is estimated with SEA-RAFT [26] and used as an additional conditioning signal. We use Mmax = 4, Rseg = 120 ◦, Dseg = 10 m, Lmin = 15, and α = 1. 

Evaluation datasets and metrics. To evaluate VGC ’s performance, we study two scenarios in which camera motion is introduced. In the first setting, camera motion is specified by camera poses. Following Geometry Forcing [10], we evaluate on RealEstate10K [27] using 100 randomly sampled test videos and DFoT [28] as the source generator. We compare against Geometry Forcing and Geometry Forcing + REPA [29], and report PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS [30], and revisit error (RVE). Following [10], RVE measures long-range consistency under a full camera rotation by comparing the generated view at the end of the trajectory with the corresponding reference view. In the second setting, camera motion is induced by the text prompt. We use three source generators in this setting: CogVideoX-5B I2V, CogVideoX-5B T2V, and CogVideoX 1.5-5B T2V. We randomly sample 200 prompts from the held-out DL3DV test set. Following VideoGPA [16], we reconstruct each generated video independently using Depth Anything 3 [31] and compare its original frames with frames reprojected from the reconstructed 3D geometry to compute PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS [30], MSE, and 3D consistency score (3DCS). Unlike the pose-conditioned setting, these metrics do not require ground-truth video frames. In both settings, VGC is applied as a post-hoc corrector to the outputs of the source generator, demonstrating its flexibility across different video model families.

With increased model capacity, VGC not only corrects geometric distortions under camera motion, but also improves dynamic videos by recovering more plausible human and object geometry. ## 5 Conclusion 

We presented Video Geometry Corrector ( VGC ), a post-hoc video-to-video model for correcting geometric distortions in generated videos under camera motion. We argued that these failures are largely a data problem: current video training data provides limited supervision for viewpoint-induced appearance change, causing camera motion to be entangled with scene dynamics. To address this, we constructed VG-Pairs from real videos with diverse camera motion and trained VGC to map geometry-distorted videos to geometry-consistent targets. We further incorporated optical flow as an auxiliary cue to help distinguish what should be corrected from what should be preserved. Experiments on pose-conditioned and prompt-driven benchmarks show that VGC improves geometric consistency across different source generators and can be flexibly combined with existing geometry-aware methods. With increased model capacity, the same correction framework also extends beyond static-scene geometry toward more general video refinement.
