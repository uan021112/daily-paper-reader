Counting Contributions, Not Views:
Occlusion-Aware Densification
for 3D Gaussian Splatting
Marcel Rogge1,2, René Schuster1,2, and Didier Stricker1,2
1 University Kaiserslautern-Landau – RPTU
2 German Research Center for Artificial Intelligence – DFKI
Abstract. 3D Gaussian Splatting grows a dense scene from a sparse
initialization through adaptive density control (ADC), driven by per-
Gaussian statistics gathered during rendering. These statistics pass a
frustum-based visibility filter blind to occlusion: fully occluded Gaus-
sians still count as observed, diluting the averaged gradient signal and
suppressing densification precisely where geometry is occluded. We cor-
rect this with an occlusion-aware filter that restricts densification statis-
tics to views in which a Gaussian actually contributes, and replace the
criterion’s raw view-count normalizer with an average-contribution one.
Integrated into a StopThePop-based framework, the two together im-
prove every image-quality metric over the occlusion-blind baseline on
occlusion-heavy scenes (up to 6% lower LPIPS), at less than half the
Gaussians a view-count normalizer requires. Under the longer schedules
these large scenes require, the count stays bounded where most criteria
diverge, and on smaller benchmarks our method stays competitive at a
fraction of the competitors’ count.

tion · 3D Reconstruction · Large-Scale Scenes · Novel View Synthesis

Introduction
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [13] represents a milestone in 3D scene recon-
struction and novel-view synthesis. It introduced a large multi-component sys-
tem, with a tile-based rasterizer and adaptive density control (ADC) purpose-
built for the new representation. Follow-up works improve these components
largely in isolation, many focusing on ADC, which is essential for growing a
dense reconstruction from the sparse initialization. However, we find that the
accumulation of the densification statistics at the root of all ADC decisions
has a fundamental flaw: the frustum-based visibility filter that gathers them
tests only whether a Gaussian could appear in a view, not whether it actually
contributes to the rendered image. A fully occluded Gaussian yields zero gradi-
ents, yet the filter still counts its view in the criterion’s denominator, dragging
the average down and stalling densification wherever geometry is occluded. The
resulting under-densification grows with the degree of occlusion and degrades
quality most where high detail and occlusion co-occur. Large, occlusion-heavy
scenes such as multi-room apartments are affected most severely (Fig. 1). We
correct this with an occlusion-aware visibility filter that updates a Gaussian’s
statistics only from views in which it is actually rendered; densification then
reaches occluded regions whenever their level of detail requires it.
We further find that normalizing the accumulated gradient by a raw view count
does not match the contribution-weighted nature of the tracked gradients. We
derive a more natural normalizer that accumulates a Gaussian’s average con-
tribution to each rendered view. To validate our occlusion-aware densification,
we build a framework around StopThePop [20] that consolidates established
densification improvements from separate prior methods into a strong base-
line. We evaluate both changes on this baseline and directly on StopThePop.
On the occlusion-heavy Zip-NeRF [2] scenes, the resulting method outperforms
StopThePop on all image-quality metrics, reducing LPIPS by up to 12%. Its
Gaussian count also stays bounded under the longer schedules these large scenes
require, where most competing criteria diverge. On smaller benchmarks, it per-
forms comparably to the state of the art with up to 63% fewer Gaussians. Our
contributions are as follows:
– To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to replace the visibility fil-
ter itself: the frustum-based test that underlies all densification statistics.
Our occlusion-aware filter updates a Gaussian’s statistics only from views in
which it is actually rendered, independent of the criterion built on top.
– We introduce a contribution-based densification criterion that is more closely
related to the gradient signal used by ADC, leading to better densification
decisions, demonstrated on small- and large-scale scenes.
– We combine both contributions with established densification improvements
and other refinements into a strong framework that does not require per-
scene tuning and has fewer hyperparameters (e.g. eliminating the unstable
scene extent). We will make the code available to the research community.

Background and Related Work
2.1
Adaptive Density Control
3D Gaussian Splatting [13] represents a scene as a set of 3D Gaussians, each
defined by a 3D mean position, a 3D covariance matrix (parameterized by a
rotation and per-axis scale), an opacity o, and a view-dependent color c derived
from spherical harmonics [7]. For rendering, each Gaussian is projected onto the
image as a 2D footprint with a screen-space mean and radius. Starting from
a sparse point cloud, 3DGS grows and prunes its set of Gaussians via adaptive
density control (ADC), executed every 100 iterations after a 500-iteration warm-
up and disabled after the first half of training. A scene extent E, computed from
the training cameras, provides the reference scale for several ADC decisions.
Criterion. During rendering, a frustum-based visibility filter accumulates per-
Gaussian statistics: the 2D screen-space positional gradient gi,v of Gaussian i
in view v, the screen-space radius, and a view count M. At each ADC event,
a Gaussian is marked for densification if its accumulated gradient magnitude,
averaged over the view count, exceeds a predefined threshold (0.0002 by default).
Densification. To resolve the problem of an insufficient number of Gaussians
capturing a region’s textural or structural detail (from here on described as
under-densification), a marked Gaussian is densified in one of two modes: if it is
large relative to the scene extent, it is split into two downscaled children sampled
from the parent’s distribution, otherwise it is cloned, i.e. duplicated, in place.
Pruning. Redundant or noisy Gaussians are removed using pruning mechanisms.
An opacity prune removes every Gaussian whose opacity oi falls below a pre-
defined threshold (0.005). A size-based prune removes Gaussians whose screen-
space radius or world-space scale exceeds a fixed threshold. To clear floater ar-
tifacts, 3DGS additionally resets all opacities to near zero (0.01) every 3000
iterations; important Gaussians recover quickly, floaters stay dim and fall to the
opacity prune. StopThePop [20] offers a gradual opacity decay as an alternative
to the drastic reset. We evaluate both as the (Reset) and (Decay) variants.
2.2
Adaptive Density Control Improvements
All of the following ADC improvements inherit the frustum-based visibility filter
described above, which counts every in-frustum Gaussian, occluded or not. We
make this filter occlusion-aware (Sec. 3.1).
Densification Criterion. AbsGS [28] and GOF [29] counter gradient cancellation
in the accumulation, which otherwise causes under-densification of high-detail
regions. PixelGS [31] instead questions the normalization by the observation
count and proposes a pixel-count-weighted mean. Grubert et al. [9] adopt the
PixelGS strategy with an ascending threshold that shifts densification earlier.

Taming3DGS [18] replaces the threshold with a per-Gaussian multi-term score
under a fixed densification budget. However, budget-controlled methods require
a target count as input, a value that is not known and that a good densification
criterion should find. We propose to normalize a Gaussian’s accumulated gradi-
ent by the accumulated average contributions of its rendered views (Sec. 3.2),
rather than the observation count of 3DGS or the visible-pixel count of PixelGS.
We also adopt GOF’s absolute-gradient signal against cancellation.
Densification Step (Split & Clone). GOF observes that clones remain clustered
and instead samples the child’s position from the parent’s distribution, while
Bulò et al. [22] show that the clone operation introduces an opacity bias. EFA-
GS [23] and Deng et al. [4] drop the clone entirely, and Deng et al. additionally
propose a geometrically more meaningful split procedure. Grubert et al. ad-
dress inconsistencies in the scene-extent computation that governs some ADC
decisions by also accounting for the input point cloud. Other works replace the
densification step more radically [3,6,15,24]. Our experiments also support drop-
ping the clone: we notice large floater clusters due to excessive cloning on scenes
where the scene extent is not well matched to the size of detail (Tab. 4). We
therefore follow the split-only approach of EFA-GS and Deng et al., including
the latter’s split procedure. This removes one dependence on the scene extent.
Pruning. Grubert et al. restrict the opacity prune to Gaussians that also con-
tribute little; EAGLES [8] and OC2DGS [21] prune low- or non-contributing
Gaussians at intervals long enough to observe every training view; ReLaGS [26]
and RadSplat [19] prune by maximum contribution over a full pass, at isolated
points of the optimization. We prune by maximum contribution, as in ReLaGS
and RadSplat, but throughout the optimization, as in EAGLES and OC2DGS.
2.3
Large-Scale Scenes
Large-scale scenes harbor a range of problems for Gaussian Splatting: Kerbl et
al. [14] partition them into chunks optimized individually after a coarse full-
scene pass. They note problems with the regular densification regime and adopt
the maximum 2D screen-space gradient as the criterion. OccluGaussian [17]
makes the partitioning occlusion-aware, clustering by what each camera sees
so that no chunk contains heavy occlusions, and optimizes chunks individually
before merging them. RadSplat observes that lighting variations in the Zip-
NeRF dataset [2] cause 3DGS to struggle and produce overly smooth results even
with an exposure-handling module. SMERF [5] performs extensive densification-
related hyperparameter tuning on Zip-NeRF yet reports low Gaussian counts,
indicating under-densification. Lowering the gradient threshold raises the count
only at the cost of more floaters. Neither work identifies the root cause. We
argue it is occlusion-blind densification (a cause that exposure correction and
hyperparameter tuning leave unaddressed) and provide supporting evidence in
Sec. 4.3. Unlike OccluGaussian, we do not avoid this problem by optimizing
occluded regions as individual small scenes, but instead make the densification
occlusion-aware, enabling a single full-scene optimization.

Method
We build on StopThePop [20], a state-of-the-art Gaussian Splatting renderer
whose exact per-pixel depth ordering removes popping artifacts and keeps oc-
clusion boundaries intact. Hybrid transparency [10], in contrast, sorts only the
front-most Gaussians and composites all remaining ones without enforcing occlu-
sion ordering. Leaving even fully occluded Gaussians with a nonzero contribution
stands in opposition to our motivation of occlusion-aware densification. However,
our method is not dependent on the per-pixel sorting of StopThePop: it relies
only on the per-pixel blending weights that virtually every Gaussian Splatting
rasterizer already computes. Our contributions can therefore transfer to other
bases (evaluated on 2DGS [12] and 3DGS [13] in Sec. A.2 of the supplementary
material).
Differentiable rasterization. Projected onto the image plane, a Gaussian i forms
a 2D footprint Gi,p ∈(0, 1] over pixels p [32], yielding the per-pixel opacity αi,p =
oiGi,p. 3DGS renders with a tile-based differentiable rasterizer: a preprocessing
step culls Gaussians against the camera view frustum, maps the remaining ones
to the image tiles they overlap, and sorts them by view-space depth. Each pixel
p is rendered by front-to-back alpha compositing of the depth-sorted Gaussians
that overlap it, yielding its color Cp:

3.1
Occlusion-Awareness
As discussed in Sec. 2.1, densification-related statistics are gathered via a vis-
ibility filter based on a view-frustum test. This reflects potential, not actual,
observations of a Gaussian. From here on, we refer to Gaussians that are not
rendered at all as occluded: transmittance saturates before reaching them at ev-
ery pixel they cover. With a frustum-based filter, occluded Gaussians thus enter
the accumulated densification statistics despite not participating in the rendered
image. Although their gradients are zero, every occluded view still increases the
criterion’s observation count and updates the maximum-radius tracking. Increas-
ing the observation count results in a downward pressure on the densification
criterion of any Gaussian that lies within the frustum while being obstructed.

The consequence is under-densification in occlusion-heavy scenes such as the
multi-room apartments of Zip-NeRF [2], as also observed by prior work [5,19].
We propose an occlusion-aware visibility filter that restricts the influence of
rendering statistics to those Gaussians that actually participate in rendering the
image. For the filter itself, a binary signal suffices: a Gaussian counts as observed
in a view only if it contributes to at least one pixel. We obtain this signal from
the total contribution the Gaussian accumulates during rendering: the sum of
its blending weights wi,p over all pixels of the view. This quantity is reused
by our criterion in Sec. 3.2. If the total contribution is zero, the Gaussian did
not participate in the rendering and is therefore occluded (Fig. 2). Replacing
the original frustum-based visibility filter with our occlusion-aware filter thus
ensures occlusion-aware densification statistics.
The occlusion signal could equally come from a Boolean rendered flag as com-
puted in OC2DGS [21] or a rendered-pixel count as in PixelGS [31]. However, it
is essential that the filter gating the densification statistics is derived from such
a rendering signal rather than remaining based on the view frustum.

through the transmittance Ti,p, it attenuates partial occlusion, grazing observa-

is weakest. The floor ensures that scores increase smoothly and monotonically

weights wi,p, not on how the 2D gradients are reduced to a scalar value. There-
fore, it applies unchanged to the absolute-gradient formulations of AbsGS [28]
and GOF [29]. We apply the same criterion computation to all gradient terms.
4
Experiments
4.1
Datasets and Metrics
We evaluate on the common Gaussian Splatting scenes: Mip-NeRF360 [1] (9
scenes), Tanks & Temples [16] (2 scenes), and Deep Blending [11] (2 scenes).
Additionally, we evaluate on Zip-NeRF [2], whose 4 large apartment scenes
(alameda, berlin, london, nyc) exhibit heavy occlusions between rooms, with an
occlusion rate of 0.63 against 0.15–0.48 on the other datasets (definition and per-
scene values in Sec. D). Zip-NeRF is the established stress test for this regime:
prior work reports 3DGS under-densification and over-smoothing on these scenes
despite extensive tuning [5,19]. For Zip-NeRF, we use images at half their orig-
inal resolution, mirroring common 3DGS target resolutions, and hold out every
8th image as the validation set, following the standard protocol [13]. We report
peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity (SSIM) [25], learned per-
ceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS; VGG backbone) [30], the final number of
Gaussians, and the training time. Large-scale scenes are prone to over-smoothing
due to under-densification [5,19]. We therefore also report the gradient magni-
tude similarity deviation (GMSD) [27], a metric specifically sensitive to blur. We
read these metrics jointly: perceptual ones such as LPIPS credit reconstructed
detail that e.g. PSNR penalizes when not pixel-aligned. Likewise, a lower Gaus-
sian count is only better at comparable quality: a starved model is not more
efficient.
4.2
Implementation
We embed our contributions in the integrated framework: the publicly available
StopThePop [20], extended with the following improvements proposed by re-
lated work, which we do not claim as contributions. Unless stated otherwise, all
runs use the opacity decay variant from StopThePop, and reported numbers are
averaged over three training runs. All experiments run on an Nvidia A40 GPU.

Absolute gradient. We include the absolute-gradient condition of GOF [29],
which improves high-frequency detail at an increased Gaussian count (Tab. 4).
Gaussian densification. We follow a split-only strategy, as also adopted by EFA-
GS [23] and Deng et al. [4]. In our setting this removes a dependence on the scene
extent E and avoids the excessive cloning that leads to pronounced floaters or
total failure (Tab. 4). We also adopt Deng et al.’s geometrically meaningful
long-axis split, which slightly reduces the count at competitive quality.
Pruning. Following the recommendation of Grubert et al. [9], we disable size-
based pruning, which we find has no significant impact on quality or Gaussian
count (Tab. 4). Disabling it also removes another dependence on the scene ex-
tent E. We additionally prune Gaussians whose maximum blending contribution
wmax stays below a fixed threshold (0.005), as discussed by ReLaGS [26] and
RadSplat [19]. We track wmax on the fly and prune throughout training [8,21]
on an automatic, hyperparameter-free schedule synchronized with the training
queue. A single cheap pass over all training views at the end guarantees that the
final model contains no fully occluded Gaussians (scheduling details in Sec. E.1,
occluded-Gaussian counts per method in Sec. E.2).
Densification statistics. Original 3DGS accumulates densification statistics al-
ready during warm-up, where the largely transparent scene does not yet reflect
its geometry. We exclude the warm-up period, which affects only the first den-
sification event and is confirmed safe (Tab. 4).
Competitor comparison. We replicate PixelGS [31] and Grubert et al. [9] on
the same StopThePop renderer in two settings: their full methods on vanilla
StopThePop for the state-of-the-art comparison (Tab. 3), and their criterion
alone inside the integrated framework, for the criterion comparison (Tab. 1).
4.3
Impact of Occlusion-Awareness
Integrating the adopted densification components into StopThePop already
yields a strong but still occlusion-blind baseline. It surpasses StopThePop on
all image-quality metrics on both datasets (Zip-NeRF: LPIPS by 6%, PSNR
by 0.57 dB; StopThePop comparison in Sec. B). On top of this baseline, we
isolate the occlusion-aware visibility filter (Sec. 3.1), leaving every other com-
ponent unchanged. The top two rows of Tab. 1 compare the occlusion-blind
baseline against its occlusion-aware counterpart. Making the filter occlusion-
aware improves all image-quality metrics while enabling the densification that
occlusion-blindness suppresses (LPIPS improves by a further 9%; the count
rises from 0.56M to 2.57M). Merely lowering the occlusion-blind threshold is
no equivalent control: it densifies indiscriminately rather than where occlusion
suppressed it, and SMERF [5] reports that this produces floaters on these scenes.
Occlusion-blind densification is thus a primary cause of under-densification on
large, occlusion-heavy scenes. On Mip-NeRF360, where occlusions are far lighter,

4.4
Impact of the Average Contribution Criterion
We now compare densification criteria directly, holding the occlusion-aware filter
and all other components fixed and varying only the criterion. We examine four
criteria: the occlusion-aware observation count, the PixelGS [31] pixel-count-
weighted mean without and with distance scaling (PixelGS, PixelGS + DS),
and the average contribution (Ours). All criteria run at a single fixed threshold
across every scene and dataset: the competitors at their published defaults, ours
calibrated once on the Mip-NeRF360 sweep subset (Sec. F.1) and left unchanged
elsewhere. Table 1 reports the four criteria on the occlusion-heavy Zip-NeRF
scenes (Mip-NeRF360 counterpart in Tab. S12). On the smaller Mip-NeRF360
scenes the criterion barely affects quality (across the four criteria, PSNR spans
0.13 dB and LPIPS 3%), so compactness is decisive: our average-contribution
criterion produces the fewest Gaussians (roughly 55–75% of the other criteria’s
counts) at matched quality. The criteria differ sharply on the large Zip-NeRF
scenes. The raw pixel-count criterion is poorly behaved on its own: it over-
densifies to 6.6M Gaussians, by far the largest count, yet reaches the worst
PSNR. Under a long training schedule it diverges catastrophically (Sec. 4.5).
PixelGS therefore couples the criterion with distance scaling as a guard rail
against runaway densification. This bounds the count, but on these large multi-
room scenes the guard suppresses densification that is actually needed: quality
degrades to the worst SSIM, LPIPS, and GMSD of the four. We attribute this
to the reliance of distance scaling on the scene extent. We find the scene extent
to be unreliable in general but especially on large scenes (Sec. 4.2). Our method
is instead fully independent of it. Our average-contribution criterion needs no
guard rail: it is intrinsically self-limiting, giving the fewest Gaussians of the four
at competitive quality. The occlusion-aware count is 0.5%, 3%, and 3% better
on SSIM, LPIPS, and GMSD and 0.06 dB worse on PSNR, at 2.3× our count.

4.5
Scalability under a Matched Training Budget
The large Zip-NeRF scenes are unevenly observed: training cameras cluster in
interior hotspots and cover peripheral regions far more sparsely (camera map
for the alameda example in Fig. S3). They also hold about seven times as many
training images as Mip-NeRF360, so the default 30k schedule grants each image
only 22 optimization steps against Mip-NeRF360’s 159, and lets it contribute
to only 11 densification events against 77. Sparsely covered regions therefore
receive few densification opportunities, and blur there can signal missing op-
portunity rather than poor densification decisions. Longer schedules restore this
opportunity and reveal whether a criterion resolves the remaining detail or leaves
these regions under-densified, and whether its growth stays bounded while doing
so. We therefore additionally train the large Zip-NeRF scenes under a matched
schedule of 210k iterations, which restores both quantities (157 steps and 76
densification events per image), and sample around it at 100k and 300k. Every
other result keeps the standard 30k protocol for comparability. Table 2 shows
the results up to the matched schedule; the 300k table and schedule details are
in Sec. G.2.
Under these schedules the naive pixel-count criterion (PixelGS without distance
scaling) is not merely inefficient but unstable: on every Zip-NeRF scene it over-
densifies past the ∼16.8M trainable-count limit until training crashes (count-
versus-iteration plot in Fig. S4). That limit lies nearly an order of magnitude
beyond ours. The full PixelGS baseline, which includes the distance-scaling guard
rail, does not diverge at its default threshold; the instability is a property of
the pixel-count criterion in isolation. Our average-contribution criterion stays
bounded throughout (2.75M even at 300k) and continues to improve in LPIPS
and GMSD, whereas the guarded PixelGS remains behind on every image-quality
metric while using 62% more Gaussians. Section G.4 traces this remaining gap to
the guard itself: the distance term systematically suppresses near-object detail.

5
Conclusion & Limitations
We expose the occlusion-blindness of the adaptive density control in standard
3DGS and propose a modular solution, transferable to existing Gaussian Splat-
ting methods, that ensures occlusion-aware densification statistics. We further
present a novel densification criterion that normalizes a Gaussian’s accumulated
screen-space gradients by the accumulated average contributions of its rendered
views. This avoids the view- and pixel-count biases of previous criteria under oc-
clusion, large footprints, and varying distance. This leads to perceptually better
reconstructions at fewer Gaussians on large, occlusion-heavy scenes. We addi-
tionally integrate established densification improvements into a framework that
reduces the dependence on unreliable heuristics such as the scene extent.
Large-scale reconstruction nevertheless does not yet reach small-scene quality.
Beyond occlusion, the imbalanced camera coverage of the Zip-NeRF [2] scenes
limits what densification can recover (Sec. 4.5). Longer schedules restore the
missing densification opportunity, but a fairer distribution of observations would
address the imbalance at its source. Several works group cameras by scene sec-
tions to pursue divide-and-conquer strategies [14,17]. Combining occlusion-aware
densification with a balanced training strategy should enable higher-quality
large-scale reconstructions via single joint optimizations. Our code will be made
available at https://github.com/MarcelRogge/occlusion-aware-3dgs.

Generalizability (2DGS & 3DGS)
Our contributions rely only on the per-pixel blending weights, so they should
transfer to most other Gaussian Splatting renderers. To test this, we port both
contributions to 2DGS [12], whose surfel-based rasterizer and regularization dif-

which shares StopThePop’s splatting formulation but none of its per-pixel sort-

generality of our proposed contributions. We report results on each vanilla base,
the occlusion-aware filter alone (+ filter), and the filter with criterion (+ filter
+ criterion).
Mip-NeRF360. We calibrate each port with a small threshold sweep on the

reported in Tab. S1. Both ports behave the same way. At our original threshold
of 0.002, the criterion improves every quality metric over both the baseline and

of the gain. Raising it further under-densifies, with every metric falling below

it in our StopThePop framework raises the Mip-NeRF360 count by only 4%

clone operation.

This approach of measuring occlusions requires a Gaussian model to render from.
We considered two different types of models: initializations, which are method-
independent, and fully optimized models (30k iterations), which have the most
complete geometry but depend on the method used to produce them. We tried
initializations with both the original initial opacity of 0.1 and a raised opacity
of 0.75. Both initializations are unreliable and fail in opposite directions: 0.1
returns an occlusion rate below 0.02 for 13 of the 17 scenes. At 0.75, four scenes
(including Mip-NeRF360 scenes) exceed an occlusion rate of 0.90. While a low
opacity obviously reduces occlusions, we believe that e.g. floaters in the sparse
point cloud can exaggerate occlusions in the case of high opacity initialization.
When evaluating occlusions on fully optimized models, there might be a bias
depending on the method that was used to optimize the model. To avoid this
bias, we compared occlusion rates between models produced by five different
methods: StopThePop (Reset), StopThePop (Decay), PixelGS, Grubert et al.,
and ours. We found that the occlusion rankings correlate strongly (Spearman
0.92–0.99), which indicates that the occlusion rate is a property of the scenes
rather than the methods used to optimize the model. Therefore, we choose to
report occlusion rates measured on the fully optimized models, averaged over
the five methods.
Table S6 reports the occlusion rates per dataset. The four Zip-NeRF apartments
are the most occluded at 0.63, followed by Tanks & Temples at 0.48, while
Mip-NeRF360 and Deep Blending are far lower at 0.19 and 0.15. This ordering
follows the geometry: the Zip-NeRF scenes are multi-room interiors in which
most surfaces are hidden behind walls from any given camera, whereas the Mip-
NeRF360 and Deep Blending scenes are largely centered on a single object or
a single open space. Tanks & Temples seems like an outlier: despite containing
small scenes with a camera orbiting an object, it has a large occlusion rate. The
reason is that the objects in this dataset (a truck and a train) are very large
such that they occlude significant amounts of the scene from any given camera.

E.1
Contribution-Prune Implementation
We employ contribution pruning, as used by RadSplat [19] and ReLaGS [26].
However, our pruning runs alongside training, similarly to OC2DGS [21] and
EAGLES [8]. We improve the scheduling to be automatically synchronized with
the training-view queue: a pruning decision is made only after every view has
been seen once since the last decision. This prevents Gaussians in rarely observed
regions from getting pruned due to a lack of opportunity. We further integrate
the pruning with the existing opacity prune, which avoids additional pruning
calls due to a misalignment of the training queue and densification intervals.
This is done by recording a pruning mask when the pruning decision can be
made, and postponing the actual pruning to the next densification event. After
densification finishes, contribution pruning continues to remove redundant Gaus-
sians on its own. Lastly, a post-training step iterates once through all training

Threshold Calibration
We calibrate our densification threshold once, with a single-run sweep of our
full configuration on the calibration subset (Tab. S9). The subset consists of
four representative Mip-NeRF360 scenes: bicycle, bonsai, counter, and garden.

F.2
The Denominator Floor
Our criterion normalizes the accumulated gradient by 1 + P
v ¯wi,v, where the
constant is a fixed floor (Sec. A.1). A vanishing accumulated contribution is not
the failure case: a Gaussian that was never observed accumulates no gradient
either, and is never densified. The failure case lies strictly in cases where a Gaus-
sian has only few observations with low contributions, causing the accumulated
contribution to fall between 0 and 1. There the accumulated contribution divides
the gradient sum by less than one, inflating the score instead of normalizing it,
and this inflation grows without bound as the sum approaches zero. So, den-
sification stops responding to the signal the criterion is meant to measure. We
sweep the floor over 0, 0.5, 2 and 4 on the Zip-NeRF scenes to establish two
things: whether the floor is necessary, and what effect its specific value has.
Without the floor, training diverges. We evaluate the behavior of the criterion
without a floor in Tab. S10. At floor 0 and our default threshold, all four scenes
cross the 224 ≈16.8M trainable limit and crash within roughly 1,800 of the 30,000
iterations. Raising the threshold delays the divergence but does not remove it: the

the score is thus a reparameterization of the densification threshold rather than
a quality parameter. We keep the floor at 1 for all experiments, since it is the
minimal value that guarantees that the denominator will not fall below 1. We

The PixelGS-ours Hybrid
Our criterion and the PixelGS [31] criterion differ in two aspects. The first is the
per-view weight: our average contribution versus PixelGS’s visible-pixel count.
The second is how that weight enters the update: PixelGS forms a weighted
mean of the per-view gradients, whereas we sum the gradients and normalize
by the accumulated average contributions. To isolate the aggregation rule, we
evaluate the PixelGS-ours hybrid: PixelGS’s weighted-mean structure fed our
contribution signal, using each view’s total contribution Wi,v = P

The transmittance weighting does not repair the weighted-mean aggregation: at
its operating point the PixelGS-ours hybrid lands almost exactly on the raw Pix-

through our normalizer, stays far more compact at 1.1M. Under the extended
100k schedule of the main paper, the PixelGS-ours hybrid diverges on three of
the four scenes and reaches 7.4M Gaussians on the fourth (single runs). This is
the same long-schedule instability as the raw pixel-count criterion.

Extended Schedules and 300k Results
For the matched 210k schedule of the main paper and the 100k and 300k sched-
ules sampled around it, we lengthen the training schedule and adjust its hyper-
parameters accordingly, and we report single runs owing to their cost. Relative
to the 30k base, we scale the schedule by a common factor s = 10/3, 7, and 10
for the 100k, 210k, and 300k schedules. The iteration count becomes s × 30,000.
The positional learning-rate schedule, the densification window (iterations 500
to 15,000 at the 30k base), the opacity-reset interval, and the opacity-decay in-
terval all scale by the same factor of s. The densification interval is instead held
fixed at 100 iterations. This ensures that the densification opportunity per image
also scales with s. Table S18 extends the main paper’s extended-training com-
parison to a 300k schedule: ours stays bounded (only 10% more Gaussians than
at 210k) with LPIPS still improving marginally, while the guarded PixelGS [31]
also remains bounded but at 62% more Gaussians and 40% more training time
than ours.

G.5
Per-View Analysis
Averaged over full test sets the distance-scaling asymmetry appears modest
(Tab. 2 of the main paper) because it is spatially concentrated: on alameda
at 210k, ours improves LPIPS over PixelGS + DS on 133 of 217 test views by
more than 0.005, with the largest margins on the near-object close-ups (up to
0.10 LPIPS and 2.4 dB better on the Lego toy). The few views favoring Pix-
elGS + DS by that margin (27 of 217, not surpassing 0.05 LPIPS) are severely
under-observed corners, where its near suppression yields an inoffensive smooth
blur, while the densification ours enables appears as floater speckle (Fig. S6). We
note that the ground truth images show pixel-level color artifacts, which explain
the colorful speckles. Ours attempts to reconstruct this although it might be
impossible to reconstruct this high-frequency noise that also changes between
different viewpoints. Here, PixelGS is rewarded for its blurred reconstruction.
Our criterion’s lowest-quality views are intrinsically difficult views on which ev-
ery method degrades together: on Mip-NeRF360 only a single test view falls
below 18 dB and none exceed 0.45 LPIPS. At the default schedules all three cri-
teria place nearly the same number of views below 18 dB (one on Mip-NeRF360;
17–18 on Zip-NeRF [2] at 30k). At 210k the occlusion-aware count instead col-
lapses to a cluster reaching 12.5 dB on berlin and london, where ours leaves
only a single view below 18 dB (minimum 16.0 dB). The 30k per-view statistics
are averaged per view over the three repetitions, while the extended-schedule
statistics come from single training runs.

Additional Qualitative Results
Figure S8 shows the three teaser locations of the main paper as full test frames.
We show full-frame renders and error maps against vanilla PixelGS [31]
(StopThePop base) on three Zip-NeRF scenes in Fig. S9. We visualize gradient-
magnitude similarity (1 −GMS) rather than absolute error, because absolute
error barely separates the two methods: the main failure is blur caused by insuf-
ficient reconstruction, which is favored by per-pixel metrics. Gradient-magnitude
similarity instead responds to lost structure, which is where the two methods dif-
fer. For each scene we show the test views on which our LPIPS advantage over
PixelGS is largest and smallest, respectively. Where the advantage is largest,
PixelGS visibly loses fine structure such as tiles, foliage, and clutter. Its error
map is correspondingly brighter: its GMSD is 0.127, 0.178, and 0.159 against our
0.088, 0.129, and 0.103. Where the advantage is smallest, the two error maps are
nearly indistinguishable. On nyc our LPIPS is in fact better on every test view.
Our weakest views relative to PixelGS are therefore not views where it recovers
more detail, but views where the two reconstructions are structurally equivalent.
