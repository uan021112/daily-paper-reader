Abstract 

Feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction from sparse posed monocular frames requires reasoning over observations that vary jointly in viewpoint and time. Existing methods either predict Gaussian primitives from observation-aligned features or aggregate multi-frame information through compact latent queries. However, the former tightly couples Gaussian alloca-tion to individual frames, while the latter does not explicitly structure observed evidence according to its temporal relation to the requested timestamp. Consequently, evidence across spatial scales, viewpoints, and timestamps can be difficult to align and integrate. We present HiQGS , a query-based hier-archical framework for feed-forward Gaussian scene recon-struction from sparse monocular frames. HiQGS introduces a 

Hierarchical Spatiotemporal Encoder that organizes multi-resolution geometry-appearance visual features according to image location, observation time, and camera pose, thereby preserving complementary appearance and structural cues. Our Time-Adaptive Decoder use target timestamp queries the resulting hierarchical representation to retrieve relevant spa-tiotemporal evidence and predicts the corresponding Gaussian scene state. Experiments on static and dynamic scene recon-struction benchmarks demonstrate strong rendering quality and geometric accuracy, validating the effectiveness of hierar-chical visual encoding and target-time-aware Gaussian query-ing. 

# 1 Introduction 

Feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction has emerged as an effi-cient alternative to per-scene optimization, directly mapping sparse visual observations to renderable 3D scenes (Charatan et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2025a). Extending this paradigm to posed monocular videos enables dynamic scene reconstruction and novel-view ren-dering at arbitrary timestamps in a single forward pass. How-ever, reconstructing dynamic scenes from sparse monocular frames cannot be achieved by processing each input inde-pendently. Each observation encodes appearance, geometry,

timestamp, and camera pose, while camera motion couples viewpoint variation with temporal scene changes. Existing representations struggle to organize such heterogeneous ev-idence across observations, limiting their ability to infer the scene state at novel target time. This motivates a hierarchical spatiotemporal representation that preserves complementary visual cues and enables target-time evidence querying for Gaussian reconstruction. Existing feed-forward Gaussian methods often predict Gaussian primitives from observation-aligned features and model temporal variation through deformation or additional temporal attributes, such as lifespan and low rank veloc-ity (Lin et al. 2025a; Xu et al. 2025b; Ma et al. 2025a; Lin et al. 2026). Such observation-aligned allocation may repeat-edly assign primitives to overlapping scene content, introduc-ing representational redundancy, while dispersing comple-mentary cues across frame-specific slots and making them difficult to consolidate into a coherent target-time scene state. Recently, scene-level query-based approaches (Ren et al. 2026; An et al. 2026; Itkin et al. 2026; Kim et al. 2026) have explored decoupling Gaussian prediction from individual ob-servations by introducing compact latent queries. However, although these methods incorporate target-time conditioning, it is often introduced primarily through the latent queries, while the context representation provides limited structure for relating multi-frame evidence to the requested time. Con-sequently, integrating sparse observations across viewpoints and timestamps remains challenging when camera motion is entangled with scene dynamics. To address these challenges, we present HiQGS, a hi-erarchical spatiotemporal querying framework for feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction from sparse posed monoc-ular frames. HiQGS formulates target-time reconstruction as two complementary stages: organizing multi-frame observa-tions into a structured spatiotemporal context and querying this context for the scene state at a requested time. Specif-ically, the Hierarchical Spatiotemporal Encoder combines multi-level geometry features with fine-grained appearance features, while Temporal-Projective RoPE grounds the re-sulting visual tokens by patch location, observation time, and camera pose. The Time-Adaptive Decoder then condi-arXiv:2610.27604v1 [cs.CV] 20 Oct 2026 tions scene-level Gaussian queries on the target timestamp to retrieve complementary evidence from the hierarchical con-text and decode the corresponding Gaussian scene state. This design requires neither explicit motion parameterization nor cross-time primitive correspondence, and allows the encoded context to be reused for multiple target timestamps. Experi-ments on RealEstate10K(Zhou et al. 2018) and Waymo(Sun et al. 2020) demonstrate strong rendering quality and ge-ometric accuracy across static and dynamic reconstruction settings. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 

• We propose HiQGS, which formulates target-time Gaus-sian reconstruction as hierarchical spatiotemporal evi-dence organization followed by target-conditioned scene querying. 

• We introduce a Hierarchical Spatiotemporal Encoder with complementary geometry–appearance features and Temporal-Projective RoPE, together with a Time-Adaptive Decoder for target-time-conditioned Gaussian state prediction. 

• Experiments on RealEstate10K and Waymo demon-strate leading performance in static novel-view synthesis, dynamic-region reconstruction, and sparse-depth geome-try estimation. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Optimization-Based 4D Reconstruction 

Optimization-based neural rendering methods optimize scene-specific spatiotemporal representations for individ-ual sequences. Dynamic NeRFs model temporal varia-tions through time-conditioned deformations into canonical fields (Pumarola et al. 2021; Park et al. 2021), which have been extended to Gaussian representations with deforma-tion fields and spatiotemporal features (Yang et al. 2024a; Wu et al. 2024). Other approaches directly encode dynam-ics through 4D primitives, spatiotemporal Gaussians, or ex-plicit motion trajectories (Yang et al. 2024b; Li et al. 2024; Luiten et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2025b; Chen et al. 2026b). While achieving high-quality re-construction with strong temporal priors, these methods re-quire sequence-specific optimization and often rely on dense observations or scene-specific assumptions, limiting gener-alizable reconstruction from sparse videos. HiQGS instead focuses on feed-forward reconstruction from sparse posed monocular videos without per-scene optimization. 

2.2 Observation-Aligned Gaussian Prediction 

Recent feed-forward Gaussian methods typically predict Gaussian primitives from observation-conditioned features, where pixels, rays, or patches provide direct references for Gaussian generation. pixelSplat, MVSplat, and GS-LRM es-tablish this formulation, while DepthSplat and AnySplat fur-ther improve geometric grounding through depth priors and cross-view aggregation (Charatan et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2025a; Jiang et al. 2025). Extending this paradigm to dynamic scenes, DGS-LRM, 4DGT, 4D-LRM, and MoVieS introduce deformation, motion, or temporal attributes for efficient 4D reconstruc-tion (Lin et al. 2025a; Xu et al. 2025b; Ma et al. 2025a; Lin et al. 2026). However, these approaches remain closely tied to individual observations, potentially introducing represen-tational redundancy and making it difficult to consolidate ev-idence across timestamps and viewpoints. HiQGS instead or-ganizes multi-frame evidence into a hierarchically grounded spatiotemporal representation for target-time reconstruction. 

2.3 Scene-Level Gaussian Queries 

To reduce the dependency on individual observations, recent methods explore scene-level query representations, where compact latent variables aggregate observations and decode Gaussian primitives. LeanGaussian (Wu et al. 2025b), and Coca-Splat (Wu et al. 2025a) introduce query-based Gaus-sian reconstruction for object- and scene-level tasks, while C3G (An et al. 2026), TokenGS (Ren et al. 2026), Global-Splat (Itkin et al. 2026), and UniQueR (Peng et al. 2026) ex-tend scene-level querying to scalable reconstruction. For dy-namic scenes, TokenGS and C4G further incorporate target-time embedding for temporal Gaussian decoding (Ren et al. 2026; Kim et al. 2026). Nevertheless, such temporal con-ditioning is typically applied to the latent queries, while the context representation provides limited structure for re-lating multi-frame evidence to the requested time. HiQGS instead grounds hierarchical geometry and appearance fea-tures in a joint spatiotemporal reference defined by image location, observation time, and camera pose, and employs a Time-Adaptive Decoder to retrieve target-relevant evidence for Gaussian scene-state prediction. 

# 3 Method 

Our goal is to enable feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction from sparse posed monocular observations with a fixed num-ber of Gaussian primitives that can generalize across diverse environments and dynamic states. Given V sparse obser-vations {(Iv , Cv , t v )}Vv=1 from a monocular video, where 

Iv , Cv , and tv denote the RGB frame, camera parame-ters, and timestamp, respectively, we aim to predict the Gaussian scene state G(t∗) at a target time t∗. Specifically, 

G(t∗) = {(xi, ri, si, α i, ci)}Ni=1 contains the position, rota-tion, scale, opacity, and appearance of each Gaussian, and can be rendered from an arbitrary target camera C∗.Achieving this goal presents two challenges: (1) Sparse observations provide complementary appearance and geom-etry cues with different levels of spatial detail and semantic abstraction across time and viewpoints. These heterogeneous observations need to be organized into a unified representa-tion while preserving their spatial, temporal, and geometric relationships. (2) The scene state at a target timestamp is not directly observed, which must be decoded from sparse con-text observations according to their temporal and geometric relations with the target state. HiQGS addresses these chal-lenges with a Hierarchical Spatiotemporal Encoder (HSE) and a Time-Adaptive Decoder (TAD), as illustrated in Fig-ure 1.

To capture these complementary properties, we introduce a Hierarchical Spa-tiotemporal Encoder (HSE) that integrates geometry and appearance branches with different granularities. HSE fur-ther employs Temporal-Projective Rotary Positional Encod-ing (TP-RoPE) to establish spatiotemporal correspondence among visual tokens based on their image locations, obser-vation timestamps, and camera geometry. 

Geometry encoder. The geometry branch adopts VGGT-

Ω (Wang et al. 2026a) to extract geometry-aware represen-tations from the context sequence. VGGT-Ω alternates intra-frame reasoning and inter-frame aggregation, enabling the features to capture both local structure and cross-view geo-metric relations. To preserve geometry information at differ-ent abstraction levels, we collect intermediate features from layers L = {4, 11 , 17 , 23 } and fuse them into a unified rep-resentation. The resulting features are projected to obtain the geometry feature sequence Fgeo .

Appearance encoder. In parallel, the appearance branch employs a lightweight ViT-style encoder (Dosovitskiy et al. 2021) with a smaller patch size Papp < P geo . The denser to-ken grid preserves fine-grained photometric details, includ-ing textures and object boundaries, that may be underrepre-sented in the geometry branch. The content pathway receives only RGB patches and performs self-attention, while camera and temporal information are introduced separately through 

TP-RoPE . This design avoids explicitly concatenating ray or camera embeddings into appearance features, preserving a clean separation between visual content and spatiotemporal grounding. Together, Fgeo and Fapp provide complementary evidence: the geometry branch captures multi-view structural relations, while the appearance branch preserves fine-grained visual details required for faithful rendering. 

Temporal Projective RoPE (TP-RoPE). To establish a unified spatiotemporal reference for heterogeneous visual to-kens, we introduce TP-RoPE , which extends the projective camera encoding of PRoPE (Li et al. 2025) with an explicit temporal component. For token i from frame v in branch 

b ∈ { geo , app }, we define its positional operator as:

follows the projective camera encoding in PRoPE. Here, ⊕

denotes a channel-wise direct sum. The four components oc-cupy separate channel subspaces and rotate the query and key projections before attention. This formulation explicitly associates each visual token with its image location, observa-tion time, and camera geometry, enabling attention to reason over spatiotemporal correspondences across observations. The spatial coordinates (hbi , w bi ) are defined on the native patch grid of each branch, allowing TP-RoPE to accommo-date heterogeneous token granularities. We apply TP-RoPE 

to the attention blocks of both branches. In the geometry branch, the projective and temporal components help estab-lish cross-view geometric correspondence across observa-tions. In contrast, the appearance branch focuses on preserv-ing fine-grained visual details, where TP-RoPE associates appearance tokens with their acquisition conditions, includ-ing image location, observation time, and camera context.

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Implementation details. We initialize the geometry en-coder from the pretrained VGGT-Ω model (Wang et al. 2026a), which uses a patch size of 16 × 16 . The appear-ance branch employs a lightweight ViT-style encoder with a patch size of 8 × 8. All input images are resized to 256 × 256 .The decoder maintains a fixed bank of Nq = 1 ,024 learn-able, role-agnostic Gaussian tokens. Each token is decoded into M = 64 Gaussian primitives, yielding NG = Nq M =65 ,536 Gaussians for each target timestamp. The predicted Gaussians are rendered using the gsplat rasterizer (Ye et al. 2025). We train the model using the RGB reconstruction loss, the SSIM loss with λssim = 0 .2, and the projection regular-izer with λproj = 1 .0. For sequences with sparse LiDAR supervision, we additionally apply a masked depth loss over valid projected LiDAR pixels, with λdepth = 0 .2. We use AdamW with a one-cycle learning-rate schedule and a learn-ing rate of 4 × 10 −5. Training runs for 150 K optimization steps, corresponding to 300 epochs, and takes approximately 

30 hours on eight NVIDIA H800 GPUs. 

Datasets. We evaluate HiQGS on static scene reconstruc-tion using RealEstate10K (RE10K) and dynamic street-scene reconstruction using the Waymo Open Dataset. For RealEstate10K, we follow the evaluation protocol used by recent feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction methods (Ren et al. 2026; Xu et al. 2025a; Chen et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2026). We sample V context frames from each sequence and evaluate novel-view synthesis on held-out frames under the interpolation and extrapolation settings. For the Waymo Open Dataset, we use the official split of 798 training scenes and 202 validation scenes. Following re-cent feed-forward street-scene reconstruction methods (Yang et al. 2025; Yu et al. 2026), each sample contains a 20-frame front-camera clip. Frames at indices 0, 5, 10 , and 15 are used as context observations, and the remaining frames are used as targets. Sparse LiDAR depth is projected onto the front-camera images and used for depth supervision and geometry evaluation. 

Baselines. We compare HiQGS with representative feed-forward methods from three categories. First, visual ge-ometry models, including VGGT-Ω (Wang et al. 2026a) and π3 (Wang et al. 2026b), predict dense scene geom-etry but do not directly produce a renderable Gaussian scene state at an arbitrary target time. We therefore include them only in the geometry evaluation. Second, we compare with observation-aligned Gaussian reconstruction methods. The feed-forward 3DGS variant of DA3 (Lin et al. 2025b) decodes pixel-aligned Gaussians for novel-view synthesis, while DGGT (Chen et al. 2026a) predicts per-frame pixel-aligned Gaussian maps and models temporal visibility and motion for dynamic driving scenes. Finally, TokenGS (Ren et al. 2026) and C3G (An et al. 2026) use learnable Gaus-sian queries to aggregate multi-view evidence into compact scene-level representations.

# 5 Conclusion 

We presented HiQGS, a feed-forward framework for recon-structing renderable Gaussian scene states from sparse posed monocular video frames. The key idea is to separate the organization of context observations from the decoding of a requested scene state. HiQGS directly predicts a target-specific Gaussian field without prescribing a motion model or enforcing primitive correspondence across time, and the encoded context can be reused to decode multiple target timestamps. Experiments on both dynamic and static recon-struction benchmarks demonstrate strong rendering and ge-ometric accuracy, supporting hierarchical context encoding and temporally grounded querying as an effective design for generalizable feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction. 

Limitations. Although HiQGS performs well on RE10K and Waymo, these datasets cover only a limited range of real-world scene dynamics and capture conditions. For rare or highly complex motions that differ substantially from the training distribution, the predicted Gaussians may become spatially diffuse, leading to localized degradation in recon-struction quality. We hypothesize that the limited diversity of motion patterns in the current training data is one con-tributing factor. Moreover, HiQGS does not explicitly model or supervise motion velocity; instead, motion rates must be inferred implicitly from sparse spatiotemporal observations. Consequently, temporal extrapolation beyond the context in-terval remains less reliable, particularly for fast, accelerating, or non-linear motions. An important direction for future work is therefore to scale HiQGS to larger and more diverse video collections and investigate velocity-aware temporal model-ing or motion priors, with the goal of improving robustness to long-tail dynamics and out-of-range temporal extrapolation.
