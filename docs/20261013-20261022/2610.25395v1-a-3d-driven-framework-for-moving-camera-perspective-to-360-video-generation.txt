Abstract
Turning a moving-camera perspective video into a 360◦
video requires more than just generating content outside the
input frame: observations revealed over time must be pre-
served, unseen regions completed, and the resulting scene
kept consistent as the camera viewpoint changes. Exist-
ing methods assign these different requirements to a sin-
gle conditional video model, requiring them to be jointly
learned from panoramic video data. We instead present a
modular, 3D-driven framework that distributes these three
responsibilities across specialized components, all con-
nected through a shared scene representation. Input ob-
servations are reconstructed explicitly in 3D, while a pre-
trained panoramic image model completes unobserved con-
tent from a selected panoramic render. This generated con-
tent is lifted into the shared 3D scene, where it remains spa-
tially anchored across camera viewpoints. Without requir-
ing any additional panoramic-video training, our frame-
work achieves state-of-the-art input-view fidelity, percep-
tual quality, and geometric consistency on a static-scene
subset of the Argus evaluation set. These results support
our decomposed, 3D-driven formulation as a promising al-
ternative to direct video generation in this setting.

1. Introduction
A captured video’s camera framing determines what view-
ers can observe in a scene and how they experience it.
In conventional perspective videos, this framing is entirely
fixed: viewers have no control over the field of view, view-
ing orientation, or camera translation. On the opposite end
of the spectrum, 3D environments provide full navigational
control, but are considerably harder to capture and recreate.
Panoramic video therefore offers a practical middle ground
between conventional video and freely navigable 3D. It al-
lows viewers to choose their viewing direction and field of
view, while camera translation remains fixed to the captured
trajectory.
This makes the moving-camera setting of panoramic
video particularly important; translation is solely deter-
mined by the capture, yet it also allows viewers to observe
the environment from multiple positions. In experiences
such as virtual tours or cultural site exploration, the cap-
tured trajectory can provide a more immersive experience
by revealing scene content that would otherwise not be vis-
ible from a static viewpoint.
Following this motivation,
we study how to generate such moving-camera panoramas
from perspective videos of predominantly static scenes.
Existing perspective-to-360◦methods [6, 18, 22, 27, 35]
largely formulate this task as conditional video generation,
typically using a per-frame projection of the input frames as
conditioning and relying on a video prior to synthesize the
missing panoramic content. While straightforward, this for-
mulation requires the single video model to jointly perform
three distinct responsibilities:
1. Aggregation requires the model to collect content ob-
served anywhere in the input video and preserve it
throughout the output, remembering its appearance and
inferring where it belongs in the panorama once no
longer directly available.
2. Completion requires the model to generate plausible
content for regions never observed in the input, while
also learning panoramic distortion and seamless bound-
ary continuation.
3. Persistence requires both observed and generated con-
tent to retain consistency in texture and geometry as the
camera viewpoint changes.
For video models, learning these responsibilities jointly
is further complicated by the high resolution required for
panoramic video, making video-model training particularly
expensive. The issue is not that models are incapable of
solving the task, but that direct video generation assigns
a single expensive model to solve multiple responsibilities
with different characteristics at the same time.
Motivated by this, we reformulate the task according to
the three defined responsibilities, using a modular frame-
work built around explicit 3D. Rather than regenerating
panoramic content at every timestep, we use 3D as a shared
scene representation that can be queried for observed con-
tent and updated with newly generated content. A feed-
forward 3D reconstruction model aggregates observations
across the input, while a pretrained panoramic image model
completes the unobserved scene in a single frame. Lifting
this generated content into the shared 3D representation al-
lows both observed and generated content to persist when
rendered along the input camera trajectory.
Through this 3D-driven formulation, we provide a
framework that explicitly separates aggregation, comple-
tion, and persistence without requiring any additional train-
ing on panoramic video data.
On a static-scene subset
of the Argus [22] evaluation set, our framework improves
upon the strongest prior results by 1.81 dB on input-view
PSNR and 20.4% on VBench [13] Imaging Quality, while
reducing MEt3R [1] geometric inconsistency by 21.8%. To-
gether, these results establish our 3D-driven formulation as
an effective alternative to direct video generation for this
moving-camera perspective-to-360◦setting.
2. Related Work
Panoramic Generation.
Panoramic image and video
generation look to synthesize complete 360◦content while
accounting for panoramic distortion and boundary discon-
tinuity.
Text-to-panorama methods adapt pretrained dif-
fusion models for panoramic generation [8, 9, 24, 40,
44], or stitch multiple generated perspective views into a
panorama [10, 15, 19]. Image-conditioned methods further
complete panoramas from a single observed view through
masked generation [12, 26, 34, 46]. Panoramic video gen-
eration extends this task across time, following a simi-
lar paradigm of adapting pretrained video models through
spherical sampling, panoramic decoding, or interaction be-
tween multiple perspective views [20, 30, 36]. These meth-
ods establish strong priors for panoramic content genera-
tion, but are limited to image-only generation or text-only
conditioning for video. We instead focus on the task of con-
verting perspective video into a panoramic version.
Perspective-to-360◦Video Generation.
More closely
related to our task, existing methods generate panoramic
videos conditioned on a perspective video input [6, 18, 22,
23, 27, 35]. Most methods explicitly place or project each
input frame into a panoramic representation and use the re-
sulting incomplete video as conditioning. Of these meth-
ods, Argus [22] and Imagine360 [27] generate directly in
the equirectangular space, while others such as CubeCom-
poser [18] and ViewPoint [6] operate over a collection of
perspective views to better reuse perspective video priors.
A more recent work, 360Anything [35], instead learns the
perspective-to-panorama mapping without explicit camera
geometry. Despite these differences, all of these approaches

3D Representations for Video Generation.
Explicit 3D
representations provide a natural way to preserve scene
content without requiring a video prior to learn this be-
havior.
One such representation, 3D Gaussian Splat-
ting [16] (3DGS), uses 3D Gaussian primitives for effi-
cient, high-quality novel view synthesis.
Constructing a
3DGS typically requires knowledge of camera parameters
and scene geometry, which is often unavailable (especially
for in-the-wild videos). Recent geometry foundation mod-
els [17, 29, 31–33] look to bridge this gap by jointly es-
timating cameras and scene structure from image observa-
tions, with some methods further predicting a 3DGS scene
as well [4, 5, 14, 21, 38, 39]. Such advances have been in-
corporated into generative pipelines by rendering estimated
3D geometry as a conditioning signal for video genera-
tion [11, 25, 37, 42, 43]. In comparison, our framework uses
explicit 3D for both the observed and the generated content,
where the final panoramic video is rendered directly with-
out needing video generation priors.

3.1. Aggregating Video Observations in 3D
Our first objective is to aggregate the scene content ob-
served across the input video, rather than treat each perspec-
tive frame independently. We achieve this by reconstructing
the observed scene into a shared 3D representation, which
can subsequently be rendered from each camera position to
identify content already supported by the input.

later propagate its generated content through 3D.

served and generated content across viewpoints while main-
taining coherent structure and appearance.

4. Experiments
We evaluate our framework on a subset of predominantly
static scenes from the Argus [22] evaluation set, using both
real and simulated camera trajectories. The evaluation com-
pares against state-of-the-art perspective-to-360◦methods
through input-view fidelity, perceptual video quality, and
geometric consistency. We further provide qualitative com-
parisons and analyze our framework design choices, scene
coverage, and runtime.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Implementation Details.
We employ HunyuanWorld-
Mirror [21] as the feed-forward reconstruction model,
DiT360 [8] as the panoramic image completion model, and
PaGeR [3] as the panoramic monocular depth estimator.
HunyuanWorld-Mirror predictions are used directly without
camera priors or splat optimization. For panoramic comple-
tion, we use DiT360’s inversion-based image editing, with
50 sampling steps, an insertion time of 10, and guidance
scale of 2.5.
We additionally apply a source-region key
scale of 1.05 to strengthen the influence of observed con-
tent during denoising. We use Qwen3-VL [2] for generating
text prompts, and for panoramic rendering we implement an
icosahedron-based renderer to produce 1024 × 2048 ERP
outputs. We report both the direct rendered output, denoted
OURS, as well as an optional post-processed variant, OURS
+ FILL, which applies Telea inpainting [28] to low-opacity
regions. All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA
L40S GPU.
Evaluation Data.
We construct our evaluation set from
the test set used in Argus [22], retaining both its real and
simulated camera settings. Following the Argus implemen-
tation, we extract perspective inputs at a width of 480 pix-
els, with the height determined by the corresponding field
of view. Because our method targets predominantly static
scenes, we filter a subset by removing clips with substan-
tial scene dynamics, severe camera obstruction, or unus-
ably low input resolution. We still retain clips containing
minor localized dynamics, such as a small number of mov-
ing pedestrians or flowing water, as long as the dominant
scene geometry remains static. This results in 35 scenes,
each evaluated under both camera-trajectory settings.
Metrics.
We follow the Argus evaluation protocol for the
reported metrics.
Input-view fidelity is measured using
PSNR and LPIPS [45] between the ground-truth perspective
crop and the corresponding view extracted from each gen-
erated panorama. Perceptual and temporal video quality are
measured using three VBench [13] dimensions: Imaging

The lower panel of Table 3 evalu-

content over time.

poser, although it is slower than the 1K outputs of View-
Point and Argus. Panoramic outpainting accounts for most
of our runtime and remains the primary computational bot-
tleneck. Since panoramic rendering is comparatively inex-
pensive, higher output frame rates incur relatively little ad-
ditional cost.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced a 3D-driven formulation
for moving-camera perspective-to-360◦generation, built
around three responsibilities: aggregation, completion, and
persistence.
Rather than assigning these responsibilities
jointly to a single video model, our framework separates
them across specialized components connected through a
shared 3D scene representation. Without requiring addi-
tional panoramic video training, this design improves input-
view fidelity, visual quality, and geometric consistency over
existing state-of-the-art methods. These results support ex-
plicit 3D as an effective alternative to direct video genera-
tion in this setting.
Limitations.
Our framework is designed for predomi-
nantly static scenes and is not capable of modeling indepen-
dently moving objects, which may be incorrectly incorpo-
rated into the shared 3D representation (see Figure 3b). In
addition, because generated geometry comes from a single
anchor, large translations may reveal unsupported surfaces;
multiple anchors or iterative completion could address this
limitation. Finally, the performance and efficiency of our
framework depend on the models used for reconstruction,
completion, and depth estimation; the modular design, how-
ever, allows these components to be replaced as stronger
models become available.

A. More Qualitative Results
Additional qualitative comparisons are provided in the
project page gallery. The gallery presents the input perspec-

FILL. Each ERP video can be opened in an interactive 360◦

ing spatial and temporal artifacts that are difficult to assess
from static figures. The gallery also provides a side-by-side
visualization of the framework and persistence ablations for
an example scene. Ablation outputs are shown without op-
tional filling, consistent with the protocol described in Sec-

B. Additional Implementation Details
We use the same notation and definitions from the main
text and provide additional details to choices not previously
specified. All components use their publicly released im-
plementation and weights, no optimization or fine-tuning is
done to the models or 3D Gaussian Splats.
Models and input processing.
We instantiate aggre-
gation with HunyuanWorld-Mirror v1 [21], completion
with the FLUX.1-dev backbone and released DiT360
LoRA [8], and panoramic depth with the released PaGeR
checkpoint [3]. Reconstruction is done over 2 FPS sampled
frames from the original 25 frame, 5 FPS evaluation videos.
The estimated camera trajectory can then be interpolated
to any desired output FPS. After obtaining the interpolated
camera trajectory, we stabilize it and use that stabilized tra-
jectory throughout the rest of the pipeline.
Coverage computation.
Candidate anchors are scored at
each reconstructed pose from the 2 FPS estimated trajec-
tory. Each coverage score uses a 1024×512 ERP alpha im-
age assembled from six 256×256, 90◦cubemap renders.
We do not use the full 2K resolution icosahedron rendering
at this stage since anchor selection only requires a relative
ranking of coverage. We additionally filter any unusually
large 3D Gaussians to prevent isolated or diffuse splats from
artificially increasing the observed area. A pixel is consid-
ered covered when its filtered alpha exceeds 0.5.
Icosahedron ERP rendering.
Whenever a full-resolution
panorama is required, we render the 3DGS through 12 fixed
perspective cameras whos optical axes point towards the 12
vertices of an icosahedron. Each camera has a 90◦field of
view, producing overlapping perspective images that jointly
cover the viewing sphere. We reproject these images into
equirectangular space and combine overlapping RGB and
alpha values using squared-cosine angular weights, giving
greater weight to views in which a pixel lies closer to the
icosahedron vertex. We do not blend depth values, since
averaging depth from different views can create incorrect
intermediate geometry. Instead, each ERP pixel takes its
depth from the camera best viewing its ray direction. We
render the perspective views at 1024 × 1024 resolution as a
supersampling step before stitching them into a 2048×1024
panorama.
Conditioning and masked panoramic completion.
At
the selected anchor pose, we construct a 2048 × 1024
panoramic RGB condition using the icosahedron renderer.
Alpha values above 0.5 are masked as pixels to preserve
within the keep mask. Before VAE inversion, we conser-
vatively downsample the keep mask to the latent patch res-
olution. This ensures that a latent patch is only preserved
when its entire region is observed, preventing the unren-
dered buffer from incorrectly seeping into the output as an
observed region. We use 50 inversion and sampling steps,
seed 29, insertion parameter 10, guidance scale 2.5, and
source-key scale 1.05.
Depth estimation and alignment.
PaGeR [3] automati-
cally selects an indoor or outdoor prediction mode and re-
turns both panoramic depth and sky-validity mask. We fil-
ter out unreliable depth near discontinuities by comparing
each pixel with its four immediate neighbors. A pixel is re-
jected when both the absolute and relative depth difference
exceed 0.1. Scale alignment uses pixels that are both ob-
served in the keep mask and valid according to PaGeR. We
then discard the outer 10% of the depth-ratio distribution
and use the median of the remaining ratios to calculate the
scale alignment. Pixels classified as sky are assigned the
farthest valid depth so that generated sky content is placed
in the background, while rejected pixels from depth discon-
tinuities are not lifted.
3D Gaussian initialization and filtering.
When unpro-
jecting generated content into 3D, a new Gaussian is cre-
ated for each pixel outside the keep mask using the aligned
panoramic depth.
Each Gaussian is initialized as a sur-
fel, where its horizontal and vertical scales match the an-
11


gular area covered by one ERP pixel at the predicted
depth [12, 41]. The orientation of the surfel is aligned with
the viewing ray. The RGB value at the pixel determines the
color of the Gaussian, and its opacity is set to 0.99.
Final rendering and optional filling.
Final ERP frames
are rendered using the icosahedron renderer at 2048 × 1024
resolution. We recover the final RGB values by removing
alpha premultiplication wherever the rendered alpha is at
least 0.1; pixels below this threshold are marked as uncov-
ered. Our direct output retains these uncovered regions. For
the optional OURS + FILL variant, we apply Telea inpaint-
ing independently with a radius of 15 to the uncovered pix-
els in each frame. This filling is performed entirely in im-
age space after rendering and does not modify the Gaussian
scene or original content in each frame.
C. Evaluation Protocol
C.1. Evaluation Data
Evaluation-set construction.
We construct the evalua-
tion set using perspective extractions from ground truth
equirectangular videos in Argus [22] evaluation set. The
camera trajectories and fields of view are also provided by
Argus. We evaluate on both their real and simulated cam-
era trajectories and follow their implementation for input
extraction: 25 frames at 5 FPS with a fixed width of 480
pixels. Since the scope of our method is mainly for predom-
inantly static scenes, we do not use all 101 videos from the
original Argus test set. We instead filter a subset by exclud-
ing clips that contain substantial scene dynamics, severe
physical camera obstruction, or unusably low perspective-
view resolution. Minor dynamics such as isolated people or
moving water are retained. This results in 35 scenes across
both real and simulated trajectories, totaling to 70 evaluated
cases.
Generating captions.
We use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [2]
to generate captions for each video in the filtered subset of
Argus [22] test set. We feed the extracted perspective video
at 1 FPS to the VLM and use the same prompt across both
real and simulated camera trajectories. In addition, a pre-
fix is added to the generated caption, “This is a panorama
image. The photo shows”, as DiT360 [8] is always trained
with this prefix. When using the prompt for other baseline
models, we remove the prefix. We provide the prompt used
for generation as well as the IDs of each video in the evalua-
tion subset and generated prompts at Table S1 and Table S2.

C.2. Baseline Setup
We use publicly released implementations and checkpoints
for Argus [22], CubeComposer [18], Viewpoint [6], and
Imagine360 [27]. All methods receive the same extracted
perspective input video and scene caption (if applicable).
Their outputs are evaluated over the same 25 timestamps
spanning 5 seconds. Imagine360 and CubeComposer gen-
erate slightly more than 25 frames, so we pad the inputs
and then trim outputs to the first 25 frames. We retain each
method’s camera-conditioning setup and output resolution;
resolution normalization is instead performed inside each
metric.
C.3. Metrics
Input-view fidelity.
We use PSNR and LPIPS to measure
preservation of observed input content. Generated and ref-
erence ERPs are first downsampled to the same resolution
of 1024×512 (if applicable). At every timestep, the ground
truth camera rotation and field of view are used to extract
corresponding perspective views from the ERP frame. We
use the same Argus implementation for extracting these
generated views as the ground truth inputs. The resulting
images have the same 480 × H resolution as the inputs and
are compared over the complete frame.
Our protocol for extracting these views for comparison
differs from the evaluation used by Argus. Argus evalu-
ates over the union of each ERP-level mask, resulting in one
ERP-space mask applied over every ERP frame for calculat-
ing PSNR and LPIPS. This formulation for evaluation has
two limitations: (1) a temporal union contains all scene con-
tent observed at some point in the video, but under camera
motion or scene dynamics, those directions are not neces-
sarily observed at all timesteps. Reconstruction metrics are
therefore being evaluated over not only input-reconstructed
regions, but also generated regions, where reconstruction
metrics do not work well as a form of evaluation. (2) stan-
dard LPIPS using a standard VGG backbone is trained on
regular perspective images with rectangular shape. How-
ever in this setting, LPIPS is calculated within the ERP-
12


distorted image space, and must only be calculated over an
irregularly-shaped mask. This departs from the unmasked
perspective-image input that LPIPS is usually calculated on.
Perceptual quality.
We evaluate VBench [13] Imaging
Quality, Aesthetic Quality, and Motion Smoothness follow-
ing the same Argus protocol. Each ERP video is converted
into front, back, left, and right 256×256 perspective videos;
top and bottom views are excluded. Each dimension is com-
puted independently for each face, and face scores are aver-
aged to obtain a video score.
Geometric consistency.
MEt3R [1] is evaluated on the
same four cubemap faces that perceptual quality evaluation
uses, also at 256 × 256 resolution. We evaluate at 5, 2,
and 1 FPS by selecting the frame nearest to each sampling
timestamp and forming pairs between consecutive selected
frames. The 5 FPS setting compares adjacent frames from
the original video, while 2 and 1 FPS compare frames sep-
arated by progressively greater temporal difference. This
multi-framerate evaluation distinguishes short-term frame-
by-frame smoothness from longer-range geometric persis-
tence.
Scene coverage.
We measure scene coverage using the
binary visibility ERP masks produced by our renderer as fi-
nal output. Pixels with alpha above 0.1 are considered ”cov-
ered” and set to full opacity. This threshold retains valid but
partially transparent support near Gaussian boundaries; an
otherwise stricter threshold would incorrectly classify as a
hole and then (optionally) inpaint.
D. Ablation Details
D.1. Framework Ablations
The framework ablations are evaluated on all 35 scenes un-
der both real and simulated trajectories.
Unless directly
modified by an ablation, all variants use the same config-
urations as the original full pipeline. We evaluate on direct
rendered outputs without the optional filling.
Panorama-Only 3D.
We retain the same reconstruction,
anchor selection, panorama completion, depth estimation,
and depth alignment as the full method. However, rather
than lifting only the unobserved region and merging it
with the input reconstruction, we lift the entire completed
panorama into a new 3DGS. The original reconstructed
3DGS is then discarded, so both observed and gener-
ated scene content are represented solely by the generated
panorama’s geometry and appearance.
This isolates the
benefit of retaining the original reconstructed observations,
rather than representing the complete scene through a single
generated panorama.
Unmasked 3D Fusion.
The full method prevents gener-
ated content from being lifted wherever the input recon-
struction already provides support. In this variant, that re-
striction is removed; the complete outpainted panorama is
converted into a full 3DGS and fused with the reconstructed
3DGS. As a result, generated regions of the scene can over-
lap with their corresponding input-reconstructed regions.
This tests whether visibility-aware fusion is necessary to
protect input-reconstructed geometry and appearance.
Single-Frame Conditioning.
Instead of creating the
DiT360 condition from the full-video reconstruction, this
variant projects the middle input frame into an ERP at its
corresponding stabilized pose. All downstream stages, in-
cluding depth estimation, masked lifting, and rendering,
remain unchanged.
The difference is therefore whether
panoramic completion can condition on observations gath-
ered over the entire video or only the content visible in one
perspective frame.
D.2. Persistence Ablation.
Per-Frame Completion.
This variant renders an incom-
plete stabilized panorama at each output pose and completes
all 25 frames independently with the same prompt, seed,
and sampling configuration. The completed panoramas are
assembled directly into a video, bypassing generated-depth
estimation, lifting, and fusion. This tests whether temporal
persistence can arise from repeatedly querying the image
prior without persisting content through a shared 3D repre-
sentation.
