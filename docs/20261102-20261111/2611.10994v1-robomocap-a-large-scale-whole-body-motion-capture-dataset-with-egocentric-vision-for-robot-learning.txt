## Abstract 

Humanoid robots must coordinate locomotion and manipu-lation to navigate complex environments and perform every-day tasks. Learning such behaviours from human demon-strations requires accurate whole-body motion paired with egocentric vision, particularly for lower-body movements and foot placements that are frequently invisible from a first-person perspective. However, existing egocentric datasets often lack high-fidelity optical motion capture or system-atic coverage of diverse loco-manipulation behaviours. To address this gap, we introduce RoboMoCap, a large-scale multimodal dataset combining egocentric vision with marker-based optical motion capture, specifically designed for locomotion-centric humanoid learning. RoboMoCap comprises over 200 hours of recordings from 14 partici-pants, featuring six head-mounted cameras, wrist-mounted visual and inertial sensing, and optical full-body motion 

1arXiv:2611.10994v1 [cs.RO] 7 Nov 2026 capture at 120 Hz. The dataset systematically covers di-verse locomotion primitives, dynamic whole-body move-ments, and loco-manipulation tasks, including variable-speed walking, turning, constrained navigation, terrain traversal, object transportation, and manipulation during locomotion. It provides temporally aligned multimodal ob-servations and metric whole-body motion representations, enabling the study of human movement beyond directly ob-servable body regions. We systematically evaluate exist-ing methods on three benchmark tasks: egocentric full-body motion reconstruction, foot contact and placement es-timation, and human-to-humanoid motion retargeting. By pairing egocentric vision with high-fidelity human motion across diverse locomotion and interaction scenarios, Robo-MoCap provides a foundation for egocentric motion under-standing and humanoid learning from human demonstra-tions. 

## 1. Introduction 

Humanoid robots are increasingly expected to operate in human environments, where successful task execution re-quires the seamless coordination of locomotion, balance, and manipulation [18, 23, 30, 35]. Everyday activities, such as carrying objects across a room, navigating narrow passages, stepping over obstacles, or approaching a table to place an object, demand continuous coordination between the upper and lower body. Rather than treating locomotion and manipulation as independent skills, these behaviours re-quire adapting foot placement, body posture, and movement trajectories to the surrounding environment and ongoing in-teractions. Learning such coordinated behaviours from hu-man demonstrations offers a promising direction for devel-oping versatile humanoid robots. However, this approach fundamentally depends on datasets that capture not only what humans observe and interact with, but also how their entire bodies move throughout these activities. Egocentric sensing provides a natural perspective for learning from human behaviour, capturing visual obser-vations closely aligned with the information available to an embodied agent. Large-scale egocentric datasets have demonstrated the value of first-person recordings for ac-tivity understanding, human-object interaction, and robot learning. Nevertheless, recovering precise whole-body mo-tion from egocentric observations remains challenging. In particular, the lower body is frequently outside the field of view of head-mounted cameras, making foot placement, support transitions, and lower-body articulation difficult to infer directly. Furthermore, similar head trajectories may correspond to substantially different locomotion patterns, while head orientation does not necessarily align with body heading or travel direction. These ambiguities become par-ticularly pronounced during turning, lateral or backward walking, dynamic posture transitions, and object transporta-tion. Consequently, connecting egocentric perception with accurate whole-body motion supervision is essential for un-derstanding locomotion and its interaction with manipula-tion. Existing datasets capture complementary aspects of hu-man motion. AMASS [38] provides detailed human kinematics but lacks synchronised egocentric observations. Nymeria [36] pairs wearable first-person sensing with full-body motion, while EgoHTR [6] focuses on challenging terrain traversal. More recently, ACE-Data-0 [8] com-bines egocentric observations with optical motion capture, and Ego-Exo4D-HM [37] provides dense motion recon-structions from multi-view recordings. However, datasets combining synchronised egocentric observations and high-fidelity optical whole-body supervision across diverse loco-motion and loco-manipulation behaviours remain limited. Table 1 situates RoboMoCap within this broader landscape, comparing sensing modalities, motion supervision, and be-havioural coverage. To bridge this gap, we introduce RoboMoCap , a large-scale multimodal human motion dataset designed for locomotion-centric humanoid learning. RoboMoCap com-bines a custom six-camera head-mounted sensing platform with wearable wrist sensors and a marker-based optical mo-tion capture system. The wearable cameras record comple-mentary scene-facing and body-facing perspectives, while optical motion capture measures full-body motion at 120 Hz. Through temporal synchronisation and spatial cali-bration, we associate egocentric observations with metric body motion, tracked camera trajectories, and fitted SMPL-X [43] representations. Crucially, marker-based optical motion capture provides whole-body supervision indepen-dently of egocentric visual inference, enabling direct eval-uation of lower-body motion even when the corresponding body regions are not visible in the wearable cameras. A central design objective of RoboMoCap is to capture human locomotion beyond conventional forward walking. To this end, our collection protocol systematically organises activities into locomotion primitives, posture and whole-body dynamics, and loco-manipulation. Locomotion tasks span variations in speed, movement direction, turning, ob-stacle negotiation, and terrain conditions, while dynamic activities introduce changes in posture, support configura-tion, and movement intensity. Loco-manipulation tasks fur-ther couple locomotion with object acquisition, transporta-tion, and placement under varying loads and environmental constraints. Together, these activities capture both isolated motion primitives and coordinated whole-body behaviours, enabling systematic analysis of how locomotion adapts to environmental conditions and concurrent manipulation de-mands. ## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Motion Capture Datasets 

Motion capture provides high-quality references for hu-man motion modelling and synthesis. AMASS [38] unifies marker-based datasets under a common body-model param-eterisation, while LAFAN1 [17] and 100STYLE [40] offer diverse locomotion and stylised gait sequences. Interaction-oriented datasets such as GRAB [51], OMOMO [29], and HUMOTO [32] extend motion capture to whole-body ob-ject manipulation. Although these datasets provide detailed body motion, they generally lack corresponding real ego-centric observations, limiting their direct use for learning vision-conditioned humanoid behaviours. Moreover, loco-motion and object manipulation are typically studied sepa-rately rather than through systematic variations of their co-ordination. 

2.2. Egocentric Human Motion Datasets 

Early egocentric pose datasets employ body-facing cameras to observe the wearer. EgoCap [47] and Mo 2Cap 2 [56] ex-plore this setting, while xR-EgoPose [52], UnrealEgo2 [1], and EgoWholeBody [54] provide synthetic training data. Real-world datasets such as UnrealEgo-RW [1], EMHI [13], Ego-Elec [63], and Eva-3M [10] further sup-port egocentric body reconstruction. Scene-facing datasets, including EgoBody [60], HPS [16], and ADT [42], capture human activities in their environmental context. EgoBody3M [61], Ego-Exo4D [15], Nymeria [36], and NymeriaPlus [11] extend real-world egocentric motion capture through larger-scale recordings and alternative body supervision. FRAME [7] further addresses real-world egocentric body reconstruc-tion, while EgoSim [19] and EgoWholeView [21] explore multi-camera wearable configurations for whole-body motion capture. More recently, EgoHTR [6] targets terrain traversal, while ACE-Data-0 [8] combines egocentric observations with optical motion capture for long-horizon household activities. Despite these advances, large-scale optical supervision with systematic coverage of locomotion dynamics, foot placement, and loco-manipulation remains relatively limited. 

2.3. Human Motion for Humanoid Learning 

Human motion datasets increasingly support humanoid control through retargeting and imitation. Humanoid-X [39] derives 240 h of motion from internet videos, while PHUMA [27] curates 73 h of physically refined locomotion and BONES-SEED [34] provides 288 h of motion capture for humanoid training. OmniRetarget [57] further enables interaction-preserving retargeting involving objects and ter-rain. However, these motion-oriented datasets generally lack paired egocentric observations. Conversely, egocentric datasets for robot learning, such as EgoDex [20] and EgoVerse [44], provide large-scale human manipulation demonstrations, while HumanVerse-500 [55] and EgoExplorer [62] extend first-person observa-tions to whole-body loco-manipulation using estimated hu-man motion. RoboMoCap bridges these directions by pair-ing egocentric observations with optical whole-body mo-tion across diverse locomotion and loco-manipulation tasks, supporting egocentric motion understanding, humanoid re-targeting, and vision-conditioned humanoid learning.

3.2. Capture Environment 

A central design consideration of RoboMoCap is to cap-ture diverse whole-body movements while maintaining re-liable optical motion tracking. Data collection is con-ducted in NOKOV’s motion capture studio, comprising a 

7 × 7 m recording area (Fig. 2-(D)). Unlike conventional manipulation-centred capture setups [20, 44, 51] that pri-marily focus on stationary workspaces, our recording en-vironment accommodates continuous locomotion, changes in movement direction, dynamic postural transitions, and interactions with surrounding objects. The recording area is configured to support a wide range of locomotion be-haviours, including straight-line walking, turning, circular and figure-eight trajectories, and repeated shuttle move-ments. Task-specific arrangements introduce spatial con-straints and environmental variations, including narrow pas-sages, obstacles, inclined surfaces, and changes in eleva-tion. For loco-manipulation activities, interaction areas are arranged to accommodate object pickup, transportation, and placement at different locations and heights. Objects with varying physical properties and carrying requirements are incorporated to capture the coordination between locomo-tion, posture, and manipulation. These configurations allow both controlled motion primitives and continuous whole-body activities to be recorded within a common sensing en-5vironment. 

## 4. RoboMoCap Dataset 

4.1. Design Principles and Action Taxonomy 

RoboMoCap is designed to support egocentric whole-body motion understanding and humanoid robot learning, with a particular emphasis on locomotion and its coordination with manipulation. We adopt four key design principles to guide data collection. 

(i) Locomotion-centric diversity: Human locomotion in-volves substantially more than steady forward walking. Everyday movement requires changes in speed, direction, body orientation, and foot placement to accommodate en-vironmental constraints. We therefore emphasise diverse locomotion behaviours, including turning, lateral and back-ward walking, obstacle avoidance, terrain traversal, and dy-namic postural transitions. This systematic coverage aims to capture a broad distribution of lower-body movements relevant to humanoid locomotion. 

(ii) Egocentric motion observability: A fundamental chal-lenge in egocentric motion reconstruction is that the lower body, particularly the feet, is frequently outside the cam-era’s field of view. Moreover, similar head movements can correspond to different body configurations and foot place-ments. We design activities that expose these ambiguities, including changes in gaze, walking direction, and body ori-entation. By pairing egocentric observations with optical whole-body motion measurements, RoboMoCap provides supervision for both visible and unobserved body move-ments. 

(iii) Locomotion–manipulation coordination: Humanoid robots must coordinate locomotion with object interaction rather than execute these behaviours independently. We therefore incorporate tasks involving object pickup, trans-portation, placement, and manipulation during locomotion. Variations in carrying configuration, object load, and navi-gation constraints capture how upper-body activities influ-ence whole-body coordination and locomotion strategies. 

(iv) Structured and continuous behaviours: We combine controlled motion primitives with extended, multi-stage ac-tivities to capture both individual movement patterns and their temporal transitions. Structured task variations en-able comparisons across movement conditions, while con-tinuous recordings preserve natural transitions between lo-comotion, postural adjustments, and object interactions. This design supports both fine-grained motion analysis and longer-horizon embodied tasks. 

Action Taxonomy. To systematically capture diverse whole-body behaviours, we organise RoboMoCap around three core action categories: locomotion primitives, pos-ture and whole-body dynamics, and loco-manipulation. Be-yond these core protocols, the recorded dataset also in-cludes static manipulation, environment-coupled whole-body activities, dynamic recovery, human–human loco-manipulation, and long-horizon composite tasks. These ad-ditional activities extend the dataset towards more complex whole-body interactions and multi-stage behaviours. Tab. 2 summarises the taxonomy and its principal variations. The collection protocol comprises 291 predefined tasks, system-atically varying locomotion patterns and body configura-tions. 

4.2. Collection Protocol 

Data collection follows predefined protocols specifying task instructions, scene configurations, repetitions, and move-ment variations. Participants are instructed to move natu-rally at a comfortable pace without imitating robotic move-ments. Task conditions are systematically varied through movement direction, path geometry, obstacle arrangements, object loads, and interaction targets. Left- and right-sided variations are explicitly instructed to encourage balanced coverage. Repeated movements are recorded in continuous takes rather than isolated repetitions, preserving natural transi-tions between motion phases. Loco-manipulation tasks cap-ture object acquisition, transport, and placement, includ-ing activities requiring simultaneous walking and manip-ulation. To isolate the influence of locomotion, selected static manipulation tasks are paired with corresponding loco-manipulation protocols, using the same participant and objects. Long-horizon tasks specify initial scene configu-rations and goals while allowing participants to determine their own execution strategies. Each take is associated with participant and action iden-tifiers, task conditions, and execution metadata. Objects are reset between takes, while carried loads and reach-ing heights are adjusted to individual comfort. Equipment checks and task-specific safety procedures are incorporated for dynamic movements and recovery activities.

4.4. Multimodal Annotation Generation 

Figure 3 illustrates the RoboMoCap capture and annotation pipeline, which integrates synchronised egocentric obser-vations with optical motion capture to generate consistent representations of full-body motion, articulated hand poses, and object trajectories. 

Whole-Body Motion Reconstruction. To obtain a con-sistent parametric representation of human motion, we fit the SMPL-X body model [43] to the optical motion capture measurements. The fitting process leverages the articulated skeleton reconstructed by NOKOV for pose initialisation, while the observed 3D marker trajectories provide geomet-ric constraints on the body surface. We first estimate body shape from individual recording sessions and aggregate these estimates to obtain a consistent participant-specific shape. With the body shape fixed, we optimise the time-varying body pose and global translation, together with session-specific marker attachment offsets, to minimise discrepancies between observed marker positions and their corresponding locations on the fitted SMPL-X body. 8The resulting SMPL-X parameters provide a consistent representation of articulated body motion, including global position, body orientation, joint articulation, and mesh ge-ometry. Unlike motion estimated solely from egocentric ob-servations, the fitting process is constrained by optical Mo-Cap measurements of the entire body, including the legs and feet, which are frequently outside the wearable cameras’ field of view. This enables the derivation of lower-body kinematics and foot trajectories for locomotion-oriented analysis and evaluation. 

Hand Motion Estimation. To complement the optical whole-body measurements with articulated hand motion, we use Grounded API [2]. Its multi-view hand tracking model processes calibrated egocentric camera observations to estimate metric 3D hand poses represented using the MANO hand model [48]. By combining information from multiple camera views, the method estimates hand articu-lation under challenging egocentric conditions, including partial visibility and hand–object occlusion. Unlike the optically measured body markers, these hand annotations are vision-derived estimates. We therefore distinguish es-timated finger articulation from optical body motion mea-surements in the released annotations. To produce hand reconstructions that are consistent with the body meshes, we align Grounded API’s local hand pose with the SMPL-X wrist pose. We fine-tune the model on a small training set (34.9 minutes), use the validation set to select the best checkpoint, and report the Procrustes-Aligned Mean Per-Joint Position Error (PA-MPJPE) on a test set (9.2 minutes). We use the same subjects for these splits as in the rest of the dataset. 

Scene Reconstruction. We reconstruct the static 3D en-vironment scene using MapAnything [26], a feed-forward metric 3D reconstruction model. For this, we use the front-facing ego stereo cameras, together with their calibrated intrinsics and camera poses obtained from NOKOV opti-cal tracking, to estimate scene depth. The predicted depth maps are back-projected into 3D and transformed into the NOKOV world coordinate system using the corresponding optically tracked camera poses. Consequently, the recon-structed scene geometry is directly expressed in the same coordinate frame as the captured human motion, without requiring any additional point-cloud alignment. 

Task Annotations. We provide detailed textual descrip-tions for each task, specifying the intended activity and its execution conditions. Each recording is additionally anno-tated with the start and end timestamps of individual task episodes, enabling the extraction of temporally localised ac-tion sequences. 

Data Quality. Each session passes automated checks on stream integrity, calibration and marker labelling before release. Ego and mocap streams are aligned by cross-correlating the head-IMU gyroscope with the angular speed of the head-mounted rigid body (median peak correlation 0.985); offsets estimated in independent windows of a take deviate from the per-take estimate by a median of 0.5 ms (95th percentile 26 ms). The fitted soles rest a median of 10 mm above the floor (IQR 4–16 mm), consistent with footwear on a barefoot body model. Per-frame validity masks are provided for the body, marker, skeleton, head-rig, camera-pose and object streams.

5.2. Foot Contact and Placement Estimation 

Task. We further evaluate lower-body reconstruction through foot contact, placement accuracy, and kinematic plausibility for the Sec. 5.1 baselines. Contact is estimated from reconstructed foot joints using a shared height- and velocity-based rule, with thresholds calibrated on the train-ing participants. 

Metrics. We evaluate foot contact with the F1 score against contact labels derived from sole markers, following prior contact evaluation protocols [45]. Since the baselines do not predict contact explicitly, we derive it from their foot joints using a single height and speed rule, calibrated once on ground-truth bodies of the training participants and shared by all methods. Following [46, 58, 59], we measure foot skating as the horizontal speed of the foot during reference stance ( Skate ), and ground penetration as the mean depth of the lowest foot joint below the floor [50, 59]. We further introduce two footstep-level measures: the position error of each planted foot relative to the ground-truth body ( Foot-print ), and the drift-free step displacement error, expressed in the frame of the preceding footprint ( Step ). 

Results. Tab. 6 reports results on 543 test sessions ( ≈18 h), covering approximately 50k stance intervals. EgoAllo per-forms best across all metrics, achieving 90.5% contact F1, 85.1 mm footprint error, 154.1 mm step displacement error, and 54.8 mm/s foot skating. Despite this, substantial foot-placement errors persist. Even reference SMPL-X motion achieves only 93.6% contact F1 against sole-marker labels, highlighting the limitations of kinematics-derived contact estimation and the need for explicit lower-body evaluation. 

5.3. Human-to-Humanoid Motion Retargeting 

Task. We evaluate the transfer of human motion to a 29-DoF Unitree G1 [53], assessing both kinematic fi-delity and geometric feasibility. RoboMoCap provides MoCap-derived SMPL-X motion and sole-marker-based foot-contact annotations, enabling evaluation beyond pose similarity. We retarget 543 test sessions ( ≈18 h, 30 Hz), us-ing identical input sequences and a shared robot collision model for all methods. 

Metrics. Kinematic fidelity (Tab. 4) is evaluated against height-scaled SMPL-X motion using 13 corresponding body segments. We report mean per-body position er-ror with a constant horizontal offset removed per sequence (Eg-mpbpe ) and relative to the hip midpoint ( Empbpe ) [33], alongside foot and wrist position errors and geodesic orien-tation error. Feasibility and contact preservation (Tab. 5) are assessed using the robot’s collision geometry. We re-port foot-contact F1 against sole-marker labels, foot skating (speed > 0.1 m/s during shared stance), ground penetration (above 5 mm and maximum depth), and foot floating dur-ing human stance. We additionally measure near-joint-limit frames (within 0.01 rad) and self-collision frames (penetra-tion > 5 mm). A clip is considered successful if its root-relative error is below 15 cm for at least 99% of frames, maximum ground penetration is below 5 cm, and self-collision occurs in at most 5% of frames. 

Baselines. We compare three retargeting approaches: GMR [4], which solves inverse kinematics on scaled hu-man targets; PHC [33], whose shape-fitting and motion-12 optimisation procedure we adapt to G1; and OmniRetar-get [57], which incorporates contact and kinematic con-straints. 

Results. OmniRetarget achieves the highest kinematic fi-delity, reducing global body-position error from 90.7 mm (GMR) to 34.8 mm, with approximately fivefold lower wrist error. It also achieves the best contact preservation (87.7% F1), lowest skating rate (2.2%), and minimal ground penetration (0.2 cm maximum). PHC achieves low root-relative error (64.9 mm) but substantially higher global error (173.9 mm) and ground penetration (up to 37.1 cm). GMR exhibits ground penetration in 72.5% of frames, yielding only 7.4% clip success. Despite its superior motion fidelity, OmniRetarget achieves 53.6% clip success, compared with 79.6% for PHC, which exhibits substantially fewer self-collisions (1.1%). All methods approach joint limits in over 70% of frames, while OmniRetarget and GMR exhibit ap-proximately 10% self-collision rates. These findings reveal a trade-off between motion fidelity and geometric feasibil-ity, highlighting the challenges of retargeting diverse whole-body movements to humanoid robots. 

## 6. Conclusion 

We introduced RoboMoCap, a large-scale multimodal dataset that bridges egocentric perception and high-fidelity whole-body motion capture, with a particular emphasis on locomotion and loco-manipulation. By combining synchro-nised wearable sensing with marker-based optical motion capture, RoboMoCap provides accurate motion supervi-sion for lower-body movements and foot placements that are often unobservable from egocentric views. Its struc-tured collection protocol captures diverse locomotion pat-terns, whole-body dynamics, and object interactions, en-abling systematic investigation of how humans coordinate locomotion and manipulation. Our benchmarks provide reference evaluations for egocentric full-body motion re-construction, foot contact and placement estimation, and human-to-humanoid retargeting. By bridging egocentric perception and accurate whole-body motion, RoboMoCap opens new opportunities for learning coordinated locomo-tion and manipulation from human demonstrations. 

Limitations. Despite its scale and motion diversity, Robo-MoCap is collected within an instrumented optical mo-tion capture environment, limiting the range of environ-ments and terrain conditions compared with unconstrained real-world settings. Although marker-based tracking pro-vides precise motion measurements, occasional marker oc-clusions and body-model fitting errors may affect the re-sulting annotations. Furthermore, the dataset covers a finite set of participants, objects, and interaction scenarios, and does not represent the full variability of human locomotion and manipulation. Finally, differences in human and hu-manoid morphology, kinematics, and physical capabilities mean that recorded motions cannot always be directly trans-ferred to robotic platforms without retargeting and control adaptations. Extending RoboMoCap to more diverse envi-ronments and validating its utility through downstream hu-manoid execution are promising directions for future work.
