# RefineSplat: Reconstruction-Guided Object-Centric Gaussian Splatting for Open-Vocabulary Querying 

Yiren Lu, Disheng Liu, Sreya Srinidhi, Yunlai Zhou, Yiran Qiao, Chaoda Song, Tuo Liang, Jing Ma, Huan Wang, Yu Yin 

Abstract —Open-vocabulary 3D scene understanding aims to segment and retrieve arbitrary objects in 3D scenes using natural language queries. Most existing methods typically follow a splat-then-segment paradigm, where 3D Gaussian Splatting is first re-constructed and then augmented with labels or language features for subsequent segmentation. However, this pipeline often suffers from view inconsistency, ambiguous object boundaries, and weak object-level correspondence, especially in complex static and dynamic scenes. Building on our prior Segment-then-Splat frame-work, we present RefineSplat, a reconstruction-guided extension that retains its object-centric paradigm: it first tracks objects in 2D and then reconstructs each object with a dedicated set of 3D Gaussians, preserving explicit object-Gaussian correspondence for unified open-vocabulary segmentation and retrieval. To improve robustness, we further close the loop between 2D tracking and 3D reconstruction. Since off-the-shelf video trackers can produce unreliable masks that propagate errors into object reconstruction, we introduce a reconstruction-guided feedback mechanism that refines object tracking using rendered masks and multi-view consistency. Moreover, instead of assigning each object a single language embedding, we propose a viewpoint-aware embedding assignment strategy, where each object maintains multiple view-clustered embeddings to capture view-dependent semantic cues. We also introduce Blender-OVS , a synthetic benchmark of static and dynamic sequences with exact object meshes and point clouds, enabling direct evaluation of extracted geometry. Experiments on static and dynamic benchmarks show improved rendered-view segmentation and lower object-level Chamfer distances, with competitive scene reconstruction quality. 

Index Terms —Open-Vocabulary Scene Understanding, 3D Gaussian Splatting, Language Embedded Scene Representation, Language-guided Segmentation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

3D open-vocabulary querying is a key step toward language-driven interaction with 3D environments, allowing users to localize, segment, and retrieve objects without predefined cat-egory labels. Such a capability is fundamental for large-scale scene exploration, scene understanding, robotic navigation and manipulation, where free-form language serves as a natural interface between human intent and machine perception. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] has recently become a compelling representation for this problem because it supports

However, standard 3DGS is primarily a radiance representation: its anisotropic Gaussian primitives encode geometry and appearance, but not ob-ject identity or language-aligned semantics. To enable open-vocabulary querying, recent methods [2]–[5] typically augment a reconstructed 3DGS scene with semantic labels or vision-language features. We refer to this family as the splat-then-segment paradigm because these methods first reconstruct the scene with 3DGS and subsequently assign semantic or identity embeddings to individual Gaussians for object segmentation and open-vocabulary querying. Although it benefits from the recon-struction quality of 3DGS, it inherits a structural mismatch between scene-level radiance optimization and object-level language querying. First , the Gaussians are optimized to explain the entire scene rather than to form object-level entities. Without explicit constraints on object boundaries and fine-grained geometry, individual Gaussians may overlap or span multiple objects, and object boundaries are only implicitly captured through appearance. This leads to object-level geometric ambiguity . Second , language supervision is often imposed through alpha-blended feature rendering. This constrains the accumulated feature along a camera ray, but does not guarantee that each contributing Gaussian has an object-consistent semantic meaning. The resulting semantic ambiguity is especially pronounced around occlusion bound-aries, thin structures, and regions where multiple objects project to nearby pixels. Consequently, a method may render a plausible 2D relevance map while lacking a clean 3D object representation that can be directly queried. We reverse this pipeline and propose the Segment-then-Splat framework [6]. Instead of first reconstructing a scene-level Gaussian field and then searching for objects inside it, it first obtains multi-view object masks, assigns object identities to Gaussians, and reconstructs the scene as a composition of object-specific Gaussian sets. Enforcing this object-level decomposition from the very beginning of reconstruction encourages Gaussians to align with object boundaries, and hence reduce geometric ambiguity. Language features are then attached to each object rather than mixed along each viewing ray, reducing both geometric and semantic ambiguity. Related studies, including COB-GS [7], COS3D [8], and ObjectGS [9], also emphasize object-level structure in Gaus-sian representations. This paper presents RefineSplat , a reconstruction-guided ex-tension of Segment-then-Splat that closes the loop between 2D supervision and 3D object reconstruction (Fig. 1(c)). Our key insight is that an intermediate object-specific reconstruction consolidates evidence across multiple views and can thus serve as a geometric consistency signal for assessing the reliability of its 2D supervision. We render reconstructed objects into the training views and compare their support with the tracked masks to reject globally unreliable tracks (track-level filtering) and select suitable observations for object-level supervision (observation-level filtering). The retained masks then provide cleaner supervision for the final object-specific Gaussian opti-mization, preventing tracking errors from propagating into 3D. We instead introduce a viewpoint-aware embedding assignment strategy. By grouping object observations according to viewpoint and maintaining multiple view-clustered embeddings for each object, the pro-posed strategy preserves view-dependent semantic cues and enables more discriminative open-vocabulary querying. Finally, we address an evaluation gap in open-vocabulary 3D segmentation. Together with direct 3D evaluation, Blender-OVS complements rendered-view metrics and enables a more faithful assessment of 3D open-vocabulary segmentation. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Gaussian Scene Representations 

Neural radiance fields (NeRF) [16] have driven substantial progress in novel-view synthesis by representing a scene as a continuous volumetric field and rendering it with differen-tiable volume integration. However, their dense ray sampling and repeated network evaluation make training and render-ing costly, especially when additional semantic or language fields are optimized on top of them. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] replaces implicit volumetric sampling with explicit anisotropic Gaussian primitives and fast differentiable raster-ization, achieving high-fidelity reconstruction with real-time rendering. This efficiency has made 3DGS a strong substrate for 3D scene understanding beyond view synthesis. Dynamic 3DGS and 4D Gaussian representations further extend this explicit representation to time-varying scenes [17]– [19]. These methods typically combine a canonical Gaussian representation with time-dependent deformation, enabling re-construction of moving objects and changing visibility. To-gether, static and dynamic Gaussian representations provide the reconstruction backbone for open-vocabulary querying, but their original formulations model appearance and geometry rather than object identity or language semantics. 

B. Language-Embedded Scene Representations 

Open-vocabulary 3D scene understanding requires associ-ating 3D structures with free-form language queries. Early approaches build upon neural radiance fields [13], [20]– [23], distilling features from vision-language or segmentation foundation models into continuous 3D fields. These methods demonstrate the potential of combining neural scene represen-tations with open-vocabulary semantics, but they often inherit the high computational cost of implicit neural fields, especially during optimization and rendering. Gaussian-based methods associate language with explicit primitives or auxiliary feature fields. LEGaussians [2] and LangSplat [3] compress language features for efficient scene-specific learning, while Feature 3DGS [4] distills foundation-model features through multi-view rendering. FMGS [24] combines Gaussian Splatting with multi-resolution hash en-codings and pixel-aligned feature supervision. GAGS [25] ad-dresses inconsistent feature granularity, and LangSplatV2 [26] learns sparse coefficients over a shared dictionary to render language features efficiently. Beyond scene-specific optimiza-tion, OVGaussian [27] trains a cross-scene Gaussian segmen-tation network using cross-modal consistency learning. A complementary line lifts features onto reconstructed Gaussian scenes without additional semantic-feature training. Occam’s LGS [28] aggregates multi-view language features using alpha-blending weights and filters noisy Gaussians. LUDVIG [29] uplifts visual features through weighted ag-gregation and refines them with graph diffusion informed by 3D geometry and feature similarities. These approaches avoid iterative feature-field fitting, but feature aggregation may also introduce semantic ambiguity. For dynamic scenes, language-embedded Gaussian repre-sentations further model time-varying semantics. DGD [30] distills 2D semantic features into dynamic 3D Gaussians, enabling prompt-based segmentation and tracking over time. 4D LangSplat [31] uses object-wise video captions generated by multimodal large language models to construct a 4D lan-guage field for time-sensitive and time-agnostic queries. Lang-Field4D [32] introduces identity-adaptive Gaussian grouping to model time-varying object affiliations and learns continuous spatio-temporal language features. 

C. Instance Grouping and Object-Level Geometry 

Instance-aware methods explicitly address the grouping of Gaussian primitives. Gaussian-Grouping [5] learns object iden-tity encodings from tracked masks, while OpenGaussian [33] learns point-level features and associates them with language. GAGA [34] associates masks across views through a 3D-aware memory bank, reducing dependence on continuous camera trajectories. SAGA [35] learns scale-gated affinity features for multi-granularity segmentation from visual prompts. Ob-jectGS [9] couples object-aware reconstruction and scene un-derstanding. SuperGSeg [36] organizes Gaussians into sparse Super-Gaussians carrying language features and supporting hierarchical segmentation. COS3D [8] connects instance and language fields through collaborative training and adaptive query refinement. Other methods modify Gaussian geometry to improve object boundaries. SAGD [37] introduces a training-free segmen-tation pipeline with decomposition of boundary Gaussians. COB-GS [7] uses semantic-gradient statistics for boundary-adaptive splitting and subsequently restores appearance. BEA-GS [38] optimizes both visible and occluded Gaussian ge-ometry for precise object extraction. These studies highlight that semantic assignment and geometric separation are distinct requirements. Mask-dependent pipelines remain sensitive to errors in their 2D supervision, with motion and changing visibility introducing additional inconsistencies. Following Segment-then-Splat [6], RefineSplat assigns object identities before reconstruction and optimizes object-specific Gaussian groups. III. M ETHOD 

We introduce RefineSplat , an object-centric framework for 3D open-vocabulary segmentation and retrieval in static and dynamic 3D scenes based on Gaussian Splatting. An overview of our reconstruction-guided pipeline is shown in fig. 2. Following Segment-then-Splat [6], our framework decomposes the scene into object-level components prior to reconstruction, reversing the conventional splat-then-segment pipelines and enforcing an explicit object-Gaussian correspondence. Given multi-view object masks produced by an off-the-shelf video tracking model (section III-B), we assign each initialized Gaussian an object ID at each mask granularity, forming object-specific Gaussian sets at both levels (sec-tion III-C1). After that we perform object-level reconstruction (section III-C2), to better preserve Gaussian-object correspon-dence and improve object-level geometry. On top of this backbone, RefineSplat contributes two new components. First, to prevent tracking errors from propagating into the 3D representation, we introduce a reconstruction-guided supervision filtering mechanism (section III-D) that uses intermediate object-level reconstructions as feedback to reject unreliable object tracks and individual mask observa-tions. Second, after reconstruction, we propose a viewpoint-aware embedding assignment strategy (section III-E) that equips each object with multiple view-clustered language em-beddings, capturing view-dependent semantic cues for open-vocabulary querying (section III-F).

This multi-granularity supervi-sion supports both detailed object structure and larger semantic regions while preserving the associated object identities.

E. Viewpoint-aware Embedding Assignment 

Unlike previous methods [6], [33] that collapse all observa-tions of an object into a single object-level embedding, we group object observations according to their camera view-points and aggregate CLIP features within each viewpoint cluster. The intuition is that many objects exhibit view-dependent semantic cues. For example, the packaging bag in fig. 3 bears a Pikachu graphic on its front, providing a distinctive cue for queries such as “Pikachu” that may be absent from other viewpoints. A single globally averaged embedding can dilute this cue, making retrieval from such specific descriptions less discriminative. We first group training views according to their camera poses. For each view t, we construct a pose feature by concatenating the camera center and viewing direction. The pose features are standardized and clustered into K viewpoint groups using average-linkage hierarchical clustering, produc-ing a global view-to-cluster assignment shared by all objects. A. Datasets 1) 2D Pixel-level Open-vocabulary Segmentation : For static scenes, we evaluate rendered-view open-vocabulary seg-mentation on LERF [13] and Mip-NeRF360 [14]. Following prior 3D open-vocabulary segmentation works [2], [3], these benchmarks evaluate segmentation quality in the 2D image space by rendering the queried 3D representation from selected camera views and comparing the resulting masks with pixel-level annotations. For dynamic scenes, we evaluate on Hy-perNeRF [15], following the rendered-view open-vocabulary segmentation protocol of Segment-then-Splat [6]. This pro-tocol measures whether a method can localize language-specified objects from observed viewpoints and provides a direct comparison with existing language-embedded scene representations. However, since the evaluation results are defined on rendered images, these datasets do not directly assess whether the underlying 3D object geometry is complete, consistently segmented across views, or correctly separated from neighboring objects. 

2) 3D Instance-level Open-vocabulary Segmentation : To evaluate open-vocabulary segmentation at the 3D object level, we use six scenes from ScanNet++ [42], which provides manually annotated semantic and instance labels on scan-derived indoor meshes. Additionally we introduce Blender-OVS , a synthetic benchmark with complete and exact object-9

level geometric annotations. While ScanNet++ enables 3D evaluation on real captures, the coverage and accuracy of its reference geometry depend on scanning and reconstruction, and it contains no dynamic objects (table I). Blender-OVS fills both gaps: each scene provides calibrated camera intrinsics and extrinsics, object identities, appearance attributes and mesh geometry in the world coordinate system, all exported directly from the source assets. The images, instance masks, and 3D geometry share the same rendering scene and camera parameters, providing 

exact 2D–3D alignment . This correspondence is defined di-rectly by the renderer, without estimating alignment through reconstruction or scan registration. Each object is paired with language tags linked to the same instance identity used for its rendered masks and source mesh, providing a consistent correspondence between language, image-space annotations, and 3D geometry. These annotations support evaluation of both rendered-view segmentation quality and 3D object extraction quality against the corresponding ground truth. 

3) Benchmark construction : We construct Blender-OVS by collecting diverse 3D assets from publicly available sources [44], [45] and composing them into complex scenes using Blender [46]. The benchmark contains 10 scenes in total: 5 static scenes for evaluating multi-view segmentation under fixed object layouts, and 5 dynamic scenes with object motion and changing visibility ( e.g. , objects entering or leaving the field of view and mutual occlusion).

D. Reconstruction Quality 

In both the 2D pixel-level and 3D instance-level experi-ments, we evaluate scene reconstruction quality using Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) [47], Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) [48], and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [49] between rendered novel views and ground-truth images. PSNR measures pixel-level reconstruc-tion fidelity, SSIM evaluates structural similarity, and LPIPS reflects perceptual image similarity in a learned feature space. Higher PSNR and SSIM indicate better reconstruction quality, while lower LPIPS indicates better perceptual quality. V. E XPERIMENTS 

As mentioned in the previous section, we conduct two types of experiments to evaluate our method: 2D pixel-level open-vocabulary segmentation and 3D instance-level open-vocabulary segmentation. The former (section V-B) focuses on rendered-view segmentation quality, where the predicted 3D representation is queried by language, rendered into se-lected camera views, and compared with pixel-level ground-truth masks. This setting evaluates whether our method can accurately localize language-specified objects from observed viewpoints and produce precise object boundaries in the image space. The latter (section V-C) focuses on object-level 3D ge-ometry quality, where the extracted 3D Gaussians are directly compared with ground-truth 3D object geometry. This setting further examines whether our method can recover complete object geometry and correctly separate target objects from surrounding distractors in 3D space.

D. Ablation Studies 1) Reconstruction-guided Filtering : We ablate the track-and observation-level feedback mechanisms on LERF in table IV. Using the original SAM2 masks without reconstruction-guided feedback provides the baseline perfor-mance. Introducing track-level filtering improves mIoU from 54.672 to 61.704 and mBIoU from 48.935 to 56.182, cor-responding to gains of 7.032 and 7.247 points, respectively. These gains are consistent with the intended role of remov-ing globally inconsistent tracks before final reconstruction, thereby reducing unreliable object-level supervision. Adding observation-level filtering provides a further improvement to 62.845 mIoU and 57.298 mBIoU, while more clearly improv-ing the reconstruction quality relative to track-level filtering. The additional segmentation gains are 1.141 mIoU and 1.116 mBIoU, suggesting that the two filtering stages contribute dif-ferently: track-level filtering provides the main segmentation improvement, while observation-level filtering further refines the segmentation and improves appearance reconstruction. 

2) Viewpoint-aware Embedding Assignment : Table Vcompares different strategies for aggregating multi-view object embeddings on LERF. The per-view strategy performs worst because individual observations can be sensitive to occlusion and viewpoint-specific ambiguity. Averaging all views im-proves robustness but collapses distinct viewpoint-dependent cues into a single embedding. Both view-clustering strate-gies alleviate this issue by preserving multiple representative embeddings for each object. Hierarchical clustering achieves the best results, reaching 62.85 mIoU and 57.30 mBIoU. It improves over multi-view averaging by 3.91 mIoU and 4.20 mBIoU, and over K-means clustering by 3.37 mIoU and 3.44 mBIoU. We attribute the advantage of hierarchical clustering to its ability to adapt to non-uniform camera trajectories, whereas k-means tends to split dense pose regions and merge sparse ones.

These additional feature-processing or 2D segmentation steps increase query latency. VI. L IMITATIONS AND DISCUSSION 

Relational queries. RefineSplat associates language embed-dings with individual Gaussian groups and matches queries to objects independently. It therefore does not explicitly model relations between objects and cannot reliably resolve queries such as “the cup to the left of the plate.” Supporting such queries would require reasoning over the spatial or semantic relationships among multiple objects. 

Computational overhead. Reconstruction-guided feedback requires intermediate reconstruction and additional rendering and mask-consistency checks, followed by reinitialization for final reconstruction. These steps introduce extra computational overhead during scene training compared with optimizing the final representation alone. Although this overhead does not enter inference, reducing the cost of supervision refinement remains an important direction for future work. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented RefineSplat , a reconstruction-guided object-centric framework for open-vocabulary segmentation and re-trieval. By decomposing a scene before reconstruction and optimizing dedicated Gaussian sets for individual objects, our segment-then-splat formulation preserves explicit object-Gaussian correspondence and enables queried objects to be directly retrieved as 3D Gaussian groups. To reduce the impact of noisy tracking supervision, we introduced a reconstruction-guided feedback mechanism that uses intermediate object reconstructions to identify unreliable tracks and masks. We further proposed a viewpoint-aware embedding assignment strategy that maintains multiple view-clustered embeddings for each object, avoiding the loss of discriminative semantic cues caused by averaging heterogeneous observations. We also introduced Blender-OVS , a benchmark with object-level mesh and point-cloud annotations for directly evaluating open-vocabulary object extraction in 3D, complementing con-ventional rendered-view evaluation. Across the evaluated static and dynamic benchmarks, RefineSplat improves rendered-view segmentation and achieves lower Chamfer distances for queried 3D objects.
