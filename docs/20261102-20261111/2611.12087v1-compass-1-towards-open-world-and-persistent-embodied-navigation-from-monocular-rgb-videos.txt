Abstract
Embodied navigation requires agents to achieve persistent spatial awareness and long-horizon
execution stability in open-world environments. However, existing navigation architectures struggle
to sustain both capabilities, remaining effective only over a handful of recent frames. We present
Compass 1, the first embodied navigation foundation model to unify language understanding
and video generation while enabling memory backtracking through nearly two minutes of visual
history.
Without altering the underlying architecture, we introduce History Representation,
which integrates sparse visual keyframes with an explicit History Representation Map to preserve
long-term spatiotemporal context.
To effectively leverage this history, we further introduce
History Utilization, a structured VQA supervision protocol comprising three categories: task-stage
grounding, exploration consistency, and spatiotemporal target retrieval, enabling map-grounded
reasoning and stable persistent long-horizon navigation. To fuel this system, we establish a complete
annotation pipeline for monocular RGB videos and open-source a massive dataset featuring over 500
hours of high-quality real-world interactions and over 2,000 hours of annotated web videos. Trained
mainly on the distribution of 1.0 meter (1m) height camera, Compass 1 generalizes seamlessly
across diverse embodiments and scenarios, ranging from indoor environments to pedestrian-dense
streets, spanning wheeled robot at 0.35m, quadruped dog at 0.5m, humanoid at 0.85m, and aerial
drone at 1.5m. Compass 1 achieves a dominant overall success rate of 79.4% across open-world
evaluations, compared to 17.8% for the strongest baseline. It demonstrates exceptional zero-shot
execution exceeding 90% on foundational tasks, a 250% boost in exploration, and an unparalleled
leap from 0% to 58% in challenging persistent history backtracking.

Introduction
Embodied navigation in unstructured, open-world environments requires physical agents to dynamically
perceive visual signals, reason over high-level task instructions, and generate precise motor controls. Historically,
the field has been dominated by the traditional training-free paradigm [5, 22, 41].
These systems rely
heavily on specialized hardware, such as LiDAR sensors [26, 44] for dense point-cloud acquisition and depth
cameras [10, 42] for spatial geometry, to reconstruct explicit topological maps onto which off-the-shelf Vision-
Language Models (VLMs) are plugged in for decision-making. Despite their modularity, these systems suffer
from severe error accumulation and spatial degradation when confronting complex dynamic scenes, exposing
a fragile generalization ceiling. More recently, emerging agentic navigation paradigms have attempted to
execute decision-making via harness [48] or pre-defined skill primitives [49]. Yet, their practical deployment
remains significantly bottlenecked by high inference latency, leaving them at a preliminary stage for physical
deployment. In stark contrast, large-scale training-based navigation paradigm [45, 47] offers a far more
effective alternative – by unifying perception, reasoning, and execution within a monolithic and streamlined
learning formulation.
Notwithstanding the impressive metrics achieved by recent large-scale training-based navigation approaches [11,
14, 34, 51] on simulated benchmarks [17], a pronounced disconnect emerges in physical deployment: prior arts
overfit to simulation excessively and fail to generalize to real-world environments. For instruction-following
navigation tasks (IFN) [17], these methods frequently exhibit erratic behaviors, such as premature stopping,
reversed turning, drifting off the intended direction specified by language instructions, and failing to stop
2


accurately even when the target is directly in sight. For beyond-the-view navigation tasks (BVN) [45], these
models are prone to left-right turning deadlocks driven by intention inconsistency between consecutive inference
steps. Worse still, existing long-horizon formulations typically either naively feed the entire uncompressed
visual history into the backbone [14] or apply heuristic, non-uniform compression [34]. We observe that
both strategies yield merely spurious context usage, failing to preserve granular spatiotemporal topology and
rendering true history backtracking navigation (HBN) completely unachievable. These pervasive failure modes
underscore the fundamental challenges in building a truly persistent, robust embodied navigation system in
the real-world.
To resolve these deficiencies, we introduce Compass 1, a navigation foundation model that addresses open-
world and persistent embodied navigation without architectural modifications. For long-horizon history
representation, we replace full visual streams with sparsely sampled visual keyframes as input and render their
associated actions into a History Representation Map (HRM), which is processed natively by the backbone
as an image input. We further design three visual-question-answering (VQA) categories encompassing task-
stage grounding, exploration consistency, and spatiotemporal target retrieval, thereby fully unleashing the
history utilization capabilities. To better incorporate spatial information and alleviate the short-horizon
supervision problem inherent to language backbones [45], we naturally house these mechanisms within a
unified understanding and generation backbone.
Beyond algorithmic innovations, a primary bottleneck to achieving robust open-world navigation stems from
the scarcity of high-quality training data. While scaling monocular RGB videos offers a promising avenue
to bridge this gap [45], raw video clips inherently lack metric action trajectories, language instructions, and
the specialized VQA annotations, making the whole labeling process prohibitively expensive. To overcome
this bottleneck, we establish a semi-automated annotation pipeline designed for minimal human overhead.
Human annotators are tasked solely with verifying language accuracy at stage boundaries and inspecting
visualized action trajectories to filter out severe noise, significantly reducing human labor and annotation cost.
Leveraging this pipeline, we curate a massive navigation corpus comprising over 500 hours of high-quality
real-world data and 2,000 hours of annotated web-scale video data, providing an unprecedented multi-modal
foundation to fuel open-world embodied navigation.
We structure our real-world evaluation benchmark around essential navigation capabilities across highly diverse
physical scenarios. Across these evaluations, Compass 1 achieves exceptional zero-shot execution exceeding
90% on foundational tasks, including initial-sight target navigation, basic two-stage navigation, and basic
history backtracking. In exploration tasks covering target-driven navigation and demand-driven navigation,
Compass 1 achieves an average success rate of 84%, delivering a 250% performance improvement over the
strongest baseline. Most notably, across challenging long straight-line navigation, multi-stage navigation,
and persistent history backtracking, Compass 1 demonstrates an unparalleled performance leap, boosting
the average success rate from 5% to 60%. Crucially, despite being trained primarily on a single camera
height distribution at 1.0 meter (1m), Compass 1 exhibits exceptional zero-shot transferability across varied
embodiments, smoothly adapting to distinct camera heights, ranging from wheeled robots at 0.35m and
quadrupeds at 0.5m to humanoids at 0.85m and aerial drones at 1.5m.
1.1
Research Questions
The pivotal gist in this work boils down to – How could a model excel at executing open-world and persistent
navigation while generalizing across diverse scenarios and embodiments?
This breaks into three concrete questions:
• How can we efficiently represent navigation history such that the policy can pinpoint previously observed
targets, track current task stages, and discern how to navigate back over long horizons?
• How can we utilize this history representation to support diverse navigation tasks and learn a generalizable
policy from limited high-quality data?
• How can we establish an effective annotation pipeline for raw video corpora to significantly reduce
human labor and resource overhead?

Contributions
The main contributions are:
• Keyframe-Level Unified Backbone & History Representation Map: To our knowledge, we present the first
keyframe-level foundation model built on a unified backbone for embodied navigation. By condensing
keyframes and actions into a compact HRM, our method grants the agent persistent spatiotemporal
reasoning and nearly two-minute memory backtracking capabilities with zero architectural modification.
• Structured VQA Supervision Protocol: We design three structured VQA categories to fully utilize
the history reasoning capability and effectively resolve physical execution failure modes including
premature stopping, reversed turning, direction drifting, left-right turning deadlocks, and spurious
history utilization.
• Cost-Effective Data Annotation Pipeline: We establish a semi-automated annotation pipeline requiring
human oversight solely at stage boundaries and trajectory noise filtering, enabling the release of a
massive navigation corpus featuring over 500 hours of real-world interactions and over 2,000 hours of
annotated web videos.
• Thorough Physical Evaluation & Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Generalization: Extensive real-world
experiments demonstrate that Compass 1 drastically outperforms strong baselines across all physical
navigation tasks. Remarkably, despite being trained mainly on a fixed camera height distribution, it
generalizes zero-shot across diverse embodiments, and substantial camera height variations.

2.1
History Representation
Given the intrinsic scarcity of high-quality, real-world navigation data, a primary design philosophy of Compass
1 is to preserve the pre-trained foundation backbone without introducing any architectural modifications.
To achieve this, our history representation is built upon two core aspects: keyframe-level sparsification and
history representation map construction.
Keyframe-Level Sparsification: Existing methods face inherent trade-offs in long-horizon history modeling.
Naively appending all visual inputs severely degrades attention quality with heavy computational overhead [46].
Uniformly sampling visual frames at a fixed frequency to fit a limited context window leads to severe information
loss [6], whereas employing specialized pooling mechanisms inevitably introduces extra network modules [34, 47].
To circumvent these limitations, we summarize visual context through action-guided keyframe selection. This
strategy effectively isolates critical spatial observations without adding structural overhead. Details of keyframe
selection are deferred to Section 3.3.
Crucially, deploying a policy trained on keyframe sequences proves essential during inference for mitigating
the impact of control uncertainty across diverse embodiments. Particularly for embodiment like humanoids,
micro-actions often fail to overcome physical execution limits to produce actual physical movement, rendering
continuous visual inputs non-uniform and inducing severe out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts relative to contin-
uous training video frames, which inevitably degrades policy performance. We find that simply triggering
keyframe selection at inference time based on fixed distance and angle thresholds effectively addresses this
issue and yields impressive navigation performance.
History Representation Map Construction: For tasks requiring true history utilization, such as HBN, the policy
must not only pinpoint previously observed targets but also discern how to physically navigate back to them.
Over such extended horizons, language-based summaries fail to adequately capture subtle scene information
and historical action trajectories [31]. Inspired by explicit map construction in training-free paradigms [22, 41]
and trajectory visualization during data annotation, we format past action trajectories into a 2D visual
canvas, termed the History Representation Map (HRM). Specifically, keyframes are projected onto the HRM
canvas and labeled with their corresponding frame indices. To resolve spatial clutter in densely visited regions,
spatially proximal keyframe indices are dynamically aggregated (e.g., denoted as “2–5” to indicate multiple
keyframes within the localized neighborhood). Furthermore, since video streams possess an intrinsic temporal
directionality, directional arrows are rendered along the trajectory to encode past headings. The current
step is explicitly highlighted with a red letter C accompanied by a red arrow specifying its heading. By
directly feeding the generated HRM alongside keyframes into the backbone as native image inputs, Compass 1
achieves long-horizon spatiotemporal reasoning with zero architectural modification. Under this design, while
the visual sequence fed into the backbone remains compact and keyframe-sparse, the HRM provides dense,
explicit topological grounding over the entire navigation trajectory.
2.2
History Utilization
To maximize the efficacy of historical contexts without architectural modifications, the optimal strategy is to
intrinsically embed spatiotemporal reasoning capabilities into the foundation backbone. We achieve this by
formulating a structured VQA supervision protocol. Empirically, we observe that naively taking the HRM
as an input causes the backbone to ignore this guidance. Thus, our VQA protocol serves as an essential
map activation mechanism. To resolve pervasive execution failures across Instruction-Following Navigation
(IFN), Beyond-the-View Navigation (BVN), and History Backtracking Navigation (HBN), we design three
specialized categories of VQA objectives:
Task-Stage Grounding (for IFN): Standard IFN policies frequently suffer from execution flaws, including
premature stopping, direction mismatch, and failing to stop accurately even when the target is directly in
5


sight [14, 51]. To rectify these issues, we first employ Qwen-3.8-27B [25] to parse raw language instructions
into structured task stages. The backbone is then queried to identify the current stage from the overall
instruction breakdown, predict the immediate next-step high-level intention aligned with the current stage,
and determine whether a termination criterion is met. Notably, to accelerate inference latency and allow
sufficient execution buffer, our termination supervision is constructed anticipatorily 4 steps prior to actual
stopping (see Section 2.3).
Exploration Consistency (for BVN): In open-world BVN, exploration trajectories inherently exhibit multimodal
distributions where turning left, right, or moving straight are all plausible when the target is absent [14, 45].
As conventional approaches lack visibility into prior intentions, step-by-step inference becomes decoupled,
leading to left-right turning deadlocks [14]. We mitigate this by querying the backbone on whether the left
or right spatial regions have been previously visited, anchored on the HRM. Furthermore, we establish an
intention propagation mechanism, passing the predicted high-level intention from the previous inference step
into the successive prompt text, enforcing execution consistency throughout exploration.
Spatiotemporal Target Retrieval (for HBN): For long-horizon HBN, policies typically fail to extract actionable
context from visual history. Combining the parsed task stage with the HRM, we design a joint spatiotemporal
retrieval question. Given a target query, the backbone simultaneously identifies the historical keyframe index
capturing the target and predicts the relative direction required to navigate toward that keyframe from the
current position. This general formulation not only resolves complex history backtracking but also naturally
enhances target-recovery maneuvers when a previously observed target is inadvertently bypassed during
ongoing navigation.
2.3
System Design
To seamlessly unify VQA reasoning with fine-grained spatiotemporal prediction, Compass 1 adopts Cosmos3-
Edge-4B [1] as its foundation backbone. To our knowledge, Compass 1 is the first framework in embodied
navigation to leverage a unified multimodal understanding-and-generation architecture as its core backbone.
Formally, the system takes the current keyframe-level observation stream, the HRM, and prompt instructions
as native inputs, operating through two synergistic decoding heads: an autoregressive language head that
handles structured VQA reasoning, alongside a diffusion-based action-video head that jointly generates the
future and predicts action trajectories.

2.4
Training Recipe
As illustrated in Figure 3, we co-train Compass 1 using a diverse dataset comprising over 500 hours of
high-quality self-collected real-world data and 2,000 hours of annotated YouTube videos. To ensure that
the model develops balanced and robust accuracy across diverse VQA queries without overfitting to specific
prompts, we implement a selective query sampling strategy during training, where termination is queried
continuously, while other queries are sampled with a 50% probability per training sample. The entire model is
optimized end-to-end on a cluster of 32 NVIDIA H200 GPUs for a total of 60000 steps, using a global batch
size of 256. To balance multimodal reasoning, video generation, and action prediction, we apply differentiated
learning rate schedules across network heads: the visual head and language head maintain a constant learning
rate of 1 × 10−5, whereas the action head is initialized with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, warmed up over the
first 1% of total training steps, and subsequently decayed to 1 × 10−5 via a cosine annealing schedule. The
overall optimization objective is a joint loss weighted across task dimensions, with a loss ratio of 3 : 1 : 1 for
the VQA loss, video generation loss, and action prediction loss, respectively.
3
Dataset Curation
Owing to the notorious physical domain gaps and severe visual artifacts present in existing simulators [27],
relying on simulator-generated navigation data frequently degrades zero-shot real-world deployment. To
circumvent data contamination from synthetic trajectories, Compass 1 adopts a real-world data collection
philosophy inspired by Zhang et al. [45], scaling up physical navigation trajectories while aggregating web-scale
video data from YouTube.
Specifically, we collect high-fidelity egocentric video streams using DJI Action 4 cameras with hardware
stabilization enabled. Operators adhere to strict protocol guidelines to systematically record trajectories
across three core navigation tasks: IFN, BVN, and HBN. BVN is subdivided into target-driven exploration
and demand-driven exploration.
Both self-collected real-world trajectories and YouTube videos are processed through our semi-automated
annotation pipeline to extract action labels, language instructions, keyframes, and VQA supervisions, as
illustrated in Figure 4. Given the significantly larger volume of YouTube data, human verification is exclusively
applied to our self-collected trajectories.
3.1
Action Labeling from Monocular RGB Videos
Following the paradigm of NavDreamer [13], we extract metric continuous action trajectories from monocular
RGB videos by combining π3 [37] and MoGe-2 [33], as shown in Figure 4(a). However, trajectory visualizations
reveal that raw extracted actions frequently contain outliers induced by monocular ambiguity and visual noise.
These anomalies manifest in three primary forms:
• Abnormal action scales, where predicted action scale far exceed the nominal per-step physical movement
during real-world collection.
• Excessive roll and pitch variations, which typically signify camera stabilization failures or severe jitter
from human operators.
• Erratic multi-axis spikes, characterized by simultaneous large variations in dyaw, dx, and dy during
tight-radius cornering maneuvers near obstacles.
To ensure the integrity of our imitation learning corpus, trajectories exhibiting any of these failure modes are
filtered out from the training dataset.

3.2
Action-Conditioned Language Annotation
Directly prompting VLM with raw video streams to synthesize navigation instructions frequently suffers
from hallucinated temporal boundaries, direction misidentification, and stage omission. To mitigate these
limitations, we introduce an action-conditioned language annotation protocol that leverages the extracted
metric trajectories as explicit spatiotemporal priors.
Continuous action sequences are first aggregated along the temporal timeline to partition the whole video
into discrete, semantically meaningful action primitives, including moving straight, turning left, turning right,
and searching. In the pipeline detailed in Figure 4(b), this structured action timeline, alongside the raw video
stream, is fed into Doubao-Seed-Pro-2.1 [28]. Guided by these deterministic motion primitives, the VLM
reliably grounds visual landmarks (e.g., trash can or billboard) to their corresponding trajectory segments,
ultimately synthesizing fine-grained, stage-aligned instructions with guaranteed structural consistency.
These synthesized instructions serve as the foundational annotations, customized to align with specified task
categories. IFN and target-driven BVN directly deploy the raw generated instructions. For demand-driven
BVN, the target description in the searching stage is reformulated into high-level user demands. For HBN,
which mandates a backtracking at the last stage during data collection, only the final clause is replaced to
direct the agent back to its target.
3.3
Action-Guided Keyframe Selection
Processing dense video frames indiscriminately introduces redundant spatial contexts and excessive computa-
tional overhead. To address this, we propose an action-guided keyframe selection strategy that adaptively
downsamples video streams using trajectories as spatio-temporal priors.
As illustrated in Figure 4(c), candidate turning intervals are first identified by detecting localized heading
variations along the trajectory. Turning evidence is captured through a dual-condition criterion based on
step-wise yaw changes, which evaluates both an instantaneous single-step threshold and a five-frame yaw
accumulation window anchored at a central frame. This formulation effectively filters out minor heading noise
and oscillatory motion. Nearby turning steps are subsequently merged into contiguous motion segments with
bidirectional temporal buffers to preserve critical pre- and post-turn visual transitions.
Based on these extracted motion semantics, keyframes are sampled under a non-uniform priority scheme
where the initial frame is strictly retained as the first keyframe. Dense sampling is applied across turning and
transition regions whenever the yaw accumulation exceeds 30◦to prevent missing target objects during rapid
rotation, whereas straight segments are sampled sparsely at 3s intervals to eliminate background redundancy.
Through this dynamic downsampling strategy, a dense one-minute video stream (240 frames at 4 FPS) is
efficiently compressed to an average of only 40 keyframes, significantly reducing computational overhead while
retaining essential navigation semantics.
3.4
Keyframe-Guided Stage Annotation
Directly feeding dense video streams into VLM is computationally prohibitive and prone to temporal
misalignment. To overcome this limitation, stage annotation operates on the pre-extracted sparse keyframes
rather than raw video frames. As illustrated in Figure 4(d), the complete navigation instruction and keyframe
sequence are jointly fed into Qwen-3.8-27B [25] to decompose the task into target-oriented stages and assign
the stage to each keyframe.
Since the initial keyframe mapping inevitably yields coarse temporal boundaries, a fine-grained refinement
step is executed across boundary intervals. We then use Qwen-3.8-27B to inspect the interval containing raw
video frames between the boundary keyframes and their immediate successors, precisely pinpointing the exact
frame where the subgoal is fulfilled. Building upon these refined temporal boundaries, per-frame stage labels
are efficiently propagated across the entire continuous video stream.
9


3.5
Human Verification
To ensure high dataset quality while retaining scalability, we incorporate a lightweight human-in-the-loop
verification protocol. Unlike conventional annotation pipelines that require annotators to watch full video
streams, manually craft navigation instructions, and label frame-level targets [45], we streamline human
intervention into two highly efficient inspection tasks.
As illustrated in Figure 4(e), annotators directly inspect the action visualization to identify anomalous artifacts
within metric trajectories. Trajectories exhibiting excessive motion noise are directly discarded to guarantee
training stability. Subsequently, language alignment is verified using only short video intervals surrounding
stage boundaries rather than full-length sequences. Annotators directly verify whether the target objects and
physical movements within these boundary intervals remain consistent with the language instruction. If an
inconsistency is identified, the instruction is manually corrected, after which the trajectory re-enters the stage
annotation pipeline described in Section 3.4 to update temporal stage assignments.
3.6
Target Grounding
To establish fine-grained spatiotemporal alignment, we further utilize Qwen-3.8-27B [25] to ground semantic
targets by annotating bounding boxes across all extracted keyframe sequences guided by the language
instruction. Directly detecting targets frame-by-frame often introduces redundant candidates and temporal
inconsistencies. To guarantee high-quality grounding, we apply two trajectory-level structural constraints
during annotation.
As depicted in Figure 4(f), we enforce a historical temporal prior where target annotations exist exclusively
within their corresponding stage and all preceding stages. If a target object from an earlier stage reappears
in subsequent stages, its bounding box annotations in the later stages are explicitly removed to prevent
semantic ambiguity. Furthermore, to eliminate candidate redundancy caused by multiple visual instances, we
adopt an anchor-and-propagate strategy. The canonical target bounding box is uniquely identified at the
final keyframe of each stage and subsequently propagated backward through all preceding keyframes. This
backward propagation strategy filters out noisy candidates and ensures robust visual grounding across the
entire navigation trajectory.

Instruction-Following Navigation. Figures 6(e) to (h) illustrate the instruction following capabilities of Compass
1 across multi-stage navigation, long straight-line navigation, basic two-stage navigation, and initial-sight
target navigation. In multi-stage navigation, Compass 1 accurately grounds the current task stage and
unambiguously aligns the turning intention with the language instruction, eliminating directional confusion.
Upon reaching the final target, Compass 1 actively makes the decision to stop without relying on heuristic
norm-threshold stopping rules, which often trigger premature stopping near obstacles due to reduced action
magnitudes. In long straight-line navigation, Compass 1 follows forward instructions even when targets remain
invisible over extended intervals, facilitated by the history guidance embedded in HRM (see Section 4.3). For
basic two-stage navigation requiring turns exceeding 90◦, even if the target remains out-of-view due to an
incomplete initial turn, Compass 1 refrains from random wandering and instead continues the turn under
instruction guidance until the large-angle maneuver is fully accomplished. Finally, in initial-sight target
navigation, where the target is unaligned with the initial heading, Compass 1 continuously adjusts its direction
through continuous action sequences and stops precisely in front of the target.
Moreover, Compass 1 generalizes in a zero-shot manner to both indoor and outdoor scenarios. It reliably
withstands severe visual distractors, including dynamic human flows crossing the visual field and moving
background vehicles, ensuring robust navigation execution.
4.2.2
Quantitative Comparison with Baselines
Figure 7(a) presents the comprehensive quantitative comparisons between Compass 1 and existing SOTA
baselines. Averaged over four distinct scenarios and eight task configurations, Compass 1 achieves a dominant
overall success rate of 79.4%, whereas the second-best baseline AwareVLN attains only 17.8%. Other prominent
SOTA methods designed for VLN-CE yield drastically lower performance, registering 11.6%, 6.3%, and 0.3%.
These results strongly indicate that existing SOTA approaches trained purely in simulation suffer from severe
overfitting. More significantly, fine-tuning these baselines with our dataset fails to yield performance gains
comparable to Compass 1, demonstrating that high benchmark scores on traditional VLN-CE do not translate
to robust open-world deployment capabilities.
Performance across Task Categories. Figure 7(b) and (c) break down the success rates by individual tasks
and categories. For highly challenging HBN, the strongest baseline achieves a mere 8% success rate restricted
to basic backtracking, while dropping to 0% on persistent backtracking. This reveals that existing SOTA
approaches remain fundamentally insensitive to history input even when trained on our dataset, validating
that conventional history integration mechanisms are largely ineffective. In contrast, Compass 1 leverages
effective history representation and utilization to achieve a 98% success rate on basic backtracking and an
unparalleled overall leap from 4% to 78% on HBN.
In BVN, the strongest baseline exhibits comparable performance between target-driven and demand-driven
tasks, suggesting that existing SOTA approaches inherently possess a basic capability to align natural language
demands with object categories, albeit constrained by a low performance ceiling. Compass 1 attains an
aggregate success rate of 84% in this category, representing a 250% improvement over the strongest baseline.
For IFN, the strongest baseline achieves approximately 50% success in initial-sight target and basic two-
stage navigation. However, their performance drops sharply when facing challenging long straight-line and
multi-stage tasks, plummeting to 0% in multi-stage navigation. Conversely, Compass 1 achieves over 90% in
initial-sight target and basic two-stage navigation, 73% in long straight-line navigation, and 50% in multi-stage
navigation, yielding an overall IFN success rate of 78% which is 3.0× that of the strongest baseline.
Performance across Real-World Scenarios. Figure 7(d) illustrates the aggregated success rates across different
deployment scenarios. Both the strongest baseline and our method maintain relatively stable performance in
indoor scenarios and outdoor scenarios with minimal dynamic disturbances. However, in densely populated
street environments characterized by heavy dynamic disturbances, the performance of the strongest baseline
drops by nearly 65%. Meanwhile, Compass 1 maintains superior anti-interference capabilities, exhibiting only
a minor performance drop.
Failure Case Analysis per Task and Baseline. Figure 7(e) provides a granular breakdown of success rates
across all task types and baseline models, allowing for an in-depth analysis of failure modes:
15


• Initial-Sight Target and Basic Two-Stage Navigation: Approaches with discrete action outputs, such
as AwareVLN and NaVIDA, tend to output straight actions over prolonged distances before executing
sharp turns toward targets. While AwareVLN demonstrates superior turning capabilities compared to
NaVIDA, this strategy often causes the robot to get blocked by walls after turning in indoor environments,
resulting in target occlusion and navigation failure. Although LightNav-0 also utilizes discrete actions, it
approximates continuous control for smoother turns. However, control-induced drift during locomotion
frequently causes it to deviate and lose the target, such as when traversing stairs. WNM-3D struggles to
stop when the target is out of the center of the current field-of-view, highlighting the limited semantic
understanding of current world-model-based approaches. Additionally, these three baselines are heavily
impacted by dense dynamic disturbances and struggle to execute further turning adjustments when an
initial turn is insufficient to align with the target. In contrast, Compass 1 maintains stable performance,
with remaining failures primarily stemming from target occlusion caused by dynamic obstructions or
excessive rotation that leads to selected keyframes missing the target.
• Long Straight-Line Navigation: Approaches with discrete action outputs, such as AwareVLN and
NaVIDA, persistently output forward actions during initial stages, which ultimately lead to navigation
failure either through uneven terrain and accumulated control errors that cause severe trajectory drift,
or via premature stopping in the middle of paths. LightNav-0 frequently exhibits premature turning
behaviors that lead to task failure. Although WNM-3D outputs continuous actions and adjusts its
heading dynamically via predicted dy, it still fails to maintain a consistent heading direction. While
Compass 1 occasionally exhibits unintended turns during long straight-line navigation, the frequency is
significantly lower than that of LightNav-0.
• Multi-Stage Navigation: Baseline models consistently suffer from various forms of premature stopping
and target loss induced by turning inaccuracies. Compass 1 experiences occasional navigation failures
similarly stemming from accumulated turning inaccuracies that cause target loss, particularly in scenarios
where targets blend into complex backgrounds due to glass reflections or extreme lighting variations.
• Beyond-the-View Navigation: LightNav-0 exhibits a high probability for premature turning behaviors,
struggling to follow instructions along exploratory paths (e.g., go along the way to find the target), and can
directly collide with obstacles in dead ends. AwareVLN and WNM-3D are prone to aggressive exploratory
turns that frequently trigger collisions or lead to left-right turning deadlocks. Although AwareVLN
generates reasoning text and passes it to the next inference step, its reasoning primarily emphasizes
global scene understanding while lacking awareness of previously explored directions. NaVIDA exhibits
virtually no active exploration capability. In contrast, Compass 1 may occasionally drift from the target
direction or experience collisions during exploration, though its collision rate remains significantly lower
than all comparative baselines. Additionally, it may exhibit transient left-right oscillation behaviors,
but typically manages to escape from such loops and resume navigation.
• History Backtracking Navigation: Among all baselines, only AwareVLN exhibits a slight tendency to
turn back, yet it fails to achieve a full 180◦turn, succeeding only by coincidence when objects happen
to be visible with a 120◦turn. All baseline models completely fail at persistent backtracking. The
sole failure mode observed for Compass 1 on basic tracking involves minor collisions with intermediate
targets during U-turn execution. Persistent backtracking remains challenging, where occlusions by
obstacles during the return path can cause the robot to lose its return heading despite knowing the
approximate target location.

To analyze the internal mechanisms and validate the design choices of Compass 1, we conduct comprehensive
ablation studies across six variants as illustrated in Figure 7(g).
• w/o Task-Stage Grounding: This variant removes task-stage grounding VQA and passively determines
episode termination based on the norm of action outputs. To preserve intention propagation under this
setup, continuous action predictions are discretized and passed in natural language format. This removal
causes noticeable performance degradation in basic two-stage navigation due to premature stopping,
while dropping performance to zero on multi-stage navigation. This confirms that task-stage grounding
VQA provides a critical capability for multi-stage instruction-following. Furthermore, lacking this
capability negatively affects other tasks reliant on multi-stage execution, (e.g., persistent backtracking
navigation) though its overall impact on exploration and basic backtracking capabilities remains minimal.
• w/o Exploration Consistency: This variant removes the exploration consistency VQA and intention
propagation.
Performance evaluations indicate that this removal primarily impairs target-driven
navigation and demand-driven navigation tasks, whereas performance across remaining evaluations stays
largely unchanged.
• w/o Spatiotemporal Target Retrieval: This variant removes the spatiotemporal target retrieval VQA.
Removing this mechanism severely degrades history reasoning capabilities, rendering input HRM
completely ineffective without this explicit reasoning trigger. Crucially, this QA mechanism benefits not
only history-dependent backtracking navigation, but also mitigates error accumulation in multi-stage
navigation. When the model inadvertently miss targets during execution, this spatiotemporal reasoning
enables target relocation, thereby increasing overall navigation success rates.
• w/o HRM: This variant removes HRM as an extra visual input while retaining all structured VQA tasks.
Without HRM, the model preserves partial reasoning capacities, achieving strong performance on basic
two-stage navigation alongside reasonable success on multi-stage and basic backtracking navigation.
However, when deployed on highly challenging scenarios such as long straight-line and persistent
backtracking navigation, relying solely on VQA proves insufficient to satisfy the memory demands.
• w/o Video Generation: This variant removes the inverse dynamics model auxiliary objective and directly
attaches a diffusion action head for trajectory generation. Results demonstrate that predictive video
generation can improve action generation, proving particularly beneficial for challenging exploration,
multi-stage, and history-backtracking navigation.
• w/o YouTube Data: This variant restricts model training exclusively to our collected real-world dataset.

though the magnitude of these gains remains constrained by the inherent limited quality of web data.

scaling capabilities. Simultaneously, introducing the asynchronous single-GPU execution strategy yields a
speedup of nearly 50% over synchronous execution. These optimizations boost the deployment responsiveness,
enabling our policy to compute 12 continuous actions in 0.43s at the beginning, which corresponds to an
effective control frequency of approximately 28Hz, and maintain 0.71s latency at 63 keyframes, representing
approximately 17Hz.

We further evaluate the performance of Compass 1 using established benchmarks. Note that HBN currently
lacks a simulation benchmark for comprehensive evaluation. We report navigation error (NE), oracle success
rate (OS), success rate (SR), and success weighted by path length (SPL) as evaluation metrics.
• R2R-CE [2] (for IFN): We train Compass 1 from scratch using R2R with DAgger augmentation. As shown
in Table 1, Compass 1 achieves SOTA performance among existing monocular navigation algorithms.
• OVON [43] (for target-driven navigation of BVN): We train Compass 1 from scratch using HM3D-
OVON data and augment the training with DAgger. As shown in Table 2, Compass 1 achieves SOTA
performance among existing monocular navigation algorithms.
• IntentionNav [24] (for demand-driven navigation of BVN): IntentionNav serves as a demand-driven
navigation benchmark designed to evaluate whether the policy can locate the target based on user
demands. Since the data source in IntentionNav is entirely unseen by all evaluated methods, we
partition its 500 trajectory samples into a training set of 300 trajectories and a testing set of 200
trajectories. These 300 training trajectories are utilized to fine-tune Compass 1 and all baselines without
DAgger augmentation. Detailed records of trajectory split and baseline implementations are documented
in Section A.2. As shown in Table 3, Compass 1 achieves SOTA performance on IntentionNav.

evaluation on the evaluation set. Since IndoorUAV does not provide ground-truth shortest trajectories,

Related Work
History Representation in Embodied Navigation Foundation Models. Existing end-to-end foundation models
predominantly process visual history through three paradigms: feeding dense uncompressed streams or
sliding windows into the backbone [14, 46], applying heuristic token merging and multi-scale memory
pooling [31, 34, 47], or uniformly subsampling frames into a fixed context window [6, 11]. However, these
strategies either flood attention with redundant tokens or drop critical spatial information [45], rendering
HBN unachievable. Although recent frameworks like MemoryVLN [40] and MapNav [50] have introduced
explicit memory banks and semantic maps as additional inputs, the policy tends to largely ignore them due
to the lack of explicit supervision, making them still insufficient for HBN.
VQA Supervision in Embodied Navigation. To bridge high-level semantic reasoning and low-level motion
control, recent methods increasingly incorporate VQA and CoT supervision during training. Within embodied
navigation, Lin et al. [19] and Liu et al. [20] supervise policies with step-by-step navigational CoT traces, while
Wang et al. [35] and Guo et al. [11] dynamically trigger interleaved reasoning tokens to switch between the
reasoning of scene and action generation. Similarly, Wang et al. [34] and Zhu et al. [51] augment policy training
with latent point reasoning and action-grounded visual dynamics. However, these auxiliary formulations
predominantly target general scene understanding, future prediction, or coarse subgoal identification [5, 48, 49],
leaving history reasoning largely underexplored and lacking the mechanism to analyze and resolve closed-loop
physical execution failures [14, 45].
Learning from Real-World Navigation Videos and Datasets. Large-scale learning-based navigation policies have
historically relied on synthetic trajectories scaled within photorealistic simulators such as Habitat [17, 27, 39].
Despite claims of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer [6, 14, 47], simulator-trained policies suffer from visual artifacts
and physical domain gaps that degrade real deployment. While real-world robot datasets such as ViNT [29]
and NoMaD [30] aggregate multi-embodiment physical trajectories, they focus on point-goal or image-goal
navigation and lack fine-grained language annotations. To harvest open-world visual priors with minimal
collection overhead, Hirose et al. [12] and Mei et al. [23] annotate in-the-wild YouTube videos, while Huang
et al. [13] extract metric pseudo-trajectories via 3D vision foundation models [33, 37]. However, these pipelines
yield only single-object labels or unverified motion primitives without the stage-aligned instructions needed for
embodied navigation. Recently, Zhang et al. [45] demonstrated strong physical deployment via real-world data
collection, yet their framework relies on labor-intensive labeling and lacks VQA supervision. Consequently, it
is crucial to develop a lightweight data labeling pipeline that minimizes manual effort.
6
Conclusion
In this work, we present Compass 1, an embodied navigation foundation model designed to pursue open-world
and persistent navigation capabilities with nearly two minutes of history backtracking. By integrating keyframe-
level sparsification with an explicit HRM, our method effectively preserves long-horizon spatiotemporal
context without introducing architectural modifications. Complementing this, the proposed structured VQA
supervision protocol serves as an essential map activation mechanism, successfully mitigating pervasive
real-world execution failures and enforcing history-aware reasoning across instruction-following, beyond-
the-view, and history backtracking navigation tasks. Furthermore, our semi-automated dataset curation
pipeline significantly reduces human overhead and enables the scalable acquisition of diverse multi-modal
interaction data. Extensive real-world and simulation evaluations demonstrate that Compass 1 achieves
superior performance and robust zero-shot generalization across diverse physical scenarios and robotic
embodiments. Coupled with detailed failure case analyses, our work provides comprehensive insights into
closed-loop execution and lays a solid foundation for the future development of navigation embodied systems.

A.1
Implementation and Reproducibility Details for Baselines in Real-World
We initialize all four baselines from their officially released checkpoints and adapt them to our real-world data
following their original training frameworks. To reflect the peak performance of each baseline, we report the
higher success rate between its original open-sourced weight and its fine-tuned counterpart in our evaluation
results. We detail the baseline implementations as following:
AwareVLN [11]. Based on normalized forward displacement and rotation magnitude, we map continuous
real-world motions to four action categories: moving forward by 25 cm, turning left by 15◦, turning right
by 15◦, and stopping. The model takes historical and current RGB observations together with the task
instruction as input. Since reasoning outputs require additional text annotations, we apply cross-entropy loss
only to the action output, without additional reasoning supervision. We reduce the global batch size from
768 in the original configuration to 256 and empirically adjust the learning rate from 2 × 10−5 to 1 × 10−5
and find it yields better performance. To ensure a fair comparison, we employ the same number of training
samples as in the training setup of Compass 1. As shown in Figure 9(a), the training of AwareVLN approaches
24


near-convergence around 60,000 steps. We find that despite the absence of direct supervision on reasoning
outputs during fine-tuning, AwareVLN successfully preserves its pre-existing reasoning abilities.
LightNav-0 [34]. Since depth information is unavailable in real-world videos, we approximate the affordance
point by using camera parameters to project the action position at the 4th future step onto the image plane. As
for the goal point, it can be directly obtained by taking the midpoint of the target’s grounding bounding box.
We retain the original autoregressive cross-entropy objective and jointly supervise the pointing-of-affordance
token, pointing-of-goal-object token, three RVQ action tokens, and end-of-sequence token. We reduce the
global batch size from 320 to 64 and empirically find that setting the learning rate to 1 × 10−5 yields better
performance. To maintain the same number of training samples of Compass 1, we train LightNav-0 for 240,000
steps. As shown in Figure 9(b), the training curve exhibits a clear convergence trend.
NaVIDA [51]. We discretize continuous actions following the identical mapping protocol of AwareVLN. We
then use HPAC to process the discrete action sequences, merging adjacent actions of the same class with a
probability of 0.7, which strictly follows the official open-source configuration. Under this setup, we further
aggregate the continuous action in the same type to make sure each action block contains at most three action
segments (e.g., turn left, turn right, forward). Fine-tuning is conducted using the original global batch size of
64 and the default learning rate for 240k steps to match the total training samples of Compass 1. As shown
in Figure 9(c), the training curve exhibits a clear convergence trend.
WNM-3D [14]. To ensure a fair comparison, we maintain a global batch size of 192 and the original learning
rate, training for 80,000 steps to match the training setup of Compass 1. Since the model natively supports
continuous actions, we directly align three-dimensional actions with RGB videos without discretization. As
shown in Figure 9(d), the training curve exhibits a clear convergence trend.

deployment environments. In total, 32 distinct navigation tasks are defined, encompassing instruction-following
navigation (IFN), Beyond-the-View Navigation (BVN), and History-Backtracking Navigation (HBN).
