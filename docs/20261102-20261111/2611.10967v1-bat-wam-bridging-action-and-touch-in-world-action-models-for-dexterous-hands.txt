Abstract
Dexterous manipulation with world action models (WAMs)
relies on hand configuration and contact information, yet
joint-state and whole-hand tactile observations may be par-
tially or entirely missing. RGB images provide scene con-
text and hand–object geometry but are insufficient to reli-
ably recover these missing modalities. We further observe
a correlation between hand state and tactile information.
Motivated by this observation, we introduce BAT-WAM, a
framework that bridges visual, joint-state, and tactile rep-
resentations for WAMs. BAT-WAM learns separate code-
books and decoders for the tactile and action streams. An
RGB-conditioned continuous latent bridge connects the two
streams for bidirectional learning, while visual information
serves as continuous context. Under the HTBench evalua-
tion protocol, BAT-WAM reduces the root mean squared er-
ror (RMSE) of tactile reconstruction by 34.73% under fully
masked conditions and 38.84% under partially masked con-
ditions, relative to an image-conditioned HandTouch check-
point. For future tactile prediction conditioned on target-
time RGB and historical actions, it reduces mean RMSE by
85.56% relative to OpenWAM with HangTouch. Real-robot
experiments further show that BAT-WAM consistently out-
performs related WAM baselines in dexterous manipulation.
Generalization experiments on unseen objects and scenes
also demonstrate substantial improvements over baseline
models.
These findings support joint modeling of visual
context, hand state, and touch as a representation approach
for multimodal WAMs.

1. Introduction
Contact-rich dexterous manipulation depends on the in-
terplay between scene geometry, hand configuration, and
physical contact.
RGB images provide object appear-
ance and visible hand–object geometry; tactile observations
(Touch) capture contact locations and pressure distribu-
tions; and joint states (States) describe hand configuration
and kinematic coordination. These modalities provide com-
plementary information, but similar visual observations can
conceal different interaction states. For example, two im-
ages of a hand grasping a cup may appear nearly identical,
even when one grasp maintains stable contact and the other
is beginning to slip. Such ambiguity motivates represen-
tations that capture fine differences in hand configuration
and contact, consistent with the importance of tactile feed-
back in reactive manipulation [11].
Recent work has de-
veloped several interfaces for incorporating proprioception
and dexterous hand states into world action model (WAM)
systems. For example, OpenWAM-α [16] linearly projects
the robot’s 80-dimensional proprioceptive state into a sin-
gle context token. This token is appended to the language
context and conditions both the video and action streams
through cross-attention. For dexterous hands, UniDexTok
learns discrete state representations through a standardized
hand interface, while UniHM couples cross-embodiment
hand-motion tokenization with language-guided manipula-
tion [3, 21].
These approaches address the challenge of
making structured, high-dimensional hand information ac-
cessible to token-based models.
In parallel, tactile VLA and world action model (WAM)
research has expanded the role of touch in policy learning.
Tactile-VLA and VLA-Touch incorporate tactile informa-
tion into action generation, feedback, and control [1, 8].
VT-WAM, Tactile-WAM, and DreamTacVLA model future
tactile observations alongside visual or action information
[13, 17, 19], while N0-VTLA uses predictive latent tac-
tile tokens to condition action generation [10]. Building
on these interfaces, we investigate a specific representation

problem: how can Touch and Joint supervise one another
within an RGB-conditioned interface while retaining their
distinct physical meanings and data structures?
This question is motivated by the physical coupling be-
tween hand configuration and contact. Joint states constrain
where fingers can engage an object, while tactile measure-
ments provide evidence about the contacts realized by that
configuration. RGB supplies the scene context needed to in-
terpret these relationships. HT-Bench, which pairs egocen-
tric vision with full-hand tactile observations, provides a ba-
sis for studying visually conditioned contact representations
[9]. We hypothesize that explicitly learning conditional de-
pendencies between Touch and Joint can help resolve am-
biguities that remain in visual observations and support in-
ference when part of either modality is unavailable.
We introduce BAT-WAM, a multimodal representation
interface designed for use by WAM backbones. BAT-WAM
bridges state and contact information, while retaining RGB
as the context.
A continuous bridging module balances
modality-specific token budgets and models interactions
among hand configuration, contact state, and scene con-
text.
This design preserves distinct quantization and re-
construction pathways for Touch and Joint while allowing
their representations to interact. Through conditional re-
construction, each modality can provide information for re-
covering the other: a modality serves as observed context
in one masking configuration and as a supervision target in
another, with RGB conditioning the inference.
The interface supports both static masked completion
and conditional future tactile prediction. Each task explic-
itly specifies which observations are visible to the model
and which signals are used only as supervision.
Static
completion examines reconstruction of missing information
from the available context, while future prediction examines
how observed joint-state history informs subsequent tactile
states.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. We uncover a previously overlooked cross-modal rela-
tionship: a strong correlation between dexterous hand
states and tactile observations.
2. Building on this finding, we introduce BAT-WAM, a
novel cross-modal representation framework that inte-
grates discrete tactile and state representations with con-
tinuous RGB context through a multimodal bridging
mechanism.
3. BAT-WAM achieves state-of-the-art performance on
benchmarks for masked tactile completion and future
tactile prediction, and outperforms the corresponding
baselines in real-world experiments and zero-shot gen-
eralization evaluations.
2. Related Work
2.1. Proprioceptive State Representations
Proprioceptive state representation learning focuses on en-
coding internal robot states, such as joint configurations,
and integrating them with visual observations.
Exist-
ing methods adopt different interfaces for incorporating

these states: π0 linearly projects continuous proprioceptive
states into the Transformer embedding space [2]; HPT uses
embodiment-specific input encoders to map proprioceptive
states and visual observations from heterogeneous robots
into token sequences processed by a shared backbone [15];

UniDexTok addresses state heterogeneity across hand em-
bodiments by organizing human and robot joint states
through a unified 22-DoF semantic interface and learning
embodiment-conditioned discrete state representations [3].
These studies provide foundations for state integration,
cross-modal reconstruction, and unified state representa-
tions across embodiments. Building on this direction, BAT-
WAM incorporates standardized Joint states and Touch into
a common representation interface while retaining separate
codebooks and decoders for the two modalities.
Condi-
tioned on continuous RGB context, it learns bidirectional
dependencies between hand configuration and contact state
through conditional reconstruction.
2.2. Full-hand tactile representation
Tactile representation learning includes continuous fea-
tures, cross-sensor pretraining, and discrete tokenization.
Sparsh learned visual-tactile representations with self-
supervision, AnyTouch sought a common representation for
static and dynamic information across visuotactile sensors,
and VITaL supported tactile and non-tactile manipulation
policies [4–6]. VQ-VAE provided a standard route from
continuous observations to a finite latent vocabulary [14],
but pressure maps must also retain contact regions, spatial
topology, and local intensity.
HTBench/HandTouch was the closest full-hand tactile
representation setting to ours. It used bimanual pressure
maps and egocentric vision to learn discrete tactile repre-
sentations and reconstruct pressure maps under visual con-
ditioning [9]. BAT-WAM retains this spatial pressure in-
terface but gives Joint state its own codebook and decoder,
so configuration information enters the bridge in a recon-
structable form.
2.3. Tactile-enhanced VLA and world-action mod-
els
Tactile information entered policies through continuous fu-
sion, feedback control, prediction branches, and latent to-
kens.
3D-ViTac, Reactive Diffusion Policy, and T-Rex
used vision and touch for fine-grained or reactive manipu-
lation [7, 11, 18], while TouchWorld developed a predictive
3


model for tactile forecasting and reactive manipulation [22].
In VLA and WAM systems, Tactile-VLA and VLA-Touch
used visual-language physical knowledge or layered feed-
back, while VT-WAM, Tactile-WAM, DreamTacVLA, and
N0-VTLA studied visuotactile action prediction, asymmet-
ric attention, tactile world models, and latent tactile to-
kens [1, 8, 10, 13, 17, 19].
These systems demonstrated the value of touch for con-
tact control, but their inputs typically entered as continuous
features, external feedback, prediction branches, or latent
tokens. BAT-WAM specifies a narrower interface: Touch
and states have separately decodable physical vocabularies,
cross-modal relations are learned in a continuous bridge,
RGB remains continuous context, and the resulting prefix
is used for backbone observation optimization.
3. Method
BAT-WAM encodes tactile observations and hand joint
states under RGB conditioning, producing a set of addi-
tional input tokens for the π0.5 backbone. Figure 2 gives
the complete interface.

3.2. Cross-Modal Bridging
The cross-modal bridge integrates Touch, Joint, and RGB
information. Touch describes contact locations and pressure
distributions, Joint describes the current hand configuration,
and RGB provides object and scene context. Each modality
is processed through its own encoding path and mapped into
a common 256-dimensional feature space, where a Trans-
former models their relationships.
Tokens receive the corresponding modality and view
identity embeddings, allowing the model to distinguish their
sources. RGB features remain continuous, and the RGB en-
coder remains frozen during training.

4.1. Experimental Setup
Hardware.
We conduct real-world experiments using a
6-DoF AgileX PiPER robotic arm equipped with a Linker
Hand L6 dexterous hand.
The L6 hand is fitted with a
full-hand tactile sensing glove from Juqiao Industrial. An
Intel RealSense D435 provides top-view RGB-D observa-
tions, while a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D405 provides
wrist-view RGB-D observations. The complete experimen-
tal platform is shown in Figure 4.
Tasks Description.
We design 7 diverse tasks spanning
pick&place, stacking, pull&push, and long horizon manip-
ulation to evaluate our WAM models. For each task, we
collect 200 demonstrations.
Benchmark.
We use HT-Bench as our offline bench-
mark [9]. HT-Bench is a multitask benchmark for tactile
representation learning in robotic dexterous manipulation
that combines egocentric vision with full-hand tactile data.
Through fine-grained tactile retrieval, masked tactile com-
pletion, vision-to-tactile synthesis, and multimodal tactile
frame prediction, it evaluates contact structure modeling,
visual–tactile alignment, temporal prediction, and general-
ization to unseen tasks.

4.3. Real-World Experiment
Real World.
Figure 4 presents seven real-world tasks
spanning four categories—pick-and-place, fine manipula-
tion, pull-and-push, and long-horizon tasks—all conducted
in a shared tabletop setup. BAT-WAM achieves the high-
est mean success rate of 79.3%, outperforming OpenWAM-
α at 66.4% and OpenWAM-α + HandTouch at 69.3%. It
achieves the highest success rate on every task except Close
Drawer. The largest task-level advantage is observed on
Wipe Whiteboard, where BAT-WAM achieves a success
rate of 45%, compared with 15% for OpenWAM-α and 25%
for OpenWAM-α + HandTouch. We apply a strict success
criterion to this task: an execution is considered success-
ful only when all writing has been completely erased. This
criterion helps explain the relatively low success rates.
Adding HandTouch to OpenWAM-α increases the mean
success rate from 66.4% to 69.3% and improves perfor-
mance on Pick White Flange, Pick Black Flange, Stack
Blue Cube, and Wipe Whiteboard, suggesting that tactile
information can benefit model performance.
BAT-WAM
further improves the mean success rate by 12.9 and 10.0
percentage points over OpenWAM-α and OpenWAM-α +
HandTouch, respectively. These results demonstrate con-
sistent transfer of BAT-WAM’s multimodal interface across
manipulation, grasping, wiping, and stacking tasks, yield-
ing the strongest overall real-world performance among the
evaluated methods.
Zero-shot generalization.
Table 5 evaluates real-world
zero-shot generalization across variations in object color,
size, shape, scene layout, and object pose.
BAT-WAM
achieves the highest success rate in all five unseen set-
tings, with an average of 54.5%, compared with 38.5% for
OpenWAM-α and 42.0% for OpenWAM-α + HandTouch,
yielding gains of 16.0 and 12.5 percentage points, respec-
tively. The largest advantage occurs under unseen layouts,
where BAT-WAM achieves 80.0% success, compared with
57.5% and 60.0% for the two baselines. It also reaches
80.0% under unseen colors, indicating strong performance
under the tested color and layout variations. Generaliza-
tion to unseen sizes and shapes remains more challenging:
despite outperforming both baselines, BAT-WAM achieves
only 37.5% and 27.5%, respectively, compared with 87.5%
and 72.5% in the corresponding seen settings. These results
show improved zero-shot performance across the evaluated
variations, while identifying changes in object geometry as
a remaining limitation.
5. Conclusion
We introduced BAT-WAM, a multimodal representation in-
terface that bridges tactile contact and hand configuration
for world-action models. Separate codebooks and decoders
represent touch and states, while a shared Transformer
learns their conditional relationships using continuous RGB
context. This design supports static masked completion and
conditional future tactile prediction within a common inter-
face. On HT-Bench, BAT-WAM improves full-mask tactile
synthesis and local-mask completion under aligned evalu-
ation protocols. It also improves conditional future tactile
prediction using historical touch and states together with
target-time RGB. Across seven real-world tasks in a shared
tabletop setup, BAT-WAM achieves a mean success rate of
79.3%, compared with 66.4% for OpenWAM-α and 69.3%
for OpenWAM-α + HandTouch.
