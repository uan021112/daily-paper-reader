Abstract
Robots operating in the real world are inherently subject to partial observability. This limitation
is particularly pronounced in multi-robot collaboration, where an individual robot’s observa-
tions may be insufficient for effective task execution. A central challenge is therefore how to
integrate complementary observations from the environment, particularly partner robots, to
inform collaborative decision-making. We argue that inter-robot information sharing should
go beyond passively transmitting raw observations or generic visual features. Instead, robots
should selectively extract and encode task-relevant information for sharing based on their task
objectives and current execution progress. To this end, we propose a task-oriented active con-
text sharing framework that encodes robot observations into compact context representations,
preserving information critical to collaborative decision-making under a constrained communi-
cation budget. Our framework adopts a two-stage training paradigm. In the first stage, onboard
robot observations and task instructions are jointly encoded into compact context representa-
tions. Task-oriented future prediction and soft task-progress correspondence learning guide
this collaboration-agnostic compression process to preserve information relevant to individual
robot’s task objectives, state transitions, and execution progress. In the second stage, the learned
context representations are integrated into a pretrained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy,
enabling bidirectional context exchange between robots. Each robot integrates complementary
information from its partner with its local observations to guide action generation. Supervision
of the resulting actions, in turn, jointly refines the shared representations and the policy, aligning
information sharing with the requirements of collaborative decision-making. Our controlled
experiments demonstrate substantial performance gains under constrained communication bud-
gets, while three real-world experiments further validate the approach in complex dual-robot
collaborative tasks. These results highlight a shift from passive visual information transmission
toward active, task-oriented context sharing, in which compact representations are learned to
directly support collaborative decision-making.

1. Introduction
“Gaze following into joint attention, social manipulation into cooperative
communication, group activity into collaboration.”
— Michael Tomasello and Malinda Carpenter, Shared Intentionality
Partial observability is a fundamental constraint on perception in the physical world. For robots,
limited fields of view, sensor placement, spatial constraints, and environmental occlusions
restrict access to the information needed to execute complex tasks. In multi-robot settings, this
limitation also creates an opportunity: robots can exploit complementary observations of their
shared environment. Consider two robots collaboratively transporting a large cabinet through
a narrow passage. The cabinet may obscure critical regions, leaving obstacles or clearance
visible to one robot but hidden from the other. These observations can directly affect whether
its partner should slow down, adjust its posture, or change direction. Similar dependencies
arise in warehousing, assembly, and everyday collaborative manipulation, where information
distributed across robots jointly informs task execution. We argue that the potential of multi-
robot systems lies not only in their collective physical capabilities but also in the complementary
perspectives afforded by distributed sensing. Translating this perceptual complementarity
into coordinated action requires each robot to integrate the observations of its partners into its
own decision-making, a fundamental challenge and opportunity in tightly coupled multi-robot
collaboration.
A common approach to multi-robot coordination relies on centralized planning, in which a
high-level planner decomposes global objectives into subtasks or commands that individual
robots execute using local observations. Although effective for task allocation and high-level
coordination, systems that communicate primarily through task descriptions or discrete instruc-
tions provide limited support for sharing fine-grained perceptual information. Object states,
spatial relationships, and execution progress observed by one robot may therefore remain un-
available to another robot’s action policy. This limitation is particularly pronounced in dynamic,
partially observable environments, where effective collaboration requires continuous adaptation
to environmental changes and partners’ actions. Without access to complementary perceptual
information, coordination is often restricted to parallel task execution or sequential handoffs,
rather than tightly coupled behaviors grounded in shared situational awareness.
Conditioning a robot’s policy on its partners’ visual observations provides a natural mechanism
for coupling perception and action across robots. However, transmitting raw images or generic
dense visual features incurs substantial communication and computational costs and introduces
redundant or task-irrelevant information. Moreover, the value of an observation depends on
task objectives, robot roles, and execution progress: visual details essential to one decision may
be irrelevant to another. Generic visual compression may preserve appearance or semantic
content without retaining the information most useful for collaborative action. The central
challenge is therefore to selectively encode local observations into compact representations that
preserve their relevance to collaborative decision-making. We refer to this process as active
context sharing. Here, active denotes task-conditioned information compression, rather than
selection of communication timing or recipients; these decisions may still be managed by a
centralized planner. Active context sharing aims to preserve task-relevant perceptual content
together with relationships among task objectives, observed states, and execution progress,
providing partners with informative context under a limited representation budget.

In this work, we propose a two-stage active context sharing framework for multi-robot col-
laboration. In Stage 1, we introduce a task-oriented context compression module that uses
learnable queries to aggregate frozen multi-camera visual features into compact latent tokens
conditioned on task instructions. We train this module with two complementary objectives
to preserve task-relevant visual information and capture the structure of task execution: (i)
task-oriented future prediction encourages the latent tokens to encode information predictive
of future visual states, emphasizing task-relevant regions and state transitions; and (ii) soft
task-progress correspondence learning structures the latent space according to task identity and
execution progress, promoting inter-task discriminability and intra-task temporal continuity.
In Stage 2, we perform joint context optimization and collaborative policy learning. Context
tokens produced by the Stage 1 encoder are projected into the hidden space of a pretrained
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy and integrated into its multi-modal prefix, conditioning
action generation alongside local visual observations, task instructions, and proprioceptive
states. This interface supports variable numbers of shared tokens without modifying the action-
generation architecture. Joint optimization of the context encoder and collaborative policy
allows the receiving robot’s action supervision to refine the sending robot’s representations for
collaborative decision-making. Symmetric encoding and policy interfaces enable bidirectional
sharing, allowing each robot to incorporate complementary context from its partners.
We systematically evaluate our framework across diverse real-world dual-robot manipulation
tasks involving visual occlusion, spatial separation, state-dependent interactions, and role co-
ordination, all of which require robots to leverage complementary observations for successful
execution. We compare our approach against communication-free policies and alternative
information-sharing strategies under varying communication budgets, while conducting abla-
tion studies to assess the contribution of key design choices.
Our main contributions are as follows:
1. Active context sharing framework for multi-robot collaboration. We propose a two-
stage training framework that encodes each robot’s local observations into compact, task-
conditioned latent representations, which are integrated into partner robots’ action policies
and jointly optimized through downstream action supervision. This approach comple-
ments high-level coordination with shared perceptual context, enabling information from
complementary observations to directly inform collaborative action generation.
2. Progress-aware, task-conditioned observation compression. We introduce a query-based
compression module supporting joint and per-camera visual encoding. Task-oriented
future prediction and soft task-progress correspondence learning jointly encourage the re-
tention of predictive visual information, inter-task discriminability, and intra-task temporal
continuity under a fixed representation budget.
3. Joint context optimization for collaborative policy learning. We integrate compressed
context into pretrained VLA policies through a flexible conditioning interface support-
ing variable token budgets and bidirectional context sharing. Action supervision from
the receiving robot jointly optimizes the context encoder and policy, aligning shared
representations with the requirements of collaborative decision-making.
4. Real-world evaluation and representation analysis. We systematically evaluate our
framework on dual-robot manipulation tasks through comparisons against no-sharing and
alternative information-sharing baselines, communication-budgets, and component-wise
ablation studies.

2.3. Stage 1: Collaboration-Agnostic Context Compression
Stage 1 focuses on optimizing a compact, task-oriented representation of each robot’s local ob-
servations under a fixed token budget, without assuming or modeling any specific collaboration
pattern. We achieve this through two complementary objectives: (i) task-oriented future fea-
ture prediction, which encourages the representation to preserve predictive information about
task-relevant visual state transitions, and (ii) soft-label contrastive learning, which structures the
representation space according to task identity and execution-stage proximity. Together, these
objectives yield compact, generalizable representations that capture task-relevant dynamics and
preserve meaningful relationships across tasks and execution stages, providing a reusable basis
for downstream collaborative policy learning.

3. Experiments
3.1. Experimental Setup
We evaluate the proposed framework on real-world dual-robot manipulation tasks using two
Astribot S1 robots (Gao et al., 2025). At each decision step, each robot receives its local camera
observations, task instruction, and proprioceptive state, which are jointly encoded into task-
conditioned context tokens and subsequently exchanged between the two robots. Each robot
integrates the received context into a pretrained 𝜋0.5 policy to generate continuous actions. The
framework does not impose predefined sender–receiver roles: both robots can simultaneously
encode, exchange, and utilize contextual information from their partners, with their respective
active and supporting roles emerging dynamically from the current task stage.

dataset, enabling the model to learn context encoding and action generation across different
role configurations. Unless otherwise specified, all methods share the same data and action

We design two dual-robot tasks to evaluate the effectiveness of cross-robot context sharing. The
first is an occluded-target selection task, which evaluates whether shared context tokens can
convey target information that is unavailable in the acting robot’s local observations. The second
is an alternating leader-follower marker retrieval task, which further investigates whether tar-
get identity and the partner’s execution progress can support bidirectional information sharing
and continuous collaborative control. The former minimizes the influence of action coordination,
isolating the effectiveness of cross-robot information transfer, whereas the latter requires the two
robots to alternate between leader and follower roles within a single task sequence, evaluating
whether shared context can directly inform action generation. Representative rollouts are shown
in Fig. 3.
The main comparison settings are defined as follows: (1) No Sharing, where each robot relies
exclusively on its local visual observations, task instruction, and proprioceptive state; (2) Generic
SigLIP, where the partner’s visual tokens are directly shared without task-oriented compression;
(3) Ours-Frozen, where the context tokens learned in Stage 1 are shared while the context
encoder remains frozen during policy training; and (4) Ours-Joint, where the context encoder
and VLA policy are jointly optimized, allowing downstream action supervision to further shape
the information encoded in the shared latent tokens.

3.2. Occluded-Target Selection
The occluded-target selection task involves two robots standing face-to-face, with three boxes
placed between them. A human repeatedly places a blue plate into one of the boxes. This target-
defining action is visible to Robot A but occluded from Robot B. For each experimental setting,
we conduct 18 trials. This task provides a controlled evaluation of whether shared context
tokens can convey target information unavailable from the acting robot’s local observations.
Robot A remains stationary and does not participate in object manipulation, but has access to
the visual cues needed to identify the correct target box. Robot B performs the pick-and-place
operation, while its local observations alone are insufficient to disambiguate the destination.
Successful task completion therefore requires Robot B to leverage the context tokens shared by
Robot A to resolve the target ambiguity and select the correct box.
The information-providing robot is intentionally kept stationary to minimize confounding
factors arising from action synchronization, physical interaction, and control errors. This task
is designed not to evaluate the execution of basic pick-and-place skills, but to isolate whether
the shared representations can convey the target-defining information needed by an otherwise
capable manipulation policy to select the correct destination among the three available boxes.
We use target-box selection accuracy as the primary evaluation metric. Table 1 summarizes the
results across different settings. Ours-Joint substantially outperforms No Sharing, demonstrat-
ing that compact context tokens effectively convey complementary, target-relevant information
beyond the acting robot’s local observations. This interpretation is further supported by the be-
havior under No Sharing: the robot tends to select the nearest box, suggesting that its decisions
rely primarily on local spatial cues rather than the target-defining information available from
its partner. To evaluate the effectiveness of our representation against high-bandwidth visual
sharing, we further compare Ours-Joint with Generic SigLIP, which serves as a visual-sharing
reference with a token budget of 160. Despite using only 32 shared context tokens, Ours-Joint
achieves a target selection accuracy of 88.9%, outperforming Generic SigLIP by 16.7 percentage
points. This result suggests that simply transmitting more visual information does not necessar-
ily lead to better collaborative decisions. Instead, task-oriented context compression enables
more effective transmission of target-defining information, substantially improving cross-robot
information sharing while reducing the token budget by 80%.

3.4. Ablation of Stage 1 Training Objectives
This section investigates the contributions of the two representation-learning objectives, task-
oriented future prediction and task-progress contrastive learning, on the Alternating Leader–Follower
Marker Retrieval task. We consider two ablation variants: w/o Prediction, which removes fu-
ture prediction while retaining the complete contrastive objective, and w/o Contrastive, which
removes both self-pair correspondence and task-conditioned progress correspondence while
retaining future prediction. The full method combines both objectives.
Each variant undergoes Stage 1 pretraining with its corresponding objectives, followed by joint
Stage 2 training. The context encoder remains trainable in all settings. During Stage 2, receiver-
side action supervision is combined with the auxiliary objectives retained by each variant, while
the removed objectives remain disabled. This comparison isolates the contribution of each
representation-learning objective within the complete two-stage training pipeline.

4. Related Work
4.1. Vision-Language-Action Models
Recent Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have advanced generalist manipulation through
pretrained semantic representations, diverse training data, and multi-modal learning objec-
tives. Pretrained vision-language backbones enable semantic knowledge transfer to robot
control (Black et al., 2024; Driess et al., 2023; Intelligence et al., 2025; Zitkovich et al., 2023), while
large-scale, cross-embodiment training extends task and platform coverage (Kim et al., 2024; Li
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; O’Neill et al., 2024; Team et al., 2024). Multi-modal co-training and
auxiliary visual guidance further strengthen perception–action grounding (Brohan et al., 2022;
Intelligence et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Recent approaches also introduce intermediate represen-
tations and explicit reasoning to bridge semantic understanding and action generation (Huang
et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025, 2026; Zawalski et al., 2024; Zhao
et al., 2025). Despite training across diverse embodiments and tasks, these policies typically
control a single robot using its local visual observations, language instructions, and proprio-
ceptive state. Helix (Figure AI, 2025) extends this paradigm by using shared model weights to
control two humanoid robots in a collaborative, long-horizon manipulation task. While this
demonstrates the potential of generalist VLAs for multi-robot collaboration, how to organize,
compress, and exchange task-relevant perceptual information remains insufficiently explored as
a design problem. Our work addresses this cross-robot perceptual interface by encoding each
robot’s multi-camera observations into compact, task-conditioned latent representations that
directly condition its partner’s action policy. This formulation makes perceptual context sharing
an explicit component of collaborative policy learning.
4.2. Task Orchestration for Multi-Robot Collaboration
LLM- and VLM-based agents are able to support multi-robot collaboration through task decom-
position, capability matching, skill invocation, execution monitoring, and failure recovery. By
incorporating execution feedback, these systems adapt their plans and coordination strategies
to environmental changes and unexpected outcomes. InstAgent (Luo et al., 2026) extends this
18


paradigm with recursive self-improvement (RSI) for heterogeneous robot teams. Its agentic
behavior trees encode capability allocation, inter-robot dependencies, and task handover con-
ditions. Following an execution failure, the system revises the relevant collaboration subtree,
validates the revision through re-execution, and retains successful modifications for subsequent
reuse. These approaches primarily address task orchestration and execution management
through high-level coordination interfaces, using shared memory and iterative refinement to
improve task allocation, execution ordering, and failure recovery. Our work investigates the
complementary problem of cross-robot perceptual context sharing during policy execution.
Agentic orchestration and RSI can thus manage high-level coordination, while our framework
supplies shared perceptual context for fine-grained collaborative action generation.
4.3. Collaborative Perception
Collaborative perception mitigates partial observability by enabling agents to exchange comple-
mentary information. In autonomous driving, V2VNet (Wang et al., 2020) shares compressed
intermediate features for perception and motion prediction, while When2com (Liu et al., 2020)
and Where2comm (Hu et al., 2022) improve communication efficiency through learned agent
selection and spatially selective feature exchange, respectively. These approaches primarily
optimize communication for perceptual objectives rather than downstream action generation.
In embodied manipulation, Sequential Asymmetric Imitation (Chen et al., 2026c) learns coor-
dinated behaviors without explicit inter-robot communication, whereas MAAP (Chen et al.,
2026a) integrates complementary wrist-camera observations for collaborative control. Recent
approaches explore explicit cross-robot information exchange. LatentToM (He et al., 2025)
learns individual and team-consensus representations for explicit communication and implicit
partner-state inference. DuoMind (Zhou et al., 2026) coordinates VLA policies through semantic
information from a VLM orchestrator, while Latent Telepathy (Wang et al., 2026) shares frozen
perceptual latents to provide task-relevant information unavailable from local observations. Our
work learns compact, task-conditioned visual representations that directly condition a partner’s
VLA policy. Action supervision from the receiving robot jointly optimizes the context encoder
and policy, aligning shared perceptual representations with collaborative action generation
under a limited representation budget.
4.4. Predictive and Progress-Aware Representation Learning
Predictive representation learning models environmental dynamics through future-state predic-
tion in latent space. V-JEPA2 (Assran et al., 2025) learns dynamics through joint-embedding pre-
diction and supports planning with an action-conditioned latent world model. FLARE (Zheng
et al., 2025) aligns policy features with future observation representations, while LaWAM (Chen
et al., 2026b) uses compact latent visual subgoals to guide VLA policies. These approaches
introduce predictive information into robot control without requiring pixel-level future genera-
tion. Task progress provides a complementary signal for structuring embodied representations.
XIRL (Zakka et al., 2021) learns progress-sensitive embeddings from cross-embodiment videos
and derives rewards from latent distances to goal states. VIP (Ma et al., 2023b) and LIV (Ma et al.,
2023a) exploit temporal structure and value-based objectives for representation learning and
reward modeling, with LIV additionally incorporating language goals. PROGRESSOR (Ayalew
et al., 2024) estimates progress relative to initial and goal observations, while ProgVLA (Kim
et al., 2026) and ProgressVLA (Yan et al., 2026) incorporate progress prediction or guidance into
long-horizon VLA policies. Whereas these methods primarily use progress for reward modeling
19


or individual-robot control, our work uses it to structure shared perceptual representations
for multi-robot collaboration. A partner’s execution phase can inform whether a robot should
wait, proceed, or coordinate a handover. We combine task-oriented future prediction with
soft task-progress correspondence learning to preserve predictive visual information while
promoting inter-task discriminability and intra-task temporal continuity. Progress serves only
as a training signal, rather than an explicit prediction or communication target. The result-
ing compact representations directly condition a partner’s VLA policy, linking predictive and
progress-aware representation learning to collaborative action generation.
5. Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work
We propose a two-stage active context sharing framework for multi-robot collaboration, in which
each robot encodes its multi-camera observations and task instruction into a compact latent rep-
resentation that is directly integrated into its partner’s VLA policy. The first stage learns shared
representations that capture task-relevant information and execution-state structure, while the
second stage further aligns these representations with collaborative decision-making through
downstream action supervision. Extensive real-world dual-robot experiments demonstrate that
the proposed framework provides decision-relevant information unavailable from local obser-
vations, improves target selection under visual occlusion, and enables bidirectional sharing of
target identity and execution progress in alternating leader–follower tasks. Further ablations and
communication-budget analyses validate the effectiveness of the two-stage training strategy and
joint optimization, demonstrating that compact task-oriented tokens can achieve performance
comparable to high-bandwidth generic visual features at substantially lower communication
cost.
Although our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, the se-
mantic content of the shared tokens remains underexplored. In particular, how visual semantics,
task states, and execution-stage information are encoded in the latent representations, and how
these representations influence the receiver’s subsequent decisions and actions, remain open
questions for further investigation.
Future work will explore extensions to larger robot teams, asynchronous and selective informa-
tion sharing, and multi-modal collaborative representations that incorporate temporal memory,
tactile sensing, and proprioception. More broadly, our results point toward a paradigm in which
multi-robot collaboration moves beyond exchanging high-level commands or generic visual
features, enabling robots to actively distill and communicate compact, task-relevant context
shaped jointly by the sender’s task objectives and the receiver’s decision requirements.
