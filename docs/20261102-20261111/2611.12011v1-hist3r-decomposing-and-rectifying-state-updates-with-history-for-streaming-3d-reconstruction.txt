Abstract 

Streaming 3D reconstruction with recurrent states enables long-video scene under-standing under bounded memory, but turns reconstruction into a state-evolution problem: each incoming frame updates a compact scene memory on which all sub-sequent predictions depend, so unreliable updates overwrite historical context and accumulate into long-horizon geometric drift. We analyze CUT3R-style recurrent updates through a direction–magnitude decomposition, in which the magnitude sets the update gain, i.e., how strongly an observation is written into the state, and the direction encodes the geometric intent, i.e., where the update drives the state. This decomposition provides a unified view of CUT3R and its variants, revealing that existing training-free extensions perform magnitude-only control: they can attenuate an unreliable update but cannot correct its direction. We propose HIST3R, a training-free framework built on CUT3R that controls both components using history. HIST3R rectifies the update direction using locally consistent historical states, and modulates the update magnitude according to the visual discrepancy be-tween the current observation and its history, suppressing redundant updates while preserving complementary evidence. Across camera pose estimation, 3D recon-struction, and video depth estimation, HIST3R consistently improves long-horizon performance while retaining the memory footprint and inference speed of CUT3R. With 1000 input frames, it reduces ATE by 23.87% on ScanNet and 33.65% on TUM-Dynamics relative to the strongest competing method; with 500 frames, it improves median reconstruction accuracy over the strongest CUT3R-based variant by 56.52% on 7-Scenes and 33.96% on NRGBD.

1 Introduction 

Recovering dense 3D geometry from 2D observations is a central problem in computer vision, supporting applications across autonomous driving [1–3], augmented reality (AR) [4–7], and em-bodied AI [8–10]. Recent Transformer-based foundation models [11–13] have increasingly replaced classical optimization-based pipelines [14–18] due to their strong generalization ability and effi-cient feed-forward inference. Yet this advantage does not scale to long videos: reasoning jointly over an ever-growing set of views makes feed-forward reconstruction increasingly memory- and compute-intensive. Streaming 3D reconstruction addresses this scalability issue by processing frames sequentially [19– 27]. Existing methods preserve historical context through either explicit memory or implicit memory . Explicit-memory methods retain processed frames, features, or tokens in an external memory bank, preserving rich history at the cost of memory or computation that grows with sequence length [19–21]. Implicit-memory methods, including ours, instead compress history into a fixed-size recurrent state with bounded memory [23–25]. This compression, however, shifts the central

We study this update problem through CUT3R [23], a representative implicit-memory streaming model. For each incoming frame, CUT3R produces a candidate update that writes the new observation into the recurrent state. Such an update must determine two things: how strongly the observation should modify the accumulated scene representation, and what geometric content it should contribute. We refer to the former as the update gain and the latter as the geometric intent . In the recurrent state space, these two roles correspond naturally to the magnitude and direction of the candidate update, which motivates a direction–magnitude decomposition of recurrent state updates. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this decomposition provides a unified view of CUT3R and its recent training-free extensions. Vanilla CUT3R commits the candidate update as is, while TTT3R [24] and TTSA3R [25] can be interpreted as magnitude-only update control: they estimate a scalar gate from auxiliary signals, such as state-to-image attention or temporal–spatial dissimilarity, to modulate the update gain while leaving the update direction unchanged. As a result, they can attenuate an unreliable or redundant update, but cannot correct the geometric intent that determines where the recurrent state is driven. This limitation becomes increasingly critical over long input sequences. When an incoming frame induces a biased candidate update, magnitude-only gating may reduce its step size, yet the state still moves along the same biased direction. Since the recurrent state compactly carries past information, such directional biases persist and compound across frames, gradually pulling the state away from coherent scene structure and leading to geometric drift. Reliable long-horizon streaming reconstruction can therefore be viewed as a state-space evolution problem, requiring control over both how strongly the recurrent state is updated and how its geometric evolution is directed. Motivated by this view, we propose HIST3R , a training-free framework built on CUT3R for long-sequence streaming 3D reconstruction. HIST3R decomposes the CUT3R candidate update into 

direction and magnitude , enabling separate control over geometric intent and update gain. To rectify geometric intent, HIST3R uses recent recurrent states as a local reference for plausible state evolution and adjusts the observation-induced update toward a history-consistent endpoint. To modulate the update gain, HIST3R estimates how much new evidence the incoming observation provides to the existing recurrent state. It therefore compares the current visual context with historical visual contexts 2and adapts the update magnitude according to their discrepancy, suppressing redundant state changes while preserving responsiveness to observations that depart from recent history. We evaluate HIST3R across camera pose estimation, 3D reconstruction, and video depth estimation under both standard and long-sequence streaming settings. Across these evaluations, HIST3R consistently improves long-horizon performance over prior streaming methods while maintaining competitive short-sequence accuracy, all without additional training. These results demonstrate that direction–magnitude decomposition provides an effective mechanism for controlling geometric intent and update gain in implicit-memory streaming reconstruction. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce a direction–magnitude decomposition of recurrent state updates, which unifies CUT3R and its variants and reveals update direction as the missing control axis for long-horizon reliability. • We propose HIST3R, a training-free extension of CUT3R that rectifies geometric intent with historical recurrent states and modulates update gain with observation–history visual discrepancy, while preserving the memory footprint and inference speed of CUT3R. • We demonstrate consistent long-horizon improvements across camera pose estimation, 3D reconstruction, and video depth estimation. At 1000 input frames, HIST3R reduces ATE over the second-best method, TTSA3R, by 23 .87 % on ScanNet and 33 .65 % on TUM-Dynamics; at 500 frames, it improves median reconstruction accuracy over TTSA3R by 

56 .52 % on 7-Scenes and 33 .96 % on NRGBD. 

2 Related Work 

Offline Reconstruction Foundation Models. Recent learning-based methods have shifted 3D reconstruction from classical geometric pipelines [14–18] toward generalizable feed-forward foun-dation models [11–13, 28–31]. One line of work builds on pairwise reconstruction. DUSt3R [11] formulates 3D reconstruction as direct pointmap regression from image pairs, and subsequent meth-ods improve matching, dynamic-scene robustness, and reconstruction quality within this pairwise paradigm [12, 28, 32]. Although effective for local two-view geometry, these methods still require global alignment or aggregation to reconstruct larger image collections. Another line of work performs all-at-once multi-view reconstruction. VGGT [13] jointly processes multiple images with global transformer attention to predict camera poses, depth, pointmaps, and tracks, with recent variants improving inference speed, geometric priors, or scalability [33–37]. However, offline and all-at-once models assume a fixed input set and reason over all views jointly, making memory and computation grow rapidly as more frames are added. This scalability bottleneck motivates online reconstruction foundation models, which process frames incrementally while maintaining historical scene information. 

Online Reconstruction Foundation Models. Online reconstruction foundation models can be broadly grouped into explicit-memory and implicit-memory approaches. Explicit-memory methods retain processed frames as externally accessible entries, such as spatial memories, cached features, or historical tokens. [19–22, 26, 27, 38]. Spann3R [38] adapts DUSt3R into an online setting by storing previous predictions as key-value features, while Point3R [19] introduces a spatial pointer memory associated with 3D positions. StreamVGGT [20] and STream3R [21] instead use causal attention with key-value caching to process incoming frames incrementally. Although these methods avoid full reprocessing of all frames, their memory or computation still tends to grow with sequence length. InfiniteVGGT [22] mitigates this issue through token pruning inspired by streaming language models [39, 40], but its pruning strategy mainly addresses cache efficiency rather than explicitly correcting accumulated geometric errors. Implicit-memory methods, represented by CUT3R [23], compress history into a fixed-size recurrent state, keeping memory bounded regardless of sequence length [23–25] . Since all subsequent predictions depend on this compact state, reconstruction quality hinges on how each observation updates it. 3.2 Rectifying Geometric Intent via Historical State Consistency

4 Experiments 

We evaluate HIST3R across camera pose estimation (Sec. 4.1), 3D reconstruction (Sec. 4.2), and video depth estimation (Sec. 4.3), followed by ablations that validate the proposed direction–magnitude decomposition view (Sec. 4.4). 

Baselines and protocol. We compare HIST3R with recent online reconstruction methods. CUT3R [23] serves as the base implicit-memory model. Our main comparisons are TTT3R [24] and TTSA3R [25], two training-free CUT3R extensions that modulate the update gain while leav-ing the geometric intent unchanged. We also compare with explicit-memory methods, including Point3R [19], StreamVGGT [20], and InfiniteVGGT [22]. All hyperparameters are selected once on the ScanNet training split and remain fixed across all datasets, tasks, and sequence lengths, as detailed in Appendix A.2. Since HIST3R is designed for long-horizon streaming reconstruction, the main paper focuses on long-sequence evaluation. Sparse-view and standard short-sequence results are reported in Appendix B. All methods are evaluated on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU with 80GB memory.

4.2 3D Reconstruction 

Following prior work [19, 22, 23], we evaluate 3D reconstruction on 7-Scenes [47] and NRGBD [48]. We assess reconstruction quality by comparing predicted pointmaps with ground-truth point clouds using accuracy, completeness, and normal consistency (NC). Accuracy measures the nearest-neighbor distance from the predicted reconstruction to the ground truth, while completeness measures the distance in the reverse direction; for both metrics, we report mean and median values. To evaluate long-sequence reconstruction, we test each method on input sequences of 300, 400, and 500 frames. As shown in Table 1, HIST3R achieves the strongest reconstruction quality on 7-Scenes, obtaining the best accuracy and completeness across long input sequences while remaining competitive in normal consistency. On NRGBD, HIST3R achieves the best or second-best results on most metrics. Com-pared with explicit-memory methods such as InfiniteVGGT and Point3R, no single method dominates every metric, and HIST3R remains competitive in accuracy and completeness. Its main advantage lies in efficiency: by maintaining a compact recurrent state, HIST3R requires substantially less memory and runs considerably faster than explicit-memory methods, as analyzed in Appendix A.4. The advantage is especially clear within the CUT3R-based implicit-memory family. HIST3R con-sistently outperforms CUT3R, TTT3R, and TTSA3R across nearly all datasets, sequence lengths, and reconstruction metrics. At 500 frames, HIST3R improves median accuracy over TTSA3R, the second-best CUT3R variant, by 56 .52 % on 7-Scenes and 33 .96 % on NRGBD. This margin indicates that controlling both geometric intent and update gain substantially strengthens CUT3R’s long-horizon reconstruction capability.

Component ablation. We isolate the two update controls by applying only direction rectification or only magnitude modulation. As shown in Table 2, both improve over CUT3R across camera pose estimation, video depth estimation, and 3D reconstruction, and their combination achieves the best overall performance. This supports the decomposition view in Eq. 4: reliable long-horizon updating benefits from jointly rectifying the geometric intent and modulating the update gain. 

Design ablations. Table 3 ablates the design choices within each component, applying direction rectification and magnitude modulation separately. Direction rectification consists of three stages: aggregating historical states with cosine weights to form the history-proposed endpoint in Eq. 7, fusing it with the candidate endpoint through the alignment gate in Eq. 8, and using the rectified endpoint only to set the update direction in Eq. 5. Removing any of these stages degrades performance. Replacing cosine weights with uniform averaging weakens the history proposal, and removing the alignment gate, which imposes history unconditionally, causes the largest drop. Directly adopting the rectified endpoint as the new state, which alters both direction and magnitude, also degrades performance, supporting the separate control of the two components. For the history window, performance improves as k increases and remains stable for 8 ≤ k ≤ 32 , while an overly long window introduces less relevant states; we therefore use k = 8 as the smallest stable window. For magnitude modulation, state-token-specific attention pooling clearly outperforms global average pooling, confirming that different state tokens rely on different image regions. Using only the first-layer attention map performs on par with aggregating multiple layers while allowing the remaining layers to keep FlashAttention [51], which keeps HIST3R nearly as fast as CUT3R, as shown in Appendix A.4. 

5 Conclusion 

We presented HIST3R, a training-free framework that improves long-sequence streaming 3D recon-struction on top of CUT3R. The key idea is to view recurrent state updates as controllable state-space evolution and to decompose each update into direction and magnitude, which correspond to its geo-metric intent and update gain. This decomposition unifies CUT3R and its training-free variants, and reveals that existing variants control only the update gain, leaving the geometric intent as a missing control axis. HIST3R controls both components with history: it rectifies the geometric intent using the local structure of recent recurrent states, and modulates the update gain using observation–history visual discrepancy. Together, these two controls allow the compact recurrent state to incorporate useful new evidence while avoiding redundant or unstable updates over long sequences. Experiments across camera pose estimation, 3D reconstruction, and video depth estimation show that HIST3R consistently improves long-horizon performance while preserving the memory and speed of CUT3R. Overall, our results suggest that controlling both where and how strongly the recurrent state is updated provides an effective principle for robust long-horizon 3D perception.

CUT3R recurrent states naturally exhibit strong local affinity and low-dimensional variation, and HIST3R further accentuates this structure through history-guided rectification. To further support the local-structure assumption used in HIST3R, we empirically analyze the recurrent state evolution of CUT3R during streaming inference. Given a sequence of recurrent states, we examine two complementary properties: the temporal affinity between states and the intrinsic dimensionality of their variations. Specifically, we compute the cosine affinity between recurrent states over time and apply PCA to the corresponding state differences. As shown in Fig. 6, representative sequences from 7-Scenes [47], KITTI [50], NRGBD [48], and ScanNet [45] consistently exhibit strong local structure. Adjacent CUT3R recurrent states have high cosine affinity, indicating that state evolution changes smoothly over short temporal windows rather than following arbitrary frame-wise transitions. Moreover, the PCA results show that state variations are largely concentrated in a small number of principal directions, suggesting that recent states trace a compact local state manifold. These observations provide empirical evidence for using recent history as a local reference for rectifying recurrent updates. In particular, when the CUT3R candidate endpoint is compatible with the local evolution pattern, it can be approximated by locally similar historical states, consistent with the self-expressiveness principle in low-dimensional subspace modeling [41]. Inference memory. As shown in Fig. 7, naive explicit-memory-based methods require increasingly large memory as the sequence length grows. StreamVGGT quickly exceeds the 80GB memory budget, while Point3R steadily accumulates memory due to its explicit pointmap memory and approaches the memory bound at 1000 frames. Although InfiniteVGGT mitigates this issue through cache pruning, it still requires substantially higher memory than implicit-memory-based methods. In contrast, CUT3R-style implicit-memory methods, including HIST3R, maintain the lowest and nearly constant memory usage across different sequence lengths, since historical information is compressed into a compact recurrent state rather than stored as an ever-growing explicit memory. 

Inference speed. In terms of inference speed, explicit-memory-based methods are substantially slower because they rely on attention over large explicit memories or caches. By maintaining historical information in a compact recurrent state, implicit-memory-based methods use much smaller attention matrices and achieve faster streaming inference. Among CUT3R-based extensions, HIST3R maintains nearly identical FPS to the original CUT3R and is substantially faster than TTT3R and TTSA3R. HIST3R only exposes the state-to-image attention map from the first decoder layer for constructing visual summaries, allowing the remaining decoder layers to retain FlashAttention [51]. By contrast, TTT3R and TTSA3R require attention maps from all decoder layers, which materializes attention matrices throughout inference and introduces significant overhead. These results show that HIST3R improves long-sequence reconstruction quality while preserving the memory and speed advantages of compact recurrent state-space inference.

D Discussion 

Limitations. As a training-free method, HIST3R is ultimately bounded by the representation and candidate updates produced by the pretrained CUT3R model. Although HIST3R improves recurrent state updates by modulating the direction and magnitude of each candidate update at inference time, it cannot recover geometric information that is not encoded by the underlying model or fully correct failures caused by severely inaccurate candidate predictions. A promising direction is to incorporate this direction–magnitude view into pretraining or fine-tuning, so that the model can learn to produce more reliable geometric intent and update gain rather than correcting them only at inference time. 

Broader impact. This work analyzes the evolution of implicit recurrent states in streaming 3D reconstruction from a geometric perspective. By decomposing state updates into direction and magnitude, HIST3R provides an interpretable view of how online reconstruction models accumulate, preserve, and revise geometric information over long sequences. We believe this perspective can inform the design of future state-space reconstruction models, encouraging update mechanisms that explicitly reason about both geometric intent and update strength. Such models may benefit online 3D perception systems that require stable long-horizon reconstruction under bounded memory, including embodied AI, augmented reality, and robotics. At the same time, advances in streaming reconstruction may strengthen downstream applications involving spatial understanding and scene capture, and should therefore be deployed with appropriate consideration of privacy and safety when reconstructing real-world environments. 24 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
