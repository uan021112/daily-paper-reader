# ACT PRUNE : Action-Grounded Visual Token Pruning for Efficient Vision-Language-Action Models 

Sungwon Moon 1, Minji Kim 2, Hyemin Boo 2, Daeun Song 2,†, and Jiyoung Lee 1,2,†

Abstract — Vision-language-action (VLA) models perform versatile manipulation tasks from language instructions, but their inference cost limits how fast a robot can respond in closed-loop control. Visual token pruning reduces this cost; however, prior methods estimate token redundancy from en-coded visual embeddings or attention, only after the visual encoder has processed the entire observation. We propose ACT PRUNE , a training-free token pruning method that moves the pruning decision ahead of the vision encoder by grounding it in a geometric prior derived from the robot’s own action. The action chunk predicted at the previous control step is already available before the current observation is encoded, and it specifies the end-effector and camera motion between consecutive observations. The same action affects the two standard camera views asymmetrically: in the fixed third-person view, A CT PRUNE retains patches along the projected end-effector trajectory together with semantic anchors, while in the wrist view, it prunes patches that remain unchanged after compensating for the action-induced camera motion. Ex-tensive experiments on two simulation environments (LIBERO and VLABench) and four real-robot tasks demonstrate that ACT PRUNE achieves up to 1.61 × speedup and 59.5% FLOPs reduction with negligible degradation in success rates. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

A long-standing goal in robotics is to develop a single policy that can perform diverse manipulation tasks from natural language instructions. Vision-language-action (VLA) models have made progress toward this goal. Many of them build on pretrained vision-language models and generalize to new objects and instructions by inheriting web-scale representations. However, this capability comes with high inference cost. VLA models rely on large vision-language backbones that process hundreds of visual tokens at every control step. In closed-loop control, this computation is repeated for every new observation. As a result, inference latency can limit how fast the robot can respond. Recent methods have explored different ways to reduce the inference cost of VLA models [1]–[4]. Visual token pruning instead reduces computation by removing redundant visual tokens during inference. Existing pruning methods identify redundant tokens from encoded visual features [5], [6], attention [7], [8], temporal changes [9], [10], or action information [10], [11]. In these approaches, redundancy is mainly determined from the visual observations themselves, after the vision encoder has processed every token.

A robot in closed-loop control, however, does not simply observe a sequence of images. Between two observations, the robot executes an action that changes its state and interaction with the scene. The action therefore provides information about where and how the next observation may change. More importantly, this action is already available before the next observation arrives. Unlike visual features or attention scores, it does not require the current observation to be encoded first. The previous action can therefore provide an early signal for identifying visual redundancy before visual encoding. The effect of the action on the observation depends on the camera viewpoint. A standard manipulation setup pairs a arXiv:2611.08827v1 [cs.RO] 6 Nov 2026 fixed third-person camera with a wrist-mounted camera [12]– [14]. In the fixed third-person view, the scene stays stable across steps. Change is confined to the region the manipula-tor occupies. In contrast, the wrist view changes globally and dynamically across steps. These changes arise from camera motion and viewpoint shifts induced by the actions. The action therefore implies contrasting patterns of new information across camera views. Based on these insights, we propose A CT PRUNE (Fig. 1), a training-free framework that exploits the closed-loop struc-ture of robot manipulation to reduce visual computation. Specifically, the action chunk predicted at the previous step is available before the current observation is processed in the visual encoder. It provides a physically grounded prior on where visual computation should be spent without requiring a vision encoder forward pass. A CT PRUNE leverages this prior to prune visual tokens, projecting the anticipated end-effector trajectory onto the fixed third-person view to mark newly visualized information, while removing motion-compensated redundant patches for the wrist view. The remaining budget is applied after the encoder, where encoder attention ranks the retained tokens. We summarize our contributions as follows: 1) We introduce A CT PRUNE , a training-free framework that derives geometric priors directly from robot ac-tions, enabling visual token pruning before the vision encoder. 2) We identify that the same action induces distinct re-dundancy patterns in fixed and wrist-mounted views, and exploit this asymmetry with view-specific pruning. 3) ACT PRUNE achieves state-of-the-art efficiency on LIBERO [13] and VLABench [14], with up to 1.61 ×

speedup and 59.5% FLOPs reduction while preserving competitive performance. We further validate its real-world advantage on a 7-DoF Franka Research 3. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Efficient Vision-Language-Action Models 

VLA models enable robots to perform general-purpose manipulation tasks. However, they require substantial com-putation at every control step, and inference latency limits the control frequency. Existing approaches improve VLA efficiency at different levels of the inference pipeline. Lightweight architectures [1], [2] replace or compress com-ponents of the backbone, quantization methods [3] reduce the numerical precision of model parameters, and early-exit methods [4] terminate computation once intermediate rep-resentations suffice. While effective, these methods require retraining or modification of the underlying model. Another line of work targets action generation rather than reducing the cost of individual policy inference. High-frequency VLA frameworks [15], [16] increase control fre-quency through action chunking or asynchronous decoding. By predicting multiple actions at once or decoupling exe-cution from inference, these methods reduce how frequently the full policy must be invoked. However, each policy infer-ence still processes the vision-language backbone, making per-inference visual computation an important bottleneck. Our work is complementary to these approaches. Rather than modifying the policy architecture or action-generation process, we reduce the visual computation required during closed-loop inference without additional training. 

B. Visual Token Pruning for VLA 

Visual token pruning has been widely explored in VLMs to reduce this cost. Existing methods estimate token importance using encoded visual features [5], [6], [17] or language-model attention [7], [8]. Tokens with low estimated impor-tance are then pruned or merged before subsequent trans-former layers. This reduces language-model computation by limiting the number of visual tokens. These approaches are effective for static vision-language inputs, but typically determine redundancy from each observation independently. They therefore do not exploit the temporal structure of closed-loop robot control. Recent VLA acceleration methods extend visual token pruning to closed-loop control. Temporal redundancy can be identified from changes across consecutive observations [9], [10]. Frame differencing provides a lightweight measure of this redundancy, but becomes unreliable under camera motion because it compares pixels at fixed image coordi-nates. Action information has also been incorporated into token selection [10], [11], [18]. In these methods, actions are combined with signals obtained after visual encoding to estimate token importance. As a result, token selection occurs only after visual encoding. In contrast, A CT PRUNE uses the executed action to identify redundancy in the subsequent observation before visual encoding. III. A. Overview & Problem Formulation 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, A CT PRUNE leverages the previous action At−1 to derive view-specific pruning signals before visual encoding. The same action affects the two views asymmetrically: (i) in the third-person view, it reveals where the manipulator will move within a static scene. A CT PRUNE 

projects the end-effector trajectory from At−1 onto the image plane and retains patches around it. Semantic an-chors, extracted once from the first observation, additionally preserve task-relevant regions. (ii) In the wrist view, the action instead moves the camera itself, so temporal redun-dancy under motion compensation reveals prunable regions. Previous methods [9], [10] compare patches at correspond-ing spatial locations across consecutive frames, where even static regions exhibit large appearance changes under the dynamically translated wrist view. In contrast, A CT PRUNE 

compensates for the camera motion induced by At−1 by reprojecting patches from the previous observation into the current view using depth maps from the wrist-mounted RGBD camera. Patches whose features remain unchanged after compensation carry no additional information beyond the previous observation. A CT PRUNE therefore merges them into context tokens that preserve their coarse content. Specifically, given a target retained ratio γ, we allocate the retained token budget into a patch-level ratio γpatch and an embedding-level ratio γembed , such that γ = γpatch + γembed .Consequently, only 2Nv · γ visual tokens are forwarded to the LLM backbone; since visual tokens constitute the vast majority of the input sequence compared to the short instruction ( i.e. , Nv >> N ℓ), this directly translates into a substantial reduction in VLA inference cost.

B. Evaluation Benchmarks 

LIBERO [13] consists of four task suites: Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. Each suite contains 10 tasks, and we evaluate 50 episodes per task, resulting in 500 episodes per suite. We follow the evaluation protocol of OpenVLA-OFT [15] and use the official model weights. 

VLABench [14] is a large-scale language-conditioned ma-nipulation benchmark comprising 100 task categories and over 2,000 objects with extensive domain randomization. We evaluate 10 primitive tasks with 50 episodes per task using the official π0.5 checkpoint fine-tuned on these tasks. 

Real-world Benchmark. We further evaluate the real-world performance of A CT PRUNE on four manipulation tasks con-sisting of placing a banana on a plate, picking up a doll ,closing a tool box, and placing a towel on a box, as shown in Fig. 3. These tasks span different object properties and interaction types: rigid, deformable, and articulated objects, and both pick-and-place and in-place manipulation. For each task, we conduct 10 trials with variations in object poses and initial configurations. Task success is determined according to predefined task-specific completion criteria. 

C. Main Results 

To evaluate whether A CT PRUNE preserves task perfor-mance under visual token reduction, we compare it with existing token reduction methods across different VLA mod-els and manipulation benchmarks. In particular, Table I and Table II report the results on LIBERO with OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5, respectively. On OpenVLA-OFT, A CT PRUNE 

consistently achieves the highest average success rate across all retention ratios, reaching 96.9%, 96.1%, and 88.6% at 50%, 25%, and 12.5% retention, respectively. The advantage is particularly evident at 25% retention, where A CT PRUNE 

retains performance close to the vanilla policy while reducing FLOPs from 4.739 to 1.921 and achieving a 1.61 × speedup. At 12.5% retention, A CT PRUNE further reduces FLOPs to 1.474 while maintaining the highest average success rate among the compared methods. The same trend holds for π0.5.As shown in Table II, A CT PRUNE achieves the highest aver-age success rate at all three retention ratios. Fig. 4 visualizes the retained tokens (colored patches) in A CT PRUNE . Even at a 25% retained ratio, they concentrate on the workspace and the target objects ( e.g. , alphabet soup , basket ). We further assess A CT PRUNE on VLABench using π0.5,as reported in Table III. At 50% retention, ACT PRUNE 

achieves an average success rate of 26.3%, which remains competitive with existing pruning methods. At 25% reten-tion, performance degrades substantially for all methods, with ACT PRUNE remaining competitive with the VLA-specific baselines. Across both settings, A CT PRUNE attains the lowest FLOPs, reducing them to 2.063 at 25% retention. Overall, the results demonstrate that A CT PRUNE effectively reduces visual computation while preserving manipulation performance across different VLA models and benchmarks.

When the gripper occupies a large portion of the wrist view as in VLABench, its patches move with the camera and are conservatively retained. In addition, extreme prun-ing ratios gradually trade task performance for efficiency. A patch removed before the encoder significantly reduces visual computation but neither receives global context nor can be recovered downstream. This motivates our two-stage design: patch-level pruning before the encoder reduces visual computation, while token-level refinement based on encoder attention provides a more informed pruning decision. Finally, since ACT PRUNE assumes calibrated cameras and depth sensing, extending the framework to uncalibrated or RGB-only settings is a promising direction for future work. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented A CT PRUNE , which leverages the previously predicted action chunk as a physically grounded prior avail-able before the current observation is processed to derive view-specific signals: trajectory prediction with semantic anchors for the third-person view, and motion-compensated redundancy for the wrist view. By compressing a substantial portion of patches before the vision encoder, A CT PRUNE 

reduces the computation of both the visual encoder and the LLM backbone.
