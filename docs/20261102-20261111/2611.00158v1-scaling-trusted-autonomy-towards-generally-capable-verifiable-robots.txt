Abstract of “Scaling Trusted Autonomy: Towards Generally Capable Verifiable Robots” by Benedict
Quartey, Ph.D., Brown University, October 2026.
Robots powered by foundation models are increasingly able to interpret open-ended language, recog-
nize unfamiliar objects, and operate across diverse environments. Yet broader capability does not by
itself establish that a robot has understood a request, that its actions will produce the intended effects,
or that its internal representation still corresponds to reality. Classical symbolic planning provides
explicit structure for composition and verification, but its application to robotics has traditionally
depended on hand-engineered skills and representations specified in advance. Learned policies can
broaden the robot’s repertoire, but provide weak guarantees about what will happen and why. This
thesis argues that scaling trusted autonomy requires a neurosymbolic approach that unifies the gen-
erality of learned models with the symbolic structure needed for robust robot behavior. It explores
three complementary directions toward this goal. First, LIMP uses foundation models and spatial
reasoning to ground natural-language instructions in explicit task constraints and instruction-specific
semantic maps, enabling task-and-motion planning while exposing the robot’s interpretation for in-
spection. Second, REALM extends this framework to an evolving world. It maintains an editable,
photorealistic 3D Gaussian scene, predicts the consequences of planned manipulation, and locally rec-
onciles the scene so that subsequent instructions obtain their grounding and planning geometry from
an updated state. Third, PACTS learns visuomotor skills that jointly generate action trajectories and
predictions of their symbolic effects, supporting skill composition, progress monitoring, and recovery
through replanning. Together, these contributions provide mechanisms for constructing task-relevant
abstractions when needed, connecting learned behavior to its predicted symbolic effects, and main-
taining an evolving world representation for subsequent tasks. This body of work preserves explicit
structure for reasoning and verification while broadening how it is obtained, providing a foundation
for generally capable robots whose behavior can be assessed under the assumptions of their models.

Chapter 1
Introduction
A central ambition of robotics is to build machines that can perform a wide range of useful tasks in the
environments people inhabit. Such robots would allow people to specify goals and constraints without
prescribing each movement or engineering a separate system for every activity. Their usefulness would
depend on the ability to interpret unfamiliar requests, apply learned abilities in new circumstances,
and sustain purposeful behavior as their surroundings change. The challenge is to make this breadth
of capability compatible with behavior that people can understand, assess, and rely upon.
Foundation models have opened new possibilities for pursuing this goal. Their training on broad
collections of data allows knowledge acquired across tasks and settings to inform the behavior of
individual robot systems (Bommasani et al., 2021). They support interfaces through which people
can describe tasks and objects beyond a fixed vocabulary, and connect pretrained language and visual
knowledge to robot control across diverse tasks and settings (Minderer et al., 2022; Zitkovich et al.,
2023; Kim et al., 2025). This progress changes what can reasonably be asked of a robot. It also brings
a longstanding requirement into sharper focus: as robots undertake a broader range of activities, there
must be a corresponding basis for assessing whether their behavior meets the requirements of those
activities.
Greater capability does not automatically provide that assurance. A robot may respond fluently
to an instruction while leaving its interpretation implicit, or produce effective movements without
exposing the conditions those movements establish. The consequences matter when people delegate
tasks in homes and workplaces, where intentions must be communicated, constraints respected, and
decisions made from incomplete information. The scientific problem is therefore to extend the scope
of autonomous behavior while retaining the means to examine what the robot understood, reason
about what it will do, and assess whether continued reliance on its decisions is warranted. Generality
and assurance must advance together.
Classical planning provides an important foundation for this objective. Explicit task represen-
tations and action models support the composition of behaviors, while formal specifications make
requirements available for checking and behavior synthesis (Fikes and Nilsson, 1971; Kress-Gazit
et al., 2009).
Task and motion planning connects this reasoning to geometric feasibility (Garrett
1


et al., 2021). These strengths, however, depend on suitable descriptions of the task, the robot’s skills,
and the world. Constructing and maintaining those representations across varied environments has
traditionally required substantial engineering, limiting the settings in which structured reasoning can
be applied. Learned models offer ways to broaden perception and behavior; planning offers structure
for reasoning about their use. The challenge is to bring these strengths together without allowing the
assumptions that support reliable reasoning to become hidden inside increasingly capable components.
The central research question of this thesis is therefore: how can robot capability be extended across
tasks and environments while preserving the explicit structure needed to interpret, compose, and check
its behavior? This thesis develops an answer through grounded language understanding, composable
state-reporting skills, and evolving representations of the world. These contributions connect learned
capabilities to representations that a planner can inspect and use.
1.1
From Tasks to Robot Behavior
Two complementary approaches help explain both the opportunity and the difficulty of this problem.
Planning constructs behavior by reasoning over a model of the task and environment, whereas policy
learning acquires behavior from experience. The former makes the organization of behavior explicit;
the latter provides a way to acquire the perception and control needed to realize it. Understanding
their respective strengths requires examining how each turns a requested outcome into physical action,
and how knowledge acquired for one task becomes useful in another.
1.1.1
Explicit Structure and Compositional Planning
A physical scene contains far more information than is needed for any particular decision. Symbolic
planning makes reasoning manageable by representing the objects, properties, and relationships rele-
vant to a task, together with models of the actions that can change them (Fikes and Nilsson, 1971).
Predicates express conditions that can be evaluated as true or false, such as whether an object is on
a surface or held by the robot. An action model specifies the conditions under which an action is
applicable and the effects it is expected to produce. The initial state describes the conditions that
currently hold, while the goal describes those the planner must establish. This abstraction allows
the planner to reason about changes in the world without representing every detail of their physical
realization.
For example, the instruction “bring the red book on the table to the sofa” can be expressed using
objects for the book, table, and sofa, and predicates describing the book’s location and whether the
robot is holding it. Picking up the book might require an available gripper and a reachable book;
its modeled effects include holding the book and removing it from the table. The planner can then
search for a sequence in which the effects of each action establish the conditions required by the next:
approach the table, pick up the book, approach the sofa, and place the book. Figure 1.1 illustrates
this connection between a requested outcome and a compositional description of behavior.

The value of this representation extends beyond finding one successful sequence. If the request

with different object instances and arrange their applications to satisfy the new goal. There is no
need to store a separate procedure for every combination of familiar objects and goals: the model
describes reusable relationships between conditions and actions (Konidaris et al., 2018). This form of
composition gives structured planning its own source of generality: a domain can support many tasks
that were never individually specified as complete behaviors.
Physical realization requires a further connection between discrete decisions and continuous con-
trol. The robot must select grasps, configurations, and collision-free motions that support the pro-
posed sequence; a failed geometric search may require the task plan itself to change. Task and motion
planning reasons across these levels (Garrett et al., 2021). Its actions can be implemented through
reusable skills, such as grasping or navigation, that unfold over multiple control steps and have condi-
tions governing when they can begin and terminate (Sutton et al., 1999). A skill may use an analytic
controller, a motion planner, or a learned policy. Planning and learning can therefore contribute to
the same behavior, with planning organizing the skills and learning supplying some of their control
policies.
Explicit task structure also permits requirements to shape the course of an activity. For the book-
transfer task, reaching the sofa while avoiding a specified region is a different requirement from merely
ending at the sofa. A final-state goal captures the destination but does not, by itself, express every
constraint on the intervening behavior. Formal methods ideas like temporal logic provide a language
for requirements such as ordering and avoidance, and support the synthesis of behavior that satisfies
a specification under a given model (Pnueli, 1977; Kress-Gazit et al., 2009). Planners can thus reason
about both what should be achieved and which sequences of events are acceptable.
3


The reach of this reasoning depends on the representations supplied to it. The planning system
needs a formal task grounded in the relevant objects and skills equipped with appropriate action
models. A prebuilt semantic map can supply associations between spatial structure and meaning,
but future requests may refer to properties or objects that were not recorded when the map was
created. Such a map is not inherently closed-world: that assumption arises when represented facts
are treated as complete and unrepresented facts as false. Even a detailed map may require revision
as the environment changes. Extending planning to less structured settings therefore requires ways
to construct and maintain its inputs without engineering them anew for every task.
1.1.2
Learning Behavior from Experience
Learning from demonstration offers a way to acquire control from examples of successful behavior.
For the same book-transfer task, an expert supplies trajectories containing observations and robot
actions, from which a visuomotor policy learns to produce actions in response to visual input and,
where available, robot state and a task description (Mandlekar et al., 2022). Figure 1.2 illustrates
this route from demonstrated experience to a control policy. During deployment, a closed-loop policy
repeatedly receives current observations, allowing its movements to respond to the scene as execution
proceeds. The acquired behavior is consequently a mapping from situations to actions, rather than a
recording of one sequence of movements.

A useful control representation must accommodate the fact that several different movements can
accomplish the same task. There may be multiple valid approaches to an object, and averaging their
trajectories need not yield a valid approach. Generative policies address this multiplicity by modeling
distributions over actions or action sequences. Diffusion Policy, for example, generates observation-
conditioned action trajectories and executes them through receding-horizon control (Chi et al., 2025).
By generating a trajectory, executing part of it, and then responding to another observation, the
policy combines coordinated movement with feedback. Such methods provide a basis for acquiring
manipulation skills whose detailed motions would be difficult to specify by hand.
The same policy can also cover several tasks when its output is conditioned on the requested
behavior. A task identifier can select an activity, a goal image can depict an outcome, and language
can describe what should be accomplished. In the shared example, conditioning distinguishes bringing
only the book from bringing both the book and the vase. How well the policy responds to an unfamiliar
request depends on the coverage of its training experience and its ability to generalize beyond that
experience. This makes the source and organization of training data central to the breadth of learned
behavior.
Longer activities place additional demands on that behavior. Errors can change the observations
encountered later in an execution, taking a policy into situations poorly represented in its demonstra-
tions (Ross et al., 2011). Feedback may correct a deviation within a skill, while recovery at the task
level may require selecting a different skill or revising their order. A planner making that decision
needs to know which relevant conditions hold and which actions can restore progress. An action-
producing policy may use such information internally without exposing it in a form that another
component can interpret. The ability to perform a behavior and the ability to supply the information
needed to compose it are therefore distinct aspects of a learned skill.
1.2
The Capability–Assurance Gap
Planning allows familiar actions to be composed for new goals; learning broadens the behavior a
robot can acquire from experience. Both extend what the robot can do, but neither alone establishes
whether a particular behavior satisfies the requirements of the task. Capability concerns what a robot
can accomplish and how broadly that competence extends across tasks and conditions. Assurance
concerns the evidence and reasoning that support confidence in its behavior under stated conditions.
For example, recognizing the objects mentioned in an instruction does not establish that the robot has
resolved the instructor’s intended referents, and a high task success rate does not establish compliance
with every constraint in a particular execution. An account of dependable autonomy must connect
evidence about individual capabilities to the requirements of the activity in which they are used.
Symbolic representations make these requirements available for reasoning, but the conclusions of
that reasoning remain conditional on the model. A plan can satisfy a specification even when the
specification misrepresents the instruction; similarly, a valid transition in an action model may fail to
describe what occurred during execution. Closing the capability–assurance gap requires connecting
an instructor’s intended task to the robot’s interpretation, and the effects anticipated by the planner
5


to what happens during execution. The interfaces between perception, planning, and control must
expose enough information to check these connections.
This thesis uses trusted autonomy to describe the objective of making reliance on task-level robot
behavior warranted by such evidence and reasoning. The term verifiable refers to explicit specifications
and representations against which aspects of behavior can be checked. Formal reasoning establishes
properties within a model, while empirical evaluation examines how well grounding, control, and state
predictions correspond to the environment. These forms of evidence support different claims; together,
they identify both the basis for a decision and the assumptions on which that basis depends.
1.3
Three Interfaces for Trusted Autonomy
These connections raise three questions about the information on which a robot relies. Before planning,
the robot needs a representation of what the user intends. During execution, it needs to determine
whether the effects of its actions support continued progress. Across actions and successive tasks,
it needs to maintain the world state on which further interpretation and planning depend. These
responsibilities define the Intent, Action, and World interfaces developed in this thesis. Each inter-
face addresses a different part of the same activity: interpreting the task, assessing progress, and
maintaining the context for further decisions.
1.3.1
Intent: Did the Robot Understand What the User Meant?
Language communicates a task through descriptions whose meaning depends on the environment and
on their relationships to one another. In the book example, “the red book on the table” identifies an
instance through an attribute and a spatial relation. An instruction may additionally constrain the
order of operations or specify what the robot must avoid. Grounding must connect these expressions
to objects, locations, and behavioral requirements, so that the planner reasons about the requested
task.
An explicit task specification exposes the interpreted requirements, while grounded referents make
the selected objects and locations available for inspection.
This permits the interpretation to be
examined separately from the plan constructed to satisfy it.
The research problem at the intent
interface is to obtain these representations from expressive language and perceptual observations
without assuming that every relevant concept was encoded in a complete semantic map beforehand.
The corresponding contribution is grounded language understanding.
1.3.2
Action: Did the Robot’s Actions Produce the Intended Effects?
Composition relies on the effects of one skill establishing the conditions required by another. For the
robot to carry the book, its preceding grasp must establish that the book is held; a command to close
the gripper is not sufficient evidence of that condition. The planner needs a state description at the
level of its action models, even when the skill itself operates through images and continuous motor
6


commands. This creates an interface between the quantities used for control and the conditions used
for task reasoning.
For learned behavior, the research problem is to acquire skills that expose those conditions as
execution unfolds. The thesis develops composable state-reporting skills, which generate control actions
together with predictions of the associated symbolic state. Such reports allow a planner to monitor
expected effects and revise a skill sequence using updated state estimates. Their usefulness depends
on how well they agree with the consequences of the generated actions: evaluating predicate accuracy
alone does not establish action–predicate coherence. The symbolic report must therefore be studied
as part of the skill’s behavior.
1.3.3
World: Does the Robot’s Internal World Still Match Reality?
Task-level state reports do not by themselves maintain the spatial context required for sustained au-
tonomy. A later instruction may refer to an object by its relationship to other objects, while geometric
planning needs its current shape and location. Although these decisions use different abstractions,
they must draw on a compatible account of the environment. As the robot intervenes in that en-
vironment, the representation must preserve relevant changes, object identity, and unaffected scene
structure across tasks.
The research problem at the world interface is to maintain that continuity through a representation
that can predict task-relevant changes and supply updated inputs to grounding and planning. This
motivates evolving representations of the world, in which action-conditioned transitions connect the
state used for one task to the state available for the next. The world interface provides the persistent
spatial context that complements the action interface’s reports of skill-level conditions. For these
interfaces to support continued operation in the physical world, predicted effects must be reconciled
with observations, so that later decisions reflect what actually happened.
1.4
Thesis and Contributions
The central argument of this dissertation is:
Scaling trusted autonomy requires unifying grounded language understanding, composable
state-reporting skills, and evolving representations of the world.
The common design principle is to introduce explicit symbolic and spatial structure where learned
outputs become inputs to robot reasoning. At these interfaces, a system can expose its interpreted
requirements, expected action effects, and persistent world state for inspection, composition, and
checking. The contributions develop this principle through three complementary forms of generality:
broader instruction grounding, continuity across world changes, and new compositions of learned
skills.
The technical chapters follow the order LIMP →REALM →PACTS. LIMP establishes the
language-to-plan framework and exposes two assumptions that limit its continued use: a world rep-
resentation that remains valid after mapping, and an available library of skills with suitable planning
7


interfaces. REALM addresses the first by evolving the world state used for subsequent tasks; PACTS
addresses the second by learning skills that expose planning-relevant state.
Chapter 2 develops the shared foundations needed to examine the contributions: foundation mod-
els, task specifications, task and motion planning, semantic grounding, spatial world representations,
and learned visuomotor skills. These concepts provide the basis for the technical chapters, where each
method is also situated within its related work. Chapters 3–5 present LIMP, REALM, and PACTS in
the dependency order described above. The concluding Chapter 6 brings these contributions together
around the construction and maintenance of task-relevant abstractions. It returns to the capability–
assurance question through the three trust interfaces and develops a research agenda for continued
autonomy, including physical integration, uncertainty management, and recovery.
1.4.1
LIMP: Grounded Language Understanding
Chapter 3 introduces Language Instruction Grounding for Motion Planning (LIMP), which connects
expressive instructions to explicit task constraints and spatially grounded plans. Language and vision-
language models supply descriptions of the task and its referents, while spatial reasoning resolves
object instances and connects the resulting specification to motion planning. The central advance
is to construct the semantic information required by an instruction at runtime, using observations
retained during metric mapping, while preserving an explicit representation of its temporal and spatial
requirements. This makes learned semantic breadth available to structured planning without requiring
a complete prebuilt semantic map.
1.4.2
REALM: Evolving Representations of the World
Chapter 4 develops REALM, an action-conditioned spatial world model that carries a persistent
representation across successive tasks. It derives the semantic and geometric abstractions needed for
grounding, planning, and rendered inspection from a common 3D Gaussian scene (Kerbl et al., 2023).
Action-conditioned transitions predict geometric change and update the affected parts of the scene
while preserving object identity and unaffected scene content. These predicted changes provide the
world state used to ground and plan subsequent tasks. The scene becomes a world model through
these predictive transitions and its reuse for later decisions. The contribution is a persistent spatial
basis for planning whose evolution remains explicit.
1.4.3
PACTS: Composable State-Reporting Skills
Chapter 5 introduces Predicate-Action Skills (PACTS), which learn control and its symbolic interface
through joint generation of action trajectories and temporally aligned predicate-belief trajectories.
Conditioned on current observations, each generated sample pairs proposed actions with the symbolic
conditions the skill expects as those actions unfold. These reports supply an off-the-shelf planner with
state estimates for sequencing skills, checking effects, and replanning. They are refreshed through
8


observation-conditioned rollouts, but remain predictions rather than independent measurements of
action success.
Together, these contributions address three requirements for extending robot capability while pre-
serving a basis for assurance. Broader language understanding must remain connected to explicit
task requirements; learned skills must expose the state needed to assess and compose their behavior;
and the world representation used for planning must account for change. By making these connec-
tions explicit, this thesis develops a foundation for robots whose capabilities can expand while their
interpretation and behavior remain open to examination.

Chapter 2
Background
Robots become more useful when they can carry what they have learned into tasks that were never
individually programmed or demonstrated. The introduction argued for pursuing this breadth through
a combination of learning and planning. Learned models can bring new instructions, objects, and skills
within a planner’s reach, while planning can organize those capabilities to accomplish new goals. The
challenge is to preserve the meaning of a task as it passes from a person’s request into the robot’s
decisions and, ultimately, into physical action.
Making this connection requires choices about what the robot represents and what it can infer
from those representations. A planner can reason that a grasp must precede a placement only if
the consequences of grasping are expressed in terms it can use.
Perception must establish which
object those terms refer to, and execution must supply information about whether the conditions for
continuing the task hold. The representations connecting these processes determine both how their
capabilities can be combined and what can be checked about the resulting behavior.
This chapter develops the ideas needed to understand and construct those connections. It begins
with the breadth of knowledge available through foundation models and examines how that knowledge
can enter explicit reasoning about robot tasks.
Following this connection from interpretation to
action leads to the physical grounding of task specifications, the maintenance of a changing world,
and the acquisition of skills that a planner can reuse. Together, these foundations explain how the
opportunities for broader capability give rise to the interface problems addressed by the technical
chapters.
2.1
Foundation Models and Broader Generality
The knowledge required for a robot task often extends beyond the robot’s own experience. A person
may describe an object through its appearance, its use, or its relationship to another object, using
words that were never included in a task-specific training set.
Foundation models provide a way
to bring knowledge learned from broad collections of data into such settings. The term describes
models trained at scale on broad data and adapted to a range of downstream uses (Bommasani et al.,
10


2021). For robotics, the important change is that much of the representation learning can precede the
definition of a particular task or deployment environment.

Visual representations supply a complementary connection to the scene. In an open-vocabulary
detector, a text query can specify the object category or description to localize, rather than selecting
only from a fixed list of detector labels. OWL-ViT, for example, adapts image–text pretraining to text-

identifies the pixels belonging to a candidate region. These are distinct operations: a box around a
book may include part of the table, whereas a segmentation mask can provide more selective support
for projecting that book into three dimensions (Kirillov et al., 2023).
Open vocabulary expands the descriptions that can be submitted to perception; it does not imply
exhaustive recognition of every concept or reliable identification of every instance. Several books may
match “red book,” and a correct category detection may still select the wrong book for a relational
instruction. Language understanding, object detection, and referent resolution therefore contribute
different information. The task becomes grounded only when the expressions in the instruction are
associated with the relevant entities and conditions in the robot’s environment (Tellex et al., 2020).
2.1.2
From Pretrained Knowledge to Robot Behavior
Pretrained representations can enter robot behavior at different levels. Vision-language-action models
connect language and visual knowledge to control learned from robot experience. RT-2 studies this
transfer by incorporating robot actions into a vision-language model’s output representation (Zitkovich
et al., 2023). OpenVLA develops policies from diverse robot demonstrations and supports adaptation
to additional robot setups (Kim et al., 2025), while π0 combines pretrained vision-language repre-
sentations with continuous action generation (Black et al., 2025). In each case, robot data connects
semantic knowledge to the actions available to a particular embodiment.
11


The same knowledge can also support explicit intermediate reasoning. SayCan combines language-
model scores with learned skill value functions to select skills using estimates of their relevance to the
instruction and their feasibility in the current state (Ichter et al., 2023). Code as Policies generates
programs that call perception and control interfaces (Liang et al., 2023). Language-to-temporal-logic
methods instead produce task specifications that can be passed to a planner (Liu et al., 2023). These
approaches differ in what the learned model supplies: motor commands, skill choices, executable
code, or formal requirements. That choice determines which decisions remain available for separate
examination.
A language-conditioned action policy can use task meaning without expressing it as a checkable
specification. Conversely, a generated specification can be checked for logical properties while still
referring to the wrong object. The value of an explicit interface is that it separates these questions
and makes the relevant outputs accessible. To understand what a planner can establish from such an
interface, it is first necessary to describe the states, actions, and requirements over which planning
operates.
2.2
Symbolic State, Planning, and Task Specifications
To use knowledge about a task in planning, the robot needs a way to reason about what its actions
would change. For the book-transfer task, the relevant connection is that grasping the book makes it
available to be carried, and carrying it to the sofa makes the requested placement possible. Symbolic
planning captures such relationships while leaving much of the detail of their physical realization to
perception and control. The resulting abstraction allows the planner to explore how familiar actions
could accomplish a new goal. Its construction begins with the conditions that distinguish one task-
relevant state from another.
2.2.1

configuration, object poses, contact conditions, and quantities that are not directly observed.

arguments define the condition of interest; an evaluator connects that condition to geometry, sensor
data, or another state estimate.

absent from s is false. That convention is convenient for search, but it must be distinguished from
incomplete perception. Failure to detect a book is not evidence that no book exists. Similarly, a fixed
semantic map is not inherently closed-world; the assumption arises when its contents are treated as
complete for the relevant reasoning. The symbolic domain must be understood together with the
procedure that supplies its facts.
2.2.2

2.2.3
Automata, Synthesis, and the Scope of Verification
Temporal specifications can be converted into automata that track progress as propositions become
true (Vardi, 1996). For a co-safe specification, satisfaction is witnessed by a finite good prefix: once
that prefix has occurred, every continuation satisfies the specification (Kupferman and Vardi, 2001).
Such requirements can be represented by a finite automaton accepting their good prefixes. This differs
from claiming that every LTL requirement is decidable from a completed finite task. For example,
observing a safe run so far cannot establish G¬b for an unbounded future. An avoidance requirement
limited to reaching a goal can instead be written (¬b) U (g ∧¬b), which requires eventual achievement
of g and excludes b through that achievement.

Verification and synthesis use the specification in different ways. Model checking asks whether
a supplied model satisfies a property; synthesis constructs behavior to satisfy the property under a
model and stated assumptions (Vardi, 1996; Kress-Gazit et al., 2009). Combining a task model with
an automaton allows a planner to reason about both the environment state and progress through the
specification. In robot applications, the resulting logical plan must also admit a physically feasible
realization. Thus a correct-by-construction plan is correct relative to its specification, grounding, and
action model. Whether that specification expresses the user’s intent, and whether execution follows
the modeled transitions, are additional questions.
2.3
Task and Motion Planning with Reusable Skills
A symbolic plan can establish that picking precedes placing without determining how either action
is carried out. Physical execution introduces choices that affect the validity of the sequence: where
the robot stands, which grasp it uses, how it approaches the object, and whether the object can pass
through the available space. Task and motion planning (TAMP) treats the discrete action sequence
and these continuous choices as connected parts of one problem (Garrett et al., 2021).
2.3.1
Geometric Feasibility
A robot configuration q ∈C specifies the variables needed to determine its geometry, such as joint
angles and mobile-base pose.
For a fixed environment and manipulation mode, the collision-free
subset Cfree contains configurations that satisfy the chosen collision constraints. A geometric path
γ : [0, 1] →Cfree connects a start configuration to a goal configuration or goal set. The path parameter
specifies progression along the curve; a time-parameterized trajectory additionally specifies when
configurations are reached. Kinematic feasibility, collision avoidance, and dynamic feasibility impose
different restrictions on such a trajectory.
Manipulation changes the geometry relevant to those restrictions.
An object attached to the
gripper becomes part of the moving assembly, and a release changes its pose in the scene. A task-level
choice of grasp can therefore affect the feasibility of later transport and placement. Methods such as
PDDLStream connect symbolic search to procedures that generate and test continuous candidates,
including grasps, poses, and motions (Garrett et al., 2020). The planner may need to revisit an earlier
discrete or continuous choice when a later motion is infeasible.
Task requirements can also create geometric constraints beyond ordinary collision avoidance. An
instruction to avoid a particular object or region changes which paths are acceptable even when those
paths are physically traversable. Once a referent is localized, a proposition such as near(o) can label
a region of space. For a selected automaton transition, positions can then be distinguished according
to whether they advance the task, violate its constraints, or leave its progress unchanged. This is the
15


role of the Task Progression Semantic Maps developed in Chapter 3. They connect logical progress
to geometric search; they are not models of physical dynamics.
2.3.2
Skills as Temporally Extended Actions
The planner need not select every motor command individually. A skill can encapsulate a closed-loop
behavior that runs over several control steps, such as approaching, grasping, or opening a door. The
options framework formalizes this temporal abstraction through
O = (IO, πO, βO),
(2.7)
where IO is the initiation set, πO the policy used during execution, and βO the termination rule (Sutton
et al., 1999). In the stochastic formulation, βO(ξ) is the probability of terminating in state ξ. The
policy may itself use an observation or history rather than the full physical state. A deterministic
stopping condition is a special case of this formulation.
An option’s duration can vary with its starting state and execution outcome. At the level of skill
selection, a transition model may therefore describe both the terminal state and elapsed duration,
p(ξ′, d | ξ, O). This is the source of the semi-Markov formulation associated with options: decisions
occur after temporally extended behaviors, rather than necessarily after a single motor step. The
options framework can support reinforcement learning, but the abstraction does not require that
every policy be learned through reward optimization. An analytic controller, a motion planner, or a
policy learned from demonstrations can each implement a skill.
An initiation set and a termination rule are not interchangeable with symbolic preconditions and
successful effects. A symbolic precondition summarizes when an operator is applicable; its physical
realization requires the corresponding skill to be able to run from the actual state. Likewise, a skill
may stop because it succeeded, timed out, or encountered a failure. Termination alone cannot establish
its intended effects. Composition requires a connection between the states one skill can produce and
the states from which the next can operate (Burridge et al., 1999).
This connection explains why state abstraction and skill design belong together. The useful sym-
bolic distinctions depend on the behaviors available to the robot and the questions the planner must
answer about them (Konidaris et al., 2018). A representation that records only whether a book is on
a table may suffice for one task model but omit the accessibility information needed by a particular
grasping skill. The planner’s symbols must therefore be grounded at a level that supports the intended
composition, rather than chosen solely because they are convenient labels.
2.4
Semantic Mapping and Spatial Grounding
The ability to reuse a skill depends on recognizing where it can be applied. A model of picking up a
book can serve tasks in different rooms, but each use requires the robot to identify a particular book,
locate it, and relate its geometry to the motion being considered. The same abstraction that makes
a planning model reusable must therefore be connected to the details of the present scene. Semantic
16


mapping and grounding establish this connection, allowing task reasoning to draw on what the robot
perceives.
2.4.1
From Images to a Metric Scene
Observations from different viewpoints must first be brought into a common spatial frame so that de-
tections and planned motions refer to the same environment. Simultaneous localization and mapping
(SLAM) addresses this problem by estimating the robot’s state while constructing a representation
of the environment from its observations (Cadena et al., 2016). A metric map provides spatial re-
lationships in that common coordinate system. A semantic map adds associations with categories,
instances, attributes, or relations. These are complementary forms of information: a collision map
may support navigation without identifying a book, while a text label alone does not provide the
geometry needed to reach it.

For this thesis, the useful

observations or a suitable scene representation, rather than exhaustively enumerated at mapping

2.4.2
Resolving Objects, Relations, and Identity
Referent resolution must distinguish an object’s description from its identity. “The red book” is a
description that may match several objects; “the red book on the table” adds a relation that can
restrict the candidates.
An instruction can nest these relations, as in a book on a table beside
a sofa.
Resolving it requires evaluating candidate combinations, not simply detecting each noun
17


independently.
The result should bind a task expression to an object instance that can be used
consistently by the planner.
Spatial predicates also depend on their reference frame and geometric interpretation. “Left of” can
refer to the observer’s viewpoint or an object’s intrinsic orientation, whereas “above” may be defined
relative to gravity. Different languages and contexts make different use of spatial frames (Majid et al.,
2004). A numerical comparison between object centers is meaningful only after the relevant frame has
been fixed. Other relations, such as support or containment, may require shape, contact, or volume
information rather than a comparison of centers alone.
The same distinction matters over time. If the robot moves a book, its description may change while
its identity persists. Repeated detection provides new candidates but does not automatically establish
their correspondence to earlier objects. Similarly, a semantic identification does not determine which
geometric elements should move with the object.
An instance record and its geometric support
answer different questions: which object the task concerns, and which part of the scene represents
that object. Maintaining both becomes necessary when the representation must account for the robot’s
interventions.
2.5
World Models and State Change
Grounding makes a representation useful for action, but action can change the very conditions on
which that grounding depends. Once the robot has moved the book, later instructions and plans
must refer to the scene that results. Continued autonomy therefore requires a way to carry knowledge
of the environment forward, preserving what remains useful while accounting for what has changed.
A world model supports this reasoning by describing how aspects of the scene evolve. For ma-
nipulation, this thesis adopts a functional view: the model should be grounded in the environment,
predict task-relevant changes, and account for the actions that cause them. The appropriate mecha-
nism depends on what the robot needs to predict. An analytic transformation, a learned transition
distribution, or a combination of the two can supply the required information without modeling every
physical process.

describe the same revision of the scene. A correct update to one representation cannot compensate
for another component continuing to use an old one.

local updates are possible and why visual reconstruction alone does not complete a world model.
2.6
Gaussian Scene Representations
For a world model to remain useful after manipulation, its scene representation must support the
changes the robot needs to predict. When an object moves, the robot needs a way to change its
represented position while retaining the surrounding scene.
When a person inspects a proposed
action, the representation should make the relevant geometry and spatial relationships visible. Local
editing and rendered inspection place different demands on the information stored about the world.
A point cloud records samples of three-dimensional geometry; a mesh connects surface elements;
and an occupancy representation describes whether regions are occupied. A radiance representation
also models appearance as a function of viewpoint, allowing images to be synthesized from camera
poses other than those used during capture. Combining this ability to render new views with an
explicit spatial structure offers a basis for inspecting a scene and updating the parts affected by
manipulation.

2.7
Learning Visuomotor Skills from Demonstrations
A current world representation allows the planner to reconsider what can be done as the scene changes.
The range of behaviors it can construct, however, still depends on the skills available to it. Extending
that range requires a way to acquire control policies whose detailed movements would be difficult
to engineer for every object and situation. Learning from demonstration approaches this problem
through examples of successful behavior.
For manipulation, those examples can contain images, robot state, and the commands issued
during execution. The policy learns how observations relate to actions without requiring the designer
to specify every movement. The quality and coverage of the demonstration data, the observation
representation, and the policy architecture all affect the resulting behavior (Mandlekar et al., 2022).

2.8
Symbolic Interfaces for Composition and Monitoring
The value of an acquired skill extends beyond the movements it has learned to produce. To use a
grasping skill as part of a longer activity, the planner needs to reason about whether the object will
be held and whether subsequent transport or placement is appropriate. These are the conditions
represented by the action models in Section 2.2. Connecting the learned policy to those conditions
gives the planner a basis for composing the skill with others and assessing progress through the task.
The remaining question is how the skill’s state reports relate to the behavior it produces.
2.8.1
State Estimates and Predicted Outcomes
A predicate evaluator can estimate current symbolic conditions from an observation, for example
through scores bj(ot) ∈[0, 1] for predicates j = 1, . . . , J. A threshold converts those scores into a
discrete valuation for a classical planner. This conversion is an interface decision: it does not make
an uncertain estimate certain. Nor is a score between zero and one automatically a calibrated proba-
bility. Calibration concerns whether stated confidence agrees with empirical correctness frequencies,
a property that must be assessed separately from classification accuracy (Guo et al., 2017).
A predicted predicate trajectory answers a different question from a current-state evaluator. For

produced by executing the associated actions. Accurate marginals do not establish that correspon-
dence, and joint generation is a modeling choice rather than a proof of physical agreement. Evaluating
paired rollouts test the relationship on which the planner relies.
2.8.2
Composition, Progress, and Recovery
With state estimates and action models available, a planner can select a skill, monitor its intended
effects, and plan again from updated conditions. This differs from replaying a fixed order of skills: the
next choice depends on the state presented to the planner. If a door closes, the original sequence may
no longer be applicable even when the current controller continues to produce valid motor commands.
Recovery requires recognizing the changed condition and selecting behavior that can restore progress.
The policy and planner can operate at different timescales. The policy uses frequent observations
to adjust movement; the planner reasons over task-relevant conditions to decide which skill should run.
This separation permits reuse across goals, provided the skill’s initiation conditions, effects, and state
reports remain meaningful in the new composition. It does not remove the need for a suitable symbolic
vocabulary or an action model. Learning a controller, learning a predicate evaluator, discovering useful
predicates, and learning operator preconditions and effects are distinct problems.
Similarly, zero-shot composition concerns what is new at the level of the requested behavior. The
constituent skills can be trained from demonstrations while their requested combination has no end-
to-end demonstration. This is different from performing a skill that the robot has never learned. The
distinction connects the two sources of generality introduced earlier: learning broadens the available
behavior, while planning can arrange that behavior into additional tasks.
Learned knowledge and behavior become useful to planning when the robot can relate them to
explicit requirements, conditions, and changes in the world. The same connections make it possible to
examine whether the planner’s reasoning applies to the task and environment at hand. Constructing
these connections is therefore central to advancing capability and assurance together.
The first such connection is needed before a plan can be constructed. A person’s instruction must
become a task that the planner can reason about, with its referents identified and its temporal and
spatial requirements made explicit. Chapter 3 develops that connection without assuming a complete
semantic map in advance. Its language-to-plan framework then provides the basis for examining how
the world representation and the skill library can themselves be extended in Chapters 4 and 5.

The usefulness of a general-purpose robot depends in part on the range of tasks a person can ask

3.2
Related Work
NLMap (Chen et al., 2023a) uses pretrained vision-language models to make a scene representation
queryable through open-vocabulary descriptions.
Object availability and location then ground an
LLM-based planner. LIMP connects such grounding to an explicit representation of spatial relations
and temporal constraints, allowing these requirements to guide task and motion planning.
Lang2LTL (Liu et al., 2023) uses entity extraction and replacement to translate language instruc-
tions into temporal logic. Lang2LTL-2 (Liu et al., 2024a) extends this approach with visual and textual
landmark descriptions and spatial-relation reasoning. Both ground symbols using a supplied semantic
map or landmark database. LIMP instead constructs instruction-specific semantic information from
the robot’s retained observations.
Action-Oriented Semantic Maps (AOSMs) (Rosen et al., 2023) construct navigation abstractions
that connect portable manipulation skills in new environments. Their representation is organized
around the supplied manipulation skills and their symbolic abstractions. LIMP develops a complemen-
tary connection: an instruction determines which open-vocabulary referents and spatial relationships
must be grounded for planning.
3.2.1
Temporal Specifications for Instruction Following
The temporal-logic semantics and automata construction in Section 2.2.2 provide the basis for LIMP’s
task representation. The choice of LTL allows ordering, eventual achievement, and avoidance require-
ments to be expressed together (Pnueli, 1977; Menghi et al., 2021).
Recent approaches translate
natural language into such specifications (Liu et al., 2023; Berg et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2023; Cosler
et al., 2023; Fuggitti and Chakraborti, 2023; Chen et al., 2023b); LIMP couples this translation to
the resolution of open-vocabulary spatial descriptions.
Temporal task structure can also support learning. Quartey et al. (2023) combine temporal-logic
task representations with language-model-derived object embeddings to generate contextually related
auxiliary tasks that can be learned from shared experience.
The planner uses the specification to synthesize behavior rather than checking an independently
generated plan afterward (Vardi, 1996; Kress-Gazit et al., 2009; Colledanchise et al., 2017).
The
resulting correctness claim is relative to the translated specification, its grounding, and the modeled
actions. Inspection of the translation and the referent map addresses whether these inputs express
the user’s intended task; successful physical execution is a further requirement.

3.3
Problem Definition
Given a natural language instruction l, our goal is to synthesize and sequence navigation and manip-
ulation behaviors to produce a policy that satisfies the temporal and spatial constraints in l. Spatial
constraints determine task success based on the sequence of robot poses traversed during execution;
temporal constraints determine the sequencing of these spatial constraints as a function of task pro-
gression. We assume a robot with an RGB-D camera has already navigated a space, capturing images
and camera poses. From these observations, the robot builds a metric map m (e.g., point cloud,
3D voxel grid) of the environment, defining the space of possible SE(3) poses P and enabling robot
localization (i.e., estimating probot ∈P). Unlike previous work leveraging temporal logic (Liu et al.,
2023), we do not assume access to a semantic map with prespecified object locations or predicates.
LIMP uses the foundation-model interfaces introduced in Section 2.1: a task-agnostic vision-language
model σ for text-conditioned detection or segmentation, and an LLM ψ for instruction translation.
Navigation: Navigation is formalized as an object-goal oriented continuous path planning problem,
where the goal is to generate paths to a goal pose set Pgoals ⊂P while staying in feasible regions
(Pfeasible ⊂P) and avoiding infeasible regions (Pinfeasible = P C
feasible). Infeasible regions include
environment obstacles as well as dynamically determined semantic regions that violate constraints in
the instruction l.
Manipulation: Manipulation behaviors use the option formulation from Section 2.3.2, with an object
parameter θ. For Oθ = (Iθ, πθ, βθ), initiation and termination depend on the robot pose and object
attributes, and the policy is conditioned on the target object. The initiation set Iθ denotes the global
reference frame robot positions and object-centric attributes––such as object size––that determine if
the option policy πθ can be executed on the object θ. To execute a manipulation skill on an object,
an object-goal navigation behavior must first be executed to bring the robot into proximity with the
object. We assume access to a library of these manipulation skills and demonstrate our approach on
multi-object goal navigation and open-vocabulary mobile pick-and-place (Yenamandra et al., 2023;
Yokoyama et al., 2024).

3.4.3
Task and Motion Planning Module
Finally, our TAMP module synthesizes and sequences navigation and manipulation behaviors to pro-
duce a plan that satisfies the temporal and spatial constraints expressed in the given instruction.
Progressive Motion Planner (PMP): The TAMP module compiles the supported co-safe LTL
specification with parameterized robot skills into a finite-state automaton (Figure 3.5[a]) to guide task
and motion planning. A path from the initial to the accepting state in this automaton is a high-level
task plan that interleaves navigation and manipulation objectives required to satisfy the instruction.
The planner selects such a path with a strategy that incrementally selects the next progression state
until the accepting state is reached, ensuring the plan obeys all temporal subgoal objectives.
As
shown in Figure 3.5[a], automaton states are connected by transition edges representing the logical
expressions required for transitions. For each transition, our algorithm executes the necessary low-level
behaviors: for manipulation subgoals, it executes the appropriate parameterized skill; for navigation
subgoals, it dynamically generates Task Progression Semantic Maps (TPSMs) to localize goal and
constraint regions and performs continuous path planning using the Fast Marching Tree algorithm
(FMT∗) (Janson et al., 2015).
Task Progression Semantic Maps (TPSM): A TPSM augments a 3D scene with navigation
constraints specified by logical state transition expressions, enabling goal-directed, constraint-aware
navigation. Regions of interest in a TPSM are defined using a nearness threshold specifying proximity
to an object. This threshold can be set globally or included in the language instruction module’s
prompting strategy, allowing an LLM to infer its value based on the instruction. Like our spatial
grounding module, TPSMs are agnostic to temporal logic representations and can be used with vari-
ous planning approaches for semantic constraint-aware motion planning. For the ground mobile robot
used in the evaluation, the module converts 3D TPSMs into 2D geometric obstacle maps, where con-
straint regions are treated as obstacles (Figure 3.5[b]). The spatial representation also permits direct
three-dimensional search, although the reported evaluation concerns ground mobile manipulation.
3.5
Evaluation
Our evaluations test the hypothesis that translating natural language instructions into LTL expres-
sions and dynamically generating semantic maps enables robots to accurately interpret and execute
instructions in large-scale environments without task-specific training. We focus on three key ques-
tions: (1) Can our language instruction module interpret complex, ambiguous instructions? (2) Can
our spatial grounding module resolve specific object instances described in instructions? (3) Can our
TAMP algorithm generate constraint-satisfying plans?
To answer these questions, we compare LIMP with two baselines: an LLM task planner (NLMap-
Saycan (Chen et al., 2023a)) and an LLM code-writing planner (Code-as-Policies (Liang et al., 2023)),
representing language-model task sequencing and code generation for robot instruction following. Both
baselines use the same LLM (GPT-4-0613), prompting structure, and in-context learning examples as
32


our language instruction module. In Code-as-Policies, in-context examples are converted into language
model-generated program (LMP) snippets (Liang et al., 2023). To ensure competitive performance, we
integrate our CRD syntax, spatial grounding module, and low-level robot control into these baselines,
allowing them to query object positions, use our FMT∗path planner, and execute manipulation skills.
We also evaluate ablations of our two-stage language instruction module due to its importance in
instruction following. In our full approach, the first stage prompts an LLM to generate a conventional
LTL formula ϕl from instruction l by dynamically selecting relevant in-context examples from a
standard dataset (Pan et al., 2023) based on cosine similarity. Our first ablation selects in-context
examples randomly; and the second ablation skips this stage entirely, directly sampling our LTL
syntax with parameterized robot skills φl from l.
We conduct a large-scale evaluation across five real-world environments on a diverse task set
of 150 instructions from multiple prior works (Chen et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2024b, 2023).
This
task set consists of 24 tasks with fine-grained object descriptions (NLMD), 25 tasks with complex

3.6
Discussion
Beyond the verification benefits of symbolic planning, LIMP outperforms baselines in most task sets,
notably in complex temporal planning and constraint avoidance. While NLMap-Saycan and Code-as-
Policies effectively generate sequential subgoals, they struggle with strict temporal constraints—for
example, avoiding a specific referent while approaching another. LIMP incorporates the instruction’s
avoidance regions into geometric path planning, accounting for its stronger performance on the CT and
CST task sets. As shown in Table 3.2, LIMP underperforms only against NLMap-Saycan in the NLMC
task category. This task set, introduced in the same paper as the baseline (Chen et al., 2023a) (which
outperforms LIMP), includes instructions with implicit details such as: “I like fruits, can you put
something I would like on the yellow sofa for me.” NLMap-Saycan is better suited to infer and generate
plans with possible fruit options, whereas our few-shot LTL translation process is not designed for this.
3.7
From Grounded Intent to Continued Autonomy
LIMP connects expressive instructions to explicit task constraints and the scene geometry needed for
planning. Instruction-conditioned semantic maps let the robot work beyond prespecified landmarks,
while inspection exposes the translated task and its referents to the user.
This result depends on the task and world models. The planner handles co-safe specifications,
whose satisfaction has a finite good-prefix witness (Section 2.2.2). Grounding remains sensitive to
perception and spatial interpretation, and plan synthesis does not guarantee physical success or opti-
mality. Suitable manipulation skills and a static mapped environment are still assumed.

These assumptions motivate the next contributions. Successful manipulation can invalidate the
representation used to plan it; later tasks must therefore be grounded in a world that accounts
for earlier actions. Chapter 4 develops this evolving representation. Chapter 5 then addresses how
additional behaviors can be learned together with the symbolic information needed to compose them.

Chapter 4
Evolving World Representations for
Planning
This chapter presents joint work by Benedict Quartey and George Konidaris, developed in REALM:
Action-Conditioned Spatial World Models for Long-Horizon Mobile Manipulation. The authorial
“we” refers to this collaboration.
Understanding an instruction establishes what the robot should accomplish; it does not keep the
world used to interpret that instruction current. Chapter 3 connected language to task constraints
and grounded those constraints in a captured environment.
That connection becomes vulnerable
as soon as manipulation changes the scene. An object can keep its identity while acquiring a new
location, and the next request may depend on both facts. For autonomy to extend across successive
tasks, the representation supporting one plan must account for its consequences before it supports
another.
This chapter develops evolving representations of the world, addressing the world interface of trust.
REALM extends the language-to-plan framework with a persistent spatial representation from which
grounding, planning geometry, and visual inspection draw their information. A planned manipulation
predicts a change to that representation; local visual reconciliation produces a successor for the next
instruction. The central contribution is continuity between tasks: object identity, geometry, and the
information supplied to the planner advance together. A sequential study examines this continuity in
a captured scene using simulated manipulation plans and synthetic post-action imagery, distinguishing
the predicted world from evidence of what a physical robot has actually changed.

the result, and promotes the accepted successor. Stable ownership binds grounded objects to the

4.4.1
From the Persistent World to the Current Instruction
At mapping time, Realm starts from posed scene images and a renderable Gaussian reconstruction.
In the garden study, the captured input is the Mip-NeRF 360 image dataset (Barron et al., 2022);
depth used for subsequent view lifting is rendered from the reconstruction. The Gaussian scene is the
persistent spatial representation, but it is not the task plan. Given an instruction such as “get the
blue ball from under the table and leave it beside the potted lime-green conifer,” Realm first renders
views of Wt in which the named referents can be detected. Multi-view detections are lifted into 3D,
consolidated into stable object records, and inserted into a task-progression semantic state.
From the same Wt, Realm constructs only the geometric abstractions required by that instruction.
Task-relevant surfaces and objects become a digital twin for manipulation; the remaining geometry
becomes a collision cloud for navigation and whole-body planning. LIMP translates the language
into temporal constraints and produces navigation, pick, and release trajectories from these state-
bound abstractions (Quartey et al., 2025). Because the underlying world is renderable, the robot and
candidate trajectories can also be composed with Gaussian views from several cameras for visual plan
inspection. Grounding views, semantic records, twin geometry, collision geometry, and plan renders
serve different consumers, but they all depict the same named world.
Each state stores a checkpoint, object registry, digital twin, planning cloud, optional observation
bundle, source plan, transition record, and validation report. Artifact digests bind these products to
the revision from which they were derived. The scientific purpose of that binding is consistency: a
grounded object cannot come from one world while the collision cloud or plan comes from another.

4.7
Discussion
4.7.1
From Verifiable Plans to an Evolving World
LIMP establishes a path from expressive language to a grounded, verifiable plan. Realm extends
that path through time. The plan no longer terminates the representational process; it becomes an
49


intervention that predicts a new world. That successor is reconciled, promoted, and used to interpret
the next instruction.
Together, the two systems connect an interpreted instruction to a planned
intervention and to the world representation used for the next task.
The world interface concerns the premises of the planner’s reasoning. Even when an instruction
has been interpreted correctly, a plan grounded in an obsolete scene can concern the wrong location
or geometry. Carrying the representation forward preserves the connection established by language
grounding across successive tasks.
4.7.2
One Representation, Four Connected Uses
The Gaussian scene supports grounding, planning geometry, visual inspection, and action-conditioned
updating through one persistent representation. These uses depend on different information, but must
agree about which world they describe. The sequential study makes that dependency concrete: updat-
ing the ball’s rendered appearance would be insufficient if grounding or collision checking continued
to use its original location.
That distinction explains why regeneration matters. The semantic task state, digital twin, collision
cloud, and plan views are useful precisely because they describe the current world. After promotion,
rebuilding them from Wt+1 carries object identity and geometry forward together. Reusing any one
of them from Wt would reintroduce the stale-map failure through a different interface.
4.7.3
From a Renderable Scene to a Spatial World Model
The distinction developed in Section 2.5 is now visible operationally. A Gaussian render provides a
view of a scene, whereas REALM’s action-conditioned update produces a successor on which later
grounding and planning can operate. Its world-modeling role comes from representing task-relevant
change while preserving object identity.
World models are often associated with learned latent dynamics or generated future video. Realm
instead models a task-relevant spatial consequence in an explicit 3D representation.
The action
transition is analytic; visual reconciliation can contain learned components; and the promoted state
remains directly renderable and consumable by later grounding and motion planning.
The known–inferred–trusted decomposition makes this hybrid construction explicit and is useful
precisely because the planner already knows an important part of the future: where a successfully
manipulated rigid object should be released. Prediction records what the plan says should change, rec-
onciliation records what subsequent evidence adds, and promotion records which world later reasoning
is allowed to consume.
4.7.4
From an Evolving World to Composable Skills
The garden study shows how the same object can remain available to later grounding and planning
after its represented location changes. Its evidence concerns continuity and local reconstruction under
planned rigid-object motion. Because the manipulation is simulated and the post-action imagery is
50


synthetic, agreement with the updated scene does not establish successful physical execution. In a
physical deployment, new observations would need to correct predictions when a grasp fails, an object
slips, or the environment changes independently of the robot.
An evolving representation still leaves the planner dependent on supplied skills.
Learning ad-
ditional policies broadens what the robot can do, but composition also requires information about
when those policies can be used and what conditions their actions are expected to produce. Chapter 5
develops this symbolic interface through joint learning of actions and predicted predicate trajectories.

Chapter 5
Learning Composable Skills
This chapter is adapted from Jointly Learning Predicates and Actions Enables Zero-Shot Skill
Composition, coauthored by Benedict Quartey, Sebastian Castro, Eric Rosen, Wil Thomason,
George Konidaris, and Stefanie Tellex (Quartey et al., 2026). The authorial “we” refers to this
collaborative research.
A planner gains new possibilities when it can compose behaviors the robot has learned, rather than
depend entirely on a supplied library of controllers. The preceding chapters established how expressive
instructions can become grounded plans and how the representation used for planning can evolve across
tasks. Both developments still depend on suitable skills being available. Learning those skills from
demonstrations offers a way to extend the robot’s repertoire, but a policy that produces movements
supplies only part of what composition requires. The planner must also reason about the conditions
those movements are expected to produce.
This chapter develops composable state-reporting skills, addressing the action interface of trust.
PACTS learns a skill’s control policy together with a symbolic trace of its expected effects, connecting
continuous behavior to the predicates used in task planning. The resulting interface supports new
compositions of learned skills and replanning from updated predicate predictions. The contribution
complements LIMP’s grounding of intent and REALM’s evolving world representation by examining
how learned behavior can participate in symbolic reasoning. The experiments study this skill interface
with a supplied predicate vocabulary and symbolic domain; their relationship to the preceding systems
motivates the synthesis in Chapter 6.
5.1

The generative policies introduced in Section 2.7 can represent several ways to carry out a behavior.
For composition, however, plausible movements are insufficient: the symbolic conditions associated
with a particular action sequence matter to the next skill. Different ways to manipulate an object can
lead to different outcomes, even when both begin from the same observation. A policy and a separately

We instantiate this joint model with diffusion and flow-matching objectives and with UNet and

5.2
Related Work
Generative visuomotor policies for imitation learning
Behavior cloning learns skills from
demonstrated observation–action pairs (Mandlekar et al., 2022), and recent work models distribu-
tions over action trajectories to capture multiple ways to perform a behavior. Diffusion-based poli-

lations. OpenVLA and π0 connect pretrained vision-language representations to robot control (Kim
53


et al., 2025; Black et al., 2025). Other approaches structure the learning problem through equivari-
ance (Wang et al., 2025a), object-centric representations (Yuan et al., 2022), or affordance-centered
task frames (Rana et al., 2025). Diffusion has also been used for planning: Diffuser (Janner et al.,

Policy (Chi et al., 2025) treats control as learning the distribution over action sequences, conditioning
on observations without denoising them for efficient real-time inference. We adopt this conditional-
policy perspective, but depart from action-only modeling by learning a coupled generator p(x, z | o)
that jointly samples actions and a low-dimensional predicate-belief rollout z aligned with PDDL pre-
conditions/effects, yielding an outcome-aware interface for execution-time monitoring and planning-
based composition. Vision–language–action models similarly couple control with language semantics,
but their semantics are typically part of the conditioning interface rather than an explicit planner-
consumable outcome trajectory.
Skills, abstraction, and planning-based composition
The symbolic planning framework in
Section 2.2 requires both a physical implementation of each operator and a grounding for its predicates.
In robotic systems, these abstract actions are typically instantiated as hand-designed controllers,
including statecharts (Harel, 1987) and behavior trees (Colledanchise and ¨Ogren, 2018), and predicates
are grounded through engineered perception and state estimation modules (Migimatsu et al., 2022).
Several frameworks support such symbolic planning architectures (Diab et al., 2020; Mart´ın et al.,
2021; Mayr et al., 2023; Paulius et al., 2025; Quartey et al., 2025).
Recent approaches reduce this manual engineering by learning the components that connect behav-
ior to symbolic planning. BLADE learns compositional behaviors and their preconditions and effects
from demonstrations and language (Liu et al., 2025b); Pixels-to-Predicates uses pretrained vision–
language models to ground predicates for symbolic world-model learning (Athalye et al., 2026). These
approaches establish symbolic interfaces for planning, but do not jointly sample an action trajectory
and its associated predicate trajectory. In long-horizon manipulation, actions and symbolic outcomes
are tightly coupled: different action modes correspond to different predicate transitions, and treating
them as separate predictions can lead to inference-time inconsistency. Pacts addresses this coupling
directly by modeling a skill as a joint generative process over action trajectories and predicate-belief
trajectories. Joint sampling allows the model to represent the dependence between action modes and
symbolic outcomes, which independently sampled heads do not explicitly represent.

5.3.4
Architecture
As shown in Fig. 5.2(a), Pacts factorizes into (i) an observation encoder and (ii) a conditional
trajectory generator. The observation encoder maps the current observation ot into a latent embedding
used to condition the generator. The generator implements either a DDPM noise-prediction network
ϵθ(ot, yk, k) (Sec. 5.3.2) or a CFM velocity field vθ(ot, yτ, τ) (Sec. 5.3.3), operating on the joint
trajectory y = [x | z].
We instantiate the trajectory generator with two backbones: (i) a temporal UNet that applies
1D convolutions over the horizon dimension, and (ii) a Transformer that treats time steps as tokens
and models long-range temporal dependencies via self-attention. In both cases, conditioning enters
through (a) the observation embedding and (b) the diffusion/flow time parameter (discrete k for
DDPM, continuous τ for CFM), injected via standard conditional modulation.
Like prior trajectory-generating visuomotor policies, Pacts can be executed in a receding-horizon
manner: at each control cycle, the policy conditions on the current observation ot, samples a length-
H action chunk x (and its paired predicate-belief trajectory z), executes a short prefix of x, then
re-observes and re-samples.

5.5
Evaluation
We evaluate Pacts across three tasks in simulation and a real-world multi-step task. Our experiments
are designed to answer three questions:
1. How does joint predicate–action generation affect action performance and predicate classification
accuracy?
2. How do modeling choices–DDPM denoising versus conditional flow matching, and UNet versus
Transformer backbones–impact performance?
3. Can the learned predicate-belief interface support planning-based recomposition and replanning-
based recovery on novel goals without retraining?
5.5.1
Tasks and datasets
We evaluate Pacts in three settings that trade off control, realism, and compositional complexity (see
Fig. 5.3).
2D PushBarrier (compositional benchmark). We introduce a multi-step 2D manipulation task
adapted from the Push-T environment (Chi et al., 2025). The workspace contains two objects (a T
block and an L block) that must each be pushed from their respective start regions to corresponding
goal regions. Successful completion requires not only reaching the goal regions but also achieving the
correct final orientation/alignment of each object with its goal marker. A barrier separates the start
and goal regions, and the robot must first press a button to open the barrier before either object can be
58


transferred to the goal side. This environment provides a controlled setting with clear compositional
structure and is our primary benchmark for systematic evaluations and ablations.
We use PushBarrier in two complementary evaluation modes. To address Questions (1) and (2)
in an end-to-end setting, we train single long-horizon policies on the full task and compare task
performance and predicate classification accuracy against our baselines. To address Question (3) in
a compositional setting, we construct a library of short-horizon skills and a task-relevant predicate
set, segment demonstrations into per-skill datasets, and train a separate Pacts model for each skill.
We then evaluate planning-based compositions that require sequencing skills, including novel skill
sequences not observed during training.
RoboMimic (3D manipulation). To evaluate under realistic visual and geometric complexity, we
additionally evaluate on two RoboMimic (Mandlekar et al., 2022) tasks with MimicGen (Mandlekar
et al., 2023) demonstration data: Kitchen and Coffee Preparation.
These tasks involve long-
horizon manipulation under visually grounded observations and serve as a complementary test of
whether joint modeling improves or maintains performance beyond the controlled 2D benchmark.
For each task, we define a predicate set aligned with task structure and implement predicate oracles
inside the simulator that compute ground-truth predicate values from simulator state. These predicate
labels are used for both training and evaluation, enabling direct comparisons of predicate classification
accuracy across methods. We use the base-difficulty (d0) variants, converted to absolute action space,
with 200 demonstrations per task: kitchen d0 and coffee preparation d0.
Real-world demonstration We include a qualitative real-world demonstration on a cube-packing
task. A tabletop robot must pick and place four colored cubes from fixed start regions into a container.
We collect monolithic, long-horizon demonstrations that complete the full task (packing all four cubes),
then use our segmentation and labeling toolkit to decompose each episode into skill-centric sub-
demonstrations corresponding to per-cube packing behaviors (e.g., pack red, pack green, etc.). We
train a separate Pacts model for each subskill and compose them with an off-the-shelf PDDL planner.
This enables recompositions beyond the original demonstrations, such as executing individual packing
skills in isolation or packing only a specified subset of cubes (e.g., “pack red and yellow”), providing
a concrete test of zero-shot compositionality in the real world.

Planning-based composition and recovery. We demonstrate planning-based recomposition of

replanning-based correction without retraining.
5.7
Limitations
Pacts inherits several limitations from predicate and skill learning. First, performance depends on the
choice and coverage of the predicate set: incomplete or mis-specified predicates that are misaligned
with task structure can make planning brittle and limit the expressivity of the outcome interface.
Second, while joint modeling couples action and outcome prediction, it does not eliminate errors
from perception and state aliasing—especially under occlusions and long horizons—and execution
can still fail under stochasticity, partial observability, or distribution shift. Finally, our composition
results assume a manually defined symbolic domain (e.g., PDDL operators and goals); automatically
discovering predicates, operators, or abstractions remains an open research direction. A promising
avenue is to leverage pretrained vision–language models to propose and evaluate candidate predicates
directly from demonstrations (Athalye et al., 2026), then distill a compact planner-compatible set for
downstream planning.
5.8
From Composable Skills to Scaling Trusted Autonomy
PACTS connect learned control to the symbolic conditions through which a planner composes behav-
ior. Joint generation makes the action trajectory and its predicted predicate trace parts of the same
skill model. The evaluations test control performance, agreement between predicted and executed
outcomes, and composition for goals without end-to-end demonstrations of the requested sequence.
The contribution is a way to acquire control and state reporting together within an explicit symbolic
planning domain.
Together, the technical chapters connect grounded intent, an evolving world representation, and
learned behavior to symbolic reasoning. The concluding Chapter 6 develops their shared principle
of constructing and maintaining task-relevant abstractions, and examines how these interfaces can
support continued learning, observation, and recovery in a physical closed-loop system.

Chapter 6
Scaling Trusted Autonomy
The ambition of generally capable robotics is to make useful behavior possible without anticipating
every task or engineering every response in advance. As the range of requests, environments, and
learned abilities grows, so does the difficulty of establishing why a robot’s behavior should be relied
upon. A system may understand more language while leaving its interpretation difficult to examine,
acquire more skills while obscuring their effects, or construct a detailed scene whose contents become
outdated through its own actions. The central challenge is to develop the means of assessing behavior
alongside the capability to produce it.
This thesis has approached that challenge through the connections between learned models and
explicit reasoning. The preceding chapters develop grounded task specifications, persistent spatial
representations, and learned skills with symbolic state reports. Together, they support a broader
position: the structure required for planning need not all be fixed before the robot encounters a task.
Some of that structure can be constructed from language and observations when it becomes relevant;
some can be learned with behavior; and some must be revised as the world changes. Understanding
how to construct and maintain these connections is central to scaling trusted autonomy.
6.1
From Greater Capability to Trusted Autonomy
The introduction established two requirements for robots that operate in human environments. Peo-
ple should be able to specify useful goals without prescribing each movement, and they should have a
basis for assessing whether the resulting behavior satisfies their requests. Foundation models make a
much broader range of language and perceptual knowledge available to the robot. Symbolic planning
provides a means of expressing requirements and reasoning over combinations of actions. Their com-
plementary strengths become useful together when the robot can connect the particular request and
environment to the representations on which planning depends.
The contributions develop this connection along three dimensions of capability. LIMP expands the
instructions that can be grounded into explicit temporal and spatial requirements without a complete
prebuilt semantic map. REALM carries a persistent spatial state across planned changes, allowing
65


subsequent instructions to obtain their grounding and planning abstractions from an evolved world.
PACTS connect learned control to predicted symbolic conditions, enabling a planner to compose
trained skills for requested goals without demonstrations of the entire sequence. These are different
forms of expansion: across descriptions, across successive world states, and across combinations of
behavior. They make the question of scaling concrete without equating it with a measured scaling
law or unrestricted competence.
Each expansion also exposes something that the robot must be able to assess.
An expressive
interpretation must identify the intended task and referents. A skill sequence must have effects that
support the planner’s next decision. A persistent representation must distinguish a predicted change
from one supported by observation. The scientific value of the three interfaces lies in making these
dependencies explicit. They identify what connects a plan that is correct within its model to a task
that is correctly understood and carried out in the world.
This perspective changes where the burden of engineering lies. A planning system needs more
than an algorithm for searching a supplied domain; it needs a way to obtain and maintain the domain
information that makes its reasoning applicable. The thesis develops parts of that process through
learning, grounding, and world evolution. Its unifying question is therefore how a robot can acquire
the structure needed for a particular decision while retaining enough information to reconsider that
structure when circumstances change.
6.2
Constructing the Abstractions a Task Requires
The world contains far more detail than a planner can use directly. Even a familiar room admits
many descriptions: objects have appearances, spatial relationships, possible uses, and properties that
matter only for particular activities. An abstraction makes planning tractable by retaining distinctions
relevant to a decision and setting others aside. The difficulty is deciding those distinctions before
knowing what the robot will be asked to do. A representation sufficient for one instruction may omit
the very attribute or relationship needed to understand the next.
The approach developed in this thesis is to defer task-specific representational commitments until
the task provides a reason to make them. The robot retains observations or a richer spatial repre-
sentation from which it can construct the semantic and geometric descriptions required for grounding
and planning. The resulting model is bounded and explicit, but it need not be the only model that
the robot can construct from the available information. This is the sense in which delaying closure
offers a path toward planning in open-world settings: the robot constructs a model for the current
task without treating it as a complete description of everything it might later need to know.
LIMP makes this principle concrete at the interface between language and the mapped environ-
ment.
An instruction determines which referent descriptions and spatial relationships the system
resolves using retained observations. The semantic map is consequently shaped by the task rather
than limited to an exhaustive catalogue of named landmarks prepared beforehand. Foundation mod-
els supply the breadth of language and visual concepts used in this construction, while explicit task
66


structure and spatial reasoning connect their outputs to the planner. The important combination is
semantic flexibility with a representation whose interpretation and consequences can be examined.
REALM extends the principle through time. If the underlying scene is fixed, newly constructed
abstractions can still describe an obsolete world. REALM therefore maintains a persistent Gaussian
scene with object identity and an explicit history of action-conditioned changes. Grounded objects,
planning geometry, collision representations, and rendered views are derived from the state associated
with the current task. Their continuity comes from that common state, while their form depends on
the question they must answer. The predictive transitions and subsequent use for decisions give the
scene its world-model function; the Gaussian representation alone does not provide it.
PACTS addresses a complementary source of rigidity: the connection between a learned controller
and the symbolic conditions used to compose it. Its action trajectories and predicate-belief trajec-
tories are learned jointly, so the skill exposes predicted state in a form the planner can use. The
predicate vocabulary and operator model are supplied, and the constituent skills are trained before
their composition. PACTS therefore does not demonstrate runtime discovery of the planning domain.
It shows how part of the interface that would otherwise require separate engineering can be acquired
with behavior, making those learned abilities available for new combinations at runtime.
Deferring these commitments does not remove the need for prior structure. LIMP depends on cap-
tured observations, spatial representations, and available skills; REALM depends on a reconstructed
scene and specified transition mechanisms; PACTS depends on demonstrations and a symbolic do-
main. Nor does constructing a model at runtime change the logical semantics of the planner that
consumes it. A finite or fixed map is not inherently closed-world; that assumption enters when what
it contains is treated as a complete account of the relevant world. The broader contribution concerns
when and how the robot obtains such an account, and how it can replace it as new tasks or evidence
make different distinctions necessary.
This principle also reveals a further requirement. Information is relevant to a task when it can
affect the task’s interpretation, feasibility, or outcome, whether or not the instruction mentions it.
The robot may need to account for an obstacle, an unstable support, or a property that becomes
apparent only during interaction. Retaining richer observations creates an opportunity to recover
omitted information, but does not ensure that it was observed or can be recovered correctly. Trusted
runtime abstraction therefore requires a way to assess whether the constructed model is adequate for
the decision it supports. The trust interfaces make that requirement more precise by locating the
connections through which an inadequate model can mislead the robot.

6.3
Trust Across Intent, Action, and World
The three questions introduced at the beginning of the dissertation can now be considered in light of the
technical results. Did the robot understand what the user meant? Did its actions produce the intended
effects? Does its internal world still match reality? Figure 6.1 brings the corresponding contributions
together in this conceptual order. The chapter sequence followed the assumptions exposed by LIMP;
returning to Intent, Action, and World shows how those contributions bear on the successive decisions
involved in carrying out a request.
Figure 6.1: The three trust interfaces and their complementary contributions.
LIMP exposes a grounded task and
motion plan; PACTS jointly generates actions and predicted predicate trajectories; REALM supplies an evolving spatial
representation for subsequent tasks.
At the intent interface, assurance begins with making the interpreted request explicit. LIMP rep-
resents temporal requirements in a co-safe task specification and connects its propositions to spatial
referents and feasible motion. Its evaluation across 150 instructions in five real-world environments
tests the combination of language interpretation, grounding, and planning. The results in Tables 3.1
and 3.2 show the benefit of this structure particularly for complex temporal requirements and avoid-
ance constraints. The weaker performance on instructions with implicit preferences is also informative:
a formal specification can expose and preserve an interpretation without ensuring that it is the inter-
pretation the user intended.
This distinction gives human inspection a clear role. LIMP presents its interpreted task structure
for review and supports reprompting after a user’s correction; the reported translation evaluation
measures the initial output without that correction. Inspection offers a means of identifying errors
before the planner acts on them. It is distinct from checking whether a plan satisfies the resulting
formal specification. The latter establishes a relationship between a model and a plan, conditional on
the interpretation, grounding, and action assumptions that made the model meaningful.
At the action interface, a planner needs to know how execution bears on its symbolic state. PACTS
learns to generate a proposed action trajectory together with the predicate conditions expected along
it. The composition and perturbation examples show how this interface supports new sequences of
68


trained skills and replanning when updated beliefs invalidate a plan. The action–predicate evaluation
in Chapter 5 goes beyond measuring predicate classification alone: it compares predicted beliefs
with outcomes under executed action chunks. This directly tests the connection that matters for
composition, within the evaluated tasks and horizons.
The same distinction between representation and evidence applies here. Joint sampling makes
actions and predicate predictions parts of one learned model, but the architecture does not prove that
they agree with physical outcomes. The reported state is refreshed through observation-conditioned
rollouts, yet predicted future predicates are not independent measurements of successful execution.
The contribution is an interface through which learned behavior can participate in symbolic reasoning
and through which its predicted effects can be evaluated. Reliable use depends on how well those
predictions track the behavior and observations encountered during operation.
At the world interface, the question extends beyond the current skill to the state on which later
tasks will depend. REALM’s two-instruction study shows that a persistent representation can carry
object identity and planned change forward while regenerating the next task’s grounding and planning
geometry. Object-local transitions preserve unaffected Gaussian content, and the recorded history per-
mits exact recovery of ancestor states. The representation and image-consistency results in Section 4.6
establish specific properties of that update process.
That boundary is essential to the world interface’s role in continued autonomy. A planned endpoint
predicts where an object should be if the action succeeds. A consistent rendering shows what the
resulting representation implies.
An observation supplies evidence about what the robot actually
changed.
Keeping these roles distinct allows an evolving world model to support scrutiny rather
than merely accumulate plausible predictions. REALM provides an explicit structure for carrying
changes and their provenance forward; physical observations would have to test those changes during
deployment.
Taken together, the results provide different kinds of evidence at complementary interfaces. Spec-
ification satisfaction, empirical agreement between predicted and executed outcomes, and consistency
of world-state updates answer different questions. Their value comes from connecting them without
allowing one to stand in for another. A plan can satisfy the wrong specification, accurate skill-level
predictions can be grounded in the wrong object, and an internally consistent world can depart from
reality. The thesis makes these dependencies available for examination and develops mechanisms for
several of them. Establishing assurance across their composition is the next scientific step.
6.4
Toward Continued Autonomy
The contributions suggest a robot whose understanding, behavior, and world representation can de-
velop together over successive tasks.
An instruction would determine the abstractions needed for
planning; learned skills would expose the conditions relevant to their execution; and subsequent ob-
servations would inform the state used for the next request. Realizing that loop poses questions about
how the components should share evidence, recognize inadequacy, and change their decisions. The
following directions arise from those questions and from the assumptions of the demonstrated systems.
69


6.4.1
Recognizing When an Abstraction Is Insufficient
Runtime construction makes it possible to build different models for different tasks. The next challenge
is to determine when the chosen model leaves out something consequential. Suppose an instruction
identifies a book to be moved from a table to a sofa. Correctly identifying those objects may be
sufficient for understanding the request, while planning and execution also depend on objects ob-
structing the grasp or the stability of the intended placement. A description adequate at the intent
interface may therefore be insufficient at the action interface. The robot needs a way to discover and
incorporate distinctions as they become relevant across these uses.
One research direction is to connect planning and execution failures to targeted expansion of the
abstraction. Repeatedly failing to find a feasible grasp could prompt the robot to inspect nearby
geometry; an unexpected effect could reveal a property absent from its action model. Foundation
models could propose additional descriptions or relationships, while geometric checks and observations
would test whether those proposals explain the difficulty.
The key question is how to select an
informative revision without rebuilding an exhaustive model of the environment. Success would involve
both improved task decisions and a justified account of why the new information was required.
This question also applies to the meaning of absent information. Failure to observe a relevant
object or condition should be distinguished from evidence that it is absent. A system using bounded
classical planning models could manage that distinction around the planner, acquiring information
or revising the domain before making a consequential commitment. Evaluating this ability requires
tasks with deliberately omitted but relevant entities, hidden conditions, and changed instructions.
Such tests would measure whether the robot recognizes the limits of its abstraction, rather than only
whether it solves tasks already expressible within it.
6.4.2
Connecting Predictions to Observations
Continued autonomy depends on deciding which predicted changes deserve to become part of the
robot’s working account of the world. The intended effect of a skill provides a useful expectation: it
identifies what might have moved and where new evidence may be needed. It also creates a risk if the
system promotes that expectation without checking it. A failed grasp, slip, or unmodeled interaction
could otherwise affect every later grounding and planning decision. The action and world interfaces
must therefore connect predicted effects to observations that can confirm or contradict them.
PACTS and REALM provide complementary starting points for this connection. Predicate beliefs
describe changes in conditions used by the planner, while a persistent spatial representation records
objects and their geometric relationships. An integrated system could use a reported effect to direct
re-observation and then compare the observed scene with the predicted transition. Agreement would
support reuse of the updated state; disagreement would require reconsidering the skill outcome, the
object association, or the world update. The challenge is to relate symbolic and geometric evidence
without assuming that either is an infallible authority.
Observation should itself become a purposeful decision. The robot need not reconstruct its entire
environment after every action, but it needs evidence about changes on which later decisions depend.
70


Selecting viewpoints, inspecting an occluded object, or asking a person to resolve an ambiguous ref-
erent can each reduce a different source of uncertainty. An account of the value of such information
should consider both its cost and the consequences of acting without it. This would extend run-
time abstraction from deciding what to represent to deciding what must be observed before that
representation can be relied upon.
6.4.3
Expanding Skills and Their Symbolic Meaning
Composition extends the usefulness of existing skills, but its reach is bounded by the abilities and
conditions available to the planner. PACTS assumes a supplied predicate vocabulary and symbolic
operators. Extending this work toward continued learning requires a way to introduce new behaviors
together with the descriptions that make them usable alongside existing skills. A controller alone can-
not tell the planner when the skill applies, which conditions it may establish, or how those conditions
relate to the rest of the domain.
Demonstrations and interactions could provide evidence for learning these relationships. A pre-
trained model might propose candidate predicates or describe a skill’s intended effect, but those
descriptions would need to be grounded and tested against execution. Different controllers that claim
to establish the same condition should support a consistent interpretation of that condition. Con-
versely, persistent failures may indicate that an operator omits a prerequisite or that one predicate
conflates states requiring different behavior. Learning the interface would therefore involve revising
symbolic distinctions as well as fitting better action and state predictions.
The central evaluation question is whether each addition expands the planner’s useful choices while
preserving the meaning of existing ones. This calls for testing both new compositions and previously
achievable tasks after the domain changes. It also requires relating predicate predictions to the actions
actually executed, especially when new skills alter observation distributions or introduce unfamiliar
interactions. PACTS’s coherence evaluation provides one starting point for this assessment; broader
coverage, confidence calibration, and validation under distribution shift would be needed to support
sustained expansion.
6.4.4
Managing Uncertainty Across the Interfaces
Uncertainty has different meanings at different points in the autonomy loop. The robot may be unsure
which object a phrase refers to, whether a skill will establish a condition, or whether a scene update
reflects a physical change. A single confidence score can obscure those differences. For uncertainty
to inform behavior, the system needs to associate it with a particular proposition or prediction and
identify what decision depends on it.
The interfaces also share sources of error. A mistaken object association can influence the task
specification, skill input, and world update simultaneously.
Agreement among these components
may then reflect their common mistake. Multiplying independently reported confidences would be
unjustified without accounting for such dependencies, and a predicate-belief score is not automatically
71


a calibrated probability. The research problem is to preserve enough information about the origin and
meaning of uncertainty to determine which additional evidence could resolve it.
Such an account would make recovery more selective. Ambiguity in intent may call for clarification,
uncertainty about an effect may call for observation, and an infeasible plan may call for a different
skill or abstraction. The robot should also be able to recognize when none of its available actions has
adequate support and seek assistance. Evaluations should examine whether these decisions improve
task outcomes and reduce unsupported commitments, alongside the time, observations, and human
intervention they require. This would make assurance part of the robot’s decision process rather than
a judgment applied only after execution.
6.5
Concluding Perspective
Generally capable robots will encounter tasks and circumstances that cannot all be described in ad-
vance. The work in this dissertation shows how planning can draw on learned semantic knowledge,
persistent spatial representations, and learned behavior to address parts of that variability. Its central
position is that scaling trusted autonomy requires unifying grounded language understanding, compos-
able state-reporting skills, and evolving representations of the world. Each contribution makes explicit
a connection needed to interpret a request, reason about behavior, or carry useful state into the next
task.
The broader lesson concerns how that structure is obtained. By deferring task-specific commit-
ments, constructing relevant abstractions when they are needed, and maintaining the information
from which they are derived, a robot can extend the reach of explicit planning beyond a single world
description fixed in advance. Learning can help supply the missing semantics and behavior; reasoning
can make their consequences available for inspection and testing. Continued autonomy will depend on
carrying this relationship further, so that the robot can recognize when its abstractions are inadequate
and use experience to revise them. Progress toward generally capable, verifiable robots requires both
a growing range of useful behavior and a growing ability to establish when that behavior can be relied
upon.

A.3
Spatial Grounding Module
The spatial grounding module detects and localizes specific instances of objects referenced in a given
instruction by first detecting, segmenting and back-projecting all referent occurrences and then filter-
ing based on the descriptive spatial details captured by each referent’s composable referent descriptor
(CRD). We use the Owl-Vit model (Minderer et al., 2022) to detect bounding boxes of open-vocabulary
referents and SAM (Kirillov et al., 2023) to generate masks from detected bounding boxes. To illus-
trate referent filtering via spatial information, consider an example scenario where the goal is to resolve
the composable referent descriptor below:
whiteboard :: isinfrontof(green plush toy).
(A.1)
Let W = {w1, w2, . . . , wn} and G = {g1, g2, . . . , gm} represent the set of representative 3D posi-
tions of detected whiteboards and green plush toys respectively. The cartesian product of these sets
enumerates all possible pairs (w, g) for comparison.
W × G = {(w, g) | w ∈W, g ∈G}
(A.2)
The ‘isinfrontof(w, g)’ comparator is applied to each pair, yielding a subset S that contains only
those ‘whiteboards‘ that satisfy the ‘isinfrontof’ condition with at least one ‘green plush toy’.
S = {w ∈W | ∃g ∈G such that isinfrontof(w, g) is true}
(A.3)

A.4
Task and Motion Planning Module
We present pseudocode for our Progressive Motion Planner (Algorithm A.1) and our algorithm for
generating Task Progression Semantic Maps (Algorithm A.2).
Algorithm A.2 generates a TPSM
Mtpsm by integrating an environment map (M) and a referent semantic map (Mrsm) given a logical
transition expression (T ), a desired automaton state (S′), and a nearness threshold (θ). The algorithm
first initializes Mtpsm with a copy of M and extracts relevant instruction predicates from T . For
each predicate (parameterized skill), the algorithm identifies satisfying referent positions in Mrsm,
generates a spherical grid of surrounding points within a radius θ, and assesses how these points affect
the progression of the task automaton towards S′. These points demarcate regions of interest, and are
assigned a value of 1 if they cause the automaton to transition to the desired state, -1 if they lead to
a different automaton state or violate the automaton, and 0 if they do not affect the automaton. The
points are then integrated into Mtpsm, yielding a semantic map that identifies goal and constraint
violating regions.

A.5
Robot Skills
We define three predicate functions: near, pick and release for the navigation, picking and placing
skills required for multi-object goal navigation and mobile pick-and-place. As described in Chapter 3,
we formalize navigation as continuous path planning problems and manipulation as object parame-
terized options. Chapter 3 discusses navigation, so here we focus on the pick and place manipulation
skills.
Pick Skill: Once the robot has executed the near skill and is at the object to be manipulated, it takes
a photo of the current environment to detect the object using the Owl-Vit model. The path planner
uses the backprojected object position to compute yaw angles that orient the robot toward the target.
After detecting the object, the robot obtains a segmentation mask from the detected bounding box
using the Segment Anything model and computes the center pixel of this mask. It passes this pixel
to the Boston Dynamics grasping API to compute a grasp motion plan.
Release Skill: We implement a simple routine for the release skill which takes two parameters: the
object to be placed and the place receptacle. Once a navigation skill gets the robot to the place
receptacle, the robot gently moves its arm up or down to release the grasped object, based on the
place receptacle 3D position. More expressive placement strategies could extend the use of LIMP’s
spatial grounding of instruction-specific receptacles. Demonstrations are available on the website.
A.6
Evaluation and Baseline Details
All computation including planning, loading and running pretrained visual language models was done
on a single computer equipped with one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU. We leverage GPT-4-0613
as the underlying LLM for our instruction understanding module and all our baselines. In all exper-
iments we set the LLM temperature to 0, however since deterministic greedy token decoding is not
guaranteed with GPT4, we perform three (3) queries for each instruction and evaluate on the most
recurring response (mode response).
We compare LIMP with baseline implementations of NLMap-Saycan (Chen et al., 2023a) and
Code-as-policies (Liang et al., 2023). Both baselines use the same GPT-4 LLM, prompting structure,
and in-context learning examples as our language understanding module.
We integrate our com-
posable referent descriptor syntax, spatial grounding module and low-level robot control into these
baselines as APIs. This enables baselines to execute plans by querying relevant object positions, using
our FMT* path planner to find paths to said positions and executing manipulation options.

B.2
Ownership Diagnostics
The selector in Eq. 4.14 combines complementary evidence. Positive depth-consistent voting locates
rows that project through the object; visible contradictions reject support inconsistent with other
cameras; covariance-footprint overlap tests whether the rendered row actually covers the mask; surface
and oriented-box distances bound the object spatially; and scale and anisotropy prevent scene-scale
splats from becoming movable object support.
The legacy ball support illustrates their interaction. Of 724 selected centers, 470 lie outside the
fitted ball OBB, 443 lie more than 0.01 world units outside it, and 180 have fewer than three footprint-
supported views. Median anisotropy is 95.529 and the maximum is 177,390.8. These rows visibly
include table and ground content. The combined selector retains 254 rows and is used throughout
both transitions. Exact thresholds and per-row diagnostics are stored in the ownership-quality artifact.
Ownership construction rejects empty or insufficient supports, duplicate and out-of-bounds indices,
and overlap between independently movable objects. The current analysis covers one ball; extending
ownership to thin, articulated, reflective, transparent, and heavily occluded objects remains a central
generalization problem.

B.4
Observation and Reconciliation Protocol
Each observation frame records intrinsics, a rigid camera transform in the Realm world frame, a
fit or holdout role, and hashed RGB, metric depth, and masks. Physical bundle types cannot carry
synthetic-pixel masks; synthetic bundles require one for every frame. Implemented physical adapters
accept registered robot/OSG frames, Record3D, COLMAP, or an already prepared bundle.
For the reported study, rendered geometry determines source-disocclusion and target-insertion
masks. Generated color is allowed only inside those masks, after which source pixels are restored
exactly outside them. Target depth remains the predicted metric depth rather than generated depth.
Stage 1 contains 21 image-edit requests (12 source and 9 target); stage 2 contains 24 (12 source and
12 target). Requests, model identity, masks, and results are content-addressed and cached.
Reconciliation inserts 12,170 unowned disocclusion seeds at stage 1 and 3,551 at stage 2, activates
mask-supported scene regions, and optimizes the selected Gaussians. Densification and pruning are
disabled for action-owned rows. Predicted owner means, scales, orientations, and opacities are restored
after fitting; spherical-harmonic appearance may change. The demonstrated candidates require non-
increasing changed-region L1 and an increase of at most 10−4 in outside-region L1, both measured
against the same synthetic targets on validation views.
