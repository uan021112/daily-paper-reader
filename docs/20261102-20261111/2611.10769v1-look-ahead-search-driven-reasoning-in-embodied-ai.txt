# Abstract 

Search allows a robotic policy to evaluate candi-date futures before committing to an action. Mo-tivated by test-time planning for vision–language– action (VLA) models, we study selective search over action chunks in PushT using a frozen Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) policy. Beam search generates perturbed alternatives around the policy prediction and scores them through rollouts from cloned simulator states. Across 50 evalua-tion episodes, beam search raises task success from 0% to 18% and increases average cumula-tive reward from 39.44 to 103.2. We introduce 

One Step Ahead (OSA), a gate that simulates the nominal policy chunk and invokes full search only when its predicted coverage drop exceeds a thresh-old. In the reported ablations, OSA preserves or improves success while reducing runtime by ap-proximately 3× relative to full beam search. Ab-lations over tree depth, branching factor, beam width, and replanning frequency show that larger search budgets do not consistently improve per-formance. The results support selective test-time search for action-chunking policies; evaluation on language-conditioned VLAs and physical robots remains future work. 

# 1. Introduction 

Vision–language–action (VLA) models map visual observa-tions and task instructions to robot actions, offering a route toward generalist control (Black et al., 2024; Shukor et al., 2025; Octo Model Team et al., 2024). Predicting plausible actions, however, does not ensure that their consequences will complete a task. In contact-rich manipulation, a locally reasonable movement can displace an object away from its goal, and errors can accumulate over successive action chunks. Policy-guided search combines a learned action prior with a transition model and a scoring function. The policy restricts exploration to plausible actions, the model predicts their effects, and the score identifies promising futures. This can improve action selection without updating policy parame-ters, but repeated policy queries and simulated transitions increase inference cost. The practical question is therefore when additional search changes the decision enough to jus-tify that cost. We investigate this question in PushT, a simulated planar manipulation task in which an agent pushes a T-shaped ob-ject toward a target pose (Chi et al., 2025). We augment a pretrained ACT policy (Zhao et al., 2023) with beam search over locally perturbed action chunks. ACT is a vision-based action-chunking policy rather than a language-conditioned VLA. This isolates action selection from language ground-ing while retaining the proposal-and-rollout interface used in VLA planning. Full search often returns the same nominal action chunk that the policy would have executed directly. To reduce this overhead, we introduce One Step Ahead (OSA). OSA first simulates the nominal chunk and invokes beam search only if its final predicted coverage is lower than the current coverage by more than a fixed threshold. # 2. Related Work 

Action-chunking policies and VLAs. ACT predicts short sequences of actions from visual observations and robot state using imitation learning (Zhao et al., 2023). Dif-fusion Policy represents visuomotor behavior through ac-tion diffusion and uses receding-horizon execution (Chi et al., 2025). Generalist policies extend these ideas to broader task specifications: Octo supports language- and goal-image-conditioned control, π0 combines a pretrained vision–language backbone with a flow-based action model, and SmolVLA emphasizes efficient language-conditioned action generation (Octo Model Team et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Shukor et al., 2025). Our method operates on the action-chunk interface and keeps the policy frozen. Its evaluation uses ACT, so transfer to language-conditioned policies is an architectural possibility rather than an estab-lished result. 

Search for VLA policies. VLA-Reasoner uses online Monte Carlo tree search with a learned world model and value estimates to evaluate action-conditioned futures (Guo et al., 2025). VLAPS integrates model-based search with pretrained VLA action priors and evaluates language-specified manipulation in LIBERO (Neary et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023). Its experiments assume access to an accurate simulator. These approaches establish the relevance of test-time planning for VLA control. We study a simpler local beam-search procedure in PushT, using continuous coverage feedback and a gate based on the nominal chunk’s predicted effect. Our experiments focus on the cost of tree expan-sion and the value of selective search, rather than a direct performance comparison with these methods on different benchmarks.

3.2. Simulator-Based Beam Search 

A search state contains the simulator configuration, includ-ing the agent coordinates, object position and orientation, and the dynamic variables needed to restore the state. At each planning point, we snapshot the current simulator state. Every hypothetical rollout starts from the appropriate re-stored state, and the root state is restored before any action is committed to the environment. The task provides a coverage measure c ∈ [0 , 1] describing overlap between the movable T and the target T. We use this feedback to rank candidate futures. Success is defined by at least 95% coverage; an episode ends at success or after 

T = 300 environment steps. Unlike real-world control, this setup provides both a simulator transition model and privileged access to task coverage. At each expanded node, ACT predicts a nominal chunk. A candidate set contains this chunk and locally perturbed alternatives:

Each candidate is simulated from its parent state, producing a child state scored by its coverage-based rollout outcome. At each level, beam search retains the BW highest-scoring nodes and expands them until depth D, success, or the episode limit. The selected action is the root chunk lead-ing to the best-scoring explored outcome. The controller restores the real state and commits its first H actions. Only committed transitions contribute to episode reward; hypo-thetical search rollouts do not. The execution horizon determines how often the controller replans. 4.2. Effect of Tree Structure 

Table 1 shows that beam search improves substantially over the evaluated ACT baseline. The configuration with D =4, C = 8 , and BW = 4 achieves the highest average cumulative reward, 103.2 versus 39.44 for direct inference. Its average maximum reward rises from 0.36 to 0.81, and success improves from 0% to 18%. This comes at a runtime increase from 2 to 47 seconds, or approximately 23 .5×.Increasing depth alone yields little improvement. With 

C = 4 and BW = 2 , depths 2, 4, and 8 produce the same reported average reward values and 18% success. In-creasing the number of candidates generally increases ASR: at D = 4 , BW = 2 , ASR rises from 37.5% with C = 2 to 75.6% with C = 8 . This shows that a larger candidate set more often changes the policy’s action, but the correspond-ing gains in task success remain modest. Wider beams also do not guarantee better outcomes. At D =4 and C = 8 , increasing BW from 4 to 8 raises runtime from 47 to 87 seconds while reducing average cumulative reward from 103.2 to 92.2 and success from 18% to 10%. Search quality depends on the proposal distribution and coverage heuristic as well as on the number of explored nodes. 

4.3. Replanning Frequency 

Figure 2 compares D = 2 , C = 4 , BW = 2 with D = 4 ,

C = 8 , BW = 4 . More frequent replanning generally improves cumulative reward. As the horizon increases, the controller commits to longer action prefixes and loses op-portunities to correct deviations. The larger tree performs best at short horizons, but its advantage disappears at longer horizons. These results indicate that frequent feedback can matter more than deeper or wider search at infrequent deci-sion points. 

4.4. Selective Search with One Step Ahead 

Figure 3 compares OSA with full beam search. The prelimi-nary coverage check reduces runtime by approximately 3×

in the reported ablations. Success is preserved or improved in the displayed settings, even when cumulative reward de-creases. These metrics need not move together: cumulative reward measures progress throughout an episode, whereas binary success records whether the coverage threshold is reached. The widest beam provides a useful example. Full search with BW = 8 performs worse than the smaller beam, while OSA improves its success rate. One possible explanation is that skipping unnecessary expansion limits exposure to perturbed actions favored by a short-horizon heuristic. This is an interpretation of the observed behavior, rather than a separately measured causal effect. OSA does not remove the cost of look-ahead: it still simu-lates the nominal chunk at every planning point and runs the full tree when the gate fires. Its benefit comes from avoiding redundant alternatives when the nominal prediction passes the coverage check.

# 5. Discussion and Limitations 

The experiments support selective search for a frozen action-chunking policy in PushT. They do not establish general improvements for VLA models: ACT is not language-conditioned, only one checkpoint and one task family are evaluated, and there is no direct comparison with VLA-Reasoner or VLAPS under a shared benchmark. Extending the controller to language-conditioned policies and tasks such as LIBERO is necessary to test that transfer. The planner relies on restored simulator states and task-specific coverage feedback. Physical deployment would require a reliable dynamics model and a progress estimator available from real observations. Model errors could change both candidate rankings and the OSA trigger. The current evaluation does not quantify this sensitivity or validate the controller on real robots. The nominal coverage gate is deliberately narrow. It can miss stalled behavior and does not detect every harmful ac-tion. The threshold is fixed, and the tree configuration is se-lected before a rollout. Adaptive budgets, uncertainty-aware triggers, and alternative search algorithms may improve the trade-off between task performance and computation. The quantitative results summarize 50 episodes per config-uration without confidence intervals or reported variability across independent seed sets. Small differences in success therefore warrant caution. In addition, the reported baseline has zero success, and the strongest full-search configuration reaches only 18%; these gains do not imply that the task is solved reliably. Further evaluation with stronger check-points and matched compute budgets would clarify when search is preferable to improving the base policy. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We study look-ahead action selection motivated by search for VLA control, using a frozen ACT policy in PushT. Beam search over perturbed chunks improves success and cumu-lative reward over the evaluated baseline, but larger trees 5Look Ahead: Selective Search for Robotic Control 

do not consistently improve performance. One Step Ahead reduces redundant tree expansion by checking the predicted coverage change of the nominal policy chunk before search-ing. In the reported ablations, this gate preserves or im-proves success while reducing runtime. These findings mo-tivate selective planning for robotic policies and evaluation on VLAs and physical robots.

# Impact Statement 

This work studies inference-time action selection in a simu-lated manipulation task. Better allocation of planning com-putation may make robotic policies more efficient to evalu-ate and deploy. The experiments do not establish safety for physical robots: simulator access and privileged progress feedback do not account for all consequences of real-world actions. Deployment would require validation of the tran-sition model, task feedback, and controller behavior in the intended operating environment.
