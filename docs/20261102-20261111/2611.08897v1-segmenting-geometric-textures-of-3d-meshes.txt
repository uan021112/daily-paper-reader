Abstract 

Recognizing geometric texture patterns on 3D models is critical for applications such as heritage protection, model editing, and 3D printing. Existing 3D model segmentation methods predominantly focus on identifying high-level semantic parts, thereby failing to capture middle-level textures. While recent Vision-Language Models can perform image segmentation with appropri-ate text prompts, they still struggle to recognize 3D geometric textures. In this work, we present a method that segments 3D mesh models based on middle-level geometric textures. We further introduce a new geometric texture segmentation benchmark and fine-tune SAM3 accordingly. The segmented texture regions are then clustered based on DINO feature similarity and lifted from 2D to 3D. Additionally, we develop an interactive tool to enable both 3D model editing based on our segmentation re-sults and model retrieval from the dataset based on geometric and texture similarity. Experiments demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms existing methods in segmenting geometric textures and can facilitate various applications.

2. Related Works 

Geometric Texture over 3D model surfaces are high-frequency, repetitive surface details distinct from global shape and random noise (excluding hollowing and winding structures). Existing re-search on geometric textures mainly focused on synthesis and in-teractive editing. Early works established procedural and example-based methods for texture synthesis [BIT04,Elb05,ZXZD24], a di-rection recently advanced by deep generative models [HHGCO20, YCK ∗25]. Complementarily, editing tools have evolved from man-ual manipulation of displacement maps [LHGM] to recent texture generation based on the vision-language model [MWS ∗25]. How-ever, as the prerequisite for automated analysis and editing, the fun-damental problem of geometric texture recognition and segmenta-tion is significantly less explored. Several studies have attempted to bridge this gap. One direction is to separate texture and non-texture regions using learning-based methods [GDJ ∗24, PTB24]. Tortorici et al. [TBOW21] introduced a convolution-based descriptor for rec-ognizing user-specific textures. Despite these efforts, current methods still exhibit poor general-ization when dealing with multiple textures, multi-scale structures, and complex topologies. To the best of our knowledge, an open-source benchmark, a robust and automated solution for geometric texture segmentation and recognition that handles diverse and com-plex patterns over a complicated model, is still missing in the liter-ature [BTB ∗18, PTB ∗25]. 

Model Segmentation is an essential task in computer graphics and vision. Early methods mainly rely on low-level geometric primi-tives such as curvature, surface normals, and geodesic distances to partition 3D models, including region growing [LYW ∗20], graph cuts [KT03], spectral clustering, and MRF/CRF-based optimiza-tion [Sha08]. Although these methods perform stably on segment-ing homogeneous low-level geometric regions (e.g., planar sur-faces, smooth curved patches), they fail to capture high-frequency, repetitive geometric patterns that define geometric textures. Re-cent deep learning works focus on semantic or part-level segmen-tation. For native 3D representations, point cloud networks (e.g., PointNet [QSMG17, QYSG17], DGCNN [WSL ∗19]) and mesh-based networks [HHF ∗19] achieve state-of-the-art performance on benchmarks such as ShapeNet Part [CFG ∗15] and PartNet [MZC ∗19]. However, these methods are limited to coarse-grained category or part labels (e.g., separating chair legs from seats) and cannot recognize fine-grained geometric textures [YLX ∗24, XYQ ∗25, LUX ∗25]. Recent works following a 2D-to-3D pipeline, leverage pre-trained 2D foundation models (e.g., SAM [KMR ∗23], CLIP [CLZ ∗25]) to project multi-view 2D predictions back to 3D [ZXL ∗24, TZF ∗24b, TCC ∗26, ZHR ∗26]. While these methods enable open-vocabulary segmentation, they still focus on semantic object categories rather than geometric texture patterns. To the best of our knowledge, the segmentation of geometric textures on 3D models remains an open problem [PTB ∗25]. Cur-rent attempts indicate that image-like local descriptors offer the most promising solution for geometric texture recognition and seg-mentation [BTB ∗18]. Hence, we borrow the ability from the visual foundation model and employ the 2D-to-3D pipeline to address this problem. 

Image Segmentation aims to partition an image into regions that are coherent in semantics, instance identity, or visual at-tributes. Earlier deep segmentation methods primarily focus on dense prediction and instance mask modeling [LSD15, RFB15, HGDG17]. Recent segmentation research has increasingly shifted toward unified and promptable foundation models. MaskFormer

3. Geometric Texture Segmentation Benchmark 

To begin, we construct a large-scale geometric texture segmenta-tion benchmark, consisting of synthetic and annotated 3D models. Collecting reconstructed models through scanning is extremely ex-pensive and labor-intensive. Instead, we choose to sculpt 3D mod-els via texture displacement maps, see Figure 3. 

Texture Displacement Maps Dataset. We use Chord [YRWX25] to generate displacement maps based on the open-source 2D texture images dataset DTD [CMK ∗14]. We construct a displacement map dataset, TDSet , consisting of 350 diverse displacement maps. The displacement maps in TDSet are employed to synthesize 3D geo-metric textures over 3D models. 

3D Geometric Texture Benchmark. Next, we construct a geometric texture benchmark called GTSeg , which consists of a training set and a test set. For the training set, we collect 80 synthetic mod-els from Thingi10K [ZJ16]. We randomly select four or five dis-placement maps from TDSet and apply them to the surface of each model. All geometric texture instances are automatically annotated. For each model, we repeat this process ten times. The final training set consists of 800 models, each with multiple geometric texture categories and instances. Moreover, we construct a test set com-prising 20 synthetic models generated following the above pro-cedure (with no overlap with the training set) and 10 real scans from [XZZ ∗14] and public domain. We manually annotate the ge-ometric texture instances on the real scans.

Grayscale Curvature and Mask Rendering. Surface curvature is an intrinsic geometric property that remains strictly invariant to lighting conditions and rigid transformations. To bridge 3D geom-etry with 2D segmentation, we compute per-vertex mean curvature as the raw scalar si. To mitigate the impact of mesh singularities and ensure high image contrast, we clip outliers according to the 5th/95th percentiles. The curvature values are then normalized to [0,1] via min-max normalization. Here, we split the models in the training set of GTSeg into two sets: 76 models are used for training and 4 for validation. We ren-dered each model to generate curvature maps and ground-truth masks from 14 uniformly sampled viewpoints. That is to say, the training set contains 10,640 image and mask pairs, while the vali-dation set contains 560 image and mask pairs. All rendered views and synthesized variants derived from the same source model are assigned to the same split to prevent data leakage. Finally, we gen-erate a dataset of 11,200 pairs of view-dependent curvature maps and corresponding ground-truth masks. The training set will be em-ployed to adapt the vision foundation model to our task later.

5. Geometric Texture-oriented Model Segmentation 

In this section, we introduce a framework to address the problem of geometric texture segmentation, via formulating it as a task of multi-view consistency perception. The overview of our framework is visualized in Figure 4. Given a 3D mesh M, we first render a set of grayscale curva-ture maps {Ci}Ni=1 from a collection of predefined camera poses 

{Pi}Ni=1. Subsequently, the rendered images are fed to our GTSAM to predict a set of pixel-level geometric texture masks. We intro-duce a feature association module based on DINOv2 to maintain semantic consistency across disparate views. This module extracts and clusters high-dimensional feature descriptors from each mask, thereby establishing label correspondences across different view-points in a unified latent space. Finally, multi-view masks with con-sistent labels are projected onto the model surface M. To resolve prediction conflicts arising from occlusions or perspective distor-tions, we implement a spatial-consistency weighted voting mecha-nism. This mechanism performs per-face arbitration to make defini-tive label decisions, yielding precise and smooth geometric texture segmentation results. 

5.1. Texture-oriented Image Segmentation 

We set N cameras to render the input mesh, ensuring complete cov-erage and sufficient overlap. In our implementation, we let N = 50. The t-th camera renders a curvature image It . Next, we send the rendered curvature images to our GTSAM. For It , GTSAM pre-dicts a set of Nt instance masks. Each instance mask Mt,m is paired with a confidence score ct,m ∈ [0, 1].For each predicted mask Mt,m, we perform 8-connected compo-nent analysis. Components with an area smaller than the thresh-old Amin are discarded. We retain those sufficiently large connected components and treat them as independent mask instances. This prevents disconnected 2D regions from being merged into the same texture cluster.

6. Applications 

Geometric Texture Enhancement and Removal. For model editing, normal filters are capable of removing all the high-frequency lo-cal characteristics, such as noises [ZDZ ∗15, XZSJ18]. Specifically, the static/dynamic filter effectively identifies all the relief struc-tures over the model surface, without recognizing their higher-level patterns. Based on our segmentation results, we employ the static/dynamic filter to interactively enhance or remove a specific type of geometric texture over the model (see Figure 1). Our seg-mentation method dramatically improves editing efficiency. 

Geometric Texture Replacement. After removing the user-specific geometric texture instance, the user can select an arbitrary texture from our DTSet, and apply it to the target region to achieve replace-ment of geometric texture (see Figure 1). 

Model Retrieval. Effective retrieval of the reconstructed model that shares similar geometric texture is critical for the projection and restoration of the heritage. The query in Figure 1 is the brick wall texture of Merlion. The retrieval is performed among four models. For each model, we performed segmentation and extracted the DI-NOv2 feature tokens of all the texture instances. Then, we measure the similarity between all the texture instances and the target (Eq. 3), returning the model that contains the texture instance with the highest similarity.

7. Experimental Results 7.1. Ablation Study 

Firstly, we evaluate the contribution of three key components. The LoRA-adapted SAM3 is replaced with the original zero-shot model. Next, the step of feature-based association is removed from the framework. All the masks are grouped solely according to the overlap of their projected 3D regions. Then, feature-based associa-tion is removed from the framework, and masks are grouped solely according to the overlap of their projected 3D regions. The experi-mental results are shown in Table 1. For all quantitative evaluations, the results are presented as percentages. The results indicate that replacing the fine-tuned model with zero-shot SAM3 brings the largest degradation in segmentation ac-curacy. Removing feature-based clustering also substantially re-duces performance. Consistency filtering provides a further im-provement over the unfiltered variant. The complete method con-sistently achieves the best performance across all metrics. Overall, the results demonstrate that all three components are necessary for accurate and reliable 3D geometric-texture instance segmentation.

8. Conclusion 

Existing model segmentation methods focus predominantly on se-mantics or low-level geometry. In this work, we presented a method for the automatic recognition and segmentation of geometric tex-tures on 3D surface models. We first constructed a specialized benchmark for geometric texture segmentation. Then, we proposed a geometric texture-oriented segmentation method that follows a 2D-to-3D pipeline. By leveraging a fine-tuned visual segmentation model and DINO feature-based clustering, our method effectively identifies repetitive texture patterns across multiple views. Further-more, we designed a weighted voting fusion strategy to achieve precise 3D segmentation. Building upon this, we developed a tool that enables users to segment geometric texture instances and to edit or retrieve models based on the segmentation result.
