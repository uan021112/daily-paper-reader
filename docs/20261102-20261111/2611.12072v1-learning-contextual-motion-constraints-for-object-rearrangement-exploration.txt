Learning Contextual Motion Constraints for Object Rearrangement Exploration
Lexing Zhang1,2
Zeyu Zhang2:
Gang Zeng1
Hangxin Liu1:
Abstract— The motion of an articulated part in cluttered
surroundings is constrained not only by an object’s intrinsic
joint limits but also by extrinsic obstacles. However, existing
approaches primarily consider object articulation properties
alone. Here we present Motion Constraint Prediction Network
(MoCo-Net), a two-stream neural network that jointly predicts
the continuous motion ranges of articulated parts and obstacle
heatmaps highlighting potential collision regions from partial-
view point cloud observation. By capturing both internal and
external motion constraints, MoCo-Net produces more accurate
feasible motion range prediction and obstacles recognition,
improves generalization for novel objects in new scenes, and
enables robots to construct a scene configuration space for
efficient exploration of rearrangement actions. Our experiments
show that MoCo-Net’s two-stream design enhances each other
to deliver more accurate predictions, which subsequently form
a compact configuration space for a customized planner to
efficiently rearrange the scene.
I. INTRODUCTION
Understanding the structure of articulated objects is fun-
damental to their effective manipulation. Accordingly, prior
research has devoted substantial effort to reconstructing part-
level geometry [1–3] and decomposing objects based on their
articulation [4–6]. Further progress has been made in estimat-
ing articulated joint parameters [7–9] and predicting motion
priors [10–12], thereby enabling more informed manipulation
planning [13].
Because articulation is visually ambiguous, many studies
rely on sequential observations of an object’s part motions
to infer its structure and latent articulation properties [14–
16]. Active interaction and probing provide an additional
means of disambiguating articulation [17–19]. In contrast,
another line of work seeks to infer articulation properties
from static observations, using only a single view [20–22]
or a small set of views [23–25]. Although fully recovering
articulation properties from static data is inherently ill-posed,
these approaches are more versatile because they avoid the
need for dense interactions or long observation sequences,
which are not always available.
Despite these advances, most existing approaches over-
look the influence of surrounding objects and contextual
constraints on articulated parts. This is largely due to their
object-centric modeling perspective; for instances, obtain-
ing complete geometry from object reconstruction [2, 26],
identifying articulated components by predicting movable
parts [27–29], and inferring potential object interactions
through affordance prediction [30–32]. These approaches

Although some studies incorporate contextual cues such
as support, containment, and non-penetration [33, 34] to
improve object segmentation in scene reconstruction, they do
not consider articulated objects or their parts in motion. As a
result, they fail to capture how the feasibility of part motions
or manipulation actions is shaped by constraints imposed by
the surrounding environment.
To address this gap, we propose Motion Constraint Predic-
tion Network (MoCo-Net), a two-stream neural network that
jointly predicts the continuous feasible motion ranges of ar-
ticulated parts in cluttered scenes and the obstacle heatmaps
that restrict part movements. MoCo-Net is motivated by the
insight that motion feasibility depends on both an object’s
intrinsic kinematic constraints and the extrinsic constraints
imposed by surrounding obstacles. To capture both aspects,
MoCo-Net performs scene-conditioned reasoning on a point-
cloud input through two coordinated streams: one estimates
collision-aware motion limits, while the other predicts an
arXiv:2611.12072v1  [cs.RO]  9 Nov 2026


obstacle heatmap highlighting regions where the part is likely
to collide, indicating constraints on part motion (Fig. 1).
Our experiments show that these complementary pre-
dictions substantially improve accuracy and generalization,
especially on unseen layouts and objects. Beyond prediction,
the estimated feasible motion range provides a continuous
parameterization of an articulated part’s possible movements.
This representation can be used to construct a scene configu-
ration space that captures feasible prismatic and revolute part
motions and supports subsequent analysis of rearrangement
actions. A customized downstream planner is designed to
leverage this configuration space to efficiently explore possi-
ble motions, assess action feasibility, and prioritize obstacle
rearrangement based on predicted heatmaps, which reduces
the need for exhaustive searches in high-dimensional action
spaces. Because configuration space is a key concept in
robot motion planning, MoCo-Net could offer a convenient
and interpretable bridge between raw scene perception and
actionable manipulation planning [35].

Sec. II introduces the proposed method. Sec. III discuss
the application of the feasible range and obstacle heatmap
in planning. Sec. IV describes the experimental setup and
analysis of the results, and we conclude the paper in Sec. V.

B. Implementation and Model Training
We implement the aforementioned predictors using a two-
thread neural network, the architecture is illustrated in Fig. 2.
The first thread predicts the feasible range through the
function fθ, where we optimize the parameters θ. The second
thread identifies movement-impeding obstacles through the
function gϕ, with ϕ as the learnable parameter.
The feasible range prediction thread takes in the partial
scene point cloud PS and the query point MT and out-
puts the feasible range RT . This thread employs a Point
Transformer-based [36] scene encoder ES to encode the point
cloud input into a latent representation ZS, and a decoder
DR that predicts the feasible range. The obstacle heatmap
prediction thread processes three inputs: the partial scene
point cloud PS, the query point MT , and the feasible range
RT . This thread shares the scene encoder ES with the prior
thread and feeds local features from each encoder stage,
along with the scene latent representation ZS and feasible
range RT , into decoder DH to predict the obstacle heatmap
HT .
We jointly train both predictors by minimizing the loss
function in Eq. (1). The architecture R4H creates a bene-
ficial interdependence: gradients from the obstacle heatmap
backpropagate to the feasible range predictor, enhancing its
accuracy, while the feasible range serves as prior information
that allows the obstacle heatmap predictor to focus on
potential collision areas.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method,
we measure its performance across three aspects: feasible
range prediction, obstacle heatmap prediction, and the impact
of these predictions on downstream planning. Additionally,
we conduct ablation studies to examine the architectural
design of our model, including the joint training of the
two predictors and leveraging predicted feasible ranges for
obstacle heatmap prediction.

vironment and source objects from the ShapeNet dataset [38]
and Partnet-Mobility dataset [39], which together provide
a diverse collection of articulated objects across multiple
categories. Our virtual scenes consist of target articulated
objects placed with random joint states, surrounded by 0
to 15 randomly positioned obstacles. We focus on common
interactive objects frequently encountered in daily environ-
ments, spanning a variety of household object categories such
as tables, storage furniture, refrigerators, microwaves, etc.

B. Feasible Range and Obstacle Heatmap Prediction
Setup: To systematically evaluate the performance of our
method, we carefully curate the training and testing sets.
The training set consists of 16,000 scenes, while the testing
set is divided into two subsets: (i) Layout Var., comprising
5,000 scenes where target articulated objects and obstacles
are sampled from the same object pool as the training set but
with different object combinations, layouts, and viewpoints,
and (ii) Unseen Obj., consisting of 5,000 scenes with entirely
new target articulated objects, obstacles, layouts, and view-
points. These subsets enable a comprehensive assessment of
the generalization capability of the methods under varying
scenarios.
Since no prior work directly addresses the problem we
investigate, we compare our method with a set of carefully
designed baselines and ablations, including a geometry-
based simulation reference as well as variants that analyze
backbone selection, the benefit of joint training, and cross-
branch interaction in our framework.
‚ GT-Sim. A baseline that computes feasible ranges and
obstacle heatmaps by simulating part motion using
ground-truth joint types, joint parameters, and part
segmentation masks directly on the input point cloud.
‚ Backbone-PointNet and Backbone-PointNet++. Variants
of our full model where the backbone is replaced by
PointNet [41] and PointNet++ [42], respectively, while
keeping all other components unchanged.
‚ FR-Only and OH-Only. Single-task models that predict
only the feasible range or only the obstacle heatmap,
respectively, by removing the other prediction branch
while retaining the same Point Transformer backbone.
‚ Joint. A shared-encoder model with two task-specific
heads that jointly predicts the feasible range and obsta-
cle heatmap, but without cross-branch conditioning.
‚ Joint-H4R. A variant of Joint, where the predicted
obstacle heatmap is fed into the feasible range branch.
‚ Joint-R4H (Ours). Our full model, where the predicted
feasible range is used to guide obstacle heatmap esti-
mation via R4H.
To ensure a fair comparison, our model and all ablations are
trained under identical conditions, i.e., 100 epochs using the
AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 10´3 and a weight
decay of 10´4.

Results: Tab. I reports the quantitative evaluation results
using the proposed metrics for feasible range prediction and
obstacle heatmap estimation. Our method (Joint-R4H (Ours))
achieves the best performance across all metrics and evalua-
tion settings and performs well under Unseen Obj., indicating
accurate feasible range prediction, precise potential collision
area identification, and strong generalization ability.
The GT-Sim baseline shows poor performance as it per-
forms collision checking directly on the observed partial
point cloud, where incomplete observations and sparse point
sampling lead to inaccurate collision estimation during sim-
ulated motion. Additionally, replacing the Point Transformer
(Joint-R4H (Ours)) backbone with PointNet (Backbone-
PointNet) or PointNet++ (Backbone-PointNet++) leads to
consistent performance degradation in both feasible range
and obstacle heatmap prediction, although PointNet++ im-
proves over PointNet. This suggests that the attention mech-
anism in Point Transformer more effectively captures spatial
and structural relationships among points and object parts,
which is crucial for motion constraint reasoning.
For ablation, compared with single-task training, Joint
outperforms both FR-Only and OH-Only on their respective
tasks, indicating that jointly learning feasible range and ob-
stacle heatmap yields more expressive shared representations.
This demonstrates the superiority of the coupled formulation
in Eq. (1). Our models Joint-R4H consistently surpasses
both Joint and Joint-H4R, highlighting the effectiveness of
the proposed R4H design. In contrast, feeding heatmap
predictions back to the range branch (Joint-H4R) does not
bring similar benefits and slightly harms range estimation.
We attribute this to the higher complexity of heatmap predic-
tion: inaccurate heatmap estimates may introduce misleading
signals, whereas the predicted feasible range provides a more
stable motion prior for guiding collision reasoning.

key metrics: (i) Collision-free Success Rate (CSR) measures
the percentage of instances where the articulated part suc-
cessfully achieves the target state within 1% of the intended
manipulation amount without any collision; (ii) Average
Rearrangement Steps (ARS) quantifies planning efficiency
by counting the average number of rearrangement actions
required to reach the goal; Since each rearrangement step
corresponds to a physical manipulation executed by the
robot, this metric is also closely related to the execution
time of the task; (iii) Average Collision Number (ACN)
evaluates the safety factor by measuring the average number
of collisions occurring during planning execution. Together,
these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of plan-
ning effectiveness, efficiency, and safety.
Results: We present the quantitative results in Tab. II. The
DirectManip baseline exhibits a high ACN and a zero CSR,
indicating that directly manipulating the target part consis-
tently leads to collisions and fails to achieve the goal safely.
In contrast, the FR+Greedy baseline achieves a significantly
higher CSR of 80.0% with a substantial reduction in ACN
compared to DirectManip, suggesting that our predicted
feasible range enhances safety during manipulation. Com-
pared to the FR+Greedy approach, our method reduces the
arrangement steps by 0.713, highlighting the effectiveness
of the predicted obstacle heatmap in identifying potential
obstacles. Overall, our method achieves a higher success rate
while requiring fewer actions and producing fewer collisions
during execution, demonstrating that our predicted motion
constraints effectively guide configuration space exploration
and lead to more efficient and safer planning in cluttered
environments.
Qualitative examples of the rearrangement sequences are
shown in Fig. 5. Compared with the FR+Greedy baseline,
our method more accurately identifies obstacles and produces
more efficient rearrangement sequences to achieve the goal.
V. CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced a novel approach for pre-
dicting feasible ranges and obstacle heatmaps from partial
3D point clouds to facilitate the manipulation of articulated
objects in cluttered environments. Our method addresses a
critical gap in existing work by modeling the continuous
motion constraints on articulated objects imposed by both
intrinsic articulated joints and extrinsic surrounding obsta-
cles. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrated that
our approach accurately predicts feasible ranges and gen-
erates meaningful obstacle heatmaps across diverse scenes
