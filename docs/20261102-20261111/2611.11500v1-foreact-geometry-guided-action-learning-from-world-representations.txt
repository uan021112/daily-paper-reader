Abstract — Robotic manipulation requires representations that capture both scene geometry and its evolution during interaction. Existing approaches have largely treated these two aspects separately. World action models capture such dynamics by coupling action prediction with future video generation, but their reliance on iterative diffusion or rectified-flow inference leads to high computational cost, while their representations lack explicit spatial structure. Explicitly modeling scene ge-ometry typically requires auxiliary modules that increase both parameter count and computational cost. Conversely, geometry-centric policies primarily recover observed spatial structure and often lack explicit supervision for anticipating future geometric changes during interaction. To address these limitations, we introduce ForeAct, which repurposes a pretrained video world model as a single-pass representation backbone for low-latency control. ForeAct directly processes the clean latent of the cur-rent observation in one forward pass, eliminating future-frame synthesis and iterative denoising while retaining the backbone’s pretrained spatiotemporal priors. Rather than introducing a separate geometry backbone, ForeAct uses learnable tokens supervised by next-frame relative depth to shape the shared video representation through bidirectional self-attention. This compact interface turns the spatial knowledge already encoded in the backbone into a future-aware representation for action generation, with little parameter overhead. The lightweight geometry readout is used only during training and removed at deployment, enabling low-latency, geometry-aware control without depth input or an external geometry backbone. With-out additional large-scale pretraining, ForeAct achieves 96.6% average success on LIBERO, 63.5% on SimplerEnv-Bridge, and a 74.4% success rate across 90 real-world rollouts.

A. Vision-Language-Action Models 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models align the semantic representations of large-scale vision-language pre-training with robot action spaces, establishing a common interface from visual observations and language instructions to control. Early approaches discretize actions as language tokens [1], [13], whereas later architectures predict continuous action chunks and scale training across heterogeneous robot data and embodiments [14], [2], [15], [3], [16]. This progres-sion improves semantic grounding, instruction following, and transfer across tasks and embodiments [17]. However, most VLA policies still condition the action decoder pri-marily on the current observation and language, leaving the temporal consequences of interaction and the geometry of future scene configurations to be learned implicitly from robot demonstrations. ForeAct preserves the general VLA action interface while providing the action decoder with a predictive, geometry-aware representation extracted from a pretrained video world model, thereby introducing explicit spatiotemporal structure into action learning. 

B. World Action Models 

World Action Models (WAMs) extend the reactive VLA interface by jointly modeling future observations and robot actions. Their predicted future states provide temporal con-text for planning and long-horizon control. Representative systems jointly predict future images and robot actions, use visual goals as intermediate reasoning states, or couple action learning to conditional video generation [18], [19], [20], [4], [5]. Fast-WAM [6] further suggests that the control benefit can arise from the learned world representation rather than from the final sampled frames. A central limitation of diffusion-based WAMs, however, is that action decoding is tied to noisy latents or intermediate denoising states indexed by the noise realization and diffusion timestep. These synthesis-oriented features yield a stochastic control state and require iterative future-frame sampling [4]. ForeAct processes the clean current observation in a single world-model forward pass and passes the resulting visual tokens to the Action DiT, avoiding noisy future generation while enabling efficient control. 

C. Geometry-Aware Policy Learning 

Explicit 3-D representations provide spatial relations es-sential for contact-rich manipulation and precise placement. GeoAware-VLA [7], VGGT-DP [8], and VGA [9] transfer representations from pretrained geometric models to action prediction, establishing geometric perception as a backbone paradigm for policy learning. GP3 [21] likewise explores ge-ometry for robotic control. Future geometry is also explicitly modeled by predictive approaches such as GAM [22] and PointWorld [23]. In this section, we first present the overall design of ForeAct (Sec. III-A). As illustrated in Fig. 2, ForeAct takes an RGB observation and a language instruction as inputs and couples a pretrained video world model with action gener-ation and predictive geometric supervision. A. ForeAct Overview 

Problem Formulation. Given an RGB observation It and a language instruction c, ForeAct predicts an H-step action chunk at:t+H−1 without requiring proprioceptive state and, during training, jointly estimates the relative depth of the next observation. The task prompt augments c with “Next-Frame Geometry & Actions” (Fig. 2). We formulate this joint prediction as follows: 

(ˆ at:t+H−1, ˆdt+1 ) = Fθ (It, c ), (1) where H is the action horizon and ˆdt+1 denotes the normal-ized relative depth of the immediate next observation. This state-free formulation supports deployment when robot-state measurements are unavailable or vary across embodiments, while the auxiliary depth target encourages the learned rep-resentation to capture short-horizon scene evolution. Depth supervision is used only during training and is never provided to the action policy at inference.

B. Latent World Representation 

Diffusion-based world action models (WAMs) commonly obtain predictive context by denoising noise-initialized fu-ture latents and coupling intermediate generative features to action prediction [4], [5], [6]. While effective for visual forecasting, this formulation makes the control representation depend on sampled noise and diffusion timesteps, incurs re-peated backbone evaluations, and prioritizes future synthesis over observation-aligned evidence. Recent findings further suggest that the control benefit of video modeling arises largely from its learned representation rather than the final generated frames [6], [12]. ForeAct therefore uses the pre-trained video world model as a conditional representation backbone: a clean observation latent is processed once at the fixed diffusion endpoint to directly expose its spatiotemporal prior. The resulting representation is stable, anchored to the current scene, and efficient for action conditioning without explicit future-frame sampling. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the current RGB observation It

is encoded by the frozen video VAE into a clean visual latent zt, while the frozen text encoder maps the language instruction c to ec. After patch embedding, the visual tokens are concatenated with a set of learnable future-geometry tokens Q0 and processed once by the pre-trained world model backbone at the clean endpoint:

The visual tokens Vt provide the observation-aligned world representation used to condi-tion the flow-matching action DiT, whereas Qt summarizes global spatial structure for the latent geometry branch. Dur-ing training, the latter is combined with observation-derived VAE features and decoded into the relative depth of the next frame, allowing future geometric supervision to directly shape the shared world model representation. At inference, the geometry decoder is removed and actions are produced from the single-pass visual representation, requiring neither future-frame generation nor depth observations. 

C. Future Geometry Learning 

Geometry-centric policies condition action prediction on spatial representations transferred from geometric foundation models [7], [8], [9]. Such pretraining provides strong struc-tural priors, while anticipating future spatial changes requires predictive learning beyond reconstructing observed geome-try. ForeAct starts from the temporal priors of a pretrained video world model and exposes its geometric knowledge through a compact set of learnable future-geometry tokens 

Q0. Jointly updated with visual tokens by unmasked self-attention, these tokens aggregate spatial context and transmit next-frame depth supervision to the action features. This aligns future geometry with action learning within the video backbone, without requiring depth input or an additional geometric encoder at inference. B. Evaluation on Simulator 

We evaluate ForeAct on two established simulation bench-marks to assess generalization across task structures and ro-bustness to visual and embodiment shifts. Figure 4 visualizes simulator RGB observations and the model’s predicted depth for the next time step. 

LIBERO Benchmark Results. LIBERO evaluates language-conditioned manipulation across four suites that vary spatial arrangement, object identity, goal semantics, and execution horizon. As reported in Table I, ForeAct ob-tains 98 .4% , 98 .8% , 97 .2% , and 91 .8% on LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long, yield-ing a 96 .6% average success rate. It achieves the best Spatial score and the second-best Object and Goal scores, demon-strating strong transfer of action-relevant geometry. Among world action models without additional robot-action pre-training, ForeAct improves over WorldVLA and FlowVLA by 14 .8% and 8.5% , respectively, while remaining compet-itive with Fast-WAM and Cosmos Policy, with performance gaps of only 1.0% and 1.9% . It also exceeds the geometry-aware DepthVLA and GeoPredict by 1.7% and 0.1% without additional depth input. The lower Long score reflects the accumulated difficulty of extended-horizon execution. 

SimplerEnv Benchmark Results. SimplerEnv-Bridge complements LIBERO by evaluating visual-matching manip-ulation on the WidowX platform with BridgeData V2 demon-strations under variations in object appearance, placement, and visual context, testing whether policies preserve task-relevant geometry across such changes. As shown in Table II, ForeAct ranks first overall with a 63 .5% average success rate, exceeding GR00T N1.5 by 1.6 percentage points. It performs best on Spoon on Towel ( 87 .5% ) and ties the best result on Eggplant in Basket ( 100 .0% ), demonstrating robust spatial precision across these variations. ForeAct achieves 54 .2% 

on Carrot on Plate, whereas its lower performance on Stack Blocks ( 12 .5% ) reflects the difficulty of sequential multi-object alignment. Overall, these results indicate that future-geometry supervision transfers useful spatial structure across visual variations without requiring depth input or an external geometry model at deployment.

D. Latency Analysis 

To assess inference efficiency, we compare ForeAct with representative VLAs and WAMs on LIBERO in terms of average success rate, latency, and parameter count, as shown in Fig. 6. Latency is evaluated following the corresponding inference settings on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU with batch size 1, covering observation encoding through action-chunk generation. 

Inference Efficiency. ForeAct achieves a 96 .6% average success rate at 168 ms with approximately 2.1B param-eters, placing it in a favorable accuracy–latency regime. It approaches the accuracy of larger world action models with fewer parameters and lower latency, while substantially outperforming the faster conventional VLA baseline. At a comparable success rate, its latency is roughly one-third that of Cosmos Policy, and it is also faster than Fast-WAM while using a considerably smaller model. This efficiency comes from extracting predictive representations and decoding ac-tions with a single world-model backbone pass, without iterative future-frame denoising, making ForeAct suitable for closed-loop manipulation. 

E. Ablation Study 

We ablate ForeAct on LIBERO to evaluate single-pass world representations and token-mediated future-geometry supervision. Table III examines component contributions, while Fig. 7 analyzes geometry-token capacity and super-vision horizon. 

Components and Design. Table III shows that the clean action-only baseline outperforms Diffusion WAM ( 94 .3% 

vs. 93 .2% ), supporting a single video-backbone pass for action prediction without iterative video denoising. Adding geometry tokens without future-depth supervision yields a modest gain to 94 .7% , whereas incorporating future-depth supervision raises success to 96 .6% . This result indicates that the improvement extends beyond additional token capacity. The supervision pathway is also important. Directly imposing depth supervision on the visual features yields only 93 .4% ,supporting a dedicated geometry-token interface. Restricting attention to one-way visual-to-geometry information flow reduces success to 94 .9% , supporting bidirectional interac-tion through which depth-supervised geometry tokens can shape the visual features before action decoding. Together, these results demonstrate the complementary benefits of clean world representations and token-mediated geometric supervision.

and 94 .7% , respectively. This suggests that a compact to-ken set provides sufficient capacity for effective geometric supervision, while additional tokens provide no further gain. Fig. 7(b) shows that next-frame depth supervision outper-forms current-frame depth supervision ( 96 .6% vs. 95 .2% ), supporting the value of anticipating spatial changes beyond encoding observed geometry. 94 .9% , suggesting that immediate-future geometry provides a more effective learning signal under the single-observation formulation. These findings support the default choice of eight geometry tokens with next-frame depth supervision. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented ForeAct, a unified backbone combining pretrained spatiotemporal priors with future-geometry super-vision for efficient robotic manipulation. ForeAct extracts action features from clean observation latents in one video-backbone pass, without future-frame synthesis or iterative denoising. Learnable geometry tokens receive next-frame relative-depth supervision and interact with visual features through bidirectional self-attention within the shared video backbone. Depth is used only for training, so deployment requires neither depth observations nor a separate geomet-ric backbone. Experiments on LIBERO, SimplerEnv-Bridge, and real-world tasks demonstrate strong performance in sim-ulation and physical settings. Ablations support the comple-mentary roles of the representation and geometry-supervision designs.
