Abstract: Recent robot manipulation benchmarks have enabled systematic eval-
uation of general-purpose robot policies across diverse objects, scenes, instruc-
tions, and embodiments. However, most existing benchmarks still assume that
the robot is the only active agent and that task semantics are determined pri-
marily by the instruction and object state. This assumption overlooks a com-
mon source of real-world variation: humans actively sharing the workspace. In
such settings, humans may reveal an implicit goal, complete part of the task, tem-
porarily change object availability, or constrain safe robot motion while working
nearby. We introduce HARP-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating robot manip-
ulation policies in workspaces shared with active humans. HARP-Bench treats
human activity as part of the manipulation problem, rather than as background
scene variation. It organizes human-present manipulation into four conditions:
Cueing, Contribution, Interruption, and Co-presence. These conditions are in-
stantiated through everyday manipulation tasks, including paired human-present
variants for controlled comparison with standard manipulation. HARP-Bench
also provides a demonstration-generation pipeline for dynamic human-present
settings. We evaluate VLA models, imitation-learning methods, and heuristic
policies on HARP-Bench. Human-present variations challenge current policies,
and higher task success does not consistently improve safety. Quantitative failure
analysis identifies manipulation and recovery as the dominant remaining chal-
lenges. Training with HARP-Bench demonstrations improves human-present per-
formance in simulation. A real-world study on one task family shows improved
performance under human contribution, but not interruption, providing initial ev-
idence of the data’s utility and identifying limits of transfer. Code is available at
https://github.com/UMass-Embodied-AGI/harp.
Keywords: human-robot interaction, dataset, benchmark

Introduction
Recent robot manipulation benchmarks [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8] have become increasingly scalable
and systematic, evaluating policies across diverse tasks, objects, scenes, instructions, embodiments,
and temporal horizons. Despite this progress, most benchmarks vary the passive conditions under
which a robot acts [9, 10]. Objects, layouts, and instructions may change, but the robot is typically
the only agent that changes the workspace. Consequently, the meaning of a task is largely fixed by
the language instruction and the current object state. This assumption breaks down in workspaces
shared with people, where human actions can alter what the robot should do, which objects are
available, and where the robot can move safely.

In human-present workspaces, the robot’s next action may depend on human activity rather than
on object state alone. For example, a person reaching toward a task-relevant object may require
the robot to pause, while a person completing part of the task may require the robot to update its
plan. More broadly, human activity can change the robot’s goal, task progress, object availability,
and motion constraints. Evaluating such behaviors requires benchmarks that go beyond standard
object-centric manipulation.
Several recent benchmarks have begun to consider humans in embodied evaluation. However, most
of these settings either operate at a high level of abstraction [11, 12], where interaction is represented
through planning or skill-level actions, or remain limited in scale and visual richness for evaluating
modern vision-language-action manipulation policies [13]. This leaves a gap for scalable, visually
grounded benchmarks that test autonomous manipulation policies in human-present workspaces.
We introduce HARP-Bench, a simulation benchmark for evaluating autonomous robot manipulation
policies in human-present workspaces. HARP-Bench organizes human activity into four evaluation
conditions: Cueing, Contribution, Interruption, and Co-presence. These conditions capture whether
human behavior indicates the robot’s objective, completes part of the task, changes object availabil-
ity or execution timing, or adds motion constraints while working nearby. HARP-Bench implements
these settings with animated human avatars and closed-loop avatar events during policy rollouts,
together with RGB-D observations, natural-language instructions, safety-aware metrics, and data
interfaces for VLA and imitation-learning policies.
Beyond task design, HARP-Bench provides an evaluation and data-generation pipeline for human-
present manipulation. Avatar behaviors are triggered during closed-loop policy rollouts, so policies
are evaluated against human activity at execution time. HARP-Bench also supports scripted demon-
stration collection, VLA-oriented trajectory recording, and a LeRobot-compatible data interface,
with additional conversion paths for other VLA and imitation-learning pipelines.
We use HARP-Bench to evaluate two expert references and representative imitation-learning and
VLA policies under the same human-present evaluation protocol. We report task success across the
four conditions, together with aggregate safety metrics and a quantitative failure breakdown. Higher
success does not consistently improve safety, and manipulation/recovery dominates the failures of
both imitation-learning and VLA policies. Training on HARP-Bench demonstrations improves per-
formance on matching human-present variants, suggesting that the benchmark can support both
evaluation and data generation for robot policies around active humans.
Our contributions are as follows:
Benchmark. We introduce HARP-Bench, a simulation benchmark with 37 tasks for evaluating
robot manipulation in human-present workspaces, organized into four conditions: Cueing, Contri-
bution, Interruption, and Co-presence.
Evaluation. HARP-Bench provides closed-loop avatar-driven evaluation, where human activity
occurs during policy rollouts. Policies are evaluated by both task success and safety metrics.
Data generation. HARP-Bench supports scripted demonstration collection, VLA-oriented trajec-
tory recording, and a LeRobot-compatible data interface, with additional conversion paths for other
VLA and imitation-learning pipelines.

growth of large-scale robot datasets and open training infrastructure, which provide diverse experi-
ence across environments, objects, tasks, and robot embodiments [25, 26, 27, 28]. As these policies

Existing manipulation benchmarks have expanded along these axes, from reusable simulated en-

2.2
Human-Centered Embodied Benchmarks and Avatar Simulation
Recent work has introduced humans into embodied and robotic benchmarks through household
goals, collaborative planning, human-in-the-loop interaction, high-level assistance, and responsible
decision making [33, 11, 12, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. However, these settings often abstract away
low-level manipulation execution, focusing instead on planning, user interaction, assistance policies,
or risk-aware reasoning and detection. As a result, they do not directly evaluate autonomous manip-
ulation policies acting around active humans from visual observations and language instructions.
Low-level HRC work is more directly related, but existing simulation benchmarks are limited in
task scale and visual richness [13], while real-world studies are typically task-specific and difficult
to scale [40, 41, 42]. This leaves a gap for scalable, visually grounded simulation benchmarks for
human-present manipulation. Avatar simulation and motion resources make it possible to generate
repeatable human activity in simulation [43, 44, 45]. HARP-Bench builds on these tools to construct
controlled human-present manipulation conditions with closed-loop avatar events, safety-aware met-
rics, and data-generation interfaces for imitation-learning and VLA pipelines.
3
HARP-Bench: Robot Manipulation around Active Humans
HARP-Bench is a simulation benchmark for evaluating robot manipulation policies in human-
present workspaces. It extends standard object-centric manipulation tasks with avatar-driven hu-
man activity that can change the robot’s objective, object availability, action timing, and motion
constraints. The benchmark consists of 37 benchmark tasks organized around four human-present
conditions, and provides closed-loop evaluation, safety-aware metrics, and data-generation inter-
faces for imitation-learning and VLA pipelines. Table 1 summarizes the scope of HARP-Bench
relative to related benchmarks.

the object configuration. In human-present workspaces, the same object-level skill may require
different behavior depending on what the human is doing. We organize these differences into four
evaluation conditions: Cueing, Contribution, Interruption, and Co-presence. Figure 1 illustrates
these conditions with representative task instances.
Cueing tasks evaluate whether the robot can infer the appropriate objective from human activity.
For example, the human may carry an object, offer an object, or perform an ongoing activity that
indicates what the robot should do next. Contribution tasks evaluate whether the robot can account
for human-completed subtasks, such as a human placing one item while the robot completes the
remaining items. Interruption tasks evaluate whether the robot can adapt when the human temporar-
ily changes execution conditions, such as inspecting, holding, or occupying a task-relevant object.
Co-presence tasks evaluate whether the robot can complete its original manipulation goal while a
human performs unrelated work nearby.
These conditions connect to established HRI problems: human-intent inference and human-action
prediction for Cueing [46]; human-progress estimation and timely proactive assistance for Contri-
bution [41]; reactive task replanning and recovery under asynchronous human actions for Interrup-
tion [42]; and human-aware motion planning and shared-workspace safety for Co-presence [47].
They respectively capture changes to the goal, remaining subtasks, action availability or timing, and
motion constraints. This organization isolates effects on manipulation rather than constituting an
exhaustive taxonomy of human behavior.
The 37 tasks are task-condition instances, with multiple human-present variants sharing an under-
lying manipulation skill. This paired design supports comparisons while keeping object-level goals
comparable; it does not imply 37 distinct manipulation skills. The suite includes tabletop object
manipulation and articulated-object interactions such as microwave and cabinet operation.
3.2
Closed-Loop Evaluation with Simulated Human Activity
As Figure 2 shows, HARP-Bench uses animated human avatars to model task-relevant human activ-
ity in shared manipulation workspaces. Our avatar simulation follows the avatar-robot joint simula-
tion framework used in Virtual Community [43], adapted here for tabletop robot manipulation. Each
avatar is represented as a skinned human model driven by retargeted motion clips. Avatar appear-
ances are drawn from an avatar pool. The motion library combines Mixamo’s motion-captured ani-
mations performed by professional actors [45] with AMASS-derived motions, which originate from
optical motion-capture datasets unified in a common body-model representation [44]. The avatar
controller supports idle and walking motions, object inspection, pick-and-place motions, drawer-
pull motions, and hand-object attachment for one- or two-handed interactions.
For each task, avatar behavior is specified through manipulation-relevant events. The avatar may
approach the table, hold or offer an object, inspect a task object, place an item, open a drawer, or
perform unrelated nearby work. For human-object interactions, objects can be attached to the avatar
hand at specified animation frames and detached later. For some human-side scene changes, such

Avatar events occur during closed-loop robot execution. Cueing typically begins with a motion
indicating the intended action. Contribution events begin at an early randomized time, Interruption
events can depend on the gripper position near a relevant substep, and Co-presence uses randomly
selected nearby motions. Although the source motions are based on human performances, task
events and timing remain scripted for controlled, repeatable evaluation. This does not capture the full
variability of real human behavior or continuous human-robot adaptation; adaptive human models
remain future work.
Evaluation metrics. Task success uses task-specific completion criteria, such as object placement,
pose, height, or arrangement. An analytic collision checker computes signed distances between
sampled robot-link geometry and query-only avatar body geometry; a robot-avatar collision makes
the episode a failure. We additionally report three safety metrics: Collision, the fraction of episodes
with a robot-avatar collision; Min. Dist., the mean of each episode’s minimum robot-avatar distance;
and Violat., the fraction of timesteps with distance dt < τ, using a benchmark-specific proximity
threshold τ = 0.10 m. These metrics distinguish collision incidence, closest approach, and expo-
sure to close proximity. The proximity criterion is inspired by the speed-and-separation monitoring
principle in ISO/TS 15066:2016 [48]. The fixed 10 cm threshold is our evaluation convention, not a
standard-prescribed protective distance; it neither implements a speed-dependent protective separa-
tion calculation nor establishes real-world safety compliance.
3.3
Data Generation and Policy Interfaces
As shown in Fig. 2, the data-generation branch uses the same task initialization and avatar simula-
tion, but records scripted rollouts as training trajectories. For data generation, each task provides
scripted rollouts that produce successful demonstrations under the corresponding human-present
condition. These rollouts are used to collect videos and trajectory data, but are separate from policy
evaluation, where learned policies act in a closed loop with avatar events triggered during execution.
For VLA and imitation-learning pipelines, HARP-Bench records trajectories with visual observa-
tions, robot state, language instructions, and action labels. The recorder stores synchronized camera
observations and robot measurements at the policy frequency, so the resulting data can be used
directly for policy training or offline analysis.

HARP-Bench provides a LeRobot [28]-compatible data interface, with additional conversion paths
for other VLA and imitation-learning pipelines. It also exposes a common policy interface for
evaluating learned policies from visual observations and language instructions, allowing different
policy families to be compared under the same human-present task and safety metrics.
4
Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Data and splits. We train and evaluate policies using demonstrations collected from scripted rollouts
in HARP-Bench. The training set contains 11,100 demonstrations across 37 tasks, with 300 per
task, including 7 Cueing, 10 Contribution, 10 Interruption, and 10 Co-presence tasks. We hold out
100 evaluation seeds per task and report results over the corresponding 100 evaluation episodes.
Unless otherwise specified, learned policies are trained with the same visual observations, language
instructions, and action representation. VLA instructions use human-authored templates; action
labels come from the recorded robot trajectories.
Evaluation protocol. All policies are evaluated with closed-loop avatar events under the same task
initialization and success criteria. We report task and condition-level success, aggregate safety met-
rics as defined in Sec. 3.2, and primary failure categories. Evaluation seeds vary rollout conditions;
they do not measure variability across independently trained policies.
Baselines. We compare two expert references with representative imitation-learning and VLA poli-
cies.
Expert references. Both experts observe object poses and robot state, and share task-specific state
machines, annotated grasps, inverse kinematics, and RRT motion planning. The full-state expert
additionally observes avatar pose, event start/end states, and the current event-conditioned subgoal.
It serves as a privileged feasibility reference, while the second expert lacks this explicit human-event
information.
ACT. [14] A transformer-based action-chunking imitation-learning baseline.
Diffusion Policy. [15] A diffusion-based imitation-learning baseline that predicts continuous action
sequences from visual observations.
π0. [20] An end-to-end VLA baseline pre-trained with a massive amount of robotic data.
SmolVLA. [49] A compact vision-language-action model designed for efficient robot policy learn-
ing, generating robot actions from multimodal observations and language instructions.
4.2
Main Results
Table 2 reports task success across the four human-present conditions. The full-state expert achieves
0.99 success, compared with 0.48 for the no-intent expert, indicating that the tasks are executable un-
der the benchmark protocol. Learned policies show substantially lower success, with π0 performing
best overall at 0.19, followed by ACT at 0.10, DP at 0.09, and SmolVLA at 0.06, with performance
varying substantially across conditions. In particular, π0 achieves 0.21 success on Dump Bin under
Co-presence, but drops to 0.16 under Interruption, suggesting that human-present settings introduce
policy challenges beyond standard object manipulation.
Task success and safety. Table 3 shows that higher success does not consistently improve all
safety metrics. Among the four learned policies, π0 achieves the highest success (0.19), while
ACT performs best on all three safety metrics, with the lowest proximity-violation rate (0.080), the
largest mean minimum separation (0.133 m), and the lowest collision rate (0.102). Thus, success
alone is insufficient to rank behavior around humans, even though collision already counts as a task
failure.

Case Study: Failure Analysis. We analyze representative failures caused by human activity. As
shown in Fig. 3, policies may fail to complete the remaining subtasks after human contribution,
fail to adapt when a human changes an object’s state, or violate safety around the human. In the
primary failure analysis, Manipulation/recovery includes failed manipulation and recovery after
human-induced object or task-state changes; Collision denotes robot-human collision; and Intent
denotes explicit grounding errors, such as selecting the wrong object or ignoring a cue. Manipula-
tion/recovery accounts for 60–66% of learned-policy failures, followed by collision at 22–27%. π0
retains a broadly similar failure composition despite its higher overall success rate. This does not
establish identical underlying mechanisms, and the recovery category includes interaction-induced
errors rather than only basic manipulation.

Setup. We evaluate sim-to-real transfer on a real xArm tabletop setup. We use the dump bin task
family, which includes a base task and two human-present variants: Contribution, where the human
completes a complementary part of the task, and Interruption, where the human inspects or occupies
a task object. All real-world policies use the ACT architecture and are trained from data collected in
HARP-Bench. We selected these variants because their primary outcomes can be evaluated by task
success in the same tabletop setup. Co-presence depends more directly on human-robot separation,
which this setup does not measure precisely. The study tests whether HARP-Bench data improves
policy learning; it does not fully validate all four benchmark conditions or real-world safety.
Protocol. We compare five train-test settings: base policy on the base task, base policy on the con-
tribution task, base policy on the interruption task, contribution policy on the contribution task, and
interruption policy on the interruption task. The base policy is trained only on robot-only demon-
strations, while the contribution and interruption policies are trained on the corresponding human-
present demonstrations from HARP-Bench. Training data size and training steps are matched across
settings. Each setting uses 30 trials with different object positions. For each real-world trial, the hu-
man follows the same high-level event specification as in simulation, but the exact timing and motion
are not simulator-controlled.
Results. Table 5 reports real-world task success on the xArm setup. The base ACT policy achieves
0.40 success on the robot-only task, but drops to 0.13 under Contribution and 0.00 under Inter-
ruption. This shows that human-present variants also degrade policy execution in the real world.
Training with human-present demonstrations from HARP-Bench improves the Contribution variant
from 0.13 to 0.33, indicating that benchmark data can help policies handle cases where the human
completes part of the task. However, the Interruption policy still fails to complete the real-world
interruption task, suggesting that object contention and timing around human motion remain chal-
lenging under sim-to-real transfer. Overall, these results support the need for human-present training
data while also highlighting the difficulties for current policies.
5
Conclusion
We presented HARP-Bench, a benchmark of 37 human-present task instances spanning Cueing,
Contribution, Interruption, and Co-presence. Its closed-loop evaluation and demonstration pipeline
support both policy assessment and training. Current policies struggle with these settings: higher
success does not consistently improve safety, and manipulation/recovery remains the dominant fail-
9


ure category. Human-present training helps in simulation and in real-world Contribution, while the
negative Interruption result identifies an unresolved challenge.
6
Limitations
Scripted avatar events enable repeatable evaluation but do not capture continuous human-robot adap-
tation or the full variability of human intent, motion, and communication. The suite emphasizes
tabletop manipulation, with limited interaction diversity and no systematic progression of human-
interaction difficulty. It includes articulated-object interactions but does not yet cover mobile ma-
nipulation or long-horizon collaboration; the present focus is to isolate the additional challenges
introduced by active humans. Sensing and contact dynamics are simplified. Success and geomet-
ric safety metrics do not directly measure appropriate waiting, rerouting, recognition of human-
completed subtasks, or social comfort. Simulated safety is not evidence of safe deployment. Broader
tasks, adaptive human behavior, behavior-specific metrics, and larger real-world studies remain fu-
ture work.

Object asset pool. Fig. A1 shows the 147 object asset directories in the HARP-Bench asset pool.
These assets include rigid tabletop objects, articulated fixtures, containers, food items, tools, appli-
ances, blocks, and table variants. Each asset can be loaded by the task code as a visual and physical

initions specify the functional role of an object, including the target object for the robot, the target
object for the human, and the container to place into. Depending on the task configuration, a rollout
can use a fixed task object or sample from a task-specific subset of the pool.

by the way human activity changes the robot’s manipulation problem: Cueing changes what the
robot should infer or do, Contribution changes which subtasks remain for the robot, Interruption
changes object availability or timing, and Co-presence adds a nearby human without changing the
original robot goal. The 37 tasks consist of 7 Cueing tasks, 10 Contribution tasks, 10 Interruption
tasks, and 10 Co-presence tasks.

Asset usage across tasks.
The 37 tasks use a shared object pool constructed from assets in
SAPIEN [50, 51, 52], RoboTwin 2.0 [9], and Sketchfab [53]. Across different tasks, the robot
and human avatar interact with objects sampled from the same pool, ensuring that human-present
variants are built on a consistent set of physical assets. Since imported assets can have scale mis-
matches when loaded into Genesis, we manually resized each object to maintain reasonable physical
dimensions and task-specific usability.

C.2
Avatar and Motion Assets
HARP-Bench uses animated avatars to generate the human activity in its human-present task vari-
ants. In the implementation, avatar appearance, avatar motion, and task-level event logic are handled
separately. The avatar model determines how the human looks, the motion or control primitive de-
termines how the avatar moves, and the task code specifies when the event occurs and how it affects
the scene. This separation makes it possible to reuse the same tabletop task family across different
human-present conditions while keeping the robot-side goal comparable.
Avatar appearance pool.
Fig. A2 shows the 46 avatar appearances used in HARP-Bench. These
avatar models are inherited from Virtual Community [43]. We adapt the original avatar simulation
pipeline to work with Genesis v1.2.0 [54, 55], and integrate it into our benchmark in a lightweight
form, so that each task can instantiate human motion and appearance without adding unnecessary
simulation overhead.

Motion and control primitives.
Avatar behavior is built from a motion library collected from
AMASS [44] and Mixamo [45]. We manually trim the downloaded motions into task-relevant clips
and annotate keyframes for object interaction. At these annotated frames, the target object can
be attached to or detached from the avatar hand, enabling motions such as holding, handing over,
placing, tossing, or manipulating objects. In addition to replaying stored clips, we also use IK-based
adjustment on top of the motion library, which allows the avatar to generate flexible pick-and-place
motions conditioned on the object pose in each task instance.
Task-level event execution.
The triggering logic of avatar motion depends on the task condition.
In Cueing tasks, the avatar typically performs an initial motion that indicates the intended robot
action. In Contribution tasks, the avatar starts at an early randomized time and completes part of the
task before or during robot execution. In Interruption tasks, the avatar motion is triggered based on
the robot gripper position, so that the avatar randomly interferes when the robot is about to execute
a relevant substep. In Co-presence tasks, the avatar performs a randomly selected task-irrelevant
motion inside the shared workspace, creating human presence without changing the robot’s original
goal. Fig. A3 summarizes the motion and control primitives used by the registered benchmark tasks.
