Abstract
We address geometric video alignment for the same scene captured by multiple
freely moving cameras. Existing methods, which are based on image stitching
or video stabilization, are typically limited to two cameras or must assume the
restrictive setting of a fixed linear ordering of the cameras. We propose MViS,
a lightweight test-time optimization framework for joint multi-view alignment
with temporal consistency. MViS uses a spatial-transformer-inspired net to jointly
process frames from all views within a short temporal window and predict per-view,
per-time homographies. Optimization proceeds in two stages: geometry-guided
alignment followed by appearance-based refinement, with both stages enforcing
multi-view and temporal consistency across the sequence. The method requires
no scene-specific hyperparameter tuning, handles arbitrary and time-varying view-
intersection graphs, and remains robust under rapid camera motion. On a standard
two-view video benchmark, MViS matches or surpasses the state of the art. To
facilitate evaluation in more complex scenarios, we introduce an annotated dataset
with more than two freely moving cameras and establish the first benchmark for this
setting. Project page: https://bgu-cs-vil.github.io/MViS-project/.

Introduction
Geometric alignment of multiple videos of the same scene captured by freely moving cameras is a
challenging and underexplored task arising in multi-user handheld capture, drone footage, or mobile
multi-camera devices. The goal is to estimate, for every frame in every view, transformations that
bring all videos into a spatially aligned and temporally coherent configuration.
Applying Multiple Image Stitching (MIS) independently at each time yields spatially coherent but
temporally inconsistent panoramas. The resulting video exhibits spatially dependent shaking that
standard stabilization cannot correct, since different regions require conflicting temporal corrections
that themselves may vary from frame to frame. This calls for a video-specific formulation that
enforces spatial and temporal consistency jointly. Although pairwise alignment of moving-camera
videos has been studied, extending it beyond two cameras is problematic: without global consistency,
errors compound across views and time. Moreover, camera ordering and view intersections may vary
over time, violating assumptions of fixed layouts or persistent overlaps.
Video stitching has long been treated as a hybrid of image stitching and video stabilization, either
handling the two tasks sequentially in constrained setups or addressing them jointly. Most moving-
camera methods still follow these pipelines and are limited to two cameras.
A prime example is StabStitch [1], which handles two moving cameras, and StabStitch++ [2], which
extends to three cameras but only under a fixed linear spatial ordering. No existing method supports
more than two freely moving cameras, where the view-intersection graph (defined by pairwise field-
of-view overlaps) is arbitrary or time-varying. More broadly, existing approaches operate pairwise
and lack a principled mechanism for global consistency across views and time.

We propose Multiple Video Stitching (MViS), an unsupervised optimization method that aligns videos
from multiple freely moving cameras (see Fig. 1) without requiring fixed or known camera ordering.
It only assumes that the view-intersection graph is connected at each time. MViS uses a lightweight
alignment network (inspired by, but different from, the Spatial Transformer Network (STN) [3])
that takes a local multi-view temporal window as input and jointly predicts the corresponding per-
frame transformations. These predictions are optimized using losses that enforce multi-view and
temporal consistency through relative compositions of transformations (i.e., composing one per-frame
transformation with the inverse of another) for pairs of frames, either across views at the same time
or across consecutive times in the same view. This yields a scalable optimization that enforces global
consistency over cameras and time, rather than independent pairwise alignments.
The optimization proceeds in two stages: geometry-guided alignment based on noisy pairwise
transformations, followed by appearance-based refinement. The resulting regularization-free losses
enable stable stitching under fast motion and time-varying view-intersection graphs without per-scene
hyperparameter tuning. Operating on short time windows allows MViS to scale to hundreds or
thousands of frames while adapting to diverse capture conditions.
Our key contributions are:
1) A method for stitching videos from multiple freely moving cameras, the first in this setting to
handle more than two cameras with arbitrary, time-varying view-intersection graphs.
2) A scalable unsupervised formulation that jointly aligns all views over time, while avoiding
per-scene hyperparameter tuning through regularization-free losses.
3) A new annotated multi-view video dataset and benchmark for video stitching under unconstrained
multi-camera motion.
4) A variance-based geometric metric for assessing alignment quality in multi-view video stitching.

Related Work
Multiple Image Stitching (MIS). Classical MIS methods often follow an incremental pairwise
strategy, stitching one image at a time in a predefined order, which makes them prone to error
accumulation and drift. A popular alternative, AutoStitch [4], addresses this by jointly optimizing
the transformations of all images. Yet, despite many MIS methods (e.g., [5, 6, 7, 8]), most classical
and learning-based methods still handle only two input images. Methods supporting more than two
images often rely on predefined sequences or spatial layout assumptions, such as known left-right
ordering or a fixed reference image [9, 10]. In multiple video stitching from freely moving cameras,
MIS methods produce good panoramas at individual time steps but yield temporally incoherent results
when applied frame by frame. This failure, along with the difficulty of post-hoc video stabilization,
stems from the fact that, due to independent camera motions, different panoramic regions usually
move differently between times t and t + 1, which per-frame stitching cannot capture.
2


Video Stitching. Early works handled the task sequentially, first stitching and then stabilizing
videos [11], but only in restricted setups with stationary cameras or fixed rigs. Lin et al. [12] instead
stabilized videos before stitching, but showed results only for two or three cameras (and, to date,
have not released code). Several methods [13, 14, 15] incorporate video stabilization techniques
such as bundled camera paths [16]. Among these, Guo et al. [13] first jointly optimized stitching
and stabilization for moving cameras. Although they suggested extending the method beyond two
cameras, it relied on camera-by-camera optimization with an alternating bundled-path scheme,
resulting in high computational cost and limited scalability. Subsequent works (e.g., [14]) noted
that such optimization is prohibitively hard. Moreover, the authors of [13] released no code, and
the multi-camera extension was not demonstrated experimentally. In contrast, MViS is simple and
scalable: each stage optimizes its loss over all views and sliding windows with standard solvers
(e.g., ADAM), without alternating procedures or hand-crafted schedules. Perazzi et al. [17] stitch
videos from linearly arranged wide-baseline cameras, seemingly using calibrated camera parameters.
Similarly, [18] stitch videos from fixed camera rigs. The restrictive assumptions of both methods
make them incompatible with unconstrained setups of multiple freely moving cameras, as also noted
in [1].
The methods above improve short-term coherence, but remain limited to two-camera setups, fixed
arrays, or stationary capture, and are inapplicable to more general, practical settings. Other methods
target constrained capture setups. Lai et al. [19] propose a learning-based approach for linear camera
arrays, training a pushbroom-style interpolation network that learns dense flow between adjacent
views. Lo et al. [20] focus on dual-fisheye 360° cameras with geometric calibration and seam
optimization. Both assume rigid, synchronized hardware and do not extend to freely moving cameras.
The state-of-the-art (SOTA) video stitching method, StabStitch [1], is the most relevant to our work.
It stitches two videos and jointly performs stabilization in an unsupervised, online framework based
on UDIS++ [21]. Its slower extension, StabStitch++ [2], improves alignment and stabilization via a
differentiable bidirectional decomposition and a warp-smoothing model. StabStitch++ can handle
three cameras, but only with a fixed linear ordering, and does not generalize to unknown, dynamic
topology or beyond three videos. In contrast, MViS handles arbitrary numbers of cameras and flexible
view-intersection graphs.
Image Alignment. Several methods [22, 23, 24] jointly align tens of still images using high-
dimensional DINO features [25]. They act on still images with masked objects and do not scale
to videos: even [24], the most efficient, runs out of memory when the number of frames exceeds
70. While MViS shares with [24] the idea of comparing images via relative transformations, its
formulation, architecture, and losses differ: MViS acts on videos and processes multi-view temporal
windows rather than single images. Moreover, [24] is quadratic in the number of images, while MViS
is linear in the number of frames and quadratic only in the (typically small) number of cameras.
Keypoint matchers such as RoMa [26] or LoFTR [27] operate on image pairs and do not account
for temporal or multi-view consistency. MViS uses such matchers solely to generate noisy observed
relative transformations that guide the geometric stage.

3.5
Regularization-free Losses and Global Invariance
The losses from § 3.3 and § 3.4 are defined via compositions of predicted absolute transformations;
e.g., for views i and j, a loss term depends on the relative transformation H−1
j
Hi. In the geometric
stage, this transformation is applied to corresponding points; in the appearance stage, to image
content. A central consequence is that no explicit regularization over the predicted transformations
is required. Each transformation must explain observed data, either geometric correspondences or
image appearance. Degenerate transformations (e.g., excessive shrinking or severe distortion) would
increase inter-view misalignment and hence the loss. Thus, the loss cannot be trivially reduced
through collapse or uncontrolled growth of the transformations. Consequently, regularization terms
(e.g., on transformation size or lack of smoothness) are unneeded, freeing us from scene-specific
6


hyperparameter tuning and the optimization complexity such regularization entails (see also § C.2).
Note that regularization-free losses and their associated benefits have also been demonstrated in
several of our earlier joint-alignment works in various other settings, including [33, 24, 30, 34].
A second property is that no explicit atlas or canonical panorama is needed: all comparisons are made
directly between views rather than to a learned template. This is important in multi-camera video,
where the scene evolves over time and maintaining a stable global reference is nontrivial. Crucially,
although the loss terms are defined over pairs of views, MViS jointly predicts the transformations of
all views in the sliding window, yielding a globally consistent alignment rather than a collection of
independent pairwise alignments.
Lastly, the losses are invariant to a common global transformation: if all predicted transformations are
left-composed with some transformation G, then their relative transformations are unchanged, since
(GHj)−1(GHi) = H−1
j
Hi. Thus, the minimizers are defined only up to a global transformation
and form an equivalence class under such compositions. While this implies infinitely many solutions
in terms of transformations (a phenomenon shared with [24, 30] and works on synchronization
over Lie groups, e.g., [35, 29], or SfM), all members of a given equivalence class induce the same
inter-frame correspondences. In practice, for visualization, we apply to all predicted transformations
the inverse of the predicted transformation of an arbitrarily-chosen frame (e.g., the first frame of
the first video). Since this choice is post-hoc, it has no effect on alignment quality. Finally, while
relative-transformation losses were recently used in [24, 30], their roots go back to classical methods
such as [36] and even [37].

After optimization, we create a panoramic video by applying the predicted Ht
i to the frames F t
i , while
accounting for the resizing. Since the solution is defined up to a global transformation, visualization
requires choosing a reference. We either apply, at each t, the inverse of Ht
1 to all frames at that time,
or apply the inverse of H1
1 to all frames across all times and views. This postprocessing is purely for
visualization: MViS gives no special weight to the first video or to its first frame.
3.7
Temporal Stabilization
MViS implicitly obtains temporal stabilization through three mechanisms: 1) overlapping windows
(St contains times t, t+1, while St+1 contains t + 1, t + 2, etc.); 2) the alignment net’s inductive
smoothness bias, whereby similar images yield similar predicted transformations; and 3) the temporal
alignment terms in the losses.
4
Multiview Moving Camera Dataset (MMCD)
To our knowledge, no available dataset supports multiple video stitching with N > 2 freely moving
cameras. Thus, we created MMCD, a new multiview dataset. MMCD contains 53 scenes, each with
3–6 approximately synchronized handheld videos captured on diverse mobile devices under varied
environmental and lighting conditions. Camera models, resolutions, and motions vary substantially,
providing a broad evaluation of MViS. Overall, MMCD contains 221 videos and 47,040 frames. It is
divided into 4 categories based on N, the number of cameras: 22 scenes with N = 3, 10 with N = 4,
11 with N = 5, and 10 with N = 6. Same-scene videos have the same length, with recordings
between 5 and 10 seconds. For video-length statistics across different values of N and overlap
statistics, see § E.4. To facilitate quantitative evaluation, we manually annotated keypoints in the
overlap regions of the first frames and propagated them through the sequences using CoTracker3 [38].
The annotations, used solely for evaluation, are available with the dataset.

Experiments and Results
In all experiments, we optimize MViS using ADAM with a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler and
learning rates of 5×10−4 (Stage 1) and 5×10−5 (Stage 2). Batch size is 6 for N = 2 and 4 for
N > 2, and each stage is optimized for 600 epochs on an RTX 6000 GPU. We use RoMa [26] as
the image matcher; MViS is not tied to this choice, and in § C.3 we report results using LoFTR [27].

since different cameras, and hence different panoramic regions, can have different and possibly
time-dependent motions.
3. Comparison with Video Stitching (VS): Among existing video stitching methods, StabStitch++
is the only one supporting more than two inputs, but it is limited to N = 3 and requires a fixed spatial
ordering of the cameras, as noted in its GitHub repository: “video1 should overlap with video2, and
video2 should overlap with video3”. This assumes that the cameras’ relative ordering remains fixed,
whereas in freely moving multi-camera capture the view-intersection graph can change over time.
Thus, StabStitch++ is not fully applicable to three moving cameras; in practice, when cameras swap
order, its output degrades. MViS outperforms StabStitch++ in both alignment and stability metrics.
For N > 3, no existing video stitching method is applicable, so we report results only for MViS.

Ablation Study and Direct Optimization without a Net
Table 3 summarizes an Ablation Study on StabStitch-D (N = 2) and MMCD (N > 2). Removing
Stage 1 causes frequent failures, confirming its importance. Removing Stage 2 degrades alignment,
especially when inter-view overlap is small. Optimizing the Stage 1 and Stage 2 losses simultaneously
usually harms performance, highlighting the need for our two-stage approach. Using unnormalized
RGB also hurts performance. As expected, the temporal loss terms mainly affect video stabilization.
To validate the role of the network, we also directly optimize the proposed losses over the homo-
graphies, without a neural network. We test three variants: geometric loss only, appearance loss
only, and geometric-then-appearance sequential optimization. The resulting videos are spatially
aligned but exhibit severe temporal jitter. Adding regularization over homographies could reduce
this jitter, but would require per-scene hyperparameter tuning and, more problematically, supervision
for selecting appropriate values. In contrast, MViS obtains temporal smoothness implicitly, partly
through the inductive smoothness bias of the network (see § 3.7): consecutive frames tend to be
similar, and so are the predictions. Since Stability and GV are computed only in overlap regions,
where temporal instability is less visible, their numerical differences remain small despite the clear
qualitative difference; CMSA captures it well. See the Appendix for visual and quantitative results.

Conclusion
We presented MViS, a method for stitching videos from multiple freely moving cameras. MViS
combines a regularization-free joint-alignment formulation with a two-stage optimization over
geometric and appearance consistency, enabling accurate stitching across arbitrary numbers of views
and flexible view-intersection graphs. Experiments show clear advantages over existing methods,
particularly for N > 2, where prior video-stitching methods are either inapplicable or require
restrictive fixed-ordering assumptions.
Limitations. 1) Runtime: optimization time would not suffice for real-time applications. 2) Although
the relative-composition framework can support richer invertible transformations, such as nonrigid
diffeomorphisms, our current implementation uses homographies, a standard and cost-effective
choice that already performs well in our experiments. 3) When the image matcher fails, e.g., due to a
dominant textureless region, the ORTs may be unreliable and performance can temporarily degrade,
although MViS typically recovers once reliable matches reappear later in the video.
We are not aware of any negative societal impacts associated with this work.

with an equivalence class of invertible matrices, where all of the class members are proportional to

SL(3) is the matrix Lie group of real-valued 3-by-3 matrices with determinant 1, while sl(3) is

SL(3) can be identified with the group of homographies in R2 and the matrix exponential maps
sl(3) →SL(3) via Θ 7→exp(Θ). Thus, we set our alignment network to predict zero-trace matrices
by predicting 8-length vectors of the form θ = (θ1, . . . , θ8), where the obvious bijection between
the vector θ and the zero-trace 3-by-3 matrix Θ is as in Eq. 9. Then, via the matrix exponential, we
obtain homographies. In a slight abuse of notation, we will write exp(θ) instead of exp(Θ). Also
useful are the following two facts: 1) a zero-vector θ = 08×1 corresponds to the identity matrix (i.e.,
H = I3×3), and 2) the inverse matrix of exp(θ) is, conveniently, exp(−θ). Finally, for two different
8-length vectors, θi and θj, in general exp(θi) exp(θj)̸ = exp(θj) exp(θi) (i.e., the homography
group is not Abelian); this detail means that the order in which composition of the transformation is
done (e.g., in our loss functions) is important.
Remark: Recall that in general any invertible matrix defines a homography, and that if that matrix
is multiplied by a nonzero scalar it still defines the same homography. In other words, in general
a homography corresponds to an equivalence class of invertible matrices (that are proportional to
each other). However, when using the SL parameterization above, this becomes a nonissue since,
by construction, the det = 1 constraint essentially boils down to picking, for each such equivalence
class, exactly one representative: the one whose determinant is 1.

C.5
Robustness to ORT Corruption
We perturb 10%, 20%, or 30% of the temporal and multi-view ORTs using projective perturbation
matrices constructed from eight Gaussian parameters (σ = 0.01 for affine/translation components
and 0.0015 for projective components), following Appendix B, Eq. (9).
Because each mask is derived by warping an all-ones mask with its estimated ORT, perturbing an
ORT also perturbs the corresponding mask. Independently perturbing the masks would decouple
them from the ORTs and would not reflect the pipeline’s actual failure mode.

The Alignment Network Architecture
Following the parameterization described in Appendix B, the alignment network predicts an 8-
dimensional Lie-algebra vector for each camera and time step. We initialize all weights in the final
fully connected layer (FC2) to zero, so that the initial predicted transformations are the identity.
The number of parameters in the CNN module is independent of the number of cameras N: all
BatchNorm and Conv2d layers operate on 2BN input frames and contain only a few thousand
parameters. The dependence on N appears only in the fully connected module, whose parameter
count grows linearly with N (10,240N+32 for FC1 and 264N for FC2).

E.2
The Multi-Camera Benchmark Evaluation Protocol
For MIS methods, we run each method independently on every frame. When a method yields
panoramas in a drifting coordinate system, we post-align the per-frame outputs to a fixed reference to
correct this drift.
If a method fails to produce a panorama for any frame in a scene, we mark the entire scene as
collapsed. Quantitative metrics are averaged over non-collapsed scenes only, and we additionally
report the number of successfully processed scenes. Full per-scene, per-metric, and per-method
results are provided in Table 12- 17.
GSP, GES-GSP, and OBJ-GSP require an adjacency graph over overlapping image pairs and the
selection of a reference image. We construct this graph independently at each time step t, and, for
improved temporal stability, we consistently choose the first camera as the reference image.
In addition, for these methods, we anchor the resulting panoramas to a shared coordinate system by
estimating a similarity transformation that maps the optimized vertices of the reference mesh to its
initial vertices, and then applying this same transformation to all mesh vertices of all views at time t.
This is conceptually similar to the panorama construction used in MViS.

E.3
Implementation details of the Extended MIS Variants
For evaluation in the video setting, we extended GSP, GES-GSP, and OBJ-GSP in two ways, as their
original formulations optimize each frame independently.
Temporal Regularization (TR). We augment the original optimization with a temporal smoothness
term that regularizes each mesh vertex in the current frame toward its optimized position in the
previous frame, thereby reducing frame-to-frame jitter. Concretely, these temporal constraints are
appended as additional equations to the sparse linear system, using the optimized vertices from the
previous frame as references.
Post-hoc Smoothing (PS). We also consider a post-processing variant in which the original method
is first applied independently to each frame, producing a per-frame set of optimized mesh vertex
positions. We then treat each vertex coordinate as a 1D signal over time and apply a Gaussian filter
along the temporal axis. Finally, all frames are re-rendered using the smoothed vertex trajectories,
yielding a temporally smoother panoramic video.
We experiment with σ ∈{0.5, 1.0, 1.5}, where σ is the standard deviation of the Gaussian kernel
in PS and w ∈{0.5, 1.0, 1.5} is the weight of the temporal regularization term in TR. The results
are summarized in Table 10. The main paper reports results for the setting w = 1.0 and σ = 1.0.
Across all evaluated hyperparameter settings of the MIS extensions, MViS attains the best Stab. and
CMSA scores. A visual comparison between OBJ-GSP, OBJ-GSP+TR, OBJ-GSP+PS, and MViS is
provided in 7_mis_variants_vs_mvis.mp4.
