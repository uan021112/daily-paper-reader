Abstract — While imitation learning has made substantial progress toward generalizable robot manipulation through scal-ing, viewpoint robustness remains a major bottleneck. Visuomo-tor policies rely heavily on visual inputs, yet images provide an inherently ambiguous signal under viewpoint change: similar scenes may appear markedly different across viewpoints, while distinct states may share certain visual overlaps. A policy must therefore infer not only the task-relevant state, but also how it is visually projected under a particular camera configuration. This entanglement of viewpoint-dependent appearance and task-relevant state can make learning inefficient and limit generalization to unseen viewpoints. Recent work has begun to address this challenge through camera-ray conditioning and representation learning using geometric cues to induce invari-ance. However, providing camera information as additional context does not by itself ground visual representations in how the task scene is visually projected, while geometric alignment without camera context can introduce spurious correspondences by ignoring viewpoint-dependent projection. We propose PAIR, a contrastive learning framework that aligns joint image-ray representations using robot proprioception. PAIR encourages observations with similar robot states to remain close in representation space across viewpoints, providing a structured grounding signal for learning viewpoint-robust features. PAIR is a highly adaptable framework: it can be used to train Diffusion Policy (DP) or post-train vision-language-action (VLA) models. In simulated and real-world manipulation benchmarks with camera variation, PAIR consistently outperforms the behavior cloning baseline without ray conditioning by 12.7 % and 26.7 % points, respectively, demonstrating stronger generalization to unseen viewpoints. Robot imitation learning has made significant progress to-ward general-purpose manipulation by scaling training data, model capacity, and compute [1]. Recent visuomotor policies can learn increasingly more general manipulation behaviors directly from image observations, and can transfer across

This challenge stems from a fundamental property of vi-sual observations: images do not directly encode the physical state of the world, but instead capture a viewpoint-dependent projection of that state. As the camera viewpoint changes, a fixed underlying task state can give rise to substantially different visual observations through changes in perspective and visibility. Conversely, distinct physical states may appear deceptively similar when they share similar camera-relative geometry or when the task-relevant context is only partially captured within the camera’s field of view (See Fig. 1.A). This ambiguity is further exacerbated by typical camera-independent action spaces, in which visual observations vary with camera viewpoint while the corresponding action targets remain unchanged. Consequently, to infer which actions are appropriate for the given task, a visuomotor policy must not only capture the task-relevant information in the image, but also reason about how exactly they are visually projected. Consider a robot approaching a mug for grasping. From one view, the gripper may appear to the left of the mug, while from another view, the same physical configuration may make the gripper appear to the right of the mug. Yet this change in image-space geometry does not imply a corresponding change in what the robot should physically do. The policy must therefore determine how the observed visual relationship between the robot and the mug corresponds to their underlying spatial relationship. This implicitly demands that the policy look for additional visual cues such as the robot base to establish the robot’s spatial frame or fuse the vision features with the internal robot state for viewpoint-invariant action prediction. However, such visual context is not always guaranteed to be available in full, nor reliably utilized when learning from demonstrations. Recent studies have explored improving viewpoint invari-ance for visuomotor policies through camera-ray condition-ing [5] to use the camera state governing the visual projection as input, or use geometric signals such as proprioception to construct positive pairs for representation alignment [6, 7]. However, while camera-ray conditioning provides useful viewpoint context, it does not by itself ground visual rep-resentations in the projected task state. Similarly, while alignment using physically grounded signals can encourage invariance across physically similar states, this can produce conflicting supervision by ignoring viewpoint-dependent pro-jection without camera context. In light of this, we propose PAIR, a contrastive learning framework for viewpoint-robust manipulation that jointly learns image–ray representations using Pl¨ ucker embeddings with supervised proprioceptive alignment. PAIR is a highly adaptable framework: it can be used to train lightweight policy classes such as diffusion policy (DP) or fine-tune pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. We make the following contributions. First, we introduce PAIR, a general training framework that unlocks the benefit of proprioceptive alignment through joint image–ray repre-sentation learning. Second, we show that PAIR consistently improves the performance of both diffusion policies and VLA models when trained on the same demonstration data. Third, we conduct extensive evaluations across multiple simulated and real-world benchmarks under systematic view-point variations, demonstrating strong robustness to changes in camera viewpoint both quantitatively and qualitatively. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Vision Representations for Robotic Manipulation 

A dominant paradigm in visual representation learning is vision-language alignment, which has enabled VLA models with strong semantic understanding and instruction-following capabilities. Accordingly, many VLM and VLA architectures adopt pre-trained vision encoders such as CLIP [8] and SigLIP [9], whose representations are explicitly aligned with language semantics through large-scale image-text pre-training. While this semantic alignment provides strong high-level visual understanding, such representations often lack the fine-grained spatial and geometric awareness required for robotic manipulation [1]. Several methods study viewpoint robustness directly from RGB observations with data augmentation techniques includ-ing systematic data collection [10] or novel-view synthe-sis [11], while other methods improve the learning signal through cross-view supervision [12] and contrastive learn-ing [6, 7]. Recent efforts attempt to directly use camera extrinsic information to account for viewpoint changes. For example, Zhang et al. [13] express robot actions in the camera frame to unify action representations across het-erogeneous robot datasets, while Jiang et al. [5] condition the policy on Pl¨ ucker ray maps derived from the camera pose. Rather than using camera geometry solely as an input signal or as a reference frame for expressing actions, PAIR grounds the image-ray representation in the underlying robot proprioceptive state using contrastive learning. 

B. Rays as Positional Embeddings 

Recent vision models increasingly incorporate camera rays as an explicit geometric signal for vision tasks including video generation [14], novel-view synthesis [15], and pose estimation [16]. Similar lines of work have also explored ray injection as positional encoding. For example, RayPE [17] incorporates Pl¨ ucker ray geometry directly into Transformer attention for video generation. In robotics, Xu et al. [18] builds on RayPE for modality-balanced action generation in VLAs, while RayViT [19] injects patch-aligned Pl¨ ucker ray features into a pre-trained DINOv3 ViT backbone as geometry-aware positional embeddings. PAIR similarly in-troduces Pl¨ ucker rays as positional embeddings, but applies this during post-training of SigLIP/SigLIP2 [9, 20] encoders to ground the camera-ray-aware visual representations using robot proprioception. A. Problem Statement 

We consider visuomotor policy learning from visual ob-servations o, robot proprioceptive state s, camera rays r, and a task specification g (e.g., language), where we augment imitation learning with a spatially grounded visual represen-tation learning problem. Specifically, a vision encoder fϕ

maps the visual observation and optionally its associated camera geometry to a latent representation z = fϕ(o, r ),where the ray information r provides a geometry-aware positional encoding for the visual tokens. We assume that camera positions change across episodes, but remained fixed within each episode. Our goal is to learn fϕ such that is grounded in the underlying robot state s. In particular, observations of the same physical robot configuration from different viewpoints should induce consistent latent representations. The resulting representation is then used by a visuomotor policy πθ (a1 |

z, s, g ), which predicts a sequence of future robot actions over an action horizon Hp.

In this section, we test the effectiveness of PAIR for view-point generalization of visuomotor policies under various settings. To understand whether the benefits arise specifically from proprioceptive alignment, we compare against temporal alignment [23], which defines correspondence based on the temporal distance between observations. We then examine how these alignment signals interact with camera-ray condi-tioning and affect downstream policy learning. Specifically, we address the following research questions: 

RQ1 Does PAIR improve visual robustness across robotic embodiments (single-arm vs bi-arm), learning settings (single-task vs multi-task), and policy classes (Diffusion Policy vs VLA)? 

RQ2 Is proprioception a better alignment signal than time when learning from expert demonstrations? 

RQ3 Is ray conditioning necessary to unlock the benefits of proprioceptive alignment for visual representations? 

A. Benchmarks 

We evaluate PAIR on three simulated robot manipula-tion benchmarks spanning different embodiments, tasks, and learning settings. 

a) MimicGen : [24] is a benchmark for imitation learning with synthetically augmented demonstrations. We use MimicGen as a standard single-task learning benchmark to study viewpoint robustness. We include the Square, Three Stack, and Three Piece Assembly tasks using the D1 distri-butions. Each task includes 1,000 demonstrations. 

b) AlohaGym : [25] provides a canonical bimanual manipulation task on the ALOHA platform. We evaluate PAIR on the cube transfer task, where the dataset consists of 50 human and 50 motion-planned demonstrations, totaling 100 episodes. 

c) LIBERO : [26] is a manipulation benchmark com-prising four task suites: Object, Goal, Spatial, and Long, which emphasize object variation, spatial reasoning, goal-conditioned behavior, and long-horizon task execution, re-spectively. LIBERO evaluation uses language conditioning to distinguish among tasks. Policies are trained on the full LIBERO dataset spanning 6,500 episodes in 130 tasks. For all three benchmarks, we re-collect the original demonstrations under diverse camera viewpoints by varying the camera extrinsics while keeping the underlying task definitions unchanged. This procedure introduces controlled variation in the visual observations for a direct evaluation of visual robustness. For single-task settings, we define the camera look-at point as the midpoint between the target objects and the robot end-effector, and sample camera dis-tances for which the end-effector falls within the camera’s field of view. For LIBERO, we pre-sample 50 camera poses for each task, all distinct from those used during data collection, and apply the same sampling criterion to ensure that the robot end-effector remains visible throughout each rollout. In addition, we evaluate both single-view and multi-view scenarios, where multiple viewpoints are provided for each training demonstration in the multi-view setting. A comparison of the camera settings is provided in Table II, and the distribution of camera poses is shown in Fig. 3.

C. Training Objectives and View Settings 

All methods share the same backbone, optimizer, and schedule, and differ only in the auxiliary objective added to the BC loss. The policy always consumes a single camera view; single-view and multi-view settings differ only in how the remaining views are used during training. Critically, proprioception features used to align the vision features in PAIR is provided as extra input modality for all cases. 

a) BC : Standard behavior cloning without auxiliary loss. In the multi-view setting, the input view is randomly pooled from the candidate views. 

b) TCN : The TCN [23] objective as the auxiliary loss. We follow the original implementation for both the single-view and multi-view settings. 

c) PAIR : Proprioceptive alignment loss in Eq. (12) as the auxiliary loss. In the multi-view setting, the target view is sampled from the set of candidate camera views to provide cross-view learning signals.

E. Simulation Results 

Tables III and IV summarize the performance of PAIR across single-task and multi-task learning settings, respec-tively. In single-task learning, PAIR with ray conditioning improves the maximum success rate on average by 16.2%, 9.1%, and 9.5% points over BC without ray conditioning, ray-conditioned BC, and PAIR without ray conditioning, re-spectively. On LIBERO, PAIR with ray conditioning achieves the highest average success rate of 88.9%, outperforming ray-conditioned BC by 5.6% points. Compared with TCN, PAIR further improves the maximum success rate by 4.9% and 3.3% points with and without ray conditioning, respec-tively. 

F. Real-World Experiments 

We design three real-world tasks using a Franka Research 3 robot and two RealSense D435 cameras capturing images at 30 FPS, as illustrated in Fig. 3. For each of the three tasks, 100 demonstrations are collected using VR-controlled teleoperation. For each rollout, cameras are re-positioned, and their poses are estimated using AprilTags [21]. Similar to LIBERO, we fine-tune the pre-trained SmolVLA on all three tasks jointly with language conditioning. We compare BC and PAIR with and without ray conditioning, using 30 rollouts per task in each setting. To ensure that evaluation is conducted from genuinely unseen viewpoints, after each camera calibration, we resample a camera pose if both its camera position and ground-plane look-at point are within 1 cm of those of any viewpoint sampled during data collection. As shown in Fig. 4, PAIR with ray conditioning achieves the highest success rates across all three tasks, averaging 76.7% and 57.8% points on subtask and final task completion, respectively. This improves over BC without ray conditioning by 23.3% and 26.7% points, ray-conditioned BC by 21.1% and 25.6% points, and PAIR without ray conditioning by 13.3% and 18.9% points, respectively.

Notably, these gains hold across different robotic embodiments, single- and multi-task learning settings, and both Diffusion Policy and VLA policy classes, indicating that the benefits of PAIR generalize across diverse robotic learning settings. In the multi-view learning setting, PAIR improves upon its single-view counterpart by leveraging observations from multiple viewpoints. The improvement reflects PAIR’s abil-ity to exploit cross-view signals from observations of the same task scene for proprioceptive alignment. By effectively extracting these shared cross-view signals, PAIR shows the potential to scale to more diverse viewpoint settings and support cross-view representation learning. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we presented PAIR, a framework for align-ing image-ray representations with robot proprioception to improve viewpoint robustness. While PAIR significantly im-proves generalization to unseen camera poses, there are sev-eral limitations. First, existing robotic datasets often contain demonstrations collected from a limited or skewed distribu-tion of camera viewpoints, and the effectiveness of PAIR on such datasets may depend on the degree of viewpoint diversity present in the data. Second, collecting data under systematically diverse viewpoints with accurate camera cal-ibration requires additional human effort and increases data-collection time. Finally, as shown in Table VII, the pro-prioceptive alignment objective incurs extra computational overhead during training. Future directions include building large-scale real-world datasets with systematically controlled viewpoint diversity, leveraging camera pose estimation for scalable image–ray pre-training on existing robotic datasets, and extending PAIR to cross-embodiment learning.
