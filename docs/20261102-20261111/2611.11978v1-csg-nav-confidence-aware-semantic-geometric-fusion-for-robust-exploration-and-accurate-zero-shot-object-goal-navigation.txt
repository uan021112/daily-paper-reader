Abstract 

Zero-shot object-goal navigation requires autonomous agents to explore un-seen environments and locate target objects without task-specific training, posing challenges in perceptual reliability under observation uncertainty and physically grounded goal termination. Although foundation-model-based ap-proaches improve generalization, they remain vulnerable to unreliable long-range perception, hallucination-induced directional bias, and semantically plausible but geometrically incorrect stopping decisions. To address these limitations, we propose CSG-Nav, a zero-shot navigation framework cen-tered on confidence-aware multimodal information fusion. CSG-Nav inte-grates three complementary fusion mechanisms. First, semantic-source fusion integrates open-vocabulary detection results, detection confidence, scene ob-servations, and relational reasoning to produce more reliable direction-level semantic scores and suppress hallucination-induced directional bias. Sec-ond, spatial-confidence fusion weights multi-view semantic evidence using a Gaussian decay function, suppressing unreliable distant observations while accumulating spatially consistent exploration preferences. Third, semantic-geometric decision fusion combines semantic target detection with depth-based 3D localization to support physically grounded stopping decisions. Experiments on HM3Dv1, HM3Dv2, and MP3D demonstrate competitive

1. Introduction 

Object-goal navigation is a fundamental capability for autonomous robots operating in human-centered environments, where an agent must explore an unseen environment and locate a target object specified by a natural-language command, such as “find a sofa”. Training-based approaches have achieved significant progress in this task [1], but their reliance on extensive task-specific training limits scalability and generalization in open-world envi-ronments. Zero-shot object navigation has therefore emerged as a promising paradigm, requiring agents to navigate without task-specific training. In practical robotic systems, however, zero-shot navigation is not merely a se-mantic recognition problem; it is also an information fusion problem. The agent must integrate heterogeneous evidence from object detectors, vision-language observations, depth sensors, spatial maps, and sequential multi-view observations to make reliable decisions under partial observability, noisy per-ception, and uncertain spatial evidence. Recent foundation-model-based methods have improved open-vocabulary generalization by using pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) and large language models (LLMs). Early methods, such as ZSON [2] and CoW [3], employ pretrained VLMs such as CLIP [4] for open-vocabulary object local-ization, while VLFM [5] projects VLM-based semantic similarity scores onto a top-down value map to guide exploration. Other approaches, including L3MVN [6] and ESC [7], leverage LLM-based commonsense reasoning to in-fer object-scene relationships and prioritize candidate search regions. More recently, WMNav [8] uses VLMs as a world-model component to predict future observations and reduce ineffective exploration. Despite these ad-vances, many existing methods still propagate semantic outputs directly into planning without sufficiently modeling how reliable, spatially consistent, or physically grounded these outputs are. Object detectors provide object-level 2evidence, including categories, bounding boxes, and confidence scores. VLM observations provide complementary scene-level context. The key challenge is how to fuse these heterogeneous sources with reliability modeling, multi-view consistency, and geometric reachability for robust navigation decisions. From an information fusion perspective, three critical gaps remain insuf-ficiently addressed. First, there is a lack of context-aware information medi-ation between perception and reasoning. When semantic scores generated by VLMs are treated as direct control signals, hallucinated or weakly supported cues can propagate through the decision chain and bias navigation toward semantically plausible but unreliable directions. Second, the spatial degrada-tion of multi-view visual evidence is often ignored. According to sensor char-acteristics, distant observations usually suffer from reduced image resolution, lower object-detection confidence, and noisier depth estimates; consequently, global semantic projection without distance-aware reliability modeling can contaminate the preference map with low-confidence long-range evidence, as shown in the top panels of Fig. 1. Third, semantic target detection is of-ten treated as sufficient evidence for arrival. However, as illustrated in the bottom panels of Fig. 1, visual recognition does not guarantee geometric reachability or a valid stopping pose. This distinction is especially impor-tant for downstream manipulation and service tasks, where the robot must reach a physically meaningful position before interacting with the target ob-ject. These limitations motivate a confidence-aware fusion mechanism that can integrate semantic, spatial, and geometric evidence while suppressing unreliable observations. To address these challenges, we propose CSG-Nav, a confidence-aware semantic-geometric fusion framework for robust zero-shot object-goal navi-gation. Rather than treating foundation-model outputs as isolated decision signals, CSG-Nav performs multimodal information fusion at three levels. First, semantic-source fusion integrates open-vocabulary detection results, detection confidence, VLM observations, and structured LLM reasoning to produce more reliable semantic evidence and reduce hallucination-induced directional bias. Second, spatial-confidence fusion models the reliability of multi-view observations using a Gaussian decay function, allowing the agent to suppress unreliable distant evidence while accumulating spatially consis-tent exploration preferences. Third, semantic-geometric decision fusion com-bines semantic target detection with depth-based 3D localization to verify whether the target is physically reachable before issuing the stopping deci-sion. Through these fusion mechanisms, CSG-Nav improves both exploration 3robustness and goal-termination reliability under uncertain perception. 2. Related Work 

2.1. Zero-shot Object Goal Navigation 

Zero-shot object-goal navigation requires an agent to locate a target ob-ject in an unseen environment without task-specific training. Early methods such as ZSON [2] and CoW [3] use pretrained VLMs for open-vocabulary ob-ject localization, while VLFM [5] projects semantic similarity onto a spatial value map. Subsequent studies introduce richer semantic reasoning and ex-ploration strategies. ESC [7] uses LLM commonsense to reason about target-relevant regions. Related learning-based object-navigation studies have also investigated task decomposition. Multiple Thinking [9] separates object nav-igation into specialized meta-abilities, whereas Dual Adaptive Thinking [10] distinguishes target search from post-detection navigation. Recent methods further investigate unified goal representations, episodic memory, and adap-tive exploration, including UniGoal [11], ReMemNav [12], curiosity-driven navigation [13], and Stairway to Success [14]. Across these lines of research, existing methods have advanced semantic reasoning, goal representation, memory, exploration, and task decomposi-tion from different perspectives. However, the reliability of heterogeneous evidence across semantic perception, spatial accumulation, and stopping de-cisions has received comparatively less attention. CSG-Nav focuses on this aspect by combining complementary semantic cues, confidence-aware spatial evidence accumulation, and geometric verification within a unified navigation framework. 

2.2. Foundation Models for Navigation 

Foundation models have been widely used in embodied navigation as semantic scorers, commonsense reasoners, and world models. As semantic 5scorers, VLMs estimate the relevance between observations and navigation goals. CoW [3] selects target-related directions using VLM cues, VLMaps [15] fuses vision-language features with spatial maps for language-conditioned search, VLFM [5] builds a semantic value map from VLM scores, and TopV-Nav [16] performs reasoning over top-view spatial representations. As commonsense reasoners, LLMs provide object-scene and object-room priors for high-level planning. L3MVN [6] and ESC [7] use LLM com-monsense to infer likely target regions, while LFG [17] incorporates LLM-generated heuristics into navigation. LROGNav [18] further injects LLM-derived room-object relations into a modular map-based pipeline and uses multimodal fusion to improve search efficiency. A third line of work explores predictive world models and generative policies for embodied navigation and robot decision-making. NWM [19] uses video generation to predict future visual observations for navigation plan-ning, while Unified World Models [20] jointly models visual dynamics and robot actions through video and action diffusion. For object goal navigation, WMNav [8] employs VLM-based world modeling with curiosity memory to preserve predicted environmental states and support subsequent planning. Related generative navigation policies, such as FLUX [21], use rectified flow and static-to-dynamic learning to generate navigation actions across differ-ent robot embodiments. More broadly, recent work on agentic world model-ing [22] discusses predictive environment representations as a general mech-anism for supporting interactive agents. These predictive and generative approaches mainly improve navigation through future-state prediction or di-rect action generation. In contrast, CSG-Nav focuses on reliability-aware fusion of currently available semantic, spatial, and geometric evidence. 

2.3. Multi-Source Information Fusion in Navigation 

Multi-source information fusion is central to embodied navigation because agents must integrate visual observations, language goals, spatial represen-tations, and historical information for action selection. Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) provides a representative example of this problem. A re-cent survey [23] organizes VLN methods according to their action and model paradigms and emphasizes the joint use of visual observations, language instructions, spatial context, and navigation history for decision-making. OmniNav [24] further unifies instruction-goal, object-goal, and point-goal navigation with frontier-based exploration through a fast-slow architecture. These studies demonstrate the importance of integrating visual, linguistic, 6spatial, and historical information. Building on this broader perspective, CSG-Nav further emphasizes the reliability of semantic, spatial, and geo-metric evidence during zero-shot object-goal navigation. Map-based methods accumulate semantic observations into spatial repre-sentations to support exploration [25, 5, 26]. Recent approaches further incor-porate uncertainty into these representations. Uncertainty-Aware Gaussian Map [27] estimates geometric, semantic, and appearance uncertainty within a semantic Gaussian map and incorporates them into a unified 3D value map. Beyond navigation, uncertainty-aware 3D reconstruction [28] models observation-dependent uncertainty to suppress unreliable measurements in dynamic underwater scenes. Unlike learned or probabilistic uncertainty-estimation methods, CSG-Nav does not aim to model all sources of perceptual uncertainty. Instead, it focuses on distance as an explicit and readily available reliability factor during spatial evidence fusion, since observation quality generally degrades with increasing sensing range. A training-free Gaussian distance prior is therefore used to reduce the influence of distant observations. 

3. System Design 

3.1. Overview of Multimodal Fusion Architecture 

We propose CSG-Nav, a confidence-aware multimodal fusion framework for zero-shot object-goal navigation, as shown in Fig. 2. Rather than treat-ing perception, mapping, planning, and verification as independent stages, CSG-Nav integrates heterogeneous evidence from open-vocabulary object de-tection, vision-language scene understanding, structured language reasoning, multi-view spatial observations, accessibility estimation, and RGB-D geom-etry. At each navigation step, the agent first acquires multi-view RGB-D ob-servations. Semantic-source fusion integrates object-level and scene-level ev-idence to produce direction-level semantic scores. Spatial-confidence fusion then combines these scores with distance-conditioned reliability and accessi-bility constraints to update a global preference map for exploration. When the target is detected, semantic-geometric decision fusion combines target detection with depth-based 3D localization to determine whether the geo-metric arrival condition is satisfied before issuing the stop decision. 3.4. Exploration Task Planning Module 

Based on the fused global preference map, CSG-Nav performs hierarchical navigation planning in two stages: direction selection and executable action selection. This module does not introduce an additional fusion mechanism; instead, it converts the fused semantic-spatial preferences from Sec. 3.3 into navigation actions. The agent first selects a promising observation direction and generates a corresponding subtask, and then chooses an executable local action within the reachable region.

4.1. Experimental Setup 4.1.1. Datasets 

We evaluate CSG-Nav using the Habitat [30] simulator on three standard object-goal navigation benchmarks: Habitat-Matterport 3D (HM3D) [31], HM3Dv2 [32], and Matterport 3D (MP3D) [33]. HM3Dv1 provides 2000 validation episodes from 20 scenes and covers 6 object categories. HM3Dv2 improves scene quality and semantic annotation scale, providing 1000 vali-dation episodes from 36 scenes and 6 object categories. MP3D contains 2195 episodes from 11 scenes and includes 21 object categories. These datasets cover different scene distributions, annotation settings, and target-category scales, enabling evaluation across diverse ObjectNav benchmarks. 

4.1.2. Metrics 

We report Success Rate (SR) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL). SR measures the percentage of episodes in which the agent successfully stops near the target object. SPL further evaluates navigation efficiency by comparing the executed path length with the shortest path length. Therefore, SR reflects the reliability of goal reaching, while SPL reflects both success and path efficiency. Since CSG-Nav emphasizes physically grounded stop-ping, SR is particularly important for evaluating whether semantic-geometric fusion reduces false arrival decisions. 

4.1.3. Baselines 

We compare CSG-Nav with representative object-goal navigation meth-ods on HM3Dv1 [31], HM3Dv2 [32], and MP3D [33]. The baselines cover both zero-shot and training-based settings. For zero-shot object-goal navigation, we include perception- and semantic-map-based methods, including ZSON [2], CoW [3], VLFM [5], PSL [34], PixNav [35] and OpenFMNav [26]. We also compare with reasoning- and foundation-model-based approaches, including ESC [7], L3MVN [6], EIK-Nav [36], UniGoal [11], ReMemNav [12] and WMNav [8], which further in-corporates world-model-based prediction into navigation planning. For training-based comparison, we include Habitat-Web [37], PIRLNav [38], SemEXP [25], PONI [39], and ProcTHOR [40]. Together, these baselines pro-vide comparisons across perception-driven, semantic-map, reasoning-based, foundation-model, world-model, and supervised policy-learning paradigms. 16 4.1.4. Implementation Details 

We set the maximum number of navigation steps to 40. The agent moves with a minimum step length of 0.5 m and a maximum movement distance of 1.7 m. The agent height is set to 0.88 m, and the agent radius is set to 0.18 m. The RGB-D camera is mounted with a downward tilt of approximately 14 degrees. It has a field of view of 79 degrees and outputs images at a resolution of 480 × 640 . The navigation success threshold is set to Sthres = 1 .0 m. An episode is considered successful if the shortest path distance between the agent and the target object is less than 1.0 m when the agent stops. For confidence-aware preference map generation, we initialize the prefer-ence value as V0(x) = 10 and the confidence value as C0(x) = 0 .2. The for-mer provides an optimistic exploration prior for unobserved regions, while the latter allows subsequent observations to have greater influence on preference-map updates. Semantic observations are then fused using distance-conditioned reliability weights. For the model configuration, CSG-Nav uses Qwen3-VL-4B as the main VLM for scene understanding, subtask decomposition, and action selection, and Qwen3-4B as the LLM for structured reasoning and direction-level se-mantic scoring. YOLO-World provides explicit object-level detections, detec-tion confidence, and bounding boxes for semantic-source fusion and semantic-geometric decision fusion. The VLM and LLM are invoked sequentially ac-cording to their respective roles in the navigation pipeline. We adopt this configuration as the default compact model setting for CSG-Nav. Its compu-tational efficiency is further evaluated in Sec. 4.5 through navigation-step latency, model inference latency, spatial-fusion overhead, and peak GPU memory, with an additional comparison against a larger configuration us-ing Qwen2.5-VL-7B as the VLM and Qwen3-8B as the LLM.

A notable advantage of CSG-Nav is its compact model configuration. Many existing LLM/VLM-based methods rely on large closed-source models, such as GPT or Gemini-scale models. In contrast, CSG-Nav achieves com-petitive zero-shot performance using 4B-scale VLM and LLM components. This suggests that explicitly fusing semantic evidence, confidence-weighted spatial memory, and geometric verification can partially reduce the depen-dence on extremely large foundation models. Among all compared zero-shot methods, CSG-Nav achieves the high-est reported SR among the compared zero-shot methods on HM3Dv1 and HM3Dv2. However, its SPL is lower than some baselines. We attribute this mainly to the conservative nature of our semantic-geometric stopping strat-egy. Unlike methods that may stop once strong semantic target cues are observed, CSG-Nav requires additional geometric verification before termina-tion. When the target is detected but the geometric stopping condition is not 18 yet satisfied, the agent may perform local adjustment and re-observation to confirm arrival. This behavior improves stopping reliability and contributes to higher SR, but it also increases redundant motion near the goal and reduces SPL. In addition, the compact 4B LLM may limit the quality of long-horizon reasoning, which can further affect planning efficiency. Compared with training-based methods, PIRLNav achieves the highest SR on HM3Dv1 by using 77k human demonstrations. Nevertheless, CSG-Nav narrows the gap to about 5 percentage points without task-specific training. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed zero-shot fusion frame-work under limited supervision. 

4.3. Ablation Study 

To evaluate the contribution of each fusion component, we conduct con-trolled ablation studies on HM3Dv2. We first compare different spatial-fusion strategies while using VLM-based semantic scoring and semantic-only stop-ping. We then fix the Gaussian spatial-fusion strategy and evaluate different combinations of semantic sources, with the stopping strategy kept unchanged. Finally, we retain the Gaussian spatial fusion and the full semantic-source configuration and compare different arrival-verification strategies. This se-quential design enables staged comparisons of different fusion configurations while keeping the remaining navigation settings fixed within each compari-son. All ablation experiments are conducted on a fixed subset of 100 HM3Dv2 episodes. We partition the ordered validation set into 50 subsets and select the first two episodes from each subset. The selected episodes cover all 36 validation scenes and all six target-object categories. The same subset is used for the spatial-confidence, semantic-source, and semantic-geometric fusion ablations, as well as the Gaussian-parameter sensitivity and failure analyses. Each configuration is evaluated once, so the reported results are single-run measurements. Accordingly, the ablation results are presented as controlled comparisons across different fusion configurations. 

Spatial-Confidence Fusion Ablation . We first evaluate the contribution of spatial-confidence fusion while keeping the semantic-scoring and stopping strategies fixed. The purpose of this ablation is to examine whether distance-conditioned reliability improves the aggregation of semantic evidence into the global preference map. In particular, long-range observations may provide less reliable semantic evidence than nearby observations, so assigning equal 19 influence to all projected observations can introduce unstable preferences during map updating. As shown in Table 3, the baseline follows the WMNav-style value-map construction. Uniform weighting assigns the same confidence weight to the projected semantic evidence, while linear and Gaussian decay progressively reduce the influence of observations with increasing distance. On the eval-uated subset, Gaussian distance weighting achieves a higher SR than the baseline, whereas uniform weighting and linear decay do not show the same pattern. These results suggest that the choice of distance-conditioned relia-bility weighting affects navigation outcomes.

4.6. Failure Analysis 

We further analyze the failure cases of the final CSG-Nav configuration on the same fixed 100-episode HM3Dv2 subset used in the ablation studies. As illustrated in Fig. 7, unsuccessful episodes are categorized according to their primary cause, including planning, perception, verification, and environment-related failures. Each failed episode is assigned to one category based on its navigation trajectory and execution logs. Planning-related cases account for the largest proportion of the remain-ing failures. These cases mainly occur after the global preference map has selected a promising exploration direction, but local navigation becomes dif-ficult in narrow or cluttered regions, or the target area cannot be reached within the maximum step limit in large scenes. This suggests that the re-maining planning challenges are mainly associated with local execution and long-horizon navigation rather than semantic preference estimation. Perception-related cases mainly arise from ambiguous visual evidence. Visually or semantically similar objects may occasionally be confused, such as sofas with beds or benches with chairs. These cases indicate that diffi-cult visual ambiguity remains challenging even after combining object-level detection, scene-level understanding, and relational reasoning. 24 Figure 7: Failure analysis of the final CSG-Nav configuration on the fixed HM3Dv2 subset. 

Verification-related cases are mainly observed during target approach un-der occlusion or unfavorable viewpoints. In such situations, stable object detection and depth-based localization may be temporarily unavailable, de-laying reliable arrival verification. When consistent target observations are available, semantic-geometric fusion provides explicit geometric evidence for the stopping decision. Finally, some unsuccessful episodes are associated with challenging scene layouts or evaluation conditions, such as difficult-to-exit regions or cases close to the success boundary. Overall, the failure distribution shows that the re-maining challenges are concentrated mainly in local navigation, ambiguous observations, and target visibility, providing clear directions for further im-proving the navigation pipeline.

6. Discussion and Conclusions 

In this paper, we propose CSG-Nav, a zero-shot object-goal navigation framework based on confidence-aware multimodal information fusion. CSG-Nav integrates heterogeneous semantic evidence, distance-conditioned spatial reliability, and RGB-D geometry through three fusion levels: semantic-source fusion for direction scoring, spatial-confidence fusion for preference-map con-struction, and semantic-geometric fusion for arrival verification. Experiments on HM3D and MP3D show competitive zero-shot navigation performance, while component-level ablations illustrate the effects of differ-ent fusion configurations on navigation outcomes. Spatial-confidence fusion reduces the influence of less reliable long-range observations, semantic-source fusion benefits from complementary object-level and scene-level evidence, and semantic-geometric fusion improves goal termination through depth-based 3D localization. Reliability and sensitivity analyses further support distance as a practical reliability cue, while real-world experiments demonstrate the feasibility of transferring the same fusion pipeline to indoor and outdoor robotic environments with local onboard execution. 29 These results suggest that object-level detection, scene-level understand-ing, and language-based reasoning provide complementary semantic infor-mation; reliability-aware spatial aggregation improves exploration preference estimation; and geometric proximity provides an additional signal for reli-able arrival verification. Together, these findings support reliability-aware multimodal fusion across multiple stages of zero-shot navigation. Several challenges remain. The current spatial-confidence module relies on a hand-designed distance-conditioned prior and fixed confidence initial-ization rather than learned or calibrated uncertainty estimation. Additional sensitivity experiments show that the initial preference V0 substantially in-fluences navigation performance. The initial confidence C0 also affects the results. These findings highlight the sensitivity of the current heuristic ini-tialization and motivate future work on adaptive confidence estimation and updating strategies. Perception can also become less stable for high, oc-cluded, or out-of-view targets, which motivates active viewpoint adjustment and cross-time-step target tracking. In addition, the current real-world ex-periments demonstrate deployment feasibility but do not yet provide a sys-tematic robustness assessment under controlled lighting variation, dynamic environments, or sensor degradation, nor matched baseline and component-level comparisons on the same robotic platform. Future work will therefore include controlled real-world evaluations under these environmental and sens-ing variations, together with baseline comparisons and component ablations, to assess system robustness and quantify the contribution of each fusion mechanism.
