DAC-MACE: Context-Conditioned Expert Routing
for Compact Scene Coordinate Regression in
Camera Relocalization
Dingying Fan1,†, Mingkai Liu2,†, Haohua Que1,3,†, Haojia Gao4, Xianliang Huang5, Qian Zhang1,3,
Xueqian Wang4, and Fei Qiao1,∗
Abstract—A camera becomes an absolute pose sensor when
its images can be relocalized against a prebuilt map of the
scene. On mobile robots and augmented-reality devices, that map
must be small and quick to build. Scene coordinate regression
(SCR) stores the scene in a compact network that predicts a 3D
coordinate for every pixel, and mixture-of-experts (MoE) variants
extend its capacity to larger outdoor scenes. In existing MoE-
based SCR, however, the router assigns each pixel to an expert
from the same local descriptor that the experts regress from,
so repeated facades and windows at different locations receive
similar routing inputs. We present DAC-MACE, which conditions
soft expert routing on recalibrated and contextualized features.
A residual context adapter (RCA) reweights the channels and
locations of frozen encoder features, a global context encoder
(GCE) mixes them with multi-scale self-attention, and an image-
level pooled descriptor is added to the router input. A dual-
threshold RANSAC–Levenberg–Marquardt estimator recovers
the pose. On Cambridge Landmarks, DAC-MACE attains a
mean per-scene median translation error of 13.0 cm over three
seeds, the lowest among the RGB-only SCR methods compared
(GLACE 13.6 cm, MACE 14.4 cm), and reduces the error on
the largest scene from 24 to 19 cm relative to MACE. In a
cumulative study, the MoE head, GCE, RCA, and the dual-
threshold estimator lower the mean error from 17.0 cm to 14.4,
14.0, 13.6, and 13.0 cm. On 7-Scenes, adding GCE to a single
ACE head raises Recall@(5 cm, 5◦) from 86.56% to 87.60%.
Index Terms—Camera relocalization, image sensors, mixture
of experts, robust pose estimation, scene coordinate regression,
self-attention, visual localization.

Structure-based pipelines match local features against a
structure-from-motion (SfM) point cloud [3], [4] and are accu-
rate, but their maps occupy hundreds of megabytes (Table II).
Scene coordinate regression (SCR) stores the scene implicitly
in a network that predicts a 3D scene coordinate for every
pixel; the pose then follows from the 2D–3D correspondences
with RANSAC and a perspective-n-point (PnP) solver [5]–[7].
ACE [8] reduced SCR mapping to minutes and the map to
4 MB by training only a small regression head on the features
of a frozen, scene-agnostic encoder. The capacity of a single
head is shared by the entire scene, however, and on Cambridge
Landmarks [1] the median translation error of ACE grows
from 5 cm on the smallest scene to 43 cm on the largest.
Partitioning the scene and assigning a regressor to each part
raises capacity. ESAC [9] and the Poker ensemble of ACE [8]
rely on fixed partitions, while MACE [10] learns a router that
weights ACE experts pretrained on spatial clusters. In all three,
the routing decision is computed from the same local encoder
descriptor the experts regress from. Each descriptor covers an
81×81-pixel receptive field, so repeated facades, windows,
and weakly textured surfaces at different locations produce
similar descriptors and hence similar routing inputs [11]. We
refer to this as perceptual routing ambiguity. It differs from
expert-load collapse, in which assignments concentrate on a
few experts [12], [13]. Load balancing regularizes the marginal
usage of the experts, but it does not tell the router which expert
is geometrically appropriate for a given pixel.
This paper studies whether conditioning the router on infor-
mation beyond the local descriptor improves MoE-based SCR.
We propose DAC-MACE (Dynamic Attention-driven Context
MACE, Fig. 1). Two lightweight trainable modules are placed
between the frozen ACE encoder and the experts: a residual
context adapter (RCA) that rescales the channels and locations
of the encoder features, and a global context encoder (GCE)
that mixes features with multi-scale self-attention. An image-
level pooled descriptor is further added to the router input,
and the pose is recovered with a dual-threshold RANSAC–

The contributions of this paper are as follows.
• A context-conditioned soft MoE for SCR that keeps the
encoder frozen and the ACE training pipeline intact,
together with an explicit account of the context each
module observes during training and at test time.
• A dual-threshold RANSAC–LM pose stage with an inter-
pretable selection rule, evaluated on fixed trained models
and analyzed through per-scene branch statistics.
• An evaluation on Cambridge Landmarks and 7-Scenes
covering three-seed accuracy, a component-wise analysis,
qualitative trajectory and feature-response diagnostics,
expert usage, and model complexity. Among the RGB-
only SCR methods compared, DAC-MACE has the low-
est mean of per-scene median translation errors, every
added component lowers that mean, and the largest gain
over MACE is on the largest scene (Great Court, 24 to
19 cm).
II. RELATED WORK
A. Structure-Based Localization and Retrieval
Feature-matching pipelines establish 2D–3D correspon-
dences between query keypoints and an SfM model [3], [4],
[14], often after image retrieval narrows the search [11],
[15]. InLoc [16] extends this to indoor scenes with dense
matching, CAMNet [17] combines coarse-to-fine retrieval with
relative pose estimation, and PixLoc [18] aligns learned feature
maps directly. These methods are accurate but store descrip-
tors for every 3D point; compression [19] or descriptor-free
matching [20] reduces the map at a large cost in accuracy
(Table II). Absolute pose regressors [1], [21], [22] are compact
but considerably less accurate.
B. Scene Coordinate Regression
The DSAC family [6], [7], [23] learns per-pixel scene coor-
dinates end to end with differentiable RANSAC. HSCNet [24]
and HSCNet++ [25] predict coordinates hierarchically, first
classifying the scene region and then regressing within it,
and HSCNet++ adds transformer-based conditioning across
levels. SRC [26] replaces regression with few-shot region
classification, and SANet [27] regresses coordinates in a scene-
agnostic manner. ACE [8] trains a scene-specific head on a
buffer of frozen encoder features and maps a scene in about
five minutes. GLACE [28] concatenates a pretrained image-
retrieval descriptor to every local feature and predicts coordi-
nates through a position decoder over clustered camera centers,
which lets a single head scale to larger scenes. R-SCoRe [29]
revisits the training of SCR for large-scale benchmarks such
as Aachen Day–Night [2]. The image-level residual of our
router is close in spirit to the global descriptor of GLACE; it
differs in that it pools the scene-adapted features rather than a
separately pretrained retrieval feature, and in that it conditions
the router rather than the regressor.
C. Partitioned and Mixture-of-Experts Regression
ESAC [9] combines a gating network with expert regres-
sors and samples hypotheses across experts. Poker [8] trains
independent ACE heads on spatial clusters and selects the
pose with the most inliers. MACE [10], the direct predecessor
of this work, pretrains ACE experts on clusters of mapping-
camera positions, routes features with a Gumbel-softmax
gate [30], and balances the experts with an auxiliary-loss-free
bias update [31]. DAC-MACE retains the experts, the position
decoder, and the bias update of MACE. It changes the router
input (RCA, GCE, and the pooled residual), adds a standard-
deviation penalty on mean expert usage, evaluates all experts
with soft weights at test time, and replaces the pose stage. In
the broader MoE literature, load imbalance is countered with
auxiliary losses [12], [13], [32]; routing ambiguity caused by
uninformative router inputs has received less attention in SCR.
D. Robust Pose Estimation
RANSAC [33] remains the standard for PnP with outliers.
LO-RANSAC [34] interleaves local optimization with hypoth-
esis sampling, MAGSAC++ [35] marginalizes over the inlier
threshold, and graduated non-convexity [36] solves robust
estimation without sampling. DSAC* [7] and ACE score
hypotheses with a soft inlier count and refine the best one
iteratively. Our dual-threshold stage is a lightweight heuristic
in this space that targets the varying coordinate accuracy of
SCR predictions.

G. Training Protocol
We keep the two-step, buffer-based protocol of ACE [8].
1) Buffer fill. The frozen encoder is run in evaluation mode
without gradients over the augmented mapping images.
For each image, 1,024 valid feature vectors are written,
together with their pixel coordinates, inverse camera
pose, and intrinsics, into a shuffled CPU buffer of 64 M
entries; features are stored in 16-bit precision. The buffer
holds raw encoder features, i.e., before RCA and GCE.
2) Context-routing stage. Each training step draws B =
163,840 buffer entries, reshapes them into B/512 blocks
of 16×32 features, and applies RCA, GCE, the router,
and the experts. The experts are trained sequentially: for
each k, expert k, the router, RCA, and GCE are updated
for 16 epochs while the encoder and the other experts
stay frozen.
We use AdamW with weight decay 10−4 and no gradient
clipping, a one-cycle learning-rate schedule with peak rates
of 5×10−3 for the active expert, RCA, and GCE and 5×10−4
for the router, and mixed precision. Apart from K, all training
and pose-estimation settings are shared across scenes; Table IV
lists them.
What context the modules observe. The 16×32 blocks in
step 2 are not image regions: their 512 entries are drawn
from different mapping images and locations. During training,
therefore, the channel gate of RCA pools statistics over a
random set of scene features; the spatial gate and the 2×2
pooling of the scale-2 GCE branch combine unrelated neigh-
bors; and scale-1 attention, which is permutation-equivariant
because it has no positional encoding, aggregates over features
from many images. At test time, the same operators act on the
coherent feature map of one image. GCE is therefore trained
as a content-based aggregation over sets of scene features and
applied within a single view at test time. This preserves the fast
buffer-based training, and the results in Section IV show that
the resulting modules improve accuracy on average; a training
scheme with image-coherent feature windows is left to future
work (Section IV-J).

H. Dual-Threshold Pose Estimation
Coordinate accuracy varies across scenes and images: a
strict inlier threshold yields precise hypotheses when coor-
dinates are accurate, whereas a loose threshold retains enough
support when they are noisy. Given the dense prediction
{(p, yp)}, we therefore run two RANSAC searches with
thresholds τs < τl, each with 256 hypotheses from mini-
mal PnP samples and soft-inlier scoring as in DSAC* [7],
yielding Ps and Pl. A looser threshold admits more inliers
by construction, so the two hypotheses are compared by the
threshold-normalized support
ρ(P, τ) = |I(P, τ)|/τ,
(16)
where I(P, τ) is the set of correspondences with reprojection
error below τ, and the hypothesis with the larger support
is selected. The rule selects the loose hypothesis only if
|I(Pl, τl)| > (τl/τs) |I(Ps, τs)|, i.e., if it gathers at least
τl/τs
= 1.67 times as many inliers. The rule does not
assume that inlier counts grow linearly with the threshold;
it fixes the exchange rate between precision and support so
that the strict hypothesis is preferred unless the loose one
is substantially better supported. The selected pose is refined
by Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) [43] on the reprojection error,
first over inliers at 12 px and then at 5 px, with at least
50 correspondences required before each round. Like LO-
RANSAC [34], the procedure combines hypothesis search
with local optimization; unlike it, the two operating points are
5


searched independently and the local optimization is applied
only to the selected hypothesis. The constants of both branches
are listed in Table IV.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Datasets, Reference Poses, and Metrics
Cambridge Landmarks [1] contains five outdoor scenes with
SfM reference poses; the numbers of mapping and query
images are listed in Table VIII. Images are rescaled to a
height of 480 px. 7-Scenes [5] contains seven indoor scenes
(Chess, Fire, Heads, Office, Pumpkin, Red Kitchen, and Stairs)
recorded at 640×480. For 7-Scenes we use the original D-
SLAM (KinectFusion) reference poses for all methods in
Table V; methods evaluated against SfM pseudo ground truth
are not directly comparable [44]. We report per-scene median
translation and rotation errors. For Cambridge, “Avg.” is the
arithmetic mean of the five per-scene medians, not a pooled
median, and is given with one decimal because the differences
between the strongest methods are about 1 cm; for 7-Scenes
we also report the fraction of queries within (5 cm, 5◦).
B. Implementation Details
DAC-MACE is implemented on top of ACE [8]; all settings
are listed in Table IV. The Cambridge results of DAC-
MACE are means over three training seeds; per-scene standard
deviations are below 0.5 cm and 0.05◦. All models are trained
on a single NVIDIA GPU with mixed precision. Following
MACE [10], the mapping time is the wall-clock time of the
routing stage, from the first buffer write to the saved model,
and excludes the ACE pretraining of the cluster experts; the
map size counts the expert heads in 16-bit precision, with
1 MB = 220 bytes. The context modules (RCA, GCE, and
the router) add 3.32 M scene-specific parameters, which are
reported separately in Table X. Baseline accuracies are those
reported in the original papers.
C. Comparison on Cambridge Landmarks
Table II compares DAC-MACE with feature-matching
(FM), absolute pose regression (APR), and SCR methods.
Among the RGB-only SCR methods, DAC-MACE has the
lowest mean translation error, 13.0 cm, against 13.6 cm for
GLACE and 14.4 cm for MACE, and it is the best or tied
best on Great Court, King’s College, and St Mary’s Church.
The largest improvement is on Great Court, the largest scene,
where the error falls from 24 cm (MACE) to 19 cm, matching
GLACE; GLACE remains better on Old Hospital and Shop
Facade by 1 cm. Relative to the RGB-only DSAC* (18.6 cm),
whose mapping takes 15 h, DAC-MACE lowers the mean error
by 5.6 cm with 35 min of mapping. The ranking does not
depend on the pose stage: with the single-threshold stage of
ACE and MACE, DAC-MACE averages 13.4 cm (Table VII),
still below GLACE and MACE.
Fig. 2 places the methods in the map-size–accuracy plane.
The expert heads of DAC-MACE occupy 10 MB for K=4,
between MACE (5 MB) and GLACE (13 MB) and 20–80
times less than the structure-based methods, which reach 10.8–

I. Results on 7-Scenes
7-Scenes is small and indoor, so we use it to test whether the
context module also helps a single ACE head without MoE
(Table V). Adding GCE to ACE raises the aggregate recall
from 86.56% to 87.60%, the highest in the table and above
the depth-supervised DSAC* (85.2%) and HSCNet (84.8%).
It lowers the median translation error on Office, Pumpkin, and
Red Kitchen (by 1 cm each), lowers the median rotation error
on Chess (0.71 to 0.55◦) and Red Kitchen (1.31 to 1.05◦), and
keeps or improves the remaining errors except the Pumpkin
rotation (1.11 to 1.14◦) and Stairs. On Stairs, the median
translation error rises from 4 to 13 cm. Stairs consists largely of
identical steps, and content-based attention without positional
encoding can aggregate features of different steps that look
alike. The context module therefore transfers to small indoor
scenes, with repetitive geometry of near-identical appearance
as its failure mode.
J. Limitations
The full system is evaluated on Cambridge Landmarks,
whose scenes span up to about 8,000 m2; city-scale bench-
marks such as Aachen Day–Night [2] and LaMAR [45],
together with a comparison to R-SCoRe [29] on them, are
left to future work, as are changing illumination and seasons.
The component analysis is cumulative, and its intermediate
rows come from a single seed. The dual-threshold stage was
evaluated on DAC-MACE against the single-threshold stage of
ACE, but it was not applied to the baselines or compared with
LO-RANSAC [34] or MAGSAC++ [35]. Routing ambiguity
is illustrated qualitatively (Fig. 5) rather than measured; its
reduction is inferred from the accuracy changes in Table III.
RCA and GCE are trained on shuffled features (Section III-G);
training on image-coherent feature windows may let the spatial
operators learn genuine image-space context. Finally, soft
routing evaluates every expert, and the complexity in Table X
is computed analytically rather than measured on embedded
hardware; sparse top-1 inference and lighter attention would
reduce the query cost.
V. CONCLUSION
DAC-MACE conditions the expert routing of scene coor-
dinate regression on recalibrated, context-mixed, and image-
pooled features instead of on the local descriptor alone, and
recovers the camera pose with a dual-threshold RANSAC–LM
stage. On Cambridge Landmarks it obtains the lowest mean of
per-scene median translation errors among the RGB-only SCR
methods compared, 13.0 cm, with 10 MB of expert parameters
and 35 min of routing-stage mapping, and reduces the error on
the largest scene from 24 to 19 cm relative to MACE. Each
proposed component lowers the mean error, GCE mainly on
the largest scene and RCA on facade scenes, and the pose
stage adapts its operating point to the scene. On 7-Scenes,
the context module raises the recall of a single ACE head
and lowers or keeps its median translation error on six of
seven scenes. These properties make context-conditioned SCR
a compact option for camera-based relocalization on mobile
platforms; evaluation on city-scale and long-term benchmarks
and a lighter attention design for embedded processors are the
next steps.
