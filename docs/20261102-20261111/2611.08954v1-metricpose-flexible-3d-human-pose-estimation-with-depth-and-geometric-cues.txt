# MetricPose: Flexible 3D Human Pose Estimation with Depth and Geometric Cues 

Shifan Zhu, Sabad Modi, Hang Yang, Arushi Agrawal, Steven Gao, Meghan Huber, Nathan Wycoff, and Donghyun Kim †

University of Massachusetts Amherst, Amherst, MA, USA 

Abstract —In this paper 1, we present a 3D human pose esti-mator that predicts metrically accurate human joint positions in the camera coordinate frame. This task requires recovering the configuration of the body joints, scale, and metric camera-local placement. In monocular images, body size and camera distance are coupled through perspective projection and therefore cannot be determined uniquely from image alone. This ambiguity can be reduced by combining three complementary sources of information: learned RGB priors, direct depth measurements, and geometric constraints (e.g., ground contact) from the environ-ment. We extend a pretrained full-body estimator with optional metric depth- and geometry-processing modules that improve camera-distance estimation and depth-guided body refinement while preserving RGB-only operation. The resulting unified model supports RGB, RGB-Ground, and RGB-D using shared learned parameters. Unlike pelvis-relative error, camera-local error also measures placement relative to the camera. Compared with the existing foundation model SAM 3D Body, our methods with RGB, RGB-Ground, and RGB-D modes reduce camera-local joint-position error by 12.16%, 31.00% and 54.61% respectively. 

Index Terms —Human Detection & Tracking, RGB-D Percep-tion, Sensor Fusion 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent monocular 3D human pose estimators can recover body-joint configurations accurately under occlusion, trunca-tion, and unusual poses [1], [2]. Most progress, however, is measured after subtracting the pelvis and, in some cases, aligning body scale. These protocols isolate root-relative pose but discard errors in lateral position, camera distance, and physical scale. Camera-local evaluation instead compares pre-dicted and ground-truth joints directly in camera coordinates, without translation, rotation, or scale alignment [3]. This dis-tinction matters when applications depend on metric distances and motion. Learning collaborative behavior from human demonstrations, such as two people carrying a table, requires preserving their relative positions and trajectories in a common metric frame [4]. Social-interaction analysis similarly depends on interpersonal distances and spatial arrangements [5], while biomechanical measures require physical scale and global displacement [6]. Pelvis centering and scale alignment remove these quantities [3]. Metric 3D human pose estimation in camera coordinates re-quires recovering body-joint configuration, physical scale, and camera-relative placement, with scale and placement coupled

Monocular estimators commonly address this ambiguity using priors learned from large-scale in-the-wild image collections. These priors encode typical body proportions, poses, and camera configurations, but their reliability depends on the training distribution. For underrepresented body shapes, such as children, a model may explain the image as an adult-sized body farther from the camera, producing a plausible image-space reconstruction with incorrect physical scale and placement, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Measured depth provides direct distance information, help-ing constrain camera distance and resolve the size–distance ambiguity. Depth is also not the only source of metric in-formation available at inference. In many fixed or robot-mounted systems, camera height is known from the mounting configuration, while gravity is available from calibration or an onboard inertial sensor. Together, they define the ground plane in camera coordinates and, under a valid foot–ground contact, constrain body scale and camera-relative placement. Our key insight is therefore to use RGB priors, measured depth, and ground geometry at complementary stages of reconstruction. Building on SAM 3D Body (3DB) [1], learned RGB-D feature fusion incorporates depth context, a two-pass local depth-residual module refines pose from discrepancies between observed surface depth and predicted landmark depth, and geometric corrections adjust metric scale and camera-relative placement. These components share one parameter set and activate according to input availability, supporting RGB, RGB-Ground and RGB-D. We evaluate the unified model across all three input modes. arXiv:2611.08954v1 [cs.CV] 6 Nov 2026 We report mean per-joint position error (MPJPE), the aver-age Euclidean distance between corresponding predicted and ground-truth 3D joints, under three protocols. Pelvis-relative MPJPE centers both skeletons at the pelvis, removing global translation; Procrustes-aligned MPJPE (PA-MPJPE) addition-ally removes global rotation and isotropic scale; and camera-local MPJPE applies no alignment, so it also penalizes errors in camera distance and lateral position, including those caused by incorrect body scale. The main contributions are:  

> •

Unified optional-input estimator. One shared-weight 3DB-based model operates with RGB, RGB-Ground, or RGB-D by activating the available learned and geometric pathways.  

> •

Complementary depth and geometry processing. 

Learned depth fusion and a two-pass local-residual re-finer improve pose, while deterministic depth-surface or ground-contact corrections jointly rescale the body and camera translation while preserving the 2D projection.  

> •

Improved camera-local accuracy. Our RGB, RGB-Ground, and RGB-D modes reduce errors in physical scale and camera-relative placement that aligned metrics obscure, lowering camera-local MPJPE by 12.2%, 31.0%, and 54.6%, respectively, relative to released 3DB 2. In a controlled simulation with a small-stature avatar 5 m from the camera, SAM 3D Body misestimates its distance by more than 4 m , whereas our RGB-D mode reduces this error to about 7 cm .II. R ELATED WORK 

Monocular human reconstruction estimates body pose and shape from a single RGB image, but monocular evidence alone cannot separate physical body scale from camera distance. Prior work therefore relies on learned priors or adds depth, scene geometry, or optional sensing. 

Monocular human reconstruction and camera-local placement. Methods such as MHFormer and HybrIK recover body-joint configuration, which is commonly evaluated rela-tive to the pelvis [8], [9]. RootNet estimates root depth from an assumed physical person size [7], while MeTRAbs and NLF combine 2D and metric 3D predictions with camera intrinsics to obtain absolute poses [3], [10]. SPEC and CameraHMR estimate camera parameters [11], [12], which corrects perspec-tive but not the size–distance ambiguity, and BLADE regresses the person’s pelvis depth and then solves for focal length and translation at close range [13]. Multi-HMR predicts multiple whole-body meshes and their camera-local locations [14], while Metric-Aware HMR builds metric pseudo-ground truth by jointly optimizing crowd translations with height priors and monocular depth [15]. More recently, 3DB combines large-scale visual pretraining, promptable decoding, and a structured human representation [1], and Fast 3DB improves its compu-tational efficiency [2]. For all-age scenes, BEV estimates age groups to order people in depth [16], and Anny-Fit fits an

all-age body model in camera space using VLM-estimated age and predicted metric depth [17]. These methods infer metric scale from image evidence, average body-size priors, or predicted depth. Our method corrects the inherited RGB estimate with measured depth or known ground geometry, processing each detected person independently. 

Depth-assisted human pose and mesh recovery. Depth sensing provides direct metric observations of the visible body surface. Existing methods integrate RGB and depth features volumetrically [18], reconstruct bodies from back-projected partial point clouds [19], lift joints detected in RGB using depth sampled at their image locations [20], or fit parametric bodies to image keypoints and measured point clouds [21]. RGB-D sensing has also been used for unified body, hand, and face estimation [22]. RELI11D shows that pelvis-relative ac-curacy does not guarantee global accuracy [23]. Nevertheless, measured depth represents the visible clothing or skin surface rather than the underlying anatomical joint centers. Existing RGB-D approaches also generally require depth during every inference. In contrast, our model learns to use depth without sacrificing overall RGB-only accuracy. 

Scene geometry and physical constraints. Scene infor-mation and physical contact provide constraints on human placement. Classical single-view metrology recovers physical measurements and camera location from reference-plane and vanishing-point geometry [24]. Fei et al. estimate camera in-trinsics, the ground plane, and distances between people from their poses, assuming an average human height [25]. SA-HMR uses human–scene contact to recover absolute body position in a pre-scanned scene observed by a localized camera [26]. IPMAN introduces contact and pressure consistency with a ground plane to improve physical plausibility [27]. Our ground correction instead takes the plane from known camera height and gravity, needing no scene scan or height prior, and uses foot contact to rescale body size and camera distance together. 

Human reconstruction with optional sensing. Depth has been used as privileged information during training before being removed at inference [28], although such cross-modal transfer is not always beneficial [29]. Modality dropout trains networks to tolerate missing inputs [30], and we adopt it for depth. PromptHMR accepts optional spatial and semantic prompts and camera intrinsics [31], but unlike depth and ground geometry, none of these inputs constrains metric scale or distance. Privileged-information methods cannot use depth when it becomes available during deployment, while existing RGB-D pose estimators assume fixed inputs [18], [22]. Overall, previous methods generally rely on monocular priors, require metric sensing, or need scene scans or height priors to exploit scene geometry. Our method combines these sources for metric camera-local reconstruction while preserv-ing operation when optional metric inputs are unavailable. III. M ETHOD

A. 3DB Backbone and Adaptation 

We build on 3DB [1]. Given a 512 × 512 person crop, its DINOv3 ViT-H/16+ encoder produces image tokens processed by a six-layer promptable body decoder. A trainable MLP maps the decoded pose token to Momentum Human Rig (MHR) parameters, including global orientation, body pose, shape, and skeletal scale, while the frozen, differentiable MHR body model converts them into a mesh and MHR70 joints. A separate perspective-camera head predicts crop-relative camera parameters, which are converted to camera-local translation using the person box and effective intrinsics. We apply LoRA to the pretrained image encoder and body decoder, while training the MHR and camera heads and the new depth, ground, and pose-refinement modules. In the image encoder, we use rank-16 LoRA for attention and rank-8 LoRA for feed-forward layers; in the body decoder, we use rank-8 LoRA for self- and cross-attention. The remaining pretrained encoder and decoder weights, the hand branch, and the MHR body model remain frozen. The camera head receives a stop-gradient pose representation so that placement losses do not update the body representation through this branch. 

B. Depth Encoding and Cross-Modal Feature Integration 

Depth enters the model through complementary feature-level and measurement-level pathways. The feature-level path-way encodes depth into spatial and global tokens that condition the RGB representation through encoder residual writes and decoder cross-attention. The measurement-level pathways use metric depth: local residuals refine body pose in Section III-C, while surface agreement corrects metric scale and camera-local placement in Section III-D. To construct the feature-level pathway, we align depth with the RGB crop and encode bounded depth, camera rays, and validity using a small pre-trained trunk. Depths outside [Dmin , D max ] are marked invalid. For crop pixel p = ( u, v ) of person n, let Dn,p denote depth in meters and Vn,p ∈ { 0, 1} denote its validity. A. Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We fine-tune 3DB on equally sampled, source-homogeneous batches from BEDLAM2 and CMU Panoptic, both providing metric depth registered to RGB. BEDLAM2 depth is rendered in the RGB frame, while Panoptic sen-sor depth is projected using the provided calibration and temporal associations. Invalid projections are masked. We evaluate 3DPW [35], EMDB [36], and RICH [37], with depth-enabled results on held-out BEDLAM2, CMU Panoptic, HuMMan [38], and EgoBody [39]. 

Geometric-correction settings. The depth-surface similar-ity correction uses a two-pixel point-splat radius, a three-pixel-eroded support mask, the ratio band 0.5 < D/ ˆD < 2, at least 50 support pixels, and a maximum median absolute log residual of 0.1. The ground correction requires the contact direction to lie more than 5◦ below the floor horizon and accepts factors in (0 .5, 2) .

Pelvis-distance correction settings. The optional depth-based pelvis-distance correction adjusts camera-axis transla-tion, and therefore pelvis depth, using visible-surface depth residuals. It uses a 0.6 m band around the predicted pelvis depth, requires at least 64 visible mesh vertices, and abstains when the median absolute residual exceeds 0.1 m .

Joint-refinement settings. The refiner uses 24 ×24 residual grids sampled at two-pixel intervals, clips centered residuals to 

±0.5 m and scales them by 0.25 m , and employs a three-layer, four-head transformer. 

Training augmentation. We use crop-scale jitter in 

[0 .8, 1.3] , 5% translation, ±15 ◦ in-plane rotation, RGB pho-tometric augmentation, sensor-specific depth corruption, and up to two pixels of RGB-depth misregistration. Horizontal reflection is disabled, and full depth-modality dropout is linearly increased to 15% during the first 30% of training. Without depth, depth-dependent updates are gated off; ground processing remains active when ground geometry is provided. 

Metrics. We report pelvis-relative MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, and camera-local MPJPE in millimeters, using pelvis centering, Procrustes alignment, and no alignment, respectively. Camera-local MPJPE retains pose, scale, and placement errors. 

Comparison protocol. All reevaluated methods use iden-tical oracle boxes and crops. We compare released 3DB, our shared-weight input modes, the optional training-free pelvis-distance correction, and published Fast 3DB results where available. RGB and RGB-D differ only in depth availability. RGB-D results use paired samples, with uncertainty estimated by bootstrapping acquisition or sequence groups.

RGB-Ground performs best on BEDLAM2 and CMU Panoptic, whereas RGB-D performs best on HuMMan and EgoBody, showing that the two metric cues are complementary but that ground-based correction depends on dataset and contact conditions.

F. Ablation 

We perform one-at-a-time inference-time pathway knock-outs on HuMMan with sensor depth and EMDB with mesh-rendered depth, keeping all other components fixed and dis-abling the optional pelvis-distance correction in every condi-tion. Table V reports ∆ = Edisabled − Efull ; positive values indicate degradation after removing a pathway. This analysis measures the jointly trained model’s reliance on each pathway, not the effect of retraining without it. The depth-surface correction accounts for most of the camera-local placement gain: disabling it increases camera-local MPJPE by 41 .0 mm on HuMMan and 49 .5 mm on EMDB, while PA-MPJPE is unchanged at the reported preci-sion. Among the learned pathways, the joint refiner provides the largest pose improvement, followed by backbone fusion. These results indicate that surface correction primarily im-proves metric placement, whereas the refiner and backbone fusion improve joint configuration. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a unified extension of 3DB for metric 3D human pose estimation with optional depth and ground geome-try. A shared model supports RGB, RGB-Ground, and RGB-D inference, combining learned RGB-D feature fusion and depth-guided pose refinement with deterministic metric corrections. Across the benchmarks, the addition of depth consistently re-duced camera-local MPJPE, while its smaller effect on pelvis-relative and Procrustes-aligned errors showed that the princi-pal gain is in physical scale and camera-relative placement. Ground geometry provided substantial improvements when its support-plane assumptions held, but remained dataset-dependent. The controlled small-stature experiment further exposed the monocular size–distance ambiguity and showed that adding depth largely removed its distance-dependent error. 

Limitations. Performance remains sensitive to missing or misregistered depth and calibration error. Ground correction assumes known gravity, camera height, planar support, and valid contact, while the deterministic corrections use calibrated thresholds. The depth branch also increases computation. 

Future work. We will investigate uncertainty-aware fusion, calibration robustness, temporal or multi-view cues, and more efficient depth processing.
