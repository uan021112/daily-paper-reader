## ABSTRACT 

World models have increasingly been applied as a model-based control method for robotics tasks such as manipulation and locomotion. While early world model architectures relied on decoders to prevent mode collapse and learn a predictable latent space, recent works on world models, such as Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture or LeWorldModel, increasingly aim to learn embeddings without a grounding decoder, utilising only a latent forward dynamics predictor and a stabilising entropy term. In this paper, we set out to demonstrate that the further introduction of two additional latent losses to LeWorldModel, a backward dynamics predictor and an inverse dynamics action predictor, enforces further structure on the latent embedding, leading to further gains in both performance and planning efficiency. These are critical properties for improving the applicability of such methods to real-time model-based control of real-world robots. We demonstrate these gains on standard world model benchmarks and two simulated robot manipulation tasks.

## 1 Introduction 

World models have become increasingly useful for model-based control of robots operating in complex observation spaces such as images or high-dimensional proprioceptive data [1–5]. Instead of planning directly in complex high-dimensional spaces, the core idea is to learn an embedding space of observations and perform planning in a dimensionally reduced space efficiently and quickly [6]. This requires an embedding function that projects high-dimensional observations into latent features where a transition function can be learned to perform model-predictive control. In this work, we investigate ways to improve world model learning by incorporating multiple learning signals during optimisation. Early world models have relied heavily on encoder-decoder architectures, such as variational autoencoders [7], to prevent mode collapse and ensure reliable learning for dynamics learning and planning. The decoder’s prediction of the original observation typically imposes artificial restrictions on the learned latent space: each observation feature must be fully predictable to satisfy and minimise the reconstruction loss. Instead, in the context of planning and model-based control, our main objective is a structured latent space that places similar observations close together, while maximising the ability of a learned transition function to predict the next latent state given an action. As prior work has shown, removing the decoder, while challenging, yields better-structured latent spaces and improved planability, leading to performance gains in robot control tasks [8]. Notably, recent work such as LeWorldModel (LeWM) [9] shows that using only a latent transition loss that predicts the next latent features, along with a Sketched-Isotropic-Gaussian Regularizer to prevent mode collapse in latent representations, enables training world models applicable to robotic tasks such as object manipulation. In this work, we go one step further: we show a natural and intuitive extension to LeWorldModel, motivated by insights from information theory, in which an additional backward transition-dynamics loss and an inverse dynamics loss induce further structure on the world model’s latent embedding space. We show that this leads not only to better performance, but also to computational efficiency by allowing reductions in both horizon length and inference steps, which lowers the test-time planning compute of the model-predictive control process and better real-world applicability in robotics. In summary, the contributions of our paper are: C1 We propose a new extension of LeWM, namely Σ-LeWM, which introduces backward and inverse dynamics as auxiliary losses/tasks to learn a more structured & predictable latent embedding. C2 We show that this approach has an intuitive grounding in information theory, where this learning approach is motivated by maximising the S-Information. C3 Our experiments demonstrate that Σ-LeWM leads to better performance than standard LeWM, and that it also does so on more realistic robotics tasks such as gear assembly. 

## 2 Related Works 

Latent World Models and JEPA. Generative world models like the Dreamer family [6], PlaNet [10], and DIA-MOND [11] learn latent dynamics through reconstruction with decoders and reward signals. TD-MPC [8], instead, drops the decoder and learns the latent dynamics via temporal-difference objectives. Recently, JEPA [12] has gained increasing attention with no need for reconstruction or reward, and learns to predict latent representations using an encoder and a predictor. Methods like I-JEPA [12] and V-JEPA [13] use JEPA for self-supervised representation learning by predicting the latent representation of masked inputs. However, latent prediction does not guarantee informative representations and can suffer from representational collapse. 

Anti-collapse in Latent World Models. Recent JEPA-like methods use various approaches to alleviate representation collapse and stabilise training: DINO-WM [14] freezes a pretrained encoder to prevent the prediction from collapsing; while PLDM [15], Sub-JEPA [16], and RC-aux [17] use auxiliary objectives to regularise the latent space. However, preventing collapse alone does not guarantee that the latent space captures control-relevant structure, which is useful in robot learning and planning. 

Inverse Dynamics in Latent World Models. Recent works increasingly use inverse dynamics to make latent representations more control-relevant. SMWM [18] and AC-MTM [19] use inverse action prediction from the two latent representations as an auxiliary task when learning representations. On the other hand, GC-IDM [20] and ACID [21] use inverse dynamics only for downstream planning, without changing the latent representation learning. 2Σ-LeWM PREPRINT

4.1 Overview of Σ-LeWM 

We extend the standard latent world model, specifically LeWM, with two additional predictive functions: the backward and inverse transition functions. 

Components: Therefore, our proposed extension to LeWM contains the following main components: Observation encoder: zt = EO (ot) (8a) Action encoder: ˜at = EA(at) (8b) Forward Dynamics: zt+1 = f (zt, ˜at) (8c) Backward Dynamics (aux.): zt = fback (zt+1 , ˜at) (8d) Inverse Dynamics (aux.): ˜at = finv (zt, zt+1 ) (8e) Building on LeWM, Σ-LeWM retains the standard observation and action encoders E(·) and EA(·), together with the forward dynamics model f (zt, ˜at). These auxiliary objectives ( fback. (·) and finv. (·)) are used only during training and do not introduce an additional dynamic model at inference time. They can be trained implicitly by embedding them directly in the DCL loss (see Sec. 4.2). Consequently, test-time planning remains identical to LeWM. Nevertheless, by maximising the backward and inverse information objectives during training, Σ-LeWM imposes additional structural biases on the learned latent space.

Notably, we set out to optimise the sum of the three joint mutual information terms given above. It has been shown that such a sum directly relates to the Total Correlation (TC) [25] and Dual Total Correlation (DTC) [26], i.e. 

Iforward + Iback . + Iinv .

= TC( Zt, ˜At, Z t+1 ) + DTC( Zt, ˜At, Z t+1 ). (19) The decomposition in Eq. (19) provides a useful information-theoretic interpretation of the proposed objective. TC quantifies the overall statistical dependence among Zt, ˜At, and Zt+1 , vanishing only when the three variables are mutually independent. DTC, by contrast, captures a complementary form of multivariate dependence that reflects how strongly each variable is constrained by the remaining variables. More importantly, their sum admits a particularly intuitive form: 

Σ( Zt, ˜At, Z t+1 ) = TC( Zt, ˜At, Z t+1 ) + DTC( Zt, ˜At, Z t+1 ), (20) which is known as the S-Information [27]. Thus, rather than encouraging only forward predictability, our objective promotes predictive dependence in all three directions: the next state from the current state and action, the current state from the next state and action, and the action from the two consecutive states. ## 5 Experiments 

In our experiments, we set out to investigate whether (RQ1) the proposed Σ-LeWM shows performance gains over LeWM, (RQ2) what effect the auxiliary tasks have on the latent embedding and its structure, and, finally, (RQ3) if maximising the S-Information provides an advantage over maximising the individual terms. To answer the first question, we evaluate our method’s predictive capacity under standard world model benchmarks in Section 5.2. We also show the performance gain under longer planning horizons and lower compute effort, which is crucial in robotic scenarios, in Section 5.3. Furthermore, we introduce two more realistic robot manipulation tasks (Section 5.5) to evaluate Σ-LeWM’s end-to-end capability on more challenging tasks. To answer (RQ2) we perform dimensionality reduction on the learned latent space of Σ-LeWM and investigate whether the physical quantities of the task (e.g., object position) are preserved in the structure of the embedding (Section 5.6). We also learn a GC-IDM [20] planner for both LeWM and Σ-LeWM, evaluating whether the learned latent of Σ-LeWM is easier for other planner methods to perform. Finally, for (RQ3), we conduct a factorial ablation study on the two auxiliary tasks of backward and inverse dynamics, illustrating that only combining both, i.e., maximising the S-Information, leads to better task performance (Section 5.7). 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Across the tasks, we use the same set of auxiliary training parameters in the results unless mentioned, including the weight of the multi-step forward prediction λmulti = 1 .0 with maximum prediction offset m = 5 , and the weights with respect to the auxiliary objectives across all the experiments: λback = 0 .1 for the backward, and λinv = 0 .05 for the inverse; an ablation study on these parameters is shown in Section 5.7. We use DCL with temperature 0.1 and in-batch negatives (batch size 128). Consistent with LeWM and to be comparable, we use the same encoder, predictor, optimiser, training protocols, and set of parameters related to the forward prediction training (learning rate, SIGReg weight λsig = 0 .09 , temperature of the SIGReg τ = 0 .1) and the planning scheme using a CEM solver sampling 300 action sequences per iteration and selecting the top 30 candidates to update the sampling distribution. 

5.2 Results on the Standard Benchmarks 

First, we evaluate our method and baselines on LeWM’s four standard benchmarks and OGBench-Scene. 

Task setup. We use the standard Push-T, TwoRoom, Reacher, OGBench-Cube, and OGBench-Scene [28] tasks and use default training and planning settings in LeWM. We follow LeWM’s default test-time planning settings: a CEM solver with 30 optimisation steps, a 5-step planning horizon, and environment timesteps set to 25 with a 5-step frame skip. We compare against LeWM, Sub-JEPA [16] which replaces SIGReg with a subspace Gaussian regulariser, RC-aux [17], which uses two auxiliary objectives for time/space alignment, and SMWM [18] and AC-MTM [19], which add an inverse dynamics loss, in contrastive form for AC-MTM. For LeWM and Sub-JEPA, training seed 3072 is the authors’ released checkpoint and seeds 1 and 2 are retrained with the released recipe; RC-aux applies the authors’ fine-tuning recipe to each LeWM seed. All baselines keep their original objectives and therefore train without our multi-step forward term. On Scene, which has no released checkpoints, we train every method ourselves. To separate the contribution of the multi-step term from that of the backward and inverse objectives, Table 1 also reports LeWM trained with the multi-step term alone on all five tasks (the “base” arm of Section 5.7). We report the mean and standard deviation over 3 training seeds × 5 evaluation seeds × 50 episodes. 

Results. Experiments (Table 1) show that Σ-LeWM is best on Scene by about 20 points and on Push-T by 4.5 points; on the other three tasks it is within noise of the best method: TwoRoom (92.4 vs. 93.6 for Sub-JEPA), Cube (82.6 vs. 81.9 for SMWM) and Reacher (86.0 vs. 86.0 for LeWM). The multi-step forward term alone stays close to LeWM (Push-T 88.9, TwoRoom 92.8, Reacher 83.6, Cube 71.0, Scene 53.6), so the gains of Σ-LeWM on Scene (80.7) and Cube come from the backward and inverse objectives. It is worth noting that TwoRoom is a relatively low-complexity task, in which a point-mass has to navigate a gap.

5.7 Ablation Study: Why Backward/Inverse are Necessary Ablation on the objective types. ## 6 Conclusion 

We propose a new set of auxiliary tasks to enable the learning of robust and well-structured latent embeddings in world models such as LeWorldModel. Our proposed loss-functions are based on insight from information theory, and complement the standard objectives of LeWM in a theory-grounded way. Adding loss functions for backward and inverse dynamics in the latent space is equivalent to maximising the Dual and Total Correlations, in short the S-Information. This approach leads to an increase in determinism, or predictability, between the latent variables while rewarding their alignment at the same time. Experiments demonstrated that the proposed method Σ-LeWM shows strong performance improvements over LeWM, especially in robot manipulation benchmarks. Furthermore, we note an increased stability for long-horizon planning, an important feature for robotic tasks in the real world. Future work will investigate the use of the current auxiliary tasks, the backward and inverse latent dynamics, at planning time and the deployment of Σ-LeWM on real-world robot tasks.

## A Quantitative Probes of the Frozen Representation 

To complement the qualitative PCA views of Figure 7, we measure how much simulator state a linear probe recovers from the frozen latent representation on OGBench-Cube. 

Task setup. We freeze all the encoders, and all frames come from fresh episodes unseen by every encoder. We fit ridge regression, or logistic regression for binary targets, on standardised features, using episode-grouped 60/20/20 splits that are shared by all encoders and repeated with five seeds. 

Results. We find that the two terms play different roles (Table 3). We observe that the multi-step forward term improves the robot’s own state, increasing the end-effector accuracy from 0.84 to 0.98 , but that it does so at the cost of object information: the block’s planar position falls from 0.93 to 0.65 . However, adding the backward and inverse terms recovers most of this loss while preserving the improvement in end-effector representation. It also improves the representation of the relative position between the end-effector and the block compared with either alternative. We also find that the gripper state is already saturated under LeWM. 

## B Measured Planning Latency 

Section 5.4 reduces the CEM search budget, which counts optimisation iterations rather than time; we therefore measure the latency of the planner itself. 

Task setup. We evaluate the inference time of the standard single-environment MPC solver on a single desktop GeForce RTX 3090 and, as a data centre reference, a single NVIDIA H100, with 5 warm-up solves and 50 repetitions per setting. 

Results. We find that solve time grows linearly with the number of CEM iterations, almost all of it spent by the predictor rolling out candidate plans (Table 4). We observe that cutting CEM30 to CEM4 makes a solve about seven times faster on both GPUs. We also find that the H100 is only about 1.5× faster than the GeForce RTX 3090, since a ViT-tiny predictor scoring 300 candidates is latency-bound rather than compute-bound.
