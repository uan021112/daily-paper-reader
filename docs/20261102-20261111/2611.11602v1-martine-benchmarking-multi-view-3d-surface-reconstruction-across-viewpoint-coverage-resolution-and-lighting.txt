Abstract
Controlled acquisition setups are widely used for high-accuracy multi-view 3D sur-
face reconstruction, as they allow sensor configuration, image resolution, viewpoint
distribution, and illumination to be specified. Yet benchmarking remains difficult,
since reconstruction accuracy depends jointly on the reconstruction paradigm,
object properties, and acquisition regime. Existing benchmarks often restrict view-
point coverage, resolution, or illumination, which can bias comparisons towards
methods suited to the chosen setting. Others lack accurate 3D ground truth or quan-
tified image-to-geometry alignment, limiting their use for metric surface evaluation.
We introduce MARTINE, a benchmark for controlled 3D object surface reconstruc-
tion. MARTINE comprises 28 objects, including transparent materials and fine
structures, captured at 9568×6376 pixels from 84 views over a spherical cap under
both single-light and multi-light regimes. It provides object-independent camera
calibration, accurate 3D ground-truth scans, calibration and image-to-geometry
accuracy quantified on test images, and 3D property masks for analysing errors by
material properties, visibility, curvature and fine structures. Experiments across
photogrammetry, classical multi-view stereo, neural implicit surfaces, Gaussian-
splatting-based reconstruction, and multi-view photometric stereo show that MAR-
TINE enables regime-aware evaluation with explicit control over reference-data
accuracy. The benchmark resolves sub-millimetric reconstruction differences, with
a best observed Chamfer distance of 0.058 mm over the evaluated visible surface.

Introduction
3D surface reconstruction is a central problem in computer vision, with applications in cultural
heritage preservation, industrial inspection, robotics, augmented reality, and digital content creation.
Across these applications, the objective is often not only to recover a visually plausible shape, but
to estimate surface geometry with sufficient accuracy for measurement, analysis, or downstream
processing. Multi-view RGB surface reconstruction has achieved increasingly accurate results, in
particular through advances in photogrammetry software and classical multi-view stereo (MVS) [1–5],
neural implicit surface methods [6, 7], and recent multi-view multi-light variants [8, 9]. While 3D
reconstruction can be performed in unconstrained environments [10–14], controlled acquisition setups
provide important advantages when high accuracy is required. Such setups allow the acquisition
conditions to be specified in terms of sensor selection and configuration, image resolution, viewpoint
distribution and illumination.

Existing controlled benchmarks cover this acquisition space only partially.
DTU [15] and
Skoltech3D [16] provide dense but azimuth-limited coverage, rather than broad object-centric cover-
age for multi-view surface reconstruction. While both include several illumination conditions, the
lighting variations are primarily used to evaluate multi-view single-light methods under different
static illuminations, rather than to support multi-view photometric stereo, which requires dense
illumination sampling. This limitation is quantitatively studied by [17], which evaluates the suitability
of existing controlled datasets for photometric-stereo-based surface reconstruction.
Conversely, datasets designed for multi-view photometric stereo combine several viewpoints with
multiple controlled illumination conditions, so that surface geometry can be inferred not only from
multi-view consistency but also from shading variation. These datasets provide illumination condi-
tions suitable for photometric reconstruction, but each covers the remaining benchmark dimensions
only partially, such as viewpoint density, elevation coverage, image resolution, object count, or
surface ground truth. DiLiGenT-MV [19] remains the standard benchmark for multi-view photo-
metric stereo, but contains only five objects, each captured from 20 views at 612 × 512 resolution.
More recent datasets increase resolution or object count, but remain limited in viewpoint eleva-
tion coverage: LUCES-MV [18] contains 12 views at 2368 × 1552 resolution, and EvalMVX [21]
contains 20 views at 2448 × 2048 resolution; all three acquire views at a single elevation. Such
design choices can also bias cross-paradigm comparisons. EvalMVX [21], for example, compares
single-light and multi-light reconstruction using 20 views over 360◦azimuth at a single elevation,
rather than a denser single-light regime such as the widely used DTU benchmark [15], which
provides 49–64 views over approximately 0◦–110◦azimuth. OpenSubstance [20] substantially im-
proves view and illumination coverage, with 270 viewpoints and more than 1000 lighting conditions,
and is a valuable resource for novel-view synthesis, appearance modelling, and object relighting.

A further issue is that the RGB input used by single-light reconstruction methods in multi-view
photometric-stereo datasets is often synthesised from the multi-light stack, for example by taking
the median [23, 24] or average [18] image over illuminations at each viewpoint. This aggregation
has no explicit image-formation basis for single-light reconstruction and is likely motivated by the
mitigation of specularities, since specular highlights are often localised and therefore appear only in
a subset of the illumination stack. These results remain valuable, but they correspond to a hybrid
setting, namely multi-light acquisition followed by single-light reconstruction. They therefore do
not directly measure the performance of single-light reconstruction methods under a single-light
acquisition regime. As a result, current benchmarks do not jointly provide broad object-centric
viewpoint coverage, dense camera-mounted illumination, high-resolution imagery, accurately aligned
3D ground-truth scans, and acquisition regimes that allow single-light and multi-light methods to be
compared under conditions designed for each paradigm.
Beyond viewpoint and illumination coverage, image resolution is another source of ambiguity in
surface-reconstruction evaluation. This is particularly important for mature photogrammetry pipelines
[2, 3], whose patch-based matching procedures can benefit directly from high-resolution imagery.
While higher-resolution images provide more information, they do not consistently lead to lower
reported reconstruction errors across existing benchmarks. As reported in [17], LUCES-MV [18]
and Skoltech3D [16] yield higher errors than DiLiGenT-MV [19], despite substantially higher image
resolutions, 2368 × 1552 and up to 7296 × 5472, compared to 612 × 512. This discrepancy is
difficult to interpret because resolution is confounded with dataset-specific differences in acquisition,
objects, reference scans, and image-to-geometry alignment. It may indicate either that the reference
data are not accurate enough for high-resolution evaluation, or that current implementations do not
fully exploit high-resolution inputs. Distinguishing between these explanations requires benchmarks
with high-resolution imagery, accurate 3D ground truth and quantified image-to-geometry alignment
errors.
A further limitation concerns how reconstruction errors are reported. Most benchmarks report
aggregate scores per object [15, 16, 18–21], although each object combines several geometric
and optical properties. This makes it difficult to isolate failures due to visibility, curvature, fine
structures, transparency, specularity, low texture, or other factors. Prior work has already shown
that improvements on high-curvature or low-visibility regions can be poorly reflected by global
Chamfer-distance or F-score averages [9, 17]. However, such analyses remain scattered across
individual papers and are rarely integrated into public benchmark protocols.
To address these limitations, we introduce MARTINE2, a controlled benchmark for 3D object surface
reconstruction. Its main contributions are as follows.
• A benchmark spanning acquisition regimes. MARTINE comprises 28 objects placed on a
motorised turntable and imaged with a high-end camera mounted on a robotic arm. For each
object, we acquire 84 views over a spherical cap at 9568×6376 resolution under two lighting
regimes. The single-light regime uses a reflecting cone and a softbox flash to approximate
rotation-invariant ambient illumination around the turntable axis, while the multi-light
regime uses 30 camera-mounted LED light sources activated sequentially. This design
provides acquisition conditions suited to dense-view single-light reconstruction as well as
multi-view multi-light reconstruction. The dataset includes challenging geometries and
materials, including fine structures, transparent objects, low-texture surfaces, and specular
or glossy regions.
• Reference data with quantified accuracy. MARTINE provides object-independent camera
calibration, calibration accuracy measured on test views, accurate 3D ground-truth scans,
and easy-to-interpret 2D–3D registration residuals on test images. These elements make it
possible to evaluate surface reconstruction while explicitly accounting for reference-data
accuracy.
• Challenge-level performance analysis. We provide evaluation code and 3D ground-truth
masks to report reconstruction errors by optical properties, visibility, curvature and fine
structures, rather than only by object identity.

Dataset and Acquisition Design
2.1
Overview
Objects. The object set is designed to cover a range of geometric and optical properties relevant to
image-based surface reconstruction. It includes simple textured and matte objects, as well as more
challenging cases with fine structures, deep concavities, transparent objects, low-texture surfaces,
and highly specular materials. Objects are shown in Figure 2(a).

Viewpoints. The proposed viewpoints follow a Fibonacci-sphere distribution restricted to a spherical
cap of 80 cm radius around the object. We acquire 84 views between 20◦and 70◦elevation, providing
near-uniform sampling of the cap. Each view is captured at 9568 × 6376 resolution with a Sony α7R
V camera. Viewpoints are shown in Figure 2(b).
Single-light setup. The single-light regime uses an ambient illumination setup designed to be approx-
imately rotationally symmetric around the turntable axis. This yields nearly view-invariant lighting
for the object volume above the turntable, and provides favourable conditions for standard multi-view
reconstruction methods. In particular, it substantially reduces the view-dependent appearance changes
that would occur if the object were rotated under a fixed asymmetric light source. See Figure 3(a).
The rotational symmetry of the illumination was evaluated quantitatively by analysing intensity
variations along the isophotes of a white 25 cm sphere placed at the centre of the turntable. The
measured standard deviation across viewpoints ranges from 0.5% to 2.7% of the intensity range
(mean 1.4%). See details in the appendix (Section C).
Multi-light setup. The multi-light regime uses a rig of 30 LEDs rigidly mounted around the camera.
This number of illumination conditions is consistent with the quantitative evaluation of the state-of-
the-art photometric stereo method UniMS-PS [25], which reports no improvement from increasing
the number of input lights beyond 30 in their setting. It also reflects a practical trade-off in our
acquisition design, as each additional light source multiplies the number of images, while the dataset
already provides high-resolution 16-bit imagery, dense viewpoints, and two acquisition regimes. This
design provides dense illumination for multi-view multi-light reconstruction. See Figure 3(b).

Challenge-level masks. We provide 3D masks on the ground-truth geometry to support evaluation
beyond object-level averages. The masks identify Lambertian or matte regions, textureless regions,
transparent parts, specular regions, high-curvature regions and fine structures. The material and
structure masks (Lambertian, textureless, transparent, specular, fine structure) are segmented manually
on the ground-truth mesh, as conservatively as possible: only regions whose property is unambiguous
are labelled, and regions where the label was uncertain are excluded from the evaluation. High
curvature is computed automatically from the ground-truth geometry. We also provide visibility
levels, defined from the number of views observing each surface point. Low-visibility regions
generally correspond to concavities or self-occluded areas, and can also be affected by inter-reflections.
Examples of 3D masks are shown in Figure 2(c).
2.2
Acquisition Setup
View acquisition. The setup incorporates a Kuka LBR Med 14 industrial robotic arm and a Noxon
360° turntable with an 80 cm diameter. The combination of these two tools allows us to define
viewpoints derived from a Fibonacci sphere. This specific trajectory is achieved physically through
the ROS 2-managed synchronisation of 10 turntable rotations and precise robotic arm movements
(see Figure 3). The turntable is reset between the single-light and multi-light acquisition sequences.
This step is intended to maintain consistency between the two capture modes.
Figure 3: Acquisition setup. (a) Single-light acquisition regime, using an ambient illumination setup
designed to be rotation-invariant around the turntable axis. (b) Multi-light acquisition regime, using
a rig of 30 LEDs rigidly mounted around the camera. At each viewpoint, the LEDs are activated
sequentially while the camera pose remains fixed.
Lighting setup. We acquire each object under two illumination regimes (Figure 3(a) and (b)). In
the single-light regime, the setup uses a 120 cm softbox flash together with a reflecting cone placed
around the turntable to produce an approximately rotation-invariant ambient illumination around the
object. In the multi-light regime, illumination is provided by a spherical-cap rig containing 30 Luxeon
Rebel LEDs of 220 lm each. The 85 cm-diameter rig is rigidly mounted to the camera, ensuring a
fixed relative configuration between the camera and the light sources across viewpoints. The LEDs
cover a range of lighting directions around the optical axis while remaining at approximately the
same distance from the object. Acquisition is performed in a darkroom, and the LEDs are activated
sequentially following a one-light-at-a-time protocol. A black image per viewpoint is also captured
for the calibration.
2.3
Ground Truth Acquisition
We acquire reference geometry with an Artec Spider optical scanner [26], specified at 0.05 mm 3D
point accuracy and 0.1 mm 3D resolution. As in high-accuracy object-reconstruction benchmarks
such as Skoltech3D [16], specular and transparent objects are sprayed with Aesub Blue, a sublimating
opacifying spray, to reduce optical artefacts during scanning.
The scans are acquired manually rather than with a fixed scanner trajectory. This choice is more
time-consuming, but gives the operator direct control over the sensor path and allows coverage to
be checked continuously during acquisition. It is particularly useful for objects with concavities,
5


thin structures, or self-occluded regions, where a fixed scanner and turntable setup may leave parts
of the surface insufficiently observed. In total, 21 of the 26 ground-truth meshes are obtained with
the Artec Spider scanner. For objects with narrow concavities or thin structures that are difficult to
observe with optical scanning (PLANT, GRID, and SPINE), or for which the opacifying spray proved
ineffective (PAW PAD and BOWL), the ground truth is instead obtained from computed tomography
scans acquired with a NAEOTOM Alpha CT scanner at a voxel size of 0.117 mm; for the WARRIOR
HEAD, the Artec and CT scans are fused. The scans are then segmented using Materialise Mimics
26.0 [27].
This workflow follows a monitored pipeline in which each reference model is inspected manually.
Among the 28 objects, two do not have ground-truth scans. The first is PILLOW, because its
surface texture is partly interpreted by the Artec Spider as geometric variation. The second is COIN,
because neither scanning modality could capture its fine geometric details reliably. Both objects are
nevertheless released with their images, object masks and calibrated cameras, but they are excluded
from the evaluation: all quantitative results are reported on the remaining 26 objects.
2.4
Camera Calibration and Scan Alignment
This section describes the reference-data pipeline used to place all images and ground-truth scans in
a common metric coordinate system. We first present the camera calibration and its validation, then
describe the scan-alignment procedure and its validation.
Camera calibration.
The camera-calibration procedure is designed to be object-agnostic while
providing quantified accuracy. Object-agnostic calibration is important because camera poses should
not depend on the geometry, texture, or material of the reconstructed object. Otherwise, pose errors
could vary with object difficulty, making reconstruction errors harder to interpret.
Several strategies are possible. One option is to rely on the robot and turntable poses, but prior
work [28] has shown that this can be insufficiently accurate at this image resolution. Another option,
adopted by EvalMVX [21], is to place a calibration board below the object. This provides image
features for pose estimation, but is not well suited to calibrated photometric stereo because the
checkerboard can introduce inter-reflections and make object-contour extraction less robust during
the subsequent alignment procedure. A related strategy, used in OpenSubstance [20], places markers
on a vertical cylindrical support below the object, which likely reduces inter-reflections compared
with a planar checkerboard. In contrast, our calibration uses only the textured black fabric covering
the turntable, with the object and background excluded from feature extraction. In our evaluation, this
provides the lowest and most consistent reprojection errors on a common set of test corners, while
avoiding inter-reflections from markers.
Concretely, all images from each acquisition are calibrated jointly in a single Structure-from-Motion
(SfM) run. This includes the single-light images, the multi-light images acquired with all LEDs active,
and the opacifying-spray images when available. We use Segment Anything Model segmentation to
generate turntable masks that restrict feature extraction to the black fabric region. SfM is performed
with Meshroom [3], which builds on OpenMVG [29], using DSP-SIFT [30] features. Additional
details on the calibration are provided in the appendix (Section A).
Calibration accuracy. We compare the proposed calibration procedure with three alternatives, some
of which are representative of existing benchmarks. The first is COLMAP [4] applied to the same
textured cloth. The second uses a ChArUco board [31], analogous to the ArUco-board calibration
used with Metashape in EvalMVX [21]. The third uses an ArUco cylinder placed on the turntable
base, analogous to the ARTag fiducial markers [32] used in OpenSubstance [20]. We use ArUco
markers [31] instead of ARTag markers, as they have been reported to provide better performance
in prior evaluations [33, 34]. Accuracy is evaluated on an 18 × 18-corner checkerboard placed on
the turntable, which is visible in the calibration images but excluded from feature extraction by the
segmentation masks, so that its corners are never used for calibration. We measure the reprojection
error of the checkerboard corners on the 20 views with the largest viewing angle with respect to
the board plane (55.6◦–89.0◦), the same selection for all four configurations, so that all of them
are evaluated on the same 6 480 corners. Our SfM-based approach achieves the lowest reprojection
error, with a mean of 0.542 px (median 0.524 px), corresponding to approximately 0.04 mm at the
nominal object distance. It is followed by the ArUco cylinder with a mean of 0.666 px (median
0.542 px), COLMAP with 0.809 px (0.799 px), and ChArUco with 1.086 px (0.924 px). Note that

Scan alignment. We formulate scan alignment as a contour-based ICP problem in the image
domain.3 The goal is to align the ground-truth mesh with the calibrated image sequence by matching
projected mesh silhouettes to object contours extracted in the images. Since the ground-truth scans
are expressed in metric units, while SfM camera poses are recovered up to an unknown scale, the
alignment estimates a 7-DoF similarity transform consisting of rotation, translation, and uniform
scale. The inverse transform is then applied to the camera poses, expressing them in the metric
coordinate frame of the ground-truth scan and yielding a metrically consistent image-to-geometry
alignment. Target contours are extracted from single-light images using Canny edge detection within
a local search band defined from SAM object masks, with contour-extraction parameters optimised
per object. For specular or transparent objects, we use images captured after applying the white
sublimating opacifying spray, which increases the contrast between the object and the black turntable
near the silhouette. The registration then optimises the similarity transform by minimising the
Euclidean distance from the target image contour points to the projected silhouette of the ground-truth
mesh. The mathematical formulation of the optimisation and the selection of the contour-extraction
parameters are provided in the appendix (Section B).
Alignment accuracy. The alignment is validated on control views excluded from registration. For
each object, we select eight control views by k-means clustering of the camera centres. At each
iteration, the similarity transform is estimated from the remaining views using the object-specific
contour-extraction parameters, and the reprojection residual is measured on the excluded view.

Taxonomy of reconstruction challenges. Existing benchmarks typically report one aggregate score
per object, even though a single object may contain several distinct surface properties, such as matte
regions, low texture, high curvature, transparency, or specularity. As a result, an object-level score
does not indicate which properties drive the reconstruction error. To make this analysis possible, we
annotate each ground-truth mesh with per-vertex labels describing relevant geometric and optical
properties. The seven property labels are defined as follows. Lambertian denotes matte regions
whose appearance is approximately diffuse. Specular denotes regions with strong view-dependent
reflectance, including glossy or metallic surfaces. Transparent denotes transmissive regions, for which
image evidence can be affected by refraction, reflection, or background visibility. Textureless denotes
regions with absent surface texture, which provide very limited local image correspondences. Fine
structure denotes thin geometric elements with small cross-section, such as wires, blades and spikes.
High curvature denotes vertices with large local maximum principal curvature (|k| > 0.75 mm−1),
estimated using the Voronoi Covariance Measure (R = 0.5 mm, r = 1 mm) on a densified ground-
truth mesh (edge splitting > 0.15 mm). Low visibility denotes vertices observed in only a small
number of calibrated views, thresholded at 1 to 3 views in our experiments. This property-level
evaluation is particularly useful for regions that occupy only a small fraction of the object surface.
Errors on such under-represented regions can be diluted in global metrics, such as mean F-score or
Chamfer distance over the full object.

3
Experiments
3.1
Baselines
We evaluate representative methods from the reconstruction paradigms supported by the dataset.
For photogrammetry, we use Meshroom [3], an open-source pipeline based on CMPMVS [35],
and RealityScan [2], a widely used closed-source software package. As a classical MVS baseline,
we use COLMAP [4, 22]. We also evaluate PGSR [36] for 3D Gaussian Splatting-based surface
extraction, and two neural implicit surface reconstruction methods, NeuS2 [37] and Neuralangelo [7].
For multi-view photometric stereo, we evaluate SuperNormal [8] and RNb-NeuS2 [17], using the
state-of-the-art photometric stereo method UniMS-PS [25] to estimate per-view normal maps from
the multi-light images. Method settings are given in the appendix (Section E.4). For the per-object
and per-property analyses, each family is represented by one method: RealityScan, COLMAP, PGSR,
NeuS2 and RNb-NeuS2. For neural implicit surface reconstruction, we decide to use NeuS2 as
the representative method because Neuralangelo takes about 160 times as long to run at quarter
resolution.
3.2
Evaluation Protocol
Image resolutions. Each method is evaluated at up to four image resolution levels: full resolution
(9568 × 6376), half resolution (4784 × 3188), quarter resolution (2392 × 1594), and one-eighth
resolution (1196 × 797). Only the two photogrammetry pipelines (Meshroom and RealityScan)
process full-resolution images: all other methods fail at this resolution for lack of GPU memory and
are evaluated at lower resolutions. RealityScan is run at all four levels; COLMAP, PGSR, NeuS2 and
RNb-NeuS2 are run at half, quarter and one-eighth resolution, and Neuralangelo and SuperNormal at
quarter resolution only. View subsets of 40, 20, 10 and 5 views, selected by k-means, are evaluated
at quarter resolution for RealityScan, COLMAP, PGSR, NeuS2 and RNb-NeuS2 (Table 7 in the
appendix).
Evaluation protocol. Before evaluation, reconstructed meshes are filtered by removing vertices not
visible from any camera, and unreliable regions of the ground-truth meshes (e.g., mounting bases)
are excluded using per-object 3D masks; both are then cleaned and resampled identically. Surface
accuracy is measured using precision, recall, and F-score curves for distance thresholds between 0
and 4 mm.4 Precision (accuracy) is the fraction of reconstructed points within a given distance of
the reference surface, recall (completeness) the fraction of reference points within that distance of
the reconstruction, and the F-score their harmonic mean. Full details are given in Section E.1 of the
appendix.
3.3
Results
Figure 4 reports precision, recall, and F-score curves pooled over all objects at quarter resolution. In
this configuration, the neural implicit methods reach the highest F-scores (77.9% for Neuralangelo
and 75.5% for NeuS2 at 0.5 mm), followed by COLMAP (70.6%), whose precision is the highest
beyond 0.6 mm but whose recall is lower. The multi-light methods do not outperform their single-
light counterparts: RNb-NeuS2 (66.7%) has a higher recall than COLMAP but a lower precision,
and remains below NeuS2, on which it builds, while SuperNormal ranks last (60.8%). For the
present benchmark and implementations, the photometric cues estimated from the multi-light images
therefore do not translate into a higher global accuracy, which indicates that further work is needed to
fully exploit photometric-stereo information in multi-view surface reconstruction.

conclusions. On this object, COLMAP and PGSR degrade under viewpoint sparsity and fail with

sparse-view setting, possibly thanks to the normal cues provided by photometric stereo. Reducing
the image resolution mostly affects the photogrammetry and MVS pipelines: RealityScan drops

Conclusion and Future Work
In this work, we introduced MARTINE, a benchmark for object surface reconstruction in controlled
environments designed to address limitations of existing datasets. MARTINE provides 28 objects,
including challenging cases such as transparent materials and fine geometric structures, and controls
acquisition parameters such as viewpoint distribution, image resolution, and illumination. This
enables reconstruction paradigms to be compared on the same objects under well-defined acquisition
regimes. The emphasis on reference-data accuracy, through object-independent calibration and
quantified image-to-geometry residuals, supports a clearer interpretation of reconstruction errors. In

The current version of the dataset nevertheless has limita-

the manual masks label only unambiguous regions. Future

measured on a continuous scale, evaluation could show how
reconstruction performance varies across that scale. Second,
this work focuses on surface reconstruction. However, the
dataset is directly usable for novel-view synthesis, relight-
ing, and inverse rendering, even though we do not evaluate
these tasks. Third, the time required to acquire and validate
the data limits the benchmark to 28 objects. Each object

workflow takes about one day per object, including image
acquisition, scanning, alignment, and quality control. We
adopted this protocol to prioritise the accuracy of the refer-

by the technical challenges they address, rather than only
through aggregate rankings. We also hope it will promote
more systematic reporting of ground-truth accuracy, camera-

A.2
SfM Configuration Details
Camera calibration is performed with Meshroom [3] using DSP-SIFT [30] features, which improve
robustness to scale changes compared to standard SIFT by pooling descriptors across multiple domain
sizes. Meshroom is used with the ultra preset and the SfM matching is configured in exhaustive
mode with the following parameters: distance ratio = 0.75, maximum reprojection error = 1.0 px.
Meshroom is run directly on the native 16-bit EXR images, preserving the full radiometric precision
of the acquisition and avoiding the degradation of sub-pixel feature localisation that can result from
8-bit quantisation. The resulting bundle adjustment achieves a root-mean-square reprojection error of
0.095 px, used here as an internal consistency metric.

C
Evaluation of the View-Invariant Lighting Setup
To validate the efficacy of our single-light acquisition regime, we conducted a two-stage experimental
analysis focusing on the temporal stability and the rotational invariance of the single-light setup.
As detailed in the main manuscript, this setup utilises a top-mounted softbox flash and a reflecting
cone encircling the turntable. When perfectly aligned, this configuration is designed to produce a
view-invariant lighting environment optimised for multi-view reconstruction.
16


Photometric stability. We first assessed the repeatability of the flash intensity by capturing 50
successive images of a static object from a fixed viewpoint. By computing the standard deviation for
each pixel across the sequence, we observed an average standard deviation of 0.005 on a normalised
intensity scale of [0, 1]. This low variance confirms the photometric stability of the flash and its
suitability for consistent data acquisition.
Rotational invariance. To evaluate the view-invariant nature of the setup, we performed a symmetry
analysis using a high-fidelity white sphere (25 cm in diameter) positioned at the centre of the turntable.
Following camera calibration and the geometric alignment of a 3D spherical model with the image
data, we identified a series of isophotes—contours of equal elevation—across the sphere’s surface.
Given the rotational symmetry of the capture environment, the radiance values along these isophotes
should ideally remain constant regardless of the turntable’s rotation. We extracted intensity data from
41 viewpoints across multiple isophotes spanning the height of the sphere. The resulting distribution,
visualised in the box-and-whisker plots in Figure 11, demonstrates the precision of our lighting
invariance. The observed standard deviations range between 0.0054 and 0.0267 (mean 0.0141).
Considering that a portion of this variance is attributable to the inherent flash instability identified
previously, these low values confirm that the lighting remains largely independent of rotation.
Figure 11: (Left) A visualisation of a single isophote (green) on the calibration sphere. (Right)
Distribution of pixel intensity variations for each isophote across the sphere’s height. The standard
deviation ranges from 0.0054 to 0.0267, with an average of 0.0141, confirming consistent illumination
across viewpoints.
D
Light Calibration
D.1
Multi-light Calibration
Multi-light methods are evaluated on our dataset using normals predicted by UniMS-PS [25], a
neural photometric-stereo method commonly used in recent multi-light reconstruction work and not
requiring calibrated light sources. However, many calibrated photometric-stereo methods require light
positions, directions, or intensities as input. To support such methods, we provide additional images
for light calibration. Following standard sphere-based calibration procedures, we acquire multi-view
multi-light sequences of two registered spheres placed in the object coordinate frame. The first is a
matte sphere of diameter 60.5 mm, which can be used to estimate photometric response and intensity-
related quantities. The second is a glossy sphere of diameter 60.2 mm, whose highlights provide
constraints on light directions. Because the sphere poses are known from our image-to-geometry
alignment, we used them to estimate the light parameters based on two models: an anisotropic model
and an isotropic model. These models are provided alongside the data. Further details regarding the
light calibration are available on the project page.
D.2
Photometric Calibration
To support photometric accuracy and texture consistency, we include captures of a professional colour
calibration target (Calibrite ColorChecker Classic) for each LED and the single-light scenario. These
reference images allow for the correction of light colour temperatures in photometric stereo pipelines
and ensure that the final reconstructed meshes feature accurate and consistent textures.

Additional Information
E.1
Evaluation Protocol
Mesh cleanup. Reconstructed and ground-truth meshes are filtered in the same way, by removing
vertices that are not visible from any camera view or that project outside the object masks. Mounting
bases are removed from the ground-truth meshes and from the masks of the objects, and unreliable
regions of the ground-truth meshes (e.g., the base of the CANDLE or sharp rims) are excluded using
per-object 3D masks. The ground truth of WOOD and SNEAKERS is open (no underside, no interior);
reconstructed surfaces seen through these openings, in per-view masks, are removed.
View subsets. Subsets of N views are selected by k-means clustering of the 3D camera centres (N
clusters, fixed seed), keeping the camera closest to each centroid; all methods using a sequence share
its subsets.
All quantitative evaluations are performed by comparing the reconstructed mesh P against the ground-
truth mesh G. Following standard benchmarking protocols, we report precision, recall, F-score and
Chamfer distance.

Distances larger than 4 mm are discarded from the Chamfer distance; precision and recall use all

To ensure the reliability of these metrics, it is essential that the point densities of the reconstructed
mesh P and the ground-truth mesh G are comparable. A significant discrepancy in density can bias
both the Chamfer distance and the F-score, as sparsely populated regions may disproportionately
affect the nearest-neighbour search. To mitigate this, we implement a consistent resampling pipeline
applied to both the ground-truth and reconstructed meshes prior to evaluation. Each mesh is first
converted into a dense point cloud by sampling each face on a regular grid of 0.05 mm spacing. This
is followed by a spatial downsampling filter with a 0.05 mm radius to eliminate local clusters and
enforce a uniform distribution. This procedure yields, for every object, two point clouds (ground truth
and reconstruction) with the same inter-point distance of about 0.05 mm, providing a standardised
and fair baseline for the final computation of the Chamfer distance and F-score.
The precision, recall and F-score curves and the radar chart pool the point distances of all objects, so
that larger objects weigh more; a failed reconstruction keeps its ground-truth points in the pool, all
counted as missed, which lowers recall and F-score but leaves precision unchanged.
Curvature. High-curvature regions are identified on the ground truth. First, each ground-truth mesh
is densified by splitting all edges longer than 0.15 mm at their midpoint, with the same threshold for
every object. The densified mesh describes exactly the same surface as the original one, but with
a fine and regular sampling. This step is needed because some ground-truth meshes, mainly those
obtained by CT scanning, contain very elongated triangles near which curvature would otherwise be
overestimated.
Then the curvature is computed at each vertex of the densified mesh with the Voronoi Covariance
Measure (VCM) [40], a robust estimator based on the shape of the Voronoi cells of the surface
samples. It depends on two radii: an offset radius R, which bounds the extent of each cell, and an
integration radius r, which sets the size of the neighbourhood over which the curvature is measured.
At each vertex, it gives the two principal curvatures, of which we keep the one of largest magnitude,
k. Each point of the ground-truth point cloud then takes the curvature of its nearest vertex in the
densified mesh.
Finally, points with |k| > 0.75 mm−1 form the high-curvature region and the remaining points the
flat region. The values R = 0.5 mm, r = 1 mm and the threshold of 0.75 mm−1 were chosen after
testing several values on objects of the dataset, as the setting that best separated the high-curvature
regions. The precision, recall, F-score and Chamfer distance are computed separately on each region,
which isolates the behaviour of each method on the most demanding parts of the surface.

E.2
Data Release and Format
For each object, the release provides the single-light images at four resolutions, d1 (full resolution,
9568 × 6376), d2, d4 and d8 (downsampled by factors of 2, 4 and 8 with area averaging), and the
multi-light images (84 poses × 30 LEDs) at d2, d4 and d8. Images are stored as 16-bit half-float
OpenEXR files, together with binary PNG object masks (at each lower resolution, the area average
of the full-resolution mask thresholded at 128 of 255) and an AliceVision sfm.json file containing
intrinsics and poses, with the centre of the top-left pixel at (0, 0). For each evaluated object, the
ground-truth kit contains the mesh, the point cloud sampled for evaluation, per-point curvature and
visibility, the 3D challenge masks and the evaluation masks; the light calibration is shared by all
objects. The release totals 680 GB and is described by a Croissant metadata file. Converters to the
input formats of all evaluated methods, with their pixel-centre conventions, and download scripts are
provided in the code repository.
E.3
Hardware and Acquisition Parameters
The single-light acquisition setup was controlled using a Godox DP1000III-V strobe with a maximum
power of 1000 W, paired with a SmallRig RA-D120 softbox to ensure uniform light diffusion. All
images were captured using a Sony α7R V camera. To maintain consistent depth of field across
varying object geometries, a narrow aperture of f/20 and a sensitivity of ISO 100 were utilised for
all captures.
The exposure settings were adjusted according to the acquisition paradigm as follows:
• Single-light acquisition: To effectively freeze the flash output and eliminate ambient light
interference, the shutter speed was set to 1/250 s.
• Multi-light acquisition: To accommodate the LED intensities, a longer shutter speed of 1 s
was employed.
