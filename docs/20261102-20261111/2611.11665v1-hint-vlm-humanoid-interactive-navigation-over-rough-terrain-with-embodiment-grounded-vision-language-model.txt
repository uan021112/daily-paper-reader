HINT-VLM: Humanoid Interactive Navigation over
Rough Terrain with Embodiment-Grounded
Vision-Language Model
Ziwon Yoon1, Seok Hwan Kim1, Yiyu Chen2, Quan Nguyen3, Lu Gan1, and Ye Zhao1
Abstract—Humanoid robots have achieved significant progress
in locomotion and manipulation, enabling traversal over rough
terrain and richer interaction with the environment, such as
moving obstacles. However, most traditional navigation meth-
ods assume non-interactive environments and focus only on
traversal. While Navigation Among Movable Obstacles (NAMO)
approaches relax this assumption, prior efforts have largely
focused on flat environments and reactive interaction, rather
than predicting object movability and planning ahead. These
limitations become critical in outdoor rough-terrain scenarios,
where robots must jointly reason about terrain traversability
and object movability. In this work, we address NAMO for
humanoid robots in cluttered outdoor rough terrain by proposing
a hybrid planning framework that combines semantic priors
from a Vision-Language Model (VLM) with embodied robot
interaction data through a Gaussian process (GP) residual model.
The GP corrects the VLM-derived movability prior to produce
embodiment-grounded probabilistic movability estimates, which
are then incorporated into hierarchical uncertainty-aware plan-
ning. The proposed approach allows humanoids to jointly lever-
age locomotion and manipulation for efficient and safe navigation,
representing the first solution to NAMO in rough terrain through
predictive movability reasoning and probabilistic VLM integra-
tion. Project page: https://yzwfromk.github.io/HINT VLM/

interactions. In contrast, relatively few studies [8], [10] address

critical for efficient long-horizon planning.
The need for predictive planning becomes more pronounced
in rough-terrain settings, where the robot must reason jointly
about path length, terrain traversability, and object movability.
In such scenarios, successful displacement depends not only
on the object itself but also on the terrain conditions beneath,
which influence the outcome of interaction. As a result, a
humanoid robot must jointly plan rough-terrain traversal and
object interactions based on its locomotion and manipulation
capabilities to determine an optimal navigation path. This
setting motivates the study of NAMO in outdoor, unstructured
environments, which remains largely unexplored.
Fortunately, recent advances in Vision-Language Models
(VLMs) offer a promising approach for inferring object
movability before physical contact. Through large-scale pre-
training, VLMs acquire strong language-conditioned semantic
understanding and generalization capabilities that are diffi-
cult to achieve with conventional neural networks trained
from scratch for language-conditioned navigation tasks [11]–
[14]. Motivated by these capabilities, we propose to leverage
VLMs to infer object movability without requiring large-scale,
language-annotated training data. This enables predictive plan-
ning by allowing the robot to determine which obstacles are
preferable to manipulate before committing to physical inter-
action. However, directly treating VLM predictions as reliable
estimates can be problematic because their generic semantic
priors do not reflect the physical capabilities of a specific robot
embodiment and controller. We therefore correct the VLM
prior using a residual Gaussian process (GP) learned from
robot interaction data, producing embodiment-grounded mov-
ability estimates. Movability is represented probabilistically
and incorporated into uncertainty-aware planning, allowing the
planner to explicitly account for the potential cost of failed
object interactions.

II. RELATED WORKS
A. Navigation Among Movable Obstacles
Predictive NAMO methods often rely on restrictive assump-
tions about object geometry or movability supervision. For
example, the work in [15] considers only box-shaped obsta-
cles, while the authors in [10] approximate objects using geo-
metric primitives such as cylinders and cuboids and estimate
movability through hand-designed geometric heuristics. More
recently, the study of [8] predicts movability from DINO-based
visual features but relies on game-derived data with limited
object diversity and manually assigned scores that are not
conditioned on robot or controller capabilities. Consequently,
estimating the movability of diverse unseen objects from visual
observations without privileged physical information or hand-
designed heuristics remains underexplored. We address these
limitations by combining VLM semantic priors with robot
interaction outcomes rather than relying on manually assigned
movability scores.
B. Rough Terrain Navigation for Humanoids
Many NAMO approaches focus on flat indoor environments.
Extending NAMO to cluttered rough terrain presents an inter-
esting opportunity for applications such as construction sites
and disaster response [16], where humanoids can leverage
both dynamic locomotion and manipulation capabilities. For
such rough-terrain navigation, a large body of work has
studied traversability estimation for legged robots. Early ap-
proaches [17], [18] rely on manually designed rules, while
more recent methods [5] employ neural networks to learn
richer traversability representations. However, these works
primarily assume static environments and do not consider
movable objects, which are common in cluttered rough terrain.
This limitation makes the integration of NAMO and rough-
terrain navigation a particularly compelling direction.

approaches generally follow one of two paradigms: end-to-end
Vision-Language Navigation (VLN), or modular approaches


in which VLM outputs are integrated into a classical plan-

in rough terrain [12]. Modular approaches retain classical
planning while leveraging VLM semantic reasoning [14], [19],
but typically treat VLM outputs as heuristic scores without
grounding them in robot capabilities or incorporating them into
probabilistic planning. We instead ground VLM movability
predictions in robot interaction outcomes through a residual
GP, yielding embodiment-grounded probabilistic estimates for
uncertainty-aware planning.
III. OBJECT MOVABILITY ESTIMATION WITH
EMBODIMENT-GROUNDED VLM-GP
For each target object, our goal is to estimate its movability
as the probability that the deployed robot and controller
can successfully move the object. While a pretrained VLM
provides a broad semantic prior for movability, this prior
does not account for the capabilities of a specific robot.
We therefore develop an embodiment-grounded VLM-GP, in
which the VLM provides the semantic movability prior and a
residual GP, learned from robot interaction outcomes, corrects
the prior toward robot-specific movability.
A. Two-Stage Object Identification and Movability Inference
Inspired by the work of [14], we decompose the inference
process into two stages. First, a pretrained VLM identifies
potentially movable objects from the input image and outputs
their semantic labels. Given each identified object label to-
gether with the image, the embodiment-grounded VLM-GP
then estimates its movability as a probability of successful
interaction. This probabilistic formulation is advantageous in
two respects: it provides a natural VLM prior that can be
residual-corrected using robot interaction data, and it enables
uncertainty-aware reasoning in the downstream planner. These
aspects are described in detail in Section III-B and Sec-
tion V-A.
B. Embodiment-Grounded VLM-GP for Movability Estimate
The movability estimator takes the target object and its
corresponding image as input and predicts the probability that
the robot can sufficiently displace the object while maintaining
stability. In principle, the mismatch between generic VLM
movability priors and robot- and controller-specific capabil-
ities could be addressed by fully adapting the model using
diverse robot interaction data. However, large-scale real-world
collection is impractical, and simulation still lacks sufficiently
diverse, visually realistic, and physically faithful assets [20],
particularly for objects with wheels or articulation. We there-
fore retain the broad semantic prior of the pretrained VLM
and use the available robot interaction data to learn robot-
specific corrections. Specifically, we use the VLM prediction
as the prior mean and learn a residual correction with a GP.
This formulation preserves the VLM prior in regimes weakly

procedure yields approximately 20,000 interaction samples.

path planning, it must be associated with the spatial extent
of the corresponding object in the bird’s-eye-view (BEV)
map used by the planner. We reuse the GSAM object mask
obtained during movability estimation to establish this spatial
correspondence. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the masked depth
pixels are lifted into a 3D point cloud and projected onto the
BEV map, yielding the object footprint for planning.

While such object grounding is generally sufficient for
interactive navigation on flat terrain, rough-terrain settings
introduce an additional requirement. Even if an object is
movable, the terrain beneath it may still be unsafe to traverse.
We therefore reconstruct both the terrain geometry and its
traversability beneath each candidate object. Specifically, we
remove the depth pixels within the object mask and use
LaMa [25] to inpaint the occluded region, providing an es-
timate of the terrain that would be observed in the absence
of the object. The reconstructed region is then lifted into 3D
and integrated into the terrain map. We subsequently apply
STATE-NAV [5] to estimate traversability, represented as the
maximum speed at which the robot can safely traverse each
region. If the reconstructed terrain is deemed untraversable at
the target pushing speed defined in Section III-B, the planner
excludes the corresponding passage even when the object itself
is predicted to be movable.
V. NAVIGATION SYSTEM FOR NAMO
A. Uncertainty-Aware Path Planning with VLM Output as
Probability
Given the estimated movability of each candidate object, the
planner jointly considers travel distance, terrain traversabil-
ity, and object interaction costs. Prior works incorporate
VLM-derived semantic scores into classical planning [14],
[19] and learned object movability scores into interactive


navigation [8], typically through additive costs of the form
c = cpath + wcobject, where cpath is the path cost, cobject is
the object-related cost, and w is a tunable weight. However,
such formulations do not explicitly account for uncertainty in
interaction outcomes or the downstream cost of failure. We
instead evaluate the expected cost of interacting with object i
as
coi = cstart→obsi + ˆpoicobsi→goal + (1 −ˆpoi)cfallbacki.
(4)
Here, cstart→obsi is the cost of reaching the object, cobsi→goal
is the remaining cost after successful interaction, and cfallbacki
is the cost of retreating and reaching the goal via an alternative
route after failure. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the success and
failure costs are weighted by their corresponding probabilities,
while non-interactive detours are evaluated directly by their
path costs. The planner selects the option with minimum
expected cost. For example, the planner may prefer a non-
interactive detour even when an object has high predicted
movability, if approaching the object incurs substantial cost
or a failed interaction requires significant backtracking. All
path-dependent costs are computed using terrain-aware traver-
sal costs and A* planning [5], consistently accounting for
traversability across successful interactions, failed interactions,
and detours.
B. Finite State Machine and System Integration
When the global planner selects a route requiring object
interaction, a finite state machine (FSM) switches the robot
from normal path following to the pushing behavior introduced
in Section III-B. As the robot approaches the target, it adopts
a forward-reaching posture and slowly pushes the object
along the planned obstacle-passing route. During interaction,
VideoSAM [26] tracks the object and updates its position on-
line. Once sufficient clearance is created, the FSM terminates
the interaction and resumes path following toward the goal.
For local path tracking, we employ the terrain-aware controller
from [5].
VI. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
We organize our evaluation around five research questions
(RQs). First, we evaluate the movability estimator to investi-
gate (RQ1) how effective a pretrained VLM is for movability
estimation of arbitrary objects, and how much residual GP
correction using robot interaction data improves prediction
and sim-to-real transfer. We then introduce the navigation
benchmarks, experimental setup, and evaluation metrics used
to investigate (RQ2) what new navigation capabilities the
proposed framework enables, (RQ3) how residual correction
using robot interaction data affects navigation performance,
(RQ4) whether uncertainty-aware planning with probabilistic
movability estimates improves NAMO performance on rough
terrain, and (RQ5) whether the proposed navigation stack
transfers to a physical humanoid in the real world.

2) Performance Analysis: As shown in Table I, the DINO-
based predictor and the LoRA fine-tuned VLM slightly out-
perform the VLM-GP and pretrained VLM on the simulation
validation set. However, their performance degrades substan-
tially on the real-world data. Although the LoRA fine-tuned
VLM and DINO-based predictor achieve lower Brier scores
than VLM-GP on Real-Tried, both learned baselines exhibit
substantially lower resolution, indicating that their favorable
Brier scores partly arise from conservative predictions with
limited discrimination. This suggests that directly adapting
high-dimensional visual representations to simulated interac-
tion data can lead to poorer transfer to real-world observations.
By comparison, VLM-GP retains the pretrained VLM prior
and learns only a residual correction in a low-dimensional
physical attribute space, which is less sensitive to visual
domain shift.
The effect of residual correction is particularly evident on
Real-Tried, where VLM-GP shows a larger improvement over
the pretrained VLM than on Real-All or Real-OOD. This
indicates that the GP provides useful robot-specific corrections
when the test objects are supported by the interaction data. The
LoRA fine-tuned and DINO-based predictors degrade more
strongly on Real-OOD, whereas VLM-GP is less affected
because its residual correction diminishes away from the
training distribution and the prediction reverts toward the
pretrained VLM prior.
Finally, the raw pretrained VLM exhibits particularly poor
Brier performance on the uncalibrated simulation set despite
providing useful semantic movability predictions. Unlike the
raw VLM, the learned estimators are trained directly on binary
robot interaction outcomes, providing supervision on the prob-
ability scale of interaction success. Representing movability
as an event probability rather than an unscaled semantic
score also allows the prediction to be refined or calibrated
using interaction outcomes directly obtainable from robot ex-
ecution. Such embodiment-grounded probabilistic processing
is important for uncertainty-aware planning, since directly
treating raw VLM outputs as interaction-success probabilities
can produce poorly scaled estimates and distort downstream
planning decisions.
B. Experimental Setup for Planner Testing
We deploy various planners on a Unitree G1 robot equipped
with an RGB-D camera. Planners must generate a path and
motion plan that guide the robot safely through obstacle-
cluttered rough terrain while minimizing instability, clearing
objects when necessary, and reaching the goal efficiently.
1) Planner Benchmarks: We evaluate six planners to isolate
the contributions of the proposed framework:
• Proposed: Uses VLM-GP movability estimates for
uncertainty-aware planning while reconstructing terrain
traversability beneath objects.
• Pro-NoRecon: Assumes the terrain beneath objects is
traversable without reconstruction. All other components
remain identical to Proposed.
• STATE-NAV [5]: A non-interactive rough-terrain planner
that treats objects as terrain obstacles while sharing
the traversability estimator and underlying planner with
Proposed.
• Pro-VanillaVLM: Replaces VLM-GP with the uncali-
brated pretrained VLM evaluated in Table I, leaving all
other components unchanged.
• VAMOS [12]: An end-to-end VLN method that gen-
erates candidate paths from language instructions, from
which we select the one with the lowest traversability
cost. Among the tested prompts, the best-performing is
“{object} is {probability} likely to be clearable. Clear
it and continue, or go around it.”, where both variables
are provided by the same VLM-GP used in Proposed for
a fair comparison.
• Pro-NoUnc (similar to [8], [14]): Treats VLM-GP mov-
ability estimates as scalar scores in an additive planning
objective, c = cpath + w · (1 −ˆpoi), where w is a tunable

C. Planner Testing Results
RQ2: What new navigation capability does the proposed
framework enable? We examine the navigation capabilities
enabled by the proposed framework across the planner bench-
marks in Table II. In Garden, the non-interactive STATE-
NAV planner cannot generate a path because movable objects
block every route to the goal. In Construction, STATE-NAV
achieves a 100% N-SR but a higher mean T because it
cannot exploit the shorter interactive route, whereas successful
interactive runs complete the task in approximately 15 s. In
Park, Pro-NoRecon exhibits a notably lower N-SR and higher
T-CVaR0.9 because it more frequently selects obstacle-passing
routes without accounting for the traversability of the terrain
beneath the object. This leads to falls or backtracking when
the revealed terrain cannot be safely traversed, demonstrating
the importance of reasoning about the terrain beneath movable
objects. By jointly reasoning about object movability and
terrain traversability, the proposed framework enables reliable
navigation through cluttered rough terrain.
RQ3: Does embodiment-grounded VLM-GP improve nav-
igation performance? To assess how movability estimation
affects navigation, we compare the proposed VLM-GP with
the vanilla VLM beyond the prediction-level results in Table I.
The vanilla VLM exhibits a substantially higher Brier score,
and Pro-VanillaVLM correspondingly achieves a lower N-
SR in Table II. More importantly, I-SR degrades notably
in Garden and Construction, indicating that inaccurate mov-
ability estimates can lead the planner to select difficult-to-
move objects or avoid favorable interactions. These errors also
worsen tail performance, with Pro-VanillaVLM exhibiting a
higher T-CVaR0.9 in all environments, particularly in Gar-
den. Overall, more accurate embodiment-grounded movability
estimates enable better interaction decisions and improve both
navigation reliability and tail performance.

we compare the proposed planner with VAMOS and Pro-
NoUnc to examine planning formulations that jointly rea-
son about terrain traversal, object interaction, and alternative
routes. As shown in Table II, VAMOS performs notably worse

to reflect limitations in the global spatial reasoning of its end-

Pro-NoUnc exhibits a different limitation. For Table II, we
report its best-performing configuration from an environment-
specific sweep over the weight w. Although this tuning yields
N-SR and mean T comparable to the proposed method,
its I-SR and T-CVaR0.9 are notably worse in Construction
despite using the same movability estimator, indicating that the
difference arises from the planning formulation. Pro-NoUnc
combines path-traversal and object-related costs through an
additive weighted sum without explicitly accounting for the
consequences of successful and failed interactions, so a fa-
vorable weighted score can still lead to a costly fallback
after interaction failure. Moreover, the best weights differ
substantially across environments, with w = 1, 20, and 10 for
Garden, Construction, and Park, respectively, and Fig. 9 shows
strong performance sensitivity to w within each environment.
This sensitivity arises from directly combining quantities
with different meanings and scales, making the appropriate
weight dependent on the environment and route geometry.
By contrast, the proposed formulation treats movability as the
probability of interaction success and uses it to combine the
navigation costs of the corresponding success and failure out-
comes within a common cost domain. Together, these results
indicate that explicit spatial memory and uncertainty-aware
probabilistic planning provide a more robust formulation for
humanoid NAMO on rough terrain.
RQ5: Does the proposed navigation stack transfer to the
real world? The real-world results in Table III evaluate the
feasibility of deploying the proposed framework on a physical


humanoid. Given the limited number of trials, we focus on
Proposed, Pro-VanillaVLM, and STATE-NAV to illustrate how
movability estimation affects navigation decisions. For the
three movable objects, the vanilla VLM predicts an aver-
age movability of 0.33, whereas the proposed embodiment-
grounded VLM-GP estimates 0.90. Consequently, Proposed
favors interaction routes, while Pro-VanillaVLM and the non-
interactive STATE-NAV favor detours. Although all three plan-
ners achieve 100% N-SR, Proposed reduces both navigation
time and distance. We also test VAMOS in two trials, both of
which require manual termination due to imminent instability,
prompting us to discontinue further testing for hardware safety.
To assess terrain reconstruction, we additionally introduce
artificial rough terrain consisting of wooden boards and bricks
beneath the chair and the wagon. As shown in Fig. 8, the
proposed method reconstructs the underlying terrain with an
average height RMSE of 5.0 cm across the chair and wagon
tests, although some sharp terrain features are smoothed. This
reconstruction enables Proposed to identify the underlying
terrain as hazardous and select a detour, whereas Pro-NoRecon
attempts to traverse the same region and requires early ter-
mination due to imminent instability. A video of the Pro-
NoRecon failure is available on our project website.
Finally, we demonstrate the framework in a natural outdoor
environment, as shown in Fig. 7, where the robot selects
a shortcut by clearing a chair blocking the path. Together,
these preliminary experiments demonstrate the feasibility of
the integrated framework on a physical humanoid and suggest
that embodiment-grounded movability estimation and terrain-
aware planning improve navigation efficiency and robustness.
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS
In this paper, we present a framework that extends humanoid
navigation beyond obstacle avoidance to physical interac-
tion on rough terrain. Our embodiment-grounded VLM-GP
combines pretrained semantic priors with robot interaction
outcomes to produce embodiment-specific probabilistic mov-
ability estimates. By integrating these estimates with object
grounding, underlying terrain reconstruction, and uncertainty-
aware planning, the framework evaluates interaction opportu-
nities while accounting for potential failure costs. Extensive
simulation experiments and real-world validation demonstrate
the effectiveness and feasibility of the proposed framework.
Nevertheless, the current framework relies on a relatively
simple pushing strategy without explicitly optimizing object
displacement for subsequent navigation. Scalability to densely
cluttered environments also remains challenging, particularly
when distinguishing multiple object instances and repeatedly
evaluating their movability using VLMs. Future work will
investigate more sophisticated interaction strategies and scal-
able perception and planning to further advance long-horizon
humanoid NAMO in complex environments.
