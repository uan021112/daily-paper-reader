Abstract 

Human videos contain abundant object-manipulation demonstrations, but they do not directly provide motion cues that a robot can follow. We present HOC2R, a Hand-Object 

Contact trajectory extractor for Robot motion that combines frozen DINOv3 and SAM 2.1 features with learned heads for hand-object association and contact localization. HOC2R supports two modes: count-aware automation that detects, counts, and localizes visible con-tacts in untrimmed videos, and fixed-two lo-calization for two-finger grippers when con-tact frames are known. On a 30-task point-annotated benchmark, count-aware automa-tion improves contact-count accuracy from 58.1% to 71.4% and reduces mean localization distance from 19.81 to 10.46 pixels relative to an adapted VRB2 baseline. On seven unseen RGB-D rollouts, a five-task fixed-two check-point reduces mean localization distance from 67.67 to 27.02 pixels. Across six robot evalu-ation sequences, the 30-task fixed-two check-point produces four feasible trajectories and succeeds on three tasks, compared with two feasible trajectories and one successful task for each adapted baseline. HOC2R is competitive in the evaluated single-hand scenes, robust to the hand-color shift, and strongest in the eval-uated multi-hand scenes. These results support HOC2R as a practical pseudo-labeling method for extracting robot-usable contact trajectories from human demonstrations. 

Keywords: Robot Learning, Human-to-Robot Transfer, Hand-Object Interaction 

1 Introduction 

Large-scale robot learning is limited by the cost of collecting diverse robot demonstrations, while human videos contain abundant examples of grasping and ob-ject manipulation. Prior work learns from human play, predicts visual affordances, or transfers motion across embodiments [Wang et al. , 2023; Bahl et al. , 2023; Xie et al. , 2025]. A persistent gap is the interaction ge-ometry connecting observed human demonstrations to robot actions. Boxes and masks identify participants, but not the contact locations needed to derive a robot-gripper trajectory. We study hand-object contact trajectories : visible con-tacts linked across frames of a human demonstration. They provide an interaction-centered cue for down-stream robot learning without requiring paired robot ac-tions. Extraction is difficult because fingertips are small or occluded, background corners produce competing re-sponses, and multiple hands and objects create asso-ciation ambiguity. Changes in hand color and appear-ance, viewpoint, illumination, background, and camera setup further challenge transfer from limited point an-notations. HOC2R addresses these failures by combining comple-mentary frozen visual representations. A proposal head over DINOv3 features identifies likely hand-object inter-action regions, while a learned heatmap head predicts contact likelihood from whole-image SAM 2.1 features. Proposal-box touch scores rerank heatmap candidates. Count-aware automation distinguishes contact from no-contact frames and predicts how many contacts are vis-ible. For downstream robot control with a two-finger gripper, fixed-two localization assumes that consecutive frames containing hand-object contact have already been identified. We call these frames the active contact seg-ment. Fixed-two then returns two proposal-constrained points. This separation is important: count-aware au-tomation is strongest on held-out in-domain tasks, while fixed-two localization is more reliable after domain shift. HOC2R provides a practical starting checkpoint for extracting robot-usable contact trajectories from human manipulation videos. Its principal advantage appears un-der multi-hand ambiguity, where adapted baselines can arXiv:2611.08920v1 [cs.RO] 6 Nov 2026 follow a resting or non-manipulating hand. We further test whether the extracted contacts remain useful for robot control after RGB-D lifting to 3D and geometric safety filtering. Our contributions are: 

• HOC2R, a hand-object contact-trajectory extractor that combines proposal geometry learned from DI-NOv3 features with point localization learned from SAM 2.1 features for count-aware automation and fixed-two localization. 

• evaluations across held-out tasks, domains, and model components that expose multi-hand robust-ness, domain sensitivity of contact-presence and contact-count prediction, and the effect of small but diverse adaptation sets. 

• calibrated RGB-D robot evaluation showing that the extracted contacts can produce feasible post-contact trajectories for grasping, pushing, and two-hand pen motion. Code and trained checkpoints are publicly available. 1

2 Related Work 

Learning from human demonstrations offers a path to-ward scaling robot learning beyond trajectories collected directly on each target platform. Prior work has stud-ied which properties of offline demonstrations most af-fect manipulation learning, showing that demonstration quality, observation design, and training choices can matter as much as dataset size [Mandlekar et al. , 2021]. MimicPlay uses human play to guide long-horizon imi-tation [Wang et al. , 2023], while ImitationNet retargets human motion across embodiments [Yan et al. , 2023]. Paired human-robot data further support direct action prediction in Human2Robot [Xie et al. , 2025], and ATM learns arbitrary point trajectories from video [Wen et al. , 2024]. These methods address policy learning, mo-tion retargeting, or cross-embodiment action prediction. HOC2R instead extracts visible contact locations from human demonstrations without paired robot actions. Affordance and hand-object understanding pro-vide complementary descriptions of interaction. Affor-danceNet predicts pixel-level functional regions of ob-jects, associating image areas with semantic actions such as grasping or cutting [Do et al. , 2018]. At the instance level, 100-DOH detects hands, their contact states, and associated objects in unconstrained images [Shan et al. ,2020]. EgoHOS provides hand, interacting-object, and contact-boundary segmentation [Zhang et al. , 2022]. To-gether, these works show that contact can be represented through affordance categories, detections, states, masks,

and boundaries. These outputs are not interchangeable with contact-point trajectories observed in a particular demonstration. VRB is the closest baseline to our objective because it learns contact affordances and post-contact motion cues from human videos [Bahl et al. , 2023]. Its formu-lation is designed to predict actionable future behav-ior, whereas HOC2R extracts visible contacts from each observed frame. HOWTransfer reconstructs hand mo-tion and contact intervals for grasp retargeting, while ContactFlow uses 3D contact trajectories to condi-tion a cross-embodiment world model [Shi et al. , 2026; Azirar et al. , 2026]. HOC2R instead learns visible 2D contact points from limited point supervision and lifts their midpoint trajectory into robot-frame 3D for post-contact motion. We adapt VRB-style hand-object intersections and EgoHOS contact boundaries to point outputs so that all methods can be evaluated under the same local-ization and robot-trajectory protocols. HOC2R instead trains lightweight point-supervised heads over frozen DINOv3 and SAM 2.1 features [Sim´ eoni et al. , 2025; Ravi et al. , 2024]. This design follows evidence that reusable human-video representations and diverse visual pretraining can support data-efficient embodied learn-ing [Nair et al. , 2023; Majumdar et al. , 2023]. Our ex-periments ask whether such representations can pro-duce contact trajectories that remain useful after domain shift, RGB-D lifting, and robot execution.

3.2 Count-Aware Automation and Fixed-Two Localization 

HOC2R supports two deployment modes sharing pro-posal boxes, heatmap candidates, and proposal-box touch predictions. Count-aware automation handles untrimmed sequences by predicting contact presence and count. Fixed-two localization assumes a known active contact segment and always returns two points for a two-finger gripper. Both modes extract local maxima from the contact-likelihood heatmap. Count-aware automation reranks heatmap candidates using touch scores and emits the predicted number of points. Count-aware automation is intended for untrimmed video. The enclosing-region head first predicts contact presence and then the contact count, one or two, for positive frames. The decoder emits the corresponding number of reranked heatmap peaks. 

Fixed-two localization is intended for a known ac-tive contact segment. It bypasses counting, requires can-didates to lie inside individual proposal boxes, and re-turns the two highest-ranked points. The fixed-two mode therefore prioritizes an uninterrupted sequence of spa-tially constrained predictions over automatic onset and release detection. 3.3 From Image Contacts to Robot Motion 

For transferring predicted contacts to robot motion, we compute the 2D midpoint before depth lookup, sample the median valid aligned depth in a 3 × 3 neighborhood, deproject the point with RealSense intrinsics, and trans-form it into the Kinova manipulator’s base frame using calibrated eye-to-hand geometry. A shared offline filter flags missing depth, workspace violations, and isolated geometric jumps. Short gaps are interpolated, accepted trajectories are resampled to bounded waypoint spacing, and trajectories exceeding fixed repair limits are rejected before inverse-kinematics execution. Every method uses the same calibration, filter, workspace, and controller. Calibration, workspace limits, and trajectory-repair cri-teria are detailed in Appendix C. 

4 Experimental Setup 

Annotated benchmark. We annotate 210 blue-hand human-view frames from 30 Human2Robot tasks [Xie 

et al. , 2025]. These comprise 87 no-contact frames, 39 with one visible contact, and 84 with two visible con-tacts, totaling 207 visible points. The tasks span grasp-ing, pushing, pulling, writing, cloth handling, and re-lated task variants, while the selected frames cover start, pre-contact, first-contact, motion, placement, and release stages. Evaluation uses five task-level folds, with com-plete tasks assigned to training, validation, or testing. In each round, 18 training tasks fit the lightweight heads, six validation tasks select checkpoints and thresholds, and six held-out tasks provide the final evaluation. This prevents visually similar frames from one demonstra-tion from crossing the train-test boundary. The selected frames, point annotations, derived labels, and annota-tion tool are publicly available. 2

External and robot evaluation. Unlike the anno-tated blue-hand benchmark, external evaluation uses 207 annotated frames from seven RGB-D rollouts with natural-skin hands, unseen objects, and changes in view-point, background, and lighting. The pencil rollout con-tains two visible hands. No external frame is used for training. Robot evaluation uses six evaluation sequences in a cluttered lab: two grasp-and-place sequences, two pushing sequences, and two pen-motion sequences with two visible hands. Each sequence starts from estab-lished gripper contact, so it evaluates post-contact mo-tion rather than autonomous grasp acquisition. Figure 3 summarizes the evaluation workflow.

Baselines. We compare HOC2R with two adapted hand-object contact baselines. We refer to our two-point adaptation of the contact-extraction stage used by VRB [Bahl et al. , 2023] as VRB2. It combines 100-DOH hand and object detections [Shan et al. , 2020] with hand-color segmentation, then reduces the resulting hand-object boundary intersection to two centers using a weighted Gaussian mixture model. Similarly, EgoHOS-Point is our two-point adaptation of the pretrained Ego-HOS hand-contact boundary predictor [Zhang et al. ,2022]. It converts the predicted boundary into two rep-resentative points. Neither baseline is retrained on our point labels. We evaluate their released pretrained pre-dictions through fixed point-output adapters and ap-ply the same localization, trajectory-filtering, and robot-control protocols to all methods. This setup measures practical transfer to unseen external and robot data. 

Evaluation metrics. We measure contact presence, contact count, and localization. Contact-presence accu-racy measures whether a frame contains contact, while contact-count accuracy requires the predicted count 0/1/2 to match the annotation. Point F 1@10 uses one-to-one matching and counts a predicted point as correct when it lies within 10 pixels of an annotation. Condi-tional mean and median localization distance are com-puted only when a positive frame has both an annotation and an emitted prediction, so we also report missed posi-tive frames and false contact on no-contact frames. Con-fidence intervals are estimated by resampling complete tasks 10,000 times with replacement and recomputing each metric. For robot evaluation, a trajectory is feasi-ble if it passes the shared pre-execution filter and can therefore be executed safely. Task success requires the executed motion to achieve the predefined task objec-tive. Rejected trajectories are counted as task failures. 

5 Results 

We organize the results around four questions: in-domain contact prediction, sensitivity to training-task count, zero-shot external transfer, and utility for robot motion. 

5.1 RQ1: How Does HOC2R Compare with Contact-Point Baselines? 

On the held-out in-domain benchmark, the complete count-aware model is the strongest overall HOC2R con-figuration. Table 1 reports results across the five held-out folds. HOC2R count-aware automation improves contact-count accuracy from 58.1% to 71.4% over VRB2 and raises F 1@10 from 25.8% to 62.0%. Its mean local-ization distance is 10.46 pixels versus 19.81 pixels for VRB2. EgoHOS-Point has no in-domain result because its pretrained hand detector returns no prediction for any blue-hand frame. Fixed-two predicts neither contact presence nor contact count, so both accuracies are un-defined. Its point scores also include predictions on no-contact frames because it always emits two points. 5.3 RQ3: Does HOC2R Transfer to New Scenes and Multi-Hand Interactions? 

External evaluation includes several domain shifts, with multi-hand scenes providing the most important change absent from training. Although none of the anno-tated training frames contains a multi-hand scene, HOC2R remains effective under this shift and can distin-guish the manipulating interaction from resting or non-manipulating hands. Diagnostic outputs show that the proposal boxes still identify plausible interaction regions and that the learned whole-image heatmap retains useful contact-point candidates. However, contact-presence prediction often suppresses these candidates, causing the 30-task count-aware checkpoint to miss 63 of 129 positive frames and reach only 0.9% F 1@10. This failure motivates eval-uating fixed-two localization when the active contact segment is known. Fixed-two bypasses the unreliable cross-domain contact-presence and contact-count deci-sions and evaluates whether the spatial components can still localize contact. 5.4 RQ4: Do the Trajectories Support Robot Motion? 

The robot study asks whether the image-space advantage survives depth lifting, camera-to-robot transformation, and geometric safety checks. Across six robot evaluation sequences per method, the 30-task HOC2R fixed-two checkpoint produces four feasible trajectories and suc-ceeds on three tasks. VRB2 and EgoHOS-Point each pro-duce two feasible trajectories and succeed on one task. The five-task checkpoint succeeds on both executed tri-als, but four other trajectories are rejected. The 30-task checkpoint therefore provides the strongest robot result despite the five-task checkpoint having the best aggre-gate 2D external score. 6 Discussion and Limitations 

Practical use. HOC2R is intended as a contact-trajectory extractor for downstream robot learning. The released 30-task fixed-two checkpoint is the appropriate default when a known active contact segment is avail-able because it gives the highest trajectory feasibility and task success. For a new environment, our data-efficiency results motivate post-training on a small set selected for diversity across hands, objects, viewpoints, and actions. This is a promising adaptation strategy rather than ev-idence for a universal five-task recipe. 

Where HOC2R helps. The clearest advantage is hand-object association in the evaluated multi-hand scenes. HOC2R can preserve candidate regions around multiple hand-object interactions while assign-ing stronger proposal scores to the contact associated with the demonstrated manipulation. In contrast, the adapted baselines can be distracted by resting hands, background hands, or unrelated nearby objects. HOC2R also remains effective under the evaluated hand-color shift. HOC2R transfers from blue hands in the anno-tated benchmark to natural skin in the external RGB-D rollouts without changing its inference procedure. In comparison, VRB2 requires a different skin-color range, while EgoHOS-Point produces no hand predictions on the blue-hand frames. In ordinary single-hand scenes, HOC2R is broadly competitive with VRB2, but it does not outperform the baseline for every interaction pat-tern. 

Limitations and scope. Our conclusions are subject to several limitations. The annotated benchmark is a small dataset with 210 frames from 30 tasks, and the external study uses one camera system and seven roll-outs. The representation is limited to two-finger interac-tions, and the model predicts each frame independently. RGB-D lifting can fail through depth noise, calibration error, or a midpoint that samples the wrong surface. Robot evaluation covers six sequences that begin from established gripper contact and use open-loop inverse kinematics. VRB2 and EgoHOS-Point are point-output adaptations rather than evaluations of their original ob-jectives. These constraints bound our claims to contact localization and post-contact motion cues rather than autonomous grasping or end-to-end imitation learning. 

7 Conclusion 

We presented HOC2R, a contact-trajectory extraction framework that connects observed human demonstra-tions to post-contact robot motion. Frozen DINOv3 and SAM 2.1 features support four lightweight heads and two deployment modes. Count-aware automation gives the strongest contact-presence, contact-count, and localiza-tion results on held-out in-domain tasks, while fixed-two localization transfers more reliably when the active con-tact segment is known. The five-task fixed-two check-point improves aggregate external localization over both adapted baselines, while the 30-task checkpoint produces more feasible and successful trajectories in the robot study. The main finding is that HOC2R learns useful con-tact localization from limited point annotations, trans-fers across the evaluated hand-color shift, and shows its clearest advantage under multi-hand ambiguity. HOC2R provides a practical starting checkpoint for extracting contact trajectories and pseudo-labeling human demon-strations. Reliable large-scale use will require broader data, temporal contact segmentation, uncertainty-aware filtering, and evaluation across more environments and manipulation patterns.

Invalid-point detection. Using the fixed tool orien-tation and table configuration, we manually validated the Kinova workspace as x ∈ [0 .25 , 0.73] m, y ∈

[−0.33 , 0.33] m, and z ∈ [0 .016 , 0.40] m. A frame is in-valid when it has no 2D prediction, no usable aligned depth, or a point outside this workspace. Depth values below −0.03 m are catastrophic errors. We also flag a depth that differs from the mean of two mutually con-sistent neighbors by more than 0 .10 m, or a 3D point whose residual from its interpolated neighbor position exceeds 0 .06 m when the surrounding displacement is at most 0 .10 m.
