Abstract
Depth perception is fundamental for autonomous navi-
gation, industrial inspection, and mixed-reality systems. It
often operates under strict constraints on power and form
factor. In such cases, monocular depth estimation (MDE)
is often used. Even as recent advances continue to improve
monocular depth quality, conventional frame-based MDE
systems remain challenged by fast motion and high-dynamic-
range scenes. This work introduces eSharp, an MDE frame-
work that addresses these problems using an event camera.
eSharp repurposes pretrained diffusion priors for event-only
MDE and produces accurate metric depths with unparal-
leled sharpness on real data. In contrast, existing event-
based MDE methods often fail to recover fine spatial detail.
Real-world event datasets have only sparse LiDAR depth
maps available for ground truth supervision, making dense
prediction challenging. We address this with cross-modal
distillation, using LiDAR-calibrated dense pseudo-labels
generated from a spatially co-registered frame camera to
train eSharp. Experiments show that eSharp outperforms
prior event-to-depth baselines in both metric depth accu-
racy and spatial fidelity, establishing diffusion models as a
promising foundation for event-driven 3D perception 1.
1. Introduction
Recent advances in 3D imaging and multimodal fusion have
enabled many complex tasks, such as autonomous naviga-
tion [3, 30], augmented/mixed reality [29, 40] and medical
imaging applications [13, 45]. Many applications, such as
drone navigation, are further constrained by utility, form
factor, and hardware complexity. In such settings, monocu-
lar (single-camera) depth estimation (MDE) is particularly
attractive because it requires minimal sensing hardware, lead-
ing to rapid progress in this area [20, 41, 49].

Current MDE methods, while powerful, rely on conven-
tional frame cameras, which suffer in applications involving
fast motion or high-dynamic-range scenes. These cameras
integrate intensity over finite exposure windows and require
a minimum readout time per frame. This acquisition pro-
cess limits temporal resolution and the dynamic range of the
cameras. Rapid motion and extreme changes in illumination
can thus cause loss of edges, texture, and contrast, leading to
inaccurate or incomplete depth predictions. In autonomous
driving, for example, a rapid transition from a tunnel into
daylight (Fig. 1a) or strong back-lighting (Fig. 1b) can sat-
urate bright regions, whereas objects in low-light scenes
may be severely underexposed and visually indistinguishable
(Fig. 1c). In contrast, event cameras [6] are neuromorphic
differential sensors well-suited to these scenarios. They asyn-
chronously detect per-pixel changes in log intensity rather
than capturing complete image frames. Their differential
arXiv:2611.08731v1  [cs.CV]  5 Nov 2026


sensing mechanism and sparse readout allow high temporal
resolution and wide dynamic range (60–80 dB more than
frame cameras), making them apt for depth estimation under
rapid motion and challenging illumination conditions [7].
Despite these sensing advantages, MDE from events
alone remains challenging. Event cameras’ sparse read-
out provides no absolute intensity measurements and can
miss regions with insufficient texture. Previous work has
therefore estimated depth by fusing events with conventional
intensity frames in stereo [10, 31] or monocular [8] sys-
tems, which provide complementary cues for depth percep-
tion. More recent methods perform event-only monocular
depth estimation [1, 15, 24], eliminating the need for a syn-
chronized frame stream but making depth inference more
under-constrained, as scene geometry must be recovered
from sparse event measurements. Consequently, the exist-
ing event-only monocular depth estimators often produce
over-smoothed predictions with poorly localized depth dis-
continuities on real data. Moreover, evaluation is typically
limited to sparse LiDAR-valid pixels, leaving large regions
and fine structures unevaluated. Aggregate metrics, there-
fore, do not fully capture the completeness or geometric
fidelity of predicted depth.
To address these challenges, we propose adapting a pre-
trained diffusion model as the backbone of our monocular
depth estimator with dense pseudo-depth ground truth to en-
able the generation of sharp images. First, the dense metric
depth labels for training are generated by a novel, accurate
process using a frame-to-depth teacher MDE model [49].
Second, we leverage the diffusion model’s strong pretrained
priors over images and depth maps to produce sharp, se-
mantically consistent estimates. In practice, we fine-tune
Marigold [20] and Lotus [14] as the event-to-depth back-
bones. To adapt to this event-sensing domain, we fine-tune
them by replacing the RGB image input with a three-channel
event representation. This is formed by partitioning a time
window into three sub-windows and simply accumulating
events within each. Third, these priors learned from large-
scale natural scenes and depth maps enable strong general-
ization beyond the event-camera training distribution. We
call our proposed event-based MDE framework eSharp 2.
In summary, event cameras overcome the sensing lim-
its imposed by frame cameras, while diffusion models and
dense ground-truth labels bridge the gap in monocular depth
estimation quality between frame and event modalities.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• Structure-preserving sharp, high-accuracy metric depth
estimates from monocular event streams, also showing a
downstream segmentation task on the depth maps.
• Novel dense pseudo depth based training setup for event-

2. Related Work
Monocular depth estimation. Monocular depth estima-
tion is a difficult inverse problem that involves generating
depth maps from a single-camera image. Initial papers in
this field used a multi-scale deep network [5], which was
followed by Monodepth [11] using a convolutional network,
Monodepth2 [12] using U-Net architectures. MiDaS [34]
introduced large datasets and showed zero-shot cross-dataset
transfer, and DPT [35] introduced a transformer-based archi-
tecture for this task. Marigold [20] introduced sharp depth
maps using a pretrained latent diffusion model. This branch
of models was further developed in CH3Depth [25] and Lo-
tus [14]. More recent vision transformer (ViT) based models,
such as DepthAnything/v2/v3 [26, 48, 49], Depth Pro [2],
Metric3D [50], VGGT [41], and MoGe 1,2,3 [23, 43, 44],
have further pushed the state of the art for metric MDE.
Event-based depth estimation. Previous work in event-
based depth estimation has often used either a stereo system
of event cameras or an event-frame pair [10]. Zhu et al. [52]
first predicted sparse monocular depth jointly with relative
pose using a feed-forward neural network. Dense monocular
event depth estimation was first proposed in e2depth [15]
using a recurrent architecture. EMoDepth [53] uses a U-Net-
style architecture with cross-modal consistency from event-
aligned intensity images during training for test-time monoc-
ular estimation. EReFormer [28] proposes the use of a re-
current transformer modeling both the global spatial context
and rich temporal cues from the event stream. More recently,
EDE-Distill [17], DepthAnyEvent [1], EventDAM [54], and
Distil-E2D [24] use cross-modal distillation to transfer spa-
tial and semantic knowledge from an image-based teacher
to an event-based student model. AIMDepth [19] and ES-
Depth [42] build on state space models and perform well
on the real dataset MVSEC [51], but do not show results on
the more recent diverse DSEC [9]. More recently, GLU-Net
uses uncertainty optimization with a global-local event fu-
sion network [47]. RAMNet [8], SRFNet [33], FUSE [39],
UniCT [18], and the full model in HypoDepth [27] perform
joint image-event MDE, requiring a frame image as an addi-
tional input and thus operating in a different sensing setting
from event-only methods.
Recent event-only MDE methods have made substantial
progress, but dense predictions on real-world datasets such
as MVSEC [51] and DSEC [9] often lack the spatial de-
tail seen on simulated datasets such as EventScape [8] and
DENSE [15]. In addition, the strongest reported estimates
are often obtained by scaling relative-depth predictions with
LiDAR measurements rather than by directly predicting met-

3. Preliminaries
Diffusion models [16, 37, 38] are generative models that
generate images by learning to iteratively remove noise from
a complete Gaussian noise. Latent diffusion models [37] per-
form denoising in the latent space of a pretrained VAE [21]
for efficiency. Furthermore, these models [37] can be trained
on text to perform text conditioned image generation. In a
variance-preserving noise schedule [16], given a clean latent
z0, the forward process produces:

 \ alph a _t z_0 + \sigma _t \epsilon , \quad v_t = \alpha _t \epsilon - \sigma _t z_0, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal {N}(0,I), (1)
where αt and σt are signal and noise coefficients, and vt
is the v-prediction (velocity) target. A denoising network
vθ predicts vt from the noisy latent zt, the conditioning
input, and timestep t. An alternative parameterization is x0-
prediction, in which the denoiser directly estimates the clean
latent z0 rather than the velocity. This formulation reduces
prediction variance in dense prediction tasks and is adopted
by Lotus [14]. Although x0 prediction does not inherently
require single-step inference, Lotus couples it with training
at only the terminal timestep t = T, allowing the denoiser
to estimate z0 in a single network evaluation. We adopt this
formulation for a faster version of our method (eSharp-fast),
which therefore uses one denoising step at inference.
Monocular depth estimation aims to infer a depth map

from a single image x ∈RH×W ×C, where
channel C = 3 for RGB images. Given the excellent abil-
ity of large diffusion models in generating realistic images,
Marigold [20] repurposed the Stable Diffusion model to per-
form monocular frame-to-depth estimation. Specifically, it
concatenates the image latent with a noisy depth latent at
each denoising step, and fine-tunes the denoising U-Net to
recover the depth latent, which is decoded by the frozen VAE
decoder to produce a dense depth map. Our proposed event-
to-depth model follows the same latent depth-generation
principle as Marigold, but works with much sparser encoded
event stream rather than dense RGB input image.
Event cameras respond to per-pixel changes in log intensity
rather than capturing images at a fixed frame rate. When the
change in log intensity at a pixel exceeds a set threshold ϵ
(controlled through bias parameters of the camera), an event
is generated and read out asynchronously. An i-th event
is represented as ei = (xi, yi, ti, pi), where (xi, yi) is the
pixel location, ti is the timestamp, and pi ∈{−1, +1} is the
polarity of the intensity change. The resulting sequence of
sparse events forms an event stream. The differential sensing
and sparse asynchronous readout of event cameras enable
high dynamic range and low latency.
4. Method
Event-only MDE is typically trained using events accumu-
lated over a temporal window together with a corresponding
depth map for supervision. In real-world datasets such as
DSEC [9] and MVSEC [51], LiDAR depth measurements
are spatially and temporally aligned with the event stream
and commonly serve as ground-truth depth. These datasets
additionally provide temporally aligned frame images for
3


reference. In MVSEC, the frames are directly from the event
cameras while for DSEC, they are from a separate cam-
era. We train eSharp exclusively on real-world event data
because existing simulators [4] do not fully capture the event-
generation process and sensor nonidealities of physical event
cameras. As a practical example, event cameras operate in
near-continuous time, whereas simulated events are derived
from upsampled discrete frames, limiting their actual tempo-
ral precision. An overview of eSharp is shown in Fig. 2; its
components are detailed in the following subsections.
4.1. Dense Metric Pseudo-Depth Supervision.
As LiDAR sensors provide ground-truth depth measurements
only at sparsely sampled points, getting dense supervision
for real data is challenging. Similar to recent works [1, 24],
we address this gap using cross-modal distillation: during
training, we use temporally aligned frame images and co-
register them to the event-camera image plane to generate
pseudo-depth labels. We distill depth priors from a frame-to-
depth model to train an event-to-depth model.
For each temporal window of the event stream, we obtain
the corresponding frame image xframe from the neighboring
frame camera in the dataset. We first geometrically register
this image to the event-camera image plane (for DSEC). We
use Depth Anything V2 [49] to predict dense relative depth
and the frame-camera intrinsics to back-project it into a rela-
tive 3D point cloud x3D−rel. We then use the frame-aligned
LiDAR depth map to recover metric scale, yielding x3D. Us-
ing the inter-camera extrinsics, we transform these 3D points
into the event-camera coordinate system and project them
onto the event-camera image plane, yielding the registered
frame xe
frame. In contrast to approximate frame-event regis-
tration used in prior work [1, 7], this accurate depth-aware
registration accounts for scene geometry.
Next, we run the same MDE teacher model, Depth
Anything V2 [49] on xe
frame to obtain a normalized dense
relative-depth map ˜drel ∈[0, 1]H×W . We then convert
˜drel to metric depth ˜d by least-squares fitting over pix-
els with valid ground-truth LiDAR measurements d∗(i.e.,
sparse ground-truth depth). For datasets with low-resolution
grayscale frames, the frame-to-depth teacher becomes less
reliable, so we apply quantile matching instead of least-
squares fitting to improve pseudo-label robustness; details
are provided in Supplementary Sec. 8.
The resulting pseudo-labels combine dense RGB frame-
derived scene structure with metric LiDAR measurements
and are used only during training, requiring neither RGB
frames nor LiDAR at inference.

ing U-Net. Accordingly, we double the input dimensionality
of the U-Net’s first convolution from four to eight channels.
Consequently, the diffusion denoising process is conditioned
on event observations to generate the depth latent.
We introduce two variants of our model based on the
pretrained MDE diffusion model we adapt. Our base model
eSharp is fine-tuned on Marigold [20] and operates best in
the native 10-step diffusion inference mode. We also have a
fast version of this model that does one-step inference which
we call eSharp-fast. This is fine-tuned on Lotus [14].
4.3. Two-Stage Event-to-Depth Adaptation
Adapting the pretrained diffusion models to event inputs and
recovering accurate metric depth involves two objectives:
learning a sharp depth map prediction and estimating the
correct shift and scale values for accurate metric prediction.
We therefore separate training into two stages: first, we adapt
and fine-tune a pretrained MDE diffusion model to recover
dense scene structure from event observations; second, we
explicitly correct residual global scale and shift errors for
metric depth prediction.

5. Experiments
Implementation Details. Our latent diffusion backbone fol-
lows the Marigold [20] formulation, and we use DepthAny-
thing V2 Large [49] for pseudo-label generation. Stage I
trains the event-conditioned U-Net for 40k iterations with
learning rate 3e−5, followed by 16k Stage-II iterations
jointly optimizing the U-Net and calibration head with learn-
ing rates 3e−6. We use an effective batch size of 8 and train
all models on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU. Additional
training details are provided in Supplementary Sec. 9.
Datasets.
We evaluated eSharp on two established
event-based monocular depth benchmarks: DSEC [9] and
MVSEC [51]. DSEC uses a higher-resolution event camera
(480 × 640) paired with RGB frames, while MVSEC uses a
lower-resolution DAVIS346 event camera (260 × 346) that
also provides grayscale intensity frames. We use cropped
event representations of 320 × 640 for DSEC and 256 × 344
for MVSEC. Following standard evaluation protocols used in
prior works [1, 15, 28], DSEC contains 19, 217 training sam-
ples and 7, 167 validation samples; we train on the MVSEC
outdoor day2 sequence and evaluate on outdoor day1 and
outdoor night1–3. MVSEC is comparatively worse in image
quality, sample size, and diversity. MVSEC dataset limita-
tions and details of all images from MVSEC and DSEC used
in our figures are provided in the Supplementary Sec. 11.
MVSEC provides spatially aligned events, intensity
frames, and metric depth. In DSEC, however, the RGB
and event cameras have different viewpoints; the temporally
corresponding RGB frame was therefore projected into the
event-camera coordinate system during pseudo-label genera-
tion. Dense metric pseudo-depth supervision was generated
as described in Sec. 4.1.
eSharp vs. eSharp-fast. With its one-step x0-prediction
formulation, eSharp-fast produces slightly smoother depth
boundaries than eSharp, which uses ten-step vt-prediction,
as shown in Fig. 4. The difference is concentrated primarily
around depth discontinuities and leaves the overall scene
structure largely unchanged. Since the aggregate depth met-
rics are computed only over valid LiDAR pixels, they do not
explicitly evaluate the sharpness of the depth map. Hence,
the sharper predictions of eSharp are not fully reflected in the
quantitative metrics. Nevertheless, eSharp-fast runs 6.3×
faster than eSharp and achieves 14 frames per second (FPS)
on DSEC using a single A100 GPU, details are provided in
the Supplementary Sec. 9.

6. Discussion
Our results show that dense, high-quality supervision is criti-
cal for event-based depth estimation. Because our pseudo-
label quality depends on sharp frame images, performance
is substantially better on DSEC, which provides high-quality
frames, than on MVSEC, where lower-quality imagery lim-
its supervision fidelity. We also utilize frame MDE teacher
models because frame cameras perform well under standard
conditions, enabling accurate pseudo-label generation. At
inference, event cameras retain advantages under challeng-
ing conditions such as high dynamic range and fast motion,
where frame sensing degrades. Advances in frame-based
MDE models thus can directly improve event-based methods
through distillation. Recent stereo-based foundation mod-
els [46] can also be employed to generate more accurate
dense depth labels. Our findings also motivate the creation
of newer datasets with synchronized stereo events, frames,
and LiDAR for better dense supervision.
Our method also generalizes to out-of-distribution event
streams, including indoor scenes, indicating that pretrained
diffusion models transfer useful scene and depth priors to
the event domain Fig. 7. We further use simple temporal
binning rather than interpolation or nonuniform binning. Its
strong performance suggests that supervision quality and

7. Conclusion
We introduced a diffusion model for sharp monocular metric
depth estimation from event streams by adapting pretrained
diffusion models with dense pseudo-label supervision. Our
framework includes two models for different speed require-
ments: (a) eSharp, a multi-step diffusion model that pro-
duces high-fidelity depth maps with fine spatial detail, and
(b) eSharp-fast, a one-step diffusion model that is approxi-
mately 6× faster with a small reduction in fine-detail qual-
ity. Our method substantially improves both quantitative
accuracy and prediction quality over prior monocular event-
based approaches, while directly estimating metric depth. On
the higher-resolution DSEC dataset, it also outperforms the
scale-relative depth predictions of state-of-the-art methods,
which are scale-aligned using LiDAR maps. More broadly,
our results demonstrate that pretrained diffusion models can
be effectively adapted to event-based depth prediction even
with limited high-quality training data.

8. Pseudo-Label Generation Details
Dense accurate depth map generation.
The camera-matrix homography used by [1, 7, 33] provides an efficient, calibration-
only transformation between the RGB and event-camera viewpoints, without requiring scene geometry at inference time.
However, as noted by Gehrig et al. [7], this transformation does not provide exact pixel-wise correspondence, since a single
global homography cannot fully account for depth-dependent parallax. Nevertheless, it offers a strong and computationally
inexpensive alignment baseline. We therefore investigate whether incorporating monocular depth can refine this coarse
geometric warping from the frame-camera coordinate system to the event-camera coordinate system and produce more
accurate event-space supervision.
We compare this baseline with several depth-aware warping configurations based on state-of-the-art monocular depth
estimation (MDE) models Depth Anything v2 [49] and Depth Anything v3 [26]. The first column of Table 3 specifies the
method used to warp the original RGB frame into the event-camera image plane. The second column specifies the model used
to estimate depth from the resulting event-space RGB image. “DA2/LiDAR” denotes Depth Anything v2 followed by robust
metric alignment to the sparse event-view LiDAR, whereas “DA3/LiDAR” denotes Depth Anything v3 depth, followed by an
additional robust scale-shift alignment to LiDAR. For the depth-aware warps, the source-view depth is likewise aligned to
LiDAR reprojected into the RGB camera before performing the geometric projection. The 10 m baseline instead assumes a
single plane for the entire scene at 10 m. The “DA3-metric” baseline directly uses the Depth Anything v3 metric depth without
LiDAR-based alignment.
We evaluate all configurations on the same set of 1,000 frames, uniformly sampled without replacement from the DSEC
frames across all 41 in Table 3. Errors are calculated with respect to the event-view LiDAR depth only for pixels that contain
valid LiDAR measurements and valid predictions. Depths are restricted to the range [0, 80] m. For this ablation, pixels
for which either the prediction or LiDAR depth reaches the 80 m upper bound are excluded. Because LiDAR is available
during pseudo-label generation, the same sparse measurements are used for metric alignment and evaluation. The experiment,
therefore, measures the attainable quality of LiDAR-calibrated dense supervision rather than the generalization to held-out
monocular depth.

eSharp Training Details.
We optimize eSharp using Adam with an IterExponential learning rate scheduler, with a final
learning rate (lr) ratio of 0.02 and 200 warm-up iterations. The same optimizer and scheduler configuration is used for both
datasets and both training stages. We set λaff = 0.7, λmetric = 0.3 (details of which are shown in the ablation study in Table 8),
with equal weighting of the scale and shift regression terms. Loss coefficient wcal(k) linearly ramps up from 0 to 1 during
the first 2k iterations of the [latent+metric] optimization. For validation and final inference, we use 10 denoising steps with
vt-prediction for eSharp.
eSharp-fast Training Details.
Training eSharp-fast follows the same overall framework as eSharp, with modifications to
the latent-optimization and metric-calibration procedures. During latent optimization, we follow the Lotus training protocol
and optimize the one-step x0-prediction model using AdamW with a learning rate of 3 × 10−6, weight decay of 0.01,
(β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999), and 200 warm-up iterations under a constant learning-rate schedule. We use ϵ = 10−8 and clip the
gradient norm to 1.0. Before joint latent-and-metric optimization, we briefly freeze the U-Net and optimize only the calibration
head to obtain a stable initialization. We then unfreeze the U-Net and jointly optimize the backbone and calibration head using
Adam, with the same calibration architecture and losses as eSharp. For validation and final inference, we use one denoising
step with x0-prediction for eSharp-fast.

Smooth L1 Affine Regression.
In the metric optimization stage, the predicted affine scale and shift are supervised using a
Smooth L1 loss against the least-squares targets described in the main paper. Compared with a standard ℓ2 objective, Smooth
L1 behaves quadratically for small residuals and linearly for larger residuals, providing stable optimization while reducing
sensitivity to occasional large errors in the estimated affine targets. The affine regression loss is defined as
  \m a t
h cal { L}_ { \ma t hrm {aff}} = \frac {1}{2} \left [ \rho (s-s^\ast ) + \rho (\beta -\beta ^\ast ) \right ], 
(7)
where ρ(·) denotes the Smooth L1 function and (s∗, β∗) are the per-sample least-squares scale and shift targets. We weight the
scale and shift terms equally in all experiments.

of the outdoor_day2 is used for training. Since the frame images also come from the low-resolution event camera, their
