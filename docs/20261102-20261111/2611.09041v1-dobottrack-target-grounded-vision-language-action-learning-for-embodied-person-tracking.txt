Abstract—Language-guided embodied person tracking re-
quires a mobile agent to identify a target from a referring
description and consistently follow the target under egocen-
tric observations. We present DobotTrack, a target-grounded
vision-language-action (VLA) framework that integrates auxil-
iary grounding supervision with efficient temporal modeling. We
systematically investigate five auxiliary tasks—person segmenta-
tion, bounding-box grounding, visual-spatial classification (VSC),
future DINO feature prediction, and future-frame prediction—
and adopt complementary mask and bounding-box supervision
to enhance target delineation and localization. We introduce
Q-identity, a target-conditioned historical visual compression
module that leverages target-grounding predictions to selectively
preserve identity-discriminative appearance features and spatial
cues in compact historical tokens. On EVT-Bench, our 0.8B-
parameter model achieves an average success rate of 77.9%,
exceeding OmTrackVLA by 16.9 percentage points across the
three tasks. We further construct a real-world person-tracking
dataset collected using a quadruped robot, comprising 126
episodes and 596,323 mask-annotated frames. The target masks
enable automatic derivation of bounding boxes and visual-spatial
labels encoding horizontal position and apparent image scale,
providing additional grounding supervision without separate
manual annotation of these labels. Together, our framework and
annotation pipeline support learning target-grounded tracking
policies from simulated and real-world data. Code is available at
https://github.com/Dobot-Team/dobot_track.
I. INTRODUCTION
Tracking a person specified in natural language requires an
embodied agent to determine whom to track while continually
deciding how to move. Unlike tracking in a recorded video, the
agent’s actions change its viewpoint and the target’s visibility,
coupling visual recognition with motion generation [1], [2].
Appearance variation, occlusion, and nearby distractors make
this coupling particularly important: a plausible trajectory is
insufficient if the agent tracks the wrong person. Language-
guided embodied tracking therefore calls for representations
that connect the referring description, the intended target’s
image location and the agent’s future motion.
Vision–language–action models provide a way to learn these
capabilities within a shared model. Uni-NaVid unifies several
embodied navigation tasks, including person tracking [3],
while TrackVLA shares a language-model backbone between
target recognition and trajectory planning [4]. Subsequent
work introduces explicit spatial reasoning, target-identification
memory, and image-grounded referring decisions [5], [6]. Dur-
ing sustained tracking, changes in illumination and viewpoint
can substantially alter the same person’s visual appearance,
making target association and re-identification after occlu-
sion difficult [7], [8]. These variations motivate retaining
target-discriminative information across observations. These
challenges, illustrated in Fig. 1, raise two complementary
questions. First, how can a tracking policy be encouraged to
represent the specified target explicitly, rather than relying on
trajectory imitation alone? Second, how can historical visual
observations be efficiently compressed while preserving target-
discriminative appearance cues and scene context essential for
reliable person tracking?
We address these questions through target-centric percep-
tual supervision and identity-aware history compression. We
present DobotTrack, a target-grounded VLA framework built
on InternVL3 [9]. We systematically investigate person seg-
mentation, bounding-box grounding, visual-spatial classifica-
tion, future DINO feature prediction, and future-frame pre-
diction as auxiliary tasks to improve tracking policy learning.
The final model combines mask and box supervision to couple
fine-grained foreground delineation with explicit target local-
ization. Building on this grounding, Q-identity compresses
historical observations into compact tokens that retain identity-
discriminative appearance and spatial context. The language
model captures temporal dependencies for trajectory predic-
tion from the chronological sequence of compressed historical
and current-frame tokens.
The model processes an appearance-based instruction to-
gether with a temporal window of RGB observations. The
learned mask token and box token precede the waypoint
tokens in the causal sequence, allowing the waypoint states
to attend to their target-related representations. Task-specific
heads decode the resulting hidden states into the target mask,
bounding box, and future trajectory in one forward pass.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Language-Guided Embodied Visual Tracking
Embodied visual tracking couples target perception with
active motion control. Follow Anything integrates open-set
detection and tracking into a robotic following system [1].
RSPT learns a structure-aware representation by reconstructing
the surroundings and predicting target motion [2], while Zhong
et al. combine visual foundation models for target segmenta-
tion with offline reinforcement learning for control [11]. These
methods establish the importance of target-aware representa-
tions for active tracking.
Recent VLA approaches learn language-conditioned per-
ception and action within shared models. Uni-NaVid supports
multiple navigation tasks, including person tracking [3]. Track-
VLA couples recognition-oriented language modeling with an
anchor-based diffusion trajectory decoder [4]. TrackVLA++
introduces Polar-CoT spatial reasoning and a gated Target
Identification Memory [5]. ReferTrack selects the referred
target from indexed candidate boxes, predicts waypoints con-
ditioned on this decision, and injects historical box information
into visual context [6]. Our work systematically examines
target-centric auxiliary tasks for tracking policy learning and
combines mask supervision for learning target representations
with box supervision for spatial localization. We further inves-
tigate how target-grounding predictions can guide historical
visual compression for subsequent action prediction.


B. Visual Grounding and Target-Centric Supervision
Visual grounding associates language with image regions.
Grounding DINO uses language-conditioned visual represen-
tations for open-set detection [12], while Kosmos-2 represents
grounded text with location tokens [13]. At pixel resolu-
tion, SAM 2 supports promptable segmentation in images
and videos [14]. LISA connects a segmentation token in a
multimodal language model to mask prediction through an
embedding-as-mask formulation [15]. These works provide
relevant foundations for language-conditioned localization and
segmentation; token-conditioned mask decoding is not specific
to embodied tracking.
DobotTrack uses grounding as supervision for a tracking
policy, rather than treating localization as the final task. Mask
prediction and box regression supervise the spatial extent of
the referred person, while waypoint regression supervises the
agent’s motion. The real-world structured VSC labels further
express box-derived position and scale as categorical labels.
Because these annotations originate from the same masks, their
role is to provide different learning objectives over shared
target information, not to introduce independent geometric
measurements. We compare these auxiliary objectives under
a shared tracking framework and combine mask and box
supervision in the final model.
C. Identity-Preserving Temporal Memory
Maintaining target identity over time is essential for person
tracking, particularly when pedestrians cross paths, undergo
appearance changes, or temporarily disappear. Visual tracking
and video segmentation provide relevant mechanisms for tem-
poral association. Online person re-identification [8] addresses
appearance changes and distractors during robot following.
MOTR [16] propagates and updates track queries across
frames to represent individual objects over time. XMem [17]
combines memory stores operating at different temporal scales
and consolidates working-memory features into compact long-
term representations. SAM 2 [14] uses streaming memory to
support object segmentation in videos. While these methods
primarily address temporal consistency at the perception level,
embodied tracking additionally requires historical target infor-
mation to inform the agent’s movement.
Recent embodied models incorporate several forms of tem-
poral memory. TrackVLA++ [5] maintains target identity
through gated memory updates, ReferTrack [6] embeds his-
torical target boxes into visual context, and VLingNav [18]
combines linguistic summaries with visual features in persis-
tent memory. These developments motivate further investiga-
tion into which information should be retained under a lim-
ited memory budget. For long-video understanding, LLaMA-
VID [19] represents each frame with compact context and
content tokens, while LongVU [20] adaptively reduces spatial
and temporal visual redundancy. BLIP-2 [21] provides a
query-based mechanism, Q-Former, for extracting compact
visual representations. Building on query-based compression,
Q-identity places target conditioning within the formation
of compact historical visual tokens. Target-region features
condition learned compression queries, which also aggregate
full-image context under a fixed token budget. The module is
designed to retain target appearance and spatial cues together
with scene context, while the language model performs tempo-
ral reasoning over the chronological sequence of compressed
frames.
D. Real-World Data for Person Tracking
The data associated with TrackVLA and EVT-Bench sup-
port large-scale simulated tracking and recognition learn-
ing [4]. TPT-Bench complements simulation with long-term,
robot-egocentric target-person tracking sequences collected
using a sensor-equipped cart [10]. In addition to target boxes, it
includes odometry and other synchronized modalities, making
it relevant to both temporal perception and robot-following
research.
Our dataset is collected with a quadruped and is organized
around sequence-level VLA supervision: observation histories
and target descriptions are paired with future robot waypoints
and current-frame grounding annotations.

ℓand Ot, the agent predicts a sequence of P = 8 future

Model overview. As shown in Fig. 2, our model builds on the
InternVL3 [9] vision-language backbone. The visual encoder
and multimodal projector map each RGB observation ot to

representations through causal attention. Task-specific heads
jointly predict the current target mask, bounding box, visibility,
and future egocentric waypoints in a single forward pass.
During online inference, cached historical box and visibility

history compression.

B. Target-Centric Supervision and Trajectory Prediction
We systematically investigate five auxiliary tasks: person
segmentation, bounding-box grounding, visual-spatial classi-
fication, future DINO feature prediction, and future-frame
prediction. The final model jointly learns person segmentation,
bounding-box grounding, and trajectory prediction. Segmen-
tation supervision delineates the target representation, while
box supervision captures its overall location and extent. These
complementary objectives encourage the shared backbone to
capture the target’s appearance and spatial extent for trajec-
tory prediction. In this section, we focus on two auxiliary
tasks: person segmentation and bounding-box grounding. The
remaining auxiliary tasks are introduced and comparatively
evaluated in the ablation study section.
Person segmentation. Let hmask denote the contextualized
hidden state of the mask token. The segmentation head com-
bines this state with the dense current-frame features to predict
the referred person’s foreground probability map:
ˆmt = σ(fmask(Xt, hmask)) ,
(1)
where fmask consists of trainable visual and prompt projec-
tions followed by a pretrained SAM2 mask decoder [14], and σ
denotes the sigmoid function. The projections map the dense
current-frame features and the mask-token hidden state into
image embeddings and a sparse prompt, respectively. This
branch provides explicit pixel-level supervision for the target
representation used by subsequent waypoint tokens.
Bounding-box grounding. For the bounding-box grounding
task, a box head maps the grounding token’s hidden state hbox
to normalized image-space coordinates:
ˆbt = fbox(hbox),
(2)
where ˆbt = (ˆcx, ˆcy, ˆw, ˆh) represents the normalized box cen-
ter, width, and height. The head also predicts target visibility.
This task supervises the referred person’s location and spatial
extent.
Trajectory prediction. The action head maps the hidden state
hp of the p-th waypoint token to its corresponding egocentric
waypoint:
ˆwp = fact(hp),

The current target mask, bounding box, and future waypoints
are obtained in the same forward pass.
C. Q-identity: Target-Conditioned Visual History Compres-
sion
Visual history supports target association and motion rea-
soning, but retaining dense tokens from every frame increases
the language model’s input length. Motivated by compact
video representations [19], [20] and query-based compres-
sion [21], we introduce Q-identity, which compresses each his-
torical frame into Nq target-conditioned tokens. The compres-


sor first incorporates localized target features through target–
conditioned self-attention, then aggregates full-image features
through cross-attention. This design gives the compressed
representation access to both target appearance and location
cues and the scene context needed for planning.
Target context construction. Let τ index a historical frame,
and let Xτ
∈RN×d denote its visual features after the
visual encoder and multimodal projector. We linearly project
these features into dc dimensions and add spatial embeddings
derived from normalized 2D grid coordinates, obtaining full-
image features Fτ ∈RN×dc.
During training, target regions are determined from per-
turbed ground-truth boxes; at inference, we use cached box
and visibility predictions from earlier decisions. The visual
tokens corresponding to each selected region form the target
context Tτ, providing target-specific appearance and spatial
cues, while Fτ retains the surrounding scene information.
When no valid target region is available, we use a learned
no-target token.

Predicted mask logits are bilinearly resized to the ground-truth

1. The IoU-score term is a mean squared error against the
detached IoU of the thresholded predicted mask and ground

a positive label when the ground-truth mask is nonempty.
Bounding-box grounding. The grounding head predicts a
normalized bounding box (cx, cy, w, h) and target visibility.
Its objective is
Lbox = βregLSmoothL1 + βiouLGIoU + βvisLvis.
(9)
The loss weights are set to (βreg, βiou, βvis) = (5, 2, 1).
Coordinate regression and generalized IoU loss are averaged
over samples with visible targets. Visibility is supervised using
binary cross-entropy across the batch, with labels determined
by whether the target mask is nonempty. Trajectory and mask
objectives are averaged over the batch.
We freeze the visual encoder and jointly optimize the
multimodal projector, language model, Q-identity, mask head,
grounding head, and action head. During training, input boxes
are perturbed with probability 0.8, using center offsets pro-
portional to box size and multiplicative size perturbations with
scale 0.1. We also compress the current frame with probability
0.3, using a box derived from its ground-truth mask. The
mask decoder always receives the uncompressed current-frame
features.
Online inference. At each decision step, the model receives
the instruction and an updated RGB window. Past frames
are compressed using their cached bounding-box predictions,
while the current frame retains its dense visual tokens. One for-
ward pass predicts future waypoints, the current target mask,
bounding box, and visibility. The current box and visibility
prediction are then cached for use when that observation
becomes a historical frame in a later decision. Predictions
with visibility below 0.5, and frames without a cached pre-
diction, use the no-target fallback. This ordering ensures that
compression uses only predictions available before the current
decision.

IV. DATASET
Our training data combine simulation data with a real-world
robot-tracking dataset. The two sources are complementary:
simulation provides large-scale and controllable supervision
over diverse tracking conditions, while the real-world data
expose the model to realistic human appearances, illumination
changes, occlusions, distractors and camera motion. Both
sources provide trajectory, segmentation-mask, and bounding-
box annotations, along with structured visual-spatial classifi-
cation labels. These annotations and labels support multi-task
VLA training.
A. Simulation Dataset
Data source. Our simulation data are derived from EVT-
Bench [4], which is built on Habitat 3.0 using indoor scenes
from HM3D and Matterport3D. Animated human avatars serve
as tracking targets and distractors. We use the three tracking
settings defined by EVT-Bench: Single-Target Tracking (STT),
Distracted Tracking (DT), and Ambiguous Tracking (AT). The
resulting simulation training set contains approximately 1.8
million sequence samples with an STT:DT:AT ratio of 1 : 2 :
1. Each sample provides forward-facing RGB observations,
an appearance-based target instruction, an egocentric expert
trajectory, and target-centric auxiliary annotations.
Target-centric annotation. Target masks are extracted from
simulator panoptic observations using the semantic identifier
of the tracked person. Bounding boxes are the tightest axis-
aligned rectangles enclosing visible foreground masks, nor-
malized as (cx, cy, w, h). Bounding boxes are used to derive
left/center/right and small/medium/large VSC labels, using
horizontal center thresholds of 1/3 and 2/3 and normalized
area thresholds of 0.05 and 0.20, respectively. Valid metadata
yield near/medium/far distance labels at 1.5 and 3.0 m. Binary
visibility labels indicate whether the target is visible in the
current camera view.
B. Real-World Dataset
Real-world robot data collection. To reduce the sim-to-real
gap, we construct a real-world human-tracking dataset using
a quadruped mobile robot operating in indoor offices, outdoor
open environments, and public pedestrian areas. The dataset
contains approximately 10.2 hours of synchronized robot-
following data, including 596,669 original RGB frames. After
pose alignment and trajectory preprocessing, these recordings
produce 565,994 final training samples. The dataset comprises
126 person-tracking episodes. We use the rectified front-facing
RGB stream at a resolution of 960 × 540. Robot-following
motions are generated through both human teleoperation and
an expert model. RGB observations are timestamp-aligned
with robot states and poses. Future egocentric trajectories are
recovered from the aligned robot motion. Each sequence is
additionally associated with a natural-language description of
the designated tracking target, including appearance attributes
such as clothing, hairstyle, and carried objects. The dataset
contains 33 distinct target appearance profiles captured under
diverse tracking conditions.

Diverse real-world tracking conditions. The real-world
dataset covers four representative tracking conditions: indoor
single-person tracking, indoor tracking with occlusion, outdoor
open-area tracking, and complex outdoor street tracking. The
dataset includes approximately 5.68 hours of indoor recordings
and 4.53 hours of outdoor recordings.
Of these recordings, approximately 1 hour contains nearby
or visually similar distractors. These recordings introduce
challenging cases such as partial occlusion, people crossing
paths, temporary target ambiguity, and target re-identification.
Together with the dedicated occlusion and complex-street
scenarios, these data expose the model to realistic visual and
behavioral variations that are difficult to reproduce faithfully
in simulation.
Target-centric annotation. We construct dense target-centric
annotations for the real-world recordings using a semi-
automatic annotation pipeline. Qwen3.5 [31] is first used to
localize the described target and generate a target bounding
box, which is then provided as a prompt to SAM3 [32] to
obtain the corresponding segmentation mask. The generated
annotations are then manually reviewed and corrected to
address localization and segmentation errors.
The final annotations provide 596,323 dense target masks
and corresponding bounding boxes. Bounding boxes are rep-
resented in the same normalized (cx, cy, w, h) format as in the
simulation data.
We further derive lightweight structured VSC supervision
from the target annotations. Horizontal position is categorized
as left, center, or right, and apparent target scale as small,
medium, or large using the same definitions as in the simu-
lation dataset. Metric target distance is not reliably available
in the real-world recordings. Real-world VSC supervision is
restricted to horizontal position and apparent scale.
V. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
Benchmarks and data. We evaluate language-guided person
tracking on EVT-Bench [4], using its STT, DT, and AT settings
to assess single-target tracking, resistance to distractors, and
target disambiguation, respectively. Training uses the simula-
tion and real-world data described in Sec. IV.
Evaluation metrics. Following the EVT-Bench protocol [4],
we report success rate (SR), tracking rate (TR), and collision
rate (CR) separately for each setting. SR measures the fraction
of episodes ending with the agent facing the target at a dis-
tance of 1–3 m. TR measures the fraction of simulation steps
satisfying the benchmark’s tracking condition. CR measures
the fraction of episodes terminated by a collision with the
target person. Higher SR and TR and lower CR indicate better
performance.
Implementation details. The policy takes an appearance-
based instruction and H = 8 RGB observations sampled
at an interval of ∆= 0.2 s and resized to 448 × 448.
It predicts P
= 8 future egocentric waypoints at 10 Hz,
spanning 0.8 s. Each frame supplies N = 256 visual tokens;
the target-conditioned compressor uses three attention blocks

to retain Nq = 16 tokens per historical frame. The target-
conditioned ablation holds all settings except guidance fixed;
the token-budget comparison evaluates 256-token pooling, 16-
token pooling, and 16-token Q-identity. Dense current-frame
features remain available to the mask head. We train for about
28,000 optimization steps on eight NVIDIA B300 GPUs with
a global batch size of 256 (32 per GPU), using AdamW and
BF16 mixed precision. The visual encoder is frozen, while
the multimodal projector, language model, Q-identity, and task
heads are jointly optimized. We use peak learning rates of
2 × 10−5 for the language model and SAM2 mask decoder,
and 4 × 10−5 for the remaining trainable parameters, with
a weight decay of 10−3. Learning rates follow a one-cycle
schedule with 100 warmup steps and cosine annealing.
Real-world
deployment. The real-world platform is a
quadruped robot equipped with front-facing cameras, a LiDAR
sensor, and an onboard Orin Nano computer, as shown in
Fig. 4. The tracking policy uses an RGB observation window
and a target description to predict egocentric waypoints for the
robot’s motion controller.

C. Ablation Study
We examine the auxiliary supervision, target-conditioned
historical compression, and real-world training data used in
DobotTrack. Unless otherwise stated, experiments use the 0.8B
backbone and 16 tokens per historical frame.
Auxiliary supervision. We evaluate five auxiliary tasks pro-
viding different forms of spatial and temporal supervision.
VSC classifies spatial attributes of the target person, including
image-plane position (left, center, or right) and distance to
the agent (near, medium, or far). Future DINO feature pre-
diction regresses visual representations extracted by a frozen
DINO encoder from future observations, providing feature-
level supervision for anticipating scene changes. Future frame
prediction reconstructs future RGB observations, providing
pixel-level supervision of future visual appearance. Each task
is evaluated as an auxiliary objective alongside trajectory pre-
diction. Table II compares auxiliary tasks for tracking policy
learning. Structured VSC improves DT SR over trajectory-
only training, showing that categorical target attributes provide
useful supervision. Box grounding achieves the same SR as
VSC, while mask supervision yields higher SR and lower
CR. Joint mask and box supervision further raises SR to
63.7%, supporting their complementary roles in learning target
representations and spatial localization.
Motivated by latent-space video prediction in DINO-
world [33], we also evaluate DINO future-feature prediction.
It improves SR over the trajectory-only baseline, but achieves
a lower SR and a higher collision rate than the model trained
with joint mask and box supervision. This comparison sug-
gests that explicit target-level supervision is more effective
than self-supervised future-feature prediction in our tracking
setting.

VI. CONCLUSION
We
presented
DobotTrack,
a
target-grounded
vision-
language-action
framework
for
language-guided
embod-
ied person tracking. The framework combines mask and
bounding-box supervision with Q-identity, which uses target-
region cues and full-image context to compress historical ob-
servations for trajectory prediction. On EVT-Bench, our 0.8B
model achieves success rates of 90.2%, 72.5%, and 70.9%
on STT, DT, and AT, respectively. Under mask supervision
and the same budget of 16 tokens per historical frame, Q-
identity improves DT and AT success rates over pooling by
6.3 and 8.6 percentage points, with a 2.4 ms increase in
P95 inference latency on Orin Nano. We also construct a
quadruped-collected dataset comprising 126 person-tracking
episodes and 596,323 mask-annotated frames; incorporating
these real-world data further improves tracking success on the
simulation benchmark. These findings support target-centric
supervision and target-conditioned history compression as
effective components of compact embodied tracking policies.
The current design relies on the quality of cached grounding
predictions and a finite observation window. Future work will
investigate robustness to incorrect target associations and pro-
longed occlusion, and quantify closed-loop performance across
a broader range of real-world scenes and target identities.
