## Abstract 

Understanding three-dimensional physical structure has become essential for vision-language-action models (VLAs). However, existing approaches either rely on ex-plicit geometric inputs at inference time or adopt a single visual pathway, potentially compromising the visual-semantic knowledge inherited from pre-trained models. We present SPARK-VLA, Spa tial Representation distillation and Knowledge rout-ing for VLA s, a preserve-specialize-route framework for spatially adapting VLAs without requiring external 3D inputs or experts at inference. SPARK-VLA consists of two key components: (1) spatial representation distillation, which transfers 3D understanding from a frozen 3D expert to a LoRA-adapted visual branch by aligning attention responses while preserving the original visual pathway, and (2) a lightweight knowledge router, which selectively forwards action-relevant visual tokens to the language model using routing supervision derived from action-loss gradients. Across LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, CALVIN, and real-world manipu-lation, SPARK-VLA achieves strong performance with only a 0.5B language backbone and approximately 53M trainable parameters, providing an effective and parameter-efficient approach to 3D-aware VLA learning. Code is available at 

https://github.com/park-jungin/spark-vla .

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) learn to generate robot actions from visual observations and language instructions, offering a unified approach to perception, instruction understanding, and control. Recent systems have benefited from large-scale vision-language pre-training, robot demon-strations, and increasingly expressive action decoders [59, 20, 5, 48, 56]. Nevertheless, recognizing the objects involved in an instruction does not necessarily provide the geometric information required to manipulate them. Reaching, grasping, and placing an object also depend on its position, orientation, extent, and spatial relationship to the robot and surrounding objects. Even with multiple camera views, aggregating 2D visual features insufficiently yields geometrically consistent representations across viewpoints [52, 13, 12]. In other words, understanding and acting in the physical world is a fundamental capability for embodied intelligence, requiring joint modeling of perception, language, action, and three-dimensional spatial structure .Recent studies have increasingly suggested that spatial awareness remains a key bottleneck for reliable robot manipulation and have investigated explicit 3D geometric priors, such as depth maps [3], point clouds [42], or other geometric cues [39, 57], and implicit alignment between 2D and 3D representations [26, 21] with pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs). Despite their promise, both directions have important limitations: (1) Explicit 3D integration typically depends on extra sensing or reconstructed geometric inputs that are not uniformly available across robot platforms and datasets, and (2) implicit alignment often distorts the original 2D representation space, which can weaken the perceptual capability inherited from strong vision-language backbones [58, 27].

We propose SPARK-VLA ( Spa tial Representation distillation and Knowledge routing for VLA s), a framework that enhances the 3D spatial understanding of a pre-trained VLM while preserving its original perceptual capability. Specifically, SPARK-VLA establishes a spatial pathway with low-rank adapters [15] parallel to the original visual pathway within the pre-trained vision backbone. During training, the original pathway remains frozen, while the adapted pathway learns geometry-aware representations from a pre-trained 3D experts ( e.g. , VGGT [45]) through attention response distillation [36]: the adapted spatial query is shared with the expert’s keys and values, allowing the spatial pathway to learn the geometry-aware information aggregated for the query. SPARK-VLA further introduces an action-driven knowledge router to combine these complementary pathways. The router predicts instruction-conditioned scores over generic and spatial tokens and selectively forwards informative tokens to the language model, determining whether each region is represented by generic tokens, spatial tokens, both, or neither. To train the router without manually annotated supervision, we derive routing targets from the gradients of the action loss with respect to the router gates that indicate the usefulness of the tokens for action prediction. Together, preservation of the original pathway, specialization through spatial distillation, and action-driven routing provide a unified approach to integrating geometric knowledge into pre-trained VLAs. Experimental results on simulation benchmarks, including LIBERO [29] and CALVIN [33], show that SPARK-VLA with only a 0.5B language backbone and 53M trainable parameters achieves performance comparable to or even better than substantially larger VLAs, as shown in Figure 1. In addition, SPARK-VLA generalizes robustly to challenging settings such as LIBERO-Plus [10] without any additional training. Experiments in real-world environments further demonstrate that our model achieves high sample efficiency under data-constrained conditions, outperforming much larger baselines. 

## 2 Related Work 

Vision-language-action models (VLAs). VLAs have emerged as a promising paradigm for gen-eralist robot learning by jointly modeling visual perception, language understanding, and action generation [9, 59, 20, 11, 24, 35, 5]. Recent advances have explored diverse action generation strategies, including autoregressive action tokenization [20, 38], diffusion-based policies [8, 48, 14], and flow matching [5]. Alongside these developments, several studies have investigated efficient adaptation and compact architectures [19, 46, 41, 16], as well as reasoning and long-horizon manipu-lation [56, 58, 47]. Despite their progress, transferring visual-semantic knowledge to robotic control does not necessarily provide the fine-grained geometric information required for precise manipulation. We addresses this gap through implicit spatial knowledge adaptation. 

Spatial understanding in VLAs. Spatial reasoning is essential for robotic manipulation, particularly when actions depend on object geometry, relative positions, and physical interactions. Existing

More recently, several studies have explored implicit spatial learning by transferring knowledge from pre-trained geometric models or aligning visual representations with spatial priors [21, 26, 1]. Other approaches have emphasized preserving pre-trained semantic representations during efficient VLA adaptation [58, 27]. However, incorporating geometric knowledge into an existing visual pathway introduces potentially competing requirements: acquiring spatial information while retaining generic visual-semantic knowledge. Our SPARK-VLA preserves the original visual pathway and specializes a parallel LoRA-adapted pathway through attention response distillation [36], allowing spatial specialization without modifying the preserved representation. 

Visual token selection and routing for action generation. Recent work has shown that selective visual processing is important for efficient and effective action generation in VLAs [51, 25, 23, 22, 44, 31]. Existing methods exploit temporal redundancy through token caching and pruning [51, 31], identify task-relevant information through instruction-conditioned selection [23, 22], or incorporate action-aware importance into token reduction [44, 25]. These approaches primarily focus on identi-fying informative tokens within a given visual representation. In contrast, SPARK-VLA addresses a different selection problem: determining which knowledge source should represent each spatial position when both generic and spatially specialized features are available. Our action-driven router jointly ranks tokens from the two pathways using supervision derived from downstream action loss.

Existing efforts to enhance vision-language-action models (VLAs) with spatial intelligence generally follow two directions: introducing explicit 3D inputs, such as depth maps or point clouds, or implicitly injecting spatial knowledge into the visual representation. While the former often depends on additional sensing or reconstructed 3D signals, the latter can weaken the generic visual-semantic capability inherited from strong pre-trained vision-language backbones by forcing a single visual pathway to absorb both general perception and spatial reasoning. To address this issue, we propose SPARK-VLA, which preserves the original visual pathway while introducing a lightweight spatially adapted pathway. During training, we use a pre-trained 3D expert ( e.g. , VGGT [45]) to distill spatial knowledge into the adapted branch. To effectively use the two branches, we further introduce a lightweight knowledge router that takes visual tokens, conditions on the language instruction, and predicts token-wise gate probabilities indicating which tokens are useful for action generation. The selected tokens are then forwarded to the language model for action prediction, allowing SPARK-VLA to exploit complementary generic and spatial cues while suppressing redundant or noisy representations. Note that the 3D expert is used only during training and is discarded at inference time. The overall architecture of our SPARK-VLA is shown in Figure 2. 

3.2 Spatial representation distillation 

To incorporate spatial understanding into the VLA’s visual encoder without disrupting its original visual-semantic capability, we introduce a spatially adapted branch built on the pre-trained visual encoder. 3Dual-branch visual encoding. Let f vθ denote the visual encoder of a pre-trained vision-language model ( e.g. , Qwen2.5-VL [2]) and f vθ+∆ θ denote its LoRA-adapted counterpart, where only the low-rank parameters ∆θ are trainable. Given an RGB observation It, SPARK-VLA extracts two complementary representations: 

Zv = f vθ (It), Zp = f vθ+∆ θ(It), (1) where Zv preserves generic visual-semantic knowledge and Zp is adapted to capture spatial reasoning. During training, we further employ a pre-trained 3D encoder f pϕ as a frozen teacher that provides spatial supervision to the adapted branch. In this way, the original branch preserves the pre-trained visual representation, while the adapted branch learns to encode additional spatial knowledge.

3.3 Knowledge routing 

While Zv and Zp provide complementary generic and spatial cues, not all tokens are equally useful for a given instruction and action prediction. Directly feeding all tokens from both branches into the LLM may introduce redundant or noisy information, especially when spatially adapted tokens are irrelevant to the current instruction. We therefore introduce a lightweight knowledge router that feeds instruction- and action-relevant visual tokens into the LLM. 

Router architecture. We first collect the visual tokens from the generic and spatial branches and denote the merged token set by 

Z = [ Zv ; Zp] = {zn}Nn=1 , (4) where N is the total number of visual tokens. As shown in Figure 2, the knowledge router consists of (1) a linear layer to project Z into the same dimensional space with the text embeddings H =

{hm}Mm=1 ; (2) a cross-attention layer to compute the correlations between the visual and text representations; and (3) a lightweight MLP (Linear-GeLU-Linear). The output of the last linear layer followed by a Sigmoid activation serves as the gate probabilities p:

p = σ(Router( Z, H)/τ ) = {pn}Nn=1 , (5) where σ(·) is a sigmoid function and τ is a temperature parameter to control the sensitivity. 

Token selection and knowledge fusion. Once the gate probabilities p are obtained, we select the top-k tokens among all N tokens: 

S = TopK( p, k ), k = max( kmin , round( ρN )) , (6) where TopK( p, k ) returns the indices of the k largest gate probabilities, ρ indicates the routing ratio, and kmin prevents overly sparse routing. We then project the selected tokens to the LLM’s input space 4by fusing them according to their spatial positions. At each spatial position, the routed representation is constructed from the selected tokens among its generic and spatial pair. When both tokens are selected, their embeddings are concatenated and projected into the LLM input space. If only one token is selected, it is concatenated with a zero embedding of the same dimension and passed through the same projection. Positions for which neither token is selected are discarded. This position-aware fusion preserves the alignment between generic and spatial knowledge, while producing a compact set of visual tokens with a unified input format for the LLM. 

Gradient-based gate supervision. The main challenge in training the knowledge router is that there is no supervision signal indicating which visual tokens should be preserved. Instead of relying on heuristic salience measures [44, 31] or learning token selection only through downstream action optimization [23, 22], we derive routing targets from the gradient of the action loss with respect to the router gate probabilities. Given the action loss Lact , the gradient of Lact w.r.t. the gate probability 

pn, i.e. , gn = ∂Lact /∂p n, measures the influences of the n-th gate on action prediction. A negative gradient indicates that increasing the gate probability would reduce the action loss, suggesting that the corresponding token is useful. Therefore, we define the importance score as

## 4 Simulation Experiments 

4.1 Experimental setting Dataset. We evaluate SPARK-VLA on three benchmarks, including LIBERO [29], LIBERO-Plus [10], and CALVIN [33]. • LIBERO [29] is a widely used benchmark for lifelong robot manipulation that contains four task suites, namely LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long, where each suite includes 10 language-conditioned manipulation tasks with 50 human demonstrations per task. These suites are designed to probe different types of generalization, including spatial reasoning, object-level transfer, goal-conditioned behavior, and long-horizon compositional manipulation. • LIBERO-Plus [10] extends LIBERO with controlled perturbations over seven dimensions, includ-ing object layouts, camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting conditions, background textures, and sensor noise, thereby providing a substantially more challenging testbed for generalization under realistic variations. To further examine robustness beyond the standard LIBERO setting, we additionally evaluate the trained model on LIBERO-Plus without re-training. • CALVIN [33] is a long-horizon language-conditioned manipulation benchmark built on four simulated environments. CALVIN contains 34 manipulation tasks and 1,000 language instructions, and evaluates a policy by the average number of successfully completed sub-tasks in multi-step task sequences. Following the standard protocol, we report results on the benchmark’s compositional generalization settings to measure long-horizon control and transfer to unseen environments. 

Backbone and implementation details. We follow the implementation of [20] and [21] to build SPARK-VLA on top of OpenVLA-OFT [19]. Unlike the original setting, we use Prismatic VLM [17] with Qwen2.5-0.5B [2] as the language backbone, and adopt only DINOv2 [34] as the visual encoder without SigLIP [53]. On top of this backbone, SPARK-VLA keeps the original visual pathway frozen, augments it with a LoRA-based spatial branch, and introduces a lightweight knowledge router for selecting action-relevant visual tokens. We use VGGT [45] as the pre-trained 3D expert, which is kept frozen and used only during training for spatial representation distillation. ## 6 Conclusion 

We presented SPARK-VLA, a simple and effective framework for enhancing spatial understanding in vision-language-action models. Instead of relying on explicit 3D inputs at inference time or directly reshaping the original visual representation, SPARK-VLA preserves the pre-trained visual pathway and introduces a LoRA-adapted spatial branch learned from a frozen 3D expert through attention response distillation. To effectively integrate generic and spatial cues, we further introduced a lightweight knowledge router that selects instruction- and action-relevant tokens from both branches using gradient-derived supervision. Extensive experiments on simulated and real-world manipulation benchmarks demonstrate that SPARK-VLA achieves strong performance with a compact 0.5B language backbone and a small number of trainable parameters, showing that spatial knowledge can be effectively transferred to VLAs without sacrificing deployment simplicity. 

Broader impact Our work contributes toward more efficient and spatially aware robot policies that can operate without additional 3D sensors during inference. This can reduce hardware dependency and make VLA-based robot learning more accessible across different platforms. By improving 9spatial reasoning with a compact model, SPARK-VLA may also help lower the computational cost of deploying embodied agents in practical environments. At the same time, more capable robotic systems should be deployed with careful consideration of safety, reliability, and human oversight, especially in environments involving people or fragile objects. 

Limitations Despite its effectiveness, SPARK-VLA still relies on a pre-trained 3D expert during training, and the quality of spatial distillation may depend on the teacher model and the visual diversity of the training data. In addition, the router is trained with pseudo supervision derived from action-loss gradients, which can be noisy when the action model is not sufficiently optimized. We mitigate this issue with warm-up training and momentum-based score accumulation, but more principled routing supervision remains an interesting direction. Finally, although SPARK-VLA shows promising real-world performance, our real-world evaluation is still limited in task diversity and scale. Extending the framework to broader manipulation scenarios, more diverse embodiments, and dynamic human-centered environments is an important future direction.

A.2 Training regime Warm-up and joint optimization. Reliable router supervision requires meaningful spatial repre-sentations and a reasonably trained action prediction head. We therefore adopt a two-stage training procedure. In the first stage, we warm up the model without the router objective: 

Lwarm = Lact + λattn Lattn . (13) This stage trains the LoRA-adapted spatial branch with the attention response distillation loss while optimizing the action head with the action loss. After warm-up, we enable the knowledge router and optimize the full objective in Equation (12). In this stage, the router is trained with pseudo targets derived from action-loss gradients, while the spatial branch continues to receive distillation supervision from the frozen 3D expert. This procedure stabilizes routing training by first establishing geometry-aware visual features and meaningful action gradients, and then learning to select tokens that are useful for action generation. 

Inference without 3D experts. At inference time, the pre-trained 3D expert is completely removed. Given an RGB observation It and a language instruction, SPARK-VLA extracts the generic visual representation Zv from the original visual encoder and the spatial representation Zp from the LoRA-adapted branch. The knowledge router predicts token-wise gate scores conditioned on the visual and language features, selects the top-ranked tokens according to the target keeping ratio, and fuses the selected generic and spatial tokens before forwarding them to the language model for action prediction. Thus, SPARK-VLA benefits from spatial knowledge distilled during training while requiring neither depth maps, point clouds, nor external 3D experts at inference time.

We investigate different strategies for transferring geometric knowledge from the pre-trained 3D expert to the spatial pathway. Specifically, we consider four alternatives to our attention-response distillation: (1) action-only adaptation; (2) feature matching; (3) relational match-ing; and (4) attention-map matching. Action-only adaptation learns spatial representations solely from the downstream action objective, without explicit geometric supervision. Feature matching directly aligns the student and teacher embeddings, while relational matching transfers pairwise similarities between visual tokens. Attention-map matching instead aligns the query-key attention dis-tributions, capturing where spatial information is aggregated. Unlike these approaches, our attention response distillation additionally transfers the teacher’s aggregated value representations, providing geometry-aware contextual information conditioned on the VLA queries. We compare these objectives on LIBERO-Goal while keeping the remaining architecture and training configurations unchanged. As shown in Table A.3, action-only adaptation achieves a success rate of 81.6%, whereas feature and relational matching improve the performance to 94.8% and 95.6%, respectively. Attention-map matching further increases the success rate to 96.4%, indicating the benefit of transferring query-conditioned relationships beyond static feature alignment. Our attention-response distillation achieves the highest success rate of 98.0%, outperforming feature matching by 3.2% and attention-map matching by 1.6%. These results suggest that transferring both the attention distribution and its aggregated spatial content is more effective than supervising either latent representations or attention patterns alone. 

Knowledge fusion. We explore different strategies for integrating generic visual representations with spatially specialized features. Specifically, we compare our action-driven knowledge router with two alternative fusion mechanisms: direct concatenation and feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) [37]. Direct concatenation retains all tokens from both pathways and combines their representations without distinguishing their relevance to the downstream task. Momentum-based moving average. In our SPARK-VLA, only routed tokens are forwarded to the LLM, making tokens that are not selected in the current training step cannot directly affect the action loss and thus do not provide reliable instantaneous gradient signals. In other words, the supervision would be biased toward the currently routed subset, as the routing targets were derived only from the current gradients. To mitigate this issue, we maintain a momentum-based moving average of the token importance scores:

A.6 Visualization Does spatial representation distillation indeed transfer 3D information into the vision encoder? 

We present a new implicit adaptation strategy to inject spatial knowledge to the pre-trained vision encoder. To examine this question, we use VGGT [45] as a diagnostic model: we replace its original DINOv2 encoder with our LoRA-adapted vision encoder, while keeping the remaining components of VGGT fixed, and predict depth maps from the resulting representations. If the adapted encoder has acquired spatial knowledge, the modified VGGT should still recover meaningful scene geometry 17 despite the encoder replacement. As shown in Figure A.3, the predicted depth maps preserve the global scene structure and object boundaries, demonstrating that the adapted encoder captures geometry-aware representations through the proposed attention response distillation. This suggests that spatial representation distillation injects useful 3D understanding into the vision encoder, rather than merely improving action prediction through task-specific adaptation. 

Which tokens are preserved and excluded in the knowledge router? We further analyze the behavior of the proposed knowledge router to examine which visual tokens are preserved for action generation. Specifically, we visualize (a) the original exocentric video and the selected tokens from the exocentric view in (b) the original visual and (c) spatial branches. We also visualize (d) the original wrist view video and the selected tokens from the wrist view in (e) the original visual and (f) spatial branches. As shown in Figure A.4, the router exhibits view-dependent selection behavior. In the exocentric view, it preserves only a sparse subset of tokens around task-relevant objects, interaction regions, and spatial context, while discarding large background areas. This is because the exocentric camera provides a wide and relatively stable scene-level view, where many tokens correspond to static background or redundant global context. In contrast, the router selects a denser set of tokens from the wrist view. Unlike the exocentric view, the wrist camera is closely coupled with the end-effector and directly observes fine-grained manipulation cues, such as object pose, gripper-object contact, local geometry, and occlusion changes. Therefore, a larger portion of wrist view tokens can contribute to action prediction, especially when precise control depends on local spatial evidence near the manipulated object. This indicates that the router does not simply prune tokens uniformly across views, but adaptively allocates the visual budget according to action utility. Across both views, the original branch tends to preserve semantically informative regions, while the spatial branch emphasizes geometrically relevant areas around objects and contact regions. These visualizations suggest that knowledge routing selectively balances sparse contextual evidence from the exocentric view with dense local manipulation evidence from the wrist view.
