# Bridging Task Planning and Execution for Language-Conditioned Bimanual Manipulation 

Emanuele Cuzzocrea ∗,1,2, Giuseppina Iannotti ∗,1,2, Luca Rossini 1, Arturo Laurenzi 1, and Nikos Tsagarakis 1

Abstract — Robots are increasingly expected to execute natural-language manipulation tasks in real-world environ-ments. This is particularly relevant in household assistance and logistics, where clutter requires reasoning about both task objectives and execution feasibility. This challenge becomes even more pronounced for bimanual humanoid platforms, where coordinating two manipulators introduces additional interaction and kinematic constraints. We present a zero-shot vision-language framework for execution-aware bimanual manipulation that jointly reasons about task objectives and physical feasibility. Planning is grounded in a structured scene representation that combines semantic object relations with execution constraints, enabling the selection of obstacle relo-cations and execution choices while preserving task semantics. Compatible actions are organised into parallel bimanual stages with explicit pre- and post-conditions. A hierarchical validator evaluates these conditions under perceptual uncertainty and uses stage-level execution history to support local recovery or targeted replanning. Experiments on the CENTAURO hy-brid wheeled-legged humanoid robot across nine language-conditioned manipulation scenarios show higher task success than a non-execution-aware baseline. In dedicated failure sce-narios, hierarchical validation recovers from execution failures with fewer model queries than always replanning. These results demonstrate the benefits of combining execution reasoning directly into task planning. Videos and additional material are available on the Project Webpage.

This prob-lem naturally arises in household assistance and logistics, where robots may need to retrieve requested objects, re-organise cluttered shelves, or manipulate items in dynamic workspaces. In these settings, each manipulation can alter object accessibility and the feasibility of subsequent actions, requiring continuous reasoning about both task objectives and execution constraints. This is particularly relevant in cluttered scenes, where retrieving a target object may require removing obstructions, selecting suitable relocation regions, and preserving the feasibility of later interactions [4], [5], [6]. Language-conditioned robotic manipulation spans several complementary research directions, including task planning, structured scene representation, bimanual coordination, and execution monitoring. Although recent methods increasingly ground high-level reasoning in robot observations and ac-tions, the physical choices that connect task-level plans to feasible manipulation often remain implicit. This also limits the possibility of relating execution feedback to the planning decisions that generated it. Early approaches to language-conditioned manipulation primarily focused on task-level planning. SayCan [1] com-bines language reasoning with learned skill affordances to generate executable manipulation plans. Subsequent methods extend this paradigm through program generation and mul-timodal reasoning [9], [3], [21]. Related work on integrated task-and-motion planning explicitly connects symbolic deci-sions with motion feasibility [22], [23]. More recent meth-ods further reduce the abstraction gap between semantic reasoning and robot execution. Despite this progress, the physical choices and conditions required to execute and validate individual manipulation actions often remain implicit. One approach to reducing this gap is to ground planning in structured scene representations that organise the envi-ronment into semantic entities and spatial relations. Object-centric approaches [13], [14], [15] capture semantic and spa-tial relationships relevant to task planning. Complementary work on mechanical search and object rearrangement [4], [5], [6] shows that successful manipulation also requires explicit reasoning about physical accessibility in cluttered environments, an aspect that remains challenging for current VLMs [27], [28]. These works highlight the importance of representing not only semantic relations but also the geometric constraints that determine how objects can be approached and manipulated. Physical grounding becomes particularly important in bi-manual manipulation, where actions must also be assigned and coordinated across two manipulators. LABOR [29] uses language models to orchestrate sequential and concurrent skills, while LLM+MAP [30] combines language reason-ing with symbolic multi-agent planning. Beyond planning and coordination, robust manipula-tion requires detecting deviations from the expected scene evolution and adapting the remaining strategy accord-ingly. DoReMi [16], REFLECT [17], AHA [18], and Ex-ploreVLM [15] support execution monitoring through mis-alignment detection, multimodal failure interpretation, or scene-based replanning. III. M ETHOD 

Figure 2 provides an overview of the proposed frame-work for language-conditioned bimanual manipulation in cluttered environments. Starting from a visual observation of the scene and a natural-language task instruction, the framework constructs an executable manipulation strategy while explicitly accounting for execution constraints and validating task progress throughout execution. The frame-work comprises four main components: structured scene representation, language-guided task planning, parallel ex-ecution scheduling, and hierarchical execution validation. The individual components are described in the following sections. 

A. Object Geometric Representation 

Objects are represented as oriented bounding boxes de-fined by their position, dimensions, and orientation. Each bounding box comprises three pairs of opposing parallel faces ( left-right , front-back , and top-bottom ), whose labels are assigned with respect to the robot reference frame and therefore describe workspace-relative approach directions. In particular, the front face is defined as the face oriented towards the robot, while the remaining labels follow accord-ingly. The proposed framework assumes objects that can be grasped through opposing contacts on one pair of parallel faces, allowing grasp accessibility and object interactions to be inferred directly from geometry. Objects requiring instance-specific grasp affordances, such as a mug that must be grasped by its handle, fall outside the scope of the frame-work. This is a deliberate scoping choice: many rigid objects commonly encountered in tabletop and logistics settings can be approximated through this geometric representation, and these are precisely the scenarios in which mutual obstruction constitutes the dominant difficulty addressed here. 

B. Structured Scene Representation 

Given an RGB image It acquired from the robot viewpoint at time t, scene construction is performed in two stages. First, a VLM processes It to identify the object instances in the scene, infer their semantic attributes and qualitative pairwise relations, and determine the interaction state of the robot end-effectors. This produces a symbolic description of the scene. Second, FoundationPose [31], in its model-based mode with CAD models of the objects, provides 6D object pose estimates that geometrically ground the detected objects and their relations. This geometric information is used to define the six robot-relative sides of each object and enrich the symbolic representation with the interaction constraints described below. The scene is represented as a graph 

Gt = ( Vt , Rt ), (1) where Vt = Ot ∪ Et is the set of scene entities, composed of object nodes Ot and end-effector nodes Et , and Rt is the set of pairwise relations between them. Objects are represented as discrete instances with semantic attributes such as category and colour, together with any property relevant to manipu-lation, such as fragility. End-effectors are instead modelled through their current interaction state, namely whether they are empty or holding an object. Unlike objects, they are not associated with persistent scene properties, since their pose depends on the current robot configuration. The scene structure inferred by the VLM is captured through pairwise relations 

Rt ⊆ Vt × T ×Vt , (2) where T defines the set of admissible relation types, namely

The symbolic graph is therefore enriched using the estimated object poses and dimensions. Specifically, the six sides of each object are an-notated with their interaction status in terms of accessibility

For each spatial relation inferred in the scene graph, the distance separating the corresponding facing sides of the two objects is computed from their estimated poses and dimensions. This distance is compared with a safety threshold derived from the end-effector geometry. When a side is blocked, the object or support surface responsible for the obstruction is also recorded, so that each unavailable interaction direction is represented together with its cause. 

Reachability is evaluated separately, since geometric acces-sibility does not guarantee that a side can be approached given the robot kinematics. For each candidate interaction direction, reachability is determined by checking whether the corresponding approach pose admits a feasible inverse kinematics solution. 

C. Manipulation Planning 

Given the structured scene representation Gt , the natural-language task instruction Ht , a VLM generates an ordered manipulation plan 

Πt = ( a1, a2, . . . , an), (3) which describes a sequence of scene transformations that progressively establishes the conditions required to complete the task. Each action is represented as 

ak = ( action k, target k, mode k, contact k, side k), (4) where action k denotes the manipulation primitive, chosen among grasp , place , push , and release ; target k identifies the manipulated object or the placement destination; mode k

specifies whether the action is performed with the left arm, the right arm, or both arms jointly; contact k indicates whether the interaction is performed with or without engaging the claw; and side k identifies the object side, or pair of oppos-ing sides, from which the interaction should be executed. This representation connects task-level decisions with their physical realisation. For instance, a grasp must involve an accessible pair of opposing sides reachable by the selected arm, whereas a push specifies the side on which contact is established. D. Scheduler 

The manipulation plan Πt is initially generated as asequential list of actions. Since this representation does not explicitly exploit the robot’s capability to execute com-patible actions in parallel, a dedicated scheduling module reorganises the plan into a sequence of execution stages while preserving the original action semantics. Actions are grouped according to their compatibility, considering object interactions, spatial constraints, and the allocation of the robot end-effectors, ensuring that concurrent execution does not introduce conflicts. The resulting stage sequence is 

St = ( s1, . . . , sm), (5) where each stage si is a set of mutually compatible actions to be executed concurrently, e.g., si = {ap, aq} with ap, aq ∈ Πt .For each stage, the scheduler additionally defines a local goal g i, together with symbolic pre-conditions C pre i and post-conditions C post i . The local goal summarises the intended outcome of the concurrent actions, while the symbolic con-ditions describe the required scene state before execution and the expected state afterwards. Rather than representing these conditions as complete scene descriptions, they are expressed as collections of atomic predicates, each describing Fig. 4: Example execution of the Fragile Box scenario, where the task is to grasp the white box. It then grasps and places the fragile green box on the table. Once both obstacles have been removed, the robot grasps the white box. a single independently verifiable property of the scene. By associating each group of compatible actions with a local goal and explicit symbolic conditions, the stage becomes the fundamental unit of supervision, providing the information required to assess the intended state transition. This represen-tation naturally supports local recovery whenever possible, while global replanning is reserved for situations in which the remaining schedule can no longer be reconciled with the observed scene.

When a failure is detected, a structured report is built. If the pre-conditions are violated, the stage has not been executed and cannot be repaired locally, so replanning is se-lected directly. If the post-conditions are violated, the report collects the unsatisfied conditions with their justifications, the executed actions, the scene graphs reconstructed from I pre i

and I post i , and the history Li of previous attempts on the stage. Comparing the two scene graphs reveals whether execution made progress towards the local goal gi and whether the pre-conditions of si still hold in the updated scene, while Li

exposes recurring failures and the strategies already applied. Together, these elements indicate whether execution made partial progress, whether the failure can be attributed to an action attribute, or whether the stage is no longer applicable. Recovery then follows a local-to-global policy, which attempts to repair the current stage before revising the whole plan. Repeat is selected when execution made progress towards the local goal without completing it: the scene is per-ceived again and the failed stage is re-executed unchanged. 

Modify is selected when the action is not completed and the failure can be attributed to an action parameter. In this case, the scene is perceived again and an Action Revision VLM compares the observations of the previous attempts. Based on the failed conditions and the parameter values already tried in Li, the VLM revises mode k, contact k, or side k to address the observed failure. The stage is then re-executed with the revised actions. Replan is selected when the stage is no longer applicable in the updated scene, when the evidence remains insufficient, or when neither local strategy is supported. In this case, the scene is perceived again, and manipulation planning and scheduling are re-invoked to regenerate the remaining plan. To avoid repeating the same failure, the planner additionally receives a summary of the failed stage, its violated conditions, and the strategies already tried in Li. Moreover, abort terminates execution when no strategy remains within the allowed number of attempts. B. Results and Analysis 

In this section, we first compare the complete framework with two baselines that share the same VLM backbone, task instructions, manipulation primitives, and low-level execu-tion modules. The first baseline ( Non-execution-aware & Binary validation ) provides the planner only with symbolic object relations, without side-level accessibility and reach-ability information, and relies on a binary success/failure validator. The second ( Execution-aware & Binary validation )adopts the proposed execution-aware scene representation but retains the binary validator. The proposed framework (Execution-aware & Hierarchical validation ) additionally replaces the binary validator with hierarchical validation. Furthermore, we analyse the role of the individual modules, the benefit of parallel execution, and the choice of the VLM backbone. 

1) Overall Performance and Baselines: Table III reports the performance of the three configurations on the nine scenarios. The benefit of execution awareness depends on how objects obstruct the target. When an obstacle rests on top of the target, as in Stacked Boxes , this dependency is already captured by the symbolic relations, and the non-execution-aware planner also solves the scenario. When objects are placed side by side, as in Boxes Aside , Fragile Box , and 

Long Horizon , however, the symbolic representation cannot tell whether the free space between them is sufficient for the end-effector to reach the target. The non-execution-aware planner therefore does not recognise a laterally placed object as an obstruction and attempts to grasp the target directly, failing in all executions (0% SR). By encoding side-level accessibility and reachability, the execution-aware planner instead identifies and relocates the blocking objects, reaching 85%, 70%, and 50% SR. Fig. 4 shows an example execution of Fragile Box . Similar gains appear in Bottles on Box 

(from 70% to 95%) and Push Failure (from 15% to 95%), suggesting that these scenarios rely on the same interaction-relevant information. The main contribution of hierarchical validation lies in efficiency. A binary validator only reports whether a stage succeeded and, without information on the cause of a failure, must always replan, spending 3 queries per failure. 2) Module-level Analysis: The module-level columns of Table III break down the performance of the proposed framework by module. Execution scheduling achieves the highest SR, equal to 100% in seven of the nine scenarios, since its task is limited to grouping compatible actions and its input is purely textual. Manipulation planning also receives only textual input, but its SR depends strongly on the task: generating a plan with many steps and heterogeneous in-teractions, as in Long Horizon (67%), is considerably harder than solving a scenario such as Stacked Boxes (100%). Scene perception and validation, instead, take both text and images as input, and their failures stem less from the task than from scene ambiguities, perspective effects, and suboptimal viewpoints caused by the head-mounted camera. The same pattern emerges from the latencies. Scene perception and validation have fairly uniform latencies, whereas planning and, to a lesser extent, scheduling scale with the complexity of the task. In particular, the planning latency grows from 5.79–12.82 s in the simpler scenarios to 22.30 s in Fragile Box and 31.78 s in Long Horizon .

V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This work presented a zero-shot, language-conditioned framework for execution-aware bimanual manipulation in cluttered environments. By explicitly encoding blocking re-lations, side-level accessibility, and kinematic reachability, the proposed framework reasons jointly about task objectives and their physical realisation. Furthermore, the proposed hierarchical validation strategy explicitly relates execution feedback to planning decisions, enabling targeted recovery and replanning when the observed scene evolution deviates from the expected one. Experimental results on the real CENTAURO platform demonstrate that combining struc-tured scene understanding, execution-aware planning, and closed-loop validation improves the robustness of language-conditioned manipulation in cluttered environments. Promis-ing directions for future work include relaxing these as-sumptions. Learned manipulation policies could replace in-verse kinematics to enable more adaptive and contact-rich behaviours, while more flexible skill generation and compo-sition could extend the set of available actions. Moreover, reachability is currently evaluated from a fixed base pose, so kinematically unreachable sides are simply discarded; jointly planning manipulation and base motion would allow the robot to reposition itself and turn these constraints into additional planning decisions.
