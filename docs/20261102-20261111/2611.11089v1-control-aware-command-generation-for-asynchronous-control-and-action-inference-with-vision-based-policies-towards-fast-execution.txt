Abstract 

Vision-based control policies, including visionlanguageaction models, have en-abled robots to perform increasingly complex tasks. However, their execution speed is often limited by the high computational cost of action inference. In this work, we investigate what can be achieved at the control level without modifying the underlying policy learning process. We introduce an asynchronous inference and control framework that enables fast task execution while maintaining smooth and stable robot motions. By formulating the generation of continuous target po-sitions from discontinuous model predictions as a tracking control problem, our method makes asynchronous inference practical for real robots. We further ac-celerate execution through adaptive action skipping based on the variance of pre-dicted positions. Experiments with Action Chunking Transformer and OpenVLA-OFT on a bimanual robot demonstrate substantial speedups while preserving high task success rates. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-based control policies, including large-scale vision-language-action (VLA) models, have re-cently been applied to a wide range of robotic tasks [1]. Compared with approaches that explicitly estimate object poses, they simplify system design and can be trained from human demonstrations through imitation learning [2, 3]. By leveraging visual and language inputs, such policies have the potential to make robot control more accessible and expand the range of robotic applications. 2 Related Work 

Vision-based control policies, including large-scale VLA models such as RT-X [1], Octo [4], Open-VLA [5], π0 [6], and π0.5 [7], have recently achieved remarkable progress in robotic manipulation. Related approaches that predict actions from image observations and robot states have also been widely studied [8–11], with ACT [8] being a representative example. A common limitation of these methods is the high computational cost of inference, which slows down task execution. Several recent studies have explored accelerating execution in imitation learning [12, 13]. The method of [12] exploits a high-fidelity robot controller to guide action generation, but its applica-bility to arbitrary robotic platforms remains unclear. Guo et al. [13] accelerate execution by down-sampling demonstrations before training, yet do not explicitly address motion smoothness during execution. In contrast, our approach focuses on command generation at deployment time. There-fore, our contribution is orthogonal to that of Guo et al. [13], and the two methods can be readily combined. A closely related effort is [14], which tackles discontinuities caused by asynchronous inference through an inpainting formulation inspired by image generation [15] and diffusion guidance meth-ods [16]. However, the approach is restricted to diffusion or flow-matching policies and is not directly applicable to architectures such as ACT [8] or OpenVLA [5]. Moreover, because it relies on learned action generation, it does not provide explicit guarantees on constraints such as acceleration limits.

4.2 Implementation with Multithreading 

To achieve high control frequency and fast task execution, we separate the control loop and the inference loop into different threads, as shown in Fig. 4, which enables asynchronous inference. The control thread updates observations, shares them with the inference loop, and periodically checks for new action chunks. If a new chunk is available, it is used. Otherwise, previously received data are used. Since one inference step produces a sequence of nchunk actions, delays can be tolerated for up to nchunk steps. Each observation and action chunk is tagged with a time stamp, denoted as tinf 

for inference, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Because the control loop runs faster, texe is typically greater than tinf when actions are retrieved. 

4.3 Fast and Adaptive Task Execution 

While asynchronous action inference and control provide one means of achieving fast task execution, another straightforward approach is to skip certain steps within action chunks rather than executing all of them. However, skipping introduces two key challenges. First, it produces non-smooth action sequences. This can be mitigated using the control-aware command generation described in Sec-tion 4.1, which smooths non-smooth desired positions qdes  

> t

. Second, skipping reduces the reliability of older inferred actions, since it increases the gap between the inference step tinf and the execution step texe . To address this, we adaptively adjust the execution speed: the robot moves faster when the inferred actions are reliable and more slowly otherwise. 6 Conclusions 

We presented a framework for fast task execution with vision-based policies by enabling asyn-chronous action inference. We addressed the problem of generating action sequences that stably follow those produced by a policy model while satisfying high-speed constraints such as accelera-tion. Experimental results showed that our framework executes tasks faster than standard approaches. Through experiments with ACT and OpenVLA-OFT, we demonstrated that the proposed method re-duces acceleration and jerk, thereby enabling the use of asynchronous inference in vision-based policies. In the present experiments, we used the default parameters of OpenVLA-OFT without tun-ing, including the chunk size. As future work, we plan to conduct experiments with larger models and explore effective methods for them. In addition, we acknowledge that the current study does not include comparisons with generic smoothing methods or acceleration approaches designed for diffusion-based policies such as Real Time Chunking [14]. A systematic comparison of execution-time acceleration strategies remains an important direction for future work. More broadly, the significance of this work is not limited to the specific methodology for enabling asynchronous execution. Our results suggest that substantial improvements in execution speed and task performance can be achieved by appropriately designing the system that converts policy outputs into robot commands, without modifying the underlying policy model itself. This finding highlights the importance of considering the entire deployment pipeline of robot learning systems, including the execution layer, rather than focusing solely on policy learning algorithms. 7Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

B.1 Task details in the experiment 

In the real robot experiment, the following three tasks, shown in Fig. 6, were used to evaluate the control schemes. 1. “case stacking” The right arm picks up the red case and places it next to another red box. Then, the left arm picks up the black case and places it on top of the red cases. Episode length: 800 steps. Training: 50 demonstrations. Evaluation: 10 trials for each of three random seeds. 2. “box handover” The right arm picks up a box and passes it to the left arm. Episode length: 500 steps. Training: 50 demonstrations. Evaluation: 10 trials for each of three random seeds. 3. “pen storage” The right arm opens a drawer, and the left arm grasps a pen and passes it to the right arm. Then, the right arm places the pen in the drawer and closes it. Episode length: 1200 steps. Training: 50 demonstrations. Evaluation: 10 trials for each of three random seeds. 

B.2 Other details 

The experiments with ACT was conducted based on the authors’ implementation 1 The model ob-tained through OpenVLA-OFT was evaluated using the authors implementation 2, in which infer-ence and robot control were executed sequentially, and results were compared with and without CAC. In addition, we conducted experiments with our alternative implementation, in which in-ference and robot control were executed asynchronously and in parallel through multithreading when CAC was applied. It is worth noting that our implementation is simple and can be inte-grated by replacing a single file responsible for action inference and robot control in the ACT or OpenVLA-OFT repositories. The implementation of the proposed framework is available at 

https://github.com/TakaOsa/ACT_Async .In the real-robot experiments, computations were performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon w5-2455X CPU and an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU.
