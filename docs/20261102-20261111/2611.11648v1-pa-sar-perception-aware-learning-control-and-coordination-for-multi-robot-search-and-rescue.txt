Abstract 

Autonomous SaR requires robots to explore unknown or partially known environments, avoid hazards, coordinate team activities, and guide discovered victims to safety. Improving individual capabilities of robots does not necessarily improve mission outcomes: safer motion can restrict the exploration behavior, faster discovery can increase the rescue demand, and effective guidance depends on victim responses. We introduce PA-SaR, a mathematical and architectural framework for coordinating heterogeneous local control, learning-based exploration, supervisory task allocation, and adaptive approach-lead victim guidance around mission-level objectives. PA-SaR distinguishes environmental ground-truth, robot-specific perceptions, and simulated victim behavior. This provides a common basis for integrating complementary mechanisms and analyzing their interactions across mission stages. We demonstrate the framework through single- and multi-robot simulation studies incorporating model-predictive and learning-based exploration, perception-based safety shielding, event-driven task allocation, and adaptive victim guidance. The results show trade-offs between collision avoidance and search completion, indicate that supervisory allocation improves search completion beyond information sharing alone, and demonstrate that increased exploration does not necessarily yield more rescues. Adaptive guidance increases rescues relative to continuous leading, without a detected improvement in mean or final victim trust or a reduction in mortality. These findings support evaluating local control, team coordination, and evacuation as related but distinct aspects of autonomous SaR within the considered simulation environments. 

Keywords: Search and rescue robotics, perception-aware control, tube-based model predictive control, shielded reinforcement learning, multi-robot task allocation, robot-assisted evacuation. 

# 1 Introduction 

Search-and-Rescue (SaR) missions are time-critical: the probability of finding survivors decreases as the mission is prolonged [1]. This urgency, combined with the danger faced by rescue workers, motivates the use of robotic systems to extend their reach into other-wise inaccessible areas while reducing their exposure to hazardous conditions [2]. When these robots op-erate autonomously, mission safety becomes partly dependent on decisions made from incomplete informa-tion. Unsafe robot actions may injure victims, damage robots that are crucial for the mission, or block a route, 1arXiv:2611.11648v1 [cs.RO] 8 Nov 2026 while poor victim guidance can leave a discovered victim stranded. Discovery alone does not bring a vic-tim to safety, and delaying rescue until exploration is complete can postpone assistance to those victims. This motivates coordinating continued exploration with rescue: explorers, tasked with mapping and victim de-tection, continue searching while rescue-capable robots guide located victims toward an exit. Coverage and dis-covery are therefore important intermediate measures, but evacuation outcomes are also needed to assess mission effectiveness. Safe exploration requires local motion planning that accounts for uncertain sensing and disturbances. Model Predictive Control (MPC) optimizes predicted motion subject to constraints [3], while tube-based MPC tightens these constraints to accommodate distur-bances within a prescribed set [4]. A fixed disturbance bound can exclude plans that would be feasible under smaller local disturbances. State-Dependent Dynamic Tube-based Model Predictive Control (SDD-TMPC) addresses this conservativeness by adapting the tube to state-dependent disturbances and has been evaluated in simulated SaR scenarios [5]. However, these model predictive controllers still require online optimization [6], motivating complementary approaches to action selection. A trained Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy can select actions without explicitly searching over future control sequences at each decision step [7]. Deep RL, which combines RL and deep learning, has been ap-plied to maritime search coverage, prioritizing likely victim locations while reducing repeated coverage [8]. However, optimizing a reward does not itself ensure safe behavior: a learned policy may select unsafe ac-tions or exploit deficiencies in the reward specification [9, 10]. Safety mechanisms can supplement these poli-cies. For instance, barrier-function shielding has been used for UAV coverage with collision-avoidance and connectivity requirements [11], while predictive safety filters assess and modify proposed inputs using a dy-namical model [12]. In a shielded architecture, the learned action is executed when it passes the model-based check; otherwise, a backup controller supplies a replacement [9]. Such interventions can restrict ex-ploration, and their safety properties depend on the information and model used for checking. Local motion planning alone does not determine how effectively a team of robots executes their tasks to complete the SaR mission. Independent explorers may duplicate coverage, cluster around the same frontier, or compete for narrow passages. Coordinated explo-ration distributes robots across distinct targets [13], while dynamic task allocation revises assignments as new tasks emerge [14]. Event-driven supervisory con-trol combines local decision making with centralized intervention when mission conditions require realloca-tion [15]. In [16], centralized MPC and local heuristic decision making are integrated to provide hierarchical coordination of individual robots for achieving both area coverage and victim localization under imperfect sensing. This provides a precedent for connecting local planning with team objectives. Victim guidance introduces a further requirement, i.e., detected victims should be willing and able to follow the robot [17, 18]. Trust influences reliance on automation [19, 20], while evacuation experiments show that people may follow a robot despite previ-ously observing unreliable behavior [21]. The GT–BDI framework combines game theory with belief–desire– intention modeling to represent both individual deci-sion making and social interaction of victims during evacuation, including the influence of trust on robot-assisted evacuation [22]. Complementary guidance re-search examines shepherding [23] and motion planning that balances exit-directed progress with remaining visible to an evacuee [24]. These studies motivate evalu-ating guidance through evacuation outcomes alongside victim trust and following behavior. These requirements are interdependent at the mis-sion level: conservative planning can restrict explo-ration, greater discovery need not increase evacuation, and guidance depends on victim responses. The result-ing question is how improvements in local control and team-level search translate into evacuation outcomes under incomplete information. We introduce Perception-Aware Search-and-Rescue (PA-SaR) as a mathematical and architectural framework for examining these mission-level relation-ships. Complementary simulation studies evaluate individual mechanisms and selected combinations. 

## Contributions 

The paper makes three contributions: • A mathematical formulation and architecture for mission-level SaR: We formulate PA-SaR as a perception-aware framework connecting local mo-tion planning, exploration, supervisory task allo-cation, and adaptive approach-lead victim guid-ance. The formulation distinguishes environmental 2ground-truth, robot-specific perceptions, and simu-lated victim behavior, while separating component responsibilities from the algorithms that implement them. • An implementation of heterogeneous control and decision-making mechanisms: We implement model-predictive and learning-based exploration, perception-based safety shielding, event-driven al-location, and adaptive victim guidance within this architecture. This includes a finite-action grid adapta-tion of state-dependent tube activation and recurrent learned exploration with a model-predictive safety backup. • An empirical investigation of local-control, coordi-nation, and evacuation trade-offs: Complementary simulation studies distinguish the effects of per-ception uncertainty, local constraint enforcement, information sharing, task allocation, rescue-team size, and adaptive guidance on search and evacuation outcomes. The remainder of the paper is organized as fol-lows. Section 2 presents the architecture, environment, robot and victim models, local controllers, and super-visory coordination. Section 3 reports the experimental configurations and comparative results. Section 4 sum-marizes the findings, limitations, and directions for future research. 

# 2 PA-SaR: Perception-Aware Search-and-Rescue Architecture 

The Perception-Aware Search-and-Rescue (PA-SaR) architecture organizes mission execution around a re-peated observation–decision–execution cycle. Each robot updates its local perception through automatic environment scans, and its controller uses this informa-tion to select the next control action. When supervisory coordination is enabled, scan reports and mission events also update a shared belief used by the supervi-sor to allocate exploration and rescue tasks. Assigned targets and routes direct local planning; unassigned robots explore autonomously. Once a rescue task enters active guidance, a guidance policy replaces the robot’s search controller to guide the victim toward an exit. A selected victim movement is canceled if its des-tination is occupied by a robot or another victim at the beginning of the step. If multiple victims select the same available destination, only the highest-priority victim moves there, with uniform random tie-breaking among victims of equal highest priority. The others remain in their current cells for that step, without selecting alternative destinations. An egoistic victim competing with a selfish victim becomes selfish for subsequent decisions.

2.3.1 Model-predictive controllers 

The three model-predictive controllers use the same receding-horizon procedure. At step k, each controller evaluates sequences of N candidate inputs. Candidate positions are obtained by applying the commanded movements along the prediction horizon. The displace-ment is not added to this nominal trajectory; instead, its possible effects are represented by the controller-specific feasibility conditions described below. Only the first input of the selected sequence is applied, af-ter which the robot scans again and the optimization is repeated. Perceived traversability and victim occupancy are held constant throughout the prediction horizon; victim motion is not predicted. Perceived vulnerability also remains unchanged unless measurement noise is en-abled, in which case predicted scans perturb the stored vulnerability values of cells predicted to be observed. Candidate movements update the robot’s predicted position and self-occupancy. At each predicted position, the sensing-radius and line-of-sight rules discussed in Section 2.2.2 determine which cells would be observed, using perceived traversability to determine occlusion. Confidence is then updated according to (3), with scan quality evaluated from the predicted position using (4) when distance falloff is enabled. Predicted observa-tions mark cells as explored, while previously explored cells remain explored. These updates model informa-tion acquisition without accessing previously unknown ground-truth cell values. Fire propagation is predicted using an internal stochastic forecast with assumed ignition probability

Within the prediction model, a grid cell is classified as feasible if it is perceived as traversable and free of victims and is not predicted to be burning. Perceived robot occupancy is not included in this common feasi-bility condition because the controllers do not jointly predict the movements of the other robots. Nominal MPC requires only the predicted nomi-nal positions to remain feasible. This controller provides the reference against which the additional robustness and conservativeness of the tube-based controllers are evaluated. ## 3.1 Common environment and evaluation procedure 

The experiments use a discrete-time simulator imple-menting the models and decision rules introduced in Section 2. The simulator maintains the ground-truth en-vironment and updates robot motion, victim behavior, and environmental dynamics. The evaluation comprises five scenario families and eight base environment configurations: EMPC for local MPC comparisons and measurement-noise tests, 

ERLD for RL development at three map scales, ERLE 

for memory and final-controller comparisons, ESV for homogeneous and heterogeneous supervisory studies, and EDR for dynamic rescue. Study-specific settings are introduced below, and additional parameters and results appear in Appendices B and A, respectively. Map dimensions vary across these complementary studies: the larger supervisory environments accom-modate distributed search by multiple robots, while RL-development study explicitly varies map size to assess exploration as the search space grows. Each simulated map consists of rooms connected by corridors, generated procedurally using the dungeon-style map-generation approach of [34]. For the vulner-ability layer defined in Section 2.2.1, the experiments use νsafe = 0, νmoderate = 0.6, and νhigh = 1,adopting the numerical levels of the normalized com-bustibility classification in [35] as terrain-risk values. All cells initially have zero vulnerability. Each corridor and room is independently selected for vul-nerability assignment with a configured corridor- or room-specific probability. For each selected region, a second random draw determines whether all its cells receive the moderately vulnerable or high-risk value, using the corresponding conditional high-risk probabil-ity. These assignments are applied first to corridor cells and then to room cells, so a selected room’s vulnera-bility value takes precedence at cells belonging to both regions. Victims are placed by uniformly sampling a room and then an unoccupied cell within it. Initial fire loca-tions are sampled similarly, excluding victim-occupied cells and previously selected fire locations. These cells are initialized as burning, with eligible adjacent cells marked as flammable, following Section 2.2.1. 14 Comparative evaluations use matched map seeds, giv-ing methods the same initial geometry, terrain-risk distribution, victim placement, and fires. Victim treatment distinguishes detection from evac-uation. In EMPC , ERLD , and ERLE , victims are sta-tionary detection targets and motion obstacles, and neither detection nor fire removes them. In ESV and 

EDR , victims occupying burning or burnt cells are re-moved as fatalities after the victim-movement stage and before fire propagation. In homogeneous supervisory missions, victims remain stationary. Detection comple-tion requires at least one surviving victim and detection of every survivor. The heterogeneous supervisory and dynamic-rescue studies additionally evaluate evacua-tion, with victims considered rescued upon reaching an exit. All robots scan the environment at k = 0 be-fore selecting their first command. Each subsequent step processes robot commands and movement con-flicts, victim movement where enabled, stochastic displacements, and a fire update, followed by automatic scanning. Robot scans update the local perceptions used for control, while dynamic victims scan their sur-roundings to update the perceptions used in their trust and movement rules. In EDR , an additional fire update follows scanning, allowing newly observed fire information to become outdated before the next decision. This represents an observation–action mismatch relevant to evacuation planning with time-dependent environmental knowl-edge [36]. Both fire spread and burning duration therefore advance twice per step. Controller constraints and safety checks use per-ceived conditions, whereas movement execution uses the ground-truth environment. However, incomplete perception, stochastic displacements, or conflicting robot commands can still produce an invalid destina-tion. Furthermore, unshielded RL lacks these feasibility checks. Irrespective of the controller, commanded move-ments and stochastic displacements directed outside the grid or into an obstruction (terrain collisions), or into a victim-occupied cell (victim collisions), are blocked and recorded. Blocking a command preserves the current position, whereas blocking a displacement preserves the post-command position. Conflicting robot commands are also blocked and recorded as inter-robot collisions. Fire collisions are recorded when a robot occupies a burning cell after command processing, before stochas-tic displacements and fire propagation. Burning cells do not themselves block movement execution. This fire-collision counter is absent from the implementation used for ERLD and ERLE , including its model-based baselines. Each recorded collision contributes one harm event. To represent harm from vulnerable terrain beyond recorded collisions, each robot incurs one additional harm event with probability equal to the ground-truth vulnerability of its post-disturbance cell at each step. This is a modeling convention: physical contact, im-pact dynamics, and degradation of robot capabilities are not simulated. Coverage is the fraction of traversable cells ob-served. In EMPC , ERLD , and ERLE , recall is the fraction of initially placed victims detected, and suc-cess requires detecting all of them before the deadline. Homogeneous supervisory results use the detection-completion condition specified above, and rescue stud-ies report victims rescued, deceased, and remaining. Coverage, recall, and completion rates are expressed as percentages, and all Confidence Intervals (CIs) are at the 95% level.

## 3.3 RL-based controllers 

We examine how reward design and observation mem-ory affect learned exploration using PPO. Reward and sensitivity studies first assess a policy receiving four stacked observations, followed by a held-out compari-son with the recurrent policy of Section 2.3.2. Network architectures, training settings, and experimental vari-ants are listed in Table B1, Appendix B. During reward development, inactive behavior was observed with a preliminary design that directly penal-ized vulnerability and fire exposure. A second design removed these penalties and rewarded the first physical visit to each cell, while retaining a terminal timeout penalty. This penalty provides no distinction between partial exploration and little progress when neither leads to completion. The final reward in (11) therefore emphasizes ex-ploration progress through coverage-potential shaping and terminal coverage, replacing the first-visit bonus and omitting the timeout penalty. Detection and com-pletion rewards and step and collision penalties are retained. # 4 Conclusion 

This paper presented Perception-Aware Search-and-Rescue (PA-SaR) and evaluated how local control, collective search, and victim guidance contribute to search and evacuation outcomes. For local control, State-Dependent Dynamic Tube-based Model Predictive Control (SDD-TMPC) com-pletes more missions than fixed-tube Tube-based Model Predictive Control (TMPC) while retaining the observed protection against terrain and victim col-lisions under noise-free conditions. State-dependent tightening therefore offers a practical balance between exploration and disturbance protection, although larger perception errors and spreading fire remain challenges. For learned exploration, the evaluated recurrent policy achieves higher coverage and completion than the four-frame policy. Shielding the learned policy eliminates recorded terrain and victim collisions while retaining shorter decision times than the model-based baselines. Nominal Model Predictive Control (MPC) nevertheless achieves higher completion in the tested open maps, demonstrating the trade-off between decision speed, collision avoidance, and mission completion. The coordination studies distinguish the benefits of shared perception from those of task allocation. Sharing observations improves collective search, while supervisory allocation further reduces redundant ex-ploration and increases detection completion under the study’s remaining-victim endpoint. In the het-erogeneous configuration, however, exploration gains accompany fewer rescues. By representing search and rescue as coupled responsibilities, PA-SaR exposes this mismatch and motivates allocation strategies that account for evacuation capacity. The dynamic-rescue studies show diminishing re-turns from additional rescuers and improved evacuation through adaptive approach–lead guidance with a fixed team. The guidance benefit appears in the number of completed rescues, without a detected increase in logged trust or reduction in mortality. Together, these findings demonstrate the importance of evaluating robot–victim interaction alongside team composition and victim discovery. Future work should evaluate more complete con-figurations combining local control, supervisory al-location, and adaptive guidance. Priorities include uncertainty-aware constraints for evolving hazards, in-dependent variation of sensing and fire-update rates, and learned-policy evaluation across training seeds and map distributions. Rescue-workload-aware allo-cation and measurements of robot–victim separation and guidance interruptions would clarify how search gains translate into evacuation. Physical validation is a further step toward establishing performance beyond the simulated environments and behavioral models considered here. 23 Together, these studies establish the value of PA-SaR as a framework for identifying where improve-ments in local control and collective search support evacuation, and where they fall short. By making these dependencies explicit, PA-SaR provides a ba-sis for selecting controllers, allocating rescue capacity, and designing guidance policies according to mission outcomes rather than isolated performance measures.
