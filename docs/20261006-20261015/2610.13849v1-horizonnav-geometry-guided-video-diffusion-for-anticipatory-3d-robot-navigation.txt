# HorizonNav: Geometry-Guided Video Diffusion for Anticipatory 3D Robot Navigation 

Hajira Amjad, Valerii Serpiva, Viacheslav Liubimov, Didar Seyidov and Dzmitry Tsetserukou 

Abstract — We present HorizonNav, a video diffusion-based framework for anticipatory robot navigation that transforms a single RGB image and a text prompt into a geometrically aligned representation of plausible surrounding 3D space. Given the initial observation and a textual description of the reconstruction objective, a vision-language model, guided by designed instructions, interprets the scene and constructs structured prompts for a video diffusion model. The resulting video sequence provides plausible future observations of the environment, which are subsequently processed using monocu-lar ORB-SLAM3 for camera pose estimation and Video Depth Anything for dense depth prediction. Our central contribution is a geometry-guided depth alignment stage that combines the estimated camera poses with triangulated visual land-marks to constrain dense depth predictions across multiple views. This alignment improves the geometric consistency of the reconstructed 3D environment, addressing inconsistencies that temporal coherence of the generated video alone cannot resolve. The experimental evaluation of navigation in imagined 3D environment shows that across a 19-scene horizon study, planning-feasible scenes increase from 13/19 at P20 to 15/19 at P81, while successful RRT trials increase from 62/90 to 75/90. In a separate corridor study, 6 of 25 evaluable generated-camera trajectories violate the reconstructed collision constraints; RRT planner recovers feasible alternatives in 5 of those 6 cases. A larger archive contains 58 usable reconstructions from 67 attempted cases, of which 36 support a downstream plan and 22 do not, exposing reconstruction and free-space failure modes rather than hiding them without lidar Across 15 corridor reconstructions, the mean reconstruction error is approximately 0.30 m at matched structural landmarks. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous navigation in unfamiliar environments re-quires robots to act before their surroundings are fully explored. Visual simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) reconstructs observed scene structure, while regions outside the current field of view remain unknown. A robot that can form plausible spatial hypotheses before motion may be able to evaluate candidate routes earlier, but such hypotheses are useful only if their geometry is treated cautiously rather than as ground truth. Recent generative world models use predicted obser-vations for navigation and action reasoning. Navigation World Models [1] predict action-conditioned observations, while ImagiNav [2] combines language-guided video gen-eration with trajectory extraction. WonderWorld [3], World-Explorer [4], and GEN3C [5] further demonstrate that gen-erative models can expose spatial structure beyond a single

image. However, a generated trajectory need not be safe to execute, and generated views may contain deformations or inconsistent occlusions that alter free-space connectivity. Dense depth estimation provides per-pixel depth informa-tion for generated observations, but it does not by itself enforce a geometrically aligned 3D reconstruction across views. Video Depth Anything [6] produces temporally co-herent depth maps and explicitly achieves this without intro-ducing geometric priors or video generation priors. However, temporal consistency alone does not necessarily guarantee accurate multi-view geometry. In contrast, methods such as VGGT [7] and Depth Anything 3 [8] explicitly target multi-view geometric estimation, while World Reconstruc-tion From Inconsistent Views [9] addresses contradictions between generated observations through non-rigid alignment. These developments motivate combining exploratory video generation with explicit geometric constraints to transform temporally consistent depth predictions into spatial represen-tations that are more geometrically coherent and useful for robot planning. We present HorizonNav a framework that transforms a single RGB image and a language prompt into a geo-metrically aligned representation of plausible surrounding space. Starting from one RGB observation and a language task, a vision-language model produces structured explo-ration instructions and Wan 2.2 generates complementary virtual camera motion. ORB-SLAM3 [6] estimates the cor-responding virtual camera poses and sparse 3D landmarks, whereas Video Depth Anything predicts dense relative depth maps for the generated frames. We align the dense depth estimates with the sparse geometric structure recovered by ORB-SLAM3, jointly resolving scale and geometric consis-tency across views. A measured scene reference resolves monocular scale, after which RRT [7] searches the result-ing obstacle representation independently of the generated camera motion. HorizonNav combines exploration-oriented VLM prompting, sparse-landmark-guided depth alignment, and multivideo map fusion to connect generative visual exploration with explicit spatial reasoning for navigation. This design deliberately separates three questions that are often conflated: whether generated frames contain enough repeatable visual structure for multi-view reconstruction, whether the resulting spatial hypothesis preserves free-space connectivity well enough for planning, and whether a map-feasible plan transfers to the physical robot. arXiv:2610.13849v1 [cs.RO] 9 Oct 2026 Fig. 1. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Generated Futures for Robot Planning 

Generated video is increasingly used to reason about robot motion before execution. Navigation World Models [1], ImagiNav [2], and NavDreamer [8] use predicted observa-tions to evaluate candidate behavior or derive actions and waypoints. Schr¨ odinger’s Navigator [9], Aerial Navigation World Model [10], Uncertainty-Aware Navigation World Model [11], and GVP-WM [12] extend this idea by reason-ing over multiple futures, uncertainty, or learned feasibility constraints. These methods show that generated futures can be useful for navigation; in many cases, the final decision remains tied to the generated sequence or to a learned world representa-tion. HorizonNav studies a different use of generated video. The generated video serves as a form of visual imagination, enabling the reconstruction of a plausible 3D representation of the surrounding environment, which is subsequently used for robot navigation and path planning. 

B. Geometric Reconstruction from Generated Observations 

Several recent systems connect generative models with explicit 3D structure. Dream-SLAM [13] introduces dreamed observations into an active SLAM loop, while WorldMAP [14] converts generated futures into persis-tent spatial representations. WonderWorld [3] and WorldEx-plorer [4] build explorable 3D environments from generated content. Voyager [15], Sora3R [16], Geometry Forcing [17], and GEN3C [5] improve geometric or camera consistency by changing the generation process itself. Direct multi-view geometry methods provide another rel-evant comparison. DUSt3R [18], MASt3R [19], VGGT [20], Depth Anything 3 [21], and MASt3R-Nav [22] estimate geometry directly from visual observations. World Recon-struction From Inconsistent Views [23] specifically addresses contradictions in generated sequences through non-rigid alignment. HorizonNav instead asks whether generated views can provide enough trackable multi-view evidence for a planning-oriented spatial hypothesis without modifying the generator. 

C. 3D Reconstruction and Navigation Planning 

Single-image depth methods such as MiDaS [24], Depth Anything V2 [25], Depth Pro [26], and MoGe [27] provide dense geometric cues from individual images. Video Depth Anything [28] improves temporal consistency across video frames. In HorizonNav, dense depth is not assumed to be ge-ometrically correct merely because it is temporally smooth; we evaluate VDA separately as a densification diagnostic. ORB-SLAM3 [6] provides the primary sparse multi-view geometry used in the reported planning experiments. RRT [7] is the downstream planner. Neither ORB-SLAM3 nor RRT is presented as an algorithmic contribution. The research question is whether generated virtual observations can supply enough geometric evidence to construct a useful pre-motion spatial hypothesis, while keeping the executed plan indepen-dent of the generator’s camera trajectory. B. RRT-Based Navigation in the Reconstructed 3D Space 

We employ the Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) al-gorithm for planning collision-free paths in the reconstructed 3D representation. The point cloud P3D is first converted into an occupancy map, in which observed obstacles are inflated by the robot radius and a safety margin. This produces a conservative free configuration space Xfree for navigation. RRT is well suited to this task because the reconstructed environment can contain irregular obstacle boundaries, nar-row passages, and incomplete geometry. In contrast to gradient-based trajectory optimization methods, RRT does not require a differentiable map or an accurate analytical model of the environment. It iteratively samples candidate configurations, connects them to the nearest node of the search tree, and accepts an extension only when the cor-responding motion segment is collision-free. For a ground robot, the sampled configuration can be represented as 

qground = [ x, y, ψ ]⊤, (15) whereas for a UAV the planner operates directly in 3D: 

qUAV = [ x, y, z, ψ ]⊤. (16) Therefore, the same planning framework can be applied to both platforms, while accounting for the additional altitude dimension of aerial motion. The primary objective is to quickly obtain a feasible and safe path in a partially known environment rather than to find the globally shortest trajectory. This property is particularly useful when the 3D hypothesis is updated after new obser-vations become available: the planner can be executed again from the current robot state using the updated occupancy map. The resulting RRT path can subsequently be shortened and smoothed before execution. Since the reconstructed scene is a geometrically grounded hypothesis rather than an exact map of unseen space, obstacle inflation and online perception remain necessary during execution. Thus, RRT provides a practical planning layer that converts the imagined 3D representation into conservative, executable navigation trajectories. IV. E XPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 

The evaluation is organized around the main scientific question of HorizonNav: whether generated virtual views can provide geometric evidence that is useful for independent pre-motion planning. We evaluate four aspects separately: planning utility as more generated viewpoints are incorpo-rated, independence between generated camera motion and the executed plan, external geometric diagnostics of the recovered spatial hypothesis, and transfer of a precomputed path to a physical robot. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, feasibility is defined with respect to the reconstructed ob-stacle representation; it is not treated as proof that unseen physical geometry has been recovered correctly. 

A. Planning Utility Across Virtual-View Horizons 

We evaluate generated-view prefixes at P20, P40, P60, and P81. For each horizon, we report (i) the number of scenes for which the reconstructed spatial hypothesis admits at least one RRT path and (ii) seed-level RRT success across the available trials. This experiment measures whether additional generated viewpoints improve downstream planning utility. It does not by itself measure physical reconstruction accuracy. 

B. Alternative Virtual Exploration and Independent Plan-ning 

The second experiment tests a central design choice of HorizonNav: generated camera motion supplies virtual ob-servations for reconstruction, but it is not assumed to be an executable robot trajectory. The corridor study contains two distinct real trigger ob-servations, S5 and S6. For each trigger image, four explo-ration commands—FORWARD, LEFT, RIGHT, and ENTER RIGHT—are generated under two random seeds. This pro-duces 16 videos. Each video contains 81 frames at 12 fps and is evaluated at P40 and P81, giving 32 branch-prefix cases. A branch-prefix case is evaluable when the recovered virtual camera trajectory and the independently planned path can both be expressed in the same metric obstacle representation. For each evaluable case, the ORB-SLAM3 camera trajectory is transformed into that representation and checked against the same collision geometry used by RRT. A generated trajectory is considered infeasible when any point along it violates the 0.20 m collision criterion. The corridor planner uses a step size of 0.08 m, a goal bias of 0.45, and at most 15,000 iterations. Planner robustness is evaluated by rerunning RRT with 20 fixed seeds while holding the generated video, recon-struction, metric scale, and obstacle geometry unchanged. This isolates planner randomness from variation introduced by generation or reconstruction. 

C. Geometric Diagnostics 

The third experiment separates two questions that should not be conflated: whether locally reconstructed structure agrees with independently measured geometry at selected features, and whether the recovered global metric scale is consistent. For a thin-aperture diagnostic, the S06 gate scene is evaluated using the sparse SLAM reconstruction and the corrected dense VDA+SLAM reconstruction. The opening is deliberately sensitive to small depth errors, so this compar-ison tests whether dense predictions can alter traversability even after timestamp and metric-frame alignment. For external reference, we use an independently acquired Livox LiDAR map of the corridor. For each available P40 corridor reconstruction, we first register HorizonNav ge-ometry to LiDAR with an SE(3) transform while keeping recovered scale fixed. We then repeat the registration with a similarity transform that introduces one additional scale parameter. The resulting post-hoc scale indicates how much global rescaling would be required after metric anchoring. Separately, selected structural landmarks—door-frame cor-ners, wall intersections, and corners of fixed objects—are compared after rigid registration. These measurements are diagnostics of matched structure and global scale, not a complete surface-reconstruction benchmark. LiDAR is used only for evaluation and is not available to HorizonNav during reconstruction or planning. 

D. Real-World Flight Validation 

The final experiment asks whether a path that is feasible in the reconstructed spatial hypothesis remains executable on the physical robot. This is distinct from map-level planning: physical execution also introduces reconstruction mismatch, vehicle dynamics, controller error, and trajectory tracking error. We perform six quadrotor flight trials in the gate envi-ronment. In every trial, HorizonNav computes the complete waypoint path before takeoff from the generated-view spatial hypothesis. B. Reconstruction Yield and Failure Analysis 

A separate archive contains 67 attempted generated-view reconstruction cases. ORB-SLAM3 produces a usable re-construction in 58 cases, giving an empirical reconstruction yield of 58/67 on generated sequences. This is evidence that feature-based reconstruction is often viable on the gener-ated videos, but it is not a real-versus-generated keypoint-repeatability benchmark. Of the 58 usable reconstructions, 36 support a feasible downstream plan and 22 do not; thus 37.9% of usable spatial hypotheses fail at the planning stage. Inspection of the failed cases identifies recurring mechanisms rather than a single planner defect: insufficient or degenerate virtual camera motion limits triangulation; tracking/reconstruction degradation produces sparse or distorted obstacle structure; and erroneous or expanded geometry can close narrow free-space connections. Longer generation can therefore help by adding viewpoints but can also hurt when later imagined geometry expands into the planning corridor. We conse-quently report reconstruction yield and planning feasibility as separate outcomes. 

C. Independent Planning over Generated Futures 

Of the 32 corridor branch-prefix cases, 25 contain the artifacts required to evaluate both trajectories in the same re-constructed metric map. Among these 25 cases, 6 generated-camera trajectories violate the 0.20 m collision criterion. Independent RRT recovers a collision-feasible alternative in 5 of those 6 cases. This comparison is important because the planner and the generated trajectory are evaluated against the same obstacle geometry. The result therefore does not arise from giving RRT a different map. Instead, it shows that the virtual camera motion can provide useful observations for reconstruction even when that motion itself should not be executed. The P40 reruns remain stable across the 20 planner seeds used in the robustness test. This indicates that the observed feasibility pattern is not explained by one favorable RRT seed.

The experiment does not support a claim that VDA fusion is more accurate than sparse SLAM. VI. D ISCUSSION 

The experiments support a narrower claim than full 3D scene reconstruction: generated virtual observations can pro-vide enough trackable structure for useful pre-motion plan-ning in some scenes. The 58/67 reconstruction yield in the larger archive directly tests the premise that ORB-SLAM3 can operate on generated frames, while the 36/58 down-stream planning rate shows that successful reconstruction is not sufficient. Geometry must also preserve the obstacle boundaries and free-space connectivity that matter to the planner. The independent-planning experiment strengthens this dis-tinction. Among 25 corridor cases evaluable in a common reconstructed metric map, six generated-camera trajectories violate the reconstructed collision constraints, and indepen-dent RRT finds alternatives in five of those six cases. The benefit therefore does not come from executing the motion imagined by the video model; generated motion can be geometrically useful as a source of observations even when it is unsuitable as the robot trajectory. The external diagnostics also limit what can be claimed about reconstruction quality. The LiDAR scale experiment indicates that little additional global rescaling is required after metric anchoring, yet selected matched structural land-marks still show approximately 0.30 m mean discrepancy. In S06, corrected dense VDA+SLAM geometry closes an aper-ture for which the sparse SLAM representation supports 5/5 RRT trials. Global scale consistency, local surface accuracy, and planning utility are therefore distinct properties. Five of six physical flights pass through the gate using paths computed before takeoff. This provides limited end-to-end evidence in the tested gate configuration, not sta-tistical validation of general reconstruction accuracy. The present study is also limited to indoor scenes, one evaluated video generator, fixed structured prompting without a prompt ablation, and no protocol-matched single-image-depth base-line. Historical single-image depth diagnostics used different camera/planner settings and are intentionally excluded from quantitative comparison. A controlled single-image baseline, broader surface-level 3D evaluation, additional environments and robot platforms, and online verification against new physical observations are important next steps. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented HorizonNav, a framework that treats gen-erated video as virtual sensing for pre-motion geometric planning from a single RGB observation. Wan 2.2 proposes hypothetical camera views, ORB-SLAM3 reconstructs sparse geometry, a measured reference resolves metric scale, and goal-biased RRT searches the resulting obstacle representa-tion independently of the generated camera trajectory. Across the 19-scene horizon study, planning-feasible scenes increase from 13/19 to 15/19 and successful RRT trials from 62/90 to 75/90. In the separate corridor study, independent RRT recovers feasible alternatives in five of six cases where generated-camera motion violates the recon-structed collision constraints. A larger 67-case archive yields 58 usable reconstructions, of which 36 support planning and 22 fail, making the principal failure modes explicit. External LiDAR diagnostics show approximately 0.30 m mean discrepancy at selected matched structural landmarks and a median additional global scale factor of 0.995 × across 15 P40 reconstructions. Five of six physical quadrotor trials traverse the gate using paths computed before takeoff. These results support generated virtual views as a source of planning-oriented geometric evidence, not as proof that the true unseen 3D scene has been recovered. The dense-depth diagnostic further shows that adding geometry can reduce, rather than improve, planning utility when local depth errors create false obstacles. Future work should add a protocol-matched single-image baseline, surface-level reconstruction metrics against independent 3D ground truth, generator and prompt ablations, broader robot/environment testing, and online verification that can accept, revise, or reject imagined geometry as real observations become available.
