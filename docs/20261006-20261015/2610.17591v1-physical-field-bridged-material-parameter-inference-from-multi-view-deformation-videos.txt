Abstract
Estimating simulation-ready material parameters from visual observations is crucial for
deformable-object manipulation, yet remains challenging because material properties are
only indirectly expressed through physical responses such as contact, deformation, recovery,
and stress concentration. We introduce physical-field-bridged material inference, a single-pass
framework that treats dense physical responses as intermediate evidence between RGB defor-
mation videos and a discrete constitutive type together with continuous material parameters.
This design addresses a practical limitation of existing inverse-physics methods, which of-
ten rely on per-scene reconstruction, online tuning, or simulation-in-the-loop optimization,
while feed-forward visual estimators can exploit appearance or category shortcuts. Given
multi-view RGB videos, our model predicts force/contact masks, projected motion-flow fields,
and projected stress-response maps, then conditions constitutive-type classification and pa-
rameter regression on these fields. To train and evaluate this formulation, we construct
DeformFieldBench, a 3DGS–MPM learning environment with 5,000 simulations over 100
object shapes, five mechanically informative loading modes, and dense pixel-aligned physical
supervision. Our method achieves the best median errors on all four continuous parameters,
raises stress-hotspot recall from 0.0762 to 0.5121, and reaches 0.8676 SSIM/36.82 dB PSNR
in same-simulator replay on unseen objects. Under a protocol-matched GSO evaluation,
it performs inference and future-state prediction in approximately 30 seconds rather than
the 15–120 minutes required by per-scene optimization; it also produces measurable replay
agreement on five real-world sequences. These results support dense physical fields as an
effective bridge for fast material inference, while cross-simulator and real-world tests expose
remaining domain and identifiability gaps. The dataset is available on Hugging Face, and
source code is available in the DeformFieldBench GitHub repository.

1 Introduction
Embodied agents need to predict how objects respond to physical interaction. For deformable
objects, this requires estimating not only geometry or semantic category, but also material
parameters such as Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, density, and yield stress. These parameters
are simulation-ready: once inferred, they can be inserted into a physics simulator to forecast
deformation under new actions. Recent progress in inverse physics, neural rendering, and physics-
aware 3D representations has started to bridge visual perception and physical simulation, enabling
material estimation from videos or reconstructed geometry [1–5]. Such a bridge is crucial for
robotic manipulation, where perception should support actionable physical prediction rather than
recognition alone.
However, current visual system-identification methods remain difficult to use as real-time per-
ception modules. Many pipelines estimate material parameters through per-scene reconstruction
[2, 6, 7] or simulation-in-the-loop optimization [1, 4, 5, 8], repeatedly rolling out a simulator,
comparing rendered future states with observations, and updating parameters. These methods
are physically grounded, but are often computationally expensive, scene-specific, and dependent
on reconstructed geometry, camera information, or iterative test-time tuning. A more scalable
alternative is amortized feed-forward inference from RGB images or videos [9–13]. Yet one-shot
visual material inference is highly under-constrained: physical parameters are only indirectly
expressed through appearance and motion, and existing feed-forward methods often rely on large
vision-language models, semantic feature fields, interaction priors, or manually designed con-
straints. While useful, such priors can bias predictions toward object category, texture, or coarse
material semantics. For deformable objects, a successful model should instead infer material
properties from observed physical responses, such as contact, deformation propagation, recovery,
and stress concentration.
This motivates a missing ingredient: dense physical supervision for learning the visual evi-
dence behind material parameters. Existing deformable-object datasets and physical-reasoning
benchmarks [14–20] often provide RGB videos, object states, or global labels, but rarely expose

where external forces are applied, motion fields reveal how deformation propagates, and stress re-

limited geometries or action patterns dominated by dropping and impact, whereas compression,

To address this gap, we construct DeformFieldBench, a 3DGS–MPM simulation environment
for learning simulation-ready material inference from multi-view RGB videos. It contains 5,000
simulations over 100 everyday object shapes, five mechanically informative loading modes,
multiple material categories, and broad parameter ranges. Each sample provides synchronized
three-view RGB deformation videos, object masks, force/contact masks, projected motion-flow
fields, projected stress-response maps, and ground-truth material parameters. By combining
the rendering efficiency of 3D Gaussian Splatting with the physical interpretability of MPM,
DeformFieldBench provides pixel-aligned physical supervision while keeping the inference-time
input purely visual. We use this benchmark not merely as a dataset contribution, but to study a
central modeling question: how should dense physical fields be used to improve feed-forward
material-parameter inference?
We introduce a physical-field-bridged multi-view model to answer this question. Unlike direct
video regression, which maps RGB observations to material parameters through a single opaque
representation, our model predicts intermediate physical responses and uses them as evidence
for parameter estimation. Given multi-view RGB deformation videos, it extracts static geometric
cues and dynamic deformation cues, fuses them across views, and decomposes the representation
through a physics bottleneck. It then predicts force/contact regions, projected motion-flow fields,
and projected stress-response fields. These predictions are not auxiliary outputs only: they are
encoded and fused back into the material regressor, so that parameter estimation is conditioned
on where the object is loaded, how motion propagates, and where stress concentrates. Since
stress is less directly observable than contact and motion, we adopt a staged design that first
learns visually grounded contact and flow prediction, then infers stress response, and finally
regresses material parameters from the predicted fields and bottleneck representation.
The resulting framework provides an amortized feed-forward alternative to online system
identification. It predicts a constitutive type, continuous material parameters, and dense physical-
response fields in a single test-time forward pass, without per-scene reconstruction, camera-pose
input, online tuning, or simulation-in-the-loop optimization. Experiments on object-level general-
ization show that our method achieves lower median errors on all four continuous parameters than
VLM-based estimators and direct supervised video-regression baselines. It also substantially im-
proves dense physical-field prediction, especially stress-hotspot localization. When the predicted
constitutive model and parameters are fed back into the simulator, our method produces dynami-
cally plausible future deformation on unseen objects. Protocol-matched, held-out-interaction,
input-robustness, cross-domain, and real-world evaluations further characterize when this fast
amortized inference transfers and where domain gaps remain.
In summary, our contributions are three-fold:
• We construct DeformFieldBench, a 3DGS–MPM-based learning environment for feed-forward,
simulation-ready material inference, with diverse objects, mechanically informative load-
ing modes, multi-view RGB deformation videos, simulator-ready material labels, and dense
physical-field supervision for force/contact, motion flow, and stress response.

2 Related Work
Physics-aware Gaussian representations and dynamic scene modeling. Recent advances in
3D Gaussian representations have extended Gaussian primitives from static scene reconstruction
to non-rigid dynamics and physically grounded modeling. Dynamic Gaussian manipulation
methods study how Gaussian-based scene representations can support robotic interaction and
non-rigid manipulation [21]. Generative Gaussian methods further extend this representation to
4D asset generation and dynamic content synthesis [22, 23]. These methods provide flexible visual
representations for dynamic objects, but they do not directly target the recovery of simulator-ready
material parameters from deformation observations.
Another line of work couples Gaussian representations with physical simulation. PhysGaussian
and related physics-integrated systems connect 3D Gaussians with MPM-based solvers, making it
possible to generate or simulate physically plausible deformable dynamics from Gaussian primitives
[24, 25]. These systems show that Gaussian representations can be made compatible with
continuum simulation, which motivates our use of a 3DGS–MPM pipeline for dataset construction.
However, their main focus is on forward simulation, reconstruction, or physically grounded
generation, whereas our benchmark targets feed-forward material-parameter inference from
multi-view RGB deformation videos.
Several recent methods also incorporate semantic or multimodal physical priors into Gaussian
representations. Property-aware Gaussian methods use large multimodal models to infer coarse
physical attributes or semantic-level material information [9, 10]. More recent physical Gaussian
models and real-video-to-physics pipelines explore feed-forward 4D synthesis and physics-aware
demonstration generation [26, 27]. These methods suggest that high-level priors are useful
for physical reasoning, but they often struggle to capture fine temporal cues such as rebound,
oscillation, and localized stress concentration. Our work instead uses dense stress, flow, and
contact fields as explicit intermediate supervision for material inference.
Inverse physics and simulation-ready material inference. System identification and inverse-
physics methods aim to recover physical parameters from observed motion, deformation, or
reconstructed geometry. Representative approaches include unified constitutive modeling for
inverse simulation [28], material-agnostic system identification from videos [1], learning neural
constitutive laws from motion observations [29], and Gaussian-informed continuum modeling for
physical property identification and simulation [2, 3]. These methods are physically grounded
and have demonstrated strong reconstruction or simulation performance in controlled settings.
Despite their strengths, many existing inverse-physics pipelines depend on assumptions that
limit their use as real-time perception modules. Some methods require known geometry, depth
observations, or reconstructed object states; others rely on iterative test-time optimization, where
a simulator is repeatedly rolled out and matched to observations. This per-scene optimization

can be expensive when the goal is to quickly infer physical parameters for downstream robotic
manipulation. In addition, many methods optimize global parameters through future-state
matching, but do not explicitly supervise intermediate physical responses. Without field-level
supervision, a learned model may rely on object appearance, texture, or semantic category
shortcuts rather than material-discriminative deformation cues. Our method addresses this issue
by predicting motion flow, force/contact regions, and stress distributions before classifying the
constitutive family and regressing Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, density, and yield stress.
Feed-forward physics inference. PIXIE predicts discrete material categories and continuous
physical properties with supervised feed-forward learning from static multi-view appearance and
geometry [30]. Our setting is complementary: it observes deformation videos under external
interaction and explicitly predicts contact, motion-flow, and stress-response fields before material
inference. This dynamic evidence is useful when objects with similar texture and geometry differ
in stiffness, plasticity, density, or internal structure, properties that static appearance alone may
leave ambiguous.
Datasets and benchmarks for physical scene understanding. Datasets and simulation en-
vironments have played an important role in physical scene understanding. General-purpose
environments such as Physion [14] and ThreeDWorld [15] provide standardized platforms for
evaluating physical prediction and intuitive physical reasoning. However, these environments
usually abstract away simulator-ready material parameters and internal physical response fields,
which makes them less suitable for training models that infer numerical material properties.
Deformable-object benchmarks provide more relevant settings for manipulation and dynamics
learning. SoftGym [16], Cloth3D [17], and DEDO [18] focus on control-oriented deformable-
object tasks, garment dynamics, or dynamic environments with deformable objects. These datasets
are valuable for learning interaction policies and deformation dynamics, but they typically do not
pair visual observations with explicit Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, density, yield stress, or
dense stress-field annotations.
Real-world resources provide complementary signals. DOFS [19] and PokeFlex [20] contain
real deformable-object observations, geometry, or force-related measurements. These datasets
are important for grounding deformable-object research in real measurements, but their scale,
parameter coverage, and dense field annotations are limited for training generalizable material-
inference models. Recent simulation-aligned pipelines such as Vid2Sim [7] and PhysTwin [5]
reconstruct geometry and physics from videos, but remain centered on per-scene reconstruction
rather than large-scale benchmarking of feed-forward material inference.
DeformFieldBench is designed to complement these resources. It provides diverse object shapes,
five mechanically informative loading modes, broad material-parameter ranges, and pixel-aligned
physical fields for stress, flow, and force/contact regions. This enables systematic evaluation of
whether models can infer simulation-ready material parameters from visual deformation evidence
rather than from appearance alone.
3 DeformFieldBench: A Learning Environment for Material Inference
A central difficulty in feed-forward material-parameter inference is that the target variables are
not directly observable from RGB appearance. Young’s modulus, density, Poisson’s ratio, and
yield stress are expressed through how an object responds to external loading: where contact
occurs, how deformation propagates, how the object recovers after release, and where stress

concentrates. Existing datasets usually provide visual observations or reconstructed states, but
rarely expose these intermediate physical responses in a form that can supervise learning. As a
result, video-based models are often forced to learn a black-box mapping from appearance and
motion to global material parameters, which makes them vulnerable to object-category or texture
shortcuts.
We construct DeformFieldBench to study a different formulation: material inference as learning
from dense physical responses. The benchmark is built on a 3DGS–MPM simulation pipeline and
contains 5,000 simulations over 100 everyday object shapes, five loading modes (press, stretch,
shear, bend, and drop), multiple material categories (jelly, metal, and plasticine), and broad
material-parameter ranges. Each sample includes synchronized three-view RGB deformation
videos, object masks, force/contact masks, projected motion-flow fields, projected stress-response
maps, action labels, and ground-truth material parameters

extrinsics are not provided to the model.
3.1 Benchmark Design
DeformFieldBench is built on a 3DGS–MPM simulation pipeline. Starting from a 3D Gaussian
object representation, we convert visible Gaussian primitives into volumetric particles for MPM
simulation, assign material parameters sampled from predefined ranges, and evolve the particles
under external loading. The deformed particle states are then mapped back to Gaussian primitives
and rendered into multi-view RGB videos and aligned physical-field videos. This design combines
the rendering efficiency and appearance diversity of 3D Gaussian Splatting with the physical
interpretability of MPM-based deformable simulation. A key design principle is to decouple visual
appearance from material properties. By varying object geometry and surface style independently
from E, ν, ρ, and σy, the benchmark discourages models from predicting materials through
category or texture shortcuts. Instead, models must rely on deformation behavior. To make such
behavior informative, we include five loading modes: press, stretch, shear, bend, and drop. These
actions expose complementary physical cues, including local contact, compressibility, stiffness,
tangential deformation, flexural recovery, inertia, and impact response. For actuator-driven
actions, object-aware loading policies determine contact regions and trajectories from object
geometry, and the simulation continues after release to capture rebound, oscillation, and residual
deformation.

3.2 Dense Physical Field Annotations
The main distinction of DeformFieldBench is that RGB deformation videos are paired with dense,
pixel-aligned physical fields. These fields are not privileged inputs at test time; they supervise the
intermediate physical responses that should mediate material inference. Force/contact masks
identify the regions affected by external actuation or contact, specifying where deformation
is caused. Projected motion-flow fields are rendered from particle displacements between
consecutive frames and describe how deformation propagates over the visible surface. Projected
stress-response maps are computed from MPM stress and rendered into image space, revealing
force concentration and yielding-related responses that may not be directly visible in RGB motion.
We export these annotations in image space rather than requiring full 3D particle trajectories
or volumetric stress tensors. This keeps the learning problem aligned with visual perception,
reduces storage and training cost, and still preserves the physical signals most relevant to material
inference. The resulting supervision allows a model to learn not only what parameter values to
predict, but also which physical evidence supports the prediction.
Efficient multi-modal field export. A practical challenge in building dense physical supervision is
that full 3D particle trajectories and per-particle stress tensors are expensive to store and difficult
to use for video-based learning. We therefore export physical responses directly as image-space
fields through Gaussian rendering. Specifically, particle motion is projected and encoded into
rendered flow maps, while stress magnitude is mapped into rendered stress-response images. This
design preserves dense, high-frequency physical signals while keeping the dataset lightweight and
naturally aligned with RGB observations. It also makes DeformFieldBench scalable: compared
with prior deformable physical-reasoning resources, our benchmark jointly supports broader
object diversity, more loading types, wider parameter ranges, and stronger pixel-aligned physical
supervision [3, 20, 27, 28].

3.4 Tasks and Evaluation Protocol
The primary task is to infer a constitutive type ˆc and continuous material parameters from
synchronized three-view RGB videos: ˆp = [ ˆE, ˆν, ˆρ, ˆσy]. We evaluate E, ρ, and σy in log space due
to their large dynamic ranges, and evaluate ν in raw space. Besides per-parameter errors, we
report sample-wise parameter error to measure whether a method predicts a coherent parameter
set for each simulation sample.

We use an object-level split, with 90 objects for training and 10 unseen objects for testing,
to evaluate generalization beyond memorized shapes or appearances. We also evaluate dense
field prediction: contact-region quality for force masks, coverage and velocity-aware quality for
motion flow, and hotspot localization for stress response. Finally, we perform simulation replay by
feeding the predicted constitutive type and parameters back into the simulator and comparing the
rendered deformation with the ground truth. This replay protocol is important because different
parameter combinations may induce similar visible motion; a useful estimate should therefore
be not only numerically accurate, but also dynamically plausible. Together, parameter accuracy,
field quality, object-level generalization, and replay performance provide the basis for evaluating
the physical-field-bridged model introduced next. Details are reported in Section 5.

Dataset name. DeformFieldBench: Physical-Field Material Parameter Dataset with 5,000 3DGS–
MPM simulations.
Hosting and access. The dataset artifacts are available on Hugging Face. Source code, docu-
mentation, and release updates are available in the DeformFieldBench GitHub repository. The
released files include rendered multi-view RGB deformation videos, projected physical-response
fields, masks, action labels, material parameters, and train/test split metadata.
Dataset purpose. DeformFieldBench is designed for simulation-ready material-parameter in-
ference of deformable objects from multi-view RGB deformation videos. It supports both global
parameter regression and dense physical-response prediction.
Dataset scale. The dataset contains 5,000 simulation samples over 100 object shapes. Each
object is simulated under five loading modes: press, stretch, shear, bend, and drop. Each sample
contains 512×512 observations from three stationary canonical cameras distributed approximately
horizontally around the normalized scene, together with aligned physical-field annotations.
Modalities and annotations. Each sample contains: (1) multi-view RGB deformation videos;
(2) object masks; (3) force/contact masks; (4) projected motion-flow fields; (5) projected stress-
response maps; (6) action labels; (7) a ground-truth constitutive family; and (8) continuous
material parameters, including Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, density, and yield stress.
Generation pipeline. The dataset is generated using a 3DGS–MPM simulation pipeline. Starting
from 3D Gaussian object representations, we densify the object volume into MPM particles,
assign material parameters, apply controlled mechanical loading, and render RGB videos and
image-space physical-response fields through the Gaussian rendering pipeline.
Splits. We use an object-level split to evaluate generalization. Specifically, 90 objects are used
for training and 10 completely unseen objects are reserved for testing. This prevents identical
object shapes from appearing in both training and test sets.
Evaluation tasks. The benchmark supports: (1) constitutive-type classification and material-
parameter regression; (2) dense stress-response prediction; (3) projected motion-flow prediction;
(4) force/contact mask prediction; (5) simulation replay evaluation using the predicted constitutive
type and material parameters.

Known limitations. The dataset is synthetic and generated by one 3DGS–MPM pipeline. It

4 Physical-Field-Bridged Material Inference
Our feed-forward model maps multi-view RGB deformation videos to simulation-ready material
parameters through explicit physical evidence. Direct regression can entangle material cues with
category and texture, while fields trained only as auxiliary outputs need not influence the final
prediction. As shown in Fig. 2, our model instead fuses frame-, patch-, and multi-scale features,
factorizes them through a physics bottleneck for contact, deformation, and stress, and supervises
the corresponding dense fields. Encodings of the predicted fields are then fused back into the
bottleneck, conditioning constitutive and parameter inference on where loading occurs, how
motion propagates, and where stress concentrates.

4.3 Dense Physical Field Prediction
A shared spatiotemporal decoder combines the global condition, physical latents, frame and
patch features, and multi-scale spatial features; task-specific heads predict force/contact, motion
flow, and stress response. The heads follow an observability hierarchy: contact and flow are
grounded in actuator location and visible deformation, whereas stress is less directly observable
and is therefore conditioned on both predicted responses. This sequential dependency ties stress
to loading and deformation propagation rather than surface appearance alone. Object masks
suppress background responses, especially for sparse stress and flow signals.
4.3.1 Dense Physical Field Decoder
Dense physical fields are predicted by a shared conditional spatiotemporal decoder. The decoder
combines global multi-view information, frame-level features, temporal embeddings, physical
latents, patch-level features, and multi-scale spatial RGB conditions. These conditions are first
projected into a low-resolution seed feature map, then refined by temporal mixing blocks and
upsampling layers to produce a shared dense feature tensor:
G = Dshared(z, fv,t, zphys, cv,t, pv,t),
(42)
where cv,t denotes multi-scale spatial RGB conditions and pv,t denotes patch-token conditions.
The shared tensor G is then used by task-adaptive heads to predict flow, force, and stress.
Each task head modulates the shared feature using its corresponding physical latent:
ˆYk = Hk(G, hk),
k ∈{f, m, s},
(43)
where ˆYf, ˆYm, and ˆYs denote the predicted flow, force/contact, and stress fields, respectively.
This design allows the three dense prediction tasks to share common spatiotemporal features
while preserving task-specific behavior.

4.5 Training Objective
The model is trained with a multi-task objective that combines constitutive classification, parameter
regression, dense field supervision, and physics-consistency regularization:
L = Lreg + λcLcls + λsLstress + λfLflow + λmLforce + λpLphys.
(49)
Here Lcls is cross-entropy over the three constitutive families. The regression loss is computed in
the transformed parameter space. Invalid or undefined dimensions are masked: yield stress is
supervised only for samples whose ground-truth law includes yielding, and purely elastic samples
are excluded from yield-stress parameter-error statistics. During replay, yield stress is ignored if
the predicted type is non-yielding; if an elastic sample is misclassified as yielding, the predicted
yield stress is used and the resulting replay error remains included. For dense fields, stress
and flow are supervised with foreground-weighted reconstruction losses and edge-consistency
regularization. Force prediction is supervised using a combination of binary cross-entropy and
Dice loss.

4.5.1 Target-space Transformation and Validity Masking
We do not regress the raw physical values directly. Instead, we transform the target parameters as

further construct a per-dimension validity mask so that only physically meaningful targets
participate in the loss. Specifically, we require E > 0, ρ > 0, and ν ∈(0, 0.5]; yield stress
additionally requires σy > 0 and a yielding ground-truth constitutive family. The transformed
targets are then normalized using training-set statistics before regression loss is applied.
4.6 Staged Optimization
To reduce gradient competition among tasks, we adopt a staged training schedule. The model
first learns flow and force prediction, which are more directly observable from RGB videos. It
then trains the stress branch conditioned on the learned flow and force fields. After that, the
full dense-field pipeline is optimized jointly. Finally, the dense-field components are frozen and
the constitutive classification and parameter regression branches are refined using the predicted
physical responses.
This training order follows the physical dependency encoded in the architecture: contact and
motion provide low-level evidence, stress is inferred from these responses, and the constitutive
family and continuous parameters are estimated from the resulting physical state.

reconstruction is less important than identifying high-response regions, since stress hotspots

and region-overlap metrics. For flow, correct coverage of moving regions is not sufficient: the
predicted motion should also carry meaningful velocity direction and magnitude. We therefore
report both coverage IoU and velocity-aware motion quality. Force is a contact/force mask, so its
evaluation is more direct and uses masked reconstruction and region overlap.
Our model improves stress hotspot recall, stress region overlap, flow coverage, velocity overlap,
and temporal consistency over the supervised baseline. Compared with the supervised baseline,
which directly predicts all outputs from shared video features, our method performs stress infer-
ence through a chained physical reasoning process. The predicted flow and force representations
provide additional cues about motion discontinuities, contact regions, and force concentration, all
of which are closely related to stress localization. Moreover, our model uses both static geometric
features and dynamic deformation features, allowing stress prediction to depend on object struc-
ture as well as temporal response. These additional physical and spatiotemporal cues make stress
hotspots easier to identify, explaining the gain over direct supervised field regression. Qualitative
examples of the predicted flow, force/contact, and stress fields are provided in Section 6.2.3.

6.3 Object-level Generalization and Ablation Study
We use an object-level train/test split to test whether the model learns transferable physical rules
rather than memorizing object-specific appearance. Under this setting, our method achieves the
strongest robust sample-wise parameter accuracy. In particular, Table 5 shows that the full model
obtains the best unfiltered median SPE and the best top-20% filtered SPE. Although the “w/o
multi-stage training” variant has a slightly lower unfiltered Param Mean MAE, it performs worse
in SPE, indicating that per-parameter averages alone do not fully capture whether a method
predicts a coherent parameter set for each sample.
The ablations confirm the importance of the full design. Removing boundary reasoning weakens
edge/contact-sensitive supervision. Removing field auxiliary learning degrades the physical inter-
mediate representation. Removing multi-stage training reduces the stability of the field-bridged
learning process. The full model achieves the best range-balanced parameter-set prediction,
validating the role of explicit physical fields and staged reasoning in object-level generalization.

6.5 Protocol-Matched Cross-Benchmark Evaluation
Under Vid2Sim’s GSO protocol (16 inference and 8 prediction frames), Vid2Sim reaches 25.07

6.5.1 GSO Future-State Protocol
Following Vid2Sim, we use the first 16 frames for material inference, align the simulated state
with the final observed frame, and predict the subsequent 8 frames. Table 7 reports all methods
on this common protocol. Runtimes are measured or reported on one NVIDIA RTX 4090.
Vid2Sim is trained on approximately 50K falling-object sequences closely aligned with the GSO
evaluation, whereas our original training set spans multiple actively applied forces and is not
specialized to dropping. Per-scene approaches can specialize reconstruction and parameters to
each sequence; our method instead amortizes inference across scenes. The result should therefore
be interpreted as an accuracy–efficiency trade-off rather than an accuracy claim over per-scene
optimization.

6.8 Real-world Object Application
We quantitatively evaluate five real deformation sequences involving two previously unseen
objects: a wallet under bending, compression, and stretching, and a thin-walled plastic bottle
under bending and compression. No real-data fine-tuning or ground-truth force, stress, material
parameter, or constitutive-type label is used.
Replay protocol. For each interaction, we capture synchronized multi-view RGB videos, predict
the constitutive type and continuous parameters, align the initial object geometry and cameras,
and replay the observed interaction with the predicted material configuration. The same fore-
ground mask is applied to the aligned real and rendered sequences. Masks are initialized with
SAM and manually refined to remove background regions and areas occluded by the manipulating
hand. We then compute image-space PSNR and SSIM between the real and rendered foreground
sequences.
The plastic-bottle compression sequence has the strongest agreement, where interaction and
boundary conditions can be aligned most reliably. Residual differences arise from geometry and
camera alignment, unknown contact forces, approximate boundary conditions, hand occlusion,
trajectory mismatch, heterogeneous object composition, and constitutive behavior outside the
current simulator. PSNR and SSIM therefore measure replay consistency at the deformation-
behavior level; they do not establish that the inferred numerical parameters equal unknown real
material properties.

7 Discussion and Limitations
Our results suggest that simulation-ready material inference benefits from explicitly decomposing
visual observations into dense physical fields before predicting a constitutive family and global
material parameters. Compared with direct video-to-parameter regression, force/contact, motion
flow, and stress response provide more interpretable physical evidence, especially when rebound,
oscillation, recovery, and stress concentration are subtle.
Despite these benefits, our study has several limitations. First, DeformFieldBench is primarily
synthetic. The real-world replay study provides quantitative behavior-level evidence but lacks
calibrated force sensing and ground-truth material parameters, so it cannot validate absolute
recovery. Second, zero-shot performance drops across simulator and interaction domains. Damp-
ing, object filling, integration, contact, and boundary-condition implementations can change a
trajectory even when nominal material parameters match. Third, the original model is trained
with a canonical three-camera arrangement, does not consume extrinsics, and remains sensi-
tive to whether a view exposes contact and deformation. Fourth, its flow and stress outputs
are image-space projections rather than volumetric fields. Finally, continuous-parameter errors
remain substantial even when replay appears plausible, reflecting both observation ambiguity
and the many-to-one relationship between parameters and trajectories.
Homogeneous-material assumption. The current output is one global constitutive type and
parameter set per object. Dense physical fields act as spatial evidence for global system iden-
tification; they do not recover regional material properties. For a composite object such as a
wallet made of leather, fabric, stitching, and metal, the result should be interpreted as an effective
global approximation of the dominant observed deformation, not as the physical property of
each component. A spatial extension could lift multi-view and field features to surface points,
volumetric elements, or MPM particles and predict region-level constitutive types and parame-
ters. Such an extension requires heterogeneous training objects, material-boundary annotations,
image-to-particle correspondence, region-level supervision, and simulators that handle material
interfaces.
We observe that most long-tail parameter errors are concentrated on high-stiffness metallic
samples. This failure mode is partly caused by an identifiability limitation of the current load-
ing protocol, rather than only by insufficient model capacity. In our simulator, press, stretch,
shear, and bend actions are imposed by kinematic actuators with prescribed trajectories. For
high-stiffness metallic objects, this can resemble applying an effectively large external force until
deformation occurs. Under such force-saturated loading, metallic objects with different Young’s
moduli or yield stresses may exhibit similar visible behavior, including limited elastic recovery,
permanent deformation, and saturated relative stress responses. Consequently, their RGB defor-
mation trajectories, projected flow fields, and normalized image-space stress maps become less
discriminative than those of more recoverable materials such as jelly-like or plasticine-like objects.
A direct way to reduce this ambiguity is to include absolute force or stress measurements. For
example, a robotic force-torque sensor, tactile array, pressure sensor, or calibrated actuator-force
signal could reveal the force magnitude at which deformation starts, which would be informative
for distinguishing near-rigid metallic materials. Our current dataset does not include such absolute
external-force measurements or per-particle absolute stress targets; instead, it provides rendered
and normalized physical-response fields. This design keeps the benchmark lightweight, visually
aligned, and suitable for dense pixel-level supervision, but it also limits the identifiability of
high-stiffness materials whose visible deformation responses are weak or saturated.

Future versions of DeformFieldBench could incorporate calibrated loading magnitudes, per-
point absolute stress, tactile or force-torque measurements, spatially varying materials, and
broader randomization of simulator settings. Training with randomized camera angle, elevation,
view count, view dropping, and view order would reduce reliance on the canonical arrangement;
a pose-conditioned set encoder could explicitly support arbitrary cameras and variable view
counts. These are future extensions rather than components of the original 5K-result model. The
framework could also be integrated with vision-language priors and robotic sensing to support
downstream reasoning, planning, and interaction.
8 Conclusion
We introduced physical-field-bridged material inference, which organizes observable contact,
motion, and stress evidence before predicting a constitutive type and continuous parameters from
multi-view RGB videos. DeformFieldBench supplies the aligned dense fields and simulator-ready
supervision required to study this formulation. The physical fields improve median parameter
accuracy, field quality, object-level generalization, and same-simulator replay; protocol-matched,
held-out-interaction, input-robustness, adaptation, and real-world evaluations characterize trans-
fer beyond the original split. The current scope remains bounded: absolute parameter recovery
is difficult, image-space replay measures behavioral consistency rather than material truth, and
performance is sensitive to camera evidence and simulator configuration. Within this scope,
the results support physical-field bridging as a practical basis for fast material inference while
motivating pose-aware aggregation, broader simulator randomization, calibrated sensing, and
spatially varying material prediction.
