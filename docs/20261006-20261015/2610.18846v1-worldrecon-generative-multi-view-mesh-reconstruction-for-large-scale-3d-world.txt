ABSTRACT
Reconstructing complete, high-quality meshes of large-scale scenes from unposed
multi-view images requires faithful geometric grounding while plausibly com-
pleting surfaces that are unobserved or only sparsely captured. We present Worl-
dRecon, a generative multi-view reconstruction framework for recovering com-
plete, large-scale 3D scene meshes from unposed RGB images. WorldRecon uses
a coarse, noisy global point cloud and camera poses estimated from the input im-
ages by a reconstruction model as a global scaffold for whole-scene generation and
partitions it into overlapping spatial blocks. To robustly leverage these imperfect
geometric priors, we equip the native 3D generative model TRELLIS.2 with a ded-
icated reconstruction-based conditioning architecture that generates each block
from its local point-cloud crop and block-relevant image observations, producing
sparse structure, detailed geometry, and PBR materials. To maintain scene-level
coherence, we introduce neighbour-aware latent inpainting and a causal world-
generation procedure that propagates structural, geometric, and material context
across overlapping blocks. Together, these designs scale multi-view generative
reconstruction to large-scale scenes, yielding coherent, unified meshes. Extensive
experiments demonstrate that WorldRecon achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction
performance, reducing Chamfer distance by 14.9% and 43.1% while improving F-
score by 17.3% and 12.0% over the strongest baselines on synthetic and real-world
indoor scenes, respectively, thereby demonstrating faithful and complete gener-

INTRODUCTION
Recovering a large-scale scene from unposed RGB images is a fundamental problem in computer
vision, with broad applications in virtual and augmented reality, embodied intelligence, and digital
content production. Reconstruction-based methods Wang et al. (2024); Leroy et al. (2024); Wang
et al. (2026); Barron et al. (2022); Yu et al. (2022); Kerbl et al. (2023) recover scene geometry from
visual observations, but often leave occluded regions incomplete and produce noisy geometry that
is difficult to convert into clean meshes with detailed surfaces and materials.
Generative priors improve reconstruction completeness by inferring plausible geometry and appear-
ance beyond the observations. Building on native 3D generative models Xiang et al. (2025; 2026);
Zhang et al. (2024), recent multi-view extensions Chang et al. (2025); Huang et al. (2026) directly
reconstruct structured 3D assets from unposed RGB images. Extending these methods to scene
scale, however, remains challenging as the spatial extent and geometric complexity of an environ-
ment exceed the capacity of a single high-resolution generative representation.
Recent scene generation methods Chen et al. (2025); Wu et al. (2024b); Meng et al. (2025); Li et al.
(2026); Engstler et al. (2025) scale to substantially larger environments by decomposing a scene
into overlapping spatial blocks and generating them jointly or progressively. However, they are typ-
ically conditioned on text or layouts and thus lack visual guidance and 3D spatial grounding. More
closely related to our task, scene-level generative reconstruction methods represent large scenes us-
ing hierarchical voxel latents Ren et al. (2024) or overlapping scene chunks Schmid et al. (2026).
However, they require accurate camera poses to project image features into a 3D feature volume,
making reconstruction quality sensitive to pose errors.
In this work, we introduce WorldRecon, a generative multi-view framework for reconstructing large-
scale scene meshes from unposed RGB images. We employ VGGT-ΩWang et al. (2026) to estimate
coarse camera poses and recover a global point-cloud scaffold, which we partition into overlapping
blocks. Our reconstruction-based conditioning leverages local point-cloud crops and block-relevant
image observations, enabling the generation of complete, high-fidelity mesh blocks with PBR ma-
terials from imperfect geometric and camera estimates. To promote consistency across blocks, we
train neighbour-aware latent inpainting models that condition each new block on overlap latents
from previously generated neighbours. At inference, we progressively expand the scene following
a causal block order, propagating sparse structure, geometry, and material context through shared
world representations. This enables neighbouring blocks to transition seamlessly in both geometry
and appearance before being decoded and assembled into a unified scene mesh.
Extensive experiments on 3D-FRONT Fu et al. (2021) and ScanNet++ Yeshwanth et al. (2023)
demonstrate that WorldRecon reconstructs globally accurate and complete indoor scenes with fine
geometric and material details from unposed RGB images on both synthetic and real-world settings.
In summary, our main contributions are as follows:
• We present WorldRecon, a framework that integrates 3D reconstruction and generation to recover
large-scale, high-fidelity scene meshes with PBR materials from unposed multi-view RGB images.
• We develop reconstruction-based block generation with neighbour-aware inpainting, producing
faithful mesh blocks from local observations and imperfect geometric and camera estimates.
• We propose a causal world-generation pipeline that propagates sparse structure, geometry, and
material context across overlapping blocks for scalable and seamless scene generation.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in large-scale mesh reconstruc-
tion from unposed multi-view images across synthetic and real-world indoor scenes.

RELATED WORK
Multi-View 3D Reconstruction.
Traditional 3D reconstruction relies on Structure-from-
Motion Schonberger & Frahm (2016); Pan et al. (2024) and Multi-View Stereo Furukawa &
2


Hern´andez (2015); Sch¨onberger et al. (2016) to recover camera poses and scene geometry through
triangulation. Learning-based MVS methods Yao et al. (2018; 2019); Wang et al. (2021a) further im-
prove quality and efficiency via differentiable cost volumes. More recently, pose-free models Wang
et al. (2024); Leroy et al. (2024); Wang et al. (2025; 2026) cast the pairwise reconstruction problem
as a regression of pointmaps, jointly regressing cameras and dense geometry. Beyond discrete point
clouds, Neural Radiance Fields Mildenhall et al. (2021); Wang et al. (2021c); Barron et al. (2022)
reframe reconstruction as inverse volumetric rendering for photorealistic view synthesis. Neural
implicit surface methods Wang et al. (2021b); Yariv et al. (2021); Yu et al. (2022) represent scenes
as continuous signed-distance functions. 3D Gaussian Splatting Kerbl et al. (2023); Huang et al.
(2024); Charatan et al. (2024) optimize scenes via differentiable rasterization for fast rendering.
However, these methods suffer from incomplete reconstruction and tend to produce over-smooth,
blurred details in occluded regions.
Generative Multi-View 3D Reconstruction. Generative priors are introduced in multi-view 3D
reconstruction to mitigate reconstructive incompleteness. Early efforts Liu et al. (2024); Gao et al.
(2024); Wu et al. (2024a); Guo et al. (2025); Xu et al. (2025) leverage 2D or video diffusion priors to
synthesize novel views or depth maps, but still require per-scene optimization and suffer from cross-
view inconsistency. Other works Jin et al. (2024); Tang et al. (2024b); Ziwen et al. (2025) regress
target-view pixels or 3D Gaussians in a single feed-forward pass, but output unstructured primitives
rather than meshes. Recently, native 3D generative models Xiang et al. (2025; 2026); Zhang et al.
(2024) have enabled direct mesh generation from image conditioning. ReconViaGen Chang et al.
(2025) and UniRecGen Huang et al. (2026) extend this paradigm to multi-view conditioning, but
remain confined to the object level. SCube Ren et al. (2024) and GenRecon Schmid et al. (2026)
further extend multi-view 3D reconstruction to the scene level, yet both rely on accurate camera
poses to project image features into a 3D feature volume, which induces cross-view conflicts and
degrades reconstruction quality under imperfect poses. In contrast, our method conditions on pose-
free RGB images, recovering coherent scene mesh block-by-block via a native 3D generative prior.
Unbounded 3D Scene Generation. Extending 3D generation from objects to unbounded scenes has
recently drawn increasing attention. Early efforts focus on bounded environments such as a single
room, either retrieving assets from a fixed database Tang et al. (2024a); Wang et al. (2021d), or
hallucinating RGB-D frames with 2D diffusion models under text or layout conditions H¨ollein et al.
(2023); Schult et al. (2024). To scale beyond bounded rooms, recent works pursue unbounded scene
generation via block-wise generation, where neighbouring blocks are either jointly denoised and
seamlessly blended Chen et al. (2025), or progressively synthesized by an extrapolation prior Wu
et al. (2024b); Meng et al. (2025); Li et al. (2026); Engstler et al. (2025). However, these methods
are either unconditional or conditioned only on text or layout, lacking both visual guidance and
3D spatial grounding. Our method instead grounds the scene in a coarse predicted point cloud and
conditions each block on a few multi-view images, scaling multi-view generative reconstruction to
arbitrarily large scenes.

WORLDRECON METHOD
Given an arbitrary number of uncalibrated RGB images {In}N
n=1 of a large-scale indoor scene, our
goal is to recover a complete, high-fidelity, scene-level 3D mesh endowed with PBR materials. Our
generative multi-view reconstruction framework couples a reconstruction prior for global layout
consistency with a generative prior for surface fidelity and completeness. To this end, we curate a
block-level training dataset (Section 3.1). We finetune the object-level generative prior of TREL-
LIS.2 Xiang et al. (2026) into a block-level mesh generator conditioned on each block’s point-cloud
slice and multi-view RGBA images, together with a neighbour-aware 3D inpainting variant (Sec-
tion 3.2). At inference, we first extract a global point cloud with VGGT-ΩWang et al. (2026) as
a coarse geometric layout cue and partition it into overlapping point cloud blocks. Starting from a
seed block, we sequentially generate the remaining blocks across three generation stages, condition-
ing each block on its overlaps with already-generated neighbours (Section 3.3). The overall pipeline
is shown in Figure 2. In this way, the point-cloud scaffold pins the scene to the observed geometry,
while the block-wise generative prior fills in complete, high-fidelity surfaces.

3.1
DATA CURATION
Scene Slicing. To enable block-level scene generation, we first construct a structured, scalable 3D
block dataset. We build on two synthetic indoor scene datasets, 3D-FRONT Fu et al. (2021) and
SAGE-10k Xia et al. (2026), which together cover diverse layouts, furniture styles, and material
distributions. For each scene, we render RGBD views from randomised eye-level cameras under
varied lighting. We then propose a scene-slicing strategy. The scene is partitioned into a regular
grid of canonically oriented cubic blocks with side length ℓ, each covering the full vertical extent
of the scene. This yields roughly 56k blocks from 6k 3D-FRONT scenes and 61k blocks from
10k SAGE-10k scenes, totalling around 117k training blocks. Each mesh block is normalised to
canonical [−0.5, 0.5]3 space.
Condition Extraction. Each block then yields a training tuple (Mb, Pb, {(Iv, Kv, Rv, tv)}16
v=1),
denoting the block-local mesh, point cloud, RGBA renders, and camera poses, respectively. Every
rendered RGBD view is back-projected into the block’s canonical space, yielding a block-local
camera pose (Kv, Rv, tv). The pixels falling inside the block cube form the RGBA render Iv, with
the in-cube mask as alpha. We retain the top-16 views per block by in-cube pixel coverage. For
Pb, we randomly sample from two complementary noisy sources during training: unprojected GT
depth from a random subset of views with augmented noise, and a VGGT-ΩWang et al. (2026)
reconstruction from {Iv}16

controllable augmentation and the target inference distribution, keeping generation robust when
only VGGT-Ωpoint clouds are available at test time.

Training masks are sampled to emulate the overlap layouts arising at inference. Since all scenes fit
within a single block along the vertical axis, we restrict masking to the XY plane. On each iteration,
we sample the number of neighbour-supplied edges E ∈{1, 2} of the block, enforcing an L-shape
constraint (one X-edge plus one Y -edge) when E = 2. The mask configurations cover the 1- and 2-
neighbour cases encountered under our voxel-priority generation ordering (Section 3.3). The known
margin along each edge is sampled between 3ℓ/16 and ℓ/4. At inference, we shrink the raw overlap
of width ℓ/4 by one quarter, leaving a known margin of 3ℓ/16 and an editable transition band of
width ℓ/16. This design preserves sufficient neighbour context for cross-block consistency while
allowing the model to adjust geometric and material transitions near block boundaries.

Block Preparation. We run VGGT-ΩWang et al. (2026) to recover coarse camera parameters and
a global point cloud from the input RGB images. After confidence filtering and outlier removal,
we align and rescale the reconstruction to the canonical scene coordinates. We partition its XY
bounding box into overlapping cubic blocks of side length ℓ, with adjacent blocks overlapping by
ℓ/4. For each block, we retain only the in-block pixels of each input RGB image and consider images
with at least 15% in-block pixel coverage as candidate views. We voxelise the corresponding visible
points and select up to V views whose union maximises the block’s voxel coverage. This produces
the training-compatible tuple ( ˆPb, {(Iv, ˆKv, ˆRv, ˆtv)}V
v=1), without requiring ground-truth geometry
or calibrated cameras.
We determine a voxel-priority generation order based on block-wise voxel occupancy. Starting from
the block with the highest occupancy, we greedily expand a connected frontier while ensuring that
each block has at most two previously generated four-connected neighbours. This ordering matches
the 1- and 2-neighbour masks used to train the block inpainters.

Metrics. We evaluate reconstructed meshes in both 3D and 2D. In 3D, we measure geometric
accuracy and coverage using Chamfer distance (CD), F-score (10 cm), and normal consistency (NC,
thresholded at 20 cm). In 2D, we report geometric errors (RMSE, AbsRel, and angular normal
error), computed over mutually valid pixels; perceptual and semantic metrics (LPIPS and CLIP) on
depth and normal renderings; and completeness over valid reference pixels.
Baselines.
We compare against five representative baselines spanning different reconstruction
paradigms. VGGT-ΩWang et al. (2026) predicts per-view depth maps and camera poses, then
fuses them into a mesh via TSDF integration Newcombe et al. (2011) and Marching Cubes. All
of the following methods consume its predictions as input. Murre Guo et al. (2025) conditions a
large monocular depth estimator on the predicted point cloud and fuses the resulting depth into a
mesh. MonoSDF Yu et al. (2022) optimizes a per-scene signed distance field guided by monocular
depth and normal priors. FineRecon Stier et al. (2023) performs feed-forward TSDF regression
conditioned on multi-view depth guidance. GenRecon Schmid et al. (2026) tiles the scene into
overlapping chunks and projects multi-view image features into per-chunk 3D feature volumes.
Implementation details. We partition scenes into 4 m cubic blocks, each conditioned on up to
4 local views and an 8192-point cloud crop. Starting from the 512-resolution TRELLIS.2 pretrain
model, we fully finetune the Sparse Structure and Geometry stages and apply LoRA Hu et al. (2021)
to Material stage. At inference, we use a 12-step Heun sampler with guidance scales of 7.5, 7.5,
and 3.0 and time-rescaling factors of 5.0, 3.0, and 3.0. All models are trained on 32 NVIDIA GPUs.
Details are provided in Appendix.

QUANTITATIVE RESULTS
Synthetic scene reconstruction.
We evaluate 2D and 3D reconstruction performance on 3D-
FRONT Fu et al. (2021) using 32 unposed RGB images. The evaluation set primarily comprises

QUALITATIVE RESULTS
We present qualitative comparisons on both synthetic and real-world scenes in Figure 3. As a
reconstruction-based approach, VGGT-Ω+TSDF recovers visible surfaces but leaves unobserved re-
gions incomplete. Deviations in its predicted depths and camera poses can further propagate through
TSDF fusion, resulting in noisy artifacts and distorted walls. Methods that use geometric predictions
from VGGT-Ω, such as Murre and FineRecon, also exhibit artifacts and broken surfaces. GenRe-
con completes some unobserved regions; however, its feature-projection conditioning mechanism
injects the distorted point cloud and inaccurate camera poses into the generative model, resulting in
fragmented artifacts and distortions of the global scene layout. The optimisation-based MonoSDF
completes missing regions and reduces geometric noise, but its over-smoothed surfaces lose fine
geometric details. In comparison, WorldRecon produces more complete and geometrically coherent
reconstructions, preserving sharper object boundaries, thin structures, and local details despite noise
in the initial geometry and camera estimates.
Beyond geometric reconstruction, WorldRecon produces scene-level meshes with high-fidelity PBR
materials. As illustrated in Figure 1, our method consistently reconstructs detailed geometry and tex-
ture from diverse input sources, including real-world captures, synthetic images, and frames from
generated videos Seedance et al. (2026), demonstrating robust generalization across input domains.

dRecon with an agentic baseline.

Nevertheless, because the blocks are generated independently, inconsistencies can remain in their
overlapping regions. As shown in Figure 4, adjacent blocks may exhibit misaligned surface heights
at their interfaces, accompanied by visible discontinuities in geometry and texture. Adding Inpaint
propagates context between neighbouring blocks, encouraging their surfaces to connect coherently
and improving the depth and normal metrics. However, small inconsistencies at block boundaries
may remain visible as seams, explaining the slight degradation in the 3D metrics reported in Ta-
ble 3. The complete WorldRecon pipeline employs stage-wise world latent clamping and blending
(Stitch) to maintain cross-block continuity and global scene coherence, thereby eliminating visible
boundary discontinuities. It consequently achieves the best performance across all reported metrics
and produces the reconstruction most consistent with the ground truth.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented WorldRecon, a generative reconstruction framework for recovering complete, large-
scale scene PBR meshes from unposed RGB images. WorldRecon leverages coarse geometry and
camera estimates predicted by a reconstruction model as effective global grounding despite their im-
perfections. Based on this global scaffold, reconstruction-based block generation produces faithful
mesh blocks with detailed geometry and PBR materials. Neighbour-aware latent inpainting and the
causal world-generation pipeline stitch these blocks together, ensuring consistent transitions across
block boundaries. WorldRecon achieves state-of-the-art performance in large-scale indoor scene
reconstruction, with strong sim-to-real generalization despite training exclusively on synthetic data.
More broadly, our work provides a practical path toward reconstructing editable 3D scene assets
from casually captured or generated videos, supporting downstream applications such as simula-
tion, embodied AI, and content creation.
Limitations and Future Work. As our training data comprise only indoor scenes, the current appli-
cability of WorldRecon is limited to indoor environments, and its generalization to outdoor scenes
9


remains to be established. In addition, our current scene representation comprises a single layer of
4 m-high blocks, restricting reconstruction to single-story environments and precluding the model-
ing of multi-story buildings. Extending the training distribution to more diverse indoor, outdoor, and
multi-story environments, together with supporting block generation along the vertical dimension,
constitutes an important direction for future work.

A
DATA CURATION DETAILS
We construct the block-level training set from two synthetic indoor scene datasets, 3D-FRONT Fu
et al. (2021) and SAGE-10k Xia et al. (2026), as described in the main paper. We detail its rendering,
scene-slicing, and condition-extraction procedures below.
A.1
SCENE RENDERING
For each scene, we render a set of full-scene RGB observations to simulate the multi-view images
used for room reconstruction. We additionally render the corresponding depth maps for point-cloud
construction during data preparation.
To increase the appearance diversity of 3D-FRONT, we augment its architectural surfaces with PBR
materials from ambientCG (formerly CC0 Textures)1. For each scene, we construct separate material
pools for floors (wood, planks, carpet, tiles, marble, and terrazzo), walls (wallpaper, marble, and
plaster), and structural elements such as doors and baseboards (wood and marble). We first sample
a scene-level material-assignment strategy
s ∼Categorical(0.3, 0.6, 0.1),
s ∈{instance, category, original}.
(S1)
Under the instance strategy, each architectural component independently draws a material from
its corresponding pool. Under the category strategy, components belonging to the same category
share a sampled material, producing a more coherent appearance. The original strategy applies no
additional material augmentation and preserves the scene as provided by 3D-FRONT. When either
instance or category strategy is selected, floors are always assigned a new material, while walls and
structural elements are augmented with probabilities 0.6 and 0.8, respectively. Furniture is excluded
from material augmentation and always retains its original 3D-FUTURE materials.
For illumination, in addition to the original light sources provided by each 3D-FRONT scene, we
assign a randomly selected HDRI environment map. SAGE-10k scenes additionally receive one
ceiling area light because their enclosed layouts receive limited illumination from the environment
map. We randomise the horizontal position, size, energy, and warm/cool colour of each area light.
To mimic natural human capture, we sample near-level camera poses inside individual rooms at
heights between 1.4m and 1.8m. Subject to the availability of valid poses, views are distributed
across rooms approximately in proportion to their floor areas. We reject poses closer than 1m to
scene geometry to avoid overly close-up observations and encourage broader scene coverage.

A.4
POINT-CLOUD PREPARATION
For each block, we construct two point-cloud conditions. To obtain the augmented depth-derived
cloud, we randomly select 6–10 retained views and apply depth and camera perturbations, visi-
bility dropout, and sparse outlier injection before unprojection. We unproject the perturbed views
into the canonical block frame, merge and crop the points to [−0.5, 0.5]3, and sparsify the cloud to
8192 points. The VGGT-Ωcloud is reconstructed from the retained block-masked RGB views de-
scribed above. We first apply confidence filtering and statistical outlier removal. We then estimate a
Horn Sim(3) transformation from the corresponding VGGT-predicted and canonical camera centres,
jointly recovering the relative scale, rotation, and translation between the two coordinate frames. The
estimated transformation is applied to the reconstructed point cloud, which is subsequently cropped
to the canonical block bounds and sparsified to 8192 points.
When both sources are available, they are registered as separate training instances, ensuring balanced
exposure to both point-cloud distributions within each epoch. For blocks with only one valid source,
the available point cloud is used.

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
C.1
BASELINES
We use VGGT-ΩWang et al. (2026) prediction as the common geometric prior for all methods. It
provides camera intrinsics and extrinsics, per-view depth and confidence, and a point cloud obtained
by unprojecting the predicted depths. Each baseline follows its official pipeline, with any required
ground-truth geometric inputs replaced by the corresponding predictions from VGGT-Ω.
• VGGT-Ω+TSDF directly integrates the predicted RGB-D frames using the VGGT-Ωcameras
through TSDF fusion Newcombe et al. (2011).
• Murre Guo et al. (2025) receives the RGB frames and sparse depth samples selected from the
VGGT-Ωdepth maps using confidence, spatial subsampling, and multi-view consistency. Its re-
fined dense depths are fused with the VGGT-Ωintrinsics and aligned poses using TSDF.
• FineRecon Stier et al. (2023) receives the RGB frames, confidence-filtered VGGT-Ωdepths, in-
trinsics, and aligned poses. The predicted depths are used for depth-guided feature back-projection
and as an additional TSDF feature channel.
• MonoSDF Yu et al. (2022) uses the VGGT-Ωintrinsics and aligned poses together with the RGB
frames. Following its original formulation, its monocular depth and normal supervision is pre-
dicted from the RGB images by Omnidata, rather than taken from the VGGT-Ωdepth maps.
• GenRecon Schmid et al. (2026) uses the aligned VGGT-Ωpoint cloud to determine the scene
extent and chunk layout, and uses the predicted cameras to project multi-view RGB features into
each chunk’s 3D feature volume.
C.2
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
As VGGT-Ωestimates camera poses from uncalibrated images, its reconstructed coordinate frame
has no absolute scale or canonical horizontal orientation. Our scene-alignment procedure recovers
gravity and dominant wall directions, but does not resolve the remaining global similarity ambiguity
with respect to the ground-truth frame. For evaluation, we therefore estimate a Sim(3) transform
from corresponding predicted and reference camera centres using Horn alignment and apply it to
the complete predicted mesh. The same transform is used for all 2D and 3D metrics. We convert
dataset-specific axis conventions before computing the metrics.
Following the evaluation protocol of GenRecon Schmid et al. (2026), for synthetic scenes, we restrict
prediction triangles to the GT axis-aligned bounding box enlarged by 20%, preventing geometry far
outside the scene extent from dominating the evaluation. For ScanNet++, we restrict the evaluation
domain to the observed scanner surface, represented by a 1.1 cm voxel grid and dilated by 15 cm.
C.3
METRICS
3D Metrics. We uniformly sample 200k points from each mesh surface and compute bidirectional
nearest-neighbour correspondences. We report:
• Chamfer Distance (CD): the average of the mean predicted-to-reference and reference-to-
predicted nearest-neighbour Euclidean distances, reported in metres;
• F-score@10cm (F-score): the harmonic mean of predicted-to-GT precision and GT-to-prediction
recall, where a point is considered matched if its nearest-neighbour distance is below 0.1 m;
• Normal Consistency (NC): the bidirectional mean absolute dot product between the normals of
nearest-neighbour surface samples. Correspondences farther than 0.2 m contribute zero.
2D Metrics. We render the aligned predicted and reference geometry using the same held-out
camera poses and intrinsics. For each scene, we evaluate 50 views sampled from the held-out test
set. The synthetic test views have a resolution of 512×512, whereas the ScanNet++ test views have
a resolution of 640×480. Frames used as input to VGGT-Ωare excluded from the evaluation views.
We evaluate pixel-wise depth and surface-normal errors, pooling RMSE, AbsRel, and Angular over
all valid view–pixel pairs across the evaluation views. Let Vd denote the set of view–pixel pairs for
which both the predicted and reference renderings have finite, positive depth. The depth metrics are

LARGE SCENE GENERATION
We further demonstrate our method on large-scale indoor scenes comprising multiple, largely iso-

its reconstruction. It constructs scenes using geometric primitives and materials specified by numer-

instead of video input and does not enforce the original cost ceiling.

our method performs inference on a single GPU without external LLM calls, avoiding this addi-

D.3
FAILURE CASES AND LIMITATIONS
Figure S3 illustrates a representative failure case on a multi-room scene. The 32 sampled input views
(top-left) are visually rich, but parts of the scene are occluded or observed from only a handful
of viewpoints. As a result, the VGGT-Ωpoint-cloud scaffold (bottom-left) incorrectly estimates
the poses of several views: some regions are either left unreconstructed or filtered out due to low
confidence, while other views are projected to entirely wrong locations.
Under this shared scaffold, the TSDF baseline can only reconstruct a small fraction of the scene,
while the generative baseline GenRecon directly reprojects image features using the erroneous
poses, scattering misaligned projections across the scene and producing large fragmented artifacts
as well as distorted objects. WorldRecon likewise suffers from missing geometry in unobserved
regions due to the incomplete input coverage. Nevertheless, it remains robust to substantial pose er-
rors and still faithfully reconstructs the areas that are well covered by the input views, and is able to
hallucinate plausible completions for the missing point-cloud regions based on sparse image cues.
