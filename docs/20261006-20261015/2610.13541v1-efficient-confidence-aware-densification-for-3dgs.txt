Abstract
3DGS-MCMC has replaced the gradient-driven density control of 3D Gaussian Splatting with opacity-based sampling and
relocation, and is now the de facto standard for prior-free densification under a fixed primitive budget. Because opacity stays
high where the scene is already well reconstructed, this sampling sends most new primitives to the foreground and leaves the
background under-reconstructed. At low budgets, prior-based methods such as ConeGS and EDGS outperform it, at the cost of a
pre-trained radiance field or dense image matches and a separate initialization stage. We argue for a comeback of error-driven
densification within the fixed-budget MCMC framework. Our confidence-aware densification keeps the fixed budget and gradual
growth of 3DGS-MCMC, but places primitives by the image error instead of the opacity. New primitives are drawn mostly where
screen-space gradients are large. We favor primitives with a low learned confidence, which marks regions the reconstruction still
explains poorly, and relocate the primitives that contribute least to the rendered images to these places. Averaged over 24 scenes,
our method outperforms 3DGS-MCMC in PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS at every budget from 100k to 2M primitives. At 100k, without
a prior or an initialization stage, it recovers 0.84 dB of the 1.05 dB PSNR by which ConeGS leads 3DGS-MCMC, and from
500k on, it has the highest PSNR and SSIM of all fixed-budget methods we compare with, including ConeGS and EDGS. While
inherently prior-free, our densification can also exploit priors: from the ConeGS or EDGS initialization, it matches ConeGS in
PSNR and SSIM at 100k and is best in all three metrics at 500k.

1. Introduction
Following Neural Radiance Fields [MST∗20], 3D Gaussian Splat-
ting (3DGS) [KKLD23] has become a standard representation for
novel view synthesis, as its explicit primitives render high-quality
images at real-time frame rates. Much of the quality of 3DGS comes
from adaptive density control (ADC) [KKLD23], which clones
and splits primitives where the screen-space gradients exceed a
pre-defined threshold. Later work refined this error signal with
absolute gradients [YSG24,YLL∗24] and a per-primitive SSIM er-
ror [RBPK24]. ADC is robust across a wide range of scenes, but it

relies on an initialization from sparse structure-from-motion (SfM)
points. In contrast to Neural Radiance Fields, 3DGS only receives
gradients where primitives exist, and because ADC clones and splits
existing primitives, it cannot place new ones far from them. ADC
also offers no direct control over the final number of primitives.
When the number of primitives is capped by a fixed bud-
get, quality depends on where densification places them. 3DGS-
MCMC [KRS∗24] showed that densification can succeed even from
random initialization. It reframes densification as the relocation of
low-opacity primitives to the positions of high-opacity ones, with
noise added to the positions of all primitives, scaled so that mainly
low-opacity primitives move, to drive exploration. Due to its robust-
ness and its explicit control over the primitive count, this framework
has become the de facto standard for prior-free densification and is
adopted by recent works [CMLND∗26,LSC∗25]. At low budgets,
however, prior-based methods outperform it: ConeGS [BEG∗26]
initializes from a pre-trained Instant-NGP [MESK22] (iNGP) radi-
ance field and uses it to place primitives at high-error pixels, and
EDGS [KGO26] initializes from dense image matches, both at the
cost of a prior and a separate initialization stage. With more primi-
tives, this advantage turns into a disadvantage: on Mip-NeRF 360,
both ConeGS and EDGS end up below 3DGS-MCMC at 2M (cf.
Figure 1). A prior thus gives a head start when few primitives have
to be placed well, but not a better final result. With few primitives,
we find that 3DGS-MCMC under-reconstructs the background. It
draws the source primitives, at which new primitives are placed, in
proportion to opacity, and it relocates primitives whose opacity has
decayed below a threshold, so neither rule is tied to the image error.
Because opacities stay high in regions that many training views
observe, both rules favor the already well-reconstructed foreground,
and the imbalance compounds while the budget fills (cf. Figure 2).
We therefore argue for a comeback of error-driven densification
within the MCMC framework. We keep the fixed budget, the gradual
growth and the relocation step of 3DGS-MCMC, and change the
two decisions it bases on opacity: where new primitives go, and
which make room for them. New primitives go where the image
error is large, which we measure mainly with screen-space gradi-
ents and partly with a structural error. The gradients are weighted
toward primitives of low confidence, a per-primitive value that
CoMe [RWK∗26] learns for surface reconstruction and that drops
where the reconstruction remains poor. Room is made by the primi-
tives that contribute least to the rendered images, rather than by those
whose opacity has decayed. The two changes only work together:
either one alone barely improves 3DGS-MCMC (Section 5.2). New
and relocated primitives are placed once by sampling around their
source and take their color from a training image, which replaces
the continuous position noise of 3DGS-MCMC. Apart from one
confidence per primitive and one loss term, we need no additional
model or training stage.
Our method outperforms 3DGS-MCMC at every budget we test,
most clearly when primitives are scarce, and it reconstructs back-
grounds in more detail (cf. Figure 1): on Mip-NeRF 360, most of its
gain over 3DGS-MCMC lies in the background (Tables 1 and S.11).
Without a prior, it closes most of the gap to ConeGS at low budgets,
and for larger budgets, it reaches the highest PSNR and SSIM of
all fixed-budget methods we compare with. Priors and our densifi-

• An analysis showing that the opacity-driven sampling and relo-

• Confidence-aware densification for the fixed budget of 3DGS-

2. Related Work
Radiance Fields. 3D Gaussian Splatting [KKLD23] has become
the standard representation used for novel view synthesis. Recent
follow-up works address artifact-free rendering [RSP∗24,SKR∗25,
YCH∗24,HFW∗25], better view-dependent appearance [DSRV∗26,
CMLND∗26,LSC∗25], more lightweight models [FW26,PKK∗24,
FWW∗24] and faster optimization [CJJ∗25, HFEM26]. Beyond
novel view synthesis, 3DGS is used for surface extraction [RWK∗26,
RWD∗25,YSG24,GGM∗25]. Alternative representations that target
fully correct volumetric rendering [MHK∗25,MHK∗26,GRYT25,
GRV∗26] have also been investigated; note that their quality also
depends on densification.
Densification. Starting from a sparse SfM point cloud, 3DGS re-
lies on densification, i.e. adding and removing primitives during
optimization. The ADC of Kerbl et al. [KKLD23] splits or clones
primitives with large image-space gradients and prunes primitives
with low opacity. Later works refine this error signal with abso-
lute screen-space gradients [YLL∗24, YSG24] and per-primitive
SSIM errors [RBPK24], which we reuse together with the confi-
dence that CoMe [RWK∗26] learns for mesh extraction. Taming
2


3DGS [MGK∗24] splits and clones by tailored scores until it reaches
a budget. Simplification methods [FW26,ZJNY25] first optimize a
large set of primitives and then prune it step by step to a compact, bet-
ter distributed one. Prior-based methods start from a denser initializa-
tion, obtained from a pre-trained radiance field [NMR∗25,BEG∗26]
or from dense correspondences [KGO26], and ConeGS [BEG∗26]
also inserts primitives at high-error pixels, at the depth of its radi-
ance field. This head start requires a separate initialization stage,
which for ConeGS takes nearly as long as our entire training. 3DGS-
MCMC [KRS∗24], the prior-free baseline we build on, grows to-
wards a fixed budget, samples, relocates and perturbs primitives by
their opacity, and works even from random initialization. We keep
its budget, growth and relocation but change which primitives are
drawn and relocated and where new ones go.

4. Confidence-Aware Densification
We keep the fixed-budget framework of 3DGS-MCMC: within the
30k training iterations, densification steps every 100 iterations from
iteration 500 to 25k, growth by 5% per step until the budget is
reached, and relocation alongside growth. What we change is where
this framework places primitives: At each step, we draw source prim-
itives from a distribution that follows the image error instead of the
opacity (Section 4.1). The same draw serves growth and relocation,
so this distribution alone decides where the model densifies. We
relocate the primitives with the lowest maximum blending weight
in the training views instead of those below an opacity threshold
(Section 4.2). Each new primitive receives a position drawn from the
Gaussian of its source, which replaces the continuous position noise
of 3DGS-MCMC, and a color taken from a training image (Sec-
tion 4.3). Compared with 3DGS-MCMC, we add one confidence
per primitive and the loss of Equation (3), need no initialization
stage, and use one set of hyperparameters for all scenes and budgets.
Growth reaches the budget early in training, and relocation moves up
to 10% of the primitives per step until iteration 10k, so our sampling
acts while the distribution of primitives forms.
4.1. Sampling-Based Densification
The learned confidence is one error signal for this distribution, since
¯γi < 1 marks primitives in under-reconstructed regions [RWK∗26],
but sampling by confidence alone has a drawback. All confidences
start at ¯γi = 1, so the confidence map is uniform at first and becomes
a useful error proxy only after several thousand iterations (Figure 4).
We therefore combine the confidence with the absolute screen-
space gradient ∇abs of GOF [YSG24] and AbsGS [YLL∗24], which
is usable from the first iteration on. For each primitive, ∇abs sums the
magnitudes of the per-pixel gradients of its screen-space position,
so gradients of opposite sign do not cancel as they do in ADC.
This matters most for large primitives that cover several structures:
their per-pixel gradients cancel in the sum, so the signed gradient
stays small although such a primitive blurs detail [YLL∗24]. We
average it over the observations of the primitive since the previous
densification step. We use the ranks of both factors, so neither their
scale nor a few very large gradients can dominate the distribution.

4.3. Position Sampling and Color Initialization
Each new primitive, added or relocated, starts as a copy of its source
and then needs its own position and appearance. We keep the adjust-
ment of opacity oi and scale si of 3DGS-MCMC (Section 3) and
copy rotation, SH coefficients and confidence from the source.
For the position, 3DGS-MCMC relies on its continuous position
noise (Section 3). In our experiments, this noise helps only prim-
itives that are seen in many views and can disrupt optimization
in background regions. Windisch et al. [WKR∗26] likewise report
that it becomes problematic in large-scale reconstruction, where
primitives are rarely observed. We disable this noise. In preliminary
experiments, noise scaled by the learned confidence instead of the
opacity also performed worse than no noise. Instead, as suggested
by GOF [YSG24], we draw the mean µ′ of each new primitive from
the Gaussian N(µi,Σ′
i) of its source primitive i, whose covariance

this position sampling acts once and only on new primitives.

5. Evaluation
Implementation Details. We implement our method in the 3DGS
codebase [KKLD23] with the Faster-GS rasterizer and fused Adam
optimizer [HFEM26] and run 3DGS-MCMC in the same codebase;
all other baselines run in their official codebases. We train all our
runs for 30k iterations. If the initial SfM point cloud exceeds the
budget, we subsample it by farthest point sampling. We regularize
scale and opacity with λreg = 0.01 for all scenes, and keep the
regularizer active until densification ends at iteration 25k. We use
the confidence loss of Equation (3) throughout training, with βconf =
0.075 and the learning rate of CoMe [RWK∗26], and we clamp the
confidence map ˆC to [10−3,5].
Datasets and Comparison Methods. We evaluate on the scenes
used in 3DGS [KKLD23], i.e., 9 scenes from Mip-NeRF
360 [BMV∗22] and 2 scenes each from Tanks & Temples [KPZK17]
and Deep Blending [HPP∗18]. Following Power Foam [GRV∗26],
we add 11 scenes from DL3DV [LST∗24], for 24 real-world scenes
in total. We downsample the Mip-NeRF 360 outdoor scenes by 4
and the indoor scenes and DL3DV by 2, use Tanks & Temples and
Deep Blending at the provided resolution, and hold out every eighth
image for testing.

5.1. Novel View Synthesis
Fixed-Budget Methods. Following ConeGS [BEG∗26], we evalu-
ate a set of primitive budgets {100k,500k,1M,2M} and average the
metrics over the 24 scenes (Figure 7). Table 2 and supplementary
Tables S.1–S.4 break the results down by dataset and scene. We treat
differences below 0.05 dB PSNR or 0.002 SSIM or LPIPS as on par.
Our error-driven densification helps most where primitives are
scarce. It is better than its prior-free baseline 3DGS-MCMC in all
three metrics at every budget, by 0.84 dB PSNR at 100k and 0.34 dB
at 2M. On the outdoor Mip-NeRF 360 scenes, most of this gain
lies in the background (Table 1): from 500k on, our PSNR there
improves by about 1 dB, against at most 0.23 dB in the foreground.
When inspecting the distribution of blended primitives for low-
budget models (cf. Figure 3), we find our primitives are not as
concentrated on the foreground as those in 3DGS-MCMC.
Prior-based methods show clear benefits for smaller budgets, but
their lead over 3DGS-MCMC shrinks as the budget grows. ConeGS
is 1.05 dB ahead of 3DGS-MCMC in PSNR at 100k, whereas at
2M, 3DGS-MCMC is on par with ConeGS and ahead of EDGS.
Our method preserves the steady improvement of 3DGS-MCMC
with higher budgets on top of its established low-budget advantage.
From 500k on, it has the highest PSNR and SSIM of all fixed-budget
methods, and its PSNR lead over the second-best method grows
from 0.18 dB at 500k to 0.34 dB at 2M. Intuitively, high-quality
densification outweighs prior-based initialization as the number of
primitives grows larger.
At 100k, ConeGS is the only method ahead of ours, in all three
metrics and by 0.21 dB PSNR, mainly on DL3DV (Table 2). Note
that the priors used by ConeGS require a dedicated initialization
stage and generate associated overhead: iNGP pre-training alone
takes almost as long as our entire optimization (Table 6). Started
from their initialization, our method matches ConeGS in PSNR and
SSIM (Section 5.2).
LPIPS is our weakest metric, as it penalizes missing high-
frequency detail more than PSNR does, and such detail profits
most from a dense prior or a larger intermediate set of primitives.
From SfM points, our method never has the best 24-scene LPIPS,
but from 500k on, it trails ConeGS or EDGS by at most 0.003. At
equal budget, the gap is confined to outdoor scenes (Table 3), and it
disappears when our method starts from a dense prior initialization
(Section 5.2) or is allowed to exceed the budget during training
(Figure 10). Figure 8 compares test views of seven scenes at 500k.
Variable-Budget Methods. Our method also holds up against meth-
ods that do not explicitly limit their own primitive count. For each
comparison, we set the budget of our method and 3DGS-MCMC to
the final per-scene primitive count of the compared variable-budget
method. We also include 3DGS with absolute gradients for densi-
fication (3DGS + GOF) [YSG24]. Against 3DGS, DashGaussian
and 3DGS + GOF, our method is better in all three metrics in every
group of scenes, including DL3DV, and only tied by DashGaussian
in outdoor PSNR (Table 3, supplementary Figure S.1). It also beats
3DGS-MCMC in all metrics in every block.
The simplification methods Mini-Splatting2 and GaussianSpa are
ahead of us only in outdoor scenes. There, both show better LPIPS,
and GaussianSpa also higher SSIM, while our method keeps the
lead in PSNR. Indoors and on Tanks & Temples and Deep Blending,
our method is ahead of both or on par, and on DL3DV, it leads both
in all three metrics. Both methods first optimize a much larger set of
primitives, up to 6M for the Bicycle scene, and then prune it to the
final count. Outdoor scenes hold the most fine, distant texture, which
such a large intermediate set can cover before pruning keeps the
most useful primitives. When we similarly let our method exceed
the budget during training, its LPIPS reaches that of both methods
(Section 5.2, Figure 10). However, at the matched budget on Mip-
NeRF 360, Mini-Splatting2 and GaussianSpa need 1.7× and 2.4×
our peak memory, and GaussianSpa trains 8× as long (Table 6).
Only Mini-Splatting2 trains faster, as it stops after 18k iterations.

and our sampling distribution (w/ our sampling) each add less than
0.1 dB, while the full method is more than 0.5 dB ahead. Relocation

Growth Beyond the Budget. The LPIPS advantage of the sim-
plification methods in Table 3 most likely comes from their large
intermediate set of primitives. To test this, we let our method grow
to λgrow times the budget by half of training and then reduce the
count to the budget by iteration 25k, removing the primitives with
the lowest wi (Figure 10). At λgrow of 1.5 or 2, our LPIPS is on par
with both methods, and our PSNR stays 0.5 to 0.6 dB ahead at every
λgrow. The larger intermediate set thus captures the fine detail that
is covered late from SfM points (Section 5.1), and the reduction
keeps the primitives that carry it. At λgrow = 3, LPIPS gets worse
again, most likely because the maximum in wi favors small primi-
tives that dominate a few pixels but contribute little to the image as
a whole. Mini-Splatting2 and GaussianSpa pass through far larger
intermediate sets, whereas 1.5 to 2 times the budget suffices for our
method. Since bounding peak memory is one of our goals, we keep
λgrow = 1 by default, but where memory allows, we recommend
λgrow of 1.5 to 2 for the best perceptual quality.
6. Limitations and Future Work
At the lowest budget, our densification cannot fully make up for
a better initialization. From SfM points, ConeGS stays ahead of
our method at 100k, and even when we start from its initialization,
ConeGS keeps the better LPIPS (Table 7). A better starting point
thus still pays off under a strict budget. It remains open whether
such a start can be obtained more cheaply than by fitting Gaussians
directly: the iNGP initialization of ConeGS alone takes nearly as
long as our complete training at 500k (Table 6).
Without a dense initialization, recovering fine detail under a
strict budget remains challenging: outdoors, methods that simplify
a denser reconstruction reach the better LPIPS at equal budget (Ta-
ble 3). Growth beyond the budget closes this gap at the cost of peak
memory, but only up to about twice the budget, since the maximum
blending weight keeps primitives that matter for single pixels (Sec-
tion 5.2). Richer appearance models [CMLND∗26,LSC∗25,TBU26]
could recover this detail at a lower peak count.
Our schedules for growth and relocation are set by hand and
shared by all budgets. Relocating a larger share of primitives slightly
improves PSNR at 1M and 2M, but not at 100k (supplementary
Table S.10), so the relocation share could adapt to the budget, which
is also user-defined. Since the confidence marks regions that the
reconstruction still explains poorly, it could indicate when further
primitives stop paying off and thus set the budget of each scene.
7. Conclusion
Error-driven densification belongs in the fixed-budget framework of
3DGS-MCMC. We keep what makes 3DGS-MCMC robust, its fixed
budget and gradual growth, and replace the two decisions it bases on
opacity: the image error, weighted by a learned confidence, decides
where new primitives go, and their contribution to the rendered
images decides which primitives make room for them. This simple
change improves on 3DGS-MCMC at every budget we test. Without
any prior, our method produces the most accurate reconstructions of
all fixed-budget methods from 500k primitives on, ahead of methods
that rely on a pre-trained radiance field or dense image matches, and
it outperforms 3DGS, DashGaussian and 3DGS + GOF at their own
primitive counts.
The same densification, with one configuration for all scenes and
budgets, adapts to its starting point and goal. It trains from random
points as robustly as 3DGS-MCMC, and from 500k on, it turns the
initialization of a prior-based method into a better reconstruction
than that method itself. Allowed to grow beyond the budget, it
matches the perceptual quality of the simplification methods from a
much smaller intermediate set and remains more accurate. It needs
no pre-trained network, radiance field or image matcher, only one
confidence per primitive and one loss term, and on the optimized
Faster-GS pipeline [HFEM26], it trains a scene with 500k primitives
in under three minutes. We therefore see it as a replacement for the
densification of 3DGS-MCMC wherever the number of primitives
is bounded.

S.1. Supplementary Material
The supplementary material contains additional details and experi-
ments, which we omitted from the main material for brevity.
S.1.1. Per-Scene Results
Fixed Budget Results. We provide per-scene image quality met-
rics for all fixed-budget methods (Ours, ConeGS [BEG∗26],
EDGS
[KGO26],
Taming
3DGS
[MGK∗24],
3DGS-
MCMC [KRS∗24]) in Tables S.1–S.4. As previously described
in the main material, we attain state-of-the-art results starting
from a primitive count of 500k primitives; at 100k primitives,
the prior-based method ConeGS attains better results, which we
attribute to initialization.
Random Initialization. We provide per-scene results with random
initialization in Table S.5, where we report results at both 100k prim-
itives, as well as the setting of 3DGS-MCMC [KRS∗24]; each scene
follows the primitive count from 3DGS [KKLD23]. Our method
attains the strongest results across all tested scenes for 100k primi-
tives, while attaining better perceptual quality than 3DGS-MCMC
at high primitive counts.
Matched Initialization. In Tables S.6–S.9, we provide per-scene
results for our evaluation with matched initialization (reported in
Table 7 in the main material). Here, we compared against both
ConeGS [BEG∗26] and EDGS [KGO26]. Our method benefits from
both of these initialization types, and achieves strong results across
all tested scenes.
S.1.2. Additional Results and Details
Comparison with Revising-3DGS. We also compared our method
to the method of Rota Bulò et al. [RBPK24] in Table S.12, which is
also a controllable fixed-budget method. We omitted these results
from the main paper for brevity. Note that the authors did not re-
lease their source code; hence, we re-implemented their approach
following the details given in the original manuscript.
Qualitative Comparison with Variable-Budget Methods. We
provide qualitative comparisons with our tested variable-budget
methods (3DGS [KKLD23], 3DGS w/ GOF densification [YSG24,
YLL∗24], DashGaussian [CJJ∗25], Mini-Splatting2 [FW26], Gaus-
sianSpa [ZJNY25]); see Figure S.1.
Relocation Share. Across all primitive budgets, we also evaluate the
impact of our chosen primitive share, i.e. the maximum percentage
of low-contribution primitives to relocate — increasing this value
leads to more exploration. We evaluated this for all 9 scenes from
the Mip-NeRF 360 dataset [BMV∗22], with relocation shares from
5% up to 15%. Modifying this value has a minor impact on per-
formance, with large primitive counts in particular benefiting from
higher values. Conversely, for lower primitive counts (i.e. 100k)
the image quality metrics decrease slightly for higher relocation
shares — ultimately, our chosen values of 10% attains state-of-the-
art results against all tested comparison methods, demonstrating its
effectiveness.
Background Reconstruction for Indoor Scenes. Complementary
to Table 1 in the main manuscript, we report foreground and back-
ground reconstruction metrics for the indoor scenes of Mip-NeRF
360 [BMV∗22] in Table S.11. Our improvements in PSNR are simi-
larly strong, whereas our SSIM/LPIPS improvements are less pro-
nounced. For the tested dataset, the indoor backgrounds are easier to
reconstruct as they lack intricate details such as fine foliage, which
induces an upper bound on the quantitative deltas for SSIM/LPIPS.
Still, we attain improved results for all tested metrics/primitive
counts.
On Reproducibility. For the fixed-budget comparisons, we strictly
follow the setting of ConeGS [BEG∗26]; if the primitive count of
the initialization exceeds the budget, we retain a correctly-sized set
for initialization via subsampling. For EDGS [KGO26], we followed
this exact procedure, and train with pruning enabled; we replaced the
pruned primitives with new primitives via the standard densification
in the codebase, which ensures a fair comparisons between methods.
