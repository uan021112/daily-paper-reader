ABSTRACT
Surface reconstruction from range scans is challenging in the presence of noise
and outliers, as fidelity to observations must be balanced against sensitivity to
measurement errors. Yet, most neural approaches discard the sensor positions and
corresponding visibility information, compensating for this loss with geometric
priors or regularization terms. We instead formulate surface reconstruction as
robust regression of the predicted first surface intersection along each acquisition
ray against its measured depth, which penalizes predicted surface intersections
within the segment between the sensor and the observed point. We represent the
surface as the zero set of a 1-Lipschitz multi-layer perceptron, allowing the use of
linearly convergent sphere tracing to compute the first hit, and use a robust Huber
loss for regression. Our formulation requires neither oriented normals, sampled
off-surface points, nor an eikonal loss. Comparisons with the state of the art on
synthetic and real data with varying levels of noise and outlier contamination
demonstrate competitive performance on clean data and much improved fidelity
and robustness under even moderate corruption. Our analysis further confirms a
trade-off between fidelity and robustness: greater fitting capacity improves data
fidelity in the clean case but increases sensitivity to the measurement errors inherent
in real acquisitions, with a sweet spot typically emerging at intermediate capacity.

INTRODUCTION
Reconstructing a continuous surface from range scans is a classical inverse problem in geometry
processing and 3D vision. Surface samples constrain the unknown geometry only locally, leaving its
topology, inside–outside orientation, and behavior between or beyond the observations underdeter-
mined. Classical methods reduce these ambiguities through implicit surface reconstruction (Taubin
et al., 1994; Hoppe et al., 1992), geometric priors or acquisition-aware occupancy reasoning (Faugeras
et al., 1990; Kazhdan et al., 2006; Kazhdan & Hoppe, 2013; Labatut et al., 2009), while neural meth-
ods either optimize an implicit field independently for each point cloud (Atzmon & Lipman, 2020;
Gropp et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021; Ben-Shabat et al., 2022) or learn reconstruction priors across
collections of shapes (Park et al., 2019; Boulch & Marlet, 2022).
However, most neural implicit methods discard the visibility information provided by the acquisition
process. Under a calibrated first-return model (e.g., LiDAR), each surface measurement is associated
with a sensor, and the open segment between them is known to contain no surface intersection.
Existing visibility-aware methods account for visibility through discrete volumetric reason-
ing (Faugeras et al., 1990; Labatut et al., 2009; Jancosek & Pajdla, 2014) or auxiliary ray-based
supervision (Sulzer et al., 2022; Dai & Nießner, 2022). In contrast, we explicitly enforce visibility by
directly constraining the first surface intersection along each acquisition ray to match the measured
depth. To find this intersection, we represent the reconstructed surface as the zero level set of a
1-Lipschitz MLP fθ, which allows us to use the sphere tracing algorithm (Hart, 1996), known to
converge to the first zero of fθ along the sensor–point ray at a linear rate. We then minimize the
discrepancy between these predicted depths and the measured ones. This approach implicitly ensures
that the line segment between the sensor and the measured point is free of surface fragments.
We evaluate the method on the Surface Reconstruction Benchmark (SRB) and ShapeNet under clean
and corrupted acquisitions, as well as on real LiDAR measurements from ARKitScenes (Baruch
et al., 2021) and KITTI (Geiger et al., 2013), and on MVS reconstructions (Aanæs et al., 2016). We
achieve competitive results on clean data and improved performance under noise and outliers.
Our contributions are:
1. To the best of our knowledge, the first method to fit an implicit surface to range scans from scratch
by directly regressing the first surface intersection along each acquisition ray against the measured
depth.
2. A principled single-loss optimization formulation requiring neither auxiliary geometric supervi-
sion nor regularization losses, while retaining competitive results on clean data and improving
robustness under noise and outliers.
3. An empirical characterization of the fidelity–robustness trade-off, with the positional encoding
level L providing a simple control over sensitivity to corruption.

RELATED WORK
Point-cloud surface reconstruction.
Early methods fit implicit functions or signed-distance fields
to point samples (Taubin et al., 1994; Hoppe et al., 1992). Poisson reconstruction estimates an
indicator function from oriented point samples, while Screened Poisson (SPSR) adds interpolation
constraints to improve geometric fidelity; both rely on oriented normals, which can be unreliable
under noisy or non-uniform sampling (Kazhdan et al., 2006; Kazhdan & Hoppe, 2013). More recently,
neural implicit methods have become widely used for surface reconstruction as they provide continu-
ous, resolution-independent representations that handle complex geometry and arbitrary topology.
These methods differ mainly in how the field is constrained. A first family learns reconstruction
priors across collections of shapes, using a latent-conditioned SDF in DeepSDF, point-conditioned
local representations in POCO, or a differentiable Poisson formulation in Shape As Points (Park
et al., 2019; Boulch & Marlet, 2022; Peng et al., 2021). In contrast, direct fitting methods optimize a
neural field for each input point cloud. IGR shows that eikonal regularization provides an implicit
bias toward plausible surfaces (Gropp et al., 2020), while sign agnostic learning (SAL) extends this
setting to unoriented inputs (Atzmon & Lipman, 2020). Subsequent methods introduce more explicit
geometric mechanisms for constraining the fitted field. Neural-Pull samples queries around the input
and pulls them toward nearby observations (Ma et al., 2021), DiGS regularizes the divergence of

Visibility-aware reconstruction.
Known sensor poses reveal the free-space segment preceding
each measured surface point. Classical methods exploit this evidence through volumetric reasoning:
Faugeras et al. (1990) use visibility to identify free space in a constrained Delaunay triangulation,
Labatut et al. (2009) infer inside–outside labels on a Delaunay tetrahedralization using graph cuts,
while Jancosek & Pajdla (2014) refine to preserve weakly supported surfaces. More recent methods
incorporate visibility into learned reconstructions, through graph neural networks on Delaunay
tetrahedralizations (Sulzer et al., 2021), or by augmenting point-based models with ray-derived
features and samples (Sulzer et al., 2022). These methods have demonstrated strong robustness to
outliers and other acquisition defects (Labatut et al., 2009; Sulzer et al., 2021). Neural Poisson (Dai
& Nießner, 2022) and neural RGB-D mapping (Ortiz et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023)
also exploit sensor rays, through losses on sampled points or volume rendering, whereas we regress
the first zero of the implicit field directly.
Lipschitz neural networks.
Lipschitz-constrained architectures were developed to control the
worst-case sensitivity of neural networks, for adversarial robustness, certification, and Wasserstein
objectives (Cissé et al., 2017; Arjovsky et al., 2017). Early approaches constrain linear layers
via spectral or orthogonality-based regularization, but pointwise activations limit expressiveness.
GroupSort (Anil et al., 2019) addresses this with norm-preserving sorting activations. Scalable
1-Lipschitz architectures include convex potential layers (CPL) (Meunier et al., 2022), Sandwich
layers (Wang & Manchester, 2023), and SDP-based Lipschitz layers (SLL) (Araujo et al., 2023).
Closest to our work, Coiffier & Béthune (2024) fit 1-Lipschitz neural distance fields to point clouds
with normals or to triangle soups.

3.4
1-LIPSCHITZ MODEL
To ensure convergence of sphere tracing, we parameterize the field fθ : R3 →R as a composition of
1-Lipschitz functions. Let D denote the network’s hidden dimension, which we take to be even. The
model is defined as
fθ(x) = v⊤Φθ(Ax) + β,
(7)
where A ∈RD×3 satisfies A⊤A = I3, Φθ : RD →RD is a 1-Lipschitz neural network, v =
ev/∥ev∥2 ∈RD is the final unit-norm weight, and β ∈R is a scalar bias. As a composition of such
functions, fθ : R3 →R is 1-Lipschitz. Our first-hit formulation is agnostic to the 1-Lipschitz
architecture used. In our experiments, Φθ interleaves Convex Potential Layers (CPL) and MaxMin
activations (Anil et al., 2019; Meunier et al., 2022). Architectural details are given in Appendix A.3.

Rescaling. The architectural constraint guarantees Lip(fθ) ≤1, but can leave substantial slack,
yielding small field magnitudes and conservative sphere tracing steps. Eikonal regularization (Gropp
et al., 2020) and the hinge–Kantorovich–Rubinstein (hKR) loss (Coiffier & Béthune, 2024) encourage
unit-norm gradients; however, both degrade reconstruction accuracy in our experiments (Table 7).

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
ARCHITECTURE AND OPTIMIZATION.
Unless stated otherwise, we fit an 8-layer, width-256 CPL network with MaxMin activations using
Adam with cosine learning-rate decay and an initial learning rate of 10−4 for L = 0 and 10−2 for
L > 0. Ours (L = k) denotes the empirically rescaled variant with PE level k ≥0, where L = 0
corresponds to no PE. We use δ = 0.05 by default and δ = 0.005 when corruption is detected from
point-spacing statistics, both relative to the scene radius (Appendix C.2). Optimization runs for
5k steps on ShapeNet and 25k elsewhere. Sphere tracing uses K = 192, ε = 5 × 10−4rinit and
τg = 0.1, where rinit is the radius of the enclosing sphere. The field is initialized as an approximate
SDF of an enclosing sphere. Further implementation details are given in Appendix A.3.

SYNTHETIC DATASETS AND EVALUATION
Surface Reconstruction Benchmark (SRB). We use the five SRB shapes and synthetic range scans
of Sulzer et al. (2025), retaining each point’s sensor origin (Figure 8). These scans were generated
with a modified implementation of Berger et al. (2013)’s optical laser-triangulation simulator.
ShapeNet. For synthetic experiments on ShapeNet (Chang et al., 2015), we use the 13-category
split of Choy et al. (2016) adopted by Sulzer et al. (2025), with five shapes per category. Following
Sulzer et al. (2025), point clouds are generated by casting rays from virtual sensors and retaining first
surface intersections (Figure 9).
Baselines. We compare against neural implicit methods SAL (Atzmon & Lipman, 2020), Neural-
Pull (Ma et al., 2021), DiGS (Ben-Shabat et al., 2022), DiffCD (Härenstam-Nielsen et al., 2024),
and NDF (Coiffier & Béthune, 2024), and classical methods SPSR (Kazhdan & Hoppe, 2013) and
visibility-aware Delaunay reconstruction (Labatut et al., 2009). We adapt NDF to visibility-derived
labels and call it NDF-vis (Appendix A.4). Neural Poisson (Dai & Nießner, 2022) is omitted because
no public implementation is available. All methods use the same input points; SPSR additionally
uses estimated oriented normals, while Labatut, NDF-vis, and Ours use sensor origins.

Neural implicit baselines deteriorate sharply with increasing outliers, whereas our variants remain
stable. In particular, Ours (L = 2) retains the same performance at α = 0.2 as on clean data and
achieves lower CD1 than Labatut at every tested outlier level (Figure 24). Ours (L = 6) also remains

by fitting capacity. This behavior follows from the combination of first-hit visibility and the Huber
loss. Across our PE and no-PE variants, roughly 60% of outlier rays fall in Huber’s linear regime
and about 40% do not yield a valid traced hit, while over 91% of inlier rays remain in the quadratic
regime and only 0.8–2.4% are dropped (Table 10). First-hit supervision makes many inconsistent
outlier measurements difficult to fit without introducing premature intersections along other rays, so
these outliers cease to yield valid hits. For those that remain, the Huber loss limits their influence.

DISCUSSION
Fidelity–robustness trade-off. DiGS and DiffCD combine several weighted losses that must be
balanced during optimization and satisfying each objective is not guaranteed. For example, despite
eikonal regularization, the eikonal condition is not enforced in practice (Figure 17). In our case, L
provides a single parameter to study sensitivity to corruption: larger values fit clean observations
more closely and smaller ones are more robust to corruption. Since the noise characteristics of a
sensor are typically known, or can be calibrated once on a reference object, L can in principle be set
accordingly. Across our experiments, L = 2 provides a good intermediate value.
Role of visibility. To test sensitivity to inaccurate sensor provenance, we perturb the origins ci while
keeping the points pi fixed. We observe that moderate pose perturbations have little effect, whereas
random sensor reassignment makes most ray–point pairs inconsistent and causes the loss of valid first
hits (Appendix C.7), showing that acquisition rays carry geometric information.
Ablations. Table 5 summarizes the main ablations. Robust depth penalties matter mainly under
outliers: ℓ1 remains stable whereas ℓ2 degrades strongly, and adding a miss penalty is harmful as it
forces the surface to pass through outliers. Incidence weighting primarily improves outlier robustness,
while empirical rescaling improves performance across all three regimes.

Computational cost. Each sphere-tracing iteration requires a network evaluation, making the forward
pass costly, while implicit differentiation keeps the backward pass efficient. Full-budget training
is more expensive in wall-clock time (∼1h40min, versus up to 40min for the baselines; Table 14).
However, Ours (L = 6) reaches within 2.8% of its final CD1 after only 40% of training (∼40min).
Peak memory remains 3× below DiGS and DiffCD.

CONCLUSION
We formulate visibility-aware surface reconstruction as a robust first-hit depth regression problem.
Acquisition rays constrain both surface position and preceding free space without auxiliary geometric
supervision or regularization losses. Experiments show competitive results on clean data and
9


Preprint
consistent gains under noise and outliers. This minimal formulation allows us to investigate how
increasing L improves fitting the input but also increases sensitivity to corruption. Sphere tracing
also increases the optimization cost, although most of the accuracy is reached early in the step budget
(Table 14). Natural extensions include jointly refining sensor poses, with respect to which the loss
is differentiable, and exploring other 1-Lipschitz architectures. More fundamentally, finite first-hit
measurements do not uniquely determine the underlying surface. Relating recoverable geometric
detail to sampling density, surface regularity, and model capacity is an important direction for future
work.
