Abstract
Recently, the integration of generative priors has revolutionized
Monocular Depth Estimation (MDE), achieving state-of-the-art
(SOTA) performance with remarkably high data efficiency. How-
ever, although methods based on Text-to-Image (T2I) models excel
in general scenes, their efficacy degrades sharply when confronted
with open-world environmental perturbations, such as illumination
variations and adverse weather. Since T2I priors are optimized for
semantic synthesis rather than preserving pixel-level structure, they
struggle to decouple geometry from appearance under severe visual
shifts, easily triggering structural hallucinations. Therefore, our
core insight is to model open-world depth estimation as consistent
geometric inference across multi-style images, fully leveraging the
powerful structure-preserving editing priors of Image-to-Image (I2I)
models. Based on this, we propose OpenDepth, a robust open-world
MDE framework that introduces “Intra-Domain Awareness”—the
capability to map diverse stylistic inputs sharing identical geometry
into a unified latent space. To achieve this, we first align shallow fea-
tures within the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) to anchor degraded
observations to unified geometric representations. Additionally,
we introduce a joint RGB latent reconstruction task, requiring the
model to recover clean RGB information from degraded inputs.
This multi-stage strategy effectively decouples environmental style
interference, compelling the model to focus strictly on underlying
geometric recovery. Extensive evaluations show that OpenDepth
sets new SOTA records for robust zero-shot depth estimation across
standard and extreme weather benchmarks, demonstrating excep-
tional structural integrity and adaptability.

To overcome this fundamental limitation, our core insight is to re-

process across diverse visual styles. In this context, Image-to-Image
editing models naturally align with the task, as they excel at accom-
modating various stylistic transformations while strictly preserving
the underlying structural integrity. Nevertheless, the mere injection
of editing priors is insufficient to fully resolve the challenge. Un-
der extreme environmental degradations, severe input distribution
shifts still trigger alignment deviations within the feature space,
hindering simple, direct depth regression from extracting pure geo-
metric cues. Consequently, we argue that a robust depth estimator
must possess “Intra-Domain Awareness”—the capability to actively
map and anchor observations from diverse degradation conditions
into a unified, clean latent domain prior to executing core geometric
reasoning. This mechanism is indispensable for truly unleashing
the geometric perception potential of editing priors in complex
open-world environments.
Driven by this philosophy, we introduce OpenDepth, a robust
open-world MDE framework. Moving beyond existing approaches
that focus exclusively on regressing depth from degraded features,
our method prioritizes global alignment within the feature space.
Specifically, we implement a multi-stage alignment and reconstruc-
tion mechanism: First, we align image features from the early Single
Stream blocks to establish robust correspondence between per-
turbed observations and their unified geometric structures. Subse-
quently, by leveraging the inherent bipartite structure of the DiT
output sequence, we introduce unified RGB latent reconstruction
as a joint task alongside depth estimation. This joint optimization
compels the model to reconstruct unperturbed RGB information
from diverse conditional inputs, thereby ensuring the latent features
conform to the unified latent domain established by the editing
prior. This strategy effectively enables the model to decouple envi-
ronmental style interference and focus exclusively on recovering
high-fidelity geometry.
The contributions of this work are threefold: • Systematic Anal-
ysis of Generative Priors: We provide a comprehensive analysis
of the limitations of T2I-based generative priors in MDE, revealing
that their stochastic nature and lack of structural constraints lead
to performance degradation in complex environments. We demon-
strate the superiority of shifting toward I2I editing models as the
foundation for robust geometric tasks. • Unified Latent Align-
ment Framework: We propose an innovative alignment mecha-
nism that transcends direct regression by operating in a constrained
latent space. By integrating unified RGB reconstruction and initial
DiT block alignment, we ensure that diverse visual observations are
projected into a unified latent domain, establishing a robust “many-
to-one” mapping from perturbed inputs to ground-truth depth. •
SOTA Performance & High Data Efficiency: OpenDepth sets
new SOTA records for robust zero-shot depth estimation across
standard and degraded benchmarks. Notably, it achieves excep-

79K training samples, demonstrating remarkable data efficiency.

Related Work
2.1
Foundation MDE Model
MDE has rapidly evolved from early supervised and self-supervised
methods [13, 19, 35, 85] into powerful foundation models with
formidable zero-shot generalization. Discriminative approaches es-
tablished robust multi-source training paradigms [12, 39, 40, 73, 75],
while subsequent works improved metric accuracy and structural
fidelity via transformer decoding, internal discretization, and scale
decomposition [1–3, 23, 29, 33, 37, 38, 60, 74, 76, 86]. The Depth
Anything series [69, 70] pushed performance boundaries via large-
scale semi-supervised learning, while its latest iteration [30] further
extended this paradigm to unified multi-view and monocular geom-
etry recovery with a plain transformer architecture. Lightweight
discriminative frameworks also proved that efficiency and accuracy
can coexist [41]. Concurrently, generative models repurposed T2I
diffusion priors for dense prediction [14, 27, 82], with subsequent
works optimizing inference via Flow Matching and deterministic
single-step paradigms [20, 21, 48, 63], improving conditioning and
detail refinement [15, 32, 36, 71, 79], unifying generation and dense
perception [83], extending to video sequences [24], and adapting
diffusion priors for self-supervised learning [56]. However, as ar-
gued by FE2E [58], T2I models exhibit inherent stochasticity and
lack pixel-level structural constraints, motivating a shift toward
image editing models [4, 31, 62] whose intrinsic “editing priors”
naturally maintain structural integrity. This has given rise to a new
class of I2I-based dense geometry estimators, represented by FE2E
[58] and Edit2Perceive [46], which demonstrate that repurposing
image editing models yields superior structural fidelity over their
T2I counterparts for depth and normal estimation.
Compared to existing I2I schemes (e.g., FE2E [58], Edit2Perceive
[46]), OpenDepth addresses a critical gap: while existing meth-
ods leverage editing models to regress depth directly, severe input
shifts in extreme environments still cause feature space misalign-
ments. Rather than simply migrating editing features, OpenDepth
introduces an “Intra-Domain Awareness” alignment strategy. By
incorporating a unified RGB reconstruction task and shallow la-
tent space alignment at the earliest feature extraction stages, we
compel the model to project diverse degraded observations into a
unified latent domain defined by the editing prior, enabling superior
consistency and noise resistance.
2.2

as systematic benchmarks [22, 26, 28] reveal severe degradation
in SOTA models under adverse weather and illumination. Early
domain adaptation bridged the synthetic-to-real gap via adversar-

self-supervised methods tackled photometric consistency viola-
tions through joint nighttime enhancement [84], frequency-aware
regularization [9], masked image modeling [8], curriculum con-
trastive learning [44, 57], depth consistency regularization [49],
dynamic video training [51], IMU-guided learning [78], dual-branch

Methodology
In this section, we introduce OpenDepth, a robust Monocular
Depth Estimation framework built upon the STEP1X-Edit foun-
dation model (overview shown in Figure 2). We first review the
Multi-Modal Diffusion Transformer (MMDiT) preliminaries and
analyze the geometric advantages of I2I editing priors. Finally, we
detail our core contributions—Latent Feature Alignment and Uni-
fied RGB Reconstruction—which construct a unified latent space
for robust geometric perception.

3.2
Advantages of I2I Editing Priors: A
Motivational Analysis
Existing generative MDE methods primarily rely on T2I models
(e.g., Stable Diffusion [43]). However, T2I models are optimized
for semantic synthesis driven by text prompts and lack explicit
constraints to maintain pixel-level structural integrity. When input
images are corrupted by environmental perturbations (e.g., rain,
haze, low light, or noise), T2I priors tend to produce structural
hallucinations or blurry boundaries, as the degraded visual features
deviate from their expected distribution. Conversely, I2I editing
models possess inherent transformation priors: they are trained to
alter specific attributes (style or domain) while strictly preserving
the underlying geometry of the source image.
Motivational Experiment and Analysis. To validate this
hypothesis, we evaluate a representative T2I-based depth model
(Lotus-D [21]) against our I2I baseline (without robustness fine-
tuning) by calculating the cosine similarity of intermediate spatial
features between clean and perturbed images across different lay-
ers. As shown in Figure 3, the T2I model struggles to recognize
the geometric essence, exhibiting extremely low similarity in ini-
tial layers. Conversely, the I2I model penetrates the perturbations
early on, capturing geometric features highly consistent with the
clean input and maintaining this high similarity throughout deeper
layers. This phenomenon demonstrates that I2I models possess an
inherent “intra-domain awareness” that resists visual degradation,
serving as the logical cornerstone for adopting an I2I editing model
as our foundation for robust MDE.

Overall Objective
During training, each batch contains a clean image 𝑥𝑐and its ran-
domly perturbed counterpart 𝑥𝑝, sharing the same ground-truth
depth 𝑑𝑔𝑡. The depth regression loss L𝑑𝑒𝑝𝑡ℎcomputes the masked

depth latents. The overall robust training objective is a weighted

4
Experiments
4.1
Experiment Setup
Implementation details. We build our method upon the Step1X-
Edit [31] v1.0 framework. During finetuning, all parameters except
for the DiT module are frozen, and the language control input is
left blank. The process employs LoRA with rank = 64 and scale
factor 𝛼= 32. We trained for 20 epochs using the AdamW optimizer
with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a batch size of 8. With
gradient checkpointing enabled, the entire training was conducted
on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs.
Training datasets. We train our model on a mixture of three
datasets: Hypersim [42], Virtual KITTI [6], and Cityscapes [10].
Following the data split and preprocessing protocol of Lotus [21],
we use approximately 39k images from Hypersim (a photorealis-
tic indoor synthetic dataset), 20k samples from Virtual KITTI (a
synthetic street-view dataset with a maximum depth of 80m), and
additionally incorporate 20k samples from Cityscapes, a real-world
urban driving dataset. Emulating Marigold’s [27] dataset probabil-
ity sampling mechanism, we adopt a per-dataset sampling ratio of
8:1:1 for Hypersim, Virtual KITTI, and Cityscapes, respectively.
Perturbation strategies. To synthesize the perturbed counter-
parts 𝑥𝑝, we utilize visual degradations (e.g., low-light, fog, snow,
blur) following DepthAnything-AC [50]. Unlike applying single
random perturbations, we adopt a rigorous compounding strategy
during training: low-light degradation with noise is consistently
applied as a base perturbation to all 𝑥𝑝samples, while other en-
vironmental degradations are probabilistically injected on top in
a mutually exclusive manner. This forces the model to learn re-
silient representations under multi-factor interference. Detailed
perturbation configurations are provided in the Appendix.
Evaluation benchmarks. We evaluate the zero-shot perfor-
mance of our method on both adverse-condition and standard
benchmarks following DepthAnything-AC [50]. Specifically, we
use NuScenes-night [7], RobotCar-night [34], and Driving-Stereo
[66] for real-world adverse conditions, and KITTI-C [28] for syn-
thetic corruptions. For standard generalization, we report results
on KITTI [17], NYUv2 [47], Sintel [5], ETH3D [45], and DIODE
[55]. For all relative-depth benchmarks, predictions are resized to
the ground-truth resolution, aligned per image in disparity space
on the cropped valid region, clipped to the dataset-specific depth
range, and evaluated with AbsRel and 𝛿1, averaged over images.

Ablation Study
To validate the effectiveness of our proposed components, we
conduct an ablation study on four open-world adverse condition
datasets (see Table 4). Our baseline employs a DiT-based image edit-
ing model whose inherent transformation priors naturally preserve
geometric features in complex environments.

Mengke Zhang, Tianyi Yang, Xinzhuo Li, and Anlong Ming
Figure 6: Qualitative comparison of multi-scale edge responses under severe low-light (Dark) perturbations.
We first compare generative (G) and discriminative (D) formu-
lations of our OpenDepth baseline. As shown in Table 4, Open-
Depth(D) consistently outperforms OpenDepth(G) across all bench-
marks (e.g., AbsRel 18.0 vs. 19.3 on NuScenes-night), corroborating
the finding in Lotus [21] that deterministic prediction is more suit-
able than stochastic generation for dense geometric tasks. Conse-
quently, all OpenDepth results reported in the main comparison
tables refer to this discriminative formulation.
Effects of Perturbation Training (L𝑑𝑒𝑝𝑡ℎ(𝑥𝑝)). Introducing
perturbation training yields a consistent improvement, reducing
the AbsRel error from 18.0 to 16.6 on NuScenes-night. Notably, even
applying the same perturbation strategy to the T2I baseline (Lotus-
D) only marginally improves its performance, still substantially
lagging behind our OpenDepth(D)+𝑃(16.6). This confirms that
perturbation training alone cannot compensate for the inherent
limitations of T2I priors, and the superiority of our I2I foundation.
Effects of Feature Alignment (L𝑓𝑒𝑎𝑡). Adding L𝑓𝑒𝑎𝑡further
reduces AbsRel from 16.6 to 14.8 on NuScenes-night and from
19.6 to 18.5 on RobotCar-night, demonstrating that our alignment
mechanism effectively anchors perturbed inputs to the same fine-
grained geometric representations as clean images.
Effects of Unified RGB Reconstruction (L𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑜𝑛). Integrating
L𝑟𝑒𝑐𝑜𝑛yields the most pronounced gains, with AbsRel decreasing
from 9.4 to 8.5 on DS-fog and from 18.5 to 17.0 on RobotCar-night.
This confirms its critical role in projecting diverse degraded obser-
vations into a unified latent domain, realizing robust “many-to-one”
geometric translation in open-world scenarios.
4.5
Qualitative Analysis
As shown in Figure 5, , we visualize multi-scale edge responses as
geometric proxies under severe low-light (Dark) perturbations (Fig-
ure 6). Specifically, these responses are extracted by fusing Sobel
gradients, Canny edges, and Laplacian responses from predicted
depth maps. The Baseline suffers from severe structural degra-
dation with blurred contours and noisy responses, indicating that
distribution shifts can disrupt geometric consistency even with
I2I priors. By introducing Latent Feature Alignment, the model
suppresses noise and restores structural patterns by anchoring de-
graded observations to canonical geometric representations. Finally,
the Full Model (with RGB Reconstruction) produces sharp and
coherent feature maps, accurately recovering fine structures such
as chair-leg intersections. This demonstrates that unified RGB re-
construction effectively enforces a style-invariant latent domain
for high-fidelity geometric perception.
5
Conclusion
In this paper, we propose OpenDepth to elevate robust Monocular
Depth Estimation in open-world environments. Through system-
atic analysis, we demonstrate the inherent superiority of Image-
to-Image editing priors over Text-to-Image models in preserving
structural integrity against environmental degradations. To resolve
the representation misalignment caused by diverse perturbations,
we equip our model with “Intra-Domain Awareness” via two core
mechanisms: shallow feature alignment and joint RGB latent re-
construction. This strategy successfully projects varied degraded
inputs into a unified latent space, strictly decoupling stylistic inter-
ference from geometric perception. Extensive evaluations confirm
that OpenDepth achieves exceptional detail preservation and robust
zero-shot generalization, setting new SOTA records across both
standard and extreme weather benchmarks.

We first detail the experimental protocols, datasets, and robust

A
Experimental Settings
A.1
Evaluation Datasets
To comprehensively assess both robustness in challenging envi-
ronments and generalization to standard scenes, we evaluate the
proposed model on three groups of benchmarks: real-world com-
plex benchmarks, a synthetic corruption benchmark, and general-
purpose depth estimation benchmarks.
Real-world complex benchmarks. To measure performance un-
der real-world adverse conditions, we adopt NuScenes-night [7],
RobotCar-night [34], and DrivingStereo [66]. NuScenes-night is a
nighttime subset of the NuScenes benchmark [7] and provides real-
istic autonomous-driving scenes captured under low-illumination
conditions. Following the standard split, we evaluate on 500 night-
time images. RobotCar-night is derived from the Oxford Robot-
Car benchmark [34] and contains urban driving scenes captured
at night; we use 186 images for evaluation. DrivingStereo [66] is
a large-scale real-world driving dataset collected under diverse
weather conditions. We use its foggy, rainy, and cloudy subsets,
each containing 500 images, to assess robustness under degraded
visibility and adverse weather.
Synthetic complex benchmark. We further evaluate robustness
on KITTI-C [28], a corruption benchmark constructed from KITTI
[17] by introducing controlled synthetic degradations. KITTI-C sim-
ulates a range of challenging conditions commonly encountered by
visual perception systems. In our experiments, we consider four rep-
resentative corruption types: dark, snow, motion blur, and Gaussian
noise. These settings cover low illumination, severe weather, dy-
namic blur, and sensor noise, respectively, and provide a systematic
testbed for robustness evaluation.
General benchmarks. To verify that the robustness enhancement
does not compromise performance on standard scenes, we addi-
tionally evaluate on KITTI [17], NYUv2 [47], Sintel [5], ETH3D
[45], and DIODE [55]. KITTI [17] is a canonical benchmark for
outdoor driving scenes, and we follow the standard split with 652
test images. NYUv2 [47] is a standard indoor RGB-D benchmark
containing diverse household and office environments, and we eval-
uate on 654 test images. Sintel [5] is a synthetic benchmark with
complex motion, atmospheric effects, and significant appearance
variations; we use the Final split and evaluate on 1,064 images.
ETH3D [45] provides high-resolution indoor and outdoor scenes
with accurate depth annotations, and we evaluate on 454 images.
DIODE [55] is a high-quality RGB-D benchmark covering a wide
range of scenes and depth scales; following common practice, we
report results on its indoor validation split, which contains 325
images.
Evaluation Protocol for Fair Comparison. To ensure a strictly
fair head-to-head comparison and avoid discrepancies caused by dif-
ferent testing pipelines, we did not simply copy numbers from the
original papers. Instead, all baseline results reported in our tables
were entirely re-evaluated using their official pre-trained weights.
Specifically, for the robust monocular depth estimation baselines,
we strictly followed the evaluation settings and codebase provided
by DepthAnything-AC [50] to reproduce their results. For all other
general depth estimation baselines, we re-evaluated them under the
exact same dataset-specific evaluation crops, valid depth ranges,
and alignment strategies. Furthermore, no extra post-processing
steps (e.g., test-time augmentation or multi-scale ensembling) were
applied to any of the evaluated methods. This rigorous standard-
ization guarantees that our comparative analysis reflects the true
algorithmic capabilities of each method.

B.2
Effect of Training Sampling Probabilities
We further analyze the effect of training-set sampling probabilities
in mixed-dataset training. Following the current implementation,
dataset mixing is realized through batch-level probabilistic sam-
pling: at each optimization step, one dataset is selected according
to a predefined probability distribution and contributes the current
batch. Moreover, since the epoch length is determined by the largest
dataloader, changing the sampling ratio directly redistributes the
effective optimization budget across datasets rather than merely
reweighting samples within a fixed pool. In this study, we denote
Hypersim [42], Virtual KITTI [6], and Cityscapes [10] by H, V, and
C, respectively, and vary their sampling ratios while keeping all
other settings unchanged.
As shown in Table B2, the balanced ratio 1:1:1 yields the weakest
overall performance. We attribute this to the reduced sampling
frequency of Hypersim [42], which is the most geometry-diverse
source in our training mixture and thus provides the strongest
structural prior for monocular depth estimation. Increasing the
Hypersim proportion from 1:1:1 to 4:1:1 consistently improves all
four benchmarks, indicating that a stronger geometry-centered
supervision budget is crucial for robust open-world generalization.
When the ratio is further increased to 8:1:1, the model achieves
the best trade-off across both adverse weather and nighttime bench-
marks. This suggests that the dominant contribution of Hypersim
effectively stabilizes generic geometric reasoning, while the smaller
fractions of Virtual KITTI [6] and Cityscapes [10] still provide in-
dispensable outdoor-domain anchors. In particular, Virtual KITTI
contributes synthetic driving layouts and weather-relevant scene
structure, whereas Cityscapes injects real-world urban appearance
statistics that are beneficial for nighttime transfer.
However, pushing the mixture further toward Hypersim (12:1:1)
does not yield additional gains and instead causes slight degrada-
tion. This implies that, although geometry-rich indoor supervision
should dominate the training budget, excessively suppressing out-
door data weakens the model’s adaptation to driving-specific scene
distributions. Similarly, increasing the share of Virtual KITTI (8:2:1)
or Cityscapes (8:1:2) also fails to surpass 8:1:1. The former intro-
duces a stronger synthetic-domain bias, while the latter overempha-
sizes a comparatively narrow real driving domain, both of which
slightly compromise the global balance between geometric diversity
and outdoor robustness.
Overall, these results indicate that robust open-world monocular
depth estimation benefits from a deliberately asymmetric sampling
strategy: a geometry-rich source should dominate the optimization
process to establish strong structural priors, while smaller yet non-
negligible outdoor synthetic and real-world sources are required to
regularize domain-specific appearance statistics. This observation
justifies our adoption of 8:1:1 as the default sampling configuration
throughout the paper.

B.3
Full Training Pipeline
Overview & Data Preparation. Our training framework is built
upon Step1X-Edit [31]. During data preparation, the target depth
map and the reference RGB image are encoded into VAE latents. In
mixed-dataset training, datasets are sampled at every optimization
step according to a predefined probability distribution. Since our
method does not rely on semantic language control, the text prompt
is kept empty, and the text conditions are extracted deterministically
via the frozen Qwen2.5-VL [62] encoder.
Generative vs. Discriminative Training. For the generative base-
line, the model follows the standard flow-matching formulation,
where Gaussian noise is added to the target depth latent and a
timestep is drawn from a scheduler. In contrast, our primary dis-
criminative variant replaces stochastic denoising with direct latent
regression. The target depth latent is strictly zero-initialized, the
timestep is fixed at 𝑡= 1.0, and the model directly regresses the
ground-truth depth latent using a masked Mean Squared Error
(MSE) loss over valid pixels.
Robust Training & Objectives. Our robust training pipeline ex-
tends the discriminative formulation by jointly optimizing a clean
branch and a perturbed branch. For each batch, both the clean image
and its perturbed counterpart are processed. The perturbed branch
is supervised using the exact same ground-truth depth latent as the
clean branch to enforce output-level invariance. Furthermore, the
overall robust objective integrates shallow feature alignment (co-
sine consistency between perturbed features and stopped-gradient
clean features) and unified RGB reconstruction (mapping the per-
turbed RGB output toward the clean RGB latent).
Optimization Details. To ensure training efficiency, we freeze
the base Step1X-Edit [31] DiT backbone, the VAE, and the text en-
coder, introducing trainable parameters exclusively through LoRA
adapters. The training process leverages standard memory-efficient
techniques, including mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, and
fp8 base weights.

C.3
Sensitivity to the Number of Feature
Alignment Layers
Effect of alignment depth. Table C3 shows a clear trend with
respect to the number of aligned shallow Single Stream blocks.
Increasing the aligned prefix from the first layer to the first four
layers consistently improves all benchmarks. Compared with align-
ing only the first layer, aligning the first four layers reduces AbsRel
from 10.0 to 9.7 on DS-rain, from 8.8 to 8.5 on DS-fog, from 14.9 to
14.5 on NuScenes-night, and from 17.7 to 17.0 on RobotCar-night,
while improving 𝛿1 by 0.5, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0 points, respectively. This
result indicates that aligning only one or two layers is insufficient
to fully suppress perturbation-induced feature drift once the fused
representation propagates deeper into the network. Although the
first two layers already provide noticeable gains, the first four layers
achieve a more favorable trade-off by stabilizing a larger portion of
the shallow fused representation.
However, extending the aligned region further to the first eight
Single Stream blocks does not bring additional benefits and instead
causes slight but consistent degradation on all datasets. For exam-
ple, compared with First 4, First 8 increases AbsRel from 14.5 to 14.8
on NuScenes-night and from 17.0 to 17.3 on RobotCar-night, while
also reducing 𝛿1. This suggests that deeper alignment begins to
over-constrain mid-level fused features, limiting the model’s flexi-
bility to adapt its internal representation for downstream geometric
reasoning.
Effect of alignment position. When the number of aligned layers
is fixed to four, the position of alignment becomes equally important.
Aligning the first four Single Stream blocks clearly outperforms
aligning the middle four or the last four. Relative to Middle 4, First
4 achieves lower AbsRel by 0.3 on DS-rain, 0.2 on DS-fog, 0.5 on
NuScenes-night, and 0.6 on RobotCar-night, together with consis-
tent gains in 𝛿1. The gap becomes even larger when comparing First
4 against Last 4: AbsRel is reduced by 0.5, 0.4, 0.8, and 0.9 on the
four benchmarks, respectively. Notably, the largest margins appear
on the nighttime benchmarks, where late-layer alignment yields
the weakest results.
This behavior is consistent with the functional role of the Single
Stream stage. Early Single Stream blocks have already incorporated
preliminary cross-modal fusion, yet they remain relatively close
to the input structure. They therefore provide an ideal location
for anchoring perturbation-invariant geometric representations. In
contrast, middle and late Single Stream blocks are increasingly spe-
cialized for prediction-oriented reasoning. Applying strong align-
ment at these stages is less effective for preventing early drift and
may interfere with the model’s ability to preserve task-specific
depth cues under severe domain shift.
Discussion. Overall, the ablation reveals a consistent depth-
position trade-off for feature alignment: the alignment should be
applied sufficiently early to suppress perturbation-induced drift
before it propagates through the fused representation, but not
so deep that it over-regularizes semantically richer prediction-
oriented features. These results substantiate our architectural
choice of aligning the first four Single Stream blocks, which pro-
vides the most effective balance between perturbation invariance
and representational flexibility.

C.4
Analysis of Generative and Discriminative
Models
In this section, we provide a quantitative comparison between the
generative and discriminative variants of our depth estimation
model. Table C4 summarizes the performance of both paradigms
on the NYUv2 (indoor) and KITTI (outdoor) datasets.
Overall, the discriminative model consistently outperforms its
generative counterpart across all metrics on both datasets. On the
indoor NYUv2 dataset, the discriminative model achieves an Ab-
sRel error of 3.88 and a 𝛿1 accuracy of 98.04, demonstrating highly
precise metric depth recovery. While the generative model yields
slightly higher errors (e.g., an AbsRel of 3.90 and an RMSE of 0.1974),
the performance gap remains marginal, indicating that the gener-
ative paradigm can still synthesize highly accurate indoor depth
structures.
Conversely, the performance discrepancy becomes more pro-
nounced on the outdoor KITTI dataset, which features complex, un-
bounded scenes and large depth ranges. The discriminative model
attains an AbsRel of 6.30 and an RMSE of 3.1318, whereas the gen-
erative model degrades to an AbsRel of 6.54 and an RMSE of 3.3800.
This widening gap suggests that the iterative denoising process
inherent to generative models may struggle to maintain strict geo-
metric consistency in unbounded outdoor environments, where
scale ambiguity and distant structures pose significant challenges.
In contrast, the discriminative model, which directly regresses depth
values in a single forward pass, exhibits superior robustness and
geometric fidelity across diverse scenarios.
C.5
Generalization to Unseen Perturbations
A critical concern regarding robust training is whether the model
merely overfits to the specific degradation patterns seen during
training, or if it genuinely learns to extract invariant geometric
structures. To investigate this, we evaluate the generalization capa-
bility of our model on unseen perturbations. Specifically, as detailed
in our perturbation strategy (Section B.1), our training pipeline does
not include rain, cloudy-weather, or Gaussian noise degradations.
We evaluate zero-shot performance on DS-rain [66], DS-cloud
[66], and KITTI-C [28] Gaussian benchmarks and compare Open-
Depth with the state-of-the-art robust baseline, DepthAnything-AC
[50]. Note that both models are exposed to similar perturbation
synthesis strategies during training, making this a fair head-to-head
comparison of out-of-distribution generalization.
As shown in Table C5, OpenDepth significantly outperforms
DepthAnything-AC [50] across all three unseen degradation types.
On the DS-rain benchmark, OpenDepth reduces the AbsRel error
from 12.5 to 9.7 and improves 𝛿1 accuracy from 84.0 to 89.3. Sub-
stantial improvements are also observed under cloudy conditions
(AbsRel: 14.9 →12.5) and severe sensor noise (KITTI-C Gaussian
AbsRel: 15.3 →14.1).

memorize the training perturbations. Instead, by leveraging the

D
Failure Case Analysis
Although OpenDepth achieves better overall AbsRel and 𝛿1 on
RobotCar-night [34], Fig. D1 shows that the remaining errors are
not uniformly distributed across depth ranges. As indicated by the
white arrows, OpenDepth tends to preserve sharper local geometry
in the near field, including building contours, roadside structures,
and the geometric grouping of nearby scene components. This
tendency is consistent with our robust training pipeline, where
clean and perturbed inputs share the same GT depth target, early
Single Stream features are aligned to their clean counterparts, and
an auxiliary RGB latent reconstruction term encourages recovery
of a clean-domain representation from degraded inputs. Together,
these objectives appear particularly helpful for stabilizing local
structural cues that are easily weakened in low-light driving scenes.
The same example also suggests a characteristic tendency in very
distant regions. In the current implementation, the supervision tar-
get is not metric depth itself; instead, depth is first converted into
an inverse-depth/disparity-style representation, normalized with
trunc_disparity, and then encoded into the VAE latent space for
regression. Moreover, evaluation on RobotCar-night [34] applies
scale-and-shift fitting in disparity space under an 80 m cap. Under
this formulation, large metric-depth differences in the far field cor-
respond to relatively small changes in disparity, and the percentile-
based truncation further keeps very distant content within a com-
paratively compact representation range. As a result, the model
allocates stronger effective resolution to near- and mid-range ge-
ometry, whereas the separation of distant buildings, road extents,
and skyline structures can be less pronounced in some nighttime
scenes.
A related phenomenon appears in a small number of extremely
dark samples, where sky regions are not always mapped to the
farthest depth. This behavior is also compatible with the cur-
rent design: the model regresses a normalized disparity latent
rather than an explicit “infinite-depth” label, and the auxiliary
feature-alignment and RGB-reconstruction losses mainly promote
perturbation-invariant representation learning instead of imposing
a dedicated sky prior. At the same time, the 80 m evaluation mask
excludes pixels beyond the valid depth range, so this effect usually
occupies only a limited portion of the image and has less influence
on aggregate metrics than on qualitative inspection. Overall, this
case suggests that OpenDepth currently provides a favorable trade-
off on nighttime driving benchmarks, offering stronger near-range
structural fidelity and robustness while leaving room for further
improvement in far-range calibration and sky handling under
extremely low illumination.
E
Inference Efficiency Analysis
We benchmark inference efficiency on the whole NYUv2 [47]
dataset with raw resolution 640x480, using batch size 1 on a single
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. To ensure a fair comparison, all
methods are evaluated at the same internal resolution of 768x576.
Cached Empty-Prompt Conditioning. To address the compu-
tational bottleneck introduced by the heavy large language model
(LLM) text encoder, we introduce a cached empty-prompt condi-
tioning strategy during inference. Since our framework does not
rely on semantic language control for depth estimation, the text
prompt remains strictly empty. Consequently, the output embed-
dings from the text encoder (i.e., Qwen2.5-VL [62]) are deterministic
and constant. By pre-computing and caching these empty-prompt
embeddings offline, we completely bypass the need to load and
execute the massive Qwen model during actual inference. This
strategy effectively “prunes” the text encoding branch at runtime,
significantly reducing both GPU memory consumption and infer-
ence latency without requiring complex architectural distillation
or sacrificing any structural priors.
As shown in Table D1, with this cached conditioning strategy,
Discriminative OpenDepth achieves 704.4 ms latency and 1.42 img/s
throughput, making it about 4.0× faster than FE2E [58] while using
slightly less memory (25.05 GB vs. 25.20 GB). Lotus-D [21] remains
the fastest and most lightweight baseline overall due to its much
smaller parameter count. However, although our method is slower
than Lotus-D [21], it establishes a new SOTA performance over the
previous SOTA method, FE2E [58], while simultaneously achieving
a 4.0× speedup in inference.
