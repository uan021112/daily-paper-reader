## Abstract 

Feed-forward 3D foundation models reconstruct scenes from unposed images, but the quadratic cost of global attention limits their scalability to large view sets. We present Zebra3D, a layerwise sparse–linear hybrid attention framework that improves the efficiency of pretrained 3D foundation models without training from scratch. Across VGGT, HunyuanWorld-Mirror, and π3, we observe a broadly consistent depth-wise pattern: early stages are relatively tolerant to linear replacement, while sensitive middle-to-late stages tend to exhibit more concentrated attention. Zebra3D exploits this structure by assigning linear-centered attention to tolerant stages and tile-sparse attention to sensitive stages, using landmark summaries to preserve unselected context. Closed-form affine fitting enables training-free conversion, while optional post-conversion training combines task supervision with output and relational feature distillation. Across all three backbones, Zebra3D achieves strong reconstruction and camera-pose accuracy at high attention sparsity, with training-free variants remaining close to their dense counterparts. End-to-end speedups increase with input view count, reaching up to 7.1× at 1,024 views. After post-conversion training, Zebra3D-VGGT reaches 80.5% RealEstate10K AUC@30 at 94% mean attention sparsity, within 1.1 percentage points of dense VGGT fine-tuned on the same data and schedule. 

# 1 Introduction 

Recovering scene geometry and camera poses from images is fundamental to 3D vision, supporting autonomous systems, immersive AR/VR [6, 19], and providing geometric representations for 3D world modeling [14]. Classical structure-from-motion (SfM) and multi-view stereo pipelines combine correspondence estimation, camera optimiza-tion, and dense reconstruction [24, 25]. Neural scene representations such as NeRF require iterative per-scene optimization, typically from images with known camera poses [20]. The computational cost of scene-specific opti-mization motivates a shift toward feed-forward prediction. DUSt3R directly regresses dense point maps from image pairs, but reconstructing an image collection still requires global alignment [35]. Recent feed-forward 3D (FF3D) foundation models, including VGGT [34], HunyuanWorld-Mirror [18], and π3 [38], jointly predict camera poses and dense scene geometry from multiple unposed views in a single forward pass, enabling reconstruction without per-scene optimization. These models alternate frame-wise attention with global attention for cross-view information exchange, allowing each image to benefit from observations elsewhere in the scene. Eliminating per-scene optimization, however, does not eliminate the computational bottleneck: dense global attention scales quadratically with the number of input views. In our measurements, global attention accounts for 88% of VGGT’s end-to-end runtime at 512 views (Figure 2a). Existing acceleration methods reduce this cost by merging image tokens, subsampling key/value context, or replacing dense attention with sparse or linear operators [27, 30, 45, 32]. Applying either operator uniformly, however, does not account for differences in how pretrained stages respond to approximation. This motivates a central question: can the pretrained model itself reveal which stages tolerate linear replacement and which require more faithful softmax interactions? We investigate this question by profiling every global-attention stage in pretrained VGGT, HunyuanWorld-Mirror (HYWM), and π3 using two independent diagnostics: sensitivity to linear conversion and attention concentration. Across all three backbones, we observe a recurring depth-wise structural pattern: the middle stages are especially sensitive to linear replacement, yet their attention is highly concentrated. In the preceding and following stages, attention mass is distributed across many tokens, and these stages are surprisingly tolerant to linear approximation. This alignment turns an apparent trade-off into a common allocation principle: use sparse attention where concentrated interactions matter and linear attention everywhere else. Building on this insight, we introduce Zebra3D, a sensitivity-guided framework for upcycling pretrained dense FF3D models (Figure 1). For conversion-tolerant stages, we introduce linear-centered attention , which reuses pretrained projections and applies a centered affine correction fitted to the dense operator by a closed-form least-squares solve per stage. For sensitive stages, tile-sparse attention evaluates selected spatial tiles with softmax and summarizes omitted tiles with landmarks to preserve global context. Together, the two operators replace the entire dense global-attention stack. The converted model supports immediate use without gradient updates. To further improve the quality–efficiency trade-off, we introduce a lightweight refinement which combines task supervision with distillation of outputs, centered features, and sampled cross-view relations from the frozen dense teacher. # 2 Key Related Works 

Token and context compression. FastVGGT, LiteVGGT, HTTM, and Co-Me reduce token redundancy through saliency-, geometry-, temporal-, or confidence-guided merging [27, 29, 36, 8]. AVGGT combines early frame attention with key/value subsampling [30], FlashVGGT compresses views into descriptor tokens [39], and RegimeVGGT combines merging and key/value downsampling across depth [44]. 

Efficient attention and representations. Block-Sparse VGGT evaluates selected attention blocks [32], Lite3R distills sparse linear attention [45], and Speed3R combines compressed context with selected full-resolution interactions [22]. VGG-T 3 instead adapts a fixed-size scene MLP at test time [12]. Zebra3D uses measured conversion sensitivity to assign linear and tile-sparse attention to different stages of a pretrained model. Detailed related works can be found in Appendix A.2. 

# 3 Method 

Zebra3D converts pretrained FF3D models into sparse–linear hybrids, assigning tile-sparse attention to stages sensitive to linearization and linear-centered attention to tolerant stages (Figure 2b,c). Here, stages denote global-attention layers and are indexed independently of interleaved frame-attention layers. We describe the allocation, both operators, and relational distillation below.

## 3.3 Tile-Sparse Attention with a Landmark Tail 

Stages sensitive to linear conversion retain token-level softmax interactions through tile-sparse attention (Figure 1a). We reshape each view’s patch tokens onto their 2D image grid and partition the grid into non-overlapping 8 × 8 tiles (B = 64 tokens). Each tile therefore represents a contiguous image region, unlike blocks of consecutive positions in the flattened multi-view sequence, which may not form a coherent spatial neighborhood.

We implement a packed tile-sparse attention kernel in Triton for efficient implementation without materializing the dense attention matrix (Appendix B.4).

# 4 Experiments 

## 4.1 Experimental Setup 

Models and training. We use released VGGT, HYWM, and π3 checkpoints [34, 18, 38]. Training-free Zebra3D uses 13 held-out calibration scenes for closed-form projection fitting without gradient updates. Zebra3D-KD starts from the converted model and refines all student parameters for 3,000 optimizer updates using a frozen dense teacher (Section 3.4). All training runs use 20% subset of training data used in HunyuanWorld-Mirror training, with BF16 mixed precision on eight MI355X GPUs and at most 16 views per GPU microbatch. 

Evaluation. We evaluate reconstruction on 7Scenes, NRGBD, and DTU [28, 3, 1], and camera pose on RealEstate10K and CO3Dv2 using ten-view inputs [47, 21]. Long-sequence reconstruction is evaluated on ScanNet-50 [9] with 100– 1,000 views. All evaluation datasets are strictly excluded from the refinement training mixture except CO3Dv2, for which we use disjoint training and test splits. To evaluate reconstruction beyond densely sampled video sequences, we additionally evaluate wide-baseline reconstruction on Tanks and Temples and ETH3D [15, 26] at N ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 , 64 }

views. Timing uses compiled BF16 inference on one MI355X (Appendix B.10). 

Baselines. We compare against the original dense backbones, FastVGGT [27], SAF3R [11], RegimeVGGT [44], and Lite3R [45]. We additionally evaluate architecture-matched implementations of AVGGT-f4 [30] and Block-Sparse VGGT [32], denoted A3D and Block Sparse 3D, respectively. In addition, we fine-tune the dense VGGT backbone on the same training data, for the same 3,000 steps, and with the same ground-truth supervision used for refinement (Appendix B.6). We denote this control VGGT + GT-FT and report it alongside the dense backbones. Unsupported backbone–baseline combinations are omitted. Further details are in Appendix B. 

Configuration notation. xL/ yS uses linear attention in fraction x of stages and omits fraction y of key tiles in the remaining sparse stages. Thus, 0.5L/ 0.8S is half linear and retains the top 20% of tiles elsewhere, with landmarks summarizing omitted tiles. Reported mean sparsity also accounts for landmarks, protected tokens, and linear stages (Appendix B.11).

Training-free conversion. We evaluate Zebra3D across three backbones on reconstruction and camera-pose bench-marks. Table 1 shows that substantial attention sparsity is achievable without gradient updates across all three backbones. At 0.5L/0.6S, training-free conversion retains Re10K AUC-30 within 0.007 , 0.004 , and 0.006 of dense VGGT, HYWM, and π3, respectively, at approximately 79% mean attention sparsity. It also achieves the highest CO3Dv2 AUC-30 among the evaluated non-dense methods for each backbone, while matching dense 7Scenes accu-racy errors at the reported precision. Increasing sparsity from 0.6S to 0.8S at the same linear-stage allocation raises sparsity to approximately 89%, with additional Re10K losses of only 0.7, 0.6, and 0.5 percentage points. These results demonstrate that sparse retention is a useful control for adjusting efficiency without changing the stage allocation. Figure 4 further examines this quality–sparsity trade-off against A3D on HYWM. 

Post-conversion training. Training benefits depend on both the backbone and the benchmark. For VGGT, the complete training objective raises Re10K AUC@30 from 0.737 to 0.805 at 0.5L/0.6S. The more aggressive 0.7L/0.8S configuration also reaches 0.805 at approximately 94% mean attention sparsity, within 1.1 percentage points of dense VGGT fine-tuned on the same data and schedule. However, CO3Dv2 and NRGBD performance declines after training. We observe similar regressions in the dense control, indicating that the regressions are not specific to attention replacement. At 0.5L/0.6S, the refined hybrid remains within 0.002 CO3Dv2 AUC-30 of this control and matches its NRGBD accuracy error at the reported precision. For π3, training reduces the aggressive configuration’s NRGBD accuracy error from 0.043 to 0.017, but does not improve its conservative configuration consistently. ## 4.3 Ablation Studies 

Layer-role placement. At a fixed 0.5L/ 0.8S budget, sensitivity-guided allocation achieves the highest Re10K AUC@30 on all three backbones (Table 3(d)), while uniform, random, and reverse-sensitivity assignments degrade substantially. The replacement budget alone therefore does not determine accuracy: preserving sparse softmax in the appropriate stages matters. Back-sparse nearly matches the guided allocation on π3 (0.864 vs. 0.866 ), consistent with its sensitivity being concentrated later in the network. This supports adapting the allocation to each backbone rather than imposing a shared stage pattern. 

Attention operators. Table 3(a) separates the effects of fitting and centering. On π3, affine fitting raises Re10K AUC@30 from 0.789 to 0.811 , and centering further improves it to 0.836 , indicating that both components help adapt the linear operator to pretrained representations (detailed comparisons are in Appendix D.1). Table 3(b) shows that landmark summaries are particularly important under sparse selection: adding them to tile-sparse attention raises AUC@30 from 0.811 to 0.868 and reduces NRGBD accuracy error from 0.066 to 0.014 . Thus, retaining compressed context from unselected tiles contributes substantially beyond exact attention over selected tokens. Appendix D reports additional backbone and configuration results. 

Training objective. Table 3(c) compares training objectives at a fixed training budget. KD-only training underperforms GT-only training on Re10K ( 0.716 vs. 0.777 AUC@30), and adding output KD alone does not close this gap (Table 3c). The complete objective performs best across all three reported metrics, reaching 0.805 AUC@30 and reducing 7Scenes and NRGBD accuracy errors to 0.015 and 0.024 . These results support combining task supervision with output and intermediate-feature supervision in the tested recipe. Full results are in (Appendix D.4).

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced Zebra3D , a sensitivity-guided framework for upcycling pretrained 3D reconstruction transformers into sparse–linear hybrids. Across VGGT, HYWM, and π3, we identified depth-dependent sensitivity to linear replacement, with more sensitive stages tending to exhibit more concentrated attention. This observation motivates assigning tile-sparse attention to sensitive stages and linear-centered attention to tolerant stages, while landmark summaries preserve unselected context. Closed-form affine fitting enables training-free conversion, with optional post-conversion training incorporating task supervision and output and relational feature distillation. Zebra3D offers quality–efficiency trade-offs at 79–94% mean attention sparsity: conservative configurations preserve accuracy close to dense models, while more aggressive configurations prioritize speed. At 0.7L/0.8S, it delivers respective end-to-end speedups of 6.2×,

5.5×, and 7.1× at 1,024 views across the three backbones. Input-adaptive allocation and broader hardware support remain future work.

## A.2 Extended Related Work 

Pairwise and direct multi-view reconstruction. DUSt3R regresses dense point maps from image pairs without requiring known camera calibration or poses, but reconstructing an image collection requires selecting pairs and aligning their local predictions in a common frame [35]. The quadratic growth in candidate pairs and the subsequent global optimization limit scaling to long sequences. MUSt3R extends this paradigm to symmetric multi-view prediction in a shared coordinate frame and introduces a multi-layer memory mechanism for scalable offline and online reconstruction [5]. Fast3R also generalizes pairwise reconstruction to unordered image sets: a fusion transformer processes all views jointly and predicts local and global point maps in one forward pass, avoiding external global alignment [41]. Its train-short, test-long design and distributed inference enable sequences exceeding 1,000 images. These methods redesign the reconstruction architecture or inference pathway for direct multi-view processing; Zebra3D instead upcycles existing feed-forward 3D backbones by replacing their dense global-attention operators. 14 Zebra3D: Efficient Hybrid Foundation Models For Scalable 3D Reconstruction 

Token reduction. Token merging reduces both dimensions of the attention matrix while leaving softmax attention itself unchanged. FastVGGT merges redundant tokens while protecting the reference view, salient regions, and spatial coverage [27]. Its safeguards reflect a central difficulty in 3D reconstruction: tokens that appear redundant locally may still carry cross-view evidence needed for correspondence or camera estimation. LiteVGGT therefore selects geometry-aware anchors and caches merge assignments across layers, reducing repeated selection overhead while maintaining spatial structure [29]. HTTM performs temporal merging separately for each attention head so that head-specific interaction patterns are not forced into one shared token partition [36]. Co-Me instead learns a confidence predictor and preferentially merges low-confidence tokens [8]. These methods reduce both query and context lengths, so their savings propagate through subsequent layers, but merging is lossy and changes the token set consumed by downstream prediction heads. 

Key/value and context compression. Another strategy keeps every query but compresses the context. We refer to AVGGT and its architecture-matched HYWM and π3 variants collectively as A3D. A3D replaces early global layers with frame attention, then spatially subsamples patch keys and values while preserving queries and special tokens [30]. This preserves a prediction for every query token but reduces the evidence available to each query. FlashVGGT summarizes each frame with descriptor tokens and uses cross-attention from image tokens to those descriptors; caching descriptors enables chunk-recursive inference without reprocessing the complete history [39]. RegimeVGGT combines saliency-aware merging with protected key/value downsampling and varies compression across depth, acknowledging that redundancy is not uniform through the network [44]. Speed3R similarly combines a compressed context with selected full-resolution interactions [22]. Context-compression methods preserve the query grid, but their sampling or summary policy determines which cross-view evidence remains individually accessible. 

Sparse and linear replacements. The closest methods replace global attention itself. We refer to Block-Sparse VGGT and its architecture-matched variants as Block Sparse 3D. Block Sparse 3D uses sparse kernels to evaluate adaptively selected query–key blocks, exploiting the concentration of attention around geometric correspondences [32]. Selected blocks retain exact softmax interactions, while unselected blocks make no contribution. SAF3R introduces dynamic sparse attention with head-specific sparse-pattern assignments [11]. Lite3R instead combines sparse linear attention with partial attention distillation, replacing quadratic interactions with a model-agnostic efficient operator [45]. VGG-T 3 takes a different route: it replaces the variable-length scene representation with a fixed-size MLP adapted at test time, yielding linear scaling in the number of views [12]. Zebra3D differs along both operator selection and information preservation. Rather than applying one approximation throughout the stack or prescribing a fixed schedule, it profiles the pretrained model and assigns linear or tile-sparse attention according to measured stage sensitivity. Tile-sparse stages preserve exact softmax interactions for selected spatial regions and represent every omitted region with a landmark under the same normalization. Linear-centered stages retain the inherited projections and use a closed-form fit, enabling training-free conversion. Optional relational distillation then refines the same hybrid architecture without changing its stage assignment.

## B.2 Baselines 

The dense baselines are the unmodified released VGGT, HYWM, and π3 models [34, 18, 38]. ## B.3 Linear initialization 

For each linear stage, we first run the frozen dense model on 13 held-out multi-view calibration scenes. Teacher softmax outputs and converted linear outputs are accumulated across scenes, tokens, heads, and views. We then fit the centered affine correction in Equation 4 by a regularized least-squares solve in float64. The fit requires one teacher forward pass and one solve per converted stage; it does not update inherited weights by gradient descent. These fitted projections, together with copied dense-model weights, define the training-free Zebra3D models. 

## B.4 Triton implementation of hybrid attention 

We implement Zebra3D’s linear-centered and tile-sparse operators in Triton to reduce intermediate memory traffic and execute their computations efficiently on the GPU. For linear-centered attention, we fuse Q/K normalization, positional rotation, and channel-softmax feature mapping into the kernels that accumulate the linear-attention state and compute the readout. This avoids materializing intermediate normalized Q/K tensors between separate preprocessing and attention kernels. Sparse stages partition each image into 8×8-patch tiles, or 64 tokens per full tile. We pack the full key/value sequence into tile-major order before selecting key blocks independently for each head and query block. The retention parameter is ρ=0 .2 or 0.4, corresponding to y=0 .8 or 0.6. Unselected packed blocks contribute one key–value landmark each; special-token keys remain visible, special-token query rows retain dense attention, and every patch query retains its self key. Training and evaluation use the packed triton implementation with 64-token query and key blocks, head-level selection, one view per selection group. The operator first constructs a permutation from the model’s view-major token layout to tile-major order. Complete spatial tiles occupy aligned 64-token blocks; tiles truncated at the image boundary are concatenated in a ragged region and may share or span packed blocks. Camera and register tokens are appended as special tokens. The permutation, its inverse, the tile-to-block map, and special-token indices are cached for each frame count and patch grid. This avoids rebuilding layout-dependent index tensors and repeating the device synchronizations needed to construct them. At inference, a fused packing launch makes Q contiguous and permutation-packs K and V together. The attention kernel can then read repeatedly used K/V blocks with coalesced memory accesses, while query and output rows are mapped through the cached permutation. A fused statistics kernel reads BF16 Q, K, and V and accumulates in FP32. For each packed block and head, it emits the mean query and key used by the selector, a mean key–value landmark over non-special tokens, and the log occupancy. These blocks coincide with spatial tiles in the aligned region but can combine boundary-tile tokens in the ragged region. Each query block ranks key blocks by the mean-query/mean-key dot product and stores the selected indices together with a chosen-block mask. The selection count is ⌈ρN/ 64 ⌉, capped by the number of eligible non-special blocks and kept at least one when an eligible block exists. The main Triton program implements FlashAttention-style online softmax. It streams four contributions without constructing an N ×N score matrix: non-special tokens in selected key blocks, every special-token key, the query’s own key when its block was not selected, and one occupancy-weighted landmark for each unselected block. The landmark logit receives a log nb offset, where nb is the number of valid non-special tokens represented by that landmark. The chosen-block mask excludes selected blocks from the landmark tail, while special-token masks exclude special keys from the selected-block stream. When an explicit self key is added, it is not subtracted from its block’s landmark summary. Softmax state and output accumulators remain in FP32; BF16 matrix products use the accelerator’s matrix instructions. The kernel works in base-two exponent units, folding log 2 e into the attention scale and using the hardware exp2 instruction. The shipped forward launch uses two warps, two pipeline stages, and a 64-column landmark tile. Special query rows are evaluated exactly by a separate dense-row kernel and written into the same output tensor. This kernel distributes key tiles across workers and combines their partial softmax results after rescaling to a common maximum. On supported GPU layouts, it uses four warps, two pipeline stages, and explicit base-two exponential evaluation while retaining attention over every key. 18 Zebra3D: Efficient Hybrid Foundation Models For Scalable 3D Reconstruction 

Training uses a custom autograd bridge for BF16 [B, H, N, 64] Q/K/V , 64-token blocks, per-head selection, and a landmark tail. The forward retains the selected-block indices, chosen masks, landmark statistics, output, and row-wise log-sum-exp. Separate Triton kernels compute dQ and the owner-accumulated dK/dV in FP32, followed by dedicated gradient kernels for exact special-query rows; gradients are cast back to the input dtype at the boundary. The sparse call is an explicit compiler graph break so that raw Triton launches are not captured or rewritten, while the surrounding backbone remains compilable. The reported 3,000-step KD runs use this autograd bridge, whereas inference calls the forward kernel directly.

## B.6 Training data and optimization 

Data and augmentation. All KD runs use the same 20% preprocessed subset of the HunyuanWorld-Mirror train-ing mixture. The constituent datasets and their sampling weights are: HyperSim [23] (400), TartanAir [37] (300), Matterport3D [7] (250), DL3DV-10K [17] (250), CO3D training split [21] (250), BlendedMVS [43] (200), Unreal-Stereo4K [31] (150), MVS-Synth [13] (150), WildRGB-D [40] (100), ARKitScenes [4] (200), MapFree [2] (150), and MegaDepth [16] (150). Each GPU receives up to 16 views per microbatch. Images have aspect ratios in [0 .5, 2.0] and resolutions of 100 k– 250 k pixels. Spatial cropping and photometric augmentation follow the common multi-view data pipeline. Color jitter is applied with probability 0.9, with brightness, contrast, and saturation magnitudes of 0.5 and hue magnitude of 0.1; the pipeline also includes random grayscale augmentation. 

Calibration scenes. The reference stage assignment and the closed-form projection fit use 13 multi-view scenes shipped with the HunyuanWorld-Mirror examples. No gradient update is run on them, and none of them enters a reported metric table. Appendix C.3 repeats the sensitivity profiling on 7Scenes, DTU, RealEstate10K, NRGBD, and CO3Dv2 and compares those rankings with this fixed reference. It does not change the assignment. 

Task supervision. Task supervision in Equation 8 is backbone-specific, because the three backbones do not predict the same quantities. HYWM and VGGT use the shared camera, point-map, and depth objectives with top-level weights 5.0, 1.0, and 1.0,respectively. These weights are inherited unchanged from the released HYWM and VGGT training configurations rather than tuned for this work. In raw magnitude the camera term is 4–5× smaller than the point term, so the factor of 5.0 aligns the two contributions rather than prioritizing pose: over early training the weighted camera and point gradients stay within 1.06 –1.18 × of each other, and this ratio is nearly identical for the dense and hybrid runs, so the weighting does not confound the dense–hybrid comparisons of Section 4. We depart from the released VGGT configuration in one respect: it disables the point loss by default, whereas we enable all three terms. Point and depth losses use γ = 1 , α = 0 .2, and a valid-range setting of 0.98 . The point loss uses a normal-based gradient penalty, while the depth loss uses a spatial-gradient penalty. Normal-map and Gaussian-splat rendering task losses are disabled; for HYWM, normal predictions nevertheless remain in output distillation with unit weight, and the normal head is not frozen. For π3 we use its own published objective, because it predicts local point maps and camera-to-world transforms rather than the world-coordinate points, nine-vector pose encoding, and depth confidence that the shared losses expect. Local point maps are supervised by an inverse-depth-weighted L1 against local ground truth after a scale s⋆ obtained by a detached weighted alignment on 4,096 resampled valid points. A geometric normal term built from local point neighborhoods is enabled on the high- and mid-quality datasets of the mixture. Cameras are supervised on all relative pose pairs, using a Huber term on translations scaled by the same s⋆ and a rotation-angle term; the effective weights 20 Zebra3D: Efficient Hybrid Foundation Models For Scalable 3D Reconstruction 

are 1 on the point terms, 10 on translations, and 0.1 on rotations. Confidence supervision is disabled, as are the shared camera, point, and depth losses. 

Optimization. Training uses eight AMD MI355X GPUs with DistributedDataParallel, BF16 student mixed precision, synchronized batch normalization, and four-step gradient accumulation. Each GPU processes at most 16 views per microbatch; the dynamic sequence length sets the sequence batch size to ⌊16 /V ⌋. Thus 512 views per optimizer update is an upper bound attained only when every GPU microbatch fills the 16-view budget. Training sequences contain at most 16 views; evaluations on longer sequences test generalization beyond this training sequence length. # F Qualitative Results and Limitations 

## F.1 Additional Qualitative Reconstructions 

Figure 11 extends Figure 6 to every scene used for qualitative inspection. Scenes were selected from the per-scene Chamfer distances already recorded for Table 2, before any render was produced, so the choice is made on the reported metric rather than on appearance. 

Figure 11: Long-sequence reconstruction at 1,000 views on four ScanNet-50 scenes, coloured by distance to the ground-truth mesh (blue 0 m, red ≥ 0.30 m). Protocol is identical to Figure 6. The renders re-fit the evaluation’s similarity transform on 1–99 percentile extents, identically for every method, so that a few unprojection outliers cannot shrink the room toward the centre of the frame; no points are removed, and every reported Chamfer distance uses the evaluation’s own bounding-box alignment. A lower Chamfer distance can in principle be bought by smoothing: points spread around the true surface score well under a two-sided mean distance, while the render looks softer. That is not what happens here. Averaged over the four scenes of Figure 11, Zebra3D -KD ( 0.7L/ 0.6S) improves the median per-point error from 0.217 to 0.174 m, the 90 th percentile from 0.536 to 0.420 m, and the share of points beyond 0.30 m from 33 .0% to 23 .1% , so the gain is present at every quantile rather than only in the mean. Local surface thickness, estimated as the median distance from a point to the best-fit plane of its 16 nearest neighbours, is 0.0114 m against 0.0112 m for dense VGGT: the reconstructions are no thicker. What does differ is coverage, since more of the predicted depth clears the shared confidence threshold, which is why point counts are annotated in the figures. Point clouds are exported from the evaluation path itself, and the Chamfer distance recomputed from each exported cloud reproduces the recorded value to ±0.0000 for dense VGGT, A3D and both Zebra3D -KD configurations. FastVGGT and RegimeVGGT differ by up to 0.005 , which is expected: their token-merging decisions depend on runtime state and are not bit-reproducible across runs. No ordering in Table 2 changes. ## F.2 Limitations 

Zebra3D primarily benefits long sequences, where global attention dominates inference cost; speedups remain modest at 8–16 views (Figure 5). As attention becomes cheaper, the image encoder and prediction heads impose an increasing upper bound on end-to-end acceleration. Our stage allocation is fixed per backbone and derived from marginal, single-stage conversion sensitivity, so it does not model interactions among converted stages or adapt to individual inputs. Although the allocation is stable across our calibration datasets, broader generalization to substantially different architectures and domains remains to be established. Finally, our optimized kernels and benchmarks target Triton on AMD MI355X GPUs; portability and performance on other accelerators require further evaluation. Optional distillation also requires a frozen dense teacher during refinement, although it adds no inference-time cost. 37
