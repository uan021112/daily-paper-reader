# Monocular Navigation Relative to Unknown Spacecraft Using a Transformer-Aided Kalman Filter 

Pol Francesch Huc Department of Aeronautics and Astronautics Stanford University Stanford, CA 94305 pfrances@stanford.edu Simone D’Amico Department of Aeronautics and Astronautics Stanford University Stanford, CA 94305 damicos@stanford.edu 

Abstract— As space becomes increasingly congested and contested, robust autonomous navigation is essential for servicing, debris re-moval, and situational awareness with respect to unknown resident space objects. This work presents a novel learning-based pipeline for pose estimation of uncooperative and unknown spacecraft using only monocular images from a single servicer. The approach combines a transformer-based neural network with a Multi-State Constraint Kalman Filter (MSCKF) to estimate the pose (i.e., po-sition and orientation of the target spacecraft relative to the camera) throughout rendezvous and proximity operations. Unlike existing vision-based methods that require prior knowledge of the target shape or inertia properties, rely on additional sensing modalities such as depth, lidar, or stereo, or only recover translation up to scale, the proposed pipeline generalizes to previously unseen spacecraft using a single monocular camera. The transformer network esti-mates the odometry, the change in pose between images up to scale, from SuperPoint features matched by LightGlue. These odometry estimates serve as MSCKF pseudo-measurements. The MSCKF uses these pseudo-measurements along with an orbit and attitude kinematics model to estimate the pose of the target. In particular, the relative orbit elements, the target’s attitude with respect to the servicer’s camera (where the target’s body is defined arbitrarily by the filter), and the associated angular velocity are estimated directly by the filter. Given the monocular approach and short distance to the target, the full observability of the range to the target is recovered via attitude maneuvers by the servicer. Feature tracks and loop closures are used to bound short- and long-term drift, respectively. The filter is initialized using coarse relative orbit information that could be available from upstream methods such as angles-only navigation, and no other prior such as attitude, shape, size or inertia properties. The method is trained and evaluated on a re-rendered high-resolution version of the SPE3R dataset, which includes synthetic images of 103 spacecraft. Eleven of these spacecraft are held out during training to evaluate the generalization to unseen targets. Monte Carlo simulations are then used to evaluate the navigation pipeline on rendered trajectories of the held out space-craft. The odometry front-end is further evaluated on hardware-in-the-loop images from the SHIRT dataset, where the network retains a 4–6 fold advantage in the median translation direction error over a classical baseline. The results demonstrate that learned vision pipelines as a front-end for Kalman filters provide median errors of 

3.7◦ in attitude and 2.2% of range in ROE when navigating about unknown targets. The approach supports inspection, servicing, and debris removal missions involving targets without available shape models.

## 1. I NTRODUCTION 

The space industry is experiencing a resurgence in interest, especially in the realm of autonomous rendezvous, proximity operations, and docking (RPOD). These missions generally require satellites, denoted as servicers, to be close to their tar-get with separations on the order of meters to contact. How-ever, the target may not always be cooperative (because it is defunct, not designed for RPOD, or adversarial) and so the satellite is required to independently perform pose estimation of the target to successfully complete its mission. Unknown or damaged targets may also require autonomous shape re-construction. Cameras are an attractive choice for this kind of mission since they are generally cheap, lightweight, power-efficient, and already ubiquitous on satellites as they are used in attitude estimation. The approach taken in this work is to first estimate the pose of the target with respect to the servicer, without having access to the 3D model. Pose estimates support safe operation during image collection and provide inputs for concurrent shape estimation. The pose of the target is estimated from an incoming stream of images taken by the servicer during close range operations after being initialized by an earlier phase of the mission such as an approach using Angles-Only Navigation (AON) [1]. Although pose estimation of known targets through monoc-ular images is a mature technology [2], the same cannot be said of unknown targets. This work combines a novel odometry network with a Multi-State Constraint Kalman Fil-ter (MSCKF) [3] for monocular pose estimation of unknown targets. A notional pipeline is shown in Figure 1. Because monocular measurements are scale-free, the range must be made observable when no prior on the target’s size or range is available. To achieve this, the filter requires a known metric displacement of the camera center, in the direction transverse to the line of sight. Several options from the AON literature exist which can supply this displacement, and the particular choice and derivation are provided in Section 5. Since the target 3D model is unknown, regressing the pose of the target from a single set of features or image is difficult, as is seen by the poor pose estimates in the work by Park et al. [5]. Instead, this work estimates features in sets of images, and uses the state-of-the-art feature matcher LightGlue [6] to match the features between the images. LightGlue is fine-tuned on the novel dataset SPE3R+ to adapt it to spacecraft imagery. SPE3R+ builds upon the original SPE3R [5] dataset, which included 1,000 images and masks per model for 64 satellite models, with 7 of those models reserved for a test set (so they were never seen during training). SPE3R+ includes these 64 original models, but adds 39 new models (103 total, 11 in test set) with 5,000 higher resolution images per model, masks, and metric depth. These feature matches on their own are not sufficient to estimate the pose, and typical computer vision pipelines will use the 8-point algorithm along with RANdom SAmple Con-sensus (RANSAC), or similar, to estimate the fundamental matrix and the odometry between pairs of images [7]. As will be shown, the estimation accuracy from this method is poor, and so the Spacecraft Odometry Network (SON) is introduced to estimate the odometry from noisy feature matches. Because SON only provides an odometry measurement, sub-sequent filters are prone to divergence since this is a form of dead reckoning. Two additional measurements are used to prevent this divergence: feature tracks and loop closure. Fea-ture tracks are features observed across multiple images in the sliding window and provide multi-frame relative constraints that limit local drift. Loop closure provides a longer-baseline relative attitude constraint between the current image and a retained keyframe. An MSCKF is then introduced to estimate the pose of the target over a trajectory. The MSCKF is used to leverage the feature tracks directly, as these provide the geometric constraint needed for this kind of filter without needing to incorporate the feature locations on the target in the filter state. Outlier rejection and covariance inflation are also leveraged to remove potentially noisy feature track and loop closure measurements as well as reduce the overconfidence in the relative orbit estimate. ## 2. S TATE OF THE ART 

The state of the art in monocular pose estimation begins with known targets. DARPA’s Orbital Express demonstrated cooperative vision-based rendezvous in 2007 using a camera and target-mounted retro-reflectors [9]. D’Amico et al. [2] instead used classical computer vision techniques to detect edge features on the target and then use the known 3D model to regress the 6D pose, demonstrating uncooperative pose estimation. Improvements on the pose estimate have been made through the use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) [10], which have more recently been traded for Vision Transformers (ViT) [11] for reduced computational cost and increased robustness to the domain gap. The process has been shown to be robust to out of distribution images through the implementation of an Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) [8] and by refining the network on the target domain [12]. Recent surveys [13, 14] identify deployability on space-grade hardware, further closing of the domain gap, and reliable uncertainty under domain shift as the open problems in this line of work, all of which still assume a known target. These methods, and others, are trained on a large amount of images and their popularity has led to the release of image datasets of different satellites. The dataset by Dung et al. [15] compiles real spacecraft imagery with bounding-box, segmentation, and component level annotations, but does not provide metric poses. URSO [16] in contrast uses photo-realistic rendering to generate synthetic spacecraft images with pose labels for known target geometries. SPEED+ [17], SHIRT [8], and MAN-DATA [18] include synthetic and real images in a space-surrogate environment and provide refer-ence truth poses, but only of one target each. Finally SPE3R [5] is a dataset of synthetic images only, but it includes 64 real satellites in its dataset along with the pose labels. Due to the number of targets and the availability of poses SPE3R is most useful for this work, however the images are provided at a very low resolution and so it is improved here. Literature on unknown target pose estimation in space is much more limited. Park et al. [5] introduce a CNN to estimate the pose and shape from a single image, however the pose estimates have errors in the tens of degrees for the test set, and the network does not estimate the scale. Bates et al. [19] improve pose estimation by addressing canonical pose ambiguities, but still do not recover the metric scale. DreamSat [20] and DreamSat-Pose [21] predict shape and, in the latter case, pose, but still do not estimate the scale and provide similar errors as Park et al. [5]. CRISP [22] has better pose estimates than Park et al. [5], but requires RGB-D images. Barad et al. [23] also simultaneously esti-mate the shape and pose, but they do so using a sequence of RGB-D images which provides the scale, and tracking tends to drift over long duration trajectories. Dor et al. [24] apply Simultaneous Localization And Mapping (SLAM) using ORB-SLAM [25] to space-like imagery, but validation is limited to 3-DOF motion and tracking is inconsistent. Augenstein [26] implements a landmark-based SLAM using monocular images only, and recovers both the pose and shape but again does not estimate the scale. Capuano et al. [27] and Tweddle et al. [28] both estimate the pose of unknown targets, but they are aided by a laser range finder and stereovision, respectively. Two works do provide monocular vision-based pose estima-tion, but they also include significant drawbacks. Ticozzi et al. [29] implement a factor-graph based active SLAM method which generates an attitude guidance strategy to minimize the target shape model uncertainty. Their strategy relies on perfect initialization (the first two poses are assumed to be known) and feature matching is assumed to be perfect as well. The method is only validated over a short horizon, at most 23 poses. Legrand et al. [30] follow a two-stage approach, where a NeRF model [31] is used to generate imagery for training a conventional known target pose estimation network [12]. However, the first stage requires pose estimates which limits the online applicability. Terrestrial vision-based pose estimation on the other hand is a much larger field. Algorithms like COLMAP [32, 33] estimate the pose and structure from a set of images, but are typically run offline. These have been recently comple-mented by learning-based reconstruction from image pairs, such as DUSt3R [34] and MASt3R [35], which regress dense pointmaps with large transformers on terrestrial scenes. Feature-based methods use feature matches between two images in order to estimate the odometry between the images, mostly by estimating the fundamental matrix [7]. Early feature detectors and matchers include SIFT [36], SURF [37], and ORB [38], but they are increasingly being replaced by learning based methods in current literature. SuperPoint [4] supplies learned features for attention-based matchers SuperGlue [39] and LightGlue [6], with the latter reducing inference time through early stopping. Other learned ap-proaches include DISK [40], dense matchers such as RoMa [41], and simultaneous extraction and matching methods such as LoFTR [42]. Some terrestrial methods focus then on improving the odom-etry estimation (sometimes with scale) that must occur from these feature matches. Rockwell et al. [43] introduce a transformer which regresses the odometry directly from an image pair, and FAR [44] extends this by combining such a learned prediction with a match-based solver. Although FAR supports multiple feature and correspondence front ends, its learned metric-scale prior is trained on terrestrial scenes and does not directly address unknown spacecraft with arbitrary physical scale. Arnold et al. [45] introduce map-free visual relocalization where odometry estimation is done directly from images, but this requires a large training dataset not available for space applications. Existing work does not yet resolve the problem of accurate monocular pose estimation of unknown targets. Solutions which target space-like imagery generally make simplifying assumptions, such as the availability of additional sensors, or do not estimate the range to the target. Terrestrial methods on the other hand are not purpose built for space-like im-agery, and so do not function out of the box with the same performance as they do on the domain they are designed for. This work seeks to resolve this by leveraging some terrestrial state-of-the-art methods and adapting them to the 3space problem, along with a novel learning-based solution to odometry estimation. 

## 3. F EATURE EXTRACTION & M ATCHING ON 

## IMAGE PAIRS 

SuperPoint [4] first extracts image keypoints and descriptors for a pair of images, and LightGlue [6] estimates corre-spondences between them. In this work, a keypoint and its descriptor are denoted as a feature. Although these methods perform well on terrestrial benchmarks [46], their perfor-mance on spacecraft imagery is poor, and motivates domain adaptation through fine-tuning. Additionally, images are cropped using a target bounding box (derived from reference truth in this work, see Section 7) before feature extraction to remove noisy background features. 

Figure 2 . Features extracted by SuperPoint (top) and matched by LightGlue (bottom) from a pair of images of the ACRIMSAT satellite. For the domain adaptation, LightGlue is fine-tuned on SPE3R+ while SuperPoint remains frozen. Features are therefore generated by SuperPoint during training, but ref-erence truth correspondences are generated by projecting the extracted features between the images using the known reference truth poses and metric depth. This is followed by nearest-neighbor association with thresholds listed in Table 1 in Section 7. Evaluation uses 11 spacecraft that are withheld during training, and so represent the true unknown target case. SuperPoint and LightGlue are chosen because SuperPoint provides a fixed set of features for each image, allowing LightGlue matches to be chained into multi-image feature tracks. This property motivates their use over pair-dependent correspondences such as those produced by LoFTR [42]. The resulting tracks supply the MSCKF with the geometric constraints described in Section 6. Figure 2 illustrates the extracted features and matches.

Classical Approach

SON: Spacecraft Odometry Network 

SON is a transformer-based neural network architecture de-signed to regress the odometry between two images from noisy feature matches. The architecture is shown in Figure 3 and uses the transformer backbone from LightGlue [6], along with a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) to regress the odometry. To address the fact that LightGlue does not provide a con-stant number of feature matches, attention-based pooling is performed to select the most important portions of the feature matches and descriptors and provide a constant input size for the MLP. The reason for using LightGlue is that feature matching and odometry estimation are coupled problems: given the odometry and depth, matches follow by projection, as in Section 3. The philosophy behind using LightGlue then is that neural networks which perform well in the task of feature matching could also perform well in related tasks. The results and the ablation studies performed in this work are used to validate this claim, giving some confidence for the choice in architecture. Like the baseline, SON only estimates the odometry up to a scale. This design decision is made because doing otherwise would require the network to learn about the size of satellites since the scale of the translation vector is fully coupled with the size of the target, which is more difficult for monocular vision to do while generalizing to unseen targets. A different approach discussed in Section 5 is used to recover the scale over time. 

Transformer backbone— The transformer backbone is made up of Nt identical transformer layers [49], each of which take as input the descriptors d ∈ RNm×256 and the position encodings p ∈ RNm×128 of the feature matches for both images, where Nm is the number of feature matches. The transformer then outputs the same variables, but is trained to add context to the descriptors with self- and cross-attention blocks which is helpful for the downstream task of odometry estimation. Like LightGlue, SON uses a learnable Fourier encoder for the positional encoding [50, 51]. For particular details on the transformer architecture readers are referred to LightGlue [6], but a short summary is provided here. ## 5. S CALE OBSERVABILITY 

Although the features extracted by SuperPoint, then matched by LightGlue, and finally regressed into an odometry by SON are necessary in order to estimate the orientation and motion of the target, they provide no insight into how far the target is. Unlike on Earth, where terrestrial users can rely on objects being “human”-sized or at least providing scale diversity in busy city streets, satellites provide very little scale diversity (they are the only thing in the image) and are not homogeneous in size (they range from centimeters to tens of meters). As such, other methods are required to estimate the scale which is necessary as it provides direct insight into the distance to the target, thus informing collision avoidance. The method chosen in this work does not directly estimate the size of the target, but rather, over time, reveals the distance to it. This is because the method is derived from AON, which relies on bearings taken at ranges where the target appears as a single bright pixel, and so no size of the target can be estimated. To resolve the scale using these methods, the filter needs a known metric displacement of the camera center transverse to the line of sight. In current angles-only literature three options are available to achieve this: orbit maneuvers [53], attitude maneuvers [54], and the leveraging of the non-linear dynamics [55]. The latter is the only one which does not rely on changing the spacecraft operations, but it is only available at large distances. At the distances considered in this work the 5relative dynamics are near-linear, and so the nonlinearities are too weak to be relied upon. A nonzero camera lever arm is common in spacecraft layouts. Among the available approaches, attitude maneuver profiles are simplest to implement without significant changes to the concept of operations while still providing the necessary transverse baseline. One such profile, implemented in this work, is a constant roll about the boresight, such that the target is kept in the camera center. Because the camera is mounted at a known nonzero lever arm from the servicer center of mass, this rotation causes the camera center to traverse a known metric trajectory. The resulting change in bearing provides the metric baseline required to make the relative range observable [54]. The servicer already performs target tracking and this involves attitude maneuvers which could add the observability, however in this work the majority of the separation is in the along-track so the line of sight rotates at 0.003 –0.02 ◦/s over the separations considered. This is one to two orders of magnitude below the commanded roll, and proves to be too slow to overcome nominal filter drift. With the modeled transverse lever arm chosen in this work (see Table 2 in Section 7), the roll sweeps a 0.36 m baseline which subtends 2.1◦ at 10 m of separation, but only 0.7◦ at 30 

m, bounding the range at which the approach remains useful. 

## 6. M ULTI -S TATE CONSTRAINT KALMAN 

## FILTERING 

A Multi-State Constraint Kalman Filter (MSCKF) [3] fuses SON odometry measurements with relative dynamics and multi-frame geometric measurements. Because neither a target model nor a canonical target body frame is available, the attitude trajectory is observable only up to a single con-stant rotation. Consecutive SON measurements can therefore accumulate attitude drift, which feature tracks bound over the short term and loop closures over the long term. Because all measurements are relative, the target frame remains arbitrary. During evaluation, one constant rotation aligns the estimated and reference truth attitude histories before attitude errors are computed.

Loop closures 

Loop closure is implemented by retaining selected attitude clones Kt after they leave the normal sliding window Wt.After a warm-up period, a clone is promoted to a keyframe if its estimated attitude is at least 15 ◦ from that of every other retained keyframe until the maximum number of cloned keyframes Nk is reached. At that point, a candidate replaces the keyframe closest to its nearest neighbor whenever this increases the minimum angular separation of the set Kt.These keyframes remain correlated filter states and can be corrected by subsequent measurement updates to ensure that the filter remains self-consistent. The loop-closure measurement yt,κ  

> LC ,q

is obtained by running the same SuperPoint, LightGlue, and SON pipeline between the retained keyframe and current image. Only SON’s ro-tation output is used for loop closure, with measurement covariance CLC .To determine whether or not to perform loop closure between the current image and one of the keyframes in Kt, a threshold is used. A retained keyframe is considered only when it is at least 2Nw frames old and its predicted rotation relative to the current frame is no greater than 45 ◦. The loop-closure update is accepted when at least 100 LightGlue matches are available and its Normalized Innovation Squared (NIS) is no greater than 40. The innovation gate is deliberately loose, as once drift has accumulated a correct loop closure would produce a large innovation, and so a tighter gate may reject the measurement. The rotation threshold is used as a protection against runaway compute, since otherwise the odometry estimation portion of the pipeline would run for all Nk keyframes on every image. The recency requirement excludes keyframes which were recently promoted to Kt, as these do not add more constraints than the feature tracks. Useful loop closures then are those that occur when the target’s rotation brings a previously observed side back into view after a long interval. An accepted loop closure removes the drift accumulated since the keyframe was last used, hence allowing for consistency in the attitude history.

## 7. T RAINING & I MPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Key assumptions 

Five key assumptions are made that limit the immediate applicability of this work in flight missions. 1. Sensing : The camera boresight remains aligned with the target, and an upstream detector supplies a target bounding box. The present evaluation derives this bounding box from a dilated reference truth mask. Future work will replace the reference truth cropping and pointing with a detector and estimator-based control. 2. Servicer knowledge : The servicer position and attitude in the Earth-centered inertial (ECI) frame are known exactly. Camera intrinsics and camera-to-servicer extrinsics are also exact. Commanded attitude motion is applied without control error or delay, and its effects on nominal spacecraft operations (e.g., communications, power generation) are not modeled. In flight, these quantities are supplied by GNSS and star trackers, typically orders of magnitude more accurately than the relative estimates sought here. An upstream method is assumed to initialize the ROE state of the filter. 3. Image domain : Images are synthetic and contain neither sensor noise nor an Earth background. A sun-synchronous orbit is simulated, with the target illuminated throughout the trajectory. The odometry front-end is individually evalu-ated on hardware-in-the-loop images. No formal calibration against flight imagery is available. 4. Target : The target is assumed to be rigid, without ar-ticulating appendages. Sufficient repeatable surface features remain visible across the baselines used by the measurements. 5. Compute : Sufficient compute is assumed to be available. Inference benchmarking is performed on an NVIDIA AGX Orin which is an edge device, but does not necessarily repre-sent current flight-ready hardware. 

Datasets 

Four evaluation datasets are used in this work. Two of these are used just in the evaluation of LightGlue as is done in the original work by Lindenberger et al. [6], and so only include terrestrial scenes. In contrast, the other datasets (SPE3R+, SHIRT) include images of satellites only. In addition to these static datasets, trajectories are generated and rendered on the fly for testing the MSCKF with SON in the loop.

The precision (P) when compared against the reference truth matches at a reprojection error of 3 px is reported. The accuracy is reported by computing the mean reprojection error of the four image corners and reporting the area under the cumulative error curve (AUC) up to values of 1 px and 5 px.

SPE3R+— The original SPE3R dataset [5] includes 1,000 synthetically generated images per real satellite model along with the associated masks and poses. The dataset contains 64 spacecraft models, with 7 of them being reserved for testing only and the rest for training. The dataset is rendered using the Unreal Engine 5 at a resolution of 256 × 256 px. SPE3R+ re-renders SPE3R in grayscale at 1920 × 1200 px using an OpenGL renderer [64]. A sample set of images are shown in Figure 4 for comparison. In addition to the original 64 models, 39 new models are introduced for a grand total of 103 spacecraft of which 11 are used for testing only. These spacecraft are rendered at varying distances such that their angular size in the image plane occupies a similar distribution for all of them regardless of their true size. 5,000 images per satellite are rendered, along with masks and the metric depth which is used to estimate the true feature matches needed to fine-tune LightGlue. Although no image noise is inserted, a single light source is modeled as sunlight which varies in its incidence angle to provide a varied set of illumination conditions. The dataset only contains single images, and so to create image pairs for each satellite, images are compared against every other image in SPE3R+. A pair is kept only if the viewing direction, measured as the angle between the camera boresights in the target frame, changes by less than 30 ◦. Of the pairs used for SON, 85% have a total rotation below 

30 ◦ and are split into 7 bins of total rotation, each capped to balance the distribution; the remaining 15% have a larger rotation about the boresight, so that SON also sees large in-plane rotations. As a result, 14% of the test pairs exceed 

30 ◦ of total rotation. The pairs used to fine-tune LightGlue additionally include an equal number of wide-viewpoint pairs (viewing-direction change of at least 30 ◦, total rotation below 

120 ◦), which are needed for loop closure. For the satellites in the training set, 20% of the images per satellite are held out for validation, and then pairs are determined within the training and validation.

SHIRT— The SHIRT [8] dataset contains two rendezvous trajectories (ROE1 and ROE2) about the Tango spacecraft from the PRISMA mission [2], each with 2,371 images at a 5 s cadence. Every trajectory is imaged in two domains: synthetic and lightbox. The synthetic domain is much like the rendered trajectories described above, but includes Gaussian image noise. The lightbox domain contains images captured by a real camera within the TRON facility [65] which re-produces space-like illumination and sensor effects. Tango is not a part of SPE3R+, and so it is a previously unseen target much like the held-out test set. SHIRT does not provide image masks, which are needed by this pipeline to create the bounding box, and so these are re-generated as in SPE3R+. Since SHIRT does not induce significant attitude or orbit maneuvers, no range observability is available and so this dataset is only used to establish the domain gap in odometry. A full pipeline test with real images is beyond the scope of this work. 

Reference-truth propagation 

Reference truth servicer and target orbit and attitude trajecto-ries are propagated jointly using S3 [64, 66]. The specific orbit and dynamics models used are reported in Table 2. Target inertias are computed from voxelized shapes assuming uniform density, and scaled using the reported launch masses. In addition to the moment of inertia, a coarse 3D model is required in order to calculate the torques associated with drag and Solar Radiation Pressure (SRP). A two-box decomposi-tion (a central bus plus two symmetric thin solar panels) is fit to the calculated moment of inertia and bounding box, falling back to a single uniform cuboid if the fit fails. The bulk drag and SRP areas are instead measured as the mean silhouette area of the target mesh over random orientations because a box decomposition cannot represent panels shading the bus. 

Fine-tuning LightGlue 

The original authors of LightGlue [6] first train the model using SuperPoint features [4] on homographies sampled from 1 million images [67]. The model is then fine-tuned with the full MegaDepth dataset [61]. LightGlue is further fine-tuned on SPE3R+. The particular training parameters are provided in Table 1. LightGlue and SON use the same train, validation, and test image splits of SPE3R+, but different pair sets as per Table 1. 

Training SON 

To accelerate the training, SuperPoint and LightGlue are run as a pre-processing step on all image pairs in SPE3R+. Ta-ble 1 lists the baseline SON architecture and training settings. 

Monte Carlo analysis 

The full navigation architecture described in Figure 1 is evaluated on the 11 spacecraft reserved for the SPE3R+ test split. For each of these targets, 50 Monte Carlo (MC) trials are propagated for two orbit periods using the simulation parameters described in Table 2. The relative orbit for each trial is scaled to the standoff chosen for that target. Standoffs are set per target by hand, where being closer improves the range observability, but farther improves safety. The ROE of each trial are drawn from the distribution in Table 3 expressed as fractions of the target’s nominal range ρnom , shown in Table 7. The filter fixes the arbitrary target-frame attitude gauge by setting the initial relative attitude to identity and initializes the relative angular velocity to 0. The initial ROE estimate is obtained by perturbing the reference truth value according to the distribution provided in Table 3. Metrics for each MC trial are evaluated over the final 25% 

of each trajectory. Attitude errors are computed after the constant-rotation alignment described in Section 6. A trial is classified as converged according to the criterion reported in Table 3. A converged trial meets specification when its mean attitude error is below 15 ◦ and its total ROE root-mean-square error is below 0.1¯ ρ, where ¯ρ is the mean target–servicer separation. Across trials, estimation errors are reported using the 50th and 95th percentiles, denoted by p50 and p95 .Normalized Estimation Error Squared (NEES) is reported as the median of the per-trial evaluation window means. 

Inference analysis 

Inference time and memory are measured per pipeline stage, along with network sizes, on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin 64 GB.

Performance on SHIRT

## 9. C ONCLUSION 

This work addresses a key challenge in monocular spacecraft navigation, that of unknown target pose estimation. First, a new dataset is developed, SPE3R+, which expands upon the previous work and adds more information, in the form of more images at higher resolution and metric depth, and more targets. This dataset is then used to fine-tune a state-of-the-art feature matcher, LightGlue, which has in the past only been used for terrestrial targets, and greatly enhances its performance. Then, spacecraft odometry is enabled by the development of the novel Spacecraft Odometry Network (SON), which greatly outperforms the previous baseline even when it uses the fine-tuned LightGlue. This is coupled with a Multi-State Constraint Kalman Filter (MSCKF), which tracks the Relative Orbit Elements (ROE), relative attitude, and rel-ative angular velocity in a nominal state. This MSCKF uses the SON output as a measurement, as well as feature tracks that provide a local geometric constraint, and loop closure that uses SON again to estimate the odometry between the current image and a selected keyframe in the past to provide a global geometric constraint. These geometric constraints are required to avoid drift that is common with odometry measurements, since for attitude this is a form of dead-reckoning. For the ROE however, a known servicer boresight roll is used to add observability to the range to the target. This pipeline is validated through 550 Monte Carlo experi-ments on 11 spacecraft which range from small satellites to large communications spacecraft and are never seen by the networks during training. This represents the true test sce-nario for this pipeline, as the targets are fully unknown. One target, SMART-1, is reported for transparency but excluded from the summary statistics, because its dark exterior leaves too little image texture. For all targets, ROE errors remain at the meter level, but for those within 12 m of separation, sub-meter level errors are expected in more than 95% of cases. This represents a less than 10% ROE error when compared to the range to the target. The median attitude error on the other hand does not depend on the distance to the target, and 17 the attitude error is below 12 ◦ in 95% of cases. Ablation studies validate both the SON architecture and the MSCKF design, showing the additional performance achieved through each of the design choices. The front-end is additionally validated on hardware-in-the-loop images from the SHIRT dataset, where SON maintains a 4–6 times lower median translation unit direction error than the classical baseline. The whole pipeline takes 0.99 s per filter step on an NVIDIA AGX Orin, five times faster than the 5 s image cadence. To the authors’ knowledge, this represents the first time that monocular vision has been able to estimate both relative position and attitude for a previously unseen spacecraft at metric scale in simulation. In the future, the key assumptions outlined in Section 7 will be removed or their effects quantified. In particular, the assumption of the camera always pointing at the target and that a reference-truth box is provided can be removed through the use of a small object detection network and estimator-based pointing control. The sim-to-real gap must also be addressed, and it is likely that changes to the training of SON and the fine-tuning of LightGlue will be required to improve performance on real images. The compute of this overall pipeline would ideally also be reduced, and the overall design of LightGlue and SON must be scrutinized to reduce the network size while maintaining acceptable performance. Finally, inertia estimation will be pursued in future work to enable dynamical propagation without the need for so frequent measurements.

## BIOGRAPHY [

Pol Francesch Huc is a Ph.D. student in the Space Rendezvous Lab. He received a Bachelor of Science in Aeronautical and Astronautical Engineering from Pur-due University (2021), and a Master of Science in Aeronautics and Astronautics from Stanford University (2024). His research at Stanford focuses on vision-based navigation and characterization of resident space objects. Potential appli-cations include space debris removal and refueling of defunct geostationary satellites with unprecedented autonomy and safety measures. 

Simone D’Amico is Associate Profes-sor of Aeronautics and Astronautics at Stanford University, Science Fellow at the Hoover Institution, Co-Founder and Chief Science Officer at EraDrive Inc. He is the Director of the Space Ren-dezvous Laboratory, and Co-Director of the Center for AEroSpace Autonomy Research (CAESAR). He has 23+ years of experience in research and develop-ment of autonomous spacecraft and distributed space sys-tems. He developed and deployed the distributed Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) system of several formation-flying, swarming, rendezvous and proximity operations mis-sions. Currently, he is the institutional PI of four autonomous satellite swarms funded by NASA and NSF. Dr. D’Amico is Fellow of AIAA and AAS. He is on the Advisory Board of four space start-ups focusing on future applications of distributed space systems. He was the recipient of several awards, most recently the 2024 NASA Ames Honor Award and the 2025 SpaceNews Icon Award for the Starling mission, Best Paper Awards at AMOS (2025), IAF (2022), IEEE (2021), AIAA (2021), AAS (2019) conferences, and the M. Barry Carlton Award by IEEE (2020). He received the B.S. and M.S. degrees from Politecnico di Milano (2003) and the Ph.D. degree from Delft University of Technology (2010). 20
