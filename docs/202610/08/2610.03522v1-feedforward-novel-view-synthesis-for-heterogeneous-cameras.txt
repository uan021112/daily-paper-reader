## Abstract 

Feed-forward novel view synthesis has recently shown promising results from sparse posed images, but most existing methods assume that context and target views share a fixed camera family. This homogeneous-camera assumption breaks in practical multi-sensor systems, where perspective, fisheye, and panoramic cam-eras may coexist and where the target projection may be unseen during training. We study feed-forward NVS across heterogeneous central cameras and identify a key ambiguity introduced by tokenization: a visual token aggregates a projection-dependent bundle of pixel rays, while existing camera encodings mainly expose absolute rays or token-center relations. To address this, we combine token-center relative Camera Positional Encodings and proposed local raymaps, a token-level representation that explicitly describes the intra-patch ray distribution summarized by each token. We further propose projection-aware 2D RoPE, which replaces raw image-grid coordinates with ray-induced angular coordinates so that relative positional reasoning is aligned across camera projections. Together, these compo-nents treat diverse cameras as calibrated samplings of a shared ray space rather than separate visual domains. On ScanNet++ with heterogeneous-camera sys-tem, our method improves over camera-conditioned baselines under mixed-camera evaluation and demonstrates zero-shot generalization to panoramic views. 

## 1 Introduction 

Novel view synthesis (NVS) aims to synthesize unseen views from posed observations and remains a central topic in robotics, embodied perception, and world models. Recent feed-forward NVS methods have made substantial progress by predicting novel views from sparse posed images in a single forward pass [1–5]. Most of these methods, however, are designed, trained, and evaluated under a homogeneous-camera assumption: the input and target views share a fixed projection family, such as perspective [5] or panoramic cameras [6], but with mutually exclusive camera-specific architectures. This assumption excludes many practical capture and rendering systems. Multi-sensor platforms often combine perspective, fisheye, and panoramic cameras, while downstream applications may require rendering into a camera geometry different from the input sensors. In this setting, NVS is not only a problem of interpolating views in 3D space, but also one of reasoning about visual evidence across different samplings of ray space. A basic modeling question follows: can a single feed-forward model support heterogeneous cameras, even for camera types unseen during training? 

A purely data-driven approach would expose the model to different intrinsics, distortions, and cross-camera capture conditions. However, data-driven strategy is difficult to scale because camera variation

is continuous and real multi-camera data introduce non-geometric factors such as exposure, white balance, and calibration noise. More importantly, heterogeneous inference requires the model to exhibit few- or zero-shot generalizability to camera types broader than training data. A reusable geometric principle for unseen projections is preferable to exhaustive memorization. This motivates a local view of camera generalization. Although global projection functions can differ substantially, their local ray configurations recur across camera types. An unseen camera may define a new full-image projection, while many of its patch-level ray patterns remain geometrically similar to local patterns observed during training. We therefore formulate heterogeneous-camera generalization as learning over reusable local ray geometry, rather than over discrete camera categories or exhaustively sampled camera instances. Existing camera encodings, in contrast, operate at three other granularities: absolute pixel-level rays in Plücker raymaps [5], relative camera geometry between views [7, 8], and relative token-center rays [9, 10]. None of them describes the intra-patch ray bundle each token aggregates: two tokens with the same center ray can still cover very different ray distributions under different projections, leaving the patch-level ray geometry implicit, resulting in unnecessary learning burdens. Based on these observations, we introduce local raymaps for feed-forward NVS across heterogeneous central cameras, complementary to token-center relative Camera Positional Encodings [10]. A local raymap represents the patch-level ray configuration associated with each visual token, exposing the intra-patch ray geometry that is otherwise hidden by tokenization. We further propose projection-aware 2D RoPE [11] in ray-induced angular coordinates to align relative positional reasoning across camera projections. Under this derivation, perspective, fisheye, and equirectangular views are treated as calibrated samplings of a shared ray space rather than as separate visual domains. We evaluate the proposed formulation on ScanNet++ v2 [12] by constructing heterogeneous-camera NVS tasks from undistorted, fisheye, and panoramic images, including mixed-camera evaluation and zero-shot target-camera evaluation. In summary, our contributions are: • We introduce heterogeneous-camera feed-forward NVS, a setting where context and target views may use different central projection models, and define mixed-camera and zero-shot target-camera evaluation protocols on ScanNet++. • We apply a token-center relative Camera Positional Encodings and propose local raymaps, a token-level representation that exposes reusable intra-patch ray geometry without rely-ing on discrete camera categories, which exhibits zero-shot generalizability, supporting heterogeneous-camera NVS. • We introduce projection-aware 2D RoPE in ray-induced angular coordinates, aligning relative positional reasoning across camera projections. • We evaluate heterogeneous-camera NVS on ScanNet++ under mixed-camera and zero-shot target-camera protocols and achieve the state-of-the-art performance. 

## 2 Related Work 

Wide-FoV Cameras Across 3D Tasks. Wide-FoV cameras, including fisheye and panoramic cameras, are widely used in embodied perception and AR/VR because they observe much larger scene extents than standard perspective cameras. This has motivated projection-aware modeling across several tasks. For reconstruction, panoramic and universal-camera depth methods estimate dense geometry from equirectangular, fisheye, or arbitrary camera inputs [13–15], while feed-forward 3D reconstruction models extend point-map or point-cloud prediction to panoramic and fisheye images [16, 17]. For NVS and radiance-field rendering, omnidirectional and fisheye Gaussian-splatting methods adapt optimization and rasterization to non-perspective projection rays [18–21]. Wide-FoV images have also become a common interface for video generation and 3D world modeling through panorama diffusion and world-generation methods [22–28]. Together, these works show that wide-FoV images require geometry beyond ordinary image coordinates, motivating projection-aware novel view synthesis. 

Feed-forward Novel View Synthesis. Novel view synthesis was popularized in recent learning-based 3D vision through per-scene radiance field optimization, including NeRF [29] and its suc-2cessors [30, 31]. Feed-forward NVS [1–4, 32, 6, 5] instead synthesizes novel views, or predicts an intermediate 3D representation, directly from input images. Most methods are developed for standard perspective cameras: early generalizable radiance-field methods achieve neural rendering or predict 3D Gaussians using learned image features, while recent Transformer-based models reduce explicit 3D inductive bias and learn to directly synthesize target images, as in LVSM [5]. Recent work extends feed-forward NVS toward wide-FoV cameras, with PanSplat and Splatter-360 introducing specialized designs for sparse-view or feed-forward panorama synthesis [6, 33]. These camera-specific systems are effective, but both perspective and wide-FoV NVS methods usually assume a fixed camera family during training and evaluation. This leaves open a more general question: can a feed-forward NVS model reason when context and target views are produced by different projection models, possibly including target camera types unseen during training? We study this heterogeneous-camera setting by treating perspective, fisheye, and panoramic cameras as calibrated ray samplings of the same scene, and ask what information allows one model to share scene reasoning across them. 

Camera Positional Encodings. Reasoning across such cameras requires exposing camera geometry to the model. Transformers require positional information to make token interactions spatially meaningful [34–36]; for multi-view generation and reconstruction, this position is not only a 2D image coordinate but also the camera geometry that relates tokens across views. Existing methods therefore encode cameras either as token content or as attention positional encodings. LVSM provides absolute Plücker raymaps as token content, allowing context images and target cameras to be represented in a common 3D frame [5]. PRoPE formulates cameras as relative positional encodings, injecting camera pose and intrinsics into attention so that query-key interactions depend on projective geometry [7]. RayRoPE further studies ray-based positional encoding for multi-view attention [9], and UCPE expresses camera geometry through calibrated rays to support different projection models and lens distortions [10]. Despite these advances, camera positional encodings alone do not remove the ambiguity of token ray distributions, which is a key obstacle for reasoning across heterogeneous cameras. Attention-level encodings such as RayRoPE and UCPE describe geometric relations between token centers, but each patch token aggregates multiple pixel rays whose local distribution depends on camera projection. Our local raymaps are therefore complementary: they provide token-level evidence about the intra-patch ray bundle, while existing camera positional encodings provide cross-token or cross-view relations. 

## 3 Method 

We study feed-forward novel view synthesis for heterogeneous central cameras, where context and target views may come from pinhole, fisheye, or panoramic cameras. The key difficulty is not merely representing multiple camera types: calibrated rays already provide a common interface for token inputs and camera positional encodings, as reviewed in Sec. 3.1. Instead, heterogeneity changes the geometric meaning of Transformer tokens themselves. After patchification, each token summarizes a projection-dependent ray distribution, and the image lattice no longer provides a camera-invariant coordinate system for attention. To support heterogeneous NVS, we first extend the token content with local raymaps (Sec. 3.2), allowing the model to reuse local ray patterns rather than infer camera categories from data coverage. We then replace raw image coordinates in 2D RoPE with ray-induced angular coordinates (Sec. 3.3), aligning relative positional phases across projection models. These components allow a single decoder-only Transformer to operate across mixed and even unseen camera geometries. Fig. 1 summarizes how our encoding exposes projection-consistent token geometry compared with prior camera encodings.

3.2 Extending Camera Positional Encodings for Heterogeneous Cameras 

The prerequisites above describe two common ways to expose camera geometry to a Transformer: raymaps provide token content, and camera positional encodings modulate attention through token-center geometry. These mechanisms are often sufficient in a homogeneous-camera setting because the image lattice carries a stable geometric meaning. When all views follow the same projection family, a patch thus corresponds to a predictable local angular footprint, and a displacement on the token grid is also a stable proxy for a displacement in ray space. Heterogeneous cameras break this structural shortcut, creating a representation ambiguity for current Transformer-based methods, where tokenized visual content doesn’t share similar geometric meanings. Tokens covering the same image-space pixel range may aggregate different ray bundles under different camera projections: it can cover a narrow perspective patch, a stretched fisheye content, or a panorama footprint near a different latitude. This mismatch can hinder the model from generalizing spatial relationships consistently across camera types. To make this mismatch explicit, let Ωp denote the pixel set of patch p, and let ¯up denote its center pixel. The missing distinguishable variable is thus the local mapping from patch pixels to nearby rays. 

From raymaps to local raymaps. Explicit raymaps provide a unified interface for heterogeneous cameras by specifying where sampled pixels look, potentially indicating the missing local ray statistics. However, world-space Plücker raymaps [5] or camera-frame raymaps (CamRay [7]) inputs primarily encode absolute or center-aligned ray content, and therefore do not explicitly identify the local ray distributions hidden by patchification. This limitation may lead to the attention-sink behavior illustrated in Fig. 2, where center-ray inputs such as CamRay encourage tokens to first attend to some fixed model-learned tokens, even when their locations mismatch. Moreover, the absolute or center-aligned ray encodings introduce learning burden for models to infer patch-scale angular extent from either RGB appearance or absolute ray cues. ## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate the proposed method under heterogeneous-camera novel view synthesis, where accu-rate rendering requires both cross-view geometric reasoning and robustness to changes in camera projection. Our experiments study two complementary questions: whether the model improves reconstruction when camera types are mixed within the training distribution, and whether the learned representation transfers to a target camera type that is absent during training. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Dataset. We train and evaluate on ScanNet++ v2 [12], which captured indoor scenes with different cameras. We use fisheye (DSLR), undistorted, and panoramic images to construct heterogeneous-camera novel view synthesis pairs. For the zero-shot setting, panoramic views are excluded during training and used only at test time, allowing us to evaluate generalization to unseen, challenging camera geometry. We use the official training and validation splits, and use 224 ×336 resolution for perspective cameras and 256 ×512 for panorama cameras, at a similar scale to the training resolution used in PRoPE [7]. 

Baselines. We compare our method with representative camera-conditioning strategies under heterogeneous camera settings. For token-level camera embeddings, we evaluate (1) the Plücker raymaps used in LVSM [5] and (2) the CamRay raymaps introduced in PRoPE [7]. These baselines expose camera geometry directly to visual tokens, but differ in their coordinate dependence: Plücker raymaps encode rays in a global frame, whereas CamRay encodes local camera-frame ray directions. For attention-level camera positional encodings, we compare against PRoPE [7], which injects relative projective camera geometry into self-attention, and UCPE [10], which provides a ray-based relative camera encoding for diverse cameras. Our variants use UCPE as the camera positional encoding and differ in the token-level raymap and the coordinate domain of 2D RoPE. 

Training details. All methods are trained in the same codebase with identical hyperparameters. Following PRoPE [7], we adopt the lightweight LVSM [5] backbone with approximately 24.5M parameters to match a practical academic compute budget. 4.2 Evaluations In-distribution heterogeneous evaluation. We first evaluate NVS performance on fisheye and undistorted camera types. The evaluation protocols include two homogeneous-camera cases, “fisheye 

→ fisheye” and “undistorted → undistorted”, and a mixed-camera case where context and target views are randomly drawn from the available camera types. We also apply the random focal length similar to PRoPE [7]. We use the Splatt3R benchmark [40] for view selection, which measures the overlapping ratio between the context views and the target view. While this overlapping ratio is measured on the undistorted images, we use the same camera poses for fisheye images as proximity. Table 1 shows that relative ray-based camera positional encoding is an effective baseline for heteroge-neous NVS, and that the proposed local raymaps further improve performance. Our methods achieve the best performance across all three protocols. Replacing raw 2D RoPE with projection-aware RoPE gives consistent additional gains over Ours (Raw), especially for fisheye and mixed-camera evaluation, where the image lattice is less geometrically stable. These results support the role of local ray distributions and projection-aware positional coordinates as complementary components. 

Zero-shot generalizability. We next evaluate zero-shot generalization to panoramic target cameras, which are not used during training. We consider two protocols. In “Virtual Cams → Panorama”, 6 context images are fisheye views synthesized from panoramas with random virtual camera models. In “DSLR → Panorama”, context images come from real DSLR captures (fisheye) and targets are panoramas. To ensure enough coverage, we use 10 context images as inputs. The details of sampling method could be found in the supplementary materials. For all methods, we use the relative center-ray positional encoding (UCPE [10]). Since this setting involves cross-device appearance differences, we apply the same local histogram matching color correction module to all methods. Table 2 shows that local raymaps transfer better to unseen panorama geometry than global or center-only ray representations. CamRay fails in this zero-shot setting, suggesting that center-aligned ray directions alone are not sufficient to cover the unseen projection regime.

## 5 Conclusion 

We studied feed-forward NVS in a heterogeneous-camera setting, where different projection families are handled by a single model rather than separate camera-specific systems. Since camera variation is continuous and difficult to cover exhaustively with calibrated heterogeneous-camera data, we argue that generalization should come from reusable geometric units rather than memorized camera types. By exposing token-local ray distributions and ray-domain positional coordinates, our method improves mixed-camera synthesis and exhibits zero-shot generalization to unseen target cameras. 

Limitations. Heterogeneous-camera NVS also reflects a broader data challenge in the field. Our method relies on calibrated datasets, yet large-scale captures with diverse, accurately calibrated camera systems are difficult to collect and curate. While our results show that reusable geometric representations can reduce reliance on camera-specific supervision, richer heterogeneous-camera data would further improve robustness and help scale this setting to multi-sensor applications. 9References

## A Implementation Details 

A.1 Architecture 

We use the lightweight decoder-only LVSM backbone [5] for all methods. Each model has 6 Transformer layers, 16 attention heads, a token dimension of 768, and patch size 8, resulting in approximately 24.5M parameters. Context views and the target camera are converted into patch tokens and concatenated into one sequence before self-attention. 

Optimization. We follow the PRoPE training recipe [7]: all methods are trained for 80k iterations with a total batch size of 8 on two NVIDIA A100 GPUs. We use a cosine learning-rate scheduler with 2.5k warmup iterations. Unless otherwise specified in the experiment section, each training sample contains two context views and one target view, and all baselines use the same backbone, training schedule, image resolution, and view sampling protocol. 

A.2 DSLR-to-Panorama sampling and evaluation pair construction 

We evaluate zero-shot panorama prediction by constructing “DSLR →panorama” pairs in ScanNet++. For each scene, every panocam frame is treated as a target panorama p, and we select a fixed-size set of K DSLR context images, 

C(p) = {i1, . . . , i K }. (14) Our sampling objective is twofold: (1) context views should be spatially close to the target panorama, and (2) their viewing directions should cover as much of the target panorama’s spherical field of view as possible. 

Overall procedure. For each target panorama p, we construct C(p) with the following geometry-guided pipeline. First, we keep only DSLR frames whose camera centers are within a distance threshold of the panorama center. Second, we transform each remaining DSLR viewing direction into the local coordinate frame of the target panorama. Third, we discretize the panorama sphere into angular bins and compute which bins each DSLR image covers. Finally, we greedily add the DSLR image that contributes the largest amount of previously uncovered solid angle. The greedy selection stops once K context images have been selected. During evaluation, we only keep target panoramas whose final coverage exceeds a prescribed threshold, so every retained pair has exactly K inputs and sufficient angular support.
