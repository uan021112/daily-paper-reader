Wheel-legged robots operating in outdoor spaces shared with humans require accurate perception, socially compliant navigation,
future-scene reasoning, and scene reconstruction. However, existing datasets typically support these capabilities in isolation and
rarely provide dense spatial and behavior-level supervision on real wheel-legged platforms. We introduce LynxScenes, a large-
scale real-world dataset and benchmark collected with a LYNX M20 wheel-legged robot. It contains approximately 100K 30-s
navigation clips spanning 833.6 h and 3,294.7 km, with 3.0M annotated frames. The synchronized recordings include four fisheye
cameras, dual LiDARs, IMU, odometry, and robot states. Annotations comprise 20.82M oriented 3D boxes across 27 categories,
13.89M road-structure annotations, and 20 navigation instructions organized into three layers and aligned with continuous future
trajectories. LynxScenes supports four complementary tasks: instruction-conditioned trajectory prediction, BEV-based perception
of 3D objects and road structures, future scene prediction, and multi-view scene reconstruction. For trajectory prediction, we fur-
ther introduce a log-replay-based pseudo-closed-loop evaluator that measures safety, progress, and instruction compliance beyond
trajectory imitation. Extensive evaluations across representative navigation, perception, world-model, and reconstruction methods
reveal substantial remaining challenges in close-range outdoor robot navigation. The dataset and evaluation toolkit are planned for
public release.

Introduction
Wheel-legged robots are increasingly deployed in open outdoor environments shared with humans, including campuses, in-
tersections, and parks. Combining efficient wheeled locomotion with the ability to traverse uneven terrain, they are promising
platforms for delivery, inspection, and assistive applications. Unlike road vehicles operating within lane-structured environ-
ments or indoor robots navigating regular layouts, these robots move through loosely structured spaces from low, continuously
changing viewpoints and often interact closely with pedestrians, cyclists, and other dynamic agents. Reliable navigation
therefore requires not only safe motion but also socially compliant behavior in shared public spaces.
Such operation requires several tightly coupled capabilities: perceiving nearby agents and environmental structures, anticipat-
ing future scene evolution, generating continuous trajectories under platform-specific motion constraints, and reconstructing
representative scenes for replay and analysis. Yet these capabilities are typically studied using separate datasets and evaluation
protocols. Evaluating them on shared real-world observations enables consistent analysis under the same embodiment, envi-
ronment, and interaction distribution. In autonomous driving, datasets such as KITTI-360 (Liao et al., 2022), nuScenes (Caesar
et al., 2020), Waymo (Sun et al., 2020), and Argoverse 2 (Wilson et al., 2021) have enabled multiple autonomy problems to
be studied on common real-world data. However, their vehicle-mounted viewpoints, lane-centric topology, and interaction
regimes differ substantially from those of wheel-legged robots operating in pedestrian spaces.
Existing robot-navigation and language-conditioned navigation datasets provide only subsets of the required sensing and
supervision, as summarized in Table 1. VLN benchmarks provide instruction-following supervision (Anderson et al., 2018; Ku
et al., 2020; Krantz et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019), but their simulated or graph-based interfaces largely abstract away physical
robot dynamics and lack dense spatial annotations. Robot-centric datasets reduce this embodiment gap but remain fragmented
across perception, social navigation, traversability, localization, and mapping (Martin-Martin et al., 2021; Karnan et al., 2022;
Shah et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2025; Chaney et al., 2023; Jeong et al., 2024). Among closely related efforts, GrandTour (Frey
et al., 2026) provides rich multimodal measurements for legged-robot perception and state estimation but lacks behavior-

To address this gap, we introduce LynxScenes (Fig. 1), a large-scale real-world dataset and benchmark for socially compliant
outdoor wheel-legged robot navigation. Data are collected with a DEEP Robotics LYNX M20 operated by trained human
operators following a predefined social-compliance protocol. LynxScenes contains approximately 100K non-overlapping
30-s navigation clips spanning 833.6 h and 3,294.7 km, with 3.0M annotated frames. It provides synchronized multimodal
sensing and robot states, together with 20.82M oriented 3D boxes across 27 categories, 13.89M road-structure annotations, 20
behavior-level navigation instructions in three layers, and aligned continuous future trajectories.
Using the same sequences and training/evaluation splits, we benchmark four complementary tasks: instruction-conditioned
trajectory prediction, BEV-based perception of 3D objects and road structures, future scene prediction, and multi-view scene
reconstruction for visual replay and as a foundation for future simulation-based testing. For trajectory prediction, we further
introduce a log-replay-based pseudo-closed-loop evaluator inspired by NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024). Its Quadruped Navi-
gation Score (QNS) evaluates collision avoidance, walkable-area compliance, clearance, progress, and instruction adherence,
complementing conventional trajectory-displacement errors. We evaluate 10 VLN/VLA policies, 4 BEV perception models, 6
world models, and 5 reconstruction methods. The results reveal substantial room for improvement in close-range interactions
and complex outdoor environments. The dataset, standardized splits, and evaluation toolkit are planned for public release.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We introduce LynxScenes, a large-scale real-world multimodal dataset
collected on a wheel-legged robot under a standardized social-compliance protocol. It jointly provides socially compliant
expert demonstrations, dense 3D object and road-structure annotations, structured behavior-level instructions, and continuous
future trajectories. To the best of our knowledge, it is the largest publicly reported real-world wheel-legged robot navigation
dataset with expert trajectories and dense 3D scene annotations. (2) We establish a unified benchmark spanning instruction-
conditioned trajectory prediction, BEV perception, future scene prediction, and multi-view scene reconstruction, together with
a log-replay-based pseudo-closed-loop evaluator that measures safety, navigation quality, and instruction adherence. (3) We
systematically evaluate representative methods across all four tasks, establishing reproducible baselines, identifying persistent
challenges in close-range outdoor navigation, and providing standardized data splits and a public evaluation toolkit.

Related Work
2.1
Real-World Robot Navigation Datasets
Real-world robot datasets provide complementary supervision for localization, perception, and navigation. M2DGR (Yin et al.,
2021) and M3ED (Chaney et al., 2023) emphasize multimodal sensing and state estimation, while RECON (Shah et al., 2021),
SCAND (Karnan et al., 2022), GND (Liang et al., 2025), DiTer (Jeong et al., 2024), and GrandTour (Frey et al., 2026) pro-
vide various forms of trajectory, demonstration, traversability, or robot-state supervision. However, existing datasets typically
expose only subsets of the spatial and behavioral information required to jointly study perception and navigation. In con-
trast, LynxScenes combines synchronized multimodal observations, persistent 3D objects, explicit road structures, structured
navigation instructions, and continuous future trajectories within the same real-world wheel-legged robot dataset.
2.2
Instruction-Conditioned Navigation and Planning
Classical VLN benchmarks study instruction following primarily in simulated or image-based environments. R2R (Ander-
son et al., 2018), RxR (Ku et al., 2020), and VLN-CE (Krantz et al., 2020) focus on indoor navigation, whereas Touch-
down (Chen et al., 2019) considers outdoor street-view imagery. Recent benchmarks move closer to physical navigation:
VLN-PE (Wang et al., 2025) incorporates embodiment and realistic robot dynamics, while CoT-VLNBench (Zhao et al.,
2026) provides real quadruped navigation data with language instructions, 3D object annotations, and continuous trajectories.
However, CoT-VLNBench does not provide explicit road-structure geometry for trajectory-level spatial-safety evaluation. Our
benchmark further aligns continuous future trajectories with a three-layer instruction taxonomy together with explicit object
and road-structure annotations, enabling instruction-conditioned trajectory prediction and trajectory-level spatial-safety evalu-
ation. Inspired by replay-based planning evaluation such as NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024), we additionally evaluate predicted
trajectories using safety, progress, and instruction-compliance criteria rather than trajectory similarity alone.
2.3
BEV Perception and Spatial Scene Understanding
Autonomous-driving datasets such as nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020), Waymo (Sun et al., 2020), and Argoverse 2 (Wilson
et al., 2021) have established large-scale benchmarks for 3D and BEV perception. Methods including BEVFormer (Li et al.,
2022), BEVFusion (Liu et al., 2023), and MapTRv2 (Liao et al., 2023) further model surrounding objects and road geometry
in BEV. These benchmarks are primarily designed around vehicle-scale traffic and driving-oriented map structures, whereas
wheel-legged robots operate close to pedestrians, curbs, sidewalks, and crosswalks. LynxScenes therefore jointly evaluates
nearby 3D objects and navigation-relevant road structures in a robot-centric BEV frame, providing spatial supervision that is
directly aligned with the local constraints governing safe robot motion.

2.4
World Models and Neural Scene Reconstruction
Generative world models such as GAIA-1 (Hu et al., 2023), Navigation World Models (Bar et al., 2025), and Cos-
mos (NVIDIA, 2025; 2026) predict future visual observations from current scenes and motion or action conditions. In
parallel, 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) and recent feed-forward reconstruction methods enable efficient scene
reconstruction and novel-view synthesis. Most existing evaluation is conducted in driving or generic visual settings rather
than low-viewpoint outdoor robot navigation. LynxScenes evaluates both future scene prediction and multi-view scene re-
construction on the same real-world robot data, providing complementary benchmarks for temporal scene evolution and
viewpoint-dependent scene replay. This complementary design supports both predictive reasoning about future observations
and scene-level analysis for outdoor robot navigation.

Dataset
LynxScenes is collected with a physical wheel-legged robot and organized into approximately 100K non-overlapping navi-
gation clips of 30 s each, amounting to 3.0M annotated frames, 833.6 h of navigation, and 3,294.7 km of traveled distance.
Each clip contains synchronized surround-view images, LiDAR point clouds, and robot states, while each annotated frame
provides oriented 3D object boxes, road-structure annotations, structured navigation instructions, and a continuous future tra-
jectory. The same recordings support instruction-conditioned trajectory prediction, BEV-based perception of 3D objects and
road structures, future scene prediction, and multi-view scene reconstruction, as defined in Sec. 4.
3.1
Platform and Sensor Setup
The data-collection platform is a DEEPRobotics LYNX M20 wheel-legged quadruped (Fig. 2). It carries four fisheye cameras
facing front, rear, left, and right, each recording 1920 × 1536 images at 10 Hz, and two 96-channel RoboSense Airy LiDARs
mounted at the front and rear. Together, the LiDARs provide 360◦horizontal and 90◦vertical coverage to a range of 60 m at
10 Hz.
The platform additionally records IMU measurements, 6-DoF LiDAR-inertial odometry (LIO), and robot states. The robot-
state stream provides the longitudinal velocity vx, lateral velocity vy, and yaw rate ωz, and is used to construct the trajectory
4


supervision described in Sec. 3.3. All geometric quantities are represented in the robot-centric Front–Left–Up (FLU) coordi-
nate system, with x forward, y left, and z up. The complete sensor specifications are provided in Appendix B.2.
3.2
Data Collection and Synchronization
All data are collected through human teleoperation of the physical robot in real outdoor environments, including sidewalks,
streets, intersections, campuses, parks, narrow passages, slopes, open grounds, and rural roads. The recordings cover both
structured and unstructured terrain, static obstacles, and frequent interactions with pedestrians, riders, and vehicles.
Annotated frames are sampled at 1 Hz from the synchronized multimodal recordings. Camera–LiDAR calibration, temporal
alignment, and stream synchronization details are provided in Appendix B.2. Data collection is performed by trained opera-
tors following predefined socially compliant navigation guidelines; the collection protocol and quality-control procedure are
detailed in Appendix B.5.
3.3
Trajectory Construction
Reference future trajectories are constructed from the robot-state stream. Given the body-frame longitudinal velocity vx,
lateral velocity vy, and yaw rate ωz, the robot motion is temporally integrated and transformed into the robot coordinate frame
at the current anchor timestamp. The resulting trajectory is resampled to 10 Hz, providing a sequence of future planar poses
associated with each annotated frame. The complete integration formulation is given in Appendix B.3.
3.4
Spatial and Navigation Annotations
Beyond trajectory supervision, the benchmark provides rich spatial and semantic annotations to support multiple key tasks in
wheel-legged robot navigation. These annotations capture surrounding objects, road structures, and navigation intent, enabling
both perception- and planning-oriented evaluation. The benchmark provides 27 object categories, four road-structure classes,
and 20 navigation instructions organized into three layers. The complete annotation taxonomy is provided in Appendix B.4.
3D objects. Every object around the robot is annotated with an oriented 3D bounding box, one of 27 semantic categories, a
persistent instance identity, a dynamic state, and a velocity. Identities are maintained across frames and temporally consecutive
clips, which supports long-term association and per-object collision accounting.
In total the dataset contains 20.82M boxes, 6.94 per annotated frame on average.
Road structure. We annotate four road-structure classes, represented as polylines or closed crosswalk contours.
These annotations encode the accessible area of the robot and are the basis of the Walkable-Area Compliance in Sec. 4. The
dataset contains 13.89M road-structure annotations, 4.63 per frame on average.
Navigation instructions. Each frame is tagged with structured instructions from three independent layers. Primary navigation
describes the dominant route intent and covers the entire timeline, Special motion marks robot-specific maneuvers and Obstacle
interaction marks how the robot responds to an obstacle.
Instructions within a layer are mutually exclusive, while instructions from different layers may overlap in time. Unlike joystick
or velocity commands, these labels describe behavior-level intent, and their alignment with the future trajectory is what makes
instruction-conditioned prediction and behavior-wise evaluation possible.
Annotation procedure and quality control. All annotations and recorded expert demonstrations undergo manual quality
control for annotation completeness, consistency and compliance with the predefined socially compliant navigation guidelines.
Detailed review and filtering procedures are provided in Appendix B.5.
3.5
Dataset Statistics and Splits
As summarized in Fig. 3, LynxScenes covers a wide range of real-world outdoor navigation conditions, including diverse scene
types, environmental and illumination conditions, road structures, and human–robot interaction scenarios. Beyond environ-
mental diversity, the dataset spans substantial variation in object categories, spatial complexity, semantic navigation behaviors,
and ego-motion patterns, ranging from routine path following to special-motion and obstacle-interaction instructions. Such
broad coverage supports systematic evaluation across heterogeneous navigation conditions and interaction complexities. The
30-s clip is used as the atomic split unit, with clips divided 8:1:1 into training, validation, and test sets while approximately
preserving the navigation-instruction distribution. To avoid temporal and scene leakage, clips originating from the same con-
tinuous collection session are assigned to the same split.

Benchmark Tasks and Metrics
To validate the capability of our benchmark to support key wheel-legged robot navigation problems, we establish four rep-
resentative benchmark tasks. These tasks span navigation prediction, spatial perception, future scene modeling, and scene
reconstruction in real-world outdoor environments.
4.1
Instruction-Conditioned Trajectory Prediction
We formulate the primary benchmark task as instruction-conditioned continuous trajectory prediction. For each anchor t, a
model receives four historical timestamps {−3, −2, −1, 0} s, each with synchronized front, rear, left, and right fisheye images
(16 images total), the current motion state st = [vx, vy, ωz], and the three-layer semantic instruction. It predicts the complete
future trajectory ˆTt ∈R10×3 over 1 s at 10 Hz, where each pose is [xforward, yleft, ψ] in the anchor Body-FLU frame.
We report imitation accuracy using ADE, FDE, AHE, FHE, and complement it with a pseudo-closed-loop replay inspired
by NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024). Annotated agents are replayed against the predicted ego trajectory and six interpretable
sub-scores measure No Collision (NC), Walkable-Area Compliance (WAC), Time-to-Collision (TTC), Clearance (CL), Ego
Progress (EP), and Instruction Compliance (IC). They are combined as
QNS = NC × WAC × wEPEP + wTTCTTC + wCLCL + wICIC

4.2
BEV-Based Perception of 3D Objects and Road Structures
To evaluate spatial understanding for wheel-legged robot navigation, we consider BEV-based perception of surrounding 3D
objects and navigation-relevant road structures.
For 3D object detection, models predict confidence-scored oriented boxes in the current robot frame and are evaluated on Car
and Pedestrian using center-distance-based mAP and HDS (Caesar et al., 2020). For road-structure detection, Road Curb,
Sidewalk Curb, and Road–Sidewalk Dividing Line form an ordinary-line class, while Crosswalk is represented as a closed-
contour class and evaluated using Chamfer-distance-based AP. Detailed matching rules, thresholds, and metric definitions are
provided in Appendix E.
4.3
Future Scene Prediction
To evaluate future scene prediction, we assess whether models can generate the future egocentric visual evolution of outdoor
robot navigation scenes. Given an initial rectified front-view image I0, models generate a future RGB video and, when
supported, additionally receive future motion conditions; text prompts are used when required by the native model interface.
We evaluate complementary aspects of generation quality, including perceptual quality, temporal quality, appearance consis-
tency, and distributional realism, using Q-Align-I/V (Wu et al., 2024), CLIP-IQA+ (Wang et al., 2023), LAION-Aes (LAION-
AI, n.d.), DOVER-T (Wu et al., 2023), VBench temporal metrics (Huang et al., 2024), CLIP-I (Cherti et al., 2023), and
FVD (Unterthiner et al., 2018). Model details, temporal normalization, and metric definitions are provided in Appendix F.

Multi-View Scene Reconstruction
To evaluate multi-view scene reconstruction, models reconstruct the observed environment from multiple context views and
render RGB images at held-out viewpoints.
We benchmark representative Gaussian-splatting reconstruction methods and evaluate novel-view rendering using comple-
mentary pixel-, structural-, and perceptual-quality metrics. Detailed context-view settings, evaluation protocols, and metric
definitions are provided in Appendix G.

Experiments
5.1
Trajectory-Prediction Baselines and Results
Baselines. We evaluate five VLN models: InternNav (Wei et al., 2026), NaVILA (Cheng et al., 2025), OmniNav (Xue et al.,
2026), StreamVLN (Wei et al., 2025), and SocialNav (Qwen3-VL-4B) (Chen et al., 2026); and five VLA models: LingBot-
VLA-v2 (Wu et al., 2026), DepthVLA (Yuan et al., 2026), OpenPI π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025), OpenVLA (Kim
et al., 2025), and Isaac-GR00T-N1.7 (NVIDIA et al., 2025). All models are evaluated under the common trajectory-prediction
protocol; model-specific adaptations are detailed in Appendix D.
Results. Table 2 reports the trajectory-prediction results. Among learned policies with pseudo-closed-loop evaluation, NaV-
ILA achieves the highest QNS of 0.843, followed by StreamVLN at 0.833. For imitation accuracy, Isaac-GR00T-N1.7 obtains
the lowest ADE (0.027), while NaVILA achieves the lowest FDE, AHE, and FHE. The constant-velocity and stationary ref-
erences further show that collision avoidance alone is insufficient, as useful navigation also requires progress and instruction
compliance.
5.2
BEV-Perception Baselines and Results
Baselines. We evaluate BEVFormer-Tiny (Li et al., 2022), MapTRv2 (Liao et al., 2023), BEVFusion (Liu et al., 2023), and
SparseDrive (Sun et al., 2025). Their sensor inputs, training configurations, and checkpoint-selection protocols are detailed in
Appendix E.
Results. Table 3 shows that SparseDrive achieves the highest road-structure mAP, whereas BEVFusion obtains the highest
box mAP and HDS. The latter result should be interpreted together with BEVFusion’s additional LiDAR input. Additional
qualitative results are provided in Appendix E.
5.3

Conclusion
We present LynxScenes, a large-scale real-world benchmark for outdoor wheel-legged robot autonomy that combines synchro-
nized multimodal sensing, dense spatial annotations, behavior-level navigation instructions, and continuous future trajectories.
Built on the same real-world robot data, the benchmark supports four complementary tasks: instruction-conditioned trajectory
prediction, BEV-based perception of 3D objects and road structures, future scene prediction, and multi-view scene recon-
struction. For trajectory prediction, we further introduce a log-replay-based pseudo-closed-loop evaluator that complements
imitation metrics with safety, progress, walkable-area compliance, and instruction adherence.
Across representative VLN/VLA policies, BEV perception models, world models, and reconstruction methods, our experi-
ments reveal substantial remaining challenges in close-range interaction, spatial understanding, future-scene modeling, and
viewpoint-dependent reconstruction. These results suggest that outdoor wheel-legged robot autonomy remains difficult even
when perception, navigation, prediction, and reconstruction are evaluated on the same embodiment and scene distribution.
We hope LynxScenes provides a common real-world foundation for studying these capabilities together and supports future
progress toward more integrated and socially compliant outdoor robot autonomy.

Additional Related Work
A.1
Real-World Multimodal Robot Datasets
Existing real-world robot datasets provide complementary forms of multimodal sensing and navigation supervision.
M2DGR (Yin et al., 2021) provides synchronized fisheye cameras, LiDAR, inertial sensing, GNSS, and odometry for multi-
sensor SLAM, while M3ED (Chaney et al., 2023) extends multimodal sensing to multiple robotic platforms and challenging
environments. GrandTour (Frey et al., 2026) further provides synchronized multimodal observations, proprioception, and
high-precision trajectories for legged-robot perception and state estimation.
Several datasets focus more directly on navigation. RECON (Shah et al., 2021) provides real-robot trajectories for goal-
conditioned open-world navigation, while SCAND (Karnan et al., 2022) collects human-teleoperated demonstrations for
socially compliant navigation. GND (Liang et al., 2025) introduces semantic traversability supervision for outdoor global
navigation, and DiTer (Jeong et al., 2024) combines multimodal quadruped observations with reference trajectories and global
maps across diverse outdoor terrains.
These datasets emphasize different subsets of sensing, localization, trajectory, or traversability supervision. LynxScenes in-
stead integrates temporally aligned multimodal sensing, persistent 3D objects, road-structure geometry, structured navigation
instructions, and future trajectories in a single wheel-legged robot dataset, allowing the same recordings to support multiple
perception, navigation, prediction, and reconstruction tasks.
A.2
Navigation Policies and Planning Evaluation
Recent navigation models increasingly map multimodal observations and semantic instructions directly to continuous
robot behavior. NaVILA (Cheng et al., 2025) adapts vision-language-action modeling to legged robot navigation, while
StreamVLN (Wei et al., 2025) introduces streaming visual-language reasoning for continuous navigation. InternNav (Wei
et al., 2026) further explores generalizable vision-language navigation with a dual-system architecture.
General-purpose VLA models provide another relevant policy family. OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2025) learns continuous robot
actions from vision-language inputs, while π0.5 (Physical Intelligence et al., 2025) uses a flow-matching action expert to
generate continuous action chunks. These approaches are originally developed under different embodiments, action spaces,
and task definitions. In our benchmark, they are adapted to a common observation, instruction, robot-state, and continuous-
trajectory interface, enabling direct comparison under the same real-world navigation data.
Planning evaluation also increasingly extends beyond open-loop trajectory displacement. NAVSIM (Dauner et al., 2024)
evaluates predicted driving trajectories through non-reactive scene replay and combines multiple safety- and progress-related
criteria. Our pseudo-closed-loop evaluator follows this general replay-based principle but adapts it to wheel-legged robot nav-
igation, where nearby pedestrians and riders, curb and sidewalk boundaries, and behavior-level instructions directly constrain
acceptable trajectories.
A.3
BEV Perception for Driving and Robot Navigation
Large-scale autonomous-driving datasets including nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020), Waymo Open Dataset (Sun et al., 2020),
and Argoverse 2 (Wilson et al., 2021) have established widely used benchmarks for 3D object detection and spatial scene
understanding.
BEV-based perception methods provide a natural representation for combining surrounding observations and geometric rea-
soning. BEVFormer (Li et al., 2022) constructs spatiotemporal BEV representations from multi-camera images. BEVFu-
sion (Liu et al., 2023) combines camera and LiDAR information in a unified BEV representation, while MapTRv2 (Liao et al.,
2023) represents road and map elements using vectorized geometric predictions.
These approaches motivate the perception baselines used in our benchmark, but the spatial constraints differ from conventional
autonomous driving. Vehicle-oriented benchmarks primarily emphasize traffic participants, lanes, and HD-map elements,
whereas outdoor wheel-legged robots frequently operate near pedestrians, riders, curbs, sidewalk boundaries, road–sidewalk
dividing lines, and crosswalks. Our BEV task therefore evaluates both nearby 3D objects and navigation-relevant road struc-
tures in the robot-centric coordinate frame.
A.4
World Models for Future Scene Prediction
World models predict how future observations evolve under actions, motion conditions, or other control signals. GAIA-1 (Hu
et al., 2023) demonstrates generative world modeling for autonomous driving, while Navigation World Models (Bar et al.,
2025) generate future egocentric observations conditioned on navigation actions. Cosmos (NVIDIA, 2025) further explores
large-scale world foundation models for physical-AI applications.
13


These works demonstrate the potential of generative prediction for reasoning about future scene evolution, but are commonly
evaluated in autonomous-driving, generic video-generation, or other embodiment settings. Outdoor wheel-legged robot navi-
gation introduces a distinct visual regime, including low camera height, close-range pedestrians and riders, vegetation, curbs,
and frequent viewpoint changes. Our benchmark evaluates whether existing world models can generate future robot-centric
observations under these conditions using perceptual, temporal, conditioning-consistency, and distributional metrics.
A.5
Neural Scene Reconstruction and Scenario Replay
3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) provides an efficient explicit scene representation for novel-view synthesis. Recent
methods extend Gaussian-based reconstruction to sparse, unposed, and online observations. MVSplat (Chen et al., 2024)
uses posed multi-view cost volumes, FreeSplatter (Xu et al., 2025) targets sparse-view pose-free reconstruction, and AnyS-
plat (Jiang et al., 2025) reconstructs Gaussian scene representations from unconstrained views. ReCoSplat (Cheng et al.,
2026) performs online reconstruction through render-and-compare, while YoNoSplat (Ye et al., 2026) supports both posed and
unposed feed-forward reconstruction.
Beyond novel-view synthesis, reconstructed Gaussian scenes have also been explored for scenario replay and simulation.
HUGSIM (Zhou et al., 2024), for example, integrates Gaussian-splatting-based scene reconstruction into a photorealistic
closed-loop driving simulator. Our current reconstruction benchmark is more limited in scope: it evaluates observation-
grounded novel-view rendering on real robot navigation sequences rather than claiming full closed-loop simulation capability.
Together with the world-model task, it provides complementary evaluation of future visual prediction and explicit scene
reconstruction.

B.5
Data Collection and Quality Control
Socially compliant data collection. Data collection is performed by trained human operators following predefined socially
compliant navigation guidelines. The protocol covers common interactions in shared outdoor spaces, including yielding to
pedestrians, maintaining safe clearance, passing obstacles on an appropriate side, and negotiating encounters with pedestrians,
riders, and vehicles. These guidelines are used to improve the behavioral consistency and safety of the recorded expert
trajectories.
Annotation quality control. All annotations are created manually and pass through a dedicated review loop. Reviewers
inspect annotated frames for completeness and consistency, and frames containing missing, misaligned, or inconsistent labels
are returned for correction before acceptance.
Demonstration quality control. Recorded expert demonstrations additionally undergo behavior-level review. Human–robot
interaction segments are inspected for consistency with the predefined socially compliant navigation guidelines. Segments
containing unsafe, socially inappropriate, or inconsistent operator behavior are manually identified and excluded from the
benchmark.
C
Navigation Evaluation Details
Trajectory imitation metrics. Given predicted and reference trajectories, ADE and FDE measure the mean and final Eu-
clidean position errors, respectively. AHE and FHE measure the corresponding mean and final absolute heading errors, with
angular differences wrapped to [−π, π).
Pseudo-closed-loop replay. The replay uses the predicted pose ˆpk = (ˆxk, ˆyk, ˆψk) at 0.1-s intervals and a rectangular ego
footprint. Annotated objects are replayed as BEV rectangles by interpolating box centers and yaw between 1-Hz keyframes
using persistent instance identities; an object present in only one neighboring keyframe is held fixed. Road-structure polylines
from the anchor keyframe define the local walkable-area constraints. The replay is non-reactive.
• No Collision (NC): 0 for an at-fault collision with a person, rider, vehicle, animal, or robot; 0.5 for an inanimate object; and
1 otherwise. A collision is not at fault when the ego is stationary and another object moves into it.
• Walkable-Area Compliance (WAC): 0 if the ego footprint crosses a curb or a road–sidewalk dividing line outside a cross-
walk, unless the recorded trajectory crosses the same boundary within the horizon; 1 otherwise.
• Time-to-Collision (TTC): 0 if 1-s constant-velocity propagation from any predicted step intersects a replayed agent; 1
otherwise.
• Clearance (CL): min(1, dmin/0.5 m), where dmin is the minimum distance to any person, rider, or animal.
16


• Ego Progress (EP): predicted progress projected onto the recorded path, normalized by the recorded-path length and clipped
to [0, 1]; set to 1 when the recorded motion is below 0.2 m.
• Instruction Compliance (IC): checks instruction-specific trajectory geometry: at least 20◦signed heading change for
branches, U-turns, and in-place rotation; signed dominant lateral velocity for lateral or lane moves; negative longitudinal
velocity for reversing; displacement below 0.2 m for stationary or waiting; passage on the instructed side for obstacle
avoidance; and continued motion when the operator moved for path following or continuing straight.

Trajectory-Prediction Baseline Adaptation Details
All trajectory-prediction experiments use the 8:1:1 clip-level train/validation/test split described in Sec. 3.5. Each model
receives the same observation history, four-camera visual input, current robot state, semantic navigation instruction, and future
trajectory target, and predicts the complete 10 × 3 Body-FLU trajectory over the future 1 s. Ground-truth 3D object and
road-structure annotations are used only by the evaluator and are not provided as test-time model inputs.
InternNav and StreamVLN use four-layer Transformer trajectory decoders with Flow Matching to jointly generate the com-
plete trajectory. NaVILA and OmniNav use two-layer Transformer decoders for parallel regression of all 10 × 3 trajectory
variables. LingBot-VLA-v2 and OpenPI π0.5 retain their native Flow-Matching action experts; DepthVLA retains its Flow-
Matching action head adapted to the 10×3 output; and OpenVLA uses a two-layer Transformer decoder for parallel continuous
trajectory regression.
SocialNav builds on Qwen3-VL-4B and augments its visual tokens with camera-identity, temporal-slot, and continuous times-
tamp embeddings. A four-layer ActionFormer and motion-conditioned Flow-Matching head then generate the complete tra-
jectory.
Isaac-GR00T-N1.7 replaces the original manipulation diffusion head with a two-layer, eight-head Transformer trajectory de-
coder conditioned on learned horizon queries and the current motion state. The decoder predicts normalized residuals over a
constant-twist prior for all 10 × 3 trajectory variables.
All future trajectory variables are predicted jointly rather than waypoint-wise autoregressively. Representative trajectory pre-
dictions are shown in Fig. 7.

F.5
World-Model Metric Definitions
We evaluate four complementary aspects of generated-video quality.
Perceptual quality. Q-Align-I (Wu et al., 2024) evaluates frame-level perceptual quality. CLIP-IQA+ (Wang et al., 2023)
provides a semantic-aware no-reference image-quality score, while LAION-Aes (LAION-AI, n.d.) measures visual aesthetic
quality.
Temporal quality. Q-Align-V (Wu et al., 2024) evaluates overall video quality and temporal coherence. DOVER-T (Wu et al.,
2023) measures technical video quality, while Motion Smoothness and Dynamic Degree (Huang et al., 2024) characterize
temporal continuity and the amount of temporal variation.
Appearance consistency. CLIP-I measures appearance consistency between generated frames and the initial observation. For
each sampled generated frame, cosine similarity with the first frame of the corresponding real video is computed using an
OpenCLIP ViT-B/32 image encoder (Cherti et al., 2023) and multiplied by 100.
Distributional realism. Fr´echet Video Distance (FVD) (Unterthiner et al., 2018) measures the distance between generated
and real video distributions using I3D video features. FVD is computed jointly over all real and generated videos for each
evaluated model; lower values indicate a generated distribution closer to the real-world robot observations.

It does not directly evaluate whether the generated motion exactly follows the intended future motion condition. Explicit
motion-conditioning accuracy therefore remains an important extension of the benchmark.

Baseline configurations. All five methods are evaluated using pretrained checkpoints without additional fine-tuning. ReCoS-

Evaluation protocol. All methods are evaluated on rectified front-camera images from 200 data test clips. Rendering
quality is measured using 8-bit RGB images at 224 × 224 resolution. ReCoSplat, YoNoSplat, and AnySplat use 8–16 context
views and are evaluated on 2,495 held-out views in total. FreeSplatter-S and MVSplat use two context views and are evaluated
on 3,162 held-out views. Metric values are first averaged over held-out views within each clip and then equally averaged
across clips.
Because the methods differ in context-view count, pose requirements, native reconstruction interfaces, and held-out-view
counts, the reported results should be interpreted as system-level benchmark performance rather than a controlled architecture-
only comparison.
Metric definitions. PSNR measures pixel-level reconstruction fidelity. SSIM (Wang et al., 2004) and MS-SSIM (Wang et al.,
2003) measure local and multi-scale structural similarity, respectively. LPIPS-VGG (Zhang et al., 2018) measures learned
perceptual distance, while DISTS (Ding et al., 2020) measures deep-feature structure and texture differences. FSIMc (Zhang
et al., 2011) combines phase, gradient, and color similarity, whereas GMSD (Xue et al., 2014) measures spatial variation
in gradient-magnitude similarity. Together, these metrics cover complementary pixel-, structural-, and perceptual-quality
dimensions.
Benchmark scope. The reconstruction task evaluates observation-grounded novel-view rendering under viewpoint changes.
The reported metrics do not directly measure geometric reconstruction accuracy, future scene dynamics, or closed-loop navi-
gation safety.
