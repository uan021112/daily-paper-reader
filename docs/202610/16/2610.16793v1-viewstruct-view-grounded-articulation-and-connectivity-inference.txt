# Abstract 

Interacting with unfamiliar objects requires understanding how their parts connect and move. We intro-duce ViewStruct, a framework for jointly predicting connectivity and articulation in objects and scenes. Given an RGB image with aligned part segmentation and depth, optionally supplemented by images or video, ViewStruct predicts rigid-body groupings, attachments, and joint parameters. Its view-grounded representation links predictions to observed regions and expresses joint geometry in a gravity-aligned, view-relative frame, without requiring category-specific canonical orientations. A pretrained vision– language model is adapted to predict structure in seconds, without requiring observed articulation mo-tion or per-instance optimisation. Experiments demonstrate improved structural prediction over evalu-ated baselines and transfer to unseen categories, asset sources, and real-world human-interaction videos, despite task-specific training exclusively on synthetic data. Simulated manipulation experiments fur-ther demonstrate the utility of predicted joints for generating robot motion trajectories. Project page: 

https://bianwenjing.github.io/viewstruct/ 

# 1 Introduction 

Robots interacting with unfamiliar environments must understand which parts move together and which motions their connections permit. These constraints inform manipulation planning and the construction of interactive representations from visual observations. Geometry and segmentation alone do not establish them: a drawer front, handle, and box 

1arXiv:2610.16793v1 [cs.CV] 12 Oct 2026 may occupy separate regions yet move as one rigid body, while adjacent cabinet doors may move independently. In scenes, this reasoning must also distinguish mechanical attachments from proximity between unrelated objects. Practical structural understanding requires flexible inputs and efficient inference. A single image provides appearance and spatial cues but may leave motion constraints ambiguous. Additional views reveal more structure, while video provides evidence of shared and relative motion. A useful predictor should accommodate these observations with-out requiring every movable part to be seen in action, and produce estimates quickly enough to inform subsequent interaction. Existing methods address these settings through different routes. OPD (Jiang et al., 2022a) and OPDMulti (Sun et al., 2024) estimate openable-part motion from single-view images. SINGAPO (Liu et al., 2025) and PhysX-Anything (Cao et al., 2025) generate articulated assets from images, while Particulate (Li et al., 2026a) infers articulation from ge-ometry. Interaction-based methods such as ArtiPoint (Werby et al., 2025) and MoMa-SG (Buechner et al., 2026) recover articulation from observed motion. These methods typically target a particular observation setting, such as single-image prediction or estimation from observed motion. We instead use one predictor and output representation across static images and interaction sequences, allowing additional observations to refine the estimate without making motion a prerequisite. We introduce ViewStruct , a VLM-based framework taking a reference RGB image with aligned part segmentation and depth, optionally supplemented by images or video. The reference observation determines the part identities and output coordinate frame. Input regions need not correspond to rigid bodies: the model predicts which regions move together and how these bodies attach. By adapting a pretrained VLM, ViewStruct combines knowledge of part functions and plausible mechanical relationships with observation-specific evidence, predicting structure in seconds without per-instance optimisation or an external refinement loop. Our view-grounded representation associates predictions with observed regions through persistent part identifiers. Joint geometry is expressed in a gravity-aligned, view-relative frame, avoiding category-specific canonical orientations while stabilising the vertical direction across viewpoints. Revolute pivots are decoded from image locations and relative depth, providing explicit image correspondence. This representation applies consistently to individual objects and multi-object scenes. Experiments demonstrate improved connectivity and articulation prediction over evaluated baselines, including trans-fer to held-out categories and unseen asset sources. Despite task-specific training exclusively on synthetic data, View-Struct also transfers to real-world human-interaction videos, where motion evidence improves joint classification and localisation. We further use predicted joints to generate manipulation trajectories in simulation, testing them against reference object mechanisms under assisted contact. We summarise our contributions as follows: First , we formulate joint connectivity and articulation inference using a shared view-grounded representation for objects and scenes. Second , we develop ViewStruct, supporting single-image, multi-image, and video inputs with inference in seconds and no requirement for observed articulation motion. 

Third , we demonstrate improved structural prediction, generalisation across categories and observation domains, and utility for robotic manipulation. 

# 2 Related Work 

Articulation inference and generation. Prior work estimates articulation from static observations (Hu et al., 2017; Zeng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020), including single-view openable-part detection in objects and scenes (Jiang et al., 2022a; Sun et al., 2024). Other methods construct articulated assets from graphs (Liu et al., 2024), images (Chen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025; 2026), or meshes (Li et al., 2026a;b), or infer articulation through synthesised motion (Lee et al., 2026). ViewStruct jointly predicts mechanical connectivity and articulation over supplied regions, using a shared view-grounded representation for objects and scenes without category-specific canonical orientations. 

Articulation from motion and interaction. Motion-based approaches recover articulation from multiple observed states (Jiang et al., 2022b; Liu et al., 2023; Weng et al., 2024), including through active interaction in scenes (Hsu et al., 2023). ArtiPoint (Werby et al., 2025) estimates articulation through tracking and optimisation, while Pandora (Yu et al., 2026) and MoMa-SG (Buechner et al., 2026) incorporate interaction-derived articulation into scene graphs. ViewStruct operates from static observations, incorporating additional images or motion evidence when available without per-instance fitting.

# 3 Method 

ViewStruct jointly predicts part connectivity and articulation from visual observations, using a shared representation for objects and scenes. Its inputs comprise a reference RGB image, aligned part segmentation, depth, and optional additional images or video. Figure 2 illustrates the pipeline. 

3.1 Overall Pipeline and Problem Formulation Observation and part encoding. We denote the visual input by V = ( I, S, D, A), where I, S, and D are the reference RGB image, part-segmentation map, and depth map, respectively, and A contains optional additional observations. Depth may be rendered, measured, or estimated. Each supplied part has a persistent identifier in 

P = {0, . . . , N − 1}. A text prompt Q specifies the task, output schema, and an indexed region inventory asso-ciating each identifier with its segmentation colour, bounding box, and relative depth interval. Additional observations provide complementary evidence while retaining the reference part identities and output frame. 

Structural prediction. We adapt Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai et al., 2025) to generate 

O = fϑ(V, Q ) = ( T, ΦJ ), (1) where T : P → P ∪ {⊥} defines a forest of parent attachments and ΦJ specifies joints on articulated attachments. Attachments without joints are rigid, allowing multiple regions to form one rigid body. ViewStruct produces this structure through autoregressive decoding, without per-instance optimisation or an external refinement loop. 

3.2 View-Grounded Structure Prediction 

We define the representation of the connectivity T and joint parameters ΦJ in Equation (1). Our design addresses three ambiguities: canonical coordinates require object-specific orientation conventions, part-level parent maps impose 

3arbitrary hierarchies within rigid bodies, and direct 3D pivot regression lacks an explicit image-space correspondence. We therefore express ΦJ in a gravity-aligned, view-relative frame, recover T from joint-centred rigid groups, and reconstruct pivots from predicted image locations and depth. 

Gravity-aligned, view-relative coordinates. Canonical orientations can be ambiguous for unfamiliar or symmetric objects and differ across objects in a scene. A view-relative frame provides a shared reference without requiring a category-specific front direction. Gravity alignment further reduces variation due to camera pitch and roll, simplifying the representation of vertical hinges and horizontal sliding motions. Joint-centred connectivity. Visual regions need not correspond to distinct rigid bodies: a drawer’s front, handle, and box may be segmented separately but move together. Predicting a parent for every region requires choosing an internal hierarchy among these rigidly attached parts, although different choices describe the same physical structure. We therefore predict rigid-body groups and their articulated connections, using a deterministic convention to recover the part-level parent map T .Each object consists of a static base B0 and one moving body Bk per articulated joint. The sets Bk ⊆ P partition the object’s part inventory, with all regions in a body moving rigidly together. Entry k specifies its moving regions Bk,parent-body index q(k), and joint parameters 

ΦJ = {jk}Kk=1 , jk = ( τk, ˆak, x k, [θk, −, θ k, +]) , (4) where τk is the joint type, ˆak its unit axis, xk its revolute pivot, and [θk, −, θ k, +] its motion limits. Prismatic joints omit the pivot and express displacements in units of s; revolute limits are in radians. A moving body may attach to the static base or another moving body, supporting nested articulation such as a door mounted within a sliding assembly. To recover T , each body has an anchor region bk, listed first; for moving bodies, this is the joint-carrying region. The static anchor b0 becomes the root. Each moving anchor bk attaches to its parent-body anchor bq(k) through joint 

jk, while the remaining regions in Bk attach rigidly to bk. Thus, the model predicts physically meaningful grouping and attachment relationships, and internal rigid hierarchies are assigned deterministically. Joint entries follow a fixed image-position ordering, and object membership distinguishes separate hierarchies in scenes. Grouping also reduces output length by representing each rigid body collectively. On PhysX-Mobility, the 95 th-percentile target length decreases from 2073 to 1329 tokens, reducing the risk of truncation for objects with many parts. 

Image-grounded pivots. To associate joint geometry explicitly with the reference image, we parameterise each revolute pivot xk in Equation (4) by a normalised image location uk ∈ [0 , 1000] 2 and a relative camera-depth offset 

δk. This allows the model to localise the pivot using visual cues, such as a hinged edge, and infer its depth separately. For an image of size W × H, let ˜uk = ( W u k,x /1000 , Hu k,y /1000) denote the corresponding pixel coordinates. We decode 

zk = cz + δks, xk = ϕ π−1(˜ uk, z k) , (5) where cz is the camera-depth component of the frame centre. Thus, the pivot entering ΦJ is reconstructed from image-grounded predictions rather than independently regressed, ensuring projection consistency by construction. Axis directions ˆak are predicted directly in the reference frame, since their image projections may be degenerate or poorly resolved. The output O = ( T, ΦJ ) therefore combines deterministically expanded connectivity with decoded joint geometry.

3.3 Data Construction and Training Unified supervision. We convert annotated articulated assets from different sources into a common set of observation-grounded input–target pairs. Fixed attachments are grouped into rigid bodies, and scene examples ad-ditionally record object membership. We render observations with persistent part identifiers linking image regions to the annotated geometry, then transform joint parameters into the reference frame with consistent units. This reconciles differences in coordinate, scale, and hierarchy conventions without requiring category-specific canonical alignment. 

Static and dynamic observations. From these assets, we construct both static observations and articulation clips. Static examples supervise inference from appearance and spatial relationships, while clips provide direct evidence of relative motion. Both use the same structural targets expressed in their reference frame. We vary camera viewpoints to expose the model to different appearances and visibility patterns of the same underlying structure. 

Part-decomposition augmentation. To improve robustness to different test-time segmenters, we also vary the sup-plied part decomposition. We either split an articulated part by a plane through its centroid into two rigidly connected regions sharing the original joint, or merge two sibling regions with compatible articulation. We then update the part inventory and re-render the segmentation map S, preserving the joint parameters and underlying kinematics. This exposes the model to different visual decompositions of the same mechanical structure, reducing dependence on a particular segmentation. Ablations demonstrate improved prediction performance (Section C.2). Corpus construction and augmentation details are provided in Sections A.2 and A.3. 

# 4 Experiments 

Implementation details. We fine-tune Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct Bai et al. (2025) with LoRA for 2 epochs on the structured prediction objective with input context length 8192 tokens, an image pixel budget of 256 × 256 , effective batch size 32 , and a cosine learning-rate schedule peaking at 10 −4. The pretrained vision encoder and the base decoder weights are kept frozen: we train rank-64 LoRA adapters on the decoder together with the vision–language merger. All experiments use four NVIDIA A100 GPUs. Please refer to the supplementary for more details. 

Metrics. We report parse success (the fraction of generations that yield a parsable structured JSON) and, on the parsed subset, parent accuracy and edge F1 for connectivity, joint-presence F1, joint-type accuracy , joint-axis accuracy , joint position error and joint range IoU for articulation. Following Le et al. (2024), each failed joint is attributed to its first violated gate (type → axis → origin → limit) and the success rate is the fraction of matched joints that pass all four. We further report inference time in wall-clock seconds per object on a single A100. Definitions, thresholds and normalisation conventions are given in Section B.3. 

Baselines. We compare with the zero-shot Qwen3-VL backbone, PhysX-Anything (Cao et al., 2025), PhysX-Omni (Cao et al., 2026), URDF-Anything+ (Li et al., 2025), and Particulate (Li et al., 2026a). Their input modalities are given in the caption of Table 1; mesh-based methods receive reference geometry. Particulate, which predicts from geometry alone, is compared in Table 6. The zero-shot backbone uses the same inputs and prompt as ViewStruct. OPDMulti (Sun et al., 2024) detects openable parts and their motion parameters in a single RGB image, which is the closest published formulation to our scene setting; we evaluate it on the room views and on real video. SINGAPO (Liu et al., 2025) is evaluated on its supported category subset in the supplementary, and ArtiPoint (Werby et al., 2025) is compared on real video. Baseline conversion, part correspondence, and timing protocols are detailed in Section B. 

4.1 Evaluation of Connectivity and Articulation Prediction Object-level prediction. We evaluate all methods on 387 objects from the PhysX-Mobility (Cao et al., 2025) test split, using ViewStruct with either ground-truth depth or monocular depth estimated by MoGe (Wang et al., 2025). As shown in Table 1, ViewStruct achieves the highest joint F1, while also predicting part connectivity. PhysX-Omni achieves higher type and axis accuracy and matched-joint success ( 85 .3% versus 81 .5% ). However, these conditional metrics exclude unrecovered joints and should therefore be interpreted alongside coverage. Canonical-coordinate pre-diction also benefits from axis-alignment priors in this dataset, whereas ViewStruct expresses joint directions relative to the observation. When evaluated over all reference joints, ViewStruct achieves 78 .2% success, compared with 

66 .6% for PhysX-Omni, with the advantage largely retained using estimated depth. Inference takes 6.5 s per object, compared with 241 –267 s for the VLM-based joint prediction stages of PhysX-Omni and PhysX-Anything, excluding their geometry-generation time.

We presented ViewStruct, a view-grounded framework for jointly predicting part connectivity and articulation in objects and scenes. By combining visual and semantic priors, ViewStruct performs di-rect autoregressive prediction without per-instance optimisation, in-ferring structure from a single static observation and incorporat-ing motion evidence when available. Experiments demonstrate im-proved structural prediction over existing baselines, generalisation to unseen object categories and asset sources, and transfer from syn-thetic training data to real-world interaction videos. Simulated ma-nipulation experiments further show that the predicted joints can guide effective interaction trajectories. These results highlight the potential of view-grounded structural inference for connecting vi-sual observations to actionable representations of articulated envi-ronments.

The centre is obtained from the reference object’s silhouette-box centre at its median depth, and the scale from its largest metric silhouette extent. The horizontal axis follows the projected viewing direction. For the MoGe variant, the frame and depth intervals are recomputed from estimated depth; evaluation uses scale alignment. Segmentation colours are permuted deterministically per asset, and part identifiers are randomly permuted during training, with the same permutation applied to the prompt and target. These operations reduce shortcuts based on colours or indices. Additional RGB views and clips retain the reference inventory and coordinate frame; they do not introduce independently indexed part lists. 

Body and joint serialisation. The output lists static regions in base and moving bodies in joints . Each joint entry contains its rigidly connected parts , a parent-body reference on , and its articulation parameters. The first region in a body is its anchor; in a moving body, that region carries the joint. The remaining regions attach rigidly to the anchor. Each part appears in exactly one body, and joints are ordered by moving-part box centre, from top to bottom and then left to right. This grouping avoids predicting every internal rigid attachment, while retaining a deterministic convention for expanding the parent map. 

Pivot parameterisation. We represent each revolute pivot by a 2D image position and a relative depth, from which its 3D position is decoded using Equation (5). This explicitly grounds the pivot in the reference observation. Joint axes are predicted directly in the gravity-aligned, view-relative frame. Revolute limits are expressed in radians, while prismatic displacements are normalised by s. Prismatic joints require no pivot. 

Scene inputs. The objects field groups region identifiers by object, with the static body listed first. A joint attached to base refers to its own object’s static body. The designated reference object defines the observation centre and scale, and all other objects share that frame. Object membership and body grouping therefore yield one hierarchy per object within a jointly generated scene structure, using direct autoregressive prediction without per-instance optimisation. 

A.2 Supervision Construction 

Source meshes and joints are first converted to consistent coordinates and units. We place each asset at rest using forward kinematics, then render aligned RGB, part-ID, and depth images from one canonical and several sampled cameras. Each camera gives a separate static training example. Joint axes and pivots are transformed into the ob-servation frame, and pivots are projected to obtain image-space targets. For motion examples, a clip actuates one joint while retaining the reference inventory and frame. Scene examples additionally record the membership of all annotated objects. Fixed-joint chains are collapsed into rigid bodies before serialisation, retaining one articulated attachment per moving body. This prevents a handle fixed to a door, or two fragments of the same door, from receiving independent copies of the hinge. Meshless scaffold links are removed without discarding fixed attachments between physical parts. To vary part granularity, split augmentation divides a selected articulated leaf part by a plane through its centroid, while merge augmentation combines sibling parts with compatible articulation. The segmentation is re-rendered and the grouped target rebuilt, so fragments moving together share one joint. These operations vary the decomposition while preserving its intended kinematics.

A.3 Training Mixture and Configuration Training mixture. Our training pool comprises PhysX-Mobility (Cao et al., 2025) ( 1,633 objects, 13 ,064 views), its decomposition variants ( 4,561 variants, 36 ,488 views), PhysXNet (Cao et al., 2025) ( 40 ,000 objects, 160 ,000 views), GRScenes (Wang et al., 2024) objects ( 2,502 objects, 10 ,008 views), and 2,338 GRScenes room views. To balance source contributions, each epoch samples 50% of the decomposition-variant pool and 40% of the PhysXNet pool, while retaining all examples from the remaining sources. These sampling rates apply to the individual source pools, rather than their proportions in the final mixture. We additionally include 5,954 PhysX-Mobility articulation clips, yielding approximately 114 k examples per epoch. 

Training configuration. We adapt Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct using decoder LoRA with rank 64 and scaling factor 

α = 128 , jointly training the vision–language merger while freezing the vision encoder and base decoder weights. The merger weights are saved alongside the adapters. We minimise cross-entropy over answer tokens for two epochs using bfloat16 precision and an effective batch size of 32 , distributed across four workers with one example per worker and eight gradient accumulation steps. Training uses a cosine learning-rate schedule with a peak of 10 −4, a warmup ratio of 0.03 , zero weight decay, and a gradient-clipping threshold of 1. The context length is limited to 8192 tokens, and the image budget is 65 ,536 pixels. We enable gradient checkpointing and ZeRO stage 2. 

# B Evaluation Protocols 

B.1 Datasets and Observation Settings Objects. PhysX-Mobility contains 387 test objects. SimReady contains 242 kitchen assets from 14 categories and is absent from training; its categories overlap PartNet-Mobility, so it tests transfer across instances and asset sources. GRScenes contains 3,099 converted movable assets: 2,502 training objects, 306 test instances from seen categories, and 291 objects in seven categories excluded from its training partition: hearth, electric cooker, TV stand, tea table, sideboard, chest of drawers, and shoe cabinet. Object comparisons use one reference view unless otherwise stated. PhysX-Anything and PhysX-Omni transfer metrics are conditional on objects producing an output. 

Scenes. We render 2,897 views in 69 GRScenes-100 rooms, each camera placed in front of a target object while also annotating neighbouring articulated objects. Views contain approximately 2.2–2.9 objects and 5–7 joints on average across splits. There are 2,338 training views, 305 seen-category test views, and 254 held-out-category test views. Training views contain only training models; test views are partitioned by the target object, without imposing the same partition on neighbours. In held-out-target views, 74% of neighbours are training models and 81% of views mix partitions. In seen-target views, 12% of neighbours belong to held-out categories. These splits therefore test prediction around different target categories, not wholly unseen rooms or exclusively unseen objects. The main scene table pools all 559 test views and retains out-of-frame pivots. For PhysX-Omni, we run each articulated object separately with the background masked and place its predictions back into the scene; ViewStruct processes the room view jointly. 

Real videos. Arti4D contains 342 interactions in four indoor scenes, comprising 228 prismatic and 114 revolute joints. ViewStruct receives an initial RGB-D frame with Mobile-SAM regions and Kinect depth, optionally supple-mented by two RGB frames and a 16 -frame interaction clip as specified in Table 4. It uses no real-data adaptation or temporal camera poses. ArtiPoint receives the full posed RGB-D sequence and reference interaction cues. For visual-isation only, camera poses reproject predicted axes into successive frames, and depth determines which segments are drawn as occluded. 

B.2 Baselines and Part Correspondence Inputs. Zero-shot Qwen3-VL receives the same images, region descriptions, and output schema as ViewStruct. PhysX-Anything (Cao et al., 2025) and PhysX-Omni (Cao et al., 2026) infer joints from the image through their VLM stages; geometry generation is skipped, and reference meshes are used for association with the evaluation inventory, not as visual input to those stages. URDF-Anything+ (Li et al., 2025) receives the image and a whole-object mesh assembled from reference parts. We use its released checkpoint without shell thickening, which removed articulated components in our tests. Particulate (Li et al., 2026a) receives the merged reference mesh without an image and predicts its own decomposition and joints. OPDMulti (Sun et al., 2024) receives the reference RGB image alone and predicts a mask, motion type, axis, and origin per openable part; the released motion-state checkpoint additionally predicts the joint state, from which a motion range follows. Its masks are matched to our inventory by intersection

B.3 Metrics and Normalisation 

Predictions are paired with reference examples by identifier and expanded into parent maps and joints. Parse success is reported separately; structural and joint metrics are aggregated over parsed predictions. Thus, conditional accuracy must be read together with parse success and joint coverage. 

Connectivity. Parent accuracy measures agreement over reference parts after star-anchor canonicalisation of refer-ence instance groups, applying the same smallest-ID anchor convention to both graphs. Edge F1 compares undirected parent edges, removing sensitivity to edge orientation. The rerooted diagnostic additionally roots each connected com-ponent at its smallest identifier. Connectivity scores for generated decompositions combine hierarchy accuracy with coverage of the reference inventory. C.2 Ablations 

Two components of the recipe are isolated by retraining the reported model with a single change and evaluating on the same PhysX-Mobility single-view split. Everything else – backbone, adapters, optimisation, training mixture, target format and the number of steps – is held fixed, so the differences in Table 9 are attributable to the change alone. 

Observation frame. Replacing the gravity-aligned frame of Equation (3) with the raw camera frame, leaving the origin and unit length unchanged, costs 3.9 points of articulation success. The loss is concentrated in the axis channel (89 .9 → 86 .6) and in the success rate that depends on it, while joint presence is unchanged ( 96 .8 → 96 .9) and the 

> 16

hinge pivot is unaffected ( 0.084 → 0.082 ). This is the expected pattern: pivots are read off the image and do not depend on the 3D convention, whereas an axis expressed in camera coordinates changes with the camera’s pitch and roll, so the same vertical hinge takes a different target value in every view and the model must absorb that variance. Aligning one axis of the frame with gravity removes it for the vertical hinges that dominate the corpus; horizontal directions remain view-dependent in both frames. 

Decomposition augmentation. Removing the split-merge rows, which vary the supplied part granularity while pre-serving the intended kinematics, measures how much of the model’s robustness comes from having seen alternative decompositions of the same object rather than from the object inventory itself. It is the larger of the two effects: ar-ticulation success drops by 10 .0 points ( 81 .5 → 71 .5) although the same objects and views remain in the training set. The joints are still found ( 96 .8 → 96 .2 presence), but their parameters degrade across every channel, the axis most (89 .9 → 82 .8), followed by the motion range ( 72 .3 → 66 .3) and the hinge pivot ( 0.084 → 0.096 ). Grouping is also weaker ( 92 .3 → 88 .6 parent), which is what the augmentation targets directly: without rows in which a part is split or two parts are merged, the model associates a particular segmentation of an object with a particular answer instead of inferring which supplied regions move together. 

Error in the gravity direction. The model receives no numerical gravity: c, s and the depth intervals of Equation (6) are read from the silhouette and the depth map, and the prompt describes the frame only in words. Gravity therefore enters at a single point, when the output is interpreted, so an inertial sensor that is wrong by δ is exactly equivalent to rotating every predicted axis and pivot by δ before comparing them with the reference. We apply that rotation about a random horizontal axis, per object, and rescore; no retraining is involved. Table 8 shows the result. # D From Predicted Joints to Manipulation Trajectories 

ViewStruct provides motion constraints for generating robot end-effector trajectories. Given a contact point on a predicted moving body, the joint type determines the trajectory shape: a prismatic joint defines a straight path, while a revolute joint defines a circular arc. Persistent part identifiers associate each joint with the geometry to be manipulated. 

Mapping predictions to the robot frame. We first transform the predicted joint parameters from the normalised view-relative frame into the robot planning frame. Let R map directions from the prediction frame to the planning frame, c denote the prediction-frame origin in planning coordinates, and s denote the normalisation scale. The joint pivot and axis become 

o = c + sR ov , ˆa = Rˆav . (9) Prismatic limits are multiplied by s, while angular limits remain unchanged. 

Generating the motion path. Let q0 be the initial contact position in the planning frame. For a prismatic joint, the target position follows 

q(d) = q0 + dˆa, (10) where d is the displacement from the initial configuration. For a revolute joint, the target follows 

q(θ) = o + Rot(ˆ a, θ )( q0 − o), (11) where Rot(ˆ a, θ ) denotes rotation by θ about the predicted axis. Sampling d or θ over the intended motion produces a sequence of Cartesian waypoints, with travel constrained by the predicted joint limits. These waypoints provide the positional reference for robot motion planning. Combined with a chosen grasp orien-tation, they can be converted into robot joint configurations through inverse kinematics and executed by a controller.

D.1 Compliant Manipulation on PhysX-Mobility Simulation setup. We reconstruct each object in PyBullet using its reference joints and limits, with a fixed base, gravity disabled, and no joint motors. A compliant gripper is represented by a force-bounded point constraint attached at a supplied contact location: the mesh point farthest from the reference axis for a hinge, or the moving part’s centre for a slide. These privileged contacts and simplified physics isolate articulation prediction quality; grasp discovery and collision avoidance are not evaluated. 

Trajectories and success criteria. The gripper follows the predicted joint associated with the contacted part. For a hinge, it traces a 45 ◦ arc around the predicted axis and pivot. For a slide, it moves along the predicted direction by the smaller of the predicted range and 0.3 normalised object units. The reference mechanism constrains the resulting motion. A hinge trial succeeds when its opening reaches 30 ◦, or 60% of the reference range if that range is below 30 ◦.A slide trial succeeds at 60% of its reference travel. Success rates include all reference joints, with missing predictions counted as failures. The retry setting allows one attempt in the reverse direction. Oracle trajectories use reference joint parameters.

D.2 Panda Manipulation in GRScenes 

We evaluate a Panda arm in MuJoCo over ten trials in ten GRScenes rooms. The arm follows trajectories derived from ViewStruct’s predictions, while each object retains its reference mechanism. A compliant point constraint provides assisted contact, robot–scene collisions are disabled, and foreground geometry is cut away for visibility. Eight trials reach the opening threshold. This evaluation assesses trajectory execution under assisted contact; it does not evaluate collision-free planning or autonomous grasp acquisition. Figure 10 shows successful openings of a window ( 39 ◦), cabinet door ( 49 ◦), room door ( 40 ◦), and microwave door (32 ◦). In the two unsuccessful trials, the TV-stand drawer stops 3 cm short of its threshold, while the shoe cabinet remains stationary because the predicted sliding direction points into the reference joint limit. These cases illustrate how insufficient travel and incorrect motion direction can prevent opening even when the unsigned axis is plausible. 

# E Limitations and Future Work 

Input assumptions and observational ambiguity. ViewStruct relies on a supplied part decomposition: missing parts or regions spanning independently moving bodies can prevent correct structural recovery. Geometric accuracy also depends on depth, camera calibration, gravity estimation, and consistent part association across observations. Our current image-based experiments additionally supply the joint count; estimating it jointly with structure would relax this assumption. Even with accurate inputs, static observations may not uniquely determine hidden pivots, opening directions, or mechanical limits. Additional views and motion evidence can reduce these ambiguities, motivating future work on uncertainty estimation and active observation selection. 

Model capacity and training coverage. Computational constraints restrict our experiments to a 4B-parameter VLM and a finite collection of articulated assets. Although the model transfers to unseen categories and real-world inter-actions, its task-specific training uses exclusively synthetic data, leaving a domain gap in appearance, segmentation quality, and depth accuracy. Larger backbones and more diverse training observations may improve robustness, al-though their benefits must be balanced against inference cost. 

Closed-loop robotic interaction. Inference takes a few seconds, making ViewStruct suitable for initial structure estimation and high-level planning, while faster updates would be needed for continuous feedback control. Our ma-nipulation evaluation uses supplied contact locations and simplified physics, and does not assess autonomous grasp selection, collision avoidance, or physical-property estimation. Future work could initialise an interaction loop with ViewStruct, track part motion during contact, and refine joint parameters before replanning. Geometric fitting could provide these updates without retraining the predictor.
