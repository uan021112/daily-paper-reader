Abstract — Humans and animals do not learn vision from a fixed set of data: they move, interact, and learn from the visual consequences of their actions. Motivated by this developmental view, we study whether robots can improve visual learning by actively collecting experience through curiosity-driven interac-tion. We introduce DySCOVER, a self-supervised framework for active robot visual learning. A robot observes RGB frames, learns a visual autoencoder that encodes and reconstructs them, and trains a recurrent world model to predict the next visual latent state conditioned on the current state and action. The agent’s policy is trained only with intrinsic rewards: reconstruction error encourages visually novel observations, while the world model’s error encourages transitions whose consequences are not yet predictable. This prediction error biases the policy toward more intricate trajectories that enrich the agent’s set of experiences with observations beyond those encouraged by visual novelty alone. Our experiments conducted with robotic arms as the embodied agents show that adding predictive curiosity improves the vision model over using visual curiosity alone. Across variations in initial conditions, experience capacity, embodiment, and scene content, adding predictive curiosity consistently improved visual learning. These results show that predictive curiosity can improve active visual learning beyond reconstruction-based curiosity alone. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humans and animals do not learn vision from a fixed dataset of images. From the earliest stages of development, infants move their eyes, hands, and bodies to interact with their environment and learn from the consequences of their actions. This loop of observing, acting, predicting, and updating internal models motivates open-ended learning in robotics [1], [2], [3]. An embodied agent should therefore not only learn from observations, but also perform actions that generate experience useful for improving its internal models .Curiosity-driven learning guides exploration through in-trinsic signals such as state novelty, prediction error, model disagreement, information gain, and learning progress [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. These signals have also supported visual representation learning from self-collected experience, as in Curious Representation Learning [9]. Recently, Push, See, Predict [10] showed that prediction error can be used to collect informative object interactions and improve visual reconstruction. However, it studies a staged object-centric setting built around explicit object decomposition and predefined pushing actions. In contrast, we study a continually updated learning loop that operates directly on learned visual latents, without requiring object segmentation, in which the policy, visual model, and world model co-adapt from the agent’s own interactions. Specifically, we investigate the question: does pre-dictive curiosity improve visual learning beyond what reconstruction-based curiosity alone can achieve? This visual curiosity rewards observations that are difficult to reconstruct. Predictive curiosity instead rewards transitions whose consequences are difficult to predict. We argue that the predictive signal can bias the policy towards actions that cause more intricate motions, leading to useful experiences for the agent, that a pure vision-novelty reward may not achieve. This motivates us to study whether prediction error can provide a complementary exploration signal for collect-ing visual experience that improves the agent’s visual model. To study this question, we introduce DySCOVER 

(DynamicS-informed CuriOsity for Visual Exploration and Representation-learning), a self-supervised framework for active visual representation learning in embodied agents. DySCOVER couples three components that are updated throughout exploration: a visual autoencoder that learns from the collected image observations, a recurrent world model that predicts action-conditioned transitions in the visual latent space, and a recurrent policy that decides what experience to collect. The policy is trained only with intrinsic reward: reconstruction error encourages visually novel observations, while prediction error encourages transitions whose con-sequences are difficult to predict. The prediction error of the world model influences the policy and therefore the visual experience on which the vision model is trained. Our extensive experimental results, across various settings, consistently show that adding predictive curiosity improves visual reconstruction. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Developmental Learning and Artificial Curiosity 

The idea that an agent should use intrinsic motivation to choose experiences useful for learning has been studied ex-tensively. Early curiosity-driven systems coupled predictive models with controllers that sought improvements in their predictions [1], [2]. This idea has been extended through learning and competence progress, directing exploration to-ward experiences from which the agent can learn rather than simply those with the largest error [3], [11]. Network Distillation (RND) method rewards errors in pre-dicting the output of a fixed random network, providing an observation-based novelty signal [4]. The Intrinsic Curiosity Module (ICM) uses prediction error as curiosity: the agent is rewarded when its actions lead to outcomes that are difficult to predict and possible to be predicted, in a learned feature space [5]. Other works refine this idea by rewarding learning progress rather than raw prediction error. Active World Model Learning, for example, compares the current prediction loss with that of a slowly updated reference model, so that the agent prefers situations where its model is improving [8]. Curiosity-Critic follows a related motivation by learning a baseline for expected prediction error and re-warding deviations from this baseline, reducing the tendency to chase errors that remain high but not informative [12]. 

B. Visual Learning from Self-Generated Experience 

Physical interaction can provide supervision for visual learning. The Curious Robot learns visual representations from robotic pushing, poking, grasping, and object observa-tions [13]. Curious Representation Learning (CRL) explicitly couples representation learning and exploration: a policy maximizes the visual learner’s contrastive loss, while the learner minimizes that loss on the collected observations [9]. Haber et al. jointly train a self- and world-model to perform on external tasks [14]. Push, See, Predict is close to our approach [10], since it also uses prediction error to collect informative visual expe-rience. However, it studies a staged, object-centric pipeline in which Slot Attention decomposes each frame into a pre-defined number of object representations and segmentation masks, which are subsequently used by a graph-based world model and the exploration policy. In contrast, DySCOVER operates directly on a learned latent visual space. Moreover, we study whether dynamics-aware curiosity provides an advantage over an already active visual-only curiosity policy, within a continually updated loop where the visual and world model, and exploration policy co-adapt throughout exploration, rather than primarily over random or heuristic data collection. 

C. World Models for Exploration and Control 

World models also support exploration and control be-yond visual data acquisition. VIME rewards information gain about learned dynamics [7], while disagreement-based exploration uses variation among predictive models [6]. Plan2Explore trains an exploration policy within a learned world model, using an ensemble of one-step predictors to estimate latent disagreement [15]. PlaNet and Dreamer use latent dynamics for planning and policy learning from imagined trajectories [16], [17]. BYOL-Explore jointly learns visual representations, dynamics, and exploration through a bootstrapped latent prediction objective [18]. In DySCOVER, the world model offers a complementary curiosity signal to guide the policy towards collecting informative experiences to improve its vision model. We evaluate the effectiveness of our method primarily through the reconstruction quality of the vision model on an independently created, unseen evaluation set of visual observations. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Objective and Learning Setting 

We study active visual representation learning for an embodied agent. At time t, the agent receives a visual frame observation ot ∈ O , selects a continuous action at ∈ A ,and observes the next visual frame ot+1 . The policy state is the latent visual representation learned from the visual observations; no other simulator variables are provided to the policy or used for the exploration. The objective of the agent is to learn how to interact with the environment in order to collect data that improves its visual model. This objective is viewed as a closed loop. The policy selects actions based on the agent’s current internal models of the world, and these actions determine which observations and transitions are collected. The collected experience is stored in a replay buffer and used to update the agent’s internal models and policy. As these models improve, both the state representation and the regions where the models are uncertain or incomplete change so the policy must adapt towards collecting the data distribution that is most informative for learning. An overview of this closed-loop learning process is shown in Figure 1. We begin with self-body exploration: the scene contains no objects, so visual change is attributed to the robots own motion. This controlled setting lets the agent learn the relationship between its actions and the resulting changes in the camera view, without mixing them with contact or object motion. When objects are introduced later, the model already contains expectations about its body motions, allow-ing predictive curiosity to focus more on changes caused by interaction with the environment. It follows a simple developmental intuition: the agent first learns the effects of its own movements, then learns what happens when acting on objects around it.

E. Policy optimization and alternating updates 

The policy is a recurrent actor–critic πη (at | zt, h πt ), with parameters η, and is optimized using Proximal Policy Opti-mization (PPO) [20]. Both the actor and critic are recurrent to exploit temporal context through the hidden state hπt , which again cannot be encoded in the visual representation. The learning occurs in cycles. First, the current policy is used to collect observation-action rollouts. Second, the policy is updated using the observed rewards. Third, the vision and world models are updated using the collected data in the replay buffer. The updated models are used for the next cycle. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1. 

F. Model Architectures 

The visual model is implemented as a spatial-bottleneck autoencoder. It uses a ResNet encoder without pretraining and retains only the deepest encoder feature map as the visual embedding. The decoder receives only this bottleneck and reconstructs the image. We intentionally do not use encoder– decoder skip connections, so high-level information does not bypass the bottleneck, since the latent state used by the policy and world model should carry the full visual information. The world model is implemented as a convolutional recurrent model that predicts the next visual latent state from the current latent state and executed action. The visual representation retains its spatial structure, while the action is projected through a learned action embedding over the same spatial grid. The concatenated tensor is processed by a convolutional GRU (ConvGRU [21]), producing a prediction for the next latent and a hidden state that encodes the temporal information. The collection policy is implemented as a recurrent actor–critic model. The visual and world model are trained using the losses defined in Eq. (2) and Eq. (6), respectively. IV. C. Primary Exploration Comparison 

Vision Model Improvement. Figure 3 shows the visual-learning curves of the five exploration strategies over 10 million simulation steps, with the final reconstruction perfor-mance shown in Table I. Figure 4 further shows a qualitative reconstruction example, along with heatmaps to highlight the pixel-wise differences. The visual-only curiosity strategy serves as the main baseline, since it uses only frame reconstruction novelty exploration. Using only the WM prediction error as the intrinsic reward achieves a 3.7% lower reconstruction error than this baseline, showing that predictive curiosity alone can result in useful data collection (the vision model is still trained from the collected images). Adding the raw WM reward on top of visual curiosity gives a larger improvement of 6.8% , indicating that prediction error can complement the reconstruction novelty to bias the policy towards collecting useful experiences. The strongest result is obtained with the adjusted WM reward: DySCOVER reduces reconstruction error by 14 .5% relative to visual-only curiosity. Finally, random exploration performs 25 .9% worse than visual-only curiosity and 47 .3% worse than DySCOVER, confirming that curiosity-driven exploration offers significant advantage in visual representation learning. 

Convergence Speed. The difference is also reflected in convergence speed. The reconstruction error reached by visual-only curiosity after 10 million steps is reached by DySCOVER with raw WM reward after approximately 5 million steps, and by DySCOVER with adjusted WM reward after approximately 4 million steps.

Object-scene adaptation. We examine whether DySCOVER remains useful when the visual distribution changes and becomes richer by adding objects to the scene. The goal is still to improve the agent’s vision model, but the agent now has to adapt from handling observations of its body in an empty scene to observations that also include previously unseen objects. We continue training in the object-augmented scene for an additional million steps. Visual-only baseline and DySCOVER are initialized at their final model checkpoints from the object-free experiments, corresponding to a natural transfer setting from self-body exploration to a richer scene. We also include a visual-only curiosity experiment that is initialized from the same visual model as DySCOVER, as a sanity check, in order to rule out the possibility that its superior performance in the new scene is explained only by a better initial visual representation. As Figure 7 shows, DySCOVER offers a relative MSE reduction of 9.70% with respect to visual-only curiosity. G. How Dynamics-Aware Reward Affects Policy Behavior 

The previous sections quantitatively show that adding dynamics-aware curiosity improves the final visual model. We now also qualitatively examine how the different reward definitions affect the policy behavior that generates data to be used for model training. Figure 10 compares example rollouts during RL training at the same train step for visual-only curiosity, DySCOVER with the raw WM reward and DySCOVER with the adjusted WM reward. Each row shows sampled observed frames from one rollout and, below them, the policy statistics for an action dimension. The blue curve is the policy mean, the shaded region is the policy’s standard deviation, and the orange curve is the sampled action executed during the rollout. The visual-only curiosity policy produces a comparatively limited control pattern, leading to less diverse robot config-urations. This is expected, since the reward depends only on frame-wise reconstruction error and not whether a transition has informative consequences. In contrast, DySCOVER with the raw WM reward produces stronger and more complex action excursions. These motions expose the visual model to richer configurations as seen in the accompanying rollout frames. However, the rollout also shows that raw prediction error can encourage high-magnitude and less smooth behav-ior: large actions produce large transitions in latent and visual space, which can increase the prediction error and therefore the reward, even when those transitions are not necessarily the most informative for the visual model. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We presented DySCOVER, an active visual-learning loop in which a visual reconstruction curiosity signal is augmented by the prediction error of a learned action-conditioned world model. The world model is used to enhance the reward that the policy uses to collect experience that improves the agent’s visual model. Across multiple experiments, we show that adding the WM reward term improves the reconstruction ability of the vision model, relative to visual-only curiosity. The compact-buffer and cross-robot experiments show that the effect is not limited to the main setting. Furthermore, the object-augmented experiment suggests that the same mechanism can also improve adaptation when the visual distribution changes and becomes more complex. The qualitative analysis of the policies provides a visualization for the way the WM reward term biases the policy towards more complex behaviors that tend to produce more complex and diverse rollouts than visual-only curiosity. Future work includes the evaluation of the agent on external tasks to investigate whether the visual representation that DySCOVER learns can be used in a complex scenario, such as planning or manipulation.
