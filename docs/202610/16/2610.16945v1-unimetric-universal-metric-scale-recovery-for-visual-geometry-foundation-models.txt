Recent advances in multimodal foundation models, exemplified by GPT-6 Astra, raise the prospect of
general-purpose embodied reasoning and planning. Using Internet videos for embodied pretraining
calls for reliable physical-scale annotations. Yet visual geometry foundation models often recover
scene structure and camera motion without reliable metric scale. We show that uncertain size priors
of familiar objects can jointly constrain a reconstruction’s shared scale. Building on this insight,
we present UniMetric, a universal and training-free metric scale recovery framework for geometry
foundation models, without additional sensors or task-specific metric supervision. Specifically, Metric
Prior Acquisition elicits object height intervals from a vision–language model. Semantic–Geometric
Alignment measures reconstructed object heights along the estimated support-surface normal in each
frame and converts the priors into scale intervals. Global Scale Recovery reconciles these intervals
through a convex objective, yielding a jointly feasible scale when they overlap and a unique compromise
otherwise. The estimated sequence-level scale is applied uniformly to reconstructed points and camera
translations. Controlled experiments with real VLM priors show that combining observations improves
scale accuracy with exact relative object heights. To evaluate metric accuracy on GFM reconstructions
without ground-truth scale alignment, we introduce MetricBench, covering twelve embodied, driving,
and roadside subsets with sensor-derived ground truth. Across nine geometry foundation models,
UniMetric reduces mean depth Abs Rel, reconstruction Chamfer distance, and trajectory error by 54.2%,
83.8%, and 65.6%, respectively, improving every model on all three tasks at the suite level. We release
the UniMetric code and MetricBench data.

Introduction
Recent advances in general-purpose multimodal foundation models, exemplified by GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI,
2026), raise the prospect of a general-purpose embodied “brain” for reasoning and planning across diverse
physical platforms (Cheng et al., 2026). Grounding these capabilities in the physical world requires
understanding real dimensions, distances, and motion. This motivates enriching Internet videos with
reliable physical-scale annotations to support embodied training at scale. Visual geometry foundation
models (GFMs) (Wang et al., 2025, 2026b) can recover dense structure and camera motion from such
videos, yet their reconstructions often lack reliable metric scale. Bridging this gap is therefore key to
turning large-scale visual experience into metric data for embodied foundation-model pretraining.
The missing scale is commonly supplied by additional sensor measurements (Campos et al., 2021; Qin
et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2021) or learned from metric supervision (Bhat et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024;
Piccinelli et al., 2024; Keetha et al., 2025). These routes provide metric information through the acquisition
setup or metric-labelled training data. For a pretrained geometry model, however, a practical goal is to
recover the physical unit without introducing new sensing requirements or training an additional metric
predictor. This raises the question we study: can the content of a scene provide enough information to
recover the metric scale of its reconstruction?
Our motivation is that uncertainty about individual object sizes need not imply equally large uncertainty
about a scene’s scale. Familiar objects such as cars, doors, and pedestrians have plausible physical
size ranges, even when their exact dimensions are unknown. Each object alone provides only a coarse
reference. Within a reconstruction, however, these references constrain the same scale: a factor that
makes the car plausible must also make the door and the pedestrian plausible. A scale acceptable for

We examine this premise through complementary theoretical and empirical evidence. With exact relative
heights, averaging point-prior scale observations yields a decreasing mean-square error bound when their
errors are zero-mean, pairwise uncorrelated, and uniformly bounded in variance. A controlled study then
evaluates real VLM height intervals while keeping relative heights exact, separating semantic uncertainty
from errors in reconstructed geometry (Fig. 2). Across four dataset subsets, increasing the observation
budget reduces scale error (Table 1). These results motivate combining multiple uncertain priors in a
reconstruction, whose measured object heights introduce an additional source of error.
Building on this motivation, we present UniMetric (Sec. 3.2), a universal and training-free metric scale
recovery framework for geometry foundation models. Metric Prior Acquisition queries a pretrained
vision–language model (VLM) to identify familiar objects and provide their plausible height ranges.
Semantic–Geometric Alignment measures each object in the reconstruction along the support direction
estimated for its frame and converts its height range into an interval of admissible scales. Global Scale
Recovery combines these intervals across objects and frames by minimizing a convex objective whose
zero set is exactly their common intersection. The recovered scalar uniformly rescales the pointmaps and
camera translations, preserving relative geometry without modifying the underlying GFM.
Evaluating scale recovery requires retaining the scale error rather than removing it during alignment.
A protocol that fits scale to ground truth (Umeyama, 1991; Eigen et al., 2014) cannot reveal whether a
prediction already has the correct physical unit. We therefore introduce MetricBench (Sec. 4.1), comprising
twelve dataset subsets across driving, roadside, and embodied scenarios with sensor-derived metric
references. Its protocol evaluates depth, 3D reconstruction, and camera trajectories where applicable,
allowing rigid registration when needed but never ground-truth scale fitting. Across nine GFMs, UniMetric
reduces mean depth absolute relative error (Abs Rel), reconstruction Chamfer distance (CD), and mean
absolute trajectory error (ATE Mean) by 54.2%, 83.8%, and 65.6%, respectively, relative to uncorrected
predictions. Every evaluated model improves its suite-averaged performance on all three tasks (Table 4),
demonstrating the practical benefit of a shared scale correction across scene geometry and camera motion.
The per-subset depth, CD, and ATE comparisons (Tables 3, 5 and 6) test this transfer directly: each
sequence uses the same estimated scale for all three outputs, without fitting a separate factor for each task.

Our contributions are threefold:
1. We recast metric scale recovery as joint estimation from uncertain semantic size priors. We establish
conditions for mean-square consistency with point priors and validate the benefit of joint estimation
with real VLM intervals with exact relative object heights.
2. We propose UniMetric, a universal and training-free framework that reconciles semantic size intervals
with a GFM reconstruction and applies one recovered scale to its geometry and camera translations.
3. We introduce MetricBench, a cross-platform benchmark that measures metric accuracy across twelve
sensor-referenced dataset subsets. Paired comparisons across nine GFMs demonstrate improvements
in depth, reconstruction, and camera motion, while identifying cases where a shared scale alone is
insufficient.

Related Work
Visual Geometry Foundation Models. Building on the pairwise pointmaps of DUSt3R (Wang et al.,
2024), VGGT (Wang et al., 2025), π3 (Wang et al., 2026b), and Depth Anything 3 (Lin et al., 2026) infer
structure and cameras for many views in one forward pass, and later work extends the formulation to
longer, streaming, and dynamic inputs (Wang et al., 2026a; Xie et al., 2026; Burzio et al., 2026; Lan
et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026). The quality of relative geometry and the accuracy of its
physical scale are distinct properties. Evaluation with per-image median scaling (Eigen et al., 2014) or
similarity alignment (Umeyama, 1991) removes scale discrepancies before measuring error. MetricBench
instead retains them across depth, reconstruction, and camera motion (Sec. 4.1), while UniMetric supplies
a shared scale without altering the relative geometry.
Metric Scale from Sensors and Supervision. Metric scale is conventionally observed by calibrated
stereo, depth, or inertial sensors (Campos et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2018; Salih et al., 2026) or by a known
camera height (Tian et al., 2021). Height-based lifting (Yang et al., 2023, 2025a) and ground-aware
embeddings (Yang et al., 2024a) also incorporate scene geometry into monocular 3D detection. Metric
scale can also be learned from metric labels in single-image depth (Bhat et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2023; Hu
et al., 2024; Piccinelli et al., 2024; Bochkovskiy et al., 2025) and its video and multi-view extensions (Chen
et al., 2025; Keetha et al., 2025); language-derived scale predictors (Zeng et al., 2024; Wu and Chen,
2026) are trained in the same way. The first route requires hardware; the second inherits the scale statistics
of its training data. UniMetric uses neither: it combines the reconstruction of an existing model with
the size knowledge of an existing vision–language model, without training. Object size priors have
previously scaled filtering-based monocular SLAM, using height priors for a small set of predefined
detector classes (Sucar and Hayet, 2017); UniMetric instead obtains open-vocabulary height intervals
from a VLM and combines them with a GFM’s reconstructed geometry. The metric-depth calibration
comparison in Table 7 evaluates these alternative sources of scale using the same backbone predictions.

Experiments
The controlled study in Sec. 3.1 examines semantic priors with exact relative heights. We now evaluate
UniMetric on GFM reconstructions, where object measurements and camera motion also contain geometric
errors. MetricBench provides sensor-derived references for testing whether one recovered scale improves
these outputs together.
4.1
MetricBench
We introduce MetricBench, a cross-platform benchmark for evaluating metric geometry across diverse
Physical AI settings. It brings together twelve dataset subsets comprising 694 sequences and 51,339
frames, organized into driving-and-infrastructure and embodied-AI suites (Table 2). The subsets
are drawn from KITTI (Geiger et al., 2012, 2013), nuScenes (Caesar et al., 2020), Waymo (Sun et al.,
2020), Argoverse (Chang et al., 2019), V2X-Radar-I (Yang et al., 2025b), DAIR-V2X-I (Yu et al.,
2022), CEAR (Zhu et al., 2024), JRDB (Martin-Martin et al., 2023), FusionPortable (Jiao et al., 2022),
DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024), Humanoid Everyday (Zhao et al., 2025), and 3EED (Li et al., 2025).
MetricBench combines broad platform and scene coverage with a unified evaluation of depth, 3D
reconstruction, and ego pose. All reported results refer to these fixed evaluation subsets; the sequence
selection and sampling details are given in Appendix D.1.

Reference data and evaluation. Each evaluated frame contains a matched RGB image, a calibrated
metric point cloud, and camera metadata. Reference geometry comes from LiDAR or depth cameras, with
dataset-provided metric poses supplying trajectory references where applicable. These measurements
provide the physical units against which reconstructed depth, geometry, and camera motion are evaluated.

4.2
Experimental Settings
Models and comparison settings. We evaluate UniMetric with nine geometry foundation models:
VGGT (Wang et al., 2025), VGGT-Ω(Wang et al., 2026a), π3 (Wang et al., 2026b), Depth Anything 3
(DA-3; Lin et al., 2026), Scal3R (Xie et al., 2026), LingBot-Map (Chen et al., 2026), 4RC (Luo et al.,
2026), Déjà View (DVLT; Burzio et al., 2026), and STream3R (Lan et al., 2026). Each GFM uses its
released weights without additional training. For each sequence, None and UniMetric share the same GFM
predictions and differ only in the scale applied to depth, reconstructed points, and camera translations.
UniMetric estimates one factor per sequence and uses it for all three tasks. The VLM (Seed1.8) and
segmentation model (SAM 2.1) are shared across GFMs, while geometric filtering follows the dataset–
GFM configuration. The CEAR and FusionPortable prompts additionally include category-size tables;
VGGT-Ωuses dataset camera intrinsics on 3EED. Complete prompts, inference settings, and optimization
parameters are provided in Appendices C and D.1.
Metrics and aggregation. We report absolute relative depth error (Abs Rel) and threshold accuracy at
1.25 (δ1, %; Eigen et al., 2014). For reconstruction, Chamfer distance (CD) averages the two directed
nearest-neighbour distances between accumulated predicted and reference point clouds, in metres. For
ego pose, ATE Mean is the mean Euclidean camera-position error after rigid alignment of each sequence,
with scale fixed to one (Sturm et al., 2012). Depth errors are averaged over valid frames; CD is averaged
over sequences; ATE Mean pools position errors over valid frames after sequence-wise alignment. Suite
summaries weight subsets equally. Error reductions compare the means before and after correction over
the stated model–subset pairs. Full definitions are given in Appendix D.2. The Oracle comparison in
Sec. 4.4 is explicitly distinguished by its use of ground-truth scale fitting.
4.3
Quantitative Results
We first examine metric depth across all twelve subsets, then test whether the same scale transfers to
accumulated 3D geometry and camera trajectories. The paired comparisons isolate scale correction from
changes to the underlying GFM predictions.
Metric depth across models and scenes. UniMetric reduces Abs Rel in all 108 model–subset pairs in
Table 3, with a 54.2% reduction in the mean error. Improvements span both driving and infrastructure
scenes and embodied-AI environments. As summarized in Table 4, mean Abs Rel decreases from 0.892 to
0.338 in the driving suite and from 0.671 to 0.379 in the embodied suite. The larger reduction in driving
scenes accompanies their larger uncorrected metric errors.
The effect also depends on the model’s original scale. For π3 on KITTI, Abs Rel changes only from
0.190 to 0.188, whereas VGGT-Ωon nuScenes improves from 0.979 to 0.152. These paired results
distinguish cases with substantial scale discrepancies from those where the original prediction already has
a relatively small metric error. The complete per-subset δ1 results and depth errors in metres are provided
in Appendix E.1.

Conclusion
We presented UniMetric, a training-free framework for recovering metric scale in visual geometry
foundation models. Its central insight is that uncertain size priors of familiar objects can jointly constrain a
shared scale for scene geometry and camera motion. Theoretical analysis identifies conditions under which
aggregation reduces estimation error, and controlled experiments with real VLM intervals demonstrate
gains as more observations are combined. MetricBench tests the resulting method across nine models and
twelve sensor-referenced subsets, spanning near-field manipulation, mobile robotics, and driving scenes.
The same sequence-level correction improves depth, reconstruction, and trajectory accuracy at the suite
level, with per-subset comparisons showing both its broad benefit and cases where rescaling is insufficient.
Semantic size knowledge is thus a practical source of metric information, which could enrich large-scale
visual data with metric supervision for embodied foundation-model pretraining.
Limitations. UniMetric depends on the quality of semantic priors and reconstructed object measurements,
including the availability of anchors with measurable heights. A shared scale corrects global scale
discrepancies but cannot resolve local geometric distortions or trajectory drift.

C.1
Prompt Design and Templates
Object selection and size priors. The object-anchor template combines semantic recognition with
explicit requirements on the visibility of the measured extent. A single task parameter, {ANCHOR_GROUP},
distinguishes movable objects from static scene elements. Both groups follow the same instructions for
selecting recognizable instances, providing physical-height intervals, and reporting their image locations.
Movable objects refer to semantic categories, irrespective of whether an instance is moving in the observed
frame.
The image provides evidence about category, recognizable subtype, pose, and visibility. Physical-height
intervals are obtained from semantic knowledge about the identified object. This distinction allows
appearance to inform the choice of a relevant prior without treating apparent image size as a metric
measurement. The template requests a plausible range that reflects physical variation and instructs the
VLM to omit objects for which such a range cannot be supported.
The geometric quantity of interest is the complete extent along the vertical direction. The template
therefore emphasizes identifiable upper and lower boundaries and a pose compatible with the proposed
height prior. Lateral truncation may be acceptable when the vertical extent remains clear; missing height
boundaries or an unsuitable pose make an object unreliable as a height anchor. Fig. S2 collects these
requirements in a general template.
Replace {ANCHOR_GROUP} with either “movable objects” or “static scene elements.” The template specifies
a common selection criterion and response format without prescribing a dataset-specific category inventory

General object-anchor prompt
Task
Identify familiar objects in the input RGB image that can provide reliable physical-height priors for metric-scale recovery. Requested
anchor group: {ANCHOR_GROUP}. For movable objects, actual motion is not required.
Object selection
– Select individual, recognizable instances whose semantic category supports a meaningful physical-height range.
– Use visible semantic attributes to identify a more specific subtype only when the image supports that distinction. Do not invent
an exact product identity or unobservable object properties.
– Prefer clear instances with identifiable upper and lower boundaries. Both boundaries must refer to the same object.
– The requested dimension is the complete vertical extent of the object in its observed pose, not an arbitrary visible segment or its
longest side.
– Exclude instances whose pose or deformation makes the category-based height prior inappropriate. For people, use upright
standing instances with reliable full-body height cues.
Visibility
– Distinguish image-boundary truncation from occlusion by another object.
– Lateral truncation is acceptable only if category recognition and the complete top-to-bottom extent remain reliable.
– Reject objects with missing upper or lower boundaries, height-direction occlusion, severe blur, ambiguous instance boundaries,
or insufficient visible detail.
– Do not treat reflections, shadows, or groups of objects as individual physical anchors.
Physical-height reasoning
– Provide a plausible lower and upper height bound in metres using semantic category knowledge and common knowledge of
physical objects.
– Account for plausible variation within the recognized category or subtype. Use a wider interval when the semantic evidence is
less specific.
– Do not derive metric height from pixel size, perspective, apparent distance, assumed camera height, or reconstructed depth.
– Do not substitute a prescribed category value for semantic reasoning. If no defensible height interval is available, omit the object.
– Require 0 < lower bound ≤upper bound. Do not imply exact measurement.
Localization and response
– Draw a tight bounding box around the visible body of the selected instance. Exclude neighboring objects and unnecessary
background.
– Use integer coordinates [x1, y1, x2, y2] normalized to [0, 1000], with (0, 0) at the top-left and (1000, 1000) at the
bottom-right.
– Return one JSON object per selected instance with these fields:

depending on the scene. Their suitability depends on visible surface structure and consistency with the
surrounding objects, rather than membership in a fixed category. The prompt favors a clear, approximately
horizontal planar region and excludes surfaces whose inclination, curvature, or occlusion makes that
reference ambiguous. The VLM supplies a semantic label and image region; the surface normal is
subsequently estimated from the reconstructed geometry.

Task
Identify the single most reliable visible support-surface region in the input RGB image for establishing a vertical reference in the
scene.
Surface selection
– Select a recognizable surface that supports objects or forms the local ground reference. Depending on the scene, candidates may
include a road, floor, ground surface, or tabletop.
– Require a clear, approximately planar and approximately horizontal region that is consistent with the surrounding scene.
– Prefer a sufficiently visible, unobstructed surface patch over a larger region containing mixed surfaces or foreground objects.
– Do not select a surface solely because of its category or position in the image. Do not assume that the lower image region is
always valid ground.
– Reject walls, facades, ceilings, steep ramps, curved surfaces, and regions that combine different surface orientations or height
levels.
– If a reliable horizontal support region cannot be identified, return [].

– Draw a tight bounding box around one coherent visible surface patch.
– Exclude foreground objects, people, vegetation, reflective regions, and parts of the observing camera platform or robot.
– Avoid boxes spanning surface boundaries, steps, or adjacent vertical faces.
– Use integer coordinates [x1, y1, x2, y2] normalized to [0, 1000], with (0, 0) at the top-left and (1000, 1000) at the

Semantic inference and segmentation. We use the Seed1.8 (ByteDance Seed, 2026) VLM
(seed-1.8-max) with temperature 0.2 and extended reasoning disabled. Each query receives the full
RGB frame. Object and support-region boxes prompt SAM2.1 Hiera Large (Ravi et al., 2025) to produce
one mask per region. Segmentation is performed independently on each image, so constructing the anchor
observations does not require temporal mask propagation or persistent object identities.
Anchor reliability and scale estimation. Candidate observations are screened using their semantic
confidence, visibility, and available reconstructed geometry. Geometric confidence filtering follows
the selected GFM configuration because the confidence outputs differ across geometry models. Where
supplied by the prompt, category reliability and priority complement the observation confidence in
determining its contribution to scale estimation. Category reliability uses four levels with weights 0.95,
0.85, 0.70, and 0.55; the weighting attributes are neutral when absent. The resulting weights express the
relative reliability of individual size constraints. When a category priority rank rc is supplied, its weight is

D.2
Evaluation Protocol and Metrics
Alignment. Where prediction and reference are expressed in different coordinate frames, we register them
with a rigid transform, which removes differences of origin and orientation but leaves scale untouched.
Neither similarity fitting (Umeyama, 1991) nor per-image median scaling (Eigen et al., 2014) is applied,
so an error in the predicted scale enters every metric.
Sequences. The unit of evaluation is the sequence, and UniMetric estimates one scale per sequence
(Appendix C). Each sequence is evaluated in full, without truncation or splitting. The None and UniMetric
conditions share the same model outputs and frames and differ only in the scale applied to the points and
camera translations.
Depth. Reference depth comes from the camera’s depth sensor or from LiDAR returns projected into
the image with the dataset calibration; predicted depth is the z-component of the pointmap in the same
camera frame. Reference points are kept when they are finite, lie in front of the camera and inside the
image, and are at most 100 m away; when several project to one pixel at the pointmap resolution, the
nearest is used. A pixel is valid when both depths are finite and positive. For predicted and reference
depths ˆd and d, Abs Rel is the mean of | ˆd −d|/d over valid pixels, and δ1 is the percentage of valid pixels
with max( ˆd/d, d/ ˆd) < 1.25 (Tables 3 to S4). Depth MAE and RMSE in metres are reported in Tables S5
and S6.
3D reconstruction. The predicted point cloud of a sequence is formed by placing its pointmaps in a
common frame with the predicted poses, and the reference cloud from the sensor returns with the dataset
calibration and reference poses. Both use the same samples and 100 m range as the depth evaluation and
are expressed in the first camera frame of the sequence. After rigid SE(3) registration, obtained in closed
form from paired predicted and reference points and estimating rotation and translation only, CD is the
mean of the two directed nearest-neighbour distances, in metres (Table 5); no further subsampling is
applied. Static-camera subsets are fused with their fixed reference poses and evaluated in the same way.
Ego pose. ATE Mean is the mean Euclidean distance, in metres, between predicted and reference camera
positions after rigid alignment of the trajectories with scale fixed to one (Table 6). The static roadside
subsets are evaluated for depth and reconstruction only.
Aggregation. Depth metrics are computed over the valid pixels of each frame and averaged with equal
weight over the valid frames of a subset. CD is computed per sequence and averaged with equal weight
over sequences. For ATE Mean, each sequence is rigidly aligned independently, and the position errors
are pooled over all valid trajectory frames in the subset, weighting each sequence by its number of valid
frames. Frames or sequences without valid data are excluded from the means. Suite summaries weight
subsets equally: six subsets per suite for depth and CD, and four driving and six embodied subsets for
ATE (Table 4). Reported error reductions compare means before and after correction over the stated
model–subset pairs, 100(1−¯eUniMetric/¯eNone)%, and counts of improved pairs are taken at the precision
shown in the tables.
