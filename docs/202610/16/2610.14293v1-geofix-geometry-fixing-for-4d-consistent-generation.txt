## ABSTRACT 

Video diffusion models increasingly serve as priors for 4D generation, but the generated videos often lack geometric consistency. Recent approaches address this through post-training by designing geometric rewards, yet this can degrade texture and fine detail, requiring auxiliary appearance rewards and careful reward balancing. To address this challenge, we propose GeoFix, a unified framework that improves geometric consistency and visual quality with a single geometric reward, applicable to both training and inference-time reward alignment. Our reward measures cross-view depth consistency in static scene regions, targeting structural inconsistencies without directly constraining appearance or penalizing valid object motion. Crucially, depth agreement increases with camera field-of-view overlap even in real videos and 4D datasets, allowing a smaller camera move-ment to be mistaken for better geometry. We account for this effect by calibrating the reward against the overlap-dependent trend. GeoFix achieves a 39% improve-ment in WorldScore 3D consistency and a 4% increase in VBench Quality Score. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Video diffusion models (VDMs) (Wan et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024) have achieved impressive visual fidelity by learning from large-scale video data. However, their outputs can contain geometric inconsistencies, such as background structures deforming or drifting as the camera moves (Duan et al., 2025). These artifacts limit their use as priors for 4D generation (Wu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026), which requires scene structure and dynamics to remain coherent across time and viewpoints. Improving this geometric coherence is essential for turning visually compelling videos into reliable dynamic scene representations.

1arXiv:2610.14293v1 [cs.CV] 9 Oct 2026 Preprint. Recent work has therefore explored reward-based post-training to improve the geometric consis-tency of pretrained video models. One line of work provides indirect geometric feedback by assess-ing the visual quality or plausibility of rendered views using learned preference models or VLMs (Go et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Such feedback is informative, but it conflates geometric inconsis-tency with appearance and rendering fidelity, which limits its ability to target geometric errors. Other rewards evaluate consistency more directly through RGB reconstruction errors (Go et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026), epipolar constraints (Kupyn et al., 2026), depth agreement (An et al., 2026), or agreement between geometric reprojections and tracked correspondences (Yin et al., 2026). How-ever, optimizing these rewards can favor outputs that are easier to match or reconstruct, improving consistency at the expense of visual detail (Go et al., 2026; Kupyn et al., 2026). To mitigate these ef-fects, existing methods introduce additional rewards that penalize nearly static videos (Kupyn et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026) or reward visual quality (Go et al., 2026), or use explicit preference-data fil-tering (Du et al., 2026). This reliance on auxiliary rewards or preference-data filtering complicates alignment design, requiring additional choices about reward weights or sample-selection thresholds. Therefore, current rewards that evaluate 3D consistency fall short of the broader goal of geometric alignment: correcting structural inconsistencies without harming the visual richness of pretrained models. Since geometric inconsistencies are themselves visual defects, we argue that correcting them should also improve video quality. This motivates our central question: can a single geometric reward improve both geometric consistency and video quality without auxiliary rewards? 

In this paper, we introduce GeoFix , a framework that improves both geometric consistency and video quality using a single geometric reward. GeoFix combines three components: our FO V-

CAL ibrated geometric reward ( FOCAL ), a stitched 4D reconstructor, and alignment methods. At the core of GeoFix, FOCAL measures cross-view depth consistency to target scene structure without directly requiring colors or textures to match. We restrict the comparison to static scene re-gions to avoid penalizing valid object motion as geometric inconsistency. A key observation behind FOCAL is that depth agreement is not determined by geometry alone. Even clean real videos score higher when the camera field-of-view (FOV) overlap is larger. FOCAL therefore calibrates depth agreement against the score expected for a given overlap, using the relationship estimated from clean real videos and 4D datasets and kept fixed during optimization. This reduces the extent to which greater overlap alone is rewarded as better geometry. To compute FOCAL directly from generated video latents, we adapt model stitching (Go et al., 2026) to a pretrained 4D reconstructor (Yang et al., 2026). This bypasses RGB decoding and the reconstructor’s early layers, and is more robust to the latent noise introduced during generation (Go et al., 2026). The resulting model supplies depth and camera estimates for reward computation while producing an explicit 4D Gaussian representation from the latent. FOCAL can be integrated into existing methods for both post-training and inference-time alignment. We demonstrate this within GeoFix using DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2025) for post-training, and best-of-N and DPS-style gradient-based guidance (Chung et al., 2023) at inference time. Exper-iments on WorldScore (Duan et al., 2025) and VBench (Huang et al., 2023) show that GeoFix outperforms baselines in geometric consistency and video quality, while also improving camera ac-curacy. We further demonstrate its effectiveness in 4D Gaussian scene generation, producing more stable background geometry and coherent object motion.

Geometric Rewards for Video and 4D Generation. Building on diffusion alignment methods, recent works develop rewards to improve geometric consistency in video and 4D generation. These approaches draw on two broad types of feedback: indirect quality assessments and direct geometric consistency measurements. Indirect feedback assesses the perceived quality or plausibility of gener-ated and rendered views using preference models or VLMs (Go et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Such feedback provides useful perceptual supervision, but its dependence on appearance and rendering fidelity limits its ability to isolate structural errors. Direct feedback measures reconstruction errors (Go et al., 2026; Du et al., 2026; Park et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Ge et al., 2026), epipolar constraints (Kupyn et al., 2026), geometric correspon-dence consistency (Yin et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2025), or depth agreement (An et al., 2026). However, directly optimizing consistency does not ensure better visual quality. For example, VIST3A (Go et al., 2026) reports blurred outputs when reconstruction consistency is optimized without a qual-ity reward. Epipolar-DPO (Kupyn et al., 2026) reports oversaturation with a particular correspon-dence estimator. Existing methods use auxiliary objectives (Go et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; An et al., 2026; Kupyn et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026) or preference-data filtering (Du et al., 2026), complicating alignment design, requiring additional choices about reward weights or sample-selection thresholds. We address this challenge by designing a single geometric reward that improves both geometric consistency and video quality without auxiliary rewards, eliminating the need to balance multiple reward objectives. 

## 3 METHOD 

Our goal is to improve video quality by correcting geometric inconsistencies without unnecessarily constraining the rich textures and fine visual details of pretrained video models. To this end, we present GeoFix , a unified framework that aligns video diffusion models with a geometric reward computed directly on video latents (Figure 2). The proposed reward, FOCAL (FO V-CAL ibrated geometric reward), measures cross-view depth agreement in static scene regions. We compute FO-CAL using geometry obtained from video latents through a stitched 4D reconstructor (Section 3.1). We identify an overlap bias in raw depth-consistency scores (Section 3.2). FOCAL removes this bias by calibrating the score against the depth agreement expected at a given overlap (Section 3.3). Fi-nally, GeoFix applies to any reward alignment method without modification, which we demonstrate across training and inference-time alignment (Section 3.4). 3.1 LATENT -TO -4D M ODEL STITCHING

Observation: Overlap Bias. Although Rraw evaluates structural agreement, a higher score does not necessarily indicate better geometry. We find that even real videos and multi-view 4D datasets, which we use as reference videos, exhibit lower depth reprojection errors when camera overlap is larger (Figure 3). The same dependence holds for existing geometric rewards (Section 4.5). We refer to this effect as overlap bias. These scores cannot disentangle geometric quality from camera overlap, so maximizing them favors larger camera overlap without improvement in scene geometry. To quantify camera overlap, we use the area-weighted IoU of the two cameras’ fields of view (Lee et al., 2026). We sample a reference sphere centered at the first camera on a uniform azimuth– elevation grid, and let Af denote the sampled points within camera f ’s field of view. Motivated by this observation, we use reference videos to estimate the raw depth consistency score expected at a given FOV overlap. We then evaluate each generated video relative to this overlap-dependent reference. We estimate the reference by fitting the relationship between the raw depth score and FOV overlap:

To compare a generated video’s score with this expected score, we subtract the fitted overlap-dependent term. Results. GeoFix achieves the strongest geometric consistency among all baselines while also im-proving general video quality, without an auxiliary reward. As shown in Table 1, it obtains the highest 3D consistency and photometric consistency on WorldScore. SFT alone yields only mod-erate gains, indicating that most of the improvement comes from the reward rather than from the supervised data. GeoFix also attains the best motion accuracy and motion smoothness, and remains superior or competitive on visual quality and text alignment. Note that none of these video quality metrics is used as a reward during training. Qualitatively, our method produces the cleanest 4D scene renderings, with fewer artifacts and sub-stantially reduced background drift and flickering compared with the baselines. Geometric reward vs. video-quality reward. VBench rewards capture general video quality but are not de-signed to assess geometric consistency, and directly opti-mizing them can lead to degenerate solutions. Following DDRL (Ye et al., 2025), we train the VDM with direct reward fine-tuning on five VBench (Huang et al., 2023) rewards. The VBench-trained model produces saturated videos (Figure 8), and training longer adds blur and grid artifacts. The failure follows from what these rewards measure. Several VBench metrics reward high DINO or CLIP feature similarity between frames, collapsing inter-frame variation at the cost of blur and grid artifacts. Our geometric reward, in contrast, improves consistency and even video quality, which supports our claim that correcting geometric defects is an effective route to better video quality. Existing measures of geometric con-sistency are easier to satisfy when camera moves less, so these scores can be increased without improving geometry at all. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we presented FOCAL, a geometric consistency reward for video diffusion models, and GeoFix, which applies FOCAL to reward alignment methods to improve geometric consistency and visual quality. Our reward evaluates cross-view depth consistency in static scene regions, targeting structural inconsistencies while preserving valid object motion. The core of our method is calibrat-ing depth agreement against the expected value at a given camera overlap, reducing the extent to which greater overlap alone is rewarded as an improvement in geometric consistency. FOCAL is computed directly from video latents through a stitched 4D reconstructor, without VAE decoding. Experiments across training and inference-time reward alignment demonstrate improvements in ge-ometric consistency, video quality and camera accuracy. We further show that GeoFix produces coherent 4D scenes compared to baselines. Overall, GeoFix advances video diffusion models as priors for visually rich and geometrically coherent dynamic scene generation. 9Preprint.

A.1.1 TASKS AND DATASETS 

All three tasks produce a 4D Gaussian Splatting (4DGS) scene from a video diffusion model (VDM). In each case, a NeoVerse (Yang et al., 2026) 4D reconstructor, stitched to the VDM latent space, decodes the VDM latents into a 4DGS scene. 

Text-to-4DGS (T2V-CompBench). We use 200 T2V-CompBench (Sun et al., 2025) prompts and generate one video per prompt. At generation time, each prompt is wrapped in a camera-motion sentence chosen at random from a fixed template set. This gives the 4D reconstructor parallax to recover geometry from. The augmentation is applied identically to every method. 

Image-to-4D generation (VBench-I2V). We use the 100 image–caption pairs of the VBench-I2V benchmark (Huang et al., 2024). The input image is used as the first frame, and we apply camera prompt augmentation. For evaluation, we render every generated 4D scene along the same ReCam-Master novel-view camera trajectory. 

Camera-controlled image-to-4D generation. We use the 355 image–caption pairs of the VBench-I2V benchmark (Huang et al., 2024) and assign each image one of ten target camera trajectories (Table 5). The pan trajectories translate the camera at a constant rate in the image plane. The zoom trajectories translate it along the optical axis at twice the pan rate. A.1.2 BASELINES 

Training-time alignment. We compare against the base Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (Wan et al., 2025) with-out fine-tuning and three fine-tuned baselines: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) only, VIST3A (Go et al., 2026) with SFT, and World-R1 (Wang et al., 2026) with SFT. For a controlled comparison, all fine-tuned models, including ours, use the same SFT dataset, and the same training GPU hours, and are decoded by the same frozen stitched 4D reconstructor. We use the same frozen stitched 4D reconstructor for reward computation in VIST3A and World-R1. 

Image-to-4D generation. We use the publicly released checkpoints of MoGe4D (Zhang et al., 2026) and 4DNeX (Chen et al., 2025). Both baselines generate their respective 4D point-cloud representations at 512 ×512 resolution. Since their output representations differ from our 4DGS representation, we render all methods under a common target camera trajectory for evaluation. We derive the target camera trajectory from ReCamMaster. The predicted camera poses are first con-verted to the OpenCV camera convention and expressed as relative poses with respect to the first frame. The same relative camera motion is then applied consistently to all frames and methods. To account for the scale ambiguity of monocular 4D reconstruction, we normalize the translational magnitude of the camera trajectory such that it corresponds to 0.25 times the median scene depth. When projecting the 4D point clouds to the target views, multiple points may map to the same image pixel; in such cases, we use a standard depth test and retain the point closest to the camera. The rendering procedure is adapted to the representation produced by each baseline. 4DNeX pre-dicts a separate point map for each frame together with a corresponding input-view camera. For each frame, we transform its point map from the original input-view camera to the target ReCamMaster 14 Preprint. camera and rasterize the warped points in the target view. In contrast, MoGe4D represents the dy-namic scene as a set of points initialized from the pixels of the first frame and tracked throughout the entire 41 frame sequence, rather than predicting an independent point map for every frame. As MoGe4D directly generates the dynamic point-cloud representation without an explicit per-frame in-put camera, we render the tracked 3D point positions at each frame directly from the corresponding target camera pose. 

Test-time steering. Test-time steering uses no trained checkpoint. We apply every steering method to the base camera-control model Wan2.2-Fun-5B-Control-Camera and compare unguided sampling (None), Best-of-N (BoN) and DPS (Chung et al., 2023). A.1.3 TRAINING 

We fine-tune Wan2.2-TI2V-5B with LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) (rank 4, α=8 ) on the query, key, value, and output projections of both the self- and cross-attention layers. The stitched 4D reconstructor is kept frozen. Each optimizer step combines a supervised loss with an online reinforcement-learning loss based on DiffusionNFT (Zheng et al., 2025). 

Supervised term. We use the MultiCamVideo (Bai et al., 2025) (120,460 multi-camera videos) and the short-clip split of UltraVideo (Xue et al., 2025) (42,184 clips). Each video is 13 frames at 480 ×832. With probability 0.5, the first frame is used as an image condition, so the model is trained for both text-to-video and image-to-video generation. 

Reinforcement-learning term. Each step draws 8 prompts from the VideoUFO (Wang & Yang, 2025) training prompts and samples a group of 8 rollouts per prompt (64 rollouts per step). Each rollout has 13 frames and is sampled with 10 denoising steps and no classifier-free guidance. Each rollout is scored by our reward with γ=0 .7. The KL coefficient is βKL =5 ×10 −3.

Optimization. We use AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with a learning rate of 1×10 −4,

(β1, β 2)=(0 .9, 0.999) , a weight decay of 1×10 −4, gradient clipping at 1.0, and bf16 mixed pre-cision, and we train for 1,000 steps. A.1.4 INFERENCE

For image-to-4DGS generation, the input image is fixed to the first latent frame at every denoising step. VideoPhy-2 (Bansal et al., 2025): We use VideoPhy-2-AutoEval ( videophy 2 auto ), an mPLUG-Owl-Video model with a LLaMA-7B backbone, further fine-tuned from VideoCon-Physics on VideoPhy-2 human annotations. We evaluate 32 sampled frames with the official physical-commonsense template (Figure 9) using greedy decoding. We report the mean physical commonsense (PC) score on a 1–5 scale. 4. CLIP score (Radford et al., 2021): The cosine similarity between CLIP ViT-L/14 image and text embeddings, scaled by CLIP’s logit scale, computed on every frame and averaged over frames. 5. Camera accuracy : We compare the per-frame cameras predicted by the 4D reconstructor with the target trajectory. Predicted poses are defined only up to a similarity transform, so we express both trajectories relative to their first frame and report three errors: rotation error (the mean geodesic distance between the relative rotations, in degrees), translation-direction error (the median angle between the relative translation vectors, in degrees), and normalized ATE (the RMSE of the camera centers after a least-squares scale fit, divided by the maximum extent of the target trajectory). We use the median for translation-direction error because the first frames have almost no translation, which makes their direction ill-conditioned.

Figure 14(a) shows additional qualitative results from training with the five VBench (Huang et al., 2023) re-wards used by DDRL (Ye et al., 2025). The VBench-trained model exhibits excessive saturation and unnatural color casts, whereas our geometric reward produces more natural colors and cleaner scene structure. To examine the contribution of each reward, Figure 14(b) shows results obtained by optimizing each of the five metrics individually in the direct reward fine-tuning setting. The re-sulting artifacts reveal distinct failure modes, which we interpret below in terms of what each metric measures. 

Imaging quality. Optimizing imaging quality produces strong color banding and repetitive, high-contrast textures that obscure the scene. This reward averages the frame-wise predictions of MUSIQ (Ke et al., 2021), an image-quality predictor trained to assess distortions such as blur, noise, and exposure errors. The observed patterns suggest that direct optimization can exploit the predictor’s learned responses to contrast and texture, amplifying artificial detail without improving perceptual quality. A high predicted image-quality score does not impose explicit constraints on scene geometry or temporal coherence, allowing such structured artifacts to emerge. 21 Preprint. 

Background consistency. Optimizing background consistency produces blurred content overlaid with a regular grid. Despite its name, this metric compares global CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) image features extracted from whole frames, without explicitly isolating the background. It aver-ages the CLIP feature similarity of each frame to the first frame and to its immediately preceding frame. Consequently, reducing frame-to-frame feature variation can be favored even at the expense of image fidelity: blur can suppress changing details, while a persistent grid can provide a common appearance across frames. This offers a shortcut to feature consistency without preserving a sharp, geometrically consistent background under camera motion. 

Motion smoothness. Optimizing motion smoothness leads to an almost black, nearly featureless output. The metric interpolates intermediate frames from neighboring frames and rewards a small pixel-wise difference between the interpolated and actual frames. Temporally uniform, low-contrast content makes this prediction task easier: suppressing visible structures and their motion can re-duce interpolation error without generating realistic dynamics. The observed collapse is consistent with this incentive, since the reward itself does not require nontrivial motion or recognizable scene content. 

Subject consistency. Optimizing subject consistency replaces recognizable objects with repeti-tive, smooth blue patterns. This metric measures DINO (Caron et al., 2021) feature similarity to the first and preceding frames; it does not explicitly verify the presence or geometry of the intended subject. Repeating similar structures across frames can therefore stabilize the descriptor even when subject identity is lost. The resulting pattern illustrates how consistency in feature space can be achieved by suppressing meaningful appearance variation, rather than maintaining a coherent object as its pose or viewpoint changes. 

Aesthetic quality. Optimizing aesthetic quality yields a collage-like image with repeated faces and decorative fragments instead of a coherent scene. The reward averages a LAION (Schuhmann et al., 2022) aesthetic predictor applied to CLIP image embeddings of individual frames. Such optimization can amplify visual motifs associated with high predicted aesthetic scores while leaving their spatial arrangement and temporal evolution unconstrained. These examples illustrate the gap between evaluating generated videos with perceptual proxies and directly optimizing those proxies. Temporal feature similarity can admit degenerate content, while frame-wise quality predictors can favor artifacts outside their intended image distribution. Our re-ward instead evaluates cross-view geometric agreement in static regions, providing an explicit con-straint on scene structure. The comparison in Figure 14(a) supports the finding in Section 4.5 that improving geometric consistency can also improve visual quality. 

## E LIMITATIONS 

Our method inherits the bias of the pretrained VDM and the 4D reconstruction model. The depth and camera predictions are distilled from a pretrained 4D reconstructor, so failure cases of the teacher can produce inaccurate reward signals. Errors in the dynamic segmentation stage can penalize valid motion or leave inconsistent regions unconstrained. We expect that 4D reconstructors and dynamic tracking will further improve. Finally, computing the reward requires running the stitched recon-structor and the motion segmentation network, and gradient-based guidance additionally requires backpropagation through the reconstructor, which increases training and inference cost. 22
