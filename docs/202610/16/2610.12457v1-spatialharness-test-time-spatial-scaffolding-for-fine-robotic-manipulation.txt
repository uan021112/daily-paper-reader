# Abstract 

Frontier multimodal foundation models (e.g., GPT-6 Astra) have recently shown strong potential for direct robotic con-trol, yet their performance on fine manipulation remains limited. We argue that an important source of failure is not necessarily insufficient policy capability, but insuffi-cient spatial observability, where task-critical spatial re-lationships may be poorly revealed by the existing phys-ical camera setup. We introduce SpatialHarness, a test-time embodied harness that provides test-time spatial scaf-folding for fine robotic manipulation without policy fine-tuning or changes to the physical sensing setup. Spa-tialHarness maintains an online simulated scene synchro-nized with real-world execution, identifies task-critical spa-tial relationships, and renders complementary virtual views that expose them to a frozen multimodal policy. To keep the simulated scene aligned during interaction, we de-velop interaction-aware scene synchronization that distin-guishes static, held, and transition modes. We evaluate SpatialHarness on four real-robot manipulation tasks span-ning precise geometric alignment, object-relative place-ment, and articulated-object interaction. Using the same frozen GPT-6 Astra policy, SpatialHarness substantially im-proves task success, including from 26.7% to 66.7% on plug insertion and from 0% to 100% on Tower of Hanoi. These results indicate that improving spatial observabil-ity at test time can unlock fine-manipulation capabilities already present in strong multimodal foundation models. Given visual observa-tions and language instructions, these models can directly generate robot actions and refine subsequent decisions through closed-loop interaction, without task-specific pol-icy training. This emerging paradigm suggests a compelling alternative to robotic foundation models [4, 9, 15, 19, 32]. Instead of adapting the model to each robotic setting, an embodied harness can instead structure how the model ob-serves, reasons about, and interacts with the physical world. Existing harnesses have explored action interfaces, demonstrations, interaction histories, and execution feed-back to better expose the capabilities already present in general-purpose multimodal foundation models [7, 12, 23]. Nevertheless, strong high-level reasoning does not neces-sarily translate into reliable fine robotic manipulation [21, 31]. Tasks such as insertion, stacking, or placement onto arXiv:2610.12457v1 [cs.RO] 8 Oct 2026 narrow structures depend on task- critical spatial relation-ships, whether a plug is aligned with its socket, whether a hole is centered over a peg, or whether a grasped object is correctly positioned relative to a supporting surface. These relationships can be difficult to infer from the fixed cam-eras available in a real deployment, particularly near con-tact, where the gripper and manipulated objects frequently occlude the most relevant geometry [25]. In such cases, failure may arise not because the policy lacks the necessary manipulation capability, but because the spatial evidence re-quired to exercise that capability is poorly observable. This observation motivates us to ask a critical question: How can we improve the spatial observability of a robot’s environ-ment at test time, without retraining the policy or modifying the physical sensing setup? 

To address this question, we introduce SpatialHarness ,a test-time embodied harness that provides test-time spa-tial scaffolding for fine robotic manipulation. SpatialHar-ness maintains an online simulated scene synchronized with real-world execution and uses it to construct task-relevant spatial context, without the need to modify the policy or add extra physical cameras, as illustrated in Figure 1. Before ex-ecution, the multimodal foundation model analyzes the task goal and identifies task-critical spatial relationships. Spa-tialHarness then configures virtual viewpoints that expose these relationships, complementing the observations from the robot’s physical cameras. During execution, the harness renders the complementary virtual views from the continu-ally updated simulated scene and supplies them to the pol-icy together with structured spatial information, including object 6-DoF poses and estimates of rendering reliability. A key challenge is maintaining sufficiently accurate spa-tial context as physical interaction changes the scene. Pure physics rollout is unreliable during manipulation because errors in physical parameters and contact modeling can cause simulated object states to diverge from real execution, particularly during grasping and release [5, 20]. Spatial-Harness therefore performs interaction-aware online scene synchronization, combining visual correction grounded in real observations with programmatic correction. We dis-tinguish three object–gripper interaction modes, including static, held, and transition, and exploit different physical and observational priors in each case. Static objects pre-serve or visually update their aligned states, while objects under sustained held are propagated using robot action and programmatically validated against real observations and gripper geometry. In transition mode, small pre-contact errors can substantially alter simulated outcomes. Spatial-Harness performs hypothesis replay from a pre-interaction checkpoint using candidate poses and measured robot mo-tion, then selects a corrected state through interaction con-sistency filtering and contour matching. Consequently, Spa-tialHarness uses simulation as a runtime spatial interface: real observations ground the scene, while complementary virtual views expose task-critical spatial relationships that may be hidden, ambiguous, or poorly conditioned in the physical camera configuration. physical camera observa-tions. We evaluate SpatialHarness on four real-robot manip-ulation tasks spanning local geometric alignment, object-relative placement, and articulated-object interaction. Us-ing the same frozen GPT-6 Astra policy for both the base-line and SpatialHarness, we observe substantial improve-ments in task success, for instance, the success rate for plug insertion increases from 26.7% to 66.7% , and 0% to 100% on Tower of Hanoi, as shown in Fig. 1. These improve-ments are achieved without policy fine-tuning or changes to the physical camera setup, highlighting the importance of spatial observability as a test-time factor in fine robotic manipulation. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce SpatialHarness and formulate test-time spa-tial scaffolding as a mechanism for improving fine manip-ulation with frozen multimodal policies. SpatialHarness augments runtime observations with task-relevant virtual views and structured spatial context, without modifying the underlying policy or physical sensing setup. • We develop an interaction-aware online scene synchro-nization framework that combines visual and program-matic correction across static, held, and transition modes to maintain alignment between the simulated scene and the real environment during manipulation. • We demonstrate on real-robot fine-manipulation tasks that improving spatial observability can substantially in-crease the success rate for a strong multimodal foundation model used as a robot policy. 

# 2. Related Work 

2.1. Frontier Multimodal Models and Embodied Harnesses 

Vision-language-action models such as RT-2 [32], Open-VLA [19], and π0 [4] use robot data to adapt pretrained vision-language knowledge for action generation. As general-purpose foundation models advance [2, 8, 17, 28], frontier closed-source multimodal models have demon-strated the ability to perform robotic manipulation directly by generating actions from visual observations and adjust-ing them based on execution feedback. To support this di-rect use of general-purpose models, embodied harnesses provide interaction interfaces and structured context that help models apply their existing capabilities to specific robotic tasks. Show-Harness [7] provides a fine-grained action interface with clear semantics. RoboDawn [12] and RoboICL [23] structure demonstrations and experience gathered during interaction to help models adapt to tasks. These methods structure action interfaces and interaction context to support robotic manipulation. 

2.2. Real-to-Sim and Digital Twins 

Real-to-sim research constructs simulation environments from real-world observations for robot learning and inter-action. NeRF [26] and 3D Gaussian Splatting [18] pro-vide the basis for reconstructing renderable scene represen-tations and synthesizing novel views, while Re³Sim [13] and ARSTAG [22] further construct simulation scenes and task-relevant assets offline from real-world observations for demonstration generation and policy learning. Beyond of-fline learning, reconstructed scenes are also used during deployment for planning, state tracking, and policy obser-vations. Simify [16] reconstructs simulation-ready assets from a single RGB-D observation and uses evolutionary search with parallel physics rollouts at test time to jointly optimize object placement order and goal poses. GaussT-win [5] aims to unify simulation and online tracking of rigid bodies and deformable linear objects such as ropes, combin-ing physics-based prediction with visual correction to main-tain the interaction state; SyncTwin [14] targets safe ma-nipulation in dynamic and partially occluded scenes, con-tinuously updating object poses to provide planners with current scene geometry for collision avoidance and grasp-ing. Real-is-Sim [1] uses a synchronized twin to maintain a consistent policy observation interface across training, simulation-based evaluation, and real-world deployment of learned policies. SpatialHarness uses synchronized scenes to provide complementary virtual views and structured spa-tial information to frozen multimodal policies at runtime, improving spatial observability for precise manipulation. 

2.3. Viewpoint Selection for Robotic Manipulation 

Prior work improves spatial perception and action predic-tion in manipulation policies through multi-view rendering or active camera control. RVT [10] renders scene point clouds into predefined virtual views, allowing policies to use viewing directions unconstrained by physical camera placement. RVT-2 [11] further generates zoomed-in local views around the manipulation region predicted by the net-work to improve localization accuracy in small-scale ma-nipulation. To address occlusion and insufficient detail from fixed viewpoints, ActiveVLA [25] selects candidate views around model-identified regions of interest based on vis-ibility, distance, and view diversity, and combines them with local zooming to improve fine-grained action predic-tion. TVVE [3] and SaPaVe [24] learn to acquire observa-tions that facilitate manipulation: the former uses reinforce-ment learning to train a network to predict virtual camera poses from point clouds, optimizing viewpoints with feed-back such as action prediction errors to improve coverage of task-relevant information; the latter learns head-camera mo-tions and robot actions from camera-control and manipula-tion data, enabling the robot to find targets that are occluded or outside its field of view and continue manipulation while adapting to viewpoint changes. 

# 3. Method 

3.1. Overview 

SpatialHarness is a test-time embodied harness that pro-vides test-time spatial scaffolding for a frozen multimodal policy. Its spatial context comprises complementary virtual views and structured spatial information. At each decision step, the policy receives this spatial context together with the task instruction, real observations, robot states. The pol-icy integrates these inputs to assess the current scene and generate robot actions through tool calls. We use model for the same underlying multimodal foundation model when it reasons about constraints, generates constraint programs, or configures virtual viewpoints, and policy when it generates actions. The harness executes the programs, advances the simulation, synchronizes the scene, and renders the views. We first describe scene synchronization, followed by task-oriented view selection. 

3.2. Interaction-Aware Scene Synchronization 

As illustrated in Figure 2, interaction-aware scene synchro-nization maintains the online simulated scene from which the harness constructs spatial context. Before task execu-tion, the harness aligns the scene with the initial observa-tion. After each action, it synchronizes the scene, providing updated geometry for auxiliary virtual views and object 6-DoF poses. Physics simulation propagates simulation states through physical interactions. To account for errors in phys-ical modeling, visual correction uses real observations to select or replace object poses and replay states, while pro-grammatic correction uses model-generated constraint pro-grams to check or adjust candidates. 3.3. Task-Oriented View Selection 

Task-oriented view selection configures virtual viewpoints before execution so that complementary virtual views reveal task-critical spatial relationships. Guided by a multistep reasoning prompt, the model first identifies the criteria for task success from the task instruction and derives the spa-tial constraints that the relevant objects must satisfy upon task completion. Using object properties extracted from the 3D assets, it determines whether these constraints primar-ily concern the relative positions and orientations of whole objects or how local structures fit together based on their geometry. For spatial constraints concerning relations between whole objects, the model selects two virtual viewpoints for global views, with virtual viewpoint selection restricted to specified horizontal and vertical spatial ranges. For spatial constraints involving how local structures fit together, it se-lects virtual viewpoints for one local view and one global view. The model determines the virtual viewpoint and fram-ing for the local view from the relevant structures’ shapes, positions, and orientations, while the global view covers the robot and task-relevant objects. The current real viewing di-rection serves as a reference for virtual viewpoint selection. The harness configures virtual cameras at the proposed virtual viewpoints and renders candidate views. The model checks whether the relevant objects or parts are simultane-ously visible, whether task-critical spatial relationships can be clearly assessed, and whether each view reveals infor-mation difficult to discern in the current real observation. For unsuitable virtual viewpoints, the model proposes small adjustments to camera position, orientation, and framing, followed by re-rendering and inspection. When necessary, it requests that the harness simulate the motion of relevant objects to check whether the candidate views remain suit-able when task-critical spatial relationships need to be as-sessed. During execution, the virtual viewpoints remain fixed, while the harness performs auxiliary virtual render-ing from the continually updated online simulated scene. 

# 4. Experiments 

We evaluate SpatialHarness on four real robot tasks to as-sess its benefits for fine manipulation. With the manipu-lation model and real camera configuration held fixed, we compare task success rates and model query overhead with and without supplementary spatial observations. 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Implementation Details. We use a Franka Research 3 arm and two Intel RealSense RGB-D cameras, one mounted on the wrist and one providing a fixed third-person view. Both methods use Robocurve’s Inspect Robots control framework[29] and GPT-6 Astra [27] (low reasoning effort) as the manipulation model. The baseline receives the task instruction, RGB images from both real cameras, measured robot states, and execution feedback. SpatialHarness adds two virtual RGB views and object poses, with the rest of the control pipeline unchanged. Virtual viewpoints are selected by GPT-6 Astra (xhigh reasoning effort) before task execu-tion. MuJoCo [30] is used for physics simulation and image rendering. 

Robot Control Protocol. Both methods share the action representation, available action chunk lengths, control fre-quency, and local execution checks. Each action is a seven-dimensional vector (a=[ δx ,δy ,δz ,δr x,δr y ,δr z ,g]), compris-ing end-effector translation increments, rotation vector in-crements, and a gripper command. At each call, the model jointly generates an action chunk and its length H ∈

10 , 20 , 30 . Before execution, local checks verify output va-lidity and compliance with execution constraints, without additional model calls. 

Tasks. Figure 3 presents the four tasks: • Block stacking requires grasping a block, aligning it above another block, and releasing it into a stable stack. • Plug insertion requires matching the position and orienta-tion of a plug to its socket before insertion. • Tower of Hanoi evaluates placement of a holed piece over a target peg, where the policy must assess the relative alignment of the hole and peg. • Open drawer and place block requires opening an articu-lated drawer and then placing a block inside. Together, these tasks range from alignment of mating struc-tures to placement with more permissive final positioning. 

Metrics. Each method is evaluated in 15 trials per task. We report task success rate, the fraction of trials that achieve the task objective, and total policy queries, the cumulative number of requests to the manipulation model for action decisions across all trials. The latter measures model inter-action overhead. 

4.2. Experimental Results 

4.2.1. Quantitative Performance Real-world Experiment Results. As shown in Figure 4, , with the manipulation policy and control protocol held fixed, SpatialHarness improves success rates on all four tasks involving fine manipulation. The average success rate increases from 18.33% to 83.33%, a gain of 65.00 percent-age points. The gains are particularly pronounced on the Tower of Hanoi and drawer tasks: the baseline fails in all 15 trials of each task, whereas our method succeeds in 15 and 10 trials, respectively. Under the same real camera con-figuration, these two tasks depend strongly on auxiliary vir-tual views that reveal task-critical spatial relationships. The original observations cannot clearly reveal the relative off-set between the hole and the peg or the contact relationship between the gripper and the handle, limiting the execution of key alignment and grasping steps. These auxiliary vir-tual views make these local spatial relationships easier to discern, providing the same manipulation policy with the necessary visual information to complete these steps. More-over, plug insertion places the greatest demands on preci-sion among the tasks evaluated, requiring precise alignment in both position and orientation. Using the available real ob-servations, the baseline achieves a success rate of 26.67%, indicating that the original views support successful inser-tion in some trials. SpatialHarness increases the success rate to 66.67%, a gain of 40.00 percentage points. This result highlights another benefit of auxiliary virtual views: even when the original observations already support successful execution, more complete spatial information can still help identify subtle misalignments and substantially improve in-sertion success. Block stacking follows the same trend, with the success rate increasing from 46.67% to 100%. Together, these two results show that auxiliary virtual views can fur-ther improve fine manipulation success on tasks where the baseline already succeeds in some trials.

# 5. Limitations 

Our current study focuses on rigid and articulated ob-jects and does not address deformable-object manipula-tion, where changes in object geometry introduce additional challenges for scene representation and synchronization. Moreover, interaction-aware scene synchronization incurs some time overhead, particularly during grasp/release tran-sitions that require hypothesis replay, interaction consis-tency filtering, and contour matching, limiting the system’s responsiveness. Finally, SpatialHarness currently relies on powerful multimodal foundation models for spatial reason-ing and action generation, and its effectiveness with smaller models remains unverified. As multimodal models continue to advance, smaller open-source models may become viable replacements for these frontier models, potentially reducing inference costs and facilitating broader deployment. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We presented SpatialHarness, a test-time embodied harness that improves spatial observability for fine robotic manip-ulation. Through interaction-aware scene synchronization and task-oriented view selection, it provides frozen multi-modal policies with spatial context comprising complemen-tary virtual views and structured spatial information. Real-robot experiments show substantially improved manipula-tion success without policy fine-tuning or changes to phys-ical cameras, highlighting how test-time spatial scaffolding can help realize existing model capabilities.
