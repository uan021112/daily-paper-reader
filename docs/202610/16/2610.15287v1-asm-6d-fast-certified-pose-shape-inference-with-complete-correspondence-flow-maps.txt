ASM-6D: Fast Certified Pose–Shape Inference with
Complete Correspondence Flow Maps
Ho Jin Choi, Minku Kim, and Nadia Figueroa
Abstract—Category-level 6D pose and shape estimation re-
quires recovering an object’s pose and geometry from par-
tial observations without an exact instance model. We present
ASM-6D, which couples generative complete-geometry prediction
with explicit category-level pose–shape inference through shared
correspondences. Given an RGB-D image, ASM-6D generates
alternative complete 3D correspondence hypotheses whose point
identities are shared with a category-level Active Shape Model
(ASM), including occluded points. Each hypothesis is fitted
directly for rotation, translation, metric scale, and instance
shape without a separate correspondence-assignment stage. We
analytically eliminate translation and shape coefficients, reducing
fitting to a nine-variable scaled-rotation optimization problem
that enables efficient batched evaluation of multiple hypotheses
in real time. ASM-6D achieves state-of-the-art performance on
ROPE and HouseCat6D benchmarks. Ablations demonstrate the
importance of generative correspondence prediction and active
shape variation. The representation also supports shape com-
pletion, shape-dependent pose ambiguity, tracking, and robotic
grasping at 6-38 Hz, depending on the inference budget. Project
website: https://asm6d-project.github.io/
Index Terms—Active shape models, category-level 6D object
pose estimation, indexed correspondences, pose–shape optimiza-
tion, RGB-D perception.
I. INTRODUCTION
Category-level 6D pose and shape estimation seeks to
recover an object’s pose, metric scale, and geometry without
access to its exact instance model. For robotic interaction, pose
alone does not describe the surfaces available for manipulation,
while RGB-D observations often reveal only part of the ob-
ject. A useful representation must therefore recover complete
geometry while retaining the structure needed for joint pose–
shape inference, and represent alternative interpretations when
occlusion, symmetry, or intra-category variation makes the
observation ambiguous, all the while running in real time to
be useful for downstream robotic manipulation tasks.
Existing approaches provide complementary pieces of
this problem. Canonical-coordinate methods associate ob-
served points with a normalized object frame [1], while
completion-based methods recover missing geometry, includ-
ing completed-keypoint representations [2]–[4]. Generative
pose methods instead retain alternative solutions directly in
transformation space [5], [6]. Active Shape Models (ASMs)
provide a compact representation for estimating pose and

[7]–[10]. However, learned completion and explicit pose–
shape inference remain largely separate. Completion methods
recover geometry, while ASM-based inference assumes that
correspondences are already available. This gap is particularly
relevant for object manipulation in dynamic environments,
where inference must also remain computationally practical;
recent generative category-level methods operate around 9–11

ASM-6D bridges these reliability and computational gaps
by learning a distribution over complete camera-frame cor-
respondences whose indexing is shared with a category-level
ASM. Each sample is both a complete geometric interpretation
of the observation and a directly solvable pose–scale–shape
hypothesis that can be computed in real-time (up to 38.85
Hz in the lowest-budget known-prior setting; Table III) while
taking an RGB-D ROI directly, without requiring a mask-
segmented point cloud. ASM-6D represents each object in-
terpretation as a complete indexed correspondence hypothesis
(Fig. 1). A hypothesis predicts a camera-frame 3D location
for every point of a shared template, including occluded
points, with predicted point i corresponding directly to ASM
point i. The indexing is constructed offline by deforming a
common template onto normalized training instances while
preserving vertex identities; the same indices define the ASM
and generator targets. Each hypothesis therefore provides com-
plete candidate geometry together with the correspondences
required for direct pose, metric-scale, and shape fitting. Multi-
ple samples represent alternative interpretations of ambiguous
observations.

and shape requires jointly estimating rotation, translation,

In addition to offering reliability and computational effi-

pose estimation performance on both ROPE [5] and House-

ROPE, ASM-6D improves IoU50 AUC from 21.0 to 30.95
over RFMPose [6]. In controlled ablation experiments, we
show benefits from generative correspondence prediction and
active shape variation. We additionally evaluate the capabilities
of ASM-6D on shape completion, shape-conditioned pose
ambiguity, prior selection, tracking, and robotic grasping.
Thus, our contributions are the following:
• Generative complete correspondences for pose–shape
inference: We learn a distribution over complete camera-
frame correspondences in the same indexed space as a
category-level ASM, so each sample can be fitted directly
for pose, metric scale, and instance shape. For training, we
use the Omni6DPose [5] SOPE dataset (475K images, 4,385
CAD models across 156 categories), followed by evaluation
and adaptation on HouseCat6D [12].
• Scale-aware joint pose–shape inference: We analytically
reduce joint pose, metric-scale, and ASM-shape estimation
to a nine-variable scaled-rotation optimization problem and
solve many hypothesis–prior fits efficiently in batch, en-
abling 6–38 Hz full-pipeline inference depending on the
inference budget.
• Empirical validation and representation analysis: ASM-
6D achieves state-of-the-art pose performance on ROPE [5]
(30.95% IoU50) and HouseCat6D [12] (85.7% IoU50),
alongside 73.7% exact 156-way prior selection on Real-
NOCS [1]. Controlled ablations and experiments demon-
strate completion accuracy under occlusions, robust han-
dling of rotational ambiguity, and real-time tracking and
grasping experiments, characterizing the representation.

II. RELATED WORK
Canonical correspondences and shape representations.
Several recent methods establish geometric structure from
the observation. NOCS [1] predicts canonical coordinates for
observed object pixels, establishing correspondences between
image observations and a normalized object frame. Subsequent
approaches deform category-level shape priors [13] or learn
canonical and implicit shape representations for joint pose,
size, and geometry estimation [2], [3]. While these methods
improved shape estimation performance, their correspondence
representations are primarily tied to observed image evidence
or canonical object space. ASM-6D instead predicts the
camera-frame locations of a complete, fixed-index template,
including occluded points. The resulting geometry preserves
the point identities required for direct joint pose–shape fitting.
Completion-based pose estimation. Point-cloud completion
methods reconstruct missing geometry from partial observa-
tions [14]–[16], but completion alone does not specify how
the recovered geometry should enter explicit category-level
pose–shape inference. ComPose [4] more closely couples the
two tasks by progressively recovering keypoints and dense
geometry together with canonical correspondences. ASM-6D
instead represents each generated completion as a complete
fixed-index correspondence set shared directly with a paramet-
ric category model, enabling explicit fitting for pose, metric
scale, and instance shape without a separate correspondence-
assignment stage.
Generative pose estimation. GenPose [17] models category-
level pose distributions using score-based diffusion, while
RFMPose [6] and Flow6D [18] use flow-based generation or
refinement in transformation space. Pose-space samples retain
transformation ambiguity but do not explicitly represent the
completed geometry or indexed point identities supporting
each pose, limiting direct pose–shape fitting.
ASM-6D instead generates complete indexed geometry, so
each sample specifies both a possible pose after fitting and the
complete instance geometry associated with that interpretation.
Geometric pose–shape fitting. Active Shape Models (ASMs)
[7] represent intra-category variation using a low-dimensional
linear shape basis. Shi et al. [8] jointly estimate pose and
shape from semantic keypoints using a certifiable semidef-
inite relaxation, while ShapeICP [9] jointly refines pose,
shape, and correspondence. Recent specialized solvers further
demonstrate efficient ASM fitting with a posteriori optimality
certification [10]. These methods address geometric inference
once suitable observations or correspondences are available.
ASM-6D complements this line by generating the complete
indexed correspondences required by the shape model and
jointly fitting pose, metric scale, and instance shape across
multiple hypotheses and candidate priors.
In contrast to methods that address correspondence gener-
ation or pose–shape fitting separately, ASM-6D couples both
through a shared indexed representation that remains practical
for online inference.

B. Active Shape Model Construction
1) Indexed Shape Standardization: An ASM requires a
fixed set of consistently indexed points N across training in-


4
stances. We construct the library from all 4,385 Omni6DPose
CAD models [5] spanning 156 categories. For each CAD
model, we sample 216 surface points only as the dense target
surface for deformation. Then, we normalize the shape to a
centered unit cube, and deform a common N = 642 indexed
icosphere vertices onto the surface by minimizing the Sinkhorn
divergence [19] (Fig. 3).
Vertex indices are preserved during deformation, yielding
a consistent template indexing across standardized instances.
The resulting points define both the prediction targets and
the ASM coordinates, providing approximate cross-instance
correspondences for PCA modeling and point-to-point fitting.
2) PCA-Based Active Shape Models: For each category,
PCA is applied to the vectorized standardized point sets to
obtain a mean shape b0 ∈RN×3 and K retained deformation
modes or PCA rank B ∈RN×3×K. We retain at most 48
modes, which capture 99.9% of the variance or more across
all classes; the rank ablation in Appendix E shows that most
task-level gains are already captured by the first few modes.
Each mode is scaled by its training-set standard deviation,
yielding unit-variance coefficients. The regularization term
λc∥c∥2
2 in Eq. (2) therefore applies uniform penalization in
the normalized PCA coordinate system, corresponding to a
scaled Mahalanobis penalty in the original PCA coordinates.
3) Template-Point Projection and Visibility Labeling:
Training targets for the correspondence generator are obtained
by transforming and projecting the standardized template
points using the annotated object pose. Each of the N = 642
template indices is supervised with crop-normalized image
coordinates, camera-frame metric depth, and a visibility label.
Our Omni6DPose [5] model is trained on SOPE–a synthetic
dataset–and evaluated on the separately collected ROPE real-
world benchmark. No ROPE images are used during training,
following Omni6DPose evaluation protocol. For SOPE, the
object crop is defined by the projected 3D bounding-box
corners and resized to 224 × 224. A template point is labeled
as occluded when its projected depth lies more than 1cm
behind the ground-truth surface depth. Points that lie outside
the image frame, behind the camera, or on invalid depth pixels
are marked as unobserved. Coordinate and depth supervision
are retained for hidden points, while visibility is predicted sep-
arately. Our HouseCat6D [12] model is trained independently
using its official split, following the mesh- and mask-based
visibility procedure defined in its evaluation protocol.

The correspondence generator conditions on features from

a pixel-wise instance mask. Features from layers 4, 8, 12,
16, 20, and 24 are projected to a common token dimension
(D = 384) and combined at each decoder layer using learned
softmax-normalized weights.

VI. EVALUATION
A. Experimental Setup
Implementation
&
Hyperparameters. Unless otherwise
specified, ASM-6D uses N = 642 template points, a four-
step distilled generator, H = 40 correspondence hypotheses,
20 ADMM iterations, and visibility-weighted fitting-residual
selection. We set λc = σ = 10−5. These hyperparameter
choices balance point-cloud density, inference speed, and
convergence stability; they were tuned once on a held-out
validation set of Omni6DPose [5] and kept fixed across all
subsequent benchmarks without task-specific tuning unless
stated otherwise. All experiments are executed on a single
NVIDIA RTX 3090 Ti GPU. Detailed architectural parameters
and training configurations are provided in Appendix D.
Datasets & Training Protocols. We evaluate on two comple-
mentary category-level pose benchmarks:
• Omni6DPose (SOPE →ROPE): Models are trained
strictly on the synthetic SOPE dataset [5], which contains
475K images generated from an ASM library of 4,385
CAD models across 156 categories. We evaluate zero-
shot transfer on ROPE, comprising 65,454 real-world test
images. Crucially, no ROPE images or scene instances are
used during training or parameter tuning.
• HouseCat6D: Evaluated using its official 34/2/5 scene split
(containing 16,884 training, 1,120 validation, and 3,360 test
frames across 10 household categories). Models for House-
Cat6D are trained entirely independently on its training
split without combining or co-training with SOPE/ROPE
data unless stated otherwise. Category priors are constructed
solely from HouseCat6D training CAD meshes.
ROPE evaluates performance using a sensor-assisted global-
depth refinement step, whereas HouseCat6D disables this
refinement per its official evaluation protocol.
Baselines. On ROPE, we compare against leading discrimina-
tive and generative pose estimators, including GenPose [17],
GenPose++ [5], and RFMPose [6]. On HouseCat6D, we
evaluate against recent state-of-the-art methods: AG-Pose [34],
SpotPose [35], GCE-Pose [36], and ComPose [4]. All results
follow standard benchmark metrics under their respective
protocols.

B. Shape-Conditioned Pose Ambiguity
A partial observation can support multiple shape comple-
tions with different pose ambiguities. We study one cup under
self-occlusion, no occlusion, and external occlusion. For each
capture, we sample 16,384 correspondence sets and fit the mug
ASM independently. A geometry-based radial test separates
approximately handle-free and handle-bearing completions.
Fig. 7 shows that a visible handle yields a single concen-
trated pose mode, while occlusion preserves multiple com-
pletion types with distinct azimuthal distributions. A linear
classifier using only the fitted ASM coefficients separates these
groups with cross-validated AUCs of 0.983 and 0.997 under
self- and external occlusion, respectively. Thus, correspon-
dence sampling preserves coupled shape–pose explanations of
the same partial observation.

VIII. CONCLUSION
We presented ASM-6D, a category-level pose–shape es-
timation framework that generates alternative complete ge-
ometric interpretations directly in the correspondence space
of a category-level shape model. Because the generated and
parametric representations share point identities, each hypoth-
esis can be fitted directly for pose, isotropic metric scale,
and instance shape. Analytic elimination of translation and
shape coefficients reduces each fit to a compact nine-variable
scaled-rotation problem, enabling efficient batched evaluation
of multiple hypotheses and candidate shape priors.
ASM-6D achieves state-of-the-art category-level 6D pose
performance on both ROPE and HouseCat6D. Controlled
ablations isolate the contributions of generative prediction,
hypothesis sampling, and active shape variation. Additional
analyses show that the same indexed hypotheses retain useful
complete geometry, preserve distinct pose–shape explanations
under occlusion, support finite-library prior selection, and can
be reused for temporal tracking and downstream manipula-
tion. Together, these results establish indexed correspondence
hypotheses as a structured interface between learned geometric
prediction and explicit pose–shape inference.
ASM-6D currently assumes a provided object ROI, known
camera intrinsics, and an offline library of category-specific

ASMs. Scaling to substantially larger shape libraries may
require coarse-to-fine or learned prior retrieval rather than
exhaustive fitting. Temporal inference could also be extended
beyond the current filtering scheme by propagating pose–shape
hypotheses with explicit motion or physics models. Finally,
combining the indexed ASM representation with richer surface
models, such as Gaussian splats [42], could provide denser ge-

active perception, and grasp planning.

APPENDIX D
TRAINING AND EVALUATION DETAILS
A. SOPE Training
SOPE [5] models are trained with AdamW using a learn-
ing rate of 10−4 and a batch size of 64. Weight decay is
10−4 for task-specific modules and zero for adapters. We set
λvis = λdepth = λpose = 0.1 and λtrans = 1.0. LoRA and
DoRA use rank-32 adapters on the same 48 projection layers
of the frozen RGB-D backbone. During training, we apply
random in-plane rotations and random crops to the RGB-D
inputs. We additionally apply RGB photometric augmentation
and random erasing, depth dropout and noise, and independent
segmentation-based background masking of RGB and depth
with perturbed masks to improve robustness to appearance
variation and missing or corrupted observations.
B. HouseCat6D Training and Evaluation
HouseCat6D
[12]
(HC6D)
uses
the
official
34/2/5
train/validation/test split. Both training regimes use rank-32
LoRA, batch size 64, AdamW learning rates of 10−4 for
task-specific modules and 3 × 10−5 for adapters, and task-
module weight decay 10−3. We use the same rotation and
dropout augmentations as for SOPE. We use unit-weight
correspondence loss, λvis = 10−3, λdepth = λpose = 0.1, and
symmetry-aware pose loss with rotation/translation weights
2.0/1.5 and time weighting (1 −t)2. The HC6D model is
trained without SOPE initialization, whereas SOPE→HC6D
initializes from the SOPE-trained LoRA model.
Evaluation follows the public AG-Pose GT-instance proto-
col [34]. Both variants use four-step distillation, H = 40,
training-CAD ASMs, and unmasked completed depth. Sensor-
assisted depth refinement is disabled.
C. Shape-Completion Protocol
We evaluate completion on the SOPE test set at image stride
3 (339,840 instances) and on 14,077 RealNOCS real_test
instances matched to released DR-Pose detections at mask IoU
≥0.5. Predictions are compared with posed mesh ground truth
in the camera frame using fixed manifests, seeds, and mesh
samples. Evaluation uses 1,152 points on SOPE and 642 points
on RealNOCS.
We report F1@1%, CD-L1, and HD95, with the F1 thresh-
old set to 1% of the ground-truth bounding-box diagonal.
Point-cloud baselines receive 2,048 instance-mask-segmented
depth points, whereas ASM-6D receives an RGB-D ROI with-
out mask-segmented points as model input. The comparison
therefore reflects each method’s native perception interface
rather than an input-matched completion protocol. “w/o prior”
denotes raw generated correspondences, while “w/ prior”
denotes the ASM-fitted output.
D. Tracking Protocol
For tracking, the first frame estimates pose, metric scale,
and shape from four correspondence hypotheses generated
with two denoising steps. The estimated scale and shape are
retained over the sequence. Each subsequent frame generates
new correspondence hypotheses and selects the pose using
geometric fit and motion consistency. Translation and rotation
are smoothed using One-Euro filters [38].
The HO3D evaluation uses sequences ABF10, BB10,
GPMF10, MC1, SM2, SMu1, and SS1, comprising seven YCB
objects and 448 scored frames. Both methods receive the same
RGB-D frames, camera intrinsics, and oracle object silhou-
ette; neither receives ground-truth pose. ASM-6D uses the
corresponding category-level ASM, whereas FoundationPose
receives the exact instance CAD.
The high-motion evaluation uses a dynamic mug sequence
with OptiTrack ground truth, with mean translation and rota-
tion speeds of 0.197 m s−1 and 88.2◦s−1, respectively. Both
methods receive the same RGB-D frames, intrinsics, and of-
fline SAM2.1 [43] masks. FoundationPose uses the exact YCB
025_mug model. We evaluate native tracking and periodic re-
registration every 30, 60, and 120 frames.

A. Regression Versus Flow Matching
We compare deterministic regression and flow matching at
H = 1 to exclude the effect of sampling multiple corre-
spondence sets. Flow matching increases IoU50 AUC from
11.58 to 25.91 and 10◦/5 cm VUS from 16.98 to 29.24,
demonstrating that the improvement is not attributable solely
to multi-hypothesis inference.
B. Hypothesis Budget
We evaluate H ∈{1, 4, 8, 16, 32, 40}. Accuracy improves
monotonically but saturates at moderate H. Reducing the bud-

FPS, while exhaustive 156-prior throughput increases from
6.82 to 20.65 FPS. We use H = 40 for maximum accuracy
and report smaller budgets as an accuracy–throughput tradeoff.
C. ADMM Iteration Budget
We evaluate 3, 5, 10, and 20 ADMM iterations using
an identical correspondence set, global-depth refinement, and
selection. Most gains occur within 10 iterations. Increasing the
budget from 10 to 20 improves 10◦/5 cm VUS by only 0.12
points. At H = 40, known-class throughput changes from
10.33 to 10.26 FPS, and exhaustive-prior throughput changes
from 6.98 to 6.82 FPS. We therefore use 20 iterations as a
conservative accuracy-oriented default.
