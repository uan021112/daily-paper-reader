ABSTRACT
We present ScenePlacer, a framework for scene-level pose estimation of objects
generated by state-of-the-art native shape generation models, placing generated
3D objects into scenes given a partial geometric observation without modify-
ing the underlying generative pipeline. Existing scene generation approaches ei-
ther generate objects directly in scene coordinates, compromising their canonical-
space priors, or regress poses from complete generated shapes in the shape gen-
eration process — the latter being a poorly conditioned problem that is sensitive
to scale ambiguity and partial observations. ScenePlacer decouples object gener-
ation from pose estimation by reformulating alignment as conditional visibility-
field learning. Given an observed partial point cloud from reconstruction and a
generated mesh, it predicts a visibility field defined in object space, then estimates
the rigid transformation by aligning the partial observation to visible parts on the
mesh. We propose a pretrained 3D shape VAE as our backbone, preserving its
mesh prior while conditioning it on a separately encoded observation. We further
introduce visibility-aware, radius-constrained supervision and synthesize training
pairs using only object-level meshes and rendered views, eliminating the need for
scene-level pose annotations. On 3D-FUTURE, ScenePlacer consistently outper-
forms existing placement and pose-refinement baselines under both ground-truth
and monocular depth estimation. With generated objects, it achieves a Chamfer
distance of 0.0077 and an F-score of 92.40 under estimated depth and improves to
0.0023 and 98.27, respectively, under ground-truth geometry.

INTRODUCTION
Compositional 3D scene generation is essential for AR/VR and embodied AI. Recent 3D object
generative models provide strong priors for recovering complete geometry from sparse observations,
which can be transferred to this task. Thanks to the capabilities of 3D generative models (Zhang
et al., 2023; Xiang et al., 2025), scene quality has improved significantly compared to before, yet
there is still a considerable gap before the results are truly satisfactory, coming with degraded shape
quality, excessive memory consumption, and inaccurate pose estimation.
Several methods generate objects within the scene in place. One strategy (Huang et al., 2025) gen-
erates all objects in a shared canonical space, inevitably degraded due to limited resolution. The
other strategy (Siddiqui et al., 2026; Yin et al., 2026) tries to inject the observed partial point cloud
as a condition into the 3D generative model and generates each object sequentially. However, the
observed point clouds are incomplete, and their orientation varies with the camera viewpoint. By re-
quiring the model to generate objects with arbitrary translations and rotations, this approach violates
the common assumption in 3D generative models that objects are centered at the origin and aligned
with the coordinate axes, inevitably degrading generation quality. An alternative approach (Meng
et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026) preserves the canonical generation space and introduces a separate
pose prediction head that estimates the transformation (translation, rotation, and scale) from object
to the world coordinate frame. Although this maintains high-quality object generation, pose predic-
tion accuracy remains a major concern: Small perturbations in the transformation matrix can lead to
large geometric deviations once applied, rendering the regression problem ill-conditioned.
Instead of struggling balancing the generation quality and pose estimation accuracy like existing
methods, we view placing generated objects back into the scene as a distinct problem that warrants
dedicated investigation. This problem presents challenges beyond conventional pose estimation.
A generated object may preserve the structural or functional characteristics of the observed object
without faithfully reproducing its visible geometry, making rigid registration unreliable. Moreover,
generated objects are typically normalized to [−1, 1]3 and lack scene-scale information, introduc-
ing scale ambiguity that further complicates rendering-based pose optimization. Any6D (Lee et al.,
2025) addresses this problem through multi-stage pose and scale refinement, but relies on close geo-
metric agreement between the generated object and the observation, with errors potentially propagat-
ing and accumulating across refinement stages. We propose a dedicated single-pass pose alignment
model that operates on the generated mesh, enabling efficient and robust pose estimation without af-
fecting generation quality. Specifically, given an observed partial point cloud Pobs of an object from
reconstruction and its corresponding generated mesh Mgen, we propose to predict the mesh-aligned
visible surface Paligned and solve for the transformation between Pobs and Paligned.
Our method is based on the insight that pose estimation can be solved by finding the visible surface
on a mesh in object canonical space corresponding to the observed part. This reframing removes the
dependency on scene-level data, which remains scarce in practice. Architecturally, we observe that
our task—predicting a partial surface aligned with a given complete mesh—naturally aligns with
the prior embedded in 3D Shape VAE, which encodes and decodes complete shapes. By treating
the observed partial point cloud as a conditioning signal that modulates the mesh decoder, our net-
work only needs to learn to select the visible subset of the mesh surface. This design enables strong
generalization with only lightweight tuning. Our approach is designed to be general-purpose and
independent of the underlying generation pipeline. It does not require modifications to the gener-
ated object or retraining of the generator, enabling straightforward integration with diverse object
generators and geometry models.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We recast object estimation as learning a visibility field with respect to the complete object
mesh, transforming the pose estimation problem into a conditional surface prediction task
that is inherently object-centric and independent of scene-level context.
• We introduce a lightweight pose estimation module that can be seamlessly integrated with
existing generative models. It predicts the mesh-aligned visible surface and resolves the
rigid transformation between the observed partial point cloud and the generated object,
without altering the generation process.

RELATED WORK
Geometry Foundation Models.
Recent geometry foundation models adopt transformer architec-
ture for traditional 3D vision tasks such as depth estimation, camera pose estimation, and 3D point
tracking. Some of them focus on single-view depth estimation (Yang et al., 2024a;b; Deng et al.,
2025; Wang et al., 2025b; 2026b; Xu et al., 2026; Viola et al., 2025), aiming to learn RGB-to-Depth
transformation, while they can’t handle multi-view input. Dust3R (Wang et al., 2024) tried to han-
dle stereo reconstruction by formulating the problem as direct regression of a point map for the
second view in the first view’s camera coordinate system. Moving forward, VGGT and a series
of subsequent works (Wang et al., 2025a; Deng et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026d; Lin et al., 2025;
Deng et al., 2026a) tried to extend to multi-view input: VGGT directly predicts per-view depth maps
and per-view camera poses; DA3 adopts ray map as camera representation; Pi3 focuses on learning
pose-invariant 3D representations that are agnostic to camera motion; Sail-Recon extends VGGT to
long sequence input. However, the above works can only handle static scenes. Recent geometry
foundation model D4RT (Zhang et al., 2026) focuses on 4D scene reconstruction.
Scene Generation
Scene generation approaches fall into two categories. The first leverages 3D
object generation priors (Hunyuan3D et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; 2025b; Xiang
et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2026c; Ye et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b;
Wu et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2026), together with geometry foundation and pattern recognition
priors (Deng et al., 2026d; Carion et al., 2026; Ren et al., 2024), and extends them to decomposable
scene generation (Ardelean et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Yin
et al., 2026; Yao et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2026; Siddiqui et al., 2026); in such scenes, objects are
interactive and compatible with physics simulation. Some (Feng et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024c;
Bian et al., 2025; Hao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025c; Pfaff et al., 2026) use language models to
orchestrate scenes from text prompts, producing more orderly layouts but lacking realism and image
alignment. The second utilizes 2D image or video priors and incorporates 3D conditions (e.g.,
camera parameters) to generate multi-view image or video sequences with 3D consistency (Huang
et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2025; 2026; Liang et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Szymanowicz et al., 2025;
Li et al., 2025a; Gao et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026c; Shen et al., 2026). While
these methods achieve superior visual quality via 2D priors, lacking native 3D representation makes
ensuring 3D interaction and physical consistency difficult.
Point Cloud Registration
Point cloud registration is closely related to single-reference unseen
object pose estimation, since both tasks depend on establishing reliable correspondences between
partial 3D observations. Classical approaches typically operate on hand-crafted geometric cues. For
instance, ICP and its variants (Besl & McKay, 1992; Rusinkiewicz & Levoy, 2001) alternate be-
tween nearest-neighbor association and rigid transform refinement, while FPFH (Rusu et al., 2009)
characterizes local surface geometry via point feature histograms, and PPF (Drost et al., 2010) rep-
resents oriented point-pair relations for generating pose hypotheses. Although these methods serve
as reasonable geometric baselines, they are often fragile under outliers, repetitive structures, and
limited view overlap. Learning-based techniques attempt to overcome these limitations by predict-
ing more distinctive correspondence signals. Predator (Huang et al., 2021), for example, introduces
overlap-aware feature learning so that matching concentrates on regions that are actually shared
between two partially overlapping scans. Similarly, GeoTransformer (Qin et al., 2023) aggregates
pairwise distances and angular information into rotation-invariant geometric features, thereby en-
abling robust superpoint matching across large viewpoint changes. More recently, Any6D (Lee
et al., 2025) addresses this setting in a model-free manner by reconstructing a normalized object
shape from a single RGB-D anchor image, jointly aligning it to metric scale in 2D/3D, and selecting
render-and-compare pose hypotheses for the query image.

4.4
TRAINING DATA SYNTHESIS
Training requires paired examples of observed partial point clouds and their corresponding mesh-
aligned visible surfaces. For each object, we pre-render depth maps from 60 uniformly sampled
viewpoints to ensure coverage of diverse observation conditions. At each training iteration, we
randomly select 1–3 viewpoints per object and back-project the depth maps into the mesh coordinate
frame using the known camera poses. This yields a mesh-aligned partial point cloud Paligned.
To synthesize the network input, we apply a random similarity transformation to Paligned:
ˆPobs = {sRp + t | p ∈Paligned},

where s ∈R+, R ∈SO(3), and t ∈R3 are randomly sampled, followed by normalization to a unit
sphere. We refer to ˆPobs as a training proxy for real observations. Since the ground-truth alignment
is known by construction, the network can be trained in a self-supervised manner to recover Paligned
from ˆPobs conditioned on the mesh.

5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets and Benchmarks. We evaluate our method on 3D-Future (Fu et al., 2021), a scene dataset
composed of numerous object layouts that can be decomposed into individual meshes. For training,
we select 130,000 high-quality meshes from Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023), training with learning
rate of 1e-4 for 30000 steps. We evaluate on a randomly sampled subset of 1,000 scenes from 3D-
FUTURE test set. Ablation study are conducted on 250 scenes randomly drawn from this subset.
Baselines. We compare our method against several paradigms for scene-level layout prediction.
3DFixer performs in-place generation conditioned on a reconstructed point cloud of the target ob-
ject, directly producing a surface conforming to the observed geometry. SAM3D generates objects
in canonical space and employs a layout denoising network to predict translation, rotation, and scale
in the world coordinate frame. SAM3D+PO (Pose Optimization) initializes from SAM3D predic-
tions and refines the pose by optimizing rendering over the mask and and perform rigid registration
between the observed point cloud and the complete point cloud. This refinement aligns the complete
generated mesh against the observed partial point cloud. Any6D is a recent 6D pose estimation
framework and we utilize the 3D objects generated by SAM3D as the reference shapes and apply
the alignment scheme from Any6D to align the predicted depth and objects for scene-level layout
prediction. In contrast, our pose estimation operates directly between two partial point sets without
relying on rendering loss and SAM3D initialization: the network-predicted visible surface and the
observed partial cloud.
Metrics. We jointly assess reconstruction fidelity and layout accuracy using Chamfer Distance (CD)
and F-score at τ = 0.05 and τ = 0.10, computed over concatenated object-surface point clouds
(N = 4096 per object via triangle-area-weighted sampling). To remove global unit scale while
preserving relative placement, we apply a single isotropic normalization x′ = (x −cGT) · 1.9/sGT

COMPARISON
Table 1 reports scene-level results under ground-truth depth and MoGe-predicted depth. Across all
metrics and settings, our method consistently outperforms all baselines, as illustrated in Figure 3.
More results can be found in supplementary material.
Ground-truth depth. We use ground-truth geometry as input, which rules out the effect of im-
perfect geometry estimation, allowing us to observe and compare the output quality of different
methods. 3D-Fixer struggles to consistently produce high-quality geometry, and may even fail to
generate under conditions such as complex occlusion (i.e., no complete mesh can be extracted),
with a CD of 23.35—over an order of magnitude worse than ours. SAM3D generates reasonably
but has limited layout accuracy, with F@0.05 of 75.01 versus our 90.38. SAM3D+PO degrades per-
formance versus SAM3D alone. We attribute this to two factors. First, rendering-based alignment
is inherently scale-ambiguous: a larger, farther object and a smaller, nearer object can project to
nearly identical rendered images, so rendered loss cannot constrain the true scale. Second, its geo-
metric loss aligns a complete mesh to a partial observation; mesh regions absent from the observed
point cloud introduce a systematic bias, preventing convergence to correct surface correspondences.
Any6D, aligning SAM3D-generated objects with observed depth via its pose and size estimation,
achieves a competitive F@0.05 of 85.18, outperforming SAM3D. However, its CD remains high at
1.04, indicating its mechanism still struggles to recover fine-grained geometric details.
In stark contrast, our method achieves exceptional reconstruction fidelity and layout accuracy, driv-
ing the CD down to an impressive 0.36 and pushing the F@0.10 to 97.01. This remarkable perfor-
mance stems from the core strength of our conditional visible-surface reconstruction paradigm. By
directly predicting the mesh-aligned visible surface, our network establishes highly precise, dense
partial-to-partial correspondences with the observed point cloud. Consequently, under the perfect
geometric guidance of GT depth, our method perfectly anchors the generated objects into the scene,
demonstrating that our partial-to-partial alignment mechanism is theoretically sound and practically
optimal for leveraging high-quality depth observations.
MoGe-predicted depth. Under estimated depth input, the performance gap widens further. 3DFixer
remains severely degraded, while Any6D suffers from a significant performance drop, with its CD
soaring to 2.87 and F@0.05 dropping to 74.14. This highlights that the alignment scheme in Any6D
is highly sensitive to depth noise, likely due to scale ambiguity and the lack of robust partial-to-
partial matching under noisy observations. SAM3D+PO again fails to improve upon its initializa-
tion, consistent with the inherent limitations of full-to-partial alignment under noisy depth. With
SAM3D-generated objects, our method achieves a CD of 0.77, a 41% relative improvement over
SAM3D and a 60% improvement over SAM3D+PO. Notably, our training does not incorporate
MoGe-predicted depth for data augmentation, unlike 3DFixer and SAM3D. The robust performance
under noisy depth therefore stems from the inherent resilience of our conditional visible-surface re-
construction paradigm rather than from domain-specific augmentation.

ABLATION STUDY
We conduct ablation experiments to isolate the contribution of each key design choice. Results under
both depth settings are reported in Table 2.
The necessity of separating encoding of mesh and observation. We compare against a Shared-
Encoder variant that uses one trainable encoder for both the complete mesh and the observed partial
cloud, instead of a frozen mesh encoder and a separate observation encoder. As shown in Table 2, it
degrades under both settings: GT CD 0.34→0.57 and F@0.10 96.69→93.86; MoGe CD 0.74→0.99
and F@0.10 92.14→88.53. This confirms that separate encoding is essential to prevent the partial,
noisy observation from disturbing the well-learned mesh prior.
Attention Swap. Reversing the attention direction (Attn-Swap), where the observation latents
serve as queries and the mesh latents provide context, results in a dramatic performance drop: CD
increases from 0.349 to 1.905 under GT depth and from 0.787 to 2.071 under MoGe depth. This
confirms that retaining the mesh as the primary representation—with the observation acting as a
modulator—is essential for preserving the strong geometric prior learned by the mesh decoder.
The necessity of Radius-Constrained Sampling. Removing the exclusion radius (Ours (w/o Vis.
RCS)) degrades performance, with CD increasing from 0.349 to 0.520 under GT depth and from
0.787 to 0.869 under MoGe depth. This occurs because negative samples crowd near the visi-
ble/invisible boundary without a radius constraint, creating ambiguous supervision. Introducing a
moderate exclusion radius establishes a clear buffer zone that explicitly separates visible and in-
visible regions, providing sharp supervision that stabilizes training and improves the fidelity of the
predicted visible surface.

CONCLUSION
We introduce ScenePlacer, a scene-level object pose estimation method that formulates the
observation-to-generation correspondence by learning a visibility field. Given a generated mesh and
an observed partial point cloud, ScenePlacer predicts a visibility field defined in object space, ex-
tracts the visible surface on the generated mesh, and recovers the pose through partial-to-generation
alignment. The method leverages a pretrained 3D shape representation, 3DShape2VecSet, uses
visibility-aware supervision, and can be trained solely on object-level data without scene-level pose
annotations. Experiments on 3D-FUTURE demonstrate consistent improvements in geometric fi-

A.4
LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Despite our placement method’s strong performance, its accuracy is bounded by the quality of ob-
served partial points and generated object meshes. Feed-forward RGB depth estimation still trails
traditional geometry reconstruction, and image-conditioned object generation, though silhouette-
consistent, lacks robustness to multi-view inputs. These are fundamental challenges for the 3D
community, and improving underlying geometry foundation models is essential. Future work will
focus on enhancing generation methods’ robustness to diverse inputs and improving the geometric
priors our placement module builds upon.
