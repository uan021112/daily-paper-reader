## Abstract 

3D scene graph generation requires understanding both object semantics and metric spatial relations. However, existing open-vocabulary methods often struggle with spatial predicates because language similarity reflects semantic co-occurrence rather than explicit geometry. We propose GHOST , a Geometry-constrained Hybrid diffusion framework for Open-vocabulary 3D Scene graph generaTion. GHOST jointly models continuous node geometry and discrete edge predicates, following a geometry-to-constraint-to-predicate denoising process to ensure geometrically consistent relation prediction. It introduces language-grounded constraint induction, which maps predicate embeddings to differentiable geo-metric constraints that guide both layout refinement and predicate reweighting. A Geometry-Topology Collaborative Denoiser further couples node and edge reasoning through noise-adaptive inter-action and orientation-aware modeling. Experiments on 3DSSG demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in both closed-set and open-vocabulary settings, including over 17-point gains over Rela-tionField on spatial predicates.

## 1 Introduction 

Understanding a 3D scene requires not only recognizing which objects are present, but also reasoning about how they spatially relate. Is the pillow on or in front of the sofa? (Fig. 1(a)) Answering such questions precisely is the central challenge of 3D Scene Graph Generation (3DSGG) [2, 6, 13, 21, 29], which represents a scene as a structured graph where nodes are localized objects and directed edges encode their spatial, comparative, and functional relations. The field has progressed from closed-set models [7, 11, 19], which operate on fixed object and predicate vocabularies, to open-vocabulary (OV) methods [21, 22, 28, 30, 32, 49] that leverage vision– language model (VLM) priors to handle unseen categories. Although this transition greatly improves semantic coverage, it also exposes a key limitation: VLMs encode spatial relations from image–text co-occurrence rather than verifiable geometric constraints over metric 3D coordinates [3, 15, 17]. As a result, spatial reasoning remains fragile (Fig. 1(b)). In our controlled diagnostic (Sec. 5.2), which evaluates spatial and non-spatial predicates separately, lead-ing VLM-based pipelines [22] score about 30 R@5 points lower on spatial predicates than on non-spatial ones (the bottom of Fig. 1(a)), with failures concentrated in depth ambiguities and symmetric configurations. A natural remedy is to inject 3D cues, such as bounding-box coordinates or point-cloud features, into the VLM pipeline. In prac-tice, this often fails and may even worsen generalization. Because indoor spatial statistics are highly structured, e.g., cabi-nets usually appear above counters and chairs in front of desks, coordinate features easily become shortcuts for predicate predic-tion rather than inputs to true geometric reasoning [8, 26, 40]. The model thus learns correlations between coordinate patterns and predicates, but degrades when these correlations shift [46, 56]. The problem is therefore not the absence of geometric information, but the absence of the right inductive structure. A spatial predicate is not merely associated with geometry; it is defined by it. For ex-ample, “left of” is true if and only if the subject’s 𝑥 -coordinate is smaller than the object’s. Given the above analysis, one might propose a simpler fix, pre-dicting predicates in a single forward pass and then applying a geometric consistency check. We argue that this two-stage strategy is insufficient in principle, and confirm this empirically in Sec. 5.4. Its limitation is that one-shot prediction forces an early commit-ment before the geometric layout is fully resolved. For instance, when two objects have nearly identical depths, a one-shot model cannot represent uncertainty over their ordering and must still choose between “in front of” and “behind.” Such decisions are there-fore driven by training-set frequency rather than the true scene geometry. Post-hoc verification may detect the inconsistency, but it cannot reliably recover the correct predicate once a hard prediction has been made. This calls for a generative process in which layout and relation estimates remain soft and mutually revisable until sufficient geometric evidence has been accumulated. Building on the above insight, we introduce GHOST , a Geometry-constrained Hybrid diffusion framework for Open-vocabulary 3D Scene graph generaTion. GHOST maintains a joint distribution over continuous node geometry and discrete edge predicates through a hybrid diffusion process, resolving both iteratively across 𝑇 reverse steps. First , within each reverse step we enforce a strict geometric annealing schedule in which geometry is denoised, then projected onto a constraint manifold, and finally predicates are sampled condi-tioned on the constraint-consistent layout. This intra-step ordering encodes the definitional dependence of spatial predicates on metric coordinates as a hard structural prior at every step of the reverse pro-cess. Second , we propose language-grounded constraint guidance, a mechanism that maps any predicate’s CLIP embedding, includ-ing predicates unseen during training, to a differentiable constraint function composed over a shared basis of geometric primitives (axis displacements, proximity, containment, relative size). The resulting constraint energy steers both geometry via gradient correction and predicate probabilities via distribution reweighting, without requiring a closed vocabulary of spatial predicates. Finally, Geometry-Topology Collaborative Denoiser (GTCD) jointly refines nodes and edges via noise-adaptive gating, edge-conditioned attention that feeds relational cues back to geometry, and an orien-tation-aware edge decomposition tailored to asymmetric predicates such as “left of” and “above.” As a result, spatial predicates are inferred from recovered geometry under explicit constraints rather than directly classified from visual–linguistic features. This progres-sive co-refinement yields consistent gains over one-shot baselines, particularly for near-depth relations, highlighting the value of joint uncertainty resolution. In summary, we make the following contributions. 

• We analyze why VLM-based 3DSGG often fails on spatial predi-cates, and show that the issue mainly comes from co-occurrence bias and one-shot prediction, which post-hoc geometric correc-tion cannot fully fix. 

• We propose GHOST , a geometry-constrained hybrid continuous-discrete diffusion framework for 3DSGG. Its semantic constraint induction maps predicates to differentiable geometric constraints, enabling spatial reasoning. 

• We design Geometry-Topology Collaborative Denoiser (GTCD), which couples geometry and relations via noise-adaptive inter-action and geometry-aware edge modeling. 

• GHOST achieves state-of-the-art results on 3DSSG under both closed-set and open-vocabulary settings, with particularly strong gains on spatial predicates. 

## 2 Related Work 

3D Scene Graph Generation. Early 3D scene graph generation methods assume closed-set object and predicate vocabularies [25, 43, 45], which limits transfer to unseen categories. Open-vocabulary (OV) methods alleviate this by lifting 2D foundation-model fea-tures into 3D representations for relational reasoning over novel concepts [2, 21, 57]. Representative examples include Concept-Graphs [9], Open3DSG [21], and RelationField [22]. However, these approaches mainly ground relations through vision–language sim-ilarity rather than explicit metric geometry. Since VLMs receive limited 3D spatial supervision during pre-training [3], their spatial predictions remain unreliable under depth ambiguity and symmet-ric layouts. GHOST addresses this by grounding predicate predic-tion in explicit 3D geometry via discrete generative modeling. 

Discrete Diffusion for Structured Generation. Diffusion models have been extended from continuous generation [15, 18, 27, 37, 39, 53] to discrete state spaces. D3PM [1] introduces structured cate-gorical diffusion, VQ-Diffusion [10] applies it to vector-quantized GHOST: Geometry-Constrained Hybrid Diffusion for Open-Vocabulary 3D Scene Graph Generation MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil 

image synthesis, and DiGress [42] models graph generation as iter-ative denoising over node and edge categories. Subsequent work improves structural fidelity with continuous-time formulations [48] and unified token-based generation [35]. This paradigm is natu-rally suited to scene graphs, where both object labels and predi-cates are discrete and benefit from iterative refinement [31, 40, 50]. GHOST is the first to apply hybrid diffusion to 3D scene graphs, jointly modeling geometry and predicate tokens with an intra-step geometry-to-relation denoising schedule for spatially consistent generation.

## 4 Method 

GHOST casts 3DSGG as geometry-constrained relational decoding ,which recovers a 3D layout through diffusion, then decodes predi-cates from that layout under explicit geometric constraints, rather than classifying relations from visual–linguistic features in a single pass. First, we tokenize predicate semantics into a discrete code-book so that both geometry and predicates live in spaces amenable to diffusion (4.1). Second, we define a hybrid reverse process in which geometry is denoised first and predicates are decoded con-ditioned on the recovered layout, with a language-grounded con-straint mechanism (4.2–4.3). Third, we design a Geometry-Topology Collaborative Denoiser that couples node and edge reasoning (4.4).

## 5.2 Comparison with State-of-the-Art Models 

Overall results. Tab. 1 reports results under both settings. Un-der the closed-set setting, GHOST surpasses the strongest baseline OCRL by +3 points on both mR@3 and mR@5 while matching it on R@K, indicating stronger long-tail relation recognition. Under the

These consistent gains across both settings suggest that GHOST’s geometry-first diffusion mod-eling yields a stronger 3D scene graph prior rather than exploiting vocabulary-specific biases. 

Spatial predicate results. Tab. 2 reveals a fundamental limitation of four representative VLM-based methods: a large performance gap between spatial and non-spatial predicates. The strongest base-line RelationField achieves spatial R/mR@1 of only 0.26/0.17 versus 0.42/0.29 on non-spatial predicates—a near 2 × deficit that grows even larger for ConceptGraphs (0.08 vs. 0.26 at R@1). This disparity indicates that VLM-driven methods fit semantic co-occurrence sta-tistics rather than performing metric geometric reasoning. GHOST closes this gap decisively: spatial R/mR@1 improves to 0.43/0.30 

(+17 points over RelationField) and R/mR@5 to 0.93/0.65 (+33 points), while non-spatial R/mR@5 of 0.92/0.63 remains on par with the best baseline (0.90/0.61), confirming that geometric con-straint guidance does not compromise semantic generalization. 

Seen vs. unseen predicates. Tab. 3 shows that GHOST consis-tently outperforms RelationField on both seen and unseen predi-cates, with about an 8-point improvement on average. Importantly, the gains on unseen predicates are comparable to those on seen ones, indicating that the improvement is not simply due to better fitting of visible categories. Instead, the results suggest that GHOST learns transferable geometry–relation mappings and exhibits gen-uine open-vocabulary generalization.

## 5.3 Ablation Study 

We ablate key design choices of GHOST on predicate R@ 𝐾 and spatial R@ 𝐾 (Tab. 4). 

Input configuration. Adding vision tokens yields only marginal gains ( +1 point in predicate R@3/R@5) at roughly 2 × inference cost, so we exclude them by default. Removing geometry tokens causes severe degradation (predicate R@3/R@5: 0.61/0.64; spatial R@1/R@5: 0.12/0.32), confirming that explicit geometry is essential for reliable spatial reasoning beyond label co-occurrence. 

Geometric annealing schedule. Reversing the intra-step order to predicate-first, geometry-second degrades predicate R@3/R@5 to 0.60/0.63 and spatial R@1/R@5 to 0.13/0.33. Predicting relations before resolving geometry encourages semantic shortcuts, whose errors subsequently bias geometry estimation. 

Diffusion mechanism. Replacing stochastic posterior sampling with deterministic updates causes a marked drop in Predicate R@3/5 (0.59/0.63) and Spatial R@1/5 (0.14/0.35), revealing a core weakness of purely iterative refinement: early commitment to an incorrect relation under sparse geometric evidence locks subsequent steps on a flawed trajectory. GHOST avoids this by maintaining a soft joint geometry–predicate distribution via diffusion, deferring consensus until sufficient evidence accumulates. ## 5.4 Geometric Oracle Experiments 

VLM-based 3DSGG methods struggle with spatial predicates due to co-occurrence bias , which favors corpus statistics over metric geometry, and one-shot commitment , which fixes predictions before the scene layout is fully resolved. To assess whether post-hoc verification can address these issues, we augment RelationField [22], the strongest one-shot baseline, with a perfect geometric oracle that uses ground-truth bounding boxes to remove geometrically invalid candidates and re-rank the remainder by confidence. As shown in Fig. 5, the oracle yields only modest improvements: spatial R/mR@1 increases from 0.26/0.17 to 0.34/0.23, R/mR@3 from 0.53/0.36 to 0.61/0.41, and R/mR@5 from 0.60/0.40 to 0.67/0.45. These results remain well below GHOST’s 0.43/0.30, 0.84/0.60, and 0.93/0.65 with-out oracle access. Post-processing cannot recover correct predicates already excluded from the top-𝐾 candidates by co-occurrence bias. In contrast, GHOST jointly maintains revisable distributions over geometry and predicates throughout reverse diffusion, delaying final decisions until sufficient geometric evidence has accumulated. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented GHOST, a hybrid diffusion framework that jointly denoises node geometry and edge predicates for open-vocabulary 3D scene graph generation. By integrating geometric annealing, language-grounded constraint guidance, and orientation-aware collaborative denoising, GHOST enables verifiable metric reasoning beyond co-occurrence-based VLM priors. It achieves state-of-the-art performance on 3DSSG in both closed-set and open-vocabulary settings, with particularly strong gains on spatial predicates. 

Limitations. The iterative reverse process introduces latency, al-though fewer diffusion steps provide a favorable efficiency–accuracy trade-off. Performance in complex scenes is also limited by the expressiveness of the hand-designed geometric primitives. Future work will explore learned primitives, part-level and dynamic scenes, and larger open-world benchmarks.
