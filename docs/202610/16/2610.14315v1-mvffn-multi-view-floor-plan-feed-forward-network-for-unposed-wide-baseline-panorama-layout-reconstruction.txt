Abstract
Reconstructing accurate camera poses and floor plan layouts from sparse, unposed,
wide-baseline RGB panoramas remains a challenging open problem. Direct multi-
room floor plan prediction is especially difficult in this setting: pose, geometry,
semantics, and global layout structure are tightly coupled, yet a complete floor plan
is hard to learn as a single global output. We address this challenge with Multi-view
Ordered Wall Instance Segmentation (MOIS), a new formulation that replaces
direct global wall-sequence prediction with local cross-view wall instances and
within-room wall connectivity. Building on this formulation, we propose MvFFN,
a feed-forward multi-view transformer that jointly predicts coarse camera poses,
dense global geometry, and wall-instance-level semantic outputs with globally
consistent identities and within-room connectivity. A downstream Layout Chaining
(LC) post-processing pipeline then assembles these predictions into multi-room
floor plans with shared global coordinates, consistent wall identities, and connected
room structure. The resulting pipeline substantially outperforms prior methods on
wall- and junction-level accuracy. Moreover, the predicted wall instances enable
fully automatic downstream layout refinement, with planar bundle adjustment
improving a majority of scenes and yielding clear gains on the subset that benefits.
We also introduce ZInD-CrossView, a re-annotated dataset with globally unique
wall instances and cross-view segmentation labels for this task.

Introduction
Reconstructing floor plan layouts and camera poses from panoramic images is important for ap-
plications such as augmented reality, indoor digital twins, immersive walkthrough generation, and
autonomous navigation. Recovering complete multi-room layouts requires multiple views because of
occlusions and limited visibility. However, existing methods typically avoid the setting of unposed,
wide-baseline panoramas: they assume known camera poses Yue et al. [2023], Avetisyan et al. [2024],
Liu et al. [2024], require small-baseline captures Avetisyan et al. [2024], or rely on human-in-the-loop
adjustment Cruz et al. [2021], Li et al. [2025]. A fully automatic approach for reconstructing layouts
and poses from unposed, wide-baseline panoramas would substantially reduce capture and annotation
effort and enable broader deployment at scale.
A floor plan encodes wall geometry, local connectivity, and room-level organization. Recovering it
from multiple views requires associating wall instances across views, inferring their connectivity
despite occlusion, and separating structures that belong to different rooms. Geometry alone is often
insufficient. When camera poses are unknown and fused point maps are noisy, reconstruction often
suffers from duplicate walls, missing walls, and incorrect junctions, which reduce both wall and

junction-level precision and recall. In contrast, images provide critical semantic cues for grouping
pixels across views by wall instance, inferring local connectivity, and resolving room assignments. At
the same time, directly predicting a complete multi-room floor plan as a single global output is difficult
to learn. Large multi-room layouts often contain ambiguous room partitions, limited observations can
obscure small structural details, and small local sequence errors can cascade into large global drift.
The problem is further compounded by the scarcity of datasets that provide structured multi-room
layouts together with camera-registered multi-view panoramas. This motivates the central insight
of our paper: rather than predicting a global floor plan directly, we recast multi-view, multi-room
layout estimation around local wall relations that are easier to supervise and more robust to noisy
annotations.
To this end, we study structured floor plan and camera-pose reconstruction from sparse, unposed
360◦panoramas, producing complete layouts when view coverage is sufficient and partial layouts
under occlusion. We formalize this idea as Multi-view Ordered Wall Instance Segmentation (MOIS),
which replaces direct global floor plan prediction with local wall instances and within-room wall
connectivity that remain consistent across views. Based on MOIS, we propose MvFFN, a feed-forward
multi-view network that predicts pixel-grounded wall instances and their within-room connectivity
from cross-view geometric and semantic cues, and a Layout Chaining (LC) post-processing pipeline
that assembles these local predictions into multi-room floor plans with consistent wall identities in a
shared global frame. MvFFN also predicts coarse camera poses and dense global geometry, while the
inferred wall instances enable downstream layout bundle adjustment for further refinement. Together,
these design choices make multi-view layout estimation more learnable under scarce data and noisy
supervision.
The contributions of this work are as follows: (1) We introduce MOIS, a new formulation for
multi-view, multi-room layout estimation based on local cross-view wall instances and within-room
wall connectivity. (2) We introduce MvFFN, a feed-forward multi-view network that predicts these
local wall structures from cross-view geometric and semantic cues. We show gains both before
and after layout assembly: dense MOIS predictions improve raw cross-view wall-slot quality, while
the assembled global layouts substantially improve wall and junction accuracy over prior modular
baselines across both 5-view and 8-view settings. (3) We introduce ZInD-CrossView (ZInD-XV)
dataset with globally unique wall instances and cross-view segmentation annotations.

Related Work
Layout estimation. Most layout estimation methods combine a dense geometric front-end with
a back-end that vectorizes predictions into wall and corner instances and room topology. In the
single-view setting, methods predict monocular 1D floor boundaries Yang et al. [2019], Wang
et al. [2021], Jiang et al. [2022], Sun et al. [2021] and recover wall and corner sequences through
post-processing Zou et al. [2018], Sun et al. [2019], Pintore et al. [2020], relying on the clockwise
image-column order within a single panorama. This cue does not directly extend to sparse multi-view
settings, where each view observes only a partial layout under occlusion. For multi-room floor-
plan estimation, most methods instead assume accurate camera poses and dense point clouds, and
infer structure mainly from fused geometric cues in top-down space. Under imperfect geometry,
connectivity is typically recovered with heuristic optimization Chen et al. [2019], Wang et al. [2022]
or learned polygon and graph decoders Liu et al. [2024], Yue et al. [2023], Chen et al. [2023], Su
et al. [2023]. By contrast, cross-view semantic cues remain underused for resolving wall identity and
connectivity under pose uncertainty. SceneScript Avetisyan et al. [2024] shows that, given camera
poses and RGB video, semantic context can improve structured layout estimation.
Wide-baseline pose and geometry estimation. Wide-baseline indoor pose estimation remains
difficult for traditional Structure-from-Motion Schonberger and Frahm [2016], Moulon et al. [2016],
Lambert et al. [2022]. Recent learning-based models improve dense 3D point map and camera pose
estimation from wide baseline inputs using strong foundation model encoders Wang et al. [2024,
2025], Tang et al. [2025]. For indoor 360◦panoramas, limited training data often constrains geometry
estimation to 1D floor boundary representations rather than dense per-pixel 3D geometry Hutchcroft
et al. [2022], Tu et al. [2024], Nejatishahidin et al. [2023]. BADGR Li et al. [2025] shows that semantic
layout correspondences and geometry are complementary: once pixel-to-wall correspondences and
initial estimates are available from unposed wide-baseline images, poses and layouts can be jointly
optimized to reduce initial errors.

Overview
Large multi-room floor plans often contain ambiguous room boundaries, occluded wall connectivity,
and distant structural details, making direct floor-level sequence prediction brittle to error accumula-
tion. We therefore reconstruct layouts through dense local MOIS predictions rather than direct global
floor plan prediction.
Our bottom-up pipeline (Figure 1) takes N panoramas and evaluates MvFFN densely at every column
of every view. Each anchor column defines one MOIS target: the cross-view assignments of the
current wall and its within-room neighbors. Aggregating all anchors yields dense anchor-conditioned
MOIS assignments over multiple views, predicted jointly with global geometry under pose uncertainty.
MvFFN uses a multi-view geometric encoder, trained from scratch and inspired by VGGT Wang
et al. [2025], adapted to panoramic 2D column tokens and dense column-wise outputs. A floor-layout
semantic branch combines image and geometric features for local wall-instance reasoning. We
describe the geometric encoder and floor-layout decoder in Sec. 4.1 and Sec. 4.2, respectively.
Finally, the dense local MOIS predictions are assembled into global room layouts via Layout Chain-
ing (Sec. 4.5). The predicted pixel-to-wall correspondences and initial layouts can also support
downstream layout refinement, which we evaluate with layout bundle adjustment in Sec. 4.6.
4
Method
We formulate multi-room layout reconstruction from sparse panoramas Zhang et al. [2014] as the
recovery of global 3-DoF camera poses (2D position and 1D rotation), structured 2D floor layouts,
and a local anchor-conditioned wall representation across views.

4.2
Floor Layout Semantic Branch
Our floor-layout semantic branch predicts the dense MOIS probability field introduced in Sec. 4.
Specifically, for each anchor column, it estimates which columns across all views belong to the current
wall and to its next K −1 clockwise physically adjacent within-room neighbors. We use K = 3,
which provides enough neighboring-wall context for stable connectivity recovery in Layout Chaining
(Sec. 4.5), while avoiding the degradation of longer prediction windows (see Appendix E.2). The
branch consists of a semantic N-view encoder conditioned on geometry features, a cross-attention
transformer decoder with anchor-conditioned wall-slot queries, and a segmentation head.

4.3
Training
We train MvFFN in two stages because the geometry and semantic objectives compete under joint
training. In the first stage, we train the geometry branch for dense geometric prediction, including
floor boundaries, floor point maps, and camera poses, using a multi-task objective that combines
geometric supervision with absolute and relative pose losses. In the second stage, we freeze the
pre-trained geometry branch and train the floor-layout semantic branch from scratch under dense
MOIS supervision, reusing the geometry features from stage one. The MOIS objective is factorized
into a wall-presence term, which predicts whether a column belongs to any ordered wall slot of the
anchor, and an ordered wall-slot classification term applied to positive assignments. In both stages,
training samples are formed by drawing sparse multi-view panorama groups from a single pose graph
with sufficient co-visibility. Full loss definitions, optimizer settings, and sampling schedules are
provided in Appendix A.
4.4
Inductive Bias for Training Data Scarcity
Training sparse multi-view models is substantially harder than training 2-view models because they
require more diverse multi-view samples, while the number of scenes and unique structured training
examples remains limited. In our settings, this makes naive 3-view to 5-view training unstable even
when the same data is sufficient for strong 2-view geometric learning. This trend is also consistent
with recent geometric models such as VGGT Wang et al. [2025] and MV-DUSt3R Tang et al. [2025],
which require substantially more training data than DUSt3R Wang et al. [2024] to improve 2-view
pose accuracy. To improve convergence under this regime, we introduce two inductive biases based
on reliable 2-view CovisPose Hutchcroft et al. [2022] predictions.
Camera rotation priors. Two-view geometric models already provide reliable camera rotations, and
per-panorama vanishing points further improve indoor rotation accuracy Zhang et al. [2014], Lambert
et al. [2022]. We first run the 2-view CovisPose model and recover global camera rotations with a
minimum spanning tree, following BADGR’s initialization strategy Li et al. [2025]. These refined
rotations are then injected as conditioning signals into the angular encoder (Sec. 4.1). This reduces
the amount of scarce multi-view supervision spent on relearning camera rotation and lets the model
focus on the harder cross-view geometric reasoning problem, while retaining near 2-view rotation
accuracy without requiring substantially more N-view training data.
Column-patch correspondence priors. Under limited multi-view training data, we use precomputed
2-view CovisPose correspondences during both training and inference to restrict early cross-view
attention to a local window around predicted matches, rather than relearning global correspondence
search from scratch. We implement this by masking the first four global self-attention layers of the
alternating-attention transformer: for each column-patch token in view Ia, tokens in view Ib more
than 40 columns away from the predicted match are masked out, and fully non-covisible view pairs
are blocked entirely. This reduces correspondence ambiguity, leading to less noisy gradients and
faster convergence.
4.5
Layout Chaining
Layout Chaining (LC) is a deterministic procedure that reduces dense local MOIS predictions into
global column-to-wall assignments \protect \mathcal  {M}, room-level wall sequences \if
mmode \lbrace \else \textbraceleft \fi \mathcal {L}^m\ }, and room endpoints \i f
mmode \lbrace \else \textbraceleft \fi V^m\ }.
6


Since many anchor columns provide overlapping same-wall and neighboring-wall evidence, LC
first consolidates wall identity and then recovers within-room adjacency. It clusters same-view
current-wall predictions \prot ec t \hat  {\mathcal {S}}[n,:,n,:, 0] into per-view wall instances L_
n, then merges these across
views into global wall groups through a cross-view same-wall graph. Directed within-room adjacency
is estimated from the neighboring MOIS slots, producing a room graph whose edges are filtered by
geometric consistency and whose longest directed paths define partial room layouts \protect \mathcal  {L}^
m. Finally, wall-
line parameters are fit from supporting point-map predictions grouped by \protect \mathcal  {M}, and room endpoints
are recovered by intersecting consecutive walls. Algorithm details are provided in Appendix B.
4.6
Layout Bundle Adjustment
The layouts produced by Layout Chaining and the predicted camera poses may still violate multi-view
consistency and structural layout constraints. The predicted pixel-to-wall correspondences provide
the associations needed for refinement. We investigate whether two refinement methods, planar
bundle adjustment (BA) and BADGR Li et al. [2025], can use these correspondences to improve poses
and wall geometry. Sec. ?? reports the results.
5
Experiments
We evaluate primarily on ZInD-XV, which extends ZInD Cruz et al. [2021] with globally indexed
multi-room layouts represented as room-wise wall sequences, and per-panorama column-wise floor-
boundary and wall-instance labels derived from those layouts. ZInD-XV contains 2,706 floors and
66,834 panoramas. We additionally use HM3D-XV, a smaller auxiliary benchmark derived from
residential HM3DSem scenes Yadav et al. [2023], with 372 floors and 17,943 panoramas, to evaluate
cross-dataset transfer to furnished, mesh-rendered indoor scenes. Dataset construction and annotation
details are provided in the supplementary material.
We compare against modular baselines assembled from existing pose, geometry, and layout compo-
nents. The main evaluation measures final structured floor-layout accuracy after Layout Chaining; we
also report pose estimation and downstream refinement with planar bundle adjustment and BADGR Li
et al. [2025]. To isolate the learned representation before post-processing, we additionally evalu-
ate raw MOIS prediction quality in the supplementary material. Baseline implementation details,
HM3D-XV transfer results, and additional ablations are also provided in the supplementary material.

Ablation Studies
We evaluate three complementary ablations. The main paper studies architecture, testing whether
the two-branch, two-stage MvFFN design is necessary for stable MOIS prediction. Appendix E.1
studies inductive bias, evaluating rotation and correspondence priors across input-view counts, and
Appendix E.2 studies prediction window, where longer MOIS wall sequences become harder to
learn.
Architecture.
We ablate two core design choices in MvFFN: a dedicated semantic branch for MOIS
prediction and the two-stage geometry-to-semantics training strategy. Table 4 compares four variants:
a shared-encoder model, a two-branch model without geometry-to-semantic fusion, a full architecture

Conclusion
We introduced MvFFN, which reconstructs structured floor plans and camera poses from sparse,
unposed 360◦panoramas by predicting cross-view wall instances and within-room connectivity
before global assembly. Experiments show clear gains over modular baselines, demonstrate automatic
downstream refinement from predicted wall correspondences, and introduce ZInD-XV for future
structured multi-view layout research.

slot. For each N-view test tuple, we sample anchor columns from the conditioning panoramas and
retain anchors with valid ordered slot targets. Each anchor instantiates the cross-view slot masks
defined in Sec. 4 of the main paper. For each slot, we accumulate intersections and unions between
the predicted and ground-truth masks over all valid anchors, views, and panorama columns before
taking the ratio. This pixel-weighted pooling measures aggregate cross-view mask quality and avoids
the high variance of query-averaged \protect \mathrm  {mIoU} for small or weakly visible wall instances.

single-view layout predicted by LGT-Net, we convert it into a floor-plan polygon whose edges define
the local slots: the edge intersected by the anchor ray is slot 1, and the next two adjacent edges in
clockwise order are slots 2 and 3. CovisPose then provides cross-view column correspondences,
which propagate these source-view slots to other panoramas and produce cross-view slot masks. We
evaluate these masks against the same ordered wall-instance targets using Pooled Ordered Wall-Slot
IoU.

Experiments on HM3D-XV Dataset
We use HM3D-XV as an auxiliary cross-domain validation benchmark to evaluate whether MvFFN
and the MOIS formulation remain effective beyond ZInD-XV. HM3D-XV is derived from residential
HM3DSem scenes Yadav et al. [2023] with sufficient mesh coverage, and contains 17,943 panoramas
and 372 floors from 180 scenes. Compared with ZInD-XV, which contains 2,706 floors, HM3D-XV
is smaller and noisier, contains furnished mesh-rendered scenes with rendering artifacts, and has
sparser column-level supervision, with 27.3% of image columns masked after annotation cleanup.
Dataset construction details are provided in Sec. F.2. This makes HM3D-XV a challenging setting for
both pose estimation and layout reconstruction. We therefore use ZInD-XV as an anchor dataset and
finetune MvFFN with mixed ZInD-XV/HM3D-XV samples to test whether the learned geometry and
MOIS representations remain useful under domain shift and noisy annotations.
For evaluation, we construct N-view test splits from per-floor tuples generated by covisibility-based
sampling. Each floor receives a sample count proportional to its number of panoramas, capped by
the number of valid tuples; remaining samples are redistributed according to residual capacity. This
yields 951 tuples for the 5-view split and 991 tuples for the 8-view split, spanning all floors with
valid N-view connected components while keeping floor-level representation roughly proportional to
scene size. Both pose and layout experiments use these splits. Because some comparisons rely on
CovisPose-derived priors, we additionally report those results on the CovisPose-feasible subset defined
in Sec. D.1. Sec. D.1 evaluates pose estimation, and Sec. D.2 evaluates floor layout reconstruction.

The main paper reports the architecture ablation for the two-branch, two-stage MvFFN design. Here
we provide two additional ablations: Sec. E.1 studies the rotation and correspondence priors used for

E.1
Inductive Bias
Figure E.1: Effect of inductive bias across input views. Box plots show translation (left) and rotation
(right) errors under different inductive-bias variants. Dashed lines and diamond markers indicate
mean errors. Stronger inductive bias improves translation accuracy and suppresses extreme rotation
outliers.
We evaluate MvFFN across varying numbers of input panoramas, using three levels of inductive bias
described in Sec. 4.4 of the main paper: no inductive bias, rotation priors only, and rotation priors
with correspondence-based attention masks. As shown in Fig. E.1, stronger inductive bias has its
clearest effect on translation accuracy: the full MvFFN maintains substantially lower translation
error across all input-view settings, while the weaker-bias variants degrade much more rapidly as the
number of views increases. For rotation, the main benefit is robustness rather than uniformly lower
central statistics. In some settings, the no-bias variant can appear slightly better in the median, but this
occurs only near the center of the distribution, where all methods already achieve low angular error.
Its mean is nonetheless much higher because the error distribution develops a heavy tail: in the 8-view
setting, the no-bias variant reaches 17.5◦at p90 and 27.8◦at p95, whereas the full MvFFN remains at
2.5◦at p95. Thus, the proposed inductive bias mainly suppresses large rotation failures and produces
much more stable estimates in hard cases. Since all variants already use vanishing-angle priors, this
gain is not explained by rotation snapping alone, but by the additional regularization introduced by
the multi-view inductive bias.
E.2

We train two additional MvFFN variants with larger MOIS prediction windows of 5 and 7 ordered
walls, and report the results in Table E.1. For a controlled comparison, Layout Chaining is kept
unchanged and uses only the first three predicted wall slots for all variants, so the ablation isolates the
effect of training MvFFN to predict longer MOIS windows. Expanding the prediction window leads
to a consistent drop in layout accuracy, with 5-view wall F1 decreasing from 88.1 to 85.6 and 84.4,

respectively. Matched-wall distance also worsens in the 8-view setting. These results support our
choice of a 3-wall MOIS window: a compact local prediction target is easier to learn under sparse
wide-baseline observations, while longer ordered wall sequences accumulate more ambiguity from
occlusion, room-boundary uncertainty, and distant structural details. The degradation is gradual
rather than catastrophic: increasing the MOIS window makes the task harder, but does not break
the formulation. This suggests that locality is helpful, with 3 walls giving the best operating point
in our setting, while the broader advantage comes from replacing direct global prediction with an
anchor-conditioned local representation.

E.3
Architecture Ablation Details
We compare four architecture variants. The shared-encoder model removes the dedicated semantic
feature encoder and predicts MOIS directly from geometry-branch features. The no-fusion model
keeps separate geometry and semantic branches but removes geometry-to-semantic feature fusion,
while retaining the same downstream Layout Chaining procedure. The one-stage model uses the full
two-branch architecture but trains all objectives jointly from scratch. The full model uses separate
geometry and semantic branches with the proposed two-stage schedule.
The no-fusion variant substantially degrades layout recall, F1, and matched-wall distance, indicating
that semantic image features alone are insufficient and that geometry-conditioned semantic features
are important for cross-view wall grouping and within-room connectivity. The shared-encoder and
one-stage variants both become unstable during training: the shared-encoder model collapses after
roughly 25k steps, while the one-stage model collapses after roughly 35k steps. This suggests
conflicting optimization pressure between dense geometry prediction, which relies on precise one-to-
one cross-view matching, and MOIS prediction, which groups columns by wall identity and room
structure. Separating the geometry and semantic branches and training them in stages improves both
stability and final layout accuracy.
F
Dataset Preparation
F.1
ZInD-XV Dataset Preparation
ZInD-XV is derived from all scenes in the Zillow Indoor Dataset (ZInD) Cruz et al. [2021]. It
contains 66,834 panoramas, 167,983 unique wall instances, 23,651 rooms, and 2,706 floors from 1,524
residential house scenes in North America. ZInD-XV retains the original panoramas and room layouts
in the global frame, but augments them with globally indexed multi-room floor-plan annotations.
Specifically, each floor is represented as a set of room layouts with doors and windows, encoded
as closed room polygons over globally unique wall instances in the same format as FloorPlan60K
used in Li et al. [2025]. Because ZInD often partitions large open spaces into smaller partial rooms,
we recursively merge adjacent rooms connected by openings that do not resemble doorways. As a
result, the room partition in ZInD-XV is less fragmented than the original ZInD partition in open-plan
areas, while still preserving meaningful room-level structure rather than collapsing each floor into
a single geometric region. These global wall instances are further projected into each panorama to
produce column-wise floor boundaries and wall-instance semantic labels.
Because a single panorama can observe walls from multiple rooms, and visible room connectivity
depends on doorway state and view position, these per-panorama labels cannot be obtained by
projecting a single room polygon independently. Instead, for each panorama, we search the closest
room graph connected through open doors and select the room configuration whose rendered floor
boundary best matches the 1D floor boundary predicted by CovisPose Hutchcroft et al. [2022]. This
graph is then used to generate the per-panorama floor-boundary and wall-instance labels. Finally,
we apply a manual cleanup pass to mask panorama columns with inconsistent floor boundaries or
incorrect wall-instance assignments. We plan to release the ZInD-XV annotations, including global
floor-plans with unique wall instances, per-panorama floor boundaries, wall-instance semantic labels,
and validity masks.
F.2
HM3D-XV Dataset Preparation
HM3D-XV is constructed from residential scenes in the Habitat–Matterport 3D Semantic
Dataset (HM3DSem) Yadav et al. [2023], restricted to scenes with sufficient mesh coverage to
better match the residential scene distribution of ZInD-XV. It contains 17,943 panoramas from 372
eligible floors across 180 scenes, split into 287 training floors and 85 validation floors following
HM3DSem. Although HM3DSem provides textured house meshes with semantic labels, it does not
provide the panorama-based annotations required by our model: camera-posed panoramas, globally
indexed wall instances, room-level wall sequences, and per-panorama column-wise floor-boundary
and wall-instance labels. We construct these annotations in three stages: automated floor preparation,
room-level wall sequence annotation, and per-panorama column-wise annotation. We plan to release
the HM3D-XV annotations, including global floor-plans with unique wall instances, per-panorama
camera poses, floor boundaries, wall-instance semantic labels, and validity masks.
18


Automated floor preparation.
This stage converts each multi-floor HM3DSem scene mesh into
per-floor sub-scenes with panoramas of known camera poses and initialized global wall instances. We
first segment each scene mesh by floor using the semantic labels to identify floor and ceiling geometry.
The floor and ceiling heights used for this partition are estimated from the median vertical positions
of these surfaces, and nearby split floor regions are merged with simple geometric heuristics. For
each floor, we sample panorama centers from the Habitat navigation mesh with a density-controlled
greedy Poisson-disk strategy Bridson [2007], targeting approximately 2 panoramas per square meter
at a fixed height of 1.2 m. Using the Habitat-Sim renderer Ramakrishnan et al. [2021], we render
2048×1024 RGB equirectangular panoramas from the textured mesh with upright camera orientation.
Candidate samples that fall on clutter are discarded, and panoramas with severe visible mesh defects
are removed during manual inspection. We then initialize global wall instances at the floor level.
Vertical wall meshes are grouped by semantic wall instance label from HM3DSem and projected to
the floor plane, and a 2D line is fit to each projected wall instance with RANSAC to recover the wall
parameters defined in Li et al. [2025].
Room-level wall sequence annotation.
Starting from the initialized global wall instances, this step
produces the room-level wall sequences used as the final multi-room floor-plan layout for each floor.
We designed a custom graphical interface that lets annotators reorder, merge, and split wall instances,
and group them into room-wise ordered sequences with globally consistent wall identities. Each
room sequence may be closed or open, since incomplete mesh coverage in HM3DSem often prevents
some rooms from being represented as closed polygons.
Per-panorama column-wise annotation.
Given the annotated multi-room wall layout and
panorama camera poses, we project floor geometry and wall instances into each panorama to generate
column-wise floor boundaries and wall-instance semantic labels. A separate manual annotation pass
is then used to mask out columns with subpar projected assets, for example when floor boundaries
are misaligned with visible structure or wall-instance assignments are incorrect.
G
Baseline Details
We evaluate modular baselines under a common three-stage pipeline: (1) pose estimation in a shared
floor-plane frame, (2) construction of pose-aligned floor boundaries or point-maps, and (3) layout
vectorization. This setup lets us compare existing pose, boundary, and layout components under the
same sparse multi-view input setting. Unlike MvFFN, these baselines do not perform joint multi-view
reasoning directly from panorama images in the final layout stage; the layout backend receives only
pose-aligned geometric inputs, so upstream pose or boundary errors are already fixed before layout
prediction.
G.1
Pose Evaluation
Pose estimation baselines.
We compare against two pose baselines: CovisPose+Greedy Spanning
Tree (GST) and VGGT. For CovisPose, pairwise relative panorama poses are predicted for all
panorama pairs, and global 3-DoF poses (2D position and 1D rotation) are recovered by chaining
relative transforms along a co-visibility-ranked greedy spanning tree, following prior panoramic
pose-estimation work Nejatishahidin et al. [2023], Li et al. [2025]. VGGT Wang et al. [2025] does
not operate directly on 360^\circ panoramas, so we adapt it through perspective decomposition. We
exclude GraphCovis Nejatishahidin et al. [2023] and PanoPose Tu et al. [2024] because public code
is unavailable.
For each view-count setting, we evaluate on 1,000 N-view test tuples from ZInD-XV. Tuples are
generated following the prior pose-evaluation protocol Nejatishahidin et al. [2023]: views are
sampled from the same floor with minimum pairwise co-visibility of 0.3, and floors are sampled to
maintain broad floor coverage. Predicted pose graphs are aligned to ground truth before evaluation
following Nejatishahidin et al. [2023], Li et al. [2025]. We report per-panorama position error in
centimeters and yaw error in degrees, summarized by mean, standard deviation, and median over all
evaluated panoramas.
VGGT panorama adaptation.
Since VGGT operates on perspective images, each 360^\circ panorama
is decomposed into overlapping rectilinear crops with 120^\circ field of view, 60^\circ horizontal overlap,
19


resolution 560 \times 
560, and pitch angles 0^\circ and -30^\circ . All crops from all panoramas are processed
jointly in a single forward pass. We recover metric scale by aligning the vertical histogram peak of
the predicted point cloud to the camera height provided by ZInD Cruz et al. [2021]. We then estimate
one planar pose per panorama by compensating each crop pose for its known azimuthal offset and
robustly fitting a consensus position and yaw across crops.
G.2
Layout Baseline Pipelines
Layout baseline pipelines.
Given Stage-1 poses, all modular layout baselines reconstruct multi-
view layouts by first building geometric inputs in the global floor frame and then applying a layout
vectorization backend. Unless otherwise noted, we use CovisPose poses; GT-pose variants replace
them with ground-truth poses to isolate layout error from pose error. LGT-Net provides panorama-
level floor-wall boundary cues, while RoomFormer and PolyRoom use fused top-down geometric
inputs. We do not use VGGT poses for layout baselines because its pose accuracy is substantially
worse than CovisPose on ZInD-XV.
LGT-Net backend.
LGT-Net Jiang et al. [2022] is applied independently to each panorama using the
pretrained ZInD checkpoint without fine-tuning. Its predicted floor-wall boundary is converted into a
local floor-plan polygon using Manhattan-world post-processing with Douglas–Peucker simplification
(\ e psilon =0. 1), then projected into the global floor plane using the input poses. Polygons whose overlap
exceeds 50\ % of the smaller polygon area are grouped and merged by Shapely union. We also report
a GT-pose variant that replaces predicted poses with ground-truth poses.
Topdown training data construction.
RoomFormer Yue et al. [2023] and PolyRoom Liu et al.
[2024] operate on topdown geometric inputs rather than panoramas, so we finetune both models on
ZInD-XV training data constructed to mimic sparse multi-view observations. Starting from lifted
floor-plans with fixed ceiling height and room connectivity preserved through open doors, we project
per-panorama floor wall boundary points into the global frame using groundtruth poses and rasterize
them into 256 \times 
256 orthographic floor plane density maps. The corresponding room polygons from
ZInD-XV are used as supervision. To match test-time conditions, we train with 2, 3, 5, and 8-view
samples and add Gaussian noise with \ s igma =0. 001 on boundary locations, \ s igma =0. 1 m on panorama
positions, and \ s igma =0. 05 rad on panorama yaw.
RoomFormer.
RoomFormer Yue et al. [2023] is initialized from its Structured3D checkpoint
with a ResNet-50 backbone, 800 vertex queries, and up to 20 room polygons per scene. We fine-tune
RoomFormer on the rendered ZInD-XV density maps and room polygon supervision described above,
using AdamW with learning rate 2 \ times 10^{-4} (2 \ times 10^{-5} for the backbone), weight decay 10^{-4}, batch
size 10, and gradient clipping at max norm 0.1. Fine-tuning runs for approximately 70 epochs
from the Structured3D initialization within a MultiStepLR schedule whose learning-rate decay is
configured at epoch 400 (not reached). At inference, we render the topdown density map from the
evaluated input poses and predicted LGT-Net floor wall boundaries, then apply the same vectorization
pipeline: corners with foreground probability > 0.5 are retained, polygons with area < 100 \,\mathrm {px}^ 2
are discarded, and surviving corners are mapped back to metric floor plane coordinates using a
precomputed homography.
PolyRoom.
PolyRoom Liu et al. [2024] uses the same rendered input representation, but adds a
Mask2Former Cheng et al. [2021] room-instance segmentation stage whose predicted room contours
initialize a RoomFormer style polygon decoder instead of fixed learned queries. We train PolyRoom in
two stages: first fine-tuning a Mask2Former with Swin-L backbone initialized from ImageNet-22K
pretraining, then fine-tuning the PolyRoom decoder from its Structured3D checkpoint on the same
rendered ZInD-XV data. Stage 1 trains Mask2Former at 256 \times 
256 input resolution with AdamW
(learning rate 10^{-4}, weight decay 0.05, batch size 2) for 400 epochs, with a backbone learning-rate
multiplier of 0.1 and standard random-flip, random-resize, and random-crop augmentations. Stage
2 resumes from the Structured3D-pretrained PolyRoom checkpoint, including its optimizer and
scheduler state, and continues fine-tuning on ZInD-XV for the remainder of the original MultiStepLR
schedule (configured for 500 total epochs with a learning-rate decay at epoch 400) using AdamW
(learning rate 2 \ times 10^{-4}, 2 \ times 10^{-5} for the backbone), weight decay 10^{-4}, batch size 10, gradient
clipping at max norm 0.1, 800 vertex queries, and up to 20 room polygons per scene. At inference,
20


Mask2Former instances with score > 0.6 are converted to contours using Douglas–Peucker simplifi-
cation (\ e p silon =5\,\mathrm {px} ) and used to seed the decoder. From the decoded polygons, we retain corners with
foreground probability > 0.1 and interior angles within 30^\circ of a right angle, merge nearby corners
within 10 pixels, discard polygons with area < 100 \,\mathrm {px}^ 2, and map the surviving polygons back to
metric floor-plane coordinates.
G.3
Layout Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate wall and junction precision, recall, and F1 using instance-level matching. A predicted
wall is considered a candidate match if its orientation differs by at most 10^\circ and its perpendicular
distance is within 2.0 m of a ground-truth wall. Candidate wall pairs are then matched by Hungarian
assignment based on line overlap. Junction precision, recall, and F1 are defined analogously: a
junction is counted as matched when its two incident walls are both matched. We also report mean
matched-wall localization error. We use these structure-aware metrics because MvFFN predicts both
closed polygons and open wall sequences, making region-based \protect \mathrm  {IoU} not well defined for all outputs.
G.4
Downstream Refinement Protocol
We evaluate planar bundle adjustment (BA) Zhou [2023] and BADGR Li et al. [2025] as downstream
refinement methods applied to the MvFFN layouts from Sec. 5.3 of the main paper. For each method,
we report mean and median matched wall distances before and after refinement on the full evaluation
set, and on the subset of samples whose mean matched wall distance improves; the latter also defines
the improvement rate. Before refinement, partial room shapes are closed with a simple rule-based
procedure to avoid self-intersections. This can introduce a small number of completed walls without
image correspondences, which is especially challenging for BADGR because it assumes the number
of room walls is known. This explains why BADGR is more sensitive to initialization in our setting.

I
Limitations and Broader Impact
Limitations. MvFFN assumes straightened equirectangular RGB panoramas from a single connected
indoor pose graph, with sufficient overlap between neighboring views and known relative camera
heights. Performance may degrade when these assumptions are violated, for example under extremely
sparse captures, low co-visibility, severe occlusion, inaccurate camera-height metadata, strongly non-
Manhattan or curved architecture, low-light or textureless images, or scenes outside the residential
indoor domains evaluated here. Because structured multi-room training data remains limited, the
current model uses two-view rotation and correspondence priors during training and inference,
making performance sensitive to prior quality under domain shift. The method can return partial
or open room layouts when visibility is insufficient, and downstream refinement can degrade when
the initial layout or pixel-to-wall correspondences are noisy. The dense anchor-conditioned MOIS
inference and attention layers also scale with the number of views and image columns, so memory
and runtime should be considered when applying the method to larger view sets.
Broader impact and responsible use.
This work can support useful applications such as lower-cost
indoor digital twins, AR/VR walkthroughs, assistive indoor navigation, and robotics mapping. At the
same time, accurate reconstruction of private interior layouts can create privacy and security risks
if used without consent, and erroneous reconstructions may be harmful in downstream navigation,
robotics, or planning systems if treated as safety-critical. We therefore intend the method and released
assets for research and consented indoor reconstruction, not for covert mapping, access-control,
insurance, credit, law-enforcement, or other consequential decisions. Released annotations and code
should follow the licenses and access restrictions of the underlying datasets, document intended use
and limitations, and avoid distributing raw indoor imagery beyond what the original dataset terms
permit.

Dataset card summary.
ZInD-XV contains derived multi-view floor-plan annotations, globally
indexed wall instances, room-wise wall sequences, and per-panorama column-wise floor-boundary
and wall-instance labels. HM3D-XV contains analogous derived annotations for residential HM3DSem
scenes. The released files include train/validation/test splits, per-floor metadata, per-panorama
labels, wall-instance identifiers, room connectivity, and evaluation tuples. The README specifies
the file format, coordinate convention, label definitions, preprocessing steps, conversion scripts,
and evaluation protocol. The intended use is research on indoor layout reconstruction, camera
pose estimation, and structured multi-view scene understanding. Out-of-scope uses include covert
mapping of private spaces, security-sensitive reconstruction without consent, and consequential
decision-making based on predicted layouts.
Responsible release.
Indoor floor-plans can reveal sensitive information about private spaces. To
reduce privacy, security, and license risks, we do not redistribute raw restricted panoramas, meshes,
textures, renderings, or floor-plan source files. The released package contains only compatible derived
annotations, metadata, splits, and scripts. Users are required to obtain the original datasets from their
providers and comply with the corresponding terms. The release documentation states intended uses,
out-of-scope uses, limitations, and access restrictions. We will release all code, trained checkpoints,
and utilities upon acceptance.
Annotation provenance.
Manual annotation and cleanup for ZInD-XV and HM3D-XV were per-
formed by the authors/research team using custom annotation and quality-control tools. No crowd-
workers, contractors, or human-subject study participants were recruited. The released documentation
describes the annotation interface, label definitions, quality-control procedure, and cleanup criteria.

Compute resources.
All MvFFN experiments were run on 8 or 16 A100 GPUs with 40GB memory
per GPU, using 6 12 cpus per gpu. Note that the experiments can also be conducted on lower-tier
gpus. Stage-1 geometry pre-training used approximately 2 days for 80k iterations, and stage-2 layout
semantic training used approximately 2.5 days for 80k iterations. We used a batch size of [10,7,4,2]
for 2, 3, 5, 8 views with 6 12 dataloader workers per process.
