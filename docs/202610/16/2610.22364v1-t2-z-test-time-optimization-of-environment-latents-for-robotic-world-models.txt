ABSTRACT
Predicting how the world responds to an action requires more than what can be
seen: robots must account for environmental factors that are neither directly ob-
servable nor explicitly specified, from physical properties such as friction and
mass to human-defined environmental rules. We introduce T2-Z for test-time op-
timization of environment latents, which augments a robotic world model with
a learnable environment latent to condition world predictions. This compact la-
tent captures underlying environmental variation without supervision of what en-
vironmental factors exist or what values they take. During training, T2-Z alter-
nates between two stages: a world-model optimization stage that fixes the la-

INTRODUCTION
What a robot observes is insufficient to determine how the world will respond. Beneath visually
similar scene configurations reside hidden environmental factors, such as friction, mass distribution,
and unobserved human-defined interaction rules, that can fundamentally alter the consequences of
the same action (fig. 1 (a)). Such factors are often revealed only through interaction with the target
environments, as their influence becomes observable through the outcomes they induce. Explicitly
representing these factors (Kumar et al., 2021), however, requires specifying in advance what factors
may vary, their exact values, and how they should be parameterized, which is difficult to scale to
unknown or compounded environmental variation. On the contrary, humans reason through “analy-
sis by synthesis” (Yuille & Kersten, 2006), using internal generative models not only to anticipate
future outcomes, but also in reverse to infer latent structure that best accounts for what they observe.
This raises a natural question: can hidden environmental factors be similarly captured in a latent
representation that is directly optimized from test-time interactions?
We introduce T2-Z for test-time optimization of environmental latents, which augments an action-
conditioned robotic world model with a learnable environment latent ze. ze is a randomly-initialized
compact representation that is expected to encode the unobserved factors governing how an envi-
ronment responds to interaction, with no supervision about what environmental factors it should
represent. The world model predicts future visual observations conditioned jointly on the action
and ze. We iteratively optimize the world model and ze in an alternating two-stage procedure: 1)
world-model optimization: with ze fixed, we train the world model across all trajectories to learn
shared action-conditioned dynamics across environments; 2) environment-latent optimization: with
the world model frozen, we optimize the conditioned latent ze through prediction error, so that it
captures underlying environment-specific variation not explained by the general world model alone.
Both stages are optimized with the same flow-matching loss and alternated for multiple rounds until
convergence, allowing the environment representation to emerge directly through prediction without
predefined physical-factor supervision or a separate environment encoder.
At test time, the world model is frozen and T2-Z turns environment inference into a direct opti-
mization problem. For a previously unseen environmental configuration, we initialize a new ze and
optimize ze to explain the outcomes of one or more observed test-time interactions. Specifically,
we update ze for multiple optimization steps through the same flow-matching objective used dur-
ing stage-two training, minimizing prediction error on the observed interactions. Because inference
is optimization-based rather than amortized through a learned history encoder, T2-Z is not tied to
the trajectory count used for training and can flexibly incorporate any number of test-time trajecto-
ries. Once optimized, the latent turns the frozen world model into an environment-adapted test-time
simulator, enabling prediction and comparison of candidate action outcomes before execution.
We evaluate T2-Z across 7 simulated settings and 4 real-world robotic tasks. Quantitatively and
qualitatively, T2-Z improves world-model prediction across visual quality, physical prediction met-
rics (e.g., relevant object locations), and downstream action selection. T2-Z reduces physical pre-
diction error from 12.86 to 4.80 in simulation and tracked-state error from 19.30 to 10.85 pixels in
the real world, while improving action success from 37.9% to 74.2% and from 26.5% to 70.8%,
respectively. PCA of the learned latents reveals structured representations that reflect the underly-
ing environmental factors, including a multi-dimensional organization when more than one factors
vary jointly, despite the model never knowing how many factors are present or what they represent
(fig. 1(b)). In our multi-background setting, a unified latent structure emerges across diverse scene

Two distinct strands of work are particularly related to our problem.
Robotic world models have made rapid progress in predicting action-conditioned futures (Zhou
et al., 2025; 2024; Zhu et al., 2024; Zhen et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026; Huang et al.,
2026; Gao et al., 2026). More recent work explores test-time adaptation to improve long-horizon
memory or incorporate new behavioral supervision (Wan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Feng et al.,
2026; Yoo et al., 2025; Jang et al., 2026). These approaches predict future outcomes from visual
observations alone, without accounting for underlying environmental variation that is not directly
observable. (fig. 2 (a)). In contrast, T2-Z disentangles unobserved environmental variation from the
world model’s observable scene representation, through environment latent conditioning.
Environment representations and system identification have demonstrated the value of
environment-specific representations for system identification and rapid behavioral adaptation (Yu
et al., 2017; Nagabandi et al., 2019; Rakelly et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2020; Kumar et al., 2021; Miki
et al., 2022; Nahrendra et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024). The Rapid
Motor Adaptation (RMA) family and related methods typically rely on predefined privileged param-
eters (Kumar et al., 2021; Miki et al., 2022; Nahrendra et al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2024).
For example, RMA restricts adaptation to 5 factors and uses their ground-truth values to learn an em-
bedding that an adaptation encoder later recovers from interaction history (Kumar et al., 2021). This
prespecifies the environment representation and ties inference to a learned history-to-embedding
mapping, limiting flexibility under unknown, compounded, or out-of-distribution variation (fig. 2
(b)). These methods also generally rely on a learned history encoder (Chen et al., 2024; Xing et al.,
2026), limiting generalization when the amount of test-time interaction differs from that seen during
training. In contrast, T2-Z learns representations of environmental factors implicitly through world
prediction error, without predefined ground-truth physical parameters or a learned history encoder.
Our method is also related to the literature on Test-Time Adaptation (TTA), which has enabled
robots to improve after deployment by adapting policy, prompts, or memory rather than optimizing
a dedicated environment representation.
Existing approaches include test-time adaptation of
diffusion and flow policies (Li et al., 2025; 2026), online policy adaptation (Liu et al., 2026; Jin
& Zhang, 2026; Zhu et al., 2026), and adaptation through latent prompts, fast weights, memory, or
retrieval (Zhang et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026; Feng et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026; Park et al., 2026).
TTA has also been applied directly to predictive models through video replanning, world-model
training, and closed-loop latent adaptation (Ko et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026).

We introduce T2-Z (fig. 2 & algorithm 1), a framework for adapting robotic world models to hidden
environmental variation through direct optimization of an environment latent. Our key idea is to sep-
arate the general visual predictive capability of a world model from unobserved environment-specific
information. During training, we learn these two components through alternating optimization un-
der a shared flow-matching objective. At test time, we freeze the world model and optimize only a
newly initialized environment latent from observed test-time interaction trajectories. The optimized
latent subsequently conditions the frozen world model to predict the outcomes of diverse actions.

Unlike amortized history-encoder approaches, T2-Z uses direct optimization. This avoids commit-
ting to a fixed history mapping and searches for the representation that best explains the current envi-
ronment. The formulation can naturally incorporate any number of test-time trajectories that differs
from training, by optimizing a latent against all available evidence. Moreover, T2-Z is not restricted
to the range of factor values during training, providing greater flexibility under out-of-distribution
configurations. Ablation in fig. 5 shows this optimization remains stable across varying test-time
trajectory numbers, and is interaction-efficient with a single trajectory sufficient in most settings.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Tasks
We evaluate T2-Z across 7 simulated settings and 4 real-world robotic tasks. In simulation,
Friction requires predicting how far an object moves after a push under different surface friction;
Gravity varies gravitational strength and requires predicting the object motion; Mass Collision
varies colliding object mass and predicts the resulting collision motion. Light Switch assigns dif-
ferent human-defined hidden interaction rules that determine whether a robot action turns the light
on or off; Mass Balance predicts the tilt of a dumbbell under different mass distribution; Mass
× Friction jointly varies object mass and surface friction at the same time; Diverse Visual Back-
grounds evaluates the same friction variations across 30 diverse scene setups, testing whether the
learned environment representation remains consistent across visual changes.. In the real world,
Door Close varies the spring-lock stiffness, which changes how the door responds to closing ac-
tions; Ball Friction requires predicting the motion of a pushed ball of unknown mass; Stick Bal-
ance requires predicting the motion of a stick with objects of different masses attached to its ends;
and Soft Pull requires predicting the deformation a soft object under different rotating actions.

Evaluation Metrics.
(1) Physical Error measures task-relevant state prediction: object-location
error in pixels for Friction, Gravity, Mass Collision, and Diverse Backgrounds; lamp activation error
for Light Switch; and bar-tilt angle error in degrees for Mass Balance. For real-world tasks, we
measure tracked-state error in pixels. We report Physical Error (Mean), averaged over predicted
frames, and Physical Error (Final), measured at the final predicted frame. (2) Video Generation
Quality is measured by LPIPS and PSNR between predicted and ground-truth videos. LPIPS mea-
sures perceptual discrepancy in a learned feature space, capturing differences in visual structure and
appearance, and serves as our primary measure of generation quality. In contrast, PSNR primarily
reflects pixel-level reconstruction fidelity and is less informative about semantically meaningful dif-
ferences or whether object motion is physically correct. (3) Action Success treats the world model
as a simulator: given candidate actions, we predict their outcomes and select the action most likely
to achieve the task goal, such as pushing a ball to a target, balancing a stick, or closing a door. We
execute the chose action in real world and observe if meets the success criterions in Supp section C.

Single-Factor Environmental Variation.
We first evaluate T2-Z under individual unobserved

discrete human-defined rules. As shown in table 1, T2-Z achieves the highest action success on
all five tasks, increasing the five-task average from 36.9% for the Standard World Model to 76.6%,
while reducing average LPIPS from 0.0469 to 0.0325. It also substantially improves task-relevant
prediction, reducing mean error from 11.06 to 3.85 pixels on Friction, 21.93 to 6.45 pixels on Mass
Collision, and 1.18◦to 0.64◦on Mass Balance. These gains arise because T2-Z directly optimizes
an environment latent from observed interactions, capturing environment-specific variation more
flexibly than non-adaptive or amortized baselines.

Compounded Environmental Factors.

We test whether the learned environment represen-
tation captures underlying dynamics without becoming entangled with visual appearance. Specifi-
cally, we ask whether a unified latent structure emerges across visually diverse scenes despite sub-

that T2-Z outperforms all baselines across every metric. fig. 4(c) further shows that latents from dif-
ferent backgrounds organize consistently rather than separating according to scene appearance. This
indicates that environment-latent can isolate environment-specific information that is shared across
visually different observations, enabling a unified latent structure to emerge across diverse scenes.

ABLATIVE & QUALITATIVE STUDIES
We ablate the key design choices of T2-Z in fig. 5 and table 3, including latent optimization, interac-
tion evidence, latent parameterization, and alternating training. In fig. 5(a), we vary the learning-rate
strategy. Our schedule performs best, validating both its coarse-to-fine form and learning-rate scale.
Figure 5(b) varies test-time optimization from 10–60 steps; performance improves up to 40 steps and
then saturates, motivating our default. Figure 5(c,d) ablate the number of test-time interaction tra-
jectories. Performance largely saturates beyond the selected number of trajectories, indicating that
additional interaction provides limited benefit. Recall that T2-Z uses only one test-time trajectory
for 7 tasks, and two for 4 tasks, demonstrating that T2-Z can infer useful environment information
with as few as one or two interactions. Additional ablations in table 3 study latent parameterization
and training. All the ablated models underperform our model, supporting our alternating schedule
and latent mechanism. We provide the latent-dimensionality ablation in Supplementary table 12.

CONCLUSION
We introduce T2-Z which augments robotic world models with enviornment latent conditioning.
T2-Z alternates between world-model optimization that learns action-conditioned dyanmics, and
environment-latent optimization that captures hidden environment factors. For a new environment,
the world model is frozen and only the latent is optimized. Across 7 simulation and 4 real-world
tasks, T2-Z outperforms baselines a lot in both video prediction and action selection.

A.1
ARCHITECTURE AND LATENT CONDITIONING
Inputs and inherited backbone.
For an episode in environment e, the model receives one ini-
tial RGB frame I0 and an aligned sequence a = (at)t of 14-D robot actions, and predicts the
subsequent video frames. We initialize the video diffusion transformer (DiT) and action encoder
from BWM (BWM Team, 2026), retaining its Wan2.2(Wan et al., 2025)-based action-conditioning
pathway. The frozen video VAE encodes the initial frame into the video-latent stream and de-
codes predicted latents back to RGB. Text conditioning is disabled; the initial frame is not an extra
cross-attention token. In short, I0 enters the video-latent stream, a follows BWM’s two action-
conditioning paths, and the environment latent augments both paths.
Environment-indexed latent conditioning.
We associate each environment e with a freely opti-
mized vector ze ∈R32. Episodes assigned to an environment share its latent; their initial frames and
actions vary, so predicting their different outcomes constrains the environment representation. There
is no observation-to-latent encoder and no supervision from the numerical physical parameter. Let
D = 3072 be the DiT width. Two separate projectors turn ze into model-width vectors:

SIMULATOR AND REAL-WORLD SETUP
Simulator.
We construct the custom tasks in a LIBERO-based simulator (Liu et al., 2023) with a
Franka Panda arm. Simulated RGB videos are recorded at 20 fps and 224 × 224 resolution. The
action representation has 14 channels.
Real world.
The physical tasks use a Franka robot arm and a fixed Intel RealSense D435i camera.
We use only its main RGB view, captured at 640 × 480 and 20 fps; the task-specific saved video
crops and model-input sizes appear in Table 6. Model inputs are letterboxed to the listed sizes. All
tasks use 14-channel action vectors; the target-action representation is task dependent.

D
BASELINE IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
All four baselines use the task-level training data and per-method GPU budgets in Tables 5–6. They
use the same query videos and, for action-selection tasks, the same candidate actions and success
criteria as T2-Z. The three adaptive baselines receive exactly the same test-time interaction trajec-
tories as our latent optimizer. The tables give the common task-level batch specification; DINOv2
Context uses a subset of sampled trajectories for conditioning and all sampled trajectories as queries
during training, whereas TTT-KQV processes them as ordered within-environment streams.
D.1
STANDARD WORLD MODEL
This baseline fine-tunes the BWM-initialized DiT and action encoder on pooled task trajectories
without an environment latent or test-time adaptation. It uses AdamW with model learning rate
10−5, weight decay 0.01, 100 warmup updates, gradient clipping 0.5, and bfloat16.
D.2
LORA TTA
Starting from the Standard World Model, rank-8, α = 8 LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) are placed
on the DiT attention Q/K/V/output projections. The backbone stays frozen during test-time adapta-
tion. Adapters are reset for each environment and optimized on its test-time interaction trajectories,
then held fixed across that environment’s query actions. The Friction configuration uses 50 AdamW
21


Preprint
adaptation updates at learning rate 10−4, zero weight decay, and gradient clipping 1; task-specific
adaptation configurations are released with the code.
D.3
DINOV2 CONTEXT
A frozen DINOv2-B/14 encoder (Oquab et al., 2024) extracts conditioning-frame features, while a
learned 128-D action GRU summarizes the intervening 14-D actions. For each of N task-configured
sampled frames, the CLS and mean patch features are projected to 256 dimensions. The N −1 adja-
cent visual–action transition features (640-D each) are concatenated; a 640(N −1)→1024→1024
MLP and final projection produce the 32-D context. Multiple conditioning-trajectory summaries
are averaged before that final projection. At each training update, we sample a set of trajectories
Qe from a training environment and select a subset Ce ⊆Qe for conditioning. All trajectories in
Qe serve as training queries. The number of conditioning trajectories is sampled from the task-
configured choices (typically one or two; one to four for Soft Pull), so the predictor learns to use
different conditioning-set sizes. Every query in Qe is predicted using only the context inferred from
Ce; training minimizes the mean query-video flow-matching loss. The context head, BWM DiT,
action encoder, and two context projectors are trained jointly, while DINOv2 remains frozen. The
model and context-head learning rates are 10−5 and 10−4, respectively. At test time, context comes
from a forward pass over the observed test-time interaction trajectories, with no gradient update.
D.4
TTT-KQV
We adapt a video test-time-training fast-weight module (Dalal et al., 2025) to the BWM backbone.
Gated two-layer GELU MLP modules with expansion factor four are inserted after eight approxi-
mately evenly spaced attention blocks. Training randomly shuffles the chunks from each sampled
environment in a batch. Within each training stream, all chunks use the same sampled flow-matching
timestep (but independent noise), matching the same-timestep processing of observed interactions
and query videos at inference. At position i, the preceding chunks form the observed prefix and the
current chunk is the query. Fast weights reset to a learned shared initialization at the start of each
stream. For each chunk, the module causally writes its key–value tokens before reading its output.
