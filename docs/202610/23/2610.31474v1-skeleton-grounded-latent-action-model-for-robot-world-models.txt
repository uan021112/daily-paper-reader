ABSTRACT
Robot world models simulate how environments evolve in response to robot ac-
tions. Latent action models enable learning dynamics from videos by encoding
visual transitions as proxy actions. However, reconstruction-driven latent actions
mix robot motion with object responses and other scene changes, complicating
their correspondence with robot controls.
We propose SkeLAM, a skeleton-
guided latent action model for controllable robot world modeling. SkeLAM de-
composes latent actions into a kinematic component grounded by robot skeleton
supervision and a residual component retaining complementary visual dynamics.
Cross-modal swap reconstruction encourages RGB-derived and skeleton-derived
kinematic representations to be interchangeable during video reconstruction. The
world model incorporates both components through separate conditioning path-
ways, while branch dropout reduces its dependence on future-derived residual in-
formation. A lightweight adapter maps robot actions to the kinematic space, and
subsequent world-model fine-tuning adapts generation to these predicted condi-
tions. At inference, the model generates future observations from the current ob-
servation and robot actions. On DROID, SkeLAM reduces end-effector ADE by
19.5% and LPIPS by 11.1% compared with DreamDojo. Motion probing supports
kinematic specialization, while qualitative results demonstrate zero-shot human-
to-robot motion transfer in generated rollouts. Policy evaluation shows agreement
between predicted success rates and those measured in real robot executions.

INTRODUCTION
Robot world models simulate how environments evolve in response to robot actions, supporting pol-
icy evaluation and model-based planning (Yang et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Accurate simulation
requires modeling robot motion and resulting scene changes. Latent action models encode visual
transitions as proxy actions (Bruce et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2025), allowing world models to learn dy-
namics from large-scale videos without explicit action annotations (Gao et al., 2026). These latent
actions are learned through future-observation reconstruction, with the current observation provid-
ing visual context (Gao et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2026). For action-conditioned simulation, inferred
latent actions serve as supervision for mapping robot controls into the learned latent action space.
Learning this mapping is challenging since reconstruction objectives encourage latent actions to en-
code robot motion together with object responses and other scene changes (Nikulin et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2025). For example, similar end-effector motions may produce different object re-
sponses under different contact states (Gu et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026), introducing scene-dependent
variation beyond robot motion into the adaptation target. These interaction outcomes are useful for
world modeling, since accurate simulation requires predicting how robot motion affects the sur-
rounding scene. This motivates a dedicated kinematic component for action adaptation, together
with a residual component retaining complementary transition information for dynamics learning.
To this end, we propose SkeLAM, a skeleton-guided latent action model for controllable robot
world modeling. Robot skeletons describe robot configuration and motion while abstracting scene
appearance. Related robot world models use skeleton sequences or kinematic renderings as direct
conditioning signals for generation (Wang et al., 2025b; Wu & Gao, 2026; Xu et al., 2026). SkeLAM
uses paired RGB and skeleton transitions to learn kinematic and residual components from RGB

latent supplies complementary information for RGB reconstruction. Together, the two components
jointly support reconstruction, providing a dedicated target for action adaptation while retaining
information for dynamics learning. During representation learning, skeletons supervise the RGB

We further introduce cross-modal swap reconstruction, which replaces the RGB kinematic latent
with its paired skeleton counterpart while retaining the RGB residual. Reconstructing the same
transition under this substitution encourages compatible motion conditioning in the RGB decoder,
extending cross-modal correspondence beyond alignment in latent space. The world model learns
from both components through separate conditioning pathways, while branch dropout prepares it
for the zero-residual condition used at deployment. A lightweight adapter maps robot actions to
the kinematic space, and subsequent world-model fine-tuning adapts generation to these predicted
conditions. At inference, generation uses the current observation and robot actions.
To assess motion accuracy and visual fidelity, we evaluate SkeLAM on DROID (Khazatsky et al.,
2024). Compared with DreamDojo, SkeLAM reduces end-effector ADE by 19.5% and LPIPS by
11.1%. Motion probing shows that the kinematic component approaches the performance of the con-
catenated representation with half the latent dimensions, while latent interventions and component
ablations support its role in generation. Qualitative results further demonstrate zero-shot human-to-
robot motion transfer in generated rollouts. Open-loop policy evaluation shows agreement between
predicted success rates and those measured in real robot executions.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose SkeLAM, a skeleton-guided latent action model that separates the conditioning roles
of robot motion and complementary visual dynamics, providing a dedicated kinematic target for
robot action adaptation.
• We introduce cross-modal swap reconstruction to encourage compatible RGB and skeleton mo-
tion conditioning during generation. Combined with branch dropout, this design supports robot
action conditioning through the kinematic pathway learned from visual transitions.
• Experiments on DROID demonstrate gains in motion accuracy and visual fidelity, supported by
representation analyses and component ablations. We further show zero-shot human-to-robot
motion transfer and agreement between predicted and measured policy success rates.

Action-conditioned robot world models. Robot world models support interactive simulation (Yang

actions with 3D point-cloud latent predictions and task-completion scores. Control representations
include action embeddings, skeleton sequences (Wang et al., 2025b; Wu & Gao, 2026), and kine-
matic renderings (Xu et al., 2026). Recent models further incorporate spatial grounding (Gu et al.,
2026; Yang et al., 2026b). Separating camera and object motion also supports camera-controlled
video generation (Cheng et al., 2026b).

frozen video features across contexts. LAOF (Bu et al., 2026b) uses agent optical flow as motion

pervision (Govind et al., 2026), alongside advances in latent representations for policy learning (Liu
et al., 2026a; Shen et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). LARYBench (Nie et al., 2026)
evaluates representations using semantic action classification and physical action regression, inde-
pendently of downstream policies. A systematic study finds that proxy metrics support coarse model

METHODOLOGY
SkeLAM learns structured latent actions for video-based world modeling and subsequent adaptation
to robot controls. As illustrated in Figure 1, skeleton supervision grounds a kinematic component in
robot motion, while a residual component retains complementary visual dynamics. The world model
learns from both components, with branch dropout preparing it for action-conditioned inference. An
action adapter is then trained to predict kinematic latents from robot actions, followed by world-
model fine-tuning using these predictions.

EXPERIMENT
We evaluate the effect of the kinematic–residual decomposition on action-conditioned world mod-
eling, and examine motion transfer and rollout evaluation as downstream applications. Specifically,
we investigate the following questions:
• RQ1 (World modeling): Does adapting robot actions to a dedicated kinematic component im-
prove motion accuracy while preserving visual fidelity? (Section 4.2)
• RQ2 (Motion representation): Do skeleton-grounded kinematic latents support more accurate
linear decoding of robot motion than unstructured latent actions? (Section 4.3)
• RQ3 (Motion transfer): Can a kinematic representation learned from robot interactions transfer
human motion to generated robot rollouts in a zero-shot setting? (Section 4.4)
• RQ4 (Policy evaluation): How closely do open-loop world-model rollouts reproduce policy
rankings and success rates measured in real robot executions? (Section 4.5)
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Data. DROID (Khazatsky et al., 2024) pairs visual observations with robot actions, allowing us to
assess whether predicted dynamics follow robot controls. We train the latent action model and world
model on DROID, incorporating AgiBot (Bu et al., 2025) to broaden the range of robot interactions
observed during training. Evaluation uses 400 DROID clips, each containing one conditioning frame
and 12 future frames at 480 × 640 resolution. More details are provided in Appendix A.
Evaluation Metrics. To quantify trajectory, direction, and magnitude agreement, we report 2D
end-effector average displacement error (ADE, px), direction macro-F1 (F1dir), normalized mag-
nitude MAE (Lmag
1
), and magnitude Pearson correlation (rmag). PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS (Zhang
et al., 2018) assess pixel, structural, and perceptual fidelity, respectively, with tLPIPS reported as a
temporal diagnostic. More details are provided in Appendix C.1.
Implementation Details. We implement the latent action model using Transformer VAEs with
dkin = dres = 32 and use Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B as the world-model backbone. We train with
AdamW, using stage-specific learning rates and global batch sizes. SkeLAM training uses NVIDIA
A100 GPUs and requires approximately 2 days for one complete run. Architectural specifications,
loss weights, training schedules, and inference settings are provided in Appendix B.
Baselines. We compare SkeLAM with latent-action world models DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026)
and CD-LAM (Wei et al., 2026). Alternative conditioning strategies include direct robot actions in
IRASim (Zhu et al., 2025), Ctrl-World (Guo et al., 2026), Cosmos-Predict2.5 (Ali et al., 2025), Cos-
mos3 (Agarwal et al., 2026), and GE-Sim (Liao et al., 2025), and geometric guidance in EnerVerse-
AC (Jiang et al., 2025) and OSCAR (Wu & Gao, 2026). TesserAct (Zhen et al., 2025), and GE-
Base (Liao et al., 2025) serve as text-conditioned references for visual fidelity, with evaluation de-
tails provided in Appendix C.

4.3
MOTION REPRESENTATION ANALYSIS
Motion decodability. To examine how robot motion is encoded, we train linear probes on frozen
latent representations. As shown in Table 3, the kinematic component achieves an EEF MAE of
5.99×10−3 and a magnitude macro-F1 of 0.7467, improving on both latent-action baselines. Within
SkeLAM, it also outperforms the residual component on all three metrics. Concatenating the resid-
ual increases EEF R2 from 0.3200 to 0.3277 and magnitude macro-F1 from 0.7467 to 0.7482. The
kinematic component thus approaches the probing performance of the full representation using half
the latent dimensions, supporting its specialization for displacement and magnitude encoding.
Cross-modal compatibility. To assess cross-modal motion conditioning, we intervene on the latent
inputs to the RGB decoder. Figure 4 shows that substituting paired and mismatched skeleton kine-
matic means increases reconstruction MAE by 0.53% and 11.67%, respectively. SSIM exhibits the
same ordering. Zeroing the kinematic component increases MAE by 5.78%, compared with 0.61%
for the residual. The greater degradation under mismatched substitution than kinematic removal
indicates sensitivity to motion correspondence. These results support compatible RGB and skeleton
conditioning and a functional role for the kinematic component in reconstruction.
4.4
ZERO-SHOT HUMAN-TO-ROBOT MOTION TRANSFER
To examine whether the learned motion representation transfers across embodiments, we use hu-
man demonstration videos from UniSkill (Kim et al., 2025) as motion prompts. The RGB encoder
extracts latent actions from these videos to directly condition SkeLAM, with a robot observation
specifying the initial scene. All model parameters remain fixed during transfer. We compare with
the pretrained checkpoint of DreamDojo (Gao et al., 2026), which supports latent-action condition-
ing. As shown in Figure 5, SkeLAM generates distinct end-effector motions corresponding to the
human demonstrations: a downward reach for the towel-pushing prompt and an upward movement
for the tissue-pulling prompt. DreamDojo exhibits limited end-effector displacement in both exam-
ples. These results provide qualitative evidence that human-derived latent actions can guide robot
motion despite differences in embodiment and scene appearance.

CONCLUSION
We presented SkeLAM, a skeleton-guided latent action model for controllable robot world model-
ing. The kinematic component provides a motion-grounded target for robot action adaptation, while
the residual retains complementary visual dynamics. Cross-modal swap reconstruction and branch
dropout support reuse of the learned kinematic pathway for action-conditioned generation. Exper-
iments on DROID show improved motion accuracy and visual fidelity, with representation analy-
ses supporting kinematic specialization. Qualitative results demonstrate zero-shot human-to-robot
motion transfer, while policy evaluation yields success-rate estimates consistent with real robot ex-
ecutions. These results support structuring latent actions around robot motion while retaining com-
plementary information for generation. Future work will extend the current recorded-action replay
protocol to closed-loop policy evaluation. A key question is how prediction errors affect subsequent
policy decisions and task outcomes over longer interaction horizons.

Skeleton-Grounded Latent Action Model for Robot World Models
A
DATA AND PREPROCESSING
Data and splits.
DROID and AgiBot World (Bu et al., 2025) are used in latent-action learning,
world-model training, and action adaptation. DROID contributes 60,000 training clips to Stage 1
and 45,358 clips to each of Stages 2A, 2B, 3A, and 3B. RGB and skeleton observations are matched
by episode, camera, and frame index; overlapping windows from an episode remain in the same
partition. Normalization statistics are fitted on training data only. The DROID evaluation selects
one clip from each of 400 trajectories: 100 trajectories per motion quartile, with additional camera
stratification. AgiBot evaluation uses 400 held-out clips and 800 arm trajectories.
Preprocessing.
RGB and pre-rendered skeleton observations come from the corresponding OS-
CAR (Wu & Gao, 2026) subsets, following its kinematics, projection, and rasterization protocol.
Stage 1 letterboxes both modalities to 240×320 (height × width): resize by min(240/Ho, 320/Wo)
using OpenCV INTER AREA, round the resized dimensions, and center on a zero canvas, assigning
odd padding pixels to the bottom or right. Intensities are divided by 255. The target skeleton mask
selects pixels with an 8-bit RGB channel sum above 15, then applies one dilation with a 5 × 5 ellip-
tical kernel. It is used only in the skeleton loss. Stage 2/3 uses a 4:3 center crop resized to 480×640
for video generation; the frozen latent encoder keeps the Stage 1 resolution and uses the same source
frame indices.
Temporal alignment and action interface.
A DROID window samples 13 frames from 60-FPS
video at indices fk = f0 + 4k, k = 0, . . . , 12, giving 15 FPS and a 0.8-second span; consecu-
tive window starts differ by 24 source frames. The first frame conditions 12 future frames. Each
transition pairs (ok, ok+1) with an executed state-delta proxy
ak = [pk+1 −pk; ∆ωk; gk+1] ∈R7,
where translation is in the robot base frame, ∆ωk is the rotation vector of the relative SO(3) rota-
tion, and gk+1 is destination gripper openness. Deltas are between consecutive sampled states, not
relative to the clip start or joint-space commands. Manifests retain source indices, timestamps, and
transition-validity indicators.
Deterministic latent extraction.
The frozen Stage 1 RGB encoder exports posterior means as two
12 × 32 arrays, zkin and zres; the skeleton encoder is not used for world-model conditioning. Each
component is standardized elementwise as ¯z = (z −mz) ⊘max(sz, ϵ) using training statistics. The
same kinematic statistics define adapter targets and world-model inputs; normalization is implicit in
the main-text notation. Offline caches retain episode, camera, frame, preprocessing, and checkpoint
identifiers and receive no gradients.
B
MODEL AND TRAINING DETAILS
Architecture and initialization.
Both Transformer-VAE branches start from random weights and
use 16 × 16 patches, width 1,024, 16 encoder and 16 decoder blocks, 16 attention heads, causal
temporal attention, and zero Transformer dropout. The RGB posterior has 32 kinematic and 32
residual dimensions; the skeleton posterior has 32 kinematic dimensions. The world model starts
from official Cosmos-Predict2.5 2B weights (Ali et al., 2025), independently of the DreamDojo
baseline. The final Stage 1 teacher, video tokenizer, and text encoder remain frozen throughout
Stage 2/3.
Figure 7 summarizes the training stages, selected checkpoints, and dependencies.
B.1
LATENT-ACTION TRAINING
Stages 1A/B/C initialize RGB and skeleton reconstruction, add grounding and alignment, then add
swap, respectively (Figure 7; K = 1,000). The skeleton branch is frozen in Stage 1B/C. RGB
reconstruction uses MSE and the two KL terms in Eq. (6), with β = 2 × 10−4; swapping adds no
KL term. Grounding uses the RGB kinematic posterior mean through the frozen skeleton decoder;
alignment uses paired, ℓ2-normalized means, with the skeleton target detached. Swap reconstruction
uses the frozen skeleton mean and the same residual sample as ordinary RGB reconstruction.

Stage 2A performs 5K conditioning-projector updates, followed by 20K full-DiT and conditioning

Branch dropout.
Standardized latents are masked before projection; the carrier contains two 32-

tent and zero mask. One mode is sampled per clip and shared over all 12 transitions. For modes
(1, 1), (0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), probabilities are (0.80, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05) for global Stage 2 steps 0–999
and (0.50, 0.10, 0.05, 0.35) thereafter. Stage 2B starts at global step 5,000.

HUMAN TRANSFER AND POLICY EVALUATION
Human-to-robot transfer.
UniSkill (Kim et al., 2025) videos provide RGB-encoder latent se-
quences directly to the world model, bypassing the action adapter; a robot observation specifies the
initial scene. All weights remain fixed, and DreamDojo uses its latent-conditioned checkpoint. The
two examples assess visible motion correspondence, not task completion or reliable transfer across
arbitrary viewpoints and embodiments.
Open-loop policy evaluation.
Seven RoboArena policies are evaluated in 15 sessions
(105 paired episodes), with nine real successes and 96 failures.
Policy identifiers are
pi0 fast, paligemma fast, paligemma fast specialist, paligemma vq, pi0,
paligemma binning, and paligemma diffusion, each with suffix droid. Models re-

success labels to generated rollouts using the real-execution criterion. Actions are replayed without
policy feedback from generated observations. Episode-level metrics pool all pairs, treating suc-
cess as positive: success recall, balanced accuracy (mean sensitivity and specificity), and MCC.
