ABSTRACT
Monocular models recover 3D geometry from a single image in one pass, but most
assume a pinhole camera and produce distorted geometry on the wide-angle im-
agery produced by fisheye and panoramic lenses. Camera-aware models remove
this assumption by predicting a per-pixel ray field. However, they represent differ-
ent camera geometries using a single fixed basis to capture both the overall camera
projection and its finer variations. We argue that camera geometry can be viewed
as having a hierarchy of complexity for representing rays. We present Omni-
Ray, a feed-forward model whose Ray Module is composed of three components:
a monotone radial base that captures radially symmetric structure, a spherical-
harmonic residual for smooth deviations from it, and a free-form residual for re-
maining local variation. These components are composed as successive rotations
on the sphere, allowing the representation to accommodate diverse camera geome-
tries. Given a single image, OmniRay predicts a ray direction and an up-to-scale
radial distance for every pixel, from which a 3D point cloud is recovered without
needing camera information at inference time. Extensive experiments across dif-
ferent camera configurations demonstrate that OmniRay establishes a new state-
of-the-art in camera-agnostic monocular 3D reconstruction.

INTRODUCTION
Monocular 3D reconstruction recovers a 3D point for each pixel in an image. We consider central
cameras, in which all viewing rays pass through a single optical center, so each 3D point splits into a
ray direction and a distance along that ray. The ray direction depends only on the camera geometry,
while the distance depends on the scene under observation. Recent feed-forward models perform
well on monocular 3D estimation (Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b), but their architectures or
training data focus on pinhole cameras. When applied to wide-angle images, these assumptions often
lead to distorted reconstructed geometry. Wide-angle cameras are common in robotics (Yogamani
et al., 2019), augmented and virtual reality (Engel et al., 2023), and 360◦capture (Liao et al., 2022),
making this a practical limitation. One way to handle these images is to calibrate the camera and
rectify them to a pinhole view before reconstruction, but a pinhole cannot represent rays at or beyond
90◦from its optical axis. As such, undistortion typically leads to cropping away part of the field of
view or strongly stretching the image near its periphery, leading to low-confidence predictions from
conventional depth estimation or 3D foundation models (Guo et al., 2026).
Camera-aware models instead account for camera geometry directly (Bogdan et al., 2018; Tirado-
Gar´ın & Civera, 2025; Piccinelli et al., 2025).
DeepFocal (Workman et al., 2015) and Deep-
Calib (Bogdan et al., 2018) first use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to estimate focal length
and distortion of fixed camera models. Follow-up works generalize camera parameter estimation to
arbitrary camera models by estimating ray fields directly. In particular, UniK3D (Piccinelli et al.,
2025) predicts a per-pixel ray field using a spherical-harmonic (SH) basis and a radial distance
along each ray. It can therefore reconstruct pinhole, fisheye, and panoramic images without camera
metadata. In this formulation, the same basis must describe both the overall camera projection and
its finer variations. We argue that there exists a hierarchy of complexity in camera geometry esti-
mation, which can lead to more efficient and performant design. Many camera projections have a
simple structure: for approximately radially symmetric lenses, a smooth radial function is sufficient
to describe how viewing directions change across the image. Spherical harmonics can be used to
complement radial functions.
We therefore propose OmniRay – that takes a single image and predicts a dense, up-to-scale 3D
reconstruction without intrinsics or camera-type input (Fig. 1). A shared image encoder feeds two
modules. The Ray Module predicts a monotone radial base and the two residuals, each of which
refines the preceding ray field. The Distance Module predicts the radial distance of each pixel,
and the corresponding 3D point is obtained by scaling its predicted ray. We train OmniRay on
cameras ranging from pinhole to panorama and evaluate it on held-out scenes, datasets, and camera
configurations. An oracle analysis examines which ray fields each representation can express, while
benchmark experiments and ablations test the performance of the proposed components.
In summary, our contributions are:
1. A composite ray representation built from a monotone radial base, a spherical-harmonic resid-
ual, and a free-form residual.
2. OmniRay, a feed-forward model for single-image 3D reconstruction across central camera
geometries, without needing intrinsics or camera-type input.
3. An oracle analysis of ray representations, and experiments across seen and held-out scenes,
datasets, and camera configurations show that OmniRay improves over the evaluated baselines
in ray field estimation and monocular 3D reconstruction.

RELATED WORK
Camera calibration.
Camera calibration determines the mapping from image pixels to rays in
the camera frame. Classical methods parameterize this mapping with a low-dimensional model
whose parameters are estimated from geometric observations (Zhang, 2000; Scaramuzza et al.,
2006). Different camera geometries are handled by different parametric models: pinhole model
with radial and tangential distortion for conventional lenses, Kannala & Brandt (2006) model for
wide-angle optics, and unified camera models (Geyer & Daniilidis, 2000; Mei & Rives, 2007;
Khomutenko et al., 2015; Usenko et al., 2018) for omnidirectional cameras. Each of these models
fixes a particular functional form, so a lens whose behavior departs from that form introduces

complex lens behavior (Schops et al., 2020) at the cost of a higher-dimensional calibration problem.
Learning-based methods offer an alternative to this high-dimensional calibration process by learning
camera geometry directly from images without input calibration. DeepCalib (Bogdan et al., 2018)
regresses camera parameters, while Perspective Fields (Jin et al., 2023), GeoCalib (Veicht et al.,
2024), and AnyCalib (Tirado-Gar´ın & Civera, 2025) move toward dense geometric cues: a per-pixel
up-vector and latitude field (Jin et al., 2023; Veicht et al., 2024), or per-pixel rays (Tirado-Gar´ın &
Civera, 2025), each of which describes the camera more generally than a parameter vector. Neural
Ray Surfaces (Vasiljevic et al., 2020) take it further by learning a free-form ray surface from video
observations and directly representing the pixel-to-ray mapping. These calibration approaches shift
from explicit parameter estimation toward learning the pixel-to-ray mapping, but leave open how
this mapping should be represented to remain both compact and expressive simultaneously.
Monocular 3D.
Recent foundation models have improved depth estimation through large-scale
training and distillation (Yang et al., 2024), but do not explicitly model the camera geometry. Depth
Anything 3 (Lin et al., 2025a) instead predicts depth with per-pixel rays by modeling camera geom-
etry alongside scene structure. Camera-aware models specifically account for camera geometry in
monocular depth estimation. UniK3D (Piccinelli et al., 2025) represents a per-pixel ray field with
spherical-harmonics and predicts radial distance along each ray to handle diverse camera geome-
tries without camera metadata. Depth Any Camera (Guo et al., 2025) and UniDAC (Ganesan et al.,
2026) instead transform images into a common equirectangular representation to process different
camera types before depth estimation, but require input camera intrinsics for the transformation.
CAM3R (Guruprasad et al., 2026) and Wid3R (Jung et al., 2026) extend the spherical-harmonic ray
representation to multi-view feed-forward 3D reconstruction. Together, these works highlight the
value of explicitly accounting for the pixel-to-ray mapping for camera-agnostic 3D geometry, while
leaving open whether a single ray representation is necessary for all camera geometries.

METHOD
Given a single image I ∈RH×W ×3 from an arbitrary central camera, with no intrinsics, distortion
parameters or camera-type label, OmniRay predicts for every pixel u = (ux, uy) a unit ray d(u) ∈
S2 and a radial distance ρ(u) > 0.
A DINOv2 backbone (Oquab et al., 2023) encodes the image, and its features drive two branches
(Fig. 2): the Ray Module composes d from radial, spherical-harmonic, and free-form components,
while the Distance Module predicts ρ. Their combination gives the 3D point in the camera frame:
P(u) = ρ(u) d(u).
(1)
We predict radial distance ρ rather than perpendicular depth Z, related by ρ = Z/dz, since dz →0
as the ray approaches 90◦from the optical axis and changes sign beyond it; ρ therefore remains
well-defined across wide fields of view.
Notation.
The DINOv2 backbone produces a dense feature map F and a pooled global token g.
We use the normalized image coordinate m(u) = (u−cimg)/D, where cimg is the image center and
D is the image half-diagonal in pixels. This normalization places the image corners at ∥m∥= 1 for
any resolution and aspect ratio. Image scale is provided separately to g through log(W/H) and the
logarithm of the image diagonal. Ground-truth quantities are marked with a star, d⋆and ρ⋆, and V
denotes the set of pixels at which they are defined.

3.2
DISTANCE MODULE
The Ray Module determines the direction d along which each pixel observes the scene, but not
how far along that ray the surface lies. The Distance Module predicts this remaining quantity: the
up-to-scale radial distance ρ(u) from the optical center. It reuses the same encoder pass, fusing
four intermediate blocks with a Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) decoder (Ranftl et al., 2021)
into a dense prediction sampled at each pixel, where a softplus gives a positive distance ρ(u) =
softplus(fρ(u)) > 0. Scaling each predicted ray by its distance as in equation 1 produces the
camera-frame point cloud {P(u)} in a single forward pass.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
ORACLE TEST: REPRESENTATION CEILINGS
Before training anything, we measure what each candidate representation can express at all, by
fitting its parameters directly to analytic ground-truth rays; Sec. A.2 gives the fitting protocol. Table
1 shows that compact parametric models succeed only on the geometries their assumptions match,
and that increasing the number of knots in the monotone radial profile reduces the catadioptric error
but leaves the equirectangular error at 22.8◦, the ceiling identified in Sec. 3.1. The spherical-
harmonic field removes that ceiling but remains two orders of magnitude behind the radial profile
on the fisheye. Among the compact representations, only the composition is below 0.25◦on all
three geometries, and since it reduces to the radial base when the residual vanishes, its capacity is
bounded below by that of the base.

Random-lens Augmentation.
During training we augment each panorama with randomly sam-
pled synthetic central cameras, each defined by a viewing direction, a monotone radial profile drawn
from positive spline increments, and an imaged sensor region. This single family covers filled and
annular domains, from pinhole to wide-angle lenses. Draws whose profile falls close to a held-out
geometry are rejected, so neither withheld camera nor its neighborhood appears in training. Sec. A.2
gives the sampling ranges and the rejection criteria, and Fig. 8 shows sampled examples.
4.3
BENCHMARK AND ABLATION
Baselines.
UniK3D (Piccinelli et al., 2025) and Depth Anything 3 (DA3) (Lin et al., 2025a) jointly
predict camera geometry and scene structure and are evaluated on all metrics; we fine-tune UniK3D
on the 2D3DS and 360Loc panoramas so that it sees the same source imagery we train on. Geo-
Calib (Veicht et al., 2024) and AnyCalib (Tirado-Gar´ın & Civera, 2025) calibrate the camera without
predicting scene geometry. We convert their estimated cameras to dense ray fields for evaluation so
all methods are scored identically.
Metrics.
We evaluate the predicted ray fields, radial distance, and 3D point clouds using the fol-
lowing metrics: Ray accuracy is the mean angular error arccos⟨d, d⋆⟩in degrees, and radial distance
is evaluated with absolute relative error (AbsRel) and δ < 1.25. For point clouds, accuracy and com-
pleteness are the median nearest-neighbor distances normalized by the scene’s mean point norm, and
their mean is the Chamfer distance (CD). Predictions in different depth gauges are aligned per im-
age by s = median(ρ⋆)/ median(ρ), and perpendicular depth is converted to radial distance by
ρ = Z/dz using the predicted rays. All metrics are computed over pixels with valid ground truth.

Adding an SH residual to the radial base improves panoramic and held-out accuracy; staged training

ometries considered here. As the backbone is fine-tuned end-to-end in both settings, this comparison
suggests an effect of initialization rather than a limitation in model capacity.
Overall, these results support the staged radial →SH →free-form composition as a balance between
geometric structure and representational flexibility.
5
CONCLUSION
We began with two questions: how the pixel-to-ray mapping can remain both compact and ex-
pressive, and whether a single representation is necessary for every camera geometry. OmniRay
explores both by composing the ray field. A monotone radial base captures the structure of conven-
tional lenses, a spherical-harmonic residual models smooth deviations that a radial profile cannot
express, and a free-form residual accounts for remaining local variation. Each component is applied
as a rotation on S2, allowing the representation to adapt to different camera geometries without
camera-type input or model selection. A shared distance module scales the predicted rays into a
point cloud in a single forward pass, and sampling lenses continuously during training helps the
composition generalize to held-out camera configurations.
Limitation. A qualitative zero-shot example reveals a limitation on panoramic views that differ
from the training distribution. The training images are captured near ground level, with horizons
approximately aligned across images. As shown in Fig. 7, on an in-the-wild aerial panorama with
the horizon near the top of the frame, the predicted rays cover only a bounded, forward-facing
region rather than the full viewing sphere. This example suggests that generalization depends not
only on camera-projection diversity but also on the distribution of viewing altitudes represented
during training.

A.1
IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING
Architecture.
The encoder is DINOv2 ViT-L/14 (Oquab et al., 2023) with 1024-dimensional fea-
tures. The radial head is a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with width 256 operating on the
global token augmented with (log aspect, log diagonal), while the spherical head is a one-layer MLP
of the same width on the same input. The radial head predicts K + 8 values with K = 16 knots
spanning r ∈[0, 1.3] in half-diagonal units; the spherical head predicts (L+1)2×3 coefficients with
L = 7. These coefficients are shared across all pixels of an image. The free head is a convolutional
decoder over the dense feature map. The Distance Module uses a width-256 DPT decoder (Ranftl
et al., 2021) over encoder blocks {4, 11, 17, 23} to predict the radial distance. Both residual heads
are zero-initialized, while the radial head is initialized to a canonical camera, so training starts from
a single radial lens.
Training.
Images range from 896 × 1792 equirectangular panoramas to 5122 lens crops. We
train with a token cap of 8192 using a sampler for camera-type balancing. Within each image, we
supervise 4096 pixels drawn round-robin over the occupied cells of a 32×32 grid, ensuring coverage
across the image. Training proceeds in two phases. We first train the Ray Module alone, activating
its three components in order, and then attach the Distance Module at the final stage and train the
full model end-to-end. In this second phase we use AdamW with weight decay 0.01 and cosine
decay after a warm-up, with learning rates of 5×10−6 for the encoder and ray heads and 5×10−5
for the distance decoder. The encoder runs in bfloat16, while the heads and losses remain in full
precision. Training uses 8 NVIDIA A40 GPUs with distributed data parallelism.
A.2
EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
Oracle fitting protocol.
Each representation is fitted to the analytic ground-truth rays of a single
camera by minimizing the mean cosine residual in Sec. 3.3, with a multi-start sweep over the axis
sign, roll and, where the representation uses one, the focal length. Every row is an independent
fit under a fixed optimization budget, so each entry upper-bounds that representation’s ceiling. The
fitted error is the representation’s approximation capacity for that camera and lower-bounds the error
of any predictor built on it.
Splits and Camera sampling.
For 2D3DS we train on areas 1-4 and evaluate on areas 5-6; for
360Loc we train on the Atrium and Piatrium scenes and evaluate on Concourse and Hall. Each
synthetic camera samples a viewing orientation, an aspect ratio, a principal-point offset, an optional
central blind spot, a rectangular or circular sensor boundary, and inner and outer incidence angles;
panoramic cameras instead sample a vertical field of view, an azimuth and a small latitude stretch,
with the horizon kept gravity-aligned as in the source captures. Draws near a held-out geometry are
rejected: for the GoPro, any profile within δ = 15◦of its incidence-angle curve at every radius; for
the catadioptric camera, any sample with an inner angle in [25◦, 55◦] and a rim angle in [140◦, 170◦].
Note: The Nayar catadioptric camera (Nayar, 1997; Baker & Nayar, 1999) is a paraboloid mirror
with orthographic projection, imaging a 40◦–155◦annulus about its axis; the mirror and camera
share a single viewpoint. For PanoCity and CO3Dv2 we use the calibration provided with each
dataset to obtain ground-truth rays. Ground-truth rays and distances follow analytically from each
sampled camera. Fig. 8 shows images rendered under cameras drawn from this distribution.
