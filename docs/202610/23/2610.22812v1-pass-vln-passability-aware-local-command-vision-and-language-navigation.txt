PASS-VLN: Passability-Aware Local-Command
Vision-and-Language Navigation
Cong Zou1, Xiangsheng Shi1, Xuanhui Wang1, Fubin Wang1, Guangjun Ge1, Chao Wang1,∗, Yu Wang1,2
Abstract— Reliable vision-and-language navigation (VLN)
requires not only understanding instructions but also de-
termining whether and how a robot can physically execute
them. However, existing VLN benchmarks typically assume
executable instructions and focus on long-horizon tasks. This
leaves body-dependent local execution, including passability and
collision-free execution, largely unexamined. Evaluating these
factors within long-horizon tasks would entangle local physical
execution with task planning and long-term spatial memory,
making navigation failures difficult to attribute. We therefore
focus on grounding local navigation commands in executable
motion under environmental and robot body constraints. We
present PASS-VLN, a passability-aware local-command VLN
benchmark and model. The benchmark holds the scene, instruc-
tion, start pose, and reference route fixed while varying robot
body width, requiring target completion when the instructed
passage is feasible and stopping before blockage otherwise.
Our model encodes body width through obstacle inflation in a
depth-derived local bird’s-eye view, fuses geometry with visual
and linguistic context, and predicts discrete latent action codes
for continuous closed-loop execution. Experiments show that
PASS-VLN improves collision-free success by approximately
12 percentage points over a strong VLN model fine-tuned
on our benchmark, while adapting reach-versus-stop behavior
to body constraints and generalizing to unseen body widths.
When integrated with a high-level planner, PASS-VLN achieves
higher success with fewer local commands, while real-world
deployment further demonstrates its effectiveness as a low-level
executor. The benchmark and model will be released.
I. INTRODUCTION
Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires an em-
bodied agent to follow natural-language instructions and
navigate to a target in visual environments. R2R established
the standard indoor VLN setting [1], followed by benchmarks
for more complex multilingual instructions [2], continuous
control [3], remote object grounding [4], persistent naviga-
tion [5], and physics-aware unified evaluation [6].
Most existing VLN benchmarks provide a complete route
instruction per episode and assume that the instructed mo-
tion is physically executable. Directly incorporating body-
dependent physical execution, including passability and
collision-free execution, would couple local execution with
planning, progress tracking, spatial memory, and route-level
reasoning, making navigation failures difficult to attribute.
Many recent VLN methods emphasize stronger reasoning,
memory, and planning [7]–[14], whose gains do not nec-
essarily translate into improved local physical execution.
Conventional navigation methods based on local mapping

These considerations motivate focusing on local physical
execution as a capability that complements high-level reason-
ing and memory. A human or a high-level agent, including
one based on a VLM, can issue local commands, while a low-
level executor grounds each command in current observations
and converts it into physical motion. The local command
serves as their semantic interface, specifying an immediate
local objective and the spatial cues needed to interpret it.
For example, “move forward and stop between the sofa
and the table” specifies landmarks, spatial relations, and a
stopping condition while leaving broader task planning to
the command issuer.
Such a local executor must reason about not only where to
go, but also whether and how the motion can be physically
executed. Most existing VLN research emphasizes semantic
spatial reasoning, such as left/right relations, object order-
ing, relative positions, and path structure [18]–[23], without
explicitly considering physical spatial feasibility under body
constraints. In practice, a correctly grounded passage may
still be too narrow for the robot, and traversable passages
may require body-dependent clearance and alignment. A lo-
cal executor should therefore consider the instructed motion
and its physical feasibility under the current embodiment.
To address these requirements, we introduce PASS-
VLN, a PASSability-aware local-command VLN frame-
work comprising a controlled benchmark and a body-width-
conditioned model for planar physical execution. The bench-
mark holds the scene, instruction, start pose, and reference
route fixed while varying robot body width. For paired
configurations where passage feasibility changes under the
same command, the narrower robot is expected to reach the
target, whereas the wider robot is expected to stop before
the blockage, directly testing whether execution adapts to
the interaction between instructed motion, environmental
geometry, and robot embodiment. The benchmark covers
commands based on local observations and commonsense
spatial knowledge for visible-target navigation or bounded
exploration, as well as commands requiring broader scene
or route knowledge.
To connect language understanding with body-dependent
geometry and motion execution, PASS-VLN projects depth
into a local bird’s-eye view (BEV) and incorporates body
width through obstacle inflation, making the geometric rep-
resentation reflect robot-specific clearance. Fusing this rep-

allows the policy to interpret the instructed motion in relation

control. The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce a controlled VLN benchmark that iso-

explicitly evaluates body-width-dependent physical fea-

• We develop a body-width-conditioned VLN model for

while discrete latent actions preserve VLM-style token
prediction and enable continuous closed-loop motion.
• Experiments demonstrate generalization to unseen body
widths and improved collision-free success over the
evaluated binary passability-gating baseline. In a shared
reference-route-assisted hierarchical pipeline, PASS-
VLN achieves higher collision-free navigation success
with fewer local commands, while real-world deploy-
ment further validates its practical utility.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Vision-and-Language Navigation Benchmarks
R2R introduced instruction following in photorealistic
indoor environments [1], while RxR increased linguistic
diversity through multilingual instructions and dense spa-
tiotemporal grounding [2]. VLN-CE moved navigation from
discrete graphs to continuous environments with low-level
actions [3]. REVERIE combined navigation with remote
object grounding [4], and Touchdown studied navigation and
spatial reasoning in outdoor street-view environments [24].
AerialVLN extended the task to UAV navigation [25], and
VLNVerse introduced a scalable physics-aware framework
covering multiple VLN tasks and embodied agents [6].
PASS-VLN complements these benchmarks by isolating
local-command spatial grounding and explicitly evaluating
whether the local motion specified by an instruction remains
physically executable under different body widths, including
unseen widths.
B. Large-Model-Based VLN and Physical Execution
Recent VLN methods increasingly use large language and
vision-language models for navigation reasoning and action
generation. StreamVLN introduces slow–fast context model-
ing for efficient streaming navigation [26], while JanusVLN
uses separate visual-semantic and spatial-geometric memo-
ries to improve spatial reasoning [23]. CorrectNav introduces
a self-correction flywheel that learns from navigation-error
trajectories [27], while AwareVLN performs structural self-
aware reasoning over agent state and task progress [14].
Recent work also considers embodiment and physical exe-
cution: NaVILA combines VLA planning with legged loco-
motion skills [28], VAMOS introduces embodiment-specific
affordance evaluation [29], DualVLN couples high-level
visual-language reasoning with a continuous local diffusion
policy [30], [31], and CapNav studies heterogeneous mo-
bility and physical constraints [32]. These methods mainly
study route-level navigation, general embodied reasoning,
or adaptation across different robot platforms and mobility
types. In contrast, PASS-VLN studies reliable local physical
execution for hierarchical navigation, using local commands
as the interface between high-level reasoning and low-level
motion execution, while explicitly considering body-width-
dependent physical passability.
III. PASS-VLN BENCHMARK
A. Controlled Local-Trajectory Sampling
We define a local command as a bounded local naviga-
tion objective issued by a high-level planner according to
the current robot state. It requires the executor to jointly
ground landmark semantics, spatial relations, and select
environment-conditioned actions, e.g., “turn left after passing
the table” rather than merely execute isolated directional
commands. To construct a controlled evaluation setting, we
restrict each trajectory to at most one major heading change,
defined as a heading variation greater than 30◦. As shown in
Fig. 1.A1, we sample navigable start–goal pairs in Habitat
and categorize each path as straight, initial-turn, or mid-turn,
corresponding to forward motion, an early turn followed by
forward motion, or a turn near an intermediate waypoint.
These patterns cover common local navigation structures
while limiting repeated turns, long-term progress tracking,
and accumulated execution errors, making them suitable for
local-command execution in hierarchical navigation.
B. Evidence-Grounded Instruction Generation
For each candidate trajectory, we construct a multimodal
evidence pool of chronological egocentric keyframes, a top-
down map, semantic object identities, and a trajectory type,
as shown in Fig. 1.A2. Since a high-level command issuer
may have access to different levels of spatial context, we con-
sider two complementary command types. This distinction
concerns the context available to the command issuer; the
executor receives the instruction and onboard observations
in both settings:
• Map-free commands can be formulated without map-
or route-level information. They rely on current obser-
vations and commonsense spatial knowledge, covering
both directly visible targets and locally discoverable
goals, such as locating a nearby doorway to leave a
room.
• Map-informed commands require map- or route-level
spatial context to specify the intended local motion
more explicitly, including turns, landmarks, and stop-
ping locations that may lie beyond the current view and
commonsense knowledge.

C. Body-Width-Aware Passability Annotation
A local command feasible for a narrow agent may be
infeasible for a wider robot. PASS-VLN therefore evalu-
ates the instructed local motion under multiple body-width
configurations. Passability is defined with respect to the is-
sued command. For map-informed commands, the instructed
passage is explicitly specified. For map-free commands, we
discard impassable episodes if width-aware replanning finds
an alternative route less than 5 m longer than the original
path, indicating a comparable local detour.
For each body width, we first perform width-aware path
search along the intended route on the top-down traversabil-
ity map using width-dependent obstacle inflation as illus-
trated in Fig. 1.A3, and then validate the resulting trajectory
on the original-resolution map using the exact rotated rectan-
gular footprint at sampled poses and intermediate transitions.
If the goal is unreachable, the longest collision-free prefix is
retained, and its terminal pose defines the pre-blockage stop-
ping reference. Consequently, the success condition depends
on body width: the agent should reach the target when the
path is traversable and stop before the blockage otherwise.
D. Benchmark Configuration and Metrics
Instruction generation uses Gemma 4 31B [33]. The robot
footprint is modeled as a rectangle with a fixed longitudinal
length of 0.60 m and a variable body width. We use 0.35 m
and 0.70 m as training widths, while 0.20 m and 0.50 m are
held out for evaluating generalization to unseen body widths.
The PASS-VLN training set contains approximately 192K
episodes collected from HM3D and MP3D training scenes.
The distributions of scene sources, trajectory types, instruc-
tion types, path lengths, and passability under different
body widths are summarized in Fig. 1.B. Evaluation is
conducted on MP3D val unseen scenes disjoint from the
training environments, with 2,971 map-free and 3,470 map-
informed episodes. We further conduct human validation on
500 randomly sampled instructions, including 250 map-free
and 250 map-informed commands, of which 95.6% were
judged correct and unambiguous.
Since PASS-VLN focuses on local physical execution
rather than long-horizon tasks, we use a stricter 1.5 m
success radius than the 3.0 m criterion commonly adopted
in R2R-CE. A passable episode is successful if the agent
stops within 1.5 m of the trajectory endpoint, whereas an
impassable episode is successful if it stops within the 1.5 m
region immediately before the first blockage. Each episode
is limited to 100 execution steps.

rate on passable episodes; Blockage Success Rate (BSR),

IV. PASS-VLN MODEL
A. Model Overview
PASS-VLN is a streaming VLN model that jointly pro-
cesses language instructions, historical and current visual
observations, and current body-width-conditioned geometry.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the model contains a visual en-
coder, a body-width-conditioned geometry encoder, a causal
multimodal backbone, and a latent action decoder. At each
navigation step, PASS-VLN predicts four discrete latent-
action tokens. These tokens are decoded into a relative target
transformation, which is executed by a low-level controller.
The model then acquires new observations and repeats the
prediction process in a closed loop.
B. Body-Width-Conditioned Geometry Fusion

but do not explicitly encode passability with respect to a
particular robot body width. PASS-VLN therefore projects
the current depth observation onto a robot-centric bird’s-eye-
view grid. Valid depth points within the configured spatial
and height ranges are used to construct a binary occupancy
map Ot. To incorporate the robot body width, occupied cells

V. EXPERIMENTS
A. Implementation Details
PASS-VLN follows the streaming navigation architecture
of StreamVLN [26] because its efficient streaming design is
well suited for a low-level VLN executor, while introducing
body-width-aware geometric reasoning and latent-action pre-
diction. The multimodal backbone is initialized from LLaVA-
Video 7B [34]. We first fine-tune the model on the R2R-CE
and RxR-CE training sets to acquire general VLN capability,
and then further fine-tune it on the PASS-VLN training
set with the body-width settings in Sec. III-D. Training is
performed on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs for one epoch with
a batch size of 128, an initial learning rate of 2 × 10−5,
cosine decay with 7.5% warmup, and BF16 precision. Each
latent action uses two translation and two rotation codes,
with a codebook size of 8. The local BEV is represented as a
96×96 grid covering 4.5 m forward and ±2.5 m laterally, and
is encoded into 16 geometry tokens. The evaluation protocol
and metrics follow Sec. III-D. Unless otherwise stated, all
controlled variants use the same backbone, optimization
protocol, and low-level controller.
B. Local-Command Execution
We first compare PASS-VLN with StreamVLN, DualVLN,
and JanusVLN on local-command navigation using their
released checkpoints under a fixed body width of 0.35 m.
We additionally fine-tune StreamVLN on the PASS-VLN
training set as a controlled adaptation baseline, since PASS-
VLN shares the same base streaming architecture, enabling a
controlled comparison of task adaptation and model design.
As shown in Table I, PASS-VLN achieves the best over-
all performance among the evaluated methods. The large
OSR–SR gaps of the directly transferred VLN models
also show that strong route-level navigation performance
does not necessarily lead to precise local completion and
stopping. Fine-tuning StreamVLN substantially improves its

C. Body-Width-Aware Navigation
We next evaluate whether PASS-VLN adapts its navi-
gation behavior to robot body width, using seen widths
(0.35, 0.70 m) and unseen widths (0.20, 0.50 m). The 0.50 m
setting tests interpolation between training widths, while
0.20 m evaluates extrapolation beyond the training range.
Passability labels are recomputed for each body width using
the annotation method in Section III-C.
1) Body-Width-Conditioned Execution versus Passability
Gating: To investigate whether explicit binary passability
information alone is sufficient for reliable execution, we
compare against a decoupled GT Passability-Gated VLN
baseline. This baseline is trained with a 0.10 m body width,
making the training trajectories effectively all passable and
removing width-dependent passability supervision. At test
time, Habitat recomputes the NavMesh for the actual width
and forces STOP when the remaining instructed path be-
comes impassable. As shown in Table II, at larger body
widths, the GT passability gate provides a larger SR advan-
tage because more routes become impassable and can there-
fore be correctly terminated by the oracle gate. However,
the width-agnostic VLN leads to more collisions and larger
drops in CFR and CFSR. This shows that binary passability
gating alone is insufficient: successful execution also requires
body-width-dependent clearance, alignment, and avoidance.
PASS-VLN incorporates these constraints through its body-
conditioned geometry, allowing body width to affect both
whether and how the route is executed.
2) Paired Body-Width Evaluation: Single-width perfor-
mance alone cannot determine whether the model actually
changes its decision according to body width. We therefore
select paired episodes with identical scenes, instructions, start
poses and reference routes, where the route is passable for a
narrower body but impassable for a wider one. The narrow
robot should therefore reach the instructed target, whereas the
wide robot should stop before the first blockage. Table III
confirms that PASS-VLN changes its behavior according
to body width rather than following a width-independent
strategy.
D. Local Execution in an Oracle Hierarchical Pipeline
We further evaluate PASS-VLN as the local execu-
tion layer of a hierarchical navigation system on R2R-CE
val unseen split. All methods share the same reference route
and high-level pipeline. At each iteration, Habitat finds the
next unreached waypoint on the reference path from the
current robot pose and replans a shortest path to it. The
local segment is then extended along the reference route,
and a shared VLM (Gemma 4 31B [33]) generates a local
command from the segment and egocentric visual evidence.
After execution, the returned robot pose is used to plan
the next segment and command. All methods use the same
planner, VLM, and command-generation procedure, with at
most 20 local commands per episode.
As shown in Table IV, Avg. Cmds. is the average number
of local commands per episode. PASS-VLN achieves the
best overall performance, indicating that more reliable local
execution reduces corrective interactions and improves the
efficiency of hierarchical navigation. CFR and CFSR are
lower than in single-command evaluation as they are com-
puted over the long-horizon episode: a collision in any local
command marks the whole episode as non-collision-free.
E. Ablation Studies
We perform controlled ablations on the map-informed
subset to examine: (1) whether latent trajectories improve
physical execution over atomic actions; (2) whether explicit
geometric observations improve local physical execution; and
(3) how body width should be incorporated into geometry for
width-aware execution and generalization. Table V compares
six variants differing in input, action type, and training
widths, where RGB + Raw BEV + Width Prompt explicitly
provides the body width in the text prompt.
First, replacing atomic actions with latent trajectories
improves CFR and CFSR, with a smaller gain in SR, showing
that latent actions mainly improve collision-free execution.
Second, depth and raw BEV provide explicit local geometry
and improve physical execution to some extent, but remain
independent of body width and therefore do not directly
support width-dependent passability reasoning. Third, the
form of width conditioning affects generalization to unseen
body widths. With the same training widths, the RGB + Raw
BEV + Width Prompt variant performs comparably on the
seen pair but generalizes poorly to the unseen pair. This is
because its narrow GSR drops to only 13.0%, indicating an
overly conservative tendency to stop even when the route is
passable. Under the same multi-width supervision, geometric
conditioning therefore generalizes better than the evaluated
width-prompt baseline.

F. Real-World Deployment
Finally, we evaluate whether the local execution and body-
width-aware passability capabilities transfer to a physical
robot. We deploy PASS-VLN on a Unitree Go2-W (0.70 ×
0.43×0.50 m), using a configured width of 0.45 m including
a 0.01 m safety margin. RGB and depth are provided by the
onboard camera and a RealSense D435i, respectively, with
inference running on an external server.
As shown in Fig. 3, we first evaluate body-width-aware
passability by varying the width of a mandatory passage
from 1.0 to 0.4 m in 0.1 m increments, with three trials per
setting. PASS-VLN traverses 0.6–1.0 m passages collision-
free in all trials, while at 0.5 m it traverses in two of
three trials but with minor boundary contact. At 0.4 m,
it consistently stops before the blockage. It also selects
the feasible alternative in asymmetric layouts and detects
downstream blockages in sequential passages, demonstrating
consistent width-dependent passability reasoning on the real
robot. This design may also support changes in the effective
lateral footprint, such as added payloads, through an updated
width configuration.
We further evaluate local-command execution in an ap-
proximately 500 m2 indoor workspace and a 3600 m2 out-
door environment as shown in Fig. 4. For map-informed
navigation, sequential local commands guide the robot over
the large area, demonstrating that the local commands can be
composed for extended navigation. For map-free commands,


PASS-VLN successfully grounds nearby landmarks and spa-
tial relations, demonstrating local instruction understanding
and execution in the tested environments.
VI. CONCLUSION
We presented PASS-VLN, a passability-aware local-
command VLN benchmark and local navigation model
for body-width-dependent physical execution. By combin-
ing body-width-conditioned geometric reasoning with latent-
action prediction, PASS-VLN enables an agent to reach
feasible targets while stopping before body-width-induced
blockages, even at previously unseen body widths. Experi-
ments further demonstrate its effectiveness as a local executor
in reference-route-assisted hierarchical navigation and real-
world deployment. Future work will further improve local-
command success and collision robustness through finer-
grained stopping supervision, collision-free action learning,
and more robust geometry-aware control, particularly for
wide embodiments and narrow-clearance scenarios.
