Abstract— Stable grasping under task-specific disturbances is
important for robotic manipulation. However, most dexterous
grasp synthesis methods are developed for highly actuated
hands in free space, and low-DoF hands may not be able
to realize the contact topologies required by these grasps.
Meanwhile, support surfaces can provide useful contact forces,
but are often treated only as geometric constraints. We present
a support-aware grasp synthesis framework for object im-
mobilization with low-DoF coupled hands. Given irregular
object geometry, our method represents both hand and object
local surface regions as graphs and searches for kinematically
feasible matchings between them. We then refine the wrist pose
and motor state for geometric contact feasibility and validates
the refined grasps through physical rollouts. Starting from
the validated grasp pose, an optional task-guided local search
explores nearby grasp configurations by varying the wrist pose
and motor state, and selects one according to the specified
task condition. Experiments on diverse objects show that our
method generates various grasp configurations that achieve high
grasp success and strong disturbance resistance. Real-world
experiments further demonstrate physically executable grasps
which enable object immobilization in cooking scenario.
I. INTRODUCTION
Robotic grasping is a fundamental prerequisite for ma-
nipulation. Robotic hands are widely used to grasp and
manipulate objects in various applications such as tool use,
assembly, and household tasks. Existing grasp methods [1],
[2], [3] have primarily focused on automatically synthesizing
grasp poses that enable the hand to successfully grasp and
hold an object. Especially, previous works [4], [5] try to
synthesize grasp to stable from disturbances for high-DoF
dexterous hands, whose joints are independently actuated.
However, their practical applicability is limited by two
factors. First, they require high-DoF hands, which are cost-

prohibitive, making them impractical for widespread deploy-
ment. Second, they pay less attention to maintaining stability,
resulting in vulnerable grasps under external forces. In real-
world scenarios, such as holding an object while cutting, the
stability of the object’s pose is crucial for task success.
While low-DoF hands offer a cost-effective alternative for
widespread deployment, their coupled actuation introduces
additional challenges for grasp synthesis. Due to the coupled
joints, moving one finger region toward a desired contact
may simultaneously lead to undesired movement in other
regions. Furthermore, the planned joint motions may not be
compatible due to the limited DoF. These undesired motions
cause unintended contacts that may push or displace the
object during grasping.
To address these challenges, our key insight is to use
the supporting surface as an additional source of contact
for low-DoF grasping. Rather than relying on the hand
alone to generate all contact forces, the object can be
immobilized jointly by the hand and the support surface [6].
The surface provides passive normal and frictional forces
without additional actuation [7], [8], supplying opposition
that is difficult for a coupled hand to generate by itself.
With support from the surface, a low-DoF hand can achieve
strong resistance to external forces even with simple grasp
configurations. We thus treat the support surface not only as
a collision constraint, but as an active part of grasp synthesis
and physical validation. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the resulting
support-aware grasps maintain the object pose under external
disturbances and enable downstream tasks such as cutting
and peeling.
As shown in Fig. 2, our grasp synthesis pipeline com-
prises three stages followed by an optional task-guided local
derivation. First, given a robotic hand embodiment and object
geometry, we represent hand regions and local object surface

regions as graphs, based on distance and surface-normal
relations between each regions. We then search for multiple
feasible correspondences between the two graphs to generate
diverse grasp candidates, determining which hand region
should contact each object region geometry. After that, we lo-
cally refine the resulting grasp initialization while preserving
their correspondences, minimizing penetration and separation
between the matched contact regions. Finally, we evaluates
each refined grasp under external disturbances in physics and
retains only the grasps that pass the disturbance tests. Option-
ally, starting from a validated grasp, the local search derives
nearby configurations according to deployment-specific task
conditions without repeating the global contact search.
We evaluate the proposed framework on objects with
irregular geometries and scale under directional disturbances.
We compare the synthesized grasps with existing dexterous
grasp synthesis methods and evaluate sustainable disturbance
resistance and contact quality. We further validate our grasps
on the RH56 hand robot with real objects, demonstrat-
ing diverse grasp configurations and robustness to external
disturbances. We also demonstrate that these grasps can
support downstream applications such as cooking by stably
immobilizing objects during cutting and slicing.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Dexterous Grasp Synthesis
Classical dexterous grasp synthesis searches for stable
hand configurations using analytical measures such as force
closure and the Ferrari-Canny metric [9], [1]. Differentiable
formulations improve the scalability and physical fidelity of
this search [2], [3], [10], while recent QP-based methods
optimize contact-force feasibility [4], [11] and Dexonomy [5]
broadens the range of synthesized grasp types. However,
these methods treat contact forces as free variables bounded
only by friction cones. Simulation is used primarily to filter
candidates or train grasp evaluators [3], [4], [5], [12], and,
apart from the fingertip compliance modeled by Spring-
Grasp [10], actuator-to-contact-force transmission remains
unmodeled, so a certified force distribution need not be real-
izable by the hand. Task-oriented methods additionally eval-
uate grasps against downstream wrench requirements [13],
[14]. For example, TaskDexGrasp [15] optimizes the relation
between a prescribed Task Wrench Space (TWS) and the
Grasp Wrench Space (GWS).
Hierarchical Fingertip Space [16] organizes the grasp
search space by discretizing object contact regions, and
Matak and Hermans [17] restrict contacts to tactile-sensing
regions. Cross-embodiment methods transfer contact intent
from human demonstrations [18], [19] or infer hand-link–
object-region correspondences from learned priors [20], [21],
[22]. Although subsequent optimization may improve feasi-
bility on the target hand, the contact structure itself is not
derived from its coupled actuation. In contrast, we generate
contact topologies directly from the geometry and coupled
actuation of the target hand, determining executability during
synthesis without relying on a learned prior or seed grasp.
B. Underactuated and Coupled Hands
Reducing the number of independent actuators has long
been studied through postural synergies, mechanical trans-
missions, and underactuated hand designs. Ciocarlie and
Allen [23] optimize grasp configurations in low-dimensional
posture subspaces. Prattichizzo et al. [24] analyze how
postural synergies affect motion and force controllability,
and Gabiccini and Bicchi [25] show that the available
synergies also constrain the optimal choice of grasping
forces. Together, these results indicate that reduced actuation
restricts reachable postures and realizable contact forces at
the same time. Birglen and Gosselin [26] analyze how a
fixed transmission determines the contact forces an underac-
tuated finger can produce along its phalanges, and Pozzi et
al. [27] introduce grasp-quality measures that account for the
force directions controllable by underactuated and compliant
hands. These formulations treat coupling as a constraint on
the forces achievable for a given contact set, whereas we
make it a generation-time condition that retains only contact
sets admitting a common coupled motor configuration.
C. Environment-Assisted Grasping
Robots can exploit contacts with the surrounding envi-
ronment to extend the capabilities of the hand itself. Ex-
trinsic Dexterity [7] uses gravity and external surfaces for
manipulation with simple grippers. Kazemi et al. [28] show
that deliberately interacting with a supporting table can be
critical for robustly acquiring small objects, while Eppner et
al. [8] develop grasp strategies that exploit environmental
constraints rather than avoiding them. Manipulation with
Shared Grasping [6] goes further by defining a shared
grasp in which contacts from the hand and environment
jointly support the object, allowing environmental contacts
to substitute for hand contacts.
Environment contacts have also been integrated into
contact-rich planning, where pre-grasp motions or sliding
and pivoting trajectories are optimized together with the
supporting forces they rely on [29], [30]. BODex [4] in-
cludes a tabletop grasp dataset and table-aware collision-free
trajectories, but its grasp-force optimization is formulated
over hand-object contact wrenches and treats the table as a
collision constraint rather than a load-bearing contact. These
works use the environment to acquire or move an object,
whereas we synthesize a static grasp in which the supporting
surface remains a load-bearing contact that resists external
disturbances together with the hand during both synthesis
and physical validation.

A. Graph-Based Contact Configuration
Instead of relying on data-driven grasp priors, we synthe-
size diverse grasp candidates by explicitly exploring different
hand-object contact configurations. Specifically, we represent
the hand contact regions and object surface regions as graphs,
denoted by G = (V, E), where V and E denote the nodes and
edges, respectively. We then search for feasible matchings
between the two graphs, where each matching defines a
different contact configuration for grasp initialization. We
represent the hand and object graphs as

Econtact reduces the separation between matched hand and
object regions while encouraging their surfaces to face each


other. Epen penalizes penetration into the object and support
surface, while Eclear suppresses unintended contact from
non-matched hand regions. Ereg limits changes in the wrist
pose and motor state from the initialization, keeping the
refinement local. Since the correspondence σ is fixed and the
joint configuration follows the coupled kinematics in Eq. (1),
the refinement improves geometric contact feasibility without
leaving the realizable configuration space of the hand.
The correspondence σ remains unchanged during refine-
ment. The exact contact location may move within the
matched object region, but the contact region assignment is
preserved. Refined grasps are then filtered using geometric
checks for contact orientation, object penetration, tabletop
clearance, and task constraints before physical evaluation.
C. Physics-Based Validation and Selection
Geometric validity alone does not guarantee that a grasp
remains stable after holding. We evaluate each refined can-
didate by replaying the grasp execution in simulation. The
hand starts from a pre-grasp configuration near the planned
contacts and closes toward the target grasp without moving
the object. During holding, the hand contact forces fc and
the support reaction fs from the tabletop balance the object,
while a constant disturbance fd is applied along each direc-
tion in F6 = {±ˆx, ±ˆy, ±ˆz} to evaluate grasp stability.

D. Optional Task-Guided Local Derivation
The preceding process yields a complete grasp without
task-specific requirements. However, some tasks need the
grasp to account for external factors beyond grasp stability.
For example, when using a knife to cut an object, the
hand should remain clear of the intended cutting region.
Similarly, a grasp may need to better resist disturbances from
a particular direction. In this process, we search grasps to
accommodate such task-specific requirements.
We start from the obtained physically validated grasp
through the preceding process, which provides a reliable
starting point for task-specific derivation. Given the validated
grasp g = (T, u), we explore task-specific grasp candidates
in its local neighborhood by applying perturbations to the
wrist pose T and motor state u, including small translations
and discrete yaw rotations of the wrist, local scans of the
motor state, and contact configuration.
We define C(g) as the set of locally perturbed grasps that
satisfy the hard conditions, while c specifies the task-specific
soft conditions for ranking the feasible candidates. The final
variant is selected from this candidate set by

additional fingers to contact the object, are enforced when
constructing Cc(g). Among the resulting feasible candidates,
Sc ranks each grasp according to the specified task prefer-
ence. After that, we check geometric feasibility of the final
grasp as described in Sec. III-C. This optional stage adapts
an existing successful grasp toward application requirements
while reusing the validated grasp as the starting point.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
We extensively evaluate the proposed method in both
simulation and real-world settings to assess grasp success
ratio, physical robustness and generalization across diverse
objects. We further provide controlled analyses to validate
the contributions of support-aware synthesis and the individ-
ual components of our framework.
A. Experimental Setup
a) Simulation setup: We evaluate the proposed method
on the coupled Inspire RH56 and the Shadow Hand using
200 randomly sampled objects from the DexGraspNet [3] test
split. All methods use the same object set and are evaluated
under a shared MuJoCo protocol. We use a 1ms simulation
step, 0.1kg object mass, and a friction coefficient of 0.6. The
contact-configuration tolerances are fixed across all objects
to τd = 8 mm and τθ = 30◦for pairwise pruning in
Eq. (4). After reaching the grasp configuration, we apply an
additional finger closing command controlled by a squeeze
coefficient, with 0.5 used as the default setting.
b) Evaluation protocol: We assess grasp quality using
success, contact, and penetration metrics. Grasp Success Rate
(GSR) is the percentage of attempted grasps that resist the
prescribed disturbance, whereas Object Success Rate (OSR)
is the percentage of objects for which at least one successful
grasp is found. Penetration Depth (PD) and Self-Penetration
Depth (SPD) denote the maximum hand–object and inter-
link penetration depths, respectively. Contact Link Number
(CLN) counts hand links within 2 mm of the object surface,
while Contact Distance Consistency (CDC) is the range of
signed contact distances across fingers, with lower values
indicating more consistent contact.
For GSR and OSR, we apply constant forces along
E = {±ˆx, ±ˆy, +ˆz}. A grasp succeeds if it resists a 0.98 N
force for 2 s while maintaining translational and rotational
displacements below 50 mm and 15◦. We then increase the
force to 50 N at 8 N/s under tighter bounds of 5 mm and

C. Analysis and Ablations
a) Analysis of the proposed pipeline: Tab. III examines
the role of each component in our pipeline. The process in
Sec. III-A establishes geometrically stable contact configura-
tions, leading to high grasp success, but often places the hand
deeply around the object and causes substantial penetration
resulting high PD. The process in Sec. III-B preserves the
contact matching while moving penetrated hand parts toward
the object surface, thereby improving the most of synthesized
grasps. However, without physical validation, penetration-
free grasps may fail due to insufficient contact force, while
deeply penetrated grasps can remain as successful cases. The
process in Sec. III-C addresses this issue by restoring grasp
strength and filtering physically invalid grasps, resulting in
robust grasps with minimal penetration resulting low PD. In
summary, we first refines the most of synthesized grasps, and
then filters the final successful grasps.
b) Support contribution analysis: To quantify how the
tabletop contributes to grasp stability, we analyze the settled
contact configuration of each grasp using a static second-
order cone program (SOCP) [31]. The optimization deter-
mines the contact forces at the hand-object and table-object
contacts required to balance an external disturbance under
friction and motor-torque constraints. For each grasp experi-
ment, we solve the same optimization twice: once using both
the hand and tabletop contacts, and once after removing only
the tabletop contact while keeping the settled hand-object
configuration unchanged. As shown in Tab. IV, removing the
tabletop substantially reduces force-balance feasibility and
disturbance resistance, indicating that the support directly
contributes to balancing external forces.

c) Ablation study on the refinement objective: We ab-
late the objective used in the region-constrained geometric
refinement in Sec. III-B by replacing our geometric ob-
jective with calibrated SOCP and the differentiable task-
GWS objective of TaskDexGrasp [15], which optimizes the
grasp wrench space to cover task-specific external wrench
directions. The calibrated SOCP is implemented to maximize
the worst-case disturbance resistance certified by the contact-
force SOCP [31] on the pad contacts projected onto the
object surface. Tab. V shows that our geometric objec-
tive typically yields better physical grasp performance than
both analytic alternatives. Although SOCP and differentiable
GWS explicitly optimize wrench-based grasp quality, these
static objectives can be disconnected from the contacts
actually formed after closing and settling.
d) Local derivation for directional robustness:
We
evaluate whether the optional local derivation in Sec. III-
D can adapt a validated grasp to a specified disturbance
direction. We consider four horizontal directions and perform
the local search independently for each target direction. As
shown in Tab. VI, the derived direction-conditioned grasps
exhibit consistently stronger resistance than the original
grasps across each tested force. This result shows that an
already validated grasp can be locally adapted to a task-
specific load without repeating the global contact search.
e) Effect of squeeze strength: We analyze the effect
of closing strength by keeping the grasp pose, object, and
evaluation protocol fixed while varying only the squeeze
coefficient. As shown in Tab. VII, stronger squeezing con-
sistently increases weakest-direction resistance, while grasp
success and settled penetration remain nearly unchanged. In
particular, the median penetration remains constant across the
entire squeeze range. This shows that the increased resistance
is primarily associated with stronger contact loading rather
than deeper penetration into the object.

the updated shape to derive a new grasp configuration for
continued manipulation.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We present a support-assisted grasp synthesis framework
for low-DoF coupled robotic hands. Our key idea is to
search for contact structures that are compatible with the
coupled hand kinematics, refine them toward geometrically
realizable contacts, and validate the resulting grasps through
physical execution. Rather than treating the surface only as
a geometric constraint, our framework allows it to provide
additional support when forming stable grasps. Experiments
on the RH56 and Shadow Hand show that accounting for
hand embodiment and environmental support improves grasp
feasibility and robustness, while real-robot results show that
the synthesized grasps remain executable and adaptable to
downstream tasks. Our current pipeline focuses on static
tabletop support and known object geometry. Extending
the pipeline to more general environmental contacts along
with perception-driven object and support geometries would
broaden its applicability to less structured manipulation sce-
narios. In addition, the synthesized grasps could be organized
into a large-scale dataset or used as structured grasp supervi-
sion to inject grasp knowledge into robot learning systems.
