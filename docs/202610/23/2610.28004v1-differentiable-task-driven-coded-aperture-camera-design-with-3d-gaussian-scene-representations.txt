Design with 3D Gaussian Scene Representations

Abstract
Camera design heavily influences perception task perfor-
mance, yet cameras are typically designed by domain ex-
perts through costly experimentation, in isolation from
the tasks they serve. Existing joint-design methods either
rely on differentiable simulation limited to pre-captured
2D images, restricting them to optics or image-processing
pipeline design, or use non-differentiable camera sim-
ulators optimised with derivative-free or reinforcement-
learning methods that scale poorly with the number of de-
sign parameters.
We present a differentiable framework
for jointly designing complex camera systems and down-
stream perception tasks. We use it to design two coded-
aperture depth-from-defocus cameras, a monocular and a
stereo configuration, by jointly optimising the coded aper-
ture mask, camera parameters, and a depth estimation
network end-to-end.
Our framework represents scenes
with 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructed from real-world
captures, providing a differentiable image formation pro-
cess for gradient-based optimisation over design spaces
intractable for prior differentiable approaches.
Results
demonstrate that our differentiable method improves over
existing coded-aperture design and non-differentiable cam-
era design baselines, offering a step forward in automated
and principled camera design for autonomous systems.
1. Introduction
The quality of camera captures directly governs the per-
formance of downstream perception tasks, yet different au-
tonomous systems require different camera designs to suit
their tasks: autonomous vehicles need wide field of view
(FoV) and high dynamic range, medical imaging needs fine
resolution and colour fidelity, and robotics needs reliable
depth and colour sensing. Hence, cameras should be de-
signed in a task-specific manner.

2. Related Work
Task-Driven Camera Design Designing cameras for per-
ception tasks improves performance, though many ap-
proaches rely on non-differentiable simulators. Early work
used renderers such as ISET [39, 40] to prototype cameras
and manually tune parameters against task outcomes [4, 23–
25, 41], trading laborious manual iteration for lower devel-
opment cost. Later work automates this with reinforcement-
learning [15, 20] or evolutionary [35, 43, 44] methods that
bypass large networks for faster, more data-efficient opti-
misation. Treating the simulator as a black box, however,
makes these methods inefficient with high-dimensional or
numerous parameters such as a coded aperture mask, which
our method handles efficiently via gradient-based optimisa-
tion while retaining full camera system support.
Another line of work instead leverages differentiable
simulation to jointly optimise camera parameters and the
perception task, applied to depth of field [33, 34, 46], object
detection [8, 31, 36, 37], image classification [6, 9, 45, 49],
depth estimation [1, 5, 14, 16], HDR imaging [26, 27], and
motion deblurring [30]. These methods simulate cameras’
effect on pre-captured 2D images, however, limiting gen-
eralisation to variable FoV, resolution, unconventional con-
figurations, or multi-camera systems. Our approach instead
constructs the full environment in 3D using 3DGS, support-
ing such scenarios while retaining differentiability.
Coded Aperture Imaging Coded aperture imaging modi-
fies the aperture to encode scene-dependent blur, improving
depth and AiF recovery. Early work showed that appropri-
ately designed aperture patterns improve the conditioning
of depth-from-defocus and deblurring [21, 38, 50], and re-
cent methods jointly optimise the aperture code and recon-
struction network end-to-end using amplitude [32] or phase
masks [13, 16, 42]. Ghanekar et al. [12] extended this to
dual-pixel sensors, learning an amplitude mask that trades
depth estimation against deblurring.
These methods optimise the aperture mask alone,
whereas we treat coded aperture imaging as a broader co-
design problem, jointly optimising the mask with other
camera parameters for task-driven objectives. This enables
systematic exploration of trade-offs such as depth accuracy,
noise, and defocus blur, and we further extend coded aper-
ture imaging to stereo-camera systems.
3. Methodology
We propose a differentiable framework that jointly opti-
mises camera design and downstream perception tasks us-
ing a 3DGS scene representation, as illustrated in Figure 2.
3.1. Scene Representation and Image Formation
We represent the 3D scene using 3DGS [18], a differen-
tiable splatting-based representation reconstructed before
optimisation from a set of input images, which renders RGB
images and depth maps via alpha-compositing. We assume
a pinhole projection model, though other camera models
could be used; this yields analytical gradients with respect
to camera parameters, enabling gradient-based joint optimi-
sation of intrinsics (e.g., focal length, sensor dimensions)
and extrinsics (e.g., placement, orientation).
To simulate coded aperture defocus, we apply an
occlusion-aware layered blur model adapted from Ikoma et
al. [16]: the rendered scene is quantised into discrete depth
layers, each convolved with a depth-dependent point spread
function (PSF) determined by the coded mask, focus dis-
tance, and f-number, then composited back-to-front using
the depth maps to account for occlusion between layers.
Following [43], we augment the rendered image with an
affine noise model [11] combining signal-dependent Pois-
son noise and thermal noise calibrated on a physical cam-
era. The calibrated noise parameters generalise to arbitrary

3.2. Perception Task
Our framework is compatible with any differentiable per-
ception task, but here we focus on an RGB-D task that
jointly estimates depth from defocus blur and reconstructs
an AiF image, as it depends directly on the optical param-
eters we optimise. Our monocular network builds on the
depth-from-defocus architecture of Ikoma et al. [16], which
deconvolves the coded aperture image into a focal stack via
a Tikhonov inverse filter and predicts the AiF image and
depth map from the stack and coded image using a U-Net
backbone. We augment this backbone with a frequency in-
jection module that extracts local defocus cues from patch-
wise Fourier spectra of the coded image and injects them at
the U-Net bottleneck, improving depth separation.
To demonstrate our method’s ability to design beyond a
single camera, we extend this network to a stereo setup: we
deconvolve each view’s coded aperture image into a focal
stack as before, then fuse them into intermediate AiF im-
ages via a differentiable Laplacian sharpness measure for
photometric consistency across views. Inspired by [7], a
frozen pretrained optical flow network, RAPIDFlow [29],
estimates disparity from these images and warps the right
focal stack into the left view’s coordinate frame. The left
and warped right stacks, together with a disparity-derived
depth cue, are then fed into the same task network to pre-
dict the left view’s AiF image and depth map. Illustration
diagrams of both setups are in the supplementary material.
3.3. Optimisation
We formulate our framework as a joint optimisation prob-
lem over the camera parameters and the perception task, as
illustrated in Figure 2. At each step, the 3DGS scene is
rendered into RGB and depth images, passed through the
coded aperture and noise simulation, and fed to the percep-
tion network; the resulting task loss ltask is backpropagated
through the entire pipeline to jointly update all camera and
network parameters using the Adam optimiser [19].
Camera Parameters We optimise the optics (focal length,
f-number, coded aperture mask, focus distance) and im-
age sensor (dimensions, pixel size) for the monocular setup,
plus the baseline between cameras for the stereo setup. Be-
cause sparse view coverage near the scene can cause 3DGS
renders to contain large black regions under wide FoVs, we
apply a black-pixel regularisation loss Lblk = λblk β/focal,
where β is the rendered black-pixel fraction, focal is the op-
timisable focal length, and the weight λblk=30 is set empir-
ically to match the scale of the other losses. This loss dis-
courages the optimiser from exploiting short focal lengths
where the reconstruction is degenerate; it therefore con-
strains the effective design space rather than expressing a
preference of the downstream perception task, and reflects
a limitation of the 3DGS representation’s view coverage.
Discrete Parameters The image sensor (chosen from a
fixed catalogue for manufacturability) and the coded aper-
ture mask are inherently discrete choices, which we opti-
mise using a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation with a straight-
through estimator (STE) [17]. Given K candidates with
logits ϕi, the relaxed selection weights are

4. Experiments
We validate our method on two close-range design scenar-
ios for depth-from-defocus and AiF reconstruction, focus-
ing on short range because the defocus cue is less informa-
tive further out. The monocular case targets robotic grasp-
ing, recovering depth from a single camera over a confined
workspace.
The stereo case targets macro photography,
where a zoomed-in view with a shallow depth of field (DoF)
produces strong defocus blur; here a stereo pair adds com-
plementary photometric and geometric depth cues.
4.1. Implementations
Dataset and Environment We select close-range scenes
from MipNeRF 360 [2] and LLFF [28]: 6 scenes (leaves,
flower, fortress, orchids, bonsai, room) for the monocular
case, and 7 scenes (the same 4 LLFF scenes plus counter,
kitchen, bonsai from MipNeRF 360) for the stereo case. For
each scene, we establish a 3D Gaussian representation with
gsplat [47] as the virtual environment, then recover metric
scale by aligning the reconstruction to metric depth from
Depth Anything 3 [22]. The camera design and task net-
work are jointly optimised across all scenes, with a subset
of views per scene withheld for evaluation.

4.2. Monocular Camera
The monocular camera design task focuses on close-range
RGB-D imaging from a single capture, which is a common
requirement in robotics applications such as grasping.
Design Space The design space covers the optical parame-
ters (focal length ∈[5, 25] mm, focus distance ∈[0.1, 5] m,
f-number ∈[1.4, 16], and a 17 × 17 px circular ampli-
tude coded aperture mask) and the image sensor, chosen
from a catalogue of 43 commercial CMOS sensors collected
in [43]. Sensor size and focal length determine the FoV,
while focal length and f-number determine the aperture size.
The mask and sensor choice are optimised with the Gumbel-
Softmax and STE formulation, as in Section 3.3.
Comparison We compare against TaCOS [43], a genetic
algorithm-based camera design method that jointly opti-
mises camera parameters and the perception model, run for
the same ∼20,000-step budget (590 candidates over 34 gen-
erations, following its recommended 2×-parameter popula-
tion size for our 295 optimised parameters).
We also compare to the baseline depth-from-defocus
method of Ikoma et al. [16] with a fixed camera, testing
both their original phase mask, matched in element count,
and an amplitude mask matched to our own settings. We
use the catalogue’s largest-pixel sensor for the best SNR,
and reuse their focus distance as it targets a similar depth
range to our scenes. We scale the f-number and focal length
to reproduce their aperture diameter, matching their level
of defocus blur while keeping a reasonable FoV with our
sensor. These camera parameters are listed in Table 1.
To highlight the benefit of a coded aperture, which re-
covers an AiF image from the blur caused by widening the
aperture under low-light conditions, we evaluate all meth-
ods under two low-SNR scenarios with increased image
noise, representing low-light operation or noisier sensors.
AiF quality is measured with PSNR, SSIM, and the per-
ceptual metric LPIPS [48]; depth accuracy is measured
with RMSE, MAE, log10 error, and the threshold accuracy
δ < 1.25 that measures the fraction of predicted depths
within a factor of 1.25 of the ground truth.

lects and its optimisation method.
Our method converges to a short focal length and
high f-number, giving a small aperture, ≈1.0 mm under
Noise×100, and correspondingly large DoF that keeps the
PSF well-conditioned across the workspace, enabling accu-
rate AiF reconstruction while retaining enough residual de-
focus for the best depth results across all metrics. It also
selects the largest available pixel size to maximise SNR
at this small aperture, and the mask compensates for the
small aperture area by opening more pixels to maintain light
throughput. Under Noise×200, it modestly increases the
focal length and lowers the f-number, enlarging the aperture
to ≈1.3 mm to admit more light while keeping the same sen-
sor. Despite the resulting increase in blur, it sustains strong
performance and outperforms all baselines by a substantial
margin in both scenarios. Note that the black-pixel regular-
isation loss discourages the optimiser from selecting even
shorter focal lengths, so the converged values reported here
reflect this constrained design space rather than an uncon-
strained preference of the perception task.

4.4. Ablation Study
We isolate the contribution of key design choices in both
experiments under Noise×100. Results are in Table 3.
For the monocular method, fixing the mask to the
human-designed pattern of Levin et al. [21] makes the cam-
era optimiser widen the aperture to compensate for its lower
open fraction. Performance still slightly degrades across
all metrics, however, demonstrating that optimising camera
parameters alone cannot recover the spatial structure of a
jointly optimised 2D mask. Fixing the camera to the Ikoma
et al. [16] configuration substantially reduces AiF quality,
confirming that its large aperture and shallow DoF limit
reconstruction.
It still outperforms the equivalent fixed-
camera baseline from the main comparison, however, show-
ing that our model and losses help even without camera op-
timisation. Removing the frequency injection module (re-
verting to the Ikoma et al. backbone) leaves AiF quality
largely unchanged but degrades depth accuracy. The op-
timiser compensates with a longer focal length to increase
blur, confirming the module mainly aids depth estimation
via depth-dependent defocus features.
For the stereo method, sharing camera parameters across
views collapses both to the left-camera design, removing
the complementary focus split and degrading both AiF and
depth.
The right-view mask compensates by becoming
horizontally anisotropic, encoding directional defocus cues
since it can no longer differentiate through focus or aper-
ture. Sharing a mask across views causes a modest AiF
drop (LPIPS 0.177 vs. 0.161) but slightly improves depth
(RMSE 0.083 vs. 0.084), as identical defocus patterns give
more photometrically consistent input to the disparity esti-
mator. Replacing the coded mask with a conventional open
aperture, reducing to a standard stereo camera, significantly
degrades AiF reconstruction (LPIPS 0.236 vs. 0.161): an
uncoded circular aperture produces a PSF that is poorly con-
ditioned for deconvolution, so sharpness cannot be recov-
ered through reconstruction the way it can with the coded
pattern. Depth accuracy degrades mildly, as stereo disparity
still recovers depth correctly over most of the scene, though
the weaker AiF reconstruction locally blurs the depth pre-
diction near edges. Removing the warping stage pushes the
right camera to a much longer focus distance and wider
aperture, creating stronger and more spatially distinctive
blur that can be exploited without requiring pixel-level cor-
respondence. This only mildly hurts depth accuracy but de-
grades AiF (LPIPS 0.205 vs. 0.161). The mask also opens
more on the side overlapping the left camera’s FoV, the re-
gion of highest cross-view photometric consistency it can
still exploit without explicit correspondence.
4.5. Real-World Validation
We further validate our method on a prototype built from a
commercial FLIR Flea3 camera [10] with a C-mount lens
(Figure 5) due to manufacturing constraints. Hence, this
validation focuses on optimising the coded mask, focus dis-
tance, and perception network, with the remaining optics
fixed. The test scene’s 3DGS representation is added to the
training set, though the evaluated view itself is not part of
the training views. We validate both the monocular and
stereo settings, with setup details, PSF calibrations, and
monocular results reported in the supplementary material.
The stereo camera is achieved by moving the camera by the
baseline distance, and the corresponding results are shown
in Figure 5. The real-world behaviour matches our simu-
lation: the stereo method focuses each view at a different
depth to fuse complementary cues for AiF reconstruction,
and the recovered depth aligns with the true scene geometry,
confirming the method’s applicability beyond simulation.
5. Conclusion
We presented a differentiable framework that jointly opti-
mises camera design and downstream perception tasks, us-
ing 3D Gaussian representations reconstructed from real
captures as a differentiable virtual environment for image
formation. This avoids the scalability limits of derivative-
free optimisation. We validated the framework on monoc-
ular and stereo depth-from-defocus tasks with jointly op-
timised coded apertures, sensors, and inter-camera geome-
try. It consistently outperforms fixed-camera and derivative-
free baselines, while revealing interpretable design trade-
offs that a decoupled process would miss. We believe this
is an important step toward principled, automated camera
design for autonomous systems that accounts for the inter-
dependency between cameras and the algorithms that inter-
pret them. For future work, we aim to extend the framework
to jointly optimise camera count and array topology, and to
incorporate adaptive, task-driven control of camera param-
eters online at inference time.

A. Depth-from-Defocus Networks
We provide additional details of the depth-from-defocus
networks used as perception tasks in our experiments. The
structures of both the monocular and stereo versions are de-
scribed in this section.
A.1. Monocular
As illustrated in Figure 6, our monocular task network
builds upon the depth-from-defocus architecture of [16].
The original method first applies a Tikhonov inverse fil-
ter to the rendered coded aperture images, using the PSFs
to deconvolve them into a focal stack; each plane of this
stack contains a relatively sharp region corresponding to
that plane’s PSF. However, because the forward model for
defocus blur simulation is non-linear, the Tikhonov inverse
cannot directly invert this process to obtain the AiF image.
The focal stack, together with the rendered coded aperture
image, is therefore used as input to a convolutional neural
network with a U-Net architecture, which outputs both the
AiF image and depth map. Details of this architecture can
be found in [16].
We modify this model by incorporating a frequency in-
jection module, as shown in Figure 6, that encodes spatially-
local defocus cues directly from the coded image in the
frequency domain for better depth separation as in [21].
Rather than transforming the entire image globally, we fol-
low [32] and operate on local patches. The rendered blurry
image b is partitioned into overlapping P ×P windows with
stride S via reflection-padded unfolding, yielding Nh ×Nw
patches {bij}. Each patch is independently transformed via
the real-valued 2D Fourier transform and its log-magnitude
spectrum log(1+|F{bij}|) is computed to obtain local de-
focus cues. A lightweight 3-layer convolutional encoder
with shared weights maps each patch spectrum to a descrip-
tor via global average pooling; these are then reassembled
into a dense feature map preserving the spatial correspon-
dence between each descriptor and its originating image re-
gion. This map is then injected into the U-Net bottleneck
via a learned per-channel gated addition:
x ←x + σ(g) ⊙ffreq,
(4)
where g is initialised to −3 so that the branch contributes
negligibly at the start of training and the network learns the
appropriate mixing ratio. Fusing at the bottleneck allows
the spatially-structured frequency cues to condition the en-
tire decoder without perturbing the fine-grained spatial skip
connections.
A.2. Stereo
For the stereo camera setup, we reuse the U-Net architecture
with frequency injection from the monocular version as the

To obtain reliable optical flow estimates, we first construct
an intermediate all-in-focus image per view by fusing the
Tikhonov-inverted focal stack via a differentiable Lapla-
cian sharpness measure, which improves photometric con-
sistency across the two views. The fused images are then
passed to RAPIDFlow to predict a dense disparity map,
which warps the right-view focal stack into the left view’s
coordinates via bilinear grid sampling. This provides addi-
tional photometric and defocus cues for AiF reconstruction
and depth estimation. The left focal stack, the warped right
focal stack, and a depth map derived from the disparity es-
timate are used as inputs to the same U-Net architecture as
in the monocular setting, with the input shape modified ac-
cordingly; the network outputs the reconstructed AiF image
and depth map for the left view.

We provide further qualitative comparisons for the monoc-

perception under challenging noise conditions.
C. Additional Qualitative Results on Stereo
Camera Design
Fig. 9 presents full qualitative comparisons between our
method and the baselines across both noise scenarios. Our
stereo setup achieves higher AiF reconstruction quality by
combining photometric cues from both views under strong
defocus blur, with one view focused on the foreground and
the other on the background. Its depth estimation is like-
wise improved by exploiting disparity as an additional cue.
The baseline methods, in contrast, are limited to a single
focal distance by their hardware, resulting in lower AiF re-
construction quality and depth estimation accuracy under
strong defocus blur.

D. Additional Details on Real-World Experi-
ments
We validate our method with a real-world prototype of a
coded aperture camera. As discussed in the main paper,
we use a commercial FLIR Flea3 camera with a Fujinon
DF6HA-1B lens due to manufacturing constraints. The lens
has a fixed focal length of 6.15 mm; we additionally max-
imise the entrance pupil diameter by setting the f-number to
1.2, which eases production of the coded aperture mask. We
optimise the coded aperture mask and the lens’s focus dis-
tance for both the monocular and stereo experiments, with
the mask set to a 13×13 grid. A relay lens (Edmund Optics
#46-007) then images the manufactured mask onto the en-
trance pupil of the main lens, placing the coded aperture at
the pupil plane. The setup is shown in Figure 5 of the main
paper.
The manufactured mask’s PSF is measured by imaging a
point light source at a known focus distance and source-to-
camera distance. This measured PSF replaces its simulated
counterpart in the image formation model, and we fine-tune
the task network on the same training data for 1000 steps.
Figure 10 (a) shows the optimised coded aperture mask for
both monocular and stereo setups, the manufactured masks,
and their measured PSFs.
Figure 10 (b) shows the captured coded aperture image,
reconstructed AiF image, and estimated depth map for the
monocular setup, while the corresponding stereo results ap-
pear in Figure 5 of the main paper. Our method reconstructs
sharp AiF images and plausible depth maps under real opti-
cal conditions, though the depth estimation lags behind the
simulated results, with visible errors near the scene’s mid-
plane. We attribute this shortfall to manufacturing toler-
ances and the broader gap between simulated and captured
imagery. Comparing Figure 10 (b) with Figure 5 of the main
paper, the stereo configuration yields higher depth accuracy
than the monocular one, in line with our simulated results.
