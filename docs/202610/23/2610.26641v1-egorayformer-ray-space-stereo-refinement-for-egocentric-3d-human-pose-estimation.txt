Abstract
Head-mounted stereo fisheye cameras provide a compact
view of the wearer’s body, but strong distortion, partial vis-
ibility and a short baseline make 3D pose estimation diffi-
cult. Existing refinement decoders sample image features
independently in each view, without requiring the samples
to correspond to a common 3D hypothesis. We introduce
EgoRayFormer, a causal pose estimator that couples stereo
feature sampling and geometric refinement in ray space.
For each joint, the decoder projects shared 3D hypotheses
along and around its viewing ray into both images. A ge-
ometric branch then corrects the projected joint locations
and intersects the resulting rays; a learned gate blends this
geometric estimate with a learned pose update. This formu-
lation introduces an explicit stereo constraint while retain-
ing flexibility when the rays provide weak depth constraints.
In local evaluations on the official device-relative test
splits, EgoRayFormer achieves three-seed mean MPJPEs of
17.64 mm on UnrealEgo2 and 49.55 mm on UnrealEgo-RW,
improving to 47.57 mm with UnrealEgo2 pre-training. In
paired validation ablations, removing the geometric branch
increases MPJPE in all three seeds at both input resolu-
tions. With a common ResNet-18 encoder and input res-
olution, our system reduces RW test MPJPE by 9.09 mm
relative to the device-relative EgoPoseFormer baseline. A
separate protocol analysis quantifies the difference between
device-relative and oracle-root evaluation.
1. Introduction
Estimating the wearer’s body pose from headset-mounted
cameras enables avatar animation and interaction without
external cameras [9, 24, 28, 35].
A pair of downward-
looking fisheye cameras offers wide coverage in a compact
rig [1, 2, 37], but the resulting images are difficult to inter-
pret. Limbs appear foreshortened, distal joints occupy few
pixels, and body parts are frequently occluded or outside
the field of view. The short stereo baseline adds a geometric
difficulty: for cameras about 12 cm apart, the rays to a joint
1.5 m away meet at an angle of only about five degrees. Ac-
curate reconstruction therefore requires both useful image
evidence and a way to handle weak geometric constraints.
Recent pose decoders refine a coarse 3D estimate by
sampling features near its image projections [4, 16, 36].
This design concentrates computation around the body, but

the sampling offsets are predicted independently for each
view. The sampled image locations need not be projections
of a common 3D hypothesis, leaving the relation between
the two views implicit in feature fusion. Direct triangulation
provides an explicit relation, yet small localisation errors
can produce large depth errors when the viewing rays are
nearly parallel. These observations motivate a refinement
step that connects stereo sampling to 3D geometry while
retaining a learned alternative to the geometric estimate.
We propose EgoRayFormer, a causal decoder for stereo
fisheye pose estimation.
Each refinement layer places
shared 3D hypotheses in a local coordinate frame aligned
with the ray from the device to a joint. Projecting each hy-
pothesis into both cameras couples the locations at which
the two feature maps are sampled. From these features, a
geometric branch predicts per-view corrections, computes a
differentiable intersection of the corrected rays, and blends
the result with a learned pose update. The complete model
combines this refinement with a stereo image encoder and
causal temporal attention, and predicts joint coordinates rel-
ative to the device without ground-truth inputs at inference.
Our evaluation distinguishes system performance from
evidence about individual components.
On the official
test splits, local three-seed evaluations give MPJPEs of
17.64 mm on UnrealEgo2, 49.55 mm on UnrealEgo-RW
with RW-only training, and 47.57 mm with UnrealEgo2
pre-training. With ResNet-18 and matched input resolu-
tion, EgoRayFormer has a 9.09 mm lower RW test MPJPE
than our device-relative adaptation of the official EgoPose-
Former code. This compares complete systems, including
their temporal context and training recipes. More specifi-
cally, removing the geometric branch increases validation
MPJPE by 0.92 mm at 3842 and 2.34 mm at 2562, with the
same direction in every paired seed. These ablations sup-
port the branch as a whole.
Evaluation coordinates also matter. Device-relative es-
timation requires the model to recover body position rel-
ative to the headset, whereas root-relative scoring removes
global body translation. Some pipelines additionally use the
root to project decoder anchors into the images. We there-
fore state the inference inputs and scoring coordinates for
each comparison, and measure the complete device/oracle-
root protocol gap using one architecture. Published leader-
board values provide external context, with the unresolved
RW split correspondence stated alongside the comparison
(Sec. 4.2). Earlier development use of the public test sets is
disclosed in Secs. A and 4.1.
Our contributions are:
• A ray-space refinement decoder that couples stereo
sampling through shared 3D hypotheses and combines
corrected-ray intersection with a learned pose update.
• A multi-seed evaluation on UnrealEgo2 and UnrealEgo-
RW, including comparisons at a common backbone and
resolution, paired ablations of the geometric branch, and
analysis of residual errors and gate behaviour.
• A paired analysis of device-relative and oracle-root pro-
tocols that quantifies their combined effect for a fixed ar-
chitecture and clarifies the interpretation of cross-protocol
results.
2. Related Work
Egocentric body pose estimation.
Early systems esti-
mated body pose from helmet- or cap-mounted fisheye
cameras [24, 35], followed by headset-mounted monocular
methods [28, 29], scene-aware models [30], and fisheye-
specific representations [31].
Stereo approaches such as
EgoGlass [37], UnrealEgo [1], Ego3DPose [11] and Ego-
TAP [10] lift per-view 2D heatmaps to 3D. Akada et al. [2]
introduced UnrealEgo2 and UnrealEgo-RW and used scene
depth from structure-from-motion to support video-based
estimation. Rear cameras [3] and headset pose [5] provide
additional observations in other capture settings.
Our decoder builds on the proposal-and-refinement de-
sign of EgoPoseFormer [36], whose deformable attention
samples features around projected joints. EgoPoseFormer
v2 [16] adds causal temporal attention, per-joint uncertainty
and auto-labelling for a four-camera headset. TSR-Ego [4]
combines causal mixing of stereo feature maps with tempo-
ral, joint and per-view deformable attention. We instead tie
the sampling locations across views to shared 3D hypothe-
ses and use corrected-ray intersection within the refinement
step. This distinction concerns the spatial update; causal at-
tention and the coarse-to-fine architecture have precedents
in these methods.
Geometry-guided multi-view refinement.
Learnable tri-
angulation [8] combines calibrated views through algebraic
or volumetric triangulation. MvP [33] uses projective at-
tention, while MVGFormer [17] alternates learned 2D cor-
rections and confidences with weighted triangulation. In
multi-camera detection, PETR [20] encodes 3D position
and BEVFormer [15] projects reference points into cam-
era views. Sparse4D [18] projects shared 3D keypoints into
multiple feature maps, providing a direct precedent for our
shared-hypothesis sampling; RayDN [19] uses ray-based
samples for denoising. EgoRayFormer adapts shared spa-
tial hypotheses and geometric refinement to a two-camera
fisheye rig. The hypotheses are organised in a ray-local
frame, and the geometric estimate is blended with a learned
update to accommodate the weak depth constraints of the
short baseline.
Fisheye and camera-aware image features.
Fisheye-
ViT [31] represents fisheye images using undistorted
tangent-plane patches.
We adopt this tokenisation with
2


ray-direction inputs and stereo cross-view blocks inspired
by CroCo and DUSt3R [32, 34], using a DINOv3 back-
bone [23, 26]. Other camera-aware transformers incorpo-
rate geometry through relative positional encoding [13, 22].
We use absolute viewing-ray channels and evaluate the ad-
ditional encoder components together against a plain DI-
NOv3 encoder (Sec. 4.4).
Evaluation coordinates and inference inputs.
The Un-
realEgo2 and UnrealEgo-RW benchmarks [2] evaluate
device-relative joint coordinates.
Root-relative scoring,
also common in third-person pose estimation [7], removes
global body translation.
This scoring choice is distinct
from supplying a root position to the model.
The pub-
lic EgoPoseFormer implementation for UnrealEgo uses the
ground-truth pelvis as the projection origin; TSR-Ego re-
ports root-relative results without specifying whether its
inference-time root is predicted or ground truth. We keep
these distinctions explicit and use a paired protocol experi-
ment to assess their combined effect (Sec. 4.6).
3. Method
EgoRayFormer estimates body pose from a causal stream of
stereo fisheye images (Fig. 2). Its decoder uses a shared 3D
hypothesis as the unit of stereo feature aggregation: each
hypothesis is projected into both calibrated views, and the
paired features support pose refinement. A second path pre-
dicts image-plane corrections, intersects the resulting view-
ing rays, and blends this geometric update with a learned
update. We first describe this decoder, then the encoder,
temporal context and training objective.

3.2. Queries and coarse proposal
The encoder (Sec. 3.5) produces a stride-4 feature map with
C=128 channels for each view. The decoder contains J
learned joint queries, a body token with a learned dataset
embedding, and a track-prior token that embeds the previ-
ous predicted pose. A learned null token is used at the start
of a sequence. During training, the previous ground-truth
pose is perturbed by Gaussian noise (σ = 3 cm) and re-
placed by the null token with probability 0.3.
The proposal layer applies camera-conditioned cross-
attention to both feature maps, followed by token self-
attention, causal temporal attention and a feed-forward
layer. Cross-attention adds a learned camera embedding to
each query and linearly fuses the two view outputs [16].
Self-attention uses a learned per-head bias between joints
indexed by skeleton hop distance. A regression head maps
the joint tokens to the coarse pose P 0.

3.5. Fisheye-native stereo encoder
The encoder adapts DINOv3 ViT-S+/16 [6, 26] to 384×384
stereo fisheye images. Following FisheyeViT [31], a pre-
computed calibration grid resamples each image into virtual
16 × 16 tangent-plane patches. Three additional channels
encode the device-frame viewing ray at each virtual pixel;
their patch-embedding weights start at zero. Two cross-
view attention blocks, placed after the last two ViT blocks,
exchange features between cameras with zero-initialised
layer scales [32, 34]. A simple feature pyramid [14] pro-
duces stride-4 features with 128 channels. An inverse lay-
out map resamples them into the original fisheye image co-
ordinates for calibrated decoder sampling.
An auxiliary
head predicts 64 × 64 heatmaps for the 15 joints other
than the head, providing encoder supervision and heatmap-
confidence inputs to the gate. Section 4.4 evaluates these
encoder adaptations as a group.
3.6. Causal temporal modelling
Token history.
Every decoder layer applies temporal at-
tention separately to each token, using the current frame
and up to seven preceding frames, with rotary frame em-
beddings [16, 27]. Training processes clips of eight frames
with a causal band mask. At inference, per-layer key–value
caches support frame-by-frame execution. With identical
images, track priors and history, the streaming and parallel
causal passes agree within 10−3 cm in our unit tests. The
training prior uses perturbed ground truth, whereas infer-
ence feeds back the previous prediction (Sec. 3.2).
Trajectory-aligned feature sampling.
A query also sam-
ples the four preceding frames. For each past frame, the off-
sets of Eq. (1) are placed around that joint’s anchor from the
preceding decoder stage and projected using that frame’s
calibration.
Query-predicted weights with a learned age
bias are normalised jointly over past ages and sampling
points. The aggregated features are added to the current-
frame sampling output; past value maps are cached during
streaming. Section 4.4 evaluates this component separately.

Protocol and metrics.
Our primary evaluation follows
the official device-relative protocol: predictions are the 3D
positions of 16 joints relative to the device centre, with no
ground-truth quantity used at inference. We report MPJPE
and PA-MPJPE (after rigid alignment with scale) in mm, us-
ing a common scorer based on the public EgoPoseFormer
evaluation code.
Ablations additionally report the mean
per-joint velocity error MPJVE, the error of frame-to-frame
joint displacements in mm per frame. Every sequence is
processed in one causal stream from its first frame, in FP32.
Model selection and statistics.
We train each configura-
tion with three seeds and select each checkpoint by valida-
tion MPJPE. Before the final evaluations reported here, we
froze the checkpoints and evaluation setup; each checkpoint
was then evaluated on the test set once. We report the mean
and sample standard deviation across seeds. Confidence in-
tervals resample test sequences with the fitted models held
fixed (10,000 bootstrap samples, frame-weighted). Abla-
tions and error analyses use validation data. The test splits
have been public since December 2025, and preliminary test
evaluations informed some development decisions. No re-
sult from those evaluations is reported here, but the test sets
were therefore not unseen during development. Section A
details this history and the final evaluation procedure.
4.2. Comparison with published benchmark results
EgoRayFormer achieves a mean MPJPE of 17.64 mm on
UnrealEgo2, 49.55 mm on RW with RW-only training, and
47.57 mm on RW after UnrealEgo2 pre-training (Tab. 1).
Relative to the lowest published values listed in the table
(30.53, 104.14 and 72.89 mm), these are reductions of 42%,
52% and 35%, respectively. The mean PA-MPJPE and the
MPJPE of each individual seed are also lower than the cor-
responding listed values. The RW comparison has an un-
resolved split-version limitation: Akada et al. [2] describe
86 test sequences and 7,936 stereo frames, whereas the cur-
rent official split used here has 24,012 frames; we have not
verified the version underlying the listed RW results.
The 95% sequence-bootstrap confidence intervals for our
mean MPJPE are [16.80, 18.55], [47.25, 51.92] and [45.04,
50.11] mm.
The methods also differ in backbone, pre-training, reso-
lution, temporal context and additional inputs. We therefore
use this table to compare complete systems. The following
experiments examine performance with a common encoder

EgoRayFormer has 35.1 M parameters and uses 106.9
counted GFLOPs per streaming step, with 91% in the en-

procedure is given in Sec. I. They do not establish real-time

5. Discussion and Conclusion
Scope and limitations.
Real-world evaluation is limited
to the UnrealEgo-RW rig, and the measured computational
cost leaves deployment on a headset unresolved. External
comparisons are constrained by differences in complete sys-
tems and by the unverified RW leaderboard split correspon-
dence. Our component analyses support the combined geo-
metric branch but do not isolate its individual operations or
establish that the gate estimates reliability. The public test
sets also informed early development decisions. The final
evaluation used fixed checkpoints, but this does not restore
an unseen test set (Sec. A).
Conclusion.
EgoRayFormer connects stereo image sam-
pling and pose refinement through shared 3D hypotheses in
a ray-local frame. Corrected-ray intersection supplies a ge-
ometric candidate, which is combined with a learned update
in a causal decoder. Local evaluations on UnrealEgo2 and
UnrealEgo-RW establish the performance of the full sys-
tem, while paired ablations support the geometric branch
as a whole. The remaining errors include substantial lat-
eral error at distant joints, motivating further investigation
of image localisation alongside geometric refinement. Our
protocol analysis further shows why inference inputs and
evaluation coordinates must be specified when comparing
egocentric pose estimators.

A. Evaluation protocol and disclosure
Frozen evaluation.
Before the final evaluations reported
here, every test configuration was registered in a frozen
list: for each entry the run, the selected checkpoint and its
SHA1 hash, the training configuration, the training record,
the evaluating code snapshot, the expected evaluation iden-
tity, the calibration files, the scorer files and the report items.
The list was recorded in our experiment log before its first
use. Final result checks covered checkpoint identity, pre-
diction coverage for all test frames, finite predictions, and
agreement between the saved predictions and scoring in-
puts. The common scorer and the pipeline metric agreed to
within 0.001 mm. Each frozen checkpoint was evaluated on
the test set once. Every sequence is predicted in one causal
stream from its first frame, in FP32, with the exponential-
moving-average weights and without any inference-time
override. The evaluation pipeline also audits the inference
inputs and records whether any of them carries ground-truth

E. Protocol gap on the test set
The frozen test evaluation of the TSR-like decoder cov-
ers both protocols with three seeds each. Paired by seed,
the oracle-root runs have a 30.91 ± 6.26 mm lower MPJPE
in their own frame, a 34.02 ± 5.51 mm lower root-aligned
MPJPE and a 17.87 ± 4.27 mm lower PA-MPJPE than the
device-relative runs, in 3, 3 and 3 of 3 pairs, respectively.
As on validation (Sec. 4.6), the difference contains the train-
ing target, the projection origin at inference and the scoring
frame, and we do not attribute it to the root input at infer-
ence.

Table 9 places the root-relative results reported by TSR-

G. Implementation details
Encoder.
We use DINOv3 ViT-S+/161 at 384 × 384, i.e.
24 × 24 tokens per view, with stochastic depth 0.05. Cross-
view blocks follow the last two transformer blocks. The
feature pyramid produces a stride-4 map with 128 channels;
the auxiliary head predicts 64 × 64 heatmaps with a Gaus-
sian of σ = 1.5 heatmap pixels. The heatmap stage trains
the encoder on single frames at 256×256 for 12 epochs with
batch size 256, AdamW with learning rate 10−3 and weight
decay 5·10−3, the same layer-wise decay and encoder scale
as the end-to-end stage, and 500 warm-up steps.
Decoder.
The decoder has width 128, 4 attention heads,
feed-forward width 512, one proposal layer and three re-
finement layers, with H = 4 heads of K = 16 hypotheses
each. Temporal attention covers 8 frames with rotary em-
beddings; trajectory sampling covers 4 past frames. The
track prior is replaced by the null token with probability
0.3 and perturbed with 3 cm Gaussian noise during train-
ing. One group of denoising queries with σ = 5 cm is used.
Samples whose hypotheses project outside the image are re-
placed by the missing-view vector; for the UnrealEgo2 and
UnrealEgo2 + RW models the valid region is the fisheye im-
age circle, for the RW-only model, which was trained with
an earlier revision of the recipe, it is the image rectangle.
Training and selection.
End-to-end training uses clips of
8 consecutive frames at 384 × 384, batch size 16, AdamW
with β = (0.9, 0.999) and the schedule of Sec. 3.7, bf16
mixed precision and gradient clipping at norm 5. Photomet-
ric augmentation is shared by the two views and all frames
of a clip. A checkpoint is saved after every epoch, and the
one with the lowest validation MPJPE is selected. Every

H. Components removed during development
Earlier versions of the decoder predicted a per-layer, per-
joint uncertainty, trained with a Student-t likelihood, and
used it as an input to the gate, to the track prior and to trajec-
tory sampling. In paired development runs with a ResNet-
18 encoder under the root-relative protocol, this module did
not reduce MPJPE, and its predicted uncertainty was over-
confident: on validation, far fewer than 68% of the errors
fell inside the predicted 68% region. We removed it before
the experiments of this paper. The same development runs
did not establish an MPJPE advantage for ray-consistent
sampling alone over per-view deformable sampling, which
is one reason why we do not claim the shared sampling as
a separate contribution. These development runs were also
evaluated on the RW test split (Sec. A), and those evalua-
tions informed the decisions.
I. Efficiency measurement
Latency is measured for the model forward pass only,
with random weights, batch size 1, PyTorch eager mode
and cudnn.benchmark enabled, on an otherwise idle
NVIDIA H200. The inference graph has fixed shapes and
no value-dependent control flow, so FLOPs do not depend
on the weights; we did not compare the latency with trained
weights. For streaming models, the state (key–value caches,
trajectory history, track prior) is first warmed with 8 frames,
and one further step is timed. After 30 warm-up iterations
we time 200 iterations, synchronising the GPU before and
after each, and report the median. FLOPs are counted with
torch.utils.flop counter on the same step, with-
out bilinear sampling and fisheye projections. With bf16
autocast, all models were slightly slower than in FP32 at
batch 1, so we report FP32, the precision of our evaluation.
