ABSTRACT
Lifting vision-language features such as CLIP into 3D Gaussian reconstructions
enables open-vocabulary segmentation through natural-language queries.
Al-
though CLIP represents a broad range of semantic concepts, the lifted features of
individual scenes can exhibit strong directional concentration, while raw cosine
similarity does not account for the scene-specific feature distribution. We show
that substantial segmentation gains can be obtained by changing how a fixed lifted
feature field is compared with text. Our method partially subtracts a shared scene
direction from visual features and text prototypes and whitens text prototypes us-
ing a fixed reference vocabulary of generic terms. It then uses soft clustering
for multiclass prediction and CSLS with a relevance margin against the reference
vocabulary for single-query prediction. Beyond reducing directional concentra-
tion, scene centering improves class-relative feature separation in every evaluated
scene. Across seven lifting methods and four datasets, the resulting query-time
calibration consistently improves mean intersection over union in both multiclass
and single-query segmentation. These gains require no retraining, source-image
re-encoding, or feature re-lifting, demonstrating the value of scene-conditioned
comparison for making better use of existing lifted representations.

INTRODUCTION
High-quality 3D reconstructions provide persistent, navigable models of captured environments.
Lifting vision–language features into them lets users locate and segment objects with natural-
language queries rather than a closed classifier. CLIP supplies the shared image–text space (Radford
et al., 2021), and methods like LERF (Kerr et al., 2023), LangSplat (Qin et al., 2024), and Feature
3DGS (Zhou et al., 2024) embed or distill language-aligned features into radiance fields and Gaus-
sian representations. As reconstruction and feature lifting improve, comparing the resulting scene
with text becomes an increasingly important part of open-vocabulary segmentation.
A particular reconstructed scene typically only presents a narrower semantic distribution than the
broad image–text collection used to train CLIP. We find that its lifted features can share a pro-
nounced scene-dependent direction, while raw cosine comparisons do not explicitly account for the
actual distribution observed in that scene. This presents an opportunity to improve open-vocabulary
queries solely by adapting them to a given scene at hand, rather than assuming that global embedding
geometry is equally informative in every environment. We find that a substantial part of segmenta-
tion performance can be recovered at query time, through simple calibration rather than changes to
reconstruction or feature lifting.
This scene-conditioned comparison should remain useful whether a user supplies a full class vocab-
ulary or asks for a single object. Multiclass evaluation lets candidate classes compete for each region
through an argmax: a region belongs to the class that scores highest among the supplied alternatives.
In practice, however, a user may simply ask for a “fire extinguisher,” without naming everything else
in the scene. The task is then not to choose a winner among known alternatives, but to decide where
the target is present and reject the remaining regions. Strong multiclass performance does not by
itself establish this ability, motivating separate evaluation of single-query segmentation.
We address both settings through scene-conditioned calibration built around a fixed reference bank
of generic terms. Our approach partially subtracts a shared scene direction from visual features
and text prototypes and fits a regularized text-whitening transform solely to a scene-centered ref-
erence bank. For multiple requested classes, the calibrated similarities guide soft clustering of the
primitive features similar to TransCLIP’s assignment refinement (Zanella et al., 2024) without graph
regularization. For a single query, we apply reduced CSLS (Lample et al., 2018) to the query and
reference-bank scores. The reference bank then supplies negative competitors and we threshold the
query’s margin over their strongest reference competitor. This allows regions to be rejected without
requiring the names of the scene’s other classes. Our method improves comparison with already-
lifted features without changing the reconstructed geometry, re-encoding source images, or re-lifting
features.
Centering, whitening, and neighborhood-normalized similarity have established precedents (Mu &
Viswanath, 2018; Su et al., 2021; Lample et al., 2018) in other domains, as does the use of generic
negatives in language relevance scoring (Kerr et al., 2023). We show that these simple operations,
adapted to the scene at hand, substantially improve open-vocabulary segmentation on existing lifted
feature fields. The improvements hold across different lifting methods and extend to single-query
segmentation, without retraining or re-lifting features. In summary, our contributions are:
• Query-time improvements on fixed lifted fields. We show that simple scene-conditioned
calibration consistently improves open-vocabulary segmentation across seven lifting meth-
ods on four datasets, in both multiclass and single-query settings, without retraining,
source-image re-encoding, or feature re-lifting.
• Reference-bank calibration for multiclass and single-query segmentation. We combine
scene centering, reference-only text whitening, and scene-relative score adjustment in a
query-time method. A fixed bank of generic terms defines the text transform independently
of the requested class list and supplies negative competitors for single-query segmentation.
• Analysis of feature geometry and calibration components. We characterize directional
concentration in lifted features and show that scene centering improves class-relative fea-
ture separation in every evaluated diagnostic scene. Component ablations examine how
feature calibration, score adjustment, and reference vocabulary contribute to the final seg-
mentation gains.

RELATED WORK
Language-aware representations.
Language-aware reconstruction methods determine which se-
mantic information is available for subsequent queries. LERF (Kerr et al., 2023) embeds multiscale
language features in a radiance field, while LangSplat (Qin et al., 2024) and Feature 3DGS (Zhou
et al., 2024) distill features into Gaussian representations. LEGaussians (Shi et al., 2024) compresses
language features through quantization, FMGS (Zuo et al., 2025) combines foundation-model fea-
tures with a multiresolution feature field, and econSG (Zhang & Lee, 2025) addresses feature con-
sistency across views. Gaussian Grouping (Ye et al., 2024), OpenGaussian (Wu et al., 2024), and
N2F2 (Bhalgat et al., 2024) additionally organize instance identities or semantic scales. These ap-
proaches improve the 3D representation constructed from images.
Efficient lifting methods reduce the cost of constructing language-aware fields. Dr. Splat (Kim et al.,
2025) directly registers language features, Occam’s LGS (Cheng et al., 2025) aggregates multiview
observations, and Splat Feature Solver (Xiong et al., 2026) formulates lifting as a regularized in-
verse problem. LightSplat (Bang et al., 2026) uses compact semantic indices and 3D clustering to
associate image regions efficiently. Their feature-construction strategies are complementary to post-
lifting calibration, which can operate on their outputs without repeating reconstruction or lifting.
Dense language features provide the inputs to many such pipelines. OpenSeg (Ghiasi et al., 2022)
and LSeg (Li et al., 2022) learn dense visual-language alignment, while MaskCLIP (Zhou et al.,
2022a) extracts dense predictions from CLIP (Radford et al., 2021). SAN (Xu et al., 2023) and
OVSeg (Liang et al., 2023) adapt the segmentation representation through additional networks or
masked-region training. SCLIP (Wang et al., 2025), ClearCLIP (Lan et al., 2025a), ProxyCLIP (Lan
et al., 2025b), and CLIPtrase (Shao et al., 2025) instead rectify attention or token features without
training. These methods are concerned with improving text and image features directly, rather than
operating at query-time on already lifted fields.
Calibration also appears in dense and 3D segmentation. SCAN (Liu et al., 2024) learns proposal cal-
ibration and contextual adaptation, while ReCLIP (Wang & Kang, 2024) learns class and spatial bias
corrections. OpenScene (Peng et al., 2023) fuses dense multiview features, and Open3DIS (Nguyen
et al., 2024) associates image-guided proposals with language evidence. PLA (Ding et al., 2023),
RegionPLC (Yang et al., 2024), and AIDE (Wang & Czarnecki, 2025) include learned confidence
mechanisms for balancing known and novel classes. Our setting instead requires a label-free correc-
tion applicable to an already available field without learning a segmentation or confidence model.
Feature adaptation.
Adapting embedding geometry provides one route to improving fixed-
feature comparisons. Alignment and uniformity (Wang & Isola, 2020) characterize contrastive rep-
resentations, while modality-gap analysis (Liang et al., 2022) and Two Effects, One Trigger (Schrodi
et al., 2025) identify systematic differences between visual and text embeddings.
All-but-the-
Top (Mu & Viswanath, 2018) and isotropy analysis (Rajaee & Pilehvar, 2021) motivate removing
dominant directions, while sentence whitening (Su et al., 2021) and Whitened CLIP (Betser et al.,
2025) transform covariance structure. These works directly motivate our calibration components,
with scene-local statistics and the reference vocabulary determining the calibration of a lifted field.
Other adaptations require evidence or optimization beyond stored scene features. CLIP Surgery (Li
et al., 2025) removes image-conditioned redundant features, and CalibCLIP (Kang et al., 2025)
modifies dominant-token processing. CoOp (Zhou et al., 2022c) and CoCoOp (Zhou et al., 2022b)
learn prompts, TPT (Shu et al., 2022) and C-TPT (Yoon et al., 2024) optimize prompts at test time,
and Tip-Adapter (Zhang et al., 2022) uses labeled support examples. ORION (Chakraborty et al.,
2026) fine-tunes the text encoder to separate class prototypes. Their feature or prototype corrections
target related errors, but their image access, labeled support, or learned parameter updates differ
from our analytic transformation of frozen embeddings.
Distribution-conditioned adaptations are more direct competitors on fixed features. UMFC (Liang
et al., 2024) estimates domain-dependent biases from unlabeled visual collections and corrects both
visual and text features. PTC (Ma et al., 2026) builds visual prototypes from selected predictions
and uses them to adjust text embeddings. Frolic (Zhu et al., 2024) constructs a covariance-based
classifier, combines it with CLIP predictions, and iteratively corrects class priors. Applied to lifted
primitives, these mechanisms compete with our scene centering and reference-text whitening by
estimating corrections from the visual distribution or predicted class structure.

Score calibration.
Reference-population normalization changes how fixed embeddings compete
without modifying their encoders. Inverted softmax (Smith et al., 2017), CSLS (Lample et al., 2018),
and querybank normalization (Bogolin et al., 2022) correct retrieval scores using statistics of other
samples. Hubness (Radovanovi´c et al., 2010) and prediction-concentration analysis (Sato et al.,
2026) connect such corrections to unequal neighbor or class occurrence. Our multiclass branch
uses soft clustering to refine primitive assignments, while our single-query branch applies class-
side CSLS offsets before computing the query-versus-reference margin. Alternative scene-score
normalizations are therefore direct comparisons for the single-query score correction.
Target-distribution methods instead adapt predictions jointly across samples or classes. BBSE (Lip-
ton et al., 2018) and logit adjustment (Menon et al., 2021) connect prediction correction to class-
frequency assumptions. OTTER (Shin et al., 2024) solves an optimal-transport assignment given
an estimated target label distribution, while OnZeta (Qian & Hu, 2024) updates label statistics and
visual-space class proxies online. TransCLIP (Zanella et al., 2024) iteratively estimates class dis-
tributions and propagates assignments over a feature graph. We adopt its soft assignment refine-
ment without graph regularization for multiclass prediction and combine it with scene centering and
reference-bank text whitening.
Single-query segmentation additionally requires a decision between foreground and background.
LERF (Kerr et al., 2023) and FMGS (Zuo et al., 2025) compare a query against four canonical
negative phrases, N2F2 (Bhalgat et al., 2024) uses generic phrases in multiscale feature learning,
and NegLabel (Jiang et al., 2024) mines negative labels for out-of-distribution detection. GOI (Qu
et al., 2024) replaces fixed-threshold selection with a query-specific semantic hyperplane optimized
using a referring-expression model, while FreeCP (Chen et al., 2025) removes redundant candidate
classes to reduce visual-language ambiguity. These approaches address negative competition, query-
specific selection, or candidate-set quality.

per-query scores for both multi-class (Sec. 3.4) and single-query predictions (Sec. 3.5).

3.3
REFERENCE VOCABULARY
The fixed vocabulary represents the text-side shared concepts using generic terms. Our bank GB
contains 45 reference unit text features gb and includes only generic concepts like “object”, “things”,
“texture”, and further generic scene, material, and spatial descriptors, but no class names. Ap-
pendix B lists the complete bank and its text-encoding convention. As detailed in Sections 3.4 and
3.5, we use the reference vocabulary to determine whitening statistics for text query calibration and
in the single-query case as background competitors.
To construct the vocabulary, we start from a fixed pool of 64 generic words and phrases and suc-
cessively remove words deemed unhelpful. At each step, we test removing each entry and remove
the word that gives the largest increase in mean mIoU on held-out calibration scenes, weighting the
multi-class and single-query tasks and the scenes equally. We repeat this process until no removal
improves this objective, leaving 45 entries, and then fix the bank.

EVALUATION
We evaluate our calibration on its ability to increase class-relative separation, 3D open-vocabulary
segmentation and present ablation studies. For the open-vocabulary segmentation we use LERF-
OVS Qin et al. (2024), 3D-OVS Liu et al. (2023), ten scenes from ScanNet (Dai et al., 2017) and
twelve from ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth et al., 2023) using mean intersection-over-union (mIoU), pre-
cision and recall. We evaluate lifted features from SFS (Xiong et al., 2026), NormLift (Zang et al.,
2026), LUDVIG (Marrie et al., 2025), OpenGaussian (Wu et al., 2024), LangSplat (Qin et al.,
2024), Occam (Cheng et al., 2025) and VALA (Wang et al., 2026). To assign ground truth points
to Gaussians, we follow the respective methods’ strategies for discarding low-opacity and invalidly
lifted Gaussians, and assign the remaining eligible Gaussians using minimum Mahalanobis distance.
Appendix E presents more details on our evaluation protocol.
In Section 4.2 we compare against raw uncalibrated features, as well as previous work on CLIP
calibration. These methods were not designed for our Gaussian-lifted 3D task, but we adopt their
ideas to enable comparison. Specifically we compare to adaptations of TransCLIP (Zanella et al.,
2024), PTC (Ma et al., 2026), UMFC (Liang et al., 2024) and a Frolic (Zhu et al., 2024) inspired
baseline.
4.1
INVESTIGATING THE EFFECTS OF FEATURE CALIBRATION
We quantify the directional concentration illustrated in Figure 2 using the von Mises–Fisher (vMF)
concentration estimate κ. Higher values indicate stronger concentration around a common direction.
As intended, subtracting the shared scene direction reduces mean κ from 1600 to 517 across the
twelve ScanNet++ SFS scenes. The uncentered second moment averages feature outer products
without subtracting their mean, capturing both a shared direction and the spread around it. Its leading
eigenvalue’s share measures the fraction of total squared feature magnitude along the strongest axis,
this falls from 76.8% to 47.3%. We also analyze participation rank, which summarizes how many
axes effectively carry this magnitude: k equally weighted axes give a participation rank of k. It
rises from 1.69 to 4.15, indicating that the features are less dominated by a few directions. Together,
these descriptive statistics show that centering redistributes feature magnitude across more axes,
rather than merely reducing the mean direction.
To assess semantic discrimination, we group features by their GT class and normalize each class’s
average feature to obtain its mean direction. Between-class scatter is the average squared distance
between these class mean directions: it measures how far apart the class centers are. Within-class
scatter is the average squared distance of features from their own class’s mean direction, averaged
equally across classes: it measures how spread out each class is around its center. Their ratio mea-
sures separation between classes relative to their internal variation. The mean class-relative separa-
tion ratio increases from 1.820 to 2.056 on ScanNet (19 classes), from 2.854 to 3.163 on ScanNet++
(100 classes), and from 2.414 to 2.738 on ScanNet++ (20 classes). The ratio increases in every scene
in all three settings. We conclude that the transformation does not merely spread feature directions
more broadly, it improves their separation relative to the ground-truth semantic classes.

Single-Query Calibration.
In the bottom half of Table 1 we present results for our calibration
when using only a single query at a time. We query all labels present in a scene individually and
average over present classes, then over scenes. Unlike the multi-class ablation, these N = 1 results
threshold one query’s target-minus-bank margin rather than taking an argmax over the real class
vocabulary. The raw similarity and calibrated relevance-margin thresholds τ were determined on
held-out scenes and then fixed for all evaluation scenes and queries. The dataset-specific values and
LangSplat exceptions are stated in Appendix C.
The resulting segmentation results are generally below their multi-class counterparts. This pattern
is consistent with the absence of mutual exclusion between independently thresholded query masks.
As a result different queries could re-use the same primitives that are otherwise competed for in the
multi-class case. Nevertheless, our calibration improves IoU in all cases, especially for VALA on
LERF-OVS. To investigate why raw performs so poorly in that case, we select an oracle raw-score
threshold on each evaluation scene, yielding only 23.71% mIoU, barely improving over the held-
out threshold. It seems that sparse semantic support attenuates rendered scores differently across
pixels, affecting absolute thresholding but not class-relative argmax decisions. Qualitative results
are available in Appendix A.

5
CONCLUSION
We studied scene-conditioned calibration for open-vocabulary 3D segmentation while holding re-
constructed geometry and lifted features fixed.
Combining scene centering, regularized text-
prototype whitening, and scene-relative score adjustment improves mIoU across all evaluated lifting
methods on four datasets, in both multiclass and single-query settings. Geometric diagnostics show
that centering improves class-relative feature separation, while component ablations demonstrate the
importance of combining feature, score calibration and our fixed reference vocabulary to determine
text-whitening statistics. Multiclass prediction uses soft clustering, while single-query prediction
uses a reduced-CSLS margin against the reference vocabulary. These results establish query-time
calibration as a useful complement to feature lifting: segmentation can improve without retraining,
re-encoding source images, or re-lifting features.
Limitations.
Our evaluation is limited to indoor scenes, and the shared centering and whitening
parameters were selected using SFS results on held-out indoor scenes, leaving transfer to other
environments and embedding models untested. Single-query evaluation uses GT-present classes and
held-out-selected thresholds, so it does not establish reliable rejection of entirely absent targets or
arbitrary natural-language queries. Predictions depend on the requested vocabulary and reference
bank, whose suitability across different scenes and queries is not established. Because calibration
leaves geometry and feature lifting unchanged, it cannot reconstruct missing surfaces or recover
semantic information absent from the lifted field.
Open Questions.
For single-query segmentation, an especially promising question is how to
choose negative queries that reflect the available scene context rather than relying only on a fixed
generic bank.
Previously queried concepts, when appropriate as negative alternatives, or prior
knowledge of the scene could inform the reference vocabulary and provide more relevant com-
petitors for the current target. Growing the reference vocabulary over time as a user queries the
scene, especially when applied in similar environments, is an interesting future direction. Further,
all tested scenes are generally in an indoor environment and evaluation in outdoor environments or
substantially different scenes remains untested.

EVALUATION POPULATION AND CORRESPONDENCE PROTOCOLS
Gaussian-based semantic reconstructions differ both in their geometric parameterization and in the
rules used to transfer primitive predictions to annotated points. For example, the official OpenGaus-
sian ScanNet configuration (Wu et al., 2024) fixes the initial point locations and disables densifica-
tion, permitting evaluation on aligned point rows. Its evaluator also ignores GT labels at rows whose
Gaussian opacity is below 0.1, which can be a substantial amount in some cases. Dr. Splat (Kim
et al., 2025) instead supports unconstrained Gaussian geometry and describes a Gaussian-level eval-
uation with Mahalanobis-based pseudo-label assignment and significance weighting.
Some prior evaluation protocols discard GT points when their associated predictions are removed
by opacity or feature-validity filtering. This makes the evaluated population method-dependent and
in many cases drop a substantial amount of points (see Table 8). Our evaluation instead retains every
active GT point and assigns it to an eligible Gaussian using Mahalanobis distance before evaluating
the semantic prediction. Let Em contain the exported Gaussians of method m with opacity at least
0.1 and a valid feature under the method’s existing feature-validity rule. For every active GT point

LangSplat field adaptation.
For LangSplat (Qin et al., 2024), we use the decoded 512-

original rendered-image evaluator forms relevance maps at three levels, applies spatial smoothing,
and selects a level for each query and image using the highest peak relevance. We do not reproduce
that query-dependent level selection or spatial-smoothing stage as it interferes with out calibrations.
The fixed field provides one representation for multiclass refinement and single-query threshold-
ing without introducing an additional level-selection rule. The reported LangSplat results therefore
evaluate this fixed-field adaptation rather than the complete original rendered-image pipeline.
