Abstract
Synthesizing a monocular video from a new camera trajectory requires preserving
the observed scene geometry and motion while completing regions that become
newly visible. Feed-forward 4D reconstructors recover the structure of the observed
scene, whereas video diffusion models provide a generative prior for completing
unseen content. We introduce HOLO4D, a video reshooting framework that uses
reconstructed 3D trajectories as a holistic source-view 4D scaffold. We project
these trajectories into the requested views and encode their geometry and source
appearance to guide a pretrained video diffusion model. Together with the source
video and target cameras, the scaffold grounds generation in reconstructed obser-
vations while leaving unsupported regions to the generative prior. Experiments
demonstrate the value of trajectory-based conditioning for camera control and
novel-view synthesis. The framework accommodates different 4D reconstructors
without retraining the video generator, allowing it to benefit from future advances
in source reconstruction. More details can be found in our project page.

Introduction
Rendering a monocular video from a new camera trajectory would allow us to change the viewpoint
of a recorded scene without changing the events it depicts [1]. For example, a camera moving around
a walking person should reveal a different view of the same motion, rather than generate a different
action or alter the surrounding scene. This requires both faithful reconstruction of observed content
and plausible completion of surfaces that become visible only from the new viewpoint. The task
therefore goes beyond recovering the geometry of the input frames: it must turn partial observations
of a dynamic scene into a coherent video along a user-specified camera path.
Recent feed-forward reconstruction models provide an increasingly useful starting point. They
estimate cameras, depth, and point maps directly from images and videos [2–5], while 4D recon-
struction systems also recover the trajectories of scene points over time [6–9]. Together, these
quantities describe the observed scene in a common spatial and temporal frame: camera motion can
be separated from scene motion, and observations of a moving point can be linked across frames.
However, reconstructing the observed scene does not by itself solve novel-view synthesis. Repro-
jecting its points into a new camera leaves holes where surfaces have never been observed [10],
and inaccurate geometry can produce distortions as the viewpoint changes [11, 12]. The missing
appearance must be inferred, rather than obtained by reprojection alone.
Video diffusion models offer the complementary ability to generate plausible appearance and motion
from learned video priors [13]. Camera-controlled variants use these priors to synthesize new
views, conditioned on camera embeddings or rendered source geometry [1, 14, 12]. Yet a realistic
video need not preserve the geometry and dynamics of the particular scene being reconstructed.
Combining reconstruction with generation is therefore not simply a matter of requesting a new
camera pose: the generator also needs a representation of the scene that the camera is viewing.
This motivates our central question: how should the geometry, appearance, and motion recovered
from a source video be exposed to a video diffusion model?
An important part of this question is how the condition represents a scene over time. Lifting each
frame independently with depth yields a sequence of point clouds, but does not explicitly specify
which points correspond to the same moving surface in different frames. Vista4D [11] makes
static scene content persistent by identifying static pixels and accumulating their observations in a
common world frame. However, moving surfaces cannot be accumulated in the same way without
accounting for their motion. Our starting point is that persistent 3D trajectories provide the required
correspondence explicitly [6–8]. Specifically, a trajectory follows one scene point through time and
associates its position with its observed appearance. A static point is the special case of a constant
world-space trajectory, so static and moving points can use the same conditioning encoder.
We introduce HOLO4D, a video reshooting framework that converts reconstructed 3D trajectories
and their source appearance into geometric features for video diffusion (Figure 2). We call this
scaffold holistic because it represents observed geometry, appearance, and motion jointly for static
and dynamic points. Projecting these trajectories into the target cameras specifies where observed
scene content should appear and how it should move. We use these projected trajectories to guide
the video diffusion model, alongside source-video conditioning [15] and the requested camera
trajectory [16]. The tracks guide observed content throughout denoising, while the video prior
supplies plausible appearance in newly revealed regions.
This conditioning design has two useful properties. First, we inject track features along the channel di-
mension [17], providing geometric guidance without an additional rendered-video token stream [11].
Second, the scaffold uses explicit geometric quantities rather than reconstructor-specific features.
HOLO4D can therefore use any 4D reconstructor that provides the required geometry and trajecto-
ries [6, 8, 5, 18]. Our experiments show that replacing the upstream reconstruction can substantially
improve camera adherence without retraining the video generator.
In summary, our contributions are threefold: (1) a holistic geometric scaffold that represents static
and dynamic scene observations through persistent 3D trajectories with source appearance; (2) a
conditioning design that combines source-video context with channel-wise injection of projected
3D trajectory features to guide novel-view generation; and (3) camera-adherence and held-out-
view evaluations against strong baselines, analyses of reconstructor substitutions and trajectory
perturbations, and demonstrations on robot videos.

Related Work
3D and 4D reconstruction. Dynamic scenes can be reconstructed by fitting deformable radiance
fields [19–22] or time-varying Gaussian representations [23–25]. ViDAR [26] additionally uses a
personalized diffusion prior to supervise per-scene Gaussian reconstruction. We instead condition
a shared video generator on reconstructed geometry, without per-scene optimization.
Learned models recover point maps, cameras, and depth from images [27, 2, 4, 28, 29, 5] or
accumulate observations over video [30, 3]. Video depth and dynamic-scene estimators provide
geometry from moving-camera videos [31–37]. Recent 4D methods estimate dynamic point maps,
motion, or persistent trajectories [38, 39, 8, 40, 41, 6, 7, 9], while 4DGT [42] predicts 4D Gaussians
from posed videos. We use compatible geometric outputs as a scaffold for video reshooting.
Geometry-guided view synthesis. Geometry-guided rendering aggregates source-view information
to synthesize novel views [43–46]. Ha et al. [46] also use temporal guidance and inpainting for
multiview video. Our setting uses reconstructed 3D trajectories to condition video diffusion for
reshooting a monocular video under large viewpoint changes.
Camera-controlled video generation. Camera-conditioned diffusion models encode target views
through poses or rays [47–53], and have been adapted to video reshooting [1, 54]. Explicit con-
ditions instead ground generation in rendered source geometry [14, 12, 55] or warped observa-
tions [56]. Related methods generate multiview videos before Gaussian reconstruction [57], or
render Gaussian features for sparse-view synthesis [44].
Vista4D [11] conditions on source video and rendered point clouds with temporally persistent static
pixels. LaVR [58] uses latent reconstruction states, whereas our interface is defined by explicit
geometric quantities rather than reconstructor-specific hidden features. Go-with-the-Track [17]
conditions generation on reference images and reference-anchored 2D tracks for compositing and
motion control, and also supports camera retargeting. Edit-by-Track [59] edits camera and object
motion in a video by conditioning on paired source and target 3D point tracks. For video reshooting,
HOLO4D encodes 3D trajectory positions, source color, validity, and target-camera depth into
geometric features, alongside the synchronized source video.
Diffusion models for geometry. Diffusion priors also support geometry prediction, from monocular
depth [60, 61] to video geometry and motion [37, 15, 9]. Gen3R [62] jointly generates appearance
and geometric latents. VIST3A [63] couples a video generator to a reconstruction decoder, while
Plan4D [64] further generates plannable 4D worlds by stitching video generators and reconstruction
decoders. HOLO4D instead takes reconstructed geometry as input and generates target-view RGB.

Video models for robots. Video-generation backbones increasingly serve robot learning, e.g.,
as world action models [65]; HOLO4D instead reshoots recorded robot videos from new view-
points (Section 4.5).

Source and target frames with the same index refer to the same scene time. In Figure 2, the top
branch reconstructs persistent 3D trajectories from the source video, forming a 4D scaffold, projects

3.1
Constructing a 4D scaffold
Reshooting a dynamic scene requires specifying where observed content should appear in each target
frame and how its position changes over time. A video diffusion model conditioned only on the
source video and target cameras [1] must infer this layout implicitly from appearance. Without an
explicit scene representation, it struggles to preserve source-scene geometry and follow the requested
camera under large viewpoint changes [58, 11]. To provide explicit geometric guidance, existing
methods lift source frames into point clouds using estimated depth and render them from the target
cameras [14, 12]. However, even when static points are accumulated across frames [11], indepen-
dently reconstructed dynamic points do not explicitly establish correspondence between moving
surfaces across frames. We therefore represent the source scene by persistent 3D trajectories [66],
each carrying the appearance of a source observation.
To construct this scaffold, we use a 4D reconstructor R to recover camera poses and point trajectories
from the source video [6–8, 5, 18]. Each trajectory follows a scene point n through its positions pn,j
at successive frames j and carries a color cn, sampled from the source frame qn where the point was
queried. We express these positions and the target cameras in the coordinate frame of the first source
camera [27, 8], where static points have constant positions and moving points trace out their motion.
Any reconstructor providing these trajectories and cameras can supply the scaffold.

Finally, we convert the projected observations into a feature volume aligned with the target video.
A sinusoidal encoding [67] followed by a shared MLP maps the attributes of each observation
to a feature vector, which is scattered to its projected location on a target-view grid. Features of
observations that fall into the same cell are combined by a channel-wise maximum, and an occupancy
channel records which cells received any observation. A causal 3D convolutional adapter then brings
this volume to the DiT token grid, producing the track features T; target tokens with no projected
observations receive a learned null embedding. For a fixed target-video resolution and duration,
the resulting feature volume has a size independent of the number of trajectories. Normalization,
masking, and dataset-specific depth handling are given in Appendix B.

camera features, and the track features T. The VAE encodes the source video into latents xsrc,

The track features enter the transformer along the channel dimension, following Go-with-the-
Track [17], rather than as an additional sequence of tokens. We place them on the target half
of the sequence and extend T on the source half with the same learned null embedding used for
unoccupied target tokens. Channel-wise injection leaves the self-attention sequence length unchanged,
and our model achieves a 1.34× denoising speedup over Vista4D (Table 24).
The projected trajectories already convey the target viewpoint through their image locations. To
provide auxiliary camera information in regions without valid projected observations, we also encode
the target cameras as Plücker rays [16] into camera features C. Source cameras establish the common
reconstruction frame and are not separately embedded in the generator.

3.4
Training details and datasets
We initialize from Wan2.1-T2V-14B [70] and train on 49-frame videos for 15k steps at 144×256,
followed by 4.5k steps at 384×672. AdamW [71] uses learning rates 1×10−5 and 2×10−5 and
effective batch sizes 8 and 32 for the two stages, respectively. We train conditioning modules and
DiT self-attention, keeping pretrained cross-attention and feed-forward layers frozen [72].
Datasets. We train on a mixture of synthetic multiview videos of dynamic scenes [73, 74, 1], real
videos of static scenes [75, 76], and synthetic static-scene sequences [77, 78]. For dynamic scenes,
source–target pairs come from synchronized multiview videos. For static scenes, we form source–
target pairs from different chunks of the same monocular video, similar to 4Real-Video-V2 [79];
temporal synchronization is unnecessary since the scene is static. This mixture covers both scene
motion and camera motion, with estimated or ground-truth 3D trajectory annotations; dataset names,
sampling weights, and annotation sources are given in Appendix C.
To expose the generator to imperfect and incomplete conditions, the second training stage varies
trajectory density and query-frame sampling and applies mild geometric perturbations. Module
dimensions and augmentation settings are specified in Appendix B.

Experiments
We evaluate camera control and novel-view synthesis (Sections 4.2 and 4.3), analyze geometric
conditioning (Section 4.4), and demonstrate robot-video applications (Section 4.5).
4.1
Experimental setup
Baselines. We compare HOLO4D with several representative state-of-the-art methods, including
ReCamMaster [1], TrajectoryCrafter [14], EX-4D [55], Gen3C [12], and the concurrent Vista4D [11].
ReCamMaster is a camera-only baseline: it conditions on the source video and the target cameras.
The others are rendered-geometry baselines: they also render source geometry from target cameras;
Vista4D keeps static geometry persistent across frames.
Evaluation datasets. For camera control, we select 50 DAVIS [80] clips and randomly sample two
target camera trajectories per clip, and also evaluate on Vista4D’s released 110-trajectory benchmark.

4.5
Robot videos
Zero-shot reshooting. HOLO4D-14B is trained without robot videos, yet reshoots DROID manipu-
lation episodes and humanoid-robot clips without fine-tuning (Figures 5 and 17).
Long-video rollout. We generate overlapping 49-frame windows: the first with HOLO4D-14B, then
with an image-to-video continuation model conditioned on the last generated frame (Appendix B).
The continuation model trains on OmniWorld-DROID, so later windows are not zero-shot. Figure 6
qualitatively shows consistent appearance across two window boundaries in a 196-frame episode.
Transfer to robot policy learning. We initialize Fast-WAM’s [65] 30 video self-attention blocks
from a 5B HOLO4D variant (Appendix L) and train on LIBERO [86] alongside a matched Wan2.2-
initialized control (Table 7). Results are mixed: LIBERO has few tasks and is near saturation (95.60%
vs. 96.45%), leaving little room to distinguish the two. On LIBERO-plus [87], HOLO4D gains 1.29
percentage points overall, mainly under background-texture (+7.1) and lighting (+3.9) perturbations,
but loses on the spatial suite. Camera-viewpoint perturbations remain hard for all checkpoints (single
seed). Whether novel-view training helps embodied policies remains open and merits further study.
5
Conclusion
HOLO4D guides video diffusion with persistent 3D trajectories that carry source appearance, leaving
regions beyond the scaffold to the generative prior. It achieves the lowest trajectory and translation
errors on DAVIS and the best PSNR and LPIPS on iPhone with the two-stage backend, and works
with different reconstructors without retraining; it remains sensitive to geometric error, incomplete
source coverage, and difficult camera trajectories.
Future work. Since video generators are general-purpose vision learners [88], one model could
jointly perform 4D reconstruction and novel-view synthesis so that both improve together. Querying
trajectories in every source frame, not just the first (e.g., UniQueR [89]), would cover later-appearing
content; stronger base models such as MiniMax H3 [90] should improve visual quality.

Submitted conditioning. The submitted paper described four conditioning signals: the source video,
a hybrid RGB-D cache, target-camera Plücker rays, and 3D-track tokens. A 3D-motion threshold
separated static and dynamic pixels. Static pixels were fused from K = 7 keyframes selected by
farthest-point sampling over camera positions and viewing directions; dynamic pixels came from
the synchronized source frame. The described design merged their renders by z-buffer selection
into target-view RGB, normalized depth, and validity. Track tokens encoded 3D trajectory positions
and were fused with the RGB-D tokens. The actual submitted 14B DAVIS and iPhone results
used multiframe softsplat RGB-D conditioning; the 1.3B DAVIS results used single-frame z-buffer
conditioning. Both included 3D tracks.
Final conditioning. This version removes the rendered RGB-D branch, which supplied target-view
RGB, depth, and validity, and keeps only the 3D-track branch, together with the source video and
target-camera rays (Section 3, Figure 2 versus Figure 7). Observations encode source color and
target-camera depth (Equation (3)); validity combines upstream validity with in-image projection,
without the occlusion test of z-buffer visibility. Since a static point is a trajectory with a constant
position, the static–dynamic threshold and keyframe selection are no longer needed. In a controlled
stage-1 comparison, adding rendered RGB and masks to the trajectory features did not improve
camera control (Table 4); Tables 15 to 17 report further comparisons with rendered conditioning from
earlier checkpoints. Track features enter every DiT block along channels and add no attention tokens
(Appendix J).
Model and training. The submission reported a 14B model built on Wan2.1-I2V-14B and a 1.3B
model built on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, both trained at 144 × 256 and 288 × 512. The main results in
this version use a 14B model initialized from Wan2.1-T2V-14B and trained at 144 × 256 and then
384 × 672, with a revised data mixture (Appendices B and C). Early 1.3B checkpoints appear only
in the conditioning study of Table 15, not as main results. The submission also probed target-view
depth with a separately trained head on intermediate VAE decoder features; this version omits that
probe and evaluates depth reconstructed from generated RGB videos (Table 20).
Evaluation. Cameras of generated videos are now recovered with VGGT-Ωinstead of ViPE (Ap-
pendix D). Video quality on DAVIS is measured with VBench; FVD is reported on Syn4D, where

The frame index is normalized as ¯tj = j/ max(N −1, 1). Tildes denote normalized position and

A sinusoidal encoding and MLP map observations to 32-dimensional features. Target-view projection
determines where to scatter these features, with channel-wise maximum aggregation and an occupancy
indicator. Temporal patchification repeats the first frame to respect the VAE’s causal layout, then
applies a 3D convolution with kernel and stride (4, p, p), where p is the spatial downsampling factor,
followed by one causal residual block and projection to 156 channels. The resulting features match
the DiT token grid. Empty tokens use a learned, zero-initialized embedding. The source half of the
width-concatenated latent sequence receives null track tokens.
Camera conditioning. One shared camera encoder produces the target-camera features used in every
DiT block. These features are repeated along width to match both source and target tokens. This
repetition does not introduce source-camera rays; explicit source-camera rays would be compatible
with this design but are untested. Each block has its own track concat–projection and camera output
projector. Camera rays use the Plücker convention [o × d, d]. Pixel unshuffle and a 2D convolution
patchify the ray map; a zero-initialized pointwise 3D convolution maps its six channels to the DiT
width. Within each block, track concatenation and projection follow normalization and timestep
modulation. The camera feature is then added before self-attention, whose output passes through
an identity-initialized camera projector and the gated residual connection. Cross-attention and the
feed-forward sublayer follow.
Query-frame indicator. A query-frame indicator identifies the source frames used to initialize
trajectories. A binary sequence is one at the selected query frame and zero elsewhere; each value is
broadcast over the image and repeated across three channels for encoding with the video VAE. The
resulting control latents occupy the source half of the width-concatenated input, with zeros on the
target half. A separate, zero-initialized Conv3D patch embedding maps these control latents to the
hidden width, and its output is added to the video patch embeddings before the DiT blocks. This
input addition uses neither the per-block track concat–projection nor a concatenation with the RGB
latent channels.
Training. Stage 1 uses 49 frames at 144 × 256 for 15,000 steps, with effective batch size 8 and
learning rate 1×10−5. Stage 2 continues the same checkpoint to step 19,500 at 384 × 672, with
effective batch size 32 and learning rate 2 × 10−5 on 32 GPUs using FSDP [91]. Both stages run
on NVIDIA GH200 GPUs for about 24 hours each. Both stages optimize RGB flow matching. No
geometric-conditioning dropout is used; text-conditioning dropout is 0.1.
Long-video rollout. We extend generation beyond the 49-frame training window by following
Vista4D’s window-by-window inference [11]. (i) Split the source into 49-frame windows with
one-frame overlap: k windows cover 49 + 48(k −1) frames. Pad the last window by repeating
its final source frame. (ii) Reconstruct each window independently using the backends specified
below, querying trajectories at its first frame; identities are not shared across windows. Register each
reconstruction to the first window through the shared boundary frame, holding its source camera
fixed while fitting a scale to the shared point map. Use the corresponding segment of the requested
target-camera path. (iii) Generate the first window with HOLO4D-14B (step 19,500), conditioned
on the source video, tracks, and cameras. (iv) Generate later windows with an image-to-video
continuation model using the same conditions plus the previous window’s last generated frame. This
shared boundary image is never a source or ground-truth frame. Remove duplicate boundary frames
and padding when stitching.
The continuation model uses a Wan2.1-I2V-14B [70] backbone and the stage-1 HOLO4D-14B track
and camera modules. Its image condition enters through the first-frame VAE latent and CLIP image
feature. During training, this is the first ground-truth target frame, dropped with probability 0.3 and
noised at level 0.05. Training proceeds at 144×256 from step 15,000 to 19,000, then at 384×672 to
step 22,500, with effective batch size 32 and learning rate 2 × 10−5. The mixture is MultiCamVideo
(22.5%), Syn4D (30%), DL3DV (15%), MVS-Synth (7.5%), and OmniWorld-DROID [92, 85]
(25%); unlike HOLO4D-14B, this model has seen robot videos. Window 1 uses 4RC for DROID
and the two-stage backend for humanoid clips; later windows use 4RC without GMOS. Each 4RC
reconstruction uses all 49 source frames. All windows use 50 denoising steps, CFG 5, shift 5, and
seed 42 (Figures 6 and 18).
Condition augmentation. Stage 2 perturbs geometric conditions with probability 0.25. The con-
figured track-offset and temporal-jitter standard deviations are 0.005 and 0.002; image-coordinate
noise ratio is 0.10. Frame-depth jitter, depth offset, and depth-scale perturbation use 0.20, 0.002, and
0.003, respectively. RGB perturbations use 0.003/0.006, and mask augmentation probability is 0.10.

Camera Adherence on Vista4D Trajectories
We additionally evaluate on the 110 native Vista4D trajectories with both generated-only VGGT-Ω
recovery and Vista4D’s source-aligned Pi3 protocol (Table 6). Vista4D uses the benchmark’s released
Pi3 reconstruction, masks, and rendered inputs, whereas HOLO4D uses 4RC or the two-stage backend.
This preserves Vista4D’s native preprocessing and compares complete pipelines; it does not isolate
the video generator as the source of performance differences. For the qualitative comparisons in
Figures 3 and 16, baselines use their released sampling settings and the benchmark’s released Pi3
geometry where a point cloud is required; Vista4D videos are its official outputs. The VGGT-Ωand
Pi3 evaluators recover cameras for scoring and are separate from the source reconstruction used
for generation. With 4RC source geometry, HOLO4D has lower RPE-T under VGGT-Ωand lower
translation error under Pi3, but higher rotation error under both evaluators. VGGT-Ωevaluation
follows the normalization in Appendix D. ATE and RPE-T have 110 valid values for HOLO4D
with 4RC and 109 each for Vista4D and the two-stage condition; RPE-R uses all 110. These are
per-method valid-metric counts, not a common-subset evaluation. Scale normalization requires
non-degenerate requested and recovered camera paths; a completed video can therefore lack a valid
translation metric. Vista4D’s Pi3 protocol reconstructs source and generated frames jointly, then
aligns the source cameras by Sim(3).
The 4RC condition in Table 6 uses its reconstructed trajectories without mask-guided fusion and
aligns the geometry to the released camera coordinate system with a per-video scalar depth factor.
An alternative pointmap Sim(3) alignment of the same checkpoint and 4RC geometry reduces
VGGT-ΩRPE-R from 1.142◦to 0.348◦, but increases Pi3 RotErr from 14.98◦to 17.19◦. Thus the
source-condition comparison includes alignment effects, and the two evaluators disagree on this
preprocessing change.

only on Syn4D against ground-truth target videos. FVD remains a distribution-level video-quality
metric; camera adherence is evaluated separately using recovered camera trajectories. We assess
video quality with VBench (Table 2) and target-referenced FVD; CLIP-based scores are not reported.

Conditioning Cost, Runtime, and Memory
Source geometry and query-frame appearance are reusable across target trajectories. Target projection,
depth, validity, scattering, and camera features must be recomputed. Training uses offline geometry
annotations but still evaluates the learned adapters.
Channel-wise geometric conditioning. For hidden features h ∈RL×D and track features T ∈
RL×156, each injector projects [h; T] ∈RL×(D+156) back to RL×D. Self-attention therefore keeps
the same sequence length, avoiding Vista4D’s additional rendered-video tokens [11]. Source-video
tokens, adapters, and per-block projections still incur cost; fewer tokens do not imply a proportional
reduction in total memory.

Remaining failure modes. Fine dynamic structures such as hands can remain inconsistent across

Robot Policy Learning with Fast-WAM
Fast-WAM [65] couples a Wan2.2-TI2V-5B video expert with an action expert. Both arms share the
Wan2.2-TI2V-5B weights, VAE, action-expert initialization, and cached text features. The HOLO4D
arm replaces 30 video self-attention blocks (300 tensors) with those from a 5B image-to-video variant
at step 29,500, warm-started from a video-to-video HOLO4D-5B model. This is distinct from the
step-29,000 no-identity variant in Table 12; its pretraining mixture includes OmniWorld-DROID
robot videos. Track, camera, and domain modules are discarded because Fast-WAM lacks them. Both
arms train the full model, including the transferred blocks, for 20,000 steps on the four LIBERO [86]
suites with identical data order, normalization, seed, and optimization settings. The official release is a
separate reference. All checkpoints are evaluated with the Fast-WAM closed-loop evaluator: LIBERO
uses 10 tasks per suite and 50 trials per task (2,000 rollouts per checkpoint); LIBERO-plus [87] uses
all 10,030 perturbed tasks with one trial each. Per-suite results are in Table 25 and are discussed in
Section 4.5. On LIBERO-plus, HOLO4D improves the goal suite (+6.2) but drops 4.8 points on the
spatial suite. All runs use a single seed.

Broader Impacts
Controllable novel-view video synthesis can support content creation, immersive media, simulation,
and the construction of multi-view robot data. It can also fabricate plausible but incorrect views
of real events. Source-video and geometric conditioning constrain generation but do not guarantee
factual fidelity, especially in disoccluded regions. Track validity is an observation-support signal, not
a forensic test. Deployment should distinguish generated views from recorded footage and respect
the base model’s license and safety requirements.

NeurIPS Paper Checklist
1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The abstract and the contribution list at the end of Section 1 claim a holistic
scaffold of persistent 3D trajectories that carry source appearance, channel-wise conditioning
of a pretrained video diffusion model on these trajectories, and evaluations against reshooting
baselines, reconstructor substitutions, trajectory perturbations, and robot videos. These
claims are supported by Tables 1 to 6 and Section 4.5; the policy-learning result is reported
as mixed (Table 7).
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: Section 5 states that HOLO4D remains sensitive to geometric error, incomplete
source coverage, and difficult camera trajectories. Tables 5 and 21 quantify the sensitivity to
trajectory noise; Appendix K describes remaining failure modes (fine dynamic structures,
large disoccluded regions) and missing coverage for content that enters after the query frame
(Figure 19); Section 4.5 states that the long-video continuation model is not zero-shot and
that the policy-learning results are mixed and single-seed.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.

7. Experiment statistical significance
Question: Does the paper report error bars suitably and correctly defined or other appropriate
information about the statistical significance of the experiments?
Answer: [No]
Justification: The main video-generation comparisons report point estimates, without
repeated-training confidence intervals. Evaluation uses fixed benchmark pairs; valid camera-
metric counts can differ by method (Appendix E). The early 1.3B conditioning study reports
a confidence interval over pairs, runtime profiling reports variation across videos (Table 22),
and policy learning uses a single seed (Appendix L). These do not quantify uncertainty for
the main results.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper does not include experiments.
• The authors should answer [Yes] if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence
intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the
main claims of the paper.
• The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for
example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall
run with given experimental conditions).
• The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula,
call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)
• The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).
• It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error
of the mean.
• It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should
preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis
of Normality of errors is not verified.
• For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or
figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g., negative
error rates).
• If error bars are reported in tables or plots, the authors should explain in the text how
they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.
