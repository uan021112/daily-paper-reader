# PanoBench: A Panoptic Occupancy Prediction Benchmark for Autonomous Driving 

Yi Feng * , Zizhan Guo * , Junwu E , Yu Ma , Mengtan Zhang , Jin Wu , Member, IEEE ,Wei Ye , Member, IEEE , Hanli Wang , Senior Member, IEEE , Qijun Chen , Senior Member, IEEE ,Danwei Wang , Life Fellow, IEEE , and Rui Fan † , Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Panoptic occupancy provides a unified represen-tation of 3D scene geometry, semantic categories, and object identities, but its development is constrained by the scarcity of large-scale instance-aware occupancy annotations. Existing benchmarks predominantly provide semantic occupancy labels reconstructed from incomplete sensor observations, while avail-able panoptic ones are limited in domain coverage or an-notation completeness. This article introduces PanoBench, acamera-centric panoptic occupancy prediction benchmark with multi-resolution, high-fidelity, and temporally consistent panop-tic annotations. PanoBench unifies diverse data sources from multiple real-world datasets (e.g. , nuScenes, KITTI-360) and virtual driving simulators ( e.g. , CARLA), providing canonical data interfaces and unified evaluation protocols. For the synthetic branch, PanoBench-CarlaOcc derives complete panoptic geom-etry directly from simulated meshes and scene layouts. For the real-world branch, a new panoptic occupancy generation pipeline is proposed to construct PanoBench-nuScenes and PanoBench-KITTI360. The pipeline reconstructs background point clouds with geometric priors and topology constraints, generates fore-ground mesh assets from multi-frame multi-view images, and fuses both components into a sparse voxel-based panoptic rep-resentation. To ensure reasonable evaluation of existing models, a unified valid-mask protocol is developed to evaluate complete thing instances once they have been observed, while scoring stuff regions conservatively at the voxel level. Furthermore, extensive analyses of observation fidelity, geometric completeness, spatial continuity, and temporal consistency demonstrate the superi-ority of PanoBench over existing public occupancy prediction benchmarks. Finally, this study provides fair and reproducible comparisons of representative baselines on panoptic occupancy prediction, semantic occupancy prediction, and 4D occupancy forecasting, facilitating further advancements in autonomous driving perception systems. The proposed benchmark is available at https://mias.group/PanoBench. 

Index Terms —Panoptic occupancy prediction, autonomous driving, 3D scene understanding. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

3D scene understanding is a fundamental capability for autonomous driving systems, as it supports spatial reason-ing about free space, occupied regions, semantic categories, and surrounding traffic participants [1]–[3]. Compared with object-centric perception tasks such as 3D object detection and tracking [4], [5], occupancy prediction provides a more complete description of the driving scene by representing the environment as a structured 3D field [6], [7]. Recent semantic occupancy prediction benchmarks and methods [6]– [13] have substantially advanced this field by requiring models

These efforts have made occupancy prediction an important formulation for vision-centric au-tonomous driving perception. However, semantic occupancy alone is insufficient for holis-tic 3D scene understanding [3], [14]–[16]. It predicts voxel occupancy and semantic category, but does not distinguish different object instances within the same semantic class [3], [14], [17]. As a result, semantic occupancy prediction bench-marks cannot directly evaluate whether a model separates ad-jacent vehicles, preserves complete object shapes, or maintains instance-level consistency across temporal observations [14]– [16], [18]. This limitation becomes critical for camera-centric perception, where scene elements might be partially observed and must be inferred from multi-view and temporal cues [1], [19]. Panoptic occupancy prediction bridges this gap by as-signing each occupied voxel both a semantic category and an instance identity, thereby extending panoptic segmentation from image regions to dense 3D space [3], [14]–[17]. The main bottleneck for panoptic occupancy prediction lies in benchmark construction. Existing real-world occu-pancy datasets [6]–[8], [10], [11], [20] are mainly designed for semantic occupancy prediction, and their annotations are generally reconstructed from sparse, noisy, and visibility-limited sensor observations. Such annotations are difficult to extend to reliable instance-level 3D supervision, especially for complete foreground objects and large background regions under occlusions. Synthetic environments can provide more complete geometry through mesh-level scene access [18], [21], but a synthetic-only benchmark cannot fully characterize the appearance diversity, sensor characteristics, and annotation challenges of real-world autonomous driving data. Therefore, a comprehensive benchmark should combine high-fidelity synthetic annotations with reconstructed real-world panoptic occupancy annotations under a unified task definition and evaluation protocol. This article extends our previous work [18] from a synthetic panoptic occupancy benchmark to a unified synthetic and real-world benchmark for camera-centric panoptic occupancy prediction. Our previous study introduced CarlaOcc, a large-scale synthetic dataset with high-fidelity panoptic occupancy annotations generated from simulated meshes. This article systematizes it as PanoBench-CarlaOcc and further constructs two real-world panoptic occupancy datasets, PanoBench-nuScenes and PanoBench-KITTI360, based on nuScenes [22] and KITTI-360 [23]. By decoupling static background recovery from foreground instance completion, this design reduces reliance on direct sensor accumulation for dynamic objects and provides more effective supervision for occluded or partially observed traffic participants. Beyond dataset construction, PanoBench establishes a uni-fied camera-centric evaluation protocol. It uses temporally aware valid masks to retain complete thing instances supported by historical observations while conservatively evaluating stuff geometry within the observable camera space. This protocol makes the benchmark sensitive to whether a model can exploit temporal evidence to preserve object completeness and iden-tity, rather than only recovering surfaces visible at the target frame. As a result, PanoBench supports a more comprehensive study of instance separation, shape completion, occlusion-aware reconstruction, temporal consistency, and domain ro-bustness. A. Semantic Occupancy Prediction Benchmarks 

The semantic occupancy prediction task aims to recover occupied geometry and voxel-wise semantics from incomplete sensor observations. SSCNet [24] pioneers end-to-end seman-tic scene completion by predicting dense voxel occupancy and semantics from a single depth observation. SemanticKITTI [8] establishes an early outdoor benchmark by registering sequen-tial LiDAR scans into a common coordinate frame. However, its limited scene diversity and forward-facing setup make it difficult to assess the surround-view, camera-centric per-ception required by contemporary autonomous driving sys-tems. MonoScene [25] introduces monocular camera-based semantic scene completion, motivating subsequent vision-centric occupancy methods. Later benchmarks, such as Sur-roundOcc [10], Occ3D [6], and OpenOccupancy [7], extend occupancy supervision from forward scene completion to surrounding perception. By combining techniques such as Poisson surface reconstruction [26], occlusion reasoning, and manual label refinement, these datasets provide denser and more detailed occupancy annotations for complex driving scenes. SSCBench [11] further aligns labels and evaluation settings across various real-world datasets such as KITTI-360 [23], nuScenes [22], and Waymo [27], enabling con-trollable comparisons across sensor configurations, geographic regions, and data domains. Despite these advances, real-world occupancy annotations inevitably suffer from incomplete geometries due to the spar-sity of LiDAR point clouds and pervasive occlusions. To mit-igate this limitation, several works [28], [29] leverage virtual engines to generate high-quality simulated data. Our prior work, CarlaOcc [18], uses Unreal Engine 5 [30] to construct occupancy ground truth directly from virtual mesh assets, yielding high-fidelity, resolution-agnostic, and physically con-sistent scene representations. Nevertheless, simulation-based benchmarks still face a substantial domain gap from real-world driving environments. PanoBench addresses this limitation by bridging high-quality simulation-based occupancy annotations with more realistic and diverse driving scenarios, providing a more reliable benchmark for evaluating semantic occupancy prediction under practical domain shifts. 

B. Vision-Based Occupancy Prediction 

Vision-based occupancy prediction estimates dense 3D ge-ometry and semantics from camera observations. A com-mon line of work lifts image features into structured 3D representations. LSS [31] establishes the forward-projection paradigm that lifts multi-view image features and splats them into a bird’s-eye-view (BEV) grid, and subsequent detectors such as BEVDepth [32] further improve depth-aware view transformation. TPVFormer [9] augments the BEV represen-tation by factorizing the volume into three orthogonal feature planes, while VoxFormer [33] starts from sparse visible voxel proposals and densifies them for monocular semantic scene completion. OccFormer [34] decomposes heavy 3D processing into efficient Transformer pathways, while SurroundOcc [10] progressively upsamples multi-scale 3D volume features for multi-camera occupancy prediction. FB-OCC [35] couples forward and backward view transformations for denser 3D features, while FlashOcc [36] replaces costly 3D convolu-tions with a channel-to-height plugin for efficient occupancy decoding. Another line of work reduces the cost of dense voxel prediction by exploiting scene sparsity and adaptive rep-resentations. GaussianFormer [37] represents driving scenes with sparse semantic Gaussians, and GaussianFormer-2 [38] improves this formulation with probabilistic Gaussian su-perposition. SparseOcc [15] predicts semantic and instance occupancy from a fully sparse 3D representation and intro-duces RayIoU to reduce the depth-axis ambiguity of voxel-level mIoU. OPUS [39] further formulates occupancy as a sparse set prediction problem, directly predicting occupied locations and classes with learnable queries. These meth-ods improve computational efficiency and the compactness of 3D occupancy representations. Beyond fully supervised training, complementary self-supervised approaches based on neural radiance fields [40]–[43] further reduce reliance on dense occupancy annotations. In addition to current-frame prediction, 4D occupancy forecasting estimates future occu-pancy from past observations [44]. Cam4DOcc [19] estab-lishes a camera-only forecasting benchmark, OccWorld [45] and Drive-OccWorld [46] learn occupancy world models for joint forecasting and planning, and SparseWorld [47] and SparseWorld-TC [48] build sparse occupancy world models, the latter being conditioned on the ego trajectory. However, existing supervision and evaluation protocols remain dom-inated by semantic occupancy [20], [49], leaving panoptic instance quality and cross-dataset panoptic generalization un-derexplored. PanoBench is designed to support these compar-isons through unified semantic and instance-aware annotations across synthetic and real-world domains. 

C. Panoptic Occupancy Prediction 

Panoptic segmentation unifies semantic segmentation and instance segmentation by assigning each pixel both a category and, for thing classes, an instance identity [17]. Extending this idea to 3D scene understanding is important because autonomous driving systems must reason about both stuff regions and individual traffic participants. Early studies [50]– [53] investigate LiDAR-based panoptic perception. PaSCo [14] introduces panoptic scene completion, extending semantic scene completion with instance-aware 3D prediction and un-certainty estimation. PanoOcc [3] formulates camera-based 3D panoptic segmentation in a unified occupancy representation and aggregates multi-frame multi-view information through 4

voxel queries. SparseOcc [15] and Panoptic-FlashOcc [16] fur-ther show that panoptic occupancy can be approached through sparse queries or efficient instance-center prediction. Recent studies [54], [55] further investigate 4D panoptic occupancy tracking, covering both camera-based methods and surround-view fisheye benchmarks. These works establish important baselines for instance-aware occupancy prediction, but public panoptic occupancy datasets remain scarce. Our previous work CarlaOcc [18] addresses this issue in the synthetic domain by generating high-fidelity panoptic occupancy annotations from simulated meshes. However, a synthetic-only benchmark cannot fully reflect real-world sensor characteristics, annota-tion incompleteness, and domain shifts. PanoBench extends CarlaOcc into a comprehensive benchmark by systematizing it as PanoBench-CarlaOcc and adding PanoBench-nuScenes and PanoBench-KITTI360 with reconstructed panoptic occupancy annotations. This enables a more systematic study of panop-tic occupancy prediction, including real-world reconstruction quality, cross-domain generalization, and camera-centric eval-uation. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Task Formulation 

Let It = {Ikτ }k=1: Kτ =t−T +1: t be the input observations of multi-view RGB image sequences, where K is the number of cameras and T is the number of historical frames used for prediction. The camera intrinsics, extrinsics, and ego poses are collectively denoted by Gt. The goal of panoptic occupancy prediction is to estimate a 3D panoptic scene representation in an ego-centric voxel space: 

f (It, Gt) 7 → V t, (1) where f is a camera-centric panoptic occupancy predic-tion model. To reduce storage cost and loading latency, the canonical form of panoptic occupancy is defined as a sparse occupied-voxel set: 

Vt = {(vt,n , c t,n , i t,n ) | n = 1 , . . . , N t}, (2) where vt,n ∈ Z3 stores discrete voxel indices at timestamp t,

ct,n ∈ C denotes the semantic category, and it,n ∈ N0 denotes the instance identity. Let Cth and Cst be the disjoint thing and stuff subsets of C. Stuff voxels have it,n = 0 , whereas voxels of the same thing object share a positive instance identity. Without loss of generality, the semantics and instance identity of each voxel are encoded by the following panoptic label: 

pt,n = β c t,n + it,n , (3) where β is a predefined encoding base larger than the maxi-mum instance identity, so that each pt,n uniquely recovers the semantic–instance pair. Beyond label encoding, each frame is stored as a sparse occupied-voxel list together with a packed dense valid mask and grid metadata. Voxel coordinates and panoptic labels are serialized in Fortran order and written as compressed NPZ archives, which substantially reduces storage footprint and loading latency. This canonical representation provides a unified interface for dataset construction, training, and evaluation across all PanoBench subsets and converted public occupancy annotations. 

B. PanoBench Overview 

PanoBench is a synthetic and real-world benchmark family for camera-centric panoptic occupancy prediction. It contains one synthetic subset, PanoBench-CarlaOcc, and two real-world subsets, PanoBench-nuScenes and PanoBench-KITTI360. Ac-cording to the data domain, these subsets form a synthetic branch and a real-world branch, and they are further divided into diverse variants by camera view and voxel resolution. All variants share the canonical occupancy form, valid-mask repre-sentation, and model interface defined in Sects. III-A and III-E, enabling fair comparisons of semantic occupancy prediction, panoptic occupancy prediction, 4D occupancy forecasting, and sim-to-real transfer under a common protocol. PanoBench-CarlaOcc provides the synthetic branch. It con-tains 104 driving sequences and 104,000 frames collected from eight CARLA towns, covering 30 semantic classes and temporally consistent instance identities. To support controlled studies, it provides both forward-view and surround-view variants over a 51 .2×51 .2×6.4 m occupancy volume at voxel resolutions of 0.1/0.2/0.4 m. The forward-view variant matches the limited-view setting of classical scene completion, whereas the surround-view variant supports full camera-centric occu-pancy prediction. Because its labels are derived from simulated meshes, PanoBench-CarlaOcc supplies complete geometry and serves as the controlled source domain for resolution analysis and sim-to-real experiments. The real-world branch contains PanoBench-nuScenes and PanoBench-KITTI360. PanoBench-nuScenes follows the surround-view camera setting of nuScenes [22] and provides 34,149 frames from 850 scenes, 17 semantic classes, and 0.2/0.4 m panoptic occupancy labels within an 80 × 80 × 6.4 mvolume. PanoBench-KITTI360 follows the forward-view set-ting of KITTI-360 [23] and provides 76,473 frames from nine sequences, 19 semantic classes, and 0.2 m labels within a 

51 .2 × 51 .2 × 6.4 m volume. Unlike the synthetic subset, these real-world subsets are reconstructed from multi-view RGB images, semantic LiDAR observations, HD map evidence, and 3D object annotations, while further providing complete foreground assets and persistent instance labels. 2) Mesh-Based Scene Reconstruction: Panoptic occupancy ground truth of CarlaOcc is reconstructed from simulated meshes rather than accumulated LiDAR points. For each frame, the meshes of static background that intersect the target occupancy volume are first retrieved and transformed into the ego-vehicle coordinate system. Subsequently, the dynamic foreground instances are incorporated using their simulated poses and instance identities. For non-rigid instances such as pedestrians, their best-fitting meshes are retrieved from the simulator’s standard-mesh library according to skeleton similarities. The whole process produces a frame-level mesh-based scene representation containing the background layout, movable objects, and deformable participants in a geometri-cally consistent coordinate system. 

3) Panoptic Occupancy Generation: We voxelize the re-constructed scene meshes and assign each occupied voxel its semantic and instance labels. Spatially overlapped components are merged in a topology-aware order that preserves thin ground structures and prevents stuff regions from overwriting foreground instances. Stuff meshes of the same category are consolidated before voxelization, whereas thing instances remain separated. The dense labels are then converted to the canonical sparse form in Sect. III-A. Ground categories are regularized as single-layer surfaces to avoid redundant vertical thickness and to match the real-world reconstruction protocol. 

D. Real-World Panoptic Occupancy Reconstruction 

Although synthetic data provides high-fidelity and physi-cally consistent panoptic annotations, its scene diversity re-mains limited, and an unavoidable simulation-to-real domain gap persists. Constructing panoptic occupancy annotations for real-world scenes nevertheless remains challenging, as sensor observations are sparse, noisy, and incomplete. Asingle LiDAR sweep captures only visible surfaces, whereas conventional multi-frame aggregation adopted in previous benchmarks [6]–[8], [10], [11], [29] can introduce ghosting artifacts from dynamic objects and yield fragmented structures in sparsely observed distant regions. To address these challenges, we organize real-world panop-tic occupancy annotation into three coordinated stages: back-ground point cloud reconstruction, foreground mesh asset acquisition, and panoptic occupancy ground truth generation. As shown in Fig. 2, the proposed pipeline enables static scene structures and dynamic instances to be reconstructed from complementary sources of evidence and subsequently integrated into a unified panoptic occupancy volume, providing geometrically complete, temporally consistent, and instance-aware panoptic occupancy annotations for real-world driving datasets [22], [23]. 

1) Background Point Cloud Reconstruction: Semantic Li-DAR Point Cloud Aggregation. Points of foreground in-stances are first removed from each LiDAR sweep to prevent motion-induced trailing artifacts, and the remaining static observations are aggregated across each driving sequence to form a scene-level representation. The raw point clouds are transformed into a common coordinate system using calibrated ego poses, and their semantic labels are retained during accu-mulation. To densify sparsely observed non-planar structures, unlabeled LiDAR sweeps inherit semantics from their nearest labeled observations subject to a distance constraint. This process increases spatial coverage beyond a single frame and provides the primary geometric evidence for static background structures. 

HD Map-Guided Ground Layer Lifting. Although multi-frame LiDAR aggregation expands the spatial coverage of the scene, ground-layer measurements remain discontinuous and fragmented because LiDAR point density decays rapidly with range. Moreover, regions missed by the LiDAR sensor due to occlusion are indistinguishable from truly empty space in the voxel space, causing physically continuous surfaces to be incorrectly truncated in the reconstructed background. Furthermore, ground-layer geometry can be sufficiently repre-sented by a single lifted surface layer, since additional vertical thickness increases storage cost without providing meaningful supervision for camera-centric scene reconstruction. Topology-Aware Flat Region Recovery. Despite providing strong horizontal priors, HD map polygons may contain unlabeled holes or unobserved flat regions, even when the underlying ground surface is physically continuous. Directly voxelizing such polygons would imprint these annotation defects onto the occupancy volume as artificial disconti-nuities. We therefore introduce a topology-aware recovery strategy that reasons jointly about spatial continuity and the semantic adjacency of neighboring map layers. A connected-component analysis is first performed in the BEV grid to identify candidate flat regions that are spatially adjacent to the reconstructed ground layers but lack semantic map priors. To verify that these candidates belong to ground surfaces, the consistency between their local normals and the vertical direction is validated. Subsequently, the boundary point sets shared with the known ground regions are extracted and employed with a Thin-Plate Spline (TPS) interpolation [56] to construct a continuous 2D elevation field over the candidate domain. This formulation naturally preserves gentle slopes and curvature without introducing artificial steps. Dense query 7

points are then sampled within the BEV-convex silhouette of the candidate region, their fitted elevations are computed via the interpolated field, and semantic labels are assigned via nearest-neighbor voting [57] from the aggregated point cloud. To prevent unbounded extrapolation, we constrain the propagation range within a limited buffer distance from the known seeds, leaving distant unobserved terrains unfilled to avoid hallucination. This procedure repairs map-induced arti-facts while preserving mutually exclusive semantic boundaries and avoiding unnecessary modifications to reliably measured geometry. 

Non-Planar Region Completion and Denoising. For non-planar static structures such as vegetation clusters and man-made objects, aggregated point clouds suffer from severe spar-sity and noise due to LiDAR multipath reflections, occlusions, and ranging errors. Statistical outlier removal is therefore ap-plied: a point is discarded if its average distance to neighboring points is significantly larger than the scene-level distribution of this distance. This step suppresses isolated measurement noise and floating artifacts while preserving locally dense structures. A semi-automated interface then supports visual inspection and local correction of the background point cloud, using synchronized camera images and projected LiDAR points as reference. The combination of statistical filtering and targeted refinement yields clean, high-quality background point clouds that serve as reliable geometric priors for the final occupancy ground truth. 

2) Foreground Mesh Asset Acquisition: Foreground objects require instance-level, complete geometry rather than class-level surface reconstruction. Existing real-world occupancy prediction benchmarks typically reconstruct foreground ob-jects by directly aggregating semantic LiDAR observations over time [6]–[8], [10], [11]. This observation-driven strategy is inherently limited for dynamic instances, since temporal aggregation can introduce motion-induced ghosting and signif-icant geometric distortion. Moreover, the aggregated LiDAR sweeps capture only visible surfaces, which cannot recover the occluded portions of an object. In contrast, we decouple foreground reconstruction from sparse geometric observations and formulate it as image-conditioned mesh asset generation, in which off-the-shelf generative 3D foundation models [58]– [60] infer complete instance-level geometry from sparse visual observations. 

Multi-Frame Multi-View Instance Mining. We first retrieve all target samples and collect annotated instances whose 3D boxes intersect the occupancy volume. For each candidate in-stance, its full temporal trajectory is traversed, its 3D bounding box is projected into the synchronized multi-view image se-quences, and a tightly padded patch is cropped from each view in which the instance is sufficiently visible and not severely truncated. Each crop is then assessed from two complementary aspects: an image-quality evaluator (e.g. , MANIQA [61]) measures perceptual sharpness and exposure fidelity, while a text-prompted segmenter ( e.g. , SAM 3 [62]) produces an in-stance mask that provides foreground-coverage and occlusion cues. Views that jointly satisfy image-quality, mask-coverage, and multi-camera constraints are selected as candidate views for mesh reconstruction. When multiple observations of the same instance are available, the views with stronger instance support are retained to improve mesh reconstruction quality. This process filters out heavily occluded, motion-blurred, and poorly segmented observations, yielding a compact set of clean, viewpoint-diverse inputs per instance. 

Image-Conditioned Instance Reconstruction. After view selection, each retained instance is represented by a compact set of crop-mask pairs. These RGB crops and their binary masks are fed into a generative mesh model (e.g. , MV-SAM3D [63]), where the masks provide explicit foreground support and exclude background content. Instances with multi-ple valid observations are reconstructed through the multi-view branch, while rare single-view cases fall back to the single-view branch. The decoded meshes and Gaussians are jointly baked into a textured GLB model via differentiable rendering, after which an adaptive quadric simplification is applied to reduce polygon count while preserving silhouette fidelity. The resulting meshes serve as reusable, scene-agnostic geometric prototypes for other instances.

3) Panoptic Occupancy Ground Truth Generation: The preceding stages yield a globally consistent semantic point cloud for background stuff classes and an instance-specific mesh assignment for each annotated thing object. For each foreground thing instance, the assigned prototype mesh is scaled and posed according to the annotated 3D bounding box and then transformed into the current ego frame. Surface points are uniformly sampled from the aligned mesh and voxelized on the same lattice as the background, preserving the semantic category and persistent instance identity of the object. A direct merge of the voxelized background and foreground may assign multiple labels to the same voxel, since differ-ent stuff classes can overlap after point-cloud aggregation and foreground instances can intersect with the reconstructed background. We therefore introduce Elevation-Aware Panoptic Label Fusion to resolve these conflicts in a unified manner. A category-level elevation-priority lookup table is predefined according to the real-world topological relationships among semantic classes. For each frame, all labeled voxel candidates are assigned priorities from this table and then sorted together with their world-space elevations. Candidates are written into the occupancy grid in increasing elevation-priority order: when several labels claim the same voxel, the higher-priority can-didate is kept. This prevents ground and other low structures from overwriting higher surfaces or foreground objects. E. Valid Mask Generation 

To avoid scoring regions that the cameras cannot observe, existing occupancy prediction benchmarks commonly define valid masks from current-frame sensor evidence. Occ3D [6] provides camera visibility masks, while SSCBench [11] in-herits voxelized invalid masks from scene completion bench-marks [8], [23]. These protocols are effective for evaluat-ing visible surfaces, but they tend to encourage occupancy prediction to degenerate into depth estimation with semantic segmentation, where models are rewarded for the currently visible surfaces rather than for complete 3D structures. The problem is more severe for panoptic occupancy, because a thing object must be reconstructed as a whole instance, not as a visible fragment. Moreover, most existing valid masks are generated independently for each target frame, which ignores objects that were observed in the recent past but occluded at present. Consequently, this design understates the role of temporal observations and cannot evaluate whether a model keeps instance identity and completes an object once it is occluded. B. Occupancy Annotation Quality 

Occupancy annotation quality is characterized by four dis-tinct dimensions: observation fidelity , geometric complete-ness , spatial continuity , and temporal consistency . All cross-dataset comparisons align the region of interest, semantic taxonomy, and voxel resolution before evaluation. 

1) Occupancy Observation Fidelity: Observation fidelity measures whether an occupancy volume agrees with the corresponding 2D semantic evidence when rendered from the same camera view. As shown in Fig. 4, each occupancy grid is ray-cast into the provided camera views to obtain rendered semantic maps, which are then compared with the 2D semantic ground truth. Pixel accuracy (PixAcc) is adopted as the obser-vation fidelity score. Evaluation is restricted to pixels whose corresponding 3D points lie inside the occupancy ground-truth range. As shown in Table II, PanoBench annotations are consistently more observation-faithful than prior occupancy labels under matched voxel sizes. On nuScenes, PanoBench-nuScenes outperforms OpenOccupancy by 7.2 points at 0.2 mand Occ3D by 3.8 points at 0.4 m. On KITTI-360, PanoBench-KITTI360 reaches 81 .5% PixAcc at 0.2 m, outperforming SSCBench-KITTI360 by 11 .6 points. Smaller voxels also yield higher PixAcc, because finer grids better preserve object boundaries and thin structures under camera projection. As expected, synthetic annotations exhibit higher fidelity than real-world ones, benefiting from noise-free sensors and exact geometric ground truth in simulation. 

2) Geometric Completeness: Geometric completeness mea-sures whether occupancy annotations recover the full spatial extent of scene structures and object silhouettes, including occluded areas and regions with sparse sensor evidence. Be-cause existing benchmarks reconstruct occupancy with differ-ent pipelines, their reconstruction results are compared both qualitatively on real data and quantitatively against a mesh-derived oracle on PanoBench-CarlaOcc. Fig. 5 compares real-world occupancy annotations at both the scene level and the instance level under a shared se-mantic palette. SurroundOcc [10], OpenOccupancy [7], and Occ3D [6] exhibit broken ground layers and incomplete fore-ground objects. Their annotations are constructed mainly by aggregating and voxelizing LiDAR points, and therefore can-not recover geometry under occlusion or sparse observation. In contrast, PanoBench produces substantially more continuous backgrounds and more complete foreground shapes. A controlled oracle evaluation is further conducted on PanoBench-CarlaOcc. Real sensor observations cannot val-idate unobserved surfaces, so the Occ3D [6] and Sur-roundOcc [10] annotation pipelines are compared with PanoBench under a complete mesh oracle. Town10HD is selected as the reconstructed scene, where the sensor input is frozen and occupancy is rebuilt with three pipelines that share the same poses, LiDAR sweeps, and evaluation grid. Occ3D aggregates multi-frame static semantic LiDAR and retains dynamic classes from the current frame only before voxelization. SurroundOcc uses the same aggregation and densifies the static cloud with Poisson reconstruction before voxelization. These are style-faithful reconstructions of the corresponding annotation protocols rather than exact replicas of the original toolchains. CarlaOcc labels are remapped into coarse classes to reduce taxonomy discrepancies across pipelines. We compare the three reconstructed annotations against the mesh oracle and report geometric IoU, semantic mIoU, and per-class IoU. As shown in Table III, PanoBench achieves 58.9 IoU and 52.0 mIoU, outperforming the stronger Occ3D-style pipeline by 20.6 and 19.4 points, respectively. The largest gains occur on Vehicle, Pedestrian, Terrain, and Sidewalk. For example, Vehicle IoU increases from 5.9 to 59.1, since map-guided ground lifting and complete instance meshes recover volumes that LiDAR point clouds miss. Although the SurroundOcc-style pipeline densifies the static cloud through Poisson reconstruction, it improves neither IoU nor mIoU over the Occ3D-style pipeline, showing that denser surfaces alone do not imply more complete geometry and semantics. Building and Vegetation remain difficult for all three pipelines because distant facades and foliage are only partially observed. Fig. 6 visualizes a representative frame. The Occ3D-style pipeline yields sparse, fragmented geometry with incomplete cars. The SurroundOcc-style pipeline recovers the ground layer better owing to Poisson reconstruction, but still produces deformed and erroneous geometry for complex structures such as buildings and traffic lights. In comparison, PanoBench recovers continuous ground and complete vehicles that closely match the mesh oracle. These results indicate that the incompleteness of previous LiDAR aggregation-based benchmarks is structural rather than incidental. PanoBench addresses this issue by exploiting ge-ometric priors and reconstructing complete foreground assets, which substantially reduces missing geometry in occluded and sparsely observed regions.

For each dataset, 1,000 adjacent frame pairs are randomly sampled, and semantic mIoU and binary occupancy IoU are computed between the warped and original ground truth. As shown in Table V, PanoBench is more temporally consistent than existing benchmarks in nearly all comparisons at matched voxel sizes. On CARLA, PanoBench-CarlaOcc improves mIoU over CarlaSC [29] by 12.5 and 12.1 points at 0.2 m and 0.4 m, respectively, together with IoU gains of 5.6 and 5.5 points. On nuScenes, PanoBench-nuScenes achieves the highest mIoU at both voxel sizes and improves IoU over Occ3D [6] by 7.2 points at 0.4 m, while its IoU at 0.2 m is comparable to that of OpenOccupancy [7]. SurroundOcc [10] attains the highest IoU of 74.3 among the nuScenes annota-tions, in line with the trend that coarser voxels yield higher consistency for every dataset evaluated at multiple voxel sizes. These results indicate that the scene-level background reconstruction of PanoBench remains stable under ego-motion, whereas aggregation-based annota-tions are more prone to surface flickering and inconsistent semantics. Occ3D-Panoptic [15] exhibits noticeable temporal flickering, with object geometry and instance labels changing abruptly over time. In contrast, PanoBench preserves both foreground shape and instance identity throughout the observed trajectory. Over-all, PanoBench provides temporally coherent annotations for both static backgrounds and dynamic foreground instances, enabling reliable supervision and evaluation for temporally aware panoptic occupancy prediction. 

C. Ablation Study 

The ablation study on the occupancy construction pipeline is conducted by successively enabling its background and foreground components under matched observations. Back-ground variants are reconstructed on PanoBench-nuScenes and scored by observation fidelity on stuff pixels, follow-ing Sect. IV-B1. Foreground variants are reconstructed on PanoBench-CarlaOcc: each generated asset is voxelized in its annotated box and compared with the instance occupancy ground truth, so that occluded interiors are scored rather than visible silhouettes alone. 

Background Reconstruction. Starting from semantic LiDAR aggregation, we successively enable HD map-guided ground-layer lifting, topology-aware flat-region recovery, and non-planar refinement. As shown in Table VI, ground-layer lifting improves PixAcc from 68 .5% to 74 .1% , recovering contiguous map-supported surfaces that sparse LiDAR returns cannot fill. Flat-region recovery adds another 4.9 points, showing that map polygons still leave unlabeled holes and that topology-aware completion is required for continuous ground layers. Non-planar refinement contributes only 0.4 points: aggregated Li-DAR already covers most non-planar structures, so outlier fil-tering mainly removes floating artifacts rather than adding new geometry. Altogether, the full background pipeline improves PixAcc by 10 .9 points over semantic LiDAR aggregation alone. Because Occ3D [6] and SurroundOcc [10] reconstruct occupancy primarily by aggregating and voxelizing LiDAR points, this gap indicates that map-guided ground completion provides a clear advantage over aggregation-based annotation protocols.

2) Panoptic Occupancy Prediction: Table IX shows that instance quality does not follow volumetric or semantic accuracy. Although Panoptic-FlashOcc has a higher IoU and a nearly identical mIoU on PanoBench-CarlaOcc and PanoBench-KITTI360, SparseOcc achieves the higher RayPQ at every depth tolerance on all three subsets, most markedly on PanoBench-nuScenes, where its RayPQ@1 is over three times as high. For both methods, RayPQ@4 retains about two thirds or more of RayIoU@4 on PanoBench-CarlaOcc but about half or less on the real-world subsets, so grouping correctly classified surfaces into instances is the main difficulty in real scenes. The depth-tolerance dependence of RayPQ reveals why the two methods differ. Relaxing the tolerance from 1 m to 4 m raises the RayPQ of both methods by only about one fifth on PanoBench-CarlaOcc, but on PanoBench-nuScenes it more than triples that of Panoptic-FlashOcc while less than doubling that of SparseOcc, suggesting that many instances of Panoptic-FlashOcc are recognized but displaced in depth. This agrees with its design: thing voxels are grouped around instance centers predicted in the BEV plane after depth-based lifting, so depth errors displace entire instances, whereas SparseOcc predicts instance masks directly in 3D.

4) Discussion: These results lead to three insights. First, no single architecture dominates across metrics, because volumet-ric completeness and surface precision are distinct objectives. IoU alone can thus mis-rank methods, and combining dense coverage with sparse surface precision is a promising direc-tion. Second, instance grouping, rather than semantic labeling, is the main bottleneck in real scenes, and its sensitivity to depth errors favors instance reasoning directly in 3D. Third, rankings do not reliably transfer from synthetic to real data, and limited camera coverage, as in PanoBench-KITTI360, hampers both current and future prediction.

Each method is trained and evaluated separately at each native resolution. As shown in Table XI, the task becomes steadily harder for a fixed architecture as the voxel size decreases. Averaged across the two methods [16], [39], reducing the voxel size from 0.4 m to 0.2 m decreases IoU and mIoU by 11.5 and 9.3 points, respectively. A further reduction to 0.1 m incurs substantially larger drops of 23.7 and 20.1 points. This sharper degradation arises because the image resolution and depth cues remain unchanged, while each halving of the voxel size multiplies the number of cells in the same physical volume by eight. Consequently, small depth or localization errors displace predictions across multiple voxels, and thin structures and semantic boundaries demand substantially greater geometric precision. 3) Canonical Storage and I/O Efficiency: The practical benefit of the canonical sparse panoptic format introduced in Sect. III-A is evaluated by comparing the official annotation release with its converted sparse representation in terms of total storage footprint and per-frame I/O latency. As shown in Table XIII, the canonical format consis-tently reduces both storage footprint and I/O latency. On dense releases such as OpenOccupancy [7], SurroundOcc [10], SemanticKITTI [8], and SSCBench-KITTI360 [11], storage drops by more than 95% and per-frame latency by more than 83%. Notably, Occ3D [6] and its panoptic extension [15] already store annotations in compressed NPZ files, yet conver-sion to our canonical representation still reduces their storage footprints by approximately half, while decreasing loading latency by more than 85%. The substantial latency reduction is largely attributable to the smaller serialized payload of the canonical format, which requires fewer bytes to be read and decoded for each frame. These results indicate that the sparse panoptic representation preserves the information required for training and evaluation while making large-scale experiments substantially more efficient. V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This article presented PanoBench, a unified benchmark for camera-centric panoptic occupancy prediction across synthetic and real-world driving scenes. PanoBench-CarlaOcc con-tributes complete mesh-derived supervision, while PanoBench-nuScenes and PanoBench-KITTI360 reconstruct real scenes by combining semantic background geometry with complete instance-level foreground assets. A common sparse represen-tation, temporally aware valid masks, and persistent instance identities make it possible to evaluate geometry, semantics, instance separation, temporal consistency, and domain transfer under one protocol. Quality analyses show that, at matched voxel sizes, PanoBench annotations are more observation-faithful, more complete, and less fragmented than those of existing benchmarks, and are more temporally consistent in nearly all comparisons. Benchmark results further reveal that no single architecture dominates across metrics and that instance-aware prediction on real-world data remains challeng-ing. The benchmark is intended to shift occupancy evaluation from visible semantic surfaces toward complete, temporally supported 3D scene understanding.
