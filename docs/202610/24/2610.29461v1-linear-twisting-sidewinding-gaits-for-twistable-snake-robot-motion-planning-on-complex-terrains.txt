# Linear Twisting Sidewinding Gaits for Twistable Snake Robot Motion Planning on Complex Terrains 

Qingling Fan 1, Zhongqiang Ren 1†

Abstract — Sidewinding is an efficient gait for snake robots to locomote on various terrains, by undulating the backbone curve and shifting the contact segments along it. However, relying solely on undulatory motion limits the locomotive capability of snake robots, and combining it with twisting motion can improve their speed and robustness. This hypothesis has recently been verified by the rolling vector approach, which locomotes the robot like a differential-drive car. Yet its twisting is concentrated in a short buffer segment, imposing most of the workload on the two twist joints in the middle, which can often overburden the motors and limit its locomotion. With that in mind, this paper develops a new family of twisting sidewinding gaits that distribute the workload over the entire body with our proposed linear twisting function. Under matched turning speed and total output power, our gaits reduce the peak output power of a single twist joint by 42%. Real-robot experiments show that, with the reduced workload, our new gaits can locomote the robot in various terrains that the existing rolling vector approach cannot. Besides, our new gaits can move up to 49% faster than conventional sidewinding without twisting. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Snake robots are hyper-redundant limbless robots that can traverse complex environments [1]–[3] such as sandy slopes, pipes, and compact spaces, using versatile gaits, which are cyclic motions in the shape space. Among these gaits, sidewinding is regarded as highly efficient and robust in many terrains [4]–[7]. During sidewinding, the snake keeps its body lifted except for a few static contact segments, and undulates its backbone curve both horizontally and vertically to continuously shift these segments, transporting itself using the friction at contact. While being highly efficient and robust, sidewinding is still relatively slow to traverse even flat ground, and may get trapped on uneven terrains. In contrast, the rolling motion of wheels is way faster on many complex terrains. We take the view that relying solely on undulatory motion limits the loco-motive capability of snake robots, and that combining it with rolling motion can improve both efficiency and robustness without losing the various gaits. This hypothesis has been partially verified in simulation by the recent literature [8], which considers locomotion of a twistable snake robot (T-Snake), a modular snake-robot mechanism that can not only bend but also twist about its backbone curve. A rolling vector approach [8] is proposed to coordinate the motion of joints, which kinematically reduces the T-Snake to a differential-drive car. As a result, the twisting is concentrated within the buffer segment, and burdens the two twist joints in the middle of the body, while leaving the others almost idle (Fig. 1(a)), which can often overload the motors in the buffer segment and limit its locomotion capability. To coordinate the large number of joints while avoiding such uneven workload, this paper proposes a new linear twisting approach that distributes the twisting motion over the entire body. Specifically, we let the twisting vary linearly along the backbone curve, and refer to the resulting gaits as 

linear twisting sidewinding (LTS). Following the backbone curve approach [9], [10], this linear twisting leads to a family of gaits: When the twisting is uniform along the whole body, the entire robot rolls like a wheel during sidewinding, which we call rolling sidewinding . When the twisting varies along the body, different parts of the robot roll at different speeds, which turns the robot, and we call this gait twisting sidewinding . In twisting sidewinding, the point where the arXiv:2610.29461v1 [cs.RO] 21 Oct 2026 rolling speed is zero acts as the turning center, and it can be placed anywhere along the body. Since the twisting of the LTS is spread over the entire body, all twist joints share the load. On our hardware implementation of the T-Snake, we compare the two gaits under similar angular velocities and similar total output power during in-place turning. Under this fair comparison, the two most-loaded twist joints of the existing rolling vector approach carry 96 .8% of the total power, while those of our LTS carry only 45 .1% (Fig. 1(b)). The LTS reduces the peak output power of a single twist joint by 42% , leaving a larger margin before a joint motor saturates (Fig. 1(c)). Besides, our LTS with evenly distributed workload enables the robot to locomote on various complex terrains. We compare our LTS against conventional sidewinding without twisting on four types of outdoor terrains, and our LTS moves the robot faster than conventional sidewinding, reducing the traversal time by up to 49% for a fixed travel length (Fig. 1(d)). Real-robot experiments also validate the translating and turning motions of the proposed gaits. II. R ELATED WORKS 

The large number of redundant DoFs makes it challeng-ing to design gaits for snake robots. Existing methods for snake robot gait design can be classified into central pat-tern generators [11], [12], reinforcement learning [13]–[15], parameterized gait equations [16], [17], and the backbone curve methods [9], [18], [19]. In this paper, we leverage the geometric intuition of the backbone curve method to design and analyze linear twisting sidewinding gaits for the T-Snake. The proposed gaits are based on the sidewinding loco-motion, which is an efficient gait family for snake robots to locomote on complex terrains [4], [6], [7], [20]. Sidewinding achieves both translational and turning maneuvers in many ways. For example, conical sidewinding enables fixed-point turning [4], [21], and sidewinding with the contact length control technique allows navigation on sandy slopes [2]. Re-cent works [22], [23] further improved sidewinding motions by adding artificial scales on the snake robots and modulating the spatial frequency of the vertical body wave. Snake robots that can not only bend but also twist are designed and realized in several ways. The first class adds the limited twisting capability to snake robots’ inner backbone, using twist joints [24]–[26], cable-driven design [27], [28], and the helical spring backbone [29]. The twisting angular range of this class of robots is limited. Another class adds unlimited torsion to outer layers, such as the ARCSnake [30] and EELS [3]. The inner backbones of this class of snake robots still cannot twist unlimitedly. Different from these existing snake robots, the T-Snake mechanism considered in this paper can twist its backbone unlimitedly and meets the mechanical requirement of the proposed gaits. III. D. From Curve to Gaits 

We describe how to convert the continuous shape functions into joint commands for the discrete T-Snake mechanism. We first review the conventional integration method [32] and then introduce a piecewise-approximation method for the proposed linear twisting sidewinding gaits. 

1) Integration for T-Snake: As shown in Fig. 5(a), the T-Snake consists of n identical modules, each of length 

L0 and containing three 1-DoF revolute joints in a dorsal-twist-lateral sequence. The dorsal and lateral joint axes pass through the module’s cross-sectional center, span the backbone’s normal plane, and are mutually orthogonal in the straight configuration; the twist axis is aligned with the local backbone tangent and is therefore orthogonal to both bending axes. The complete mechanism has 3n DoFs and total length 

L = nL 0. These joints provide a discrete realization of the dorsal reference curve: lateral and dorsal rotations represent the accumulated curvatures κa and κb, respectively, while twist rotations represent the accumulated torsion τ .The integration approach from [32] can be directly applied to the T-Snake by adding an additional integration for the torsion [8]. For twisting sidewinding gaits ( a1̸ = 0 ), the torsional deformation accumulates within the T-Snake body, inducing turning motions. By applying the existing integra-tion method for these motions of the T-Snake, we observe that as the torsional deformation increases, the resulting joint angles of the dorsal and lateral joints along the body become numerically unstable, which further leads to distorted motion of the T-Snake and unexpected motion trajectories. The reason for this numerical problem is left for future work. 

2) Piecewise-Approximation Integration Method: We avoid this numerical problem by using a piecewise approx-imation integration method, which aligns with the discrete mechanism of T-Snake. For the actual T-Snake mechanism, each link is a rigid body and must be torsion-free, and the torsion can only be achieved at the twist joints (Fig. 5(c)). D. Traversal Speed on Complex Terrains 

In this experiment, we show the robustness of the proposed gait on complex terrains. We compare the traversal speed between our rolling sidewinding and conventional sidewind-ing [16] on four types of terrains: flagstone, grass, pebble, and a 20 ◦ slope. The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 9. We use a test track of length 2 m for all terrains and measure the arrival time. We fix ωt for both gaits at 

0.5 rad/s. The conventional sidewinding is configured with 

a0 = 0 , while the rolling sidewinding uses a0 = 0 .8 rad/s. Each test is repeated five times on every terrain type. As shown in Fig. 9(e), the proposed gait exhibits an obvious reduction in arrival times across all tested terrains compared to the conventional sidewinding. Specifically, the time reduction rates are 45 .1% on flagstone, 41 .0% on grass, 

47 .1% on pebble, and 49 .2% on the slope. These results indicate that the rolling sidewinding enhances the traversal speed on various complex terrains. V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS 

In this paper, we propose a novel family of linear twisting sidewinding gaits for twistable snake robots to traverse complex terrains. By integrating the twisting motion with the conventional undulatory sidewinding gait, we expand the locomotive capability of the T-Snake. Furthermore, to address the numerical problem in the existing integration method, we develop a new approach that leverages the features of the discrete T-Snake mechanism and accurately generates its joint angles. Extensive simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate that the proposed gaits not only lower the peak load on the twist joint when steering, but also achieve faster traversal speeds on various complex terrains. When computing the joint angles for twisting sidewinding, the reason for the numerical problem in the existing inte-gration method is still underexplored. Besides, the present study only considers integrating the twist function into the sidewinding gait. Our future work includes fusing the twist motion with other gaits to better traverse and interact with complex environments. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS
