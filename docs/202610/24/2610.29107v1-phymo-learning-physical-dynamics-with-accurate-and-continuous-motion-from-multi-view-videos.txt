## Abstract 

Recovering dynamic 3D scene geometry, appearance, and the physical motion states that govern scene evolution from multi-view videos is important yet chal-lenging. A key difficulty is that appearance can be deceptive. Under rendering supervision alone, a model may fit the observed images by changing color or opacity to compensate for inaccurate deformation, rather than recovering the true evolving geometry and motion. This issue is especially severe in scenes with fast or locally complex motion, where inaccurate trajectories can still produce plausible images over the observed frames, but lead to unreliable reconstruction and discontinuous future prediction. In this paper, we propose PhyMo , a frame-work for learning evolving dynamics from multi-view videos. We introduce a Motion-Aware Trajectory Representation that drives the model to recover accurate motion from deformation, rather than relying on appearance shortcuts to explain observed motion. On top of this, we propose a Continuous Motion Evolution module with bidirectional kinematic continuity constraint to promote temporally consistent evolution of displacement, velocity, and acceleration. Together, these designs yield geometrically faithful interpolation and continuous motion extrapola-tion. Experiments on four dynamic scene benchmarks demonstrate state-of-the-art performance. Code is available at https://github.com/BystanderJ/PhyMo .

## 1 Introduction 

Modeling the real, complex, and dynamic physical 3D world has long been a fundamental yet challenging problem in computer vision and robotics. A faithful understanding of how 3D scenes evolve over time is critical for a wide range of downstream applications, including embodied AI, world models, and robotic motion planning. Recent advances in 3D scene representations, such as Neural Radiance Fields[1] and 3D Gaussian Splatting[2], have enabled high-quality dynamic scene reconstruction from visual observations. With such powerful representations, current research largely centers on reconstruction quality, yet two fundamental questions remain: what constitutes dynamics in a scene representation, and how can we learn it effectively? One line of work [3–6] views dynamics as a spatial-temporal trajectory and represents motion with a deformation field. These methods typically fit trajectories by uniformly sampling timestamps and jointly optimizing the deformation and canonical appearance to match observations. However, since their motion modeling is not grounded in physics, they mainly interpolate within the observed interval and degrade quickly beyond it. A second line of work [7–10] models dynamics as physical motion states, such as velocity (and sometimes higher-order states), and learns them via integration to match observations. While this formulation enables future extrapolation, integration-based learning

More recently, hybrid approaches combine both representations [11–15], using spatial-temporal trajectories to guide the learning of physical motion states and aiming to better balance reconstruction fidelity and future prediction. However, these methods often rely primarily on photometric supervision, leaving motion states weakly constrained and lacking temporal consistency. This limitation reflects a broader gap shared by existing approaches, regardless of the specific dynamics representation. In this paper, we aim to recover physically plausible scene evolution from dynamic scenes, rather than merely reconstructing geometry and appearance, so that the learned representation supports both geometrically faithful interpolation and continuous motion extrapolation. However, this task is extremely challenging, because existing methods have not resolved two key issues: 1) Rendering fidelity is not equivalent to faithful motion . Although motion may remain smooth for long periods, recovering accurate trajectories becomes much harder during rapid changes or state transitions. Existing methods typically optimize deformation and canonical appearance jointly under rendering supervision, creating a fundamental ambiguity: while accurate motion can explain correct appearance, correct appearance alone is not evidence of accurate motion. As a result, they may produce plausible renderings while still recovering unreliable trajectories and deformations. 2) Motion evolution is inherently continuous . Displacement, velocity, and acceleration should propagate consistently over time according to the underlying kinematic relationships. However, existing methods often rely on learned trajectories and rendering supervision alone to recover these motion states, without explicitly constraining their temporal consistency. Consequently, the estimated motion states may be locally discontinuous over time, leading to unreliable motion details in both interpolation and extrapolation. ## 2 Related Works 

3D Representations. Traditional static 3D representations, such as meshes[16], point clouds[17], voxel grids[18], and primitives[19], are explicit yet inherently discrete. As a result, they often suffer from fundamental limitations in fidelity and efficiency. Recently, a series of implicit 3D modeling approaches have been proposed, such as occupancy fields[20, 21], signed/unsigned distance fields[22–24], and radiance fields[1]. Although these methods have significantly advanced novel-view synthesis and shape reconstruction, their rendering is often time-consuming due to expensive volumetric sampling. To address this issue, 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)[2] adopts a point-based representation to encode 3D properties, enabling substantially faster rendering. In our framework, we choose 3DGS as the scene representation, since it naturally integrates with of our pipeline. 

Dynamic 3D Reconstruction. Recent progress in dynamic 3D reconstruction has largely focused on extending static scene representations, such as NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), into the spatio-temporal domain [25–28, 28–36]. Most NeRF-based or 3DGS-based approaches decouple the static representation from motion or deformation, and incorporate time as an additional input to learn a deformation or motion field. While these methods achieve impressive visual quality for appearance reconstruction, their learned dynamics are often weakly constrained by rendering supervision, limiting their reliability for both past retrodiction and future extrapolation. On the one hand, the learned temporal field may fit photometric correspondences without capturing physically meaningful motion evolution, leading to poor prediction beyond observed frames. On the other hand, even within the observed time span, visually accurate rendering does not necessarily imply accurate dynamic geometry or deformation. Recent efforts [37, 38] improve dynamic Gaussian representations by relaxing the canonical-space assumption, introducing short-range motion, or modeling temporal visibility and lifespan. However, these works mainly treat such failures as optimization issues, without explicitly revealing the underlying competition between appearance fitting and motion recovery. 

3D Physics Learning. Motivated by the classical Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) framework, a growing body of research has explored learning physical attributes of 3D scenes and dynamic objects, such as Young’s modulus and viscosity [39–42]. While these methods can enable future prediction, they are often constrained to a specific set of physical laws and governing equations. However, real-world dynamics are highly complex and are frequently difficult to capture with accurate simulation-based models. Another class of methods instead directly embeds physics systems [43–48], such as graph-based interactions or mass spring structures into the network, so that the dynamics can be learned in a more structured manner. However, such methods also tend to be limited in generalizability and scalability, since the embedded physics systems are usually tailored to specific object categories, material behaviors, or interaction patterns. More recent hybrid methods [12, 14, 15] attempt to learn general physical motion states, such as velocity or acceleration, directly from dynamic videos. However, their motion-state learning is still largely dominated by rendering supervision, making the recovered physical states under-constrained: different motions may explain the observed frames similarly but yield unstable future predictions.

3.3 Training 

The training of our framework consists of two parts: learning reliable deformation at observed timestamps, and then learning physical motion states on top of these trajectories. We first optimize the canonical Gaussians together with the deformation field, and then jointly optimize the Motion Network and the deformation field with both rendering supervision and kinematic continuity constraint. We begin with a canonical warm-up at the initial timestamp, where the canonical Gaussian parameters are optimized using all observations at t = 0 . After this initialization, we train the deformation field under a gradual temporal expansion strategy. Instead of introducing all observed timestamps at once, we progressively enlarge the supervised temporal support by adding one time window after another. Deformation field is optimized only on the currently revealed observations: 

{G , F defo } ← (ℓ1 + ℓssim ) (10) This gradual schedule is naturally matched with our motion-guided appearance design. As the supervised window expands, the per-Gaussian motion confidence qi is updated from the local deformation change between adjacent timestamps. Consequently, different Gaussians in different motion regions can adaptively open or suppress the freedom of the canonical appearance parameters 

(σi, c i) at different stages of optimization. This prevents static or motion-inactive regions from reducing rendering loss through appearance shortcuts, while allowing genuinely motion-active regions to refine appearance once deformation becomes reliable. After establishing reliable deformation trajectories, we train motion network with bidirectional sampling. For each timestamp t, we sample tl = t − ∆t and tr = t + ∆ t, propagate their Gaussian states to t using RK2 (Section 3.2), and supervise the rendered results at t with additional bidirectional kinematic continuity constraints. ## 4 Experiments 

We evaluate our method on two tasks: Geometrically Faithful Interpolation and Continuous Motion extrapolation . Experiments are conducted on four dynamic scene datasets, covering both real-world and synthetic scenarios with diverse motion patterns and physics-related dynamics. 

Datasets. We use four datasets for evaluation. 1) NVIDIA Dynamic Scenes Dataset [50]. We select two real-world dynamic scenes, including a person skateboarding and a moving truck, to evaluate performance on real-world dynamic reconstruction. 2) TRACE MultiPart Dataset [14]. This synthetic dataset contains multi-part objects with piecewise motions and interpolation/extrapolation splits, which are used to evaluate motion continuity under articulated dynamics. 3) FreeGave-GoPro Dataset [12]. This dataset contains real-world dynamic scenes captured by a multi-camera GoPro rig, including challenging object interactions such as cube collisions. 4) PhysInOne Dataset [51] contains synthetic scenes with explicit physical effects. We select six scenes covering friction, gravity, wind force, buoyancy physical mechanisms to evaluate dynamic reconstruction and future prediction.

4.2 Evaluation for Continuous Motion Extrapolation 

For the Continuous Motion extrapolation task, we focus on baselines that possess explicit predictive capability. Specifically, we compare against FreeGave, TRACE and ODEGS, which model the motion state of the scene, as well as TiNeuVox, NVFi , 4DGS and Deformable3DGS(DefGS), which parameterize space-time dynamics with NeRF-based and Gaussian-based representations, respectively, and thus can perform extrapolation to some extent. For the experimental protocol, we follow the prediction settings of FreeGave and TRACE. Specifically, we normalize the observation time to unit one. We observe frames in [0 , 0.75] and predict future frames in [0 .75 , 1] .

Results and Analysis. As shown in Table 2, our method achieves the best performance on future extrapolation across all datasets. This advantage mainly comes from our continuity loss, which explicitly constrains the kinematic relationship among displacement, velocity, and acceleration. Rendering supervision alone can fit observed frames, but it does not uniquely determine the underlying motion states, making the learned velocity and acceleration under-constrained for future prediction. As a result, prior predictive baselines may recover reasonable appearances at observed times but still produce unstable or physically implausible extrapolated motion. By enforcing consistency from acceleration to velocity and from velocity to displacement, our method learns a more coherent motion evolution, leading to kinematically continuous and physically plausible predictions. The qualitative results in Figure 5 clearly reflect this effect. In the Balloon scene, FreeGave predicts an incorrect motion direction, while our method correctly captures the upward buoyancy driven trend. In the Skating scene, FreeGave and TRACE produce discontinuous future motion, whereas our method maintains a smoother and more coherent trajectory. These results show that explicit kinematic continuity is essential for reliable future extrapolation.

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced PhyMo , a novel framework that aims to recover accurate and continuous evolving dynamics rather than merely fitting visual appearance. PhyMo combines a Motion-Aware Trajectory Representation to reduce appearance shortcuts with a Continuous Motion Evolution module to enforce temporal kinematic consistency. Experiments on four dynamic scene benchmarks show that our method achieves strong interpolation and extrapolation performance, producing more accurate dynamic geometry and more continuous, physically plausible future predictions.

A.3 Implementation Details of Training Schedule 

We provide the detailed training schedule used in our experiments. The training starts with a canonical Gaussian warm-up for 3,000 iterations, where only the observations at the initial timestamp t = 0 are used to initialize the canonical geometry and appearance. 16 After warm-up, we train the deformation field with gradual temporal expansion. Specifically, we divide the observed time span into N = 10 temporal windows. Training begins from the first window, and then progressively includes one additional window until all observed timestamps are involved. This deformation learning stage takes 20 ,000 iterations in total. During this process, the motion confidence qi is updated according to the local deformation change within the currently revealed temporal support, so the freedom of canonical appearance is released adaptively. Finally, we jointly train the deformation field and the Motion Network for another 20 ,000 iterations. We use bidirectional neighboring timestamps and RK2-based propagation to obtain the Gaussian states at the supervised timestamp. The rendering loss is optimized together with the bidirectional kinematic continuity losses. In all experiments, we set the loss weights as λcont1 = λcont2 = 0 .3.

A.9 Limitation of Our Model 

Our method remains challenged by highly deformable scenes, such as Newtonian fluids, due to the inherent representation limitations shared by Gaussian Splatting-based methods. In such scenarios, the scene geometry undergoes continuous and complex deformation, making it difficult for discrete Gaussian primitives to faithfully capture fine-grained topology and appearance changes.
