# MAPLE: Mondrian-Anchored Prior for Learning-free Indoor Exploration 

Kelvin Aladum, Nisar Ahmed 

Abstract — Robots exploring unknown indoor environments must decide where to move while much of the environment remains unseen. Predicting the occupancy of unseen space beyond the current sensor range can improve these decisions, but recent approaches commonly rely on learned models that require offline training and repeated inference during exploration. We introduce the Mondrian-Anchored Prior for Learning-free Indoor Exploration (MAPLE), a training-free predictive approach that adapts the Mondrian process into an online generative prior conditioned on observed geometric cues in the current partial map. MAPLE generates multiple plausible map hypotheses and evaluates visibility separately within each to estimate frontier gain, preserving differences in predicted visibility across hypotheses. Across KTH floor-plans and real-SLAM environments at multiple sensor ranges, MAPLE consistently improves over non-predictive exploration and remains competitive with learned predictive approaches. Its lightweight procedural construction enables predictive ex-ploration with limited computational resources. Experimental results further show that more accurate map prediction does not necessarily produce better exploration, highlighting the importance of how predictions are represented and used in exploration decisions. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous exploration is a key capability for robots operating in unknown or dangerous environments, with ap-plications in search and rescue, emergency response, and infrastructure inspection [1], [2]. In these settings, robots must repeatedly decide where to move while much of the en-vironment remains unseen. Frontier-based exploration makes this decision by identifying frontiers at the boundary between observed free space and unknown space [3]. Connected frontier regions are represented by frontier candidates that serve as possible exploration goals, and selecting among them directly affects exploration efficiency. Classical frontier methods select frontiers using measures such as travel cost, expected coverage, or information gain [3]–[6]. These measures are typically computed from the cur-rent partial occupancy map, so they provide little information about occupancy beyond the sensor range, even though that occupancy can strongly affect how much new space a frontier will reveal. Predictive exploration addresses this limitation by estimating the occupancy of unseen space before frontier selection [7]–[13]. Most recent predictive approaches rely on learned models [8], [10], [11]. These methods can produce useful occupancy predictions but require offline training and repeated inference during exploration. Their computational demands can be restrictive for robots with limited compute and power, and performance may degrade when the operating environment differs from the training data. This tradeoff is particularly relevant at short sensor ranges, where frontier

These considerations motivate a lightweight alternative that can generate predictions directly from the partial map during exploration. We introduce the Mondrian-Anchored Prior for Learning-free Indoor Exploration (MAPLE), a training-free predictive approach for two-dimensional occupancy-grid exploration. MAPLE builds on the Mondrian process [15], which re-cursively partitions a rectangular region using axis-aligned cuts, and guides this process using geometric cues from the current partial map. Repeated sampling produces multiple plausible map completions of the unseen space, each treated as a map hypothesis. Different map hypotheses can imply different visibility from the same frontier candidate. MAPLE therefore evaluates each hypothesis separately and averages the resulting coverage gains before accounting for travel cost. The experi-ments examine end-to-end exploration, map-prediction qual-ity, frontier-decision quality, and computational cost. Con-trolled ablations also test the effects of the map-prediction method, gain estimator, conditioning on observed geometry, and the number of hypotheses. Our contributions are as follows:  

> •

The results show that predictive exploration depends not only on the quality of map predictions but also on how those predictions are used during frontier selection. More accurate map prediction alone does not necessarily lead to more efficient exploration. II. R ELATED WORK 

Frontier and Information-Theoretic Exploration: Classical frontier-based exploration methods provide established ways to prioritize frontiers but typically only reason from the current partial map. Early work prioritizes nearby frontiers, while later work balances expected visible area against travel cost [3], [4]. Information-theoretic approaches instead score actions by expected information gain or mutual informa-tion [5], [6]. Receding-horizon planners and hierarchical planners extend these methods to much larger and three-dimensional environments [16], [17]. MAPLE retains this frontier-selection framework but uses multiple map com-pletions to represent unseen occupancy beyond the current partial map. 

Learned Prediction of Unseen Space: Predictive explo-ration addresses this limitation by estimating occupancy beyond the current sensor range. Earlier work used previ-ously mapped environments to reason about unseen space [18]. Learned approaches later completed or anticipated unseen occupancy for exploration and navigation [7], [19]– [21]. UPEN uses an ensemble of occupancy predictors to guide exploration toward uncertain regions [8]. Ericson and Jensfelt predict unseen walls as line segments [9]. MapEx combines LaMa map completion with prediction uncertainty, and SEER learns to predict information gain for aerial exploration [10], [22], [23]. More recent methods use condi-tional consistency or diffusion models for unseen occupancy, including 4CNet and SceneSense [11], [24]. Achey et al. further integrate generative occupancy synthesis into real-world exploration [25]. These methods demonstrate the value of predicting unseen occupancy but rely on learned models and repeated inference. In contrast, MAPLE generates map completions online without offline training. 

Structural and Training-Free Priors: Indoor spaces con-tain geometric regularities that can support reasoning about unseen space. Aydemir et al. showed from over 38,000 rooms that room geometry and connectivity exhibit useful regularities [26]. Luperto et al. used prior floor plans to facilitate exploration and later predicted the remaining geom-etry of partially observed rooms from the current occupancy map [2], [27]. Related work on building and floor plan generation has used procedural modeling, stochastic layout synthesis, and learned generative models [28]–[30]. These methods primarily target layout generation or rely on prior maps. MAPLE instead uses a Mondrian-based generative process [15] conditioned on the current partial map to sample plausible map completions online. 

Multi-hypothesis Beliefs and Exploration Decisions: When multiple map hypotheses are available, they must be com-bined or evaluated to guide exploration. Prior work has addressed this mainly in the context of SLAM uncertainty. Stachniss et al. evaluate exploration actions over map and pose hypotheses represented by a Rao-Blackwellized par-ticle filter [31]. Wang and Englot combine utilities across hypotheses induced by ambiguous data association [32]. Recent learned approaches also summarize their ensembles before planning. UPEN converts ensemble disagreement into a per-cell uncertainty map [8]. MapEx computes frontier scores from the ensemble mean and pixel-wise variance [10]. CogniPlan plans with multiple layouts produced by a conditional generative model and couples them to a learned graph-based planner [33]. Ericson et al. show that planner behavior can strongly affect the benefit obtained from map prediction [14]. More broadly, planning-aware evaluation has shown that prediction accuracy can be poorly aligned with downstream decision quality [34]. Recent exploration work finds that improved information-gain estimates do not neces-sarily improve exploration efficiency [35]. MAPLE isolates this issue within a common frontier-selection framework by preserving individual map hypotheses during visibility evaluation and testing how this choice affects exploration. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

At each replanning step, the problem is selecting the reachable frontier candidate with the highest predicted gain-to-travel score despite uncertainty about unseen space. 

A. Exploration Task 

The environment is a bounded two-dimensional space represented by a ground-truth occupancy grid M , whose cells are free or occupied and which is unknown to the robot. At time t, the exploration planner receives the robot pose estimate xt and partial occupancy map Mt from an upstream localization and mapping system. Cells in Mt are labeled observed free, observed occupied, or unknown, and 

Ut denotes the set of unknown cells. Frontier cells are observed free cells adjacent to Ut,and connected frontier cells form frontier regions [3]. Each reachable frontier region is represented by the frontier cell closest to its centroid, and these representative cells form the set of frontier candidates Ft. A map completion M ′ is a binary occupancy map that preserves the observed cells of 

Mt and assigns every cell in Ut as either free or occupied. The sensor is characterized by maximum range R and field of view Φ. For a limited-field-of-view sensor, each frontier candidate also specifies a sensing heading. For f ∈ F t and map completion M ′, let V (f, M ′) denote the cells visible from f by raycasting through M ′ within field of view Φ and maximum range R. Each ray terminates at the first occupied cell or the range limit. The coverage gain is 

gt(f, M ′) = V (f, M ′) ∩ Ut , (1) where | · | denotes set cardinality. Thus, gt(f, M ′) is the number of cells in Ut visible from f under M ′.

A. Mondrian Process 

The Mondrian process recursively partitions a bounded rectangular region using axis-aligned cuts [15]. For a rectan-gular region B with width w, height h, and remaining budget 

λ, the cost E of the next cut is sampled from an exponential distribution with rate w + h:

E ∼ Exp( w + h). (5) If E > λ , B remains intact. Otherwise, the x-axis is selected with probability w/ (w + h) and the y-axis with probability h/ (w +h), producing a vertical or horizontal cut, respectively. The cut coordinate is sampled uniformly along the selected axis, and the process recurses independently on the two resulting regions with remaining budget λ − E.Regions with larger w + h therefore tend to receive more cuts, while the axis with greater extent is more likely to be selected. MAPLE retains the recursive partitioning and axis-selection rule of the Mondrian process but conditions it on observed geometry in the current partial map. In particular, it replaces the budget-based stopping rule and uniform cut-location distribution with observation-conditioned rules. We refer to this conditioning as anchoring. The observed geom-etry influences how walls and free space are extended into 

Ut, when partitioning stops, and where new cuts are placed.

E. Validity and Connectivity 

Because cuts are not rendered through predicted free space, one cut can produce several wall segments. A segment is retained only when both endpoints are grounded by ob-served or continued walls, observed or predicted free space, the boundary of the sampled building extent, or another retained segment. MAPLE iteratively prunes unsupported segments until all remaining segments satisfy this condition. The retained walls are rendered using the wall thickness estimated from Mt. If predicted free-space regions remain disconnected from observed free space, MAPLE restores connectivity by opening a doorway through a predicted wall without modifying observed occupied cells. Doorway posi-tions are sampled from doorway locations observed along walls in Mt, or uniformly along the wall if none has been observed. .V. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate MAPLE on end-to-end exploration, map-prediction quality, frontier-decision quality, and computa-tional cost. Controlled ablations then isolate the effects of the map-prediction method, gain estimator, geometric conditioning, and the number of hypotheses. 

A. Experimental Setup 

We evaluate on ten KTH floor plans and ten publicly available real-SLAM maps, using four starts per map, a 0.1 m grid resolution, and a 360 ◦ sensor with ranges R ∈{5, 10 , 20 } m. All experiments assume exact localization and noise-free sensing. The KTH maps are held-out test maps from the dataset used to train the MapEx ensemble [10]. The real-SLAM set provides a broader geometric test, including two maps that depart substantially from MAPLE’s rectilinear prior. Across all evaluated approaches, frontier candidates are generated from the current partial map using the same procedure, and travel cost is computed as the shortest path through observed free space using A*. MAPLE uses N = 16 

hypotheses with αf = 0 .85 , σJ = 0 .35 , ρs = 0 .5, and 

ds = 1 .0 m. We compare MAPLE against three baselines:  

> •

Nearest: classical frontier exploration that selects the frontier candidate with minimum travel cost [3].  

> •

MapEx: a learned LaMa ensemble that predicts occu-pancy in unknown cells and scores frontier candidates using the ensemble mean and variance [10].  

> •

IG-Hector: a learned map predictor that scores each frontier candidate by the connected unknown area its prediction would reveal [7]. We use MapEx’s released LaMa ensemble [10], [22] and port its published frontier-gain computation into our common framework. For a controlled comparison, MapEx and IG-Hector retain their respective gain estimators while using the common shortest-path travel cost defined in Sec. III. We report:  

> •

Exploration performance: coverage as a function of travel, summarized by the travel required to reach 90% coverage.  

> •

Prediction quality: ROC-AUC measures occupied-versus-free discrimination across thresholds, IoU mea-sures overlap between predicted and ground-truth occu-pied cells, Brier score measures probabilistic prediction error, and NLL penalizes confident prediction errors. Evaluation uses shared partial maps generated by same-range Nearest runs at 20%, 40%, and 60% coverage. Predictions are thresholded at 0.5 for IoU, and MAPLE probabilities are the empirical occupancy frequencies across its map hypotheses. For NLL, probabilities from all methods are clipped to [10 −9, 1 − 10 −9]. 

> •

Frontier-decision quality: one-step decision regret ( ↓), measuring the percentage loss in ground-truth gain-to-travel score for the selected frontier candidate relative to the best available candidate. 

B. Exploration Performance 

Fig. 4 shows coverage as a function of travel across both map families and all three sensor ranges. MAPLE reaches 90% coverage with less travel than Nearest and IG-Hector in all six settings. Relative to Nearest, the reduction ranges from 4% to 23%, while the reduction relative to IG-Hector ranges from 11% to 25%. The comparison with MapEx depends more strongly on the map family and sensor range. On KTH, MAPLE requires 46% less travel at 5 m and 18% less at 10 m, while the two differ by only 1% at 20 m. On the real-SLAM maps, MAPLE requires 13% less travel at 5 m but 2% and 5% more at 10 m and 20 m, respectively. On the two real-SLAM maps that depart most from the rectilinear prior, MAPLE reduces travel relative to Nearest by 12–32% across sensor range on Intel Lab. On FR101 it requires 8% and 5% more travel at 5 and 10 m but 26% less at 20 m. MAPLE therefore consistently outperforms Nearest and IG-Hector, while its performance relative to MapEx varies with the map family and sensor range. We introduced MAPLE, a training-free generative prior for predictive indoor exploration that samples multiple plausible map hypotheses and evaluates frontier visibility separately within each hypothesis before averaging the resulting gains. Across KTH and real-SLAM environments, MAPLE consis-tently outperforms non-predictive exploration and remains competitive with learned predictive methods while operating on a single CPU core. Beyond end-to-end performance, the results show that cell-level prediction accuracy does not directly determine exploration quality. How map predictions are represented and evaluated also matters. The ablations reinforce this finding and show that a few map hypotheses capture most of MAPLE’s benefit. Although the Mondrian process extends to higher dimen-sions, the current MAPLE formulation and evaluation are two-dimensional, motivating future extension of its geomet-ric conditioning to 3D occupancy maps. Future work will also test MAPLE on physical robots under sensing noise and SLAM drift, and in environments that depart more strongly from its rectilinear prior. We also plan to extend MAPLE’s multi-hypothesis representation to indoor target search, where each map hypothesis can support reasoning about both unseen space and possible target locations.
