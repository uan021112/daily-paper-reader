Demonstration-Based Robotic Feeding Using
Dynamic Movement Primitives
Kenzhi Iskandar Wong1, Kitti Thamrongaphichartkul1, Sri Harsha Turlapati2, Yanshuo Lu1, Kanichi Doi3,
Boyu Ma1, Bin Zhou1, Lin Yang1, Ananda Sidarta4, Lek Syn Lim4, Arun-Kumar Kaliya-Perumal4,
Jianfei Yang1, Domenico Campolo1,4,∗
Abstract—This paper presents a demonstration-based robotic
feeding framework that integrates human demonstration, Dy-
namic Movement Primitive-based motion planning, vision-based
scene perception, and robot control. A human operator first
demonstrates the scooping and feeding motions while the position
and Euler-angle orientation of the spoon are recorded using a
motion-capture system. The recorded trajectory is segmented
into scooping and feeding motions, which are encoded using
separate Dynamic Movement Primitives. Before task execution,
an RGB-D camera estimates the participant’s mouth position,
while two AprilTags are used for robot-frame registration and
tray localization. The detected tray pose is used to position the
scooping motion, whereas the detected mouth position defines the
positional goal of the feeding motion. The approach, scooping,
and feeding trajectories are then executed sequentially by a
Kinova Gen3 manipulator through a ROS 2-based framework.
A real-world experiment demonstrates the feasibility of the
integrated pipeline and shows that the learned spoon motions
can be reproduced within the detected experimental scene.
Index Terms—assistive robotics, robot-assisted feeding, learn-
ing from demonstration, dynamic movement primitives, RGB-D
perception
I. INTRODUCTION
Robot-assisted feeding aims to support individuals with
upper-limb motor impairments while reducing reliance on con-
tinuous caregiver assistance [1, 2]. A robotic feeding system
must coordinate several components within a complete task
sequence, including food acquisition, utensil transport, mouth
localization, motion generation, and robot control.
Previous studies have addressed different components of
robot-assisted feeding, including end-to-end feeding systems
[2], bite-transfer trajectories considering efficiency and user
comfort [3], visual and haptic sensing for inside-mouth food
transfer [4], and robust mouth perception with physical-
interaction-aware control [5]. Flexible mealtime assistance
across different activities and user preferences has also
been investigated [6]. Related robotic systems have explored

human-motion retargeting, compliant rehabilitation control,

An important component of the feeding task is the gen-
eration of the spoon motion. Scooping and feeding require
task-specific trajectories in which both the position and ori-
entation of the spoon evolve throughout the motion. Learning
from demonstration provides a practical means of transferring
human-demonstrated motions to robotic systems [10], as well
as task-relevant skills and interaction strategies [11]. Among
the available representations, Dynamic Movement Primitives
(DMPs) are well suited for encoding demonstrated motions as
stable nonlinear dynamical systems shaped by learned forcing
terms [12, 13]. DMP-based approaches have also been applied
and extended to various manipulation tasks, including contact-
rich and interaction-aware scenarios [14–16].
Despite these advances, transferring demonstrated spoon
motions into a complete feeding pipeline that adapts them to
the current task scene remains a challenge. Direct replay of
a demonstrated trajectory ties the robot motion to the condi-
tions under which the demonstration was recorded, whereas
a practical feeding system should adapt the learned scooping
and feeding motions to the current bowl and mouth locations.
This paper addresses this problem through an integrated frame-
work that combines human demonstration, DMP-based motion
generation, RGB-D-based mouth localization, AprilTag-based
tray localization, and robotic execution. The learned scooping
motion is retargeted according to the detected tray location,
while the detected mouth position defines the feeding goal.
The scope of the present work covers offline motion learning
and task execution using scene information acquired before
the robot begins moving. Continuous mouth tracking and
online adaptation, food detection, and more complex food-
manipulation tasks requiring cutting or multiple utensils are
outside the current scope.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion II introduces the DMP formulation and learning proce-
dure. Section III describes the demonstration-based feeding
framework, mouth perception, task execution sequence, and
robot control. Section IV presents the experimental setup and
results. Section V discusses the main findings and limitations,
and Section VI concludes the paper.

E. Robot Control
The desired end-effector configuration ydes(t) generated is
then transmitted through ROS 2 as the reference trajectory
for a Cartesian impedance controller. The controller tracks
the desired position and orientation while providing com-
pliant end-effector behavior during task execution, following
the commonly adopted Cartesian compliance framework for
physical-interaction tasks [18]. The detected tray and mouth
positions are fixed at the beginning of the task and remain
unchanged throughout execution.

IV. EXPERIMENT
A. Experimental Setup
The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2. The robotic
feeding system consists of a 7-DoF Kinova Gen3 manipulator
equipped with a spoon at its end effector, a ZED 2i RGB-D
camera, and a tray containing a food bowl. The participant is
seated in front of the robot, with the tray positioned within
the robot workspace. A reference AprilTag attached to the
robot base is used to determine the transformation between
the camera and robot base frames, while a second AprilTag
attached to the tray is used to localize the tray relative to the
robot.
The demonstration used for DMP learning was collected
offline using a PhaseSpace motion-capture system. A single
human operator performed one complete scooping-and-feeding
demonstration using a spoon instrumented with motion-
capture markers. The Cartesian position and Euler-angle orien-
tation of the spoon were recorded to obtain the demonstrated
trajectory yd(t) described in Section III-B. The trajectory was
subsequently divided into the scooping and feeding segments
for DMP learning. The demonstrated pre- and post-scooping
positions were represented relative to the tray frame T and
used for retargeting during robot execution.
The perception, motion-planning, and robot-control mod-
ules are implemented and integrated using ROS 2. Before
each trial, the mouth position and tray pose are detected
once. The resulting mouth position and tray pose are then
used to generate the approach, scooping, and feeding motions
described in Section III-D and executed using the Cartesian
impedance controller.

V. DISCUSSION
A key advantage of the framework is that the generated
spoon motion preserves the overall shape and orientation
evolution of the demonstrated motion while allowing its initial
and final configurations to be adapted to the detected tray
pose and mouth position. Compared with direct replay of
the recorded trajectory, the DMP representation enables the
demonstrated scooping and feeding motions to be retargeted
to the current experimental scene.
The performance of the framework depends on the accuracy
of the RGB-D mouth localization and AprilTag-based frame
registration. Furthermore, because the mouth and tray poses
are estimated only before execution, the system cannot respond
to subsequent changes, such as participant motion or mouth
closure during the feeding motion. Future work will therefore
incorporate continuous mouth perception and online motion
adaptation, including spoon retraction when the mouth closes,
to improve the responsiveness and safety of the feeding task.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presented a demonstration-based robotic feeding
framework combining human demonstration, DMP-based mo-
tion generation, RGB-D perception, and robot control. Human-
demonstrated scooping and feeding motions were encoded
independently and retargeted using the detected tray and
mouth locations. A real-world experiment with a Kinova
Gen3 demonstrated the feasibility of executing the complete
approach, scooping, and feeding sequence within the perceived
experimental scene.
