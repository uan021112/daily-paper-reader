Thinker-WM: A Unified World Model with Shared Multimodal Backbone
for Embodied Intelligence
Thinker Team, Ubtech
Abstract
World models have emerged as a promising paradigm for embodied AI, yet existing systems often
remain fragmented in the modalities they model and the functions they support. In particular,
visual dynamics, geometric structure, and executable action are commonly coupled through
separate interfaces or downstream modules rather than being jointly represented and evolved
within a shared dynamics model.
In this work, we present Thinker-WM, a unified multimodal world model that jointly models and
generates RGB, metric depth, and robot action through a shared intermediate Unified Dynamics
Transformer with modality-specific encoders and decoders. The model performs multimodal
unification at the representation level: appearance, geometry, and action are transformed into a
common dynamic representation and jointly evolved by shared parameters, while their distinct
output structures are preserved. The same parameterization supports multiple functions, including
joint video–action generation, world prediction, policy inference, action-conditioned generation,
video-to-action generation, and inverse dynamics. To examine whether the shared representation
captures action-related information, we introduce a lightweight motion probe that shows the
frozen visual representation contains recoverable motion information.
Thinker-WM is trained on a heterogeneous corpus comprising approximately 20K hours of
egocentric video, 10K hours of real-robot data, and 1K hours of simulation data, including
approximately 4K hours of real-robot data with aligned depth supervision. Extensive experiments
demonstrate strong performance across embodied world-model evaluations. Thinker-WM ranks
first on EWMBench, WorldOlympiad and WorldArena, while controlled studies further
demonstrate the benefits of action conditioning, geometric interaction, and multi-function
training within the shared representation.

Introduction
World models aim to learn how an environment evolves over time, enabling an agent to anticipate the
consequences of its interventions rather than react only to the current observation. Early work developed
compact latent simulators for planning and reinforcement learning [8, 9], while recent approaches increasingly
leverage large-scale generative models with broader visual and temporal priors [5, 11, 24, 33, 38]. For
embodied intelligence, however, predicting future appearance alone is insufficient: the evolution of the
world is simultaneously reflected in visual observations, physical geometry, executable actions, and task
intent. Actions change the environment, the resulting state constrains subsequent actions, and task intent
determines the desired evolution. This motivates world-action models (WAMs), which jointly model future
world states and the actions that produce them [3, 21, 39, 45].
Recent WAMs have made substantial progress by bringing video and action modeling into a common
generative framework. Different approaches, however, place the coupling at different levels. Some systems
compose modality-specific experts or maintain distinct processing pathways for different modalities [3, 21].
Others use a shared transformer and unify video and action through diffusion or noise configurations,
while retaining separate modality channels [6, 14, 45]. Omnimodal world models further broaden the set
of modeled modalities, but may retain embodiment-specific projections around a shared backbone [25].

Geometry has also been introduced into world-action modeling, for example through geometric-semantic
prediction or lightweight spatial representations supervised by future depth [17, 19, 37]. These approaches
demonstrate that combining complementary signals can improve embodied world modeling. Nevertheless,
the modalities are often connected through interfaces, conditioning mechanisms, or modality-specific
parameterizations rather than being expressed within a common representation that jointly carries future-
world dynamics. This raises a fundamental question: where should multimodal unification take place in a
general world-action model? We argue that the key is not merely to make multiple modalities available to
the same network, but to let them evolve within a shared representation in which their common structure
can be learned directly.
We therefore propose Thinker-WM, a unified multimodal world model that jointly models and generates
RGB, depth, and action under task-level conditioning. The central design principle is representation-level
unification. Each modality is first transformed by a modality-specific encoder into a shared intermediate
representation. A common Transformer stage then models their joint evolution, while modality-specific
decoders render the resulting representation back into RGB, depth, or executable action. The main
unification therefore occurs inside the Unified Dynamic Transformer rather than only at its input or output
interfaces. Within it, visual, geometric, and action tokens interact through common attention mechanism,
while task-level representations provide structured guidance for future prediction. The resulting formulation
treats appearance, geometry, action, and instruction as different manifestations of a coupled world process,
while retaining modality-specific interfaces where the underlying physical representations necessarily differ.
Figure 1 gives an overview of the data mixture, the architecture, and the open-loop use of the same network.
This representation-level formulation also allows geometry to become an explicit component of the
generated world state. Thinker-WM represents depth using the same latent and spatiotemporal organization
as visual generation, allowing geometric information to participate throughout the shared generative process.
Geometric supervision therefore shapes the shared representation rather than being applied only through an
auxiliary prediction branch. This makes physical structure directly available to the model alongside visual
appearance and action, providing complementary information about the spatial relationships that govern
embodied interaction. The objective is not to treat geometry as a complete solution to physical reasoning,
but to make metric structure a native signal within the world model.
A unified representation is valuable only when the training distribution captures the diversity of
2


embodied dynamics encountered in the real world. Thinker-WM is trained from a heterogeneous mixture
of general video data, publicly available robot datasets, in-house robot data, and egocentric human video.
General video provides broad visual and temporal priors; robot data provides action-grounded supervision
across different embodiments, environments, and tasks; and egocentric video expands coverage of real-world
interactions and human motion [7, 10, 13, 43]. These sources differ substantially in sensor configuration,
action representation, embodiment, and annotation format. Thinker-WM therefore maps heterogeneous
robot actions into a common representation at the model boundary, allowing shared dynamics to be learned
across embodiments while preserving robot-specific decoding. In this way, data diversity and architectural
unification reinforce each other: diverse sources broaden the distribution of world dynamics, while the
shared representation provides a common space in which those dynamics can be learned.
We evaluate Thinker-WM across embodied world-model benchmarks and a range of generative
settings, including action-conditioned future generation, depth generation, long-horizon rollout, trajectory
transfer, video-to-action generation, and instruction-conditioned generation. These experiments examine
whether a shared multimodal representation can support world prediction, geometric modeling, and action
generation within a single model. Thinker-WM ranked first on EWMBench [12], WorldOlympiad [42] and
WorldArena [29], while additional experiments demonstrate its ability to support multiple world-model and
world-action conditionals within the same parameterization.
The main contributions of this work are:
• Representation-level multimodal unification. We introduce the Unified Dynamic Transformer
in which RGB, depth, action, and task-level conditioning are lifted into a shared intermediate
representation and transformed by common parameters, placing multimodal interaction inside the
representation rather than only at the input or output interfaces.
• Geometry as part of the generative world state. We incorporate depth as a native generative
modality that shares the latent and spatiotemporal structure of visual modeling and whose supervision
directly shapes the shared representation, making physical geometry an explicit component of the
modeled world dynamics.
• Scalable heterogeneous embodied learning. We combine general video, publicly available robot
data, in-house robot data, and egocentric human video, together with a common action representation
across embodiments, to provide complementary visual, geometric, and action supervision for scalable
world-action modeling.

Related Work
We view world models as generative models that learn how an environment evolves under observations,
conditions, and interventions. Their role has gradually expanded from compact predictive simulators to
large-scale generative models with broad visual priors, and more recently to models that jointly generate
future observations and executable actions. From this perspective, we organize prior work into three stages:
simulator-oriented world models, latent and generative world models, and world models that generate
actions alongside the future. We further discuss recent efforts to introduce explicit geometric structure into
this unified formulation, which provides the closest context for Thinker-WM.
2.1
Simulator-oriented World Models
Early world models were developed primarily as learned simulators for model-based planning and
reinforcement learning. World Models [8] compress observations into a compact latent space, model
3


temporal dynamics with a recurrent mixture-density network, and enable agents to train policies inside
the model’s imagined rollouts. PlaNet and the subsequent Dreamer family [9] further demonstrate that a
learned latent dynamics model can support planning and behavior learning without requiring the policy
to interact with the real environment at every step. DreamerV3 [9] in particular shows that a common
world-modeling recipe can operate across a broad collection of tasks and domains under a largely fixed
training configuration.
The central abstraction in this family is a predictive transition model,
zt+1 ∼pθ(zt+1 | zt, at),
(1)
where zt is a learned latent state and at specifies an intervention. The model therefore functions primarily
as a simulator: given a current state and an action, it predicts a plausible future state on which planning or
control can be performed. This formulation is computationally efficient and well suited to model-based
decision making, but the learned latent space is usually optimized around the environments and tasks
encountered during training. Consequently, the visual prior and open-world knowledge available in
large-scale image and video corpora are not directly inherited by the dynamics model.
2.2
Latent and Generative World Models
Recent work increasingly treats the world model itself as a large-scale generative model rather than a
compact task-specific simulator. This shift replaces manually designed or task-specific latent dynamics with
generative models capable of capturing richer visual structure and broader physical priors. GAIA-1 [11], for
example, learns an action-conditioned generative model of driving scenes from large-scale fleet data, while
Genie [5] learns a controllable latent interface directly from large collections of video without requiring
explicit action annotations. UniSim [38] further combines image, robotics, and navigation data to construct
a general-purpose simulator whose outputs can represent the visual consequences of both high-level
instructions and low-level controls. iVideoGPT [33] integrates action-conditioned video prediction with
visual planning and model-based reinforcement learning, while Pandora [35] allows language-conditioned
interventions throughout a generated trajectory rather than restricting the condition to the initial frame.
Large-scale video generation models provide another important foundation for this transition. Rather
than learning visual dynamics from scratch, recent systems exploit pretrained video-generation backbones
to inherit strong priors over appearance, motion, and scene structure. The Cosmos World Foundation
Model [24], for example, positions a pretrained generative world model as a reusable foundation for
downstream world-model construction and synthetic data generation. Xiaomi-Robotics-U0 [16] similarly
explores broad multimodal generation and embodied scene synthesis, positioning a world foundation model
as both a generative world model and a scalable source of downstream training data. These systems
demonstrate that large-scale visual pretraining can substantially broaden the knowledge available to a world
model beyond the distribution of a single embodied environment.
Despite their generality, however, the action interface of these models is often indirect. Actions may
be represented as latent controls, natural language commands, or environment-specific interfaces rather
than as executable physical robot actions. In other cases, the generated visual trajectory is treated as a
simulator for a separate downstream policy, or the model serves as a data engine whose generated embodied
data improves a separately trained policy [16]. Thus, the generative world model provides the predictive
component of control while the executable action model remains outside the generative process. This
separation motivates the next stage of world-model development: jointly modeling the future world and the
action that produces it.
2.3
World Models with Action
This stage of the progression extends generative world modeling by incorporating an executable action
space into the same generative process as future-world prediction. Instead of the sequential formulation

where a video generator is followed by a separate inverse-dynamics model, these approaches seek to model
video and action jointly. The resulting formulation allows the model to learn not only what the world may
look like after an intervention, but also which physical action is associated with that evolution.
Motus [3] is an early example of this unified formulation.
It combines pretrained experts for
vision-language understanding, video generation, inverse dynamics, and policy learning within a Mixture-
of-Transformers architecture, allowing several world-modeling and control distributions to be represented in
one system. To exploit action-free video, Motus additionally introduces latent actions derived from visual
motion and uses a multi-stage curriculum to progressively bridge video generation and robot control. This
demonstrates that large-scale video priors and action learning can be combined within a single generative
framework, while physical embodiment grounding is still largely introduced at the later robot-specific stage.
MotuBrain [21] further develops the unified formulation with a three-stream architecture and a data
pyramid spanning web video, egocentric human video, and heterogeneous robot trajectories. In particular, it
treats egocentric video as video-only supervision rather than requiring every video to have an explicit robot
action label. Its action representation is also expressed relative to the conditioning frame, with relative
position and rotation providing a more transferable interface across robot configurations. These choices are
closely related to the setting considered in this work, where action-free egocentric video and heterogeneous
robot trajectories must coexist during training. However, MotuBrain retains embodiment-specific physical
scales rather than explicitly registering the action statistics of different robots at a shared model boundary,
and its temporal instruction structure is primarily episode-level rather than aligned to individual instruction
segments.
A complementary line unifies video and action through diffusion noise rather than through separate
modality-specific architectures. Diffusion Forcing [6] assigns independent noise levels to different temporal
tokens, enabling a single denoising model to express multiple conditional distributions under different
temporal masks. UWM [45] applies this principle to robot learning by sharing a transformer between
image and action diffusion processes, allowing the same model to operate as a policy, a world model,
or a joint video-action model depending on the masking configuration. Diffusion ReRoll [14] further
introduces asymmetric SNR-based reweighting for image and action losses. These methods provide a
flexible probabilistic view of world modeling with action, although action and image remain separate
diffusion channels and their embodiment-specific representations are not the primary focus.
Another direction expands the scope of modality unification. Cosmos 3 [25] jointly models language,
image, video, audio, and action using a shared multimodal backbone, with domain-aware input and output
projections that carry each embodiment into a shared latent action space. Ego and effector poses are
encoded as frame-to-frame relative transforms, while grasp states directly encode the current manipulation
state, and large-scale egocentric human video supplies additional pseudo-action supervision.
Recent work has additionally introduced explicit geometric structure into this joint generative process.
GeoSem-WAM [19] augments joint world and action learning with geometric and semantic future prediction,
showing that structured spatial information can provide action-relevant supervision beyond RGB appearance.
WAM4D [17] introduces lightweight spatial register tokens supervised by future depth to inject 4D geometric
priors into a video-action model, while removing the geometry branch during action inference for efficiency.
These results suggest that metric geometry can provide a useful intermediate representation for connecting
visual dynamics and physical interaction. In these approaches, however, geometry is primarily introduced
as an auxiliary supervisory signal or intermediate representation rather than as a modality that is jointly
generated throughout the same generative process.
DreamZero [39] provides the closest instance of the pretrained-backbone route. It builds a robot
foundation model on top of a large image-to-video diffusion backbone and jointly denoises visual trajectories
and robot actions under a shared objective, showing that preserving a strong visual generation prior improves
downstream robot control relative to vision-language-action baselines. Its remaining boundaries are of a
different kind: the instruction is supplied by a frozen text encoder rather than produced by the generative
process, the model is pretrained separately for each embodiment, and its action target stays at relative joint
positions rather than a shared end-effector representation. Zero-WAM [44] extends the same direction to
in-context human-video supervision, but keeps separate query, key, and value projections, feed-forward
5


networks, and output heads per modality, so that video and action meet only inside the shared attention layers.
The two therefore share at different depths: one transformer with separated decoders in DreamZero [39],
shared attention over separated parameters in Zero-WAM [44].
2.4
Position of Thinker-WM
The progression above moves from world models as compact simulators, to large-scale generative models
with transferable visual priors, and finally to world models that jointly predict the future world and executable
actions. Thinker-WM follows the latter formulation while extending it in three directions. First, it preserves
a pretrained flow-matching video-generation backbone and incorporates robot and egocentric data without
reducing action-free video to an action-labeled proxy. Second, it treats appearance, metric geometry, and
action as co-evolving outputs of a shared intermediate representation rather than using geometry only as an
auxiliary training signal. Third, it registers heterogeneous robot actions at the model boundary using a
common relative end-effector representation and per-robot statistics, allowing different embodiments to
share the same generative space while retaining exact robot-specific decoding.
Architecturally, Thinker-WM therefore places the main unification inside the UDT. Modality-specific
encoders and decoders translate video, geometry, action, and language into a common latent interface, where
shared attention and feed-forward transformations model their joint evolution. The resulting formulation
treats the world model not merely as a simulator for a downstream policy, but as a unified generative model
in which visual dynamics, physical structure, and executable action are different manifestations of the same
underlying world process.

Model Architecture
3.1
Overview: Representation-Level Unified World Model
A world model for embodied intelligence must capture more than the visual appearance of the future. The
evolution of an embodied environment is jointly reflected in what the agent observes, the geometric structure
of the scene, and the actions that cause the observed changes. Thinker-WM therefore models RGB video,
metric depth, and robot action within a single generative framework, rather than treating action generation
as a downstream module of visual prediction.
Given an observation ot and a task-level condition c, Thinker-WM models the joint distribution of future
visual, geometric, and action trajectories as
p (vt+1:t+H, dt+1:t+H, at+1:t+H|ot, c) ,
(3)
where v, d, and a denote RGB video, metric depth, and robot action, respectively. The key design principle is
representation-level unification: each generative modality is first encoded by a modality-specific encoder
into an intermediate dynamic representation. These representations then feed into a Unified Dynamics
Transformer (UDT) to participate in the shared dynamic evolution. The outputs are subsequently separated
and decoded through modality-specific generation head.
This design satisfied two requirements that are often conflated in multimodal models. On the one hand,
RGB, depth, and action have fundamentally different input statistics and output semantics and therefore
require dedicated interfaces. On the other hand, their future evolution is physically coupled: actions alter the
environment, visual observations reveal the consequences, and metric geometry constrains both perception
and interaction.
Thinker-WM is initialized from a pretrained Wan2.2 TI2V-5B video generation backbone and extends
it with dedicated depth and action pathways. A frozen Qwen3-VL-2B [1] model provides task-level

treated as a fourth generative stream. The resulting information flow can be summarized as

3.2
Multimodal Representation
3.2.1
Language Representation
Language is represented as a task-level conditioning sequence rather than a fourth generative stream. The
structured prompt is processed by a frozen Qwen3-VL-2B [1]. Its final hidden states are truncated to at
most 1000 tokens and projected into the model width through a linear bridge followed by four residual MLP
blocks, producing the conditioning sequence hund.
Instructions from heterogeneous datasets are normalized into a structured three-field prompt containing
Task, Scene, and Action. The fixed schema separates the task objective from the scene description and the
intended operation, reducing variation in prompt format across data sources and providing a consistent
semantic structure for the language encoder. Prompt structure is further regularized during training.
Redundant occurrences of the field are merged, the order of the three fields is randomly shuffled, and
random field dropout is applied. Evaluation uses a fixed Task–Scene–Action order. These transformations
encourage the model to rely on semantic content rather than field position or the presence of a complete
schema.
3.2.2
Visual Representation
RGB frames and depth maps are both compressed by the frozen Wan2.2 VAE into the same latent topology.
At 640×480 resolution, a window of 49 frames is mapped to a latent grid of 13×30×40, and patchification
with kernel (1, 2, 2) produces 13 × 15 × 20 = 3900 tokens for each visual stream. Using the same VAE and
spatiotemporal organization allows RGB and depth to occupy compatible latent coordinates while retaining
distinct modality semantics.
The two visual modalities nevertheless use separate input projections. RGB tokens are mapped
directly into the model width, while depth tokens use their own patch embedding followed by a learnable
stream embedding that distinguishes geometric information from appearance. After multimodal dynamics
modeling, the two streams are mapped back through modality-specific output projections. Thus, RGB and
depth share a common latent topology, but their input and output representations remain explicitly separated
where their underlying statistics differ. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting visual encoding paths.

modeled explicitly.

5.2
Quality Control and Gating
Quality control is implemented as a set of boundaries along the sample path, each with different granularity
and cost. Offline gates operate on whole Episodes and windows, rejecting timelines with non-finite values
or magnitudes beyond per-source guards, filtering stationary windows, and excluding Episodes shorter than
a training window. Egocentric recordings receive an additional filter that removes windows dominated
by camera self-motion, keeping only start positions whose rotation and translation stay within calibrated
thresholds. Online gates at load time validate action and state ranges before video decoding, retry missing
or corrupt videos against a bounded number of alternates, and blacklist persistent failures. Batch-level
checks validate shapes, value ranges, finiteness, and the model input contract; a genuinely absent modality is
marked rather than filled. At the step level, samples with extreme unified-action magnitudes have their loss
weight zeroed, non-finite values are replaced before zeroing, and any step affected by non-finite parameters,
15


loss, or gradients is skipped. The depth stream carries its own gates: a selection file marks usable Episodes,
depth frames are checked against video frames for alignment, unreadable depth is marked absent and loses
its loss share, and depths are clamped to the codec range.

5.4
Load Balancing across Ranks
In synchronous training, all ranks must update the same parameters at the same step. If ranks train different
branch subsets, the reduction operates over different tensor sets and can deadlock under ZeRO-1. We
therefore make the function mode a pure function of the step number, sampled from configured proportions
with a generator seeded only by that step, persisted with the checkpoint, and advanced identically by every
rank. This guarantees agreement across ranks and across restarts. The same reasoning requires identical
token layouts: windows are cut to fixed lengths so no padding is needed and every rank carries the same
number of tokens. Figure 10 illustrates the arrangement.
Source assignment offers two deterministic schedules. The default assigns the same source to every
rank at a given step, keeping batches internally consistent. The alternative distributes rank slots across
sources in proportion to their weights, mixing the corpus more finely at the risk of stragglers. Both require
no inter-rank communication. Losses are averaged across ranks, and non-finite and outlier signals are
reduced so that a condition seen by one rank is acted on by all.

5.5
Evaluation Orchestration
Evaluation runs every fixed number of steps on a fixed set of samples, with deterministic assignment to
ranks derived from the same weights as the training mixture. To avoid competing with training for GPUs, a
background process decodes evaluation clips into a shared-storage cache and writes a completion manifest.
The foreground evaluation only reads the cache, so prepared evaluations take seconds and never occupy a
GPU needed by training. The cache is content-keyed and reused across restarts and runs; an evaluation
whose cache is not ready is skipped rather than stalling training. Out-of-memory shards are rerun with
smaller micro-batches, keeping the evaluated set complete, and results join the same logging channel as
training metrics.

Training Strategy
Thinker-WM supports multiple control modes and is trained on heterogeneous data sources. Large-scale

6.2
Stage-wise Parameter and Geometry Schedule
We initialize from the Wan2.2 TI2V-5B backbone [30] and reuse its pretrained blocks, patch embeddings,
modulation layers, and positional geometry across all three architectural depths (Section 3.2). Stage 0
leaves these weights untouched. Stage 1 updates the visual pathway only, adapting the inherited prior to
robot-view and first-person statistics without disturbing branches that have no counterpart in the pretrained
model. From Stage 2 onward, all trainable parameters are active: the action tower, the geometric pathway,
and the shared UDT are optimized jointly. The frozen components remain fixed throughout, as the VAE
and the VLM keep their pretrained weights.
Geometry enters in Stage 2 and is integrated into the shared representation so that the model jointly
predicts future observations, geometry, and actions. While the mixture is dominated by data with coarse
or partial depth annotation, the geometric channel is one-way: geometry reads appearance and action,
but neither reads geometry, so the geometric objective shapes the shared weights only through its own
loss gradient. Once training is restricted to datasets with accurate depth, which in this curriculum is the
target-robot fine-tuning stage, the remaining direction is opened so that appearance and action also read the
geometric stream (Figures 3 and 4).

Motion Probe
Chapters 3 and 6 establish that Thinker-WM jointly models RGB, depth, and action through a shared
Unified Dynamics Transformer (UDT). A natural question is whether the shared representation truly
captures cross-modal coupling, or merely processes modalities in parallel. We introduce Motion Probe, a
lightweight diagnostic module that evaluates whether motion information can be recovered from the shared
video representation, thereby providing direct evidence for the effectiveness of the UDT. Motion probe’s
effect is evaluated in Section 8.2.4.
7.1
Probe Design and Offline Motion Teacher
The probe rests on a simple logic: if the UDT truly unifies appearance and action, the video tokens should
carry sufficient motion information to reconstruct end-effector displacement aligned with the executed
action, even without action-specific supervision on the video pathway. We test this by attaching a lightweight
Motion Head to the frozen UDT video tokens. The head is deliberately small—spatial pooling followed
by temporal regression—so that its capacity cannot compensate for missing motion information in the
shared representation. The UDT weights are kept frozen throughout the probe, and only the Motion Head is
trained.

8.1.1
EWMBench Evaluation

for embodied world models. It scores a generated video along eight dimensions covering visual scene
consistency, end-effector motion correctness, and semantic alignment with the instruction, and reports
their sum as Overall. Its standard evaluation set contains 21 episodes drawn from 7 manipulation tasks.

and the official instruction, the best candidate is selected by Hausdorff distance, and each dimension is
normalized to [0, 1] and averaged over all valid trials, 63 videos in total. Generation uses 48 frames at
a resolution of 480 × 640 with 50 denoising steps and a classifier-free guidance scale of 5.0. Since the
benchmark computes its metrics at 30 FPS, generated results are upsampled from 5 FPS to 30 FPS so that
the comparison follows the protocol on which the benchmark is defined.

8.2
Ablation and Analysis
We conduct a set of controlled experiments to examine the functional behavior of Thinker-WM and the role
of its shared multimodal representation. The experiments address four complementary questions. First,
does the model actually consume an action sequence when it is provided as a condition? Second, does
depth contribute to downstream control beyond improving geometric prediction, and does the direction of
RGB–depth interaction matter? Third, can the six function modes share the same UDT parameters without
introducing substantial optimization conflict? Finally, can additional motion-level supervision improve
action prediction without degrading the existing video representation?

8.2.3
Gradient Compatibility across Function Modes
The function modes of Thinker-WM are optimized with a shared set of UDT parameters. This design raises
a basic optimization question: do the different modes provide compatible training signals, or do some
objectives interfere with one another? We investigate this question directly through the geometry of the loss
gradients on the shared UDT.
For each function mode, we evaluate its loss on the same training batches and with the same noise
realization, while forcing the modes to run independently on the shared weights. We compute pairwise
cosine similarity between the resulting gradients with respect to the shared UDT parameters, together
with the corresponding gradient norms. The measurement is repeated at five checkpoints spanning the
joint-training stage, so that the observed geometry can be separated from checkpoint-specific variation.
Figure 14 summarizes the results. First, the shared UDT shows no measurable pairwise gradient
conflict: the minimum cosine similarity across the six modes is approximately −0.005, within the variation
observed for pairs whose true alignment is near zero. Most mode pairs are therefore either aligned or
approximately orthogonal rather than negatively aligned. Second, the degree of alignment follows the
functional relationship between the modes. The joint WAM mode shows its strongest alignment with
the inverse-dynamics, unconditional, and policy modes, with cosine similarities of 0.19, 0.17, and 0.14,
respectively. In contrast, mode pairs with less overlap in their supervised outputs remain close to zero,
including 0.000 between the WM and VLA modes and −0.001 between the A2V and V2A modes. This
structure is consistent with the functional overlap among the different training objectives.
The auxiliary modes also produce substantial optimization signals on the shared UDT. Their mean
gradient norms range from 0.34 to 0.50, compared with 0.68 for the joint WAM mode. Thus, the near-
orthogonal pairs do not result from vanishing gradients; the corresponding objectives actively update the
shared representation. The same pattern is more pronounced on the action branch, where the gradient
alignment with the inverse-dynamics, unconditional, and policy modes reaches 0.36, 0.35, and 0.28,
respectively. This localization is consistent with the fact that these objectives directly supervise the action
pathway.
The action-conditioned mode provides a complementary case. Its gradient alignment is close to
orthogonal on both the shared UDT and the action branch, with cosine similarities of 0.037 and 0.006,
respectively. Attention measurements on the same checkpoints show a strongly asymmetric interaction

8.3.4
Zero-Shot Manipulation across Diverse Scenes
Our model is trained exclusively on demonstrations collected under the clean configuration of RoboTwin,
with a fixed camera configuration and no viewpoint variation in the training data. The baseline uses this
original head-camera configuration, with a downward viewing angle of approximately 52◦relative to the
horizontal plane. At evaluation time, we directly deploy the trained model in scenes with randomized
backgrounds and distractor objects, using either the baseline view or a steeper 65◦view absent from training,
without additional fine-tuning. Here, zero-shot refers to transfer to scene and camera configurations not
encountered during training, rather than to unseen task categories.
Figure 23 presents successful executions of two tasks: pressing an alarm clock and grasping a roller
with both arms, each specified using a new instruction phrasing. The two baseline rollouts share the same
initial scene and object arrangement, allowing us to demonstrate different task executions conditioned on
the instruction while holding the visual input fixed. The model selects and manipulates the instructed target
despite the presence of other objects. Both tasks are also successfully executed under the 65◦camera view
in another scene with a different background. These examples demonstrate qualitative transfer from clean,
fixed-view training data to more visually varied evaluation scenes, including a camera configuration absent
from training. Because the background and camera configuration change together in the displayed examples,
the figure illustrates joint scene and viewpoint generalization rather than an isolated viewpoint effect.
Beyond these qualitative examples, we further evaluate our model on all 50 tasks of RoboTwin 2.0 under
the standard Clean and Randomized settings. Following the benchmark’s closed-loop evaluation protocol,

Conclusion and Future Work
This work presented Thinker-WM, a unified multimodal world model that jointly models RGB, depth,
and robot action within a shared intermediate representation. Modality-specific encoders and decoders
preserve the distinct structure of each modality, while a shared Unified Dynamics Transformer (UDT)
models their joint evolution with a single set of weights. This design supports multiple world-action
functions under one parameterization, including joint video-action generation, world modeling, policy
inference, action-conditioned generation, video-to-action generation, and inverse dynamics. Trained on
approximately 20K hours of egocentric video, 10K hours of robot data, and 1K hours of simulation data,
about 4K hours with aligned depth, Thinker-WM uses a staged curriculum with replay to stabilize shared
34


representations and specialize to the deployed robot. Across benchmarks, it achieves the best Overall score
on EWMBench (4.66), ranks first on WorldOlympiad Robotics with an All score of 0.740, and obtains the
highest 12-metric EWMScore of 71.73 on WorldArena Track-1. Supplying a recorded action sequence as
a clean condition improves Overall from 4.66 to 5.03, with HSD increasing from 0.5638 to 0.7369 and
nDTW from 0.7102 to 0.8736. Additional experiments show that bidirectional RGB-depth interaction raises
closed-loop success on RoboTwin from 60.0% to 75.0%, and that the frozen video representation contains
recoverable action-relevant motion information. These results support representation-level unification
as a viable foundation for embodied world-action modeling, while also highlighting the need for further
validation in real-world closed-loop settings.
Several directions remain open. First, although Thinker-WM achieves promising closed-loop control
in simulation (e.g., RoboTwin), its effectiveness as a closed-loop policy on real robots remains to be
established. Conducting real-world closed-loop manipulation experiments, evaluating robustness to real-
world perturbations, and investigating sim-to-real transfer of the shared action interface are critical next
steps. Second, geometric supervision covers only a subset of the training mixture; extending depth-aware
learning to larger collections of egocentric and general video could further strengthen physical representation
learning. Third, long-horizon generation is currently performed through inference-time autoregressive
rollout; training directly on model-generated trajectories and quantitatively evaluating long-horizon drift
remain open. Finally, scaling the model and training mixture, understanding how the geometric channel,
data composition, and training curriculum should evolve with scale, and optimizing the sampling ratios of
the six function modes under a fixed optimization budget are additional directions for future study.
