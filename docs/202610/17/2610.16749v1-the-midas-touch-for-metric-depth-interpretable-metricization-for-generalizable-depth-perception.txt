# The Midas Touch for Metric Depth: Interpretable Metricization for Generalizable Depth Perception 

Mengtan Zhang , Yu Ma , Shiqi Jiang , Yi Feng , Wei Ye , Member, IEEE ,Xieyuanli Chen , Member, IEEE , Hanli Wang , Senior Member, IEEE ,Qijun Chen , Senior Member, IEEE , and Rui Fan , Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Recent foundation models have drastically improved the generalization of monocular relative depth estimation, yet recovering accurate metric depth from their estimates for depth perception tasks remains challenging due to scale ambiguity and local metric-scale inconsistency. Existing approaches recover metric depth from sparse observations using either explicit global affine transformation algorithms or implicit data-driven depth completion networks. The former cannot accommodate spatially varying relative-to-metric relationships, while the latter are sensitive to the distribution of sparse observations and may not fully exploit generalizable relative depth priors. To simultane-ously address these issues, this study extends our previous work MTD and proposes MTDv2, a hybrid metric depth perception framework that combines explicit depth metricization with data-driven metric depth refinement. The explicit depth metricization process performs segment-wise affine transformations to recover spatially varying metric scales while preserving the geometric structure of relative depth, followed by pixel-wise depth re-finement that propagates metric observations along paths with minimal accumulated geometric variation. The data-driven depth refinement process subsequently extends the explicit single-path depth propagation to a learnable multi-path formulation while preserving its interpretability. Extensive experiments conducted on 16 public datasets demonstrate the robust and generalizable depth scale recovery capability of MTDv2 across diverse sources of sparse observations and different depth estimation paradigms, as well as its broad applicability to downstream 3D perception tasks. Code will be publicly available at https://mias.group/MTD upon publication. 

Index Terms —Foundation model, depth perception, depth completion, scale recovery.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Monocular depth perception has become a fundamental component of robotics and autonomous driving [1]. Recent foundation models, including the MiDaS series [2], [3] and the Depth Anything series [1], [4], have significantly improved zero-shot generalization by learning rich geometric priors from large-scale data. Nonetheless, relative depth alone remains insufficient for a variety of real-world 3D applications, where metric scale and geometric consistency are indispensable [5]. This limitation is particularly pronounced in downstream tasks such as novel view synthesis (NVS) and visual odometry (VO), where visually plausible yet metrically ambiguous depth esti-mates can lead to inconsistent 3D reconstruction or unreliable spatial reasoning. A straightforward approach to recovering metric scale is to align relative depth predictions with available sparse metric observations through global least-squares fitting [2], [4], [6]. Nevertheless, this explicit global alignment assumes that a single pair of affine transformation parameters is shared across the entire image, which is often overly restrictive [2]. This limitation arises because different objects, surfaces, and local regions exhibit distinct relative-to-metric relationships between the relative depth predicted by foundation models trained with mixed supervision and the ground-truth metric depth [1], preventing accurate metric depth recovery across the entire image. Another line of research investigates data-driven monocular depth completion, where RGB images and sparse metric ob-servations are jointly processed by deep neural networks [7]– [11]. Given sufficient training data, these methods can achieve impressive performance [7], [12], [13]. Nevertheless, their generalization remains limited because neural networks may struggle to robustly encode sparse metric observations under varying metric observation densities, spatial distributions, and scene domains [7], [14]. To alleviate this limitation, sev-eral studies first densify sparse observations using explicit preprocessing algorithms, such as morphological completion, interpolation [12], or Poisson-style reconstruction [9], thereby providing coarse dense depth estimates for subsequent neural inference. When sparse observations are of satisfactory qual-ity, such explicit preprocessing algorithms can produce more informative inputs and facilitate depth completion. However, under challenging conditions where metric observations are extremely sparse, highly noisy, or nonuniformly distributed, these explicit preprocessing algorithms may fail to produce geometrically reliable or sufficiently informative coarse depth arXiv:2610.16749v1 [cs.CV] 12 Oct 2026 2(b) Generalizable Depth Perception (a) Interpretable Depth  Metricization

Consequently, the network must compensate for preprocessing errors during training, which increases the learning burden and reduces robustness when the test-time scene domain differs substantially from that of the training data [7], [12], [14], [15]. This motivates the development of a standalone explicit metricization algorithm that provides an interpretable formu-lation with broad applicability. Following this direction, a preliminary version of this work was presented at CVPR 2026, introducing the Midas Touch for Depth (MTD) [16], a training-free framework capable of converting relative depth into metric depth using sparse observations. MTD estimates a pair of affine transformation parameters within each image segment, propagates these parameters over a sparse segment graph, and performs discontinuity-aware pixel-level depth re-finement through dynamic programming. This explicit design naturally accommodates diverse forms of sparse observa-tions, including LiDAR points, disparities, and correspondence matches, without requiring task-specific training. Nonetheless, the training-free nature of MTD also intro-duces a practical limitation on achievable accuracy. Within the explicit depth metricization process, imperfect image segmen-tation can lead to large cross-segment depth errors. In addition, handcrafted operators, manually tuned hyperparameters, and predefined propagation rules may produce suboptimal depth propagation results and leave residual depth errors, particularly in complex scenarios. While these limitations do not diminish the value of the explicit algorithm, they motivate leveraging the strengths of deep learning to model local depth variations in complex scenarios. Importantly, such capabilities should be incorporated in a complementary manner that does not compromise the interpretability of the explicit formulation. Therefore, this article extends our previous work into the Midas Touch for Depth V2 (MTDv2), a hybrid depth per-ception framework that combines explicit optimization-based depth metricization with data-driven metric depth refinement. The optimization-based depth metricization process largely follows MTD to explicitly resolve the relative-to-metric am-biguity, while making further improvements by incorporating depth information into local region segmentation, producing segments that are more geometrically and texturally coherent. This design improves the consistency of the independently estimated affine transformation parameters, thereby reducing the burden on the subsequent data-driven component to correct large cross-segment depth errors and allowing it to focus on fine-grained depth refinement. Subsequently, the explicitly metricized depth map serves as the initialization for data-driven depth refinement and provides dense geometric cues for the depth propagation head, complementing the original sparse observations. The geometry-guided depth propagation head predicts residual corrections to the geodesic costs and propagated depth estimates, while simultaneously producing propagation logits. These three outputs are jointly used to for-mulate an implicit multi-path depth propagation mechanism. Consequently, the predicted residual corrections improve local geometric fidelity and smoothness, while the multi-path depth propagation process remains consistent with the interpretable formulation of explicit optimization. MTDv2 therefore retains the flexibility and interpretability of the explicit metricization in MTD while further benefiting from the accuracy and adaptability of data-driven metric depth refinement. To validate the effectiveness and real-world applicability of the proposed MTDv2 framework, this article conducts extensive experiments on diverse depth perception benchmarks and downstream tasks. The experimental results demonstrate that MTDv2 achieves state-of-the-art (SoTA) zero-shot depth completion performance across different sources of sparse observations, including LiDAR points, RGB-D camera mea-3

surements, and correspondence matches, as well as different levels of observation sparsity, with particularly strong advan-tages when the metric observations are extremely sparse. In addition, MTDv2 is compatible with different depth estima-tion paradigms, enabling generalizable metric scale recovery. Finally, experiments on two representative downstream tasks, NVS and VO, further demonstrate the broad applicability of MTDv2. As a journal version of our preliminary work [16], this study makes substantial methodological and experimental exten-sions, with the additional contributions summarized as follows: 1) MTDv2, a unified hybrid framework that extends our previous work, MTD, by integrating explicit depth metricization with data-driven metric depth refinement, thereby leveraging the complementary strengths of both paradigms for depth perception. 2) An enhanced optimization-based depth metricization pro-cess that extends MTD by incorporating complementary geometric and textural cues into local region segmenta-tion, reducing cross-segment depth errors and the burden on subsequent data-driven refinement. 3) A data-driven depth refinement algorithm featuring a geometry-guided depth propagation head derived from the discontinuity-aware geodesic principle of MTD, en-abling learnable yet interpretable local depth refinement to improve geometric fidelity and smoothness. 4) Expanded experimental evaluations across diverse datasets, sparse observation sources, observation densities, and depth estimation paradigms, demonstrating improvements of MTDv2 over MTD in accuracy, robustness, and generalizability. The remainder of this article is organized as follows: Sect. II reviews SoTA methods for depth prediction and depth comple-tion. Sect. III introduces the proposed MTDv2. Sect. IV reports extensive experimental results, demonstrating the superiority of the proposed method and its applicability to downstream tasks. Finally, Sect. V concludes this article and discusses future research directions. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Depth Prediction 

Foundation models have greatly improved the zero-shot gen-eralization of monocular depth prediction [1], [17]. Represen-tative approaches, including MiDaS [2], [3] and Depth Any-thing [1], [4], typically employ inverse-depth parameterization together with affine-invariant losses to learn relative depth from large-scale datasets with diverse depth distributions. Diffusion-based approaches [6], [18]–[20] further improve local details by incorporating generative priors into dense depth prediction, albeit at the expense of higher computa-tional cost. Recent studies, such as Pixel-Perfect-Depth [21], exploit additional geometric priors to further enhance struc-tural consistency. Despite these advances, existing foundation models primarily estimate relative rather than metric depth, limiting their applicability to tasks that require absolute scale, geometric consistency, or direct fusion with sparse metric observations. Monocular metric depth prediction has also attracted consid-erable attention in recent years [5], [22]–[24]. These methods aim to predict metric depth directly from a single image and have achieved impressive performance through large-scale supervised training. More recently, relative-to-metric depth transfer has emerged as an alternative paradigm. For example, TR2M [25] learns pixel-wise rescaling maps to convert relative depth predictions into metric depth. Nevertheless, compared with relative depth prediction, metric depth prediction remains substantially more sensitive to scene distribution, camera con-figuration, and training data coverage [1], [3]. The proposed MTDv2 complements them by preserving their strong cross-domain geometric priors while explicitly recovering metric scale from sparse metric observations. 

B. Depth Completion 

Depth completion approaches generate dense metric depth from a single image based on sparse metric observations [10]– [12], [15], [26], [27]. Data-driven approaches have achieved compelling performance by combining sparse observations with image features and refining dense predictions through learnable seed propagation [7], [14], [26], [27]. Early meth-ods are primarily evaluated on in-domain benchmarks with LiDAR-projected depth maps [28], [29], whereas more recent studies [8], [13] increasingly emphasize generalization across diverse scenes, varying observation densities, and challenging conditions with noisy or extremely sparse metric observations. For instance, OGNI-DC [13] introduces optimization-guided neural iterations to couple gradient prediction with depth integration, while OMNI-DC [8] further improves robustness through multi-resolution integration and large-scale training with synthetically generated sparse metric observations. More-over, PriorDA [30] leverages monocular geometric priors for depth completion, whereas InfiniDepth [31] models depth as a continuous representation to enable arbitrary-resolution depth completion. Nevertheless, the generalizability of these methods still depends, to some extent, on how sparse metric observations are preprocessed before or during neural infer-ence. This limitation motivates the incorporation of explicit geometric priors or a front-end module, particularly when met-ric observations are extremely sparse, noisy, or nonuniformly distributed. A variety of approaches employ non-parametric preprocess-ing algorithms to densify sparse metric observations before neural inference. For example, LRRU [12] adopts traditional interpolation and morphological operations to construct an initial dense representation, while LDCM [9] uses a Poisson-based reconstruction strategy together with depth cues from a foundation model to generate coarse geometric priors. Such preprocessing algorithms are effective because they alleviate the ambiguity associated with directly processing extremely sparse metric observations. However, the resulting depth estimates are often coarse and can become unreliable when the metric observations are extremely sparse, noisy, or nonuniformly distributed [30]. Therefore, the subsequent network must compensate for both preprocessing errors and local structural artifacts, increasing the learning burden and reducing robustness. 4

Instead of developing a purely learning-based monocular depth completion network, our previously proposed MTD [16] introduces an explicit metricization algorithm that converts the relative depth predicted by foundation models into metric depth based on sparse observations, without requiring task-specific training. This design provides the framework with strong flexibility, interpretability, and robustness across diverse types of metric observations. In this extended version, the explicitly metricized results can further serve as reliable ge-ometric priors for data-driven depth refinement. As a result, MTDv2 supports both training-free metric depth completion and learning-based depth refinement, bridging explicit metri-cization and data-driven depth refinement within a unified framework. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Framework Overview 

This article presents MTDv2, a hybrid framework (see Fig. 2) that combines explicit metricization with data-driven depth refinement. The framework takes an RGB image and a sparse observation map as inputs. The sparse metric obser-vations can originate from different sources, such as LiDAR points, depth measurements, and correspondence matches, and are typically projected onto the image or associated with the corresponding pixels when necessary. The RGB image is divided into local segments based on complementary textural and geometric cues. Within the explicit metricization process, segment-wise scale recovery is first performed by estimating independent scale and shift parameters of an affine transfor-mation, denoted by a and b, respectively, for local regions containing sufficient metric observations and propagating these parameters through a sparse segment graph, as detailed in Sect. III-B. The resulting coarse metric depth is subsequently refined at the pixel level through discontinuity-aware dynamic programming, which determines the optimal depth propaga-tion route with minimal accumulated geometric variation, as presented in Sect. III-C. Building upon this explicit metriciza-tion process, data-driven metric depth refinement is further performed using a geometry-guided depth propagation head that translates the same discontinuity-aware principle into a learnable local propagation mechanism, as introduced in Sect. III-D. For subsequent mathematical analysis, a depth map is represented as a scalar field z : Ω → R over the image plane Ω ⊂ R2, where z(p) denotes the depth value at pixel 

p = (u, v )⊤ ∈ Ω. In particular, zrel : Ω → R denotes the relative depth predicted by the foundation model, zopt :Ω → R denotes the refined metric depth obtained through optimization-based depth metricization, and zhybrid : Ω → R

denotes the final metric depth after data-driven metric depth refinement within the complete hybrid framework. 

B. Scale Recovery via Sparse Graph Optimization 

Relative depth predicted by foundation models [1], [4] preserves informative scene geometry but lacks a reliable metric scale. When converting relative depth into metric depth based on sparse metric observations, a single global trans-formation is often insufficient because the relative-to-metric relationship may vary spatially across different regions of the scene. MTD tackles this problem by segmenting the image into numerous texturally consistent regions and independently estimating local affine transformation parameters for region-wise depth metricization. The effectiveness of this approach therefore depends on whether regions sharing the same affine transformation parameters are sufficiently geometrically con-sistent. Nevertheless, purely texture-based image segmentation may produce fragmented regions even on geometrically con-tinuous surfaces. Since the affine transformation parameters are optimized independently for each segment, such fragmen-tation introduces unnecessary parameter variations and may lead to inconsistent affine transformations among adjacent regions despite their naturally similar relative-to-metric rela-tionships. Conversely, adjacent regions that are geometrically discontinuous but exhibit insufficient textural differences may be incorrectly merged into a single segment, causing them to share the same affine transformation and consequently resulting in significant depth metricization errors. Therefore, MTDv2 extends the purely texture-based seg-mentation used in MTD [16] by incorporating relative depth as an additional geometric cue and employing the superpixel segmentation algorithm [32] to divide the image into local segments {S i}Ni=1 that are consistent in both appearance and relative depth, where N denotes the total number of local segments. The resulting local segments provide more reliable support for local scale recovery and subsequent graph propa-gation. The index set of local segments containing sufficient valid sparse metric observations, referred to as qualified local segments, is denoted by Q ⊆ { 1, . . . , N }. For each qualified local segment Si with i ∈ Q, its affine transformation parameters are obtained by fitting the relative depth to the sparse metric observations within Si, expressed as follows:

C. Explicit Depth Refinement via Geodesic Dynamic Pro-gramming 

The above scale recovery process enables the relative depth within each segment to be metricized via a shared affine transformation while preserving the original local geometric structure in the resulting metric depth. Nevertheless, such a region-wise depth metricization strategy also prevents the met-ric observations from being exactly preserved at corresponding pixels, possibly introducing local errors at or around these observations. In contrast, depth completion approaches [8], [13], [33] typically treat sparse metric observations as ex-plicit anchors and infer metric depth around them, with relative depth serving as an auxiliary geometric prior to guide depth inference. Although such approaches can minimize local errors around the observations more directly, the fine-grained structures in the original relative depth maps cannot be fully preserved during depth completion. To combine the strengths of these two paradigms and achieve interpretable and generalizable depth metricization while preserving the fine-grained geometric structures in the original relative depth maps, this study proposes an explicit observation-guided pixel-wise depth refinement paradigm. This paradigm utilizes metric observations as anchors, progressively propagates metric depth information to neighboring pixels through local surface mod-eling, and iteratively fuses the propagated depth estimates with the current depth values. D. Data-Driven Depth Refinement via Learnable Multi-Path Propagation 

The aforementioned explicit depth refinement pipeline relies heavily on local surface modeling and predefined propagation rules. Nevertheless, it is often difficult to estimate local depth variations associated with thin structures, intricate geomet-ric details, or irregular surfaces via local surface modeling, while the predefined propagation rules may make suboptimal propagation decisions in these challenging regions, inevitably introducing noticeable errors into the refined depth [14], [26]– [28]. By leveraging knowledge implicitly learned from large-scale training data, neural networks are particularly effective in estimating local depth variations in complex scenarios. Nonetheless, they can also be susceptible to overfitting the training data and are typically less interpretable [2], [8], [16]. To address these limitations, MTDv2 introduces data-driven network estimates built upon the explicit depth metricization process. First, the explicitly metricized dense depth is introduced as a complementary input to the sparse or coarsely preprocessed dense depth maps commonly adopted by depth completion networks [7], [8], [12]–[15]. Providing this complementary input relieves the network from the burden of directly recov-ering a complete metric depth map from sparse observations, 7

thereby reducing its sensitivity to data distribution shifts. E. Pseudo Label-Based Depth Propagation Head Training 

Conventional data-driven depth completion approaches [8], [13]–[15], [27] typically require large-scale accurate dense metric depth annotations for network training. Acquiring such annotations in real-world environments remains challenging because commonly used range sensing modalities either pro-vide accurate yet sparse measurements, such as the point clouds acquired by LiDAR or radar sensors, or dense measure-ments affected by limited range and sensor-specific artifacts, as commonly encountered with RGB-D camera and time-of-flight sensors. Existing methods therefore generally rely either on relatively limited real-world datasets [36], [37] or large-scale synthetic datasets [38]–[40]. Nevertheless, training on limited real-world data often restricts generalization across diverse scenes, whereas large-scale synthetic datasets, despite 8

improving scene diversity, inevitably suffer from the domain gap between synthetic and real-world data [1], [2], [4], thereby limiting model performance in real-world applications. In MTDv2, dense depth maps with recovered metric scales are already provided by the explicit metricization process, eliminating the need for the network to learn globally ac-curate metric scales from training data and thereby enabling effective supervision using large-scale pseudo metric depth labels. Although the pseudo metric depth labels may contain considerable local depth errors or exhibit global distribution shifts, the sparse observations sampled from these labels naturally inherit the same errors and shifts. By applying the explicit depth refinement strategy to these sparse observations, the relationship between the explicitly metricized depth and the pseudo labels remains consistent with that encountered in real-world scenarios. Therefore, their discrepancy reflects the residual of the explicit refinement process and provides effective supervision for training the subsequent geometry-guided propagation head. MTDv2 can therefore be trained using unlabeled real-world images with pseudo metric depth labels, without imposing stringent requirements on the reliability of these pseudo labels. Specifically, in addition to high-quality synthetic datasets including TartanAir [40], VKITTI2 [38], Hypersim [39], IRS [41], and BlendedMVS [42], diverse unlabeled real-world images from the BDD100K [43], SA-1B [44], and MegaDepth [45] datasets are incorporated into the training set. Their dense pseudo metric depth labels are generated using the high-performing metric depth estimation model [17]. These dense pseudo labels are subsequently sampled according to different observation patterns to simulate various real-world sparse depth acquisition mechanisms, producing the corresponding sparse metric depth observations for training. Additionally, samples with insufficient valid pixels or limited depth variation are discarded because they provide insufficient geometric information for learning meaningful local depth refinement. This data filtering strategy is particularly important for images in the SA-1B dataset that predominantly contain planar regions, such as paintings, posters, and stamps. In such cases, the outputs of the explicit metricization process may already be close to the dense pseudo labels, leaving only a small residual discrepancy and thereby providing insufficient supervisory signals for training the propagation head. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Implementation Details 

Within the optimization-based depth metricization pipeline, Depth Anything V2 (DAV2) [1] with a ViT-L backbone is employed as the default relative depth estimation network. For the experiments in Sects. IV-E and IV-H, different depth estimation networks are adopted according to the specific evaluation objectives. For the explicit depth refinement process in Sect. III-C, spatial derivatives are computed using finite differences, and the maximum number of depth propagation steps is empirically set to 3. An ablation study on the number of depth propagation steps is presented in Sect. IV-G. Within the data-driven metric depth refinement pipeline, the scaling factors ρz , ρϕ, and λϕ, defined in (12), (13), and (14), are set to 0.1, 0.02 , and 0.05 , respectively. The positive constant ϵ in (13) is set to 10 −6, while the update factor ηt

in (15) is progressively decreased over the depth propagation steps. Model training is conducted on four NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs for 20 epochs with a batch size of 12. The Adam optimizer is employed with an initial learning rate of 10 −3. A multi-step learning rate scheduler is adopted, with the learning rate decayed by a factor of 0.3 every 5 epochs. Input images are resized and cropped to 480 × 640 pixels, with random scaling, horizontal flipping, and color jittering applied for data augmentation. The loss functions used to train the depth propagation head follow those adopted in the prior work [13]. 

B. Datasets 

As MTDv2 is proposed as a general depth metricization approach, this study evaluates its performance both as a depth completion model and as a general depth scale recovery algorithm. In the depth completion experiments, MTDv2 is additionally evaluated using correspondence matches as sparse observations for depth metricization, and its performance is compared with SoTA stereo matching networks under a zero-shot setting. Moreover, the recovered metric depth is further applied to downstream tasks that rely on depth perception, with visual odometry and novel view synthesis selected as two representative applications to evaluate its broader applicability. Overall, these evaluations involve 16 public datasets. The datasets and corresponding evaluation protocols are summa-rized as follows:  

> •

The nuScenes [46] dataset comprises large-scale au-tonomous driving data collected in diverse urban en-vironments using multiple cameras, LiDAR, and radar sensors. It is used for the evaluation of zero-shot depth completion performance. Specifically, this study uses the test split, which contains 150 scenes with 6,019 front-camera images. Sparse metric observations are obtained by projecting the single-frame LiDAR measurements onto the corresponding image plane. Ground-truth depth maps are generated by aggregating LiDAR measurements from multiple temporal frames, following the prior work [47]. Samples exhibiting noticeable projection artifacts are discarded from the evaluation.  

> •

The DDAD [48] dataset contains diverse urban driving sequences collected using six synchronized cameras and long-range LiDAR sensors. It is used for the evaluation of zero-shot depth completion performance. Following the prior work [33], this study uses 3,950 front-facing images from the official validation split for performance evaluation. Approximately 20% of the available LiDAR depth measurements are sampled as sparse observations, using the same outlier filtering strategy as that adopted in the official KITTI Depth Completion [49] benchmark.  

> •

The Make3D [50] dataset contains outdoor scenes that differ from those in typical autonomous driving datasets, with depth measurements acquired using a laser scanner. Following the previous study [51], its official test split of 134 images is used for the evaluation of zero-shot depth completion performance. 9 

> •

The DIODE [52] dataset contains diverse indoor and outdoor scenes, with RGB images and accurate long-range depth measurements acquired using a unified sensor system, enabling complementary evaluation across differ-ent scene types. It is used to evaluate the performance of both depth completion and depth scale recovery. For depth completion, 325 indoor and 446 outdoor images are used. For depth scale recovery, the same evaluation split is adopted, with the valid depth range set to 256 m.  

> •

The ETH3D [53] dataset provides high-resolution RGB images captured by DSLR cameras and a synchro-nized multi-camera rig, together with accurate geometric ground truth acquired using a high-precision laser scan-ner. It is first used to evaluate the performance of depth completion with sparse metric observations obtained from either range sensors or correspondence matching. For the former setting, 454 images are used, following the prior work [8]. For the latter setting, this study follows the evaluation protocol adopted in the prior work [54] and uses 27 stereo image pairs. This dataset is further used to evaluate depth scale recovery and novel view synthesis performance. For depth scale recovery, the same split as that used for the depth completion is adopted, with the valid depth range set to 65 m. For novel view synthesis, the dataset is divided into a training set containing 13 scenes and a test set containing 12 scenes collected in diverse indoor and outdoor environments with complex geometric structures. Following the prior work [55], 1/8 of the views in each scene are randomly selected as test views while the remaining 7/8 are used as training views.  

> •

The ScanNet [56] dataset, covering a wide range of indoor room layouts and scene structures, is used to evaluate both depth completion and depth scale recovery performance. Following the previous study [18], a total of 800 samples are selected for performance evaluation after removing frames containing a large proportion of invalid ground-truth depth pixels. For depth scale recovery, the same evaluation split is adopted, with the valid depth range set to 10 m.  

> •

The VOID [57] dataset provides real-world indoor and outdoor RGB-D data collected using an Intel RealSense sensor for sparse-to-dense depth completion. Sparse depth maps are provided with densities of 150, 500, and 1,500 points per frame. Following the previous study [33], models are evaluated on the official test set comprising 800 frames, using the provided sparse depth maps with 1,500 points per frame.  

> •

The SUN-RGBD [58] dataset contains diverse indoor scenes captured using four different RGB-D cameras, providing substantial variations in scene layouts and sen-sor characteristics. It is used to evaluate zero-shot depth completion performance. Samples that are also included in the NYUv2 dataset are excluded from the official test split, resulting in 4,395 images for performance evaluation.  

> •

The HAMMER [59] dataset contains challenging scenes involving reflective and transparent objects captured using a multimodal sensing system. It is used to evaluate zero-shot depth completion performance. Since no universally adopted evaluation protocol exists for this dataset, this study randomly samples 400 images from the official test scenes using a fixed random seed. These images will be released together with the source code upon publication to enable reproducibility.  

> •

The iBims-1 [60] dataset provides accurate indoor ge-ometry and fine-grained depth structures, with ground-truth geometry obtained using a highly accurate laser scanner. It is used to evaluate zero-shot depth completion performance. This study uses the official evaluation split containing 100 images.  

> •

The KITTI [37] dataset consists of 56 real-world driving scenarios collected using a stereo camera, with depth measurements derived from the LiDAR point clouds. This dataset is used to evaluate MTDv2 as a general depth scale recovery algorithm. Following the previous study [1], this study adopts the Eigen evaluation split comprising 652 images and restricts the valid depth range to 80 m.  

> •

The NYUv2 [36] dataset contains diverse indoor scenes captured using a Microsoft Kinect RGB-D sensor and is used to evaluate depth scale recovery performance. Following the previous study [1], the standard test split containing 654 images is adopted, with the valid depth range set to 10 m.  

> •

The Middlebury [61] dataset contains high-resolution stereo image pairs with accurate ground-truth disparity annotations obtained using a structured-light scanner. It is used to evaluate the performance of MTDv2 with sparse metric depth observations obtained from correspondence matches. Following the zero-shot evaluation protocol adopted in the prior work [54], 15 stereo image pairs are used.  

> •

The KITTI Stereo 2012 [37] and KITTI Stereo 2015 [62] datasets provide stereo image pairs from outdoor driving scenes with ground-truth disparity an-notations. They are used to evaluate the performance of MTDv2 with sparse metric depth observations obtained from correspondence matches. The corresponding eval-uation sets, containing 194 and 200 stereo image pairs, respectively, are adopted.  

> •

The KITTI Odometry [63] dataset provides 22 driving sequences, among which Seqs. 00–10 are accompanied by ground-truth camera poses, enabling quantitative tra-jectory evaluation. It is used to evaluate the metric depth produced by MTDv2 in the visual odometry task. Follow-ing the previous study [64], the sequences with available ground-truth poses are employed, excluding Seqs. 01, 03, and 04 because their relatively short trajectories provide limited information for the evaluation of the proposed method [65]. For datasets without predefined sparse metric observations or a dataset-specific sampling protocol described above, this study synthesizes sparse inputs for depth completion by ran-domly retaining 0.01 –0.1% of the valid depth measurements. The sampling density is varied in the sensitivity analyses in 10 

Sect. IV-H to assess the robustness of MTDv2 under different levels of observation sparsity. For the evaluation of depth scale recovery performance, 1.0–1.5% of the ground-truth depth measurements in each sample are retained as sparse metric observations for MTD and MTDv2. 

C. Evaluation Metrics 

Following previous studies [8], [13], [33], root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) are adopted to quantify MTDv2’s performance on the depth com-pletion task. To compare MTDv2 with SoTA stereo matching approaches when sparse metric observations are obtained from correspondence matches, BP-1 and BP-2, which denote the percentages of valid pixels with absolute disparity errors greater than 1 and 2 pixels, respectively, are reported, and D1, which denotes the percentage of valid pixels whose disparity error exceeds both 3 pixels and 5% of the corresponding dis-parity ground truth, is reported, following the prior study [54]. When evaluating MTDv2 as a general depth scale recovery algorithm, commonly used metrics including absolute relative error (Abs Rel) and threshold-based accuracy δ1 are employed, following the previous study [4]. For the evaluation of downstream tasks, novel view syn-thesis quality is quantified using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity (SSIM), and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS), following the prior study [55]. Since the primary goal of evaluating this downstream task is to examine the benefit of leveraging recovered metric depth, RMSE between the rendered depth and ground-truth metric depth is additionally reported on the test views. In addition, visual odometry performance is quantified using the absolute trajectory error (ATE) in meters, following the previous work [64]. 

D. Depth Completion Results 

The proposed MTDv2 is first compared with SoTA depth completion methods [7], [12], [14], [15], [33] to evalu-ate its zero-shot depth completion performance. Specifically, CFormer [15], BP-Net [14], and LRRU [12] are evaluated using their official weights for the KITTI [37] and NYUv2 [36] datasets in outdoor and indoor scenarios, respectively. For DMD 3C [7], this study uses its officially released weights for the model trained on large-scale datasets. Notably, the training data of DMD 3C include the nuScenes dataset. Since the official weights of PromptDA [66] are obtained on spe-cific datasets and exhibit limited generalizability to unseen scenarios, PromptDA is retrained using the same training setting as MTDv2 for a fair comparison. Marigold-DC [33] is evaluated using its official weights, with the officially released efficient denoising configuration adopted for all experiments. Both MTD [16] and MTDv2 are included for comparison. The quantitative results in Table I show that both MTD and MTDv2 achieve SoTA performance among the compared depth completion approaches. In particular, MTDv2 reduces RMSE by 4.0% –47 .5% compared with the previous best results, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed in-terpretable depth metricization paradigm for zero-shot depth completion. Compared with MTD, MTDv2 further reduces depth completion errors by a substantial margin across most datasets, highlighting the benefit of combining explicit depth metricization with data-driven metric depth refinement to exploit their complementary strengths. On the DDAD dataset, although MTDv2 shows a slight increase in RMSE, suggesting some larger depth errors may remain, it substantially reduces MAE, indicating improved overall depth accuracy. Moreover, previous approaches exhibit noticeable performance degrada-tion on challenging datasets with complex scene structures, such as ScanNet, SUN-RGBD, and HAMMER, as well as on datasets with highly sparse metric observations, such as nuScenes. In contrast, MTDv2 maintains consistently strong performance under these challenging conditions, demonstrat-ing superior robustness and generalizability. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 3, MTDv2 recovers more accurate met-ric depth with sharper object boundaries than previous ap-proaches, particularly in regions with sparse observations, fur-ther demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed paradigm. In addition to acquiring sparse metric depth using range sensors, multi-view images with known relative camera poses can also provide accurate metric depth information. For in-stance, stereo image pairs with known baselines enable metric depth recovery through stereo matching. Nevertheless, ac-quiring large-scale ground-truth disparity annotations in real-world scenarios is labor-intensive [70], making the training and adaptation of stereo matching models to diverse real-world environments expensive. Conventional stereo matching also relies on accurate extrinsic calibration and stereo image rectification to constrain correspondences to the same epipolar line. In practice, calibration and rectification errors may violate this constraint and degrade dense stereo matching perfor-mance. Even with a well-trained model and accurate extrinsic calibration, dense correspondence matching can remain unre-liable in challenging regions, such as areas with ambiguous textures and occlusions [71]. MTDv2 provides an alternative and more robust solution. Instead of directly estimating dense disparity maps, it first employs a general correspondence matching algorithm [72] to obtain sparse yet highly reliable correspondences. These correspondences are then converted into sparse metric depth observations, from which MTDv2 recovers dense metric depth. The resulting dense metric depth is subsequently converted back into disparity maps, enabling direct comparison with SoTA stereo matching networks [54], [71], [73]–[81]. All compared stereo matching networks are trained only on the Scene Flow [82] dataset. The quantitative results in Table III demonstrate that, with-out learning highly discriminative features specifically for stereo matching, MTDv2 still significantly outperforms most existing stereo matching approaches across diverse indoor and outdoor scenarios. Even compared with the representative vision foundation model-based stereo matching approach [54], which achieves strong performance in zero-shot disparity estimation, MTDv2 reduces the disparity error by 3.6%, 9.4%, and 16.3% on the Middlebury, KITTI Stereo 2012, and KITTI Stereo 2015 datasets, respectively. H. Sensitivity Analyses 

In practice, only sparse metric depth observations are typ-ically available because LiDAR and radar sensors cannot ac-quire sufficiently dense measurements within a limited sensing 16 

time. Therefore, this study evaluates MTDv2 under different sampling densities to analyze its sensitivity to observation sparsity. The experimental results are presented in Fig. 6. First, MTDv2 with Depth Anything V2 consistently outperforms MTD with either DAV2 or MoGe-2, even though MoGe-2 itself provides more accurate depth estimates than DAV2. Moreover, the performance advantage becomes increasingly pronounced as metric observations become sparser. This trend can be attributed to the fact that explicit depth refinement becomes increasingly constrained by the limited metric ob-servations available for depth metricization, making data-driven depth refinement more important under highly sparse observation settings. 

I. Discussion 

Despite the compelling results achieved, the proposed framework still has the following three limitations. First, the computational efficiency of MTDv2 remains lim-ited. A detailed breakdown of the inference time is given in Fig. 7(a). It can be observed that the relative depth estimation network and the network components used for data-driven depth refinement employ independent backbones for feature extraction, together accounting for approximately 50% of the total inference time. Therefore, a more compact network design with appropriate training strategies could be explored to facilitate effective feature sharing between these components while preserving their respective functions, thereby reducing inference overhead without compromising depth completion performance. In addition, the superpixel segmentation algo-rithm used for segment-wise scale recovery relies solely on CPU execution, and GPU-accelerated implementations could be explored to further improve computational efficiency. Fur-thermore, the depth propagation head in the data-driven depth refinement process introduces substantial inference overhead when predicting residuals and propagation logits, motivating the development of a more lightweight architecture. Second, the proposed method becomes less robust when the relative depth estimates are inaccurate. As shown in Fig. 7(b), under poor illumination conditions, the estimated relative depth of the car becomes erroneous, and the resulting error is also observed in the outputs of both MTD and MTDv2. This is because the explicit depth refinement process largely preserves the initial geometric structure derived from the relative depth estimation and is therefore incapable of correcting substantial depth errors. Although the data-driven metric depth refinement process can reduce depth residuals, its effectiveness remains limited when similar challenging conditions are insufficiently represented in the annotated training data. Therefore, one possible direction for addressing this issue is to incorporate effective domain adaptation strategies or robust unsupervised learning methods, enabling both the relative depth estimation network and the learnable depth refinement module to better adapt to challenging scenarios by leveraging additional unla-beled data. Finally, the proposed framework still relies on accurate extrinsic calibration between the camera and range sensor, provided by public datasets. However, in practical use, the ac-tual extrinsic parameters may deviate from their initial values obtained through offline target-based calibration due to sensor mounting variations, mechanical perturbations, or long-term system changes, which can degrade depth metricization accu-racy. Integrating the proposed method with high-performance online target-free extrinsic calibration approaches, such as our previous work [85], could provide a more practical system capable of operating robustly under imperfect calibration con-ditions. V. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This article presented MTDv2, a hybrid metric depth re-covery framework that combines explicit optimization-based depth metricization with data-driven metric depth refinement. The proposed framework first resolves relative-to-metric am-biguity through segment-wise affine parameter estimation and sparse graph-based parameter propagation, followed by discontinuity-aware dynamic programming to improve local geometric fidelity. Building upon the explicitly metricized depth, the hybrid refinement process further incorporates learn-able residual correction and geometry-guided multi-path depth propagation to overcome the limitations of handcrafted surface modeling and propagation strategies. Extensive experiments on 16 public datasets demonstrate that MTDv2 achieves robust and generalizable metric depth recovery under diverse sparse observation settings, with competitive performance in depth completion and downstream 3D perception tasks. Future work will focus on improving computational effi-ciency, enhancing robustness under challenging observation conditions, and developing unified calibration-free depth per-ception frameworks.
