ABSTRACT
Set-based variational autoencoders (VecSet VAEs) represent 3D shapes as com-
pact sets of latent tokens. Although each token originates from a query at a sam-
pled surface location, whether these latents truly align with geometry remains
unclear. We investigate this alignment and identify four issues: (i) attention-
sink-like tokens that attract queries from across the shape; (ii) spatially periodic
repetitions in decoder attention responses; (iii) mismatched normal information
in encoder and decoder query features; and (iv) missing supervision of the in-
trinsic relation between signed distance fields and surface normals. To address
these issues, we introduce Align-VAE with four corresponding modifications: (i) a
shared bank of learnable decoder tokens provides a workspace for global compu-
tation; (ii) distance-biased cross-attention guides retrieval toward nearby predicted
spatial anchors; (iii) normal-free encoder queries align the two stages’ access to
normal information while retaining normals in the encoder keys and values; and
(iv) a screened Poisson-inspired regularizer supervises surface values and normal-
direction derivatives. These changes align latent retrieval and field prediction with
geometry without enlarging the per-shape code. On the ABO dataset, Align-VAE
reduces Chamfer distance by 26.6% compared to the standard VecSet VAE.

INTRODUCTION
Compact 3D representations provide an interface between detailed geometry and latent generative
models. VecSet variational autoencoders (VecSet VAEs) compress a surface into a fixed number of
latent tokens and reconstruct it through an implicit field queried at arbitrary spatial locations (Zhang
et al., 2023). Their token budget can be chosen independently of the resolution at which the field is
decoded, making them attractive for scalable shape modeling (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a;
Team Hunyuan3D et al., 2025). Increasing output sampling density cannot recover detail that the
code fails to preserve or the decoder fails to retrieve. Figure 1 previews how Align-VAE improves
local responses during encoding and decoding while retaining the stored code budget.

In a point-query VecSet encoder, each latent originates from a query at a sampled surface point. This
assigns a spatial origin, but does it also establish a spatial role? Encoder and decoder cross-attention
retrieve features by content similarity, while self-attention mixes information across tokens. None
of these operations requires a token to describe its originating neighborhood. Global context helps
describe a coherent shape, while local field queries need spatially precise evidence. We hypothesize
that sharing limited latent capacity across these roles can leave spatially seeded latents poorly aligned
with the local geometry they are meant to represent.
Our profiling of a standard VecSet VAE reveals gaps between spatial origins and geometric roles.
Encoder queries often favor shared inputs, while decoder attention concentrates on a small subset of
latents (Figures 2(b) and 2(c)). Individual latents can respond at separated locations, and decoded
field gradients deviate from surface normals (Figures 3(b) and 3(d)). Encoder queries also use
observed normals that arbitrary decoder queries lack. Section 4 explains how these diagnostics
motivate changes to local encoding, spatial retrieval, and field supervision.
We introduce Align-VAE to address these issues while retaining the stored VecSet code. A shared
bank of learnable decoder tokens supplies a workspace for global computation. Predicted spatial
anchors and a distance bias guide retrieval from the remaining shape tokens toward nearby geome-
try. Normal-free encoder queries remove the normal-information mismatch while keeping observed
normals in the support keys and values. Finally, a regularizer inspired by screened Poisson re-
construction (Kazhdan & Hoppe, 2013) constrains surface values and variation along the observed
normals. Together, these changes govern how local evidence is encoded, retrieved, and translated
into a geometric field with spatial and orientation cues.
Our contributions are threefold:
• Detailed analysis of VecSet VAEs. We examine attention allocation, spatial retrieval,
query information, and field orientation to characterize the geometric roles of spatially
seeded latents throughout encoding and decoding.
• Targeted improvements. We introduce Align-VAE, combining a shared decoder bank,
distance-biased retrieval, normal-free queries, and normal supervision without enlarging
the stored per-shape code.
• Extensive evaluation. We evaluate Align-VAE through reconstruction benchmarks, com-
ponent ablations, and geometric diagnostics. On the ABO dataset, we achieve a 26.6%
reduction in Chamfer distance compared to the standard VecSet VAE.

RELATED WORK
Compact 3D shape latents.
3DILG (Zhang et al., 2022) associates latent features with irregular
spatial samples, while 3DShape2VecSet (Zhang et al., 2023) uses attention to connect a compact
latent set to continuous field queries. Michelangelo (Zhao et al., 2023) aligns shape latents with
image and text features, and CLAY (Zhang et al., 2024), TripoSG (Li et al., 2025a), and HY3D-
2.1 (Team Hunyuan3D et al., 2025) scale set-based shape modeling. Dora (Chen et al., 2025) studies
sampling and evaluation for 3D VAEs. LATTICE (Lai et al., 2026) anchors latents to a coarse voxel
grid; MSVS-VAE (Hao et al., 2026) combines anchored latent hierarchies with local decoding. We
analyze the geometric role of VecSet attention.
Spatially structured representations.
XCube (Ren et al., 2024) and TRELLIS (Xiang et al.,
2025) organize 3D features on sparse spatial grids. Direct3D-S2 and Sparc3D model detailed shapes
with sparse representations (Wu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025b). These methods provide explicit spa-
tial organization; VecSet methods decouple the stored token count from a dense decoding grid. We
report accuracy, storage, and decoding cost separately because token widths and layouts differ.
Computational workspace and oriented geometry.
Registers provide additional tokens for inter-
nal computation in vision transformers (Darcet et al., 2024). We test this workspace interpretation in
a 3D VAE by separating an unanchored decoder bank from spatially addressed shape tokens. Poisson
and screened Poisson reconstruction connect oriented surface samples to an implicit field (Kazhdan
et al., 2006; Kazhdan & Hoppe, 2013). Our regularizer draws on surface interpolation and normal-
direction constraints to supervise the learned field during training. Inference evaluates this field
without solving a Poisson reconstruction system.

DO SPATIAL QUERIES PRODUCE GEOMETRICALLY ALIGNED LATENTS?
Figure 2 profiles attention in a standard VecSet VAE. Each encoder query starts at an FPS surface
point and gathers information from the input cloud into a latent token. The decoder then reads
these tokens from arbitrary 3D query locations to predict an implicit field. Our profiles examine
where information is collected, where it is retrieved, and how it determines surface orientation.
Appendix B.2.1 illustrates the profiles that motivate the four components of Align-VAE.
4.1
NONLOCAL ENCODER SELECTION AND UNEVEN DECODER ATTENTION
Encoder input selection.
To test whether distributed query origins yield local reads, we link each
of the 8192 FPS encoder queries to the input point with its highest head-averaged attention weight
(Figure 2(a)). Figure 2(b) reports the fraction selecting one of its 64 nearest inputs in Euclidean
distance and the fraction selecting the shape’s most-selected input. The mean rates are 0.07% and
94.1%, respectively: dominant selections largely converge on a shared point despite distributed FPS
origins. This behavior resembles attention sinks in LLMs (Xiao et al., 2024) and the use of spatial
tokens for global computation in ViTs (Darcet et al., 2024). A per-head analysis also finds two heads
with mean local-selection rates above 99% (Appendix B.3), showing that shared selection coexists
with local information collection.
Decoder attention concentration.
We next measure how field prediction uses the latent budget.
A token’s attention mass is its average attention weight over decoder queries, representing its share
of the decoder’s attention. We evaluate queries throughout the volume and on the input surface

4.2
SPATIAL REPETITION IN DECODER CROSS-ATTENTION
Surface response maps.
After measuring how much attention each latent receives, we examine
where it is read. A token’s spatial response is the attention assigned to it as the decoder query moves
through 3D space. Figure 2(d) visualizes this response on the surface for tokens ranked first, tenth,
and median by surface attention mass. Each map scales colors by that token’s own peak response,
while crosses mark the originating FPS points. Comparing the colored regions with these origins
reveals the spatial extent and displacement of retrieval. These maps show diffuse, smoothly varying
responses over broad surface regions, rather than compact peaks around their FPS origins.
Response slices and traces.
Figure 3 extends this view into the volume. Figure 3(a) marks the
location where each token receives its strongest attention. Figure 3(b) shows the same selected
tokens’ responses on 2D slices, and Figure 3(c) follows a straight line through a response maxi-
mum. The separated peaks show that the same token is retrieved at multiple locations, including
regions far from its FPS origin. This is a retrieval-locality mismatch: a token seeded at one surface
neighborhood is read as evidence for distant field queries.
Similarity across spatial shifts.
To summarize the response structure, we shift each one-
dimensional attention curve along its spatial axis and correlate the shifted curve with the original.
We denote the highest correlation over a fixed set of nonzero shifts by Crep: larger values indicate
more similar responses at separated locations. Mean scores are 0.475, 0.518, and 0.274 for the first,
tenth, and median-ranked tokens, respectively (Figure 3(c)). Together with the separated response
peaks, this profile motivates an explicit spatial reference for decoder retrieval. We predict an anchor
for each shape token and bias attention toward anchors near the query, so retrieval uses both feature
similarity and geometric proximity.
4.3
QUERY-NORMAL ASYMMETRY
The encoder–decoder interface introduces a second retrieval mismatch. Encoder queries originate
at observed surface points and include their normals. Decoder queries can lie anywhere in space,
where a surface normal is unavailable. The encoder can therefore organize information using an ori-
entation cue that the decoder cannot supply when retrieving it. This motivates normal-free encoder
queries: removing normals from the query features makes both stages locate evidence without an

4.4
FIELD–NORMAL MISALIGNMENT
Geometric alignment also depends on how retrieved features become a field. At the zero level set,
the field gradient determines the surface normal, so correct orientation requires the predicted field
to vary in the appropriate spatial direction. At observed surface points, we query small offsets
along each coordinate axis, estimate the field gradient, and measure its angular difference from the
observed outward normal. Figure 3(d) shows a mean error of 13.52◦. This discrepancy exposes
an orientation gap in supervision based on field values alone. It motivates coupling field regression
with surface and normal-direction constraints: the field should vanish on the observed surface and
change along its normals according to the signed-distance target. Our screened Poisson-inspired
regularizer imposes these two geometric conditions alongside the attention improvements.

METHOD
Align-VAE retains the M-token variational code and changes how geometric information is col-
lected, retrieved, and supervised. Figure 4 summarizes the architecture. Each component addresses
a baseline profile from Section 4; Figure 5 compares the complete models at 8192 latents. Imple-
mentation details and training settings appear in Appendix A.
5.1
A DECODER WORKSPACE ALONGSIDE SHAPE TOKENS
The decoder mass and spatial profiles in Figures 2(c) and 2(d) motivate a register-like workspace for
global computation (Darcet et al., 2024) (Figure 4 1⃝). A learned bank L ∈RK×d, shared across
shapes, is concatenated with the projected code:
eZ = Tdec([L | Pdec(Z)]).
(3)

Here Pdec projects tokens to width d, [· | ·] concatenates them, and Tdec is the decoder self-attention
stack. This interaction makes the bank shape-dependent. Spatial queries read both populations:
shape tokens can support local retrieval, while the bank supplies context without anchors or dis-
tance penalties. Attention-mass analysis tests this allocation of capacity. The bank adds Kd shared

EXPERIMENTS
Section 4 shows that spatially seeded tokens can collect and retrieve information far from their
intended neighborhoods. We now ask whether aligning these operations improves reconstruction,
which components contribute to the gains, and how their attention changes. Chamfer distance (CD)
measures surface error, while F1 measures agreement within a distance threshold; lower CD and
higher F1 are better. Appendix B.1 gives their mathematical definitions.
6.1
RECONSTRUCTION QUALITY AND COMPACTNESS
Better reconstruction from the same code budget.
Table 1 compares Align-VAE with standard
VecSet VAE, LATTICE, and released 3D autoencoders. At 4096 tokens, Align-VAE lowers CD
relative to VecSet by 18.2% on Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023), 22.0% on ABO (Collins et al., 2022),
and 16.4% on in-the-wild shapes. The gain reaches 26.6% on ABO at 8192 tokens. F1@.001 also
improves across all three datasets, indicating closer surface agreement. At 8192 tokens, Align-VAE
leads every reported metric in Table 1.
Reconstruction across Dora Bench levels.
Table 2 extends the comparison to the four levels of
Dora Bench (Chen et al., 2025). At 8192 tokens, Align-VAE reduces CD by 48.1% overall relative
to VecSet, from 4.804 to 2.495, while F1@.001 increases from 56.87% to 60.22%. The largest CD
improvement appears on Level 4, where the error falls from 11.883 to 2.884. Align-VAE achieves
the lowest mean CD on all four levels, while VecSet retains the highest F1@.001 on Level 3. At
4096 tokens, overall CD falls from 3.402 to 2.804, indicating that geometric alignment benefits
reconstruction at both latent budgets.
Preserving local structure.
Figure 6 compares Align-VAE with VecSet and LATTICE at 8192
tokens on selected shapes. The enlarged views show better separated railing bars, clearer screw
threads, and raised tire-tread blocks. These local differences are difficult to judge from the overall
silhouette alone. These improvements need not reduce whole-shape reconstruction error.
Storage and cost.
At 4096 tokens, both models store 512 KiB per shape. The shared bank in-
creases the decoder sequence from M to M + K tokens while preserving the MCz stored latent
values. Latent refinement takes 56.32 ms for Align-VAE and 53.99 ms for VecSet (Table 10).

HOW DOES GEOMETRIC ALIGNMENT IMPROVE?
Figure 5 revisits the profiles from Section 4 at 8192 latents. Relative to VecSet, Align-VAE reduces
response correlation from 0.422 to 0.057 and top-1% shape-token attention from 9.83% to 2.78%.
Local encoder selection rises from 0.073% to nearly 100%, while normal error falls from 13.52◦to
11.15◦. Table 4 examines the component contributions through cumulative variants at 4096 tokens,
using the same sequence of additions as the reconstruction ablation in Table 3.
Separating context from spatial retrieval.
In A1, the learnable bank receives 31.19% of surface-
query attention, providing a route for shared context. Adding distance bias in A2 reduces the mean
read distance from 0.175 to 0.035 and response correlation from 0.344 to 0.052. These changes
address the displaced and repeated retrieval patterns observed in the baseline while the bank remains
available for shared context.
Collecting evidence near each encoder query.
With normal-free queries, A3 selects a neighbor-
ing input point for every encoder query, compared with 0.043% in A2. The attention assigned to
nearby inputs also rises from 9.77% to 53.82%, so the change extends beyond the single largest
attention weight. Reconstruction remains unchanged when query normals are zeroed, perturbed,
or flipped (Table 13). Removing the unavailable query cue thus aligns the encoder’s information
collection with the decoder’s inputs.

CONCLUSION
A token’s spatial origin does not guarantee geometrically aligned attention. Align-VAE combines a
shared decoder workspace, normal-free encoder queries, spatially biased retrieval, and field supervi-
sion while preserving the stored code budget. The resulting representation improves reconstruction
and exhibits more local encoder selection and decoder reads. Reconstruction is also insensitive to
perturbing encoder query normals. The shared bank keeps per-shape storage fixed but adds decoder
computation, so storage efficiency and inference cost must be evaluated separately. The distinction
between surface accuracy and field orientation also shows the value of examining how information
moves through a 3D representation, beyond measuring its reconstruction error alone. Future work
should investigate how to balance surface reconstruction and field orientation, particularly when
normal supervision improves one but not the other.

A.1
LEARNABLE DECODER BANK
Equation 3 prepends K learned tokens of width d to the projected M-token variational code. Joint
decoder self-attention makes the bank shape-dependent, and spatial-query cross-attention reads all
K + M refined tokens. The bank has no direct access to the input surface and enters only after the
variational bottleneck. Its Kd shared parameters add decoder capacity without changing the stored
per-shape code or the KL penalty.
Bank tokens have no assigned coordinates and receive no distance penalty. They provide an unan-
chored route for context, while the shape tokens support spatially addressed retrieval. Both popula-
tions compete in the same softmax, with no prescribed attention quota for the bank.

A.3
NORMAL-FREE ENCODER QUERIES
Equation 4 masks only the query normal channels. Support keys and values retain observed normals,
and both branches retain their sharp-edge labels. Masking preserves the feature width and introduces
no parameters. The encoder and decoder thus construct queries without supplied normals, although
their projections and remaining features differ.
Encoder cross-attention uses scaled dot products, query/key normalization, and a softmax over sup-
port points. The residual block includes value aggregation and a feed-forward sublayer. Encoder
self-attention then refines the M tokens, and a projection predicts the mean µ and log variance of
a diagonal Gaussian posterior (Kingma & Welling, 2014). We sample Z from this posterior during
training; the decoder bank is added afterward.
