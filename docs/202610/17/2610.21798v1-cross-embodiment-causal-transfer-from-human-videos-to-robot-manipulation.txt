## ABSTRACT 

Scaling robot manipulation models requires large amounts of interaction data, yet collecting diverse real-robot experience remains costly. Human manipulation videos, in contrast, provide abundant and diverse task experience that could potentially benefit robot learning. However, differences in morphology, action spaces, and control mechanisms make human motions unsuitable as direct su-pervision for robot actions, raising a scientific question: what knowledge in human videos should be transferred across embodiments to robot manipulation? To address this question, we analyze subtask transfer through the lens of causal beams, focusing on which contextual conditions and interaction dependencies should be retained to support local task effects. Based on this perspec-tive, we propose Causal-Beam-Guided Subtask Transfer (CBST). CBST learns manipulation-related representations from human videos and grounds them in task-level semantic variables using robot demonstrations. Within a predefined manipulation model, causal-beam constraints guide contextual information selection by requiring retained assignments to sustain local mechanism outputs while preserving nontrivial responsiveness to retained inputs. The selected semantic information condi-tions a world-action model, allowing the robot to generate actions from its own observations without requiring human–robot motion correspondence. Experiments on robot manipulation benchmarks show improvements in task success over the corresponding world-action model baselines. 

## 1 Introduction 

Recent robot manipulation models are evolving toward World Action Models (WAMs) that jointly model future scene evolution and robot behavior, enabling predictions of interaction outcomes [Kim et al., 2026, Li et al., 2026]. How-ever, scaling real-world robot data requires dedicated hardware, human operation, and physical deployment, making broad coverage of tasks, objects, and scenes expensive. In contrast, human manipulation videos are abundant and capture diverse task processes and object interactions, making them an attractive source of additional experience for robot learning [Khazatsky et al., 2025, Ma et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2023]. Yet humans and robots differ substan-tially in morphology, action spaces, and control mechanisms, so concrete human actions cannot be directly reused as robot supervision [Bahl et al., 2022, Xu et al., 2023, Kim et al., 2025a]. This leads to the central question of this work: what knowledge in human manipulation videos should be transferred across embodiment differences to robot manipulation? 

A complete human manipulation sequence contains two types of information: object-interaction relations that support task progress, and concrete motion realizations that are closely tied to the human embodiment. For long-horizon ma-nipulation tasks, we first organize continuous execution into local task processes using temporally extended subtasks, thereby avoiding the requirement that the robot reproduce human motion step by step. Examples of placing a bowl, opening a lid, and lifting a book motivate the question: what task information should transfer beyond embodiment-specific motion and goal endpoints? only specifies the local scope within which transfer takes place; it does not further determine which information within that scope should be preserved together with the task experience. Retaining only the task intention or boundary states of a subtask may discard the conditions and interaction relations that determine how a local effect is realized, whereas retaining the complete human execution trajectory reintroduces substantial embodiment-specific motion details. We study this selective information-preservation problem through the lens of causal beams [Pearl, 2009]. Given a structural model and a specific context, a causal beam allows part of the inputs to be projected out in a context-dependent manner, while requiring the actual values of the retained variables to sustain the actual outcome of the current mechanism and the projected mechanism to retain nontrivial responsiveness to its retained inputs. This per-spective motivates separating task-level abstraction from contextual information selection. Task-level variables provide an abstraction of execution, while causal-beam conditions constrain which semantic dependencies are retained within a specified model. Based on this, we propose Causal-Beam-Guided Subtask Transfer (CBST). CBST first learns manipulation-related temporal representations through human-video pretraining and grounds them, under robot demonstration supervision, into a candidate SCM instantiated from shared manipulation templates. A contextual subtask-conditioning module then selects a contextual causal beam from the candidate SCM according to the current execution context, producing the local task effect, relevant conditions, and their effect-relevant dependencies. This structural information conditions the target robot WAM, while concrete actions are still generated from the robot’s current state and its own action space. CBST therefore combines human-video representation transfer with causal-beam-constrained selection of contextual subtask information for robot execution. Our contributions are summarized as: ( 1) We study human-to-robot learning from the perspective of cross-embodiment transfer, focusing on which task-progress structures should remain transferable after embodiment-specific motion is discarded; ( 2) We propose Causal-Beam-Guided Subtask Transfer (CBST) , which combines a shared candidate manipulation SCM with learned contextual causal beams to preserve effect-relevant conditions and dependencies for conditioning robot WAMs; ( 3) We systematically evaluate CBST on WAMs including LingBot-VA and Cosmos Policy, examining the contributions of human video pretraining, context-specific causal selection, and their effects on robot control. 

## 2 Related Works 

Human-to-robot transfer aims to exploit abundant human demonstrations while addressing the embodiment gap be-tween human and robot execution. Motion-oriented approaches seek representations that remain useful across embodi-ments while retaining execution information. WHIRL aligns human and robot videos to infer task intent and initializes robot policy learning from third-person human demonstrations [Bahl et al., 2022], while EgoMimic uses egocentric human videos with 3D hand tracking, cross-domain alignment, and joint training on human and robot demonstrations [Kareer et al., 2024]. Latent Action Pretraining (LAPA) instead learns discrete latent actions from videos without robot 2A PREPRINT - O CTOBER 16, 2026 action labels and subsequently maps them to robot actions using limited robot data [Ye et al., 2025]. Other approaches move toward higher-level transferable representations. MimicPlay learns latent plans from large-scale human play videos and uses them to guide a low-level robot controller [Wang et al., 2023]; XSkill discovers cross-embodiment skill prototypes from unlabeled human and robot videos and maps them to robot actions with a conditional policy [Xu et al., 2023]; Translating Robot Skills learns unsupervised correspondences between skill sequences across morpho-logically different agents [Shankar et al., 2022]; and UniSkill learns embodiment-agnostic skill representations that allow skills extracted from human video prompts to guide robot policies trained on robot data [Kim et al., 2025a]. More recently, Gen2Act generates human future videos for novel tasks and conditions a robot policy on the generated motion information [Bharadhwaj et al., 2024]. Temporal abstraction organizes long-horizon behavior into reusable units beyond primitive actions. The options framework formalizes temporally extended policies through initiation sets, internal policies, and termination con-ditions [Sutton et al., 1999], while later robotic methods model skill feasibility and composition, such as SayCan [Ahn et al., 2022] and relational skill-precondition learning. Another line of work represents task progress through intermediate subgoals: Video Language Planning generates hierarchical visual and language plans [Du et al., 2023], TaKSIE conditions visual subgoal generation on task progress [Kang and Kuo, 2024], and Latent Space Backward Planning recursively constructs intermediate latent goals from the final task goal [Liu et al., 2025]. More recent world-model methods integrate such temporal abstraction directly into planning: Hierarchical Planning with Latent World Models learns dynamics at multiple temporal scales and uses high-level latent subgoals to guide low-level planning [Zhang et al., 2026], while SAGE similarly exploits temporally extended latent subgoals for action-sequence genera-tion [Cheng et al., 2026]. World models learn predictive dynamics to support action selection and planning. Early latent-dynamics approaches such as PlaNet perform planning directly in learned latent spaces [Hafner et al., 2019], while more recent methods extend this idea to visual robot manipulation: DINO-WM predicts future visual features for zero-shot planning [Zhou et al., 2025], V-JEPA 2-AC learns action-conditioned latent dynamics for model-predictive control [Assran et al., 2025], and IRASim models fine-grained action-conditioned visual futures for simulation and planning [Zhu et al., 2025]. Recent World-Action Models further unify future prediction with action generation. WorldVLA jointly models visual and action tokens in an autoregressive framework [Cen et al., 2025]; Cosmos Policy generates robot actions together with future-state images and value estimates [Kim et al., 2026]; LingBot-VA jointly learns future-frame prediction and policy execution in a shared visual–action latent space [Li et al., 2026]; and τ0-WM further combines video prediction, action generation, and action evaluation within a unified model [Zhou et al., 2026].

3.3 Causal Analysis of Cross-Embodiment Transfer 

Humans and robots may accomplish the same task through different motion realizations because of differences in morphology, action spaces, and control mechanisms. This motivates describing local task processes using semantic variables for task conditions, manipulation attempts, object-state changes, and effects, without requiring step-by-step correspondence between human and robot motion. Defining these variables, however, does not determine which conditions and dependencies should be retained in a particular execution context. We use causal-beam conditions to guide this contextual selection within a specified task-level structural model. To formalize the task-relevant structure under consideration, we use a candidate SCM as Fig.2(a) for a local manipu-lation process. We assume that human and robot executions of the same local manipulation can be described using a shared set of task-level variable semantics and candidate dependency relations, while their embodiment-specific struc-tural functions may differ. This shared schema defines the hypothesis space in which cross-embodiment structural correspondence is considered; it does not assert that every candidate dependency is active in every execution context. Sharing this schema does not guarantee that a beam valid in one embodiment remains valid in another; the beam con-ditions must be evaluated relative to the instantiated mechanisms and execution context. Let X denote task-relevant conditions at the beginning of the interval, I the manipulation attempt, D the resulting object-state change, and Y the realized local effect: 

X = UX , I = UI , D = f eD (X, I, U D ), Y = f eY (X, D, U Y ), (5) where e ∈ { h, r } denotes the embodiment. Thus, humans and robots share the task-level variables and the hypothesis space of possible dependencies, but not necessarily the concrete mechanisms that realize these dependencies. The mechanisms f eD and f eY may therefore differ between humans and robots. The distinction between I and D separates an attempted manipulation from the object change that is actually realized. ## 4 Method 

We propose Causal-Beam-Guided Subtask Transfer (CBST). Human-video pretraining initializes a visual–temporal module, which is then adapted with robot demonstrations to estimate task-level semantic variables from robot ob-servations. Using these estimates, a learned selector chooses contextual parent assignments subject to causal-beam constraints within a predefined manipulation model. The selected assignments are rendered as language conditions for WAM, which combines them with the robot’s native observations to generate actions and future observation predic-tions.

## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

We pretrain CBST’s ResNet18–GRU visual-temporal encoder on Something-Something V2 (SSv2) [Goyal et al., 2017] and adapt the planner using robot demonstrations, keeping the WAM and its text encoder frozen. We evaluate LingBot-VA on RoboTwin 2.0 and LIBERO, and Cosmos Policy (Predict2.5) on LIBERO and RoboCasa [Chen et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2023, Nasiriany et al., 2024]. Comparisons include the underlying WAMs and representative pub-lished policies, using officially reported baseline scores [Li et al., 2026, Kim and Gu, 2026]. We report task success rates (%) under official success criteria, averaged across evaluated tasks. Dataset composition, baseline descriptions, implementation and hardware configurations, and evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix B. 

5.2 Results on Simulation Benchmarks 

We evaluate CBST with two frozen WAM backbones across three simulation benchmarks (Tables 1–3). On RoboTwin 2.0, LingBot-VA + CBST achieves 94.00% and 92.54% success under Easy and Hard settings. On LIBERO-Long, CBST achieves 98.60% with LingBot-VA and 97.33% with Cosmos Policy (Predict2.5). On RoboCasa, Predict2.5 + CBST reaches 72.44% with 50 demonstrations per task, compared with 71.1% for the backbone. Figure 4 illustrates a CBST execution on LIBERO. We examine human-video pretraining, contextual structural selection, and mechanism-consistency supervision on RoboTwin Easy with LingBot-VA as the frozen backbone (Table 4). Pretraining variants remove human-video initial-ization or shuffle its temporal order; conditioning variants use state-only information, the full candidate structure, or size-matched random subsets; the objective variant removes mechanism-consistency supervision. Robot adaptation data and evaluation settings remain fixed, while conditioning variants share the same trained predictor. More results across both datasets, detailed variant definitions, and computational costs are provided in Appendix D. 

5.4 Real-World Experiments 

We evaluate CBST on an AgileX dual-arm platform across ten object-placement tasks, with 20 trials per task and method. Each task specifies an object and a fixed target location. In cross-workspace configurations, no single arm can reach both the object’s initial position and its destination, requiring coordinated transport through a shared handover region. We compare Cosmos Policy with the Predict2.5 backbone against the same backbone augmented with CBST, and measure full-task success after object release. Task definitions, deployment settings, and the complete result table are provided in Appendix E. Figure 5 shows a recorded CBST cup-placement execution, including the intermediate handover. Both variants use the same ResNet18–GRU architecture, action labels, sampled frame content, and opti-mization budget; only the temporal order of the pretraining inputs differs. ## 6 Conclusion 

We presented Causal-Beam-Guided Subtask Transfer (CBST), which uses a causal-beam perspective to guide con-textual information selection for human-to-robot transfer. Human-video pretraining initializes manipulation-related representations, while robot demonstrations ground these representations in task-level semantic variables. Within a predefined manipulation model, causal-beam conditions constrain which contextual assignments are retained and sup-plied to a frozen world-action model for closed-loop control. Experiments on robot manipulation benchmarks show task-success improvements over the corresponding backbone baselines.

## A Details of Causal Beams 

A.1 Formal Details of Causal Beam Construction 

We consider a recursive structural causal model M = ( U, V, F), where U denotes the exogenous variables, V the endogenous variables, and F the set of structural mechanisms. Each non-root endogenous variable Vi is determined by Vi = fi(PA i, U ), where PA i ⊆ V denotes the endogenous parents of Vi. A context U = u determines the actual value Vi(u) of every endogenous variable. For a variable set Z, we write ZW ←w(u) for its value in the original model under the intervention W ← w and context u.

Definition A.1 (Causal Beam [Pearl, 2009]) . Given a recursive structural causal model M = ( U, V, F) and a context 

u, a causal beam Mu = ( u, V, Fu) is constructed by projecting the structural mechanisms as follows. For each projected non-root mechanism Vi = fi(PA i, U ), partition its parent set as PA i = Ci ˙∪ Ni, where Ci denotes the retained parents and Ni the remaining parents. Selections across different mechanisms must additionally be compatible. In particular, Ni ∩ Cj = ∅ for i̸ = j,and whenever intervention assignments overlap across mechanisms, they must assign the same value to every shared intervened variable. The compatible projected mechanisms, together with the retained assignment rules for root variables, constitute the causal beam Mu = ( u, V, Fu).

The sustaining condition requires the actual values Ci(u) to preserve the actual mechanism output under arbitrary assignments to the remaining parents Ni. The responsiveness condition serves a different role: it requires the projected mechanism to preserve a nontrivial dependence on its retained inputs. These two requirements distinguish a causal projection from a representation that merely records the observed output. A special case is obtained when no non-actual contingency is introduced. 

Definition A.2 (Natural Beam [Pearl, 2009]) . A causal beam is called natural when Wi = ∅ for every projected mechanism. In this case, the remaining parents are fixed at their actual values under context u, and the projected mechanism reduces to f ui (ci) = fi(ci, N i(u), u ).

A variable that is fixed in a particular projection is not thereby declared irrelevant in general. The projection is defined relative to the specified model and context, and a different context may admit a different set of retained parents and hence a different projected mechanism.

E.3 Baseline and Evaluation Protocol 

The comparison pairs Cosmos Policy with a Predict2.5 backbone and its CBST-augmented counterpart. The intended controlled comparison holds the task-adapted low-level policy checkpoint, robot observations, control settings, and evaluation conditions fixed, while adding CBST’s contextual structural conditioning. The official Cosmos Policy ALOHA experiments provide a related tabletop-manipulation reference [Kim et al., 2026]; those published real-world results use Predict2, and the official Cookbook states that Predict2.5 was not trained on ALOHA [Kim and Gu, 2026]. Those results are therefore not substituted for evaluation on our AgileX tasks. Each method is evaluated for 20 trials on each of ten tasks. This specifies the number of executions, not the number of distinct layouts or random seeds. Initial object poses, target geometry, and the mapping of initializations between methods must be documented to establish comparability. For the final protocol, the same initialization set should be used for both methods, with scene resets between executions and balanced method order. 

Success criteria. Full-task success requires the designated object to occupy its specified target region or receptacle, satisfy the task-specific placement requirement, and remain stable after gripper release within the evaluation time limit. Handover is neither an additional task objective nor an independently rewarded outcome. Grasping or transferring an object without completing its final placement does not constitute success. The handling of autonomous retries, intermediate drops, and human interventions must follow the same documented rule for both methods.
