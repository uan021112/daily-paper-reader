Abstract
Keypoint detection plays a central role in geometric vision pipelines. Existing
detectors exhibit complementary strengths across different scene structures and
keypoint budgets, and reliance on a single detector often results in suboptimal
keypoint distributions. Motivated by this observation, we propose Mixture of
Detectors (MoD) for dense matching pipelines, a unified framework for comple-
mentary keypoint detection through the adaptive composition of heterogeneous
detectors. MoD first distills heterogeneous detectors into a unified model, allowing
diverse detection maps to be predicted efficiently within a single forward pass.
Detector selection is subsequently formulated as a patch-wise, budget-conditioned
decision process, in which a lightweight router assigns the most suitable detector
to each local region. To avoid heuristic supervision, the router is trained with
task-driven rewards derived from downstream matching quality, jointly captur-
ing both keypoint quantity and precision. Extensive experiments on MegaDepth,
ScanNet, and HPatches demonstrate that MoD consistently outperforms strong
baselines across a wide range of keypoint budgets, demonstrating its effectiveness
in improving keypoint distribution and downstream geometric performance.

Introduction
Keypoint detection aims to identify distinctive image structures and constitutes a fundamental
component of geometric vision pipelines, including Structure-from-Motion (SfM) Schonberger and
Frahm (2016), Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) Engel et al. (2017), and image
matching Sarlin et al. (2020). In classical sparse matching pipelines, two sets of keypoints are
detected and subsequently matched, and the quality of matching relies heavily on repeatability under
varying illumination and viewpoint conditions. Recent dense matching approaches Edstedt et al.
(2024c); Lu et al. (2026) have partially relaxed this requirement by detecting keypoints in one image
and directly establishing correspondences in another, shifting the focus from strict repeatability to
the spatial distribution of detected keypoints. As a result, how to obtain an effective and adaptive
keypoint distribution becomes increasingly important for downstream performance.
In practice, different detectors exhibit complementary behaviors across scene structures and
keypoint budgets.
This variation stems from their distinct design choices, including nor-
malization strategies (e.g., local or global Softmax versus independent Sigmoid), train-
ing objectives (e.g., supervised learning, self-supervised learning, or reinforcement learn-
ing), and the types of geometric primitives they capture (e.g., points, lines, and edges).

Prior work such as RCM+ Lu et al. (2026) shows

challenges: (i) running all candidate detectors

lection must jointly account for local textures
and global keypoint budget, and (iii) no direct
supervision is available for detector selection.
To address these challenges, we propose Mix-
ture of Detectors (MoD), a unified framework
for adaptive detector composition. Motivated by
the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm Shazeer et al. (2017), we treat point-, line-, and edge-based
detectors as specialized experts, and formulate selection as a routing problem over these experts. (i)
To reduce the cost of multiple detectors, MoD distills heterogeneous detectors into a single model
that predicts diverse detection maps in a forward pass, preserving complementary behaviors while
maintaining efficiency. (ii) Detector selection is formulated as a patch-wise, budget-conditioned
process. A lightweight router assigns detectors to regions based on local texture, while a budget-
aware mechanism introduces a global prior that modulates preference by desired keypoint density.
(iii) To address the lack of supervision, detector selection is guided by task-driven rewards from
downstream matching performance, providing patch-level supervision that captures keypoint quantity
and precision.
To summarize, this paper makes the following contributions: (1) We present a new perspective on key-
point detection by modeling the complementary behaviors of heterogeneous detectors, and introduce
a detector selection problem conditioned on spatial regions and keypoint budgets. (2) We introduce a
unified detector that distills heterogeneous detectors into a shared model, enabling efficient prediction
of diverse detection maps in a single forward pass. (3) We propose a patch-wise, budget-aware router
that adaptively selects detectors based on local texture and global keypoint budget, trained with
task-driven rewards that jointly capture keypoint quantity and precision. (4) Extensive experiments
on MegaDepth, ScanNet, and HPatches show that MoD consistently improves downstream geometric
estimation across a wide range of keypoint budgets while maintaining competitive efficiency.

D2-Net Dusmanu et al. (2019), DISK Tyszkiewicz et al. (2020), and ALIKED Zhao et al. (2023) use
deep neural networks to jointly learn detection and description from data, improving robustness across
applications. Furthermore, DeDoDe Edstedt et al. (2024b,a) and DaD Edstedt et al. (2025a) adopt a
decoupled detection-description paradigm, motivated by inconsistent optimization objectives between
detection and description. Joint optimization can lead to suboptimal solutions and restrict flexibility
of keypoint distributions. Following this line, MoD adopts a fully decoupled detection formulation.
Beyond point-based detection, line segment detection methods such as LSD Grompone von Gioi et al.
(2010), SOLD2 Pautrat et al. (2021), and DeepLSD Pautrat et al. (2023) capture structured geometric
primitives, particularly in man-made environments with prevalent linear structures. Similarly, edge
detection methods, including Canny Canny (1986), EDTER Pu et al. (2022), and MuGE Zhou et al.
(2024), identify object boundaries and fine-grained contours often missed by point-based detectors.
These detection methods provide complementary information to point-based detection, enriching
spatial coverage. However, the complementarity among different detection methods remains largely
under-explored.
Mixture of Experts.
Mixture of Experts (MoE) Shazeer et al. (2017); Riquelme et al. (2021);
Zhou et al. (2022); Chen et al. (2023) is a widely adopted paradigm that improves model capacity
through experts and a routing mechanism. In most works, particularly in large-scale language and
vision models, experts correspond to different feed-forward networks with the same architecture,
and the router activates a subset of them for each input token. Such designs enhance representation
learning and computational efficiency via conditional computation. In this work, different types
of detectors, including point-, line-, and edge-based methods, are treated as experts rather than
multiple networks with similar functions. These detectors capture distinct geometric structures and
exhibit complementary behaviors across scenes, improving keypoint distribution and downstream
performance in dense matching pipelines.

Method
As illustrated in Figure 2, MoD employs a shared encoder to extract features, followed by multiple
detection heads that produce detection maps. In Stage 1, distillation from six state-of-the-art detectors
enables MoD to generate six maps in a single forward pass. In Stage 2, the encoder and heads are
frozen, and a router uses texture features and keypoint budget to assign the most suitable detector to
each 8 × 8 patch. Routing decisions are evaluated with an off-the-shelf matcher Edstedt et al. (2024c),
which provides rewards based on keypoint quantity and precision to supervise training.
3


3.1
Stage 1: Detection Distillation
3.1.1
Feature Extraction
We adopt a VGG Simonyan and Zisserman (2015) network as the encoder to extract multi-scale dense
feature maps from the input image I. VGG provides rich local texture features, essential for keypoint
detection and subsequent routing. Specifically, features are collected from different backbone stages
to form a feature pyramid {F1, F1/2, F1/4, F1/8}, where each subscript denotes the spatial resolution
relative to I. The unified encoder aggregates knowledge from multiple teachers during distillation,
yielding more robust and generalizable texture representations. As a result, the student may even
outperform individual teachers.
3.1.2
Detection Head
Because detectors produce detection probabilities via different mechanisms, we introduce two types
of detection heads: image-level and patch-level. Specifically, ALIKED Zhao et al. (2023), DeDoDe
Edstedt et al. (2024a), DaD Edstedt et al. (2025a), DeepLSD Pautrat et al. (2023), and MuGE Zhou
et al. (2024) generate keypoint probabilities at the image level, whereas SuperPoint DeTone et al.
(2018) predicts probabilities at the patch level.
Image-level head. For detectors operating on full-resolution maps, we adopt depthwise convolution
refiners from DKM Edstedt et al. (2023). The prediction follows a coarse-to-fine refinement across
{F1/8, F1/4, F1/2, F1}. At each scale, a refiner takes the encoder feature and predicts a residual
added to upsampled logits from the previous scale. This progressive refinement recovers fine spatial
details while preserving global consistency. After obtaining the final logits, different normalization
functions are applied to align with teacher behaviors. For DeDoDe and DaD, we apply Softmax over
all pixels. For ALIKED, DeepLSD, and MuGE, we apply Sigmoid independently per pixel.
Patch-level head. For SuperPoint, which predicts keypoints at the patch level, we introduce a head on
F1/8. Specifically, a VGG-style block predicts a 65-channel logit map, where 64 channels correspond
to the 8 × 8 locations within each patch and one channel represents a dustbin class, i.e., a no-keypoint
class. Softmax is applied over the 65 channels, then the dustbin channel is discarded. The remaining
probabilities are reshaped to recover a dense detection map at the original resolution.
3.1.3
Learning Objective
To faithfully distill heterogeneous detectors, we adopt different objectives according to how each
method generates detection probabilities. (1) Image-level Supervision. For DeDoDe and DaD, which
generate probabilities via spatial Softmax over image-level logits, we use image-level Kullback-
Leibler (KL) divergence to align the predicted distributions. (2) Patch-level Supervision. For
SuperPoint, which predicts probabilities in each 8 × 8 patch, we compute KL divergence within each
patch to align the predicted distributions. (3) Point-level Supervision. For ALIKED, DeepLSD, and
MuGE, which model probabilities independently per pixel using Sigmoid, we regress the logit maps
with an ℓ2 loss. The overall objective sums the losses over all detectors.
3.2
Stage 2: Reward-guided Router Learning
3.2.1
Router
In Stage 2, a router is trained with reward-guided supervision to select the most suitable detector
per patch based on local texture and global keypoint budget. As illustrated in Figure 3, the router
has two branches, a budget branch and a texture branch, whose outputs are fused for final routing
decisions.
Texture branch. The texture branch captures local patterns to determine the appropriate detector for
each region. It takes the frozen encoder feature at 1/8 resolution and processes it through a CNN-
based encoder to produce a spatial feature map. This branch models local structures such as corners,
edges, and low-texture regions, enabling the router to exploit spatial heterogeneity. Let h = H/8
and w = W/8. The output, denoted by ˆR ∈R(K+1)×h×w, provides patch-wise preferences over
detectors. Here, K is the number of detectors, and the additional class represents a background option
where no detector is selected.

3.2.2
Reward
We construct a patch-level reward
from matching quality of keypoints
predicted by different detection heads
under varying budgets. We extract
keypoints from all K detection heads
under M budgets in source image, and
use an off-the-shelf matcher Edstedt
et al. (2025b) to find correspondences
in target image. Let x denote key-
points in source image, xp their pre-
dicted matched positions, and xgt the ground-truth matched positions. We compute the error e for
each match as the Euclidean distance between xp and xgt. Given threshold τ, a keypoint is an inlier
if e < τ, yielding two rewards.
Quantity reward. The first reward rqty
p,k evaluates the ability of each detector to produce a larger
number of reliable keypoints within a patch, where p indexes the patch and k ∈1, . . . , K denotes
the detector class. We compute the number of inliers for each detector within each patch. Detectors
are then ranked according to the inlier counts within each patch, and a reward of 1 is assigned to the
top-k detector, while the remaining detectors receive 0.
Precision reward. The second reward rprec
p,k measures the precision of keypoints within a patch. For
each detector, we compute the average error of its inlier matches within each patch, since detectors
whose keypoints are more precisely localized on distinctive textures tend to produce more accurate
correspondences in the target image. Detectors are then ranked by this average error in each patch,
and a reward of 1 is assigned to the top-k detector, while the remaining detectors receive 0.

Experiments
4.1
Implementation Details
In stage 1, the encoder is initialized with ImageNet pretraining and jointly optimized with all detection
heads using AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter (2017). The learning rate is 1 × 10−4 with weight decay
1 × 10−5, and the model is trained for 200,000 iterations with batch size 4. In stage 2, the router
is trained with AdamW using a learning rate of 2 × 10−4 and weight decay 1 × 10−5, for 20,000
iterations with batch size 4. Rewards are assigned to the top-2 detectors for both quantity and
precision rewards. The error threshold is 1 pixel, and the trade-off parameter in the reward is α = 0.8.
All models are trained on a NVIDIA 4090 GPU. Following DaD Edstedt et al. (2025a), the longer
image side is resized to 1024 during evaluation. We use RoMa v2 Edstedt et al. (2025b) as the
matcher during training, and evaluate with both RoMa Edstedt et al. (2024c) and RCM+ Lu et al.
(2026) to assess cross-matcher generalization and avoid overfitting to a single matcher.
4.2
Pose and Homography Estimation
Datasets. MegaDepth Li and Snavely (2018) contains one million Internet images spanning 196
outdoor scenes, with sparse 3D reconstructions from COLMAP and depth maps from multi-view
stereo. We evaluate on 1,500 image pairs from the Sacre Coeur and St. Peter’s Square scenes
following LoFTR Sun et al. (2021). ScanNet Dai et al. (2017) contains 1613 monocular sequences
with ground-truth poses and depth maps. We evaluate on 1,500 image pairs from SuperGlue Sarlin
et al. (2020). The HPatches dataset Balntas et al. (2017) includes 52 illumination and 56 viewpoint
sequences. Following DaD Edstedt et al. (2025a), we evaluate on the 56 viewpoint sequences with
geometric transformations.
Metrics. For MegaDepth and ScanNet, we report the AUC of pose error at 5◦under five keypoint
budgets: 512, 1024, 2048, 4096, and 8192. For HPatches, we report the AUC of end-point error at 3
pixels under the same five keypoint budgets.

original detectors in most settings, indicating effective multi-teacher distillation. On MegaDepth,

detection maps. The avg. det. map and the max. det. map denote non-learnable fusion schemes that

indicate that the proposed router more effectively selects suitable detectors across different regions.

across most budgets. A balanced setting (α = 0.5) improves over single-term variants. The best
performance is achieved by the full model with α = 0.8. This indicates that emphasizing keypoint
quantity while accounting for precision yields more effective routing decisions.

5
Limitations and Discussion
We investigate the use of pose estimation error as an additional reward signal for router training.
However, this strategy does not yield observable improvements over the current design. This
observation suggests that directly using downstream task errors as supervision is challenging, likely
due to the indirect and noisy relationship between detector selection and final task performance.
Future work may explore more principled approaches for incorporating downstream objectives, such
as homography or pose estimation accuracy, into the reward formulation through more stable and
structured supervision mechanisms.
6
Conclusion
We presented MoD, a unified framework that integrates multiple heterogeneous keypoint detectors
through knowledge distillation and budget-aware routing. By distilling diverse detectors into a shared
9


encoder with multiple lightweight heads, MoD preserves their complementary inductive biases while
achieving efficient inference within a single forward pass. Building on this, the proposed router
adaptively selects detectors at the patch level by jointly considering local texture characteristics and
global keypoint budget, and is trained via a reward signal that captures both keypoint quantity and
precision. Extensive experiments on MegaDepth, ScanNet, and HPatches demonstrate that MoD
consistently outperforms strong baselines across a wide range of keypoint budgets, validating the
effectiveness of both the distillation and routing components.
