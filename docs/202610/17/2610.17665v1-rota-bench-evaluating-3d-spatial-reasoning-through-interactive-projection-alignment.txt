## Abstract 

Can vision-language models turn 2D observations into 3D understanding that supports precise action? This requires inferring object geometry, anticipating the effects of rotation, and refining actions through visual feedback. We introduce RoTA-Bench to evaluate these abilities through interactive 3D-to-2D projection alignment. Given a target silhouette, a model observes a rotated object and chooses successive rotations to recover the target projection within a limited action budget. Our automated pipeline constructs silhouette-preserving 3D objects from 2D icons and uses reversible initialization to guarantee solvability, yielding 674 tasks and a stratified 100-task test set. Empirically, we find that (i) a substantial human-model gap persists: GPT-5.6-Sol succeeds on only 17% of tasks, whereas 20 undergraduate participants collectively complete the test set four times with a 100% success rate; (ii) image-space and pose-space progress can diverge: at matched states, the model more often improves silhouette overlap while worsening pose alignment than humans (19.4% vs. 10.9%); and (iii) fine-grained control is sensitive to interaction history: removing it leaves 96.8% of the model’s actions at a coarse 30-degree step size and sharply reduces observed task success. These findings highlight the challenge of converting visual evidence into precise 3D control, beyond recognizing or matching individual images. 

# 1 Introduction 

Psychological and cognitive-science research establishes spatial perception as a foundational domain of cognitive ability (Carroll, 1993), essential for maintaining object constancy across viewpoints (Shepard & Metzler, 1971) and recovering three-dimensional geometry from two-dimensional projections (Richards et al., 1987) through the mental representation, manipulation, and inference of shape and orientation. As vision-language models are increasingly applied in visual question answering, interactive agents, and embodied systems (Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026), agents in interactive settings such as embodied exploration and geometry-aware navigation must translate spatial understanding into physical actions (Zhu et al., 2025). However, current models continue to face challenges in reasoning about three-dimensional positions, orientations, and spatial relationships (Ma et al., 2025). Therefore, addressing these challenges requires evaluation tools that assess whether models can turn 2D observations into 3D understanding that supports precise action. However, current visual and spatial benchmarks fall short of this requirement. Static benchmarks evaluate spatial rea-soning through passive question answering on fixed images (Johnson et al., 2017; Hudson & Manning, 2019; Yue et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024), lacking action execution and closed-loop feedback entirely. Conversely, existing interactive benchmarks spanning software interfaces, visual mazes, and sliding puzzles (Zhou et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Ro-driguez Salgado, 2026; Mayer et al., 2025) focus on high-level task planning, tool invocation, or spatial pathfinding

2.2 Static Benchmarks for Visual and Spatial Reasoning 

Static benchmarks evaluate visual and spatial capabilities on fixed inputs. Foundational suites probe spatial relation queries on synthetic 3D scenes (Johnson et al., 2017) and natural scene graphs (Hudson & Manning, 2019), alongside multidisciplinary visual diagrams (Yue et al., 2024) and geometric problem-solving (Lu et al., 2024). Recent diagnostic benchmarks target 6D object pose (Wang et al., 2025) and multi-step action preconditions under complex indoor scenes (Xie et al., 2026). While A2RBench (Ma et al., 2026) procedurally generates formally verifiable, multidimensional abstract reasoning tasks, it focuses on symbolic rules rather than grounded 3D spatial perception. Concurrently, offline spatial analyses demonstrate that high performance on static visual mazes often stems from serial search in token space rather than genuine visual planning (Rodriguez Salgado, 2026). Crucially, these benchmarks are strictly non-interactive, evaluating spatial reasoning in a single turn without any action or visual feedback. 

2.3 Interactive Benchmarks for Visual and Spatial Reasoning 

Interactive benchmarks evaluate visual and spatial reasoning through multi-turn closed-loop interaction. In embod-ied AI, interactive benchmarks and agent architectures evaluate spatial reasoning through embodied navigation, long-horizon task execution, and scene exploration (Shridhar et al., 2020; Savva et al., 2019; Driess et al., 2023; Rana et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025); however, they predominantly emphasize macroscopic agent locomotion and high-level semantic planning. Across digital domains, benchmarks test 2D layout grounding and interface parsing for web browsing and operating system control (Zhou et al., 2024; Koh et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024), yet they operate strictly within 2D planar graphical displays. Closer to geometric reasoning, interactive benchmarks evaluate spatial

# 3 RoTA-Bench 

RoTA-Bench evaluates whether vision-language models can turn 2D observations into 3D understanding that supports precise action through interactive projection alignment (Figure 1). Figure 2 summarizes task construction and closed-loop evaluation. Below, we detail the benchmark design, task formulation, automated task construction, and evaluation protocol. 

3.1 Motivation and Design Rationale 

Classical studies in cognitive vision and psychology demonstrate that underlying 3D geometry and orientations can be inferred from 2D visual contours (Richards et al., 1987) and compared through mental rotation (Shepard & Metzler, 1971). Inspired by these foundational insights, we formulate 3D orientation alignment as a projection-matching task: rotating a 3D object until its 2D silhouette matches a target image. Restricting physical interaction to 3D rotation provides two key methodological advantages. First, out-of-plane rotations induce nonlinear silhouette deformations, aspect changes, and self-occlusion, creating an inherently visual challenge that resists textual representation. Second, pure rotation cleanly decouples spatial orientation reasoning from extraneous physical dynamics—such as translational drift, grasp affordances, and contact friction—allowing us to examine whether models can turn 2D observations into 3D understanding that supports precise action. To reduce reliance on semantic shortcuts and encourage visual-spatial reasoning, RoTA-Bench synthesizes 3D objects from 2D vector icons rather than drawing directly from common 3D CAD repositories, where familiar shapes may trig-ger pre-trained priors. By extruding 2D icons within a spherical volume, this automated generation produces abstract, novel 3D geometries while mathematically guaranteeing that the canonical front view reproduces the original icon, preserving its fine-grained details (Appendix E). Finally, generating initial poses via sequences of reversible discrete rotations guarantees by construction that every task is solvable. 

3.2 Task Definition 

Following the design rationale in Section 3.1, we formulate the interactive projection-matching task as follows. Each task instance is defined by a rigid 3D object G ⊂ R3 centered at the coordinate origin, an initial pose R0 ∈ SO (3) , and a canonical target pose R⋆ = I3. Let Π( ·) denote the orthographic projection operator that renders the binary silhouette mask of object G at a given pose. At step t, the environment provides an observation triplet: 

Ot = ( I⋆, V t, I t), I⋆ = Π( G, R ⋆), It = Π( G, R t), (1) where I⋆ ∈ { 0, 1}H×W represents the invariant target silhouette, and It ∈ { 0, 1}H×W is the current projection silhou-ette. To complement the binary silhouette with missing depth and occlusion cues, the agent also receives a depth-shaded auxiliary view Vt ∈ [0 , 255] H×W , rendered from the identical viewpoint as It.Conditioned on this visual feedback, the agent interacts with the object through a discrete action space. At each step 

t, the agent selects an action at from a vocabulary A consisting of 24 camera-fixed rotation actions and a terminal STOP command. These 24 actions span six directional senses across three principal camera axes—pitch (up/down), yaw (left/right), and in-plane roll (clockwise/counterclockwise)—paired with four step sizes ∆θ ∈ { 1◦, 5◦, 10 ◦, 30 ◦}

to enable both coarse and fine pose adjustments. Letting Q(at) ∈ SO (3) denote the rotation matrix for action at, the object pose updates via: 

Rt+1 = Q(at)Rt. (2) Executing at updates the 3D state and re-renders observations It+1 and Vt+1 .To evaluate projection alignment, we compute the projection Intersection over Union (projection IoU) between the current projection It and the target silhouette I⋆:

An episode succeeds whenever st ≥ τ , where we set τ = 0 .90 . This threshold enforces high visual alignment fidelity while providing a necessary tolerance margin for the discrete action space, where exact alignment is difficult to achieve in finite steps. Furthermore, because geometric symmetries and orthographic self-overlap can produce identical silhouettes from distinct 3D poses, the task naturally admits multiple equivalent target poses. We formalize this goal set as: 

Rgoal (G, I ⋆) = {R ∈ SO (3) | IoU (Π( G, R ), I ⋆) ≥ τ }. (4) Finally, an episode terminates upon task success ( st ≥ τ ), voluntary halting via STOP, reaching a maximum action budget, or stagnation after consecutive steps without improving the best IoU. 

3.3 Dataset Construction 

Our automated pipeline constructs the benchmark across three sequential phases: task generation, reference search, and dataset construction, as summarized in Figure 2. First, in the task generation phase, following the design rationale in Section 3.1, we curate 2D vector icons from the open-source Game-icons library (Appendix B), rasterizing them into high-resolution binary masks after geometric filtering on connectivity, occupancy, aspect ratio, and foreground point count (Appendix G). To construct a 3D object from each 2D icon while strictly bounding its spatial profile under arbitrary rotations, the planar point set P ⊂ R2 is scaled within a disk of radius r and symmetrically extruded along the depth axis under a spherical envelope: 

h(x, y ) = √max (r2 − x2 − y2, 0) , z ∈ [−h(x, y ), h (x, y )] . (5) The resulting solid G = {(x, y, z ) | (x, y ) ∈ P, |z| ≤ h(x, y )} is discretized as a dense 3D point cloud (Appendix G). Moreover, because 3D rotations preserve Euclidean norms, any rotated pose satisfies x2 + y2 + z2 ≤ r2. This spher-ical invariance prevents viewport clipping and fixes the camera scale globally across all tasks. To provide monocular depth cues and bridge discretization gaps between sampled points, visual observations are rendered via orthographic projection using depth-dependent shading and Gaussian smoothing (Appendix G). To preserve the original 2D icon’s fine contour details (Appendix E), the canonical target pose is fixed to R⋆ = I3,and initial poses R0 = Q(bL) · · · Q(b1)R⋆ are generated via L ∼ Uniform {6, . . . , 16 } discrete rotations. At each step, 

bk ∈ A 30 ◦ rotates by 30 ◦, with its direction sampled uniformly at random from the six candidate camera directions (each with probability 1/6 ), forbidding the immediate inverse ( bk̸ = b−1

) to avoid cancellations and guarantee genuine displacement. Initial poses already projection-aligned ( s0 ≥ τ ) are discarded. Crucially, because every rotation in SO (3) is invertible, executing the reversed sequence Q(b1)−1 · · · Q(bL)−1 restores R⋆, ensuring 100% reachability and task solvability by construction. In total, this generation pipeline yields 674 canonical tasks with unique source icons. Second, in the reference search phase, we compute reference paths using pose-guided beam search and 30 ◦-restricted breadth-first search. These discover significantly shorter trajectories than raw reversed generation paths (Appendix H). Finally, in the dataset construction phase, a stratified test set of 100 tasks was frozen across reference path lengths (≥ 4 steps) using proportional stratified sampling via Hamilton’s method and deterministic hash sorting, reserving the remaining 574 tasks for future training and research (Appendix I).

# 4 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate nine vision-language models—GPT-5.6-Sol, GPT-5.6-Terra, GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026a), Gemini 3.6 Flash (DeepMind, 2026), Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5 (Anthropic, 2026a,b), Qwen 3.7 Plus (Qwen Team, 2026), GPT-6-Astra (OpenAI, 2026b), and SpatioLM-Understanding-InternVL3.5 (Wu et al., 2026)—as well as two GPT-5.6-Sol-based agent frameworks, ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) and Magentic (Fourney et al., 2024), 20 undergraduate participants (Human; Appendix C), two stochastic baselines (Uniform random and Random with IoU rollback), and two greedy base-lines. All evaluations follow a common task protocol, action space, decision budget, success criterion, and termination rules. All models are evaluated at temperature 0; closed-source models are queried via APIs (total cost: $1,482.07), while SpatioLM is deployed locally on a server equipped with eight NVIDIA A100 (40GB) GPUs. Complete prompts, hyperparameters, agent tools, and evaluation settings are detailed in Appendices L and M. 

# 5 Results 

In this section, we present main results, conduct rotation direction and angle analysis, perform an ablation of auxiliary views and interaction history, analyze action association with projection and pose discrepancies, and discuss limitations and future work. 

5.1 Main Results 

Table 1 presents alignment performance across 100 test tasks, with success counts and confidence intervals in Ap-pendix K.1. Frontier models struggle markedly on interactive 3D projection alignment: GPT-5.6-Sol achieves the highest success rate at only 17.0%, followed by Gemini 3.6 Flash at 10.0%, while ReAct attains the highest final IoU of 0.628 (16.0% success). Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Opus 5, and Qwen 3.7 Plus fail entirely (0.0% success), with final IoU values (0.484–0.504) barely exceeding uniform random exploration (0.473). Notably, SpatioLM-Understanding-InternVL3.5 suffers a catastrophic policy collapse, where 91.12% of its actions degenerate into YAW_POS 10 ◦, yielding 0.0% success and the lowest final IoU of 0.463 (below random actions). In stark contrast, human participants achieve a 100.0% success rate, reaching an average final IoU of 0.923 in 16.22 steps. As shown in Figure 3, human trajecto-ries exhibit overall IoU gains surpassing the 0.90 threshold, whereas model trajectories plateau early between 0.50 and 0.63. The two greedy baselines sharpen this distinction: at each step, each enumerates all 24 legal rotation successors using the ground-truth simulator, then selects the action that maximizes successor projection IoU or minimizes true 3D pose error, respectively. The IoU-greedy baseline succeeds on only 39.0% of tasks (final IoU 0.725), showing that greedily maximizing immediate projection overlap is insufficient on many tasks; by contrast, the pose-greedy baseline solves every task (100.0%, final IoU 0.924 in 8.32 steps), showing that reliable access to the true 3D pose error is suf-ficient to complete all of them. Overall, the success rates and IoU trajectories show a substantial human–model gap on RoTA-Bench.

5.4 Action Association with Projection and Pose Discrepancies 

We next examine whether rotation-angle choices rely more on visible projection overlap or on the underlying 3D pose discrepancy. Figure 4 and Appendix Table 10 report their correlations with pre-action IoU and pose error. For GPT-5.6-

8MAC-AutoML 

Sol, the correlation is stronger with IoU than with pose error ( ρ = −0.536 , 95% CI [−0.617 , −0.443] vs. ρ = 0 .368 ,95% CI [0 .166 , 0.532] ). Human angles are strongly associated with both ( ρ = −0.696 , 95% CI [−0.763 , −0.633] ,and ρ = 0 .751 , 95% CI [0 .675 , 0.828] ). Most evaluated models show the same relative pattern as GPT-5.6-Sol. These results suggest that most models rely more on projection overlap when choosing rotation angles, whereas humans use both projection overlap and 3D pose discrepancy. To conduct a comparative analysis under the same conditions as closely as possible, we examine action consequences across the 211 exact state pairs shared by Human and GPT-5.6-Sol, where direct visual coverage before the matched actions is close (42.01% vs. 46.21%, Figure 5 and Appendix Table 8). We find that image-space and pose-space progress can diverge even when actions start from the same pose. Humans execute substantially more DC actions (33.18% vs. 23.70%), showing a willingness to accept transient drops in 2D overlap to reduce underlying 3D pose error. Conversely, GPT-5.6-Sol executes nearly twice as many UF actions as humans (19.43% vs. 10.90%), showing a greater willingness to accept increases in 3D pose error to improve 2D overlap. For the remaining outcomes, humans achieve far higher UC actions (50.24% vs. 34.60%) and avoid DF moves (5.69% vs. 22.27%). Together, these results show that image-space and pose-space progress can diverge: at matched states, GPT-5.6-Sol more often improves silhouette overlap while worsening pose alignment than humans. 

5.5 Limitations and Future Work 

This work has several limitations. First, the 3D objects are extruded from a limited set of 2D icons, which constrains shape diversity and cannot fully represent complex 3D geometries. Second, each task features only a single target un-der discrete rotation actions, making the interaction dynamics relatively simplified compared to continuous physical environments. Future work can expand task and icon diversity by leveraging generative models to synthesize richer 2D icons or directly construct more expressive 3D meshes. In addition, the benchmark can be extended by introducing continuous rotation control, external occlusions, and multi-object alignment or assembly tasks to further heighten the spatial reasoning challenge. Finally, automatically generated tasks could provide scalable environments for reinforce-ment learning fine-tuning, offering a way to study whether interactive training improves the use of visual feedback for precise 3D control. 

# 6 Conclusion 

RoTA-Bench evaluates whether vision-language models can turn 2D observations into 3D understanding that supports precise action through interactive projection alignment. Its automated pipeline constructs silhouette-preserving 3D objects and uses reversible initialization to guarantee solvability, yielding 674 tasks and a stratified 100-task test set. Evaluations reveal a substantial human–model success gap, show that image-space and pose-space progress can diverge, and find that fine-grained control is sensitive to interaction history. These findings highlight the challenge of converting visual evidence into precise 3D control.

# D Computation of Object Symmetries 

Symmetry allows different orientations to represent the same object pose. We estimate these symmetries from the normalized 2D points used to build each object, without using evaluation results. 

Finding symmetries. We test rotational orders n = 12 , 11 , . . . , 2. Order n requires every rotation by 360 ◦k/n, k =1, . . . , n − 1, to pass the point-matching check below. We keep the first accepted order; otherwise, we keep only the identity rotation. For reflections, we test Q(α) = Rot (α) diag (1 , −1) , where α is twice the mirror-axis angle. We search 0◦ ≤ α < 360 ◦ in 2◦ steps and refine the four best candidates within ±2◦. A reflection and all its combinations with the retained rotations must pass the same check. 

Checking point matches. A symmetry should map the point set onto itself. We allow a small error because the points are sampled. Using up to 1,500 evenly indexed points, we estimate the median distance h to the nearest other point in the full set and set τ = 3 h + 10 −6. We measure nearest-neighbor distances in both directions between the original and transformed sets. In each direction, the 99th-percentile distance must be at most τ , and the maximum must be at most 

2τ . We screen candidates with the sample, then check accepted candidates with all points.

# H Search Reference Construction 

The inverse initialization sequence provides a feasible solution independently of search. Replaying it checks that the implemented transformations and projection scoring reproduce successful alignment. We combine pose-guided Beam-256 search with a breadth-first search (BFS) restricted to 30 ◦ rotations, following the standard discrete-state search formulation (Hart et al., 1968). At depth d, Beam-256 computes the pose residual for each of the six directions, selects the closest angle from the four allowed angles for each direction, and therefore generates six candidate actions per retained path. It then keeps at most 256 candidates ranked by pose distance to the target and orientation diversity: 

Bd+1 = Top 256 ({p a | p ∈ B d, a ∈ A} ) , |A| = 6 . (12) The fixed-angle search instead performs BFS over rotation-matrix states, expanding every state at depth d with the same six actions and stopping at the first depth whose replayed projection reaches the success threshold: 

Sd+1 = {R(a)R | a ∈ A , R ∈ S d}, S0 = {Rinit }. (13) Both searches replay candidate paths and verify them with the same projection IoU threshold used in evaluation. We retain the shorter verified candidate as the Search reference, although it is not necessarily optimal. Restricting the search keeps reference construction computationally feasible, while internal poses and reference paths remain hidden from evaluated models, agents, and humans. The complete implementation is provided in the open-source code. K.7 Oracle Action Decomposition 

To separate direction and angle selection, we evaluate the 2,366 states visited by GPT-5.6-Sol on the 100-task test set. For each state, we enumerate all 24 legal actions and compute the successor pose error of each action. Here, the best action is defined as the legal action that minimizes the symmetry-adjusted successor pose error. The direction oracle keeps Sol’s selected angle and chooses the best rotation axis and sign; the angle oracle keeps Sol’s selected direction and chooses the best legal angle; the joint oracle chooses the best action without either constraint. Uniform random averages successor pose error over all 24 actions. As shown in Table 17, correcting direction yields a larger one-step improvement than correcting angle, while correcting both yields the lowest expected error. These results indicate that GPT-5.6-Sol still has substantial room for improvement in both rotation-direction and rotation-angle selection.
