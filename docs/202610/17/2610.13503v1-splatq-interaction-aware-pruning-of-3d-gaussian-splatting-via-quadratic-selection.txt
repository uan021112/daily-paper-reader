SPLATQ: INTERACTION-AWARE PRUNING OF 3D
GAUSSIAN SPLATTING VIA QUADRATIC SELECTION
Hongbin Ye, Haixu Liu, Shenwei Kang, Wenxiu Wei, Tinglan Wang, Hongdong Zhu, Fan Tang
Qi Gao, Yin Ma, Kai Wen, Hai Wei, Nuo Qian, Wei-Shi Zheng, Shaobo Chen, Fa-Ting Hong, Jingke Meng
ABSTRACT
Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) pruning methods primarily rely on global
Top-K selection based on independent importance scores, lacking regional budget
control and ignoring joint deletion effects. Consequently, they cannot ensure bal-
anced reconstruction quality across a scene. We propose SplatQ, a combinatorial
pruning algorithm that coordinates regional budget allocation and joint Gaussian
selection under a fixed budget. SplatQ partitions Gaussians by spatial proximity and
appearance similarity into size-limited blocks, decomposing global selection into
subproblems of a specific size. It then smooths importance-based block retention
quotas to prevent excessive budget concentration. Finally, it constructs a quadratic
approximation of joint deletion effects using the responses of representative pixel
colors to changes in Gaussian opacity. Individual responses, pairwise interactions,
and quota penalties are incorporated into a quadratic unconstrained binary opti-
mization (QUBO) objective, solved using simulated annealing (SA) or a coherent
Ising machine (CIM) to jointly select Gaussians. Experiments on 5 benchmarks
demonstrate that SplatQ outperforms state-of-the-art pruning methods in aggregate
reconstruction quality, spatial quality balance, and rendering speed. Ablations con-
firm that budget smoothing improves local detail reconstruction, while interaction
modeling improves Gaussian selection quality under a fixed budget.

INTRODUCTION
Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023) enables high-quality real-time rendering through an explicit
Gaussian representation, providing a practical foundation for the reconstruction and interaction of
3D digital assets. Complex scenes, however, often contain large numbers of Gaussians, whose
storage, memory, and rendering costs limit asset transmission and deployment (Fan et al., 2024;
Girish et al., 2024). Gaussian pruning reduces these costs by removing primitives. Methods such
as LightGaussian (Fan et al., 2024) and PUP 3D-GS (Hanson et al., 2025b) combine importance
estimation with post-pruning fine-tuning to balance model size and rendering quality.
Under a fixed budget and aggressive pruning, we observe that results with similar overall image-
quality metrics can exhibit different patterns of local degradation: structures and textures remain
clear in some regions, while other regions become visibly blurred or distorted. Existing methods
also make different regional trade-offs. For example, PUP 3D-GS discusses the trade-off between
foreground detail and background quality (Hanson et al., 2025b), whereas GaussianPOP emphasizes
the preservation of detail-rich regions (Lee et al., 2026). These choices have their own motivations,
but they raise a question: when the number of retained Gaussians is strictly limited, how can pruning
preserve both overall rendering quality and reconstruction quality across different regions?
We have two observations from previous global importance ranking works (Fan et al., 2024; Hanson
et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2026): First, global ranking does not directly control how the retention
budget is distributed across a scene. Such methods score each Gaussian separately and retain the
highest-scoring Gaussians globally (Fan et al., 2024; Hanson et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2026). The
number retained in each region therefore depends primarily on the score distribution. If Gaussians
in certain regions consistently receive higher scores, the limited budget may concentrate there,
leaving other regions with insufficient representational capacity. Thus,relying on a global ranking
cannot guarantee that the rendering quality of all regions is well preserved under aggressive pruning
Figure 1(a) illustrates this spatial quality imbalance: pruning baselines preserve some structures while
losing detail in other regions of the same scene. Second, Gaussian pruning under a fixed budget is a
combinatorial optimization problem. It requires selecting, subject to a retention constraint, a set of
Gaussians that renders well together. Independent scoring followed by Top-K ranking reduces this set-

combination that achieves better reconstruction quality.
To address these limitations, we propose SplatQ, a post-training Gaussian pruning framework that
performs similarity-guided partitioning, budget assignment, and interaction-aware quadratic selection
in sequence. SplatQ aims to reduce disparities in local reconstruction quality under aggressive pruning
and use the retained Gaussian budget more effectively, improving overall and local reconstruction
quality with the same number of Gaussians. First, SplatQ partitions Gaussians into local blocks
according to spatial proximity and appearance similarity. These blocks provide units for budget
assignment while keeping the selection problems at a specific size. Second, to control local retention
counts, SplatQ derives an initial quota for each block from importance estimates and adjusts the
quota distribution to reduce the risk of concentrating the budget in a few blocks while leaving others
with insufficient capacity. Finally, to account for the joint effects of deleting multiple Gaussians,
SplatQ uses rendering responses to changes in opacity multipliers to estimate individual deletion
effects and pairwise interactions. Figure 1(b) illustrates how block quotas, budget assignment, and
quadratic selection progressively improve detail preservation across the highlighted regions. It then
formulates and solves a QUBO (Glover et al., 2018) under each block’s quota to select a retained
set with a smaller estimated joint deletion effect. Because solving QUBOs with classical methods
requires more computation than direct Top-K ranking, we also examine a coherent Ising machine
(CIM) as a hardware solver backend and assess its potential to improve solving efficiency. After
pruning, SplatQ fine-tunes the retained Gaussians to recover rendering quality.
We conduct extensive experiments on 27 scenes from 5 benchmarks covering indoor, outdoor, and
objects, comparing SplatQ with eight open-source pruning methods. Under a common 90% pruning
setting, SplatQ achieves the best overall image quality and rendering speed among the compared
methods, while providing more uniform reconstruction quality across image regions. We further
evaluate different pruning ratios, fine-tuning budgets, and combinations of pruning rounds and per-
round fine-tuning iterations, facilitating comparisons with the reported settings of methods without
released code. SplatQ also remains effective in a picture-free setting, where neither pruning nor fine-
tuning uses the original training photographs and fine-tuning relies on images rendered by the fixed
pretrained model. Even under this constraint, SplatQ surpasses the strongest baseline, MesonGS (Xie
et al., 2024), in dataset-macro PSNR. Ablation studies further validate the effectiveness of partition-

RELATED WORK
Gaussian compression and pruning.
Existing 3DGS pruning methods often rank or sample
Gaussians by rendering contributions, using compositing weights, coverage, volume, opacity, or
gradients (Girish et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; Ali et al., 2024; Mousa & Maalouf,
2026). These criteria may overlook compensation by other Gaussians and undervalue sparsely
observed regions. Other methods use parameter sensitivity or its approximations (Hanson et al.,
2025b; Chen et al., 2026; Niedermayr et al., 2024), or estimate single-Gaussian deletion error relative
to renderings of the current or original model (Lee et al., 2026; Ramlot et al., 2026). However,
sensitivity is not equivalent to actual deletion error, and individual deletion errors do not directly
characterize joint removal. Learned scores, masks, or sparse representations enable reconstruction-
driven adaptation but require additional learning (Yang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2024; Zhang et al.,
2024; 2025; Liu et al., 2025b). Further methods consider relationships among Gaussians through
neighboring appearance differences or local overlap (Lee et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2026), or merge
Gaussians into new representatives (Wang et al., 2025). In contrast, SplatQ partitions pretrained
Gaussians by spatial and appearance similarity, initializes and smooths blockwise retention quotas
under an exact global budget, and jointly selects Gaussians within each block using a quadratic
objective built from individual and pairwise linearized rendering responses.
Balancing Rendering Quality Across Image Regions.
NeRF++ (Zhang et al., 2020), Mip-NeRF
360 (Barron et al., 2022), and HoGS (Liu et al., 2025a) study how to represent near and distant
content during scene reconstruction. For a pretrained 3DGS model, pruning additionally requires
deciding how many primitives to retain in different parts of the scene under a limited Gaussian budget.
PUP 3D-GS reports a trade-off between foreground detail and background quality under aggressive
pruning (Hanson et al., 2025b). These regional differences motivate SplatQ to partition Gaussians
with similar spatial positions and appearances into size-limited blocks, smooth the block quotas
derived from importance estimates while preserving the exact global retention count, and perform
joint selection within each block. In addition to overall reconstruction quality, we evaluate pruning
results by measuring quality differences across image patches.

METHOD
Given a pretrained 3D Gaussian Splatting model with N Gaussians, SplatQ aims to retain exactly
K Gaussians while accounting for their joint effects on the rendered image and reducing disparities
in retention across scene regions. We represent the model as G = {Gi = (µi, si, ri, ci, hi, oi)}N
i=1
where each Gaussian is parameterized by its center µi, scale si, rotation quaternion ri, zeroth-order
(DC) and higher-order spherical-harmonic coefficients ci and hi, and opacity oi.

Previous methods suffer from two limitations: 1) They may produce imbalanced Gaussian retention
across near and distant regions. 2) Moreover, independent Gaussian scores cannot capture the joint
rendering effects of simultaneous deletions. SplatQ addresses these limitations through blockwise
retention ratio allocation and interaction-aware quadratic selection, respectively.
SplatQ decomposes this global selection problem into five stages. Partitioning in Section 3.1 first
converts Gaussians G into several size-limited blocks {Cb}. Opacity response modeling in Section 3.2
then produces a response matrix Rb for each block. Budget allocation in Section 3.3 uses part of
the matrix Rb to assign a retention quota Kb to every block, with P
b Kb = K. Given Rb and Kb,
quadratic selection in Section 3.4 determines which Kb block members to retain. Finally, we fine-tune
the retained attributes without changing the count of Gaussians in Section 3.5.
3.1
SIMILARITY GUIDED PARTITION
Independent importance scores do not capture how the image changes when several Gaussians are
deleted together. To better describe these changes, we approximate their joint rendering effects using
a quadratic objective over binary retention variables. Each variable equals 1 if the corresponding
Gaussian is retained and 0 if it is deleted, as detailed in Section 3.4. Formulating this optimization
problem over the entire 3D-GS model requires N binary variables and an N × N matrix of pairwise
coefficients. For models containing millions of Gaussians, the variable count and memory require-
ments make this approach impractical. We therefore divide the Gaussians into blocks of at most M
members and solve a smaller pruning problem within each block.
Simply dividing Gaussians into consecutive blocks in their stored order ignores their spatial and
appearance relationships. Nearby Gaussians with similar appearance may fall into different blocks,
preventing their joint effects from being considered in the same pruning problem. We therefore
use spatial proximity and appearance similarity to guide partitioning, so that potentially redundant
Gaussians can be evaluated together. Finding related Gaussians through all pairwise comparisons
itself requires quadratic work. We therefore use a 3D grid and Morton ordering to efficiently generate
candidate pairs, whose spatial and appearance similarity is measured by an affinity.
Candidate generation.
Gaussian centers are assigned to grid cells, and the occupied cells are
ordered by their Morton order to preserve spatial proximity. Then we divide the resulting Gaussian
sequence into groups of approximately equal size, each within a size limit of M. These groups are
temporary search windows rather than the partition results Cb.

4
EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
Datasets and metrics.
We evaluate SplatQ on 27 scenes from 5 benchmarks: Mip-NeRF 360 (9),
Deep Blending (2), Tanks and Temples (2), NeRF Synthetic (8), and Ref-NeRF/Shiny Blender (6).
These benchmarks cover indoor scenes, outdoor scenes, and objects. We compare against unpruned
3DGS and eight pruning baselines, reporting average PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS-VGG, and rendering FPS.
To assess spatial quality balance, we divide each test image into a 16 × 16 grid of non-overlapping
patches and compute PSNR against the corresponding ground-truth region for each patch. For each
image, we calculate the variance and range of its 256 patch PSNR values. We then average these
statistics over test views within each scene and equally over scenes within each dataset. Considered
alongside full-image PSNR, lower patch variance and range indicate smaller quality differences
across image regions. Macro scores average the five dataset means equally. FPS is measured on
Ubuntu 22.04 servers equipped with RTX 5090 GPUs and Intel Xeon Gold 6459C processors.
Experimental protocol.
All methods start from the same 3DGS model pretrained for 30k iterations
for each scene and follow a shared two-round pruning and fine-tuning schedule. The first round
removes 80% of the original Gaussians, and the second removes 50% of the survivors, leaving 10%
of the original Gaussians. Each round includes 10k fine-tuning iterations, for 20k in total. Baselines
that originally did not use fine-tuning receive the same schedule. All methods use the refinement
loss defined in Section 3.5, with densification disabled. We preserve each baseline’s Gaussian
importance estimation procedure. For methods that do not natively enforce an exact retention count,
we apply Top-K selection using their importance scores to meet the target retention ratio. To facilitate
comparison with reported results from methods without released code, we additionally evaluate
pruning ratios from 10% to 90%, fine-tuning budgets from 0 to 30k iterations, and combinations of
one to four pruning rounds with 5k to 30k fine-tuning iterations per round at 90% final removal. We
also evaluate a picture-free setting mentioned in Section 1.
Ablation settings.
Global Top-K retains the K Gaussians with the highest individual importance
scores Di across the entire model. Uniform block quotas assigns the same retention ratio to every
block, whereas budget assignment uses our smoothed quotas. Both variants retain the highest-scoring
Gaussians within each block according to Di. The Quadratic variants use the full objective with
smoothed quotas and are solved using greedy selection, simulated annealing (SA), or a coherent
Ising machine (CIM). Quadratic, uniform quotas instead combines uniform block quotas with greedy
selection. For each variant, we repeat the complete pruning and fine-tuning pipeline five times with
random seeds 0 through 4, starting from the same pretrained models.

COMPARISON WITH STRONG BASELINE
Quality and rendering speed.
As shown in Table 1, SplatQ achieves a macro-average PSNR
of 28.426 dB, exceeding MesonGS and PUP by 0.224 dB and 0.300 dB, respectively. Among the
compared pruning methods, it achieves the highest PSNR on four of the five datasets (MesonGS leads
on Tanks and Temples), as well as the highest SSIM and lowest LPIPS-VGG on all five. The lower
entry in each quality cell reports the picture-free result. Under this setting, SplatQ achieves macro-

average PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS-VGG values of 28.250 dB, 0.88216, and 0.17936, respectively,
with its macro-average PSNR exceeding MesonGS by 0.238 dB. SplatQ also achieves the highest
rendering FPS among the pruning methods on all five datasets. Figure 3 compares the Gaussian
volume distributions of the nine pruning methods. At matched cumulative fractions, SplatQ generally
yields smaller normalized Gaussian volumes. Details of volume normalization and cross-scene
aggregation are provided in Appendix B.1. Prior work shows that, for a fixed number of Gaussians,
rendering cost is related to the number of screen-space pixels processed per Gaussian (Liu & Han,
2026; Hanson et al., 2025a; Feng et al., 2025). Thus, the smaller Gaussian volumes in SplatQ
may correspond to more localized screen-space footprints and hence a lower rendering workload,
providing a possible explanation for the observed FPS gains. We attribute the smaller Gaussian sizes
to our pruning’s ability to maintain better spatial relationships among the Gaussians, which reduces
the need for compensatory enlargement during fine-tuning.

Spatial quality balance.
To evaluate how effectively SplatQ alleviates regional imbalance in
reconstruction quality, Figure 4 compares SplatQ (SA) with eight pruning baselines across five
benchmarks. Alongside full-image PSNR, we use the variance and range of patch PSNR introduced
in Section 4.1 to measure spatial variation in reconstruction quality. SplatQ (SA) achieves the
lowest patch-PSNR variance and range on all five benchmarks, while attaining the highest full-image
PSNR on four benchmarks and remaining close to the best result on Tanks and Temples. These
results indicate that SplatQ reduces quality differences across regions while maintaining high overall
reconstruction quality, thereby alleviating regional quality imbalance.

Sensitivity to pruning and fine-tuning.
The pruning-ratio study evaluates removal ratios from 10%
to 90% in increments of 10 percentage points, together with seven fine-tuning budgets ranging from 0
to 30,000 steps. The pruning-round study evaluates one to four rounds with 5,000–30,000 fine-tuning
steps per round, at a final removal ratio of 90%. The main paper presents the 2D slice of pruning-ratio
results at 10,000 fine-tuning steps and the full 3D sweep over pruning rounds and fine-tuning steps
(Figure 5). SplatQ outperforms the compared open-source pruning methods in reconstruction quality
under the vast majority of evaluated configurations, demonstrating that its advantages extend across
different pruning ratios, numbers of pruning rounds, and fine-tuning budgets. Under a few settings on
Tanks and Temples, some quality metrics of SplatQ are slightly below those of the best-performing
baseline. This suggests room for further improvement on datasets containing distant scene regions.

A.7
GREEDY SELECTION, SIMULATED ANNEALING AND CIM SOLVERS
Again use local R, n, K and deletion cost Edel(z) = zTRz.
Greedy selection.
Starting with z = 0, deleting a retained member j changes the cost by
∆add(j) = 2(Rz)j + Rjj.
(19)
Greedy selection takes the smallest increment at each step until exactly n −K members are deleted.
It satisfies the count constraint by construction and does not require a count penalty.
Simulated annealing.
Simulated annealing (SA) minimizes the penalized objective with the count-
penalty coefficient λ = 1, using the Ising encoding in Appendix A.6. We initialize multiple random
spin configurations and apply Metropolis updates while reducing the temperature geometrically. For
a proposed spin flip with energy change ∆H, the acceptance probability at temperature θ > 0 is
paccept = min{1, exp(−∆H/θ)}.
(20)
Energy decreases are always accepted, whereas temporary increases allow the search to leave local
minima. Lower temperatures reduce the probability of accepting such increases. By default, the
initial and final temperatures are 100 and 10−3, respectively, with a cooling factor of 0.99. We use 10
iterations per temperature and a solution limit of 100 per block. We select the returned state with the
lowest Ising energy and decode its retention variables using the reference spin.
Coherent Ising Machine (CIM).
The coherent Ising machine (CIM) uses the same Ising formula-
tion and reference-spin encoding as SA, but with a different count-penalty coefficient, λ = 0.0625.
Before submission, we rescale and truncate the matrix coefficients to a signed 14-bit representation.
We request multiple samples and select the returned state with the lowest energy under the submitted
matrix. The selected state is then decoded into binary retention variables. Neither SA nor CIM
guarantees an exact retained count, we therefore apply the following postprocessing.
A.8
POSTPROCESSING
Capacity repairing.
For an infeasible solver output, count repair changes one bit at a time. If too
many Gaussians are retained, it deletes the retained member with the smallest increase in unpenalized
energy. If too few are retained, it restores the deleted member with the smallest increase. The
coefficients and current state determine each increment exactly, and the process stops when the
retained count equals K. Repair can increase the unpenalized cost because feasibility takes priority.

and deletes j. Its energy change is

EXPERIMENT
This section supplements the experimental results in Section 4.
B.1
EXPERIMENT SETTINGS
Datasets and Metrics
We evaluate 27 scenes from five benchmarks: Mip-NeRF 360 (9), Deep
Blending (2), Tanks and Temples (2), NeRF Synthetic (8), and Ref-NeRF/Shiny Blender (6) (Barron
et al., 2022; Hedman et al., 2018; Knapitsch et al., 2017; Mildenhall et al., 2020; Verbin et al.,
2022). We compare against the unpruned 3DGS reference and eight pruning baselines, reporting
dataset-average PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS-VGG, and measured rendering FPS. Macro scores average the
five dataset means equally.
Evaluation settings.
FPS measurements were conducted on an Ubuntu 22.04 server equipped with
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPUs and Intel Xeon Gold 6459C processors. The inspected evaluation
pipeline saves test-view renderings and computes PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS-VGG. The FPS testing
procedure warms up with ten test views and times three passes over all test views.
Implementation Details.
For each scene, all pruning methods start from the same 3DGS model
pretrained for 30k iterations and retain 10% of its original Gaussians. All methods follow the same
two-round schedule: removing 80% of the original Gaussians, then 50% of the survivors. Each round
includes 10k fine-tuning iterations, for 20k in total, using the same loss 0.8L1 + 0.2(1 −SSIM) with
densification disabled. We adapt the baselines by retaining their Gaussian selection mechanisms
within this shared pruning and fine-tuning pipeline. Methods originally evaluated without fine-tuning
also receive the same fine-tuning schedule, and attribute compression is disabled for all methods.
SplatQ uses simulated annealing (SA) in the main comparison, with greedy and CIM evaluated as
solver ablations. Further details are provided in Appendix A.1 and Table 5.
Ablation settings.
Table 6 compares budget assignment and selection variants. Top-k globally
retains the K Gaussians with the largest diagonal scores Di. Uniform block quotas assigns the
same retention fraction to all blocks, whereas Budget allocation uses our logarithmically smoothed
quotas. Both variants select the highest-scoring Gaussians within each block using Di. The Quadratic
variants use the full objective with smoothed quotas and are solved using greedy selection, SA, or
CIM. Quadratic, no allocation variant instead uses uniform block quotas and greedy selection. For
each variant in this table, we repeat the complete pruning and fine-tuning pipeline five times using
seeds 0 through 4, starting from the same pretrained models.

M = 512 and k = 4, with at most 512 Gaussians in each temporary comparison group. Successive
spatial passes change the grid origin using half-cell offsets. Graph construction uses two passes,
and parallel block growth runs for at most 16 iterations. For each block, we use all training views
with image width and height reduced to one quarter of their input dimensions. At this resolution, we

QUBO solver settings.

penalty. The SA backend uses Kaiwu’s simulated annealing optimizer with an initial temperature of
100, a cutoff of 10−3, and a cooling factor of 0.99. It uses 10 iterations per temperature, eight worker
processes, and a solution limit of 100 per block. The SA random seed is the base seed 0 plus the
block index. After decoding the lowest-energy returned state, the implementation repairs its count
and applies up to nb improving one-swap exchanges. The CIM backend additionally truncates its
Ising coefficients to signed 14-bit precision and applies the same repair and refinement.
Fine-tuning settings.
Fine-tuning uses Adam with numerical stabilizer 10−15 and one randomly
selected training view per update. The initial learning rates for DC coefficients, higher-order co-
efficients, opacity, scale, and rotation are 0.0025, 0.000125, 0.05, 0.005, and 0.001, respectively.
These rates decay by a factor of 0.95 every 400 refinement steps. Position updates use the inherited
exponential schedule, with endpoints 1.6 × 10−4 and 1.6 × 10−6 multiplied by the scene scale.
Refinement and final evaluation preserve input resolution when image width is at most 1600 pixels.
Wider images are resized to approximately 1600 pixels in width while preserving aspect ratio. In
the picture-free setting, neither pruning nor fine-tuning uses the original training images. We retain
the training camera parameters and supervise fine-tuning with images rendered by the fixed, original
pretrained model. Ground-truth test images are used only for evaluation.

within each scene, then equally over scenes within each benchmark. Full-image PSNR is reported
alongside these statistics to distinguish overall reconstruction quality from spatial quality variation.
