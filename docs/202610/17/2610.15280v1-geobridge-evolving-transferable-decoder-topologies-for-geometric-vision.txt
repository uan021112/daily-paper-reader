GeoBridge: Evolving Transferable Decoder
Topologies for Geometric Vision
Rui Wang, Haoyu Zhang, and Ran Cheng, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Depth estimation and Gaussian reconstruction share
a need for multi-scale feature processing, yet use different pre-
diction interfaces and training objectives. Whether a decoder’s
internal computation can be optimized once and reused across
these systems remains a structural design question. Search over
primitive configurations alone leaves the organization of transfor-
mations fixed. To address this gap, we propose GeoBridge, an evo-
lutionary framework for transferable decoder topologies. First,
GeoBridge makes stage-internal topology searchable through
residual, sequential, and parallel composition of configurable
primitives. Shape-preserving composition retains the stage inter-
faces of a fixed multi-scale scaffold. Second, a functional-tree en-
coding supports stage-aware crossover and hierarchical mutation,
while a gradient sensitivity-alignment proxy evaluates candidates
without training. Multi-objective evolution balances this proxy
with computation and parameter cost. Third, selected topologies
are instantiated and trained in target systems with task-specific
prediction interfaces. A single source search takes approximately
12 GPU hours. GeoBridgeNet-A3 achieves the best depth accu-
racy among four adapted decoder baselines under a common
encoder and training protocol on NYU Depth V2 and KITTI.
The same topology reaches 27.11 dB PSNR in Gaussian novel-
view synthesis on RealEstate10K, and its refined representations
support open-vocabulary segmentation on LERF. These results
support decoder topology as a reusable design object across the
evaluated geometric vision systems. Code and pretrained weights
are available at https://github.com/EMI-Group/GeoBridge-NAS.
Keywords: Decoder topology, architecture transfer, geomet-
ric vision, evolutionary neural architecture search, functional
composition, training-free evaluation.
I. INTRODUCTION

ECODERS connect visual features to geometric predic-
tions through multi-scale processing and spatial detail
recovery. In monocular depth estimation (MDE), they map im-
age features to dense depth [1]–[3]. In feed-forward Gaussian
reconstruction, decoded features contribute to depth inference
and the prediction of a renderable scene representation [4]–[6].
These systems have different prediction interfaces and training
objectives, yet share a need to organize feature transformations
across spatial scales. This common role motivates a structural
question: can a decoder’s internal computation be optimized
in one geometric setting and remain useful in another?
Answering this question requires treating the organization
of decoder computation as a design variable. In a decoder

parallel, and residual arrangement fixed. These arrangements
determine how transformations accumulate along a path and
how branch outputs are aggregated. Neural architecture search
(NAS) has explored operation connectivity, multi-scale fusion,
and hierarchical motifs [7]–[9]. For decoder reuse across geo-
metric systems, the specific design problem is to expose stage-
internal composition to search while retaining the interfaces
needed by the surrounding network.
We investigate the hypothesis that decoder topology can
serve as a reusable design object across geometric prediction
systems. Here, topology specifies the residual, sequential, and
parallel organization of transformations within each decoder
stage. Transfer retains this organization and the primitive
configurations; each target system trains its own weights
and prediction interface. A fixed multi-scale scaffold supplies
compatible stage boundaries, and variable internal topologies
provide alternative ways to process the features at those bound-
aries. This separation permits one source search followed by
target training under different prediction objectives.
To realize this design, we propose GeoBridge, an evo-
lutionary framework that searches decoder topologies in a
depth-estimation scaffold and reuses the selected structures
in target geometric systems. A bounded composition gram-
mar constructs each stage from shape-preserving primitives
through residual, sequential, and parallel operators. Functional
trees encode these compositions, so stage-aware crossover
and hierarchical mutation can vary complete stages, subtrees,
and primitive configurations. A gradient sensitivity-alignment
proxy (GSAP) evaluates independently initialized candidates
under a common teacher-derived regression stimulus. NSGA-
II [10] balances this training-free score with computation
and parameter cost to obtain a set of decoder structures for
subsequent reuse.
Experiments test whether the structures selected in this
source setting retain their utility after target training. One
search on TartanAir [11] produces the GeoBridgeNet family in
approximately 12 GPU hours. GeoBridgeNet-A3 achieves the
best depth accuracy among four recent decoder baselines under
a common encoder and training protocol on NYU Depth V2
and KITTI. The same topology reaches 27.11 dB PSNR after
integration into a Gaussian novel-view synthesis (NVS) system
on RealEstate10K. Its Gaussian outputs also support open-
vocabulary segmentation on LERF after scene refinement and
semantic optimization [12]–[14]. Controlled studies character-
ize the decoder designs, the structural coverage of evolutionary
variation, and the ranking quality of GSAP.

II. RELATED WORK
The design of transferable decoder topologies connects
architecture search with structural reuse. We first review how
NAS exposes visual computation to optimization and how
architectures are transferred between prediction tasks. We
then examine the representations and evaluation methods that
support evolutionary search over composed structures.
A. Architecture Search for Visual Decoding
NAS combines an architecture space, a search strategy,
and a performance estimator to automate network design.
Early studies focused on image classification and established
reinforcement learning [15], gradient-based optimization [7],
and evolutionary search [16] as major search strategies. Evo-
lutionary NAS directly manipulates discrete operations and
connections through selection, crossover, and mutation. Its
population-based formulation also supports the joint optimiza-
tion of prediction quality and computational cost.
Dense prediction has motivated architecture spaces tailored
to multi-scale processing. NAS-FPN and FNA++ search fea-
ture fusion and network structures for object detection [8],
[17]. Auto-DeepLab searches multi-resolution structures for
semantic segmentation [18], while SCTNet-NAS incorporates
deployment efficiency [19]. MDE-oriented methods further
specialize search to depth prediction [20]–[22]. Task-specific
search spaces also address feature extraction and fusion in
other vision problems. TREFE searches the spatial and se-
quential feature extractors of text recognition networks under
a latency constraint [23], and NAS-PED searches hybrid
convolution–transformer backbones for pedestrian detection in
crowded scenes [24]. SPOSGait applies single-path one-shot
search to gait recognition in unconstrained environments [25],
while DCNAS searches cross-modality feature extraction and
fusion structures for guided image super-resolution [26]. These
approaches establish topology and operation choice as NAS
design variables. GeoBridge investigates their combination
within decoder stages whose external multi-scale connections
remain fixed across the search space.

the transfer unit between dense depth prediction and Gaussian
reconstruction. Each target system retains the selected compo-

network for its own objective.
C. Structural Encoding in EvoNAS
Architecture encoding determines the units on which evo-
lutionary variation operates [30]. Direct encodings specify
operations and connections explicitly. EvoCNN, for exam-
ple, uses variable-length chromosomes to represent layer se-
quences [31]. NASNet searches reusable normal and reduc-
tion cells and stacks them into a full network [15]. Main-
stream gradient-based NAS methods, including DARTS and
P-DARTS, search over this cell-based encoding [7], [32]. Such
encodings expose local architectural choices to crossover and
mutation, while the decoding procedure determines how those
choices form a valid network.
Early work on evolving artificial neural networks also
used indirect encodings, in which the genome describes a
construction process instead of listing every unit and con-
nection. Kitano encodes a connectivity matrix through graph-
rewriting rules [33], and cellular encoding grows a network
from a single cell by a program of division and modification
instructions [34]. NEAT instead evolves explicit node and
connection genes, with historical markings that align genomes
during crossover [35], and HyperNEAT generates connection
weights from a function of neuron coordinates [36].
More recent encodings expose larger structural units through
composition. SLGE constructs architectures with grammar-
based transformations [37], hierarchical NAS assembles motifs
from lower-level operations [9], and tree-based genetic pro-
gramming has been applied to encoder–decoder networks [38].
GeoBridge adopts function composition within a fixed multi-
scale decoder scaffold. Its residual, sequential, and parallel

and any subtree define executable feature transformations.
This organization supports structural variation at several levels
without changing the connections between decoder stages.
D. Efficient EvoNAS
Candidate evaluation is a major cost in population-based
NAS. Multi-fidelity evaluation reduces the resources allocated
to candidate training [39]. Weight sharing reduces this cost
by evaluating candidates within a trained supernet. SPOS
uses uniformly sampled single-path subnetworks [40], while
Once-for-All supports elastic depth, width, kernel size, and
input resolution [41]. BossNAS divides the supernet into
blocks trained by self-supervised ensemble bootstrapping,
which improves architecture ranking in hybrid convolution–
transformer spaces [42]. WIGEvoNAS uses weight inheritance
to shorten candidate training in each generation of evolutionary
search [43]. Applying these strategies to composed decoder
trees requires a supernet that specifies how parameters are
shared across different structural positions. GeoBridge instead
evaluates independently instantiated candidates and uses a
training-free proxy to control their evaluation cost.
Surrogate-assisted EvoNAS predicts candidate quality from
previously evaluated architectures. Sun et al. [44] use an offline
random-forest predictor, and Ma et al. [45] learn Pareto-wise
dominance relationships for multi-objective search. CFMOGP-
NAS combines the forecasts of multiple regression models
within multi-objective genetic programming [46], and Li et
al. [47] replace performance regression with a classifier when
evolving fuzzy neural networks for defect recognition. These
approaches require performance observations to train the es-
timator. Parameter-transfer methods such as PTF-EvoMDE
reduce candidate-specific pretraining through hierarchical pa-
rameter mapping [22]. Training-free proxies offer a com-
plementary route through responses measured at initializa-
tion, including connection sensitivity, gradient flow, activation
statistics, and combinations of such criteria [48]–[52]. GSAP
combines a parameter-scaled sensitivity statistic with gradient–
parameter alignment under the regression stimulus used in the
source search.
III. PROPOSED METHOD
GeoBridge evolves the internal topology of a decoder while
preserving the stage interfaces through which the decoder
connects to a geometric prediction system. Fig. 1 summarizes
the source search and target reuse. We first define the source
search and its multi-scale scaffold, then describe the com-
position space and training-free evaluator. The evolutionary
procedure selects structures from this space, and target-specific
training tests their reuse in depth estimation and Gaussian
reconstruction.

The selected architecture is the object reused across target
systems. For α⋆∈Qsrc, the stage compositions and prim-

source search chooses the computation structure, and target
training determines its parameters for each prediction objec-

Direct evaluation of Eq. (1) requires training every can-
didate decoder. To reduce this cost, GeoBridge uses JGSAP

The resulting search minimizes {JGSAP, CMAC, CParam} over
independently instantiated candidates. Section IV-D evaluates
the association between the proxy and retrained depth accu-
racy.
All candidate decoders share the multi-scale scaffold shown
in Fig. 2. DINOv2 ViT-S [53] supplies intermediate features
from blocks B2, B5, B8, and B11, which are projected
to scales of 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, and 1/32. Four Tree-Mamba
stages process these features along a coarse-to-fine pathway.
TartanAir images and cached features from a frozen Depth
Anything V2 teacher provide the source inputs and auxiliary
regression targets used for candidate evaluation.
The computation inside each Tree-Mamba stage is the
searchable part of the scaffold. A projection adapts the encoder
feature, which is fused with the preceding stage’s upsampled
output at all but the coarsest scale. The stage then transforms
this fused feature through its internal composition, and the
finest-scale output is mapped to a prediction by the source
head. The projections, cross-scale connections, upsampling
operators, and head remain fixed during search. Consequently,
candidates differ in how each stage processes its inputs while
sharing the same information flow between stages.

by four functional trees, α = {T32, T16, T8, T4}. Leaf nodes
instantiate configurable Mamba primitives, and internal nodes
define residual, sequential, or parallel composition. These
choices vary processing depth, branching, and residual paths
while preserving the stage interface. Fig. 3 illustrates the map-
ping from the tree representation to executable computation.

1) Stage-Wise Tree Structure: The four functional trees are
aligned with decoder resolutions of 1/32, 1/16, 1/8, and
1/4, denoted by T32, T16, T8, and T4, respectively. Each tree
receives the fused feature of the corresponding Tree-Mamba
stage and returns a representation with the same external
interface for subsequent coarse-to-fine decoding. In this way,
the fixed multi-scale scaffold determines how information
flows across stages, whereas the functional tree determines
how features are transformed within each stage.
Stage-specific bounds control the structural capacity of
the decoder. The coarse stages T32 and T16 permit at most
two levels of internal composition, while T8 and T4 per-
mit at most one. With the child order of PAR treated as
equivalent, the four one-level structures are OP, RES(OP),
SEQ(OP,OP), and PAR(OP,OP). Each coarse stage therefore
admits 1 + 4 + 42 + 4(4 + 1)/2 = 31 structures, which
gives 312 × 42 = 15,376 four-stage topologies. Including the
primitive domains in Table I gives approximately 2.5 × 1026
configurations. These bounds allocate more compositional
capacity to coarse stages, where spatial processing is less
expensive.

primitive or composed transformations are organized into
higher-level functions. Let F and G denote two shape-
preserving child transformations with input x. The three
composition operators are defined as

repeated measurements. Each measurement requires a forward
pass through the encoder and decoder, a backward pass
through the decoder, and computation of the two gradient
statistics. Three measurements yield one candidate score with-
out updating the network parameters.
D. Evolving Decoder Topologies
Multi-objective evolution selects decoder compositions ac-
cording to their GSAP response and resource costs. Each in-
dividual contains the four stage trees defined in Section III-B,
and its objective vector is {JGSAP, CMAC, CParam}. Algo-
rithm 1 gives the population update, and Fig. 1 places this
search within the complete source-to-target procedure.
The initial population is generated by recursively sam-
pling valid functional trees under the stage-specific structural
constraints defined in Section III-B. Internal nodes are se-
lected from residual (RES), sequential (SEQ), and parallel
(PAR) compositions, while operator (OP) nodes terminate
the hierarchy with configurable Mamba primitives. The depth
limits and primitive domains depend on spatial scale, which
allows greater structural flexibility at coarse resolutions while
constraining computation at finer resolutions. Each sampled
stage tree preserves the interface of the decoder scaffold.
Each candidate is evaluated through three independent
decoder initializations. Each initialization produces one raw
GSAP response from a forward–backward pass on the com-
mon source batch. Their arithmetic mean is log-transformed to
obtain JGSAP, while MACs and parameter count are measured
from the architecture. Section III-C defines this evaluation rule.

Evolutionary variation acts on the functional units defined
by the trees. Binary tournament selection chooses parents
using non-dominated rank and crowding distance. Stage-aware
crossover exchanges an entire same-scale tree between two
parents with probability pc. Hierarchical mutation acts with
probability pm and can regenerate a subtree, modify an internal
composition, or resample a primitive configuration. These op-
erations vary stage computation while preserving the external
interfaces of the decoder scaffold.
NSGA-II environmental selection maintains a population of
proxy–cost trade-offs. The parent and offspring populations
are merged, ranked by non-dominated sorting, and truncated
using crowding distance. Thus, previously selected structures
compete with newly generated candidates at each generation.

E. Instantiation in Target Systems
Target instantiation retains the selected stage computation
while connecting it to the target system’s prediction interface.
For depth estimation on NYU Depth V2 and KITTI, an
adaptive depth-bin head maps decoder features to depth, and
the network is trained with ground-truth supervision. The
decoder weights are initialized randomly, so the reused design
consists of its topology and primitive configurations.
Gaussian NVS places the selected decoder in the monocu-
lar geometric branch of a DepthSplat-based system [6]. Its
output is fused with multi-view information for depth and
Gaussian prediction. The monocular branch first undergoes
target-side distillation from Depth Anything V2 pseudo-depth,
followed by optimization through differentiable rendering. The
selected topology remains fixed through both stages. Scene-
level Gaussian refinement and COS3D semantic optimization
subsequently evaluate the downstream utility of the recon-
structed representation [13], [14].

A. Topologies Selected by Source Evolution
The source experiment characterizes the structures and
proxy–cost trade-offs produced by one architecture search.
GeoBridge evolves a population of 64 decoders for 100 gen-
erations on the TartanAir source batch. The selected structures
from this run are reused in all subsequent target systems.
The final population contains 26 non-dominated solutions.
Fig. 5 shows that normalized HV increases early in the run
and continues to improve after generations 29 and 82 before
approaching a plateau. The objective-space projections show
distinct trade-offs among JGSAP, MACs, and parameter count.
A1, A2, and A3 belong to this final non-dominated set.
The selected architectures instantiate different compositions
within the common scaffold. As shown in Fig. 6, A1 and A2
mainly use individual primitives. A3 combines three primitives
sequentially with residual refinement at s32 and uses parallel
composition at s16. Its finer stages contain a residual transfor-
mation at s8 and one primitive at s4. These examples illustrate
the design freedom introduced by stage-internal topology: a
selected stage can retain a single transformation or compose
several transformations along serial and parallel paths.
B. Reuse Across Geometric Prediction Systems
The reuse experiments retain the selected stage topolo-
gies and primitive configurations through the target training
described in Section III-E. Depth estimation tests changes
in image domain and depth range, while Gaussian NVS
tests integration into a different prediction system. All three
GeoBridgeNet variants are evaluated under both settings.
1) Depth estimation across domains: Cross-domain depth
estimation tests decoder reuse under changes in scene statistics
and depth range. The source search uses synthetic TartanAir
images, whereas NYU Depth V2 contains real indoor scenes
and KITTI contains real outdoor scenes. The target networks
retain the searched decoder structures and use an adaptive
depth-bin prediction head.
The controlled comparisons isolate decoder performance
under a common encoder and training protocol. As reported in
Tables II and III, A3 achieves the best AbsRel, RMSE, and δ1
among the four DINOv2-adapted baselines. On NYU Depth
V2, its AbsRel is 0.0876, compared with 0.0896 for DCDepth
and 0.0902 for Metric3D v2. On KITTI, the corresponding
values are 0.0555, 0.0585, and 0.0575. Metric3D v2 has lower
latency, while A3 provides higher depth accuracy on both
datasets.
Published systems place these results within a broader range
of model sizes and training settings. On NYU Depth V2,
A3 improves AbsRel over PixelFormer from 0.090 to 0.0876
with 42.5M parameters, while iDisc and IEBins with Swin-L
reach 0.086 and 0.087. On KITTI, A3 reaches 0.0555, between
AdaBins at 0.058 and the large Swin-based systems at 0.050–
0.052. The NAS-based methods in the tables report higher
AbsRel with smaller networks of 1.8–20.5M parameters. Their
published search costs range from several GPU days for PTF-
EvoMDE to more than 24 GPU days for the reproduced
DARTS, DetNAS, and GDAS searches [7], [22], [59], [60].
GeoBridge uses approximately 12 GPU hours for its source
search on an RTX 3090.
The three transferred structures retain different accuracy–
cost profiles on both target datasets. From A1 to A3, NYU
AbsRel decreases from 0.0918 to 0.0876 as latency increases
from 36.9 to 40.7 ms. KITTI AbsRel decreases from 0.0582
to 0.0555 as latency increases from 53.9 to 59.1 ms. Thus, the
source search supplies several useful decoder configurations
for target deployment.
The qualitative comparisons illustrate boundary recovery in
indoor and outdoor scenes. Figs. 7 and 8 compare A3 with
IEBins, PixelFormer, and NeWCRFs. The highlighted NYU
regions contain thin objects, cabinet edges, and a glass shower
door, while the KITTI regions contain distant vehicles and
roadside structures. A3 preserves visible object boundaries and
local depth transitions in these examples.
2) From depth decoding to Gaussian reconstruction: Cross-
system transfer reuses the decoder as a feature-processing
component in Gaussian NVS. The same stage topology is in-
stantiated in the monocular geometric branch of a DepthSplat-
based pipeline. Its output is fused with multi-view informa-
tion for depth and Gaussian prediction, and the full system
is trained through differentiable rendering. This experiment
evaluates the searched structure under a different output rep-
resentation and training objective.
The transferred decoders achieve competitive reconstruction

27.02, 27.07, and 27.11 dB for A1, A2, and A3, respectively.
A3 exceeds pixelSplat by 1.22 dB and MVSplat by 0.72 dB.

C. Decoder Design and Evolutionary Representation
The controlled studies examine decoder design and the
evolutionary representation used to explore it. Alternative
decoder designs are first compared under a common depth-
training protocol. Two search controls then separate the roles
of topology freedom and evolutionary selection. A paired tree–
flat study next evaluates how two encodings and their variation
operators explore the same composition grammar, followed by
downstream comparisons of their selected structures.
Comparison of Decoder Designs: The searched decoder
achieves lower depth error than the alternative designs under
the common NYU protocol. Table VI compares A3 with a
searchable FPN, a fixed LocalMamba decoder, and adapted
DPT and NeWCRFs decoders [3], [75]. Across three training
seeds, A3 reaches an average AbsRel of 0.0876±0.0002, com-
pared with 0.0929±0.0013, 0.0918±0.0015, 0.0981±0.0011,
and 0.0912 ± 0.0020, respectively. This comparison supports
the effectiveness of the selected decoder within the shared
encoder and training setting.
Topology Freedom and Evolutionary Selection: Two con-
trols separate the contribution of open topology from that
of evolutionary search. Under the same encoder, primitive
domain, GSAP evaluator, and 12,864 evaluation events, the
first control evolves only the primitive configurations on a
fixed stage template of residual and serial chains. The second
samples structures independently from the full composition
space with the initialization sampler of the main search. Each
group repeats this search three times with independent random
starts; every run selects one structure from its non-dominated
set under a common limit on parameters and MACs by GSAP
re-scoring, and every selected structure is trained under the
NYU protocol of Section IV-B1. Target results do not enter
the selection (Table VII).

V. CONCLUSION
This paper presented GeoBridge for evolving transferable
decoder topologies in geometric vision. Residual, sequen-
tial, and parallel composition make the internal organization
of each stage searchable while preserving the interfaces of
a multi-scale scaffold. A functional-tree representation and
GSAP support multi-objective evolution over these compo-
sitions at low evaluation cost. The selected structures retain
their utility after target-specific training for real-world depth
