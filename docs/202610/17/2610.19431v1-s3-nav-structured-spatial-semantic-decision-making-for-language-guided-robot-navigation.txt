# S3-Nav: Structured Spatial-Semantic Decision Making for Language-Guided Robot Navigation 

Chaemin Lee 1 and Hae Min Cho 1,* 

Abstract —This paper presents S 3-Nav, a structured spatial-semantic decision-making framework for language-guided robot navigation under partial observability. Rather than assuming a complete environment representation in advance, S 3-Nav in-crementally constructs persistent spatial-semantic state while exposing only a capacity-controlled, task-conditioned view to the high-level policy. This separation decouples what the robot must retain from what the policy needs to inspect, allowing decision complexity to be controlled without discarding grounded evidence required for subsequent reasoning and validation. High-level navigation outputs remain provisional and are independently grounded and validated against persistent semantic evidence and current geometric observations before physical execution or task termination. Evaluations across multiple environments and object- and relation-goal tasks demonstrate that separating persistent state, decision-facing evidence, and execution authority improves closed-loop navigation reliability while reducing policy reasoning cost. 

Index Terms —Autonomous navigation, decision making, language-guided navigation, SLAM, spatial-semantic memory. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# AUTONOMOUS mobile robots operating under partial observability must repeatedly make decisions from in-complete observations while retaining task-relevant environ-mental evidence accumulated over time. A central question is therefore how persistent decision state, decision-facing evidence, and execution authority should be organized within a closed-loop decision process. Language-guided mobile-robot navigation requires a robot to connect natural-language goals with spatial evidence accumulated over an extended trajectory. A mobile robot may need to explore multiple regions before the requested target becomes observable, while relational instructions such as “find a chair next to a plant” require more than detecting the target category. The robot must preserve observations, associate them with persistent object identities, and verify the requested relation before accepting a terminal state [1]–[4]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, navigation therefore requires both task-relevant spatial-semantic memory and a mechanism for connecting accumulated evidence to grounded decisions and stopping conditions. Existing approaches separately address semantic reasoning, persistent spatial-semantic memory, and hierarchical control [2], [5], [6]. However, combining these capabilities in closed-loop navigation introduces three distinct design challenges. First, the robot may need to preserve observations for future grounding and relational verification even when much of that

A. Spatial-Semantic Mapping and Memory 

Persistent geometric and semantic representations are fun-damental to long-horizon robot navigation because relevant environmental evidence often extends beyond the current ob-servation. Semantic mapping systems such as SemanticFusion and object-centric representations such as ConceptGraphs il-lustrate how persistent maps can be enriched with semantic labels, object identities, and spatial relationships [2], [7]. More broadly, SLAM, semantic mapping, and object-memory approaches have progressively improved what environmental information a robot can retain over time [8]–[12]. These meth-ods primarily address what information should be represented and retained, but do not necessarily distinguish the capacity of the persistent representation from the amount of evidence exposed to the high-level policy at each decision step. S 3-Nav treats these as separate system variables: the persistent state remains available for grounding and verification, while the decision-facing evidence view is independently capacity controlled. 

B. Object-Goal and Language-Guided Navigation 

Object-goal and language-guided navigation require robots to search beyond the current field of view and translate se-mantic goals into grounded navigation decisions. Approaches such as LM-Nav and SG-Nav demonstrate how language-model reasoning can guide target selection and high-level navigation, alongside broader work on exploration, semantic 3

search, vision-and-language navigation, and pretrained vision– language models [4], [6], [13]–[21]. These advances improve where the robot should search or move, but relational goals introduce an additional requirement because evidence needed to justify a decision may have been accumulated across different observations. A decision policy must therefore rea-son from task-relevant evidence without losing the persistent information needed to resolve targets, verify relations, and determine whether the task is actually complete. S 3-Nav in-stead considers how accumulated spatial-semantic state should be converted into a bounded, task-conditioned decision view without discarding the underlying evidence required later in the episode. This separates the information used for high-level selection from the persistent evidence retained for grounding, relational verification, and terminal validation. 

C. Grounded Language-Model Reasoning 

Grounded language-model control commonly separates high-level semantic reasoning from feasibility checking and physical execution. Systems such as SayCan and Inner Mono-logue illustrate this separation by combining language-based reasoning with independent mechanisms for feasibility as-sessment or interaction with the environment [22], [23]. A similar hierarchy appears in language-guided navigation, where pretrained models select high-level actions and lower-level modules execute them [6], [13], [19]. This conventional planner–controller boundary determines how a selected high-level action is executed, but does not by itself distinguish what the system persistently retains from what evidence the policy should inspect at each decision step. Nor does high-level target selection alone establish whether the accumulated semantic and current geometric evidence are sufficient to authorize terminal execution. S 3-Nav therefore separates the persistent spatial-semantic state from the evidence presented to the high-level policy, in addition to separating policy output from physical execution. The policy produces provisional grounded intents from a task-conditioned evidence view, while deter-ministic execution resolves those intents against the persistent state and retains authority over semantic, relational, geometric, and terminal validity. S 3-Nav therefore retains the persistent decision state St while con-structing a task-conditioned structured view for policy-level reasoning. Let Ct denote the ordered sequence of candidate records constructed from the persistent state St, in which goal-relevant semantic evidence is prioritized before exploration-oriented frontier records and final-fill records while retaining grounded references to the corresponding persistent entries. Records omitted from the decision-facing view remain preserved in St and therefore remain available for later grounding, relation verification, and execution. D. Decision Validation and Execution Authority 

The decision-facing view determines what evidence the high-level policy may inspect and which grounded intent it may propose, but policy selection alone does not establish that the proposed action is physically or semantically admis-sible. All provisional actions are therefore resolved through a deterministic execution layer Γ, which retains authority over grounded execution and terminal acceptance. Given at, the ex-ecution layer resolves the referenced record against St, checks semantic compatibility, applies physical safety constraints, and converts the admissible intent into an executable command. Frontier records may be used for exploration, while object and confirmed-relation records may provide physical anchors for approach. Obstacle handling and recovery may override provisional motion when execution constraints are violated, with controller thresholds and recovery parameters provided in the supplementary material. A. Experimental Setup 

The primary experiments are conducted in indoor household environments from the HSSD dataset [24] using Isaac Sim. An additional evaluation is conducted on three InternScenes navigation tasks [25] to assess the cross-environment consis-tency of the policy–record-capacity and localization patterns observed in HSSD. Both evaluations use the same differential-drive Ranger Mini equipped with an RGB-D camera and the same perception, mapping, spatial-semantic repository, policy, and deterministic execution definitions unless otherwise stated. All runs are executed on a workstation equipped with two NVIDIA RTX 5080 GPUs. Semantic perception is instantiated with YOLO26n-seg [26], while RTAB-Map [8] provides the map-frame pose and partial occupancy map under Estimated localization. C. Evaluation Metrics 

Semantic Standoff Success Rate (SSR) is the primary task-level metric and counts an episode as successful when it terminates at the correct semantic target, satisfies the requested relation when applicable, and reaches the 0.5–0.9 m standoff interval around d⋆ = 0 .7 m . SR@3m uses the same semantic and relational requirements with a relaxed terminal distance of 3 m , while Relation Success Rate (RSR) measures relation satisfaction at termination for relation-goal episodes. Ground-Truth Semantic Standoff Success Rate (GT-SSR) applies the corresponding success criteria using simulator-ground-truth semantic and terminal geometry, with episodes lacking valid GT terminal geometry counted as unsuccessful and NGT -valid 

reported separately. Trajectory Length (TL) is computed from the simulator-ground-truth robot trajectory, and localization quality is re-ported using translational and rotational Relative Pose Error (RPE). Reasoning cost is characterized by navigation-policy Qwen calls and token usage, while runtime is reported using Policy Latency, E2E Decision Latency, and real-time factor (RTF). Detailed metric definitions, conditional geometric-error metrics, aggregation procedures, and runtime timing bound-aries are provided in the supplementary material.

V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented S 3-Nav, a structured spatial-semantic decision architecture for language-guided mobile-robot navigation that separates persistent state retention, decision-facing evidence, and execution authority. The central premise is that a robot may need to retain more evidence than its high-level policy should inspect at a given decision, and that evidence sufficient for target selection need not be sufficient to authorize task completion. S 3-Nav operationalizes this distinction by preserv-ing grounded spatial-semantic state, constructing a capacity-controlled task-conditioned view for policy reasoning, and treating high-level navigation intents as provisional while retaining deterministic execution authority over grounded ex-ecution and terminal acceptance. The evaluation shows that closed-loop navigation depends not only on localization qual-ity or the amount of retained information, but also on how accumulated evidence is exposed to the decision policy, with bounded record views improving task success and reducing reasoning cost in the evaluated settings. More broadly, the results motivate treating state retention and decision-facing evidence as distinct system variables, while S 3-Nav further enforces a separate execution boundary for grounded action and terminal validation. PROTOCOL 

A. Simulation and Navigation Stack 

The primary experiments use HSSD household environ-ments in Isaac Sim, while an additional evaluation is con-ducted on three InternScenes navigation tasks. Both evaluation suites use the same differential-drive Ranger Mini equipped with an RGB-D camera and share the same perception, mapping, spatial-semantic repository, deterministic execution, and terminal-validation stack unless otherwise stated. Policy, record capacity, and localization source are varied according to the experimental condition. All runs are executed on a workstation equipped with two NVIDIA RTX 5080 GPUs. Se-mantic perception is performed using YOLO26n-seg instance segmentation, and retained instance masks are grounded in three dimensions using aligned depth and camera intrinsics, transformed into the active map frame, and integrated into the spatial-semantic repository. Under Estimated localization, RTAB-Map supplies the map-frame robot pose and partial oc-cupancy map used for object grounding, relation construction, frontier extraction, and downstream geometric processing. Under the GT Odometry condition, simulator ground-truth robot poses replace the estimated odometric pose source while the RTAB-Map RGB-D backend and occupancy-mapping pipeline are retained. B. Policy and Record-Capacity Conditions 

The primary HSSD evaluation includes four policy–record-capacity conditions under Estimated localization and one Qwen K14 condition with GT Odometry. All Qwen conditions use Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 through the same local vLLM-compatible serving configuration, with a maximum model length of 6,000 tokenizer-level tokens including prompt and completion tokens. Full and K14 use the same persistent repository and candidate-construction procedure and differ only in whether the final decision-facing record view is subject to a 14-record capacity. Qwen and heuristic policies use the same selected record identities under the regular inference path while retaining their policy-specific input representations. Detailed decision-facing context construction is described in Sec. VII. 

C. Scenario Construction and Initial Visibility 

The primary HSSD evaluation consists of five comple-mentary navigation scenarios covering initially available se-mantic evidence, exploration for unseen targets, the sup-ported next_to and on predicates, object-goal search, and a distractor-sensitive relational setting. Initial visibility is determined geometrically rather than by the output of the semantic perception model. For an object goal, the designated target is Seen when it lies within the configured camera field of view and sensing range with an unobstructed line of sight from the initial robot viewpoint. Otherwise, it is Unseen. A geometrically visible target missed by the instance-segmentation model remains a Seen case and is treated as an initial perception miss. For a relation goal, the case is Seen only when both designated target and reference instances are geometrically observable from the initial viewpoint. If either required instance is not observable, the relation-goal scenario is classified as Unseen. The designated target and reference instances are fixed for the corresponding scenario and repeat. For relation-goal tasks, the target object provides the physical navigation and stopping anchor, while the reference object supplies the evidence re-quired for relational validation. The evaluated predicates and terminal requirements follow the goal schema and stopping conditions defined in the main paper.

The K14 condition limits the capacity-controlled decision-facing record view to at most 14 structured records after the standard staged context-construction procedure. The record-capacity limit counts structured records rather than tokenizer-level tokens. For Qwen, the navigation phase, parsed goal schema, partial-map and navigation summary, and relation-search summary are serialized as top-level prompt fields and are not counted toward this record capacity. Full uses the same candidate generation, stage ordering, within-stage ranking, and type-specific selection procedure without the additional global record-capacity constraint. The capacity-independent top-level fields and the decision-facing record view are not strictly disjoint representa-tions. For implementation consistency, the candidate record sequence may additionally contain goal_schema and 

partial_map records that duplicate information avail-able in the corresponding top-level fields. When se-lected, these record-form representations consume capacity in V(κ) 

> t

, whereas their top-level counterparts do not. The 

goal_schema record is prioritized early in staged selection and therefore typically occupies one record slot, while the 

partial_map record participates in the later ranked-fill stage and may be excluded when higher-priority candidates exhaust the available capacity. Regardless of whether these duplicate record forms are selected, the top-level goal schema and partial-map and navigation summary remain available to Qwen. The K14 setting predates the K3/K7/K14/Full sensitivity experiment and was used as the default bounded-record budget in the baseline Qwen policy implementation. It is therefore not selected post hoc from the sensitivity results and is not claimed to be a theoretically optimal record capacity. The value 14 instead represents a pre-existing engineering operating point intended to retain multiple goal-relevant semantic records while leaving limited capacity for exploration or auxiliary evidence. Context construction proceeds through staged candidate selection rather than a single global top-K ranking. The selector first prioritizes a small set of high-priority goal-related entries, then admits goal-relevant object, relation, and relation-evidence records, followed by frontier cues and ranked fill records. The implementation considers up to two high-priority goal-related entries, up to ten task-relevant semantic records, and up to five frontier candidates before the final global capacity is applied. Because these stage-wise candidate limits precede the global cap, K14 does not impose a fixed per-type allocation. The actual composition of the 14-record budget therefore varies with the evidence available at each decision step. This design allows unused capacity from an earlier stage to be occupied by frontier or other ranked records at a later stage. For each record-capacity condition, the Qwen and heuristic policies are constructed from the same upstream structured state and, under the regular inference path, use the same selected record identities. XII. Q UALITATIVE FAILURE ANALYSIS 

The main paper presents a representative successful closed-loop navigation trace with online map construction. Here, we complement the component ablation results by examining representative failure mechanisms associated with persistent memory and explicit frontier cues. The memory-ablation condition fails to achieve valid semantic-standoff termination in each of the four complete attempts, with one additional attempt lacking a complete run record. In an inspected failure trace, a goal-compatible relation is temporarily confirmed at a surface range of 1.873 m, outside the 0.5–0.9 m stopping interval. The relation disappears in the subsequent observation and cannot be reused because cross-step persistence is disabled. All complete memory-ablation attempts reach the 600-s time limit. These observations show how the absence of persistent evidence can interrupt the connection between relation discovery, continued approach, and valid termination. The frontier-ablation traces show a different failure mech-anism. Four of the five attempts succeed despite the removal of explicit frontier records in the decision-facing view. In the unsuccessful run, the requested relation is discovered, but the chair RGB-D anchor is lost during approach, invalidating the fresh terminal range. Together, these traces illustrate that successful relational navigation requires not only discovering goal-compatible ob-jects but also maintaining usable evidence and obtaining valid terminal geometry during approach. They also show that explicit frontier cues are not the only means of reaching a target in the evaluated scenario, while failures may still arise from interactions between high-level selection, target sensing, and deterministic execution. 8
