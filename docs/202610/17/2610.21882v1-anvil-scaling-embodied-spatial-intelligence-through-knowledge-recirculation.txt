## ABSTRACT 

Embodied spatial intelligence requires more than visual recognition: models must localize entities, reason about their relations, and express this knowledge in action-relevant forms. Yet joint training can conflate the knowledge needed at different stages because supervision is fragmented across incompatible geometric and semantic outputs. We introduce A NVIL ,a unified framework for data generation, training, and benchmarking for embodied spatial intelligence. ❶ANVIL -G EN is a heterogeneous data flywheel that combines form-specific generation, cross-stage consistency verification, and failure-driven recollection across 11 Ground–Relation–Act forms. ❷ANVIL -B EN provides scene-aligned supervision for learning diverse embodied spatial capabilities and scene-linked assessments that distinguish stage competence from error propagation. ❸ANVIL -MOPD is a 9B student that consolidates heterogeneous spatial knowledge through distillation from specialist teachers. Semantic-first-fault routing targets teacher supervision to the student’s earliest decisive failure, while stage normalization controls the loss scale across native outputs of different lengths. We construct 5.8M source-grounded records spanning 160K images, reserving 580K spatial records for held-out evaluation and using the remaining 5.22M records for training. On average across seven public benchmarks, A NVIL -MOPD outperforms Qwen3.5-9B and RynnBrain-8B by 9.29 and 11.03 percentage points, respectively, while reaching 80.67% on VABench-Point. Controlled studies support targeted coverage, selective transfer, and feedback-guided repair as complementary framework components. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Embodied spatial intelligence requires more than visual recognition: a model must localize entities, reason about relations, and identify possible interactions (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026c). Recognizing a coffee machine, for example, does not determine where a cup can be placed beside it. Learning these capabilities requires supervision that connects visual observations to precise, action-relevant spatial judgments. Web images, reconstructed scenes, and simulated environments offer complementary sources, but differ in their coverage and available geometry (Song et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Zhi et al., 2025). Scaling embodied spatial intelligence therefore requires both broader supervision and a way to consolidate the knowledge expressed in different output forms. 

Recent data-driven approaches have advanced spatial grounding and reasoning through synthetic supervision, geometric reconstruction, and capability-diverse training (Chen et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2026). Broader multimodal and embodied training also demonstrated the value of integrating diverse visual experience and tasks (Tong et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; 2026c; Zhang et al., 2026a; Tan et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c). These studies support scaling spatial supervision, although gains vary across capabilities and transfer settings (Cai et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c). benchmarks also revealed differences that aggregate scores can obscure (Du et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026e). Building on these advances, we investigate a data-centered question: 

How can heterogeneous spatial expertise be turned into reliable supervision and consolidated in a model that also supports subsequent data verification? 

We introduce A NVIL , which connects a spatial data flywheel and model training through knowledge recirculation (Figure 1): A NVIL -G EN operates as a heterogeneous data flywheel, where form-specific generation produces spatial supervision, cross-stage verification identifies inconsistencies, and failure feedback guides targeted data regeneration and collection. A shared Ground–Relation–Act interface connects 11 native output forms through common entities and explicit dependencies, allowing the same capabilities to be specified across generation, learning, and evaluation. A NVIL -B EN organizes the aligned supervision into A NVIL -T RAIN for learning and A NVIL -E VAL for held-out assessment through native-output and scene-linked tasks. A NVIL -MOPD integrates knowledge from specialists trained on this supervision through multi-teacher distillation, building on on-policy knowledge transfer (Agarwal et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2026a; Gao et al., 2026). Semantic-first-fault routing directs teacher supervision to the earliest decisive student error, while stage normalization controls the loss scale across output lengths. Validated records can refresh specialists, while the distilled generalist returns verification signals to the data flywheel. Our contributions are threefold: 

(i) A NVIL -G EN : a scene-aligned, cross-verified data flywheel. Shared scene and entity references align source annotations and specialist outputs, while geometry-aware CoT structures schema-constrained generation. Cross-form adjudication turns dependencies among outputs into explicit verification constraints, and failure feedback guides targeted repair across sources and forms. 

(ii) A NVIL -B EN : a scene-aligned benchmark for spatial learning and evaluation. Aligned scene images and 11 native annotation formats support training in A NVIL -T RAIN and held-out assessment in A NVIL -E VAL , covering spatial understanding, reasoning, and state-conditioned plan verification. Native-output tasks measure individual capabilities; linked assessments and controlled upstream substitutions measure how errors propagate across dependent stages. 

(iii) A NVIL -MOPD: specialist-to-generalist transfer with multi-teacher distillation. Semantic-first-fault routing jointly selects a specialist and correction span, while stage normalization balances supervision across heterogeneous outputs. The resulting generalist can assist A NVIL -G EN under evidence-backed verification. Benchmark comparisons and controlled studies demonstrate the benefits of structured supervision and selective specialist transfer. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Spatial intelligence. Spatial intelligence research spans relation recognition, geometric estimation, and reasoning about possible interactions. Early evaluations of relational understanding (Liu et al., 2023; Kamath et al., 2023) have been complemented by image-based, multi-view, and video-based benchmarks (Tong et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025b), with further emphasis on transformations, planning, and spatial-functional reasoning (Zhang et al., 2026e; Wu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026b;c). Training has likewise moved beyond image–text pairs toward supervision derived from geometry and robot experience (Chen et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025), including precise, interaction-relevant grounding (Yuan et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Procedural geometry and executable representations broaden the available targets (Assouel et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026a), while program execution and trajectory consistency help check automatically generated annotations (Zhi et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024; Blank et al., 2026). This expanding coverage raises a broader evaluation question: when does success on a particular spatial task indicate competence that transfers to other tasks? 

Embodied foundation models. Large-scale pretraining has connected visual–linguistic knowledge to embodied reasoning and robot control (Driess et al., 2023; Zitkovich et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025). Autoregressive and diffusion policies provide alternative action interfaces, with generalist policies supporting adaptation across robot platforms (Octo Model Team et al., 2024). Knowledge distillation. Distillation transfers teacher knowledge through output distributions or generated sequences (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016); subsequent language-model methods address student behavior through reverse-KL and on-policy learning (Gu et al., 2024; Agarwal et al., 2024). Multi-teacher extensions introduce choices about both knowledge sources and supervision budgets (Ma et al., 2026a; Gao et al., 2026). Selection can operate over examples and tasks through rationale consolidation, answer verification, or privileged context (Jin et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026b), or within trajectories through discrepancy-triggered intervention and failed-prefix recovery (Koo et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Related approaches target informative tokens (Liu et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026a), cross-view representations (Nguyen et al., 2026), and intermediate reasoning steps (Lightman et al., 2024), while iterative bootstrapping reuses answer-checked model outputs for further training (Zelikman et al., 2022). Selective supervision thus raises a question of reliability: 

how can useful teacher knowledge be retained without also reinforcing its errors? 

To address these gaps, A NVIL treats supervision reliability and knowledge transfer as coupled prob-lems. Scene-aligned annotations support specialist-to-generalist distillation and held-out evaluation through knowledge recirculation; the resulting student can then assist further data verification. The knowledge recirculation mechanism links data refinement to model learning so that their effects on spatial competence can be studied together.

Controlled ablations and retention tests examine how supervision coverage and teacher allocation affect spatial learning and existing capabilities ( RQ2: Learning mechanisms ). We then measure the efficiency of source-based supervision construction and feedback-driven repair ( RQ3: Production efficiency ). Finally, task-structure and input controls test whether predictions depend on relevant scene evidence and task dependencies ( RQ4: Evaluation validity ). 34 .62 -point advantage over the base (Table 7), supporting a localization benefit beyond additional guesses. 4.3 MECHANISM ABLATIONS 

Observation 2: targeted coverage improves adaptation, while replay mitigates forgetting. We use preliminary controlled studies to isolate three decisions: which supervision to add, where to apply teacher correction, and how to repair rejected outputs ( RQ2 ). Mixed training improves Where2Place over replay by 30 .25 points across two seeds and also benefits RoboSpatial context (Figure 3a). Fixed updates and exposures separate these gains from simply training longer; increasing the decoding budget alone does not recover placement performance. Vacancy-only training favors placement, while replay better preserves pointing and depth. This trade-off motivates targeting A NVIL -G EN at missing capabilities while retaining replay. Mixed training does not improve every task, and unique images and realized tokens remain unmatched (Appendix C). 

Observation 3: selective teacher correction improves transfer with limited retention loss. We test where teachers should intervene. With common initialization and a matched maximum rollout budget, semantic-uniform routing improves strict Ground–Relation success over flat distillation by 2.13 points (Figure 3b). Semantic references expose incorrect but well-formed geometry that structural checks miss. Teacher supervision reaches 63 .00% of sampled responses, while reference learning covers all active stages, with 99 .83% general-VQA retention. The contrast supports selective correction over flat distillation. 

Observation 4: failure-specific feedback improves repair yield at comparable cost. We test whether failure-specific information improves repair. Using Qwen3.6-27B as the repair model, both arms receive the same 885 rejected candidates and one call per candidate. Named feedback recovers 10 .09 × as many source-correct records as generic retry at comparable GPU cost (Figure 3c). It identifies the failed category without reference coordinates or ranks, preserving the task while localizing correction. Observation 5: source evidence amortizes supervision construction. We next examine where supervision production incurs computational cost ( RQ3 ). From 9,608 prepared RGB-D frames, eight CPU workers construct 2,463 linked records in 325 .25 seconds without generative annotation. Registered depth and instance geometry provide the shared references needed to link Ground and Relation targets. A separate pass rechecks 598 ,891 stored records in 13 .26 seconds, covering reading, parsing, hashing, and rule evaluation across four shards (Appendix E). These measurements show that available source evidence supports both target construction and large-scale structural checking with CPU computation. They are component timings: source preparation, image loading during rechecking, and semantic review remain outside the measured scope. For candidates requiring model-based repair, the relevant efficiency measure is source-correct output per unit of computation. Normalizing the repair comparison by allocated GPU time gives 39 .38 

correct records per GPU-hour for named feedback and 3.98 for generic retry, a 9.90 × advantage. The images, questions, and candidate pool are fixed, so this gain reflects the value of localized error information under the same repair workload. Thus, we reuse source geometry, apply inexpensive structural checks, and direct model calls to unresolved semantic errors. The practical implication is to allocate computation according to the evidence available for each record. Source-supported targets can be constructed and checked directly; unresolved candidates receive model-based adjudication or repair. Observation 6: scene-linked supervision substantially improves spatial composition. We first test whether independently learned Ground and Relation outputs compose under a shared interface. On 282 images from 50 held-out physical scenes, linked supervision reaches 69 .86% strict Ground– Relation success, substantially exceeding the atomic mixture at matched updates (Figure 5a). To separate missing delimiters from errors in the predicted content, we close stage tags in the saved responses without changing their JSON outputs. This raises the atomic mixture to 44 .68% while leaving linked performance unchanged. The remaining gap indicates that output syntax is only part of the composition problem. A downstream stage must also receive the correct entities and geometry from its predecessor. Scene-linked supervision trains this dependency explicitly, making intermediate outputs useful inputs to subsequent reasoning rather than merely separately correct answers. 

Observation 7: spatial predictions depend on images and upstream geometry. We next examine dependence on the evidence supplied to these stages. With questions and targets fixed, blank images reduce the source-filtered model’s accuracy on the 1,463-example grounding panel from 57.35% to 7.66%. Cross-scene shuffling also degrades grounding, while BLINK-depth approaches chance (Figure 5b). Keeping images and depth targets fixed but shifting upstream boxes reduces the Relation specialist’s accuracy by 10 .64 points (Table 18). In particular, errors in Ground can alter the context available to Relation even when the scene image remains fixed. These interventions establish sensitivity to both visual content and consumed geometry, motivating joint evaluation of stage accuracy and linked success. 

Observation 8: action rules support state-conditioned plan verification. We extend the evalua-tion to action order under state constraints. From 506 ALFRED trajectories (Shridhar et al., 2020) across 92 scenes, we construct 1,012 balanced judgments, pairing each valid plan with an invalid permutation of the same actions. This fixes vocabulary and length, so action occurrence alone cannot distinguish the labels. Given the initial image, complete Boolean state, goal, and action rules, the Action specialist improves over the base by 6.72 points (Figure 5c). Class-wise results remain uneven: accuracy is 89 .53% on invalid plans but only 55 .53% on valid ones (Table 19). The improvement mainly comes from recognizing more valid plans, although many feasible sequences are still rejected. 

Summary and discussion. The experiments suggest that spatial learning depends on how supervi-sion is organized, not only how much is available. Coverage controls favor task-relevant additions with replay; routing controls favor localized specialist correction; and repair controls show the value of actionable failure feedback. Source-based construction complements these mechanisms by reusing available geometric evidence. Beyond aggregate benchmark gains, the composition, input, and action-order controls test whether predictions remain useful when later decisions depend on their content. These findings motivate scaling spatial supervision through capability coverage, selective correction, and explicit task dependencies. 9Anvil: Scaling Embodied Spatial Intelligence through Knowledge Recirculation 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented A NVIL , which connects spatial data generation, evaluation, and multi-teacher learning through knowledge recirculation. Shared scene references support cross-form verification, guide selective specialist transfer, and govern the acceptance of returned supervision. The final model improves the seven-benchmark mean by 9.29 points over its base; controlled studies support targeted coverage, selective transfer, and feedback-driven repair. Together, these findings support jointly designing reliable supervision and knowledge consolidation for embodied spatial intelligence.

## C TRAINING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOLS 

Checkpoints and study roles. The main table reports the final A NVIL -MOPD on seven public tasks; RoboSpatial provides an additional coverage control. The coverage study continues an earlier source-filtered checkpoint with replay, vacancy, or mixed supervision, while routing continues a common linked-SFT checkpoint. Composition and data-quality comparisons start from the base model. The image interventions compare the base and the earlier source-filtered A NVIL checkpoint; box interventions compare the Relation specialist and joint SFT. Plan verification additionally evaluates the Action specialist. Throughout the appendix, A NVIL † denotes the earlier source-filtered checkpoint, not the final main-table model . The Qwen3.6-27B repair model has a separate production role: both repair arms process the same 885 failures with one call per candidate. 

Evaluation and uncertainty. Local comparisons fix prompts, decoding, and scorers; published values retain the modalities and subsets marked in the main table. Strict linked success requires valid serialization and correct dependent outputs; plan validity uses exact VALID/INVALID classification. Malformed or incomplete responses remain in the denominator. Paired scene-bootstrap intervals use 5,000 resamples, keeping all records from a sampled physical scene together and using identical scene draws for the compared models. For routing, per-image outcomes are first averaged across the two recorded seeds. The resulting intervals condition on those seeds and do not estimate variability over unobserved initializations.

## G LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

From predefined interfaces to open-world spatial supervision. The current framework organizes supervision through eleven Ground–Relation–Act forms with explicit entities and dependencies. Open-world environments introduce spatial concepts and interaction requirements beyond this inven-tory. Extensible interfaces could incorporate new relations, affordances, and temporal dependencies while retaining a common basis for generation, verification, and learning. The challenge is to expand this vocabulary without losing the scene evidence supporting each target. 

From specialist consolidation to compositional spatial competence. Anvil studies knowledge transfer within specified spatial tasks and stage dependencies. Future work could extend specialist-to-generalist learning to unfamiliar combinations of goals, environments, and embodiments. The question is whether grounding, relational reasoning, and action knowledge can compose into reusable spatial expertise, supporting new tasks with less task-specific supervision. 

From knowledge recirculation to learning through interaction. The present framework draws on curated sources and explicit verification signals. Spatial uncertainty and encountered failures could guide what an agent observes and attempts next, with physical interaction outcomes grounding the returned supervision. Connecting experience collection to specialist refinement and continued generalist learning remains a future direction: stronger spatial competence should support both better decisions and more informative data acquisition. 28
