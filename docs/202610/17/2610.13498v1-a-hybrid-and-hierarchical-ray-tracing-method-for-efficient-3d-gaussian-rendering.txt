A Hybrid and Hierarchical Ray Tracing Method for
Efficient 3D Gaussian Rendering

Abstract—3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a powerful rep-
resentation for real-time neural reconstruction and rendering.
While existing 3DGS systems rely on rasterization for efficiency,
recent work explores ray tracing to enable secondary lighting,
highly distorted cameras, and depth-of-field effects. However,
compared with rasterization, ray-traced 3DGS introduces three
major challenges: (1) the highly non-uniform spatial distribution
of massive numbers of Gaussian primitives creates severe load
imbalance; (2) neighboring pixels often generate rays with highly
similar traversal paths, leading to substantial redundant traversal
overhead; and (3) supporting both Gaussian and triangle prim-
itives yields heterogeneous intersection workloads that are not
well matched to existing GPU architectures. We present H2-Ray,
a hybrid and hierarchical ray tracing architecture for efficient
3D Gaussian rendering. H2-Ray introduces three techniques.
Load-Balanced Spatial BVH (LB-SBVH) partitions the scene into
uniform grids and uses density-aware representations to convert
an imbalanced global workload into balanced local traversal
units. Ray-Cluster Path Reuse (RCPR) lets a proxy ray share
traversal state with neighboring rays to amortize redundant
traversal. The Unified Intersection Engine (UIE) supports mixed
Gaussian and triangle intersections through a mode-multiplexed
datapath that shares 40% of its floating-point units across both
primitive types. Across 8 scenes, H2-Ray achieves a geometric-
mean speedup of 3.77× and a maximum speedup of 9.26×
over RaySplats on the NVIDIA RTX A5000, combining a cycle-
accurate model of the modified RT Core with measured CUDA
timings.

I. INTRODUCTION
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become a compelling
scene representation for neural reconstruction and render-
ing, delivering high visual quality at real-time rates [8]. Its
anisotropic primitives and rasterization-friendly rendering cap-
ture fine geometric and appearance details at interactive rates.
However, the rasterization-centric design that makes 3DGS
efficient also limits its ability to model real-world optical
effects. In particular, rasterization-based Gaussian rendering
struggles to support (1) secondary-lighting effects such as re-
flections and refractions needed for mirror-like and transparent
materials [17], (2) highly distorted fisheye cameras used in
autonomous-driving surround-view systems [24], (3) depth-
of-field effects arising from finite-aperture camera optics [4],
and other rendering modes that benefit from per-ray visibility
reasoning. As summarized in Figure 1, ray tracing broadens

This gap stems from 3D Gaussian ray-tracing workloads,
which differ from rasterization and triangle-based ray tracing.
First, Gaussian primitives are highly non-uniform across space,
so a monolithic acceleration structure must simultaneously
serve extremely dense and extremely sparse regions, leading
to poor traversal balance and inefficient parallelism. In rasteri-
zation, imbalance mainly arises from the number of projected
fragments in 2D screen space. In ray tracing, which operates
in 3D, secondary-ray effects (e.g., reflections and refractions)
further amplify spatial non-uniformity, resulting in more severe
load imbalance and irregular execution. Second, neighboring
pixels often generate rays that traverse highly similar BVH
path prefixes, so processing these rays independently causes
substantial redundant traversal work. This redundancy is most
pronounced at high resolutions, where dense pixel sampling
induces strong spatial coherence among rays and deep BVH
hierarchies lead to long shared traversal prefixes. Third, ad-
vanced effects increasingly mix ray–Gaussian and ray–triangle
intersections, creating a heterogeneous workload mismatched
to fixed-function specialization around one dominant primitive
type. Modern GPU architectures provide dedicated hardware

Recent efforts have brought ray tracing to 3D Gaussian ren-

To address these challenges, we present H2-Ray, a hy-
brid and hierarchical ray tracing architecture. H2-Ray is
hybrid in two senses: it combines rasterization with ray
tracing for fast region-of-interest culling, and it supports mixed
ray–Gaussian and ray–triangle intersection workloads. It is
hierarchical because it addresses the workload at three levels:
scene-level spatial partitioning, ray-level traversal reuse, and
intersection-level execution adaptation. Rather than replacing
modern graphics hardware, H2-Ray adopts a practical co-
design approach, adding targeted support at key points in the
GPU rendering pipeline. Our contributions are as follows.
• We propose Load-Balanced Spatial BVH (LB-SBVH), a
density-aware spatial partitioning scheme that converts
globally skewed Gaussian distributions into balanced
traversal regions, achieving a microarchitectural traversal
speedup of up to 1.31× over a monolithic BVH.
• We propose Ray-Cluster Path Reuse (RCPR), which
shares traversal across neighboring rays to reduce redun-
dant BVH operations. In matched multi-view evaluation,
H2-Ray decreases average PSNR by only 0.034 dB rela-
tive to RaySplats.
• We design a Unified Intersection Engine (UIE) that
supports both Gaussian and triangle primitives within
a single reconfigurable datapath, sharing 31 of its 78
floating-point units (approximately 40%) across primitive
types, with 15 units (approximately 19%) dedicated to
triangles.
We evaluate H2-Ray on 8 scenes from Mip-NeRF 360 [2]
and Deep Blending [6] (Figure 2), combining cycle-level
modeling of the modified RT Core with measured RTX A5000
timings for all CUDA work. H2-Ray achieves a geometric-
mean speedup of 3.77× (up to 9.26×) over RaySplats on the
A5000 while enabling rendering effects beyond rasterization-
based pipelines.

B. Ray Tracing for 3DGS Rendering
Recent work revisits 3DGS rendering from a ray-based
viewpoint. 3DGRT [13] builds a BVH over Gaussian prim-
itives, encapsulates each with a stretched bounding mesh,
and uses hardware ray–triangle intersection. This design im-
proves flexibility, but traversal and ordering remain proxy-
based: primitives are gathered through bounding-mesh hits,
and integration follows proxy intersections rather than exact
sample locations along the ray. 3DGUT [23] instead preserves
splatting for primary rendering while extending 3DGS to
nonlinear cameras through Unscented-Transform sigma points.
3DGUT further aligns its rendering formulation with 3DGRT
so that primary rays can still be splatted efficiently while
secondary rays are traced in the same representation. This
bridges rasterization efficiency and ray-tracing flexibility, but
primary rays still use a sigma-point approximation rather
than direct ray–Gaussian intersection, while secondary rays
follow the ray-traced representation. These designs emphasize
rendering flexibility and camera-model support, whereas our
architectural study focuses on the execution costs exposed by
direct Gaussian traversal.
RaySplats [3] moves further toward a direct ray-based for-
mulation. Instead of using bounding polytopes as the primitive
representation, RaySplats explicitly computes intersections
between rays and Gaussian confidence ellipsoids, avoiding
polytope approximation and remaining applicable to flat Gaus-
sians. Importantly, this ellipsoid is not a different primitive
from (1); it is an iso-surface induced by the same Gaussian

C. Why Hybrid Rendering Matters
Once Gaussian rendering is expressed in a ray-tracing
framework, it inherits many of the benefits of ray tracing.
Prior work shows that ray-traced 3DGS can naturally sup-
port reflections, refractions, shadows, stochastic ray sampling,
highly distorted cameras, and rolling-shutter effects [3], [13],
[23]. It also becomes easier to combine Gaussian scene
content with triangle-based geometry, mesh proxies, or stan-
dard graphics assets within the same rendering pipeline. For
instance, 3DGRT demonstrates compatibility with ray-based
visual effects and mesh interactions, 3DGUT performs hybrid
rendering with splatted primary rays and traced secondary
rays, and RaySplats shows that Gaussian scenes can be ren-
dered together with mesh-based objects and lighting effects.
These capabilities make mixed primitive support relevant even
when Gaussian rendering remains the dominant workload: the
architecture must handle the less frequent triangle path without
duplicating an entire rendering backend.
Hybrid scenes therefore create a genuine mixed-intersection
workload: different rays may encounter Gaussian primitives,
triangles, or both. Figure 11 later quantifies this behavior for
perspective, fisheye, and mesh-augmented rendering. We treat
this workload as an architectural requirement for unified ray–
Gaussian and ray–triangle execution.
III. MOTIVATION
We characterize a RaySplats-style renderer at the scene,
ray, and intersection levels. Its workload is shaped by spatial
imbalance, redundant neighboring-ray traversal, and scene-
dependent Gaussian–triangle mixtures, motivating the three
components of H2-Ray.

Distributions

surfaces, leaving empty or weakly observed regions sparsely
populated. The skew varies by scene: the coefficient of varia-

B. Ray-Level Challenge: Redundant Traversal Across Neigh-
boring Rays
A second inefficiency remains even with a fixed acceleration
structure: neighboring rays often share BVH prefixes because
adjacent pixels enter the same coarse Gaussian regions before
diverging near boundaries (Figure 4). This image-space co-
herence is especially strong at high resolution and in primary
visibility.
Current execution nevertheless fetches and tests these upper-
level nodes repeatedly, even though much of the traversal
state is shared. This wastes memory bandwidth and RT Core
cycles in coherent workloads such as mild lens distortion
and some secondary-ray queries. The wasted work is not
limited to duplicate node tests; repeated address generation
and state updates also increase pressure on the traversal stack
and memory system.
Takeaway. Ray-traced 3DGS contains substantial inter-ray
reuse. RCPR amortizes BVH traversal over a ray cluster
instead of paying the full cost for every ray.

The final bottleneck appears when Gaussian rendering ex-

or conventional ray-traced effects, where rays require Gaussian
tests, triangle tests, or both.
A rigid pipeline specialized for one primitive type is there-
fore inefficient: a triangle-centric datapath loses efficiency
when Gaussian intersections dominate, while a Gaussian-only
datapath cannot serve triangle work. The challenge is both
mixed execution and a dominant primitive that varies with
scene, camera, and task.
Takeaway. Mixed workloads require an adaptive backend.
UIE shares floating-point units between Gaussian and tri-
angle paths and selects the active mode per test.
IV. H2-RAY ACCELERATION FRAMEWORK
H2-Ray combines three mechanisms: LB-SBVH for scene
partitioning, RCPR for traversal reuse, and UIE for mixed-
primitive intersection. Together, they form a hybrid and hi-
erarchical ray-tracing framework for efficient 3D Gaussian
rendering.
A. The End-to-End Rendering Pipeline
The H2-Ray pipeline reorganizes conventional ray tracing
into four density-aware stages:

a) Raster-Assisted Culling: Rather than starting each ray
from a cold BVH traversal, H2-Ray first runs a lightweight
low-resolution rasterization pass to generate a grid hit map
that records per-grid hit counts (Figure 5). The map prunes
empty regions and skips rays with negligible Gaussian energy,
reducing initial traversal pressure.
b) Balanced Scene Partitioning: The scene is partitioned
into uniform spatial grids by LB-SBVH, with each grid clas-
sified as dense or sparse by primitive count. Dense grids
are assigned dedicated local BVHs, while sparse grids store
primitives in flat lists for brute-force enumeration. Culling then
dispatches rays only to active grids, concentrating traversal on
occupied regions with bounded local workloads.
c) Coherent Path Traversal: For each ray cluster, the
RCPR unit selects a proxy ray that performs BVH node
fetching and internal intersection tests. Its traversal state
is broadcast to worker rays, amortizing memory bandwidth
across the group.
d) Adaptive Intersection & Accumulation: When rays
reach leaf nodes, the UIE selects the appropriate Gaussian
or triangle mode for each primitive, using a shared pool
of floating-point units with mode-multiplexed inputs. The
pipeline then performs front-to-back alpha blending and color
accumulation.
B. Load-Balanced Spatial BVH (LB-SBVH)
3D Gaussian scenes are highly non-uniform, making a
monolithic BVH prone to divergence and hotspots. The Load-
Balanced Spatial BVH (LB-SBVH) converts this global work-
load into locally balanced traversal units (Figure 5 (a)).
a) Density-Aware Sub-Region Construction: LB-SBVH
partitions the scene into a uniform spatial grid and chooses
a representation per grid. Dense grids (N ≥100 Gaussians)
construct specialized shallow BVHs to accelerate traversal in
regions with high primitive density. Sparse grids (0 < N <
100 Gaussians) bypass BVH construction and store primitives
in flat lists for direct enumeration, avoiding unnecessary tree
overhead. Empty grids (N = 0) are excluded from both
categories.
b) Coverage-Preserving Grid Bounds: Ownership and
traversal bounds are separate. Each Gaussian is assigned once
by its center, avoiding duplicate occupancy counts. Each non-
empty grid then builds a tight conservative AABB around the
full support of its assigned Gaussians. These AABBs may
expand beyond or shrink within the original cells and may
overlap one another, so any ray intersecting a Gaussian support
reaches its owning grid even when it misses the center cell.
The local BVH or sparse list performs the exact intersection,
ensuring complete coverage without duplicating Gaussians.
c) Hardware Support for LB-SBVH: LB-SBVH adds two
hardware mechanisms. Hybrid Metadata Storage (HMS)
combines a meta-tree with meta-bitmaps to encode BVH
addresses and sub-region positions, allowing threads to locate
target trees with little decoding overhead. The Raster-to-
RT Forward Path (R2RFP) forwards BVH pointers directly
from the Raster Engine to the RT Cores, avoiding DRAM
synchronization and redundant top-level traversal.
Summary. LB-SBVH addresses the scene-level challenge
by partitioning an imbalanced Gaussian distribution into
local traversal regions with bounded cost. HMS and R2RFP
provide the metadata and dispatch support needed to realize
this partitioning efficiently in hardware.
C. Ray-Cluster Path Reuse (RCPR)
For primary and coherent secondary rays, neighboring paths
through the BVH are highly correlated. RCPR shares interme-
diate states within localized 3 × 3 ray tiles (Figure 5 (b)).
a) Proxy-Ray-Based Cluster Traversal: For each tile, the
center proxy ray performs a full BVH traversal. When it
reaches a leaf, hardware “hops back” by c levels (e.g., c = 2
or 3) to define a coherence volume. The eight worker rays are
dispatched to these intermediate nodes, bypassing redundant
upper-level tests.

shared BVH prefixes efficiently.

primitive selection local to leaf-level intersection while ray

E. Hardware Integration
Figure 6 shows how LB-SBVH, RCPR, and UIE are in-
tegrated into the GPU pipeline. At the system level (left),
each Graphics Processing Cluster (GPC) contains a Streaming
Multiprocessor (SM), an H2-Ray RT Core, and a Raster Engine
connected by R2RFP. The Pinned L2 Cache sits beside the
DRAM controller to support RCPR. This organization leaves
shader execution, memory hierarchy, and rasterization largely
intact; H2-Ray adds only the paths needed to pass culling and
reuse information to the RT Core. This section describes the
target hardware path; the evaluation separately reports modeled
RT Core behavior and measured CUDA kernels, as detailed
in Section V.
The H2-Ray RT Core (center) adds four blocks to a con-
ventional RT Core: (1) a Raster-to-RT Interface that receives
BVH pointers without a DRAM round-trip, (2) a Shared Prefix
Memory (SPM) for proxy-ray path pointers, (3) a Reconfig-
urable Intersection Unit that switches between Gaussian and
triangle logic, and (4) a Direct Color Duplication unit that
offloads pixel-copy operations from the SM. These additions
are placed around the existing traversal and intersection flow:
R2RFP supplies the starting BVH handle, SPM changes where
worker rays resume traversal, UIE changes the leaf test, and
color duplication handles repeated output writes.
Execution proceeds in two phases (right). In the Proxy
Ray Phase, the Raster Engine performs low-resolution culling,
forwards valid grid BVH handles through R2RFP, and the RT
Core writes proxy-ray prefix pointers into SPM. In the Real
Ray Phase, worker rays load these pointers, skip redundant
upper-level nodes, and proceed to the Reconfigurable Inter-
section Unit. The two phases therefore reuse traversal state
while leaving the front-to-back blending semantics unchanged;
the quality impact of this approximate reuse is evaluated in
Section V. The Pinned L2 Cache (bottom) protects marked
cache lines from eviction so Gaussian primitives found during
the proxy phase remain resident for worker rays.

B. End-to-End Results
a) Efficiency Analysis: Figure 8(a) combines the mod-
eled modified RT Core with measured CUDA execution and
reports a 3.77× geometric-mean speedup over RaySplats. The
arithmetic-mean runtime decreases from 514.59 to 131.65 ms,
with per-scene speedups ranging from 1.89× on Stump to
9.26× on Garden; drJohnson and Playroom reach 5.58× and
5.36×. The average runtime and average speedup use different
means, so 3.77× is not the ratio of the two average runtime
entries.
The same figure decomposes H2-Ray latency. Since only
each tile’s center ray is traced on the RT Core, parallel
CUDA work largely hides RT latency. The RT portion is
estimated with the modified RT Core model, while CUDA
intersection and sorting are measured on the RTX A5000.
Intersection dominates CUDA latency in six scenes; sorting
dominates in Stump and Playroom, where per-ray hit counts
vary more. In this evaluated pipeline, worker-ray intersection
and sorting run as CUDA kernels, so the end-to-end runtimes
do not include any benefit from executing worker rays on the
proposed Reconfigurable Intersection Unit.

D. Ray Tracing with Heterogeneous Primitives
Figure 11 characterizes perspective, fisheye, and hybrid
mesh workloads supported by the same pipeline. The four
examples use the Room scene at its evaluation resolution
with the same acceleration structure and scheduling flow,
while varying the camera model and mesh insertion. They
use the default 2-hop RCPR setting and 33 grid partition.
Across all modes, Gaussian tests dominate the intersection
workload. Perspective-plus-mesh performs 1.90 billion ray–
Gaussian tests but only 1.38 million ray–triangle tests, while
fisheye-plus-mesh performs 1.75 billion and 0.73 million,
respectively. Triangle tests therefore remain a small fraction
of the intersection workload.
The fisheye camera changes ray directions and coverage,
reducing ray–AABB and ray–Gaussian counts relative to per-
spective rendering. Adding meshes introduces triangle tests
while leaving the Gaussian workload nearly unchanged (1.87
to 1.90 billion tests under perspective rendering). These results
justify the UIE design: the datapath supports both intersection
types and shares arithmetic units with the less frequent tri-
angle path. UIE consequently broadens the rendering modes
supported by H2-Ray without a separate triangle-only pipeline.
This experiment establishes the mixed-primitive workload
need rather than an isolated UIE speedup or area reduction;
the architecture section reports UIE’s FP-unit sharing as a
structural resource breakdown.
VI. RELATED WORK
a) Neural Rendering Accelerator: NeRF accelerators tar-
get repeated point sampling and neural-field evaluation. Prior
designs optimize multiresolution encoding and MLP infer-
ence [14], fused scene learning [19], and sampling through co-
design [5], [10]. H2-Ray instead accelerates explicit Gaussian
traversal and intersection.
b) 3D Gaussian Splatting Accelerator: GauSPU [22],
GauRast [12], Splatonic [7], ORANGE [11], and Cambricon-
GS [21] accelerate SLAM, rasterization, sparse pixels, blend-
ing, or training. Unlike H2-Ray, these systems retain splatting-
style projection rather than ray-traced visibility.
c) Ray Tracing Accelerator: NVIDIA RT Cores [15],
[16] and AMD RDNA units [1] accelerate BVH traversal and
triangle tests. GRTX [9] adds Gaussian BVHs and single-ray
traversal checkpointing.1 GRTX stores breakpoints along one
ray, whereas RCPR lets independent neighboring rays reuse a
nearby ray’s traversal path; these optimizations are orthogonal.
H2-Ray further adds LB-SBVH for BVH partitioning and
UIE for Gaussian–triangle datapaths. These systems therefore
optimize different levels of the pipeline: prior accelerators pri-
marily change representation or primitive execution, whereas
H2-Ray co-designs scene partitioning, inter-ray reuse, and
mixed-primitive support around direct ray–Gaussian traversal.
