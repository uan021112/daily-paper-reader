Autoregressive robot video world models based on discrete visual tokens remain unreliable over long rollout
horizons. Although these models typically retain the entire generated history, a growing context does
not necessarily improve prediction quality. We investigate a possible memory-role conflict in Full Context
decoding: persistent scene information, recent visual–action states, and stale generated history compete for
attention despite their different predictive roles. This perspective motivates ReCAP (Receding Context
with Anchor Preservation), a training-free context-scheduling method for long-horizon robot video world
models. ReCAP keeps the pretrained tokenizer, world model, and visual decoder frozen. At inference time,
it preserves the initial scene anchor and a contiguous window of recent complete visual–action blocks while
evicting older generated history. The schedule preserves within-block structure and temporal order, requires
no additional modules or parameter updates, and bounds active-context growth independently of rollout
length. We evaluate ReCAP on RT-1, BridgeV2, LIBERO-90, and CALVIN using frozen autoregressive
world models. Across the evaluated environments and rollout horizons, ReCAP improves visual fidelity,
action-conditioned consistency, and robot structure preservation relative to Full Context. On BridgeV2,
it reduces LPIPS by 63.3%. In the system benchmark at H = 64, it reduces the logical KV footprint by
73.3% and p50 latency by 6.51%. Attention analyses show increased attention to retained recent states,
while history interventions associate larger amounts of stale context with higher prediction error. These
observations are consistent with the memory-role-conflict hypothesis, although they do not isolate it from
context-length and position-handling effects. Our results support inference-time context scheduling as a
practical approach to improving long-horizon robot video prediction and computational efficiency without
retraining.

Introduction
Robot video world models predict how visual observations evolve under actions, providing a reusable substrate
for planning, policy evaluation, and synthetic experience generation (Finn et al., 2016; Ebert et al., 2018; Ha and
Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2023). Recent progress in discrete visual representation learning (van den Oord
et al., 2017; Mentzer et al., 2024) and autoregressive sequence modeling (Vaswani et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2021) has
made it possible to express observations and actions within a unified causal token sequence. This paradigm scales

naturally from game environments (Micheli et al., 2023; Bruce et al., 2024) to interactive and robotic worlds (Wu
et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025, 2026). Yet robot world models must do more than generate plausible
short clips: they must preserve scene identity, robot morphology, object state, and action responsiveness over
long horizons. These properties often deteriorate during autoregressive generation, limiting world models for
long-horizon reasoning and control.
Most token-based video world models use full-history decoding, which we denote as Full Context: the initial context,
action conditions, and every generated visual state remain active throughout the rollout (Yan et al., 2021; Bruce
et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). This strategy appears natural because additional history should, in principle, provide
more evidence for future prediction. Our experiments reveal the opposite behavior. As shown by the gray curve in
Fig. 1(a), the attention assigned to the most recent visual state steadily decreases as the rollout grows, even though
distant context and early generated states remain accessible. The resulting predictions are initially plausible but
progressively exhibit structural omission, weakened action response, and motion drift. Meanwhile, the active context
and KV cache grow monotonically with the rollout horizon. This leads to the central question:
Key question: How should an autoregressive robot video world model organize its inference context to remain
dynamically informative and memory efficient over long horizons?
We hypothesize that this failure partly reflects the different roles of rollout history: persistent scene context, recent
visual–action states, and older generated history. Full Context places these tokens in a shared attention pool; as
history grows, the relative attention to recent states can decline. We call this possible interference memory-role
conflict. The traces in Fig. 1(a) motivate this interpretation, but do not by themselves establish causality. Attention
sinks and streaming inference have been studied in language models (Xiao et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2025a; Barbero
et al., 2025); robot video prediction additionally requires preserving the structure and alignment of visual–action
blocks.
Existing long-context and KV-cache methods primarily identify important individual tokens or compress a language-
model cache (Zhang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Directly transferring token-level
eviction to robot video rollouts is non-trivial because a physical time step is represented by a structured block of visual
and action tokens; fragmenting this block can disrupt spatial coherence and causal alignment. Recent long-context
video models also recognize that nearby frames and distant context play different roles (Gu et al., 2025b), but
typically address this distinction through architectural design or training. We instead ask whether the inference
context of an already trained world model can be reorganized without modifying its parameters. This shift turns
long-horizon rollout from a problem of retaining more history into one of retaining the right history.
2


We introduce ReCAP (Receding Context with Anchor Preservation), a training-free context scheduler. It preserves
the initial anchor and a contiguous window of recent complete visual–action blocks, evicting older generated blocks
after each update. This retains within-frame structure, action alignment, and temporal order while bounding active
context independently of rollout length. The tokenizer, world model, and decoder remain frozen. Unlike methods
requiring long-context training or architectural changes (Gu et al., 2025b), ReCAP changes only the inference
schedule.
Figure 1 summarizes the resulting gains under its dedicated overview protocols. Context reorganization repeatedly
restores the influence of recent visual states, yielding a 13.4-percentage-point advantage over Full Context at the late
extrapolation horizon (Fig. 1a). On its 200-pair BridgeV2 evaluation split, ReCAP improves perceptual fidelity,
pixel quality, and robot-trajectory consistency, reducing LPIPS by 63.3% (Fig. 1b). On 200 paired RT-1 episodes,
Qwen3-VL,
InternVL3, and
GPT-5.5 yield 12/12 positive judge–criterion score differences (Fig. 1c;
Appendix D). Under the corresponding efficiency profile, ReCAP reduces mean and peak active KV-cache usage
by 41.4% and 56.0%, respectively, and lowers peak reserved GPU memory by 30.5% (Fig. 1d), with near-neutral
wall-clock overhead. These overview results use different samples, extrapolation lengths, and aggregation rules from
the fixed-horizon analyses in Section 5; their numerical values should therefore not be compared directly. Across
RT-1, BridgeV2, LIBERO-90, and CALVIN, the main experiments nevertheless show that the improvements
persist across environments and rollout horizons.
History interventions associate additional stale context with increased prediction error, although length and position
effects remain confounded. A separate prefix-length study tests scene/history budget allocation (Appendix E),
while human comparison and rubric sensitivity analyses assess the semantic evaluation (Appendices D.6–D.7). Our
contributions are:
1. Empirical diagnosis. We identify memory-role conflict as a recurring failure mode of Full Context decoding
in long-horizon robot video world models, and connect it to recent-state attention decay, structural drift, and
unbounded context growth. 2. Training-free context scheduling. We introduce ReCAP, which preserves a
persistent context anchor and recent visual–action states while evicting stale generated history, without retraining or
architectural modification. 3. Multi-level validation. We demonstrate consistent improvements in prediction
quality, semantic consistency, and memory efficiency across robot datasets, supported by controlled interventions,
attention analysis, and context-component ablations.

Related Work
Robot Video World Models. World models learn action-conditioned dynamics for planning, policy evaluation,
and synthetic experience generation (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2023). Early robot video models
predict future observations in pixel space (Finn et al., 2016; Ebert et al., 2018), whereas discrete tokenizers and
autoregressive Transformers represent observations and actions in a unified causal stream (van den Oord et al.,
2017; Yan et al., 2021). Genie, iVideoGPT, and RoboDreamer extend this paradigm to interactive and robotic
settings (Bruce et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), with reinforcement learning
and reward alignment further improving action consistency (Wu et al., 2025, 2026). These studies mainly target
representations, architectures, or training, leaving inference-time context organization largely unexplored.
Long-Horizon Autoregressive Video Modeling. Autoregressive video world models condition on generated
states to support causal, flexible-length rollouts (Yan et al., 2021; Bruce et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2025).
Yet train–inference mismatch compounds errors over time, degrading scene identity, object structure, motion, and
action responsiveness (Bengio et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Retaining complete history also
increases attention and KV-cache costs without guaranteeing better predictions (Gu et al., 2025b; Po et al., 2025).
Hierarchical context, context packing, and state-space memory balance local and distant information, but typically
3


require specialized architectures or additional training (Gu et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2025). In contrast, ReCAP
reorganizes the existing visual–action context of a frozen robot world model at inference time.
Memory and Context Management. Memory management spans three main directions. H2O, Scissorhands,
FastGen, and SnapKV evict or compress language-model KV caches according to token importance (Zhang et al.,
2023; Liu et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Streaming and sliding-window methods preserve initial and
recent tokens (Xiao et al., 2024), while attention-sink analyses explain the role of early tokens (Gu et al., 2025a;
Barbero et al., 2025). Transformer-XL, Compressive Transformer, and Recurrent Memory Transformer instead
propagate or compress hidden states across segments (Dai et al., 2019; Rae et al., 2020; Bulatov et al., 2022).
These approaches mainly operate on language tokens or activations. Robot rollouts instead require complete
visual–action blocks, since fragmenting a physical step can disrupt spatial coherence and action alignment. Unlike
streaming language models, ReCAP preserves a semantic scene anchor together with recent dynamics and schedules
complete blocks without architectural modification or additional training.

Experiments
Research questions.
We organize our experiments around four research questions:
RQ1: Effectiveness and generality. Does ReCAP consistently improve prediction quality across datasets and
evaluated backbones?
RQ2: Long-horizon robustness. How effectively does ReCAP mitigate degradation over increasingly long
autoregressive rollouts?
RQ3: Mechanism and components. Why does ReCAP work, and how do the scene anchor, recent trajectory,
and stale-history removal contribute?
RQ4: Efficiency and scalability. How does ReCAP trade prediction quality against context size, KV-cache
memory, and inference cost?

5.1
Experimental Setup
▶Datasets and backbones. We evaluate BridgeV2 (Walke et al., 2023), RT-1 (Brohan et al., 2023), LIBERO-
90 (Liu et al., 2024), and CALVIN (Mees et al., 2022) with RLVR-World (Wu et al., 2025), iVideoGPT (Wu et al.,
2024), and RobotAlign-R1 (Wu et al., 2026). Appendices B.4–B.6 document dataset adaptation of the 127.1M
RLVR-World transformer and its tokenizer, including splits, action mappings, training curves, and checkpoint
selection. Adaptation precedes the scheduling comparison: each paired test uses the same frozen checkpoint.
“Training-free” refers to applying ReCAP.
▶Evaluation metrics. We evaluate generation quality from three complementary perspectives. PSNR, SSIM, and
LPIPSmeasurevisualfidelity. ArmScore, ShapeIoU,andnormalizeddynamictimewarping(nDTW)evaluaterobot
shape and trajectory consistency. Qwen3-VL (Bai et al., 2025), InternVL3 (Zhu et al., 2025), and GPT-5.5 (OpenAI,
2026) further assess interaction quality, instruction following, task progress, and motion naturalness.
▶Evaluation protocol and implementation. Paired rollouts share initial observations, actions, and generation
seeds. For the dedicated 200-pair RT-1 VLM study, judges score the same 16 sampled frames per video with a shared
four-criterion rubric and hidden method identities (Appendix D, including the complete prompt). Horizon-AUC
and component comparisons report paired bootstrap 95% intervals. The efficiency benchmark uses vLLM in
BF16; the additional BridgeV2 study uses a separate FP32 PyTorch/Transformers backend. Appendix B details the
hardware and implementation settings.

6
Conclusion
We presented ReCAP, a training-free context scheduler that preserves the initial anchor and recent complete visual–
action blocks. Across evaluated datasets and backbones, it improves long-horizon prediction over Full Context.
Prefix-allocation and human–VLM analyses provide complementary evidence under their respective protocols.
History and attention analyses are consistent with memory-role conflict, while context-length and backend effects
remain unresolved. At H = 64, ReCAP reduces logical KV use by 73.3% and achieves 4.28× throughput at 256
concurrent requests. These results support context scheduling for efficient robot video prediction with frozen
models. Context composition thus merits attention alongside model capacity in long-horizon prediction. Future
work should isolate content, position, and cache effects through matched controls. Adaptive context allocation and
closed-loop robot evaluation would further clarify when this schedule is most useful.

LIBERO-90 Adaptation and Training Details
Dataset and task-level split.
LIBERO-90 (Liu et al., 2024) contains 90 tasks with 50 demonstrations each. We
lexicographically sort the task files and hold out nine evenly spaced tasks, yielding 81 training tasks (4,050 episodes;
596,637 time steps) and nine validation tasks (450 episodes; 72,406 time steps). Demonstrations remain intact;
training samples contiguous eight-frame segments at stride one.
We use the static agentview_rgb camera. Native 128×128 images are bilinearly resized to 256×256, edge-padded
to 256 × 320 as in CALVIN, and scaled to [0, 1], without augmentation.
Action conversion.
We use the CALVIN seven-to-13-slot mapping and 256 uniform bins per dimension. Ranges
are fitted on training actions only, with a 2% margin: [−1.04, 1.04] for the gripper, [−0.975, 0.975] for translation,
[−0.35394, 0.38929] for roll, [−0.39, 0.39] for pitch and yaw, and [−1, 1] for unused slots.

Tokenizer initialization, representation, loss, optimizer coefficients, and

intermediate images from random validation batches are not used for checkpoint selection.

B.6
BridgeV2 Adaptation and Short-Horizon Evaluation
Data and action representation.
We preserve the train/test split of BridgeV2 TFDS 0.1.0 (Walke et al., 2023):
25,460 training episodes (864,292 time steps) and 3,475 validation episodes (118,603 time steps), with mean length
approximately 34 frames. Training samples contiguous eight-frame segments at stride one within episodes. Images
use the native 256 × 320 geometry and the same 1,280-token context, 80-token dynamics representation, and
4,375-code FSQ streams as CALVIN.
Bridge’s action mapping differs from CALVIN and LIBERO: translation occupies slots 0–2, rotation 3–5, gripper 6,
zeros 7–11, and termination 12. Each dimension uses 256 bins with ranges fitted on training actions only, preserving
the 93-token visual–action block.
For the additional qualitative study, we take the first 24 validation files in lexicographic order with at least 33 frames
and retain their first 33 frames: one observation and 32 prediction targets. This candidate pool is separate from the
main-text BridgeV2 evaluation sets.
Stage 1: tokenizer adaptation.
We fine-tune the released RLVR-World RT-1 tokenizer (Wu et al., 2025)
with an adversarial reconstruction objective. Table 7 lists the optimization settings. Both AdamW optimizers
use (β1, β2) = (0.9, 0.999) and ε = 10−8; gradient checkpointing is enabled and the seed is 20260906. The final
checkpoint is shared step 60,000, reached in approximately 18 h 49 min.
Before shared step 10,000, only the tokenizer is updated. Thereafter, generator and discriminator branches alternate
after groups of four accumulation micro-batches. Each learning-rate schedule advances only with its corresponding
optimizer. Thus, the 60k shared counter does not denote 60,000 generator updates.
Augmentation and reconstruction objective.
Tokenizer training uses clip-consistent color jitter: brightness,
contrast, and saturation factors in [0.9, 1.1], and hue shifts in [−0.05, 0.05]. Random resized crops use area scale
[0.8, 1.0] and aspect ratio [1.0, 1.4], with output size 256 × 320. The same sampled transform is applied across each
clip. The online validation loader inherits these augmentation settings; the fixed-subset reconstruction evaluation
below instead uses unchanged input frames.
Let xc denote the context frame and x1:7 the dynamics frames, with reconstructions ˆx. For this run, the generator
objective is
LG = L1(xc, ˆxc) + L1(x1:7, ˆx1:7)
+ LLPIPS(xc, ˆxc) + LLPIPS(x1:7, ˆx1:7)
+ 0.1 ⊮[s ≥10,000] Ladv,
Ladv = −E[D(ˆx)].
(11)
Each dynamics term averages over seven frames. Context and dynamics terms have unit weight, without temporal-
occupancy balancing. FSQ commitment losses are zero, and the adversarial weight is fixed at 0.1.
Adversarial objective.
Following adversarial perceptual tokenization (Esser et al., 2021), a frame-level discrim-
inator processes real and reconstructed context and dynamics images. It uses six stride-two convolutional stages,
spectral normalization, LeakyReLU activations, intermediate instance normalization, and a spatial logit head. With
reconstructed frames detached, its loss is
LD = E[(1 −D(x))+ + (1 + D(ˆx))+] + RGP.
(12)
The real-input gradient penalty has coefficient 10 and unit target norm, using gradients of summed logits with
respect to real dynamics inputs. The discriminator is used only during tokenizer adaptation. Full Context and
ReCAP share the resulting frozen tokenizer and transformer; we do not separately estimate the effect of adversarial
training.

D
VLM Evaluation Protocol
D.1
Evaluation Scope and Inputs
We evaluate 200 held-out RT-1 episodes, each with paired Full Context and ReCAP predictions sharing the initial
observations, actions, and sampling seed (400 videos per judge). Videos contain 26–113 frames (median 42) at
256 × 320 resolution and are encoded as H.264 at 25 frames/s before sampling.
Each judge receives one video’s sampled frames and the episode instruction, without method identity, the competing
prediction, or ground-truth video. Scores are compared afterward across matched episodes. The instruction specifies
the evaluation goal; it is not a language input to the world model. An additional 80-pair human comparison and
sensitivity analysis appear in Appendices D.6 and D.7.
D.2

D.3
Judge-Specific Preprocessing and Generation
Qwen3-VL-8B.
We use the native chat template and smart resize, with sampling disabled and at most 400 output
tokens.
InternVL3-8B.
Frames are bicubically resized to 448 × 448, normalized with ImageNet means (0.485, 0.456,
0.406) and standard deviations (0.229, 0.224, 0.225), and converted to BF16. Each frame uses one tile, without
dynamic tiling, and is labeled in temporal order. The square resize changes the source aspect ratio.
GPT-5.5.
The API receives the same sampled images and rubric with provider-default decoding parameters;
repeated responses need not be identical.
D.4
Shared Rubric and Required Output
The fixed prompt requires an integer score from 1 to 10 and an evidence-based reason for each of four criteria,
returned as JSON. Judgments must use only visible evidence. The episode {instruction} is the sole runtime text
substitution. Table 9 summarizes the rubric; Appendix D.10 reproduces the complete prompt.
Mandatory caps and score guidance.
The hallucination check caps Instruction Following at 4 when human
hands replace the expected robotic arm. The static penalty caps Motion Naturalness at 4 when the arm shows
essentially no meaningful motion or interaction, even if the scene appears smooth. The prompt also encourages use
of the full range, intermediate odd scores between even anchors, and distinct scores for distinguishable videos. The
latter instruction may affect tie rates.

D.9
Limitations of VLM Evaluation
Sparse frames can miss transient errors or late completion, particularly in longer episodes. Shared evidence and rubric
also make the judges’ errors potentially correlated; the 12 positive differences are not independent replications. The
static penalty explicitly penalizes freezing, a common Full Context failure, so Motion Naturalness measures more
than smoothness. Sensitivity tests retain the aggregate preference direction, but the 80-pair human comparison
offers only limited corroboration. These scores assess visible plausibility and instruction compatibility, not verified
physics, closed-loop performance, or real-robot task success.

VLM Judging Prompt
**VIDEO INSTRUCTION:** {instruction}
You are an expert evaluator for robot interaction videos. You are evaluating videos generated for **embodied AI
manipulation scenarios**, specifically focusing on robotic arms interacting with objects in tabletop environments.
**EVALUATION CONTEXT:**
−Target scenario: Robotic manipulation (e.g., pick−place, push, grasp)
−Expected agent: **Robotic arm/end−effector**, NOT human hands
−Expected environment: Tabletop with objects, typical for robot manipulation tasks
−Expected physics: Realistic robot−object interactions following physical laws
**CRITICAL EVALUATION PRINCIPLES:**
1. Base ALL judgments ONLY on what is visually observable in the sampled frames
2. DO NOT infer information not shown (no assumptions about unseen parts)
3. Evaluate temporal coherence across the sampled frames
4. For instruction following: Compare STRICTLY against the provided text instruction
5. **You MUST use the FULL 1−10 scale**. Avoid defaulting to the middle (5−6) when uncertain; instead, pick the odd
anchor between the two neighboring even anchors that best matches the visual evidence. Two visually similar videos
should NOT receive identical scores unless they are indistinguishable frame−by−frame.
**EVALUATION DIMENSIONS & SCORING RUBRICS (integer 1..10):**
1. Interaction_Quality (Quality of robot−object interactions)
−Score 1−2: Objects pass through robot or other objects; no proper contact
−Score 3−4: Contact exists but interaction is unrealistic (e.g., sliding without friction, incorrect force response)
−Score 5−6: Mostly plausible interactions with minor issues (e.g., slight penetration, imperfect grasping)
−Score 7−8: Realistic contact physics (proper friction, force transfer, object deformation)
−Score 9−10: Perfect interaction physics; indistinguishable from real robot manipulation
2. Instruction_Following (Adherence to given instruction: **VIDEO INSTRUCTION**)
−**HALLUCINATION CHECK**: If the video shows human hands instead of robotic arms, score <= 4 immediately
−Score 1−2: Completely different from instruction (wrong action, wrong objects, wrong scene)
−Score 3−4: Partially related but major errors (e.g., wrong target object, incorrect manipulation type)
−Score 5−6: Follows general intent but with execution errors (e.g., correct action sequence but imprecise)
−Score 7−8: Mostly correct with minor deviations (e.g., slight position error, extra unnecessary motion)
−Score 9−10: Perfect execution of all specified elements (action, object, scene, outcome)
3. Task_Progress (Task completion from first to last frame)
−Score 1−2: No progress; scene at last frame is essentially identical to first frame w.r.t. the task
−Score 3−4: Robot initiates motion but no meaningful progress toward the goal state
−Score 5−6: Partial progress (e.g., object approached / grasped but not delivered)
−Score 7−8: Task mostly completed with minor issues (e.g., object placed near but not exactly at target)
−Score 9−10: Task fully completed as described by the instruction
4. Motion_Naturalness (Smoothness AND presence of physically plausible arm motion)
−**STATIC PENALTY (MANDATORY)**: This metric evaluates the naturalness of *executed* motion, NOT merely the
absence of visual artifacts. If the robotic arm exhibits essentially no meaningful motion across the sampled frames —
i.e. the end−effector position barely changes, no interaction with objects is initiated, and the scene at the last frame
looks almost identical to the first frame — you MUST score <= 4, regardless of how smooth or artifact−free the
(non−)motion appears. A perfectly still or barely−moving arm is NOT natural robot motion; it is a failure to act.
−Score 1−2: Severe teleportation, jitter, or discontinuous jumps in end−effector position; OR the arm is completely frozen
/ drifts imperceptibly without executing any manipulation.
−Score 3−4: Frequent unnatural motion (visible jitter, non−smooth trajectory); OR the arm makes only tiny, aimless
movements and clearly fails to attempt the instructed motion.
−Score 5−6: The arm clearly executes meaningful motion toward the task, but with occasional stutter, unnatural
acceleration, or minor kinematic issues.
−Score 7−8: The arm executes meaningful motion smoothly with realistic joint kinematics; only very minor imperfections
in transitions.

Interpretation and scope.
This experiment tests joint allocation between scene context and recent history,
without isolating specific scene semantics. It complements the LPIPS-AUC ablation in Figure 6(a), where ReCAP
and Recent Only show no measurable difference under that protocol. Implementations retaining identical tokens,
block boundaries, positions, and cache updates realize the same schedule; this comparison distinguishes prefix
allocations.
