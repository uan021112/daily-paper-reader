# Perceptual Characterization of Scan-induced Artifacts in Dynamic 3D Meshes 

Huong Hoang, Truong Nguyen, Pamela Cosman Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, University of California, San Diego, CA, USA 92093 

Abstract —Dynamic meshes reconstructed from real-world scenes often exhibit scan-induced artifacts such as surface jitter and missing geometry, which strongly impact perceived visual quality. Objective assessment remains challenging due to the absence of artifact-free ground truth and limited perceptual understanding of artifact interactions. This paper presents a sys-tematic and controlled perceptual study of scan-induced artifacts in dynamic meshes, focusing on surface jitter and holes. To enable systematic analysis, we introduce a physically grounded artifact synthesis framework in which holes are synthesized using a recon-struction uncertainty model derived from multi-view geometry. The study reveals asymmetric cross-artifact interactions, non-additive integration, and motion-dependent temporal masking. Based on these findings, we propose a compact, perceptually grounded no-reference quality assessment framework that en-codes the observed perceptual mechanisms. 

Index Terms —Dynamic mesh quality assessment, percep-tual quality, 3D reconstruction, cross-artifact interactions, no-reference metrics. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Dynamic mesh reconstruction from real-world scenes is in-creasingly used in immersive telepresence, virtual reality, and free-viewpoint video. Despite advances in multi-view capture and reconstruction algorithms, dynamic meshes reconstructed from real objects remain affected by scan-induced artifacts, such as surface jitter and missing geometry/holes (Fig. 1). These artifacts arise from fundamental limitations in camera coverage, correspondence estimation, and triangulation, and persist even in high-quality capture pipelines. Assessing the perceptual quality of such dynamic meshes remains an open challenge. Unlike synthetic animations, re-constructions of real scenes lack an artifact-free ground truth; reference sequences are themselves reconstructed and there-fore contain residual noise and temporal inconsistencies. As a result, reference-based metrics may penalize deviations that are perceptually beneficial. This limitation motivates percep-tual studies and no-reference quality assessment approaches that do not assume access to a perfect reference. Most existing objective metrics for 3D geometry focus on static meshes or assume reference-based evaluation. While recent work has extended quality assessment to dynamic meshes, these efforts primarily address compression artifacts and rely on reference sequences. More fundamentally, existing approaches typically assume that multiple distortions combine additively or independently. In this paper, we present a systematic perceptual study of re-construction artifacts in dynamic meshes, focusing on surface jitter and missing geometry. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Perceptual Study and Assessment of Static 3D Geometry 

Objective quality assessment of static 3D meshes has been extensively studied. Early metrics focused on purely geometric deviation measures, such as vertex-to-surface distances and global surface deviation, such as Metro [3] and GL [4]. These methods quantify numerical error but do not explicitly model human perception. Subsequent work introduced perceptually motivated geo-metric metrics, validated through controlled subjective ex-periments. Notable examples include 3DWPM [5], MSDM [6], MSDM2 [7], FMPD [8], and DAME [9]. These models incorporate curvature, structural distortion, or local saliency considerations, demonstrating significantly improved correla-tion with human opinion scores. In parallel, psychophysical studies such as [10] investigated human sensitivity to static geometric distortions without proposing explicit metrics. Despite their success, these approaches are inherently frame-based and temporally agnostic. They assume static in-spection and do not account for temporal integration, temporal masking, or motion-induced perceptual effects. Consequently, they cannot explain quality judgments of dynamic 3D meshes where temporal instability plays a dominant role. 

B. Perceptual Study and Assessment of Dynamic 3D Meshes 

Compared to static meshes, perceptual quality assessment of dynamic 3D meshes remains less explored. Early work, such as STED [11], computes the spatiotemporal edge differ-ence to estimate the quality of dynamic meshes, while [12] conducted subjective and objective evaluations, highlighting the perceptual importance of temporal distortions. However, most existing dynamic mesh metrics assume consistent mesh topology across frames, enabling vertex correspondence and temporal tracking. This assumption breaks down for real-world dynamic reconstructions from multi-view capture systems, where topology may vary due to tracking failure or occlusion. More recent studies have attempted to bridge this gap. Yildiz et al. [13] extended image-space sensitivity models to dynamic 3D representations. Yang et al. [14] provided a comprehen-sive subjective and objective evaluation of existing dynamic mesh metrics. Nevertheless, these approaches operate within a reference-based paradigm, assuming access to a pristine ground-truth mesh sequence. In practice, however, ground-truth dynamic meshes, especially those reconstructed from real multi-view systems, already contain residual artifacts. This limits the interpretability and reliability of purely reference-based evaluation for real-world capture scenarios. 

C. Perceptual Study of Scan-induced Arifacts 

Existing perceptual studies on dynamic meshes [12]–[14] primarily focus on controlled geometric distortions such as simplification, quantization, or compression noise. These dis-tortions are typically introduced through explicit parametric operations, enabling systematic control over their magnitude and statistical properties. In contrast, scan-induced artifacts such as holes originate from multi-view reconstruction pro-cesses and are driven by visibility inconsistencies, triangula-tion uncertainty, and reconstruction confidence. Rather than being explicitly parameterized, their spatial structure reflects the underlying imaging geometry and reconstruction dynam-ics. A key obstacle is stimulus generation. Unlike additive geometric noise, realistic missing geometry cannot be gen-erated by random vertex removal. Their structure depends on visibility, triangulation uncertainty, and surface completeness constraints. As a result, perceptually controlled experiments on scan-induced artifacts remain scarce. This gap motivates a principled framework for generating and studying scan-induced artifacts under controlled conditions. 

D. Reconstruction Uncertainty and Perceptual Relevance 

Geometric uncertainty in multi-view 3D reconstruction has been extensively analyzed in computer vision and photogram-metry. Concepts like covariance propagation and uncertainty ellipsoids are well established in 3D reconstruction literature. Classical treatments describe how image noise propagates to 3D point estimates through Jacobian-based covariance analysis [15], explaining the anisotropic, view-dependent, and baseline-sensitive nature of reconstruction errors. However, this body of work is mainly used for numerical accuracy and has never been used to synthesize perceptually controlled stimuli or to study how scan-induced artifacts are perceived. In this work, reconstruction uncertainty is repur-posed as a generative mechanism to create perceptually con-trolled, physically plausible scan-induced artifacts, bridging geometric estimation theory and perceptual quality modeling. 

E. Integration of Multiple Distortions 

The perceptual integration of multiple distortions has been widely studied in image and video quality assessment. Prior work consistently shows that perceived quality does not follow additive rules when distortions co-occur [16], [17]. Interaction-aware models with multiplicative or non-linear coupling terms significantly outperform additive formulations [17]. In the context of 3D perceptual quality assessment, however, distortion interaction remains largely unexplored. Existing 3D 3

mesh metrics typically assume independence between artifact types and aggregate features additively. To our knowledge, no prior work reports experimentally derived iso-quality contours or explicitly models interaction effects in dynamic 3D scan-induced artifacts. This represents a significant conceptual gap between 2D video perception theory and 3D dynamic geometry assessment. 

F. Motivation for No-Reference Modeling 

Most existing objective metrics for dynamic meshes rely on reference-based evaluation, implicitly assuming that the ref-erence sequence represents artifact-free geometry. In practice, however, reference dynamic meshes are typically reconstructed from real multi-view capture systems and therefore already contain residual scan-induced artifacts. As a result, reference-based metrics may penalize processing methods, such as compression or filtering, that deviate from the reference while improving perceived visual quality. Moreover, for reconstructions of real objects, an artifact-free ground truth does not exist. This fundamentally limits the applicability of absolute reference-based evaluation and motivates perceptual studies and no-reference quality models that do not assume access to a perfect reference, but instead rely on perceptually meaningful characterization of artifacts. III. A RTIFACT GENERATION METHODS 

To study the perceptual impact of scan-induced artifacts in a controlled manner, we synthesize two dominant scan-induced artifacts commonly observed in multi-view capture systems: surface jitter and holes. The two artifacts are generated independently and parametrically, enabling precise control over their severity and combinations while preserving the underlying geometry and motion of the original sequence. Importantly, while surface jitter is modeled using a generic stochastic process, holes are synthesized using a physically grounded uncertainty model derived from multi-view recon-struction theory. To our knowledge, this is the first perceptual study that explicitly leverages geometric uncertainty theory to synthesize missing geometry in dynamic meshes. 

A. Surface Jitter Synthesis 

Surface jitter refers to small, high-frequency perturbations in the positions of reconstructed vertices. Jitter may emerge from fundamental limitations and inconsistencies in data rep-resentation and transformation, including noise, discretization, information loss, compression artifacts, and imperfections in data processing pipelines. In reference mesh reconstruction, it is commonly caused by calibration noise, limited camera resolution, correspondence errors, and view-dependent trian-gulation instability. B. Stimulus Construction 

Artifact severity is controlled independently for surface jitter and holes.  

> •

Surface jitter severity is determined by the standard de-viation of the Gaussian noise applied to vertex positions. Five monotonically increasing levels are selected to span the full perceptual range from imperceptible to strongly annoying jitter.  

> •

Hole severity is controlled using the average removed surface area ratio %Atarget , defined in Section III-B. Five target values of %Atarget are chosen to produce perceptually distinct levels of hole visibility, ranging from sparse, barely noticeable holes to large, dominant missing regions. To enable systematic analysis of artifact interactions, stimuli are generated according to three conditions:  

> •

Jitter-only: Clean sequences with surface jitter applied at each of the five jitter severity levels, without holes.  

> •

Holes-only: Clean sequences with holes synthesized us-ing the uncertainty-based method at each of the five hole severity levels, without jitter.  

> •

Combined artifacts: All pairwise combinations of jitter and hole severity levels. This design allows direct comparison between single-artifact and multi-artifact conditions and supports analysis of cross-artifact perceptual effects. In total, the study comprises 280 5

stimuli. The stimuli are divided into two non-overlapping sub-sets, each containing four motion sequences, to limit session duration and participant fatigue. To monitor attention and rating reliability, five clean stimuli are inserted at random positions within each session. 

C. Rendering and Presentation 

Some related work used a rotating camera to capture dif-ferent angles to avoid generating separate videos for multiple viewpoints. However, the rotation path and speed may mask certain artifacts. To avoid that, we keep the camera fixed. Each clip is rendered from two static views (front and back). Each stimulus will display both front and back views simultaneously (split-screen format). Similar to [14], we use the MPEG 3D renderer to visualize the dynamic sequences at a resolution of 1920×1080. The rendered outputs are then encoded and saved as MP4 videos using FFmpeg. Following [14], we apply a constant rate factor of 10 during compression, ensuring smooth playback and visually lossless quality. 

D. Experimental Procedure 

The experiment follows a single-stimulus Absolute Cate-gory Rating (ACR) methodology. Each stimulus is presented in a continuous loop, and participants are asked to provide ratings along three dimensions: Jitter annoyance, Hole annoyance, and Overall visual annoyance. Ratings are collected using a five-point impairment scale: (1) Imperceptible; (2) Perceptible, but not annoying; (3) Slightly annoying; (4) Annoying; and (5) Very annoying. Before the main session, participants complete a training phase with example stimuli illustrating the full severity range for each artifact type. These examples are distinct from the test stimuli and are shown to ensure consistent interpretation of the rating scale. The experiment is conducted in a controlled laboratory environment following ITU-R BT.500 recommendations [19]. Viewing distance, ambient illumination, and display settings are kept constant across sessions. A total of 46 individuals (ages 18-40; 39 men and 7 women) participated in the study, with 23 participants allocated to each stimulus subset. Each session lasts approximately 45 minutes, including training and scheduled breaks. Participants are encouraged to take breaks as needed, with a mandatory break midway through the session. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of UC San Diego (Protocol 210286, Date 4/1/2021). Post-screening of subjects is performed using correlation-based criteria as specified in ITU-R BT.500 [19]. One par-ticipant whose ratings show insufficient consistency with the group was excluded from further analysis, and data from 45 participants were retained as valid.

V. P ERCEPTUAL FINDINGS 

This section presents the main perceptual findings. We first analyze the interaction between surface jitter and holes, then examine their joint integration into overall annoyance, and finally study the influence of motion and observer variability. Together, these results reveal systematic, asymmetric, and nonlinear perceptual behaviors. 

A. Asymmetric Cross-Artifact Interaction Effects 1) Amplification Effect: Influence of Holes on Perceived Jitter: To assess whether holes influence the perception of surface jitter, we analyze jitter annoyance ratings while holding the jitter severity constant and varying hole severity. As shown in Fig. 6, perceived jitter annoyance increases monotonically with hole severity, despite the underlying jitter magnitude remaining unchanged. 

> Fig. 6. Amplification effect: increasing hole severity amplifies perceived jitter annoyance.

These positive slopes indicate an amplification effect ,whereby the presence of holes biases observers toward harsher judgments of jitter. An increase in hole severity is associated with a higher perceived annoyance of the same jitter level. In particular, the appearance of holes can visually mimic surface jitter, especially when multiple tiny holes repeatedly appear and disappear across frames. This behavior is most clearly illustrated by the bottom curve in Fig. 6, corresponding to the “clean” Jitter level, where no surface jitter has been added. Despite the absence of any injected jitter, perceived jitter annoyance increases monotonically with hole severity. It can also be seen that the strength of the amplification effect depends on the underlying jitter severity. At low jitter levels, hole severity has a strong influence on perceived jitter annoyance, but this influence diminishes as jitter becomes dominant. This behavior indicates a perceptual saturation effect: once jitter becomes sufficiently salient, additional im-pairments contribute progressively less to the perceived jitter annoyance. 

2) Masking Effect: Influence of Jitter on Perceived Holes: 

The reciprocal interaction of how jitter affects hole perception is fundamentally different. When hole severity is held con-stant and jitter severity is increased (Fig. 7), perceived hole annoyance decreases for moderate and high hole levels.  

> Fig. 7. Masking effect: increasing jitter severity reduces perceived hole annoyance at moderate hole levels.

This negative dependency demonstrates a masking effect ,in which strong surface instability reduces the visibility or perceptual salience of holes. The effect is not uniform across hole severities. For very low hole levels, masking is negli-gible, indicating that imperceptible holes cannot be further suppressed. As hole severity increases beyond a perceptual threshold, masking becomes evident and reaches its maximum at intermediate hole levels. For very severe holes, masking weakens again, as large missing regions dominate perception regardless of jitter strength. This reveals that masking is not a simple monotonic phenomenon, but depends on the prominence of the masked artifact. 

B. Motion-Dependent Temporal Masking Across Sequences 

Beyond artifact interactions, perceived annoyance also de-pends on motion characteristics of the underlying sequence. Fig. 8a shows that sequences with faster motion (larger TI) consistently receive lower jitter annoyance ratings for the same artifact severity level. This behavior indicates systematic 

motion-dependent temporal masking , whereby increasing motion strength reduces sensitivity to high-frequency surface instability. Moreover, the perceptual difference between low-and high-motion sequences increases as jitter severity in-creases. In contrast, hole annoyance (Fig. 8b) does not exhibit a comparable dependency on motion speed. This asymmetry is consistent with well-established findings in visual perception showing that high-frequency spatiotemporal components are more susceptible to motion masking, whereas low-frequency or spatially structured distortions remain salient [20]. Surface jitter introduces rapid, high-frequency positional fluctuations that are increasingly masked as motion energy rises, reducing their perceptual visibility. Holes, by contrast, correspond to persistent spatial incompleteness and structural discontinuities, which are dominated by lower-frequency spatial components and therefore remain perceptually salient even under fast motion. 7 

> Fig. 8. Artifact perceived annoyance. Each curve represents one content sequence, and color encodes motion magnitude.

C. Non-Additive Integration of Multiple Artifacts 

To understand how jitter and holes jointly contribute to over-all visual annoyance, we analyze iso-annoyance contours in the two-dimensional artifact severity space. The resulting contours (Fig. 9a) are smooth and exhibit rounded corners. Such contour geometry is inconsistent with additive integration, which yields linear iso-response boundaries, and with hard max pooling, which produces axis-aligned contours. Instead, it is more con-sistent with nonlinear pooling mechanisms, such as Minkowski integration, widely adopted in perceptual modeling [21]. In these formulations, the pooling exponent governs the degree of dominance between components, allowing one artifact to dominate perception without fully suppressing the contribution of the other.  

> Fig. 9. Iso-quality contours in the jitter–holes annoyance plane.

To model this behavior, overall annoyance ( AO ) is fitted using a Minkowski pooling formulation that combines jitter (AJ ) and hole ( AH ) annoyance: AO = α1(ApJ + α2ApH )1/p .The fitted model (Fig. 9b) accurately reproduces the ob-served iso-annoyance contours and captures the gradual tran-sition of perceptual dominance between artifacts. The fitted exponent ( p = 3 .9) lies well above unity, confirming a strongly nonlinear integration regime intermediate between linear sum-mation ( p = 1 ) and max pooling ( p = ∞). The estimated weighting parameters ( α1 = 0 .91 , α 2 = 0 .88 ) indicate that jitter and holes contribute comparably to overall annoyance, with a slight dominance of the jitter dimension, indicating that dominance emerging primarily through nonlinear pooling rather than through large differences in linear weights. These results suggest that additive quality models are insuf-ficient for describing the interaction between holes and jitter in dynamic mesh perception, and that nonlinear pooling models are well-suited to describe their interaction. 

D. Inter-Observer Variability and Perceptual Dominance 

Analysis of individual observer responses reveals substantial variability in artifact sensitivity. When correlating overall annoyance ratings with jitter and hole severity separately, participants separate into two dominant groups: jitter-dominant observers and hole-dominant observers (Fig. 10).  

> Fig. 10. Participant sensitivity profiles to jitter and holes. Sensitivity is quantified as the correlation between overall annoyance ratings and artifact severity levels for each participant.

The observer distribution in Fig. 10 reveals a clear trade-off in perceptual sensitivity. Instead of concentrating around the equality line, the distribution is oriented approximately perpendicular to it, indicating that increased sensitivity to one artifact systematically coincides with reduced sensitivity to the other. This suggests that observers adopt distinct perceptual strategies, selectively prioritizing either jitter or holes when forming overall quality judgments. Importantly, this structure implies consistent perception rather than inattentive or noisy responses. The dominance pattern is symmetric: observers who are highly sensitive to jitter are no less extreme than those who are highly sensitive to holes. Despite this dichotomy, all observers show non-negligible sensitivity to both artifacts, indicating that no participant is perceptually “blind” to either distortion. VI. T OWARDS A NO-REFERENCE PERCEPTUAL METRIC 

The perceptual findings in Section V provide evidence that scan-induced artifacts in dynamic meshes are perceived in 8

a nonlinear, asymmetric, and motion-dependent manner. In particular, jitter and holes interact perceptually through ampli-fication and masking effects, integrate via nonlinear integration rather than additive rules, and exhibit distinct sensitivity to motion-induced temporal masking. These findings collectively motivate the design of a no-reference (NR) perceptual metric that encodes such structured perceptual behaviors explicitly, rather than relying solely on geometric distortion measures. Fig. 11 presents a block diagram of the proposed Perceptual Scan-induced Artifact Metric (PSAM) framework. It oper-ates directly on a reconstructed dynamic mesh sequence and produces an overall annoyance score. First, artifact-specific features are extracted independently to quantify hole severity and jitter severity. Next, the jitter component is modulated by a motion-aware temporal masking stage, reflecting reduced perceptual sensitivity under fast motion. The artifact responses are then passed through a cross-artifact perceptual coupling stage, which models asymmetric amplification and masking interactions between artifacts. Finally, the adjusted artifact re-sponses are combined to yield an overall annoyance estimate. Each stage corresponds directly to a perceptual phenomenon identified in the subjective study.  

> Fig. 11. Overview of the PSAM framework.

A question is whether the perceptual mechanisms, dis-covered in isolation, retain predictive value when combined in a unified model. In other words, do temporal masking, cross-artifact interactions such as amplification and masking, and nonlinear pooling, provide complementary (additive) ex-planatory power, or do they exhibit partial redundancy when combined? Accordingly, the PSAM framework is intention-ally modular; each perceptual mechanism (temporal mask-ing, cross-artifact amplification, and cross-artifact masking) is implemented as an optional nonlinear operator that can be independently enabled or disabled. When a module is disabled, it reduces to an identity mapping, and the model collapses to a simpler baseline.

C. Cross-Artifact Perceptual Interactions 

The perceptual experiment revealed systematic and asym-metric interactions between holes and jitter: holes can exagger-ate the perception of jitter (amplification effect), whereas jitter suppresses the visibility of holes (masking effect). To encode these behaviors, the artifact severities are adjusted through a cross-artifact interaction stage that models both effects as level-dependent nonlinear interactions. The amplification effect (holes → jitter) is modeled as: 

ˆJ = 1 − (1 − ˜J) e−γa H (18) where ˜J is the temporally masked jitter magnitude and γa

controls amplification strength. As H increases, the expo-nential term decreases, reducing the residual factor (1 − ˜J)

and thereby increasing ˆJ. The amplification is bounded and level-dependent, preventing over-enhancement at high jitter levels while capturing the perceptual exaggeration of jitter in the presence of holes. When the cross-artifact amplification module is disabled (equivalently, γa = 0 ), the transformation becomes identity, ˆJ = ˜J, and the model no longer models hole-driven exaggeration of jitter saliency. The masking effect (jitter → holes) is modeled as:

The exponential form ensures smooth, bounded attenuation with ˆH = H when ˜J = 0 . Overall, the model captures the experimentally observed nonlinear behavior: jitter selectively suppresses moderately visible holes, with reduced influence at very low or very high hole severities. When the cross-artifact masking module is disabled (i.e., γm = 0 ), the mapping reduces to ˆH = H, and the model no longer accounts for jitter-induced suppression of hole visibility.

A. Evaluation Protocol 

To evaluate generalization to unseen content while avoiding parameter overfitting, we adopt a nested leave-one-content-out (LOCO) cross-validation framework. Each outer fold holds out all stimuli derived from one sequence for testing, while the remaining sequences are used for parameter estimation. This design explicitly prevents content leakage and reflects the intended deployment scenario in which the metric is applied to previously unseen content. For each fold, the following metrics are reported:

1) Nested Cross-Validation Procedure: For each outer LOCO fold, model parameters are estimated exclusively on the training data using an inner cross-validation loop. Specif-ically, a 5-fold inner cross-validation, stratified by distortion condition, is employed to optimize the model parameters by maximizing agreement with subjective MOS. The optimization objective is defined as

We use the L-BFGS-B algorithm to optimize the parameters, performing 5 random restarts with different initial points to reduce the risk of convergence to local minima. 10 

2) Stratification: The inner cross-validation folds are strat-ified according to distortion conditions (holes-only, jitter-only, and combined distortions). This stratification mitigates the inherent imbalance in the dataset, where combined distortions are more prevalent than single-artifact cases, and ensures that each inner fold contains representative samples of all distortion conditions. This prevents the parameter optimization from being dominated by combined-distortion stimuli and improves the stability of the learned parameters. 

3) Feature Normalization: The hole severity H, jitter sever-ity J, and motion magnitude M are derived from different geometric quantities and therefore may exhibit substantially different numerical ranges. Directly combining features with mismatched scales can bias parameter optimization and affect numerical stability. To ensure balanced contribution of each feature during model estimation, we apply linear min–max normalization. To prevent information leakage, normalization parameters are computed exclusively from the training portion of each outer LOCO fold and subsequently applied to the corresponding test split. Min–max normalization preserves monotonic relationships and relative ordering within each fold. As a result, the normalization does not alter correlation structure or distort linear relationships between features and subjective scores, while improving numerical conditioning during parameter optimization. 

B. Systematic Module Combination Analysis 

PSAM’s modular design allows each perceptual mechanism to be treated as a hypothesis-driven operator whose contri-bution can be evaluated independently and in combination. Rather than viewing the model as a fixed pipeline, we interpret it as a design space defined by temporal masking (TM), cross-artifact amplification (CA), cross-artifact masking (CM), and pooling strategy. The central question is whether these mechanisms provide complementary explanatory power when jointly instantiated, or whether they exhibit partial redundancy by modulating a shared saliency dimension. We therefore eval-uate all structurally meaningful combinations under the same nested LOCO protocol. This analysis provides a controlled comparison of model variants and enables empirical validation of the perceptual mechanisms inferred from the subjective study. Results are summarized in Table I. 

a) Baseline Behavior: Single-artifact models (hole-only and jitter-only) yield substantially lower correlations, con-firming that overall annoyance cannot be explained by either artifact dimension in isolation. Simple average pooling (AVG) of hole and jitter severities provides a strong baseline (PLCC = 0.9199), indicating that both artifact types contribute meaning-fully to perceived annoyance. Replacing naive additive fusion with Minkowski pooling of hole and jitter severities, 

Q = ( w ˆHp + (1 − w) ˆJp)1/p (22) improves PLCC (0.9271), indicating that without perceptual modules, nonlinear integration is beneficial when combining raw artifact magnitudes. 

b) Effect of Individual Perceptual Modules: Each per-ceptual module introduces a hypothesis-driven structural mod-ification to the baseline. TM improves rank correlations and reduces RMSE, demonstrating that motion-dependent attenua-tion increases monotonic alignment with subjective judgments. CA produces the largest individual improvement over the baseline, confirming that hole-induced exaggeration of jitter is a dominant perceptual effect. CM also improves correlation relative to baseline, validating the experimentally observed suppression of moderately visible holes by jitter. These re-sults confirm that each mechanism independently captures a perceptually meaningful component. 

c) Cross-Artifact Interaction Models: When CA and CM are combined (M8), the model achieves strong and consis-tent performance under LOCO (PLCC = 0.9383 ± 0.0142), slightly outperforming all single-mechanism variants. Impor-tantly, both interaction parameters remain stable across LOCO folds ( γa = 0.30 ± 0.06 , γm = 0.63 ± 0.18 ), indicating that amplification and masking represent complementary and consistently estimable perceptual contributions. Although the correlation improvement over CA alone (M6) is modest relative to fold variability, M8 is the minimal formulation that jointly instantiates both asymmetric cross-artifact mechanisms observed in the subjective study. We therefore adopt M8 as the primary model, denoted PSAM-I, as it achieves the best trade-off between predictive strength, parameter stability, and perceptual interpretability. For applications prioritizing reduced complexity, M6 (AVG + CA), denoted PSAM-A , provides a lightweight alternative. It achieves comparable predictive accuracy with a single parameter. 

d) Interaction Between Temporal Masking and Cross-Artifact Interactions: Adding temporal masking to the cross-interaction model (M9) yields comparable overall performance but does not improve correlations relative to M8, while mod-estly improving RMSE. Under joint modeling, the amplifica-tion parameter magnitude collapses ( γa = 0 .02 ± 0.06 ), while cross-artifact masking and temporal masking remain relatively stable ( γm = 1 .40 ± 0.24 , α = 0 .59 ± 0.25 ). This behavior indicates partial redundancy: both temporal masking and cross-artifact amplification modulate jitter dimen-sion, and when modeled simultaneously, their effects over-lap. This provides confirmation that perceptual processing is layered and partially overlapping rather than strictly additive, especially when applied to the same feature dimension. We therefore interpret M9 not as a superior predictive model, but as evidence that motion-dependent masking and cross-artifact amplification operate along closely related per-ceptual dimensions. Temporal masking is thus validated as an independent perceptual effect (M5), but its separability from amplification is limited under joint parameterization. 

e) Flexible Pooling as an Upper Bound: Replacing av-erage pooling with Minkowski integration (M10) improves correlation (PLCC = 0.96). However, the pooling parameters consistently approach optimization bounds and exhibit sub-stantial fold-to-fold variability. The best-performing FR metric (D2) achieves PLCC of 0.9122, while the proposed NR metric achieves PLCC of 0.9383. The performance gap between the proposed NR metric and existing FR metrics should be interpreted in light of their design assumptions. Most full-reference mesh quality metrics were originally developed for compression and simplification scenarios, where the reference geometry is generally assumed to be complete. These metrics primarily measure geometric deviation between corresponding surfaces, such as point-to-point distance, Hausdorff distance, or curvature distortion. In contrast, the reconstruction artifacts studied in this work include topological defects such as holes. Missing surface regions fundamentally violate the assumption of relative corre-spondence to a complete reference mesh. As a result, geomet-ric deviation metrics may underestimate the perceptual severity of holes, particularly when the missing regions occupy limited surface area but are perceptually salient. Our analysis confirms this behavior. Metrics based on sur-face distance or curvature variation exhibit strong correlation with jitter-related annoyance but substantially weaker corre-lation with hole-related annoyance. This distortion-dependent sensitivity suggests that these metrics are effective at capturing geometric noise but do not explicitly model missing structure. The PSAM-I metric, in contrast, includes an explicit hole severity feature that directly quantifies surface loss. This struc-tural inclusion enables the model to account for perceptual sensitivity to topological defects that are not well represented by geometric deviation alone. The improved overall correlation therefore arises not merely from nonlinear modeling, but from incorporating artifact representations aligned with the types of distortions present in dynamic reconstructions. VIII. C ONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

This paper presents a systematic perceptual characterization of surface jitter and missing geometry, two types of scan-induced artifacts in dynamic 3D mesh reconstructions. To enable systematic experimentation, we introduced a physically grounded artifact synthesis framework in which hole formation is driven by multi-view reconstruction uncertainty. This design produced realistic stimuli and enabled a comprehensive sub-jective study spanning isolated artifacts and their interactions. The resulting stimuli and perceptual ratings are consolidated in the DynaScan dataset, establishing a controlled benchmark for studying scan-induced artifacts. The subjective results reveal that reconstruction artifacts in dynamic meshes are perceived through structured, non-additive mechanisms rather than independent or purely ge-ometric error accumulation. We found (i) asymmetric cross-artifact interactions : holes amplify perceived jitter (amplifica-tion effect), while jitter suppresses hole visibility (masking ef-fect); (ii) motion-dependent temporal masking that selectively attenuates perceived jitter under faster motion, with compar-atively limited impact on hole perception; and (iii) nonlinear integration of multiple artifacts, evidenced by smooth iso-annoyance contours that are inconsistent with linear summa-tion or hard max pooling. 13 

Guided by these findings, we proposed a compact NR perceptual quality framework that encodes the identified mech-anisms. We examined whether the perceptual mechanisms retain additive explanatory power when combined in a unified NR quality model. The results show that asymmetric cross-artifact interactions provide stable and complementary predic-tive benefit beyond linear fusion, while temporal masking ex-hibits partial redundancy with cross-artifact amplification, re-vealing overlapping saliency mechanisms. The adopted PSAM-I metric achieves a strong correlation with subjective overall annoyance and outperforms representative full-reference mesh metrics on this reconstruction-focused benchmark. Overall, this work advances the perceptual understanding of dynamic mesh reconstructions and provides (i) a methodology for generating physically plausible, controllable scan-induced artifacts, (ii) an extensive perceptual and structural analysis that uncovers asymmetric cross-artifact interactions, motion-dependent masking, and non-additive integration, and system-atically evaluates whether these mechanisms retain additive explanatory power when jointly instantiated, and (iii) a percep-tually grounded NR quality assessment with strong correlation with human perception. We expect these contributions to support future work in perceptually optimized reconstruction, filtering, and compression. Several directions naturally extend this work. First, while this study focused on surface jitter and holes, real-world reconstructions may exhibit additional artifact types, such as temporal topology changes, surface swimming, or texture instability. Extending the perceptual framework to incorporate these artifacts and to study higher-order interaction effects would enrich the understanding of dynamic mesh perception. Second, the ablation analysis revealed potential partial over-lap between temporal masking and cross-artifact amplification modules. Future work could explore hierarchical or factorized formulations that explicitly model shared underlying percep-tual channels rather than treating mechanisms as independent operators. Third, the current framework models average observer be-havior, but as dominance weighting between artifact types may vary across individuals, incorporating adaptive or observer-dependent weighting schemes could improve predictive robust-ness and enable personalized perceptual quality assessment. Finally, the perceptual findings reported in this work can inform algorithm design beyond quality assessment. Recon-struction, denoising, and compression pipelines may benefit from explicitly accounting for asymmetric artifact interactions and motion-dependent masking. By integrating perceptual sen-sitivity models into optimization objectives, future systems can better allocate distortion where it is perceptually less salient while suppressing artifacts that disproportionately degrade visual experience.
