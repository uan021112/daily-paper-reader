# Geometry Regularization for Vanilla NeRF via Self-Supervised Cross-Ray Consistency 

ZONGJI WANG 

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) achieve high-quality novel-view synthesis by representing a scene as a contin-uous volumetric radiance field. However, the geometry encoded by the density field of vanilla NeRF can be noisy and inaccurate, since different combinations of shape and radiance may explain the same input images. To reduce this ambiguity, we augment vanilla NeRF with a geometry-optimization branch based on self-supervised cross-ray consistency. Specifically, this branch samples candidate surface points from the density field, generates virtual rays that intersect at each point from different directions, and renders their appearance using the NeRF network. Based on the visibility-weighted color consistency across these rays, we constrain the implicit geometry. We further regularize the implicit geometry by penalizing opacity at candidates with inconsistent appearance predictions and encouraging local normal smoothness. These geometric constraints are derived from the NeRF predictions and require no ground-truth depth or surface-normal supervision. Experiments on eight Replica scenes show that the proposed method reduces the mean Chamfer distance by 30.8% compared with vanilla NeRF. Additional Key Words and Phrases: neural radiance fields, implicit geometry, 3D reconstruction, cross-ray consistency 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Recovering 3D shape from 2D observations is a cornerstone problem of computer vision and graphics. This long-standing research topic supports various applications in AR, VR, games, movies, 

etc. [1, 3] With the advances of inverse graphics, differentiable renderer can be integrated in neural network pipelines, making it feasible to directly optimize the 3D geometric parameters using observed 2D images. Among various 3D geometry representations, implicit function represents 3D content continuously and without discretization. With its small memory footprint, implicit neural representation is popular in recent studies. One of the most successful approaches is NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields) [16]. NeRF is a scene representation method presented by Mildenhall et al. [16]. In that work, a deep neural network (typically, a multilayer perceptron, MLP) is applied to parameterize a continuous implicit function, which takes the position 𝑥 ∈ R3 and viewing direction 𝑑 ∈ R3 as input, and predicts the density 𝜎 (𝑥 ) ∈ R+ and view-dependent color 𝑐 (𝑥, 𝑑 ) ∈ R3. Through differentiable volume rendering, dense colors from multiple 2D images can be used directly to optimize the neural radiance field. Encoding the information from multi-view 2D images, this powerful representation shows promising results in capturing complex scene structures and synthesizing 3D-consistent images with fine details. The superior rendering effects ignite research enthusiasm towards NeRF-like methods. Subsequent works include modification for dynamic scene modelling [9, 14], large-scale scene representation [22, 23, 28], baking approaches for real-time rendering [5, 12, 31], encoding extra layers for scene understanding ( e.g. intrinsic [33] and semantic [37] layers), and 3D-aware content or scene generation [6, 8, 10]. Although these approaches have shown impressive results for novel view synthesis, the 3D geometry extracted from the underlying volume density is far from satisfactory, usually suffering from noise and inaccuracy [30, 31]. Neural implicit functions help circumvent the limitations about complex pipelines in classic depth-based multi-view stereo (MVS) [21] and expensive memory consumption in voxel-based approaches [24, 26]. However, it is still a fundamentally under-constrained problem to recover accurate geometry from 2D images, even for approaches based on differentiable rendering [2]. arXiv:2610.27776v1 [cs.CV] 20 Oct 2026 2 Wang

A lot of effort has been dedicated to improve the 3D reconstruction quality of implicit neural representations. To put it briefly, there are works applying signed distance function (SDF) to model the 3D surface [18, 25, 30–32], combining neural implicit field with explicit 3D representations such as voxel [15, 29] and polygon mesh [5, 17], optimizing sampling approach of ray marching [15, 19, 20, 25], applying regularizers about smoothness and sparsity to the implicit field [16, 19, 34], adding priors about depth and structure to constrain the geometry [11, 19, 27, 35], and using multi-view consistency as MVS-based reconstruction [4, 7, 27]. In this paper, we directly refine the implicit geometry of vanilla NeRF to reduce its shape-radiance ambiguity. We choose vanilla NeRF [16] because its simple radiance-field representation supports complex scenes and arbitrary camera poses. However, as its geometry is represented only by volume density, the extracted surfaces often contain noise and inaccuracies. To improve the geometric fidelity, we build on the observation that the appearance of a surface point cannot vary arbitrarily across nearby viewpoints [13]. Existing neural reconstruction methods use this photo-consistency cue through feature matching or image warping between captured views [4, 7, 27]. We instead extend the NeRF architecture with a branch that samples candidate surface points, generates virtual viewpoints around them, and renders the corresponding cross-ray appearances. 2 RELATED WORK 2.1 3D Geometric Representations 

3D geometry is commonly represented by volumes, point clouds, meshes, or implicit functions. Volumetric representations encode spatial structure directly [26], but their memory consumption often limits resolution. Point clouds are compact and flexible, although they lack an explicit neigh-borhood structure. Meshes are compatible with conventional graphics pipelines [31], but changes in topology require modifying their connectivity. Implicit representations instead describe geometry through continuous functions, such as occupancy fields or signed distance fields (SDFs) [18, 30]. Neural networks parameterize these functions without maintaining a dense spatial grid. We focus on the geometry encoded by implicit neural representations. 

2.2 Implicit Neural Representations 

NeRF [16] represents a scene with a neural network that maps 3D positions and viewing directions to density and view-dependent color. Through differentiable volume rendering, it learns the radiance field directly from posed images. Its continuous representation supports novel-view synthesis without explicitly reconstructing a surface during training. Subsequent work extends this representation to dynamic scenes [9, 14], large-scale environ-ments [22, 23, 28], and scene understanding [33, 37]. Specifically, Li et al. [14] model scene motion through forward and backward flow fields, while Fang et al. [9] use time-aware voxel features. For large environments, Mega-NeRF and Block-NeRF decompose scenes into local radiance fields, whereas BungeeNeRF progressively grows its representation. Semantic extensions jointly encode scene labels and appearance, and intrinsic models separate reflectance, shading, and residual com-ponents. Neural fields also support 3D-aware content generation [6, 8, 10]. However, high-quality rendered images do not ensure accurate geometry: the surfaces extracted from the underlying density field can still contain noise and inaccuracies [30, 31]. We therefore focus on improving implicit geometry through neural rendering. 

2.3 Reconstruction through Neural Rendering 

Recovering geometry from images remains under-constrained because different combinations of shape and radiance can explain the same observations [2, 36]. Existing methods address this ambiguity through surface representations, sampling strategies, geometric priors, and multi-view constraints. One approach is to make the surface representation more explicit. Niemeyer et al. [18] proposed Differentiable Volumetric Rendering (DVR), which models shape with an occupancy network and differentiates the surface projection through implicit differentiation. VolSDF [30] and NeuS [25] instead optimize SDF-based surfaces through volume rendering. DVR uses object masks during training, while SDF-based methods such as VolSDF and NeuS can reconstruct surfaces without 4 Wang 

mask supervision. In NeuS, the volume-rendering formulation reduces the geometric bias in the recovered zero-level set. Appropriate initialization and a bounding-sphere prior also guide its surface optimization [25]. UNISURF [19] unifies implicit surfaces and radiance fields and progressively narrows the sampling region around the estimated surface, allowing reconstruction without input masks. Hybrid representations combine neural fields with explicit spatial structures. Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF) [15] organizes local implicit fields in sparse voxels, which also restrict the sampling region during ray marching. Its sparse voxel structure avoids evaluating empty regions, and progressive optimization supports the recovery of finer scene geometry. Munkberg et al. [17] optimize a discrete SDF on a deformable tetrahedral grid and render the resulting mesh during training. Their framework estimates geometry, spatially varying materials, and lighting from posed images and object masks. For efficient rendering, MobileNeRF [5] encodes appearance and opacity on polygon faces and bakes the result into textures, while BakedSDF [31] optimizes a hybrid surface-volume representation for unbounded scenes and bakes it for rasterization. Such approaches modify the representation or rendering pipeline to connect neural reconstruction with explicit geometry. Besides the representation, geometric regularization and additional cues can improve reconstruc-tion. NeRF [16] injects noise into density predictions to regularize ambiguous regions. UNISURF [19] encourages local normal smoothness, and PlenOctrees [34] uses a sparsity prior to favor empty space in ambiguous regions. MonoSDF [35] incorporates monocular depth and normal estimates to constrain the surface. Based on predictions from general-purpose monocular estimators, these cues improve reconstruction quality and reduce optimization time. Guo et al. [11] apply planar constraints to wall and floor regions based on the Manhattan-world assumption. Semantic seg-mentation identifies these regions, and the constraints encourage their surface normals to follow parallel or orthogonal directions. Other approaches use direct geometric observations: Rematas et al. [20] use lidar supervision to constrain reconstruction and limit ray-marching samples to a bounded region. These constraints reduce ambiguity by imposing smoothness, sparsity, or prior geometric information. Multi-view methods obtain geometric cues from agreement between captured images. MVS-NeRF [4] builds plane-swept cost volumes for geometry-aware scene reasoning and combines them with volume rendering to reconstruct a radiance field from a small set of nearby views. Nerfing-MVS [27] uses learned depth priors, adapted with sparse structure-from-motion observations, to guide NeRF optimization. NeuralWarp [7] combines image warping with volume rendering to opti-mize photo-consistency across source views. These methods use captured images or depth priors to constrain the geometry through multi-view observations. Direct photo-consistency methods must also account for source-view selection and visibility when comparing projections of the same 3D point. In this paper, we constrain the density field of vanilla NeRF through agreement among virtual rays rendered by the same network. Specifically, the cross-ray constraint compares appearance predictions around each candidate point, with visibility weights reducing the influence of occluded rays. This provides a self-supervised geometric constraint without image warping or additional depth, normal, or correspondence supervision in the training losses.

5 CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION 

We proposed a framework that augments vanilla NeRF with a geometry-optimization branch. Specifically, the branch samples candidate surface points, generates virtual cross-rays through them, and renders their appearance from multiple viewpoints. Based on the visibility-weighted 12 Wang 

disagreement of these rendered colors, cross-ray consistency constrains the density field, while occupancy suppression and local normal smoothness reduce geometric noise. On eight Replica scenes, the method reduces the mean Chamfer distance by 30.8% relative to vanilla NeRF. The current experiments use a reference mesh to define sampling bounds and scene-specific settings for Room 1 and Office 2. Future work should estimate the bounds from training cameras, isolate the effect of the added branch through an RGB-only continuation from the same checkpoint, and investigate appearance models that are less sensitive to specular surfaces.
