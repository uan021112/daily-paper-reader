Abstract 

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) excel at 2D recognition but often fail to maintain consistent 3D relations across sparse viewpoints, leading to spatial hallucinations under occlusion and perspective changes. Existing methods commonly fuse all frames with dense geometry, incurring substantial com-putation and context overhead while encouraging blind re-liance on imperfect estimators. We present Think-Capture , a query-conditioned framework that first identifies task-critical objects and then acquires their geometry on demand. A 3D toolkit lifts zero-shot masks into a common coordinate frame, and a Symbolic 3D State Abstraction compresses object and camera states into structured text. Hierarchical Group Rela-tive Policy Optimization (GRPO) trains the VLM on cases in which geometry either helps or misleads, teaching it when to use tool evidence and when to fall back on visual cues. Think-Capture-4B reaches 54.4% on MindCube-Tiny (+21.5 points over its backbone), 39.1% on the MMSI-Bench positional subset (+7.9), and 42.8% on ViewSpatial (+5.2). It also re-mains robust under coordinate and mask perturbations. The full stack fits one 24 GB GPU and serves as an asynchronous high-level reasoner on an Agibot G1. Our code is available at github.com/819346675/Think-Capture .

Most existing approaches follow an implicit perception-and-fusion paradigm (Fig. 1a): they encode dense scene geometry and fuse it with image features before seeing the query. Processing task-irrelevant geometry increases com-putation and context length and can introduce distracting evidence [8, 23, 40]. Agentic alternatives let VLMs invoke external 3D tools [60]. These interfaces make geometric evidence explicit, but errors in the tool output or its interpre-tation can still propagate to the answer. Think-Capture instead performs query-conditioned acqui-sition (Fig. 1b). The VLM names only the objects needed to answer the question; a 3D Capturing Toolkit then esti-mates their masks, coordinates, and cross-view relations. A 

Symbolic 3D State Abstraction converts these measurements into compact object and camera descriptions, filtering irrele-vant geometry while preserving the relations required by the query [3, 42]. We train a 4B backbone with GRPO [12, 30, 35]. The 1arXiv:2610.29232v1 [cs.CV] 21 Oct 2026 training set distinguishes queries for which 3D evidence cor-rects the vision-only model from those for which imperfect geometry is misleading. Hierarchical rewards encourage correct use of helpful geometry and incorporate negative ex-amples to retain correct answers when tool augmentation is misleading. The resulting policy learns to combine explicit 3D state with the original frames instead of trusting either source unconditionally. We evaluate on MindCube [40], MMSI-Bench [53], and ViewSpatial-Bench [20], covering mental rotation, cross-view relations, and perspective taking. We further test back-bone scaling, zero-shot transfer, controlled tool corruption, and a matched 2D-box baseline. Finally, we deploy Think-Capture as an asynchronous high-level spatial reasoner on a real Agibot G1. In summary, our main contributions are: • We introduce query-conditioned 3D acquisition and a com-pact symbolic interface that exposes task-relevant object and camera geometry to a VLM. • We propose hierarchical 2D–3D GRPO rewards that pro-mote useful geometry integration and improve robustness to noisy tool evidence. • We demonstrate consistent gains across backbones and benchmarks, robustness to controlled tool corruption, and real-robot integration on consumer hardware. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. Implicit Spatial Learning in VLMs 

Object-centric grounding [22, 56] and 3D-informed learn-ing [5, 21, 24, 44, 45, 52] enrich spatial representations. Visual-tool use and video reasoning provide complemen-tary strategies [9, 37, 38]. Geometry estimators such as VGGT and MapAnything recover scene structure from im-ages [17, 39]. These advances motivate combining learned visual priors with explicit, query-relevant geometric evi-dence. 

2.2. Spatial Reasoning From Multi-views 

Multi-view benchmarks test cross-view understanding [54], mental rotation [40], relations between entities that never co-occur [53], and perspective transformations [20]. Mental models provide a cognitive account of internal spatial repre-sentations [14, 15]; recent evaluations reveal gaps in VLM spatial understanding [6, 34]. Perspective-aware reasoning and spatial-reasoning surveys motivate structured geometry as a complement to visual cues [19, 58]. 

2.3. Explicit Spatial Reasoning 

MindCube [40] trains VLMs to generate cognitive maps, whereas GPT4Scene-style systems [33, 62] provide exter-nally constructed metric or bird’s-eye-view maps. Both reconstruct-then-reason approaches represent the whole scene. Tool-using methods such as Think3D [60] instead let an agent manipulate point clouds or estimate camera an-chors [42]. Think-Capture differs in three coupled design choices: query-conditioned target selection, a common sym-bolic interface for object and camera state, and hierarchical GRPO that trains the VLM to accept or reject imperfect tool evidence. The controlled corruption and matched 2D-box experiments in Sec. 4 evaluate robustness and the value of the symbolic 3D representation.

Meaning of the hierarchical reward. The hierarchy is sample-conditioned: the positive subset contains 1,927 examples and the negative subset contains 1,105 examples, determined by the two screening runs above. Cases in which the screening models agree are not retained. These labels describe screening-time disagreement; question difficulty can vary within either subset. The negative subset captures observed regressions under tool augmentation, which can arise from erroneous geometry or incorrect integration of otherwise useful evidence. Its purpose is to train against such regressions while the positive subset promotes useful tool integration. The +1 / + 2 values specify sample-conditioned raw outcome rewards, and the optimization signal is deter-mined by the total rewards after within-group normalization. Supplementary Sec. 4. Experiment 

4.1. Datasets 

We evaluate Think-Capture on three challenging benchmarks designed to probe 3D spatial reasoning capabilities: • MindCube-Tiny [40] contains 1,050 questions: 600 Among, 250 Around, and 200 Rotation questions. Ac-curacy is computed over the valid evaluated examples in each run after excluding rare invalid evaluation cases; the category counts are nominal split sizes, not necessarily the per-run denominators. • MMSI-Bench [53] requires relating entities across dis-joint frames. The main comparison uses its positional-relation subset; the supplement also reports the full bench-mark. • ViewSpatial-Bench [20] evaluates perspective taking across egocentric and allocentric reference frames. 

4.2. Implementation Details 

We train Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct on the 3,032 disagreement-mined MindCube training examples, with learning rate 

5 × 10 −7 and G = 8 trajectories per prompt. The vision encoder is frozen, and the effective batch size is 16 with gradient accumulation. Training uses four A100 (80 GB) GPUs for approximately 38 hours, stopping at 1,600 training steps (approximately 152 GPU-hours for this training run). Correct positive and negative samples receive +1 and +2 ,respectively, and format compliance receives +0 .5. Sup-plementary Sec. B provides the optimizer, clipping and KL settings, prompts, and resource details. 

Additional training configuration. We use AdamW with a cosine learning-rate schedule and 50 warmup steps. The GRPO clipping parameter is 0.2, and the KL coefficient is 0.04. Training uses BF16 and DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 with the vision encoder frozen, reaching a peak GPU memory usage of 75.6 GB. The reported 152 GPU-hours refer to the 1,600-step GRPO training run. At inference, the toolkit retains at most four frames, while query-conditioned target selection restricts segmentation and symbolic aggregation.

4.4. Generalization and Matched Controls 

Table 3 applies the same acquisition, abstraction, and train-ing recipe to 2B, 4B, and 8B Qwen3-VL backbones. Mind-Cube gains remain within 19.9–21.5 points and full-MMSI gains within 8.8–10.0 points. The similar gains across model sizes suggest that explicit task-conditioned geometry com-plements backbone capacity. This pattern extends the benefit observed in the principal 4B comparison to both smaller and larger models. The matched control in Table 4 tests whether target lo-calization or colored visual prompting alone explains the improvement. Replacing the symbolic state with 2D boxes derived from the same masks removes depth, metric scale, and camera pose while preserving which entities Stage 1 selected. It trails symbolic 3D state by 17.5 points on Mind-Cube, 7.1 on full MMSI, and 4.9 on ViewSpatial. Under these tested input configurations, symbolic 3D state is more effective than 2D highlighting alone.

4.6. Qualitative Analysis 

Figure 3 asks what is behind an observer standing at the pose of image 2. This is not answered by locating what appears farthest away in that image: “behind” is the half-space oppo-site the camera’s facing direction. The baseline follows the image-plane background and selects the sofa. Think-Capture first requests the blue table, walls, sofa, and seating bench. It uses the blue table as the visible facing anchor and the symbolic cross-view relations to place the seating bench on the opposite side of the observer. It therefore infers an object in the blind spot rather than copying a visible background ob-ject. The point cloud and arrows visualize the reconstructed spatial relations; Stage 2 takes the compact symbolic text and original frames as its inputs. The second complete case (Fig. 4) isolates a different failure: the queried object and the reference camera are observed in different views. The baseline recognizes the mirror but narrates it relative to the house layout, not to the photographer in image 2, and consequently reverses front/back. Think-Capture explicitly captures the image 2 camera pose and the mirror geometry. The camera stream fixes the photographer’s forward direction toward the front door; the object stream places the kitchen and mirror to the photographer’s left. Transforming the mirror from the shared world frame into this egocentric frame yields front-left . Together, the two cases show that the method addresses both unseen-space inference and cross-view reference-frame conversion. 

4.7. Joint Reasoning Dynamics 

The positive subset contains samples on which tool augmen-tation repairs a vision-only error, whereas the negative subset contains samples on which it overturns a correct visual an-swer. During GRPO training, positive-sample accuracy rises and stabilizes near 85%, while negative-sample accuracy in-creases from about 30% to above 60%. Accuracy and mean reward eventually oscillate around a high plateau. Improve-ment on both screening subsets, together with the corruption results in Table 2, supports more robust integration of sym-bolic geometry and visual evidence. 4.8. Real-World Deployment 

We deploy Think-Capture-4B as an asynchronous high-level spatial reasoner on an Agibot G1 with the StarVLA plat-form [36] (Fig. 5). At initialization, it captures task-relevant medicines and reference objects, resolves the target from symbolic 3D state, and supplies a low-frequency global way-point; a separate StarVLA policy performs high-frequency arm control. The complete VLM+SAM3+ π3 stack peaks at 21.5 GB on one RTX 4090. In the A100 benchmark, geom-etry takes 3.1 s of the 21.8 s pipeline (14%); direct-answer decoding cuts total latency by over 40% while retaining 53.2% MindCube-Tiny accuracy. This architecture couples low-frequency spatial reasoning with high-frequency arm control; supplementary Sec. D.2 describes the integration. The timing breakdown is 6.8 s for target planning, 3.1 s for geometric capture, and 11.9 s for Stage 2 reasoning. Think-Capture runs asynchronously: after receiving a waypoint, StarVLA continuously closes the visual-action loop while the high-level module is idle or processes the next request. 

Efficiency. Stage 2 decoding is the largest latency term. Direct-answer decoding reduces end-to-end time by more than 40% while retaining 53.2% MindCube-Tiny accu-racy, 1.2 points below full chain-of-thought decoding. The 21.5 GB peak covers the VLM, SAM3, and π3 resident to-gether on one RTX 4090. Supplementary Sec. D.1 details the measurement setup and runtime distribution.

4.10. Limitations and Scope 

Think-Capture depends on the observability and calibration available to its perception tools. A severely occluded target, systematic depth bias, or accumulated pose drift can make a symbolic relation wrong rather than merely uncertain. Hi-erarchical GRPO and the empty-target fallback reduce the effect of such failures but cannot recover geometry that is absent from every view. The current templates discretize continuous direction into linguistic bins suited to benchmark relations and waypoint selection. Fine manipulation addi-tionally requires local shape and contact information at a higher spatial resolution. In the robot deployment, Think-Capture selects task-relevant entities and generates a global waypoint, while the independently trained StarVLA policy handles fast visual feedback, collision-sensitive motion, and grasp execution. The 21.8 s reasoning cycle operates asynchronously from StarVLA’s high-frequency motor-control loop. Training uses disagreement-mined MindCube examples, with zero-shot evaluation on MMSI and ViewSpatial. Broader training scenes, uncertainty-aware symbolic states, and temporally persistent coordinates are promising directions for extending the framework to dynamic environments. 

5. Conclusion 

Think-Capture replaces passive dense fusion with query-conditioned geometry acquisition and a compact symbolic interface. GRPO trains the VLM to combine visual and 3D evidence, with improved robustness to noisy tool outputs in the tested settings. Consistent benchmark, perturbation, scaling, and real-robot results support active acquisition as a practical route to spatially grounded VLMs. The repre-sentation controls favor symbolic 3D state over the tested 2D-box input. The robot study demonstrates practical high-level integration. Future work will extend the interface to uncertainty-calibrated, temporally persistent geometry, en-abling query-conditioned state refresh within the same hier-archical perception-and-control architecture. 8Acknowledgments

D. Efficiency and Deployment 

D.1. Efficiency & Latency Analysis 

We measure latency over all 1,050 MindCube-Tiny samples on one NVIDIA A100 (80 GB). 

D.1.1. Latency Breakdown 

Tool execution accounts for 3.1 s, or about 14% of the 21.8 s end-to-end time. Stage 1 planning (6.8 s) and Stage 2 reason-ing (11.9 s) dominate because both generate text. Targeted segmentation and the four-frame budget keep perception comparatively small. When Stage 2 predicts the final answer without intermediate chain-of-thought tokens, end-to-end latency falls by more than 40% while MindCube-Tiny accu-racy remains 53.2% (versus 54.4% with full reasoning). 

D.1.2. Resource Consumption 

The complete VLM-4B, SAM3, and π3 stack exhibits a peak GPU memory usage of 21.5 GB . This indicates that the full 3D spatial-agent pipeline can be deployed on consumer-grade hardware such as an RTX 4090 with 24 GB VRAM, lowering the deployment barrier compared with systems that require 40 GB or 80 GB accelerators. 

D.2. Real-World Deployment Details 

We integrate Think-Capture-4B with the StarVLA stack [36] on an Agibot G1. At task initialization, Think-Capture re-ceives the robot’s visual observation, identifies the medicines and reference objects needed by the instruction, and invokes SAM3+ π3. The resulting target coordinates and relations are formatted exactly as in benchmark inference. Think-Capture resolves the target and emits a global spatial waypoint at low frequency; a separate StarVLA motor policy converts that waypoint into high-frequency arm commands. This separation keeps VLM generation outside the time-critical control loop. Similar planning-execution separation appears in robot control [61] and autonomous data collection [43]. The four snapshots in the main-paper deployment figure show observation, approach, and grasp execution. The deployment combines high-level semantic grounding with continuous visual-action feedback from StarVLA [16, 36, 59]. Geometry is acquired at initialization, so moving targets and substantial scene changes require re-acquisition. With a 21.8 s reasoning cycle, Think-Capture provides asyn-chronous waypoint updates; faster decoding and state refresh would extend this design to more reactive tasks. 

E. Limitations 

Think-Capture has three main limitations: • Foundation-model errors: Although hierarchical GRPO and the empty-return fallback improve tolerance, severe 

π3 reconstruction or SAM3 segmentation failures can still propagate to the answer. • Symbolic information loss: Axis-aligned boxes and dis-crete relations omit fine shape, object orientation, and contact geometry needed for dexterous manipulation. • Latency and dynamics: VLM generation dominates run-time, and the current robot integration captures geometry at initialization. Reactive deployment will require faster decoding and state refresh. Broader training coverage, explicit uncertainty modeling, and efficient geometric state updates could improve robust-ness in dynamic environments [28, 46–48]. 

Temporal extensions. Extending acquisition to changing scenes calls for tests that separate motion from appearance variation. Follow Your Pose [25] conditions character-video generation on body pose, while Follow-Your-Motion [26] decouples spatial appearance and temporal motion for video motion transfer. These forms of control suggest ways to construct temporal reasoning cases with specified changes in pose or motion. For appearance variation, LiveLight [27] provides interactive 3D lighting control for streaming re-lighting with geometry-guided training, and LiveEdit [41] performs causal streaming video editing with content preser-vation. Together, these directions offer tools for future evalu-ation of when geometric evidence should be refreshed under motion, lighting changes, or visual edits.
