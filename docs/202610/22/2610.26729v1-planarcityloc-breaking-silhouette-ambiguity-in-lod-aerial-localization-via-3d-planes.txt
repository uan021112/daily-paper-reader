Abstract
Aerial visual localization is crucial for autonomous drone
navigation in urban environments where GNSS signals are
often degraded.
Lightweight textureless Level-of-Detail
(LoD) city models provide a geometric map alternative to
textured assets. Existing silhouette-based methods rely on
image-space alignment, which can leave different camera
poses indistinguishable and favor incorrect poses when seg-
mentation errors distort the building outlines. We present
PlanarCityLoc, which moves beyond silhouette overlap by
leveraging the planar geometry of building facades and
roofs. Following geometry-aware initialization, we match
image-side planar patches to LoD model planes and use
the resulting 3D-to-2D correspondences to refine pose with
Perspective-n-Point (PnP). The framework uses off-the-
shelf geometry and segmentation models without dataset-
specific training or fine-tuning. Experiments on UAVD4L-
LoDv2 and Swiss-EPFLv2 achieve state-of-the-art local-
ization recall among the evaluated off-the-shelf LoD-based
methods.
By grounding image-space alignment in 3D
planes, PlanarCityLoc enables more accurate aerial local-
ization with lightweight, textureless city maps.
1. Introduction
Aerial visual localization for Unmanned Aerial Vehicles
(UAVs), defined as estimating the precise 6-Degrees-of-
Freedom (6DoF) camera pose from a single image, is es-
sential for operations in Global Navigation Satellite Sys-
tem (GNSS)-denied urban environments [1, 37].
Cam-
era pose estimates also support geometry-guided annota-
tion of aerial imagery [15] and semantic mapping for au-
tonomous landing [13]. While matching images to textured
3D meshes [31, 39] is established, maintaining these as-
sets globally is expensive and privacy-restricted [23]. Tex-
tureless Level-of-Detail (LoD) city models provide a com-
pact geometric reference without storing facade appear-
ance [12, 26, 40, 46, 47].
One way to bridge the appearance gap between images
and textureless maps is to compare their structural pro-
jections. LoD-Loc [46] follows this approach by predict-
ing building wireframes in the query image and aligning
them with edges projected from the LoD model.
LoD-
Loc v2 [47] instead compares building silhouettes, making
alignment applicable to simpler LoD1 geometry (Fig. 1(a)).
LoD-Loc v3 [26] uses instance masks to distinguish build-
ings, reducing ambiguity and broad score plateaus caused
by merged semantic silhouettes in dense scenes.
These silhouette-based objectives still measure agree-
ment in image space. Distinct camera poses can produce
similar projections, and disjoint masks yield the same zero
intersection-over-union over a range of poses. Segmenta-
tion errors introduce a further difficulty. Missing facades,
displaced boundaries, or merged building instances change
the target silhouette, so an incorrect pose can align better
with the predicted mask than the true pose does.
Con-
sequently, even a distinct score maximum need not coin-
cide with minimum pose error, as illustrated schematically
in Fig. 1(c). Improving pose accuracy therefore calls for
constraints beyond silhouette overlap alone.

Our key observation is that building facades and roofs
provide explicit 3D planar patches within LoD mod-
els. Off-the-shelf vision models [2, 11, 16, 20, 28, 38, 43]
provide image-side geometry and segmentation without
dataset-specific fine-tuning.
Building on these cues, we
propose PlanarCityLoc. Following geometry-aware coarse
initialization, we match image-side planar patches to ren-
dered LoD planes.
This 3D planar grounding uses ren-
dered depth to establish 3D-to-2D correspondences and iter-
atively refines pose with a robust Perspective-n-Point (PnP)
solver [10] (Fig. 1(b)). The resulting geometric constraints
allow refinement beyond an image-space score peak, ad-
dressing the gap between silhouette agreement and accurate
camera pose illustrated in Fig. 1(c).
In summary, our main contributions are:
• We formulate zero-shot LoD-based aerial localization
through explicit plane correspondences.
• We introduce iterative plane association between image
patches and textureless map geometry for robust refine-
ment of the camera pose.
• We evaluate on UAVD4L-LoDv2 and Swiss-EPFLv2,
achieving state-of-the-art localization recall among the
evaluated off-the-shelf LoD-based methods across all
four splits, and analyze the contributions of geometric
scoring and plane-based refinement through ablations.
2. Related Work
Visual localization. Visual localization estimates the 6DoF
camera pose relative to a known map.
Traditional ap-
proaches match local features to a 3D point cloud con-
structed via Structure-from-Motion [24, 31], pairing feature
extractors [4] with robust matchers [8, 9, 30, 33]. Hierar-
chical pipelines [29] first retrieve relevant images before lo-
cal feature matching. MeshLoc [25] localizes directly on
3D meshes, while UAVLoc [39] uses posed reference im-
ages rendered from textured meshes for aerial localization.
Render-and-compare approaches [42] offer another route to
aerial pose estimation. MC-Loc [35] combines pretrained
image features, a renderable scene representation, and parti-
cle filtering for pose refinement without task-specific train-
ing. Maintaining detailed appearance-bearing maps at city
scale remains costly. AnyVisLoc [45], OrthoLoC [5], and
OrthoTrack [6] instead explore localization using overhead
imagery and 2.5D surface maps. Our work focuses on tex-
tureless LoD geometry, which provides a compact reference
without storing facade appearance.
LoD-based localization. LoD-Loc [46] introduced the use
of LoD city models for aerial localization through neu-
ral wireframe alignment. It predicts building edges in the
query image, scores pose hypotheses using projected map
wireframes, and refines the estimate through differentiable
alignment. LoD-Loc v2 [47] shifts from wireframes to se-
mantic building silhouettes, supporting simpler LoD1 mod-
els through coarse pose search and particle-filter refinement.
LoD-Loc v3 [26] further separates buildings using instance
segmentation trained on synthetic aerial imagery. Instance-
level alignment reduces the ambiguity of merged seman-
tic masks in dense scenes, but remains sensitive to missing
instances, incorrect boundaries, and merged or split pre-
dictions. Such errors can displace the overlap maximum
from the true pose, even when the score has a distinct peak
(Fig. 1(c)). Concurrent work, MatchCityLoc [7], explores
an alternative through matching predicted and rendered sur-
face normals. PlanarCityLoc complements silhouette align-
ment with explicit 3D plane correspondences. This ground-
ing enables refinement beyond the image-space score peak,
leveraging pretrained geometry and segmentation models
without dataset-specific training or fine-tuning.
Monocular foundation models.
Extracting structured
planar patches from a single UAV image requires pre-
cise surface geometry and semantic segmentation.
Re-
cently, monocular geometry estimation has experienced
2


a paradigm shift due to large-scale foundation mod-
els [11, 16, 18, 43]. Models such as MoGe-2 [38] can pre-
dict metric-scale 3D point maps and detailed surface nor-
mals from a single image without any retraining.
Simi-
larly, segmentation foundation models like SAM 3 [2] pro-
vide robust zero-shot building masks. The OccuFly bench-
mark [14] further highlights the challenges of applying pre-
trained metric-depth models to aerial imagery across dif-
ferent flight altitudes. We leverage these models to extract
geometric and semantic cues from aerial images.
Plane-based localization. Planes provide structural con-
straints beyond individual keypoints and have been explored
for augmented reality [32]. In indoor localization, PlanaRe-
Loc [44] matches query-image and 3D-map planes through
learned embeddings. PALMS [3] uses observed wall ge-
ometry and floorplans to initialize indoor particle-filter lo-
calization.
Related primitive-based methods include Z-
FLoc [36], which matches lines and circles extracted from
monocular reconstructions to floorplans, and outdoor ap-
proaches that align semantic or geometric primitives across
views [22, 27]. These works establish the value of structural
primitives, while their use for aerial localization against
simplified city models remains less explored. We match fa-
cade and roof planes in LoD models to patches predicted
from a single UAV image.
3. Method
Problem formulation. Given an LoD city model M, a
query image Iq ∈RH×W ×3 with height H and width W,
known intrinsics K, and a coarse GPS/IMU pose prior ξp,
our goal is to estimate the accurate 6-DoF camera pose
ξ∗= (x, y, z, θ, ρ, r), where (x, y, z) is the world-space
camera center and (θ, ρ, r) denotes yaw, pitch, and roll.
Overview. We show the pipeline in Fig. 2. We first extract
geometric cues from the query image and group building
surfaces into planar patches. Geometry-aware coarse ini-
tialization then brings the image and map into approximate
alignment. Building on this estimate, our 3D planar ground-
ing stage matches image patches to visible LoD planes and
iteratively refines the pose through robust PnP. Finally, rank
aggregation combines complementary alignment measures
to select the output pose.

3.3. Geometry-Aware Coarse Initialization
Reliable plane matching first requires approximate align-
ment between the query image and map. We obtain this
initialization by rendering pose hypotheses around the sen-
sor prior ξp and comparing them with the query predictions.
Following the image-space search paradigm of LoD-Loc v2
and LoD-Loc v3 [26, 47], we use cost-volume (CV) search
followed by particle-filter (PF) refinement, augmented with
edge and depth cues. Both stages refine translation and yaw
while keeping pitch and roll fixed at their prior estimates, as
in LoD-Loc v2 [47]. The subsequent plane-based PnP stage
refines all six pose degrees of freedom.

3.4. Iterative Plane-based PnP

image, but image-space agreement can still leave residual
pose ambiguity. We refine this estimate by grounding im-

patch provides a world-space point Xi and a corresponding
image location ui. Together, these 3D-to-2D correspon-
dences constrain the camera pose through PnP, allowing
refinement beyond the image-space score peak.

3.5. Final Pose Selection via Rank Aggregation
The particle-filter pose and the successive PnP estimates
form a candidate set V. A pose with high silhouette overlap
need not have the most accurate geometric alignment, while
prediction noise can lower the overlap of a well-aligned
pose. We therefore combine eight complementary criteria:
composite alignment score, mask recall, mask IoU, edge
alignment, depth correlation, normal error, reprojection er-
ror, and matched-plane centroid distance. The composite
alignment score follows the additive form in Eq. (4), but
uses rendered normal-map edges to also capture internal fa-
cade and roof structure. Final selection balances all eight
criteria instead of maximizing the composite score alone.
Depth correlation measures normalized cross-correlation
between predicted and rendered depth over valid building-
mask overlap. Normal error averages angular differences
over common building support. Reprojection error mea-
sures the mean image-space residual of the fitted inlier cor-
respondences, while centroid distance averages the separa-
tion of matched image and rendered patch centers.
Rank aggregation balances these criteria without directly
combining their different numerical scales. Let rk(ξ) be

4. Experiments
We evaluate PlanarCityLoc against published references
and adapted LoD baselines. Additional results and evalu-
ation details are provided in the supplementary.
4.1. Experiment Settings and Baselines
Datasets. We evaluate on the UAVD4L-LoDv2 and Swiss-
EPFLv2 query splits [26, 47], inherited from UAVD4L-
LoD and Swiss-EPFL [46], respectively. These dataset ex-
tensions introduce LoD1 maps while retaining the origi-
nal queries.
Each query benchmark has two splits: in-
Traj. and out-of-Traj. for UAVD4L-LoDv2, and in-Place
and out-of-Place for Swiss-EPFLv2. Swiss-EPFL imagery
and poses originate from CrossLoc [41]. Our main experi-
ments use LoD3 for UAVD4L-LoDv2 and LoD2 for Swiss-
EPFLv2 unless stated otherwise. The released UAVD4L-
LoDv2 priors are sensor-derived, whereas Swiss-EPFLv2
priors were synthesized from ground truth by the bench-
mark authors [46].
Baselines.
Table 1 includes textured-map methods and
LoD-based methods. We report published references along-
side adapted LoD-Loc evaluations (†).
The adapted evaluations assess localization with oracle
building masks and off-the-shelf segmentation, separately
from published trained configurations. We use rasterized
map renderings with ground-truth-rendered masks for LoD-
Loc v2 and LoD-Loc v3, and additionally evaluate LoD-
Loc v2 with SAM 3 [2] predictions. These variants contrast
ideal masks with general-purpose predictions for localiza-
tion.
Evaluation metrics.
Following prior work [26, 47], we
report median translation and rotation errors, and recall at
2m–2◦, 3m–3◦, and 5m–5◦. A query is recalled when both
errors are strictly below the corresponding thresholds.
4.2. Implementation Details
The main method uses SAM 3 [2] for building masks and
MoGe-2 ViT-S [38] for depth and normals. Plane extraction
uses normal threshold τn = 0.995 and relative depth thresh-
olds τd = 0.05 for image patches and 0.02 for rendered
patches. We discard patches smaller than 50 pixels.
Coarse localization uses two translation-and-yaw grids
followed by medium and fine particle-filter stages, with up
to 15 and 6 iterations. Plane refinement uses up to N = 5
outer iterations. Standard correspondence filtering requires
normal consistency τc = 0.7 and at least six matches, with
a 20 px RANSAC threshold. Strongly fragmented image
patches trigger stricter matching, while insufficient build-
ing coverage skips PnP. Search configurations and match-
ing thresholds were established empirically during devel-
opment and fixed for the reported evaluations.

Quantitative results.
We report the results in Tab. 1.
PlanarCityLoc outperforms the evaluated prior off-the-

and all three thresholds.
Explicit plane correspondences
substantially improve the particle-filter estimates on both
UAVD4L-LoDv2 splits and Swiss-EPFLv2 out-of-Place.
Textured-map UAVLoc remains stronger, benefiting from
richer appearance cues unavailable in textureless geometry.
Swiss-EPFLv2 in-Place is more challenging for plane
refinement, with 2m–2◦recall changing from 24.9% to
20.6%. This split has fewer visible map patches and a larger

than out-of-Place, consistent with less reliable plane associ-
ations. Refinement also produces fewer successful PnP pro-
posals, and candidate selection sometimes favors an inaccu-
rate update. These observations highlight the importance of
sufficiently rich, consistent planar evidence.
Qualitative analysis. Figure 3 shows that different meth-
ods can produce visually plausible alignments while yield-

overlap alone is therefore insufficient to establish an accu-
rate camera pose. The staged examples in Fig. 4 show how
plane correspondences improve upon coarse alignment.

4.5. Limitations
PlanarCityLoc requires a useful coarse pose and sufficient
reliable plane correspondences. Performance depends on
the accuracy of the foundation-model predictions and the
quality of the LoD geometry.
In particular, approximat-
ing complex building shapes with LoD1 can make plane
assignment noisier and reduce localization accuracy. The
multi-stage pipeline combines neural predictions with re-
peated rendering and cannot yet operate onboard in real
time without further optimization. Future work targets fast
initialization and hardware-aware real-time optimization.
5. Conclusion
We presented PlanarCityLoc, which grounds silhouette-
based aerial localization in the 3D planar geometry of
lightweight, textureless city models. By establishing plane
correspondences and refining pose through PnP, our method
achieves state-of-the-art localization recall among the eval-
uated off-the-shelf LoD-based methods without dataset-

Supplementary Material
A. Overview
As demonstrated in the main paper, PlanarCityLoc im-
proves UAV localization through 3D planar constraints that
address the ambiguities of silhouette alignment. Specifi-
cally, we first explain Computational Complexity in Sec. B
and discuss Baseline Evaluation in Sec. C. We then present
Extended Results in Sec. D, along with Extended Ablation
Studies in Sec. E and a Robustness Analysis in Sec. F. Fi-
nally, we consider Limitations and Future Work in Sec. G
and Ethical Considerations in Sec. H.
Experiments use off-the-shelf SAM 3 [2] masks and
MoGe-2 ViT-S [38] geometry unless stated otherwise. We
report joint localization recall and median translation and
rotation errors, following the main paper.
B. Computational Complexity
Our coarse stage follows the image-space search paradigm
of prior work [26, 47]. PlanarCityLoc then introduces ex-
plicit 3D constraints through plane correspondences and
PnP, avoiding another dense search over pose hypotheses.

D. Extended Results
Per-split breakdowns.
We report the complete staged
evaluation in Tab. A1. This evaluation details the perfor-
mance of the cost volume, particle filter, and our plane
matching approach across all benchmark splits. The full-
split results show substantial gains from plane matching
on both UAVD4L-LoDv2 splits and Swiss-EPFLv2 out-
of-Place, supporting the use of 3D constraints beyond sil-
houette agreement. We also report the full-split UAVD4L-
LoDv2 LoD1 ablation and both Swiss-EPFLv2 splits with
LoD2 maps. Swiss-EPFLv2 in-Place is more challenging
for refinement (Tab. A1). Figure A1 compares scene exam-
ples and planar support. At the particle-filter pose, this split
has fewer rendered map patches and a larger predicted-to-
rendered patch-count ratio than out-of-Place. The examples
contrast sparse building coverage and construction areas
with multi-building scenes. Lower map support and greater
patch-count imbalance suggest more challenging image-to-
map plane association.
Plane extraction against LoD primitives. Figure A2 com-
pares LoD planes rendered from the mesh with planes
segmented from predicted normals.
LoD planes follow
the mesh tessellation and remain largely insensitive to the
normal-consistency threshold. In contrast, predicted planes
depend on normal and depth consistency as well as mini-
mum region size. Fine settings can fragment a facade, while
coarse settings can merge distinct surfaces. The examples
illustrate this trade-off in the resulting planar representation.
Failure cases.
Planar refinement requires useful image-
to-map alignment. Figure A3 illustrates initialization fail-
ures. Off-the-shelf segmentation under domain shift can
miss small buildings, misclassify lakes or parking lots, or
produce noisy masks, favoring incorrect cost-volume hy-
potheses.
Disjoint query and rendered masks then sup-
ply no overlap-based distinction between nearby hypothe-
ses, weakening the initialization for plane matching. Be-
yond pure segmentation errors, matching can also degrade
due to weak building visibility, ambiguous facade normals
from repetitive architecture, or temporal mismatches be-
tween outdated LoD geometry (e.g., ground artifacts mod-
eled as buildings) and the true UAV capture. When geo-
metric and mask cues disagree strongly, reliable confidence
estimation is needed to avoid incorrect pose updates.

G. Limitations and Future Work
PlanarCityLoc depends on reliable coarse initialization.
Poor masks or inaccurate GPS priors can lead to incorrect
alignment that planar refinement cannot recover (Fig. A3).
Localization also degrades with incomplete or poorly mod-
eled LoD geometry (Tab. A6). Better upstream predictions
and robust initialization could mitigate these limitations.
Temporal filtering could disambiguate repetitive structures
through motion cues. Efficient initialization and hardware-
aware optimization remain necessary for onboard real-time
operation.
H. Ethical Considerations
Geographic bias and reliability.
Our zero-shot evalua-
tion is geographically constrained to specific urban envi-
ronments, and performance may vary across different ar-
chitectural styles. Lacking hard accuracy guarantees, the
system requires rigorous fallbacks before deployment in
safety-critical autonomous flights.
Privacy and data usage. Public datasets and textureless
maps do not eliminate privacy risks.
Query images and
pretrained models may contain sensitive information, and
downstream use must respect applicable data and model
terms. Swiss-EPFL map data are provided by the Federal
Office of Topography swisstopo.
Dual-use potential. Although intended for civilian inspec-
tion, aerial localization can enable surveillance and requires
safeguards against misuse.
