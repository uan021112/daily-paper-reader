Abstract
We present Register Anything Model (RAM), a unified model for generalizable
3D point cloud registration that robustly estimates 6-DoF transformations across
diverse domains spanning sensors and environments. Existing registration methods
are typically tailored to a single domain, with carefully tuned architectures and
hyperparameters, and thus degrade markedly when applied to novel domains. We
bridge this gap by training a single model on diverse data from indoor, outdoor,
and object domains. A central challenge is the severe geometric discrepancy across
domains, i.e., differences in scene scale, sensor noise, point density, and structural
patterns, which hinders processing with a unified point-based model. To address
this, we introduce a geometry-aware normalization strategy that jointly voxelizes
and normalizes point clouds into a standardized geometric space, adaptively based
on their geometric properties to preserve salient local structures while standardizing
resolution. Built on this normalization, RAM follows a hierarchical coarse-to-fine
paradigm, and employs an efficient geometric transformer that models local ge-
ometric consistency instead of globally over all points. This design mitigates
discretization noise introduced by voxelization, and improves computational ef-
ficiency. Extensive experiments on 12 benchmarks demonstrate state-of-the-art
performance in terms of inlier ratio and registration recall. Notably, our method
outperforms the counterparts trained on each separate domain, showing superior
generalization capability.

Introduction
Point cloud registration is a fundamental but critical task in 3D computer vision and robotics, which
aims to estimate the 6-D rigid transformation aligning the source point cloud to the target. It underpins
numerous applications, including 3D reconstruction, LiDAR SLAM, augmented and mixed reality
(AR/XR), autonomous driving and robotic manipulation. Contemporary approaches usually follow a
two-stage pipeline, which first establishes correspondences between the source and the target point
clouds and then estimates the transformation based on these correspondences.
Recent learning-based methods [1–6] have made remarkable advances in point cloud registration.
However, they are commonly tailored and trained for a specific domain. Different domains could
utilize different sensors, e.g., time-of-flight cameras v.s. LiDAR scanners, or present distinct environ-
ments, e.g., indoor [7, 8], outdoor [9, 10] or object [11, 12] environments, which induces pronounced

domains similar to the training domain and often fail to generalize to novel domains. While recent
work [5, 13] explores adapting a model pretrained on one domain to others, target-domain geometries
remain unseen during training. As a result, domain shifts are only partially mitigated. In practice,
this leads to inaccurate correspondences in novel domains and degraded registration accuracy.
Inspired by the recent success of vision foundation models [14–16], we consider the following
question: can we train a point cloud registration model with strong cross-domain generalizability
by leveraging diverse point cloud data spanning a wide range of domains? Our answer is yes, but
constructing such a unified model is challenging due to the irregular nature of point clouds and the
substantial cross-domain geometric discrepancy, e.g., scene scales, point densities, and structural
patterns. At the micro-architecture level, point operators [17–20] typically encode local geometry
within a fixed search radius. Due to density variability, it is suboptimal to use the same search radius
across different domains. Dynamically adjusting search radius [13] requires operator kernels to
model structures at diverse scales, which can complicate optimization and impair convergence. At
the macro-architecture level, current registration methods [3, 20] adopt domain-specific designs, e.g.,
downsampling factors, to better balance efficacy and efficiency: outdoor domains demand aggressive
downsampling for large spatial coverage and efficiency, while indoor/object scenes benefit from finer
resolutions. This heterogeneity complicates a unified architecture.
In this work, we explore cross-domain training for point cloud registration and propose a unified
registration model, Register Anything Model (RAM). RAM follows a hierarchical coarse-to-fine
paradigm [3, 21], which first extracts coarse-grained superpoint correspondences and then refines
them into fine-grained point correspondences. We address two core questions: (1) how to mitigate
the geometric discrepancy across domains, and (2) how to design an architecture that remains
effective and efficient across diverse conditions. For the first problem, we introduce a geometry-aware
normalization strategy that jointly voxelizes and normalizes point clouds into a domain-agnostic
standardized geometric space, adaptively from their geometric properties. We cast this as a point
cloud quality assessment problem: identify an as-large-as-possible voxel size that preserves salient
local structures, i.e., does not materially alter geometry, and normalize the resulting point clouds
to a reference resolution. This allows for a unified micro architecture despite domain-dependent
densities and scales. For the second problem, we further propose an efficient geometric transformer
that models inter-point-cloud geometric consistency within a limited scope rather than the whole
point cloud. This design alleviates the geometric noises introduced during voxelization and effectively
reduces the computational cost, contributing to a better balance between feature discriminativeness
and computational efficiency.
We train RAM on a collection of 4 registration datasets spanning indoor [7], outdoor [9, 10], and
object [11] benchmarks, and evaluate on 12 benchmarks. Extensive experiments demonstrate strong

generalizability and robustness across diverse domains: RAM outperforms the previous state-of-the-
art BUFFER-X [13] in Registration Recall on novel domains, and achieves an average improvement

• We study the efficacy of cross-domain training for point cloud registration and propose a unified
registration model with strong cross-domain generalizability and robustness.
• We introduce a geometry-aware normalization strategy which jointly voxelizes and normalizes
point clouds into a regularized geometry space, mitigating cross-domain geometric discrepancy.
• We devise an efficient geometric transformer which models inter-point-cloud geometric consis-

• We conduct extensive experiments across a wide range of domains and show that our method
establishes new state-of-the-art performance on 12 benchmarks.

Related Work
2.1
Point Cloud Registration
Point cloud registration plays a critical role in various computer vision and robotics applications.
Most registration methods follow a matching-based pipeline. First, a set of correspondences between
the source and the target point clouds are established by geometric feature matching. Recently,
learning-based methods [1–5, 19–24] have made remarkable advances by learning discriminative
geometric features in a data-driven manner. Early methods [1, 2, 25] usually first detect a set of
keypoints and then match them based on their features. However, as keypoint detection is an ill-posed
problem, it suffers from low repeatability and thus harms the registration performance. Recent
methods [3–5, 20, 21] bypass keypoint detection by employing a hierarchical registration framework,
which first extracts coarse-grained superpoint or patch correspondences and then refine them to
fine-grained point correspondences. And some methods [3, 5, 19, 20, 22] design rotation-invariant or
rotation-equivariant operators to improve the robustness to large rotations. After correspondences are
extracted, the rigid transformation can be estimated by robust estimators such as RANSAC [26–28]
and learning-based estimators [29, 30]. There are also end-to-end methods which directly regress
and optimize the transformation [6, 31–33]. However, they are vulnerable to complex geometric
structures and severe occlusion, which limits their application in real-world applications.
2.2
Generalizable Point Cloud Registration
Although recent learning-based methods have made remarkable progress, they usually suffer from
weak generalizability across different domains due to their significant geometric discrepancy, such
as scene scales, point density, noise distributions and structural patterns. A model trained on
indoor datasets usually has difficulty in registering outdoor point clouds, and vice versa. Recent
methods [5, 13] have studied to adapt a registration model pretrained on one domain to more domains.
BUFFER-X [13] adaptively selects the voxel size based on the sphericity of point clouds and the
search radius by considering the point density, which generalizes the BUFFER [5] model trained on
3DMatch to outdoor scenes. However, due to the lack of awareness of domain-specific geometries
during training, it still suffers from inaccurate correspondences in novel domains, which leads to
suboptimal registration in hard cases. Besides, it still needs specific tuning for different domains, e.g.,
inlier thresholds for outlier rejection and RANSAC hyperparameters, which limits its application in
real-world scenarios. In this work, we bridge this gap by constructing a unified registration model by
cross-domain training, which achieves strong generalizability and robustness.
2.3
Cross-source Point Cloud Registration
Cross-source registration [34–39] is another closely related but distinct problem to cross-domain
registration. Both must handle substantial variations in point density, geometric scale, sensor noise,
and sampling patterns. Cross-source registration aims to align two point clouds acquired from
different sources or sensing modalities, such as Kinect, LiDAR, and structure-from-motion systems.
In contrast, cross-domain registration aims to develop a unified model that can register point-cloud
pairs across different environments or sensors. In this work, the two point clouds within each pair are
generally acquired from the same source, while the source or environment may vary across pairs. We
discuss the effectiveness of our method in cross-source registration in the supplementary material.

Previous methods [2, 3, 5, 20, 21] assume that point clouds to register share the training domain,

a unified point cloud registration model on data from various domains. To address cross-domain
geometric discrepancy, we first design a geometry-aware normalization module which jointly vox-

RAM then employs a hierarchical registration pipeline [3, 20, 21], which first extracts superpoint
correspondences and then refine them into dense point correspondences (Sec. 3.3). Finally, the rigid
transformation is estimated by LGR [3]. Fig. 2 illustrates the overall pipeline.

3.2
Geometry-aware Normalization
A unified registration model must handle the geometric discrepancy among point clouds from different
domains, e.g., in scene scale and point density. For instance, outdoor LiDAR scans are sparse and
long-range (typically ∼100m), while indoor point clouds are denser and short-range (typically <10m).
Modern point operators [1, 4, 5, 17–20, 40] encode local geometry within a fixed search radius, which
is problematic across domains: a small radius on large-scale scenes yields insufficient receptive fields,
whereas a large radius on small-scale scenes oversmooths local details.
To address this issue, we design a geometry-aware normalization module to jointly voxelize and
normalize the point clouds into a domain-agnostic standardized geometric space with consistent
scales and densities, enabling effective processing with a unified search radius. Our key motivation is
to adaptively select a voxel size vraw per point cloud pair according to their geometric properties, and
then align it with a reference vref by rescaling pnorm = vref/vraw · p. We formulate the selection of vraw
as a point cloud quality assessment problem, and iteratively seek for an as-large-as-possible voxel
size whose induced geometric change remains tolerable.
Given raw point clouds P and Q, we initialize v(0) = ϕ(min(d(P), d(Q))), where d(·) is the
diagonal length of the bounding box, and ϕ(·) is a monotonically increasing function (see the
appendix for the definition). In t-th iteration, we voxelize P with v(t) to obtain P′, and sample a
set of seeds {si} from P′ via furthest point sampling. For each seed si, we collect the local patches
Ni and N ′
i within the radius of r = λ · v(t) in P and P′, respectively, where λ is a scaling factor.
Inspired by [41], we compute the eigenvalues λ1 ≥λ2 ≥λ3 of the covariance matrix of each patch
and derive three geometric properties, i.e., linearity g1, planarity g2 and sphericity g3:
g1 = (λ1 −λ2)/λ1,
g2 = (λ2 −λ3)/λ1,
g3 = λ3/λ1.
(2)
We define the geometric error ei of si by the maximum relative error on linearity and planarity
between Ni and N ′
i. The geometric error e(P) between P and P′ is the median geometric error over
the half of seeds with the largest sphericity in P. The same computation goes for Q. Our insight
behind this design is that oversmoothing primarily alters linearity and planarity, while sphericity
serves as an indicator of local geometric complexity, filtering out geometry-less patches which are
less sensitive to oversmoothing. If e(P) and e(Q) are both below a tolerance τ, we enlarge the voxel
size by v(t+1) = (1 + α)v(t) and repeat the above process until e ≥τ or by up to T times.

Superpoint matching.
Geometric transformer [3] improves superpoint feature discriminativeness
by encoding inter-point-cloud global geometric consistency [3, 4, 20, 42], but their geometric structure
embedding has memory complexity O(N 2d), which is prohibitive for large point clouds and typically
necessitates domain-specific downsampling [3, 4, 20]. Moreover, voxelization can destabilize the

3.4
Loss Functions
RAM is trained with three loss functions: (1) an overlap-aware circle loss [3] Loc to supervise the
superpoint features, (2) a negative log-likelihood loss Lnll to supervise the confidence matrices,
and (3) a binary cross-entropy loss Lbce to supervise the overlapping scores. The overall loss is
L = λocLoc + λnllLnll + λbceLbce.

Metrics.
Following [3, 13], we report the results on 4 metrics: (1) Inlier Ratio (IR), the percentage
of correspondences whose residuals are below a certain threshold under the ground-truth transfor-
mation. (2) Relative Rotation Error (RRE), the geodesic distance between the estimated and the
ground-truth rotations. (3) Relative Translation Error (RTE), the ℓ2-distance between the estimated
and the ground-truth translations. (4) Registration Recall (RR), the percentage of successfully regis-
tered pairs. A pair is successful if its RMSE is below 0.2m (3DMatch/3DLoMatch) or if both RRE
and RTE are below certain thresholds (other benchmarks). We adopt stricter RRE and RTE thresholds
to enable more rigorous performance assessment. Please refer to the appendix for more details.

outperforms all baselines on both benchmarks, e.g., by 6.6 pp in Inlier Ratio on KITTI, indicating
that cross-domain training yields more discriminative features and generates more accurate corre-
spondences. On nuScenes, despite a lower Inlier Ratio, our method achieves substantially higher
Registration Recall, demonstrating stronger robustness to difficult cases. Additionally, we report
results for RAM trained without nuScenes (marked by ⋄). This variant achieves higher Registration
Recall than the baselines on most benchmarks, except MIT, further validating our method. Because
both nuScenes and MIT employ LiDAR sensors with fewer beams than KITTI, omitting nuScenes
during training slightly degrades performance relative to the model trained with nuScenes.
Quantitative results on unseen benchmarks.
We further assess the generalizability on 4 unseen
outdoor benchmarks, i.e., ETH, WOD, MIT and Oxford. As these benchmarks all adopts spinning
LiDAR scanners, most baselines except PARENet generalize reasonably well. RAM achieves the
best performance on all metrics across all benchmarks, which again underscoring the advantage of
cross-domain training for robust generalizability. Note that the outdoor-only RAM (marked by ⋆)
trained on KITTI and nuScenes outperforms the hybrid model without nuScenes on most benchmarks,
suggesting that domain-relevant training data remain important for performance within each domain.
4.4
Experiments on Object Benchmarks
We compare with 5 baselines: RPM-Net [6], Predator [2], CoFiNet [21], GeoTrans [3], and BUFFER-
X [5], where the first 4 baselines are trained on ModelNet, while BUFFER-X uses the weights
pretrained on 3DMatch and KITTI. As shown in Tab. 3, our method consistently outperforms all
object baselines by a large margin on both ModelNet and ShapeNet. We notice that GeoTrans attains
better results in Inlier Ratio. This is because object point clouds are usually small, so the global
geometric structure embedding is less affected by the voxelization noises and contributes to better
feature representation in most cases. However, thanks to the rich geometric patterns from diverse
domains, RAM extracts more accurate correspondences in the harder cases, thus contributing to
better Registration Recall. Another important observation is that both variants of BUFFER-X fail
to achieve reasonable results on the object registration benchmarks, which unveils the significant
difficulty of generalization from scene registration to object registration due to the severe geometric
discrepancy. In contrast, benefiting from the diverse data from various domains during training, our
method achieves strong generalizability across a wide range of domains.

4.5
Ablation Studies
At last, we conduct extensive ablation studies to better understand the design choices of RAM on the
7 unseen benchmarks, i.e., ScanNet++, TIERS, ETH, WOD, MIT, Oxford and ShapeNet.
Cross-domain training.
We first investigate how cross-domain training affects the generalizability,
as shown in Tab. 4 (a). We compare 4 variants of RAM, respectively trained on (1) hybrid data, (2)
indoor data, (3) outdoor data, and (4) object data. For Registration Recall, the indoor model performs
quite well on most outdoor benchmarks except MIT. The outdoor model performs well on TIERS
but fails to generalize to ScanNet++. Both models fail on the object benchmark, while the object
model also fails on the scene-level benchmarks. For Inlier Ratio, all models suffer from significant
performance drop on different domains. The hybrid model achieves the best performance on all
benchmarks, demonstrating the efficacy of cross-domain training.
Point matching.
We then study the efficacy of the designs in the point matching stage in Tab. 4 (b).
We compare 3 variants of RAM: (1) the full model, (2) the model without the overlap scores, and
(3) the model further without the geometric transformer. By predicting the overlapping scores,
the model suppresses the non-overlapping points during matching and thus yields more accurate
correspondences. And removing the transformer causes a significant performance degradation, further
confirming the importance of the inter-point-cloud information exchange in point matching.
Geometry-aware normalization.
We evaluate the efficacy of our geometry-aware normalization
by comparing with the model with static voxel sizes in Tab. 4 (c). Following [3, 5], we set the voxel
size to 2.5cm for indoor and object benchmarks, and 30cm for outdoor benchmarks. Both models
use the same weights trained on static voxel sizes. The model with geometry-aware normalization
consistently outperforms the static counterpart on most benchmarks. Note that the benefit of our
design is not limited to the average improvement. It eliminates the need to manually select a domain-
specific voxel size at inference time. This is particularly important in practical applications where an
agent may frequently transition between environments with substantially different geometric scales.

5
Conclusion
In this paper, we investigated cross-domain training for point cloud registration and introduced a uni-
fied registration model, RAM. Extensive experiments across a broad set of benchmarks demonstrate
that RAM delivers strong cross-domain generalizability and robustness, achieving state-of-the-art
performance in diverse indoor, outdoor, and object registration scenarios.
Despite these advances, RAM can be improved in several respects. First, the KPConv-based backbone
is still rotation-variant. Although RAM already shows promising robustness to large rotations as
demonstrated in the appendix, it may still struggle under extreme scenarios. We assessed recent
rotation-equivariant backbones, but their memory footprint currently prevents processing large-scale
point clouds within a unified architecture. Second, RAM may not scale to extremely large point
clouds, e.g., urban-scale UAV captures. A practical remedy is to partition scenes into manageable
tiles and process them separately, potentially with overlap and streaming. Third, we observe that
distant outliers in point clouds can inflate the initial voxel size and oversmooth geometry. This could
be mitigated by integrating a separate point cloud outlier removal step.
Beyond registration, RAM can serve as a general-purpose model for point cloud processing, and we
hope it will help catalyze future research toward point cloud foundation models.

Training and testing.
Our model is implemented and trained with an Intel i7-12700K CPU and
an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. We use AdamW optimizer to train all models for 3M iterations, with
the batch size of 1 and the weight decay of 10−4. We warm up the training for 25K iterations, with
the learning rate linearly increasing from 10−5 to 10−4. The learning rate then decreases to 10−5
with cosine annealing. We checkpoint the model every 50K iterations and use the averaged model
of the last 30 checkpoints for evaluation. Our model is trained on the hybrid data from 3DMatch,
KITTI, nuScenes and ModelNet. For simplicity, we do not balance the samples from each dataset
when constructing the training data. The same augmentations as [3] are used in the experiments.
Following [20], we employ the random crop augmentation on 3DMatch. And we use the same
augmentations as KITTI on nuScenes. We do not use geometry-aware normalization during training
for efficiency, and set the voxel size to 0.025m for 3DMatch/ModelNet, and 0.3m for KITTI and
nuScenes. To compensate, we adopt extra random scaling augmentation ranging from [0.8, 1.2].
A.2
Benchmarks
3DMatch.
3DMatch [7] is a widely used indoor point cloud registration dataset, which consists
of 62 scenes captured by RGB-D sensors. We follow the standard split of 46 scenes for training,
8 for validation and 8 for testing. We use the same approach as [2] to generate point cloud pairs.
During testing, the overlap ratios of the point cloud pairs in 3DMatch are above 30%, and those in
3DLoMatch are between 10% and 30%. There are 20641 pairs for training, 1331 for validation, 1623
and 1781 for testing on 3DMatch and 3DLoMatch, respectively.
ScanNet++.
ScanNet++ [47] is a large-scale 3D reconstruction dataset. We use the depth images
captured using an iPhone 13 Pro. We use the same approach as [13] to process the dataset, resulting
in 2050 point cloud pairs for testing.
TIERS.
TIERS [48] is multi-modal SLAM dataset captured by various types of LiDAR scanners.
Following [13], we use the point clouds captured by Velodyne VLP-16, Ouster OS1-64, and Ouster

ETH.
ETH [49] contains 4 outdoor scenes captured by a Hokuyo UTM-30LX LiDAR scanner.
We follow the settings in [5, 13], which contains 713 point cloud pairs for testing.

model, we uniformly sample 8192 points to produce the point cloud. And we employ the same data
augmentation as ModelNet to generate the testing pairs.

Metrics
Following common practice [2, 3, 13, 20], we use different methods to compute the Registration
Recall for different benchmarks. For 3DMatch and 3DLoMatch, a point cloud pair is considered
successfully registered if its registration error is below 0.2m. For other benchmarks, a registration is
successful if its relative rotation error is below τrre and its relative translation error is below τrte. We
use stricter thresholds than BUFFER-X [13] to better evaluate the performance of different methods.

Volume of training data.
As the hybrid data contains more training samples (approximately
20K pairs from 3DMatch, 1.3K pairs from KITTI, 20K pairs from nuScenes, and 4K pairs from
ModelNet), we then extend the training of the indoor-only RAM to 120 epochs, which results in
approximately the same total number of sample exposures as the hybrid model while retaining the
same architecture and optimization settings. As shown in Tab. 8, even after matching the total training
exposure, the hybrid model consistently outperforms the indoor-only model on both 3DMatch and
3DLoMatch. These results indicate that the gains mainly come from the cross-domain training, rather
than solely the increase of total training samples.
Rotation invariance.
We further evaluate the robustness of RAM to large rotations on 3DMatch
and 3DLoMatch. Following [4, 20], we apply random rotations sampled from U(−π, π). We denote
the original setting as standard and the large-rotation setting as rotated. We report RR by directly
computing registration RMSE instead of estimating from covariance matrices [7], so the RR values
are slightly different those reported in the main paper. For a fair comparison, we evaluate both the
standard and rotated settings using the same protocol.
As shown in Tab. 9, PARENet remains stable under large rotations because of its rotation-equivariant
design. RAM experiences larger, but still limited, degradation: Inlier Ratio decreases by 0.8 and 1.0
points on 3DMatch and 3DLoMatch, respectively, while Registration Recall decreases by 0.7 and 2.7
points. Importantly, RAM retains strong absolute performance under large rotations and continues to
outperform PARENet in Inlier Ratio on both benchmarks. We attribute this robustness to two factors.
First, we apply rotation augmentation during training, which improves the efficacy of the KPConv
backbone under large rotations. Second, RAM encodes geometric consistency during both superpoint
matching and point matching, further mitigating the impact of the rotation-variant backbone.
Cross-source registration.
We also evaluate the generalization ability of RAM to cross-source
registration on the 3DCSR benchmark [37], following the preprocessing protocols of [38, 39]. As
in Tab. 10, although RAM is not trained specifically for cross-source registration, it substantially
outperforms GeoTransformer on both subsets, especially on the more challenging LiDAR subset.
These results indicate that the cross-domain training strategy also provides meaningful generalization
benefits under cross-source shifts.
However, RAM remains behind Cross-PCR, which is specifically designed for cross-source reg-
istration. We attribute part of this gap to the sensitivity of our current encoder to severe density
and sampling discrepancies between heterogeneous sources. Extending RAM with cross-source
registration models may further improve its robustness to heterogeneous modalities. However, this is
beyond the scope of this work and we will leave it as future work.

A.5
Discussions
Trade-off between generalization and per-domain accuracy.
To examine the influence of cross-
domain training on per-domain accuracy, Tab. 4 (a) of the main paper compares the hybrid model with
domain-specific models using the same architecture and optimization protocol. The hybrid model
consistently outperforms the corresponding domain-specific models across the evaluated benchmarks.
These results indicate that, under our training design, cross-domain training does not introduce a
systematic trade-off between generalization and per-domain accuracy.
17


At the same time, domain-relevant training data remain important for performance within each domain.
As shown in Tab. 2, the complete hybrid model achieves the best overall performance, showing that
cross-domain diversity is beneficial rather than detrimental. However, removing nuScenes (marked
by ⋄) leads to a performance decrease, particularly on MIT, while the outdoor-only model (marked by
star) partially recovers this loss, improving Inlier Ratio from 62.5 to 72.3 and RR from 97.4 to 98.7.
It also matches the hybrid model without nuScenes on WOD.
These results suggest that cross-domain and domain-relevant data play complementary roles: cross-
domain data expose the model to more diverse geometric patterns and improve broad generalization,
whereas data from the target domain provide important domain-specific structures and sampling
characteristics.
Effect of LiDAR sparsity on low-overlap registration.
We believe that the improvement on
3DLoMatch primarily arises from the greater geometric diversity introduced by cross-domain training,
rather than simply from a larger number of training-sample exposures. As shown in Tab. 8, extending
the training of the indoor-only model to approximately match the total sample exposure of the hybrid
model does not close the performance gap on 3DLoMatch. The hybrid model still improves Inlier
Ratio from 50.3 to 53.7 and Registration Recall from 79.2 to 81.1.
Although both sparsity and low overlap lead to incomplete observations, they actually impose distinct
difficulties on registration: sparsity weakens the local geometric cues within each point cloud, whereas
low overlap reduces the shared structure between the pair and increases correspondence ambiguity in
non-overlapping regions. Moreover, most outdoor training pairs still have relatively high overlap.
Therefore, introducing sparse LiDAR data does not directly expose the model to the same challenge
as low-overlap registration.
Instead, the sparse and incomplete observations in LiDAR data enrich the geometric and sampling
patterns during training. This diversity may enhances feature discriminativeness and improves the
model’s robustness to partial observations, therefore benefiting low-overlap registration indirectly.
A.6
Broader Impacts
This work advances robust cross-domain 3D point cloud registration, enabling accurate spatial
alignment across diverse sensors without extensive per-domain fine-tuning or re-training. It can
act as a LiDAR odometry component in 3D vision systems and benefit broad applications, such
as autonomous navigation, augmented and virtual reality, digital twins, construction monitoring,
cultural heritage preservation, etc. Another potential impact is that this work unveils the efficacy of
cross-domain training in point cloud processing tasks. The geometry-aware normalization enables
processing point clouds from diverse domains in a unified model, which could act as a fundamental
component towards point cloud foundation models.
