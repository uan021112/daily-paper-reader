## Abstract 

Digital terrain maps (DTMs) are used for landing site se-lection, terrain-relative navigation, and geological analysis in planetary exploration. Existing state-of-practice recon-struction techniques for creating DTMs from satellite im-agery use multi-view stereo, requiring complex pipelines for dealing with camera error, changing illumination, and im-ages with limited disparity. This work introduces Orbital Terrain Networks (OrTeN), a DTM construction method that uses neural inverse rendering to jointly optimize a tex-tured height map and orbital camera corrections from line-scan satellite images. OrTeN operates directly on cali-brated line-scan images with pixel values expressed as radi-ance factor (I/F) and corrects both broad trajectory pose in-consistencies and high-frequency pointing jitter jointly with surface reconstruction during training. Surface-projected patch normalized cross-correlation (NCC) enforces consis-tency between observations of the same terrain, refining an initial rough reconstruction. A semi-empirical reflectance model and shadow rendering are used to fit surface ap-pearance from multi-date imagery with differing solar il-lumination. OrTeN requires only calibrated line-scan im-ages and initial camera poses and does not use any struc-tural priors including ranging/depth measurements or ini-tial rough DTMs. OrTeN is evaluated on four planetary im-agery datasets. Aligned terrain RMSEs are 2.35 m at Jezero Crater, 2.82 m at Gale Crater, 16.18 m at Olympus Mons, and 0.43 m at McGetchin Crater. 

1arXiv:2610.24075v1 [cs.CV] 16 Oct 2026 1. Introduction 

Digital terrain maps (DTMs) are an important supporting data product in space exploration. Accurate DTMs enable landing site selection and terrain-relative navigation (TRN) during entry, descent, and landing for modern autonomous space exploration missions such as Mars 2020 [11, 21]. They also provide the topographic context needed to inter-pret planetary landforms and their geological history [29]. Current pipelines for orbital DTM production used in the space science community rely on classical multi-view stereo (MVS) techniques to infer terrain structure—features are detected and matched between radiometrically cali-brated images, bundle adjustment (BA) is performed to re-fine camera poses, and stereo correspondences are trian-gulated to produce a 3D terrain model [7, 22]. MVS can produce high-quality maps suitable for mission operations, but a number of challenges inherent to both MVS itself and its application to satellite datasets complicate the process. These challenges include the difficulty of getting reliable feature matches in the presence of shadows or low-texture regions, the line-scan geometry of most satellite images not admitting the epipolar constraints assumed by traditional MVS, and transients like dust storms or seasonal changes that alter the appearance of the terrain. Correcting for these effects can require labor-intensive preprocessing and careful manual intervention, making MVS pipelines cumbersome to use. Neural inverse graphics provides a methodology for scene reconstruction that can address many of the chal-lenges faced by classical MVS pipelines. For instance, satellite adaptations of neural radiance fields (NeRFs) model changing illumination and acquisition appearance while recovering scene geometry [15, 26, 27]. How-ever, full 3D volumetric representations admit degenerate solutions—for instance, all scene density concentrated in front of cameras, explaining the observed images without recovering the true geometry—which can be particularly problematic when working with low-disparity orbital im-agery where depth cues are weak. While structural priors can help mitigate these degeneracies [36, 38], these mea-surements are not always available or reliable. OrTeN mitigates the shortcomings of classical MVS and volumetric neural reconstruction by optimizing a textured height map directly. Critically, OrTeN requires no struc-tural priors to achieve accurate terrain reconstruction from images and approximate camera poses. It builds on Neu-ral Terrain Maps (NTM) [8], adding direct bundle adjust-ment of line-scan camera extrinsics jointly with training, a semi-empirical photometric model, and geometric shadow modeling. The contributions of this work are: 1. A pipeline for creating accurate DTMs through differen-tiable volume rendering of textured height maps includ-ing a first-order approximation of distance to the height map along the ray to improve performance at oblique an-gles. 2. Joint terrain and line-scan camera optimization, includ-ing smooth trajectory adjustment and high-frequency jit-ter removal, and a semi-empirical, spatially varying pho-tometric model including geometric shadow modeling with an image-space prior. 3. An evaluation on four Mars and Moon scenes with varied relief and illumination, using the Context Cam-era (CTX) and Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) Narrow Angle Camera (NAC). OrTeN achieves aligned height errors below the ground sampling distance (GSD) in three scenes and improves on the tested Ames Stereo Pipeline (ASP) reconstruction in those scenes. 

## 2. Related Work 

DTM creation with MVS. A number of well-established pipelines exist for creating DTMs from orbital imagery us-ing MVS, including the Ames Stereo Pipeline (ASP), satel-lite stereo pipeline (s2p), and COLMAP [7, 13, 33, 34]. All of these methods use feature extraction and matching to constrain camera alignment before performing dense stereo matching to triangulate terrain. Orbital imagery is often taken by linescan cameras, and therefore integration into standard MVS pipelines requires particular care because each detector line is acquired at a different pose. This pre-cludes the standard epipolar geometry assumptions used in perspective-camera MVS to make searching for correspon-dences straightforward. Linescan epipolar loci are generally curved, so the global rectification used for ideal perspective cameras must be replaced by local or sensor-specific con-structions [14, 19]. ASP can also refine time-varying cam-era states to correct high-frequency jitter [4]. Illumination changes, common in multi-date satellite data acquisitions, create an additional challenge for tradi-tional MVS approaches. Shadows and surface reflectance can change between acquisitions even when terrain is static and make feature selection and matching more dif-ficult. Therefore, masking shadows and other illumination-dependent artifacts is often necessary to improve the robust-ness of MVS matching. Shadows can also provide impor-tant geometric information which can be exploited in re-construction, and shape-from-shading methods exploit this to further refine existing DTMs [3]. OrTeN performs tra-jectory bundle adjustment, including jitter removal, jointly with training, and uses a semi-empirical photometric model with explicit geometric shadow handling to improve terrain reconstruction. 

Neural reconstruction of orbital imagery. S-NeRF in-troduced illumination-aware neural rendering for satellite photogrammetry, and Sat-NeRF added rational polynomial 2coefficient (RPC) camera support and transient appearance modeling. EO-NeRF built on these previous methods to establish a baseline framework for general-purpose scenes on Earth by incorporating geometrically consistent shadows with learned radiometric and camera corrections and tran-sient appearance embeddings [15, 26, 27]. Season-NeRF separated seasonal appearance from Sun and viewing geom-etry [17], while a semi-empirical bidirectional reflectance distribution function (BRDF) was incorporated into BRDF-NeRF [39]. Other methods address the geometry directly through depth supervision, surface constraints, or gravity-aligned and coarse-to-fine regularization [31, 36, 38]. Gaus-sian splatting for satellite imagery has also emerged as an alternative to methods utilizing volume rendering, offering efficient, high-quality neural scene representations [2, 9]. More recent works have begun to focus on large-scale terrain reconstruction using neural inverse graphics. Both NTM and LNEM introduce direct rendering of linescan im-agery for terrain reconstruction [8, 23]. For descent im-agery, de Almeida et al . incorporate a neural height field into reconstruction from wide-angle cameras [12]. OrTeN eschews structural priors and retains the explicitly param-eterized terrain representation, as is done in NTM, and adds ray-distance-based opacity and improved regulariza-tion with normalized cross-correlation (NCC). Its coarse-to-fine optimization follows the principle established by BARF: low-frequency structure should stabilize camera alignment before fine appearance is exposed [24].

SSIM uses the same range and 11 × 11 Gaussian windows ( σ =1.5 pixels), averaged over valid window centers. 

4.3. Training details 

All training runs use a single seed, are limited to 15,000 iterations, and use a batch size of 8,192 rays. The same configuration for OrTeN is used across all four scenes. This includes enabling trajectory BA, jitter adjustment, and the detected-shadow prior with weight 0.01. The roughing stage iteration cap is set to 8,000 updates. Comparisons with depth-supervised EO-NeRF [27] and ASP [7] are reported. EO-NeRF uses the same camera-derived scene bounds as OrTeN, 8,192 rays per update, 128 depth samples per ray, and fixed cameras. Its depth tar-gets come from the unaligned ASP stereo mosaics, with-out reference-terrain supervision or registration. ASP uses image-based BA, dense stereo, and jitter refinement an-chored to its own stereo terrain estimates. The same ASP pipeline is used for all scenes. Mean setup-plus-training times are 52.7/41.2 minutes for OrTeN on NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation/RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs, respectively. These include periodic evaluation and checkpointing, exclude final evaluation, and average different scene/configuration mixes across GPUs. ## 5. Conclusion 

OrTeN jointly reconstructs terrain and refines line-scan cameras from calibrated orbital images without depth su-pervision or stereo initialization. Across four Mars and Moon scenes, it achieves sub-GSD aligned terrain RMSE in three and improves on the tested ASP reconstruction in those same three scenes. Comparison with EO-NeRF with depth supervision also demonstrates that structural priors are not sufficient to achieve accurate terrain reconstruction efficiently. However, a number of limitations remain. Without struc-tural priors such as ranging measurements or known con-trol points, absolute geolocation is not possible. Further-more, it is possible that integrating structural priors into Or-TeN could improve reconstruction accuracy and efficiency and should be tested. While the height map representation is an effective structural prior for the datasets considered here, when detail becomes sufficient, this representation be-comes more limiting. For instance, height maps cannot rep-resent overhangs or complex 3D structures like caves. Fu-ture work should work on mitigating these shortcomings to allow ground level imagery to be integrated into the terrain reconstruction process.
