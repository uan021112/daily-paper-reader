ABSTRACT
We introduce avatar referring segmentation, which selects regions of animatable
Gaussian avatars from language queries. A query can also specify a pose or motion
to select matching intervals. This lets users interact with avatars for local editing
and motion inspection. However, making these selections with existing language
Gaussian splatting methods poses two challenges: adjacent parts and garments
are hard to separate and may be referred to indirectly (region ambiguity), while
fine-grained motions can look alike in RGB (motion ambiguity). We address these
challenges with HumanSkill-4D, a training-free framework that shares a multi-
modal large language model (MLLM) and geometric operators across avatars. For
each avatar, a semantic memory links body-part and garment categories to persis-
tent Gaussian regions and appearance descriptions. The MLLM uses category
names and descriptions to select categories by appearance, relation, or function.
To find matching intervals, it composes operators into programs that check query
conditions on driving joint trajectories stored in a motion memory. These cate-
gory choices and operator outputs make selections traceable, while composing or
adding operators supports new motion conditions. To evaluate this task, we intro-
duce three human-annotated benchmarks and show that HumanSkill-4D outper-
forms the baselines in part segmentation and 3D and 4D referring segmentation,
with compact memories and only minutes of setup per avatar.

INTRODUCTION
Gaussian avatars reconstruct a person’s geometry and appearance and animate the result using a
driving pose sequence (Qian et al., 2024; Kocabas et al., 2024). In applications such as AR/VR
and sports (Hu et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025), users may need to edit an avatar’s appearance or
inspect its motion. Clothing transfer and appearance editing (Lin et al., 2024; Ho et al., 2023), for
example, require selecting the body part or garment to modify (Where). Inspecting that region during
a particular pose or motion also requires finding the intervals in which that pose or motion occurs
(When). A language query can describe both, but the avatar has no part or garment labels on its
Gaussians and no motion labels on its frames. We therefore introduce avatar referring segmentation:
a 3D query selects the described region (Fig. 1(a)), while a 4D query also selects the intervals that
match the specified pose or motion (Fig. 1(b)).
In reconstructed scenes, language Gaussian splatting already selects regions and relevant time inter-
vals. In 3D, these methods attach image-language features to Gaussians and select regions whose
features match a query (Qiu et al., 2024). These features are learned per scene as a language field
in LangSplat (Qin et al., 2024) or registered directly in Dr. Splat (Kim et al., 2025), whereas Refer-
Splat (He et al., 2025) learns per-scene referring features from annotated expressions. To also deter-
mine when a query is relevant, 4-LEGS (Fiebelman et al., 2025) grounds video-text features in dy-
namic Gaussians, while 4D LangSplat (Li et al., 2025) learns a temporal language field from captions
generated by a multimodal large language model (MLLM). For humans, OpenHuman3D (Suzuki
et al., 2025a) segments body parts and garments with a CLIP image encoder fine-tuned on human
parsing data, and OpenHuman4D (Suzuki et al., 2025b) tracks part masks over time.
Despite these advances, enabling language querying on Gaussian avatars presents two challenges.
The first is region ambiguity. Human parts and garments lie close together and often overlap. As the
pose or viewpoint changes, different boundaries become visible. Feature-matching methods (Qin
et al., 2024) rely on class-agnostic SAM masks (Kirillov et al., 2023), which do not necessarily fol-
low these boundaries, and on generic CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) features, which poorly distinguish
body parts and garments (Suzuki et al., 2025a). Replacing the SAM masks with human-parser masks
improves the overall accuracy of LangSplat and Dr. Splat, but leaves their CLIP-based matching un-
changed. Queries may also refer to a part or garment indirectly, through its relation to another region
or its function, and CLIP-based matching remains unreliable for such references in our evaluation
(Tab. 2). The second is motion ambiguity. Even after the target region is identified, locating the in-
tervals of a fine-grained pose or motion remains difficult (e.g., “Select the upper clothing while both
upper arms lift outward until they are parallel to the ground” in Fig. 1(b)). Mask tracking (Suzuki
et al., 2025b) keeps the clothing selected over time but does not determine when the arms meet this
condition. RGB-based methods can localize intervals (Fiebelman et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), but
different motions can look alike in RGB frames while differing in their dynamics (Yang et al., 2026),
and even video MLLMs struggle with fine-grained motion understanding (Hong et al., 2025).
Gaussian avatars, however, already provide structure suited to both challenges. Each Gaussian is
defined in a canonical space and deformed to every pose (Qian et al., 2024), so it keeps its iden-
tity as the avatar moves. The driving pose sequence also provides joint trajectories. Building on
this structure, we introduce HumanSkill-4D, which organizes avatar-specific knowledge and shared
procedures into an agent skill (Tang et al., 2026) for a frozen MLLM. The skill’s knowledge lies in
per-avatar memories of the Gaussians in each part or garment and of joint trajectories. Its procedures
are geometric operators that check pose and motion conditions and are shared across avatars.
Specifically, to address region ambiguity, we accumulate labels from a 2D human parser (Khirodkar
et al., 2026) across views and poses into category scores that link body parts and garments to the
avatar’s Gaussians. A semantic memory combines these scores with category names and MLLM-
generated appearance descriptions. Given a query, the MLLM uses the names and descriptions to
select categories by appearance, relation, or function, rather than matching the query to CLIP fea-
tures, and the Gaussians linked to these categories form the selected region. For motion ambiguity,
we store the driving joint trajectories in a motion memory and check query conditions on them rather
than on RGB frames. The checks use geometric operators adapted from PoseScript (Delmas et al.,
2022), which describes poses by relations between joints, and MotionScript (Yazdian et al., 2025),
which extends these relations to motion. Given a 4D query, the MLLM composes these operators
into a program by linking them with temporal relations, and running it on the trajectories gives the
matching intervals. In Fig. 1(b), for example, the program keeps the outward movements of both up-

per arms that end parallel to the ground. Because the MLLM chooses categories and operators rather
than regions or intervals, each selection can be traced to these choices, and new motion conditions
only require composing existing operators and relations or adding new ones. To evaluate this task,
we introduce three human-annotated benchmarks for selecting regions and pose or motion intervals.
HumanSkill-4D performs best in part segmentation and 3D and 4D referring segmentation.
We make four contributions: (1) we introduce avatar referring segmentation and HumanSkill-4D, a
training-free framework that gives the structure of Gaussian avatars to a frozen MLLM as a skill,
making its decisions traceable; (2) to address region ambiguity, we build a semantic memory that
also supports part segmentation; (3) to address motion ambiguity, we localize motions on joint
trajectories with MLLM-composed programs, extensible by composing or adding operators and
relations; and (4) to evaluate this task, we introduce three benchmarks and show that HumanSkill-4D
is the most accurate on all three, with compact memories and only minutes of setup per avatar.

RELATED WORK
3D language Gaussian splatting. Most language Gaussian methods select regions by matching
a query to CLIP features that are learned per scene (Qin et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024; Wu et al.,
2024) or registered without per-scene optimization (Kim et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2026). Beyond
object names, ReferSplat (He et al., 2025) learns per-scene referring features from annotated ex-
pressions, and ReLaGS (Xie et al., 2026) adds relation-guided retrieval. Open-vocabulary part
segmentation likewise matches text to language-aligned features of 3D shapes (Ma et al., 2025)
and humans (Suzuki et al., 2025a). Whereas these methods match queries to language features,
HumanSkill-4D uses an MLLM to interpret region queries through a semantic memory of avatar
parts and garments, supporting both referring and part segmentation.
4D language Gaussian splatting. Recent work extends language Gaussian splatting to dynamic
scenes, where a query specifies a region and when it is relevant. 4D LangSplat (Li et al., 2025) and
4-LEGS (Fiebelman et al., 2025) learn temporal language features from MLLM-generated captions
and video-text features, respectively, and ST4R-Splat (Meng et al., 2026) extends referring segmen-
tation to 4D Gaussians. For humans, OpenHuman4D (Suzuki et al., 2025b) and 4D-DRESS (Wang
et al., 2024d) parse human parts over time. However, these methods mainly rely on RGB evidence,
in which different motions can look alike (Yang et al., 2026) and fine-grained motion remains hard
to capture (Hong et al., 2025). HumanSkill-4D instead measures pose and motion conditions on the
driving joint trajectories, so it can distinguish subtle differences, such as the direction and extent of
a movement, and return the queried region with its matching intervals using a compact memory.
Agent skills and executable procedures. Large language models increasingly solve complex or
fine-grained tasks through supplied procedures rather than task-specific training. Some compose
predefined actions or handlers (Khot et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025), whereas others generate code
over visual tools (Gupta & Kembhavi, 2023; Sur´ıs et al., 2023). More recently, agent skills compile
experience into reusable knowledge and procedures that accompany the query (Wang et al., 2024a;
Tang et al., 2026), and this paradigm now extends to 3D spatial and 4D spatiotemporal reasoning (Li
et al., 2026a; Sohn et al., 2026). Building on this paradigm, HumanSkill-4D gives an MLLM per-
avatar memories as knowledge and shared geometric operators and temporal relations as procedures,
so a new avatar needs only its memories, and a new motion condition requires only composing
existing procedures or adding new ones.

Gaussian avatars and motion. 3DGS-Avatar (Qian et al., 2024) represents an avatar as a fixed
set of N Gaussians gi with position, shape, opacity, and color. These Gaussians are defined in a
canonical space corresponding to the reference pose of SMPL (Loper et al., 2015), a body model
parameterized by shape and joint rotations. A driving pose sequence of T frames specifies the joint
rotations and root translation at each frame, which determine the 3D joint positions. Animation
deforms the Gaussians with the body into each target pose, so gi is the same Gaussian across frames.

METHOD
4.1
OVERVIEW
Given a language query, HumanSkill-4D selects the described region of an avatar and, for a 4D
query, every matching interval in its driving pose sequence. As shown in Fig. 2, the framework
prepares reusable memories offline, then uses a frozen MLLM ΦMLLM to interpret queries online.
In the offline phase, we build a semantic memory Msemantic that links body-part and garment cat-
egories to the avatar’s Gaussians through category scores pic registered from a frozen 2D human
parser Φparse. The memory also stores category-level appearance descriptions generated by ΦMLLM
from multi-view images (Sec. 4.2). A motion memory Mmotion stores joint and root trajectories
for each driving pose sequence. A registry R shared across avatars provides reusable templates for
checking pose and motion conditions, along with temporal relations that specify how these condi-
tions overlap or follow one another in time (Sec. 4.3).
In the online phase, ΦMLLM reads the query q and the category names and descriptions to select tar-
get categories Cq. Their stored scores determine the spatial mask sq (Where), which is the complete
output for a 3D query. For a 4D query, the model also reads the registry’s text cards and composes
operators into a motion program Πq. A deterministic executor runs this program on Mmotion to ob-
tain the temporal mask hq (When). The two masks give the spatiotemporal selection Yq (Sec. 4.4).

BUILDING THE SEMANTIC MEMORY

memory Msemantic used for online region selection.
4.3
BUILDING THE MOTION MEMORY AND OPERATOR REGISTRY
Motion memory. The motion memory Mmotion stores SMPL joint positions, root orientation, and
root translation for all T frames of the driving pose sequence. For directional measurements, we
adapt MotionScript’s first-frame normalization (Yazdian et al., 2025) to define a coordinate frame
fixed throughout the sequence. The initial root position defines its origin, gravity defines its vertical
axis, and the person’s initial facing direction defines its forward axis.
Operator registry. The operator registry R is shared across avatars and stores templates for check-
ing pose and motion conditions, along with temporal relations that specify how these conditions
overlap or follow one another in time. A template is a reusable rule that specifies a geometric
measurement and a condition to check, without selecting a particular body part. For example, the
horizontal template in Fig. 2 measures a segment’s tilt relative to gravity and checks whether it lies
in the range for a horizontal pose. Each template also specifies its coordinate frame and compatible
body parts. State templates check conditions at individual frames, whereas change templates check
how measurements change over intervals. We adapt these measurements and conditions from Pos-
eScript (Delmas et al., 2022) and MotionScript (Yazdian et al., 2025), defining 46 templates—20
state and 26 change templates—in seven measurement types: joint angle, relative position, displace-
ment, root rotation, segment tilt, distance, and height above the lowest joint (Supp. Sec. A.2). The
registry also provides a text card for each template and relation, which describes it in words and is
later given to ΦMLLM with the query.
From templates to operators. Applying a template to a compatible body part defines an exe-
cutable operator ρ. The selected body part determines which SMPL joint trajectories the operator
reads from Mmotion. For example, applying the horizontal template to the left upper arm selects
the left shoulder–elbow segment. In Fig. 2, applying the move outward and horizontal templates
to both upper arms gives two outward-motion operators ρ1, ρ2 and two horizontal-state operators

4.4
ANSWERING 3D AND 4D QUERIES
Query interpretation. For both 3D and 4D queries, we provide ΦMLLM with a text-only prompt
containing the query q, the category names and descriptions from the semantic memory Msemantic,
and a few in-context learning examples (Brown et al., 2020), following skill-based prompting (Tang
et al., 2026). For a 4D query, the prompt also includes the registry’s text cards. The model returns
target categories Cq and, for a 4D query, a motion program Πq that defines operators by applying
templates to body parts and links these operators with temporal relations.
Spatial selection. For both query types, the spatial mask sq ∈{0, 1}N selects canonical Gaussian
gi if its stored category score pic meets the threshold τ for any target category c ∈Cq:
sq,i = 1[∃c ∈Cq : pic ≥τ] .
(4)
Temporal selection. For a 4D query, the executor runs the motion program Πq on the motion
memory Mmotion. Each operator ρ returns state frames Tρ or change occurrences Oρ from Eq. 3. The
executor combines these outputs according to the temporal relations in Πq to obtain the matching
interval set Iq. The registry’s seven relations can require movements to co-occur, follow in order, or
alternate, states to hold together, or a state to hold at the end of, throughout, or during a movement.
For example, the program first identifies intervals when both upper arms move outward, and the
StateAtEnd relation then retains each interval whose last frame has both arms horizontal (relation
definitions and execution example in Supp. Sec. A.4).
We convert the matching intervals in Iq into a temporal mask hq ∈{0, 1}T :
hq,t = 1[∃I ∈Iq : t ∈I] .

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Benchmarks. To evaluate avatar referring segmentation, we introduce three benchmarks by aug-
menting existing human datasets with referring queries and human-annotated targets, following prior
evaluations (He et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). ZJU-Refer-3D extends ZJU-MoCap (Peng et al., 2021)
with dense part annotations and 20 referring queries covering part names, appearance, wearing re-
lations, and functions. HuMMan-Refer-4D extends HuMMan (Cai et al., 2022) by converting its
fine-grained body-part motion descriptions (Zhang et al., 2023) into 20 referring queries, while
ZJU-Refer-4D extends ZJU-MoCap with 20 queries in the same format. Construction and annota-
tion details appear in Supp. Secs. B.1–B.2.
Metrics. For part segmentation, we report mean per-category IoU (mIoU), overall pixel accuracy
(OA), and mean per-category recall (mAcc) (Suzuki et al., 2025a). For 3D referring segmentation,
we report mIoU over queries (He et al., 2025). For 4D referring segmentation, vIoU evaluates region
accuracy and timing jointly, penalizing both incorrect masks and extra or missed active frames (Li
et al., 2025). We additionally report tIoU, precision, and recall to assess temporal selection sep-
arately (Fiebelman et al., 2025). tIoU measures overlap between predicted and annotated active
frames. Precision is the fraction of selected frames annotated as active, and recall the fraction of
annotated active frames selected (Supp. Sec. C.1).

ABLATIONS AND COMPUTATIONAL ANALYSIS
Choice of MLLM. To test how the choice of MLLM affects accuracy, we replace ΦMLLM in both
description generation and query interpretation, with image inputs and queries fixed (Tab. 4, left).
Qwen3.8 ties for the highest score in both 3D and 4D, and Qwen3.6 performs similarly, whereas
Gemma 4 matches Qwen3.8’s 4D vIoU but has much lower 3D mIoU. We use Qwen3.8 by default
and report smaller MLLMs in Supp. Sec. D.1.
Semantic category selector. We examine whether text-embedding retrieval can replace MLLM-
based category selection. CLIP (Cherti et al., 2023), E5 (Wang et al., 2024b), and JINA-v5 (Akram
et al., 2026) rank the same category descriptions and select the top three. We keep the semantic
memory fixed and, for 4D queries, use the same temporal predictions. All three retrieval meth-
ods yield lower 3D mIoU and 4D vIoU than the MLLM (Tab. 4, middle). Qualitative examples
(Supp. Sec. D.2) show JINA selecting the patched garment correctly but adding a forearm when
asked to select both hands. These results support MLLM-based selection, because the selected set
must contain the target categories and exclude those the query does not request, which a fixed top-
three retrieval cannot ensure.
Score threshold. We test how sensitive segmentation accuracy is to τ, keeping the memory, selected
categories, and intervals fixed (Tab. 4, right). Performance remains stable for τ = 0.2–0.5, including
our default 0.4. At τ = 0.1, both mIoU and vIoU drop slightly, consistent with weakly supported
Gaussians leaking across part or garment boundaries.
Computational costs. Tab. 5 compares the time to the first query and the storage retained for later
queries (Supp. Sec. C.3). LangSplat, ReferSplat, 4-LEGS, and 4D LangSplat optimize features
for each avatar, whereas HumanSkill-4D only registers the frozen parser’s labels to the Gaussians,
generates category descriptions with the frozen MLLM, and stores the joint trajectories. Our time to
the first query is ∼4 min in 3D and ∼8 min in 4D, the shortest among the compared methods. The
resulting memories occupy about 5 MB in 3D, comparable to LangSplat and ReferSplat, and 10 MB

CONCLUSION
We introduced avatar referring segmentation, which selects avatar regions and the intervals
satisfying queried pose or motion conditions, and presented HumanSkill-4D, a training-free
framework that gives the structure of Gaussian avatars to a frozen MLLM as a skill. To address
region ambiguity, its semantic memory links body-part and garment categories and appearance
descriptions to persistent Gaussian regions, enabling direct and indirect region references.
To
address motion ambiguity, the MLLM composes geometric operators adapted from PoseScript
and MotionScript into programs executed on the driving joint trajectories. Because the MLLM
chooses categories and operators rather than regions or intervals, its selections are traceable, and
new motion conditions only require composing existing operators and relations or adding new
ones. Experiments on our three human-annotated benchmarks show that HumanSkill-4D selects
regions and intervals most accurately, with compact memories and only minutes of setup per avatar.
Limitations and future directions are discussed in Supp. Sec. E.

METHOD DETAILS
This section expands the definitions and execution rules introduced in Sec. 4 of the main paper.
Running query. We use the query “Select the upper clothing while both upper arms lift outward
until they are parallel to the ground” from Fig. 1(b) of the main paper as a running example to show
how motion operators and temporal relations determine the selected intervals.
A.1
SEMANTIC CATEGORIES
The semantic memory uses a fixed set K of 16 categories, obtained by mapping the labels of
Φparse (Sapiens2 (Khirodkar et al., 2026)): hair, face neck, torso, left arm, right arm,
left hand, right hand, left leg, right leg, upper clothing, lower clothing,
left shoe, right shoe, left sock, right sock, and eyeglass. The mapping merges
the upper and lower arm into left arm or right arm, the upper leg, lower leg, and foot into
left leg or right leg, and the lips into face neck.
A.2
OPERATOR TEMPLATES
Motion operators are built from the templates in the operator registry R. A template specifies what to
measure and what condition to check. For example, the near and approach templates both measure
the distance between two body points. The near template checks whether the points are close at a
frame (a state), while approach checks whether their distance decreases over time (a change).
Operator registry. Tab. S1 lists the templates of the registry R by measurement type, with state
and change templates in separate columns. We define these templates by adapting the geometric
pose and motion codes of PoseScript (Delmas et al., 2022) and MotionScript (Yazdian et al., 2025)
to conditions over SMPL trajectories. For example, forward and backward head tilt use the move
forward and move backward templates applied to the neck–head segment. New pose and motion
conditions can be supported by composing existing operators and relations or by adding new registry
entries, each defined by its rule and text card.
Applying templates. To apply one of these templates, we specify a compatible body part, which
determines the SMPL joints used for the measurement. For an elbow, the bend template measures
the shoulder–elbow–wrist angle, and for an upper arm, the horizontal template measures the angle
of the shoulder–elbow segment from vertical. Other templates use a point and a reference, as the
above template does for “the hand is above the head”.

Candidate intervals. A change operator uses the frame-to-frame difference mρ(t) −mρ(t −1) to
split its measurement, such as the elbow angle for the bend template, into runs in which it increases
or decreases, and keeps the runs in the direction of its template as candidates. A candidate starts
at the frame before its first change and ends at the frame before the first change in the opposite
direction, where the next run starts; frames without change in between stay in the candidate. If no
opposite change follows, the candidate ends at its last change, so a plateau at the end of the sequence
is excluded. For example, if the elbow angle drops at every frame from 10 to 25, holds for three
frames, drops again through frame 40, and rises at frame 41, the bend template yields the single
candidate [9, 40]. A candidate becomes an occurrence when it passes the two thresholds of Eq. 3 of
the main paper, a minimum total change ∆min
ρ
and a minimum length of Lmin frames. Because the
candidate fixes the direction, the absolute value in Eq. 3 measures only the amount of change.

Change magnitude. Following the angular motion codes of MotionScript (Yazdian et al., 2025), we
assign each occurrence a magnitude from the number of bins between its start and end values. One
or two bins make the change slight, three moderate, and four or more significant. Occurrences that
change by less than PoseScript’s 5◦binning noise for angles and orientations receive no magnitude.
Only joint-angle and root-rotation changes receive a magnitude, and root rotation counts the bins
of its own rotation axis. Magnitudes thus describe changes rather than states, so slightly bent is the
slight bent degree in Tab. S1, whereas slightly bending is a bend of slight magnitude.
Reference frames and body shape. All measurements use joints of the default SMPL shape (Loper
et al., 2015) rather than subject-specific shape coefficients, which keeps the thresholds comparable
across subjects. The fixed frame is set at the first frame (Sec. 4.3 of the main paper) and does not
rotate when the body later turns. Root-local measurements follow the body root, and gravity-frame
measurements use only the vertical axis (Tab. S1).

In the query interpretation of Sec. 4.4 of the main paper, ΦMLLM acts as a 3D selector or a 4D
compiler. The selector receives three in-context learning (ICL) examples (Brown et al., 2020) and
the compiler five, and Sec. G gives the core of each instruction with an ICL example.
3D selector. For a 3D query, the selector receives the query and the names and descriptions of K. It
returns the target categories Cq ⊆K or an empty list.

B
BENCHMARKS
B.1
ZJU-REFER-3D
ZJU-Refer-3D extends ZJU-MoCap (Peng et al., 2021) with two forms of human annotation. For
part segmentation, nine subjects are annotated at eight frames in four held-out cameras, yielding 288
ground-truth (GT) masks over the 16 categories of Sec. A.1. For 3D referring segmentation, each
query is evaluated on the 32 held-out images of its subject (Sec. F).
B.2
4D REFERRING EVALUATION
HuMMan-Refer-4D and ZJU-Refer-4D each contain 20 queries (Sec. F). In both benchmarks, each
query has a target mask in two held-out cameras for every active frame. For HuMMan, we select
sequences showing movements of specific body parts with clearly identifiable starts and ends, and
annotators convert the provided fine-grained motion descriptions (Zhang et al., 2023) into referring
queries. Annotators then review each clip and manually determine the intervals satisfying the speci-
fied pose or motion conditions. ZJU-MoCap queries use the same format, and annotators determine
their intervals in the same way.

Training. Train. marks per-avatar field or autoencoder training. OpenHuman3D/4D instead use

Time. Time starts with completed Gaussians, RGB views, cameras, and poses, before method-
specific preparation, and ends when the first query’s final masks are saved. It includes per-avatar
segmentation, description generation, feature extraction, training, semantic registration, pose prepa-
ration, and query execution. Model loading and all inference calls are included for every method.
All methods run on an NVIDIA A100 80GB.
Storage. Storage counts the per-avatar data retained for querying, listed under Stored information
in Tab. S4. Features are the language features each method stores for Gaussians or regions. Net-
works are layers trained for each avatar, such as the autoencoders that decode compressed features,
ReferSplat’s layers that match Gaussian features to the query text, and 4-LEGS’ layers that combine
each Gaussian’s feature with those of its neighbors at each frame. Masks are OpenHuman’s region
masks, and scores, text, and motion are our category scores, category descriptions, and joint trajec-
tories. Storage excludes shared Gaussians, camera and pose inputs, pretrained model weights shared
across avatars, training intermediates, and query outputs.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
Because HumanSkill-4D reuses a frozen parser and a shared registry, it needs no per-avatar training,
but it selects regions only among the categories of Φparse and checks motion only with the templates
in R. Details outside these categories, such as a logo or a watch, may therefore not be selectable, and
motions outside these templates, such as dancing or carrying an object, may not be checked. Future
work can extend both components within the same framework. For regions, a finer segmenter such
as SAM 3 (Carion et al., 2026), which segments objects named by short noun phrases, could be used
alongside Φparse to register such details as categories. For motion, actions could be decomposed into
part-level conditions, as in prior work on fine-grained human motion (Delmas et al., 2022; Yazdian
et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025), or object-relative templates could be added given
object trajectories.
