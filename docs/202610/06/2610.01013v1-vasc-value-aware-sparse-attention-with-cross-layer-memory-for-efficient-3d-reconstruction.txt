ABSTRACT
Feed-forward 3D vision models such as VGGT have achieved remarkable
progress, unifying camera estimation and dense scene reconstruction in a sin-
gle pass.
However, their quadratic global attention makes long image se-
quences expensive, while existing sparse methods may favor highly attended
yet value-redundant regions. To address these limitations, we introduce VASC,
a training-free sparse attention method combining value-aware block selection
and execution-aware cross-layer memory. Our value-aware block selection in-
tegrates pooled query–key relevance with neighboring value contrast, reducing
redundancy while preserving query-relevant and distinctive content. Cross-layer
memory tracks unserved demand across layers and updates this state according to
actual execution, enabling previously underserved blocks to compete under a fixed
computation budget. Experiments on 7Scenes and NeuralRGB-D with VGGT and
π3 demonstrate improved pose estimation and reconstruction quality compared
with FasterVGGT, together with up to 2.29× faster inference than dense VGGT.

INTRODUCTION
Recovering three-dimensional geometry from images is increasingly becoming a problem of learned,
feed-forward inference. Building on transformer representations (Vaswani et al., 2017; Dosovitskiy
et al., 2021), DUSt3R casts pairwise geometry as pointmap regression, while MASt3R connects

this representation to dense matching (Wang et al., 2024; Leroy et al., 2024). VGGT jointly pre-
dicts cameras, depth, point maps, and tracks, and π3 develops permutation-equivariant geometry
prediction (Wang et al., 2025a; 2026b). Scaling these models requires both accurate predictions and
efficient processing of long inputs.
Global cross-view attention is a central obstacle to this scalability: its interactions grow quadrati-
cally with the number of visual tokens. Efficient exact-attention kernels reduce memory traffic and
improve hardware utilization (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2024), but do not decide which interactions can
be omitted. Recurrent and streaming models address long inputs through spatial memory, persistent
state, or causal processing (Wang & Agapito, 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2025b; Zhuo et al.,
2026); TTT3R and InfiniteVGGT further study long-term memory (Chen et al., 2026b; Yuan et al.,
2026). We consider a complementary setting: accelerating an existing backbone’s global attention
without retraining or replacing its prediction architecture.
Training-free acceleration offers several ways to reduce this cost. Token merging consolidates sim-
ilar representations (Bolya et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2025); sparse global attention retains selected
block interactions (Wang et al., 2026a); layer scheduling changes where cross-view computation is
spent (Sun et al., 2026) (see also geometry quantization and video-generation caching; Feng et al.,
2026; Wu et al., 2025a). For sparse execution, a key question remains: which interactions should re-
ceive a fixed computation budget? Selection must protect geometry prediction without an overhead
that erases the savings.
Pooled query–key scores provide a natural relevance estimate, but sparse selection faces two linked
limitations in content and execution history. Attention weights describe query-dependent relevance,
whereas value vectors supply the aggregated content, a distinction also explored in value-aware
approximation (Gupta & Berant, 2021). Strong attention can coincide with locally similar values, so
relevance alone does not indicate how distinct the selected content is. Under a tight budget, relevant
but distinctive interactions may consequently be omitted, as illustrated by the attention ranking in
Figure 3. This limitation also extends across network depth: independent selection at each layer
does not account for whether previously assigned demand was actually served. Historical scores
and reusable sparse indices provide useful context (Zhang et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2026; Han et al.,
2026), but do not by themselves encode the gap between requested and executed computation. The
challenge is therefore to account for both value content and unmet computational demand while
preserving the efficiency of fixed-budget sparse attention. Sparse attention must account for both
what deserves computation and whether previously assigned demand was actually served.
We introduce VASC, Value-Aware Sparse Attention with Cross-Layer Memory, to address these
two aspects jointly. Value-aware selection combines pooled query–key relevance with the RMS
contrast of adjacent pooled value blocks, distinguishing content at native sparse-block granularity.
Cross-layer memory maintains one scalar balance per block pair, accumulating unserved demand
and subtracting actual execution. This computational deficit participates in subsequent ranking
alongside current demand. Both components preserve per-query capacity, the native sparse kernel,
special-token handling, and geometry heads.
Experiments on 7Scenes and NeuralRGB-D evaluate VGGT and π3 across sparsity levels and in-
put strides. VASC improves all six pose and reconstruction metrics over the corresponding sparse
baselines at S75 in the main comparisons, while retaining up to 2.29× speedup over dense VGGT.
Figures 1 and 2 summarize quality and efficiency. Component ablations, denser inputs, ScanNet
transfer, and scheduler adaptations further examine effectiveness and generality.
Our contributions are fourfold:
• Observation. We illustrate a mismatch between attention priority and local value variation
in a geometry transformer, motivating their complementary use.
• Value-aware selection. We combine pooled query–key relevance with adjacent-value con-
trast for training-free selection of distinctive, query-relevant blocks.
• Cross-layer memory.
An execution-updated pair-level state carries unserved demand
across layers to compete with current demand under an unchanged budget.
• Effectiveness and efficiency. Across two geometry backbones, VASC improves sparse re-
construction and pose estimation, with controlled ablations and up to 2.29× faster inference
than dense VGGT.

Attention relevance and value variation.
We compare attention priorities with local value varia-
tion, visualized using each value vector’s mean cosine similarity to its four spatial neighbors. Lower
similarity indicates greater directional variation. Figure 3(b) illustrates that the two maps need not
agree: highly attended connections can carry locally similar values, while lower-ranked connections
can contain more distinct content. Attention-only selection can therefore favor redundant content
under a tight budget. Visualization details are given in Appendix B.
Competition under a fixed budget.
Retaining a connection consumes capacity that could other-
wise serve another candidate. A high attention rank therefore does not, by itself, indicate how much
distinct content is preserved by that choice. Conversely, falling below the selection threshold does
not imply irrelevance to the query. The resulting trade-off concerns not only how much attention is
retained, but also which value content is represented within the available capacity.
Implications for sparse selection.

Such a ranking expresses which interactions deserve computation; the fixed budget still determines
which can actually be executed. This distinction between demand and execution underlies VASC’s
selection and feedback mechanism.

METHOD
VASC combines query–key relevance and value contrast into current demand, allocates a fixed
execution budget, and carries their accumulated mismatch across layers (Figure 4). Its low-cost,
training-free realization preserves the native sparse attention kernel and geometry heads.
Blocks and capacity.
At layer l, i indexes a query block and j a key/value child block, the unit
selected for sparse execution. Each query has capacity bl(i). Adjacent key/value children form pairs
C(p) with fixed membership across layers; p(j) denotes the pair containing child j. Pairs share a
value-contrast score and a memory state, but their children remain separate execution candidates.
Batch and head indices are omitted.

4.3
CROSS-LAYER MEMORY
Refine allocation through past service. Current demand evaluates present content; memory ad-
justs allocation using accumulated demand–execution imbalance at the block-pair level. This gives
previously underserved blocks another opportunity to compete for the fixed budget. In particular,
near the selection cutoff, past service can distinguish candidates with similar current demands.
A compact update. We apply the same averaging over query blocks and each pair’s children to Cl
and Xl, obtaining pair demand al(p) and service xl(p), both in [0, 1]. Starting from zero, we retain
their accumulated nonnegative imbalance:
Ml+1(p) = [Ml(p) + al(p) −xl(p)]+,
M1(p) = 0.
(6)
Demand adds to the balance; service subtracts from it and can reduce earlier deficits. Clipping
prevents negative memory, but receiving some computation need not clear the balance. The resulting
pair-level state summarizes demand and service across queries and paired children.
One scalar per pair, batch entry, and head is retained for updating; its bounded copy informs selec-
tion. Each layer uses its own Q, K, and V to compute attention on the selected support.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets and metrics.
We evaluate VGGT and π3 on all 18 test sequences of 7Scenes (Shotton
et al., 2013) and all nine NeuralRGB-D (NRGBD) sequences (Azinovi´c et al., 2022) at strides s ∈
{5, 10}, retaining every s-th frame without a frame cap. Generalization uses 50 ScanNet (Dai et al.,
2017) scenes with 100 frames each: the first frame and 99 uniformly sampled remaining frames.
On 7Scenes and NRGBD, we report absolute trajectory error (ATE), translational and rotational
relative pose error (RPE-t/RPE-r), reconstruction accuracy (Acc), completeness (Comp), and normal
consistency (NC). Translation and reconstruction distances are in meters; RPE-r is in degrees. Lower
is better except for NC. Results are sequence macro averages, displayed to three decimal places.
Boldface uses unrounded values.
Baselines and implementation.
S55–S85 denote target sparsity of 55–85% of patch-child interac-
tions; realized sparsity accounts for block rounding and always-kept support. Within each backbone,
baseline/VASC comparisons share checkpoints, inputs, evaluators, geometry heads, and sparse ker-
nels. We compare with FasterVGGT (Wang et al., 2026a) and our π3 adaptation, FasterPi3; tables
label both FasterVGGT. FastVGGT (Shen et al., 2025) uses its official reconstruction pipeline at
merge ratio 0.9. Timing covers full VGGT forward passes, including geometry heads, at input size
518 and stride 10 on A6000 and RTX 4090. We average per-sequence medians of three passes after
one warm-up, excluding loading and evaluation; comparisons use the same physical GPU.

CONCLUSION
We presented VASC, a training-free method for efficient 3D reconstruction. VASC combines pooled
query–key relevance with adjacent-value contrast, and uses cross-layer memory to carry unserved
demand into subsequent selection. Both components operate within a fixed budget while preserving
the native sparse kernel and geometry heads. Experiments on 7Scenes and NeuralRGB-D with
VGGT and π3 demonstrate improved pose estimation and reconstruction over sparse baselines, with
up to 2.29× faster inference than dense VGGT.
