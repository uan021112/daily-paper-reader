Abstract
Recent depth foundation models like Depth Anything 3
(DA3) achieve remarkable multi-view depth estimation but
assume static 3D scenes, limiting their applicability to real-
world dynamic environments.
Existing training-free 4D
methods like Easi3R and VGGT4D rely on correspondence-
trained backbones whose attention encodes cross-frame
matching, a property absent in depth-only models like DA3.
We present Dyna3, a training-free framework that extends
DA3 for 4D dynamic scene reconstruction without any fine-
tuning. Our key insight is that DA3’s cross-view features,
though trained only for depth consistency, implicitly encode
motion-discriminative signals when combined with best-
match feature search across frames. Its static surfaces find
consistent matches globally, while dynamic objects can-
not. We further adopt vision-language models (VLM) to
automatically generate scene-specific semantic prompts for
SAM 3, enabling precise instance-level segmentation that
distinguishes which objects move from what objects exist.
For reconstruction, we decouple the scene into a cross-
frame aligned static background and per-frame dynamic
point clouds.
Experiments on four datasets demonstrate
that Dyna3 surpasses correspondence-trained methods with
+5.5pp J-Mean over state-of-the-art VGGT4D on dynamic
object segmentation, while achieving up to 13× faster pose
estimation and 3× faster 4D reconstruction with 4 to 8×
lower memory. Dyna3 could therefore enable much denser
temporal sampling that prior methods cannot support.
1. Introduction
Reconstructing dynamic 3D scenes from monocular video
is a fundamental challenge in computer vision with ap-
plications in autonomous driving, augmented reality, and
robotics. Real-world scenes are inherently dynamic: people
walk, vehicles move, and objects deform. However, most
3D reconstruction pipelines assume a rigid, static world.
Bridging this gap without expensive per-scene optimization
or motion supervision remains an open problem.
Recent depth foundation models have advanced 3D re-
construction significantly. Depth Anything 3 (DA3) [10],
built on a frozen DINOv2 [12] backbone with dual DPT
heads, jointly predicts depth, camera rays, and poses in a
single feed-forward pass.
Compared to correspondence-
based models such as DUSt3R [24] and VGGT [20], DA3
uses a smaller architecture (135M vs 571M–1.3B param-
eters) and avoids iterative optimization, enabling efficient
inference suitable for real-time applications [29].

The second approach, exemplified by

DA3 fits neither approach. It is trained with geometric

rather than cross-frame correspondence. Its attention maps

scenes without adaptation, moving objects are treated as
static geometry, corrupting both scene structure and cam-

dynamic scene reconstruction while preserving its effi-
ciency advantages?
We demonstrate that it can. Rather than analyzing atten-
tion patterns, we identify that DA3’s patch features, though

static pixels via DA3 poses and voxel-consolidated into a

no modification to DA3’s weights or architecture.

65.04% J-Mean on DAVIS-2016 [14], surpassing state-of-

ning up to 13× faster with 8× less memory. On final 4D
reconstruction, Dyna3 also reaches comparable quality with
3× faster inference and 4× less memory. These efficiency
gains allow Dyna3 to process much denser temporal sam-
pling where prior methods could run out of memory. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:
• We present Dyna3, the first training-free framework that
extends a depth-only foundation model (DA3) to 4D dy-
namic scene reconstruction while preserving its efficiency
advantage over correspondence-based methods.
• We design a unified pipeline that introduce best-match
feature search for motion detection, VLM-guided SAM 3
segmentation for instance separation, and decoupled
static and dynamic point clouds for coherent reconstruc-
tion.
• We achieve state-of-the-art training-free results on dy-
namic object segmentation with significant efficiency
gains on camera pose estimation and 4D reconstruction,
enabling much denser temporal sampling than prior meth-
ods support.
2. Related Work
2.1. Feed-Forward Multi-View 3D Reconstruction
Traditional multi-view reconstruction methods such as
COLMAP [16] rely on iterative optimization for camera
calibration and dense matching, limiting real-time applica-
bility. DUSt3R [24] introduced a feed-forward paradigm
by directly regressing aligned point maps from image pairs.
MASt3R [9] extended it with dense feature matching, and
subsequent work scaled this paradigm to many views and
streaming settings, including Fast3R [36], Spann3R [19],
CUT3R [23], and TTSA3R [40]. VGGT [20] generalized
the pointmap formulation into a geometry-grounded trans-
former predicting depth, cameras, and tracks jointly, adding
an explicit tracking loss Ltrack for correspondence super-
vision. These methods achieve accurate geometry but use
large models (571M–1.3B parameters) and often require
iterative optimization. Depth Anything3 (DA3)[10] takes
a different path, unifying multi-view depth, camera rays,
and pose into a single feed-forward model built on a frozen
DINOv2 [12] backbone with just about 135M parameters.
DA3 is trained only for depth consistency without corre-
spondence supervision, enabling faster inference but leav-
ing open whether it can support dynamic reconstruction.
While feed-forward geometric reconstruction has also ad-
vanced in single-image settings [39], extending to dynamic
multi-view scenes remains challenging.

2.2. Dynamic and 4D Scene Reconstruction
While 3D perception has advanced through multi-modal
fusion for tasks like object detection [1, 11, 28, 30, 32],
dynamic scene reconstruction presents distinct challenges.
Classical dynamic reconstruction relies on per-scene opti-
mization, from deformable neural radiance fields [13, 15]
to dynamic Gaussian splatting [25, 37] and monocular 4D
methods such as Shape of Motion [22]. These achieve high
fidelity but require lengthy optimization and often known
poses. A complementary line adapts feed-forward pointmap
models to motion. MonST3R [38] fine-tunes DUSt3R on
dynamic data, introducing training cost and dataset depen-
dency. Easi3R [4] is training-free, repurposing DUSt3R’s
cross-attention maps to distinguish dynamic pixels, but is
restricted to two-view inputs and inherits DUSt3R’s com-
putational overhead. VGGT4D [8] is another training-free
method mining motion cues from VGGT’s attention for
multi-view 4D reconstruction with stronger accuracy, but
remains expensive. In addition, these training-free meth-
ods rely on correspondence-trained backbones whose atten-
tion encodes matching. On the other hand, depth-only mod-
els like DA3 offer faster inference, but lack such attention-
based motion cues. Our work addresses this gap by extract-
ing motion signals through best-match feature search and
leveraging vision-language models for instance-level seg-
mentation, enabling efficient training-free 4D reconstruc-
tion without correspondence-trained backbones.
3. Method
3.1. Overview
Given a monocular video {Ii}N
i=1 of a dynamic scene,
Dyna3 produces a 4D reconstruction consisting of a per-
sistent static background, per-frame dynamic objects, and
camera poses. Our framework operates entirely at inference
time using a pretrained DA3 model, with no fine-tuning or
additional training. Shown in Fig.2, the pipeline has four
stages. First, we extract per-frame depth, camera pose, and
patch features from DA3[10] (Sec.3.2). Second, we com-
pute motion score Si from patch similarities via best-match
search, producing a coarse motion prior Φi that identifies
regions containing movement (Sec.3.3). Third, we use this
motion prior to guide a vision-language model, which gen-
erates scene-specific text prompts for SAM3[3] to produce
precise instance masks Mi (Sec.3.4). Fourth, we fuse the
motion score signal with the instance masks to select dy-
namic objects (Sec.3.5) and decouple the scene into static
and dynamic components for 4D reconstruction (Sec.3.6).
3.2. Geometry Extraction from DA3
We adopt DA3 [10] as the geometry backbone. For a set
of input views, DA3 predicts per-frame depth Di ∈RH×W
and a dense ray map that encodes per-pixel camera rays.
DA3 provides two options for camera pose estimation: a
camera-token head and RANSAC [5] decomposition of the
ray map. We use the latter, which decomposes the ray map
into extrinsics Ei ∈SE(3) and intrinsics Ki, as we find it
more reliable across diverse scenes (see Sec. 4).
DA3’s DPT decoder also produces feature maps Fi ∈
Rh×w×C, where fi(u) denotes the feature vector at spatial
location u. These features, originally designed for depth
regression, encode rich appearance information that we re-
purpose for motion detection as described in Sec. 3.3.

3.3. Motion Feature via Best-Match Search
Existing training-free 4D methods such as Easi3R [4] and
VGGT4D [8] detect motion by analyzing attention pat-
terns from correspondence-trained backbones.
In these
models, attention weights concentrate on matching re-
gions for static content but scatter for dynamic objects
that violate cross-frame consistency.
Easi3R aggregates
mean and variance statistics from DUSt3R’s cross-attention
maps, while VGGT4D computes similarity matrices from
VGGT’s query-key representations. Both approaches suc-
ceed because correspondence-supervised training produces
attention distributions that inherently distinguish static from
dynamic content.
DA3’s attention maps do not carry such motion-
discriminative signals. DA3 is trained for depth consistency
rather than cross-frame correspondence, so its global self-
attention encodes depth relationships instead of matching
patterns (see Sec. 4.5 for analysis). However, we found that
DA3’s patch features still encode appearance information
useful for matching. We exploit this by constructing a mo-
tion signal through explicit feature comparison rather than
attention analysis.
For each location u in frame i, we search for its best-
matching location across a temporal neighborhood Ni by
computing cosine similarity between feature vectors.
A
static surface appears consistently across frames and pro-
duces high similarity to its best match, even when camera
motion shifts its image location. A dynamic object, how-
ever, changes appearance or position in ways that break this
consistency, resulting in lower match scores. We define the
motion score at location u in frame i as:

3.4. VLM-Guided Instance Segmentation
The motion prior Φi from Sec. 3.3 identifies regions con-
taining dynamic content but does not provide object-level
boundaries. A straightforward fix approach would apply
fixed category labels such as “person” or “vehicle” to seg-
ment potential dynamic objects, but this fails when mov-
ing objects span unpredictable categories or when generic
labels match both moving and stationary instances. We in-
stead use the motion prior to guide a vision-language model
(VLM): by focusing the VLM on regions where motion is
detected, it can identify scene-specific object descriptions
rather than relying on predefined categories. Such VLM-
guided video understanding has shown effectiveness in var-
ious vision tasks [7, 18, 26, 27, 31, 33].

4. Experiment
We evaluate Dyna3 on three tasks: dynamic object segmen-
tation (Section 4.2), camera pose estimation (Section 4.3),
and 4D reconstruction (Section 4.4).
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We use DAVIS-2016 and DAVIS-2017 [14]
for dynamic object segmentation, TUM-dynamics [17] and
Sintel [2] for camera pose estimation, and DyCheck [6]
for 4D reconstruction.
Baselines.
We compare against
MonST3R [38], DAS3R [35], CUT3R [23], Easi3R [4], and
VGGT4D [8]. MonST3R, DAS3R, and CUT3R are fine-
tuned on dynamic datasets with optical flow supervision.
Easi3R and VGGT4D are training-free like ours, but rely
on correspondence-trained backbones whose attention en-
codes cross-frame matching. In contrast, Dyna3 extends
a depth-only model without such correspondence priors.
Metrics. For dynamic object segmentation, we report J-
Mean and F-Mean following DAVIS convention. For cam-
era pose estimation, we report Absolute Trajectory Error
(ATE), Relative Translation Error (RTE), and Relative Ro-
tation Error (RRE). For 4D reconstruction, we report Accu-
racy, Completeness, and Distance. We also report inference
time and peak memory usage to evaluate efficiency. Imple-
mentation. Dyna3 uses DA3-BASE with a frozen DINOv2
ViT-Base encoder, Qwen2-VL-2B [21] as the VLM, and
SAM 3 [3] for video segmentation. Motion scores are com-
puted from last layer features with a 75th percentile thresh-
old. Camera poses are derived from ray maps via RANSAC.
All experiments run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU.

4.2. Dynamic Object Segmentation
Dynamic object segmentation evaluates whether the method
can accurately identify moving regions, which is essential
for separating foreground from background in 4D recon-
struction. Table 1 compares against training-free baselines.
Dyna3 achieves 65.04% J-Mean on DAVIS-2016, out-
performing state-of-the-art VGGT4D by +5.5 percentage
points and Easi3R by +16.1 percentage points. The gains
are even larger on boundary accuracy, with +11.5pp F-Mean
improvement over VGGT4D. This suggests that SAM 3’s
video segmentation produces sharper object boundaries
than the point-cloud-based masks from correspondence
methods. On the more challenging DAVIS-2017 with multi-
ple objects per video, Dyna3 maintains its lead with +2.3pp
J-Mean and +11.4pp F-Mean over VGGT4D. These results
demonstrate that best-match feature search provides a ro-
bust motion signal even without correspondence training,
and that VLM-guided instance selection effectively distin-
guishes which objects move from what objects exist in the
scene.

Efficiency enables dense temporal sampling.
The key
advantage of Dyna3’s efficiency is enabling denser frame
sampling that correspondence-based methods cannot sup-
port due to their quadratic memory scaling. Tables 4 and 5
show how temporal stride affects pose accuracy.
On TUM-dynamics, reducing temporal sampling stride

RTE decreases by 5.8× (from 0.140m to 0.024m) and RRE

is expected since denser sampling provides more constraints
for pose estimation and reduces the motion between con-
secutive frames. Crucially, VGGT4D runs out of memory
at stride 3 due to its quadratic memory scaling with frame
count, while Dyna3 completes successfully in 15.6 seconds
using only 24.7GB memory. Even at the sparsest stride-30
setting where both methods can run, Dyna3 remains 12.4×
faster and 7.9× more memory-efficient.

The ultimate goal of our pipeline is to produce high-quality

and 11.85GB for Easi3R (3.2× larger). This low memory
footprint enables Dyna3 to run on consumer-grade GPUs
and process high-resolution videos that would exceed mem-
ory limits on correspondence-based methods.
The quality gap between Dyna3 and VGGT4D primarily
reflects the difference in how the two methods handle dy-
namic regions. VGGT4D uses its correspondence-trained
backbone to track points across frames, enabling temporally
consistent dynamic reconstruction. In contrast, Dyna3’s de-
coupled representation reconstructs dynamic objects per-

temporal coherence but avoids error accumulation from in-
correct matches. Importantly, our decoupled static and dy-
namic separation ensures that reconstruction errors in dy-
namic regions do not propagate to the static background,
which makes up the majority of most scenes. This design
choice trades some dynamic reconstruction quality for ro-
bustness and efficiency, enabling Dyna3 to deliver compet-
itive overall quality at a fraction of the computational cost.

4.6. Analysis: How DA3’s attention layers does not
encode motion feature
In Section 3, we claimed that DA3’s attention does not
carry motion-discriminative signals, which is why we use
explicit feature comparison instead of attention-based de-
tection as baselines [4, 8]. Figure 3 provides qualitative ev-
idence for this claim. We extract cross-attention maps from
DA3’s last layer and compute attention statistics following
Easi3R’s formulation. For correspondence-trained models
like VGGT and DUSt3R, this approach successfully high-
8


lights dynamic regions because their attention learns cross-
frame matching patterns during training. However, DA3
was trained with depth objectives (Ldepth, Lray, Lpcd, Lpose)
that optimize for geometric consistency rather than corre-
spondence. As shown in Figure 3, DA3’s attention maps
are uniformly distributed across the scene and do not corre-
late with ground truth dynamic masks.
This motivates our design choice: rather than analyzing
attention patterns, we construct motion signals through ex-
plicit feature comparison. Our best-match feature search
asks whether each patch can find a similar appearance any-
where in neighboring frames. Static surfaces find consis-
tent matches regardless of camera motion, while dynamic
objects exhibit low match scores. This approach achieves
29.65% J-Mean using only the motion prior, compared to
attention-based detection which achieves only ∼8%, which
is barely above random.

Our key contributions are best-match feature

object identification. Dyna3 achieves 65.04% J-Mean on
DAVIS-2016, outperforming state-of-the-art VGGT4D by
+5.5 percentage points while running up to 13× faster

This efficiency enables dense

allowing Dyna3 to achieve competitive pose accuracy
where correspondence-based methods run out of memory.

B. Progressive Dynamic Mask Refinement
Figure 5 illustrates how Dyna3 progressively refines dy-
namic object detection through each pipeline stage. We pro-
vide detailed explanations of how each visualization is gen-
erated and analyze how the results connect to our method
architecture.
DA3 Attention Map. Following Easi3R’s formulation [4],
we extract cross-attention maps from DA3’s last global
layer. For each pixel in the target frame, we compute atten-
tion statistics over reference frames: µ (maximum attention
value indicating match confidence) and σ (attention spread
indicating uncertainty). The dynamic score is computed as
(1 −µ) · σ, where low match confidence and high spread
suggest dynamic content. However, as shown in Column 3,
DA3’s attention produces uniformly distributed responses
across the scene. This failure is a result of DA3’s training
objective: DA3 [10] is trained with depth consistency losses
(Ldepth, Lray, Lpcd, Lpose) rather than correspondence super-
vision (Ltrack), so its attention encodes depth relationships
instead of cross-frame matching patterns.
Motion Map Si. Instead of directly analyzing attention,
we construct motion signals through explicit feature com-
parison. For each pixel u in frame i, we search for its best-
matching location across temporal neighbors using cosine
similarity between DA3’s patch features (Eq. 2 in main pa-
per). The motion map Si aggregates these scores across all
pixels. Static surfaces find consistent matches regardless of
camera motion, producing low scores, while dynamic ob-
jects exhibit high scores due to appearance changes across
frames. As shown in Column 4, Si successfully highlights
dynamic regions but includes background noise from tex-
tured areas.
Refined Motion Map ˜Si.
The refined motion map ˜Si
masks Si to SAM3 instance regions, combining our motion
signals with semantic boundaries. SAM3 provides precise
instance masks {M k
i } guided by VLM-generated prompts,
and ˜Si restricts the motion signal to these semantic regions.
As shown in Column 5, this refinement eliminates back-
ground noise while preserving motion information within
object boundaries. The improvement from Si to ˜Si demon-
strates the value of semantic guidance: motion detection
alone may confuse texture variance with actual movement,
while instance-level reasoning could isolates true dynamic
objects.
Merged Dynamic Mask Mi.
The final mask Mi is
produced by motion-aware instance selection.
For
each
instance
k,
we
compute
the
dynamic
score
µ(k) = meanu∈M k
i Si(u) and select instances exceed-
ing threshold θ. As shown in Column 6, Mi provides clean
binary segmentation that closely matches ground truth.
This stage resolves ambiguity when SAM3 segments mul-
tiple instances of the same category. Only instances with
high motion scores are included, effectively distinguishing
moving objects from static ones.
The progressive improvement from Si to ˜Si to Mi validates
our pipeline design. Each component addresses a specific
limitation: best-match feature search overcomes DA3’s lack
of correspondence-trained attention, VLM-guided SAM3
segmentation provides semantic boundaries that motion sig-
nals alone cannot produce, and motion-aware instance se-
lection resolves category-level ambiguity.
Our method
achieves strong results on sequences with clear, distinct sub-
jects (camel 97.4%, dog 93.6%, blackswan 90.4%), while
still facing challenges on small or distant objects (kite-surf
35.1%) where both motion detection and instance segmen-
tation become less reliable.

These objectives encourage geo-

where attention should focus. The resulting attention pat-

than point-to-point correspondences.
Implication for Motion Detection.
The absence of cor-
respondence supervision in DA3 renders attention-based
motion detection ineffective. Applying Easi3R’s formula-
tion to DA3’s attention produces uniformly distributed re-
sponses unrelated to dynamic content, as demonstrated in
Figure 5. This motivates our approach: rather than analyz-
ing attention patterns, we construct motion signals through
explicit feature comparison via best-match search, which
does not rely on correspondence-trained representations.
D. Additional Implementation Details
Beyond the settings described in Section 4.1, we use tem-
poral neighborhood size |Ni| = 4 frames for motion score
computation, dynamic score threshold θ = 0.3 for instance
selection, and processing resolution of 504 pixels on the
longer side.
We exploit SAM3’s session reuse to cache
video features across multiple text prompts, yielding 4×
speedup over per-prompt re-encoding.
