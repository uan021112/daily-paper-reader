## ABSTRACT 

Recovering divergence-free velocity fields from sparse and corrupted observations requires reconciling physical consistency, expressive representations, and robust-ness to missing data. We introduce DFK-Former , a set-to-field transformer that reconstructs divergence-free velocity fields from sparse, noisy, and irregularly sam-pled 3D observations. The model is inherently invariant to the number and ordering of input samples and predicts the parameters of a divergence-free kernel representa-tion, producing continuous, mesh-free velocity fields that satisfy incompressibility exactly by construction rather than approximately through soft constraints or projec-tion operators. Unlike existing divergence-free kernel approaches, which estimate kernel coefficients via a separate optimization for each field, DFK-Former pre-dicts these coefficients directly in a single forward pass. This eliminates costly iterative fitting while providing a learned prior that enables accurate reconstruc-tion even when observations are insufficient to uniquely determine the field. To further enhance representational power, we introduce adaptive and anisotropic divergence-free kernels that preserve exact incompressibility guarantees while capturing more complex flow structures. Extensive experiments across varying levels of sparsity, noise, and occlusion, including transfer to real Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) turbulence data, demonstrate substantial gains in both accuracy and efficiency. DFK-Former reduces endpoint error by up to 4.6 × compared to per-field divergence-free-kernel optimization, while reconstructing each field in 

7.5 ms instead of 9.8 s, yielding a speedup of more than three orders of magni-tude. These results establish direct, feed-forward prediction of divergence-free kernel parameters as a practical and effective paradigm for physically consistent velocity-field reconstruction from sparse observations. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reconstructing dense, physically plausible velocity fields is a fundamental challenge in experimental fluid dynamics and simulation-driven graphics, with applications ranging from flow characterization and numerical-model validation to data-driven simulation. Particle Imaging Velocimetry (PIV) and its variants measure flow by tracking passive tracers (Adrian & Westerweel, 2011; Scarano, 2013; Xiong et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Alternative approaches infer velocity from light-transport measurements (Atcheson et al., 2008; Ji et al., 2013) or directly from RGB video using physical constraints (Eckert et al., 2019; Zang et al., 2020). Sensor-network approaches, in turn, infer flow fields from sparse spatial observations (Erichson et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2026). Regardless of the modality, the measurements are sparse, incomplete, and contaminated by noise, making recovery of the underlying velocity field a challenging task but ideally suited for machine learning. In many fluid-flow settings, the underlying velocity field is approximately incompressible and should therefore have zero divergence (i.e., divergence-free). However, in practice, measured observations

As a result, reconstructing the physically admissible flow involves estimating the divergence-free component of the observations, typically through the Leray (Helmholtz–Hodge) projection: v∗ = Pdiv-free (u). Although straightforward in a densely sampled setting, the problem becomes more challenging when observations are sparse and corrupted. Recent approaches to divergence-free reconstruction address complementary aspects of this problem. Divergence-Free Kernels (DFKs) (Ni et al., 2025) represent velocity fields as linear combinations of compactly-supported matrix-valued basis functions obtained by applying differential operators to Wendland polynomial C4 (Wendland, 1995). These kernels guaranty exact incompressibility by construction, while yielding mesh-free and continuously queryable velocity representations. However, DFKs are fitted on a per-instance basis. Consequently, every reconstruction requires the solution of a new optimization problem from scratch, incurring a high computational cost without learning prior knowledge about the distribution of physically plausible flows. In contrast, HDNet (Qi et al., 2024) learns a Helmholtz decomposition from data using a convolutional architecture, effectively capturing statistical regularities of flow fields through a learned prior. Yet its divergence-free output is only approximate, relying on discrete grid-based operations without exact guarantees, and the method assumes dense observations on a structured 2D grid. To the best of our knowledge, no existing method unifies learned flow priors with exact divergence-free guarantees for sparse, unstructured 3D observations. Recent advances in permutation-equivariant architectures, including Set Transformer (Lee et al., 2019), Perceiver IO (Jaegle et al., 2022), and VGGT (Wang et al., 2025), have demonstrated the ability to infer structured geometric representations directly from unordered sets of observations in a single forward pass. Building on this idea, we propose DFK-Former , which couples a transformer-based set encoder with a divergence-free kernel representation. Given an unordered set of observations O =

{(pm, um, c m)}Mm=1 consisting of 3D positions, noisy velocity measurements, and confidence scores, 

DFK-Former predicts the parameters ( Θ) of a DFK representation vΘ ≈ v∗. The resulting velocity field is exactly divergence-free by construction, remains mesh-free and continuously queryable, and can be reconstructed in a single forward pass without per-instance optimization at inference time. The main contributions of this work are as follows: • We introduce DFK-Former , which combines learned flow priors with exact divergence-free guarantees for sparse, unstructured 3D observations by predicting kernel weights, locations, and support radii directly, without per-instance inference optimization. • We develop anisotropic divergence-free kernels that capture coherent flow structures and improve reconstruction at matched model capacity. • We extend the model to unsteady flows using alternating spatial and temporal attention with Fourier-in-time kernels, preserving exact incompressibility at every instant. • We establish a comprehensive evaluation framework and demonstrate gains across sparsity, noise, and occlusion, including on real DNS data. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Flow Reconstruction from Sparse Observations. Recovering dense velocity fields from incomplete and noisy measurements is a central problem in fluid dynamics and scientific machine learning. In experimental fluid mechanics, PIV and its extensions estimate flow fields by tracking tracer particles across image sequences (Adrian & Westerweel, 2011; Scarano, 2013; Wang et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2021). Related approaches reconstruct fluid motion from light transport measurements, scalar transport observations, or monocular RGB videos (Atcheson et al., 2008; Ji et al., 2013; Eckert et al., 2019; Zang et al., 2020). Across modalities, measurements suffer from limited coverage, occlusions, artifacts, and noise. Classical remedies use interpolation, variational optimization, or data assimilation; recent learning-based methods reconstruct fields from sparse sensors, images, or scattered samples (Erichson et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2026). A closely related direction represents physical quantities using continuous coordinate-based neural fields, enabling mesh-free reconstruction from sparse observations (Sitzmann et al., 2020; Tancik et al., 2020). Applications include Neural Flow Maps (Sahoo et al., 2022) and Physics-Enhanced Implicit Neural Representations (Shen et al., 2025). However, these methods solve a separate optimization problem for each scene and enforce 2Preprint physical constraints only approximately. In contrast, our approach learns a direct mapping from sparse observations to velocity fields while targeting exact incompressibility. 

Divergence-Free Representations and Physical Constraints. Many learning-based approaches incorporate physical consistency through regularization terms, projection operators, or physics-informed objectives (Raissi et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021). For incompressible flows, several methods encourage divergence-free solutions via soft penalties, projection operators, or vector-potential pa-rameterizations (Raissi et al., 2020; Cai et al., 2021; Tompson et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026; Chang et al., 2022; Lyu et al., 2024). A complementary line of work encodes incompressibility directly in the representation itself, either through divergence-free neural architectures based on differential-form parameterizations (Richter-Powell et al., 2022) or through divergence-free radial ba-sis functions and matrix-valued kernels that guarantee analytically divergence-free vector fields over the continuous domain (Narcowich & Ward, 1994; Wendland, 1995). Building on this foundation, Divergence-Free Kernels (DFKs) (Ni et al., 2025) introduced a compactly supported, continuously queryable representation for incompressible velocity fields with exact physical guarantees. Subse-quent studies have explored integrating DFKs into broader fluid reconstruction (Tao et al., 2026) and simulation (Xing et al., 2026) pipelines. Unlike prior DFK-based approaches, which solve an optimization problem independently for each scene, our method learns to predict kernel parameters directly from observations, combining exact incompressibility with feed-forward reconstruction. 

Set-Based Geometric Inference and Operator Learning. Learning-based reconstruction replaces expensive per-instance optimization with a predictor trained across instances (Garnelo et al., 2018; Gordon et al., 2019; Cranmer et al., 2020; Marino et al., 2018), and has proven effective for inverse problems and implicit representations (Siahkoohi et al., 2021; Tancik et al., 2021). Since sparse flow measurements form unordered sets rather than structured grids, architectures that process them should be permutation-invariant or equivariant by construction. Deep Sets (Zaheer et al., 2017) established this paradigm, and attention-based architectures such as Set Transformer (Lee et al., 2019) and Perceiver IO (Jaegle et al., 2022) extended it to model interactions among set elements. Building on these ideas, recent transformers infer continuous fields or 3D structure from irregular observations, spanning scattered-data interpolation and operator learning (Ding et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024), as well as feed-forward 3D reconstruction from sparse views (Wang et al., 2025). Similarly, operator-learning methods such as DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021) and Fourier Neural Operators (Li et al., 2021) learn mappings between function spaces and have achieved strong results in fluid modeling. Several approaches leverage these ideas for sparse-sensor flow reconstruction (Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Dang & Nguyen, 2026). These methods regress field values without building physical constraints into the representation, so incompressibility is satisfied only approximately. In contrast, DFK-Former infers, in one forward pass, the parameters of a continuous divergence-free kernel representation with a permutation-equivariant transformer.

## 4 METHOD 

Given a sparse, noisy, and unordered set of velocity observations O = {(pm, um, c m)}Mm=1 , our goal is to reconstruct a continuous divergence-free velocity field. Instead of fitting a divergence-free kernel field independently for each observation set, we learn a direct mapping from observations to kernel parameters: Fθ : O → Θ, where θ denotes the network parameters and Θ the DFK representation parameters introduced in Section 3. The predicted parameters are then evaluated through Eq. 3, guaranteeing incompressibility by construction. As illustrated in Fig. 1, DFK-Former combines a Perceiver-style transformer with a structured divergence-free kernel decoder. Rather than directly predicting velocities, the network predicts the parameters of a DFK field, yielding a physically interpretable latent representation in which each latent token corresponds to a kernel in the final velocity reconstruction. 4.1 DFK-F ORMER ARCHITECTURE

Fixed-Kernel DFK Prediction. We first consider the DFK representation of Eq. 3 with fixed kernel locations and support radii. Kernel centers are initialized using Poisson-disk sampling over the reconstruction domain (Ni et al., 2025), while support radii follow a predefined (optionally multi-scale) configuration. The prediction heads decode kernel coefficients and a global mean-flow component from the transformer outputs: ωk = fω (qk) and ¯v = fmean (r). The reconstructed field vΘ is then obtained from Eq. 3. This formulation replaces per-instance estimation of DFK coefficients with a learned predictor. For fixed kernel locations and support radii, the field is linear in {ωk} and ¯v, and these parameters can be recovered by least-squares fitting under dense, clean observations. In the sparse, noisy, and partial-observation regime, however, the inverse problem becomes ill-conditioned. DFK-Former instead learns a data-driven prior that directly predicts the kernel coefficients and mean-flow term from observations in a single forward pass.

Training Data Generation. All training examples are generated on the fly rather than drawn from a fixed dataset. For each training step, we sample a clean divergence-free target field v∗ together with a corrupted observation set O. This procedure prevents memorization of a finite training set and allows continuous variation of flow complexity, sampling density, and corruption severity. Our primary field generator samples Gaussian random fields on a periodic Fourier grid and projects them onto the divergence-free subspace using an exact Helmholtz decomposition in Fourier space,

Corrupted observations are generated by adding a controlled irrotational component and Gaussian noise, then applying sparse sampling and occlusion masks. The target remains the clean divergence-free field v∗. To complement the spectral generator, we introduce a Composite Analytic-Flow (CAF) benchmark, which randomly combines multiple analytic flows, including Lamb–Oseen vortices, Taylor–Green vortices, ABC flows, shear layers, and vortex rings, with randomly sampled mixing weights. These flows introduce coherent structures that are difficult to obtain from Gaussian random fields alone. 

Capacity-Matched Sampling. Since DFK-Former predicts a continuous field representation, su-pervision is performed at randomly sampled spatial query points rather than on a fixed grid. During training, we randomize the number of observations, sampling patterns, noise levels, and flow spectra to expose the model to a wide range of reconstruction conditions. To avoid introducing irreducible approximation error, the spatial spectrum of the generated fields is band-limited according to the representational capacity of the kernel basis (Appendix C.3). This ensures that training targets remain expressible by the chosen kernel configuration, allowing optimization to focus on inference from sparse observations rather than compensating for unattainable high-frequency content.

## 5 RESULTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We evaluate DFK-Former on 582 synthetic and real incompressible flow fields, drawn from four groups: (1) Gaussian Random fields, (2) the CAF set from Section 4.3 (drawn from the training distribution), (3) five analytic families with closed-form ground truth (Taylor–Green, Lamb–Oseen, vortex ring, shear layer, ABC), and (4) four real-DNS JHTDB sets (Li et al., 2008) (Isotropic turbulence, Forced MHD turbulence, Channel Flow, and Transitional Boundary Layer). Inputs are corrupted with varying sparsity ( M ∈ [187 , 4086] ), noise ( RMS ∈ [0 , 15%] ), irrotational contamination ( χ ∈ [0 , 1] ), and occlusion (0–35%), while targets remain clean Leray-projected velocity fields. For temporal reconstruction, we evaluated on real DNS sequences, comprising 60 time-resolved JHTDB flows with three unseen midpoint frames per sequence. 

Metrics. Performance is evaluated through reconstruction accuracy (EPE, relative L2, MAE, cosine similarity, angular error), physical consistency (divergence), and computational efficiency (latency, throughput, memory, FLOPs, and parameter count). 

Baselines. We compare DFK-Former against optimization-based methods (Wendland RBF scalar interpolation, SIREN/Curl-SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020), vector-potential INRs (Richter-Powell et al., 2022), and DFK fitting (Ni et al., 2025)), operator-learning approaches (FNO and DeepONet), and physics-constrained models (PINNs with soft divergence penalties, and HDNet-3D 2). We report two DFK variants: fixed-kernels , which fits only kernel weights on a predefined kernel layout, and 

adaptive-kernels , which jointly optimizes kernel positions, radii, and weights for each field. For the temporal extension, we compare against Space-Time (ST-) SIREN/Curl-SIREN, per-frame DFK fitting, and the shared-kernel sequence fitting approach proposed by Ni et al. (2025). 5.4 ABLATIONS 

Representation vs. Backbone. To isolate the role of the DFK representation, we replace the DFK parameter head with a decoder that directly predicts velocity values while keeping the backbone and training protocol unchanged. The direct regressor achieves similar accuracy to DFK-Former (EPE 0.127 vs. 0.120 ) at comparable latency ( 6.8 vs. 7.5 ms), but produces substantial divergence (∥∇· v∥2 = 0 .26 ). This indicates that reconstruction accuracy primarily stems from the learned prior, while the DFK output space enforces incompressibility without sacrificing accuracy. Projecting the regressed field into the same DFK basis restores exact divergence-free structure but increases error (EPE 0.165 ) and runtime ( 29 ms), highlighting the benefit of directly predicting DFK parameters. 

The Role of Attention. Replacing the attention-based encoder with permutation-invariant DeepSets (Zaheer et al., 2017) pooling while keeping all other components fixed significantly degrades reconstruction quality (EPE 0.558 → 0.832 , angular error 42 ◦ → 76 ◦). Although evaluated in a reduced-scale setting, this result suggests that modeling interactions between observations is essential, whereas simple symmetric pooling is insufficient despite preserving permutation invariance.

## 6 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We presented DFK-Former , a set-to-field transformer that predicts the parameters of a divergence-free kernel representation from sparse, noisy, and irregular observations. The resulting velocity fields are exactly divergence-free, mesh-free, and continuously queryable by construction, while avoiding 9Preprint per-instance optimization at inference time. Experiments across diverse reconstruction settings demonstrate that direct prediction of divergence-free kernel parameters combines the accuracy and physical guarantees of kernel-based representations with the efficiency and robustness of learned flow priors. Anisotropic kernels and temporal modeling further extend the framework to more complex spatial and temporal flow structures while preserving exact incompressibility. Limitations include the use of synthetic training data and the limited exploration of larger model and kernel capacities. Although the learned representation generalizes to DNS turbulence without retraining, broader evaluation on experimental measurements and larger-scale studies are needed to better characterize robustness and scaling behavior. ## D FROM THE ORIGINAL DFK R EPRESENTATION TO OURS : A F ITTING STUDY 

We analyze the proposed modifications to the divergence-free kernel (DFK) representation of Ni et al. (2025) using fitting experiments only . Least-squares fits are performed on clean divergence-free fields without learning, corruption, or sparse observations, making the results properties of the representation itself. The study evaluates four modifications: (i) an explicit mean-flow term, (ii) spacing-relative support radii with strong kernel overlap, (iii) a multi-scale radius mixture, and (iv) anisotropic kernels. D.1 PROTOCOL 

All experiments use a common protocol. Targets comprise band-limited spectral flows (Gaussian Random fields), analytic divergence-free fields, and DNS snapshots from JHTDB (Li et al., 2008). Coefficients are fitted by ridge least squares, reporting the best ridge value, and evaluated on query points disjoint from those used for fitting. Because the representation is linear in {ωk} and ¯v for fixed centers, radii, and shapes, the resulting solution is the optimal approximation within the selected basis. All methods share identical Poisson-disk center placement (Bridson, 2007), use float64 precision, and enforce divergence-freeness by construction. D.2 A CONSTANT MEAN -FLOW TERM

D.5 WHAT FITTING ALONE DOES not JUSTIFY : ADAPTIVE PLACEMENT 

Placement is the primary source of remaining approximation error in the fitting study, motivating the adaptive strategy of Section 4.1. An oracle experiment that optimizes kernel centers and radii directly against the target field yields mixed results: improvements appear on flows requiring precise localization (isotropic turbulence 0.107 → 0.097 , forced MHD 0.161 → 0.126 ), while performance can deteriorate when the fixed basis already provides a good representation (channel 0.025 → 0.043 ,transitional boundary layer 0.043 → 0.056 ), highlighting the difficulty of the underlying non-convex optimization. Furthermore, adaptive placement increases the parameter count from three kernel weights to seven parameters per kernel, making reliable estimation substantially more data demanding. Together, these results suggest that adaptivity is valuable, but should be learned from data rather than optimized independently for each field. D.6 KERNEL NORMALIZATION WITH PER -KERNEL RADII 

When kernel radii vary, the DFK operator acquires an additional factor of σk−2 from the chain rule applied to the normalized coordinate r = ∥z∥/σ k. Divergence-freeness is preserved regardless of this factor, but omitting it alters the relative amplitudes of kernels with different radii. While inconsequential when a single radius is used throughout, correct normalization becomes essential in our setting because the representation combines multiple radius scales (Section D.3) and predicts radii adaptively for each example. We therefore include the σk−2 factor so that kernels with different support sizes contribute at consistent physical scales, ensuring comparable parameterizations across both multi-scale and adaptively predicted radii.

Exact incompressibility does not recover accuracy. Despite becoming exactly divergence-free, the projected models remain significantly behind DFK-Former, by factors of 2.10 × (FNO) and 3.57 × (DeepONet) in macro EPE. 

Projection helps most datasets. The aggregate degradation is largely driven by a single failure mode. Across the eleven evaluation sets (Table 16), projection improves EPE on 8/11 datasets for FNO and 7/11 for DeepONet, including all four real-DNS datasets. The macro-level penalty originates almost entirely from the shear-layer case, where FNO degrades from 0.333 to 0.420 and DeepONet from 0.399 to 0.486 . Excluding this dataset, projection changes macro EPE by only 

−0.3% for FNO and +0 .7% for DeepONet. Ablating the pipeline shows that the degradation arises from the projection itself rather than the spectral read-out. For FNO on the shear layer, spectral evaluation alone increases EPE by only 1.0% (0.333 → 0.336 ), while the remaining degradation is introduced by the projection. This behavior reflects the periodic-domain assumption of FFT-based solenoidal projection: the shear-layer example contains a strong discontinuity across the periodic boundary, which is interpreted as an irrotational component and consequently removed. In contrast, 

DFK-Former is mesh-free and imposes no periodic-boundary assumption .

F.5 GENERALIZATION 

We evaluate generalization along four axes: unseen sparsity, unseen noise, unseen flow families, and zero-shot transfer to real DNS data. For unseen sparsity and noise, DFK-Former, FNO, and DeepONet are retrained under matched restricted training distributions and evaluated outside the training range (Table 20). For unseen families, training is restricted to Gaussian-random-field and ABC flows, with all remaining coherent flow families withheld (Table 21). Real-DNS transfer requires no additional training, as none of the methods observe JHTDB data during training. 

Corruption-level extrapolation. Table 20 shows that DFK-Former generalizes well beyond the sparsity and noise levels observed during training. Restricting training to denser observations increases error by only 0.5% at the most sparse evaluation point ( M = 64 ), while restricting training to σrel ≤ 10% noise increases error by 3.1% when evaluated at 20% noise. Similar behavior is observed for restricted FNO and DeepONet models, indicating that all three architectures are relatively insensitive to moderate extrapolation along these corruption axes. Compared with the matched restricted baselines, DFK-Former consistently achieves the lowest error across sparsity levels and remains the strongest method under severe noise. The relative ranking changes little compared to the full-range models, suggesting that performance differences are not driven by exposure to the test corruption levels during training.
