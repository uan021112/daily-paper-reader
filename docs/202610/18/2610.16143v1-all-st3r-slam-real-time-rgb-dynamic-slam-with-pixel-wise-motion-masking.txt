# All-St3R SLAM: Real-time RGB Dynamic SLAM with Pixel-wise Motion Masking 

Yewon Kim 1, Paul Hongsuck Seo 2, Sang Chul Ahn 3† 

Abstract — SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) is a core technology in autonomous systems that enables them to estimate their own position and build a map of unknown environments simultaneously. SLAM systems in dynamic envi-ronments are of great practical importance. However, existing dynamic SLAM approaches still have several limitations like over-removal and high computational costs. To address these limitations, we propose a real-time RGB Dynamic SLAM framework that precisely removes moving objects at the pixel level without relying on predefined labels or segmentation models. Our system combines pixel motion predicted by ego-motion using DUSt3R with point tracking from All-Tracker, removing only actually moving pixels by analyzing discrep-ancies between the two motion sources. Unlike object-level masking methods, our approach eliminates only currently moving regions, allowing temporarily static parts to be utilized for tracking. Operating at approximately 10 FPS, the system achieves real-time performance on standard 30 FPS video streams by subsampling every 3 frames. Relying solely on RGB inputs, this significantly improves upon state-of-the-art dense dynamic SLAM approaches (0.5–3 FPS), presenting a highly practical solution. I. INTRODUCTION For embodied AI systems, such as service robots, au-tonomous drones, and mobile manipulators, Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) has become a funda-mental perception module in real-world applications [1], [2]. However, real-world environments often contain moving objects, motivating the development of SLAM systems that can operate robustly even in dynamic environments. Recent dynamic SLAM research has proposed two main strategies: (1) joint tracking of camera and dynamic objects, and (2) dynamic removal. Although tracking-based methods [3], [4] provide comprehensive scene-level understanding, they are often too computationally expensive. Consequently, the removal strategy—masking dynamic regions—has be-come the standard practical approach. Existing removal-based approaches, however, face signifi-cant trade-offs. Early Geometric methods [5] are lightweight but remain sparse, while object-level and hybrid approaches [6], [7], [8] rely on heavy segmentation or detection priors.

Thus, we propose a dense, geometry-driven dynamic RGB SLAM system capable of per-pixel motion masking that achieves real-time performance without relying on heavy object-level segmentation or detection. We integrate dense 3D matching from DUSt3R [9] with lightweight pairwise point tracking adapted from All-Tracker [10]. Our system offers several crucial advantages:

> •

Robust tracking via progressive dynamic masking: 

Unlike single-step removal strategies that risk discard-ing useful correspondences, our method progressively accumulates motion inconsistencies across frames. Combined with lightweight geometric fil-tering, our system operates at approximately 10 FPS. By subsampling every 3 frames, this throughput en-ables real-time performance on standard 30 FPS video streams. Our approach prioritizes real-time performance and high tracking accuracy using solely monocular RGB inputs. II. RELATED WORKS Recent research in dynamic SLAM has developed two main strategies: estimating the motion of objects (Tracking) and identifying dynamic objects as outliers (Removal). While joint tracking (e.g., VDO-SLAM [3] and CubeSLAM [4]) enables comprehensive scene-level understanding, it incurs substantial computational costs and struggles with non-rigid or unstructured motion. We focus on the removal-based approaches, which can be broadly grouped into three paradigms: geometric, object-level masking, and hybrid. Early Geometric methods rely purely on motion cues—identifying inconsistencies across views using epipo-lar geometry, point tracking residuals, or depth disagreement [5]. For example, Tan et al. [5] detect dynamic points by evaluating their inconsistency with the dominant scene motion. These early feature-based techniques are lightweight and training-free, but mostly sparse. 

Object-level masking methods employ segmentation net-works (e.g., Mask R-CNN), object detectors (e.g., YOLO), or semantic-rich embeddings (e.g., DINOv2 [11]) to mask po-tentially dynamic object classes. DynaSLAM [6] removes all features belonging to categories that are potentially dynamic, such as people or vehicles. However, this approach leads to systematic over-removal: stationary people or parked cars are discarded. Additionally, semantic segmentation or detection inference carries high computational costs. DynaSLAM oper-ates significantly below real-time performance (typically 1–2 FPS) [6]. Furthermore, these approaches may have difficulty generalizing to novel object categories outside their training vocabulary (e.g., COCO-80 [12]), such as electric scooters or delivery robots. 

Hybrid methods integrate object-level masking with geo-metric motion verification to overcome the core limitations of purely object-level masking methods. Rather than removing all instances of potentially dynamic classes, hybrid systems like DOG-SLAM [7] first detect candidate objects and then verify geometric consistency to identify genuinely dynamic elements. WildGS-SLAM [8] uses semantic-rich feature em-beddings as an implicit prior for uncertainty estimation and 3D Gaussian Splatting [13] for photometric-geometric con-sistency checking. It demonstrates strong trajectory accuracy on benchmark datasets but suffers from severe computational bottlenecks. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

Our proposed framework is a real-time monocular dense SLAM system designed for robust tracking and mapping in dynamic environments. We build upon MASt3R-SLAM [14] as our baseline, but introduce a fundamental shift in how geometric correspondences are constructed and dynamic outliers are handled. We adopt DUSt3R [9] as our core geometric backbone in place of MASt3R, benefiting from its faster inference and lower memory footprint. To compensate for the absence of MASt3R’s learned matching features, we integrate alightweight pairwise adaptation of All-Tracker [10], which supplies dense image-level point tracking as a comple-mentary correspondence source. Crucially, this combination serves a dual purpose: beyond providing high-fidelity cor-respondences, it enables purely geometry-driven dynamic masking without the need for any segmentation or detection models. 

A. System Overview 

To clarify our pipeline, we define three core data terms:  

> •

Frame ( If ): The current RGB input.  

> •

Keyframe ( Ik): A historically significant frame used as a reference for tracking and backend graph optimization.  

> •

Canonical Keyframe Pointcloud ( ˜Xkk ): A 3D pointmap representation of a keyframe defined in the global coordinate frame, initially generated via DUSt3R self-coupling inference ( Ik, Ik). Each keyframe maintains its own independent pointcloud. The pipeline processes each incoming frame If by pairing it with the current keyframe Ik. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the system proceeds through four sequential stages. 

Dense Geometry and Tracking Extraction. If and Ik are jointly fed into DUSt3R, which outputs two aligned dense pointclouds—the frame pointcloud X ff and the keyframe pointcloud Xkf —along with per-point geometric confidence scores and 3D matching. In parallel, All-Tracker processes the same image pair to produce dense point tracking(2d matching) with associated confidence and visibility scores. 

Confidence Reweighting. Raw confidence scores are re-fined based on 2D-3D consistency, high-frequency textures, and depth boundary filtering to suppress noisy geometries and enhance discriminative anchors for subsequent tracking stages. (Section III-B) 

Pixel-wise Dynamic Masking. Independent motions are filtered at the pixel level by computing the discrepancy be-tween ego-motion predictions and All-Tracker point tracking. This progressive frame-by-frame masking isolates moving outliers while preserving valid static points within dynamic entities. (Section III-C) 

Pose Tracking and Map Update. The refined, dynamic-free correspondence set is used to estimate the camera Sim (3) pose of If with respect to the canonical keyframe pointcloud ˜Xkk via weighted optimization. If the number of valid correspondences falls below a threshold, the current frame is promoted to a new keyframe. When a new keyframe Fig. 2. is created, the backend performs loop closure detection and global pose graph optimization to maintain a globally consistent 3D map. 

B. Confidence Reweighting 

Confidence Reweighting adjusts the importance of pixel correspondences before tracking, as accurate camera pose estimation relies heavily on precise pixel-wise corre-spondences between the current Frame and the refer-ence Keyframe. Following the optimization formulation of MASt3R-SLAM [14], these confidence scores serve directly as weights to guide the optimizer toward high-confidence matches. Unlike MASt3R-SLAM, we propose an enhanced correspondence generation strategy that fuses geometric matching from DUSt3R [9] with robust point tracking from All-Tracker [10]. By combining geometric consistency with visual tracking cues, this fusion provides confidence-aware weighting that allows the optimizer to prioritize the most trustworthy matches for pose estimation. First, we utilize DUSt3R [9] to predict dense pointmaps for the input image pair ( Ik, If ). Specifically, we obtain X ff ,the pointmap of the current frame in its own coordinate sys-tem, and Xkf , the pointmap of the keyframe image expressed in the current frame’s camera coordinate system. To establish correspondences from these pointmaps, we adopt the iterative projective matching method proposed in MASt3R-SLAM [14]. Iterative projective matching yields a set of dense geometric matches, denoted as Mgeo , which are derived purely from the 3D geometric priors provided by DUSt3R [9]. In addition, we obtain point tracking corre-spondences between Ik and If using All-Tracker, which are denoted as M f low .We then perform a consistency check between geometric matches Mgeo and point tracking matches M f low . For a given pixel u in the keyframe, if the correspondence predicted by geometric matching lies within a small radius of the correspondence predicted by point tracking, the match is verified as 

∥Mgeo (u) − M f low (u)∥2 < δpixel . (1) 

δpixel denotes a pixel consistency threshold that defines the maximum allowable distance between the two correspon-dences (set to 2 pixels in our implementation). Matches that satisfy this condition are considered to be both geometrically valid and visually consistent. For these verified matches, we increase their associated confidence scores (originally from DUSt3R [9]) by a scalar factor γ > 1.0 (empirically set to 1.2 in our implementation). This weighting strategy ensures that these highly reliable correspondences exert a stronger influence during the subsequent pose optimization phase. Beyond match-level verification, we further refine corre-spondence reliability through pixel-level geometric and pho-tometric analysis. Dense depth estimation models, including DUSt3R [9], often produce artifacts known as flying pixels near object boundaries. These artifacts arise from depth discontinuities and introduce erroneous 3D geometry, which degrades correspondence reliability. To mitigate this issue, we compute the normalized Laplacian of the depth map to detect abrupt depth variations. Pixels with large normalized responses are identified as flying pixels, and their influence in pose optimization is suppressed through reduced confidence weighting. This geometry-aware filtering limits the impact of noisy boundary regions during camera pose estimation. Conversely, to prioritize reliable tracking regions, we apply a Laplacian operator directly to the RGB images (Ik and If ) to extract high-frequency textural details. Pixels exhibiting strong edge responses represent distinct, traceable visual features. We apply a confidence boost to these high-frequency regions. 

C. Dynamic Masking 

Our system identifies dynamic regions at the pixel level by analyzing the discrepancy between the pixel motion predicted by ego-motion and the observed point tracking motion. We formulate the problem under a static world assumption where pixel motion between the Keyframe and the current Frame is governed solely by the camera’s relative pose and the scene geometry. First, we estimate the relative pose 

Trel ∈ SE (3) between the Keyframe pointcloud Xkf and the current Frame pointcloud X ff . Since DUSt3R [9] outputs dense pointmaps for both images that are intrinsically aligned in the Frame’s coordinate system, and given the one-to-one correspondence between 2D pixels and 3D points, we can straightforwardly solve for the camera extrinsic parameters using the PnP-RANSAC method. This formulation is ro-bust against the circular dependency between ego-motion estimation and dynamic detection. Since DUSt3R implicitly learns rigid view transformations through its cross-attention layers, it naturally assigns lower confidence scores to dy-namic objects that violate epipolar geometry constraints [15]. Consequently, the network outputs point clouds by focusing the alignment on the static background, ensuring a robust relative pose estimation even in dynamic environments, as shown in Section IV. Assuming all points in the Keyframe are static, we project each 3D point Pk from the Keyframe Xkf onto the image plane of the current Frame using the estimated relative pose Trel 

and intrinsic matrix K. The predicted ego-motion flow vector 

Fego (uk) for a pixel uk is defined as the displacement induced by the camera motion, 

Fego (uk) = upro j − uk, (2) which represents the expected motion of static structures in the scene under camera ego-motion. Simultaneously, we obtain the Point Tracking from All-Tracker [10], which captures the actual visual displacement of pixels based on image appearance. Let utrack be the pixel location in If

corresponding to uk as tracked by All-Tracker. The point tracking vector FpointTracking (uk) is defined as: 

FpointTracking (uk) = utrack − uk, (3) which reflects the true motion of pixels, including the move-ment of dynamic objects. For static points, the ego-motion predicted flow and point tracking motion should coincide (i.e., Fego ≈ FpointTracking ). However, for dynamic objects, a discrepancy arises because the object’s independent motion is not accounted for in the ego-motion prediction, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We define the flow discrepancy ∆F(uk) as the Euclidean distance between the two flow vectors, 

∆F(uk) = ∥Fego (uk) − FpointTracking (uk)∥2, (4) where pixels with large discrepancies indicate independent motion. Pixels where ∆F(uk) exceeds a dynamic threshold 

τdyn are classified as dynamic, where τdyn is set to seven times the median absolute deviation in ∆F(uk). This check effectively isolates independent motion (e.g., a moving arm) while preserving the static parts of an object (e.g., the human torso), maximizing the number of usable tracking features. To handle dynamic objects robustly over time, we employ a frame-by-frame mask accumulation strategy (Fig. 3). For the current Keyframe Ik, we maintain a cumulative dynamic mask Dyn k. When a new Frame If arrives at time step t,the dynamic mask dyn t is computed based on the motion discrepancy ∆F between Ik and If , and then aggregated via a union operation: Dyn k ← Dyn k ∪ dyn t . This process repeats for all subsequent frames arriving until a new Keyframe is selected, accumulating all dynamic regions observed relative

to Ik. This accumulation ensures that any dynamic region identified at any point remains masked, preventing transient static observations of moving objects from corrupting the map. Furthermore, we employ a moderate frame subsampling strategy that not only enables real-time performance but also improves dynamic region removal. By increasing the temporal baseline between Ik and If , it magnifies the pixel displacements of slow-moving objects. This increased dis-placement makes subtle motion more distinguishable from the static background, allowing the dynamic masking module to more reliably identify and remove slowly moving regions that might otherwise be regarded as static. 

D. Lightweight Two-Frame All-Tracker 

The original All-Tracker is designed for offline processing of long video clips, relying on computationally intensive temporal attention mechanisms across large sliding windows. This design induces high latency and memory usage, making it unsuitable for real-time SLAM. To address this, we re-configured the network architecture for lightweight pairwise inference. We bypassed the long-range temporal attention modules, constraining the model to accept only adjacent frame pairs (Ii, Ii+1) as input. This modification significantly reduces the computational graph and inference time while retaining the model’s superior capability in handling fine-grained pixel displacements. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluated the performance of the proposed SLAM sys-tem through extensive experiments on multiple dynamic and static datasets. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Our system was implemented in PyTorch. For all input images, we resized them such that the maximum dimension was 512 pixels. To achieve effective real-time performance, we processed the input sequences with a 3-frame subsampling strategy.

DISCUSSION & LIMITATIONS Although our framework demonstrates robust tracking in diverse dynamic environments, certain limitations remain. First, since DUSt3R does not provide absolute metric scale, if a real-world metric scale is required, it can be achieved by introducing a scale alignment module or inte-grating metric priors from an IMU to anchor the initial scale. Second, while our motion discrepancy check filters out moving objects regardless of their speed, a vulnerability can occur in slow-moving scenarios when a new keyframe is created. If an object moves slowly and a sudden change in camera motion triggers a new keyframe before the object is completely masked, unmasked residuals will remain in the map. These residuals will not be removed and will cause map corruption unless a loop closure occurs near that viewpoint. Furthermore, increasing the subsampling factor is not always beneficial because it reduces the overlapping static background region due to large baseline changes, ultimately leading to camera tracking failure. Lastly, DUSt3R occasionally reconstructs a dynamic ob-ject as a flat texture on the static background (e.g., as if printed on a wall). This lack of geometric separation pre-vents the masking module from detecting sufficient motion discrepancy, failing to filter out the dynamic object. VI. CONCLUSIONS In this work, we presented a real-time monocular dense SLAM framework capable of robust operation in dynamic environments without relying on computationally expensive priors or depth sensor inputs. By synthesizing the dense ge-ometric capabilities of DUSt3R [9] with our lightweight ver-sion of All-Tracker [10], we introduced a dense, geometry-driven approach to dynamic masking. Our core contribution lies in the pixel-wise motion dis-crepancy check, which identifies independent object motion by comparing the pixel motion predicted by ego-motion with the observed point tracking motion. This strategy addresses the limitations of existing object-level masking and hybrid methods: it minimizes over-removal by preserving static regions within moving objects and generalizes effortlessly to unseen object categories. Ultimately, our system significantly outperforms existing dense dynamic SLAM systems in speed while maintaining competitive trajectory accuracy.
