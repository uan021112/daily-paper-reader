Abstract: Vision-based tactile sensors enable fine-grained perception for dexter-
ous robotic manipulation, but current marker-based designs tightly couple sur-
face texture and contact force signals within a single observation. Existing data-
driven decoupling methods rely on serial mask-based inpainting, which suffers
from mask-sizing dilemmas caused by physical indentation effects, error reten-
tion, and lack of real paired data for supervision. To address these challenges, we
propose DeTac, a novel dual-branch generative framework that concurrently ex-
tracts clean shading and marker images from a single mixed tactile image. DeTac
leverages full-image synthesis to naturally bypass mask dependency and intro-
duces a cross-branch synergistic optimization that enables mutual supervision, ef-
fectively preventing error retention. Furthermore, we construct a strictly aligned,
real-world paired tactile dataset using custom hardware to provide high-quality su-
pervision. Extensive experiments demonstrate that DeTac achieves state-of-the-art
decoupling accuracy, significantly facilitating downstream surface reconstruction
and force estimation tasks while exhibiting cross-sensor generalization.
Keywords: Vision-based tactile sensor, robot tactile sensing, image generation

Introduction
Vision-based tactile sensors [1] transform local contact signals into visible elastomer deformations
under structured light, providing robots with fine-grained tactile perception crucial for dexterous ma-
nipulation [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. While markerless sensors capture high-resolution shading images to
reconstruct surface geometric textures [9, 10], their lack of spatial references hinders direct contact
force estimation [11]. To address this, marker-based sensors embed physical markers into the elas-
tomer [11, 12], tracking their displacements to calculate force [13, 14]. However, this design tightly
couples texture and force signals within a single observation, as the markers inherently obstruct the
surface shading that encodes geometric textures [15, 16]. Consequently, accurately decoupling these
signals to reliably recover both surface texture and contact force has become a critical challenge.
While early attempts at signal decoupling relied on complex hardware for mode switching [17],
the rise of generative models [18, 19, 20] has enabled data-driven decoupling directly from a sin-
gle mixed image. Existing data-driven methods [21, 22] typically employ a serial pipeline: they
first estimate a mask to locate the markers, then apply image inpainting [23, 24] to infer from sur-
rounding regions the occluded shading region as if the markers did not exist. However, we observe
that the physical embedding of markers, whether via sprayed ink [9] or 3D-printed pins [25], not
only occludes the regions beneath the markers but also induces indentation artifacts (Fig. 1(a) vs.
(b)): It alters the surface normals of the surrounding elastomer and therefore disrupts the perceived
geometry texture at these regions. This phenomenon results in three major limitations in current
pipelines (Fig. 1(c)). First, mask-based inpainting is confused by mask-sizing dilemma: Failing to

To overcome these limitations, we propose DeTac, a dual-branch framework that Decouples mixed
Tactile observations to a marker-free shading image and a marker image (Fig. 1(d)). Unlike mask-
based inpainting, DeTac treats shading restoration and marker extraction as concurrent full-image
generation tasks, adaptively modifying regions without relying on predefined masks, thus naturally
bypassing the mask-sizing dilemma. We further introduce a cross-branch synergistic optimization
that connects the two branches by enforcing spatial consistency between the predicted marker image
and the restoration-induced shading changes, enabling mutual supervision that refines both predic-
tions. Finally, to provide high-quality supervision, we construct a real-world and strictly aligned
paired dataset using a specialized mechanical structure that captures exactly matched data on the
same sensor before and after marker fabrication. In summary, our main contributions are:
• We propose DeTac, a novel dual-branch generative framework that uses full-image synthesis to
decouple mixed tactile signals, jointly predicting clean shading and marker images.
• We introduce a cross-branch synergistic optimization that enables mutual supervision between
shading restoration and marker extraction, effectively preventing error retention.
• We construct a strictly aligned, real-world paired tactile dataset using a specialized mechanical
structure, providing high-quality supervision for data-driven methods.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that DeTac achieves state-of-the-art accuracy in both shading
restoration (with an over 7 dB improvement in PSNR over TacDiff [21]) and marker extraction.
Furthermore, the precisely decoupled signals significantly facilitate downstream tasks, including
surface reconstruction and force estimation, while exhibiting generalization capabilities across tac-
tile sensors.

2.2
Conditional Image-to-image Generation
Conditional image-to-image generation synthesizes a target image guided by dense spatial or struc-
tural cues from a source image. Early work tackled this through generative adversarial network
(GAN) [38]-based frameworks [24, 39], which framed the task as a direct domain mapping prob-
lem. However, due to the training instabilities inherent to GANs [38], recent approaches have largely
shifted toward diffusion models [18]. Among these, masked inpainting methods regenerate only
specific regions by conditioning on the unmasked surrounding context, either by concatenating the
masked image and mask as additional input channels [40] or by employing a separate feature extrac-
tor [41]. However, these methods require the undesired and desired signal components to be strictly
separable at pixel level; otherwise, information loss or quality degradation is inevitable. Full-image
generation methods avoid this limitation by generating the complete output directly from the source
condition. ControlNet [42], T2I-Adapter [43], and Uni-ControlNet [44] inject features extracted
from the condition via dedicated encoder modules into a frozen diffusion backbone. Palette [45] and
InstructPix2Pix [46] instead train end-to-end, concatenating the source image with the noisy latent.
SDEdit [47] and Plug-and-Play Diffusion Features [48] manipulate a pre-trained model’s sampling
process without training, while BBDM [49] reformulates the diffusion trajectory as a stochastic
bridge between domains. We adopt full-image generation because the indentation artifacts cannot
be separated at pixel level, making mask-based inpainting fundamentally unsuitable.

3.1
Concurrent Dual-Branch Prediction
Given a mixture image Imix ∈RH×W ×3 captured inside a marker-based visual-tactile sensor (the
image is RGB as the structured-light illumination is color-coded), our goal is to decouple the coupled
tactile signals into a marker-free shading image ˆIsha ∈RH×W ×3 for surface geometry reconstruc-
tion and a marker image ˆImar ∈[0, 1]H×W for marker tracking and force estimation. ˆImar is a soft
mask image to account for defocus blur and ambiguous marker boundaries near the image periphery.
Previous methods rely on serial pipelines [21], where a marker mask is estimated and used for mask-
based inpainting to obtain the shading image, therefore suffering from the aforementioned mask-
sizing dilemma and error retention. To address these limitations, DeTac adopts a concurrent dual-
branch architecture: The shading restoration branch performs conditional full-image generation to
recover the shading image, while the marker extraction branch directly predicts the marker image.
Shading Restoration Branch. The shading restoration branch recovers the marker-free shading
image ˆIsha by restoring marker-occluded regions and suppressing marker-induced indentation ar-
tifacts in the mixed input Imix. We formulate this task as conditional full-image generation with a
denoising diffusion model [18], allowing the model to exploit all available information in Imix rather
than relying on a predefined inpainting mask and discarding the marker-affected pixels as in mask-
guided inpainting methods [21]. Specifically, our U-Net denoiser Dθ [50] takes the noisy target xt,
the timestep t, and the mixed image Imix as input at each discrete diffusion timestep t ∈{1, . . . , T}:

Surrounding Indentation Loss.
In tactile images, marker-induced artifacts are not limited to
marker-covered pixels. Due to local indentation introduced during marker fabrication, the neighbor-
hood around each marker is often difficult to restore faithfully. To emphasize these critical regions,
we construct an annular neighborhood around the predicted marker regions and apply focused su-
pervision there. Specifically, let Bmar = I(ˆImar > τ) denote the binarized marker prediction with
threshold τ. The annular neighborhood around the predicted marker regions is defined as
Rmar = Dilate(Bmar, r) \ Bmar,
(7)
where Dilate(·, r) denotes morphological dilation with radius r, and \ denotes set difference. Based

Lsur = ∥(ˆIsha −Isha) ⊙Rmar∥1/ (∥Rmar∥1 + δ) ,
(8)
where ⊙denotes element-wise multiplication, Rmar is broadcast to RGB channels, and δ is a small
constant. This objective concentrates restoration supervision on marker neighborhoods, encouraging
faithful reconstruction in the regions most affected by marker-induced indentation.

alone insufficient for marker supervision. To provide complementary guidance, we introduce a
change-marker consistency loss Lcon that exploits the correlation between marker locations and
restoration-induced appearance changes to provide mutual supervision.

Real-World Paired Dataset Collection
Effectively training DeTac requires paired supervision for both shading restoration and marker ex-
traction. Existing synthetic datasets [21] naively superimpose markers onto marker-free images,
which ignores real marker-induced indentation artifacts and introduces a synthetic-to-real gap. To
bridge this gap, we design a hardware-assisted pipeline to collect a real-world, well-aligned paired
dataset. Specifically, we collect tactile data by repeatedly pressing the sensor against a diverse set
of objects at multiple predetermined contact poses. Let Io,k

Hardware-Assisted Paired Data Acquisition. To obtain highly aligned shading and mixed tactile
images, we divide data acquisition into two stages. First, we collect marker-free shading images Isha
using a pristine sensor elastomer. We then fabricate markers directly on this elastomer surface to
reduce fabrication discrepancy and acquire corresponding mixed images Imix. Nevertheless, naively
repeating the pressing process can still introduce pose drift. To ensure consistent contact conditions
across the two acquisition stages, we design a mechanical alignment structure, as shown in Fig. 3.
Assisted by this structure, we follow the same pose sequence before and after marker fabrication,
enabling high-quality paired data acquisition of real physical contacts. We provide visualization of
our 3D printed hardware for data collection, including the MC-Tac [52] sensor with alignment pins
(Fig. 4a), the collection pad (Fig. 4b), and a subset of texture units (Fig. 4c).
Pseudo GT Marker Image Generation. After image acquisition, we generate pseudo GT marker
images Imar to supervise the marker extraction branch. To balance annotation efficiency and pixel-
level accuracy, we use a prompt-based segmentation pipeline. We first manually annotate the center
point of each marker in Imix. These point prompts, together with the original visual features, are then
fed into SAM [53] to segment the marker and produce Imar. Compared with fully manual pixel-wise
annotation, this strategy provides reliable spatial priors with substantially lower annotation cost.
Marker-Aware Augmentation for Cross-Sensor Generalization.
To reduce overfitting to the
fixed hardware specifications of our custom sensor and improve generalization across different
marker-based tactile sensors, we introduce marker-aware spatial augmentations. Different sensor
designs may exhibit different marker scales, densities, and boundary ambiguities due to variations
in elastomer thickness and internal illumination. To simulate these variations, we apply aligned
transformations to the triplet (Imix, Isha, Imar). Specifically, we use cropping followed by resizing
to mimic changes in marker scale, and apply blurring guided by Imar to simulate different levels of
defocus. This strategy improves the cross-sensor robustness of DeTac.

4.1
Experimental Setup
Implementation Details. The whole pipeline is trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU for
300 epochs with a batch size of 2, which takes approximately 14 hours. The shading restoration and
marker extraction branches are optimized with learning rates of 10−5 and 10−4 respectively. For the
loss part, we use λmar = 0.5, λsur = 0.05, and λcon = 0.05.
Baselines.
For shading restoration, we compare DeTac against:
the fast-marching-based
TELEA [23]; two masked inpainting models, LaMa [54] and the marker removal model TacD-
iff [21], both trained on synthetic data generated from our dataset following the latter’s method-
ology; Stable Diffusion (SD) 1.5 [20] fine-tuned on our data for full-image prediction; and three
deterministic supervised baselines trained on the same paired dataset: a U-Net regression model,
Pix2Pix, and a multi-task dual-head network. Our TacDiff reproduction strictly follows its original
training protocol, using synthetically superimposed markers. We further evaluate enlarged-mask in-
painting as an alternative strategy by dilating the marker masks to cover the surrounding indentation
artifacts and applying them to both TacDiff and LaMa. For marker extraction, we evaluate DeTac
against UVTac [17] and the segmentation model SAM 3.1 [55].

the GT shading images in a deep feature space, as well as Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and
Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) [58] for pixel-level fidelity and structural similarity. For
marker extraction, we introduce the Marker Centroid Localization Error (MCLE). For each marker,

Evaluation Results
Shading Restoration.
As shown in Tab. 1, TELEA [23], TacDiff [21], and LaMa [54] exhibit
poor performance across all metrics. Stable Diffusion [20] achieves lower FID and KID but suffers
from degraded PSNR and SSIM. The deterministic supervised baselines perform worse in percep-
tual and distributional similarity, indicating that DeTac better restores realistic tactile appearance and
fine details. Enlarged-mask TacDiff and LaMa still achieve lower performance than DeTac. DeTac
achieves the best performance across all metrics. Qualitatively, as shown in Fig. 5, TELEA [23],
TacDiff [21], and LaMa [54] leave visible residual marker traces, while Stable Diffusion [20] in-
troduces unnatural stripe artifacts that distort the shading geometry. Our method produces the most
natural results, closest to the GT.
Marker Extraction. As shown in Tab. 2, UVTac [17] and SAM 3.1 [55] exhibit significantly higher
error compared to DeTac. Fig. 6 also shows that UVTac [17] and SAM 3.1 [55] produce coarse and
incomplete masks, whereas DeTac successfully extracts all marker points with precise boundaries.
Cross-sensor Generalization. We evaluate generalization of DeTac on 1) the same type of sensor
with different internal illumination, and 2) a different type of sensor [9]. As shown in Fig. 7, DeTac
restores high-quality shading in both settings, demonstrating cross-sensor generalization.
4.3
Ablation Study
We ablate each loss term in Sec. 3.2 by removing them individually. As reported in Tab. 1 and
Tab. 2, removing Lsur causes the most severe degradation in shading quality on both novel poses
and novel objects, confirming that explicit supervision on marker-neighboring regions is critical.
Removing Lcon also degrades shading and slightly increases marker extraction error, indicating that
the change map provides indispensable guidance for marker extraction. The full model achieves the
best performance across all metrics, demonstrating that the two loss terms work synergistically.

5
Conclusion
We presented DeTac, a dual-branch full-image generation framework that concurrently recovers
shading and marker components from a mixed tactile observation. A cross-branch synergistic opti-
mization mechanism couples both branches for mutual supervision, while a hardware-assisted align-
ment structure provides paired real-world data. Experiments confirm DeTac’s state-of-the-art decou-
pling accuracy, strong downstream performance, and effective cross-sensor generalization.
Limitations. First, the shading branch requires hundreds of sequential sampling steps at inference,
limiting real-time deployment on latency-sensitive manipulation tasks; promising remedies include
accelerated samplers or distillation-based compression. Second, our data acquisition pipeline is
irreversible: markers are fabricated directly onto the elastomer and cannot be removed, so scaling the
dataset requires manufacturing new sensors, which incurs additional hardware and labor overhead.
Third, the current data collection setup does not support controlled shear forces, limiting the training
data distribution.

In this appendix, we provide additional implementation details in Sec. A, including a detailed de-

A
Additional Implementation Details
A.1
Network Architecture Specifications
Shading Restoration Branch. The shading restoration network Dθ adopts a U-Net architecture
with 22 residual blocks [60]. It directly processes a 6-channel input — formed by the pixel-space
concatenation of the mixed image Imix and the noisy target xt — to output a 3-channel noise pre-
diction. Specifically, the encoder features 4 downsampling stages with block output channels of (64,
128, 256, 256), where each stage contains 2 residual blocks. The bottleneck consists of 2 residual
blocks. The decoder features 4 corresponding upsampling stages; to effectively fuse the concate-
nated skip connections, each decoder stage employs 3 residual blocks. Every residual block utilizes
2 convolutional layers paired with Swish activations [61] and Group Normalization [62]. Finally, a
terminal convolutional layer projects the features to the target 3-channel noise space.
Marker Extraction Branch. The marker extraction network Mϕ is constructed as a lightweight
fully convolutional network [51] comprising three layers. The channel dimensions progress as 3 →
32 →64 →1, with each convolution using a kernel size of 3, a stride of 1, and a padding of 1.
ReLU activations [63] follow the first two convolutions, while the final convolution outputs logits
without an activation function. The soft marker image is obtained by applying a sigmoid function
to these logits. During training, BCEWithLogitsLoss directly operates on the logits for numerical
stability.
A.2
Additional Training Details
For the denoising diffusion forward process, the total number of discrete training timesteps is set to
T = 1000. The morphological dilation radius used to define the annular neighborhood Rmar for
the surrounding indentation loss Lsur is set to r = 2 pixels. Optimization is performed using the
AdamW optimizer [64] with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ϵ = 10−8, and weight decay = 0.01.
A.3
Data Collection Details
During paired-data collection, 20 alignment pins fix pose and press depth, while a 200 g weight
maintains consistent press force. To evaluate the effect of press-force variation, images captured
with 200 g and 500 g weights at identical depths yield a per-pixel MAE of 3.072 (0–255 scale),
indicating a constant 200 g force does not significantly limit dataset visual diversity. Markers are
directly embedded in the same elastomer to minimize fabrication-induced pattern shifts. In Fig. A1,
the left shading image, middle mixed image, and right image (their pixel-wise difference) reveal
indentation artifacts around markers.
