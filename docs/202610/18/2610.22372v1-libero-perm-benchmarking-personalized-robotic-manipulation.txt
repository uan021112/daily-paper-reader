Abstract
Personalization is essential for general-purpose robots to transition into household
environments and achieve true practicality. While general robotic manipulation
has been extensively studied, personalized robotic manipulation (PeRM) lacks a
controlled benchmark for separating task completion from preference satisfaction.
We introduce LIBERO-PeRM, a benchmark for preference learning in embodied
agents. LIBERO-PeRM studies five questions: 1) how policies follow preferences
in zero-shot settings; 2) how they learn single preferences from demonstrations; 3)
how efficiently they acquire preferences as demonstrations increase; 4) how learned
preferences generalize across layouts and tasks; and 5) how policies compose
preferences and behave under conflicts. We propose a controlled formulation and
taxonomy of observable preferences in simulation, covering motion, skill, and user-
profile choices. This formulation is restricted to language-expressible preferences
that can be instantiated in LIBERO without changing the task goal, evaluated
automatically, and reused across tasks. Building on LIBERO, our procedural
pipeline generates paired demonstrations with and without preferences. We create
five task suites (312 tasks) with high-quality demonstrations, rich metadata, and
preference-satisfaction metrics. Experiments show that current VLA policies
struggle with zero-shot personalization, while SFT makes preferences learnable.
Mixed training achieves better no-preference retention than sequential adaptation
and improves performance with contextual prompts compared with preference-only
training, but can weaken motion-preference learning; preference generalization,
decomposition, and conflict handling remain difficult. The code, datasets, and
demos can be found on the project website: https://libero-perm.github.io/.

Introduction
Household manipulation has been a long-standing goal in robotics and embodied AI, with the promise
of improving daily life by enabling robots to perform chores from natural language instructions (e.g.,
“put an apple, bread, and milk on my plate”). Recent progress in generalist robotic manipulation
has been driven in large part by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [1, 2, 3, 4], which show
strong generalization across diverse multimodal inputs [5, 6] and heterogeneous embodiments [7, 8].
However, real-world deployment demands more than completing the nominal task: embodied agents
must also execute tasks in ways that align with the preferences, habits, and constraints of individual
users, a requirement we refer to as personalization.
Preferences introduce diverse execution requirements for identical goals. For instance, placing a mug
might require a specific handle orientation or a designated landing spot. Consequently, personalized
manipulation must satisfy user-specific constraints on both what strategies are selected and how
actions are performed. In this framework, preferences are treated as a learning dimension parallel to

Nevertheless, progress on personalized robotic manipulation (PeRM) remains impeded by two major
gaps. First, PeRM lacks a shared formulation and standardized evaluation protocol across prior work.
Existing studies define personalization through diverse problem settings, including implicit preference
alignment [9, 10], multi-user style adaptation [11], user-specific object-receptacle pairing [12], and
helpful action prediction [13]; they also specify preferences through natural language [13, 14], few
user examples [9], explicit preference functions [15], human speech [5], or numerical preference
vectors [11]. Evaluation is similarly fragmented, spanning task success, user studies [9, 11, 13, 14],
and preference-based scoring functions [16, 17], making methods difficult to compare and failure
modes hard to diagnose. Second, existing benchmarks typically emphasize high-level navigation or
semantic selection over fine-grained manipulation preferences and multi-preference conflicts. The
closest preference-based RL benchmark [18] lacks robotic preference-learning datasets and multi-
preference evaluation. Other personalization benchmarks focus on object navigation or high-level
receptacle selection [19, 20], while recent memory-based manipulation benchmarks [21, 22] do not
distinguish task-instance memorization from genuine user-preference-driven personalization.
To address these gaps, we introduce a novel Personalized Robotic Manipulation Benchmark
(LIBERO-PeRM), built using the popular LIBERO [23] framework. We elevate user preferences
to a fundamental building block alongside tasks and layouts. Following the taxonomy of human
preferences [24], we define a controlled taxonomy of observable preferences in simulation, covering
Motion Preference (trajectory constraints), Skill Preference (task strategies), and User Profile (seman-
tic biases). The benchmark is restricted to preferences that can be expressed in language, instantiated
without changing the original task goal, evaluated automatically, and reused across tasks. Within this
scope, we provide 37 single-preference instances and 18 two-preference combinations.
LIBERO-PeRM features an extensible procedural generation pipeline that automatically instantiates
tasks and their preference-conditioned variants within shared layouts. To support systematic training
and evaluation, the pipeline simultaneously produces explicit preference satisfaction metrics and rich
subtask-level metadata, including language instructions, goal images, and physical attributes.
For benchmarking, we construct five task suites with 312 preference-conditioned tasks and pipeline-
generated, human-reviewed demonstrations, each targeting a core capability in personalized manipu-
lation (Figure 1, top). LIBERO-SinglePref evaluates single-preference learning; LIBERO-PrefScale
evaluates scaling across data sizes, tasks, and layouts; LIBERO-PrefConflict evaluates preference-
preference and task-preference conflicts; LIBERO-PrefCompose evaluates multi-preference com-
position; and LIBERO-ComprePref evaluates realistic preference-conditioned reasoning based on

We use LIBERO-PeRM to conduct an initial study of five key research topics in personalized
manipulation (see bottom row of Figure 1): 1) how policies follow preferences in zero-shot settings;
2) how policies learn single preferences from demonstrations; 3) how efficiently policies acquire
preferences as demonstrations increase; 4) how learned preferences generalize across layouts and
tasks; and 5) how policies compose multiple preferences and behave under conflicts. These topics
organize our experiments and highlight the core capabilities that robots must develop for reliable
personalization.
Our contributions and findings are as follows:
• We introduce a controlled formulation and taxonomy of observable preferences in simulation,
together with an automated pipeline that produces preference-conditioned demonstrations, rich
metadata, and preference-satisfaction metrics.
• We construct five task suites, LIBERO-SinglePref, LIBERO-PrefScale, LIBERO-PrefConflict,
LIBERO-PrefCompose, LIBERO-ComprePref, comprising 312 preference-conditioned tasks with
pipeline-generated, human-reviewed demonstrations and evaluation protocols for controlled per-
sonalization studies.
• Extensive experiments show that current VLA policies struggle with zero-shot personalization,
while SFT makes preferences learnable. Mixed training improves no-preference task success
relative to sequential adaptation and contextual-prompt task success and preference satisfaction
relative to preference-only LoRA, but weakens motion-preference learning; broader layout diversity
and matched no-preference data also help, while preference generalization, decomposition, and
execution under conflicts remain difficult.

Related Work
Robotic Manipulation Benchmark. Many benchmarks have been developed to evaluate different
aspects of robotic manipulation, including cross-embodiment generalization [7, 32, 33], generalized
manipulation [26, 37, 39], long-horizon reasoning [27, 28, 31, 34] [40, 41], and sim-to-real trans-
fer [25, 29, 30]. Among them, LIBERO [23] has emerged as a widely adopted benchmark due to its
high-quality demonstrations and standardized data generation pipeline, primarily focusing on lifelong
learning and task generalization. However, existing benchmarks largely focus on task completion,
without explicitly modeling user-specific preferences. In contrast, our LIBERO-PeRM targets person-
alized manipulation with preference-aware task suites that disentangle task success from preference
satisfaction. Table 1 compares commonly used robotic manipulation benchmarks.

preference satisfaction matrices. For hierarchical VLAs [69, 70], LIBERO-PeRM further provides
subtask decompositions and subgoal-image annotations, covering the key signals needed to evaluate
personalized manipulation.

The LIBERO-PeRM Benchmark
This section introduces the four components of LIBERO-PeRM. Section 3.1 defines what preferences
are; Section 3.2 describes the procedural preference data generation pipeline; Section 3.3 defines the
five task suites used for benchmarking; and Section 3.4 defines the evaluation methodologies.
3.1
Preference Formulation
Preference describes the relative desirability among alternatives [71]. Personalized robotic manipula-
tion, therefore, requires a policy to select, among feasible trajectories that complete a requested task,
one that also satisfies the user’s preference. A preference is a variable independent of the task goal:
it constrains how the same goal is achieved rather than replacing the goal. Natural language is our
interface for both variables, but this independence distinguishes the setting from merely redefining a
language-conditioned task. We organize preferences by the part of execution they constrain: motion,
skill, or subtask-level choices. Specifically, they are:
• Motion Preference. This type of preference constrains the physical properties of the trajectory
while preserving task completion. For the task “put the mug on the table”, the preference “avoid
a particular area of the table” induces a trajectory-level distance constraint, requiring the robot to
keep the end-effector and mug away from that region during execution. We denote this kind of
preference as Pmotion.
• Skill Preference. This type of preference alters the execution behavior of a specific skill c while
preserving task completion. For the task “put the mug on the table”, the preference “orient the
handle outward” induces a pose-constrained placement strategy. When executing the placement

3.2
Preference-Conditioned Data Generation Pipeline
Building upon the LIBERO framework, LIBERO-PeRM provides an extensible procedural data-
generation pipeline for preference learning and evaluation, as illustrated in Figure 3; model-assisted
generation and validation details are given in Appendix C. The pipeline consists of three stages: (1)
Preference Selection and Layout Modification; (2) Preference-Conditioned Subtask and Trajectory
Generation; and (3) Satisfaction Metric Generation.
Preference Selection and Layout Modification. The user first selects a preference instance and
an initial LIBERO layout [23]. Given these inputs, the pipeline uses a VLM [72] to minimally
modify the layout so the selected preference can be operationalized while limiting changes to the
original scene. For Motion Preference and Skill Preference preferences, the layout and task language
remain unchanged; the VLM only selects the target object or region, e.g., choosing the stove as the
object to avoid for “avoid certain object”. For User Profile preferences, which may change the
5


subtask plan, the pipeline makes minimal edits, such as adding a red object of the same category for a
red-object preference, so preference-conditioned and no-preference trajectories can be generated in
the same setting. The task language is also abstracted to cover both settings, ensuring that behavioral
differences are driven by the preference rather than unrelated scene changes.
Preference-Conditioned Subtask and Trajectory Generation. After the layout and task language
are fixed, the pipeline uses a VLM with the task language and preference language to generate both
a no-preference subtask plan and a preference-conditioned subtask plan. It then calls a library of
pre-recorded heuristic skills to generate trajectory data. To construct each heuristic skill, we first
collect one human demonstration for the skill, extract key waypoints, and use an OMPL planner [73]
with randomized initial states to generate many successful trajectories. The generated trajectories are
then smoothed; heuristic-skill parameters are manually offset-adjusted when needed. Empirically, we
find more than 72% of skills require no offset adjustment across any task, suggesting the VLM-based
edit is already very effective. Preferences are applied at different stages according to their type: User
Profile affects subtask-plan generation via a prompt; Skill Preference affects skill selection, such as
using pick_handle instead of a regular pick skill; and Motion Preference affects skill-level trajectory
generation, such as reducing OMPL-sampled waypoints for the “speed up” preference.
Satisfaction Metric Generation. After preference-conditioned trajectories are generated, the pipeline
constructs explicit preference satisfaction metrics for each selected preference. The form of each
metric depends on the preference type. For Motion Preference, we directly compute satisfaction
functions from trajectory quantities, such as the distance between the robot trajectory and the target
object for the preference “avoid certain object”. For Skill Preference, we evaluate key waypoints and
use VLM-based image checks to verify skill-specific execution, such as whether the object is grasped
by its handle. For User Profile, we monitor the executed subtask plan, including which objects are
manipulated, to verify that the plan adheres to the profile.
Because User Profile yields only discrete Satisfaction Scores, namely whether the profile-conditioned
subtask is satisfied, whereas Motion Preference and Skill Preference can yield continuous Satisfaction
Scores, we construct a mapping from Satisfaction Scores to Discrete Satisfaction Labels using
trajectory statistics and human inspection; Appendix D details the review protocol. Specifically, each
episode is mapped to a fixed four-level Discrete Satisfaction Label d ∈{0, 1/4, 3/4, 1}, independent
of the number of pipeline-generated subtasks. A continuous Satisfaction Score, such as speed,
is binned by trajectory statistics and then incorporated into this label. Raw Satisfaction Scores
provide denser feedback for training, while Discrete Satisfaction Labels provide a more interpretable
evaluation signal at inference time.
Initial Human Validation. To assess the real-world plausibility of the selected preferences and
the human-likeness of the generated demonstrations, we conducted a survey with 15 participants
using a 10-point scale. Across all preference categories, the demonstrations received an average
human-likeness score of 9.11, and the preferences received an average real-world plausibility score
of 8.35. These results provide initial human validation of the benchmark design; Appendix D reports
the protocol and category-level results.
3.3
Task Suites
Using the pipeline above, we generate five task suites: LIBERO-SinglePref, LIBERO-PrefScale,
LIBERO-PrefConflict, LIBERO-PrefCompose, and LIBERO-ComprePref. We follow LIBERO
terminology and call the inseparable combination of a task instruction, scene layout, and task-goal
condition a mission. A LIBERO-PeRM task pairs one mission with one preference condition,
which may be a specified preference or no preference; the 312 tasks include both preference-
conditioned and no-preference tasks (see Figure 2(b) for the proportions), with more than 100
trajectories per task on average and 40.8K rollout episodes in total. Randomized initial object
positions, paraphrased instructions, and repeated executions increase rollout diversity but do not
create additional tasks. The release prioritizes quality-controlled coverage over claiming the largest
trajectory count; the procedural pipeline can extend it with more initial states and missions. Figure 2(a-
c) shows distributions over preference instances, task suites, and preference types, including no-
preference tasks for comparison. Figure 2(d) shows that episode horizons and trajectory fluency
remain comparable to LIBERO. Each suite isolates a core capability for personalized manipulation,
as summarized in Figure 1.

LIBERO-SinglePref. This suite evaluates single-preference learning and execution. It contains

100 tasks. LIBERO-PrefConflict. This suite evaluates policy behavior when preferences conflict
with other preferences or with the task goal. Preference-preference conflicts pair the same layout and
task with mutually conflicting single preferences, while task-preference conflicts pair no-preference
task data with task-incompatible preference data. It contains 48 tasks. LIBERO-PrefCompose. This
suite evaluates multi-preference learning and preference composition in a controlled two-preference
setting. For each aligned layout-task pair, it provides four conditions: no preference, PA, PB, and
PA +PB, enabling controlled comparisons for composition and multi-preference learning. It contains
64 tasks. LIBERO-ComprePref. This suite rewrites prompts over tasks from the other suites so
the preference must be inferred from contextual language rather than read from a separate explicit
field. It includes explicit task-preference mixtures, where key preference words are preserved, and
implicit preference reasoning, where contextual cues imply the preference without naming it. For
example, “Put both the alphabet soup and the cream cheese box in the basket. Preference: speed
up” becomes “Put both the alphabet soup and the cream cheese box in the basket. Guests will use
them immediately.” Similarly, “Pick the mug by its handle” is conveyed as “The mug just came out
of the microwave—the body is too hot to touch.” ComprePref reuses existing tasks, so its contextual
prompts do not add to the 312-task count or the base-suite counts in Figure 2(a). Appendix E gives a
worked example; Appendix G describes the release and maintenance plan.
3.4
Evaluation
Task and Preference Metrics. We report two types of metrics: task success rate (SR), which
measures task completion, and preference satisfaction (SA), which measures how well a preference
is satisfied using the Discrete Satisfaction Label from Section 3.2. The Discrete Satisfaction Label
for episode n is dn ∈{0, 1/4, 3/4, 1}, and I[G(τn)] is its task-success indicator. We compute

Conflict Policy. For a task-preference conflict, completing the original task takes priority. For
preference-preference conflicts, SA is computed using the higher of the two preference satisfaction
scores for each episode, with task-failure episodes contributing zero satisfaction. These scoring
conventions probe execution under inconsistent instructions; they do not evaluate conflict detection,
preference weighting, or when a policy should ask the user for guidance.
Generalization. We evaluate in-domain, task-or-layout, and preference-level generalization using
SR and SA. In-domain evaluation keeps the training tasks, preferences, and layouts with small
object-position perturbations. Task-or-layout generalization changes either the task or layout, while
preference-level generalization changes tasks under unseen layouts while keeping the preference
unchanged.
4
Experiments
We conduct experiments on the five task suites introduced in Section 3.3 to evaluate zero-shot
personalization, preference learning ability, preference learning efficiency, preference generalization,
composition, and behavior under conflicts. Our experiments focus on the following research questions:
Q1. Can off-the-shelf policies follow preferences without preference-specific training?
Q2. Can supervised fine-tuning teach policies to satisfy preferences on seen tasks and layouts?
Q3. How does performance change as the number of demonstrations increases?
Q4. Do learned preferences generalize across different generalization levels?
Q5. Can a policy compose multiple preferences, and how does it behave under incompatible
preferences?

Experimental Results
Q1: Zero-shot Personalization. Q1 asks whether off-the-shelf VLA policies can follow user
preferences without preference-specific training. Table 2 evaluates six policies on the LIBERO-
SinglePref task suite. The no-preference column uses the original LIBERO task language with
minimally edited LIBERO-SinglePref layouts.
Finding. Table 2 shows that minimal layout edits from our pipeline preserve no preference zero-shot
task performance, and existing SOTA VLA models fail to follow preferences in the preference-
conditioned zero-shot setting.
Most policies maintain high no-preference SR, with π0.5 reaching 97.6%, indicating that the edited
layouts preserve the LIBERO task distribution. Thus, low SA mainly reflects preference-following
failure rather than layout-editing artifacts. Notably, π0.5 reaches high SR on Motion and Skill
Preference (97.50% and 100.00%), but low SA (24.38% and 25.00%), showing that it completes
tasks while ignoring preference. Executing with a User Profile is harder since it changes the target
object or semantic choice, reducing π0.5 SR to 19.12% and causing near-zero SR for weaker policies.
Q2: Learning Ability. Table 3 reports in-domain and unseen-layout evaluation results under LoRA-
based supervised fine-tuning (SFT). The π0.5-LoRA setting is used only on LIBERO-SinglePref to
validate the effectiveness of our dataset.
Finding. Sequential training degrades no-preference and preference-conditioned performance. Mixed
training instead yields a category-dependent trade-off: it better retains no-preference behavior and
contextual-prompt robustness, but weakens motion preference relative to preference-only LoRA.

Q3: Learning Efficiency.
This question evaluates how data scale affects learning efficiency under the same task and preference
distribution. In Figure 4, the blue curves show in-domain scaling as the number of training demonstra-
tions increases. At the 2.25K scale, L1-L4 isolate data-composition effects under a comparable budget.
L1, L2, and L3 use K = 2, K = 3, and K = 5 layouts, respectively, trading off layout diversity
against demonstrations per layout. L4 uses the same K = 3 layout setting as L2 but adds matched
no-preference missions, testing whether no-preference data helps decouple preference-conditioned
behavior from task/layout cues.
Finding. Across all data scales, in-domain preference-conditioned behavior is learned consistently
well. For in-domain preference learning, increasing layout diversity is more effective than simply
increasing the number of demonstrations. Notably, adding matched no-preference data further
improves learning even without increasing layout diversity, suggesting that such data helps decouple
preference-conditioned behavior from task/layout-specific cues.
Figure 4 provides detailed evidence for these observations. First, the in-domain SR and SA curves
are closely aligned across data scales, indicating strong in-domain preference learning: successful
executions usually also satisfy the specified preferences. Second, at the fixed 2.25K scale, L1-L3 use
the same demonstration budget but increase the number of layouts, and the improvement from L1 to
L3 shows that broader layout coverage is more effective than denser demonstrations within fewer
layouts. Third, L4 uses the same layout setting as L2 but adds matched no-preference missions; its
improvement over L2 suggests that no-preference data benefits learning beyond increasing layout
diversity, likely by helping separate task execution from preference-conditioned behavior.
Q4: Generalization. This question evaluates two forms of generalization: task-and-layout gener-
alization, where either the task or layout changes, and preference-level generalization, where both
tasks and layouts change while the preference remains unchanged. In Figure 4, the red curves
denote unseen-layout generalization, and the green curves denote unseen-layout-with-different-task
generalization. Both settings use the same L1-L4 controlled comparisons at the matched 2.25K scale.
Finding. Preference-conditioned behavior is consistently learned across settings, but jointly achieving
task success and preference satisfaction remains challenging under generalization. Unseen-layout
generalization benefits from increased data scale, whereas generalization to both unseen layouts and
different tasks remains limited. As shown in Figure 4, SR and SA are closely aligned within each
setting, suggesting that successful executions usually also satisfy the specified preference. However,
under generalization, especially unseen-layout-with-different-task, both metrics remain much lower
than in-domain performance. This indicates that learning preference-conditioned behavior is easier
than executing it successfully together with the task under new task-layout combinations.

Q5: Composition and Conflict. This question evaluates whether policies can compose prefer-
ences, decompose a target preference from joint training, and handle preference-preference or
task-preference conflicts. Tables 4 and 5 report the results. For composition/decomposition, Eval
SR/SA denote task success and target preference satisfaction on the evaluation condition. For conflicts,
Pref-Dom. measures the gap between following either preference under a joint prompt; lower values
indicate less consistent preference domination. In task-preference conflicts, Follow Task, Follow
Preference, and Incomplete denote completing the task while violating the preference, following the
preference while violating the task, and satisfying neither, respectively.
Finding. Preference composition is partially learnable, whereas decomposition fails. After learning
PA and PB separately, the policy can compose them into PA+PB with 25.0% Eval SR/SA. However,
when asked to infer PB from PA+PB and PA, the policy obtains 37.5% Eval SR but 0.0% Eval SA,
showing task success does not imply successful preference behavior execution.
Finding. Conflicting preferences reduce performance and have no dominant preference. Under
preference-preference conflicts, conflict-time SR/SA drop to 66.00%/47.75%, and Pref-Dom. is only
11.25%, suggesting no clear preference dominates the other under our either-preference success rule.
Under task-preference conflicts, Follow Task and Follow Preference are close, 33.3% vs. 29.2%, with
37.5% incomplete trials, indicating that conflicts often lead to failed execution rather than task- or
preference-following behavior.
5
Conclusion
In this work, we introduce LIBERO-PeRM, a comprehensive benchmark for personalized robotic
manipulation. By treating preferences as a first-class building block alongside tasks and layouts,
LIBERO-PeRM provides five task suites that isolate and evaluate preference learning under single-
preference, scaling, conflict, composition, and comprehensive preference reasoning settings. Exper-
iments show that current VLA policies struggle with zero-shot personalization, while SFT makes
preferences learnable. Compared with sequential adaptation, mixed training better preserves no-
preference behavior and improves contextual-prompt robustness, but it weakens motion-preference
performance relative to preference-only LoRA. Preference learning benefits more from layout diver-
sity and same-layout no-preference data than denser demonstrations, but generalization to unseen
layouts and different tasks remains difficult. Composition is only partially observed, decomposition
fails, and conflicts often degrade execution instead of producing resolution.
Limitations and Future Work. LIBERO-PeRM instantiates predefined, language-expressible, auto-
matically measurable preferences in simulation. It therefore excludes implicit preference inference,
interaction history, non-linguistic signals, and open-ended or evolving preferences. Its generated
demonstrations may contain pipeline- and skill-library-specific biases despite randomized initial
states, multiple missions per preference, and human review. The current composition suite combines
only two preferences, and the conflict suite does not test conflict detection or clarification. Finally,
the human survey provides only initial validation; no real-robot or longitudinal user study is included.
Future work could extend LIBERO-PeRM in directions ranging from three-or-more-preference
settings to non-linguistic preference signals, human-in-the-loop preference acquisition, preference
weighting and when-to-ask decisions, and real-robot evaluation.

A.1
Ethics Statement
LIBERO-PeRM is built from procedurally generated preference-conditioned tasks, layouts, trajec-
tories, and evaluation signals in simulation. Human feedback is used to review generated data and
validate preference plausibility, but no personal data or real-world user logs are released or used as
policy-training data. Its intended use is research on personalized robotic manipulation, including
preference learning, generalization, composition, and conflict handling. Our dataset construction
process is designed to reduce ethical and legal risk. We build on LIBERO [23] scenes and simula-
tor assets, then generate preference-conditioned variants through minimal layout edits, procedural
trajectory generation, and automatic preference metrics. Preference descriptions, satisfaction rules,
and benchmark annotations are created for research use rather than copied from external products or
commercial systems. Any public release should preserve attribution to upstream assets and follow
the licenses and terms of use of LIBERO and other dependencies. The benchmark also has important
limitations. Our preference metrics only capture observable execution outcomes in simulation and
cannot represent open-ended, private, or highly subjective human preferences. As a result, LIBERO-
PeRM should not be treated as a substitute for human evaluation in safety-critical or real-world
personalized deployment. We intend this benchmark to support controlled scientific evaluation, not
direct real-world decision making.
We take responsibility for the released benchmark artifacts. The LIBERO-PeRM dataset and open-
source code are released under the MIT license.
A.2
Training Details
For non-zero-shot experiments, we further tune π0.5 with LoRA [75]. We scale the training budget
with the number of rollouts: for every 4,000 rollout episodes, we use roughly 45,000 optimization
steps on eight A6000 Ada GPUs with a learning rate of 5×10−5. For the main fine-tuning comparison,
All-Pref denotes one checkpoint jointly trained on Motion, Skill, and User-Profile preference tasks; it
is not an ensemble or an average of category-specific checkpoints. We evaluate each fixed checkpoint
in 10 independent runs and report mean ± standard deviation in Table 3. The variation therefore
reflects randomized evaluation rollouts, not training seeds.
B
Preference Selection Criteria
We select a preference only if it satisfies five criteria: 1) expressible in natural language; 2) can
be grounded in existing LIBERO [23] scenes; 3) satisfiable without changing the task goal; 4)
measurable by an automatic preference metric; and 5) reusable across multiple tasks or layouts.
Examples include the Motion Preference preference “speed up”, measured by execution steps; the
Skill Preference preference “pick up an object by its handle”, measured by waypoints and contact
points; and the User Profile preference “prefer dairy product”, measured by the executed subgoal.
We exclude preferences that require scene-external information, unsafe or task-infeasible behavior, or
subjective judgments that cannot be mapped to observable execution outcomes.

C
Model-Assisted Generation and Validation
We use Claude Code with an Opus-family model and Gemini as backends for three model-assisted
stages. In layout editing, the model receives a BDDL scene specification, preference language, object
geometry, and spatial constraints, and proposes a modified BDDL layout. In personalized planning, it
receives the original task and preference instructions, baseline plan, available objects and skills, and a
scene image, and returns a sequence of (skill, object) pairs plus required, prohibited, and ordering
constraints. In image inspection, independent VLM judgments assess task completion and visually
observable preference compliance from rollout frames.
Inspired by single- and multi-agent evaluations [76, 77, 78], the layout and plan candidates follow
a generate-and-trial loop: each proposal is instantiated and tested in simulation, and any candidate
violating the output format, physical constraints, task-success condition, or preference constraints is
rejected. The observed failure is included when generating a revised candidate. The reported VLM
failure rate in each stage was below 23%. For image inspection, inconsistent independent judgments
are flagged for re-evaluation rather than accepted directly. Image judgments are restricted to visible
properties such as contact location, approach direction, gripper orientation, and final placement.
Motion speed, acceleration, trajectory smoothness, and clearance height are instead measured with
trajectory-based geometric or kinematic metrics.

D
Human Review and Initial User Validation
Construction and cross-review.
Human contributors participated in three stages: 1) providing one reference demonstration for each
newly introduced skill; 2) inspecting sampled generated demonstrations together with their satisfaction
labels and metrics; and 3) rating preference plausibility and demonstration human-likeness. Before
beginning, contributors received common instructions and task-specific training. Contributors were
assigned to different portions of the generated data, after which a second group cross-reviewed the
demonstrations and labels. Disputed cases were jointly re-examined before inclusion; the initial
reviewer disagreement rate was 11.8%. All participants provided informed consent, and contributors
were credited according to their level of involvement.
Survey protocol and results.
For initial user validation, 15 participants rated the selected preferences and their demonstrations on
a 10-point scale along two dimensions: real-world preference plausibility, whether each preference
was plausible and applicable in a household setting; and human-likeness of demonstrations, how
closely the corresponding demonstration resembled how a human would naturally perform the task
while satisfying the preference. Table 7 summarizes 37 preferences in three categories. These results
provide initial evidence of plausibility and human-likeness, but they do not substitute for real-robot,

A Worked Example of ComprePref
Underlying task: Put the white mug on the left plate.
Explicit preference: Pick by the handle.
Input prompt: Put the white mug on the left plate. The mug just came out of the microwave—the body
is too hot to touch.
Implied behavior: Grasp the handle while completing the same placement task.
What stays fixed: The task goal, scene, and preference evaluator. SR evaluates the original placement
goal; SA uses the original handle-grasp preference label with task-success gating.
What changes: The language supplied to the policy conveys the preference through context instead of a
separate explicit preference field.
A second example replaces the explicit preference “speed up” with “Guests will use them immediately”
for the task of putting alphabet soup and a cream cheese box in a basket. The motion preference is still
evaluated from the trajectory, not from a still image. These contextual prompts assess grounding of a
specified benchmark preference; they do not establish recovery of an individual user’s unobserved
preference history.
F
Adding a New Preference
Adding a standard preference requires one human reference demonstration and a natural-language
description of its satisfaction metric for the preference-conditioned generation pipeline, followed
by approximately three to five validation trials for each applicable task. For a preference applied
to roughly five tasks, the observed manual effort is about 20 minutes on average; demonstration
generation then proceeds automatically. This estimate covers preferences expressible through the
current scene representation, skill library, and automatic programmable metrics. Preferences requiring
a new skill or non-observable feedback incur additional work.
For example, to add a preference for grasping a mug by its handle, a user provides one reference
demonstration and a natural-language description of the satisfaction metric: the robot should grasp
the handle rather than the mug body. The original mug-placement goal remains unchanged. The user
then runs approximately three to five validation trials for each applicable task to check whether the
generated trajectories follow the preference and whether the metric correctly evaluates grasp location.
After validation, the pipeline automatically generates additional demonstrations.
G
Benchmark Release and Maintenance
The benchmark has five evaluation suites. The 312 tasks include both preference-conditioned and
no-preference tasks across SinglePref (100), PrefScale (100), PrefConflict (48), and PrefCompose
(64); ComprePref reuses tasks with contextual prompts and does not add new mission-preference
combinations to this count. Demonstrations are generated trajectories used as training/reference data;
evaluation rollouts are executions of the evaluated policy and are not additional benchmark tasks.
The release documentation covers installation, dataset/configuration descriptions, and examples for
generation, training, and inference. The configuration follows the five-suite organization described
here, and the Croissant metadata includes the fields rai:personalSensitiveInformation
and rai:hasSyntheticData. We will maintain the code, datasets, and leaderboard, improve the
documentation based on community feedback, and expand rollout coverage and generation diversity.
