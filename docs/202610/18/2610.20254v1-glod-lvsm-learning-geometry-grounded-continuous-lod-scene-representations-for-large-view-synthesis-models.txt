ABSTRACT
Large view synthesis models (LVSMs) reconstruct scenes from sparse views and
render novel views quickly, but they incur unnecessary storage and rendering
costs due to token redundancy. We present GLOD-LVSM, a compact, geometry-
grounded scene representation for LVSMs with continuous levels of detail. First,
instead of learning a fixed set of scene anchor initializations, GLOD-LVSM learns
a continuous field that initializes a flexible number of anchors to match the desired
budget. A condenser then aggregates scene information into these anchors, with
both the field and condenser trained end-to-end. Second, the condenser infers
reference-token and anchor geometry to guide compression through geometry-
grounded attention, improving rendering quality and enabling frustum culling
during decoding. Compared with existing LVSM scene-compression baselines,
GLOD-LVSM achieves the best trade-off between rendering quality and scene
token count. Compared with the uncompressed base model, GLOD-LVSM reduces
the scene-token count by 33.4% and accelerates rendering by 1.23× with no quality
loss. Under a relaxed quality-loss budget, the reduction increases to 85.5%, with
3.00× rendering speedup. Further analysis reveals that view-synthesis supervision
yields emergent scene depth and self-organized anchors that align with surfaces
and adapt their density to local texture complexity. Beyond rendering, we further
show that our scene representation also supports scene-semantic understanding,
highlighting its potential for broader downstream tasks.

INTRODUCTION
Large view synthesis models (LVSMs) learn a reconstruction prior for novel view synthesis (NVS) (Jin
et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026; Szymanowicz et al., 2026), reducing scene reconstruction to a single
feed-forward pass and enabling high-quality rendering from sparse reference views. Recent high-
quality LVSMs commonly adopt a highway representation, where each reference-image patch is
directly mapped to a scene token (Szymanowicz et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). While this design
preserves detailed appearance information, the resulting scene-token count grows with the number of
reference views, increasing both storage and decoding costs. This tight coupling between image and
scene-representation sizes becomes a bottleneck with more reference views.
In addition to scaling poorly with reference view count, highway representations allocate scene
capacity inefficiently in two ways. First, overlapping 3D regions observed from different views are
stored as separate tokens rather than consolidated. Second, every image patch receives the same
one-token capacity regardless of texture complexity. An ideal LVSM representation should therefore
(1) support flexible scene-token count independent of the input-view count, (2) consolidate multi-view
observations into a unified, scene-centric 3D representation, and (3) allocate capacity adaptively
to scene complexity. Thus, we propose GLOD-LVSM, which provides two key features: (1) a
geometry-grounded scene representation that supports (2) continuous level-of-detail. We refer to
scene tokens in GLOD-LVSM as anchors to emphasize their scene-centric 3D nature.
Different storage and rendering budgets require different scene-token numbers, which correspond to
different levels of scene detail. To support arbitrary scene-token counts and thus continuous levels of
detail (CLoD), we first design an anchor initialization scheme that can generate a flexible number
of tokens. Specifically, we introduce a CLoD scene-anchor initialization that parameterizes anchor
initialization as a continuous field (Sec. 3.2). Sampling this field at different densities generates
different numbers of anchors. This decouples the scene-token count from the reference-view count
and allows our model to operate under different storage and rendering budgets.
A compact scene representation should consolidate multi-view observations and allocate capacity
where it is most useful. To achieve this, we (1) use unified, scene-centric 3D anchors to consolidate
redundant multi-view tokens, and (2) learn the spatial allocation of these anchors end-to-end. Specifi-
cally, we use an end-to-end learned, geometry-grounded condenser that aggregates reference-view
tokens into 3D anchors (Sec. 3.3). The condenser predicts geometry for both views and anchors
and uses their geometric relationships to guide cross-attention. The resulting anchors have 3D posi-
tions, which further enable target-view frustum culling to reduce decoding cost (Sec. 3.4). Both the
geometry and scene-complexity-aware anchor allocation emerge from the NVS objective (Sec. 4.6).
We evaluate GLOD-LVSM across datasets, reference-view counts and scene-token budgets. Quan-
titatively, GLOD-LVSM consistently achieves the best quality–token-count trade-off among the
baselines. Qualitatively, the learned anchors emerge near scene surfaces and allocate higher density
to regions with richer texture, suggesting that NVS supervision and compression alone can induce
a geometry- and complexity-aware efficient scene representation. Beyond rendering, we further
evaluate the learned representation on scene-semantic understanding, demonstrating its potential as a
3D scene representation for broader downstream tasks. Our contributions are threefold:
• Continuous-LoD scene representation. We introduce a scene-conditioned continuous query
field that enables flexible scene anchor budgets independent of the reference-view count.
• Geometry-grounded scene condensation. We learn scene-centric 3D anchors end-to-end,
enabling efficient multi-view consolidation and scene-adaptive capacity allocation.
• Efficient and generalizable scene anchors. GLOD-LVSM achieves the best quality–scene-
token trade-off for rendering and supports scene semantic understanding.

RELATED WORK
2.1
NOVEL VIEW SYNTHESIS
Novel view synthesis (NVS) is a widely studied problem and can be broadly categorized into per-
scene optimization methods and feed-forward generalizable methods. NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2020)
2


Preprint
and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) (Kerbl et al., 2023) achieve photorealistic novel-view synthesis
by optimizing a radiance field or a set of Gaussian primitives for each scene. However, they typically
require time-consuming per-scene optimization and dense image capture to achieve high rendering
quality. In contrast, generalizable NVS methods (Sajjadi et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024; Xu et al.,
2025), including recent Transformer-based approaches such as LVSMs (Jin et al., 2025; Kim et al.,
2026; Sun et al., 2026; Szymanowicz et al., 2026), learn reconstruction priors from large datasets
and can reconstruct unseen scenes from sparse input views in a single feed-forward pass, avoiding
costly dense image capture and per-scene optimization. Our work builds on Transformer-based
generalizable NVS and focuses on learning a compact, geometry-grounded scene representation that
reduces redundancy among scene tokens, thereby lowering storage and rendering costs.
2.2
COMPACT SCENE REPRESENTATION
Compact scene representations have been widely studied for efficient NVS. For example, 3DGS
methods prune or compress Gaussian primitives to reduce representation size while preserving visual
quality (Fan et al., 2024; Fang & Wang, 2024; Lin et al., 2025). Some methods further support
multiple or continuous levels of detail (CLoD), allowing representation complexity to adapt to runtime
rendering budgets (Milef et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2026). Compact scene-token representations have
also been explored for LVSMs. LVSM-ED (Jin et al., 2025) uses a fixed latent bottleneck and cannot
support flexible compression rates. CLiFT (Wang et al., 2025) uses K-means to select representative
reference-view tokens, relying on handcrafted clustering that may be suboptimal. Our work also
targets compact scene representations for LVSMs. Unlike prior methods, we support CLoD and learn
scene anchors end-to-end, enabling flexible compression with less handcrafted design.

GLOD-LVSM: GEOMETRY-GROUNDED CLOD SCENE REPRESENTATION
Fig. 2 illustrates GLOD-LVSM. Given V reference images and their camera parameters, GLOD-
LVSM constructs a compact scene representation with a chosen budget of K anchors. A highway
scene encoder first extracts reference-view tokens (Sec. 3.1). Our anchor initializer then generates
K anchors from a continuous field (Sec. 3.2). These anchors are passed to our condenser, which
produces scene-centric 3D anchors (Sec. 3.3). The anchors will be used to render the RGB images
given the target views (Sec. 3.4). We train GLOD-LVSM end-to-end using the NVS loss (Sec. 3.5).

CONTEXTUALIZED CONTINUOUS FIELD FOR ANCHOR INITIALIZATION
Given reference-view tokens Z, our condenser compresses them into scene anchors. The first
challenge is anchor initialization. Fixed learned vectors (Jin et al., 2025) support only a predefined
anchor count. Clustering-based initialization (Wang et al., 2025) supports variable counts, but relies
on handcrafted clustering that may be suboptimal and remains image-centric rather than scene-
centric. We instead learn a scene-conditioned continuous initialization field end-to-end with minimal
handcrafted design (Fig. 2(a)). Sampling the field at different densities yields different anchor budgets,
enabling continuous levels of detail under varying storage and rendering constraints.

3.3
GEOMETRY-GROUNDED BIDIRECTIONAL CONDENSATION
As illustrated in Fig. 2(b), our condenser maps K initialized anchors and N reference-view tokens
Z to K compact scene anchors. It uses L = 8 bidirectional Transformer blocks (Szymanowicz
et al., 2026), where the two streams attend to each other. Content-only attention can mix spatially
unrelated information because it lacks 3D awareness. We therefore predict geometry for both streams
(Sec. 3.3.1) and use it to modulate the bidirectional cross-attention (Sec. 3.3.2). In the condenser, we
retain the reference-view register and camera tokens and add four anchor registers. These tokens also
participate in attention, but with no geometry modulation.
3.3.1
PREDICTING VIEW AND ANCHOR GEOMETRY
View Geometry.
We predict geometry for each reference-view token directly from its feature.
Following RayRoPE (Wu et al., 2026), a linear depth head maps token zj to a log-depth center µj
and an uncertainty scale σj. Using the known camera parameters, we back-project the four corners of
the corresponding image patch at depth dj = eµj to obtain its spatial span in 3D space pj,1:4. We
also use the unit center-ray direction vj to encode its viewing direction. Together, {pj,1:4, vj, σj}
describes the spatial support and viewing direction of reference-view token j. The geometry is
recomputed from the updated reference-view features at every condenser layer.

Rather than directly regressing unconstrained 3D coordinates for each anchor,

useful prior: it constrains anchors to the convex hull of the observed geometry rather than allowing
unconstrained 3D positions. Specifically, we use a lightweight single-head cross-attention to compute

Culling.
After scene reconstruction, the same set of anchors can be reused across different target
views. We use the anchor geometry from the final condenser layer as the geometry of each anchor.

passed to the decoder, reducing the number of scene tokens processed for each target view.
Novel-View Rendering.
We use the bidirectional decoder of LagerNVS (Szymanowicz et al., 2026),
modified with our geometry-grounded RoPE for anchor–target-token cross-attention (Sec. 3.3.2). The
target camera is converted into a ray map and patch-embedded into target tokens, which predict their
view geometry with a linear head (as for reference-view tokens) and interact with anchors through
bidirectional cross-attention. Anchors use the fixed geometry from the final condenser layer. The
target camera frame is used as the RoPE coordinate frame. Finally, a linear projection maps target
tokens to RGB patches, which form the rendered image ˆIt.

EVALUATION
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets.
We evaluate on RealEstate10K (Zhou et al., 2018) and DL3DV (Ling et al., 2024) at
resolutions of 256 × 256 and 256 × 448, respectively. We study varying-reference-view settings from
2 to 10 views. Since the official protocols cover only 2 views for RealEstate10K and 2/4/6 views for
DL3DV, we construct the higher-view settings using the method described in Supplementary A.2.
Training.
We fine-tune the pretrained general LagerNVS checkpoint (Szymanowicz et al., 2026)
on each dataset using its 1–10-view training protocol, obtaining LAGERNVS-FT, which is our
base model. We initialize our decoder from LAGERNVS-FT and train the condenser and decoder,
followed by culling-aware fine-tuning. See Supplementary A.3 for training details.

Our base model is strong.

denote view counts seen during training and hollow markers denote unseen counts. LAGERNVS-FT

GLOD-LVSM achieves the best quality–token trade-offs.
We compare the quality–scene-token

condenser design. SSIM and LPIPS show similar trends (see Supplementary Fig. S3).

confirms our method’s quality advantage. More visualizations across scenes, reference-view counts,

4.4
TOKEN REDUCTION & RENDERING SPEEDUP OVER THE BASE MODEL
We now evaluate the token reduction and rendering speedup against the base model. Efficiency
gains must be considered in the context of rendering quality. Using LAGERNVS-FT as the quality
reference, we measure scene-token reduction under PSNR losses of X ∈{2, 1, 0.5, 0} dB. Fig. 7
shows the results. On 10-view RealEstate10K, GLOD-LVSM reduces the scene-token count by
85.5% at a 2 dB quality loss. Even with no quality loss (0 dB), it still reduces the token count by 33.4%,
which we attribute to the more effective geometry-grounded representation. The corresponding
rendering speedups are 3.00× and 1.23× over LAGERNVS-FT, respectively. With culling, the
estimated speedups further increase to 3.20× and 1.55×. More speed results and evaluation details
are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary A.5.
4.5
SCALING QUALITY WITH VIEWS AND BEYOND THE HIGHWAY TOKEN COUNT
We now highlight two properties of GLOD-LVSM. First, unlike highway designs whose scene tokens
grow with reference-view count, GLOD-LVSM can exploit more views at a fixed scene-token budget.
Fig. 8 shows that, at a fixed token budget, GLOD-LVSM generally improves with more views. For
example, on RealEstate10K with a 2048-token budget, 10 views outperform 2 views by 2.8 dB. In
contrast, a 10-view highway model requires over 13K tokens. At small capacity, such as 512 tokens,
gains saturate/degrade beyond 6 views. See Supplementary Fig. S9 for more results.
Second, because GLOD-LVSM learns a continuous anchor-initialization field (Sec. 3.2), it can sample
more anchors (K) than the original highway representation tokens (N), which baseline condensers
cannot support. Fig. 9 reports the PSNR gain relative to GLOD-LVSM at the uncompressed budget.
Although GLOD-LVSM exceeds the base-model quality at the same token budget, its quality
continues to improve for K > N. See Supplementary Fig. S10 for more results.

4.6
EMERGENT GEOMETRY AND ANCHOR ALLOCATION
To understand GLOD-LVSM, we visualize its representation using four reference views with 2048
scene anchors (Fig. 1). Without explicit geometry supervision, consistent scene depth emerges across
reference views, while the learned anchors concentrate near scene surfaces. Projecting the anchors
back onto the reference images further reveals scene-adaptive allocation: anchors are denser in texture-
rich regions and sparser in textureless regions. These results show that the NVS loss can induce an
efficient geometry- and texture-aware scene representation. More visualizations are in Supplementary
Figs. S26–S31. We process raw depth before point-cloud visualization; see Supplementary A.4.
4.7
ABLATION STUDIES
Fig. 10 compares quality–decoder-token trade-offs for our ablations on 6-view DL3DV. OURS de-
notes GLOD-LVSM without culling. OURS + CULLING enables our frustum-culling (Sec. 3.4).
W/O GEO. removes geometry-dependent RoPE (Sec. 3.3.2) from both the condenser and decoder.
W/O BOX. removes anti-aliasing from the positional encoding (Eqn. 3). W/O PE. removes posi-
tional encoding and instead feeds normalized anchor indices to the field MLP. W/O CONTEXT.3
removes scene- and view-specific conditioning from the anchor initialization field (Eqn. 2).
W. K-MEANS INIT. replaces our end-to-end learned anchor initialization field (Sec. 3.2) with K-
means centroids of the reference-view tokens (Wang et al., 2025). The results show that each
component contributes to the quality–token trade-off of GLOD-LVSM. Results with other reference-
view counts show similar trends and are provided in Supplementary Figs. S11–S12.
4.8
GLOD-LVSM SUPPORTS SCENE SEMANTIC UNDERSTANDING
We further examine whether the learned anchors capture scene semantics beyond rendering. We
use VLM-generated annotations for RealEstate10K (He et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023) to categorize
scenes into eight semantic classes. For each scene, GLOD-LVSM compresses 10 reference views
into 1,024 anchors, which are mean-pooled into a single 768-dimensional scene vector. We train only
a linear probe on this vector for scene classification and achieve 80.40%/91.73% top-1/top-2 accuracy.
This result shows that learned anchors already contain scene semantics. See Supplementary A.6 for
evaluation protocol and Supplementary Fig. S2 for the confusion matrix.
5
CONCLUSION
We propose GLOD-LVSM, a compact, geometry-grounded LVSM representation with continuous
levels of detail. GLOD-LVSM achieves a strong quality–token trade-off through two key designs:
(1) an end-to-end learned continuous field with minimal handcrafted design, and (2) a condenser that
consolidates multi-view observations into scene-centric, geometry-grounded anchors. Trained with
the NVS objective, GLOD-LVSM learns geometry- and texture-aware anchor allocation. We further
show its utility on scene semantic understanding, highlighting the potential beyond rendering.

A.3
TRAINING DETAILS
We follow the 1–10-view training recipe of the LagerNVS general model (Szymanowicz et al., 2026),
with a few modifications.
LagerNVS fine-tuning.
We fine-tune LagerNVS for 100K iterations with a 1.2K-step warmup,
using learning rates of 10−4 on RealEstate10K and 10−5 on DL3DV. We follow its dynamic batching
strategy with a base batch size of 128, which decreases the batch size for larger reference-view counts.
GLOD-LVSM training.
We train GLOD-LVSM for 50K iterations with a 1K-step warmup, using
a learning rate of 10−4 and a base batch size of 64. For the culling-aware model, we further fine-tune
for 25K iterations with a 1K-step warmup, using a learning rate of 10−5 and the same base batch size.

DEPTH DENOISING FOR POINT-CLOUD VISUALIZATION
To visualize the noisy predicted reference-view depth as a point cloud, we apply a 3 × 3 median filter
to the patch-level depth and upsample it to image resolution using joint bilateral upsampling (Kopf
et al., 2007) guided by the corresponding RGB image. We then back-project the filtered depth
using the camera intrinsics, transform the points into a shared scene coordinate system using the
camera poses, and color them with RGB values. This post-processing is used only for point-cloud
visualization; the displayed depth maps are raw predictions, and the model geometry, anchors, and
rendering remain unchanged.
A.5
RENDERING-SPEED EVALUATION
We report results for GLOD-LVSM both with and without culling in Fig. S1. We evaluate rendering

baseline and our no-culling model, we directly benchmark rendering latency at fixed scene-token
counts. For the culling-enabled model, the effective decoder-token count varies across scenes and
target poses. We therefore estimate its speed by measuring rendering speed at the average post-culling
decoder-token count over the test set. The estimated rendering latency includes the measured culling

A.6
SCENE SEMANTIC UNDERSTANDING EVALUATION PROTOCOLS
We first test scene-semantic understanding using VLM-generated annotations for RealEstate10K (He
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023) to categorize scenes into eight semantic classes: living room, bedroom,
kitchen, bathroom, dining room, hallway, staircase, and outdoor. Each scene receives one class
derived from its caption. We filter out scenes that belong to multiple classes or do not belong to any
of the predefined classes. This results in 40,857 training scenes and 4,606 validation scenes. We train
for 500 epochs with a learning rate of 3 × 10−2, batch size 256, and weight decay 10−4. We use
cosine annealing to decay the learning rate to zero over training.
