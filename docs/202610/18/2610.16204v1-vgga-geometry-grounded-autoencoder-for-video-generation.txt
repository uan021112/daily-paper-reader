## ABSTRACT 

Autoencoders are the core component of latent video generative models, compress-ing high-dimensional videos into compact latent spaces for downstream generation. However, existing video autoencoders are primarily optimized for pixel-level reconstruction, providing limited supervision for learning structured latent rep-resentations. We argue that videos should not be treated merely as signals to be reconstructed, but rather as 2D projections of dynamic 3D worlds that exhibit explicit and well-structured geometric properties. Building on this insight, we pro-pose Video Geometry Grounded Autoencoder ( VGGA ), a geometry-aware video autoencoder that explicitly incorporates geometric constraints into the reconstruc-tion process. Specifically, VGGA grounds the learned representations along three complementary geometric dimensions: Structural Depth , Motion Correspondence ,and Projective Warping . These objectives are unified within a Video Geometry Decoder, which employs a shared spatio-temporal upsampling trunk to jointly optimize geometric learning and RGB reconstruction. Through these geometry-grounded objectives, VGGA encourages the latent representations to capture the underlying 3D structure and motion of dynamic scenes. VGGA consistently im-proves video generation across both geometry-centric and human-motion domains, reducing gFVD by 28.1% on RealEstate10K and 37.7% on Taichi-HD. Notably,

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Latent diffusion models (Seedance et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2025; Wan et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024b) have become a dominant paradigm for high-fidelity image and video generation. They typically employ a Variational AutoEncoder (VAE) (Kingma & Welling, 2013) to compress visual signals into a compact latent space, over which a diffusion transformer performs generative modeling (Rombach et al., 2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma et al., 2024b; Lin et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024). Beyond compression, the VAE determines what information is preserved and how the latent space is structured, thereby substantially affecting downstream optimization and generation quality (Yao et al., 2025b; Skorokhodov et al., 2025). Despite its importance, most video autoencoders are still trained primarily for pixel-level reconstruc-tion, providing limited guidance for learning generation-friendly latent representations. Recent works address this issue by aligning VAE latents with vision foundation models (Yu et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Leng et al., 2025), incorporating self-supervised representation learning (Ma et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025a), or imposing latent regularization such as smoothness and scale equivariance (Liu et al., 2025; Skorokhodov et al., 2025; Kouzelis et al., 2025). These approaches improve semantic structure and latent diffusability beyond reconstruction alone. However, these efforts still leave two limitations for video autoencoders. First, understanding-oriented supervision often encourages invariance to instance-specific details (Achille & Soatto, 2018; Tian et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023), whereas autoencoding requires preserving fine-grained appearance and motion for faithful reconstruction (Yao et al., 2025b), creating an inherent objective mismatch (Shi et al., 2025) (see Table 2). Second, existing objectives provide little video-specific grounding. Most are inherited from image representation learning or generic latent regularization, while video arises from the projection of a dynamic 3D world, as illustrated in Figure 1. Without explicitly modeling this underlying geometry, downstream generators must infer depth, motion, and viewpoint consistency implicitly from compressed RGB latents. Therefore, we propose Video Geometry Grounded Autoencoder ( VGGA ), a geometry-aware video au-toencoder for learning generation-friendly video latents. Concretely, we introduce a Video Geometry Decoder that grounds the learned representations along three complementary geometric dimensions: 

Structural Depth Grounding for per-frame 3D structure, Motion Correspondence Grounding for dense temporal correspondence, and Projective Warping Grounding for camera-induced view trans-formations. To build an effective VGGA, we focus on two key challenges: (1) how to harmonize geometric learning with RGB reconstruction , allowing geometric supervision to enrich the la-tent representations without degrading reconstruction quality; and (2) how to enable geometric grounding on large-scale videos without explicit geometric annotations. For the first challenge, a key difficulty is that video latents are compressed in both space and time, whereas geometric supervision is defined at the frame level. VGGA therefore adopts a partially shared decoder with a shared spatio-temporal upsampling trunk before the RGB reconstruction and geometry branches (see Figure 2). This design allows geometric supervisions to act directly on the decoded frame-level representations used for reconstruction, encouraging the latent space to encode geometric structure without sacrificing visual fidelity. For the second challenge, we adopt a hybrid geometric supervision strategy. For general videos, we use pseudo depth and motion cues predicted by off-the-shelf models to perform Structural Depth Grounding and Motion Correspondence Grounding . In contrast, Projective Warping Grounding 

requires reliable camera poses and assumes static scene geometry, so it cannot account for independent object motion. We therefore apply this objective only to a small set of predominantly static-scene videos with known camera poses. Through joint training, the projective geometry learned from these static scenes is absorbed into the shared latent representation and complements the depth and motion supervision from general videos, allowing camera-aware geometric priors to transfer beyond the statically supervised data and improve generation on dynamic videos. Our main contributions are summarized as follows. 2Preprint • We present a new perspective on video autoencoding by viewing videos as 2D projections of dynamic 3D scenes, and propose Video Geometry Grounded Autoencoder ( VGGA ) to inject explicit geometric priors into video latent learning. • We develop an effective geometry-grounded autoencoding framework that harmonizes geometric learning with RGB reconstruction and supports large-scale training without explicit geometric annotations. • We demonstrate that VGGA substantially improves downstream video generation while preserving reconstruction quality, demonstrating the effectiveness and compatibility of geometric priors for video autoencoding. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Video Autoencoders for Latent Generation. Latent generative models (Seedance et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2024) hinge on an underlying autoencoder to define the space for generative modeling. While Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) (Rombach et al., 2022) demonstrated that operating in a com-pressed latent space reconciles computational efficiency with visual fidelity, Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma et al., 2024a) further validated the scalability of this paradigm. This design is particularly pivotal for video, where the tokenizer must encapsulate both spatial appearance and temporal dynamics (Gao et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024b; Wan et al., 2025). Despite the heavy reliance of current systems on autoencoder quality (Liu et al., 2025), the training objective remains predominantly reconstruction-centric. 

Generation-Friendly Tokenizer Learning. Recent studies (Yao et al., 2025b) suggest that recon-struction fidelity alone does not guarantee effective generative latents, sparking interest in generation-centric tokenization (Ma et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025a; Skorokhodov et al., 2025). One research trajectory aligns VAE latents with vision foundation models (e.g., DINOv2) to infuse semantic and spatial priors (Yu et al., 2025; Leng et al., 2025). Others integrate understanding-oriented objectives, such as contrastive or self-supervised learning, to refine latent organization (Ma et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025a). Parallel efforts focus on latent "diffusability" through regularization—leveraging spectral biasing or equivariance (Liu et al., 2025; Skorokhodov et al., 2025; Kouzelis et al., 2025). While these methods enhance latent structure, they primarily adapt image-level paradigms or generic constraints, largely overlooking the intrinsic priors of video formation. VGGA fills this gap by explicitly grounding video autoencoding in geometric constraints. 

Geometry Grounding for Video as Dynamic 3D Worlds. Videos are inherently projections of dynamic 3D scenes, positioning geometry as a fundamental source of dense, self-contained supervision. While extensive research has focused on individual geometric cues, such as monocular depth (Yang et al., 2024a), optical flow (Teed & Deng, 2020), and point tracking (Karaev et al., 2024), or leveraged projective consistency for novel view synthesis (Wang et al., 2024), these efforts primarily target geometric perception. These works validate that geometry effectively distills the physical regularities manifest in video observations. VGGA departs from these perception-centric goals by repurposing geometric cues as grounding signals for video autoencoding. Consequently, the resulting latent space is explicitly sculpted by 3D layout and temporal correspondence, moving beyond the limitations of purely pixel-based reconstruction.

Videos are observations of dynamic 3D worlds, and their latent representations should therefore capture the geometric structure underlying video formation. Motivated by this perspective, VGGA introduces a Video Geometry Decoder that explicitly incorporates geometric grounding into the decoding process, rather than treating the decoder solely as an RGB reconstruction module. A key challenge arises from the spatio-temporal compression performed by video encoders. Given an input video, the resulting latent representation z ∈ R(1+ T /r t)×H/r s×W/r s×C is compressed along both spatial and temporal dimensions. In particular, when rt > 1, the latent sequence is not directly aligned with frame-level geometric signals such as depth, and projective geometry. Scaling geometry-grounded video autoencoding is challenging, as large-scale video datasets rarely provide explicit geometric annotations. Dense depth and motion labels are costly to acquire, while reliable camera poses are particularly difficult to obtain for dynamic videos. VGGA therefore adopts a hybrid supervision strategy that exploits geometric cues according to their availability. For general videos, we use pseudo depth and optical flow predicted by off-the-shelf models to supervise Structural Depth Grounding and Motion Correspondence Grounding ; sensitivity to pseudo-label quality is analyzed in Section B. In contrast, Projective Warping Grounding requires reliable camera poses and assumes static scene geometry. We therefore apply this objective only to a small subset of static-scene videos with known camera poses, where projective view transformations can be reliably established. The three grounding objectives are jointly optimized with standard VAE reconstruction objective: 

LVGGA = LVAE + λdLdepth + λf Lflow + λwLwarp , (11) where Lwarp is applied only when projective supervision is available. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 SETUP 

Architecture. The video VAE backbone of VGGA adopts the same architectural design as Wan2.2 VAE (Wan et al., 2025). To isolate the effect of geometry grounding, both VGGA and its reconstruction-only baseline, VGGA w/o Geo, are trained from scratch on identical datasets. The Video Geometry Decoder adds lightweight geometry branches on top of the shared spatio-temporal upsampling trunk. The depth head consists of two spatial upsampling blocks, while the flow head follows the optical-flow prediction design of FlowNet2 (Ilg et al., 2017). 

Training Data. We train VGGA on a mixture of a curated high-quality 10M-video dataset and RealEstate10K (Zhou et al., 2018), sampled at a 4:1 ratio. The 10M videos do not require manual depth or flow annotations. We obtain depth pseudo labels using VGGT (Wang et al., 2025) and optical-flow pseudo labels using RAFT (Teed & Deng, 2020). Structural Depth Grounding and Motion Correspondence Grounding are applied to all training videos. Projective Warping Grounding is applied only to RealEstate10K samples. Additional training details are provided in Section A. 

Evaluation. We evaluate generation on RealEstate10K (Zhou et al., 2018), UCF101 (Soomro et al., 2012), and Taichi-HD (Siarohin et al., 2019). RealEstate10K contains strong geometric structure and camera motion, and is used to assess view consistency and camera-motion stability. UCF101 evaluates action-conditioned generation, while Taichi-HD evaluates unconditional human motion generation. For reconstruction, we evaluate it on the Kinetics-400 validation set (Carreira & Zisserman, 2017). Geometry-centric generation. We evaluate VGGA on RealEstate10K to assess its ability to model complex camera motion and projective view changes. As shown in Table 1, VGGA achieves the best FVD of 64.31, outperforming SSVAE by 14.77 points under the same t4s16 c48 latent configuration and reducing FVD by 25.18 points over the reconstruction-only baseline. VGGA also improves geometric consistency, reducing Met3R from 0.0514 to 0.0421. Together with the qualitative results in Figure 3, these results demonstrate that geometry grounding improves both generation quality and geometric fidelity under complex view transformations. 

Action-conditioned generation. Beyond camera-centric scenarios, we evaluate whether geometry-grounded latents support diverse human actions. As reported in Table 1, VGGA achieves an FVD of 141.27 and an Inception Score (IS) of 70.32 on UCF101. Under the t4s16c48 configuration, VGGA reduces FVD by 27.41 points relative to SSVAE (168.68) and by 22.51 points relative to its reconstruction-only counterpart (163.78). Under t4s8c16, VGGA likewise improves FVD from 163.21 to 148.15. These results demonstrate that geometry grounding benefits action-conditioned synthesis across both latent configurations. 

Human-motion generation. Furthermore, we extend our evaluation to Taichi-HD to test the model’s capability in handling articulated human motion, which demands that the latent distribution encapsulate both fine-grained local deformations and global temporal coherence. As shown in Table 1, VGGA reduces FVD from 218.92 (Wan2.2 VAE) to 121.76 under the same t4s16c48 configuration. Compared to its reconstruction-only counterpart, VGGA reduces FVD by 73.68 points (195.44 to 121.76). These results substantiate the effectiveness of VGGA across different video generation tasks.

We analyze how geometry grounding affects latent diffusability, the recoverability of geometric information, and the transfer of static-scene supervision to dynamic video generation. 

Diffusability of video latents. We evaluate VAE’s diffusability by tracking FVD during the training of identical Latte-XL/1 generators. As shown in Figure 4, VGGA consistently achieves lower FVD and faster convergence than the reconstruction-only baseline. This suggests that geometry grounding regularizes the latent space with explicit structural cues, thereby reducing the complexity of the downstream generative task and rendering the latents more diffusion-friendly. 

Latent geometric probing. We train linear depth and optical-flow probes on frozen video latents and evaluate them on the RealEstate10K test split, using VGGT and RAFT pseudo-labels as reference targets. As shown in Figure 5(a), VGGA improves depth δ1 from 0.355 to 0.813 and reduces the flow outlier rate (F1-all) from 56.4% to 23.8%. These results demonstrate that VGGA encodes substantially stronger geometric structure and motion correspondence, making such information more linearly accessible from the latent space. 

Transfer from static scenes to dynamic generation. To isolate the contribution of static-scene supervision, we add only the projective warping objective on RealEstate10K to the reconstruction-only baseline. As shown in Figure 5(b), FVD decreases from 163.78 to 154.32 on UCF101 and from 195.44 to 163.21 on Taichi-HD. These results demonstrate that camera-aware geometric priors learned from static scenes transfer effectively to dynamic video generation and provide complementary benefits to depth and motion supervision. 4.4 ABLATION STUDY 

Geometry grounding signals. The three grounding signals provide complementary constraints on scene structure, temporal correspondence, and camera-induced geometry. As shown in Table 3, removing geometric supervision increases FVD from 141.27 to 163.78, while all leave-one-out variants degrade generation performance. This confirms that the three geometric cues contribute complementarily to learning generation-friendly video latents. 

Shared branching position. As shown in Table 4, branching after U st 2 achieves the best overall per-formance. Earlier branching lacks sufficient frame-level alignment, whereas later branching operates on increasingly RGB-specific features. A separate geometry branch is also less effective, confirming that geometric supervision is most effective when applied to the shared decoded representation. 

Proportion of warp-supervised samples. Figure 5(c) studies the proportion of samples receiving projective warping supervision. Increasing this proportion from 0% to 20% reduces FVD from 76.48 to 64.31 on RealEstate10K and from 153.24 to 141.27 on UCF101. Further increasing the proportion to 30% or 40% degrades performance on both datasets. Thus, the default 20% mixture provides the best trade-off, introducing effective camera-aware supervision while retaining general videos as the majority of training data. 9Preprint 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented VGGA , which introduces a geometry-aware perspective for learning generation-friendly video latents by grounding autoencoding in the geometric structure of dynamic 3D scenes. VGGA enables geometric learning to be effectively integrated with reconstruction and scaled to large-scale unlabelled videos. Extensive experiments demonstrate that geometry-grounded autoencoding substantially improves downstream video generation while preserving reconstruction quality. 

Limitations. VGGA focuses on frame-level and short-range geometric consistency, without explicitly modeling long-horizon dynamics or full 3D scene structure. Extending geometry grounding to longer temporal ranges and scene-level 3D modeling is a promising direction for future work. 10 Preprint

## A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

This section provides additional implementation details omitted from the main paper for clarity. We describe the evaluation metrics, pseudo-label generation for geometry grounding, autoencoder training losses, and downstream latent generator training protocol. A.1 EVALUATION METRICS 

For generation, we follow common video-generation evaluation protocols and compute Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) and Kernel Video Distance (KVD) (Unterthiner et al., 2018) in the I3D feature space. FVD measures the Fréchet distance between Gaussian-fitted real and generated video features, while KVD computes a kernel two-sample distance between the two feature sets. We additionally report Inception Score (IS) (Salimans et al., 2016) on datasets where the metric is applicable. Unless otherwise specified, generation metrics are computed with 2048 generated videos at 17 × 256 × 256 

resolution. For geometric consistency, we follow Met3R (Asim et al., 2025) and evaluate 3D consistency between adjacent frames of generated videos on RealEstate10K to obtain video-level Met3R scores. Lower scores indicate better geometric consistency. We evaluate reconstruction using rFVD, peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) (Huynh-Thu & Ghanbari, 2008), structural similarity (SSIM) (Wang et al., 2004), and learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) (Zhang et al., 2018). Reconstruction FVD (rFVD) is computed between original and reconstructed video clips using the same feature extractor as FVD. PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS are computed frame-wise and averaged over all frames and validation videos. A.2 PSEUDO GEOMETRIC SUPERVISION 

VGGA does not require manual depth or optical-flow annotations. For the curated 10M-video dataset and RealEstate10K, we generate pseudo depth maps using VGGT (Wang et al., 2025) and pseudo optical flow using RAFT (Teed & Deng, 2020). Depth and flow pseudo labels are produced offline and resized to the training resolution. We mix the curated 10M-video dataset and RealEstate10K with a 4:1 sampling ratio. For projective warping, we use RealEstate10K samples with valid camera poses and predominantly static content. Target-frame VGGT pseudo depth and target-to-source camera transformations define the backward sampling maps; depth maps and intrinsics are resized and scaled to the decoded feature grid, respectively. During mixed-data training, Structural Depth Grounding and Motion Correspondence Grounding are applied to all samples, while Projective Warping Grounding is applied only to RealEstate10K samples with valid camera poses. Sensitivity to alternative depth and flow teachers is evaluated in Section B. A.3 AUTOENCODER TRAINING LOSSES
