# Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Scene Reconstruction: Methods, Systems, and Trade-offs 

Ashim Dahal, Rabab Abdelfattah, Nick Rahimi 

> School of Computing and Computer Engineering, University of Southern Mississippi, Hattiesburg, MS, 39401, USA

Abstract 

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) allows for high-quality real-time novel-view synthesis. Since its inception in 2023, its explicit and highly configurable scene representation has been extended toward sparse-view generation, dynamic reconstruction, efficiency and compression, streaming, and simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM). Al-though Gaussian Splatting can provide real-time rendering, rendering an existing rep-resentation is distinct from constructing, updating, or maintaining the scene itself. In this survey, we synthesize current literature, categorize them and provide a con-nection of current approaches and tools towards an end-to-end pipeline. Explicitly, we categorize GS scene reconstruction into five overlapping families: single-shot gen-eration, dynamic representations, efficiency and compression, streaming and immer-sive systems, and Gaussian Splatting SLAM. We distinguish render-only throughput from task-specific scene generation, update, tracking, mapping, and optimization costs, and combine controlled sparse-view benchmarking with native evaluations to identify cross-family quality, latency, memory, and update-cost trade-offs. 

Keywords: Gaussian Splatting, 3D Scene Representation, Novel View Synthesis, Neural Rendering, Multi-View Geometry 

1. Introduction 

Kerbl et al. [1] introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as an explicit scene rep-resentation capable of high-quality interactive novel-view rendering. Subsequent work rapidly extended this formulation beyond static per-scene reconstruction. Deformable 3DGS [2] and 4DGaussians [3] introduced temporal deformation and spatiotempo-ral representations, while later methods explored feed-forward sparse-view genera-tion [4, 5], efficiency and compression, streaming, and Gaussian-based SLAM. These developments increasingly shift the systems bottleneck from rendering an existing rep-resentation toward constructing, updating, transmitting, or maintaining the Gaussian scene. This distinction is important for applications such as free-viewpoint video, im-mersive media, online mapping, and robotics. 2. Preliminaries 

Figure 1 summarizes the end-to-end Gaussian Splatting reconstruction pipeline and where our five taxonomy families intervene. This section fixes terminology and nota-tion, summarizes the core 3DGS representation and rasterization/compositing used for real-time rendering, and defines the training regimes, input/supervision assumptions, and the real-time criterion used throughout. We conclude with a compact tag schema used to structure the taxonomy and method comparisons. 

2.1. Scope and Terms 

We use Gaussian Splatting to refer to modern 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1]: an explicit set of 3D Gaussian primitives parameterized in world space and rendered by fast (typically differentiable) screen-space splatting with rasterization/compositing. Under this scope, the primary object is a Gaussian scene representation (centers, co-variances, opacities, and appearance parameters), and the key efficiency advantage comes from rasterizing a finite set of primitives rather than volumetric ray march-ing. We do not survey NeRFs [11] as a primary topic. We cite NeRF-related work only when necessary for context (e.g., datasets/metrics) or when a method explicitly transfers radiance-field ideas into Gaussian representations. Finally, we use real-time scene reconstruction in a task-specific sense rather than through a universal FPS threshold. Rendering, scene generation, dynamic or streaming updates, and SLAM are evaluated using their corresponding units of work and timing boundaries, as defined in Section 2.7. 32.2. Notations

Unless stated other-wise, each primitive is represented by ( μ j, Σ j, α j, c j), where c j may be RGB, spherical harmonics coefficients, or learned features depending on the method. 

2.3. Gaussian Representation 

Gaussian Splatting represents a scene as G = {g j}Nj=1, a set of N anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives in world coordinates. Each g j is defined by a mean μ j ∈ R3, co-variance Σ j ∈ R3×3 (commonly parameterized via rotation and per-axis scales for sta-bility), opacity α j ∈ [0 , 1], and appearance parameters c j. This explicit primitive-based representation enables high-throughput rasterization pipelines compared to radiance-field rendering [1]. Methods primarily differ in (i) which parameters are optimized vs. predicted, and (ii) how time is modeled. Static pipelines often optimize all parameters per scene with SfM/MVS/depth-based initialization; dynamic extensions introduce time depen-dence via explicit per-frame parameters, deformation models, or static/dynamic parti-tions. Online/SLAM-style systems additionally constrain updates to bounded latency and control representation growth via pruning, keyframes, and local refinement. 

Representation complexity and real-time knobs. Runtime and memory are dominated by the number of primitives N and the appearance bandwidth (e.g., SH degree or feature dimensionality). Dynamic/streaming settings add growth over time, so many systems explicitly regulate N and appearance parameters to meet real-time targets. 

Representation vs. serialization (file formats). Gaussian scenes may be stored in dif-ferent encodings (e.g., PLY-like formats or .splat -style binaries). These typically change serialization/layout and attribute encoding (e.g., quantization, SH/features) rather than the underlying representation {(μ j, Σ j, α j, c j)}Nj=1.

2.5. Training 

We group common regimes by when optimization/learning happens: per-scene/per-sequence optimization (offline fitting), amortized feed-forward generation via pre-trained models, and dynamic/online settings where temporal modeling and incremen-tal updates impose bounded-latency constraints. Per-scene optimization fits {(μ j, Σ j, α j, c j)}

for a specific sequence [1]; amortized methods predict Gaussians at inference time (op-tionally followed by brief test-time refinement) [17, 5, 4, 18]. Dynamic methods often introduce time-dependent parameterizations or deformations and are frequently opti-mized per sequence [3, 19, 2], while online/SLAM pipelines prioritize training-free or pretrained components at runtime with efficient map maintenance. 

2.6. Inputs and Supervision 

We categorize input modalities as multi-view captures, monocular video , and 

RGB-D or sensor-augmented streams (e.g., events). Pose assumptions range from 

known (calibration/SfM) to estimated/refined (tracked or jointly optimized) to pose-free . Supervision is often self-supervised via photometric consistency, but many pipelines incorporate pretrained priors (especially depth/MVS) to stabilize geometry and accel-erate initialization.

We survey Gaussian Splatting (GS) research toward real-time scene reconstruction using the task-specific definitions in Section 2.7. Our workflow is: (i) collect pa-pers broadly, (ii) apply inclusion criteria aligned with the GS reconstruction pipeline, (iii) assign a pipeline-centric taxonomy with consistent tags, and (iv) interpret reported efficiency using end-to-end-aware conventions. 

3.1. Literature Collection and Inclusion Criteria 

We prioritize recent work (2023–2025) while retaining foundational baselines needed for context (datasets, metrics, and core dynamic/NVS references). Literature was iden-tified primarily through Google Scholar and the proceedings of CVPR, ICCV, ECCV, NeurIPS, and ICLR. Seed queries included Gaussian splatting , Gaussian splatting for SLAM , efficient Gaussian splatting , fast Gaussian splatting , sparse-view Gaussian splatting , and zero-shot Gaussian splatting , together with closely related variants for dynamic reconstruction, streaming, compression, pose-free reconstruction, and real-time reconstruction. Reference lists of relevant papers were also inspected to identify related and foundational work (backward citation snowballing). We include meth-ods whose primary scene representation is an explicit Gaussian-based model (or a direct extension) and that materially affect one or more stages of a GS reconstruc-tion pipeline (initialization/generation, temporal modeling, map maintenance, stream-ing/delivery, compositing, or tracking-and-mapping). We exclude NeRF-style methods as a primary focus, citing them only when necessary for context or when they explic-itly bridge into Gaussian representations. Duplicate versions and works outside the surveyed reconstruction/system scope are excluded; dataset, metric, and foundational papers are retained as supporting references rather than surveyed GS methods. Because the literature search evolved iteratively during preparation of the survey, intermediate retrieval and screening counts were not recorded prospectively and are therefore not reconstructed retrospectively. 

3.2. Tagging and Taxonomy Assignment 

Each paper is annotated using the master tag schema in Table 3 (input/pose/depth cues, regime, temporal scope, runtime setting, and efficiency levers). We assign each method to one primary family based on its dominant contribution, while noting mean-ingful overlaps when a method intervenes in multiple pipeline stages. The five families in Fig. 2 are constructed to reflect where methods intervene operationally in the end-to-end pipeline (Fig. 1), enabling consistent comparison across tables and sections.

4. Datasets and Evaluation Protocols 

This section summarizes the datasets, quality metrics, and evaluation protocols used throughout the survey. Runtime comparisons follow the task-specific definitions introduced in Section 2.7. 

4.1. Datasets and Quality Metrics Datasets (representative regimes). Although datasets are not the main focus of this survey, they strongly influence what claims are comparable. Across the literature, benchmarks cluster into five recurring regimes. (i) Multi-view dome/studio cap-tures provide calibrated multi-camera observations and controlled dynamics, com-monly used for high-fidelity dynamic reconstruction (e.g., Technicolor Light Field [20], Neu3DV [21], Replay [22], NeRSemble [23], PKU-DyMVHumans [24], DiVa-360 [25]). (ii) Monocular/handheld sequences emphasize pose uncertainty, motion blur, and in-the-wild dynamics, often inherited from dynamic NeRF benchmarks (e.g., NSFF Dynamic [26], D-NeRF [27], Nerfies [28], HyperNeRF [29]) and more recent GS/video-generation settings (e.g., Splat4D [30]). (iii) Event+RGB settings target fast motion and low-light where conventional frames degrade, using event streams as addi-tional supervision or cues (e.g., Dynamic EventNeRF [31], E-4DGS [19]). (iv) Out-door/driving/drone captures evaluate scalability and SLAM-like conditions, often with LiDAR/SLAM pose supervision and dynamic agents (e.g., MTGS [32], DroneS-plat [33], Wide-Range-4D [34]). 4.2. Task-Specific Runtime Metrics 

Runtime is reported according to the task being measured. Render FPS (rFPS) refers only to rendering an existing Gaussian representation. Feed-forward methods use scene throughput, dynamic and streaming methods use update throughput, and SLAM methods use tracking or mapping throughput. Offline optimization is reported using training or optimization time. These quantities are compared only when they measure the same task under the same timing boundary. 

4.3. Evaluation Protocol 

Quantitative comparisons require aligned datasets, input settings, resolution, hard-ware, and timing boundaries. When these conditions are not aligned, results are treated as method-specific evidence rather than direct comparisons. We therefore report both quality and runtime together with the experimental conditions under which they were measured.

Single-shot / amortized Gaussian generation . Feed-forward construction of a usable Gaussian scene from one or few images, often using depth/MVS priors or learned regularizers, optionally followed by brief refinement. Poses may be known, refined, or pose-free, but the defining feature is replacing heavy per-scene iterative fitting with amortized inference from limited views. 

Dynamic representations beyond 4DGS . Methods that treat time as a first-class vari-able in the scene model (e.g., time-conditioned parameters, deformation/warp fields, key-time primitives, canonical-plus-delta factorization) or explicitly mitigate dynamic failure modes such as ghosting, identity swaps, occlusion/disocclusion artifacts, and temporal flicker. The emphasis is modeling temporal change, not merely delivering or replaying content over time. 

Efficiency and compression . Works whose central goal is reducing compute, mem-ory, or storage/bandwidth while preserving real-time operation, via levers such as prun-ing/sparsification, quantization/packing, view-adaptive LoD selection, or system/hardware acceleration. Let q j and s j denote the rotation and scale parameterization of Σ j. Dynamic defor-mation can then be written as (∆μ j, ∆q j, ∆s j) = Fθ(μ j, t), μ j(t) = μ j + ∆ μ j, (11) with the predicted rotation and scale determining Σ j(t). Deformable-3D-Gaussians [2] uses an MLP deformation field conditioned directly on canonical position and time. 4DGaussians [3] instead obtains the deformation from factorized spatio-temporal fea-tures, using a HexPlane-style [51] 4D encoding followed by a lightweight decoder. Thus, the former places motion complexity in the continuous deformation network, whereas the latter shifts part of it into an explicit space–time feature structure. In ta-ble 7, both reach similar quality on D-NeRF bouncingballs : 41.38 dB for Deformable-3D-Gaussians and 40.81 dB for 4DGaussians. These values show that both deforma-tion parameterizations can represent the same synthetic motion regime at high fidelity. 4DGaussians additionally renders at 36.03 rFPS in the measured native setup, showing that the deformation representation can remain compatible with interactive rendering after optimization. MotionGS [52] modifies this formulation by adding explicit motion supervision to the deformation field. Optical flow is decomposed as 

FMt→t+1 = Ft→t+1 − FCt→t+1, (12) where FC is the component induced by camera motion and FM isolates object mo-tion. The predicted 3D Gaussian deformation is projected into Gaussian flow FG and constrained by 

Lflow = sg( FMt→t+1) − FGt→t+1 1 . (13) This changes the optimization problem from learning motion primarily through pho-tometric reconstruction to explicitly matching projected Gaussian motion. This su-pervision couples deformation accuracy to rendered depth and camera pose: errors in either term perturb FC and therefore FM . For a Gaussian scene G = {g j}Nj=1, efficiency is governed by more than the primitive count N. A useful conceptual decomposition is

6.6. Cross-Family Trade-offs 

Across the five families, the main distinction is not the Gaussian renderer but which term dominates the task latency. Using the notation of section 2.7, a cross-family decomposition can be written as 

tT = tgeom + tpose + tdeform /update + trefine + trender , (25) where only task-relevant terms are active. In single-shot reconstruction, tgeom dom-inates: table 5 shows that decoder-only rFPS is much larger than scene throughput because Gaussian construction is more expensive than rasterization. Dynamic methods such as 4DGaussians [3] and Deformable-3D-Gaussians [2] shift cost toward tempo-ral deformation and per-sequence optimization. Streaming systems expose the same separation more strongly: in table 10, IGS [61] and 3DGStream [62] render an updated representation quickly, while the transition itself remains orders of magnitude slower. Gaussian SLAM adds tpose and map maintenance to this loop, making tracking and mapping, rather than rasterization, the dominant full-system cost in table 11. Efficiency methods operate on a different axis. For B(G) ≈ Nb g, pruning reduces 

N, quantization reduces bg, and LoD reduces the view-dependent active set Nvis , but none of these implies a proportional decrease in tT . The shared baseline in table 9 makes this explicit: PUP 3D-GS [57] and Compact3D [58] reduce serialized size by approximately 90% and 92.66%, respectively, while the pruned PUP model does not render faster than the vanilla baseline in table 8. Memory is therefore an independent deployment constraint rather than a proxy for speed; the failed MotionGS [52] execu-tion on an 80 GB A100 provides the complementary case where optimization becomes infeasible despite the efficiency of Gaussian rasterization. A useful cross-family oper-ating point is consequently 

P = (Q, tT , B(G), trender ) , (26) 18 and methods should be compared through the resulting trade-off rather than a single universal FPS or SPS value. These differences lead to distinct deployment regimes. Feed-forward single-shot methods are the strongest fit for rapid initialization and interactive preview because they minimize scene-construction latency, although the controlled results show a direct quality–latency–memory trade-off. Per-sequence dynamic methods are better suited when temporal reconstruction quality is more important than initialization time, since interactive rendering can be achieved after a substantially heavier optimization stage. Compression and LoD methods are most useful when storage, bandwidth, or rendering load is the limiting resource. Streaming methods directly target temporal scene deliv-ery, but the measured update rates show that interactive playback and real-time scene refresh remain different system requirements. Finally, Gaussian SLAM is the natural family for robotics and online mapping because pose estimation and scene reconstruc-tion are coupled, but the wrapped rates in table 11 show that joint tracking–mapping latency remains the principal obstacle to video-rate operation. Thus, “real-time” Gaus-sian Splatting is not a single operating point: the appropriate method depends on whether the application must optimize reconstruction latency, temporal update latency, map maintenance, memory/bandwidth, or render-only throughput. 

7. Open Problems and Future Directions 

We summarize open problems across the major components of a GS reconstruction pipeline (see Figure 1), focusing on what blocks unified end-to-end real-time systems. 

7.1. Reporting vs. Reality: Accepted Gaps 

Open problems. “Real-time” is often reported at the stage level rather than end-to-end, making cross-paper comparisons fragile. Some works report only submodule speed (e.g., VGGT-style lifting [71]), while others report FPS under narrow settings (few views, low resolution) that do not reflect realistic reconstruction workloads [17, 5]. Depth Anything 3 [18] similarly reports per-image throughput, which does not translate to per-scene throughput for multi-frame/view fusion. 

Future directions. Standardize task-specific speed–quality reporting with explicit input window size, unit of work, timing boundary, hardware, and included pipeline stages. 

7.2. Single-Shot Generators: The Deployment Gap 

Open problems. Single-shot GS generators combine heavy backbones with multi-view fusion and sometimes refinement, making “speed” hard to interpret. Across depth-conditioned lifting (DepthSplat [17]), sparse-view fusion (MVSplat [5]), and learned priors (Splatt3r [44], VGGT-style lifting [71]), runtime often shifts into feature extraction/fusion that is inconsistently included in FPS reports. InstantSplat [46] and pose-free lines [72, 73] further depend on iteration budgets and convergence behavior, while custom CUDA or non-standard rasterization/fusion components (e.g., [12, 16]) can complicate deployment and reproducible timing. 19 Future directions. (i) Co-design for deployment: prefer architectures that map cleanly to standard inference graphs with predictable memory/batching. (ii) Prun-ing/LoD as first-class: predict compact Gaussians directly (importance-aware outputs) rather than generating dense sets and pruning afterward. 

7.3. Pruning and Efficiency: An Afterthought 

Open problems. Many efficiency levers are applied after dense construction, so expensive lifting/fusion/optimization is still paid at full cost. Pruning methods re-move large fractions post hoc [57, 59]; quantization/packing reduce bandwidth/storage [74, 75]; and LoD scales playback [76, 56]. These benefits often arrive only after dense construction, and view-adaptive pruning can introduce popping/flicker without temporal stabilization [77, 78]. 

Future directions. (i) Sparse by construction: design generators and online up-daters that output compact Gaussians natively. (ii) Temporally stable budgets: learn selection/budget policies that avoid popping and flicker under fast camera motion. 

7.4. Dynamic Streaming: Long-Horizon Stability 

Open problems. Long-horizon streaming remains fragile because dynamics in-troduce identity and lifecycle issues: Gaussians must remain consistent under occlu-sion/disocclusion, topology change, and re-observation after long gaps. Spawnable/time-varying primitives (e.g., FreeTimeGS [79]) improve flexibility but exacerbate growth control and bookkeeping. System-wise, streaming pipelines must meet bounded update budgets and avoid latency spikes under cache misses or viewpoint jumps [62, 63, 61]; many pipelines still resemble offline optimization with real-time playback rather than truly online capture-to-render reconstruction [3, 2]. 

Future directions. (i) Lifecycle + map maintenance: explicit birth/death, merge/split, and re-anchoring policies to stabilize identity and bound growth [79]. (ii) Canoni-cal + residual streaming: compact canonical models plus time-dependent deltas [63]. (iii) Cache-robust updates: schedules/caches that degrade gracefully under viewpoint jumps [62, 61]. 

7.5. Rendering: Differentiability 

Open problems. Differentiable rasterization enables end-to-end optimization [1], but full gradients increase memory/overhead and complicate bounded-latency stream-ing or online updates. Faster rasterizers can prioritize throughput, yet approximations in compositing/sorting/visibility may introduce haloing, incorrect occlusion, and tem-poral flicker, especially in dense/dynamic regions [12, 16]. Recent work on composit-ing correctness (e.g., volumetrically consistent formulations) suggests some artifacts are rooted in the rendering model itself [15]. 

Future directions. (i) Train–deploy separation: train with differentiable or sur-rogate rendering, deploy with faster backends, and control mismatch [12, 16]. (ii) 

Selective differentiability: keep full gradients only for keyframes or uncertain regions. (iii) Correctness complements efficiency: improved compositing/visibility can reduce artifacts without inflating N [15]. 20 7.6. SLAM with Gaussians: The Missing Pieces 

Open problems. GS-based SLAM must satisfy tracking deadlines and main-tain a map that is both renderable and usable for localization. While recent sys-tems show real-time tracking/mapping [80, 67, 68], long-term consistency (loop clo-sure/relocalization), bounded growth via maintenance, and robustness to dynamic-object corruption remain under-emphasized in in-the-wild settings [69]. 

Future directions. (i) Loop-consistent Gaussian maps: loop-aware merge/re-anchor/de-duplicate updates to preserve render quality and pose consistency. (ii) Maintenance-first SLAM: pruning, keyframe selection, and local submap management as core com-ponents to keep latency stable [68]. (iii) Dynamic-robust mapping: lightweight mask-ing/segmentation and static/dynamic separation to prevent permanent map pollution [69]. 

8. Conclusion 

This survey reviewed Gaussian Splatting (GS) for real-time scene reconstruction. Rather than only listing methods, we organized prior work into a pipeline-centric tax-onomy and highlighted the trade-offs that determine whether a method is merely real-time in a submodule or deployable end-to-end. 

Strengths and value to the field. The main strength of this work is its synthesis across components . We connect design choices (e.g., temporal parameterization vs. stability, pruning/quantization/LoD vs. fidelity, and rasterization choices vs. artifacts) to system-level outcomes such as latency, memory growth, robustness to pose/depth noise, and long-horizon stability. The taxonomy, comparison framework, and failure-mode analysis help researchers position new contributions and practitioners reason about capture-to-render pipelines. 

Weaknesses and limitations. Our taxonomy necessarily simplifies a fast-moving hybrid space, where many systems combine amortized initialization with refinement or mix static and dynamic representations. Comparability is also limited: papers report speed and memory at different stages (e.g., per-image FPS vs. per-scene throughput), under different input-window sizes and hardware assumptions. Thus, end-to-end con-clusions about “real-time” readiness should be read as pipeline-aware guidance rather than a standardized benchmark ranking. 

Future work. The main remaining directions are task-specific standardized eval-uation, deployment-aware scene generation, sparse-by-construction representations, long-horizon dynamic and streaming maintenance, and robust Gaussian SLAM. To-gether, these problems shift the focus from fast rasterization toward maintaining qual-ity, bounded latency, and manageable state throughout the reconstruction pipeline.
