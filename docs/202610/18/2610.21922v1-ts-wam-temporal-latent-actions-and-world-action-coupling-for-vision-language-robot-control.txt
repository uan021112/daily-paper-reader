# TS-WAM: Temporal Latent Actions and World-Action Coupling for Vision-Language Robot Control 

Menghao Yin, Jiaju Yin, Xiyue Dong, Lixin Xu, Zhenhui Zhang, Ke Zhang, Yating Feng, Renjing Xu The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) 

Abstract — Many vision-language-action policies rely on action-labeled robot demonstrations and lack an explicit mech-anism for predicting future visual states. These limitations hinder learning from action-free videos and can encourage over-fitting to demonstration conditions, weakening generalization and robustness. We present TS-WAM, a lightweight Teacher-Student World-Action Model that addresses these challenges through self-supervised temporal learning and explicit future prediction. Its vlm-jepa framework learns from heterogeneous data, including robot demonstrations and action-free human videos, using an EMA target branch without requiring aseparately trained external teacher. By coupling vlm-jepa with delta-lwm, TS-WAM learns to predict latent actions from the current observation and instruction and uses them to predict future visual features that guide action generation at inference. A unified end-to-end training architecture jointly optimizes temporal representation learning, visual dynamics, and robot control without a separate latent-action or world-model pretraining stage. The EMA target branch is removed at inference, keeping the deployed policy lightweight. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and SimplerEnv, together with real-world manipulation experiments on Franka Research 3 and RevoTron, demonstrate that TS-WAM achieves strong manipulation performance, generalization, and robustness. 

I. INTRODUCTION A robot must convert a language instruction and a chang-ing visual scene into a temporally coherent sequence of actions. Recent vision-language-action (VLA) systems use a pretrained vision-language model (VLM) as a semantic back-bone and attach a diffusion or flow-matching action decoder [1]–[4]. This recipe gives strong instruction following, but the action interface is commonly learned directly from robot trajectories. The resulting hidden state need not represent the short-horizon visual change that an action is intended to cause. Consequently, the policy has no explicit internal prediction of what its action will do before it is executed. At the same time, large video collections contain abundant information about how objects and hands move, but generally do not provide robot actions. Latent action models (LAMs) address this gap by compressing temporal changes into a compact code [5]. Existing systems, however, often pretrain a LAM or a latent world model separately and subsequently adapt it to a robot policy. This multi-stage procedure intro-duces an additional checkpoint and optimization boundary, and it does not guarantee that the learned latent is useful to the final action decoder. We propose TS-WAM (Teacher-Student World-Action Model), a task-adaptation architecture that makes temporal prediction the central interface of a VLA policy without requiring a separately pretrained latent-action model. The model uses an asymmetric pair of VLM branches.

The online X-VLM receives only the current observation and language instruction. It produces latent-X tokens that a small transformer predictor maps to a predicted latent action. The EMA Y -VLM receives the current and future observations and reads the corresponding target latent action. This temporal information asymmetry turns future visual evidence into a training-only teacher signal while keeping the deployed policy causal. The latent action is coupled to visual dynamics and control. A frozen DINO encoder represents the current and future images as spatial feature grids. A newly initialized la-tent world decoder predicts the feature displacement ∆Ft =

Ft+1 − Ft conditioned on the current feature and latent action. The predicted future feature Ft + d∆F t is passed to a conditional flow-matching action head together with VLM context and robot state. The policy therefore follows an explicit chain: infer a latent intent, predict its visual consequence, and ground that consequence into an action chunk. The same training procedure can mix robot demonstra-tions and action-free human videos. Human clips contribute current–future visual pairs to the teacher and world-model objectives, whereas robot clips additionally provide state and action supervision for the flow head. Thus, action-free data expands temporal supervision without providing embodiment-specific action labels. The task-specific world decoder is initialized from scratch and co-adapts with the VLM and policy. This joint objective keeps temporal prediction aligned with the downstream control interface throughout task adaptation. Vision-language-action policies. VLA methods adapt large multimodal models to robot demonstrations and decode continuous action chunks with autoregressive, diffusion, or flow-based heads [2]–[4]. Their pretrained semantic repre-sentations are useful for language grounding, as demon-strated by recent generalist policy and multimodal-control systems [6]–[8], but the action decoder is typically the only component trained to model robot dynamics. TS-WAM retains the VLM as a semantic context encoder while adding an explicit temporal latent-action and future-feature interface. 

Latent actions and world models. World models learn compact dynamics for planning and control [7], [9]–[14]. Reconstruction-free models such as DINO-WM predict fea-tures from a frozen visual representation [15], [16], avoiding the cost of pixel prediction. Existing approaches commonly first pretrain on large-scale video or interaction data to obtain latent-action or dynamics supervision, and then fine-tune on robot demonstrations for embodiment-specific control [5], [17]. This two-stage pipeline incurs substantial additional computational cost and resource demands. TS-WAM instead jointly optimizes a causally predicted latent action and world-action model within the deployed VLM. 

Joint-embedding predictive architectures. JEPA meth-ods learn by predicting the representation of a target region or future observation from an encoded context, rather than reconstructing the input in pixel space. A context encoder and predictor estimate the target embedding, which is pro-vided by a separately encoded target. The target encoder is commonly updated by an exponential moving average, providing a stable regression target without explicit negative samples. This objective emphasizes predictable semantic and temporal structure. I-JEPA and V-JEPA apply this principle to images and videos, respectively [18]–[21]. Their EMA target encoders provide stable self-supervision for visual representation learning. TS-WAM transfers this principle to VLA: the target is a latent action extracted from a future-aware VLM, while the student is restricted to current ob-servations, making the learned representation directly usable for control. 

Learning from heterogeneous video. Self-supervised video learning extracts temporal representations without Fig. 3. Vlm-jepa architecture. action labels [19], [22], but they must still be grounded in embodiment-specific robot actions. LAPA and UniVLA address this gap by learning latent actions from videos and adapting them to robot control [5], [17]. TS-WAM uses unlabeled human videos for temporal supervision while jointly optimizing robot actions. Its shared latent-action and future-feature interfaces align video-derived representations with the robot objective throughout adaptation. A second challenge is retaining video-derived knowledge during robot adaptation. Sequential objectives can cause catastrophic forgetting [23], motivating continued video su-pervision during robot adaptation. TS-WAM retains ego-video supervision while learning the robot action objective, anchoring the shared temporal representation to both visual domains. This joint training is intended to mitigate forgetting; verifying skill retention requires dedicated evaluation. III. M ETHOD

B. Simulation Results 

The joint architecture learns informative latent-action representations. The PCA visualization in Fig. 4 shows a compact latent space early in training that becomes substan-tially richer by the end of training, with variation preserved within individual tasks rather than collapse to a task proto-type. This temporal structure is learned jointly from robot and action-free video clips and supplies the representation used by downstream control modules. 

Delta-lwm’s prediction of future feature changes de-pends strongly on latent-action guidance. In Fig. 5, pre-dictions made with the normal sample-specific latent action nearly match the ground-truth feature-increment distribution across four LIBERO task classes. Replacing it with the training-distribution mean or removing it entirely produces clearly different PCA patterns. With current features and de-coder parameters fixed, this comparison isolates latent action as the source of the prediction. The normal-LA prediction retains localized structures close to the observed change, whereas mean-LA and zero-LA predictions are diffuse or misplaced. 

The end-to-end joint training architecture performs well across simulation benchmarks.

C. Real-World Results 

The vlm-jepa–delta-lwm joint architecture transfers effectively to real robots. On the Franka Research 3 and RevoTron platforms, TS-WAM is evaluated on four tasks spanning single-arm, bimanual, and dexterous manipulation. It reaches 72.50% mean success under the original in-distribution (IND) scenes, compared with 55.00% for the real-world baseline π0.5, and improves every platform–task pair in Table IV. Joint learning from heterogeneous data improves real-world generalization. Relative to π0.5, TS-WAM maintains higher success in all 12 OOD platform–task and condition combinations. Its mean OOD success is 62.92%, versus 35.00% for π0.5; the advantage is consistent across lighting, object-layout, and background changes (67.50%, 61.25%, and 60.00% versus 41.25%, 32.50%, and 31.25%). More-over, the IND-to-OOD drop is only 9.58 percentage points for TS-WAM, compared with 20.00 points for π0.5. These results indicate that combining action-labeled robot demon-strations with action-free video supervision strengthens ro-bustness to visual and spatial shifts while preserving the underlying manipulation goals. 

D. Ablation Studies 

Figures 6 and 7 report three ablations of the full TS-WAM on standard LIBERO and LIBERO-Plus: (a) w/o human video excludes additional human-video data; (b) w/o vlm-jepa removes the self-supervised JEPA architecture; and (c) w/o Pred ∆F removes the cross-attention pathway that introduces the predicted feature delta d∆F t into the DiT action head. 

Heterogeneous training with human video improves both basic control and generalization. On standard LIBERO, removing the additional human-video data lowers the mean success from 97.00% to 93.35%, with the largest loss on the Goal suite (8.4 points; Fig. 6). This establishes that action-free video contributes to core manipulation abil-ity, even when robot demonstrations are available. The same removal causes Overall success on LIBERO-Plus to fall from 75.7% to 64.8%, showing that the benefit is larger under the seven distribution-shift categories (Fig. 7) and therefore extends to generalization. 

The vlm-jepa self-supervised architecture is the key to using the heterogeneous data effectively. Among the LIBERO ablations, removing vlm-jepa causes the largest degradation: mean success drops to 92.35% and Long-suite success falls from 93.4% to 83.2%. Its effect is even clearer on LIBERO-Plus, where the no-vlm-jepa model reaches only 61.0% Overall, below the human-video and Pred ∆F abla-tions (64.8% and 70.8%), and is consistently weaker across the seven perturbation categories. Thus, the JEPA interface is central to converting action-free temporal supervision into useful representations for robot control and robust transfer. 

Predicted feature-delta conditioning enables the action expert to use future information. Removing the Pred 

∆F pathway reduces LIBERO mean success by 3.0 points, including a 6.2-point loss on Long, and lowers LIBERO-Plus Overall success by 4.9 points to 70.8%. The consistent drop in both standard control and shifted-condition evaluation indicates that feeding the predicted future feature to the action expert provides a positive, task-relevant signal rather than redundant visual context. V. LIMITATIONS TS-WAM introduces additional training costs. The EMA teacher branch modestly increases GPU memory consump-tion during training. In addition, conditioning the DiT action expert on predicted future features slows its convergence, leading to a longer overall training time. These limitations motivate the use of data partitioning to reduce GPU memory consumption and shared attention to improve the conver-gence efficiency of the action expert. VI. CONCLUSION We presented TS-WAM, a lightweight Teacher-Student World-Action Model for vision-language robot control. Through self-supervised temporal learning and explicit fu-ture prediction, TS-WAM learns from heterogeneous robot demonstrations and action-free human videos within aunified end-to-end training framework. Experiments on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and SimplerEnv, together with real-world evaluations on Franka Research 3 and RevoTron, demonstrate strong manipulation performance and improved generalization and robustness.
