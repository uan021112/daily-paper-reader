ABSTRACT
Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) is widely used to encode relative positions
in Transformers. In multi-view settings, however, patch coordinates are defined
independently in each image: the same 3D point can therefore have different co-
ordinates across views, making them insufficient for establishing cross-view cor-
respondences. We introduce WorldRoPE, a probabilistic world-centric RoPE for
multi-view attention. At each layer, WorldRoPE back-projects each patch token
into a set of volumetric Gaussian primitives in a shared world frame. These prim-
itives represent multiple plausible 3D supports for a token, jointly capturing alter-
native depth hypotheses, depth uncertainty, and the finite spatial extent of its patch
frustum. When a key token’s support is projected into a query view, it induces a
distribution over where that key may appear in the query image. WorldRoPE
integrates the rotary operator over this distribution in closed form, combining ro-
tations at the projected primitive centers according to their mixture weights with
covariance-dependent attenuation. This makes attention less sensitive to uncertain
key locations in the query view, without rasterizing or warping token features. The
formulation reduces to deterministic RoPE when the support collapses to a point.
Across layers, the predicted geometry guides cross-view attention, while the up-
dated features refine the geometry used at the next layer. Experiments on novel
view synthesis, depth estimation, and 3D geometry reconstruction demonstrate
the effectiveness of WorldRoPE for multi-view Transformers. Our project page is
available at https://cv.snu.ac.kr/research/WorldRoPE.

INTRODUCTION
Robotics and embodied perception require an understanding of the 3D world from observations
taken at different viewpoints. Multi-view Transformers aggregate these observations through at-
tention, with positional encoding introducing spatial information into token interactions. However,
standard image-space Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) (Su et al., 2024; Heo et al., 2024) en-
codes relative displacement using coordinates defined independently within each image. The same
3D scene point can therefore have different coordinates across views, while nearby image locations
may correspond to objects far apart in 3D. These image-space relationships alone are insufficient for
establishing cross-view correspondences.
Recent camera-aware approaches incorporate viewing geometry into attention through relative cam-
era poses and projective transformations, as in GTA (Miyato et al., 2024) and PRoPE (Li et al.,
2025b). More recently, ray-based rotary encodings (Wu et al., 2026; Xie et al., 2026) further locate
tokens along camera rays using predicted depths or predefined depth anchors and project these lo-
cations into the query view. An image patch, however, observes a volumetric region spanning its
viewing frustum, rather than positions confined to camera rays, and may contain multiple objects at
different depths. For example, a patch spanning a window frame and the scene behind it contains
distinct depth cues, while the corresponding depth estimates may remain uncertain. In addition, the
rays passing through a finite image patch span a volumetric frustum in 3D. A model of this 3D sup-
port should therefore jointly capture alternative depth hypotheses, depth uncertainty, and the finite
spatial extent of the patch frustum.

To address these challenges, we introduce WorldRoPE, a probabilistic world-centric RoPE for multi-
view Transformers. At each layer, we predict depth hypotheses, their uncertainty, and mixture
weights from token features. Using these predictions, WorldRoPE back-projects each patch token
into a set of volumetric Gaussian primitives in the shared world space. These primitives represent
multiple plausible 3D supports for a token, jointly capturing alternative depth hypotheses, depth un-
certainty, and the finite spatial extent of its patch frustum. Projecting these primitives into the query
view defines a distribution over where each key token may appear. We integrate the rotary opera-
tor over this distribution to guide cross-view attention, and the updated features inform geometry
prediction at the next layer. Table 1 summarizes the differences from existing camera encodings.
We present a principled mathematical analysis of WorldRoPE and examine its properties in multi-
view attention. First, the rotary expectation has a closed form that combines rotations at the projected
primitive centers according to their mixture weights, with covariance-dependent attenuation. The re-
sulting operator guides attention without explicitly rasterizing or warping token features. Second,
the mixture operator is equivalent to a single scaled rotation for each rotary frequency block. The
inverse of its effective attenuation can be interpreted as a token-adaptive temperature. Third, the
formulation reduces to deterministic RoPE when the positional distribution collapses to a point. Fi-
nally, predicted geometry guides attention at each layer, while the updated features inform geometry
prediction in the next layer.
Experiments on novel view synthesis, depth estimation, and 3D geometry reconstruction demon-
strate the effectiveness of WorldRoPE for multi-view Transformers. Ablations and visualizations
further examine the proposed geometric representation and rotary integration. Our main contribu-
tions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce WorldRoPE, a probabilistic world-centric RoPE that models token support
with volumetric Gaussian primitives, jointly capturing alternative depth hypotheses, depth
uncertainty, and the finite spatial extent of the patch frustum.
• We derive a closed-form rotary expectation over projected Gaussian distributions and inte-
grate it into multi-view attention, coupling geometry prediction with feature updates across
layers.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of WorldRoPE across novel view syn-
thesis, depth estimation, and 3D geometry reconstruction.

RELATED WORK
Positional encodings for Transformers.
Early approaches encode token positions using fixed or
learned absolute embeddings (Vaswani et al., 2017; Dosovitskiy et al., 2021). Subsequent works (Liu
et al., 2021; Press et al., 2022; Raffel et al., 2020; Shaw et al., 2018; Su et al., 2024) instead encode
relative positions between tokens. Among them, RoPE (Su et al., 2024) encodes relative displace-
ment by rotating queries and keys according to their coordinates, supporting generalization across
positional ranges. Recent studies adapt RoPE to multidimensional visual coordinates for image
recognition (Heo et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026), 3D reconstruction (Zhang et al., 2024a; Ren et al.,
2025), generation (Zhuo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2025; Yesiltepe et al., 2026;
Zhao et al., 2025), and video understanding (Wei et al., 2025). Other studies generalize RoPE us-
ing learned commuting matrices or Lie-group parameterizations (Yu et al., 2025; Ostmeier et al.,

recently, URoPE (Xie et al., 2026) and RayRoPE (Wu et al., 2026) use ray-based geometry for rotary
encoding in the query camera frame, with predefined anchors and predicted depth, respectively. In
contrast, WorldRoPE represents the 3D support of each token as a mixture of volumetric Gaussian
primitives in a shared world frame.

WORLDROPE
We propose WorldRoPE, a probabilistic world-centric Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) for
multi-view attention, as shown in Figure 1. After reviewing notation and RoPE in Section 3.1, we
model probabilistic token geometry with world-space Gaussian primitives in Section 3.2. We then
project these primitives into the query view and integrate the rotary operator over their positional
distributions in Section 3.3. Finally, we analyze the properties of WorldRoPE in Section 3.4.

EXPERIMENTS
We first evaluate WorldRoPE for novel view synthesis against existing positional encodings. We
then conduct detailed analyses of WorldRoPE, including ablation studies. Finally, we demonstrate
that WorldRoPE can serve as a plug-and-play mechanism for other tasks using multi-view attention.
4.1
MAIN RESULTS
Experimental setup.
We test WorldRoPE within LVSM (Jin et al., 2025) on RE10K (Zhou et al.,
2018) and Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023). We compare with Pl¨ucker ray encoding (Jin et al., 2025),
GTA (Miyato et al., 2024), PRoPE (Li et al., 2025b), RayRoPE (Wu et al., 2026), and URoPE (Xie
et al., 2026), using PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018) to measure rendered image quality.

Quantitative results.
Table 2 shows that WorldRoPE consistently outperforms all baselines across
all metrics on both the RE10K dataset of real-world scenes and the Objaverse dataset of object-
centric scenes. The improvement is particularly pronounced on the RE10K dataset, with a PSNR
gain of 0.49 dB over the strongest baseline. This suggests that modeling alternative depths and their
spatial extent is particularly useful for scenes containing foreground and background structures,
where a patch can span multiple depths and correspond to a broader region in another view.
Qualitative results.
Figure 3 compares synthesized target views. WorldRoPE better preserves
thin structures and fine details, including stair railings, window boundaries, bicycle wheels, and
brass knuckles, which the baselines often blur or merge with their surroundings. These observations
suggest that modeling alternative depth hypotheses helps distinguish structures at different depths
within a patch. The qualitative improvements are consistent with the gains in PSNR, SSIM, and
LPIPS.
4.2
ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION
Component ablation.
Table 3 separates the effects of probabilistic modeling, multiple depth hy-
potheses, and the patch footprint. Removing probabilistic modeling causes the largest drop, indicat-
ing the importance of incorporating positional uncertainty into attention. Performance also declines
with a single hypothesis, supporting the representation of alternative depths within a patch. The
smaller but consistent drop without the patch footprint shows that its spatial extent contributes be-
yond the patch-center ray.
Number of hypotheses.
Table 4 tests how many depth hypotheses are needed per token. Increas-
ing the number from one to four improves all three metrics, supporting the use of alternative depths
within a patch. Eight hypotheses bring no further gain; we therefore use four primitives per token.
Visualization of depth hypotheses.
Figure 4 examines how predicted depth hypotheses vary with
patch content. In Figure 4a, red highlights tokens with multiple peaks, which often appear near
object boundaries and overlapping structures. A patch spanning vegetation and sky produces two
peaks, whereas a patch within vegetation produces one dominant peak, as shown in Figure 4b. This
contrast shows that WorldRoPE can preserve distinct depths within a patch instead of collapsing
them into a single estimate.

allows only mixed operators to use covariance between the image coordinates, as illustrated in Fig-
ures 5a and 5b. On the Objaverse dataset, the mixed operator improves WorldRoPE by 0.19 dB in
PSNR, compared with 0.08 dB for RayRoPE and 0.04 dB for URoPE (Table 5). The larger gain
suggests that incorporating off-diagonal covariance into the rotary response makes our Gaussian
modeling more effective. See Appendix A.1 for results on the RE10K dataset.
Robustness.
We test sensitivity to camera perturbations during evaluation and depth initialization
during training. Under camera zoom and rotation noise, WorldRoPE achieves higher PSNR than the
baselines (Figures 6a and 6b). Zoom changes the projected extent of a patch, whereas rotation noise
shifts its projected position. These results are consistent with the benefits of modeling the patch
footprint and accounting for positional uncertainty. We also scale the predefined depth anchors of
URoPE and the initial depth biases of WorldRoPE from 0.1 to 10 times their default values. Worl-
dRoPE maintains higher PSNR with less variation across scales (Figure 6c). Its depth hypotheses
are updated from token features during training, reducing dependence on their initial placement.
Layerwise geometry refinement.
Figure 7 shows the predicted Gaussian primitives across atten-
tion layers and their projection into the query view. In deeper layers, their smaller extents indicate
reduced positional uncertainty, while the scene structure becomes more recognizable. The projected
key primitives approximately align with the query image, although their centers no longer follow
a regular image grid and their regions vary in size and orientation. Some regions are broader near
image boundaries, consistent with perspective magnification of a key-view patch when projected
into the query view. See Appendix A.4 for additional visualizations across all attention layers.

CONCLUSION
We presented WorldRoPE, a probabilistic world-centric RoPE for multi-view attention. Our method
represents token geometry with volumetric Gaussian primitives that jointly capture alternative depth
hypotheses, depth uncertainty, and the spatial extent of the patch frustum. Integrating the rotary
operator over their projected distributions yields a closed-form encoding that attenuates uncertain
positional interactions without rasterizing or warping token features. Across layers, predicted ge-
ometry guides attention, while updated features inform subsequent geometry predictions. Experi-
ments on novel view synthesis, depth estimation, and feed-forward 3D reconstruction demonstrate
the effectiveness of WorldRoPE across multi-view tasks and architectures.

C
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
C.1
COMMON IMPLEMENTATION
Across all experiments, we implement each predictor Φ as a layer-wise linear head. Mixture weights
are normalized using a softmax, depth standard deviations are parameterized exponentially, and
depth means are clamped to be nonnegative. These predicted mixture weights, depth means, and
depth standard deviations receive no direct auxiliary supervision and are learned end-to-end through
the task-specific objective. Table 11 summarizes the implementation settings for each task.
C.2
NOVEL VIEW SYNTHESIS: LVSM (JIN ET AL., 2025)
Our experiments use the 25M-parameter LVSM architecture implemented in the URoPE code-
base (Xie et al., 2026) 2. The model consists of six Transformer layers with a hidden dimension
of 768, 16 attention heads, and a feedforward dimension of 1024. Images have a resolution of
256 × 256 and are divided into 8 × 8 patches. Each training sample consists of two reference views
and one supervised target view. We train the model for 80K steps on two GPUs with a global batch
size of 16, using MSE loss and VGG perceptual loss with weights of 1 and 0.5, respectively.
For the positional-encoding baselines, we use the Pl¨ucker Ray, GTA, and PRoPE implementations
provided in the URoPE codebase, while URoPE is evaluated using its native implementation. We
implement RayRoPE following its official codebase. All methods use the same backbone and train-
ing protocol.
We train separate models on RealEstate10K (RE10K) (Zhou et al., 2018) and Objaverse (Deitke
et al., 2023). For RE10K, we follow the train/test scene split used in the official RayRoPE code-
base (Wu et al., 2026) 3. For Objaverse, we use object renderings from eight camera directions
spanning the full azimuth range.
For the mixed RoPE (Heo et al., 2024) variants, we initialize learnable frequency vectors with the
same magnitudes as the corresponding original frequencies but with randomly sampled unit direc-
tions. For RayRoPE, Table 8 reports results from mixing the u and v coordinates without mixing
inverse depth, since additionally mixing inverse depth reduced PSNR by 0.28 dB on RE10K and
1.07 dB on Objaverse.
C.3
DEPTH ESTIMATION: UNIMATCH (XU ET AL., 2023)
We use the official UniMatch codebase (Xu et al., 2023) 4. Models are trained from scratch on the
Scenes11 (Ummenhofer et al., 2017), SUN3D (Xiao et al., 2013), and RGBD (Sturm et al., 2012)
datasets for 50K steps on two GPUs with a global batch size of 16. The Transformer has six layers,
144 feature channels, and three attention heads. Training uses 224 × 288 images, with evaluation at
240 × 320. Self-attention retains the 2 × 2 shifted-window structure, while cross-attention is global.
Other settings follow the UniMatch codebase.
The geometry-aware encodings of GTA, PRoPE, URoPE, RayRoPE, and WorldRoPE in Table 6 are
applied only to cross-attention, whereas the Pl¨ucker Ray baseline adds linearly projected Pl¨ucker
coordinates to the image features. GTA, PRoPE, URoPE, and RayRoPE follow their official imple-
mentations, adapted to UniMatch.
For evaluation, we follow the official UniMatch protocol and use the same benchmark evaluation sets
and preprocessing provided in its codebase. For unseen-data evaluation on ScanNet++ (Yeshwanth
et al., 2023), we adopt the validation and test splits defined by MapAnything (Keetha et al., 2026).
To account for scale differences between the training data and unseen ScanNet++, we align each
prediction to the ground-truth scale using least squares before computing Abs Rel and RMSE.
