## Abstract 

Generating compact and geometrically faithful 3D meshes directly from point clouds remains a fundamental challenge. Point clouds are unordered and sparse, whereas meshes exhibit irregular structure and varying topology. As a re-sult, many existing approaches rely on implicit representa-tions followed by surface extraction or reconstruction. Al-though effective, these pipelines can produce dense or over-smoothed meshes, often requiring computationally expen-sive post-processing and simplification. We present Opti-musMesh , a framework for direct compact triangle mesh generation from point clouds using sparse latent pivot con-ditioning. Our key idea is to compress 2,048 oriented in-put points into only 16 sparse latent pivots, reducing the geometric conditioning set by 128 ×. These pivots provide a compact structural representation shared across a two-stage autoregressive framework that first generates mesh vertices and then predicts triangular faces conditioned on the generated vertices and the same pivots. Compared with the evaluated recent point-cloud-conditioned autoregres-sive methods, which use 257 decoder-conditioning tokens, OptimusMesh uses only 16 , yielding a 16 .1× shorter con-ditioning sequence. Experiments show that OptimusMesh produces the most compact outputs among the compared recent autoregressive methods, using 25 .7% –94 .1% fewer faces while maintaining competitive geometric fidelity and distributional quality. 

1arXiv:2610.01148v1 [cs.CV] 1 Oct 2026 1. Introduction 

The field of 3D mesh generation has witnessed remarkable advancements in recent years in 3D computer vision and computer graphics [13, 33, 44]. 3D meshes are a funda-mental representation of 3D geometry and are extensively used in video games, movies, augmented and virtual real-ity, and architectural modeling. In many practical settings, meshes must remain compact to meet memory, bandwidth, and real-time rendering constraints, while also supporting efficient level-of-detail (LOD) rendering and transmission. Meshes represent object geometry through vertices, edges, and faces, but their irregular connectivity introduces intrinsic challenges for generative models. Mesh topology can vary widely across object instances, even within the same category. Consequently, template-based approaches [11, 15, 23, 40, 41, 45] that deform meshes with predefined connectivity can be limited in representing diverse shapes and large topological variations. Many recent methods rely on neural 3D representations such as triplanes [13, 19] and FlexiCubes [44], followed by surface extraction. Although effective, the resulting meshes can be dense or over-smoothed and may require additional simplification. Autoregressive methods instead generate explicit mesh structure directly. PolyGen [29], MeshGPT [35], MeshAnything [5], MeshAnythingV2 [6], FastMesh [18], DeepMesh [47], and MeshRipple [21] im-prove mesh tokenization, topology modeling, or output sequence efficiency. For point-cloud inputs, reconstruc-tion methods such as Screened PSR [17], SAP [32], and Point2Mesh [12] can recover geometry, but often rely on dense extraction or costly per-shape processing. In contrast, the efficiency of the geometric conditioning supplied to di-rect mesh generators remains less explored. In this work, we propose OptimusMesh , a framework for direct compact mesh generation from point clouds using sparse latent pivot conditioning. A point-cloud encoder [27] compresses 2,048 oriented input points into only K=16 

spatial latent pivots. A two-stage autoregressive model first generates mesh vertices and then predicts triangular faces conditioned on the generated vertices and the same sparse latent pivots (Fig. 1). Crucially, the same 16 latent pivots provide geometric conditioning to both generation stages, forming a compact shared structural interface between the input point cloud and the mesh decoders. This reduces the geometric con-ditioning set by 128 × relative to the 2,048 input points and uses a 16 .1× shorter decoder-conditioning sequence than recent point-cloud-conditioned autoregressive methods us-ing 257 conditioning tokens. The compact conditioning also reduces the amount of geometric context consumed re-peatedly during autoregressive decoding while preserving spatial and learned geometric information. Our main contributions are: 1. We propose OptimusMesh , a direct point-cloud-to-mesh framework that autoregressively generates com-pact triangle meshes by predicting vertices followed by triangular faces, without relying on implicit surface ex-traction or costly post-hoc simplification. 2. We introduce a two-stage vertex-and-face generation ar-chitecture with a shared sparse geometric conditioning interface , where the same latent pivots guide vertex gen-eration and subsequently face generation together with the generated vertices. 3. We design a sparse latent pivot representation that com-presses 2,048 oriented points into only 16 pivots, re-ducing the geometric conditioning set by 128 × and us-ing a 16 .1× shorter decoder-conditioning sequence than recent point-cloud-conditioned autoregressive methods. Experiments show 25 .7% –94 .1% fewer faces than the compared recent autoregressive methods while main-taining competitive geometric fidelity and distributional quality. 

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. 3D Shape Representations and Mesh Extrac-tion 

3D shapes can be represented explicitly as meshes or point clouds, or implicitly through continuous fields. Neural im-plicit representations [7, 28, 30] have shown strong perfor-mance for object and scene modeling [16, 31, 36]. Like-wise, generative approaches operate on voxels [3, 43], point clouds [25, 46, 48], or neural 3D representations [9, 10, 20]. To obtain explicit meshes, these representations commonly require surface extraction such as Marching Cubes [24] or related methods [34]. Recent pipelines based on tri-planes [13, 19], FlexiCubes [44], or intermediate point rep-resentations [27] follow a similar paradigm. Although ef-fective, the resulting meshes can be dense or require addi-tional processing and simplification. Our work instead di-rectly generates compact triangle meshes from point-cloud inputs. 

2.2. Point Cloud to Mesh Reconstruction 

Classical methods such as the Ball Pivoting Algorithm (BPA) [1] and Screened Poisson Surface Reconstruction (PSR) [17] recover surfaces directly from point samples, but are sensitive to input density and sampling qual-ity. Learning-based methods such as Shape-as-Points (SAP) [32] predict an implicit indicator field followed by surface extraction, typically producing high-resolution meshes. Point2Mesh [12] instead optimizes a mesh for each input shape through iterative refinement, providing strong single-shape fitting at the cost of per-shape optimization. 2.3. Autoregressive Mesh Generation 

Autoregressive mesh generation directly models explicit mesh structure without relying on intermediate volumet-ric or implicit representations. PolyGen [29] factorizes mesh generation into autoregressive vertex prediction fol-lowed by face generation, while MeshGPT [35] intro-duces learned discrete mesh tokens through vector quan-tization. MeshAnything [5] and MeshAnythingV2 [6] ex-tend this direction to point-cloud-conditioned artist meshes, with the latter introducing adjacent mesh tokenization. Piv-otMesh [42] uses structured pivot-vertex guidance, while EdgeRunner [38] and TreeMeshGPT [22] improve mesh se-rialization through traversal- and tree-based sequencing. Recent methods further address output-sequence com-plexity. FastMesh [18] decouples vertex and face generation to reduce token redundancy and accelerate mesh generation, DeepMesh [47] targets high-resolution meshes through improved tokenization and reinforcement-learning-based post-training, and MeshRipple [21] introduces topology-aligned generation and long-range contextual modeling. In contrast, OptimusMesh focuses on the conditioning side : it compresses 2,048 oriented input points into only K=16 

latent pivots, reducing the geometric conditioning set by 

128 × and yielding a 16 .1× shorter decoder-conditioning sequence than recent point-cloud-conditioned autoregres-sive methods using 257 conditioning tokens. The same sparse conditioning is shared by both vertex and face gen-eration under bounded mesh complexity. 

## 3. Methodology 

3.1. Overview 

We propose a framework for direct mesh generation from point clouds (Fig. 2). Given an input point cloud, we first extract a sparse set of K=16 latent points, referred to as 

latent pivots , using a point-cloud encoder (Fig. 3). Unlike dense geometric conditioning, our framework compresses the input point cloud into a compact latent interface shared across both decoding stages. These pivots condition an au-toregressive vertex model that generates mesh vertices, fol-lowed by a face model that predicts triangular connectivity using both the generated vertices and the same latent pivots. Vertices are generated sequentially using a causal Trans-former that predicts a distribution over the next quantized coordinate token. The face model takes the generated vertex set, latent pivots, and current sequence of face indices as in-put and predicts vertex indices to form triangular faces. This two-stage design directly produces compact meshes without relying on dense surface extraction or post-processing. 

3.2. Sparse Latent Pivot Encoder 

Our pipeline requires a point-cloud encoder that maps the input point cloud into a sparse set of latent pivots, each as-sociated with a feature embedding. We adopt the sparse point-cloud encoder architecture of SLIDE [27] and con-figure it to encode 2,048 oriented input points into K=16 

latent pivots of dimension D=48 . Each pivot contains a 3D spatial coordinate and 45 learned feature channels. The en-coder is trained from scratch on the mixed ShapeNet [4], Objaverse [8], and Toys4K [37] training set without cate-3FEATURE DIMENSION

K=16 latent pivots. The input to each SA module is a set of points with per-point features. Each module first subsamples the points using farthest point sampling (FPS). Features are then propagated to the subsampled points by retrieving k nearest neighbors, transforming their features with a shared MLP, and aggregating them using an attention mechanism. The SA hierarchy progressively downsamples the input point cloud while extracting multi-scale geometric features. In our setup, each input point contains its 3D co-ordinates and surface normal. We refer readers to PDR [26] for the full SA definition. The encoder transfers multi-scale features from the fi-nal SA hierarchy to the latent pivot locations using the FT module proposed in PDR [26]. Since the FT module re-quires initial features at the target locations, a lightweight PointNet++-style branch first extracts initial pivot features. Features from the final SA level are then transferred to the pivot locations and concatenated with these initial features to produce the final latent pivot embeddings. The result-ing representation captures both local geometric informa-tion and global shape structure. While SLIDE [27] uses this sparse representation for point-cloud generation in latent space, we use the resulting pivots as compact geometric conditioning for both autore-gressive vertex and face decoding.

Training. We train the vertex model with teacher forc-ing using cross-entropy over valid (non-PAD ) positions in the flattened coordinate-token sequence. During training, token-level input dropout is applied to the decoder inputs. The gener-ated coordinates are then dequantized back to the continu-ous normalized mesh space.

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Datasets and preprocessing. 

We train the mesh decoders on a mixed dataset of 35 ,907 

compact meshes comprising 16 ,578 ShapeNet [4], 16 ,271 

Objaverse [8], and 3,058 Toys4K [37] samples. The com-bined dataset contains 28 ,105 training, 2,552 validation, and 5,250 test meshes. We use ShapeNet as the primary quantitative benchmark for direct comparison with prior mesh-generation and reconstruction methods. Following prior autoregressive mesh modeling [29, 35, 42], we convert meshes to triangular representations and construct compact targets suitable for sequence modeling. For ShapeNet, we apply planar decimation in Blender fol-lowing prior autoregressive mesh preprocessing [29, 35]. For Objaverse, we follow the mesh preprocessing practice of PivotMesh [42], while retaining naturally compact assets within our target complexity. For Toys4K, mesh compo-nents are joined, triangulated, cleaned, and simplified using planar decimation. Across all datasets, decoder targets are restricted to at most 800 triangular faces and 900 vertices. Meshes are normalized and quantized to 8 bits per co-ordinate before autoregressive sequence construction. For geometric conditioning, we sample 2,048 oriented surface points per shape. Additional details on dataset construc-tion, filtering, splits, and preprocessing are provided in the supplementary material. 

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate generated meshes us-ing both distribution-level quality and paired geometric fi-delity. For distribution-level evaluation, we uniformly sam-ple 2,048 points from each generated and reference mesh and report Minimum Matching Distance based on Chamfer Distance (MMD-CD), coverage (COV-CD), and the devia-tion of 1-NNA from its ideal value, |1-NNA − 0.5|. Lower MMD-CD indicates better distributional fidelity, higher COV-CD indicates broader coverage, and a smaller 1-NNA deviation indicates better agreement between generated and reference distributions. For paired geometric evaluation, each generated mesh is compared with its corresponding ground-truth mesh. Both meshes are normalized, and 5,000 points are uniformly sampled from each surface. We report symmetric Chamfer Distance (CD-L1), Hausdorff Distance (HD), Normal Con-sistency (NC), and F1 at 2% of the ground-truth bounding-box diagonal. Mesh compactness is measured by the aver-age numbers of vertices (#V) and faces (#F). For efficiency evaluation, we additionally report the number of input points, decoder conditioning tokens, and inference time. Conditioning tokens refer to the encoded geometric sequence supplied to the mesh decoder rather than the raw point-cloud size. 

Baselines. We compare OptimusMesh with recent point-cloud-conditioned autoregressive mesh generators, including MeshAnything [5], MeshAnythingV2 [6], FastMesh [18], and MeshRipple [21]. These methods form the primary comparison for paired geometric fi-delity. We additionally include Screened PSR [17], SAP [32], and NKSR [14] in the distributional-quality and compactness evaluation to provide reference against surface-reconstruction approaches. 

Implementation details. OptimusMesh consists of three components: (i) the sparse latent pivot encoder (Sec. 3.2), (ii) the autoregressive vertex model (Sec. 3.3), and (iii) the autoregressive face model (Sec. 3.4). The point-cloud en-coder is trained from scratch on the mixed ShapeNet, Obja-verse, and Toys4K training set without category condition-ing to produce K=16 latent pivots with feature dimension 

D=48 . Encoder training takes approximately 48 hours. The trained encoder is then kept frozen while training the mesh decoders. 

Vertex model. The vertex model uses a 12-layer Trans-former decoder with hidden dimension 512, feed-forward dimension 2048, 8 attention heads, and dropout 0.1. It is trained with teacher forcing on 8-bit quantized coordi-nate tokens using Adam and a warmup-cosine learning-rate schedule. We train the vertex decoder with a global batch size of 12 on 3× NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPUs for ap-proximately 45 hours. 

Face model. The face model uses a 12-layer vertex en-coder and a 12-layer masked Transformer decoder with hid-den dimension 256, feed-forward dimension 1024, and 8 attention heads. It is trained with teacher forcing using AdamW and a progressive face-budget curriculum up to 

800 triangles. We train the face decoder with a global batch size of 8 on 4× NVIDIA Tesla V100 32GB GPUs for ap-proximately 48 hours. After encoder training, the vertex and face decoders are trained independently and can be trained in parallel.

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented OptimusMesh , a direct point-cloud-to-mesh framework using sparse latent pivot conditioning and au-toregressive vertex and face generation. By compressing 

2,048 oriented input points into only K=16 latent piv-ots, our method reduces geometric conditioning while di-rectly generating compact triangle meshes with bounded complexity. Experiments show that OptimusMesh produces the most compact outputs and the shortest conditioning se-quence among the compared methods while maintaining competitive geometric fidelity, demonstrating the effective-ness of sparse geometric conditioning for compact mesh generation.

## 7. Dataset and Preprocessing Details 

7.1. Dataset Composition 

We construct our data from ShapeNet [4], Objaverse [8], and Toys4K [37]. Table 5 summarizes the point-cloud cor-pus used for sparse-pivot encoder training and the compact-mesh corpus used for vertex and face decoder training. The encoder corpus is larger because encoder training requires only a valid oriented point cloud, whereas de-coder training additionally requires a compact triangular target satisfying our complexity constraints. Source-level train/validation/test assignments are preserved throughout preprocessing. 

7.2. ShapeNet 

We use 13 categories: airplane, bench, cabinet, car, chair, display, lamp, speaker, rifle, sofa, table, telephone, and ves-sel. Oriented point clouds contain surface positions and nor-mals. For decoder targets, we follow prior autoregressive mesh preprocessing [29, 35]. Meshes are triangulated and sim-plified using planar decimation in Blender [2]. When mul-tiple valid compact variants are available, one is randomly selected during training, while validation and test selection are deterministic. Targets contain at most 800 faces and 900 

vertices. 

7.3. Objaverse 

For Objaverse, we retain naturally compact GLB assets af-ter applying scene-graph transformations and welding geo-metrically duplicated vertices. Only valid triangular meshes with 50 –800 faces and at most 900 vertices are retained; no simplification is applied. Meshes are centered and scaled such that the longest bounding-box dimension equals one, after which surface points and normals are sampled. Objects are assigned deterministically to approximately 85% /5% /10% train-ing/validation/test partitions. 

7.4. Toys4K 

For Toys4K, mesh components are joined, triangulated, cleaned, and normalized. Surface points and normals are sampled from the normalized source mesh before simplifi-cation. Compact targets are produced using planar or edge-collapse simplification and restricted to 20 –800 faces and at most 900 vertices. Candidate meshes are se-lected using bidirectional nearest-surface error, with 95th-percentile error at most 0.035 and maximum error at most 

0.12 . Objects are split deterministically into approximately 

85% /5% /10% training/validation/test partitions. 

7.5. Point-Cloud and Decoder Processing 

Across all datasets, 2,048 oriented surface points are sam-pled per object. The SLIDE-style encoder [27] is trained from scratch on the mixed training set without category con-ditioning and produces K=16 latent pivots, each containing a 3D position and 45 learned feature channels. The trained encoder is frozen during decoder training. Decoder meshes are mean-centered and scaled to ap-proximately [−0.5, 0.5] 3, followed by 8-bit coordinate quantization. Duplicate and unreferenced vertices are re-moved, vertices are ordered by (z, y, x ), and face indices are remapped accordingly. The vertex model supports at most 900 vertices ( 2,701 tokens including EOS ); after quan-tization and filtering, 33 ,822 meshes remain for vertex train-ing. The face model uses the same processed vertices and supports at most 800 triangles ( 3,201 tokens including 

STOP ).
