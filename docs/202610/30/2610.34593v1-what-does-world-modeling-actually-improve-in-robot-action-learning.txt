# Abstract 

World Action Models (WAMs) combine future-video pre-diction with robot action learning, yet improvements in task success alone do not reveal how predictive supervision manifests in execution behavior or whether its benefits per-sist under task and environmental shifts. We conduct con-trolled future-video prediction loss ablations within Fast-WAM and OpenWAM, keeping model architecture, initial-ization, robot training data, and training steps fixed within each model family. Under clean conditions on seen tasks, predictive supervision increases success rates by 24.38 and 72.91 percentage points, respectively. Trajectory clustering further reveals higher success rates within several coarse trajectory clusters, including cases where cluster frequen-cies change little, indicating that the gains are not solely reflected in changes in the prevalence of trajectory pat-terns. However, these benefits do not consistently persist under distribution shifts: improvements on unseen tasks vary across models and tasks, while both models exhibit low success rates under the evaluated environmental random-izations, without a consistent advantage from predictive su-pervision. We further introduce future-video prediction as an auxiliary training objective for π0.5, where a lightweight Transformer predictor used only during training increases success from 82.94% to 89.19%. These findings support future-video prediction as effective auxiliary supervision for action learning, while distinguishing improved execution on familiar tasks from reliable transfer and showing that such gains need not rely on a large pretrained video diffusion transformer (DiT) in the evaluated setting. 

# 1. Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models combine vision-language pretraining with robot data to learn policies that generate actions from visual observations and language in-structions [4, 8, 10, 17]. Building on this foundation, World Action Models (WAMs) incorporate future visual predic-tion into action learning, training models to predict envi-ronmental changes alongside robot actions. Methods such as GR-1, GR-2, PAD, and InternW0 integrate these objec-tives through different architectures, demonstrating the po-tential of future prediction to support robot action learn-ing [5, 6, 9, 13]. However, improvements in task success alone do not reveal which aspects of policy execution ben-efit from predictive supervision, nor do they establish that the model has acquired transferable understanding of its en-vironment. Prior work provides an important starting point for un-derstanding these effects. Fast-WAM separately examines video co-training during training and future generation at inference, finding that removing the former leads to a larger drop in action performance [14]. This finding supports the value of predictive supervision during training, but final success rates do not reveal how the gains manifest in ex-ecution behavior or to what extent they persist across mod-els, tasks, and environments. Rather than simply revisiting whether video prediction improves task success, we investi-gate where the benefits of predictive supervision appear and under which conditions they persist. This distinction mat-ters for assessing the practical value of future prediction and determining how such supervision should be used in subse-quent model designs. To investigate these questions, we conduct controlled future-video prediction loss ablations within two WAM families, FastWAM and OpenWAM. Within each family, we keep the architecture, initialization, robot training data, and number of training steps fixed, varying only whether the future-video prediction loss is included. We first evalu-ate execution success on seen tasks under clean conditions. We then use trajectory clustering to examine changes in the 1arXiv:2610.34593v1 [cs.RO] 25 Oct 2026 frequencies of coarse trajectory patterns and their within-cluster success rates, providing behavioral evidence beyond aggregate success. We further vary task identity and en-vironment configuration separately to assess whether the gains persist on unseen tasks and under environmental ran-domization. Together, these comparisons distinguish im-provements in familiar-task execution, observable changes in behavior, and the conditions under which predictive su-pervision remains beneficial. These analyses also raise a practical design question: if future-video prediction provides useful training supervi-sion, does obtaining its benefits necessarily require a large pretrained video-generation model? To examine this, we introduce future-video prediction as an auxiliary training objective for the existing VLA model π0.5 [3]. We com-pare the original policy with variants equipped with either a lightweight Transformer predictor or a pretrained Wan DiT video-generation branch. Both auxiliary prediction branches are used only during training and removed at infer-ence. This comparison tests whether a lightweight predictor can improve action performance without introducing a large pretrained video DiT branch. Figure 1 summarizes our research questions, experimen-tal design, and main findings. Our experiments reveal a clear distinction between the benefits of predictive supervision and the conditions under which they persist. Across ten seen tasks under clean condi-tions, adding the future-video prediction loss increases suc-cess rates by 24.38 and 72.91 percentage points for Fast-WAM and OpenWAM, respectively, demonstrating sub-stantial execution gains in both WAM families. Trajectory analysis further shows that within-cluster success rates in-crease for several coarse trajectory clusters even when their frequencies change little, indicating that the gains are not solely reflected in shifts in the prevalence of trajectory pat-terns. However, these benefits do not consistently persist under distribution shifts: improvements on unseen tasks de-pend on the model and task, while both WAMs exhibit low absolute success rates under the evaluated environmental randomizations, with no consistent advantage from predic-tive supervision. For π0.5, the training-only lightweight pre-dictor increases success from 82.94% to 89.19%, showing that auxiliary prediction can provide positive gains in this setting without a large pretrained video DiT branch. These findings indicate that future-video prediction can provide effective supervision for action learning, but its benefits on familiar tasks, their manifestations in execu-tion behavior, and their persistence under distribution shifts must be evaluated separately rather than inferred from ag-gregate success alone. The lightweight auxiliary predictor further shows that obtaining benefits from future prediction does not necessarily require a large video-generation back-bone. Our results therefore provide empirical guidance for selecting training objectives in future WAMs and VLAs: predictive supervision should be evaluated in terms of its observed execution gains and the conditions under which they hold, rather than assuming that predictive capability, transfer performance, and prediction-model scale are nec-essarily linked. 

# 2. Related Work 

Future Prediction in Robot Action Learning. World Action Models (WAMs) combine future visual prediction with action generation, enabling robot policies to model fu-ture environmental changes alongside control actions. GR-1 and GR-2 integrate video pretraining with joint prediction of future visual observations and robot actions [6, 13], while PAD jointly models future images and actions through a denoising process [9]. InternW0 adopts an asymmetric ar-chitecture, using a high-capacity video expert to model vi-sual dynamics and a lighter action expert for control [5]. These approaches illustrate different ways of combining fu-ture prediction with action learning, but the role of predic-tive information differs across systems. An important distinction is whether future prediction serves as auxiliary supervision during training or explic-itly informs action generation at inference time. The former influences learning through an additional prediction objec-tive, whereas the latter uses predicted future observations or intermediate goals to guide subsequent actions. For exam-ple, CoT-VLA generates visual subgoals at inference time and uses them as intermediate conditions for action predic-tion [16]. These two approaches can coexist within a sin-gle system, but their contributions to action performance should be distinguished. Our study focuses on the bene-fits of adding a future-video prediction loss during training, rather than planning with explicitly predicted future states at inference. 

Understanding the Role of Predictive Supervision. Be-yond developing joint visual prediction and action genera-tion models, recent studies have examined the specific con-tributions of future prediction within WAMs. Fast-WAM separately investigates video co-training during training and future generation at inference, finding that removing the for-mer leads to a larger decline in action performance [14]. This provides direct evidence for the value of predictive supervision during training. OpenWAM systematically studies design choices involving generative backbones, vi-sual representations, world-action interactions, and training strategies, examining how different configurations affect world-action learning [12]. Together, these studies high-light that WAM performance depends not only on future prediction but also on architectural choices, information ex-change, and training configurations. Although Fast-WAM demonstrates that video co-training improves action performance in its system, aggre-gate success rates alone do not reveal how these gains man-ifest in execution behavior. Higher success may accompany changes in the frequencies of different trajectory patterns or arise within similar trajectory patterns. Likewise, improve-ments on familiar tasks do not establish whether the benefits persist under task or environmental changes. We extend this line of investigation through controlled future-video predic-tion loss comparisons within two WAM families, FastWAM and OpenWAM, complemented by coarse-grained trajec-tory analysis and evaluations under distribution shifts. Our study characterizes the execution gains, observable behav-ioral differences, and conditions under which predictive su-pervision remains beneficial, without attributing these ob-servations to a specific internal control mechanism. 

Auxiliary Prediction and Model Design. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models improve robot policies through vision-language pretraining, robot data, and action generation objectives. RT-2 explores transferring vision-language knowledge to robotic control [17], while OpenVLA, Open X-Embodiment, and Octo advance generalist policy learning from heterogeneous robot datasets [8, 10, 11]. For action generation, π0 uses flow matching to model continuous actions, while π0.5 further combines heterogeneous co-training with high-level se-mantic prediction to improve performance in open-world settings [3, 4]. The high-level semantic prediction in 

π0.5 differs in its supervision target from the future-video prediction studied here. We investigate whether adding future visual prediction to an existing VLA training setup can provide useful auxiliary supervision for action learning. Future prediction need not involve direct RGB video generation. In visual representation learning, V-JEPA learns representations by predicting masked video fea-tures rather than reconstructing pixels [2]. V-JEPA 2 fur-ther scales video representation learning and develops an action-conditioned latent world model for robotic plan-ning [1]. Within world-action modeling, ImageWAM ex-plores image-editing priors as an alternative to dense video generation [15]. These studies illustrate different choices of prediction targets and model architectures, although they do not directly establish which predictive design is most bene-ficial for action learning. Motivated by these alternatives, we further examine future-video prediction as an auxiliary training objective for an existing VLA. Using π0.5, we compare a lightweight Transformer predictor with a pretrained Wan DiT video-generation branch, focusing on whether auxiliary predic-tion can improve action performance without introducing a large pretrained video DiT. Because the two branches dif-3fer in architecture and training configuration, this compari-son evaluates the feasibility of lightweight auxiliary predic-tion rather than isolating model scale as the cause of perfor-mance differences. 

# 3. Study Design 

This section describes how we isolate the contribution of future-video prediction supervision to action learning. We first define the controlled variable and comparison logic, then specify task and environment conditions, and finally describe the three types of evidence used throughout the study. Figure 2 illustrates the two auxiliary video-prediction configurations for π0.5.

3.1. Controlled Variable: The Video Prediction Loss 

We compare two training-objective configurations sepa-rately within FastWAM and OpenWAM. Both configura-tions use the same visual observations, language instruc-tions, and action supervision; only the weight of the future-video prediction loss differs: 

L = Laction + λvideo Lvideo . (1) When λvideo = 0 , training uses only the action loss; when 

λvideo = 1 , it uses both losses. This ablation does not change the model architecture or parameter count: within each family, the two settings share the same initialization, module-freezing configuration, and training steps. Two clarifications are important. First, setting λvideo =0 disables the explicit future-video prediction loss; it does not remove the world-model architecture or its visual repre-sentations. Second, we repeat the same comparison across two model families to test whether the observed benefit is confined to a single implementation. Absolute performance differences between FastWAM and OpenWAM should not be treated as a controlled architecture comparison, because the two families differ in pretraining, model size, and train-ing pipeline. Beyond the WAM experiments, we extend auxiliary video prediction to the VLA policy π0.5, comparing the original policy with two training-time auxiliary predictors. The design and results for π0.5 are presented separately in Sec. 5. 

3.2. Tasks, Environments, and Evaluation Condi-tions 

All experiments use bimanual manipulation data from RoboTwin 2.0 [7] for training. The training set contains 40 tasks, with 45 demonstrations per task used for training and five reserved for validation. Tasks designated for Unseen evaluation are excluded from training. Evaluation involves four condition dimensions. Seen tasks have their task identity present in the training data; evaluation uses held-out initial states distinct from train-ing, so it tests execution from new starting configurations rather than replaying training episodes. Unseen tasks have their task identity entirely excluded from training, testing cross-task transfer. Clean environments match the standard scene configuration used during training. Randomized envi-ronments alter visual and scene configurations at test time, probing robustness to environmental perturbation. The three WAM experiments use different task sets—an important prerequisite for interpreting the results. Exper-iment 1 evaluates seen-task gains on ten Seen tasks. Ex-periment 2 analyzes trajectory behavior on a separate set of five tasks (only open laptop overlaps with Experi-ment 1). Experiment 3 uses a distinct set of five Seen tasks and ten Unseen tasks to evaluate performance under distri-bution shift. Absolute success rates should not be compared across experiments. 

3.3. Evidence Types and Analysis Protocols 

We employ three types of evidence, each addressing a dif-ferent aspect of predictive supervision. 

Within-family success-rate comparison. We report the success-rate difference ∆SR = SR( λvideo = 1) −

SR( λvideo = 0) within each model family. Experiment 1 evaluates ten Seen tasks in Clean environments with 3,200 rollouts per setting. Experiment 3 extends the evaluation to the four Seen/Unseen × Clean/Randomized conditions, yielding 19,200 rollouts per family. 

Trajectory-level behavior decomposition. Changes in aggregate success can arise from entering different coarse trajectory modes or from higher success within similar modes. To distinguish the two, Experiment 2 pools trajec-tories from both loss settings within each task and clusters them using numerical trajectory features; model identity, scene identity, and success labels are excluded from cluster-ing. We then compare mode frequencies and within-mode success rates. Each model family contributes 12,800 tra-jectories across five tasks. The resulting clusters are coarse trajectory patterns, not semantically verified strategies. 

Benefit persistence under distribution shift. The Seen/Unseen × Clean/Randomized grid in Experiment 3 tests whether the gains observed on familiar tasks extend to unseen tasks and altered environments. For the π0.5 experiments, three variants are each eval-uated with 3,200 rollouts sharing matched initial observa-tions and action noise. We report success-rate changes rela-tive to the original policy with paired scene-level bootstrap confidence intervals. # 4. What Does Predictive Supervision Change? 

This section presents the three WAM experiments, orga-nized around three progressive questions: how large is the benefit of predictive supervision on familiar tasks, how does it manifest in execution behavior, and whether it persists un-der distribution shift. 

4.1. Strong Gains on Familiar Tasks 

We begin with the most basic question: does adding the future-video prediction loss improve execution success on tasks seen during training? Table 1 reports the paired comparison on ten Seen+Clean tasks. FastWAM improves from 14.97% to 39.34% (+24.38 percentage points); OpenWAM improves from 7.00% to 79.91% (+72.91 points). Both families show substantial gains, indicating that the effect is not an artifact of a sin-gle implementation. The magnitude of the gain varies across tasks, how-ever. All ten OpenWAM tasks improve, with per-task gains ranging from 50.31 to 87.19 points. FastWAM im-proves on eight tasks, but put bottles dustbin and 

rotate qrcode show small decreases ( −3.44 and −2.50 

points). Predictive supervision thus provides strong training value under the evaluated settings, though the benefit is not uniformly positive across all tasks. These results establish that predictive supervision is ef-fective, but do not reveal how the benefit manifests in pol-icy behavior. A given success-rate improvement could arise from the policy entering different trajectory modes or from higher success within similar modes. We next examine the behavioral level.

This pro-vides behavioral evidence beyond aggregate success rates: the benefit is not limited to changing which trajectory modes are selected; it also appears as higher success within similar execution patterns. These modes are clusters of numerical trajectory fea-tures, and some may partly reflect scene geometry. We therefore interpret them as descriptive behavioral statistics, not as evidence of a specific causal mechanism. Both experiments so far evaluate familiar conditions within the training distribution. A key follow-up question is whether these gains persist when tasks or environments change. 

4.3. Limits of Transfer under Distribution Shift 

We now evaluate the four Seen/Unseen ×

Clean/Randomized conditions to test how well the benefit of predictive supervision transfers. The five Seen tasks in this experiment differ from the ten-task suite in Sec. 4.1; absolute success rates should not be compared across the two experiments. Table 3 reports the full results. Under Seen+Clean, both families again show large gains: +36.37 points for Fast-WAM and +89.25 points for OpenWAM, consistent with the findings in Sec. 4.1 and confirming the stable value of predictive supervision on familiar tasks. When evaluation conditions shift, however, the picture changes substantially. 

Cross-task benefits are model- and task-dependent. 

Under Unseen+Clean, FastWAM gains only 0.28 points, whereas OpenWAM still gains 23.38 points. The Open-WAM gain, however, is concentrated in a subset of tasks: place container plate , open microwave ,and press stapler improve by 52.50, 71.88, and 73.44 points, respectively, while other unseen tasks improve lit-tle or decline slightly. Predictive supervision thus has some cross-task transfer value, but the effect is strongly task- and model-dependent. 

No stable benefit under environment randomization. 

Under Randomized conditions, absolute success rates are low for both families (at most 3.22%), and the changes from adding the prediction loss are small and negative (Fast-WAM: −0.13 /−1.62 pp; OpenWAM: −0.25 /−2.75 pp). # 5. From Understanding to Design: Auxiliary Prediction for VLA 

The preceding experiments show that future-video predic-tion supervision provides a valuable training signal for ac-tion learning, though its benefits concentrate near the train-ing distribution and vary across tasks and models. These findings raise a design-level question: can a VLA policy that was not built as a world–action model also benefit from a training-time auxiliary prediction branch, and must that branch be a large pretrained video-generation model? We address this question through three π0.5 configurations. 

5.1. Design Question and Auxiliary Prediction Vari-ants 

We compare three configurations: the original π0.5, a vari-ant augmented with a lightweight Transformer video pre-dictor, and a variant using pretrained Wan2.2-TI2V-5B as the auxiliary video-prediction branch. All three retain the 

π0.5 action pathway unchanged. In the original π0.5, a trainable SigLIP encoder pro-cesses the current three-view RGB observations and com-bines them with language instructions and robot state to form shared vision-language prefix representations. The ac-tion expert then predicts continuous actions through flow matching. In both augmented variants, the video-prediction branch reads context from the shared prefix. Gradients from the video objective propagate through each branch into the shared vision-language prefix and visual pathway, but do not directly update the private action expert. Both video branches are used only during training and are removed at inference, so action generation does not require explicit future-video prediction. 

5.2. Branch Architectures

Supervision is constructed in the latent space of a frozen Wan2.2 VAE: the explicit inputs to the video head are noisy future latents together with the corresponding video timestep, while current-observation information is supplied through the shared prefix. Training uses a flow-matching loss; the video-loss coefficient increases linearly from 0 to 0.1 over the first 1,000 optimizer steps and remains at 0.1 thereafter. 

Wan2.2-TI2V-5B branch. The shared prefix is mapped into the Wan conditioning space through a trainable 2,048-to-4,096 prefix adapter. The same language instruction is additionally encoded and cached offline by a frozen T5 en-coder and concatenated with the adapted prefix along the token dimension to condition the pretrained Wan2.2-TI2V-5B DiT. The Wan DiT remains trainable during this exper-iment; the VAE and T5 encoder are frozen. The branch receives clean current latents and noisy future latents, com-puting the video loss only on the future latents. Its external video-loss coefficient is one throughout training, with loss weighting prescribed by the Wan scheduler. 

Training-condition matching. The original π0.5 and the lightweight variant share the same initialization, robot training split, seed (42), action optimizer, effective batch size (32), and 50,000-step training budget; the lightweight branch is the only additional component. The Wan-based variant is also evaluated from its 50,000-step checkpoint with seed 42, but differs in auxiliary architecture, initializa-tion, conditioning pathway, scheduler, and loss weighting. The lightweight-versus-original comparison is therefore a relatively controlled paired experiment, while comparisons involving the Wan branch are complementary rather than a strict ablation of video-model size or pretraining. 

5.3. Results and Design Implications 

All three variants are evaluated on the same ten Seen tasks with 100 fixed scenes, 32 rollouts per scene per vari-ant (3,200 rollouts each), sharing initial observations and matched action noise indexed by rollout and action chunk. Table 4 reports the aggregate results. The original π0.5 achieves 82.94% success. Adding the lightweight video predictor increases success to 89.19% (+6.25 percentage points); using Wan2.2-TI2V-5B reaches 86.97% (+4.03 points). Using paired scene-level bootstrap within tasks, the 95% confidence interval for the lightweight-minus-original dif-ference is [2 .31 , 10 .31] percentage points, and the interval for Wan-minus-original is [0 .16 , 8.22] points. Both inter-vals lie above zero, supporting positive aggregate gains for both auxiliary-prediction variants on the evaluated task set. The lightweight predictor achieves a 2.22-point higher aggregate success rate than the Wan variant, but the 95% confidence interval for the Wan-minus-lightweight difference is [−5.63 , 1.25] and includes zero. The lightweight variant therefore has the highest point es-timate, yet the current evaluation does not establish a statistically reliable advantage over the Wan variant. Per-task results further show that the relative perfor-mance of the two approaches is strongly task-dependent: Wan attains the highest point estimate on tasks such as 

place a2b right and place dual shoes , while the lightweight predictor performs better on open laptop 

and pick diverse bottles .These results support two conditional design implica-tions. First, the benefit of auxiliary video prediction is not con-fined to dedicated WAM architectures; it extends to a con-ventional VLA policy. Both auxiliary variants improve π0.5

on the evaluated tasks, indicating practical value of training-time predictive supervision as a general auxiliary objective. Second, in the current setting, obtaining the training ben-efit of auxiliary prediction does not require a large pre-trained video DiT. The lightweight Transformer does not load a pretrained Wan DiT yet improves substantially over the original π0.5. However, the lightweight variant still uses video latents from a frozen Wan2.2 VAE as prediction targets; the result therefore does not show that pretrained video representations are unnecessary altogether. More-over, the lightweight and Wan branches differ simultane-ously in architecture, initialization, conditioning pathway, and loss weighting, so the current comparison cannot at-tribute performance differences to model size alone. 

# 6. Discussion 

The preceding experiments establish a set of empirical facts about future-video prediction supervision: it substantially improves action learning on familiar tasks, its benefits man-ifest in execution behavior beyond aggregate success rates, it does not reliably persist under distribution shift, and ob-taining such training benefits does not necessarily require a large pretrained video-generation branch. We distill these facts into three interrelated design insights and then discuss the limitations of the current evidence. 8Training-objective gains and transferability should be evaluated separately. This is the central observation of the paper. Under Seen+Clean conditions, predictive super-vision yields gains of +24.38 and +72.91 percentage points for FastWAM and OpenWAM, respectively, with both fam-ilies showing consistent positive effects. However, when evaluation extends to unseen tasks and randomized envi-ronments, the picture changes markedly: FastWAM gains almost nothing under Unseen+Clean, OpenWAM’s cross-task gains concentrate in a subset of tasks, and both models exhibit near-zero absolute success rates under environmen-tal randomization. This contrast indicates that a training objective capa-ble of substantially improving in-distribution performance cannot automatically serve as a proxy for generalization. For model evaluation, researchers reporting the effects of predictive supervision—or any training objective—should present results near the training distribution and under ex-plicit shift conditions separately, rather than inferring the latter from the former. For model design, improving in-distribution execution and achieving out-of-distribution transfer may require different technical mechanisms; a sin-gle predictive training objective should not be expected to address both. 

Aggregate success rates do not capture the full be-havioral impact. Trajectory clustering reveals that, on several tasks, coarse trajectory-mode frequencies remain nearly unchanged while within-mode success rates increase substantially. This provides a meaningful clue beyond ag-gregate metrics: the benefit of predictive supervision is not limited to changing which trajectory modes the policy se-lects; it also appears as higher success within similar execu-tion patterns. The interpretive boundary of this evidence must also be acknowledged. The clusters are based on numerical trajec-tory features, and some may partly reflect scene geometry rather than distinct control strategies. We have not mea-sured action precision, force control, or specific kinematic indicators, so we cannot determine what changes occur in the model’s internal action representations or fine-grained control capabilities. For future work, WAM and VLA evaluation should not be limited to final success rates. Trajectory- and behavior-level analyses can reveal benefit patterns that aggregate met-rics miss, but establishing stronger causal explanations will require finer-grained behavioral characterization tools and targeted interventional experiments. 

The value of a predictive training objective should not be conflated with the necessity of a large generator. The 

π0.5 experiments show that a randomly initialized six-layer lightweight Transformer predictor, without loading a pre-trained Wan DiT, increases success from 82.94% to 89.19%. This offers a meaningful reference for engineering deci-sions: when future prediction primarily serves as training-time auxiliary supervision, the benefit does not necessar-ily require an expensive large-scale video-generation back-bone. Two important qualifications apply. First, the lightweight variant still uses latents from a frozen Wan2.2 VAE as prediction targets, so it has not entirely dispensed with pretrained video representations; whether a specific target representation space is needed, and how its quality affects the benefit, requires further independent study. Sec-ond, the lightweight and Wan branches differ simultane-ously in architecture, initialization, conditioning pathway, and loss weighting, so the current comparison cannot at-tribute performance differences to model size alone. The 2.22-point success-rate gap between the two, with a 95% confidence interval that includes zero, does not support a statistically reliable advantage for either variant. The current conclusion is therefore that a large pretrained video DiT is not a necessary prerequisite for obtaining auxiliary-prediction training benefits in the evaluated π0.5

setting. Whether specific tasks, data scales, or pretraining conditions make a large video model necessary remains an open question. 

Limitations. The following limitations affect the inter-pretive scope of our findings. The WAM controlled experiments are based on a single training run per configuration. Although each configuration is evaluated with thousands of rollouts, rollout-level varia-tion does not equal training-seed-level stability. The results support incremental trends within the tested configurations but do not confirm their reproducibility across multiple in-dependent training seeds. Under environment randomization, both models achieve low absolute success rates across all conditions. This floor effect limits our ability to distinguish between “predictive supervision does not help robustness” and “the current mod-els lack the overall capability for these conditions.” The trajectory clustering analysis uses coarse numeri-cal features and may incorporate scene-geometry differ-ences into mode assignments. The results reflect statistical changes in trajectory behavior and should not be treated as causal identification of internal control mechanisms. We have not reported systematic comparisons of end-to-end training cost, GPU memory usage, or inference latency among the three π0.5 configurations. Differences in param-eter count do not directly translate to differences in training efficiency or deployment feasibility. Finally, all experiments are conducted on bimanual ma-nipulation tasks in the RoboTwin 2.0 simulation platform. Whether the findings generalize to different robot embod-9iments, real-world environments, or larger-scale training data remains to be examined in future work. 

# 7. Conclusion 

Through controlled experiments, we investigate the practi-cal role of future-video prediction supervision in robot ac-tion learning. Within the FastWAM and OpenWAM fam-ilies, predictive supervision produces substantial execution gains on familiar tasks, and trajectory-level analysis pro-vides behavioral evidence beyond aggregate success rates. However, these gains do not reliably persist on unseen tasks or under environmental randomization, indicating that im-provements on familiar tasks and robust transfer are dis-tinct targets that must be evaluated separately. For π0.5, a training-only lightweight predictor achieves positive gains, showing that effective auxiliary predictive supervision does not necessarily require a large pretrained video-generation branch in the evaluated setting. These results provide em-pirical guidance for training-objective selection in WAMs and VLAs: future-video prediction is a valuable form of training supervision, but its execution benefits, behavioral manifestations, and transfer capabilities should be exam-ined as separate dimensions. Translating these benefits into reliable out-of-distribution generalization remains an open research challenge.

# 8. Detailed Evaluation Protocol 

This section provides additional details of the evaluation protocols used in the main paper. Experiments 1–3 com-pare controlled No-WM and WM variants within FastWAM and OpenWAM in order to isolate the effect of future-video prediction supervision as much as possible. Experiment 4 serves as a complementary extension on π0.5 and com-pares different future-video prediction designs rather than a strictly architecture-matched No-WM/WM pair. 

8.1. Experiment 1: Seen+Clean Evaluation 

Experiment 1 evaluates whether future-video prediction su-pervision improves action success under Seen+Clean condi-tions. OpenWAM is evaluated on ten Seen+Clean tasks with ten fixed initial states per task and 32 rollouts per state for each model variant, resulting in 3,200 rollouts per method. FastWAM is likewise evaluated on ten Seen+Clean tasks with 3,200 valid rollouts per method. 

8.2. Experiment 2: Behavior-Mode Analysis 

Experiment 2 analyzes how the performance differences ob-served in Experiment 1 are reflected in trajectory-level be-havior. For both FastWAM and OpenWAM, we evaluate five tasks with ten fixed scenes per task and collect 128 complete trajectories per scene from each of No-WM and WM. This results in 1,280 trajectories per task and method, and 12,800 trajectories in total for each model family. For each task, trajectories from all ten scenes and both model variants are pooled to construct a shared set of can-didate behavior modes from numerical trajectory features. Model identity, success labels, and scene identity are ex-cluded from clustering and mode construction. After clus-tering, we measure the frequency of each mode and the suc-cess rate conditioned on that mode for each model variant. These modes are candidate clusters derived from numeri-cal trajectory features rather than semantically validated ac-tion strategies. We therefore use Experiment 2 as a descrip-tive behavioral analysis and do not interpret the resulting clusters as definitive semantic strategy classes. For the four rigid-object tasks, trajectories are expressed in an object-centric frame: positions are translated by the manipulated-object center and horizontally aligned using the direction from the object’s initial position to its tar-get. Features include gripper–object contact location and object identity, end-effector orientation, the final 15 cm of the pre-contact path, up to 48 steps or 10 cm of the post-contact path, relative gripper-closing time, and the delay between the first contacts of the two arms. Path features are resampled by arc length. Only contacts between the manipulated object and fl/fr link7 or link8 are re-tained; table, base, and robot self-contacts are excluded. For open laptop , the articulated-object base pose was unavailable, so trajectories are translated using the initial object–table contact centroid but are not rotation-aligned. An arm with no valid object contact retains a binary no-contact indicator, while its position, orientation, path, and relative contact-time features are set to zero so that the static geometry of an inactive arm does not dominate clustering. Each feature dimension is centered by its task-level median, scaled by its standard deviation with a floor of 0.05, and clipped to [−6, 6] . Feature groups are additionally weighted by the inverse square root of their dimensionality. We do not normalize each scene independently. We apply K-means separately to each task after pooling both model variants and all ten scenes. Candidate values 

K = 2 , . . . , 8 are ranked by silhouette score, subject to a minimum cluster size of 32 trajectories. If the best silhou-ette score is below 0.15, a single cluster is retained. Nei-ther model identity, scene identity, nor success labels enter the features or the selection criterion. Stability is diagnosed using adjusted Rand index over three additional clustering seeds and leave-one-scene-out refits. These choices define an exploratory candidate-mode analysis rather than a statis-tical test for a natural number of semantic strategies. 

8.3. Experiment 3: Distribution Shift 

Experiment 3 examines whether the gains from future-video prediction supervision persist under task-level and environment-level distribution shifts. We evaluate five Seen tasks and ten Unseen tasks that are completely held out from training, with ten fixed initial states per task. The evaluation varies along two independent dimen-sions: whether the task is represented in the robot train-ing data and whether the environment is randomized. Their Cartesian product defines four evaluation conditions: Seen+Clean, Seen+Randomized, Unseen+Clean, and Un-seen+Randomized. Both FastWAM and OpenWAM sample 32 rollouts per state for each model variant. This results in 1,600 rollouts per method for each Seen condition and 3,200 rollouts per method for each Unseen condition. Across both model vari-ants and all four conditions, the complete evaluation con-tains 19,200 rollouts for each model family. 

8.4. Experiment 4: Extension to pi0.5 

Experiment 4 examines whether the benefit of future-video prediction supervision extends to a conventional VLA pol-S1 icy that is not explicitly designed as a WAM. We compare three variants based on π0.5: the original policy, a variant augmented with a lightweight future-video predictor, and a variant using pretrained Wan2.2-TI2V-5B as the video-prediction module. All three models are evaluated on the same ten Seen tasks, with ten fixed scenes per task and 32 stochastic policy repetitions per scene. This results in 320 rollouts per task and model, and 3,200 rollouts in total for each model. The three models use the same fixed scenes and initial observations together with matched action noise derived from rollout and action-chunk identity, yielding a paired evaluation protocol. Action inference uses an action hori-zon of 32, 24 replanning steps, and ten action-sampling steps. Because all tasks contain the same number of scenes and rollouts, the macro-average success rate across the ten tasks is identical to the aggregate success rate over all 3,200 roll-outs. The original and lightweight-video variants use the same 

pi05 base initialization, robot training split, seed 42, and 50,000 optimizer updates. Both use four GPUs, four sam-ples per GPU, and two-step gradient accumulation, giving an effective batch size of 32. Optimization uses AdamW with cosine learning-rate decay from 2.5 × 10 −5 to 2.5 ×

10 −6, 1,000 warmup steps, weight decay 0.01, global-norm clipping at 1.0, and EMA decay 0.999. The lightweight branch is the only additional component in that paired train-ing run. The Wan-based policy is evaluated from its 50,000-step EMA checkpoint trained with seed 42. Its auxiliary branch differs in architecture, initialization, conditioning, scheduler weighting, and video-loss coefficient, so compar-isons involving Wan are treated as extension results rather than a strictly matched causal ablation.

12.1. Architecture and Training Details 

All three variants retain the original π0.5 action pathway, where the current three-view RGB observations are en-coded by a trainable SigLIP image encoder and combined with instruction and robot-state information to form shared vision-language prefix representations before continuous actions are generated by the action expert through flow matching. Both auxiliary video branches read context from the shared prefix, allowing gradients from the video ob-jective to update the shared vision-language representation without directly updating the private action expert. In the lightweight video-auxiliary variant, the 2048-dimensional prefix hidden states are first mapped to 512 dimensions through a trainable context projection and nor-malization layer. A randomly initialized six-layer Trans-former with a hidden dimension of 512 and eight attention heads then serves as the video-prediction network and con-tains both self-attention and cross-attention. The video supervision is constructed in the latent space of the frozen Wan2.2 VAE. The frozen VAE is used of-fline to encode a nine-frame video window containing the current and future three-view observations, from which the two future latent temporal blocks are used as prediction tar-gets. The explicit latent input to the video head consists of noisy future latents together with the corresponding video timestep, while information about the current observation is supplied through the shared π0.5 prefix. The total objective of the lightweight variant combines the action loss with a weighted video loss, where the video-loss coefficient is linearly increased from 0 to 0.1 over the first 1,000 optimizer steps and remains fixed at 0.1 there-after. In the Wan-based variant, the shared π0.5 prefix is first mapped into the Wan conditioning space through a train-able 2048-to-4096 prefix adapter. The SigLIP en-coder and shared vision-language backbone receive gradi-ents from both the action and video objectives, the private action expert and action output head are updated only by the action objective, and the video head, Wan DiT, and their corresponding projection layers are updated only by the video objective. Both video-auxiliary branches are used only during training and are removed during action inference. At test time, only the trained π0.5 image encoder, shared vision-language backbone, action expert, and action output head are retained, so action generation does not require explicit future-video prediction. 12.2. Aggregate Results 

All three π0.5 configurations are evaluated on the same ten Seen tasks and 100 fixed scenes, with 32 rollouts per scene and model. The original π0.5 achieves a success rate of 82.94%, the lightweight future-video predictor increases the success rate to 89.19%, and the Wan2.2-TI2V-5B variant reaches 86.97%. Using a paired scene-level bootstrap within tasks, the 95% confidence interval for lightweight minus original is 

[2 .31 , 10 .31] percentage points, while the interval for Wan minus original is [0 .16 , 8.22] points. Both intervals lie above zero, supporting positive aggregate differences for the two video-auxiliary variants on the evaluated fixed task set. The lightweight predictor achieves a 2.22-point higher aggregate success rate than the Wan-based variant, but the 95% confidence interval for Wan minus lightweight is

The lightweight variant therefore has the highest point estimate, but the cur-rent evaluation does not establish a reliable performance difference between the two video-augmented variants.
