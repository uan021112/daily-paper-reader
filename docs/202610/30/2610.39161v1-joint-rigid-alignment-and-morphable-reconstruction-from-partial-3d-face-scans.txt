# Abstract 

Aligning a partial, real-world 3D face scan to a canoni-cal representation requires solving two coupled problems at once: estimating the rigid pose that brings the scan into a common frame, and completing the facial shape in regions the scan never observed. Existing pipelines typically decou-ple these steps, running a generic point-cloud registration front end followed by a separate non-rigid fitting or defor-mation stage. Building on the GeoTransformer superpoint-matching architecture, we propose a single network that predicts both a rigid pose and a shape-complete point cloud in one forward pass: a cross-attention regressor, condi-tioned on transformer point features and predicted coarse correspondences, maps a partial scan to per-patch coeffi-cients of a 3D morphable face model learned offline from a corpus of 8,000 identities. The resulting morphed template is then used, in the same forward pass, as the target for fine-grained Sinkhorn matching and rigid pose estimation, supervised purely by geometric alignment to ground-truth morphed geometry, so no hand-labeled coefficients are re-quired. Against six classical and learned non-rigid registra-tion baselines on held-out identities, our method leads the strongest ones by 3–4 × on per-vertex error and baselines that collapse the head onto the visible region by more than an order of magnitude, and is simultaneously the most ac-curate and fastest method on ground-truth-free scans from two independent real-scan datasets, NoW and NPHM. Code will be made publicly available.

3D scanners produce face geometry cheaply and at scale, but the resulting scans are rarely complete. A handheld or single-shot sensor typically observes one side of the face, misses the ears and much of the cranium, and returns a raw

point cloud in an arbitrary, unknown pose. Turning such a scan into something usable for identity comparison, an-thropometric measurement, prosthetic design, or animation retargeting requires solving two problems simultaneously. First, a rigid problem: recovering the rotation and trans-lation that bring the scan into a canonical reference frame. Second, a non-rigid problem: completing the facial shape in the parts of the face the scan never saw, using prior knowl-edge of what faces look like, whether through a generic non-rigid solver or an iterative fit of a 3D morphable face model against dense correspondences. We find this generic non-rigid prior is not just subopti-mal but actively harmful on partial face scans. Benchmark-ing a representative set of non-rigid registration methods on partial views of a face database (Sec. 4), the majority satisfy their smoothness regularizer by collapsing the head onto the visible region (Fig. 1) rather than completing the occluded geometry, reaching per-vertex ground-truth error several times worse than methods that actually complete the shape. Jointly predicting a rigid pose and a complete shape is not new, and neither is driving that completion with a 3D morphable face model. What is new here is coupling the two through superpoint-level, nonlocal geometric matching, rather than treating rigid alignment and morphable-model fitting as two stages where one waits on the other. We con-dition the morphable-coefficient regressor directly on Geo-Transformer’s coarse superpoint features and predicted cor-respondences, so the same self- and cross-attention repre-sentation that establishes where the scan overlaps the tem-plate also drives what facial shape fills the rest of it, and gradients from the completion signal flow back into that matching stage itself. Specifically (Fig. 2), a KPConv [21] backbone and GeoTransformer [15] produce coarse super-point correspondences between the scan and a fixed mean-face template. Then, a cross-attention regressor predicts per-patch coefficients of a 3D morphable model [3] from the pre- and post-transformer point features together with the predicted correspondences, deforming the template into a scan-specific, shape-complete morphed reference. This morphed reference is subsequently used, within the same arXiv:2610.39161v1 [cs.CV] 29 Oct 2026 forward pass, as the fine-matching target for Sinkhorn op-timal transport [19] and rigid pose estimation. Because the morphed template is a full, complete point cloud rather than a partial, noisy scan, morphing is supervised directly by dense geometric alignment to ground-truth morphed geom-etry, so no hand-labeled morphable-model coefficients are required. Gradients from this geometric signal flow back through the transformer and backbone, letting the coarse matching stage itself become shape-aware.

# 2. Related work 

Rigid point cloud registration. Classical rigid registra-tion alternates correspondence estimation and closed-form pose solving, from Iterative Closest Point (ICP) [2] to feature-based Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) [4] pipelines built on descriptors such as Fast Point Feature Histograms (FPFH) [17]. Learned methods replace one or both stages with neural correspondence estimation: Preda-tor [8] learns overlap-aware keypoint detection for low-

Mean-face template and partial validation scan (top), with the morphed references produced by three representative non-rigid registration baselines—OAReg [26], ClusterReg [25], and CPD [11]—and our method (bottom). overlap scenes, while GeoTransformer [15] learns geomet-ric self- and cross-attention over superpoints so accurate correspondences can be found without a RANSAC step. Dual Focus-Attention Transformer (DFAT) [6] focuses at-tention within local neighborhoods at both coarse and fine scales and Regor [24] regenerates rather than merely filters correspondences under high outlier ratios. 

Non-rigid registration and surface fitting. Non-rigid registration extends the rigid setting with a per-point or per-node deformation field, typically regularized for lo-cal smoothness or as-rigid-as-possible behavior. Optimal-step non-rigid ICP (NRICP) [1] and Coherent Point Drift (CPD) [11] are foundational classical approaches. More recent work replaces the deformation model or correspon-dence step with learned components. GraphSCNet [16] prunes outlier correspondences using local spatial con-sistency over a deformation graph before solving with embedded deformation. Test-time-optimization methods forgo learned priors entirely: Neural Scene Flow Prior (NSFP) [10] fits an unregularized flow MLP against a Chamfer objective, OAReg [26] adds an occlusion-aware correntropy term and a local linear-rigidity regularizer, and ClusterReg [25] reformulates registration as unsupervised clustering with a closed-form displacement field. None en-code a prior over what a face specifically looks like, as their regularizers constrain local rigidity or smoothness, not global plausibility; Sec. 4 shows this matters, since smoothness or rigidity regularization alone does not prevent global shape collapse on partial face scans. Our refinement (Sec. 3.7) reuses Neural Scene Flow Prior’s [10] unregu-larized, per-scan flow-field optimizer directly, but warm-started from our learned prediction rather than a generic initialization, improving accuracy with far fewer steps to converge (Sec. 4.5). 

3D morphable face models. 3D morphable models (3DMMs) represent face and head shape as a low-dimensional basis fit to a corpus of registered scans, from the original 3DMM [3] and the Basel Face Model [12] to expressive models such as FLAME [9] and large-scale face-and-head models [13]. These are most commonly fit to an input via iterative optimization, or regressed from a single RGB image by a learned encoder [18], typically assuming a roughly frontal view and a known pose. Our regressor instead operates on 3D point features and jointly recovers the unknown SE (3) pose alongside completion. We instead use a morphable model as the target representation for a reg-istration network. Concretely, the decoder regresses local, patch-wise coefficients directly from geometric point fea-tures, and the morphed points are used, in the same forward pass, as the target for classical fine-grained point matching, coupling morphable-model fitting to registration rather than treating it as a downstream step. 

Learned shape completion priors. Generic point-cloud completion networks [22, 23] learn to hallucinate missing geometry from partial input, typically trained per-category on synthetic shape collections such as ShapeNet. Our re-gressor is a domain-specialized instance of this idea: rather than a generic latent shape code, the completion target is constrained to the linear span of a face-specific PCA basis, which regularizes the output to always be a plausible face and makes the completion interpretable as anatomically lo-calized coefficients. Section 4 shows this domain-specific prior is precisely what separates methods that complete oc-cluded facial geometry from methods that merely avoid de-stroying the visible region.

3.4. Morphing the reference and fine matching 

The predicted coefficients reconstruct a morphed template 

ˆM = g(z) ∈ RNv ×3 (Secs. 3.1 and 3.3). Because the pooled backbone points at each stage are exact copies of vertices of ¯M, but KPConv pooling does not retain which vertex index each pooled point came from, we recover this correspondence via an exact-match nearest-neighbor lookup against the ordered vertex set of ¯M, yielding a fixed index for every coarse- and fine-level template point. Since ˆM preserves the same global vertex ordering as ¯M,we use these indices to directly gather the morphed posi-tions from ˆM, so the morphed geometry, rather than the mean face, is what fine matching operates on. Ground-truth coarse correspondences for the circle loss are like-wise computed after morphing. Fine matching then follows GeoTransformer’s local-to-global registration [15]: point-to-point scores within each coarse-matched patch pair are normalized with a differentiable Sinkhorn optimal-transport layer [19], dense correspondences are extracted, and a ro-bust local-to-global procedure aggregates per-patch rigid hypotheses into ( ˆR, ˆt). The rigid pose is thus estimated against a target that already reflects the network’s best guess at the scanned individual’s facial shape, not just the popula-tion mean.

4.2. Baselines 

We compare against six non-rigid registration methods spanning classical optimization, correspondence-free clus-tering, test-time optimization, and learned correspondence pruning: NRICP [1], CPD [11], OAReg [26], Cluster-Reg [25], NSFP [10], and GraphSCNet [16]. None pre-dicts pose jointly with shape, so each requires a rigid pre-alignment stage before it can run at all. We give every one the same strong initialization, a 24-start octahedral multi-start point-to-point ICP, and report that cost as part of each baseline’s time in Tabs. 2 and 3. Ours predicts (R, t ) end to end in one forward pass and never invokes this or any exter-nal rigid-alignment stage, including during its own refine-ment (Sec. 3.7). We do not include iterative 3DMM-fitting baselines: without a known or landmark-derived pose ini-tialization, unavailable for an arbitrarily-posed raw scan, it-erative fitting has no starting point short of the same external rigid-alignment stage our method avoids.

# 5. Discussion and limitations 

Evaluating completion quality without ground truth. 

Ours closes the practical, scan-referenced accuracy gap on both NoW and NPHM (Sec. 4.5 and Tabs. 3 and 4), but scan-referenced surface fit and occluded-region completion quality are different things, and we have no directly usable ground truth on either real-scan dataset to check the latter (NoW’s own reference scans serve a different task; NPHM’s full-head scans include hair, incompatible with our hairless template). Usable ground truth for NoW or NPHM would let those results be reported with the same confidence as our Val results. 

Basis capacity and dataset scale. Our morphable prior is a linear PCA basis fit to 8,000 identities, patch-partitioned to keep local anatomical detail (Sec. 3.1). This bias both enables the collapse-avoidance and accuracy in Sec. 4.4 and caps expressiveness at whatever the training population spans, so under-represented identities, expressions, or ages are unlikely to be well reconstructed. A learned, non-linear basis could relax this at the cost of the plausibility guarantee shown in Tab. 2. 

Broader impact. This work uses only point geometry, no color, texture, or identifying imagery, though improv-ing recovery of complete facial geometry from partial scans is nonetheless dual-use, like biometric and face-reconstruction research generally. Training identities are synthetic, sampled from the published UHM model [14] via the generation procedure of [5], not collected from real subjects; both are used under their published terms, as are NoW [18] and NPHM [7]. We see no immediate deploy-ment risk here. 

# 6. Conclusion 

We presented a single network that jointly predicts a morphable-model-based facial-shape completion and rigid pose for partial 3D face scans, closing with a short scan-referenced refinement. Our evaluation protocol reports per-vertex ground-truth error alongside Chamfer distance, ex-posing a collapse failure mode Chamfer distance alone con-ceals. Our method avoids this failure, leading held-out Val identities by 3–4 × over the strongest baselines (Tab. 2) and both NoW’s and NPHM’s ground-truth-free scans in accu-racy and speed (Tabs. 3 and 4).
