## ABSTRACT 

Experience can help embodied agents turn high-level plans into grounded actions, but transferring it across tasks remains challenging: previously effective strategies may be inapplicable to the current state or fail to produce their intended outcomes. We present EmbodiExp , a framework for constructing transferable experiences and composing them online during embodied task execution. Our key observa-tion is that tasks often share subgoals whose completion can be assessed inde-pendently of overall task success. We therefore introduce an outcome-grounded subgoal experience that specifies a subgoal, applicability conditions, an adaptable policy, and a verifiable outcome. During execution, applicable experiences guide action generation, while verified outcomes can establish the conditions required by subsequent experiences, enabling online composition. Subgoal-level outcome attribution further enables failure utilization: failed trajectories can still contribute verified local successes and condition-specific guidance on actions to avoid. Ex-periments on multiple embodied benchmarks under a fold-based protocol with frozen experience banks demonstrate the effectiveness of EmbodiExp .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent foundation models (Qwen Team, 2026b; OpenAI, 2026) have endowed embodied agents with strong reasoning and high-level planning capabilities (Team et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025a). However, knowing what to do does not imply knowing how to realize it under the current environ-mental conditions (Ahn et al., 2022; Agarwal et al., 2025). To bridge this gap, embodied agents can leverage experience accumulated through past interactions as empirical action-outcome knowl-edge (Wang et al., 2023a; Shinn et al., 2023; Park et al., 2023). At the core of experience is its 

transferability : whether it can effectively inform grounded execution in new tasks. To support such transferability, recent work has explored different perspectives, including retrieving task-relevant trajectories (Wang et al., 2026d; Yue et al., 2025), abstracting past executions into compact experiences (Ren et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b), and extracting reusable skills (Jiang et al., 2026; Ju et al., 2026). Despite these advances, transferability remains a central challenge: experience must retain knowledge that extends beyond its source interaction, while its application must remain grounded in the conditions and outcomes of the new task. This raises a fundamental question: what should be retained as experience, and how should it be applied? 

Experience can transfer across tasks through shared local goals, even when their overall goals and execution sequences differ (Konidaris & Barto, 2007; James et al., 2020). However, sharing a local goal alone is insufficient for reliable transfer: a strategy effective in one interaction may not apply under different conditions, and even an applicable strategy may fail to produce the intended outcome (Ahn et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022). Transferable experience should thus specify not only how to pursue a goal, but also when the strategy applies and what outcome establishes goal completion (Konidaris et al., 2018). These requirements make the subgoal level a natural granularity for ex-perience, as each subgoal specifies a coherent intermediate goal whose completion can be assessed from local interaction evidence, independently of the source task’s overall success or failure (Li

We therefore represent experience at the subgoal level, grounding its application and completion in explicit conditions and outcomes. In this paper, we propose EmbodiExp , a framework for constructing transferable experiences and composing them online during embodied task execution. At its core is outcome-grounded subgoal experience , which represents each experience through a subgoal, applicability conditions, an adapt-able policy, and an intended local outcome. EmbodiExp constructs such experiences from local action-outcome evidence and retrieves them according to the current subgoal during execution. An experience is applied only when its conditions are satisfied, and subgoal completion is established only when newly acquired interaction evidence supports the intended outcome. This design enables two key mechanisms: experience composition across trajectories and failure utilization through se-lective retention of useful knowledge from failed trajectories. For experience composition , a new task may involve subgoals whose relevant experiences originate from different source trajectories (Figure 1). Retrieving relevant experience is insufficient, because retrieval alone does not determine when each experience becomes applicable as execution unfolds. EmbodiExp therefore composes experience online through verified outcomes: the outcome realized by one experience may satisfy the applicability conditions of another, making the latter applica-ble as execution progresses. This connects experiences across trajectories through grounded state transitions rather than through static combinations based on semantic similarity alone. For failure utilization , task-level failure provides only a coarse outcome signal, since a failed tra-jectory may contain both completed subgoals and failed local attempts. Discarding the entire trajec-tory would lose useful local knowledge, whereas indiscriminate transfer could propagate behavior that did not achieve its intended outcome. EmbodiExp therefore performs subgoal-level outcome attribution on both successful and failed trajectories, admitting experience according to local action-outcome evidence rather than overall task outcomes. Within a shared experience representation, verified local successes provide guidance on how to achieve a subgoal, while explicit failure evi-dence provides condition-specific guidance on operations to avoid during future execution. Overall, our contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose outcome-grounded subgoal experience , which couples a subgoal and an adaptable policy with explicit applicability conditions and an intended local outcome. • We present EmbodiExp , a framework for constructing and applying such experience. It enables online experience composition by using verified outcomes from one experience to establish the applicability conditions of another, grounding composition in realized execution states. • We propose subgoal-level outcome attribution for failure utilization, retaining locally successful behavior and deriving condition-specific avoidance guidance from explicit failure evidence. • Extensive experimental evaluations on multiple benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of Em-bodiExp under a fold-based experience evaluation protocol with frozen experience banks. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Multimodal Embodied Agents. Foundation models enable embodied agents to perform task plan-ning and affordance-grounded decision making (Ahn et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023), generate ex-ecutable programs (Liang et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2023), and adapt execution using environment 2Preprint feedback (Huang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023b). Vision-language models enable embodied rea-soning (Driess et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2026b), while generalist robot policies connect multimodal inputs to control (Brohan et al., 2023; Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Octo Model Team et al., 2024), and unified frameworks coordinate skill selection, execution, and verification (Wang et al., 2026c). Benchmarks assess household interaction, navigation, task com-pletion, self-termination, and active spatial reasoning (Shridhar et al., 2020; 2021; Yang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2026a; Hong et al., 2026). Our work complements these systems through the construc-tion and cross-task application of transferable experience from grounded interactions. 

Agent Memory. Agent memory retains past interactions as episodic records, summaries, and struc-tured knowledge (Park et al., 2023; Packer et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), with extensions for multimodal consolidation (Liu et al., 2025) and learnable memory operations (Zhang et al., 2026b). In embodied environments, memory provides spatiotemporal context (Yang et al., 2025b; Hu et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2026a; 2024a), interaction histories (Torne et al., 2026), hierarchical experience abstraction (Ma et al., 2026), and interaction-derived object knowledge (Banwasi et al., 2026). Within this broader literature, we focus on extract-ing outcome-grounded subgoal experiences from past interactions and composing them according to the current task state to guide execution across tasks. 

Experience and Skill Learning. Experience-driven agents distill past interactions into reflections and procedural guidance (Shinn et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026c) or build workflows and skills (Wang et al., 2024b; Fang et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2023a; Ni et al., 2026). Further work explores feedback-driven skill refinement (Xia et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a; Yang et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026), evidence-backed skills with applicability and verification rules (Tang et al., 2026), hierarchical skills (Sun et al., 2026), and how skill granularity and representation affect transfer (Feng et al., 2026). MuSE-Agent (Wang et al., 2026b) retrieves state-conditioned experiences, while XSkill (Jiang et al., 2026) combines action-level experiences with task-level skills. In embodied settings, WorldMind (Ren et al., 2026) distills process and goal experience, ELITE (Wei et al., 2026) retrieves intent-aligned strategies, MineEvolve (Xie et al., 2026) builds skills and remedies from subgoal feedback, and EmbodiSkill (Ju et al., 2026) revises skills through targeted error attribution. EmbodiExp constructs 

outcome-grounded subgoal experience through local outcome attribution and composes it across tasks using applicability checks and outcome verification. 

## 3 METHOD 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Consider an embodied agent A that receives a task instruction I and can generate high-level plans while interacting with an environment. Such plans specify what to achieve, but grounded execution additionally requires selecting actions suited to the current environmental conditions and verifying that they produce the intended outcomes. We refer to this gap between semantic task planning and grounded execution as the grounded-execution gap . Our objective is to bridge this gap by extracting transferable experience from prior interactions and applying it to new tasks. 

Interaction Trajectory. Let ot denote the agent’s observation, including any available environment feedback, and at its native action at interaction step t. We represent an interaction trajectory as

Embodied Experience. A trajectory records a particular execution. We define embodied experi-ence as empirical action-outcome knowledge accumulated from prior interactions to inform future grounded execution. To support transfer across tasks, we seek a representation that connects execu-tion guidance to its applicability conditions and a verifiable completion outcome.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

In our experiments, we seek to answer the following questions: Q1: How does EmbodiExp compare with general-purpose LLM memory systems and embodied experience-learning methods? Q2: Does subgoal-level experience transfer more effectively than trajectory-level and action-level experience? 

Q3: How do experience composition and failure utilization contribute to experience transfer? 

Benchmarks and Metrics. We evaluate on EmbodiedBench (Yang et al., 2025a) and VIGIL (Chen et al., 2026a). For EmbodiedBench, we use its two environments with high-level skill interfaces: EB-Habitat for language-conditioned navigation and rearrangement in Habitat 2.0 (Szot et al., 2021), and EB-ALFRED for household manipulation in AI2-THOR (Kolve et al., 2017). Each environment contains 300 episodes across six capability categories, with 50 episodes per category. VIGIL com-plements EmbodiedBench by evaluating experience transfer over more atomic native actions. We use its Search and Interact (SI) and Sequential Manipulation (SM) subsets, which evaluate search-conditioned interaction and ordered multi-step manipulation, respectively, with 125 episodes each. We report task success rates, using world-state completion W as the success criterion for VIGIL.

Failure Utilization. Including failed source trajectories raises success from 62.7% to 68.0% (Ta-ble 3). Cross-Model Experience Transfer. Both banks improve both executors, enabling the 8B (Bai et al., 2025) executor reaching 52.3-55.0% success, above the 35B NoExp baseline (Figure 4.A). These gains suggest that transferred experience can partially compensate for limited executor capability without increasing executors’ model size. The constructor ranking nevertheless reverses across ex-ecutors: the Qwen bank performs better with 35B, whereas the GPT (OpenAI, 2026) bank performs better with 8B. Together, these results support separating offline experience construction from online execution, while indicating that the utility of a bank depends on the constructor-executor pairing. 

Positive and Negative Transfer. Across 300 paired episodes (Figure 4.B), EmbodiExp converts 62 NoExp failures into successes (20.7%), while 8 successes regress (2.7%), yielding a net gain of 18.0 percentage points. This corresponds to recovering 41.3% of baseline failures while preserving 94.7% of baseline successes. The gains therefore come mainly from solving tasks that NoExp fails, while preserving most baseline successes. However, the remaining regressions show that experience transfer can still reduce performance on individual tasks despite improving overall success.

The last criterion requires verified local facts from earlier applications to remain valid in the Task State and support later prerequisites. Long-Horizon tasks show slightly less multi-experience use than overall, but more cross-source use and state-grounded composition, with the latter occur-ring in 28.0% of Long-Horizon episodes versus 11.7% overall. These traces provide execution-level evidence of compositional experience transfer: verified outcomes from experience drawn from one source establish prerequisites for applying experience drawn from another. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we present EmbodiExp , a framework for constructing transferable experiences and composing them in embodied tasks. At its core is outcome-grounded subgoal experience , which specifies a subgoal, applicability conditions, an adaptable policy, and an intended local outcome. 9Preprint EmbodiExp composes experiences across trajectories through verified outcomes that make subse-quent experiences applicable, while subgoal-level outcome attribution derives execution and avoid-ance guidance from local successes and failures. Fold-based experiments on multiple embodied benchmarks with frozen experience banks demonstrate its effectiveness. These findings underscore the importance of applicability checks and outcome verification for compositional transfer. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

## A EVALUATION DETAILS 

A.1 BENCHMARKS AND TASK CATEGORIES 

We evaluate on EB-Habitat and EB-ALFRED from EmbodiedBench (Yang et al., 2025a), and the Search and Interact (SI) and Sequential Manipulation (SM) task families from VIGIL (Chen et al., 2026a). These settings cover experience transfer under both high-level skill interfaces and more atomic native actions. Table 4 summarizes the evaluation scope. 

EmbodiedBench. EB-Habitat evaluates language-conditioned navigation and object rearrange-ment in Habitat 2.0 (Szot et al., 2021), while EB-ALFRED evaluates household tasks in AI2-THOR (Kolve et al., 2017). Each environment contains 300 episodes, with 50 episodes in each of six capability categories. Base evaluates basic task execution; Common Sense requires interpreting indirect object references through commonsense knowledge; Complex Instruction , corresponding to the benchmark’s Complex Instruction subset, tests instruction understanding with additional context or conditional language; Spatial Awareness requires resolving spatial references; Visual Appearance 

identifies targets through appearance attributes; and Long Horizon requires longer action sequences. We retain these category assignments throughout evaluation. 

VIGIL. We use 250 episodes, comprising 125 SI and 125 SM episodes. SI requires finding an initially unseen target, approaching it, and performing the requested interaction. SM requires an ordered manipulation sequence, such as opening a container, picking an object, and placing it at a destination. These tasks complement EmbodiedBench by evaluating execution through more atomic native actions. A.2 OBSERVATION AND ACTION INTERFACES 

EmbodiedBench. The agent receives the task instruction, egocentric visual observations, the avail-able high-level action set, and textual execution feedback exposed by the benchmark. Targets are specified through the object or receptacle identifiers supported by the action interface. The agent se-lects high-level skills rather than directly controlling low-level motion. Execution feedback indicates action validity or failure reasons and can inform subsequent decisions. 

VIGIL. Following its default interface, the agent receives egocentric RGB observations without action-success feedback. It acts through task-specific native controls, including movement and pixel-grounded interaction, with a semantic report for agent-initiated termination. 

Information Access. Experience application does not introduce additional access to simulator in-formation beyond the corresponding benchmark interface. In particular, the evolving Task State St

is an agent-maintained representation derived from accessible interaction evidence, rather than a copy of the evaluator’s world state. Applicability checks and outcome verification operate on this evidence. Evaluator-side goal predicates and success labels are used for scoring and are not supplied as online completion signals. A.3 FOLD -B ASED EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

Construction and Evaluation Splits. To separate the interactions used to construct experience from those used to assess transfer, we adopt a three-fold evaluation protocol. For each environment, the episode pool is partitioned into three disjoint folds, F1, F2, and F3. In run k, Fk is held out for evaluation, while the remaining two folds provide episodes for source-trajectory collection and experience construction. Rotating the evaluation fold ensures that each episode is evaluated once. We construct the folds using outcome-blind grouped partitioning: predefined episode groups are kept intact, and neither episode outcomes nor method-specific performance scores are used to determine fold assignments. For EB-Habitat and EB-ALFRED, each run uses 200 episodes for construction and 100 for evaluation. For VIGIL, the three evaluation folds contain 84, 83, and 83 episodes, with corresponding construction pools of 166, 167, and 167 episodes, respectively. For EB-Habitat, episodes sharing an identical normalized instruction or the same (scene ID, raw episode ID) pair are grouped together, yielding 267 groups. Mixed-integer optimization assigns these groups to the three folds, with 16–17 episodes from each of the six benchmark subsets per fold. For VIGIL, all episodes sharing the same scene are assigned to the same fold. Subject to this scene-level grouping constraint, the partition balances the SI and SM task families while minimizing imbalance in platform, difficulty, functional template, and target group. VIGIL therefore uses scene-disjoint folds. For EB-Habitat and EB-ALFRED, the held-out unit remains the episode under the grouping constraints above; their evaluation is not fully scene-disjoint or task-template-disjoint. 

Held-Out Isolation. Each run constructs its experience bank exclusively from source trajectories associated with its construction folds. The held-out fold’s trajectories and outcomes are excluded from that run’s experience construction. An episode may serve as a construction episode in other runs, but never in the run in which it is evaluated. The held-out unit is the episode; this protocol does not imply scene-disjoint or task-template-disjoint evaluation. 

Frozen-Bank Evaluation. Once construction is complete, the experience bank is frozen for the entire evaluation run. Evaluation interactions are not used to add, revise, or remove experience entries. Within an episode, the agent may update St, retrieve different experiences, and revise its plan as new evidence becomes available. These episode-local updates are reset between evaluation episodes and do not produce cross-episode experience accumulation. Thus, the protocol evaluates transfer from a fixed source experience bank rather than continual experience acquisition during evaluation. 

Consistency across Methods. All compared methods use the same fold assignments and are evalu-ated on the same held-out episodes. A.4 EVALUATION METRICS 

EmbodiedBench. We report task success rate according to the environment’s task-completion eval-uator. An episode is successful only when the complete task condition is satisfied; completing individual subgoals alone does not count as task success. Results are reported separately for the six capability categories and for each environment overall.

## B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

B.1 SOURCE TRAJECTORY COLLECTION 

For each evaluation run, source trajectories are collected from the corresponding construction folds before experience construction. We use the same backbone model as in the corresponding bench-mark evaluation and collect source trajectories under the NoExp setting, without access to any ex-perience or memory. Each source episode is executed once, producing a trajectory that records the task instruction, observations, actions, available execution feedback, and the final episode outcome. Both successful and unsuccessful trajectories are used for experience construction. Since trajectory-level success does not determine the outcomes of individual subgoal attempts, we attribute outcomes based on the local interaction evidence associated with each subgoal. This allows verified local successes to be retained even from globally unsuccessful trajectories. B.2 EXPERIENCE CONSTRUCTION 

Subgoal Segmentation. The constructor C identifies subgoal attempts within each source trajectory and associates them with the relevant action-observation spans. A subgoal specifies a locally assess-able intermediate objective and may require one or more native actions. Segmentation is therefore based on the attempted subgoal and its interaction evidence rather than a fixed number of actions. Each span includes the context in which the attempt begins, the actions taken, and the resulting observations needed to assess its outcome. 

Local Outcome Attribution. For each attempt, the constructor determines whether the associated evidence supports the intended local outcome. Issuing an action or following a plausible action sequence is not, by itself, sufficient evidence of completion. Likewise, the episode-level success label cannot establish which intermediate attempts succeeded. An attempt with insufficient evidence is not treated as a verified local success. Conversely, a verified local success remains a valid source of action–outcome knowledge even when a later part of the source task fails. 

Experience Abstraction. The constructor abstracts supported subgoal attempts into experiences 

ei = ( gi, R i, π i, P i). The abstraction separates the subgoal being pursued, the conditions under which the policy applies, the execution strategy, and the outcome that establishes local completion. Task-specific details are retained when they constrain applicability or execution, while incidental details of the source episode are excluded from the transferable content. The resulting experience describes a strategy to be interpreted in a new task, rather than an instruction to replay the complete source trajectory. 

Failure Utilization. Failure utilization operates at the subgoal level. Verified local successes are re-tained independently of whether their source trajectories succeed overall. Localized failure evidence is also preserved to inform subsequent execution, but an unsuccessful attempt is not presented as ev-idence that its policy achieves the intended outcome. Task State. The evolving Task State Sk summarizes task-relevant facts supported by the interaction history. It is initialized from the current instruction and available initial observations and updated as further evidence becomes available. Relevant facts may include object locations, possession, receptacle states, and established subgoal outcomes, subject to the information exposed by the envi-ronment. The representation is episode-local and is reset when a new episode begins. State maintenance distinguishes observed progress from intended effects. Selecting an experience or issuing its actions does not automatically add Pi to Sk. Updates instead reflect what the resulting evidence supports, including partial progress or an unsuccessful attempt. As execution changes the environment, previously established facts may also require revision; the current Task State is not simply the union of all outcomes observed earlier in the episode. 

Policy Application and Outcome Verification. The selected policy informs the executor’s action choices while allowing adaptation to the current observations and available action arguments. After execution, newly acquired interaction evidence is assessed against Pi. Completion is established only when that evidence supports the intended outcome. When the evidence is insufficient, the outcome remains unverified rather than being inferred from the fact that the policy was attempted. The resulting state update informs subsequent planning and experience selection. Fallback and Replanning. When no retrieved experience is usable, the agent continues with its native planning and execution policy without conditioning on an experience. ## D LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Frozen-Bank Evaluation. We intentionally freeze the experience bank during evaluation to focus on what experience to retain and how to apply it across tasks, separating these questions from con-tinual experience acquisition. While experience selection and composition remain online, the source bank is fixed, and the current evaluation does not examine its evolution through subsequent inter-actions. We leave extending EmbodiExp to online self-evolving embodied agents that continually acquire, refine, and apply experience as they encounter new tasks for future work. 

Simulation and Task-Level Execution. Our experiments focus on task-level embodied execution in simulated environments. Although the evaluation covers both high-level skills and more atomic native actions, it does not yet examine experience transfer within vision-language-action (VLA) policies for low-level robot control.
