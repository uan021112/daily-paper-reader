PrefO2O: Zero-Shot Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning
via Offline-to-Online Latent Preference Transfer
Ruiqi Wang1†, Dezhong Zhao2†, Xuesu Xiao3, Erdem Bıyık1, Byung-Cheol Min4, and Guohua Chen2
Abstract—Preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL)
has emerged as a promising paradigm for learning robot be-
haviors aligned with human intent. However, its scalability is
fundamentally limited by the need to collect expensive human
preference labels for every new task. In this work, we present
PrefO2O, an offline-to-online latent preference transfer frame-
work that enables zero-shot PbRL on unseen target tasks without
requiring any human feedback. The core idea is to learn a task-
invariant latent preference space from a small amount of offline
preference data on source tasks, where transferable preference
structures are disentangled from task-specific variations. This
learned manifold allows PrefO2O to transfer preference struc-
tures and reward priors to new tasks under distribution shift,
while confidence-weighted optimal transport alignment gener-
ates reliable pseudo-preference supervision for online reward
adaptation. Extensive experiments on robotic manipulation and
locomotion benchmarks demonstrate that PrefO2O substantially
outperforms existing preference-transfer baselines and matches
or exceeds PbRL methods trained with real human feedback,
while generalizing across multi-to-one and one-to-one transfer
settings as well as across different robot embodiments. More
information is available at our project website.
Index Terms—Preference-based RL, Offline-to-Online Learn-
ing, Cross-Domain Transfer, Latent Learning.
I. INTRODUCTION
R
EINFORCEMENT learning (RL) has achieved remark-
able success in robotics [1], yet its dependence on
carefully engineered reward functions requires substantial do-
main expertise and remains vulnerable to reward hacking [2].
Preference-based RL (PbRL) offers a compelling alternative:
instead of specifying rewards explicitly, it learns reward mod-
els from human comparative feedback, thereby aligning robot
behaviors with human intent [3]–[6].
However, PbRL introduces a fundamental scalability bottle-
neck. For each new task, high-quality human preference labels
must typically be collected from scratch to train an effective
reward model [3], [7], [8]. This per-task annotation overhead
limits the practical deployment of PbRL in real-world robotic
systems, where robots are expected to generalize rapidly across
diverse tasks, embodiments, and operating conditions.
Several directions have been explored to reduce this scala-
bility bottleneck. One line of work leverages foundation mod-
els to generate synthetic preference labels, enabling scalable
feedback without direct human annotation [9]–[12]. How-
ever, synthetic feedback might be inconsistent, diverge from

these methods require extensive preference data during meta-
training and still demand target-task feedback, which remains

More recent work has investigated zero-shot preference
transfer by propagating pseudo-preference labels from source
tasks to target tasks through trajectory alignment, for example

In this work, we explore how to enable PbRL to transfer
across tasks without collecting human feedback on each new
target task. Our key insight is that, although task objectives and
dynamics may vary, human preferences often contain shared
structures that are transferable across related tasks. For exam-
ple, across manipulation tasks with distinct goals, humans may
consistently favor efficient approaches, penalize unnecessary
motion, and prefer stable object interactions. These recurring
preference patterns suggest that reward learning need not start
from scratch for every new task.

• We introduce a task-invariant latent preference space that

II. BACKGROUND AND PRELIMINARIES
A. Preference-Based Reinforcement Learning
PbRL aims to learn a reward model from human com-
parative feedback over trajectories [3], [17]–[22]. Compared
with standard RL, which often requires carefully engineered
reward functions, PbRL alleviates the burden of reward
specification and improves behavioral alignment with human
intent. Preference feedback is also often easier to provide
than demonstrations, since it requires only relative judgments
between trajectory segments rather than precise action-level
guidance [23], [24].

B. Preference Transfer in PbRL
Standard PbRL methods typically learn reward models from
scratch for each new task, incurring substantial annotation
costs that limit scalability in real-world settings. Preference
transfer seeks to mitigate this bottleneck by reusing preference
knowledge from previously seen source tasks to reduce, or
ideally eliminate, feedback collection on new target tasks.
Hejna et al. [14] take an initial step in this direction by
integrating PbRL with MAML [15], meta-training a reward
model across multiple tasks for few-shot adaptation. While
effective, this approach remains data-intensive at both stages:
meta-training requires thousands of labeled queries per source
task across about 10 tasks, and adaptation still demands

3
hundreds to thousands of additional queries on the target task.
In contrast, our approach operates with modest preference
budgets on source tasks and requires zero human feedback
on the target task, while generalizing across both many-to-one
and one-to-one transfer settings.
The work most closely related to ours is PEARL [16], which
enables zero-shot preference transfer by aligning source and
target trajectory distributions via optimal transport and propa-
gating pseudo-preference labels through the resulting transport
matrix. However, operating directly in the raw state-action
space can make the alignment sensitive to distribution shifts
across tasks, where trajectory-level similarity metrics may be-
come unreliable. In addition, PEARL assumes a shared state-
action dimensionality across tasks, which limits transfer across
different embodiments or sensing configurations. Moreover, it
treats preference transfer primarily as label propagation, rather
than learning a transferable preference representation that can
support online reward and policy adaptation.
In contrast, PrefO2O performs offline-to-online preference
transfer through a task-invariant latent preference space that
absorbs task-specific variation. This learned space enables
OOD-aware optimal transport for pseudo-preference genera-
tion under distribution shift, while the jointly trained trajectory
encoder and meta reward model provide transferable structure
for online PbRL on the target task.

III. METHODOLOGY
A. Problem Setting and Assumptions
We consider a multi-task PbRL setting in which K source
tasks {T k}K
k=1 (K ≥1) are available to facilitate learning on
an unseen target task T tgt. Unlike prior works that require
all tasks to share the same state-action space, such as the
same robot embodiment [14], [16], PrefO2O permits transition
dynamics, task objectives, and state-action dimensions to vary
across tasks. We assume only that the tasks belong to a
related domain family, such as robotic manipulation, in which
human preferences exhibit shared underlying structures that
are transferable beyond individual task specifications. This
aligns with prior observations that semantically related tasks
can support transferable representations grounded in shared
behavioral structure [30].

in Fig. 2, the framework operates in two stages.

D. Online Zero-Shot Preference Transfer
On the target task, PrefO2O initializes online PbRL using
the pretrained components and continuously derives pseudo-
preference supervision via OT-based alignment in the learned
latent preference space. OOD-aware confidence weights then
modulate the influence of these pseudo labels on reward learn-
ing, which in turn guides downstream policy optimization.
1) Warm-Start Initialization: PrefO2O is agnostic to the
choice of online PbRL backbone; in this work, we adopt PEB-
BLE [7], which combines preference-based reward learning
with SAC [33] for policy optimization.
Three pretrained components are transferred from the offline
phase: the trajectory encoder fϕ, the meta reward model ˆR,
and the preference VAE (qξ, pζ). The shared layers of fϕ in-
herits its offline parameters directly; the input adapter inherits
its offline initialization if the target state-action dimension
matches a source task seen during training, and is freshly
initialized otherwise.
During online learning, fϕ is fine-tuned to progressively
adapt target trajectories to the learned representation space,
whereas the preference VAE is kept frozen to preserve the
task-invariant preference manifold. This asymmetry is critical
for stable transfer: the frozen VAE provides a fixed geomet-
ric reference for preference alignment and OOD estimation,
while the adaptive trajectory encoder captures target-specific
dynamics without collapsing the latent preference structure.
2) Preference Alignment via Optimal Transport: With the
pretrained modules in place, a key challenge in online transfer
is generating reliable preference supervision for the target task
without any human annotation. PrefO2O addresses this chal-
lenge by aligning source and target preference distributions in
the learned latent space via optimal transport.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We design our experiments to address the following research
questions:
• RQ1. Can PrefO2O enable efficient zero-shot PbRL on
unseen target tasks compared to existing preference transfer
methods and PbRL baselines?
• RQ2. How well does PrefO2O generalize across diverse
transfer scenarios, including multi-to-one vs. one-to-one
transfer and intra- vs. cross-embodiment settings?
• RQ3. What is the contribution of each design component to
overall performance?
• RQ4. Can the learned latent space capture task-invariant,
transferable preference structures?
• RQ5. Can PrefO2O be deployed in real-world scenarios?
A. Task Environments and Transfer Settings
1) Environments and Target Tasks: We evaluate PrefO2O
on manipulation and locomotion tasks. For manipulation,
we use two widely used benchmarks: Meta-World [36] and
Robosuite [37]. From Meta-World, we consider five tasks as
the target tasks: Drawer Close, Button Press, Door Open,
Sweep Into, and Window Close. From Robosuite, we consider
two tasks: Stack and Pick-Place.
For locomotion, we use the DeepMind Control Suite [38]
and evaluate on Walker Walk. These tasks cover diverse ma-
nipulation and locomotion behaviors, enabling us to evaluate
preference transfer across different task objectives, dynamics,
and embodiment configurations.
2) Transfer Settings: We organize the transfer settings
along two axes: embodiment compatibility (intra-embodiment
vs. cross-embodiment) and source diversity (multi-to-one vs.
one-to-one transfer). Multi-to-one transfer serves as the default
setting, while one-to-one transfer is included to assess the
robustness of PrefO2O when source information is limited to
a single task.
Intra-embodiment transfer. In the intra-embodiment setting,
source and target tasks are selected from the same benchmark.
For Meta-World, each of the five tasks is used as the target
task in turn. Given a target task, we randomly select three
tasks from the remaining four tasks as source domains with
labeled preference data. For example, when Door Open is used
as the target task, three tasks are sampled from Drawer Close,
Button Press, Sweep Into, and Window Close as source tasks.
For Robosuite, each task is used as the target task in turn,
and the remaining task together with Lift are used as source
tasks. For example, when Stack is the target task, Pick-Place
and Lift are used as source tasks. For the DeepMind Control
Suite, we evaluate Walker Walk as the target task and use
Walker Stand and Walker Run as source tasks.
Cross-embodiment transfer. Beyond intra-embodiment trans-
fer, we evaluate whether PrefO2O can transfer preferences
across different robot embodiments. We construct cross-
embodiment settings between Meta-World and Robosuite.


JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021
7
When a Meta-World task is used as the target task, we use
Robosuite Stack and Pick-Place as source tasks. Conversely,
when a Robosuite task is used as the target task, we randomly
select three Meta-World tasks as source tasks.
One-to-one transfer. In addition to the default multi-to-one
setting, we also evaluate one-to-one transfer on Meta-World.
For each target task, we select a single task from the remaining
Meta-World tasks as the source task. This setting tests whether
PrefO2O can still extract useful transferable preference struc-
ture when only one source preference dataset is available. Note
that we remove Lmom in this setting, as no cross-source task
statistics are available.
3) Offline Preference Data Collection: We construct offline
preference datasets following protocols established in prior
preference-learning studies [34], [35]. For each source-task
dataset, we first collect a mixed-quality trajectory pool by
rolling out policies of varying performance levels, then sample
trajectory pairs from this pool and obtain 100 human prefer-
ence annotations per task. Annotators are graduate students
and staff with prior exposure to robotic manipulation tasks.
B. Baselines and Ablation Models
We compare PrefO2O against baselines from three cate-
gories:
1) Cross-Domain Preference Transfer Methods:
• PrefFew [14]: PrefFew is a few-shot PbRL method based
on model-agnostic meta-learning. For a fair comparison, we
train PrefFew using the same source tasks and the same
number of source-domain preference labels as PrefO2O. On
the target task, PrefFew is additionally provided 100 real
human preference labels for adaptation.
• PEARL [16]: Zero-shot preference transfer via OT align-
ment in the raw state-action space. Since PEARL is for-
mulated for offline transfer and requires matched state-
action dimensions across tasks, we implement an enhanced
variant that (i) shares PrefO2O’s online backbone and (ii)
zero-pads inputs to the maximum dimensionality for cross-
embodiment compatibility.
2) Foundation-Model-Based Feedback Methods:
• RL-VLM-F [10]: A PbRL method that uses a vision-
language model (VLM) to generate synthetic preference
labels from visual observations of trajectory rollouts. We
use GPT-4o as the backbone VLM and follow the original
implementation protocol for prompt construction, image
sampling, and reward model training.
3) Oracle Methods:
• PrefGT [4], [7]: A PbRL baseline trained from scratch
on the target task using preference labels generated from
the ground-truth environment reward as a proxy for human
judgment. This serves as an upper bound for PbRL methods
with privileged preference supervision.
• SAC [33]: Soft Actor-Critic trained directly with ground-
truth environment rewards on the target task. This serves
as an oracle RL reference representing the performance
achievable when perfect reward information is available.
To validate the contribution of each design component, we
evaluate the following ablations of PrefO2O:
• w/o Latent Space: This variant removes the learned latent
preference space used for transfer. Specifically, we remove
both the trajectory encoder and the preference VAE, train
the meta reward model directly on raw trajectory inputs, and
perform pseudo-label generation in the raw trajectory space.
This ablation evaluates whether transferring preferences
in a learned latent space improves robustness over direct
alignment in the original trajectory space.
• w/o Preference VAE: This variant removes the preference
VAE and performs OT-based preference alignment directly
on trajectory-pair difference embeddings produced by the
trajectory encoder. This ablation tests whether the structured
latent preference manifold learned by the VAE improves
cross-task alignment beyond learned behavioral representa-
tions alone.
• w/o Meta Reward: This variant does not transfer the
pretrained meta reward model to the target task. Instead,
the target reward model is randomly initialized and trained
only with pseudo-preference labels generated during online
learning. This ablation evaluates the benefit of transferring
a preference-aligned reward prior from source tasks.
• w/o Joint Training: This variant trains the trajectory en-
coder, preference VAE, and meta reward model separately
rather than optimizing them with the joint offline objective.
This ablation evaluates whether joint optimization is nec-
essary for learning representations that are simultaneously
preference-discriminative, geometrically regular, and useful
for reward transfer.
• w/o OOD Sampling: This variant removes the OOD-aware
curriculum sampling strategy used to construct OT batches.
Instead, target trajectory pairs are sampled randomly from
the online replay buffer for OT alignment.
• w/o OOD Weighting: This variant removes the confidence
weighting mechanism from reward learning. The target
reward model is trained under the standard Bradley-Terry
loss.
4) Evaluation: We evaluate all methods on all tasks and
transfer settings, and conduct ablation studies on Drawer
Close, Sweep Into, Walker Walk, and Stack. Each experiment is
run with five random seeds to ensure statistical robustness. We
report success rate for manipulation tasks and episode return
for locomotion tasks.
For PrefO2O, applicable ablations, and baselines (PEARL
and RL-VLM-F), we additionally report pseudo-label accuracy
to assess the quality of generated supervision. Following stan-
dard practice [7], we use a scripted teacher as the ground-truth
oracle, which produces preference labels based on cumulative
ground-truth environment rewards. This provides an objective
reference for evaluating how accurately each method recovers
true preferences without access to environment rewards during
training. All hyperparameters are held constant across all
experiments.

We first note that PrefFew performs poorly across the
board. We attribute this to its reliance on meta-training, which
fails to generalize in the source-limited regime we consider.
Whereas its original formulation assumes roughly ten thousand
preference labels spread across ten tasks, our setting provides
only one hundred labels per task across three source tasks,
mimicking the data scarcity of realistic deployment. This
contrast highlights the efficiency of PrefO2O, which transfers
effectively from only a handful of source-task preferences and
requires zero human labels on the target.
The more informative comparison is against RL-VLM-
F and PEARL, over which PrefO2O also yields substantial
gains. We attribute these gains to two factors. The first is
the quality of preference supervision on the target task. As
shown in Fig. 4, PrefO2O consistently attains the highest
pseudo-label accuracy across all benchmarks under the intra-
embodiment setting, exceeding both RL-VLM-F and PEARL
by a clear margin. The advantage widens dramatically under
the cross-embodiment setting, where PrefO2O retains most of
its accuracy while PEARL and RL-VLM-F both collapse to
a small fraction of their intra-embodiment level. RL-VLM-
F queries a vanilla VLM to interpret the visual appearance of
robot trajectories, so its labels rest on visual plausibility rather
than the underlying behavioral preference, rendering them
inconsistent and prone to missing task-relevant distinctions.
PEARL aligns source and target preferences directly in the
raw state-action space, which collapses under embodiment
shift because the state-action representation itself changes,
leaving no stable basis for alignment. In contrast, PrefO2O per-

B. Generalization Across Transfer Settings
Fig. 5 reports the learning curves for one-to-one transfer on
four MetaWorld tasks. Combined with the side-by-side com-
parison in Fig. 3, these results show that PrefO2O generalizes
well across all four transfer regimes: intra-embodiment, cross-
embodiment, multi-source, and single-source.
We first examine robustness to embodiment shift. From the
left-right comparison in Fig. 3, all transfer methods including
ours degrade when moving from the intra- to the cross-
embodiment setting, as the latter alters the state-action space
and transition dynamics, breaking the direct correspondence
between source and target trajectories. Crucially, however,
PrefO2O degrades only marginally, with a drop of 5 to 15
percentage points, whereas the preference-transfer baselines
see their relative rankings collapse entirely.
We attribute this robustness to where preference transfer
takes place. Rather than aligning trajectories in the raw state-
action space, PrefO2O performs alignment in the learned
latent preference space, which is constructed to factor out
embodiment-specific information at two levels. The per-task
input adapter absorbs dimensional and dynamical heterogene-
ity before encoding, so the shared backbone operates on
a common behavioral abstraction regardless of the under-
lying embodiment. The moment-matching and preference-
discrimination objectives then explicitly shape this space to re-
tain only preference-relevant structure, such as relative motion
quality and goal-directed progress, while suppressing absolute,
embodiment-dependent features. Because these retained fac-
tors remain meaningful across embodiments, the preference
correspondences established by optimal transport survive the
shift that derails raw-space alignment. The same embodiment-
agnostic space lets the shared backbone and meta reward
model carry over directly as warm-start priors, so the target
policy inherits not only transferable supervision but also a
preference-grounded initialization that baselines transferring
labels alone cannot provide.
We next examine one-to-one transfer in Fig. 5, where each
solid curve uses a single source task and the dashed curve
shows the multi-source reference. The multi-source setting
generally achieves the strongest performance, indicating that
broader source-task coverage improves transfer. Neverthe-
less, many single-source curves remain competitive and in
some cases approach the multi-source reference, showing that
PrefO2O can extract transferable preference structure even
from one source task. The performance gap among different
single sources further suggests that transfer depends on se-
mantic compatibility between source and target tasks. This
supports our view that PrefO2O transfers through pairwise
preference contrasts ∆z, which encode relational directions
of behavioral improvement rather than task-specific trajectory
appearance.

D. Latent Space Analysis
We further analyze whether the latent space learned by
PrefO2O captures preference structures that are invariant to
task and embodiment, and therefore transferable across tasks.
We conduct three complementary analyses using Sweep Into
as the target task. Fig. 7 examines the distributional geometry
of the learned space, Fig. 8 analyzes its semantic organization,
and Fig. 9 visualizes how the learned space supports OT-based
pseudo-label generation.
Fig. 7 compares raw trajectory-pair representations with
the learned latent preference representations, colored by task
domain in the upper row and by preference label in the lower
row. In the raw space, samples are more strongly separated by
task domain, and preference labels are more entangled. This
suggests that alignment in the raw space can be dominated
by task identity rather than preference-relevant similarity. In
contrast, the learned latent space shows substantially stronger
cross-task mixing while organizing samples more coherently
according to preference labels. This pattern appears in both
intra- and cross-embodiment settings, indicating that the
learned space reduces task- and embodiment-specific variation
while preserving preference-discriminative structure.
This raises the question of what the separating preference
direction in the learned space actually encodes. We therefore
examine whether this direction is semantically meaningful
by projecting each latent preference vector onto the learned
preference axis. As shown in Fig. 8, the horizontal axis
represents the preference-axis projection score, while the
vertical axis reports real differences in three interpretable
behavioral attributes: Approach Progress, Path Directness, and
Motion Smoothness, as defined in Appendix C. Across both
intra-embodiment and cross-embodiment settings, the attribute
trends increase along the preference axis, indicating that mov-
ing in the direction that separates preference labels also corre-
sponds to systematic improvements in behavioral quality. As
reflected by the Spearman correlations ρ, this trend is strongest
for Approach Progress, remains clear for Path Directness, and
is weaker but still positive for Motion Smoothness, consistent
with a hierarchy in how strongly different behavioral factors
contribute to the learned preference structure. Importantly, the
trends persist under cross-embodiment transfer, suggesting that
the latent space expresses preference through embodiment-
independent behavioral contrasts rather than raw kinematic
similarity.
Finally, we verify that this semantic structure is what
governs transfer in practice. Fig. 9 presents a case study of
the OT alignment for Sweep Into target pairs. The chord
diagram shows that each target pair draws transport mass
from pairs across all source tasks, but concentrated on a small
set of dominant matches that consistently share the target’s
preference label. The trajectory rollouts make the basis of
these matches explicit and reveal that different target pairs
are aligned through different criteria: Sweep Into #4 matches
Window Close #53 because both preferences are governed by
approaching efficiency, whereas Sweep Into #53 matches Door
Open #48 because both hinge on whether the manipulator suc-
cessfully engages the target object rather than on the approach
motion itself. That two pairs from the same target task are
routed to source pairs in different tasks, according to which
behavioral attribute determines their preference, confirms that
alignment is driven by transferable behavioral features rather
than task identity.

E. Real-World Robot Validation
To validate the practical applicability of PrefO2O, we de-
ploy the trained policies on a physical Kinova Jaco2 arm for
two Robosuite tasks [37]: Cube Stacking, where one cube must
be placed on top of another, and Pick-and-Place, where an
object must be grasped and placed into a target container.
Policies are first trained in simulation and then transferred to
the physical platform. The complete hardware configuration,
camera setup, sim-to-real settings, and evaluation protocol are
described in Appendix D. We evaluate each policy over 10
trials on the physical robot. PrefO2O achieves success rates
of 7/10 on Cube Stacking and 6/10 on Pick-and-Place. These
results show that policies trained with transferred preference
supervision can retain meaningful task performance under
real-world perception noise, actuation variability, and contact
uncertainty. Fig. 10 shows representative executions of both
tasks.
As a comparative reference, we also deploy PEARL on
the same physical platform. Over 10 trials per task, PEARL
achieves success rates of 2/10 on Cube Stacking and 1/10
on Pick-and-Place, trailing PrefO2O on both tasks. The gap
is especially pronounced on Pick-and-Place, where successful
execution requires accurate grasping, transport, and placement.
These results answer RQ5 by demonstrating that PrefO2O can
transfer preference-guided policies to physical robotic settings
without collecting target-task human feedback.
VI. CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
We presented PrefO2O, an offline-to-online framework for
zero-shot PbRL that transfers preference structures from a
small set of labeled source tasks to an unlabeled target task,
even across differing objectives, dynamics, and robot embod-
iments. The central idea is to perform preference transfer not
over raw trajectories, but within a learned latent preference
space that is jointly trained to be task-invariant and preference-
discriminative.
Across manipulation and locomotion benchmarks under
both intra- and cross-embodiment protocols, PrefO2O consis-
tently outperforms preference-transfer baselines and achieves
performance competitive with, and in several settings superior
to, oracle RL and PbRL methods trained with target-task
supervision. It also transfers effectively from as little as a


JOURNAL OF LATEX CLASS FILES, VOL. 14, NO. 8, AUGUST 2021
13
single source task. Our analyses suggest that these gains
arise from two complementary mechanisms: the learned latent
space provides more reliable source-target preference corre-
spondences than raw trajectory alignment, and the pretrained
trajectory encoder and meta reward model provide a strong
initialization for target-task learning. More broadly, the results
support the view that what transfers across tasks is not the raw
trajectory distribution itself, but the relational, behavior-level
contrasts that underlie human preference judgments.
PrefO2O has several limitations that suggest directions
for future work. First, the approach assumes that source
and target tasks belong to a related domain family sharing
common behavioral structure; transfer to tasks whose prefer-
ences depend on entirely novel behavioral dimensions, absent
from all source tasks, remains an open challenge. Second,
the optimal transport alignment operates on sampled mini-
batches for tractability, and although our curriculum sampling
mitigates the resulting variance, scaling alignment to very
large source pools is an avenue for further study. Third, our
analysis reveals that the latent space spontaneously organizes
preferences along interpretable behavioral attributes, which
suggests a complementary opportunity: explicitly injecting
semantic guidance during training, whether from human-
specified behavioral descriptors or from foundation models
that articulate the criteria underlying a preference, could yield
a more structured and controllable preference manifold, further
improving both transfer quality and interpretability.
Overall, PrefO2O represents a step toward general-purpose
robots that can learn from human feedback more scalably.
Rather than treating each new task as requiring a new round of
preference collection, PrefO2O shows that preference knowl-
edge can be accumulated from prior tasks and reused through
a task-invariant latent space. This perspective shifts PbRL
from task-specific reward learning toward transferable pref-
erence learning, where robots bootstrap new behaviors from
previously learned human judgments. As robots move toward
open-ended real-world deployment, such reuse of preference
supervision will be essential for reducing human annotation
burden while preserving alignment with human intent.

D. Procedure
The full sampling procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1.
Each invocation computes reconstruction errors for all pairs in
the replay buffer, classifies them under the current threshold
τ(t), and draws a mixed batch according to α(t). All oper-
ations are O(|B|) in the buffer size apart from the diversity
term in OOD sampling, which is O(nID) per OOD candidate;
in practice this incurs no measurable overhead at the batch
sizes used in our experiments.

APPENDIX D
REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
This appendix describes the physical setup used in the real-
robot experiments reported in Section V-E.
A. Hardware and Cameras
We use a 7-DoF Kinova Jaco2 arm with a two-finger grip-
per, fixed to the rear of a tabletop through a clamp-on mount. A
black curtain encloses the scene to keep the background clean.
Two cameras observe the workspace. An Intel RealSense D435
camera is attached to the robot wrist through a custom bracket,
sitting above the gripper and looking at the area in front
of it, which gives a close-up view of the gripper and the
object during execution. A USB camera on a small tripod
at the side of the table provides a fixed view of the whole
workspace. Both cameras are calibrated to the robot with
standard procedures. Each metal tray carries a printed marker
on its inner surface for pose estimation, and object poses are
likewise estimated from the calibrated camera streams. All
poses are converted into the same low-dimensional observation
format used in simulation, so the policy sees observations with
the same structure in both domains.

B. Sim-to-Real Configuration
Policies are trained in Robosuite with its built-in domain
randomization tools [37]. We randomize system dynamics
with DynamicsModder, covering object mass, friction, joint
damping, and contact parameters, and model realistic sensing
with the Observable interface, which adds sensor noise,
sampling-rate variation, and observation delay. The policy
outputs the same actions as in simulation and sends them to the
arm through the manufacturer’s control interface, with motions
clipped to a safe region above the table.
