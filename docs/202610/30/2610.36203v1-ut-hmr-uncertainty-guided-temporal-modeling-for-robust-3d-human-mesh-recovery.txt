UT-HMR: Uncertainty-Guided Temporal Modeling for Robust 3D Human Mesh
Recovery
Zhibo Wang, Sirisha Rambhatla, Yuhao Chen, Maria Koshkina, David Clausi
{zhibo.wang, sirisha.rambhatla, yuhao.chen1, mkoshkina, dclausi}@uwaterloo.ca

Abstract
Monocular 3D human mesh recovery (HMR) remains unre-
liable under weak observations caused by long camera-to-
person distances, occlusion, and motion blur. In challenging
scenarios such as sports, these factors make the problem
highly ambiguous, as different 3D poses may yield similar
2D projections. This ambiguity is especially severe for one-
stage HMR, which recovers all meshes directly from the
full image without person crops or external 2D cues. To
handle this ambiguity, we present UT-HMR (Uncertainty-
guided Temporal HMR), to our knowledge the first one-
stage HMR framework to capture correlated pose uncer-
tainty across joints. Each person query predicts a Gaus-
sian distribution over the full SMPL pose with a compact
low-rank-plus-diagonal covariance, which is converted into
joint-wise precision weights for heteroscedastic supervision
while keeping inference deterministic. Temporal early fusion
further injects neighboring-frame evidence before pose de-
coding, supplying the visual evidence needed to resolve the
ambiguity that the uncertainty captures. With a lightweight
ViT-B backbone, UT-HMR achieves state-of-the-art results
on VIP-HockeyPose-v2 (ice hockey) and WorldPose (soc-
cer), reducing MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, and PVE by up to 4.7%,
3.8%, and 5.6%, and remains competitive on 3DPW against
methods with larger backbones and higher input resolutions.
1. Introduction
3D human mesh recovery (HMR) from monocular images is
inherently ill-posed: the mapping from 2D observations to
3D pose is highly ambiguous, making mesh quality strongly
dependent on the visual evidence in the input (Fig. 2). This
ambiguity is particularly severe for one-stage HMR meth-
ods [2, 30], which recover multiple meshes directly from
the full image without cropped person regions used by two-
stage methods [9, 28, 29] as additional evidence. Never-
theless, the one-stage paradigm is particularly attractive for

real-world multi-person applications: it removes the need
for person-level detection, jointly processes all people from
the full image, and supports efficient multi-person infer-
ence [2, 30, 32]. These properties are especially valuable in
crowded scenes like sports, where the large number of play-
ers makes per-person cropping and inference costly, while
the single-pass, end-to-end design keeps the pipeline simple
and fast [2, 31–33]. We therefore seek to improve the ro-
bustness of one-stage HMR under ambiguous observations
while preserving these practical advantages.
A natural remedy is to model pose uncertainty, which
has proven effective in crop-based HMR. Prior works cap-
ture uncertainty over probabilistic 2D pose features [16],
pre-extracted 2D keypoint and depth cues [41], or joint lo-
cations [10], or learn multi-hypothesis distributions [15, 39].
None transfers directly to one-stage HMR: they depend on
crops, tracking, or explicit 2D inputs, and hypothesis-based
inference is ill-suited to estimating all people jointly.
We bridge this gap with a design tailored to the one-stage
setting. Our statistical analysis (Fig. 1) shows that the pose
error of a one-stage baseline concentrates largely in a few
dominant directions. Accordingly, we model each person’s
pose as a Gaussian whose compact low-rank-plus-diagonal
covariance captures correlated and residual uncertainty. This
distribution is predicted directly from each person query of
the one-stage decoder, requiring no crops or auxiliary cues;
the covariance provides joint-wise precision weights during

with full-pose uncertainty modeling (Fig. 2); to our knowl-
edge, UT-HMR is the first one-stage HMR framework to
capture correlated pose uncertainty across joints. We evalu-
ate it on ice hockey [36] and soccer [12], where ambiguous
observations are common, as well as on the general-purpose
3DPW benchmark [34]. UT-HMR achieves state-of-the-art

2. Related Work
2.1. Multi-Person Human Mesh Recovery
Human mesh recovery (HMR) in multi-person scenarios, typ-
ically built on parametric human models such as SMPL [20]
and SMPL-X [23], is essential for real-world applications
like sports analysis [4, 5, 36]. Existing approaches generally
follow either a two-stage or a one-stage paradigm. Two-
stage methods, such as HMR [13] and CameraHMR [21],
first detect and crop each person and then perform mesh
regression. Although cropping preserves image resolution
and usually improves accuracy, the overall performance de-
pends heavily on the quality of the detector, making these
methods less suitable for crowded scenes. In contrast, one-
stage methods jointly estimate all people from the full image.
ROMP [32] and BEV [33] regress pixel-aligned body-center
and mesh-parameter maps using CNN backbones, while re-
cent transformer-based approaches formulate multi-person
HMR as a set prediction problem [6, 31, 44]. Multi-HMR [2]
employs a cross-attention human prediction head with one
query per detected person, AiOS [31] progressively filters
and expands queries, and SAT-HMR [30] introduces scale-
adaptive tokens for real-time inference. Our method builds
upon this one-stage paradigm and further enhances the de-
coder queries with temporal context and uncertainty model-
ing, producing more robust results under weak observations.
2.2. Video-Based Human Mesh Recovery
To exploit temporal information and improve motion consis-
tency, video-based methods aggregate features across multi-
ple frames. VIBE [14] employs recurrent encoders to cap-


ture temporal dynamics, while TCMR [8] aggregates past,
future, and whole-sequence features. Attention-based meth-
ods such as MPS-Net [38] and GLoT [27] further model
long-range temporal dependencies, and PSVT [24] extends
one-stage multi-person estimation to video with progres-
sive spatio-temporal transformers. Another line of work
focuses on recovering world-grounded motion. WHAM [29]
and TRAM [35] combine pose estimation with camera mo-
tion to reconstruct trajectories in the world frame, while
GVHMR [28] performs sequence modeling in a gravity-
aligned coordinate system.
These methods mainly target long-range motion modeling
and temporal consistency over entire sequences. In contrast,
our goal is to alleviate the projection ambiguity caused by
weak single-frame observations rather than to perform full
video-based HMR. Therefore, we adopt a lightweight tempo-
ral early-fusion strategy that incorporates information from
neighboring frames before pose decoding, supplying addi-
tional evidence while preserving the efficiency of a one-stage
multi-person framework.
2.3. Probabilistic Human Mesh Recovery
Monocular human mesh recovery is inherently ill-posed due
to depth ambiguity, occlusion, and truncation. To character-
ize such ambiguity, probabilistic methods predict distribu-
tions or multiple plausible hypotheses instead of determinis-
tic point estimates. ProHMR [15] models pose uncertainty
using conditional normalizing flows, while MH-HMR [40]
and MHPro [39] generate multiple hypotheses and refine
them through inter- and intra-hypothesis communication.
More recently, HuMR [37] utilizes uncertainty encoded in
2D pose heatmaps to supervise the learned 3D mesh dis-
tribution. Different from these methods, which explicitly
sample, rank, or aggregate multiple hypotheses, UT-HMR
keeps inference deterministic. We parameterize a lightweight
low-rank-plus-diagonal Gaussian distribution around the de-
terministic pose prediction and use the predicted covariance
to derive joint-wise precision weights for heteroscedastic
training, allowing uncertainty to guide training while pre-
serving efficient one-stage multi-person inference.
3. Method
3.1. Preliminaries
In this work, we adopt SMPL [20] to represent the 3D human
body. Given an input image I ∈RH×W ×3, the SMPL
model parameterizes a human body using a pose parameter
θ ∈R24×3 and a shape parameter β ∈R10, and maps them
to a 3D mesh:
V = SMPL(θ, β),
V ∈R6890×3.
(1)
In practice, recent HMR methods [9, 28] employ a 6D
rotation representation for body pose due to its superior con-
tinuity and optimization properties [43]. Therefore, instead
of directly regressing the axis-angle pose parameter θ, the
network predicts a pose representation: µ ∈R24×6, where
each joint rotation is encoded by a 6D vector. The predicted
rotations are subsequently converted to the axis-angle repre-
sentation required by the SMPL model, yielding θ ∈R24×3.
Together with the shape parameter β ∈R10, the recovered
pose parameters are used to reconstruct the mesh V:
V = SMPL(Φ(µ), β),
(2)
where µ ∈R24×6 denotes the predicted 6D pose repre-
sentation, β ∈R10 denotes the shape parameter, and Φ(·)
converts the 6D rotation representation into the axis-angle
representation required by SMPL.
The 3D joint locations are then obtained from the mesh
using a predefined linear regressor R ∈RJ×6890:
J = RV,
J ∈RJ×3,
(3)
where J denotes the number of target joints.
3.2. Overall Pipeline
Following recent one-stage HMR methods [2, 30], we build
our one-stage multi-person framework upon a DETR-style
architecture [6]. A ViT-B/14 encoder first extracts patch fea-
tures from the input image and produces a memory feature
map M ∈RNm×c, where Nm and c denote the number
of patch tokens and the feature dimension, respectively. A
DETR-style decoder with Nq learnable queries then per-
forms cross-attention over M. Each query predicts a confi-
dence score, and queries whose confidence exceeds a thresh-
old (0.3 in our experiments) are treated as individual persons.
Lightweight prediction heads further regress the pose pa-
rameter µ and shape parameter β, which are converted by
the SMPL model into a mesh with 6890 vertices and the
corresponding 3D joints.
When directly applying this backbone to challenging
sports scenarios such as ice hockey, we observe a recur-
ring failure mode. Under weak observations caused by small
scale, occlusion, and motion blur, the predicted pose of-
ten produces a plausible 2D reprojection while exhibiting
an incorrect 3D configuration (Fig. 2). We attribute this
phenomenon to projection ambiguity: a degraded 2D ob-
servation may correspond to multiple plausible 3D poses,
yet a deterministic single-frame model is forced to commit
to one of them. To address this issue, we introduce two
complementary components. First, a temporal early-fusion
mechanism (Sec. 3.3) injects short-term motion information
before pose decoding, providing additional evidence to al-
leviate ambiguity. Second, an uncertainty modeling branch
(Sec. 3.4) estimates the uncertainty associated with each
prediction using a low-rank-plus-diagonal Gaussian covari-
ance, which is further used to guide heteroscedastic training.


Together, these two components enable more robust mesh re-
covery under ambiguous observations. The overall pipeline
is illustrated in Fig. 3.
3.3. Temporal Early Fusion
Challenging scenarios such as ice hockey and soccer often
suffer from weak per-frame observations, including small
person scale, frequent occlusion, and motion blur. Under
such conditions, a single frame may not provide sufficient
evidence to resolve the projection ambiguity inherent to
monocular HMR. Existing video-based HMR methods com-
monly exploit temporal information through temporal mo-
tion modeling, consistency constraints, or trajectory refine-
ment [8, 14, 29, 35]. However, these strategies aggregate
per-person features or refine predicted trajectories after crop-
ping and tracking. As a result, they cannot directly provide
full-image temporal evidence for an ambiguous center-frame
observation within a one-stage pipeline.
We instead aggregate neighboring frames as auxil-
iary visual evidence before the SMPL prediction heads.
Given a short clip centered at frame t, we use T=3, i.e.,
{It−1, It, It+1}. For each frame, the image encoder pro-
duces a memory feature map Mt. The three frame-wise
memories are then concatenated into a unified multi-frame
memory for the center frame:

(4)

aggregate complementary evidence from neighboring frames
at the feature level, rather than combining the outputs of
separately predicted meshes.
Only the center frame is supervised during training, and
the clip window slides over the sequence at inference time to
obtain frame-wise predictions. This design introduces tem-
poral evidence without requiring an explicit smoothness loss,
motion model, or hand-crafted fusion weights. Because fu-
sion is performed before pose regression, the enriched query
features can improve the deterministic pose estimate and pro-
vide stronger context for the uncertainty modeling branch
in Sec. 3.4, where the remaining ambiguity is modeled by a
low-rank-plus-diagonal Gaussian covariance.
3.4. Pose Uncertainty Modeling
Although temporal fusion provides additional evidence to
alleviate projection ambiguity, the recovered pose can still
remain uncertain under weak observations such as occlusion,
motion blur, and small person scale. Instead of treating all
predicted joints as equally reliable, we explicitly estimate
the pose uncertainty associated with each joint and use it to
guide training.
Let h ∈R768 denote the decoder feature of a matched
query. The deterministic pose head fθ predicts a 6D pose
µ = fθ(h) ∈R144, corresponding to 24 joints with 6D ro-
tations. In parallel, an uncertainty branch models the pose
as a Gaussian centered at µ and predicts its covariance Σ.
A full covariance over D = 144 dimensions would require
D(D + 1)/2 = 10,440 independent parameters per query,
which is unnecessarily large for monocular HMR. Human
poses are highly structured and can be effectively regularized
by compact latent pose priors [23]. Our empirical analy-
sis (Sec. 4.3.2) further shows that the learned uncertainty
remains concentrated in only a few dominant directions,
suggesting that pose uncertainty is intrinsically compact,
while excessive covariance dimensions may introduce noisy
estimates. We therefore adopt a low-rank-plus-diagonal co-
variance, where L captures shared uncertainty factors and d
models residual per-dimension uncertainty. This parameter-
ization requires only Dr + D parameters (432 for r = 2),
over 20× fewer than a full covariance.
Specifically, a low-rank head predicts L ∈R144×r from
the query feature h, modeling correlated uncertainty that
is shared across pose dimensions, while a diagonal head
predicts d ∈R144, representing independent per-dimension
variance. The covariance is then assembled from the two
heads as:
Σ = LL⊤+ diag(d),
(5)
which, together with the pose mean µ, defines the predicted
pose distribution N(µ, Σ) over the 6D pose space. The rank
r controls the number of correlated uncertainty directions
modeled by the low-rank term LL⊤; based on the empirical
analysis above, we set r = 2 in all our experiments.
This formulation compactly captures both correlated and
independent pose uncertainty. The low-rank component
models coupled ambiguities across pose dimensions, while
the diagonal component accounts for residual per-parameter
uncertainty. The predicted distribution is used during train-
ing: the covariance is supervised by a distribution loss and
further converted into joint-wise precision weights to guide
the deterministic pose estimator, as described below.

VIP-HockeyPose-v2 is an ice hockey HMR dataset with
frames sampled from 24 NHL games. Unlike the original
version [36], v2 uses the more recent SAM-3D-Body [42]

3DPW, a standard in-the-wild HMR benchmark, to assess
generalization beyond sports.
We report MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, and PVE on all datasets.
MPJPE measures 3D joint reconstruction error in the original
coordinate space, while PA-MPJPE measures the error after
Procrustes alignment, removing global translation, rotation,
and scale differences. PVE measures the average vertex
error of the recovered mesh. We also report average per-
frame inference time in milliseconds, measured on a single
NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU.
4.1.2. Implementation Details
Following SAT-HMR [30], we adopt a base input resolution
of 644 and use ViT-B/14 as the backbone. UT-HMR uses
clips of T=3 frames: each frame is encoded independently,
the token memories are concatenated, and the center-frame
queries cross-attend to the multi-frame memory through the
DETR-style decoder.
We initialize UT-HMR from the SAT-HMR checkpoint
and fine-tune the decoder, prediction heads, and the last
three ViT blocks. The newly added low-rank and diagonal
covariance heads are implemented as MLPs and trained from
scratch. We use AdamW with a learning rate of 2×10−5,
weight decay 10−4, batch size 8, and gradient clipping at
1.0. We fine-tune for 50 epochs on VIP-HockeyPose-v2 and
WorldPose to account for the sports-domain shift, and 20
epochs on 3DPW. All experiments use a single NVIDIA
RTX 6000 Ada GPU.
For fair comparison, all competing methods, including
the SAT-HMR baseline, are fine-tuned on each benchmark’s
training split, either by us using their official code, or as
reported in their papers. The sole exception is DepthHMR
[26] (code unavailable), whose 3DPW fine-tuning protocol
is unspecified; we include its reported numbers as-is.
4.2. Overall Performance
4.2.1. Quantitative Results
Tab. 1 compares UT-HMR with recent one-stage multi-
person HMR methods. Across all three benchmarks, UT-
HMR consistently improves over the SAT-HMR baseline
with only moderate computational overhead, while remain-
ing much faster than larger ViT-L-based methods. On both
VIP-HockeyPose-v2 and WorldPose, UT-HMR achieves the
best performance on all three metrics among the tested mod-
els; we do not compare with the results reported in [36],
since VIP-HockeyPose-v2 uses updated annotations that dif-
fer from the original version. On the general-scene 3DPW
benchmark, UT-HMR remains competitive with the strongest
prior methods despite using a lighter backbone and a lower
input resolution than the top-performing competitors. Over-
all, UT-HMR improves robustness in challenging sports sce-
narios while generalizing well to in-the-wild scenes.
Since most existing one-stage HMR methods operate on
single frames, we additionally train a single-frame (T=1)
variant of UT-HMR for a fairer comparison. As shown by
Tab. 1, this variant still outperforms all competing single-
frame methods on both sports benchmarks and remains com-
petitive on 3DPW against methods with substantially larger
backbones, indicating that the gains do not arise solely from
using multiple frames. Among multi-frame methods, PSVT
reports results only on 3DPW, where our three-frame UT-
HMR also outperforms its best nine-frame setting.

4.3. Ablation Studies
4.3.1. Effectiveness of Proposed Modifications
We evaluate the proposed components on VIP-HockeyPose-
v2, with results summarized in Tab. 2. Both temporal early
fusion and pose uncertainty modeling improve over the base-
line, while their combination achieves the best performance,

5. Conclusion
In this paper, we presented UT-HMR, a temporal and
uncertainty-guided framework for one-stage multi-person
human mesh recovery. UT-HMR combines temporal early fu-
sion with low-rank-plus-diagonal Gaussian pose uncertainty
modeling and uses the predicted covariance for heteroscedas-
tic joint supervision. Experiments on VIP-HockeyPose-v2,
WorldPose, and 3DPW demonstrate consistent improve-
ments over the baseline, while remaining competitive with
larger models despite using a lighter backbone.
Beyond accuracy, the predicted covariance is a usable
output in itself: it provides a per-person reliability signal at
negligible inference cost. For tracking, it can weight data
association under weak observations; for action analysis,
the joint-wise uncertainty can down-weight unreliable joints
when aggregating pose sequences. We leave a quantitative
study of such applications to future work.
