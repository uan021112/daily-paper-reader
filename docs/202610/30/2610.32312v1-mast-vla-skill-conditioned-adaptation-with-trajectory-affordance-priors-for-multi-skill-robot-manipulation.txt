Abstract: Generalist robot manipulation requires a single vision-language-action (VLA) policy to execute diverse skills across scenes and instructions. However, current multi-skill VLA policies often leave two aspects of an instruction insuf-ficiently modeled: which primitive skill to execute and where/how to contact and move objects. We propose MAST-VLA (Multi-skill Adaptation with Skill-conditioned experts and Trajectory-affordance priors), a dual-conditioning frame-work for robot manipulation. MAST-VLA introduces skill-conditioned LoRA Mixture-of-Experts (LoRA-MoE) adapters in the LLM backbone, where skill-query tokens and routing biases guide low-rank expert specialization, and con-verts a predicted affordance heatmap into a patch-level trajectory-affordance spa-tial prior for the action head. On the CALVIN ABC →D long-horizon multi-skill cross-scene generalization setting, MAST-VLA achieves state-of-the-art perfor-mance, reaching an Avg. Len of 4.65 over five-subtask evaluation sequences. Real-robot multi-skill sequence experiments further validate its deployability un-der real perception and control noise. 

Keywords: Robot Manipulation, Vision-Language-Action Models, Multi-Task Learning, Affordance Learning 

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) provide a unified interface for language-conditioned robot manipulation by connecting visual perception, language understanding, and action generation [1, 2, 3, 4]. Recent VLA policies show that pretrained multimodal models can be adapted to robot control across diverse objects, scenes, tasks, and embodiments [5, 6, 7, 8]. This capability makes VLAs a promising foundation for generalist robot manipulation, where a single policy is expected to reuse visual-language knowledge while executing and composing multiple manipulation skills [9, 10, 11]. Despite this progress, multi-skill long-horizon manipulation remains challenging because language-conditioned policies must maintain stable execution across sequential subtasks and skill transi-tions [12, 13, 14, 15]. A manipulation instruction requires the policy to infer the intended primitive operation, such as grasping, pushing, rotating, or stacking, while also grounding the contact regions, motion paths, and object interactions required for execution [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. These two factors are referred to in this work as primitive-skill semantics and object-interaction semantics . When ei-ther factor is insufficiently modeled, errors may accumulate across consecutive subtasks, leading to unstable execution under long-horizon skill transitions [12, 14, 21]. Existing VLA policies often leave these two forms of grounding implicit in language instructions, visual observations, and low-dimensional action supervision [2, 7, 22, 23]. For interaction-level ground-ing, low-dimensional action supervision provides only indirect cues about contact regions, motion trends, and task-relevant geometry, whereas prior manipulation methods have shown the importance of explicit spatial, geometric, or affordance-level signals [27, 16, 17, 18, 28, 29]. To address these limitations, we propose MAST-VLA , a dual-conditioning framework for multi-skill robot manipulation. MAST-VLA combines skill-conditioned LoRA-MoE specialization in the LLM backbone with a trajectory-affordance spatial prior in the action head. Together, these two paths reduce cross-skill interference and strengthen interaction-aware action generation. The contributions are summarized as follows: 1) A dual-conditioning VLA framework is proposed for multi-skill robot manipulation, jointly mod-eling primitive-skill structure and interaction-relevant spatial grounding within a shared policy; 2) Skill-conditioned LoRA-MoE specialization and trajectory-affordance action guidance are intro-duced. The former uses skill-query tokens and routing biases to condition low-rank expert special-ization in the LLM backbone, while the latter converts future gripper motion and scene geometry into heatmap supervision and patch-level spatial bias in the action head; 3) MAST-VLA achieves state-of-the-art performance on CALVIN ABC →D with an Avg. Generalist VLA Policies for Robot Manipulation. Generalist robot policies and vision-language-action models aim to map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions across tasks, scenes, and embodiments [9, 1, 10, 6, 3]. Recent VLA policies transfer pretrained visual-language knowledge to manipulation and support efficient adaptation to downstream robot control [30, 5, 2, 7, 22]. Action modeling has also evolved from autoregressive action tokens to action chunking, diffusion policies, and fast action decoding [31, 32, 33]. These advances provide strong generalist priors, but multi-skill adaptation still often leaves primitive-skill separation and interaction grounding implicit in language-action supervision. MAST-VLA treats multi-skill VLA adaptation as a dual-grounding problem: aligning adaptation capacity with primitive skill structure and grounding action decoding in trajectory-derived interaction geometry. 

Modular Adaptation and Skill Specialization. Parameter-efficient adaptation methods such as adapters, prompt tuning, and LoRA specialize pretrained models with a small number of additional parameters [34, 35, 24]. For robot policies, lightweight adaptation modules, action-head adaptation, and task-level LoRA experts improve efficient specialization and can reduce multi-task interfer-ence [7, 22, 36, 26]. MoE and LoRA-MoE methods further increase adaptation capacity through sparse expert routing [37, 38, 25]. However, existing VLA adaptation methods are mainly single-path or task-level, either sharing the same lightweight update across skills or allocating experts by tasks/domains rather than primitive operations. MAST-VLA uses the instruction-derived primitive skill condition to modulate both input-side skill queries and router-side biases, thereby aligning expertized adaptation capacity with primitive robot skills within a shared VLA backbone. 

Spatial and Affordance Grounding for Manipulation. Robot manipulation requires spatial rea-soning about contact regions, motion paths, and 3D object relations. Prior work improves spatial awareness through transport-based representations, voxel or multi-view policies, and 3D-aware ma-nipulation models [27, 16, 17, 18, 39, 40]. Recent VLA methods also incorporate 3D represen-tations, spatial projections, or geometry-aware visual features into robot control [41, 42, 43, 44]. Another line of work provides explicit spatial cues through affordance learning, interaction-region prediction, or trajectory-conditioned visual prompts [19, 20, 28, 29, 45]. Unlike methods that mainly use spatial cues as observation representations, target regions, or input prompts, MAST-VLA con-verts demonstration trajectories into a dense action-decoding prior and injects the predicted heatmap into action-head attention for multi-skill action generation. 

## 3 Methodology 

Problem setup. This work considers sequential multi-skill robot manipulation, where a shared vision-language-action policy executes successive primitive instructions under an evolving scene state. Given the current observation ot, language instruction l, and coarse primitive skill condition

Fine-grained manipulation commands are grouped into a compact primitive skill taxonomy, such as rotation, sliding/pushing, lifting/picking, placing/storing, stacking, and switching. For each step, the language instruction is matched to in-struction variants with normalized lexical and fuzzy matching, and the matched operation is grouped into this taxonomy to obtain the language-derived primitive skill condition st. This condition only indexes skill-query and routing-bias banks; the original language instruction and action target remain unchanged. 

Framework overview. As shown in Figure 1, MAST-VLA contains two coupled conditioning paths for multi-skill VLA policy learning. The first path performs skill-conditioned LoRA-MoE 3adaptation in the LLM backbone, where the instruction-derived primitive skill condition modulates token representations and expert routing to form skill-aware adaptation paths within a shared pol-icy (Sec. 3.1). The second path introduces a trajectory-affordance spatial prior in the action head, where demonstration-derived gripper motion and scene geometry provide affordance-heatmap su-pervision and patch-level attention guidance for action decoding (Sec. 3.2). The full model is trained with action regression, affordance-heatmap supervision, and MoE routing regularization (Sec. 3.3), coupling skill-level specialization with interaction-aware spatial grounding.

3.3 Training Objective 

MAST-VLA is optimized under a joint objective comprising action-chunk regression, affordance-heatmap supervision, and MoE routing regularization: 

L = ∥ˆat:t+H−1 − at:t+H−1∥1 + λhm Lhm + λmoe Lmoe . (8) The predicted future action chunk is supervised by an ℓ1 regression loss. The affordance-heatmap supervision term Lhm is instantiated as a weighted binary cross-entropy (WBCE) loss between the predicted affordance heatmap ˆH and the trajectory-affordance target H∗. The routing regulariza-tion term Lmoe denotes the standard router auxiliary loss, which stabilizes expert assignment and discourages routing collapse [38]. The coefficients λhm and λmoe weight the affordance-heatmap supervision and routing regularization terms, respectively. 

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

MAST-VLA is evaluated in both simulation and real-world robot settings. 

Simulation. The CALVIN ABC →D benchmark [12] is used to evaluate long-horizon multi-skill generalization, with the policy trained on scenes A–C and tested on the unseen scene D, which differs in visual textures and the spatial arrangement of static scene elements. Following the standard five-subtask protocol, each evaluation sequence executes five consecutive subtasks without resetting the scene between subtasks. Success@1–5 and Avg. Len are reported, where Success@k denotes the percentage of evaluation sequences in which at least k consecutive subtasks are completed and Avg. Len denotes the mean completed sequence length. Detailed protocol and metric definitions are provided in Appendix C.1. 5Figure 2: Representative real-robot evaluation tasks. Real-world robot. Physical multi-skill manipulation is further evaluated under perception and control noise using two tabletop scenes with length-3 primitive-subtask sequences. Detailed task sequences and success criteria are provided in Appendix C.2. Unless otherwise specified, VLA-Adapter-Pro is used as the direct baseline.

## 5 Limitations 

1) Execution-level correction. MAST-VLA does not explicitly correct low-level deviations be-tween predicted actions and realized end-effector motion. In longer or less controlled deployments, closed-loop feedback, temporal memory, and replanning may be needed to compensate for such deviations. 2) Depth-based spatial grounding. The trajectory-affordance prior assumes reasonably 8reliable RGB-D geometry. Although preprocessing handles routine depth noise and missing pix-els, extreme RGB-D failure modes, such as heavy occlusion, reflective or transparent surfaces, and large calibration errors, can still degrade the spatial prior. Future work will explore recovery-aware execution, temporally aggregated or multi-view geometry, and broader skill taxonomies. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented MAST-VLA, a dual-conditioning VLA framework for multi-skill robot manipula-tion. MAST-VLA couples skill-conditioned LoRA-MoE specialization in the LLM backbone with trajectory-affordance action guidance in the action head, enabling a shared policy to jointly model primitive-skill semantics and object-interaction semantics. Experiments on CALVIN ABC →D and real-robot sequence tasks show improved long-horizon execution stability, state-of-the-art simula-tion performance, and complementary gains from both conditioning paths. 9Acknowledgments

A.1 Primitive Skill Conditioning 

MAST-VLA uses a coarse primitive skill condition as a lightweight signal for skill-aware adaptation. This condition is used only to retrieve learnable skill-query tokens and skill-dependent routing-bias vectors; it is not inserted into the language instruction as an additional text token and is not used as an action target. For CALVIN, st is assigned by matching each language instruction to benchmark-provided anno-tation variants using normalized lexical and fuzzy matching. The matched subtask is then grouped into a compact primitive skill taxonomy, which defines the indices used to retrieve the skill-query and routing-bias banks. This preprocessing is fixed across training and evaluation, providing a repro-ducible conditioning signal without modifying the original evaluation instructions. The conditioning interface itself is agnostic to how primitive labels are obtained. For broader task collections, the tax-onomy can be constructed by clustering instruction/task representations or by using an LLM to partition and label the instruction corpus, followed by an instruction-to-skill classifier that provides the same conditioning indices. A.3 Affordance Heatmap Head Architecture 

The affordance-heatmap branch is implemented as a lightweight geometric prediction head rather than as a separate detector. Its inputs are the multi-layer VLA hidden states used by the action head and the static-view point map P ∈ RH×W ×6, where each pixel stores world-coordinate xyz and normalized RGB. First, a learnable query performs attention pooling over the selected VLA hidden states to obtain a global manipulation context vector. When proprioception is enabled, the current proprioceptive state is projected into the same hidden dimension and added to this context. Second, the point map is processed by a small CNN consisting of a 3 × 3 input convolution, two dilated 3 × 3 convolution blocks, and a 1 × 1 output convolution. The pooled VLA context generates FiLM parameters that modulate the spatial feature map before the final heatmap logits are predicted. This design lets the heatmap head combine language-conditioned VLA context with metric scene geometry. The predicted affordance-heatmap logits are also used by the action head. We apply a sigmoid, resize the static-view heatmap to the patch grid, flatten it into a patch-level prior, and concatenate a neutral all-one prior for the wrist/secondary view. In the Pro action head, this prior is added as a soft attention bias for visual task tokens inside the residual cross-attention blocks. The bias increases the relative attention to interaction-relevant static-view patches while preserving access to the full visual context. During training, future gripper trajectories are used only to construct heatmap supervision. During inference, future trajectories are unavailable, and the action head uses the heatmap predicted from the current observation and instruction. 

## B Heatmap Construction and Training Details 

B.1 Trajectory-Affordance Target Construction 

The trajectory-affordance target is generated automatically from RGB-D observations and the future end-effector trajectory in the demonstration. In CALVIN, the static camera depth is dense after preprocessing, and we use the static RGB-D frame to construct a 200 × 200 xyz-rgb point map in the world frame. 16 For real-robot data, we apply the same geometric heatmap pipeline to calibrated RGB-D observa-tions from the static RealSense camera. The depth frame is resized to 200 × 200 and converted to metric depth; pixels are marked valid only if they are finite and above a small positive depth threshold. To provide a dense point-map input to the heatmap head, invalid depth holes are filled by iterative neighbor averaging, remaining holes fall back to the median valid depth, and inpainted pixels are locally smoothed. We retain the original validity mask throughout this process. Camera intrinsics are scaled to the resized depth image, and the calibrated eye-to-hand extrinsic transform maps OpenCV camera-frame points into the robot/world frame. The final real-robot point map stores world-coordinate xyz and RGB at each pixel; originally invalid or inpainted pixels are tracked by the validity mask and down-weighted when computing the heatmap loss. For each training sample, future end-effector positions are unnormalized from the ac-tion/proprioception statistics and compared with all visible surface points. C.2 Real-Robot Evaluation Protocol 

Real-robot experiments are conducted on a Franka Research 3 arm with a Franka gripper and two Intel RealSense D435i RGB-D cameras. The input stream follows the same two-view design as simulation: a static third-person view for global scene layout and a wrist/secondary view for local manipulation feedback. Camera intrinsics and extrinsics are calibrated before data collection so that static-view depth can be represented in the robot/world frame. Training and evaluation use the same two tabletop setups, object/task families, and primitive skill set, while object positions vary within approximately a 10 cm × 10 cm workspace region. Language commands are paraphrased across demonstrations and evaluation, and the physical experiments naturally include illumination variation and RGB-D sensing noise. No additional distractor objects are introduced in the reported evaluation. Eight length-3 primitive sequences are evaluated across two tabletop scenes, with 20 trials per se-quence for each method. During each episode, one primitive instruction is issued at a time. After the current primitive is completed, the scene is not reset and the next instruction is issued. After a failure or after all three subtasks are completed, the episode terminates and the scene is reset for the next trial. Human intervention is limited to resetting the scene between trials. A pick is successful if the object is lifted above the table and remains in the gripper for a short holding period. A place/store primitive is successful if the object is released inside the target region or container. An insert primitive is successful if the flower is inserted into the target vase/container and remains there after release. A push/move primitive is successful if the object reaches the target region or intended displacement. A stack primitive is successful if the upper object remains stably on the lower object after release. A nest primitive is successful if one disposable cup is inserted into the other and remains nested after release. ## D Additional Results and Failure Analysis 

D.1 Per-Sequence Real-Robot Results 

Across all 160 real-robot trials, MAST-VLA reaches 98.8% Success@1, 92.5% Success@2, 70.6% Success@3, and an Avg. Len of 2.62. Compared with OpenVLA-OFT, π0, π0.5, and VLA-Adapter-Pro, MAST-VLA improves Avg. Len by 0.77, 0.44, 0.21, and 0.60, respectively, indicating improved completion of full length-3 sequences under accumulated contact and pose errors. 

D.2 Failure Cases 

We observe three recurring failure modes. First, severe occlusion or noisy depth can produce incom-plete trajectory-affordance targets, especially when the target surface is hidden by the gripper or by another object. Second, some primitive boundaries are semantically close, such as placing versus pushing into a container, and ambiguous language can lead to less stable skill conditioning. Third, long-horizon real-robot execution can accumulate small pose errors; a grasp or placement that is ac-ceptable for the current primitive may leave the scene in a state that makes the next primitive harder.
