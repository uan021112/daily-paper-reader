# Robust LIDAR Sensor-to-Sensor Domain Generalization via Accumulated-Point Scene Completion 

Christoph B. Rist 1 , Larissa T. Triess , Markus Enzweiler 2 , Dariu M. Gavrila 1

Abstract — We study domain generalization for LIDAR seman-tic segmentation. Deep neural networks achieve strong perfor-mance on 3D point clouds, yet real-world LIDAR exhibits sensor-specific sampling patterns that hinder transfer across datasets. In many applications, performance on completely unseen sensors is crucial. A model should preferably work on a new setup without prior access to its data or labels. To this end, we introduce a source-only training scheme that adds an occupancy prediction auxiliary objective. We aggregate source-domain scans into a simple world model and use it as the supervision signal, replacing single-scan standard segmentation training. A key property of our approach is that it does not alter the input scan. During training and testing the network consumes the same single-frame LIDAR input, only the supervision signal is enriched via the occupancy objective. At deployment, there is no runtime overhead as the auxiliary head can be dropped for pure segmentation, or kept to obtain scene completion alongside semantic segmentation. In an experimental evaluation across distinct LIDAR datasets, we demonstrate that a model’s generalization to new domains can be significantly affected by how it is supervised. In certain setups, performance on unseen domains improves without any explicit domain adaptation (no target scans, no target labels, no sensor priors) and without prediction refinement. At the same time, our method maintains competitive source-domain segmentation performance and incurs no additional inference cost. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Highly automated vehicles and mobile robots rely on deep learning–based perception models that typically require large volumes of annotated data. Yet these systems must operate across diverse domains induced by weather, geography, traffic culture, and sensor generations. Since data collection and annotation is costly and time-consuming, repeating them for every new domain is impractical. Unsupervised domain adap-tation (UDA) targets this challenge with automated methods that adapt models to new domains without requiring man-ual annotations in the target domain. Going further, domain generalization (DG) seeks to build models using only source-domain data without any access to potential target domains (neither labels nor raw sensor data) and to maximize per-formance on these unseen targets at test time. Models that generalize well to unseen domains reduce retraining efforts when experimenting with new platforms and sensor setups. They accelerate deployment and iteration, and they improve robustness in safety critical systems. Generic, feature-based domain adaptation (DA) techniques struggle with LIDAR domain shifts dominated by sampling-pattern changes, motion distortion, and mounting geome-try [1]. Moreover, they require considerable en-gineering at training time and their gains can saturate when sensor discrepancies exceed what feature alignment or entropy minimization can bridge. We address LIDAR DG by exploiting geometric structure through an occupancy prediction pretext task during training. Crucially, unlike prior DG work that modifies the input scans (e.g., sensor sparsification, pattern simulation, or domain styl-ization), our study keeps the input LIDAR data unchanged and alters only the supervision signal. Domain shifts in LIDAR arise both from sensor-to-sensor 

(e.g., beam layout, rotation rate, range noise) and scene-to-scene changes (e.g., geography, traffic mix). Prior work ad-dresses these gaps through generic feature-learning approaches that do not rely on LIDAR specifics, and LIDAR-aware approaches that exploit 3D geometry or measurement physics. We briefly mention works that use entropy minimization as feature-learning technique used in unsupervised DA. While general feature learning is modality-agnostic and broadly ap-plicable, its effectiveness on LIDAR can be limited by sparsity, severe class imbalance, and sensor-induced sampling artifacts. This motivates the LIDAR-specific strategies reviewed next, which make use of geometric and surface completion, realistic re- and subsampling, and accumulation-based training. For multi-modal DA and DG we refer to the recent survey [14], and for a LIDAR-focused overview to [15]. 

A. Entropy Minimization 

Entropy minimization is a baseline strategy for UDA, en-couraging confident predictions by minimizing the model’s output entropy on unlabeled target data without exploiting sensor- or task-specific structure [16], [17], [18]. In dense prediction settings, entropy yields a straightforward objective on the per-point categorical distribution. Beyond pointwise entropy, aligning the structure of predictions proves beneficial. Adversarial training in the output space reduces distribution shift by matching target and source prediction layouts rather than only local entropy [16]. To prevent learning from be-ing dominated by confident target predictions, a maximum-squares loss reshapes the gradient landscape by suppress-ing gradients for well-classified samples while emphasizing misclassified ones [17]. From a statistical-alignment view, Minimal-Entropy-Correlation-Alignment shows that entropy minimization is induced by optimal alignment of second-order statistics between source and target. Using geodesic in-stead of Euclidean distances better respects the non-Euclidean curvature of these statistics on symmetric positive-definite manifolds [18]. 

B. Domain Adaptation 

A common pattern in UDA for images is to attach multi-modal auxiliary tasks that are easy to supervise on both domains, most prominently monocular depth or geometry signals used to guide features and alignment [19], [20], [21], [22]. The premise is that learning on complementary, weakly supervised targets produces representations that transfer better to the target domain. For LIDAR, a pre-training strategy alternating on heterogeneous datasets shows similar robustness benefits [23]. Several works exploit 3D geometry to obtain domain-invariant features by predicting surfaces, occupancy, or both [5], [6], [7], [8]. Complete & Label [5] first completes sparse voxels to a canonical surface representation and then segments it. Because the intermediate 3D proxy is indepen-dent of a sensor’s sampling pattern, the approach bridges 

sensor-to-sensor gaps and outperforms generic entropy-based DA. Follow-ups predict occupancy (and sometimes semantics) in the neighborhood of a query point and use such heads as pre-training to improve segmentation and detection with limited target labels [6], [7]. SALUDA [8] replaces explicit completion with an implicit surface head jointly trained with segmentation so that source and target share a common surface representation. This yields gains on real-to-real and sim-to-real benchmarks. More broadly, occupancy prediction has also been shown to be an effective proxy task for learning transferable 3D representations. SPOT [24] pre-trains LiDAR backbones via semantic occupancy prediction and beam re-sampling augmentation, yielding robust cross-dataset transfer across downstream detection and segmentation tasks under varying sensor configurations. Another line performs explicit sensor-to-sensor mapping by resampling the training data. This can operate on single scans or on temporally accumulated point clouds [2], [3], [4]. Data for the occupancy prediction is generated automatically on the source domain. of predicting a canonical surface as in [5], these methods produce semi-synthetic scans with target-like properties. To assess the fidelity of such artificial scans, Triess et al. propose a learned realism metric for LIDAR point clouds, showing that local point cloud realism correlates with downstream segmen-tation performance [25]. Surface-aware re-simulation can be computationally heavy when explicit recovery is needed [3], while z-buffer ray casting on accumulations is faster [4]. A second stage can align statistics between domains, for example by geodesic correlation alignment to generalize beyond a sin-gle sensor-to-sensor pair toward dataset-to-dataset shifts [18], [26]. Finally, mixing samples from different sensors and training a student with pseudo-labels from a teacher improves target performance without requiring target annotations [27]. 

C. Domain Generalization 

Kim et al. [2] propose DGLSS , which trains on a single source and simulates unseen sensors by random subsampling with the main objective to generalize from denser to sparser sensor domains. Two density-aware constraints align features between dense and sparsified views and stabilize class rela-tions. This label-free strategy increases robustness but requires a sufficiently dense source sensor. The inverse direction from sparse to dense sensor domains is harder to achieve with DGLSS: The augment baseline and the SCC-only variant out-perform the full method in this setting. S´ anchez et al. [9] ag-gregate multiple sweeps and propagate labels to accumulated points, improving robustness across sensors. Their method includes an explicit geometric association step that assigns labels to accumulated points using hand-designed rules. The resulting labeled points are then used both as supervision and as (subsampled) model input. In contrast, our approach introduces a free-space class directly into the accumulation process and trains jointly on occupied and free-space points, without modifying the input representation. Kim et al. [28] target generalization by explicitly learning across diverse densities within the source domain. A density-aware feature module and targeted density augmentation strengthen robustness of LIDAR segmentation. Ryu et al. [29] synthesize target-like frames from accumulated source clouds using detailed sensor models that account for ego-motion, spin distortions, and sampling patterns. This removes the need for target data but requires accurate sensor specifications to apply realistic ego-motion and spin distortions as well as sampling patterns. Zhao et al. [30] define a DG setting for synthetic-to-real indoor segmentation and improve robustness with point-level pattern augmentations and instance mixing. 

D. Enriched Supervision Signal 

All of the above DG methods operate exclusively on source data but alter the source-domain method inputs via subsam-pling, accumulation, mixing, or sensor-aware rendering to ex-pose the model to domain diversity without any target samples. There are notable works in image classification/segmentation showing that enriching the supervision signal alone can im-prove robustness. Incorporating self-supervised tasks (jigsaw puzzle [31], rotation prediction [32]), predicting depth along with labels in multi-task learning [33], or privileged simulation depth as an alternative label modality [34] have led to more domain-invariant features. These works demonstrate that a model’s generalization can be significantly affected by richer supervision, all while keeping the input data unchanged. III. P ROPOSED METHOD 

A. Overview 

We aim to train a LIDAR semantic segmentation network that generalizes to unseen sensor domains without altering the input scan at test time. To this end, we introduce an additional 

occupancy prediction pretext task during training, see Fig. 2. The point-cloud semantic segmentation problem is defined over a dataset D = {(Pi, y i, p i)}Ni=1 of LIDAR sweeps Pi,point-wise labels yi, and ego poses pi. The model predicts yi

from Pi.Using the ego-motion, we aggregate successive sweeps into a denser, static world frame and sample free-space points along each registered LIDAR ray between the sensor origin and the first return. This yields an enriched supervision signal with more labeled surface points via temporal accumulation, and additional free-space points that supervise scene completion. At test time on both source and unseen target domains, the occupancy branch is simply ignored and the network is used as a standard point-cloud semantic segmenter. 

B. Training Data 

We construct the occupancy supervision by accumulating LIDAR scans over time in a common world frame using the provided ego-motion. For nuScenes, we refine the provided poses with an ICP alignment over local scan windows before fusion, improving registration accuracy. We retain an individ-ual LIDAR point in the accumulation only if its distance to its own recording sensor origin is below 100 m . This cap filters far-range, typically noisier returns and reduces the resulting point density. To keep training efficient, we limit the number of retained points per voxel, which is particularly important near the ego vehicle where raw point density is highest. After aggregation, we place a 3D voxel grid with an edge length of 16 cm over the dense point cloud to thin out high-density regions. When downsampling within a voxel, we preferentially retain labeled points to maximize the density of semantic supervision. This higher density of labeled surface points and reduced overlap among points describing the same geometry improves training efficiency as each retained point carries more supervision signal. This procedure defines our canonical, training domain shared across datasets. Visually, the right column of Fig. 3 shows that the accumulated point clouds exhibit similar spatial structure across KITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo. To avoid painting motion trails into the fused world model, we explicitly remove scan points belonging to dynamic objects from all non-current frames. We identify dynamic objects via either a dedicated dynamic flag (KITTI) or via 3D annotated bounding boxes with temporal interpolation to the LIDAR timestamp ( nuScenes , Waymo). Concretely, each non-current sweep is time-aligned to the reference frame, its boxes are interpolated to the sweep time, and all points falling inside any dynamic box are excluded from accumulation. Only the dynamic-object points observed at the current ego frame are retained in the resulting training example, preserving instan-taneous geometry without leaving history traces in the static world model. During training, we generate free-space supervision by sampling points along registered LIDAR rays between the sensor and the first return, labeling them as non-occupied. The model thus learns semantic segmentation on a larger set of surface points from temporal fusion, and a scene-completion signal via one extra “free-space” class on sampled ray points. We use only raw 3D coordinates (no reflectivity), following [9], [28]. We do not sub-sample or augment the source-domain sweeps based on domain assumptions. Only raw 3D points are consumed by the pre-processing and the model. Table I lists the training split sizes (scenes and annotated points, in mil-lions) for KITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo, including pairwise and joint combinations. As expected, temporal accumulation increases the number of semantically labeled points compared to single sweeps. 

C. Model 

Our network (Fig. 2) takes a raw 3D point list, voxelizes it into point pillars, and processes them with a multi-resolution backbone [35]. Predictions are produced by a Coordinate MLP head that can be queried at arbitrary 3D locations [10]. This design makes it straightforward to train either only on the input LIDAR scan in a default semantic segmentation setup or on the accumulated surface points for semantic segmentation and sampled free-space points for occupancy, without changing the output head. In practice, we query the head at the accumulated LIDAR point coordinates and at free-space sample locations. At test time we simply drop the free-space output class, normalize probabilities for only the semantic classes and query only the LIDAR points. Alternatively, the accumulated-point training can be implemented on a voxel grid by pooling point labels into voxels and supervising a scene-completion grid. Conversely, standard backprojection (“unpooling”) used in (sparse)-voxel and BEV-conv architectures can map grid outputs back onto the original surface and free-space samples to provide an equivalent supervision signal. A. Existing Approaches and Label Sets 

We compare against three representative methods: C&L (Yi et al. [5]) is an unsupervised domain adaptation approach that leverages access to unlabeled target-domain data. DGLSS (Kim et al. [2]) is a domain generalization method that sim-ulates unseen sensors by subsampling the input scan. 3DLa-belProp (S´ anchez et al. [9]) also accumulates points, but uses them both as supervision and (subsampled) model input. Both [5] and [2] employ MinkowskiNet [36]-based architectures and deliver source-domain segmentation performance comparable to ours. We consider the widely used KITTI , nuScenes , and Waymo LIDAR segmentation datasets. All three are automotive datasets with large numbers of outdoor LIDAR scans captured in sequences and annotated with point-wise semantics. E. Discussion 

Our results indicate that enriching the supervision signal with accumulated-points (scene-completion) targets yields a domain-robustness benefit at almost no cost to source-domain accuracy. The observed source trade-off is negligible, indicat-ing that the added objective does not overfit or dilute features critical for in-domain segmentation. This points less to a model-capacity limitation and more to supervision shaping, meaning that completion targets encourage geometry- and boundary-aware features that generalize well across sampling patterns. Importantly, our method does not alter the input point cloud at any stage. It isolates the effect of richer supervision from the effect of input transformations seen in prior DG/DA work, clarifying that more informative outputs from the same inputs can improve feature robustness. The effectiveness nonetheless depends on the source–target pairing. Gains are pronounced for Waymo ↔ nuScenes, while transfers involving KITTI remain more difficult. We do not attribute this specifically to scan pattern or density as these factors likely differ sufficiently across all datasets. Instead, broader dataset-to-dataset domain gaps might be more in-fluential. nuScenes and Waymo are captured primarily in dense urban environments, whereas KITTI spans a mix of less densely populated areas, highways, and urban scenes. In particular, the KITTI validation split offers limited diversity, which may skew cross-domain results. Future work should verify this with targeted experiments on additional datasets. Differences in scene composition and geographic context, object frequency and scale, and the balance between static and dynamic classes can all reduce the utility of features learned in one domain when applied to another. Multi-source training further indicates that a simple data pooling is no solution to the domain generalization problem. Adding KITTI can narrow gaps for source-only baselines, and accumulated-points supervision generally improves per-formance but with modest gains. It might be necessary to address the domain factors in a more targeted way. Overall, requesting more outputs from the same network on unchanged scans is a simple, stable lever for domain generalization. It is effective when inter-domain semantics overlap sufficiently. Beyond that, richer geometric supervision on unchanged scans forces the network to learn mid-level, geometry-aware cues, which improves robustness to domain shift. This is aligned with prior image-domain results where adding geometric su-pervision forces models to learn more comprehensive repre-sentations and transferable high-level semantics. We observe the same effect for LIDAR. V. C ONCLUSION 

We approached LiDAR domain generalization by keeping the input scan unchanged while enriching supervision with a scene-completion task from accumulated points, and we evaluated this setup on combinations of KITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo. Our experiments show that switching to a richer supervision signal, while keeping the input scan unchanged, can considerably shift domain-generalization outcomes for LIDAR segmentation. To our knowledge, prior work has not isolated this effect without modifying the input itself. We observe strong dataset-pair dependence.
