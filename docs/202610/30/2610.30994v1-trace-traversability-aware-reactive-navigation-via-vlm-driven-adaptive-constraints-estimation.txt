Abstract: This paper presents TRACE, a traversability-aware reactive navigation framework
designed for the autonomous operation of ground robots in unstructured forest environments.
Unlike traditional approaches, TRACE leverages VLM-based confidence maps from positive and
negative natural-language prompt sets and combines them through evidential fusion to produce
a dense traversability map that prioritizes the terrain based on signs of previously traversed
paths. Traversability-annotated points are then extracted and used to derive the left and right
boundaries of the traversable path via percentile binning followed by spline or regularized
polynomial fitting. These boundaries are then incorporated as soft barrier constraints within an
NMPC formulation, enabling safe and adaptive path following in complex natural environments.
Rigorous experimental trials carried out in real-life forest environments demonstrate TRACE
ability for smooth navigation in cluttered, deformable, and visually ambiguous terrains.
Keywords: AI-powered Robotics, Autonomous Navigation, Field Robotics, Robot Perception
and Sensing, VLMs for Robotics and Control, Semantic Scene Understanding.
1. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous robotic navigation in forest environments
plays a crucial role in numerous domains, including pre-
cision forestry management, ecological monitoring, biodi-
versity assessment, and disaster response operations such
as search and rescue or wildfire inspection. Despite its
importance, reliable ground robot navigation in such en-
vironments remains a major challenge. Forest terrains are
inherently unstructured and highly variable, characterized
by uneven ground surfaces, loose soil, roots, dense un-
dergrowth, and natural obstacles like fallen logs, rocks,
and thick vegetation. A central difficulty lies in accu-
rately identifying traversable regions within this complex
environment. Traditional geometry-based methods, such
as those relying solely on point cloud elevation, slope,
or roughness, often struggle to interpret the rich visual
and semantic complexity of natural scenes. While semantic
segmentation methods often fail to capture the functional
traversability of the environment, that is, whether a region
is truly traversable, since appearance or semantics alone do
not always correspond to physical feasibility (Borges et al.

To overcome these limitations, a more human-inspired
approach to navigation is required. Humans navigating
through forests rarely rely solely on recognizing seman-
tic categories like “dirt” or “vegetation.” Instead, we in-

trails, clearings between trees, or subtle traces of previous
traversal visible on the ground. These cues encode valu-
able information about accessibility and safety, formed by
the natural flow of movement through the environment.
Vision–Language Models (VLMs) provide a powerful foun-
dation for this human-like perception by linking visual
understanding with high-level semantic and contextual
reasoning (Zhang et al. (2024a)). Unlike conventional seg-
mentation or geometric-based methods, VLMs are trained
on vast amounts of image–text pairs, enabling them to as-
sociate visual patterns, such as textures, shapes, and spa-
tial layouts, with abstract linguistic concepts like “trail,”
“open space,” or “obstacle.” For instance, a VLM can rec-
ognize subtle cues such as faint ground traces, vegetation
bending, or lighting gradients as indicators of a traversable
path, much like how humans intuitively interpret these
cues when moving through forests. By emulating this be-

In this work, we propose a navigation framework that
leverages VLMs to enable human-like reasoning for traver-

relying solely on predefined geometric thresholds or rigid

it to identify implicit trails, clearings, and accessible re-

model predictive control (NMPC), the approach enables

1.1 Contributions
The main contributions of this work are threefold. First,
we introduce TRACE, a traversability-aware reactive nav-
igation framework that unifies vision–language perception,
constraint estimation, and nonlinear model predictive con-
trol (NMPC) for autonomous navigation in unstructured
forest environments. The proposed approach leverages
Vision–Language Models (VLMs) with evidential fusion
on positive and negative natural-language prompt sets
to infer dense traversability maps, enabling a human-
like understanding of terrain affordances that transcends
explicit semantic or geometric cues. Second, we develop an
adaptive contour extraction pipeline based on percentile-
driven boundary detection and regularized polynomial fit-
ting, which converts traversability-annotated point clouds
into smooth, continuous representations of the navigable
corridor. These contours are integrated as soft barrier
constraints within the NMPC formulation to ensure safe,
dynamically feasible motion through complex natural en-
vironments. Third, we present an extensive experimental
validation of the framework in real-world forest environ-
ments in northern Sweden using a Husky mobile robot.
The results of the field test demonstrate TRACE’s ro-
bust capabilities to generalize across varying environments
while maintaining safe navigation in cluttered, deformable,
and visually ambiguous terrains.
2. RELATED WORK
Classical Traversability Analysis and Learning-
Based Approaches. Terrain traversability analysis has
been extensively addressed in the context of autonomous
ground vehicles operating in unstructured outdoor envi-
ronments (Borges et al. (2022)). Classical geometric-based
approaches primarily estimate traversability from terrain
morphology by evaluating slope, roughness, or discontinu-
ities derived from LiDAR or stereo data. Although compu-
tationally efficient, these methods often underperform in
vegetated or deformable terrains, where visual appearance
is not a reliable indicator of physical traversability. To
enhance robustness, multimodal perception systems have
been proposed, such as the fusion of LiDAR and camera
data for terrain mapping and the combination of geometric
and visual cues for terrain classification. The introduction
of semantic segmentation further improved environmen-
tal understanding by providing categorical terrain labels.
However, such models remain sensitive to illumination
changes, occlusions, and domain shifts between training
and deployment environments.
To address these generalization issues, learning-based
methods have been introduced. Palazzo et al. (2020) in-
troduced a domain adaptation framework with a safety-
preserving loss to maintain reliable traversability estima-
tion across unseen domains. Gasparino et al. (2022) pro-
posed a predictive learning model for dynamic traversabil-
ity estimation, while Guan et al. (2022b) achieved effi-
cient terrain segmentation in outdoor environments us-
ing lightweight neural architectures. Guan et al. (2022a)
presented the Terrain Navigability System (TNS) for au-
tonomous excavators, integrating mapping and control for
operation on deformable terrain. Saucedo et al. (2024)
later introduced Environment-Agnostic Traversability
(EAT), which emphasizes reactive navigation through
models capable of generalizing across diverse terrains.
Despite these advances, most classical and learning-based
approaches rely on explicit geometric or semantic labels,
limiting their ability to infer contextual or affordance-level
cues that are essential for reasoning about traversability.
VLM-based and Context-Aware Traversability. Re-
cent works have demonstrated the potential of VLMs
for context-aware navigation and affordance reasoning.


Sathyamoorthy et al. (2024) employed VLMs for context-
aware navigation in both indoor and outdoor environ-
ments. Elnoor et al. (2025) integrated physically grounded
vision–language representations for outdoor traversal rea-
soning. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2024b) demonstrated the
use of large language and vision–language models for inter-
active navigation among traversable obstacles. Song et al.
(2025) developed a trajectory generation and selection sys-
tem that leverages VLMs for semantic reasoning in map-
less outdoor navigation. More recently, Gummadi et al.
(2025) proposed a zero-shot traversability framework for
unknown environments, while Sahu et al. (2025) combined
VLM-based perception with human-in-the-loop supervi-
sion for enhanced adaptability in off-road conditions.
In contrast to prior approaches, this work explores
a human-inspired navigation framework that leverages
VLMs to infer traversability through implicit environ-
mental cues, such as loosely structured trails and surface
traces, enabling robust, context-aware navigation in forest
environments beyond the limits of purely geometric or
semantic methods.

5. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
In this section, we first compare the performance of our
method with state-of-the-art approaches and then present
rigorous experimental validations conducted in real for-
est environments. We use CLIPSeg (L¨uddecke and Ecker
(2022)) as the visual–language model, selected for its abil-
ity to provide zero-shot segmentation from text prompts
while maintaining real-time performance.
5.1 Performance Comparison
Traversability Estimation. The performance of the pro-
posed method for traversability scoring is first compared
against the baseline for self-supervised traversable path
estimation presented in Gasparino et al. (2022). To ensure
consistency and fairness with the data use during the
training process of the baseline, only the bottom half of
the images was considered during testing, corresponding
to the baseline’s lower field of view (FOV). The compari-
son was done using the GOOSE dataset (Mortimer et al.
(2024)), consisting of multiple sequences of unstructured
off-road environments under varying conditions, including
different seasons presenting dry vegetation and snow. Fig-
ure 2 demonstrates the proposed framework’s advantages
over the baseline method (Gasparino et al. (2022)), on
different challenging samples from the dataset. Specifically,
the proposed method yields sharper and more localized
traversability activations that accurately align with the
drivable regions, rather than exhibiting the diffuse re-
sponses observed in the baseline, resulting in improved
spatial precision and boundary awareness. Moreover, the
proposed approach exhibits enhanced robustness and in-
terpretability, providing more reliable and semantically
consistent confidence maps under varying environmental
conditions.
The advantages of the proposed method are further il-
lustrated when comparing the estimated traversability
scores against human-defined ground-truth labels. Since
the current state of the art does not contemplate human-
annotated datasets for path estimation in unstructured
environments, we have opted to instead use the ground-
truth labels for terrain classification based on navigability
levels provided in Guan et al. (2022b). Table 1 presents
the results for intersection-over-union (IoU) between the
traversability estimated with the proposed method and the
ground-truth labels provided in Guan et al. (2022b) for
the GOOSE dataset (Mortimer et al. (2024)). Where the
Stable class (i.e. the most optimal terrain) presents the
highest intersection-over-union, followed by the Granular
class (i.e. the second most optimal terrain), demonstrating
a high degree of consistency between the proposed method
and the ground-truth annotations.

5.2 Field Test
The experiments took place in a real-life forest environ-
ment in northern Sweden with a Husky mobile robot. The
objective of the experiments was for the robot to follow
the loosely defined forest roads and hiking trails present
in the environment. In the first experiment, showcased in
Fig. 5, the robot was made to navigate a forest road with
multiple sections of tall grass and overgrown vegetation,
which limited the visibility of the tire tracks left by pre-
viously transiting vehicles. On the other hand, the second
experiment depicted in Fig. 6 took place in a hiking trail
consisting of narrow passages across rocks and vegetation,
presenting multiple slopes and meandering across the way.
Both figures present at the top a visualization of the
estimated traversability map, alongside a top-view of the
corresponding color map, while at the bottom, images of
the estimated traversability scores at different challenging
scenes can be found. Overall, these experiments demon-
strate the ability of the proposed framework to imitate
human-like behaviors while traversing unstructured forest
environments by tracing the loosely defined signs of previ-
ously traversed paths.

igation framework that integrates Vision–Language per-
