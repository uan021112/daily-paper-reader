LucidLyra: Feed-Forward Generative 3D Scene Reconstruction and Joint Understanding via Multi-Task Distillation
Abstract
We present LucidLyra, a framework that makes generatively reconstructed 3D scenes directly queryable
for semantics and instances in a feed-forward manner from video latents. While generative 3D recon-
struction can synthesize virtual environments from a single image, downstream applications in robotics
and physical AI require not only visual realism but also access to scene semantics and object identities.
Existing 3D understanding approaches, however, require captured multi-view scenes, have limited
generalization capabilities, or rely on expensive per-scene processing and optimization. To bridge this
gap, our method distills complementary knowledge from a camera-controlled video diffusion model
and frozen 2D vision foundation models into a unified model to jointly infer 3D Gaussians encoding
geometry, appearance, open-vocabulary semantics, and class-agnostic instance cues. The resulting scenes
support novel-view rendering, open-vocabulary segmentation, and object grouping without per-scene
optimization, while training requires neither manual 3D annotations nor captured multi-view data.
Our method outperforms baselines combining state-of-the-art 3DGS segmentation approaches with
generative 3D scene reconstruction on curated synthetic and real-image-conditioned benchmarks, while
preserving reconstruction quality. Furthermore, our latent formulation naturally extends to monocular
video, reconstructing structured dynamic 3D scenes across time. By turning renderable but unstructured
outputs into queryable 3D worlds, LucidLyra enables downstream applications including 3D scene
editing, 3D asset mining, and robotic simulation.
1. Introduction
Recent generative reconstruction methods can turn a single image into a renderable 3D environment. Building
upon learned generative models, they can imagine content beyond the observed field of view and consolidate it
into a scene that can be viewed from new perspectives [2, 14, 52, 72, 76, 85]. Generated 3D environments are
promising for scalable content generation in interactive media, physical AI simulation, and industrial applications
in engineering and manufacturing [16]. However, existing generated scene representations primarily capture
geometry and appearance, while lacking an explicit understanding of their content. Downstream applications
in embodied AI and physical simulation cannot interact with an collection of colored primitives; they require
structured scene elements with semantic roles and discrete instance boundaries to support collision reasoning,
physical manipulation, and interactive scene editing.
A natural solution might seem to generate an appearance-only scene first and recover semantic and instance
representations post-hoc. However, this two-stage route breaks down in the single-view generative setting.
Existing post-hoc methods either attach 2D foundation-model predictions to an existing reconstruction through
per-scene optimization [67, 87, 100, 105], or jointly infer geometry and understanding from multiple captured
views [26, 88, 117]. The former requires expensive scene-specific foundation model preprocessing and
optimization. The latter relies on captured multi-view inputs and is constrained by the limited scale and
domain diversity of multi-view training data. Neither route directly yields a structured 3D scene that has been
generated from a single image.
Scaling these capabilities is not merely a matter of attaching a feature head to a reconstruction model. Generated
regions lack paired ground-truth supervision, so estimated geometry, semantics, and object boundaries can
drift and disagree across views. Predictions from 2D vision models compound this, as their semantics and
masks are not guaranteed to be view-consistent. The central challenge is to turn complementary but imperfect
generative and perceptual signals into a consistent 3D representation while balancing geometry, appearance,
semantics, and instances without compromising generation quality. An integrated model should let semantic
priors and geometry reinforce each other, rather than tagging an isolated reconstruction with semantic or
object information.

LucidLyra: Feed-Forward Generative 3D Scene Reconstruction and Joint Understanding via Multi-Task Distillation
We introduce LucidLyra, a framework that unifies feed-forward generative 3D reconstruction and scene
understanding in an explicit Gaussian representation (Fig. 1). Our central insight is that a video generator
provides a unified foundation for structured 3D scene generation, serving both as the shared backbone and
as the source of training data. Building on Lyra’s latent-to-Gaussian formulation [2], LucidLyra learns a 3D
scene representation from camera-controlled video latents through multi-task distillation. Given a single input
image, we denoise the corresponding video latents across camera trajectories, and process them through two
parallel branches. The teacher branch decodes the latent into an RGB video and applies frozen vision-language,
segmentation, and tracking foundation models to obtain semantic and instance supervision. The student
branch incorporates vision cues of the input view into the video latents and directly maps the shared latent
into a 3D Gaussian scene. We consolidate the teacher predictions across the generated views and distill them
into persistent semantic and instance fields on the Gaussian primitives, jointly with geometry and appearance.
A single shared representation can therefore support novel-view rendering, open-vocabulary segmentation,
and object grouping. As an initial exploration toward generative 4D scene understanding, we also extend the
formulation to monocular video inputs and predict semantics- and instance-aware dynamic 3D scenes. For this
novel task, we introduce an evaluation protocol with benchmarks covering static scenes, real-image domain
transfer, and dynamic scenes.
On our static LucidLyra-SDG benchmark, LucidLyra improves semantic mIoU and class-agnostic instance
mTrackPR over the strongest comparable baseline by 31.7 and 9.8 percentage points, while maintaining
reconstruction fidelity, with a modest PSNR gain of 0.53 dB over Lyra. More broadly, this formulation turns
pretrained video generation into a scalable source of supervision for structured 3D learning, opening a path
from renderable but unstructured outputs to queryable 3D worlds that downstream systems can reason about
and interact with. In summary, our contributions are:
• We introduce a unified generative 3D model that produces an explicit Gaussian scene representation
with geometry, appearance, open-vocabulary semantic, and class-agnostic instance features from a single
image in a feed-forward fashion.
• We develop a multi-task distillation strategy that transfers 2D generative, semantic, and object-level
knowledge through aligned generated views into a consistent 3D representation. This enables task-specific
training without captured multi-view data or manual 3D annotations, and avoids per-scene optimization
at inference time.
• We jointly evaluate reconstruction and understanding on static and dynamic scenes. LucidLyra consis-
tently outperforms two-stage baselines across optimization-based, mask-lifting, and feed-forward 3DGS
segmentation methods, while maintaining novel-view reconstruction fidelity on static scenes with a small
reconstruction trade-off on dynamic scenes. We further demonstrate downstream applications including
robotic simulation, 3D scene editing, and 3D asset mining.
2. Related Work
From observed reconstruction to generative scene construction. Neural radiance fields [58] and 3D Gaussian
splatting (3DGS) [28] fit per-scene representations, while feed-forward methods amortize reconstruction
across scenes to predict 3D Gaussians for novel-view synthesis [6, 110] or estimate dense depth and camera
parameters [4, 54, 93]. Dynamic formulations underwent a similar evolution from per-sequence fitting [103,
104] to feed-forward estimation [32, 53, 101], yet all remain bounded by observed multi-view coverage.
Generative methods relax this constraint by synthesizing unobserved content. Early works distill diffusion
priors into per-scene optimizations or iterative view inpaintings [19, 64]. Multi-view generators synthesize
dense video trajectories for downstream reconstruction [14, 74], with later extensions incorporating panoramic
scaffolds, geometric alignment, or long-horizon expansion [18, 54, 79, 89, 92, 94]. However, generation and
3D reconstruction remain decoupled into different stages.

A closer line to our setting couples generation directly with feed-forward 3D prediction. Recent methods decode
video latents into Gaussians [52], embed Gaussian feature fields within video diffusion [76], directly generate
explicit 3D representations [77, 85], or align generative image, video, and 3D latent spaces [15, 21, 47]. Lyra [2]
self-distills a feed-forward Gaussian decoder from a camera-controlled video foundation model [59, 72], using
synthetic multi-view video trajectories as both the latent input and the supervision target for static and dynamic
3D synthesis. We build upon Lyra’s scalable, latent-to-Gaussian formulation, extending its feed-forward output
from raw geometry and appearance to joint language-grounded semantics and class-agnostic instances.
From per-scene optimization to amortized 3D scene understanding. Early works predict semantics and
instances from point clouds [9, 25, 65, 66], while subsequent methods lift open-vocabulary 2D foundation
features onto reconstructed geometry [23, 63, 86] or embed language fields into neural representations [29,
36, 81, 113]. For 3DGS, several approaches optimize per-Gaussian language fields, aggregate 2D foundation
features, or lift multi-view masks into object-aware representations [7, 31, 45, 67, 78, 87, 98, 99, 100, 105,
116]. Feed-forward methods amortize understanding across scenes, either by encoding pre-existing 3DGS
reconstructions [50] or by jointly predicting geometry alongside semantic, panoptic, or instance structure
from multi-view inputs [26, 35, 38, 40, 42, 43, 49, 56, 80, 84, 88, 107, 111, 117]. Generative understanding
approaches synthesize observations for language-aware reconstruction, modular scene assembly, or driving-
specific world models [11, 22, 55, 106]. Yet, these formulations remain confined to domain-specific priors or
assume multiple posed observations.
Existing approaches follow two main paradigms: augmenting a scene-specific reconstruction or jointly infer-
ring geometry and segmentation from multi-view inputs. The former requires a pre-existing reconstruction,
precluding single-view generation, while post-hoc lifting decouples semantics from geometry, leaving fusion
vulnerable to cross-view inconsistencies. Crucially, both rely on an expensive preprocessing stage with a 2D
vision foundation model. Feed-forward approaches jointly predict geometry and segmentation, but train
on captured multi-view datasets with narrow domain coverage limiting generalization. LucidLyra instead
unifies scene understanding with generative reconstruction. Conditioned on a single image, its amortized
latent-to-Gaussian decoder jointly predicts geometry, appearance, language-aligned semantic features, and
class-agnostic instance features in a single feed-forward pass, without prior 3D reconstructions, per-scene
optimization, or post-hoc lifting. Distilling frozen foundation models on generated video eliminates the need
for captured multi-view data or manual 3D annotations.
3. Semantic- and Instance-Aware Generative 3D Reconstruction
Given an input image I0, LucidLyra generates a queryable 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [28] scene represen-
tation whose primitives jointly encode geometry, appearance, language-aligned semantics, and class-agnostic
instance identity. Rather than supervising reconstruction alone as in Lyra [2], we use the multi-view outputs of
a camera-controlled video generator to construct aligned 2D semantic and instance targets using frozen vision
foundation models and distill them into an explicit 3D representation via an amortized feed-forward decoder
(Fig. 2).

temporal instance tracking via an online prototype codebook inspired by IGGT4D [118]. At each query time,
we represent each cluster by its normalized mean object embedding and greedily match cluster prototypes
to codebook entries one-to-one above a cosine-similarity threshold. Matched clusters inherit persistent IDs
and update the stored prototypes through averaging weighted by Gaussian count, while unmatched clusters
initialize new IDs.

4. Experiments
We evaluate LucidLyra’s joint generative 3D reconstruction and scene understanding on synthetic and real-
image-conditioned scenes, followed by an analysis of its key components. Dynamic experiments, additional
quantitative and qualitative results, downstream applications, and implementation details are provided in the
appendix.
4.1. Experimental Setup
Datasets. We augment Lyra-SDG [2] with semantic and instance teacher targets for training (Section 3.3)
and annotations for evaluation. Lyra-SDG uses GEN3C [72] to expand images and videos generated from
diverse text prompts into multiple camera trajectories per scene. Its 59 031 static and 7 378 dynamic scenes
contain RGB videos, camera poses, depth maps, and video latents. We preprocess teacher targets for all 53
million frames. A single image admits multiple plausible 3D completions. We evaluate agreement with fixed
generated trajectories and their annotations. Our three curated 25-scene benchmarks use propagated reference
annotations with human review and refinement: LucidLyra-SDG and LucidLyra-SDG Dynamic cover static and
dynamic generated scenes, while LucidLyra-ADE20K uses real ADE20K validation images [114] to condition
generated completions. All data and benchmarks will be released.
For qualitative results, we generate input images with GPT Image or FLUX [3, 62]. For dynamic examples,
we extend these to videos using Cosmos 3 [61]. We find that our method is agnostic to the choice of image-
generation model.
Evaluation. Following Lyra [2], we report PSNR and LPIPS [112] for reconstruction. Following Li et al.
[42, 45, 50], Xiong et al. [100], we evaluate semantic segmentation using mIoU and mAcc over ADE20K classes
present in each reference scene. Building on IGGT and video instance segmentation [42, 102], we evaluate
cross-view instance consistency using the mean track precision–recall product (mTrackPR) and TrackPR25 at
IoU 0.25 within ground-truth thing regions (Appendix A.3).
Baselines. We benchmark against existing 3D understanding methods combined with Lyra’s generative pipeline.
Per-scene optimization: LangSplatV2, SFS, and Unified-Lift [45, 100, 116] fit feature fields on frozen Lyra
Gaussians. Feed-forward: Chorus [50] processes frozen Lyra Gaussians, while IGGT [42] reconstructs geometry
and instances directly from generated RGB videos. References: Reconstruction-only Lyra, alongside a teacher-
lifting reference that directly assigns teacher features and instance IDs to frozen Lyra ray-aligned-Gaussians.
The latter contextualizes the quality of our segmentation training signals.
4.2. Evaluating Generative 3D Reconstruction and Understanding
LucidLyra preserves reconstruction fidelity while adding understanding fields (Table 1). Relative to Lyra,
PSNR improves by 0.53/0.13 dB and LPIPS improves by 0.025/0.018 across benchmarks, confirming that our
multi-task training does not degrade novel-view rendering quality.
LucidLyra outperforms all two-stage baselines without per-scene optimization (Table 1) in semantics, achieving
64.1/49.8 mIoU with synthetic and real-image conditioning, respectively. Plain LucidLyra exceeds the 3D-lifted
teacher reference on SDG (64.1 vs. 60.5) and performs comparably on ADE20K (49.8 vs. 50.1). LucidLyra+
input-image 3D anchoring further boosts mIoU to 67.9/53.7.
LucidLyra achieves 21.5/18.5 instance mTrackPR (Table 1), outperforming the compared segmentation base-
lines in separating individual objects across generated viewpoints. Compared to the lifted teacher reference,
mTrackPR is lower on SDG (21.5 vs. 22.8) and higher on ADE20K (18.5 vs. 17.3). LucidLyra+ gives a small
SDG mTrackPR gain. Qualitative multi-view segmentations and feature PCAs are visualized in Fig. 3.

4.4. Downstream Applications
LucidLyra’s structured, queryable scenes directly enable downstream tasks including robotic simulation, 3D
scene editing, and 3D asset mining (details and qualitative results in Appendix D).
5. Conclusion
We presented LucidLyra, which turns generated scenes into queryable 3D worlds by distilling generative and
perceptual knowledge into a feed-forward Gaussian decoder. A camera-controlled video model supplies both
latent representations and training data, enabling joint reconstruction and understanding without captured
multi-view data, manual 3D annotations, or per-scene optimization. Experiments demonstrate improved seman-
tic and instance understanding over the compared two-stage baselines while preserving Lyra’s reconstruction
quality. Furthermore, we demonstrate initial results on dynamic scenes and applications in 3D scene editing,
3D asset mining, and physical-AI simulation. Future work could scale our approach by accelerated video
generation [48] with larger-scale joint training for generation and scene understanding [13].

A. Implementation Details
We report the hyperparameters and implementation choices that determine teacher-target construction, projec-
tor pretraining, LucidLyra training, and inference.
A.1. Teacher Targets and Feature Compression
Instance supervision. We use SAM1 ViT-H [34] to initialize SAM2.1 Hiera-Base+ [71] on the shared condi-
tioning frame 0, followed by independent propagation through each 121-frame trajectory. We use the SAM
automatic mask generator with 32 points per side, predicted-IoU and stability thresholds of 0.84 and 0.86, one
crop layer, a crop-grid downscaling factor of two, box and crop NMS thresholds of 0.7, and a minimum mask
area of 100 pixels. We do not threshold SAM2 logits and merge redundant or nested tracks at a mask-coverage
threshold of 0.5. Part-level targets retain SAM multimask candidates 0 and 1 and use the same propagation
and consolidation procedure.
Semantic supervision. We build upon RADSeg [1] and C-RADIOv3-B [17] with its 1 152-dimensional
SigLIP2 [90] adapter, a crop size of 336, stride 224, and SCRA and SCGA scales of 10. RADSeg class prediction
and SAM refinement are not used. We project the dense tokens to 128 dimensions, normalize them, and resize
them bilinearly. We pool all pixels and frames belonging to a track, which yields one pixel-weighted semantic
centroid per track and trajectory. This is performed in the object-level instance tracks as well as the part-level
tracks.
Constructing semantic and instance targets for the full corpus required an aggregate of 1 580 NVIDIA A100
80 GB GPU-days offline. This is a one-time training-data preprocessing cost. We will make the preprocessed
data available.
Vision-language feature dimensionality reduction.
𝑃VL sums a 1 152→128 linear projection and
a 1 152→4 608→128 two-layer ReLU MLP. Image and text outputs share the same projection and are
ℓ2-normalized afterward. We train the projector with frozen SigLIP2 SO400M/16 NaFlex features and the
SigLIP alignment objective [109] on filtered LAION-2B [75]. During training, we use a global batch size of
16 384 across eight GPUs, AdamW [57] optimizer with learning rate 1 × 10−3, 𝛽= (0.9, 0.999), 𝜖= 1 × 10−8,
matrix-only weight decay 0.2, 10 000 warm-up steps followed by cosine decay, and gradient clipping at 1. The
SigLIP2 logit bias remains frozen, its scale is capped at 100, and images use the native processor without
additional augmentation. We train for 32 epochs and keep the projection network frozen afterward. Training
takes 2 days using 8 NVIDIA A100 80 GB GPUs.

each. We initialize the C-RADIO injection gate to 0.05, and its learnable latent-time weights as 𝑤ℓ= (1−ℓ/15)2.

B. Additional Results
B.1. Dynamic Reconstruction and Understanding
Next, we demonstrate that our method can be extended to dynamic 3D scene generation from a monocular input
video. Table 4 compares LucidLyra queried-time scene states with dynamic reconstruction and understanding
baselines [2, 46, 51, 118] in the LucidLyra-SDG Dynamic evaluation dataset.
Semantic and instance understanding. LucidLyra achieves 53.2 mIoU and 69.3 mAcc; LucidLyra+ increases
these to 62.6 and 79.3, respectively. Their mTrackPR scores are 11.7 and 12.3, with TrackPR25 scores of 19.7
and 20.4.
Reconstruction. LucidLyra obtains 21.88 dB PSNR and 0.252 LPIPS [112], compared with Lyra’s 22.16 dB
and 0.248. This represents a minimal reconstruction trade-off for dynamic scene understanding.
Our dynamic extension represents an initial step toward structured 4D scene generation and understanding,
with encouraging results that motivate further development in this direction.
B.2. Compact Vision-Language Representation
Since the semantic feature dimension directly affects the cost of representing each Gaussian, we train a
projection network inferring a lower-dimensional compressed version of the SigLIP2 [90] latent space. Next,
we evaluate the projector independently of LucidLyra on two tasks: zero-shot ImageNet-1K [73] classification
using SigLIP2 SO400M/16 NaFlex, and RADSeg [1] segmentation on the 2 000-image ADE20K [114] validation
set. In both tasks, we project image and text descriptors into the same compact space. The segmentation
analysis uses three inference resolutions following Alama et al. [1].
Table 5 shows the accuracy–capacity trade-off. The 256-dimensional projector reduces descriptor width by
4.5×, with a 2.81-point decrease in ImageNet top-1 accuracy and a 1.79-point decrease in ADE20K mIoU

We show static and dynamic examples, compare baseline predictions, and examine object- and part-level
instance fields. We further provide qualitative video results on out project website.
Figure 4 shows qualitative LucidLyra scene generation and understanding across diverse generated examples.
The qualitative results in Figs. 4 and 5 show crisp reconstructions and coherent open-vocabulary and instance
segmentation across diverse scenes and queried categories, while the feature PCA visualizations suggest clear
separation of semantic concepts and object instances in the learned representation. Figure 5 shows dynamic
qualitative LucidLyra results from generated inputs. Dynamic LucidLyra is able to reconstruct the motion and
provides temporally consistent open-vocabulary and instance segmentation. Figure 6 shows qualitative transfer
to driving scenes conditioned on real images from the Waymo Open Dataset [83]. LucidLyra generalizes well
to the real-image conditioned driving scenes.
C.1. Qualitative Baseline Comparison
Figures 7 and 8 compare semantic and instance predictions across both static benchmarks.
Open-vocabulary semantics. LangSplatV2 [45] and SFS [100] recover broad semantic structure but exhibit
class confusion and label leakage across object boundaries, with LangSplatV2 additionally showing flickering
artifacts as the viewpoint changes. The feed-forward baselines Chorus [50] and IGGT [42] exhibit incomplete
category coverage and noisy boundaries, respectively. We hypothesize that their reduced performance partly
reflects a domain shift from real-world and synthetic 3D training data to our generatively reconstructed scenes.
LucidLyra preserves coherent semantic regions across viewpoints, approaching the lifted-teacher reference
while correcting some of its assignment errors. LucidLyra+ further refines semantic assignments without
changing geometry or appearance.
Instance segmentation. Unified-Lift [116] produces coherent instance regions but exhibits local label frag-
mentation and bleeding across object boundaries. IGGT shows blurred instance boundaries and a tendency
to over-segment individual objects. Its reconstruction appears sensitive to cross-view inconsistencies in the
generated videos, producing duplicated fine-scale structures that further blur segmentation predictions. Our
methods preserve more coherent object-level partitions across viewpoints, while the lifted-teacher reference
remains competitive. Since instance clustering is conditioned on predicted semantic classes, LucidLyra+ can
modify instance partitions without changing the learned instance embeddings.
Dynamic scenes. In the dynamic setting, LucidLyra better separates foreground objects from background
surfaces and preserves finer semantic regions than 4D LangSplat [46] and IGGT4D [118] (Fig. 9). The dynamic
baselines require different scene representations and cannot be directly applied to our independently decoded
3D Gaussian states. We therefore evaluate their respective reconstructions and report reconstruction and
understanding metrics separately (Table 4).
For instance segmentation, LucidLyra maintains coherent foreground masks across queried times, with fewer
local gaps and cleaner boundaries than IGGT4D. TRASE [51] also recovers coherent foreground regions but
produces coarser background partitions. The lifted-teacher reference remains competitive, particularly in
separating smaller objects, while our method preserves complete foreground regions with less fragmentation.

D. Downstream Applications
LucidLyra generates structured, queryable scenes that support diverse downstream applications. In the following
section, we demonstrate this capability through examples of robotic simulation, 3D scene editing, and 3D asset
mining. See our project website for video demos.
D.1. Robot Simulation
We extract a mesh from the LucidLyra depth maps and camera parameters by computing a signed distance
field on a hierarchy of sparse grids. We use the generated scene, extracted mesh and the semantic labels to
set up a simulation in NVIDIA IsaacSim [60]. For navigation, the semantic labels partition the scene into free
space and obstacles, over which we build an occupancy grid and plan a route to a semantically specified goal
using a clearance-weighted Dijkstra search. A robot (Unitree G1 humanoid) then follows this route with a
pure-pursuit controller while a learned rough-terrain locomotion policy drives its joints under full rigid-body
physics. The humanoid also carries a box rigidly attached near its torso to demonstrate loco-manipulation. For
manipulation, the LucidLyra semantic labeling localizes a kitchen counter and sink in a separate reconstruction,
from which we derive a mount pose and reach-constrained pick-and-place targets for a stationary Franka
robotic arm. The arm is simulated with full articulated-body dynamics and a friction-based parallel-jaw grasp
under differential inverse-kinematics control, picks a small synthetic object (a sphere) from the sink and places
it on the counter. The extracted mesh serves as the collision proxy during simulation, while the original
Gaussian-splat reconstruction [28] is rendered for visual fidelity. Figure 11 visualizes the simulation examples.
D.2. 3D Scene Editing
We implement a simple browser-based editor. The user can select objects through text queries or clicks. Text
selection intersects the predicted semantic class with an instance ID, thereby separating objects of the same
category, while click selection retrieves the semantic–instance pair of the Gaussian center nearest the camera
ray. Users can further refine the selection using separate similarity thresholds for Gaussian semantic and
instance features relative to prototypes computed from the initial support.
Translation, rotation, scaling, duplication, and deletion operate directly on the selected Gaussian parameters.
Transformations are applied around the selection centroid and update positions, anisotropic scales, and
orientation quaternions, while color, opacity, and learned features remain attached to each primitive. Duplication
appends a transformed copy, whereas deletion removes the selected primitives. Moving or deleting an object
can expose unobserved regions. After the user commits all edits, OmniPaint [108] inpaints the original object
support, and a subsequent LucidLyra forward pass reconstructs a clean background scene. We retain only
reconstructed Gaussians near the edit whose projections fall within the exposed source support, excluding
the transformed object’s footprint. Opacity tapering converts these Gaussians into a smoothly blended local
background patch. Figure 12 visualizes our GUI as well as editing examples.

D.3. 3D Asset Mining
Much like in 3D scene editing, we can make use of the generated appearance from multiple views, together
with the instance and semantic segmentation masks, to mine high-quality 3D assets from the generated scenes.
For each selected object, we extract six segmented views, prioritizing viewpoint diversity and object coverage
without occlusion. The segmented RGB observations together with the corresponding camera parameters are
provided to the pretrained Pixal3D [41] multi-view reconstruction model to produce a textured mesh. Figure 13a
presents assets extracted from LucidLyra scenes. Comparisons in Fig. 13b show that the incorporation of
multi-view observations can substantially improve structural consistency compared to single-view reconstruction
without LucidLyra.
Furthermore, the generated meshes can also be post-processed by mesh-to-articulation pipelines. In Fig. 13c,
we employ PARTICULATE [44] to estimate moving parts and the corresponding articulation kinematic tree.

E. Datasets and Annotations
We augment the PhysicalAI-SpatialIntelligence-Lyra-SDG [2] with semantic and instance targets. For evaluation,
we introduce three benchmarks based on PhysicalAI-SpatialIntelligence-Lyra-SDG and ADE20K [114].
E.1. Static Datasets
Training data. The static Lyra-SDG corpus comprises 59 031 image-conditioned scenes spanning highly diverse
indoor and outdoor environments, people, animals, and various imaginative settings. Each scene provides
six generated camera trajectories of 121 frames at 704×1 280, together with depth, camera parameters, and
video latents. Figure 14 visualizes random training data samples.
Evaluation data. LucidLyra-SDG contains 25 manually curated diverse scenes held out from the training
data. To obtain high-quality reference annotations, we use an ensembled version of our SAM [34]/SAM2 [71]
instance preprocessing, combined with human review, refinement, and assignment of ADE20K semantic labels.
The resulting labels and persistent identities provide references across all six generated trajectories. Figure 15
shows annotated examples.
LucidLyra-ADE20K uses 25 diverse ADE20K validation images [114], selected for scene-category diversity.
Gen3C [72] expands each image into six 121-frame videos. We use a SAM2 ensemble to propagate the original
semantic and instance ground-truth labels through all camera trajectories, followed by human review and
refinement. Figure 16 shows example scenes.
E.2. Dynamic Dataset
Training data. The dynamic Lyra-SDG corpus comprises 7 378 video-conditioned scenes. Generation from
the original and temporally reversed input video provides two complementary six-trajectory batches, each
containing 121 synchronized frames per trajectory. Targets are constructed within each batch, keeping their
independently initialized track IDs separate. Figure 17 illustrates training scenes across viewpoints and time.
Evaluation data. LucidLyra-SDG Dynamic contains 25 diverse curated scenes excluded from the training set.
Annotation follows the static setup. Figure 18 visualizes annotated examples.
F. Related Work
Below, we discuss further related work not covered in the main paper.
Dynamic representations
Dynamic semantic and instance representations have likewise focused on captured video, spanning per-sequence
optimization and feed-forward reconstruction. 4D LangSplat [46] fits language features to deformable 4D
Gaussians, whereas TRASE [51] learns an instance field from per-frame masks. Related methods distill
foundation-model features or temporal cues into scene-specific dynamic representations [12, 20, 24, 27,
37, 96, 115]. Feed-forward approaches amortize language-aligned dynamic reconstruction [68, 97], while
IGGT4D [118] jointly predicts streaming geometry and persistent instances.
