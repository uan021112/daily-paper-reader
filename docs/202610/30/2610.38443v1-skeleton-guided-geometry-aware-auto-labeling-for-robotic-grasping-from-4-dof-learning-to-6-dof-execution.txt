Skeleton-Guided Geometry-Aware Auto-Labeling for Robotic
Grasping: From 4-DoF Learning to 6-DoF Execution
Ali Sabzejou
∗1, Farbod Azimmohseni
∗1, Mehdi Tale Masouleh1, Ahmad Kalhor1
Abstract
Robotic grasping relies on large manually annotated datasets, which are costly to create. This work introduces a novel
algorithm for generating two-finger grasp keypoints based on straight skeletons, identifying high-potential regions for
stable and feasible grasp pose estimation. An automated pipeline is developed to perform grasp auto-labeling without
human intervention, facilitating large-scale annotation with consistent quality. Furthermore, an architecture for 4-DoF
grasping, named the Skeleton-based Generative Grasp Convolutional Neural Network (SkelGG-CNN), is introduced,
which incorporates skeleton-based guidance during training to directly learn geometric grasping features. Finally, the
developed auto-labeling is extended to 6-DoF grasping and applied directly to point clouds via a generalized quasi-
3D skeletonization algorithm. Evaluations demonstrate that SkelGG-CNN achieves accuracies of 95.42%, 96.55%,
and 97.1% on the Jacquard V1, Jacquard V2, and Cornell benchmarks, respectively, using 4-DoF auto-generated
labels. On GraspNet, the 6-DoF auto-labeling algorithm yields an average precision of 18.33 on the novel split,
comparable to state-of-the-art model-based approaches. In simulation, the generalized 6-DoF auto-labeling method
yields success rates of 98.4% on the Dex-Net test set and 89.1% on EGAD!. Real-world experiments on a Delta
parallel robot confirm 97.4% success on single household objects and 95.0% in cluttered scenes, outperforming state-
of-the-art methods without domain adaptation. Experiments show that the framework’s 4-DoF pipeline is adaptable
across environments, generating grasp annotations that approximate human-level precision, enabling efficient training
of lightweight models like SkelGG-CNN on small datasets for real-world deployment. Additionally, the generalized 6-
DoF auto-labeling algorithm extends this automation to point clouds, providing scalable, geometry-aware annotations
for cluttered 3D scenes. Source code and videos are available at https://github.com/Farbod82/SkelGGCNN.

Introduction
Robotic manipulation has been a significant area of
research, with the task of grasping objects emerging as
one of the most widely studied topics in recent decades.
Although grasping appears simple to humans, it remains a
difficult task for robots, and various approaches have been
explored to address it. The difficulty lies in determining
appropriate grasp poses for a diverse range of objects,
each differing in geometry, gripping force requirements,
and weight distribution. These factors significantly influence
how robotic systems effectively grasp objects from specific
configurations, making the task highly complex and context-
dependent. In early studies, analytical methods were the
primary tools used to determine grasp poses, focusing on
geometric approaches to analyze the structure and properties
of objects. These methods typically relied on mathematical
models and physical principles, such as force closure and
friction modeling, to compute stable and reliable grasp
configurations Bohg et al. (2013). While effective for simple
and well-defined objects, these techniques often struggle in
complex or unknown scenarios. Additionally, these methods
tend to be computationally intensive and lack the ability to
generalize effectively in dynamic environments.
The emergence of deep neural networks and data-driven
methods in computer vision has significantly advanced
the ability to tackle the complex challenges of robotic
grasping Newbury et al. (2023). By leveraging image
processing techniques originally developed for computer
vision, grasping models have become increasingly robust,
achieving strong performance in unfamiliar environments
and real-world applications. With the help of large-scale
grasping datasets, these models can generalize effectively to
handle objects with diverse shapes, physical properties, and
environmental conditions, resulting in enhanced reliability in
grasping tasks. However, despite the impressive capabilities
of these models, significant challenges persist. One of
the most critical issues is the heavy reliance of these
data-driven methods on annotated datasets for training.

Related Work
In this section, an overview of different grasping paradigms
is provided, with a focus on highlighting the evolution from
traditional analytical methods to modern data-driven and
geometry-aware approaches. Each paradigm is examined
in terms of how it addresses the challenges of grasp pose
estimation, generalization, and computational efficiency.
This discussion provides the foundation for understanding
the motivations and contributions of the proposed method.
2.1
Analytical Grasping
Analytical
approaches
for
robot
grasping
have
been
extensively studied over the past decades, particularly before
neural networks became the dominant paradigm in the field
Newbury et al. (2023); Kleeberger et al. (2020); de Souza
et al. (2021). Arimoto (2007) addressed the grasping
problem by analysing gripper geometry. Pollayil et al. (2022)
developed a contact-based grasping approach that adapted to
the object’s geometry after initial contact. Kiatos et al. (2020)
introduced a geometric approach for suggesting grasps and a
method for optimizing them, improving the quality of grasps.
Modeling human grasping with five fingers is a highly
complex task and is often simplified by modeling the grasp
as the interaction between two opposing jaws or fingers.
This abstraction facilitates easier representation and analysis
of grasps. However, evaluating the quality of grasp poses
remains a complex challenge, leading researchers to develop
numerous methods and metrics. One of the earliest grasp
quality metrics, force-closure, evaluates whether all contact
forces applied to an object can resist any external wrench
exerted on it Nguyen (1988). Zheng (2012) introduced a
more efficient algorithm for assessing grasp quality based
on force-closure properties. However, the exact calculation
of the force-closure metric is often computationally complex

2.2
Data-Driven Grasping
Recent research has shown the effectiveness of deep neural
networks, particularly convolutional networks, in learning
and capturing relevant features for grasp planning tasks
using only RGB-D image inputs, without requiring the
full object model de Souza et al. (2021). These models
often require a large amount of training data. The Dex-
Net 2.0 dataset Mahler et al. (2017) is a large-scale dataset
containing thousands of 3D objects. In the original paper,
the authors trained a Grasp Quality Convolutional Neural
Network (GQCNN) to rank grasp poses, achieving a 93%
success rate on known objects. Deep learning approaches for
grasp pose generation can be broadly categorized into two
main types:
1. Direct Grasp Pose Prediction: Some studies have
utilized Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to directly
predict the optimal grasp poses Schmidt et al. (2018); Kumra
and Kanan (2017). These methods aim to output the grasp
configuration (e.g., position, orientation, and width) directly
from the input image.
2. Grasp Quality Prediction: Another widely used
architecture for grasping models leverages neural networks
to predict grasp quality Wang et al. (2016); Pinto and
Gupta (2016); Lenz et al. (2015). These models typically
output a quality score for potential grasp candidates, which
can then be used to select the best grasp. These data-
driven approaches typically require substantial amounts
of annotated grasp data, often obtained from large-scale
datasets. Examples of such datasets include Jacquard
Depierre et al. (2018), GraspIt! Miller and Allen (2004), and
GraspNet-1Billion Fang et al. (2020), all of which provide
labeled grasp data. However, due to the dynamic nature
of robotic environments, additional data generation is often
required, and the process of creating labeled grasps can be
both time-consuming and costly.
2.3
Geometry-Aware Grasp Learning
In the modern era, as robotic systems increasingly interact
with humans, they should be capable of grasping a wide
range of objects. This requirement can only be achieved
by developing models that can generalize across different
objects and environments. A key approach to achieving
this is to understand the role of geometric properties of
different objects. Hosseini et al. (2024) used primitive
shape segmentation to decompose the object point cloud
into simpler shapes, thereby teaching the model different
primitive geometries. Another widely used approach is the
idea of pixel-wise or point-wise quality prediction, which
has proven highly effective. Predicting quality instead of
directly generating grasp poses has enabled these models
to generalize to cluttered scenes, even when trained solely
on single-object images or point clouds Kumra et al.
(2022); Wei et al. (2024); Newbury et al. (2023); Laili
et al. (2022); Cai et al. (2022). Generative grasp models
represent a family of approaches that predict grasp quality.
These models use RGB-D images of objects to predict
pixel-wise heatmaps specifying grasp configurations in the
image. Morrison et al. (2020b) used a Generative Grasp
Convolutional Network (GG-CNN) to calculate the grasp
quality for each pixel. Their lightweight generative model
takes an RGB-D image as input and generates three heatmaps
for grasp quality, angle, and width of the grasp. Using a
Generative Residual Convolutional Grasp Neural Network
(GR-ConvNet), Kumra et al. (2022) achieved higher grasp
success rates with a more complex model architecture. Their
approach achieved 97% and 94% accuracy on the Cornell
and Jacquard datasets, respectively. Li et al. (2022) used
a gradient-based quality heatmap for more accurate grasp
center prediction. Kim et al. (2023) replaced the width
heatmap with a segmentation mask, improving model’s
width estimation. Zhang et al. (2023) introduced the
HTC-Grasp method, utilizing a transformer-based encoder
to capture the global features of the image. Xu et al.
(2022) introduced four new heatmaps—one for grasp center
prediction, and others for grasp orientation offset and
grasp keypoint prediction. Despite these improvements,
determining accurate grasp poses from RGB-D images
in real-world environments is still a challenging task. A
significant limitation is that these models rely on large-scale
datasets for training, yet labeled data is often scarce in real-
world scenarios. The proposed method in this paper aims to
address this limitation by learning the geometry of objects,

2.4
Skeletons and Skeleton-Based Grasp
Generation Methods
Geometric skeleton analysis is a broad area of research, and
skeletonization is an important concept, especially in appli-
cations where exact analysis of complex object geometries is
computationally intensive. Skeletons efficiently represent the
geometrical and topological properties of an object, and their
applications extend beyond grasping to computer graph-
ics, image processing, and shape analysis, including object
recognition, shape matching, and deformation analysis Wen
et al. (2023); Lin et al. (2021). By providing a simplified
representation of an object’s geometry while preserving its
key structural properties, skeletons enable efficient analysis
across a wide range of applications. Various skeletonization
methods exist for 2D geometries, with the most notable
being the Medial Axis Transform (MAT) Blum (1967) and
the straight skeleton Aichholzer and Aurenhammer (1996).
The straight skeleton differs from the medial axis in that
it has a straight-line structure and lower combinatorial
complexity Aichholzer and Aurenhammer (1996), which
can make it preferable in some applications. Despite their
potential to provide valuable insights into object geome-
try and grasp pose estimation, applications of skeletons in
robotic grasping have been limited. Sabzejou et al. (2023)
developed a method for grasp keypoint generation using
straight skeletons, demonstrating that skeleton structures can
effectively reduce the grasp search space by focusing on

geometrically meaningful regions of the object. However,
the method was primarily designed as a standalone ana-

into a large-scale data generation or learning framework.
Przybylski et al. (2011) proposed a medial-axis-transform-
based grasp planning method using a grid of medial spheres.
The method uses local symmetry and object thickness to
generate geometrically meaningful grasp candidates and can
reduce the search space without relying on simple primi-
tive decompositions. However, it was mainly evaluated in
simulation and depends on complete object geometry, such
as mesh or point-cloud models, which limits its practicality
under noisy and partial real-world perception. Moreover,
Pennisi et al. (2024) proposed a learning-based topolog-
ical skeleton extraction method for selective grasping of
complex-shaped industrial parts. Their method uses skeleton
keypoints to estimate object pose and select suitable grasping
poses, showing the usefulness of skeleton representations in
practical robotic manipulation. Nevertheless, the approach is
tailored to specific rigid automotive components and relies
on predefined skeleton keypoints and object-specific grasp-
ing frames, which restricts its generalization to arbitrary
unknown objects.
2.5
Generative Grasping Convolutional Neural
Networks
A common architecture for generating grasp poses from
RGB-D images is the Generative Grasping Convolutional
Neural Networks (GG-CNN) introduced by Morrison et al.
(2020b). Common generative grasping networks, such as
GG-CNN and GR-ConvNet Kumra et al. (2022), adopt
an encoder–decoder architecture that maps an input image
I ∈RH×W ×C (where C is the number of channels, e.g.,
RGB, RGB-D, or depth) to four prediction heads. Each head
produces a heatmap of size H × W, encoding a distinct
component of the grasp configuration:
• Quality head Q ∈[0, 1]H×W : assigns to each pixel
(u, v) the probability Q(u, v) of being the center of
a successful grasp. This value can also be used as the
confidence score of the predicted grasp.
• Orientation
heads
Ocos, Osin ∈[−1, 1]H×W :
predict

each

gripper opening width w(u, v) at each pixel.
At inference time, grasp candidates are generated by first
selecting local maxima of the quality map Q, and then
retrieving their corresponding orientation θ(u, v) and width
w(u, v) from the orientation and width heads. Each grasp
candidate is thus parameterized as:
g = (u, v, θ(u, v), w(u, v))
(1)
where g represents the final 2D grasp configuration. This
formulation enables the network to output an effectively
unlimited number of grasp poses for a single input image.
Although this architecture has proven effective in real-
world applications, it suffers from a key problem. The
primary issue is that the predicted quality map often
identifies multiple, distinct high-quality regions, some of
which occur near the object’s borders. This can lead to
inaccuracies in the predicted grasp parameters, such as
incorrect orientation or gripper width, ultimately reducing
the reliability of the grasp. For instance, Figure 3 illustrates
such inaccuracies in the grasp predictions produced by the
GR-ConvNet model Kumra et al. (2022). In Section 4,
problems with these architectures are addressed, and a
new model called the Skeleton-Based Generative Grasp
Convolutional Neural Network (SkelGG-CNN) is proposed
to overcome these limitations.

Problem Formulation
In this work, the robotic grasping problem is formulated as
the predicting of antipodal grasps. A grasp is considered
antipodal when the surface normals at the contact points
are antiparallel to the finger closure direction and collinear
with the line connecting the contact points Murray et al.
(2017). It is important to note that while the proposed
skeleton-based data generation algorithm is designed to
generalize across both planar (4-DoF) and volumetric
(6-DoF) spaces, the learning component of the current
framework, SkelGG-CNN, is specifically designed to use the
4-DoF representations for high-speed, real-time planar grasp
prediction. A 4-DoF grasp can be modeled on a 2D plane as a
rectangle that specifies the grasp’s position, orientation, and
the minimum gripper width required to successfully grasp
the object. This rectangle can be defined as:
Gr = (Pc, θ, w)
(2)
In the above equation, Pc ∈R2 is defined as the center of
the grasp rectangle, and θ denotes the grasp’s orientation
relative to the horizontal axis. The parameter w denotes the
minimum gripper width required to successfully grasp the
object. When projecting to a real-life 4-DoF grasp from the
image, the center needs to be defined as Pc ∈R3, where the z
component represents the depth value corresponding to the x
and y coordinates extracted from the depth image. Translated
to robot coordinates, the grasp can be defined as:
G = (Pg, Θg, Wg)
(3)

4.1
Straight Skeleton Construction
This paper extends the skeleton-based grasp keypoint
generation method proposed in Sabzejou et al. (2023). The
original work lacked a comprehensive pipeline for grasp
annotation and relied on global regions, pairing points from

different grasp regions together, which often resulted in
failure cases. As a result, the algorithm was computationally
expensive and unsuitable for large-scale grasp annotation.
In contrast, the skeleton-based approach presented here
has been significantly improved to become a more accurate
and efficient algorithm, capable of achieving real-time
performance within the automatic pipeline. This efficiency
arises from restricting the pairing algorithm to local point
candidates around each skeleton node rather than considering
global regions.
The straight skeleton of a 2D polygon P is constructed by
tracing angular bisectors at each vertex of the polygon. As
these bisectors are drawn, the polygon progressively shrinks.
During this process, two types of events may occur—Edge
Events and Split Events—both of which alter the polygon’s
structure by forming smaller polygons Aichholzer and
Aurenhammer (1996). This shrinking process is recursively
applied to the newly formed polygons until the area of the
polygons shrink to zero. The straight skeleton S(P) can
therefore be defined as a graph, whose edges are formed by
the angular bisectors. The endpoints of these edges, which
are not vertices of P, are referred to as nodes. Figure 4
illustrates the skeleton graph of a 2D polygon.

slightly increased to ensure that more points from the
object’s contour are sampled for keypoint pairing. The
selected points on each circle are paired based on specific
constraints to identify pairs that satisfy the property of

4.4
SkelGG-CNN Grasp Prediction and
Refinement with Skeleton Heatmap
The architecture of the SkelGG-CNN model includes four
output heads, each contributing to the formation of the grasp
configuration. The first three heads generate the grasp center
(Pc) and orientation (θ). In contrast to existing generative
methods, the label for the fourth head is constructed using the
sequence of offset polygons generated during the skeleton
construction of the auto-labeling process. The skeleton
heatmap is created by assigning a value of −1 to all pixels
outside the object’s polygon. Inside the polygon, pixel values
increase progressively from the outer edges toward the offset
polygons. These offset polygons shrink toward the local
centers of the object’s geometry, resulting in those areas
having the highest values. This happens because during
the offsetting process, multiple polygons may be created,

When calculating the gripper’s opening width, a line is drawn
from the grasp center along the specified orientation. The
width is determined by extending the line until it intersects
the polygon’s edges, at which point the values become
negative. The length of this line segment corresponds to the
gripper’s required opening width. Additionally, this heatmap
enables the model to refine the grasp configuration. The
refinement process works as follows: after the initial grasp
center is determined using the quality heatmap and an initial
grasp orientation is identified, the grasp is adjusted along
this orientation toward the point with the highest value in
the skeleton heatmap. Algorithm 2 details the grasp width
calculation and grasp refinement process.
This refinement offers multiple advantages. First, it
enhances the ability to reposition the grasp, thereby
improving the accuracy of the predicted grasp points, as
demonstrated in Figure 6, where the grasp is adjusted to
better align with the object’s geometry. By providing a more
reliable framework for grasp adjustment, the system becomes
more effective in real-world applications. Second, during
the development process, it was observed that learning
to generate this specific heatmap particularly facilitates
learning of the quality heatmap, leading to more robust
heatmap predictions.
As illustrated in Figures 6 and 3, the skeleton heatmap
activations consistently align with the high-confidence
regions of the quality map. This correlation is further
supported quantitatively by the ablation study in Section 5
(Figure 9), which shows that the skeleton map head of
SkelGG-CNN achieves both faster convergence and higher
final accuracy compared to the width map head, while both
variants substantially outperform the GG-CNN 2 baseline.
These results suggest that skeleton heatmaps serve as a rich
source of contextual information, enabling the model to
better predict the quality and feasibility of a given grasp.
4.5
SkelGG-CNN Architecture
The SkelGG-CNN is a fully convolutional encoder-decoder
network designed for grasp pose estimation, producing pixel-
wise heatmaps that capture grasp position, orientation, and
geometric refinement. The overall structure of the proposed
architecture is shown in Figure 7.
In the downsampling stage, features are compressed
through four convolutional blocks with increasing numbers
of filters (32–256), ReLU activations, dropout, and max-
pooling. Dropout is applied in the downsampling stage to
reduce overfitting, which is particularly important given the
relatively small size of the Dex-Net training set used in this
work.
Inception-style multi-scale convolutions were selected
because they capture features at different receptive field
sizes simultaneously Szegedy et al. (2015) and the residual
connection helps with the convergence of the model. Batch
normalization is applied at the bottleneck to stabilize
training at this high-channel-count stage. The bottleneck
expands features to 2048 channels before reducing them
back
to
512,
allowing
the
module
to
capture
rich
multi-scale representations without propagating excessive
dimensionality to the upsampling path.
The upsampling path mirrors the encoder with nearest-
neighbor upsampling and convolutions, reducing filters back
to 32. The combination of upsampling and convolution
was preferred over transposed convolutions, which, although
widely used for feature map upscaling, were found to intro-
duce training instabilities such as inconsistent convergence
and checkerboard artifacts, ultimately degrading overall
model performance. Finally, from the final feature maps, four
output heads generate heatmaps for grasp quality, orientation
(via sine and cosine), and skeleton geometry.

4.7
Generalized 6-DoF Skeleton-Based
Auto-Labeling
The skeleton-based auto-labeling algorithm presented earlier
in this section is limited to generating 4-DoF grasp
annotations. Despite this limitation, the 4-DoF grasps
remain
widely
used
in
modern
robotic
applications
due to their computational efficiency, compatibility with
common 4-DoF robotic hardware (e.g., Delta and SCARA
robots), and suitability for structured environments like
tabletop or conveyor-based pick-and-place tasks Noh et al.
(2025); Hu et al. (2025). Their simplicity enables robust,
high-speed grasping with minimal sensitivity to noisy
depth data, making them prevalent in industrial settings
despite advancements in 6-DoF grasping. Recent research,
as discussed in Section 2, validates these advantages
through data-driven 4-DoF grasping models, which achieve
high accuracy in structured scenarios. Nevertheless, this
restriction often becomes inadequate in highly cluttered
scenes, where top-down approaches may not provide feasible
grasping solutions, and the robot requires more flexible
orientations to reach and manipulate objects successfully.
The straight skeleton approach uses the geometric features
of 2D polygons to find local grasping regions in objects.
Unfortunately, this technique does not extend naturally
to 3D point clouds and meshses, as 3D meshes lack
the same well-defined polygonal properties. While several
skeletonization algorithms have been proposed for 3D
meshes and point clouds Wen et al. (2024); Cao et al.
(2010); Jalba et al. (2012), they typically assume access
to complete mesh or point cloud information. In real-
world robotic setups, however, point clouds are often noisy,
partial, and incomplete, leading these methods to produce
inaccurate or unstable skeletons. To address the discussed
issue and utilize the previously developed 2D skeletonization
approach, the following quasi-3D approach was developed.
By using this quasi-3D formulation, the developed algorithm
aims to preserve essential 3D topological features while
remaining robust to incomplete, noisy depth data.
The proposed generalized skeleton-based auto-labeling
algorithm begins by partitioning the input point cloud
into horizontal cross-sections orthogonal to the z-axis.
Within each cross-section, a Laplacian-based skeletonization
algorithm Cao et al. (2010) is applied. This iterative
contraction method progressively reduces the point set into a
one-dimensional skeleton by moving points toward the local
medial regions of the surface. The transformation at each
iteration is expressed as:

successive slices, each skeleton point in one cross-section is

layer. Within each layer, the points are further connected
using the Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) algorithm. Cross

transitions and continuity throughout the reconstructed

The final result is a skeleton graph that compactly
represents the structure of the object. As the skeleton
cross-sections have exactly the same number of points, the
final skeleton is heavily downsampled for more efficient
representation. This skeleton graph serves as a foundation
for sampling candidate grasp regions and the following pair
selection.
After constructing the skeleton graph, a set of initial grasp
centers is required to initiate the pairing process. To generate
these centers, each horizontal cross-section is projected into
2D space. A polygonal approximation of the cross-section
boundary is then computed using the alpha-shape algorithm,
which yields a 2D polygonal representation of the cut. From
this polygon, the straight skeleton algorithm is applied to
extract central points, which serve as the initial grasp centers.
These centers act only as initial points for the subsequent
refinement stage.
In the refinement step, pairs of nodes from the 3D skeleton
graph are sampled, and the graph is traversed to search for
pairs corresponding to optimal grasp configurations. The
traversal is performed using a depth-first search (DFS),
rooted at the initially sampled nodes. At each candidate pair,
a local plane is fitted to the neighboring edges of the skeleton
at that node, and the alignment of these planes is evaluated
as a grasp quality measure.
To improve efficiency, the traversal avoids revisiting
ancestor nodes once a better candidate pair with more
opposing normals has been identified.
The proposed
skeletonization procedure demonstrates strong robustness to
noisy and incomplete point clouds, which are commonly
encountered in real-world robotic setups and datasets.
Operating on localized cross-sectional structures eliminates
the need for a fully closed surface representation and
reduces the instabilities typically associated with isotropic
3D skeletonization methods. As a result, the method

different methods, making it a popular benchmark in the

such as the Jacquard dataset ensures a unified way to
evaluate performance, it does not necessarily reflect the
model’s robustness or real-world applicability. To evaluate
the effectiveness of the proposed auto-labeling algorithm and
the SkelGG-CNN model, the Jacquard dataset was processed
and labeled using the straight skeleton grasp generation

auto-generated labels and assessed on the Jacquard V1 test

This approach resulted in a grasp accuracy of 95.42% on

provide any estimation of grasp quality. In contrast, SkelGG-

but also learns grasp patterns across geometries frequently
encountered during training. This enables the model to
effectively filter out noisy or incorrect grasps that may have
been introduced during the auto-labeling process for some
geometries, resulting in cleaner and more reliable grasp
predictions.

decreases across all heatmaps toward the edges of the grasp

issue becomes more pronounced when the grasp rectangle

disjointed regions across the grasping rectangle, leading to
inconsistencies in grasp estimation.

grasp annotation algorithm. Specifically, the grasp poses
generated by the straight skeleton method consider only
the front-facing polygon of objects, neglecting variations in
elevation and 3D geometry. Despite these limitations, both
the grasp keypoint algorithm and SkelGG-CNN have been
successfully deployed for precise and reliable grasping tasks,

5.5
Evaluation on Jacquard V2 with
Ground-Truth Testing
The Jacquard V2 dataset, as detailed in Li and Yuan
(2024), builds upon the original Jacquard V1 dataset by
introducing a Human-In-The-Loop (HIL) approach to refine
its annotations. Unlike Jacquard V1, which generates all
grasp labels automatically and often suffers from problems
such as missing or incorrect grasp bounding boxes, limited
grasp orientations, and incomplete annotations, Jacquard
V2 includes human operators who selectively correct the
automated labels. This refinement process produces labels
that are significantly more akin to those created by human
experts, establishing Jacquard V2 as a more accurate
benchmark for evaluating grasp detection performance.
In this evaluation, the SkelGG-CNN model was trained
exclusively on grasp labels generated automatically by the
straight skeleton and pairing method, without any human
intervention. The model was then tested on the human-
refined Jacquard V2 dataset, achieving an improvement in
grasp accuracy from 95.42% (on Jacquard V1) to 96.55%.
This increase in performance is noteworthy because it
demonstrates that the auto-generated labels, used during
training, are highly similar to the human-refined annotations
in Jacquard V2. The ability of the SkelGG-CNN model
to achieve such high accuracy on a dataset with human-
level ground-truth labels underscores the quality of the
proposed auto-labeling algorithm. The primary purpose of
this evaluation is to assess how closely the auto-generated
labels approximate human-level annotations. The strong
performance on Jacquard V2, where the model achieves
96.55% accuracy and outperforms several existing methods
as shown in Table 5, validates that the automatic labeling
process produces annotations nearly equivalent to those
refined by humans.
5.6
Cross-Dataset Generalization Evaluation
on Cornell
To evaluate the generalization capabilities of the SkelGG-
CNN model across diverse datasets, two instances of the
model were trained using auto-labeled data: one on the
Jacquard dataset and the other on the Dex-Net dataset. Both
models were subsequently tested on the Cornell dataset,
which comprises 885 manually annotated real-world RGB-D
images of 240 distinct objects. The human-labeled ground-
truth annotations in the Cornell dataset provide a benchmark
for assessing the model’s ability to perform effectively on
data distributions different from those encountered during
training.
The SkelGG-CNN model trained on the Jacquard dataset
achieved a grasp accuracy of 97.1% on the Cornell dataset, as
measured by the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric with
a threshold of 0.25 and an orientation error less than π/6. In
parallel, the model trained on the Dex-Net dataset attained a


grasp accuracy of 96.26%. These high accuracies achieved
on a dataset with human-annotated labels demonstrate that
the automatic labeling process generates annotations closely
approximating human-level quality, reducing reliance on
extensive manual labeling. The near-equivalent performance
of the Dex-Net-trained model, despite its significantly
smaller training dataset, is particularly striking, with only
a marginal accuracy difference of 0.84% compared to the
Jacquard-trained model. This cross-dataset evaluation serves
two primary purposes:
Testing Generalization Across Datasets: The exper-
iment assesses the SkelGG-CNN framework’s ability to
generalize across varying data distributions. By training on
auto-labeled data from one dataset (e.g., Jacquard or Dex-
Net) and evaluating on the Cornell dataset with its human-
annotated labels, the results validate the robustness of both
the auto-labeling algorithm and the SkelGG-CNN model.
The accuracies of 97.1% (Jacquard-trained) and 96.26%
(Dex-Net-trained) highlight the framework’s adaptability to
real-world scenarios without necessitating dataset-specific
retraining or manual labeling. This capability is critical for
deploying robotic grasping systems in diverse, unstructured
environments where pre-labeled data may not be available.
Demonstrating Efficiency with Small Datasets: The
comparable performance of the Dex-Net-trained model
underscores the efficiency of the geometry-aware learning
approach embedded in SkelGG-CNN. Despite the Dex-Net
dataset being over 20 times smaller than Jacquard (2,622
vs. 54,000 images), the model achieves nearly equivalent
accuracy. This efficiency arises from the integration of
geometric features via the skeleton heatmap, which guides
the model to focus on high-quality grasp regions and learn
effective grasp strategies with fewer training samples. The
ability to maintain robust and generalized performance
with limited data enhances the framework’s scalability
and practicality for new tasks or environments where data
collection is constrained.
The performance of SkelGG-CNN is compared with state-
of-the-art models in Table 6. The Jacquard-trained model’s
97.1% accuracy and the Dex-Net-trained model’s 96.26%
accuracy position SkelGG-CNN competitively among recent
methods, many of which leverage advanced architectures
like Transformers (e.g., GraspFormer, TransGrasp) or
deeper networks (e.g., SE-ResUNet). However, unlike
these works, which often prioritize architectural scalability,
this study emphasizes the effectiveness of the proposed
grasp generation approach using algorithmically generated
labels. The high accuracies achieved without human-labeled
training data affirm the model’s learning efficiency.
These results confirm that the SkelGG-CNN framework
can efficiently auto-label data, train a robust model, and
generalize effectively to unseen datasets.

Robotic Environment Setup
The experimental setup, as shown in Figure 1, employs
a 3-DoF Delta parallel robot Ghafarian Tamizi et al.
(2022) equipped with a two-fingered gripper Yarmohammadi
et al. (2023) mounted on its end-effector. A PID control
scheme ensures precise positioning of the Delta robot.
The operational sequence initiates from a defined home
configuration, followed by movement to a location directly
above the target object. Alignment is achieved based on
the orientation specified by the grasp detection algorithm,
after which the robot attempts to grasp the object from the
specified configuration. The grasping process is refined by
a control algorithm that closes the gripper as it approaches
the object until an appropriate force is detected by an FSR
sensor mounted on the gripper plane. Additionally, two
cameras are utilized in the experimental setup: an RGB
camera for recording color images and a Kinect V1 sensor
for capturing depth images. The calibration procedure for the
Kinect sensor is carried out using the same method adopted
in Hosseini et al. (2024).

6.3
Grasping in Cluttered Scenes
A key strength of the SkelGG-CNN model, which utilizes
a generative grasping approach with convolutional layers,
lies in its ability to generalize from single-object scenarios
to cluttered environments, even when trained exclusively
on isolated objects. Convolutional layers enable the model
to identify graspable regions based on local and global
geometric features, enabling the architecture to be capable of
generating accurate pixel values in cluttered scenes. To test
this capability, ten test scenes were created, each containing
a mix of randomly arranged objects. Objects were randomly
placed by shaking them in a box and emptying them into the
robot’s workspace, producing diverse, unstructured layouts
for each scene. In this experiment, the robot attempted
to grasp each object and place it into a designated box
at a fixed location within the workspace. A scene was
considered successfully completed when all objects from
the initial arrangement had been picked up and placed
into the box. Performance was evaluated using both grasp
success rate (successful pick-ups) and task completion rate.
The task completion rate was computed as the average
percentage of objects successfully grasped and placed per
scene, where each scene’s percentage is the number of
successfully handled objects divided by the total objects
in that scene. The cluttered experiments involved ten test
scenes, each with a varying number of objects. For each
scene, a top-view image was captured, and grasp selection
was performed using the combination of quality score and
skeleton heatmap to reduce the risk of collision during
the grasp. This process was repeated until all objects were
cleared from the scene. Across the ten scenes, the SkelGG-
CNN model achieved a grasp success rate of 95.0% and a
task completion rate of 97.0%, showcasing its capability to
handle cluttered scenes effectively despite being trained only
on single objects. Figure 14 demonstrates the robot grasping
objects in a cluttered scene, with the model’s skeleton and
quality outputs guiding the process.

Conclusion
This research presented a novel framework for robotic grasp
detection for two-finger grippers that integrated a geometry-
aware grasp pose estimation algorithm, an automated data
pipeline, and the Skeleton-based Generative Grasp Convolu-
tional Neural Network (SkelGG-CNN) to address the chal-
lenges of grasping in diverse and unstructured environments.
The framework leveraged straight skeletons to generate accu-
rate grasp keypoints, thereby eliminating the need for manual
labeling and significantly reducing computational complex-
ity. Additionally, the auto-labeling method was extended
to 6-DoF grasping through a generalized skeletonization
approach, enabling volumetric grasp annotation directly on
point clouds for more comprehensive handling of cluttered
3D scenarios. By incorporating geometric features through a

The

and 96.26% when trained on Jacquard and Dex-Net data,
respectively. On the GraspNet benchmark, the 6-DoF auto-

and 18.33 (novel), demonstrating the strong generalization
capabilities of the skeletonization method to 6-DoF auto-

rate on the Dex-Net test set and 95.2% on unseen objects.
The 6-DoF labeling further improved these rates to 98.4%
and 96.0%, respectively, outperforming 4-DoF methods. On
the EGAD! dataset, the 6-DoF approach yielded an 89.1%
success rate, addressing limitations of 4-DoF grasping on
complex geometries. Physical experiments with a Delta
parallel robot reinforced these findings and yielded a
97.4% success rate for single-object grasping and a 95.0%
success rate in cluttered scenes, outperforming state-of-the-
art methods without requiring real-world training data or
domain adaptation.
A key strength of the framework was its efficiency, as
it achieved competitive performance with a relatively small
dataset (2,622 images from Dex-Net) and a lightweight
model architecture (approximately 15 million parameters in
its largest configuration). This efficiency stemmed from the
geometry-aware learning approach, in which the skeleton
heatmap guided the model to focus on high-quality grasp
regions, thereby enhancing learning with minimal data. The
fully automated pipeline—from data collection and labeling
to model training and deployment—represented a significant
advancement toward practical, adaptable robotic grasping
systems.
In conclusion, the developed pipeline and use of skeletons
in grasping offer a robust, efficient, and generalizable
solution for robotic grasp detection. Its extension to 6-DoF
grasping enhances its applicability to cluttered and complex
3D scenarios, bridging the gap between 4-DoF efficiency and
full volumetric manipulation. Its innovative use of straight

on 4-DoF planar grasp prediction, the proven efficacy

in handling objects with complex 3D geometries, where
some elevation variations and volumetric patterns can be
neglected by the current 2D slicing heuristic. While this
generalized quasi-3D algorithm was designed to ensure
robustness and computational efficiency on partial point
clouds, it may overlook some grasp-relevant features in
complex spatial configurations. Therefore, future research
will focus on developing geometry-aware skeletonization
algorithms that explicitly capture 3D topological structures.
Additionally, future work could extend the overarching

may
also
refine

broaden its real-world applicability.
