Recent advances in vision-language-action (VLA) and world-action models (WAMs) have significantly
improved robotic manipulation, yet deploying robots in the open world demands a higher level of
autonomy beyond executing predefined tasks. Robot agents offer a promising paradigm for this
transition, integrating task discovery, contextual reasoning, physical execution, and adaptive recovery
into a closed interaction loop. However, existing benchmarks largely evaluate isolated capabilities,
failing to capture the complexity of open-world robot autonomy. We introduce RobotAgentBench
(RAB), a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating robot agents in realistic, open-world household
environments. Through an agent-assisted, human-in-the-loop real-to-sim pipeline, RAB constructs
50 diverse household layouts with interactive objects and physically grounded tasks, encompassing
90 atomic tasks, 18 short-horizon tasks, 8 long-horizon tasks, and 4 open-ended scenarios. RAB
provides a multi-level evaluation of robot agent capabilities: (1) task-level assessments systemat-
ically examine five complementary dimensions—task discovery, context reasoning, manipulation,
failure recovery, and robustness; (2) scenario-level evaluation assesses how agents integrate these
capabilities to autonomously fulfill high-level user needs. Furthermore, RAB enables systematic
comparisons of standalone VLA/WAM models and diverse robot agent architectures under a unified
planning–execution–verification framework. Extensive experiments reveal substantial gaps between
manipulation and autonomy, demonstrate the advantages of agentic architectures and recursive
self-improvement, and highlight the remaining challenges toward open-world robot autonomy.

Introduction
Embodied AI has advanced rapidly in recent years, with vision-language-action (VLA) and world-
action models (WAMs) demonstrating increasingly strong manipulation capabilities across diverse
tasks and environments [5, 19, 40, 48, 50, 52, 55]. These advances raise a fundamental question: How

far are we from deploying robots in the open world? Unlike predefined manipulation tasks, the real world
involves numerous mundane yet complex challenges, often characterized by ambiguity, uncertainty,
and constantly changing circumstances [13, 28, 37]. Consider a household robot preparing for
guests: it must identify what needs to be cleaned, select appropriate food based on user preferences,
coordinate multiple activities, and recover when manipulation attempts fail [32]. Such challenges
are not exceptional, but intrinsic to everyday physical environments. Addressing them is essential for
transforming robots into broadly useful assistants in human environments.
Operating in the open world demands a transition from robot manipulation to robot autonomy.
Beyond executing explicit instructions, robots must autonomously discover relevant tasks, reason about
environmental and user context, plan physical interactions, and adapt to unexpected outcomes. Robot
agents provide a natural framework for this transition, integrating high-level reasoning and planning
with physical execution and feedback-driven verification into a closed loop [2, 17, 41, 46]. Such
agents must determine not only how to execute, but also what to do and how to adapt. Meanwhile,
advances in multimodal foundation models, including GPT-6-Astra, and emerging agentic embodied
systems [4, 8, 47, 54] are creating new opportunities to realize this integration. Together, these
developments suggest that embodied AI is entering a new stage, shifting from standalone manipulation
models toward autonomous robot agents capable of handling complex, dynamic physical environments.
However, existing benchmarks remain largely designed for robot manipulation rather than au-
tonomy. Widely adopted benchmarks [6, 14, 21, 27, 33, 34, 56] primarily evaluate predefined tasks
with explicit goals, overlooking autonomous task discovery and context-dependent decision-making.
Recent efforts extend evaluation toward reasoning [7] and long-horizon planning [23, 31, 42, 43, 53],
but often rely on stylized problems or abstracted physical interactions. More importantly, reasoning,
planning, and manipulation are frequently evaluated in isolation, without capturing their interdepen-
dence during real-world operation. A robot capable of individual tasks may still struggle to determine
what needs to be done, recover from failures, or adapt to human interventions. Evaluating open-world
robot autonomy therefore requires moving beyond isolated capabilities toward a comprehensive
assessment of the entire autonomous interaction loop.
To bridge this gap, we introduce RobotAgentBench (RAB), a benchmark for evaluating open-world
robot autonomy in realistic household environments. We develop an agent-assisted, human-in-the-
loop real-to-sim pipeline to reconstruct real-world scenes and expand them into 50 diverse layouts
with interactive objects and physically grounded tasks. At its core, RAB provides 90 atomic tasks
that can be flexibly composed into a wide range of household tasks, from which we construct 18
short-horizon and 8 long-horizon manipulation tasks as the foundation of our evaluation. Building
upon these tasks, we introduce diverse variants and evaluation settings to systematically assess five
complementary dimensions of robot autonomy: (1) task discovery, identifying what needs to be
done; (2) context reasoning, interpreting implicit requirements; (3) manipulation, executing physical
actions; (4) failure recovery, detecting and correcting execution errors; and (5) robustness, adapting
to environmental disturbances. Beyond these task-level assessments, we introduce four open-ended
household scenarios that require robot agents to integrate these capabilities to autonomously fulfill
high-level user needs, enabling an end-to-end evaluation of open-world robot autonomy.
Using RobotAgentBench, we systematically evaluate standalone VLA/WAM models and diverse
robot agent architectures, revealing five key findings:

RobotAgentBench
We introduce RobotAgentBench, a benchmark designed to evaluate open-world robot autonomy
beyond predefined manipulation tasks. We leverage a real-to-sim pipeline to construct diverse
environments and collect data, then build the multi-level evaluation suite for systematic comparisons
of diverse agent framework designs. Figure 1 illustrates the overview of our benchmark.
2.1
Real-to-Sim Pipeline for Environment and Data Construction
To enable realistic and scalable evaluation, we develop an agent-assisted, human-in-the-loop
real-to-sim pipeline that starts from a physical room in a real home, reconstructs it in simulation,


4
expands the reconstructed scene into diverse interactive configurations, and collects demonstrations
for training and evaluation.
2.1.1
Environment Construction
Starting from a physical room in a real home, we first reconstruct it in Isaac Sim and then expand
it into diverse household environments. We subsequently collect real-robot demonstrations and
reproduce them in simulation to generate trajectories.
Household Scene Reconstruction.
We reconstruct the physical household environment in Isaac
Sim through an iterative pipeline comprising real-world parameter collection, similar asset retrieval,
and new asset reconstruction. First, we collect photographs and measurements of the room layout,
furniture arrangements, and household objects to establish real-world references. Next, a coding agent
retrieves similar 3D assets from online repositories and adjusts their geometry, scale, and appearance
to match these references. For objects without suitable existing assets, we leverage the agent to
directly reconstruct their geometry and textures from photographs. The resulting assets are assembled
into the simulated environment with appropriate collision geometry and physical properties to support
realistic interactions. Throughout this iterative process, human reviewers continuously validate visual
fidelity and physical interaction consistency against the real-world environment, providing feedback
to the agent for refining the reconstructed scene.
Environment Augmentation.
Building upon the reconstructed home, we systematically diversify
its spatial configurations and visual appearances. We generate 50 distinct layouts by modifying floor
plans (e.g., T-shaped and L-shaped configurations), rearranging functional areas, and varying furniture
positions and orientations. Each layout is manually reviewed to eliminate geometric inconsistencies
and implausible arrangements. We further increase environmental diversity through variations in
furniture colors, textures, materials, and interior design styles.
2.1.2
Demonstration Collection
We follow a three-stage workflow comprising real-world demonstration collection, simulation
alignment, and agent-assisted trajectory expansion to generate scalable, physically grounded data.
First, we adopt the AgiBot G1 as the robot embodiment, matching its camera configurations and joint
settings between simulation and the physical platform, and collect teleoperated demonstrations in the
real home. Second, we replay these demonstrations in simulation, iteratively refining asset geometry
and physical parameters to align interaction outcomes with the real world. Finally, building upon
these reference trajectories, we employ a coding agent to generate additional successful trajectories
across diverse object positions, environment layouts, and initial robot configurations. With access to
the full simulator state, including object poses, the agent synthesizes and executes action programs
to accomplish target tasks. For challenging interactions, we provide manually implemented control
functions that the agent can invoke and compose. This workflow enables scalable trajectory generation
while maintaining consistency with real-world demonstrations. Detailed observation and action spaces
are provided in Appendix A.

2.2.1
Composable Household Task Suite
We construct a foundational task suite comprising 90 atomic tasks involving everyday household
objects and appliances. These tasks can be flexibly composed into diverse household tasks, from which
we construct 18 representative short-horizon tasks and 8 long-horizon composite tasks. The suite
covers common activities, such as retrieving food from a refrigerator, heating meals in a microwave, and
using an air fryer. These tasks involve diverse manipulation skills, including pushing, pulling, bimanual
coordination, and mobile manipulation, across rigid objects, deformable materials (e.g., tablecloths),
and granular materials (e.g., coffee beans). Short-horizon tasks combine a few atomic tasks, such
as picking up a hat from a sofa and hanging it on a coat rack, while long-horizon tasks involve
multiple subgoals, such as tidying an entire sofa area. Building upon this task suite, we construct
diverse evaluation settings by varying task instructions, contextual requirements, initial states, and
environmental disturbances. These variants enable targeted assessments of different autonomy
capabilities without requiring entirely new manipulation tasks. We further compose these tasks into
open-ended household scenarios, where the tasks to be performed depend on the environment and
user context. For each atomic task and short-horizon task, we collect 50 training trajectories; for each
long-horizon task and open-ended scenario, we provide 10 complete demonstrations. In total, the


6
dataset comprises 101.8 hours of training data. Further details are provided in Figure 2.
2.2.2
Task-level Capability Evaluation
We design five complementary evaluation settings to examine how robots determine what to
do, execute physical actions, and adapt to unexpected outcomes. Each setting focuses on a specific
capability through controlled task configurations. We use capability-specific metrics: target-object
coverage for task discovery, target-selection accuracy for context reasoning, and task progress scores
for manipulation, failure recovery, and robustness.
Task Discovery.
We evaluate whether robots can autonomously identify relevant tasks without
explicit task instructions. We configure household scenes with task-relevant objects and provide
scenario descriptions and user-specific context. The robot must explore the environment, interpret its
observations, and infer which objects require attention. To reduce manipulation demands, the robot
indicates inferred targets by touching the relevant objects with either gripper, without completing
the underlying household tasks. We measure the fraction of target objects touched under a limited
contact budget.
Context Reasoning.
We assess whether robots can infer appropriate actions from implicit require-
ments rather than explicit object instructions. We construct four task settings involving drink selection,
snack selection, waste selection, and sofa object selection. Given contextual information such as user
preferences, intended uses, or classification rules, the robot must identify the appropriate target
object. For example, waste selection requires identifying the object that belongs to the requested
category under the supplied classification rules. We evaluate reasoning through contact with the
correct target object, without requiring successful grasping or manipulation.
Manipulation.
We evaluate physical execution through the 18 short-horizon and 8 long-horizon
tasks in the foundational task suite. Given explicit task instructions, robots must execute the required
physical interactions, assessing their instruction-following and manipulation capabilities across diverse
objects, skills, and task horizons.
Failure Recovery.
We evaluate whether robots can recognize and recover from execution failures.
We construct five failure variants of the foundational tasks, including missed grasps and objects
accidentally knocked over during manipulation. Starting from these failure states, robots must inspect
the environment, identify the failure, and execute corrective actions to resume and complete the
specified manipulation objective.
Robustness.
We assess whether robots can adapt to unexpected environmental changes during
execution. We construct six task variants involving human interventions and lighting changes, such
as moving a coffee machine while the robot is pouring beans into it. Robots must respond to these
disturbances and adjust their actions to complete the specified manipulation objective.
2.2.3
Scenario-level Integrated Evaluation
Beyond individual capabilities, open-world robot autonomy requires their integration into a closed
loop of task discovery, reasoning, planning, execution, and adaptation. We therefore introduce four
open-ended household scenarios: preparing dinner, hosting guests, preparing to leave, and tidying the
room. Each scenario involves context-dependent combinations of tasks, with specific requirements


7
determined by the sampled environment and user information. Unlike task-level evaluation, robots
do not receive explicit task lists. Instead, they must autonomously explore the environment, identify
relevant tasks, interpret user needs, and plan their execution. This includes reasoning about task
dependencies, such as retrieving food from the refrigerator before heating it in the microwave, and
coordinating multiple physical interactions toward broader objectives.
We evaluate end-to-end autonomy using the final progress score aggregated over all task check-
points in each scenario. To further analyze policy and agent behavior, we introduce four detailed
metrics informed by the task-level capability dimensions: Task Coverage, the fraction of scenario tasks
with recorded task-relevant contact or native operation-start evidence; Task Progress, the total process
score earned across these engaged tasks divided by their total maximum process score; Recovery
Rate, the fraction of observable physical-failure segments followed by success of the same task; and
Stagnation, the fraction of execution time spent in repetitive, unproductive attempts or states with no
task progress. Appendix D gives the operational definitions and aggregation rules.
2.3
Robot Agent Framework for Evaluation
To systematically investigate the transition from standalone manipulation models to autonomous
robot agents, RobotAgentBench supports the evaluation of both standalone VLA/WAM policies and
diverse robot agent architectures. We introduce a unified planning-execution-verification framework
for robot agents: (1) a planner that interprets environmental context and user requirements, decom-
poses tasks, and adapts plans based on execution feedback; (2) an executor that translates instructions
into physical actions through learned policy models (e.g., VLA and WAM) or code-based control;
and (3) a verifier that monitors execution outcomes, identifies failures, and guides corrective actions.
By flexibly combining these components, we construct five representative agent architectures to
systematically examine the roles of planning, execution, verification, and recursive self-improvement
in robot autonomy. For clarity, we denote each architecture by its planner/executor/verifier (P/E/V)
configuration, where C represents code-based control, L denotes a learned VLA/WAM policy, H
denotes hybrid code-based and learned execution, and – indicates an absent component.
VLA/WAM-based Policy + Code-based Verifier (–/L/C).
A VLA/WAM policy directly generates
actions from observations and task instructions. A coding-agent verifier monitors execution feedback,
selectively executes action prefixes, and introduces local corrections before querying the policy
again [46].
Code-based Planner + VLA/WAM-based Policy (C/L/–).
A coding-agent planner interprets task
requirements, decomposes them into concrete subtask instructions, and delegates physical execution
to a VLA/WAM policy. The planner dynamically revises its instructions based on execution feedback [2,
17, 41].
Code-based Planner + Code-based Policy + Code-based Verifier (C/C/C).
A coding agent jointly
handles planning, execution, and verification by generating executable programs from predefined
control functions (e.g., move_eef_to()). It composes these functions to accomplish tasks and verifies
execution outcomes to guide subsequent actions [16, 18, 26].
Code-based Planner + Hybrid Code/VLA/WAM-based Policy + Code-based Verifier (C/H/C).
A
coding agent serves as both planner and verifier, while the executor integrates code-based control and


8
learned VLA/WAM policies as unified callable tools. The agent dynamically selects and combines these
tools according to task requirements and environmental feedback, leveraging their complementary
capabilities.
Code-based Planner + Code-based Policy + Code-based Verifier + RSI (C/C/C + RSI).
Building
upon the fully code-based architecture, we introduce harness-level recursive self-improvement (RSI),
enabling the coding agent to iteratively create, refine, and reuse control tools based on execution
feedback. Successful improvements are retained across subsequent attempts, supporting progressive
adaptation and experience accumulation [1].
3
Experiments
We conduct systematic experiments at both the task and scenario levels to evaluate standalone
VLA/WAM-based policies and complete robot agents. Building on the unified robot agent framework
we developed, we compare different harness designs and examine their effects on task performance.
We further evaluate agents in the corresponding real-world environment to investigate performance
gaps between simulation and reality.
3.1
Experimental Setup
Evaluated Models and Agents.
For standalone policy baselines, we evaluate VLA models and WAMs.
The VLA baselines include 𝜋0.5 [19], Xiaomi Robotics-1 [48], InternVLA-A1.5 [30], MolmoAct2 [10],
LingBot-VLA2 [51], HY-VLA [57], Galaxea G0.5 [29], and X-VLA [61]. The WAM baselines include
𝜏0 (low-level policy) [62], OpenWAM-Alpha [49], and LingBot-VA [25]. For complete robot agents,
we compare five representative harness designs, using Codex powered by GPT-6 Astra [35] as the
coding agent throughout and InternVLA-A1.5 as the learned policy wherever applicable. For the fully
code-based design, we additionally evaluate Claude Code powered by Opus 5.5 [3] and Antigravity
CLI powered by Gemini 4 Argon [22] to compare different coding agents under the same harness. For
the RSI setting, we run ten iterations, evaluating 30 instances per task or scenario in each iteration,
and report performance in the final iteration.
Evaluation Protocol.
We evaluate all methods across the five task-level assessments and four open-
ended scenarios. For each task or scenario, we evaluate 30 distinct instances across the full benchmark
suite. Evaluation covers all four open-ended scenario families and the 50-layout catalog, with every
applicable randomization axis enabled over its full benchmark-defined range: layout, appearance,
lighting, scene and physical distractors, and robot-arm, mobile-base, and interaction-object initial
states. The resulting comparisons measure performance across randomized instances within the
benchmark scene, task, and context families. All methods interact with the same environment and
22-dimensional command interface; model-specific observation adapters are described in Appendice C.
For open-ended scenarios, episodes terminate if the environment’s evaluator records no increase in
task progress for 120 consecutive seconds of simulation time. Coding agents are limited to 200 model
responses per episode, and each tool call executes at most 600 control steps, corresponding to 30
seconds of simulation time. We report the mean score under the corresponding evaluation metric for
each task or scenario. Comparisons and rank orders refer to these observed instance means under
the stated protocol.

Observation and Action Spaces.
We adopt observation and action spaces consistent with the
physical AgiBot G1 robot. All systems have access to the same observation sources: RGB images
from one head-mounted and two wrist-mounted cameras, proprioceptive states, task instructions or
contextual prompts, and a progressively revealed navigation map derived from static scene geometry
under shared disclosure rules. Model inputs follow their native adapters; LingBot-VA conditions on
RGB and language without proprioceptive state. The 22-dimensional action space controls the head,
torso, both arms and grippers, and mobile base. Detailed specifications are provided in Appendix A.

Standalone Policy Results.
Standalone policies appear to primarily imitate demonstrated manipu-
lation behaviors, with limited generalization to situations beyond the training distribution, such as
recovering from execution failures. They struggle particularly with task discovery, which requires pur-
poseful exploration rather than execution of a specified task. When initialized away from viewpoints
commonly encountered in the demonstrations, these models often produce erratic movements instead
of navigating to gather relevant observations. They also struggle with contextual reasoning, such
as selecting a beverage that matches user preferences, with performance generally close to random
guessing. Among the evaluated standalone models, InternVLA-A1.5 achieves the strongest overall
performance, with notably higher robustness and failure recovery scores than other models, even
surpassing some complete robot agents.
Robot Agent Results.
Introducing coding agents substantially improves robot performance. Among
fully code-based systems, Antigravity CLI has the highest reported mean score, followed by Claude
Code. For Codex, RSI further improves performance through tool refinement and experience accumu-
lation across task iterations. We also compare three ways of integrating coding agents with learned
policies. Refining policy-generated actions (-/L/C) yields modest gains but remains constrained by
the underlying policy’s capabilities. Using a coding agent as a planner (C/L/-) improves manipulation
and robustness, with the largest gains in context reasoning, as the planner translates contextual
information into explicit instructions for the executor. The best performance comes from combining
code-based control with selectively invoked learned policies (C/H/C). For example, code-based control
positions the gripper near an object, providing a favorable starting state for the policy to complete
the grasp.

Agent Harness Analysis
Building on our evaluation of robot agent systems, we further investigate how different harness
designs affect robot performance, including which coding agents are most effective as planners
and which policy models integrate best with coding-agent harnesses. We also examine how agent
performance evolves over multiple rounds of recursive self-improvement.
Which Coding Agent Makes a Better Planner?
We select the three best-performing VLA models,
Xiaomi Robotics-1, InternVLA-A1.5, and 𝜋0.5, along with the best-performing WAM, OpenWAM-Alpha,
as executors and pair each with different coding-agent planners. As shown in Figure 4, Antigravity
CLI yields the largest performance gains across the evaluated policy executors. To investigate these
differences, we randomly sample 10 open-ended episodes per planner and systematically compare
their instruction styles in Figure 5. Antigravity CLI produces shorter instructions and switches
them less frequently. Its instructions also contain fewer execution-specific details, such as grasping
strategies or hand selection, and use simpler vocabulary. In contrast, Codex often specifies detailed
manipulation procedures, including which hand to use. These observations suggest that concise,
high-level instructions may be easier for VLA/WAM-based executors to follow, potentially because
they better match the language encountered during training. Longer, more detailed instructions may
instead introduce complexity that these policies struggle to interpret.

Related Works
Robot Manipulation Benchmarks.
Robot manipulation benchmarks evaluate increasingly diverse
and complex tasks, yet largely retain predefined goals and task-specific demonstrations. RLBench [21],
Meta-World [56], ManiSkill2 [14], and LIBERO [27] assess individual manipulation skills through
dedicated tasks, while RoboCasa [34], BEHAVIOR [24, 45], and RoboTwin [6, 33] extend coverage
to diverse household activities and environments. VLABench [58] and LIBERO-Long [27] extend
evaluation to longer-horizon tasks, providing complete demonstrations that allow policies to learn
entire task sequences through imitation. More recent benchmarks, including RoboDojo [7], RoboCere-
bra [15], and RoboMME [9], assess reasoning, planning, and memory alongside physical execution,
but their narrowly specified tasks offer limited coverage of context-dependent household assistance.
RobotAgentBench evaluates diverse agentic capabilities through task-level assessments and open-ended
household scenarios, with training demonstrations primarily teaching reusable skills that agents must
select and compose according to the current situation.
Embodied Planning Benchmarks.
Beyond robot manipulation benchmarks, some work abstracts
physical execution into predefined callable functions, focusing on evaluating the planning and
reasoning capabilities of models in simulated embodied activities. ALFRED [42], ALFWorld [43],
RealFRED [23], and EmbodiedBench [53] assess household instruction following and planning,
while WorldLines [59] emphasizes persistent world state and memory. TEACh [36], DialFRED [11],
KnowNo [39], AmbiK [20], and Ask-to-Act [38] further evaluate interaction and clarification under
incomplete or ambiguous instructions. However, by abstracting away physical control, these bench-
marks provide a limited assessment of how planning and reasoning must adapt to execution outcomes,
particularly when recovering from failures in real-world tasks.
Robot Agents
Robot agents integrate reasoning and physical execution through different architec-
tures. SayCan [2], Inner Monologue [17], and Hi Robot [41] combine high-level planning by language
or vision-language models with learned skills or policies. Code as Policies [26], ProgPrompt [44],
Instruct2Act [16], and VoxPoser [18] use generated programs to connect perception, planning, and
control. Harness VLA [60] combines learned policies with analytic control primitives within an agentic
framework. Recent approaches further enable execution-time correction of policy actions [46] and
feedback-driven improvement of tools and control code [1, 12]. RobotAgentBench provides a unified
benchmark for systematically evaluating these robot agent designs.

5
Conclusion
In this work, we introduce RobotAgentBench (RAB), a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating
open-world robot autonomy beyond predefined manipulation tasks. Built upon an agent-assisted
real-to-sim pipeline, RAB provides multi-level evaluations spanning five fundamental capabilities and
open-ended household scenarios, enabling systematic assessment of robot agents from task discovery
and contextual reasoning to physical execution and adaptive recovery. Through extensive evaluations
of VLA/WAM models and diverse robot agent architectures, we reveal a substantial gap between
manipulation capabilities and autonomous task completion. Our findings demonstrate the benefits
of coding-agent harnesses, hybrid execution, and recursive self-improvement, while highlighting
persistent challenges in planning–execution coordination, failure recovery, and real-world deployment.
Despite recent advances, reliable autonomy in open-ended scenarios remains far from solved.
We hope RAB serves as a systematic testbed for understanding these limitations and advancing
the transition from robot manipulation toward open-world robot autonomy. Building on RAB, we
aim to extend agent-level recursive self-improvement (RSI) into the physical open world. Through
autonomous exploration and interaction, robot agents could discover new tasks, learn from failures,
and continuously refine their planning and execution strategies. Accumulating and transferring
reusable experience across tasks and environments could pave the way toward continually self-
improving robot autonomy with minimal human intervention.

A
Benchmark Setup
This section describes the information available to evaluated systems, the robot action space, and the shared
simulation settings. Task and scenario definitions are provided in Appendix B.
A.1
Provided Information
The evaluation interface provides all systems with access to the same information sources: task instructions
and context, camera observations, robot-state feedback, and a progressively revealed navigation map. The
same information disclosure rules apply to all systems. How this information is represented, integrated, and
used depends on the model architecture, input adapter, and agent design. Model-specific inputs are described
in Appendix C.
Task instructions and context.
Each episode provides a task instruction or a high-level scenario request.
Context-dependent settings additionally supply information such as user preferences, household routines, and
storage arrangements. The information provided for each task family is described in Appendix B.
Visual observations.
The benchmark records synchronized RGB observations from one head-mounted camera
at 1280 × 800 and two wrist-mounted cameras at 848 × 480. Learned policies receive updated observations
after executing an action prefix; the prediction horizon and executed prefix length are separate quantities.
Code-based agents can request observations through the evaluation interface.
Robot state.
We use the AgiBot G1 as the robot embodiment. Its state is represented by a 23-dimensional
vector containing twenty joint channels and the mobile base’s world-frame position (𝑥, 𝑦) and unwrapped
yaw. The joint channels comprise fourteen arm joints, two torso/waist joints, two head joints, and two gripper
channels. Torso lift is expressed in metres, while angular channels are expressed in radians. Table 4 summarizes
the state and action representations.
Navigation map.
Map-assisted evaluations provide a read-only top-down navigation map at a resolution
of 0.04 m. The map is derived from static scene geometry rather than reconstructed from RGB images. It
is progressively revealed using the measured head-camera pose, field of view, and static occlusion, with a
small local region around the robot treated as known. The same generation and revelation rules apply across
participating systems; revealed regions may differ because systems choose different viewpoints and trajectories.
The map distinguishes known free floor, static obstacles, and unexplored regions, and displays the robot’s
measured position, heading, and executed trajectory. The complete map, generated movable objects, task
labels, and target poses are not exposed. No planned routes or automatic navigation actions are provided.
Systems must choose and execute their own movements and use camera observations to assess movable objects.
A.2
Action Space
Each action is a 22-dimensional whole-body command containing fourteen arm-joint targets, two torso/waist
targets, two head-joint targets, two continuous gripper targets, and two mobile-base commands specifying
forward velocity and yaw rate. Joint commands specify target positions, while base commands specify velocities.
The channel definitions, units, and nominal ranges are summarized in Table 4.
A.3
Simulation and Execution Settings
Scene construction.
We first construct a digital twin of a real home, matching its layout, furniture dimensions,
and appearance. We then create additional layouts by modifying floor plans, including T-shaped and L-shaped
configurations, and rearranging functional areas. All layouts are manually reviewed for geometric validity and
plausible furniture arrangements. The resulting catalog contains 50 distinct layouts, with additional visual
variation in furniture colours, textures, materials, and interior styles.

Physics and control.
Robot parameters, camera calibration, asset properties, and scene settings are resolved
before each episode and remain fixed across its subtasks. Physics integration runs at 60 or 240 Hz, depending
on the configuration, while the action interface operates at 20 Hz. The integration rate and backend remain
unchanged within an episode. Cloth manipulation may use a dedicated soft-body solver. Grasping relies on
physical contact and friction between the gripper pads and objects.
Continuous execution and scoring.
Task transitions do not reset the scene, object states, or accumulated
progress. Task processes and appliance dynamics persist as the robot moves between workspaces. Progress
is tracked throughout the episode, while placement credit is updated using the current physical state, so
disturbing an earlier placement can reduce its final credit. At episode termination, all registered tasks are
finalized using their task-specific scoring rules. Task-specific completion criteria are provided in Appendix B.

B
Task and Scenario Descriptions
The following sections describe the tasks and scenarios used to evaluate robot autonomy. Input descriptions
refer to the environment interface; individual policy inputs follow the adapters in Appendix C. Manipulation
includes 18 short tasks and 8 long tasks. Task discovery and context reasoning assess whether agents identify
relevant objects through gripper contact. Failure recovery evaluates execution from predefined failure states,
while robustness evaluates adaptation to disturbances during execution. Four open-ended household scenarios
combine task discovery, reasoning, and manipulation under high-level user requests. The figures illustrate task
settings and procedures using demonstrations and diagnostic renders, rather than evaluated-agent results.
B.1
Manipulation
Task overview. We evaluate manipulation through 18 short tasks and 8 long tasks, covering both individual
manipulation skills and their composition into more complex household activities.
Input and setting. The agent receives a task instruction, camera observations, and robot-state feedback. Each
episode starts from a task-specific scene. Long tasks require continuous execution across subgoals without
resetting the environment.
Completion and scoring. Performance is measured using task-specific process scores. All 18 short tasks and 8
long tasks contribute to the manipulation score.

Task overview. We evaluate the agent’s ability to infer, plan, and execute household tasks from a high-level

Input and setting. Each room is generated from a selected layout and a scenario-specific task specification

execution, respecting dependencies such as preparing food before serving it.

a score increase resets this shared timer; switching tasks or changing locations does not. Scores are finalized at
termination. This timeout limits inactivity rather than total episode duration.

C.1
Shared training setup
Training data and split.
We fine-tune all eleven policy baselines using demonstrations from the 101.8-
hour RobotAgentBench simulation dataset, covering atomic actions, short tasks, long tasks, and open-ended
household scenarios. All models use the same episode-level training/validation split.
Data representation and preprocessing.
Demonstrations follow a unified format with 23-dimensional robot
states and 22-dimensional whole-body actions, as defined in Table 4. We adapt the data to each model’s
required input format, including image resizing, normalization, and multi-view arrangement. State and action
normalization statistics are computed from the training split. Image augmentations are applied only during
training. Model-specific adaptations are described below.

Recovery Rate.
Recovery Rate is the percentage of observable physical-failure segments followed by task-
specific success of the same task at a later control step. Repeated failures before recovery form one segment.
API errors, formatting errors, and termination-only settlement events are excluded.

ages. Zero denominators are reported as undefined.
Stagnation.
Stagnation measures the fraction of simulation time spent in repeated physical execution without
progress. An interval requires at least three executions that advance simulation; only time beyond a 30-second
grace period counts. Task-score increases, manipulation progress, or newly explored map area interrupt the
interval. Successful navigation exceeding 2 cm translation or 0.035 rad rotation is excluded.
State loops also qualify when a cycle of at most eight calls repeats at least three times over at least 30 seconds
without task or map progress; the first cycle is excluded. Matching tolerances are 2 cm for base position,
0.035 rad for joints and base yaw, and 1 cm/0.052 rad for hand position/orientation. Overlapping intervals
count once. Time follows the 20 Hz simulation clock, excluding API waiting.

D.2
Behavioural patterns and execution issues
Analysis protocol.
We analyse interaction traces of Codex, Claude Code, and Antigravity CLI across the
four open-ended scenarios. Figure 55(a) groups submitted action attempts and deduplicated public planning
statements into eight behavioural categories. Compound scripts count once, and verification includes only
standalone checks. Task-event shares exclude setup and unclassified events and are averaged equally across
scenarios after within-episode normalization.
Panel (b) summarizes identified execution issues. Calls are weighted inversely by each episode’s submitted-
action count, with equal agent and scenario weights before normalization over issue-bearing calls. Each call
receives at most one issue label. Navigation shortfalls require residual translation exceeding 2 cm or rotation
exceeding 0.02 rad. These percentages describe issue composition rather than the overall error rate.
Behavioural patterns.
Object manipulation accounts for 42.4% of Codex’s task events, compared with
16.1% for Claude Code and 28.6% for Antigravity CLI. Claude Code instead concentrates more interactions on
exploration, grounding, and base navigation, which together account for 57.3% of its events.
For all three agents, feedback-driven replanning is more frequent than explicit task and goal selection. Observed
adjustments include smaller approach steps and revised wrist orientations, illustrating the recurring need to
update actions using execution feedback.
Execution difficulties.
Motion-feasibility issues and execution shortfalls together account for 82.1% of
identified issues, compared with 15.3% for code or interface errors. These difficulties span both positioning
and object manipulation, suggesting that reliable execution requires more than correct tool calls.
Agents need to coordinate navigation, stance adjustment, and manipulation, and revise unsuccessful approaches
using execution feedback. Such revisions should be followed by verification that the intended physical progress
has occurred.
