Abstract
Robots, autonomous vehicles, and consumer 360° cameras
use fisheye lenses for wide fields of view (FoVs). Simulation-
ready digital twins require photorealistic rendering across
the full FoV and an aligned collision mesh. However, prior
fisheye Gaussian Splatting methods only rasterize up to
180° and do not produce meshes, while Gaussian surface
reconstruction methods assume pinhole training images.
We present GOFish, the first 3D Gaussian Splatting surface
reconstruction method to train directly on fisheye images,
rasterize FoVs beyond 180°, and extract an aligned mesh
from the same scene. Calibrated densification recovers the
surface across the entire FoV, while Calibrated Mip Filter-
ing and Wide-Angle Appearance Modeling keep pixel blur
and lens falloff out of the scene and mesh. Across the 200°
FIORD and 205° FullCircle benchmarks, GOFish improves
PSNR over the best prior method by 2.28 and 1.97 dB, re-
spectively. It also outperforms fisheye rendering baselines
within their own FoVs and achieves the highest mesh F-
score on FIORD and the main ScanNet++ set. Together, the
Gaussian scene and mesh form a LiDAR-free, simulation-
ready digital twin that renders in real time, ray traces the
full FoV, and provides collision geometry.
1. Introduction
Digital twins are virtual replicas of real environments used
for robot simulation and training. To support visual per-
ception and physical interaction, they must combine photo-
realistic appearance with aligned collision geometry. Fish-
eye cameras are commonly used for robotic perception and
mapping [21, 35], while consumer 360◦cameras pair back-
to-back fisheye lenses for omnidirectional scene capture [5].
LiDAR scans are expensive to collect at scale, motivating
recovery of both appearance and geometry directly from
calibrated fisheye images over their full field of view (FoV).
No prior 3D Gaussian Splatting [17] method delivers
both.
Fisheye Gaussian Splatting rasterizers [3, 14, 22,
31, 32] cover at most 180◦, while 3DGRT [24] ray traces
the full FoV but bakes pixel blur into the scene; neither
produces a mesh.
Conversely, Gaussian Opacity Fields
(GOF) [37] and other Gaussian surface methods [2, 7, 26,
27] extract aligned meshes but assume pinhole training im-
ages. Using fisheye captures therefore requires rectification,
which discards peripheral rays and prevents rendering the
native FoV required for accurate sensor simulation.
We present GOFish, the first 3D Gaussian Splatting
surface reconstruction method to train directly on fisheye
images, rasterize calibrated FoVs beyond 180◦, and ex-
tract an aligned mesh from the same Gaussian scene. A

2. Related Work
Gaussian surface methods extract meshes from 3D Gaus-
sian Splatting scenes [17].
Gaussian Opacity Fields
(GOF) [37] constructs a 3D opacity field by composit-
ing per-view Gaussian responses and taking the minimum
across views.
SOF [26] adds per-pixel sorting, geom-
etry losses, and fast extraction, while CoMe [27] adds
confidence-driven optimization.
PGSR [2] uses planar
Gaussians with geometric consistency. MiLo [7] jointly re-
fines a Gaussian scene and its extracted mesh. Other ap-
proaches use surface-oriented primitives or explicit mesh
connectivity [6, 12, 13, 39].
Existing Gaussian surface
methods assume pinhole or rectified inputs, precluding di-
rect mesh reconstruction from fisheye imagery.
Fisheye Gaussian Splatting methods train directly on dis-
torted images. Fisheye-GS [22] and DirectFisheye-GS [32]
modify the splatting projection, while Self-Calibrating
GS [3] learns a lens deformation. 3DGUT [31] supports ar-
bitrary camera models, and 3DGEER [14] evaluates Gaus-
sians exactly along rays up to 180◦. However, their raster-
izers stop short of the full per-lens FoVs of the dual-fisheye
360◦cameras used to capture FIORD and FullCircle [5, 8].
3DGRT [24] ray traces the full field of view with one ray
per pixel, baking pixel blur into the Gaussian scene while
rendering substantially more slowly than rasterized meth-
ods. Omnidirectional methods [5, 19, 20] target panoramas
and casual 360◦captures. None of these methods includes
mesh extraction. GOFish instead trains directly on fisheye
images, rasterizes the full field of view, and extracts a mesh
from the same Gaussian scene.
When unmodeled, sampling and appearance effects are
absorbed into the Gaussian scene, degrading rendering and
mesh extraction. Mip-Splatting [36] suppresses aliasing in
pinhole Gaussian Splatting with 3D and screen-space fil-
ters. Gaussian surface methods use VastGaussian’s appear-
ance model [23] to keep photometric inconsistencies from
creating spurious geometry [26, 37].
PPISP [4] instead
limits corrections to camera effects, but its low-order vi-
gnetting misses steep rim falloff in wide-FoV fisheye im-
ages. GOFish adapts Mip-Splatting’s filters to calibrated
fisheye cameras and extends PPISP to model this falloff.
3. Background
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [17] represents a scene us-
ing Gaussians, each with mean µi, covariance Σi, opac-
ity oi, and view-dependent color. Its rasterizer linearizes
a pinhole projection at each Gaussian mean [40], so the
standard rasterizer cannot consume native fisheye images
or represent rays beyond 90◦. GOFish instead builds on
3DGUT [31], which evaluates Gaussians directly in 3D
along calibrated pixel rays.

4. Method
GOFish builds on 3DGUT [31] to train one Gaussian scene
directly from fisheye images over the camera’s full field of
view (FoV) and extract an aligned mesh. Calibration defines
valid pixels, complete admission assigns Gaussians to tiles,
and calibrated densification recovers the surface. Calibrated
Mip Filtering and Wide-Angle Appearance Modeling keep
view-specific blur and sensor falloff out of the scene and
mesh. GOFish then extracts the mesh from the resulting
pixel-filter-free opacity field. Together, the Gaussian scene
and mesh form a simulation-ready digital twin.
4.1. Full Field-of-View Rasterization
3DGUT’s rasterizer defines a ray for each pixel, assigns
Gaussians to image tiles, and composites them along each
ray in depth order. As Figure 2 shows, each step breaks
at wide angles: rays outside its 160◦field of view are cut
and receive no supervision, projected extents omit Gaus-
sians from tiles their rays reach, and depth order reverses
beyond 90◦. GOFish replaces these steps with a calibration-
defined domain, complete admission, and ray-distance sort-
ing, extending rasterization to the full calibrated FoV.

4.2. Calibrated Mip Filtering
Complete ray coverage alone is not enough: each image
pixel averages radiance over a finite footprint whose size
and shape vary across a fisheye image. The 3D Gaussian
scene is shared by every view and later meshed, so this
view-specific blur must remain outside it. Without a pixel
filter, however, the exact ray response can match the blur
only by baking it into the scene. For pinhole cameras, Mip-
Splatting [36] separates a view-independent 3D scene filter
from a 2D pixel filter. The 3D filter limits scene detail to
what the finest training view can resolve, while the 2D filter
matches each view’s pixel footprint.

tion at µi. The smaller singular value σmin(Ji,v) is the
coarser of the view’s two image-space sampling rates. Us-

4.3. Calibrated Densification
The calibrated Jacobian also drives the densification that re-
covers the surface. A 3D gradient measures image-space
displacement in world units, whose pixel equivalent varies
with distance and ray angle, while a pinhole projection fails
beyond 90◦. We therefore map each mean’s gradient into
the calibrated image with J+⊤, allowing one image-space
threshold and SOF’s densification schedule to serve the full
FoV. Appendix A.4 details the mapping and schedule. Cal-
ibrated Mip Filtering prevents Gaussians from fitting each
view’s blur; Section 6.2 shows that removing it produces
several times more Gaussians without improving quality.

5. Experiments
GOFish extends the 3DGUT [31] codebase. We evaluate
GOFish with and without SOF’s Geometry Objective [26]
from Section 4.5.
Each variant uses one parameter set
across datasets except ScanNet++, where, following prior
surface methods, we add CoMe’s confidence and normal-
variance losses [27] and use SOF’s scene-extent rule for
large-Gaussian pruning. Appendices A.4 and B provide ad-
ditional optimization and evaluation details.
Datasets.
FIORD [8] and FullCircle [5] use dual-fisheye
360◦cameras. FIORD pairs synchronized nominal 200◦
views with LiDAR; FullCircle provides roughly 205◦views
without geometry ground truth. ScanNet++ [34] provides
127◦fisheye DSLR images and laser-scan ground truth. We
use 3DGUT’s six scenes, while Appendix C additionally
evaluates CoMe’s set. For pinhole evaluation, we use six
Tanks & Temples [18] scenes with ground-truth geometry.
Appendix B.1 covers FIORD’s registration and calibration
issues; Appendix B.2 details scenes, resolutions, and splits.

8. Conclusion
We introduced GOFish, the first 3D Gaussian Splatting sur-
face reconstruction method to train directly on fisheye im-
ages, rasterize fields of view (FoVs) beyond 180◦, and ex-
tract an aligned mesh from the same Gaussian scene. A
calibration-defined domain and complete admission cover
every valid ray, while calibrated densification recovers
the surface across the FoV. Calibrated Mip Filtering and
Wide-Angle Appearance Modeling keep view-specific sam-
pling and camera effects out of the Gaussian scene and
mesh.
GOFish leads every rendering metric across the
200◦FIORD, 205◦FullCircle, 127◦ScanNet++, and pin-
hole Tanks & Temples benchmarks. Its meshes also achieve
the highest F-score on FIORD and the main ScanNet++ set.
Together, the Gaussian scene and mesh form a LiDAR-free,
simulation-ready digital twin that renders in real time, ray
traces the full FoV, and provides collision geometry.

A. Additional Method Details
We make the view index explicit, so Ji,v, Ci,v, and mi,v
denote the quantities of Eq. (4) for view v. Throughout,
∥·∥is the Euclidean norm for vectors and spectral norm for
matrices, ∥· ∥F is the Frobenius norm, and B(c, r) is the
closed Euclidean ball with center c and radius r.
A.1. Camera Domain
The camera domain determines which pixels participate in
rendering, supervision, admission, and meshing. For view
v, let Uv ⊂R2 be the continuous calibrated image do-
main and Pv its finite set of raster pixel centers. Let dv(u)
denote the unit ray direction obtained by unprojecting u,
when that direction is unique. We define the valid domain
as Dv = {u ∈Uv : dv(u) exists uniquely}, and rendering
and admission operate on Pv ∩Dv. For the OpenCV fisheye
model, a ray at angle θ from the optical axis maps to image
radius ρ(θ) = θ(1 + k1θ2 + k2θ4 + k3θ6 + k4θ8). Here
k1, . . . , k4 are the calibrated radial-distortion coefficients.
Rays are unique up to θmax of Eq. (2). Dataset masks do
not alter Dv. Masks apply only to supervision, densification
statistics, and PPISP [4] pooling. Rendering, reporting, and
meshing use the full domain. Losses that compare neigh-
boring pixels, such as SSIM, include only pixels whose full
stencil lies within the domain. We measure camera-local
distance as view-space depth for pinhole cameras and radial
ray distance for fisheye cameras, and use the same conven-
tion for SOF’s distortion coordinate [26].

50 knots over the outer half of this radius, and the flat field

B. Additional Experimental Details
B.1. FIORD Data
FIORD [8] provides full-FoV fisheye images with metric
ground truth. Figure 8 shows the excluded SfM trajecto-
ries, Corridor_Out’s re-registration, and field-of-view
calibration. We render seven of its ten scenes and evaluate
geometry on six after method-independent registration and
calibration checks.
Excluded scenes.
The authors’ SfM reconstructions of
Night_Out, Building_Out, and Upstairs_In do
not form coherent camera trajectories. Every retained cam-
era is within 0.29 SfM units of another; the most isolated
cameras in these scenes are 1.21, 0.96, and 100.6 units from
the rest, respectively. In Upstairs_In, the outlier is sup-
ported by only 33 of the scene’s 22,247 keypoints and lies
234 m from the remaining trajectory. We therefore exclude
all three; Night_Out also lacks LiDAR ground truth.
Registration.
Corridor_Out’s images and poses are
internally consistent and retained for rendering, but its
SfM–LiDAR registration is invalid. Global re-registration
with Fast Point Feature Histograms (FPFH), Random
Sample Consensus (RANSAC), and robust Iterative Clos-
est Point (ICP) reduces the camera-up tilt from 14.6◦
to 1.7◦but leaves an inlier root-mean-square error of
0.114 m, above our 0.10 m limit.
We therefore exclude
Corridor_Out from the geometry mean and report it
only for rendering. A method-independent ICP alignment
between Hall_In’s SfM points and LiDAR consistently
corrects its 3.4◦tilt, and we apply it to all methods.
Field-of-view calibration.
The published FIORD intrin-
sics are extrapolated at the image-circle boundary. On six
cameras, the calibration maps this boundary to total FoVs
of 198◦to 244◦for a nominal 200◦lens. On the other eight,
it loses monotonicity before the boundary, which ends the
valid domain between 186◦and 229◦. Six of the 14 cam-
eras contain a dark band at calibrated total field angles of at
14


least 167◦, covering 3.1–5.7% of the valid image domain.
Although the calibration assigns scene rays to this band, its
95th-percentile training luma remains below 0.10 because
it receives no scene light in any training frame. A static
scene cannot reproduce this camera-fixed darkness, so the
per-camera attenuation W of Sec. 4.4 absorbs it without en-
tering the scene. We retain the full calibrated FoV, including
this band, when scoring every method. Excluding it lowers
Full PSNR by 0.07 dB for the Geometry Objective variant
and 0.04 dB for DirectFisheye-GS [32].
B.2. Evaluation Protocol
Datasets.
Camera-native methods train on 816×816
FIORD images and 720×720 FullCircle images per
lens [5], both downsampled by 4×. ScanNet++ [34] uses
876×584 DSLR fisheye images downsampled by 2×, while
Tanks & Temples [18] uses images near 980×545 from
GOF’s preprocessing [37]. Rectified methods use match-
ing 800×800 images for FIORD and FullCircle and the
official undistorted 876×584 images for ScanNet++. Ev-
ery eighth image is held out for testing, except FullCircle,
which uses separately captured tripod frames. ScanNet++
uses the six scenes of 3DGUT and Fisheye-GS [22, 31],
and Tanks & Temples uses GOF’s six scenes.
Rendering.
We use no test-time color, exposure, or pose
fitting and leave learned image processors unchanged. Full
is the calibrated FoV of Appendix A.1, excluding only the
tripod pixels of FullCircle’s test mask. Common is the recti-
fied training raster; camera-native predictions are resampled
onto it with the fixed rectification operator.
Camera-native methods use their released renderers. For
rectified methods, we port each rasterizer to the calibrated
lens while preserving its activations, filters, sorting, al-
pha rules, appearance handling, and background, with no
GOFish component.
On rectified test views, every port
matches its released renderer within 0.01 dB PSNR, 0.0002
SSIM, and 0.0004 LPIPS [38] on every scene. SOF and
CoMe [26, 27] match byte for byte. For each FullCircle
scene, we use whichever of two GOF ports, differing only
in numeric precision, passes this check.
To avoid renderer conversion, the GOFish @ FoV
columns use each baseline’s released renderer on its own
domain and score GOFish on the same pixels. That do-
main contains pixels whose ray angle is at most half the
renderer’s listed FoV. SSIM uses an 11×11 Gaussian win-
dow and LPIPS uses VGG, both restricted to each scored
region; on the GOFish @ FoV domains, LPIPS is averaged
over 64×64 tiles. On Tanks & Temples, each method uses
its released pinhole renderer; when dimensions differ, we
resize only the prediction with a fixed operator.
Geometry.
Every baseline retains its released mesh post-
processing; GOFish uses Appendix A.5. On FIORD, ob-
served ground truth comprises LiDAR points that hidden-
point removal [16] finds visible within the calibrated do-
main from at least two training cameras.
Recall counts
a point as recovered when its nearest prediction is within
10 cm.
As on Tanks & Temples and ScanNet++, preci-
sion is evaluated only in regions supported by the ground
truth. Predicted samples must lie inside the LiDAR’s axis-
aligned bounding volume and be observable by the same
test from at least one training camera. Both visibility se-
lections depend only on the ground truth and training cam-
eras. On ScanNet++, every method follows CoMe’s pro-
tocol of scaled ICP per mesh, cropping to the ground-truth
volume, 2.5 cm voxel downsampling, and a 5 cm threshold.
On Tanks & Temples, we use the official crop, alignment,
thresholds, and evaluator.
Runtime.
All times are wall-clock measurements on one
NVIDIA A10G GPU. Training includes the full schedule
and the novel-view controller phase of PPISP [4]; meshing
includes extraction and post-processing of the 30k scene.
Rendering speed is measured at each method’s evaluation
resolution over 200 synchronized held-out frames after 20
warm-ups, timing only the trained-scene renderer; GOFish
uses Appendix A.6. GOFish’s training times use an opti-
mized build with a bit-identical forward pass and gradients
that agree up to rounding; metrics use standard runs.

Self-Calibrating GS [3] initializes its lens network from
a fisheye calibration file. For the pinhole Tanks & Temples
images, we supply a zero-distortion pinhole calibration, so
the lens starts at identity rather than its equidistant fallback.
On ScanNet++, its 90◦cube-face guard rescales only the
vertical focal length, although the same intrinsics define
the fisheye-to-cube mapping. We retain the calibrated focal
length for that mapping and keep the face intrinsics consis-
tent in the evaluation cameras. We also use the dataset’s
Kannala–Brandt calibration instead of its equidistant de-
fault for the base lens mapping. Training does not save the
cubemap network, so the method’s own initialization refits
it before the released held-out render pass used for timing.
3DGUT and 3DGRT [24, 31] use a released loader that
sets FullCircle’s field-of-view limit from the image corner.
The corner lies beyond the calibration’s monotone range,
producing folded rays and collapsing 3DGUT training on
room3 to 11.7 dB Full PSNR. We cap the limit at the end of
the monotone range and assign no ray beyond it, matching
FullCircle’s treatment of border pixels.
3DGEER [14] uses KB mode on FIORD and FullCir-

following its released configurations at our splits and reso-

the KB model, because the release’s three-step Newton in-

of FIORD’s six geometry scenes [8]:
Kitchen_In,

MeetingRoom_In,

The Building_In runs used an earlier training config-
uration, while unfiltered Hall_In training ran out of GPU
memory. With only the image-space statistic, only SOF’s
schedule [26], or only the filter, the mean F-score is 0.146,
0.214, and 0.140, against 0.301 for the full row on these
scenes. On Kitchen_In and MeetingRoom_In, where
both variants produce meshes, calibrated densification with-
out the filter scores 0.573 and 0.503, against 0.560 and
0.505 with it.
With and without the filter, Full PSNR differs by at
most 0.1 dB on these four scenes and on Corridor_Out,
which has no geometry ground truth.
The filter re-
duces the Gaussian count by 2.2–5.4×, from 1.05–2.94 M
to 0.42–0.69 M, and raises real-time rendering speed on
Kitchen_In and MeetingRoom_In from 65 to 86–
90 FPS on an NVIDIA A10G GPU. Without the filter,
Bridge_Out mesh extraction ran out of GPU memory,
while Road_Out mesh extraction did not finish. Thus cal-
ibrated densification drives surface recovery, while filtering
controls scene size and memory.
Run-to-run variation.
Each of three repetitions covers
every evaluated FIORD scene for both GOFish and PGSR.
Across the six geometry scenes, base GOFish averages an
F-score of 0.245 ± 0.001 versus 0.239 ± 0.002 for PGSR,
and every GOFish run exceeds every PGSR run. Its seven-
scene mean Full PSNR varies by only ±0.02 dB. The F-
score gap therefore exceeds run-to-run variation.
Geometry by region.
We assign each FIORD ground-
truth point and predicted sample the smallest ray angle from
which any training camera observes it. We separately iden-
tify samples observed by any camera within its 90◦rectifi-
cation. This rectified region contains 94% of the observed
ground truth. GOFish leads every surface method within
the shared 90◦FoV, 0.254 against 0.243 for PGSR, and for
rays beyond 90◦from the optical axis, 0.112 against 0.102
for CoMe. Its overall advantage therefore does not come
only from evaluating the wider FoV.
C.2. Efficiency
Table 6 separates training, meshing, and rendering cost
from representation size. On FIORD, GOFish completes
training and mesh extraction in 153 minutes, faster end
to end than GOF’s 208 minutes [37].
Its real-time ren-
derer reaches 79 FPS, close to the 84 FPS of rendering-only
3DGUT + PPISP [4, 31] and more than three times the 22
FPS of 3DGRT + PPISP [24]. The same renderer reaches
99 FPS on ScanNet++ and 82 FPS on Tanks & Temples.
Despite covering the full FoV, GOFish averages 0.60 mil-
lion Gaussians on FIORD, compared with 1.03 million for
3DGUT + PPISP. The optional Geometry Objective adds
little end-to-end cost and does not reduce rendering speed.
