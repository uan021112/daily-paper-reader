# OneScene-Bench: Do Vision-Language Models Integrate Evidence Across Views? 

Swathi Suhas Anurag Das Bernt Schiele Jonas Fischer 

Max Planck Institute for Informatics, Saarland Informatics Campus, Saarbrücken, Germany 

## Abstract 

Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly process multiple views of the same scene, but it remains unclear whether they integrate these observations into a co-herent scene-level representation. Existing multi-image evaluations do not isolate this capability, as tasks may admit correct solutions through independent per-view processing without requiring cross-view correspondence. We introduce OneScene-Bench , a controlled benchmark designed to explicitly distinguish when cross-view integration is necessary. Our benchmark evaluates three complementary capabilities on real-world multi-view scenes: unique object counting, cross-view consistency, and spatial reasoning. For counting, we control object overlap across views to sep-arate cases solvable by simple per-view aggregation from those requiring instance-level correspondence. For consistency, we introduce localized edits in shared and view-specific regions, testing whether models distinguish genuine scene contradic-tions from differences caused by viewpoint and visibility. For spatial reasoning, we construct object relations that require combining spatial evidence across viewpoints to compare physical proximity. We evaluate twelve state-of-the-art open-weight and proprietary VLMs and find that counting accuracy drops substantially on comple-mentary views, ranging from 4.5–50.0%, compared with 45.5–81.5% on complete-overlap examples. Moreover, cross-view consistency remains around 50% across models, including recent proprietary VLMs, despite substantially stronger perfor-mance on spatial reasoning. Our results reveal a substantial gap between processing multiple images and integrating evidence across them, highlighting the need for evaluations that explicitly test scene-level multi-view understanding. Code and data are available at https://github.com/swathisuhas/OneScene-Bench .

## 1 Introduction 

Vision-language models (VLMs) increasingly support multiple images as input, enabling them to reason over different observations of the same physical scene. Unlike generic multi-image understanding, multi-view scene understanding requires models to recognize repeated objects across viewpoints, account for viewpoint-dependent visibility, and combine complementary observations into a coherent representation of the scene. These capabilities are particularly important in embodied settings such as robotics and autonomous systems, where an agent observes the same environment from multiple viewpoints but must reason about it as a single scene. However, multi-view input does not necessarily imply multi-view reasoning. Consider counting unique objects across two views. A model should recognize repeated instances across viewpoints and count each physical object only once. Yet when both views contain the same objects, or one contains only a subset of the other, taking the larger per-view count gives the correct answer. When each view contains instances not visible in the other, the model must instead determine which objects are shared to recover the total. Recent benchmarks evaluate reasoning across multiple images and viewpoints in diverse environments [8, 18, 37, 42]. However, these evaluations do not explicitly distinguish between predictions that require cross-view integration and those that can be solved through independent per-view processing. As a result, strong benchmark performance does not necessarily mean that a model has integrated and correctly linked information across views. A reliable evaluation must therefore control whether cross-view integration is actually necessary to obtain the correct answer. We introduce OneScene-Bench , a human-annotated, controlled benchmark for evaluating whether VLMs reconcile multiple views as observations of the same physical scene (Figure 1). Our central design principle is to control the evidence required to solve each example. For multi-view counting, we construct three configurations of cross-view object overlap: complete overlap , subset overlap , and 

complementary views . The first two admit a max-count shortcut, whereas in complementary views each image contributes instances not visible in the other, making per-view counts alone insufficient. Comparing performance across these configurations directly measures whether counting ability persists when cross-view integration becomes necessary. We probe the same capability with a second task, cross-view consistency . Differences between two views do not necessarily imply differences in the scene, as objects may be out of frame or occluded. We introduce localized edits either in regions visible from both viewpoints, creating a genuine contradiction between the views, or in regions visible from only one, leaving the pair compatible. This tests whether a model understands which evidence should agree across viewpoints, rather than merely detecting differences between images. Finally, 

multi-view spatial reasoning evaluates whether models can combine evidence across viewpoints to compare the physical proximity of objects. All examples are constructed from real-world multi-view captures [21] and manually verified. We evaluate twelve open-weight and proprietary VLMs using an open-ended protocol and analyze both their performance and failure modes. Across all models, counting performance drops on complementary views: accuracy ranges from 45.5–81.5% on complete overlap and 26.7–76.7% on subset overlap, but only 4.5–50.0% on complementary views, where cross-view correspondence and deduplication are required. Cross-view consistency accuracy remains around 50%, i.e., random 2chance, across models, with models responding similarly to edits in shared and view-specific regions, indicating limited sensitivity to cross-view visibility. We further attribute counting predictions to simple per-view aggregation strategies and study sensitivity to image order, removal of visual input, and explicit reasoning. Together, these results reveal a clear gap between processing multiple images and correctly performing visual reasoning across them. We summarize our contributions as follows: • We introduce OneScene-Bench , comprising 1,300 manually verified examples from real-world multi-view scenes across counting, consistency, and spatial reasoning. • We develop controlled diagnostics for cross-view integration : an overlap-based counting taxonomy that distinguishes cases where per-view aggregation is sufficient from those where cross-view integration is necessary, and a consistency intervention that controls whether visual differences occur in regions shared across viewpoints. • We provide a broad evaluation of twelve VLMs under an open-ended protocol, together with analyses of aggregation behavior, image-order sensitivity, no-image bias, explicit reasoning, and scaling with additional views, revealing a persistent gap between processing multiple images and integrating them into a coherent scene-level representation. 

## 2 Related Work 

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). Early MLLMs established the main paradigms for integrating vision with large language models. Flamingo [2] introduced gated cross-attention to condition an LLM on interleaved images and text, enabling multimodal few-shot learning. BLIP-2 [20] proposed the Q-Former as an efficient interface between frozen vision encoders and LLMs, while LLaVA [22] demonstrated the effectiveness of visual instruction tuning for general visual conversation and reasoning. Building on these foundations, proprietary models such as GPT-4V [26], Gemini [29, 30], and Claude 3 [4] demonstrated broad multimodal reasoning across images, videos, and other modalities. Similarly, open-weight models, including the Qwen-VL series [6, 34, 7], LLaVA-OneVision [19], InternVL [35], MiniCPM-V [39], GLM-4.6V [16], Phi-4-Multimodal [1], and Kimi-VL [32], extended visual instruction tuning to high-resolution images, multiple images, videos, and long multimodal contexts. We evaluate representative models from both proprietary and open-weight families. 

Multi-Image Understanding Benchmarks. Multi-image evaluation has progressed from task-specific benchmarks to broader evaluation suites. BLINK [14], Mementos [36], and MileBench [11] focus on visual perception, image sequences, and long multimodal contexts, respectively. MIRB [41], MIBench [23], MMIU [25], MIMIC [10], MUIRBench [33], and MMRB [9] further evaluate diverse perceptual, semantic, and reasoning relations across images. However, these benchmarks often combine related or sequential images rather than different views of the same physical scene. Multi-view benchmarks study this more specific setting across several domains. MV-RoboBench [12] targets spatial understanding and robotic execution, while UrBench [42] and CityCube [37] evaluate urban understanding across ground-level, vehicle, aerial, and satellite views. All-Angles Bench [38] includes counting and geometric reasoning in real-world scenes, whereas MultiView-Bench [40] and TriViewBench [8] study world-centric reasoning and scene recovery in synthetic 3D environments. SpatialMosaic-Bench [18] focuses on integrating fragmented spatial evidence under occlusion and limited overlap. Although these benchmarks cover important aspects of multi-view understanding, they generally do not explicitly separate questions solvable through independent per-view processing from those requiring cross-view integration. OneScene-Bench isolates this distinction by controlling object overlap for counting, the visibility of localized edits for consistency, and the distribution of relational evidence for spatial reasoning. These controlled tasks test whether models reconcile multiple observations into a coherent object-centric representation of the same physical scene.

3.1 Multi-View Counting 

Given two views of the same scene and a target object category, the model must predict the number of 

unique physical instances visible across the views. Let S1 and S2 denote the sets of target instances visible in each view. The scene-level count is 

C = |S1 ∪ S2|. (1) We control the relationship between S1 and S2 to distinguish cases that admit a simple per-view shortcut from those that require cross-view correspondence. 

Complete overlap. Both views contain the same target instances, S1 = S2. The correct scene-level count equals either per-view count. We include 200 examples. 

Subset overlap. One view contains a subset of the instances in the other, S1 ⊂ S2 or S2 ⊂ S1. The correct answer can still be recovered as the maximum of the two per-view counts. We include 300 examples. 

Complementary views. Each view contains at least one instance absent from the other, S1 \ S2̸ = ∅

and S2 \ S1̸ = ∅. Neither view alone contains all instances, and the maximum per-view count is insufficient. Correct prediction therefore requires identifying repeated instances across views while incorporating instances visible from only one viewpoint. We include 200 examples. 4Solvability. All three configurations require aggregating observations across views to recover a scene-level count, but they differ in the cross-view information required for this aggregation. In complete overlap, both views contain the same set of physical instances, while in subset overlap, the instances in one view are contained in the other. In both cases, the union in Eq. (1) equals the larger per-view set, so the scene-level count can be recovered without explicitly estimating instance overlap. In complementary views, neither view contains the complete set of instances; recovering the union additionally requires identifying which observations correspond to the same physical instances across views, i.e., determining |S1 ∩ S2|. These configurations therefore preserve the need for multi-view aggregation while controlling whether explicit instance-level correspondence is required. 

3.2 Cross-View Consistency 

Given two views, the model determines whether they are compatible as observations of the same underlying scene . This task requires reasoning about viewpoint-dependent visibility: an object appearing in only one image does not necessarily indicate a contradiction because its location may be outside the field of view or occluded in the other image. We construct controlled examples by introducing a localized object or visual artifact into one view using gpt-image-1.5 [27]. We vary only whether the edited location should be observable from the other viewpoint. An edit in a 

view-specific region , which is not observable from the other view, leaves the pair compatible with the same scene; the pair is labeled CONSISTENT . An edit in a shared observable region creates a contradiction between the two observations; the pair is labeled INCONSISTENT . Both conditions therefore contain an image-level difference, while differing in whether that difference implies a scene-level contradiction; detecting differences between images alone cannot separate them. We construct 300 balanced examples using this procedure. 

3.3 Multi-View Spatial Reasoning 

The spatial-reasoning task evaluates whether VLMs can integrate spatial evidence across viewpoints. Each question specifies three objects, A, B, and C, and asks whether B is physically closer to A or to C. Notably, these objects might be spread over both views, for example, with A and B visible in one image and B and C in the other. We construct 300 questions from pairs of views of the same DL3DV-10K [21] scene. Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct [7] proposes candidate object triplets for which the two views provide complementary evidence about relative proximity. We favor examples in which combining evidence across viewpoints informs the comparison beyond what is readily available from either view alone. Selected triplets are instantiated using a fixed question template and manually verified. 

3.4 Multi-View Scaling 

The core benchmark uses two views per example. To study how counting behaves as observations accumulate, we extend the 200 complete-overlap questions to N ∈ { 2, . . . , 6} input views, keeping all target instances visible in every selected view so that the scene-level count is unchanged. Each additional view contributes another observation of the same physical instances while increasing the number of cross-view correspondences the model must resolve. This extension therefore measures whether additional viewpoints improve object recognition and scene understanding or instead increase identity confusion and double-counting. Evaluating every question in all five settings yields 200 ×5 = 1,000 view-conditioned instances; the two-view versions already belong to the core benchmark, so the extension adds 800 configurations, for 2,100 evaluation configurations in total. 

Human Verification. All automatically proposed or generated examples undergo manual review before inclusion in OneScene-Bench; the VLM and image-generation model serve only to accelerate candidate discovery and controlled editing, and their outputs are never treated directly as ground truth. For counting, annotators verify the target category, the number of visible instances in each image, the identity of repeated instances across views, the overlap type, and the unique scene-level count. For consistency, annotators verify whether the edited region is shared between the viewpoints or visible from only one view, and whether the resulting consistency label is correct. For spatial reasoning, annotators confirm the identities of the three objects and the relative-proximity answer. ## 4 Evaluation 

4.1 Evaluation Setup 

We evaluate all models in five settings in OneScene-Bench: complete-overlap counting, subset-overlap counting, complementary-view counting, cross-view consistency, and multi-view spatial reasoning. All models receive the same task instructions and input images, and we retain their complete generated responses. We use GPT-4o [17] as an LLM judge for all benchmark results. The judge receives the question, ground-truth answer, and complete model response, and determines whether the response reaches the correct conclusion. We report judge-based accuracy, allowing semantically equivalent answers and minor differences in response format. The exact prompts used are available in Appendix C. 

4.2 Benchmark Evaluation Results 

Table 1 reports accuracy across the five evaluation settings. Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct [7] drops from 70.0% and 70.7% on complete- and subset-overlap examples to 34.0% on complementary views, while InternVL3.5-38B-HF [35] drops from 72.0% to 14.5%. Even the strongest models exhibit this pattern: Gemini 3.7 Flash [15] decreases from 81.5% to 50.0%, while GPT-5.5 [28] drops from 70.0% to 38.5%. The consistent degradation on complementary views identifies cross-view correspondence and deduplication as a key bottleneck, where models perform substantially better when the views contain largely redundant evidence than when information must be reconciled across views. Consistency accuracy remains close to chance, ranging from 47.0% to 55.7%. LLaVA-OneVision-2-8B-Instruct [3] and Phi-4-multimodal-instruct [1] predict YES for all examples and consequently obtain 50.0% on the balanced dataset. This behavior suggests that models often default to treating different views as consistent rather than verifying whether scene content agrees across viewpoints. Spatial reasoning capabilities are substantially stronger for most models. GPT-5.5 [28] achieves the highest accuracy at 92.7%, followed closely by Gemini 3.7 Flash [15] at 89.7%. Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct is a notable outlier at 15.7%, frequently selecting object B rather than one of the required reference objects A or C, indicating a failure to follow the specified answer format. 4.3 Diagnostic Analyses 

We conduct additional analyses to examine whether model behavior is affected by input ordering, reliance on non-visual priors, explicit reasoning, simple per-view aggregation, and the number of input views. 

No-Image Bias. We remove all input images while keeping the questions and evaluation procedure unchanged. Although accuracy generally drops, models retain non-trivial performance on several tasks, revealing substantial language and answer-distribution priors. Notably, LLaVA-OneVision-2 obtains 50% consistency accuracy both with and without images by predicting YES for every example, showing that unchanged performance need not indicate successful use of visual evidence. Full results are reported in Table 2; Appendix B.2 lists how often models refuse to answer because no image is provided. 

Instruct vs. Thinking Variants. We compare matched reasoning-enabled and instruction-tuned variants of Qwen3-VL [7] and Kimi-VL [32], together with direct and thinking-enabled inference for MiniCPM-V-4.5 [39]. As shown in Fig. 3a, reasoning reduces counting accuracy across nearly all models and settings; the only exception is a 1.5-point gain for Kimi-VL on complementary views. Thus, explicit reasoning does not systematically improve multi-view counting. 

Comparison with Aggregation Heuristics. We test whether joint predictions can be explained by simple aggregation of independent per-view counts. Let C1 and C2 denote individual-view predictions and C3 the joint prediction; we report the percentage of questions for which C3 agrees with either C1 + C2 or max( C1, C 2). Across models, joint predictions agree substantially more often with the maximum than with the sum (Table 3). In complete- and subset-overlap settings, maximum aggregation can recover the scene-level count when per-view counts are correct. In complementary views, unique instances are distributed across views, requiring information from both images. ## 5 Discussion 

Our results reveal a clear gap between processing multiple images and reasoning over them coherently in the current generation of VLMs. This is most evident in complementary-view counting, where performance drops substantially when models must establish cross-view correspondence and dedupli-cate repeated instances. Cross-view consistency also remains difficult, including for recent models, suggesting that robust scene-level multi-view understanding remains unresolved. The additional diagnostic analyses further expose limitations in how models use and integrate evidence across views. These findings complement existing multi-view benchmarks, on which models have demonstrated stronger spatial and geometric reasoning. OneScene-Bench shows that strong spatial reasoning does not necessarily imply reliable scene-level integration. With multi-view images becoming a dominant approach for interacting with visual 3D scenes through language, OneScene-Bench offers a new perspective on what is missing for 3D reasoning through powerful LLMs. Current models can reason about spatial relationships while still struggling with object correspondence, deduplication, and cross-view consistency, which are essential for reliable and accurate 3D reasoning. 

Limitations. OneScene-Bench focuses on three scene-level capabilities and 1,300 manually verified examples from DL3DV-10K. While larger datasets and broader model coverage would be valuable, we prioritize careful human verification and balance evaluation breadth against computational and API costs. Future work could further expand the benchmark across tasks, environments, and models. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We introduced OneScene-Bench, a controlled benchmark for evaluating whether vision-language models are able to integrate multiple views of a scene to correctly reason about visual aspects of the scene. Across open-weight and proprietary VLMs, our results revealed a persistent gap between processing multiple images and integrating them: models struggle particularly when they must establish object correspondence, reconcile complementary evidence, and detect inconsistencies across viewpoints. OneScene-Bench provides a controlled framework for measuring these capabilities and tracking progress toward more reliable scene-level multi-view understanding. This work thus showed that models of different architectures, both closed and open, are usually unable to perform basic visual reasoning across multiple views, the prevalent representation format for 3D scenes. This section provides additional details on the construction and verification procedure used to build OneScene-Bench. 

A.1 Counting Candidate Mining 

For counting, we first randomly select a location from DL3DV-10K [21] and randomly sample two frames from its capture sequence. We require the difference between their frame indices to be between 10 and 25, providing viewpoint variation while retaining sufficient visual overlap. The selected pair is provided to Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct [7], which is asked to identify a clearly visible, countable, and unambiguous object category. For each proposed category, the model estimates the number of visible instances in each view and the number of unique physical instances across the two views. If no suitable object category is identified, another pair is sampled from the same location and the process is repeated. We allow up to 10 attempts per location; locations for which no suitable candidate is found are skipped. This procedure is repeated across locations until sufficient candidates are obtained for the three counting configurations. The final overlap type is assigned manually after verifying the visible instance sets. The resulting candidates are divided into complete-overlap, subset-overlap, and complementary-view examples, yielding 200, 300, and 200 questions, respectively. 

A.2 Consistency Construction 

For the consistency task, we again begin by selecting two frames from the same DL3DV-10K [21] location using the 10–25 frame-index separation. To identify shared and view-specific regions, we first establish local feature correspondences between the two views using SIFT [24]. We use RANSAC [13] to remove geometrically inconsistent matches. Regions containing a dense concentration of verified correspondences are treated as shared across the two views, while regions spatially separated from these correspondences and containing few matched features are selected as candidate view-specific regions. Image pairs for which these regions cannot be identified reliably are discarded. We then introduce a localized object or visual artifact into one of these regions using gpt-image-1.5 [27]. The model is given freedom to select an appropriate object to insert, while the target region is determined by the desired consistency condition. An edit in a view-specific region is not expected to be observed from the second viewpoint, so the resulting pair is labeled consistent . In contrast, an edit in a shared region introduces a change that should also be observable from the second viewpoint; because it appears in only one view, the resulting pair is labeled inconsistent .The edited patch is resized to the target region and blended into the original image using a Gaussian-smoothed mask to reduce boundary artifacts. All generated examples are subsequently manually inspected to verify the edit, its visibility across the two viewpoints, and the resulting consistency label. Ambiguous or invalid examples are rejected or corrected. We construct 300 balanced consistency examples. 

A.3 Spatial Reasoning Candidate Mining 

For spatial reasoning, we again sample two frames from the same DL3DV-10K [21] location with a frame-index separation of 10–25. Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct [7] is used to identify three clearly visible objects that provide a meaningful relative-proximity comparison across the two views. The selected objects are denoted by A, B, and C, and the candidate question asks whether B is physically closer to A or to C.A dedicated annotation tool is then used to assign consistent object names and record the ground-truth proximity relationship. This allows the object references and the final answer to be standardized inde-pendently of the wording proposed during candidate generation. Candidates are retained only when the three objects can be reliably identified across the views and the relative-proximity relationship is unambiguous. A.4 Verification Procedure 

All candidate examples are manually verified before inclusion in the benchmark. Reviewers inspect the selected images, referenced objects, and ground-truth annotations and reject or correct candidates with ambiguous object identity, uncertain cross-view correspondence, unclear visibility, or ambiguous answers. For counting, reviewers verify the target category, visible instances in each view, cross-view object correspondences, unique scene-level count, and manually assigned overlap type. For consistency, reviewers verify the edited region, whether it is shared or view-specific, and whether the resulting pair is consistent with a single underlying scene. For spatial reasoning, reviewers verify the three referenced objects and the relative-proximity relationship. ## B Additional Details on Evaluation Tasks 

B.1 Image Order Sensitivity 

We study whether model predictions depend on the order in which the two views are presented. A model that forms a consistent scene representation should produce similar predictions when the two views are swapped. We evaluate four representative open-weight models on the same questions using both the original and reversed image orders, and measure the absolute change in accuracy across all five evaluation settings. Most model–task combinations are stable, with 18 of 20 changing by at most four percentage points. The largest changes occur for Gemma-4 [31] on complete-overlap counting and LLaVA-OneVision-2-8B-Instruct [3] on spatial reasoning, with differences of 6.0 and 9.0 points, respectively. Overall, models are reasonably robust to image ordering, suggesting that input order is not a major source of variation in the benchmark. 

B.2 No-Image Evaluation Setting 

To further characterize model behavior when visual evidence is unavailable, we record whether models explicitly refuse to answer in the no-image setting. A response is counted as a refusal only when the model explicitly states that the question cannot be answered without the missing visual information; incorrect or guessed answers are not counted as refusals. As shown in Table 5, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct [7], InternVL3.5-38B-HF [35], and LLaVA-OneVision-2-8B-Instruct [3] do not refuse any questions. In contrast, Gemma-4-31B-IT [31] refuses all consistency questions and 293 of 300 spatial reasoning questions, while refusing only 21 of 700 counting questions.
