OmniQuad: a Wheeled-Legged Hybrid Robot with Omnidirectional Wheels
Francesco Iottia,b, Alok Ranjana,b, Franco Angelinia,b, Manolo Garabinia,b
aDepartment of Information Engineering of University of Pisa, Via Girolamo Caruso 16 , Pisa, 56122, Italy
bResearch Center Enrico Piaggio, Largo Lucio Lazzarino 1, Pisa, 56122, Italy
Abstract
Combining wheeled and legged locomotion within a single robotic platform oﬀers the potential to harness the advan-
tages of both systems. The development of such hybrid systems remains an active area of research. While legged
robots exhibit remarkable adaptability for traversing uneven and rugged terrains, their relatively low speed limits their
practicality in indoor environments. Conversely, wheeled robots demonstrate higher eﬃciency on ﬂat surfaces but
often encounter diﬃculties when navigating obstacles such as steps. This paper introduces OmniQuad, a novel hybrid
robot that integrates mecanum wheels with legged locomotion to exploit the beneﬁts of both modalities. Mecanum
wheels provide omnidirectional mobility, enhancing the robot’s manoeuvrability by enabling agile movement in con-
ﬁned spaces and the ability to maintain a consistent orientation during task execution. In this work, we ﬁrst present
the design of a custom wheeled-foot featuring mecanum wheels. Subsequently, we describe the complete robot as-
sembly, comprising these wheeled feet and EM-Act actuators. Finally, we detail the control scheme developed to
govern the OmniQuad and evaluate its performance through experimental trials conducted in both indoor and outdoor
environments.
Keywords:
legged robots, wheeled robots, quadruped, omnidirectional movement
1. Introduction
The demand for mobile robots is substantial across diverse applications in logistics, cleaning, search and rescue,
and environmental monitoring [1]. Although the designs of these robots diﬀer from one another, they are still limited
in their ability to navigate tight spaces and interact with humans and other robots due to their lack of human-like
locomotion capabilities.
Wheeled mobile robots, owing to their simple design and implementation, are extensively used in these settings.
Their eﬃcacy ensues from the ease of achieving high speeds and executing movements with good eﬃciency. These
machines were originally designed for planetary exploration [2, 3], but in recent years, they have also been utilized in
warehouse work [4] and homes. Robots equipped with conventional wheels face a signiﬁcant limitation in manoeu-
vrability due to their restricted movement capabilities. These robots are only able to move along a direction that is
perpendicular to the centre axis of the wheels, which makes it diﬃcult for them to navigate through tight spaces or
turn quickly [5].
Various types of wheels have been developed to facilitate omnidirectional movement, including omni wheels
and mecanum wheels. Recent years have seen the integration of omni wheels in diﬀerent typologies of robots, as
referenced in works such as [6, 7]. These wheels are distinctively mounted at a 45-degree angle concerning their fore
axis, setting them apart from other wheel types. Their major problems are the low traction and stability. In addition,
they are also susceptible to vibrations and slippage. In [8], they reduce these kinds of issues by designing a speciﬁc
suspension system in a four-wheeled omni-wheelchair.
Mecanum wheels [9] consist of rollers oriented at a 45-degree angle to the fore axis. Their utilization is well-suited
for scenarios necessitating high stability and manoeuvrability [10, 11]. Additionally, their capacity to accommodate
high load capacities makes them versatile across diverse application domains, such as factories, warehouses and even
hospitals, as highlighted in [12, 13]. It is essential to note that while omnidirectional wheels oﬀer advantages, they

encountering steps or uneven terrains.
Quadrupedal robots emerge as more adept solutions for rugged terrains due to their ability to overcome obsta-
cles through climbing or jumping manoeuvres [17, 18, 19]. The major examples of legged robots, also known as
quadrupedal robots, are Spot [20], ANYmal [21], Mini Cheetah [22], HyQ [23], Oncilla [24] and Cheeta-cub [25].
However, in some scenarios, their speed may be slower compared to wheeled robots, and their operational eﬃciency
may fall short [26]. Choosing the best locomotion system can be a challenging task. To overcome this, hybrid loco-
moted robots are being developed to combine the beneﬁts of two diﬀerent locomotion systems. Currently, there are
various types of wheeled-legged hybrid robots [27] that oﬀer the eﬃciency of wheeled locomotion and the ability to
navigate through rough terrains and climb over obstacles. Vehicles with omnidirectional wheels and legged systems
are often viewed as fundamentally distinct due to their specialized use in diﬀerent scenarios. Designing a system that
seamlessly integrates vehicles with omnidirectional wheels and legged systems poses a complex challenge. It entails
not only the mechanical merging of the two components but also the development of a control system that accom-
modates their unique operational environments. The goal is to create a robot that harnesses the unique advantages of
both technologies, enabling it to excel in a diverse array of scenarios beyond the capabilities of individual systems.
By combining wheeled and legged locomotion, this integrated approach allows the robot to surpass the performance
limitations of conventional single-mode systems across diverse and demanding environments. Speciﬁcally, the robot
could achieve exceptional maneuverability within structured environments, such as warehouses, factories, or urban in-
frastructures, where omnidirectional wheels provide rapid, precise positioning and the ability to maintain a consistent
orientation, beneﬁcial for operations like inventory handling or inspection tasks. Furthermore, the incorporation of
legged locomotion extends the robot’s capabilities to negotiate uneven terrains, climb stairs or obstacles, and traverse
conﬁned spaces, tasks that are typically challenging for traditional wheeled vehicles. The robot’s legs also allow it
to dynamically adjust its height, facilitating passage beneath obstacles or through low-clearance areas, enhancing its
overall operational versatility.
The contributions of this paper are as follows: (i) Mechanical design of an Omniwheeled foot that incorporates a
motor and a gearbox within the assembly; (ii) a robotic system called OmniQuad, as shown in Fig. 1, which integrates
both mecanum wheels and legs; (iii) a control system that enables the use of both legs and omnidirectional wheels; and
(iv) an evaluation of the capabilities of the developed robot through a series of tasks in diﬀerent environments. These
tasks focused on navigating obstacles using various methods, such as passing beneath or scaling over them, taking

2. Literature Review
In recent years, various types of mobile robots have been developed to navigate diﬃcult terrains or overcome
obstacles in structured environments, such as steps.
In [28] a novel robotic system is presented with transformable mecanum wheels that overcome obstacles up to
2.5 times the radius of its wheels. The primary problems with this concept are related to the intricate method needed
to overcome barriers, which includes an alignment stage for the transformable wheel to latch onto the obstruction.
Furthermore, it appears that the robot has only been tested on obstacles with sharp edges. To simplify the climbing
on obstacles in [29], the authors developed a new type of transformable wheel that can passively transform when in
contact with the obstacle, avoiding including a motor to transform the wheel. The main limitations are the size and
dimension of the obstacle, which can limit the motion even in indoor environments. In [30] the authors introduce α-
WaLTR, a four-wheeled robot with passively transformable wheels and spring suspension. This mostly passive setup
lets the robot glide over modest obstacles and even tackle stairs using straightforward control. However, the passive
design may limit its adaptability on highly uneven or complex terrains.
Quadruped robots have improved in recent years in terms of their ability to traverse uneven terrains, although
problems can still arise indoors. To increase the performance in climbing obstacles or improve the forward velocity of
the robot, diverse types of wheeled-legged robots have emerged, each showcasing distinct design and operational char-
acteristics [27, 31]. Currently, advanced quadruped robots, such as the Go2-W from Unitree Robotics1 and the Lynx
developed by Deep Robotics2, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in traversing challenging terrains swiftly
and reliably compared to traditional legged and wheeled robots. In the research community, numerous wheeled-
legged robotic systems have been developed to improve locomotion capabilities. In [32], the authors developed a
quadruped robot that utilizes passive wheels for roller skating locomotion. However, they do not fully exploit the
potential beneﬁts of incorporating wheeled locomotion, and the increase in performance it may oﬀer. In [33, 34], the
authors present wheel-leg rovers with only one actuated joint per leg. This design is more suitable for movement on
slopes because passive joints are easier to adapt to the irregularities of diﬀerent terrains, but make it impossible for the
robot to overcome obstacles by lifting its legs because it has just one actuated joint per leg. In [35] researchers devel-
oped a wheel-legged robot with 11 DOF. Although the robot can handle various types of uneven terrain using an IMU
sensor-based control system, it lacks the ability to climb obstacles with its legs. The HyTro [36] robot is equipped
with wheels placed under the body. Thanks to this design, it can switch between wheeled and legged locomotion by
lowering or lifting its legs, but it cannot perform hybrid locomotion.
Robots that feature wheels on the knees enable switching between legged and wheeled locomotion by lifting or
lowering their feet [37]; similarly, the robot Max, introduced in [38], employs knee-mounted wheels with a specialized
mechanism to alternate between agile legged movement and eﬃcient wheeled mobility, yet it shares the limitation of
being unable to perform simultaneous hybrid locomotion.
The robot in [39, 40] is equipped with six wheel-legs. When it encounters an obstacle, it can lift two legs and use
the other four for support. However, its high weight limits the eﬃciency, in terms of electrical power and execution
time, of its leg locomotion, so it mainly relies on wheel movements. Another innovation is a hexapod with mecanum
wheels [41]. The beneﬁt of having six wheels is the total stability of the system and redundancy, which allows it to
move even if a limited number of wheels fail. The challenge with this design is the overall structure, as well as the
planning of the gait for six legs.
The Centauro [42] and Momaro [43] robots are used in disaster management and integrate wheels instead of feet
to increase their speed of action. They use 2 degrees of freedom (DOF) wheels to enable omnidirectional movements.
Both robots integrate two 7 DOF robotic arms, but Momaro has four 4 DOF legs, whereas Centauro has 5 DOF legs,
which contribute to higher manoeuvrability in the legged locomotion to move in uneven terrains. They are almost
human-sized and can be bulky for certain applications in conﬁned spaces.

The authors in [44] introduce TraQuad, a multimodal robot combining legged and tracked locomotion, enhancing
agility, maneuverability, and traction, especially in outdoor environments with complex and uneven terrains. However,
this design is less eﬃcient indoors, where tracked mobility and advanced climbing capabilities might be unnecessary
or even disadvantageous. Moreover, the conﬁguration of the robot with 8 degrees of freedom increases mechanical
complexity, making it challenging to overcome common indoor obstacles such as stairs or steps.
Wheel-leg robots with just two legs and two wheels [45] are easier to control because of their simple design.
However, they encounter diﬃculties when overcoming obstacles, as they can simply jump over them. The Swiss-mile
robot [46] is an advancement of the ANYmal robot with torque-controlled and non-steerable wheels. This robot is
versatile and can be used both indoors and outdoors, thanks to its hybrid locomotion. Regular wheels enhance the
robot’s eﬃciency during forward movements, although they can pose challenges for steering in speciﬁc situations,
potentially impacting the overall system performance. A novel wheeled-legged robot with magnetic omni wheels
has been developed in [47], which can climb wall surfaces. The robot stays ﬁxed to an inclined plane with special
magnetic omni wheels, but it is more suitable for climbing vertical obstacles than overcoming them. In addition, a
new omnidirectional platform with legs has been proposed in [48]. The platform can carry a weight of up to 80 kg.
They developed a novel planetary gear to reduce vibration and achieve agile motion. However, this robot is developed
to substitute the legged system in humanoid robots, not to be an independent system.
In this work, we present a novel wheeled-legged robot capable of utilizing both omnidirectional wheels and legs.
Our system integrates mecanum wheels to signiﬁcantly enhance maneuverability, enabling the robot to avoid steering
and maintain body orientation without exclusively relying on legs for rotation, as required by the Swiss-mile robot
[46], Go series from Unitree3, and Lynx from Deep Robotics4. Additionally, in contrast to robots such as Centauro
[42], Momaro [43], we achieve omnidirectional mobility using wheels with fewer degrees of freedom, thereby sim-
plifying the mechanical design. Furthermore, our legged locomotion addresses the inherent limitations of purely
wheeled systems, such as those encountered with transformable wheel designs, by enabling navigation over uneven
terrain, obstacle climbing, and height adjustments to traverse under obstacles. This hybrid approach provides distinct
advantages in structured environments for precise orientation and versatility in complex, obstacle-rich scenarios.
3. OmniQuad Design and Electronics
This section provides a comprehensive overview of the actuation system and its components, introduces the wheel-
foot design with a focus on its features and capabilities, and presents the OmniQuad robot, showcasing all of its
characteristics.
3.1. Actuation System
To enable omnidirectional movement of the robot on wheels, we opt for mecanum wheels instead of Omni wheels
due to their ease of implementation, mounting direction, and actuation. To maintain the low weight of the robot,
wheels made of a majority of plastic are chosen. An analysis of wheels with varying diameters is conducted to
determine the most eﬀective solution in terms of stability and minimizing the total torque to move the robot.

allows the system to generate high torque, capable of handling the signiﬁcant forces that occur when moving with the

The moteus r.411 controller6, displayed in Fig. 2c, is selected to command the motor. This driver allows multiple
servo systems to be connected in series using the daisy-chain connection method. This feature enables seamless
integration of various servo systems, providing a ﬂexible and eﬃcient solution for controlling multiple servo systems.
3.2. Design of the Wheeled-Foot
The design incorporates all mechanical components, the motor, the gearbox, and the mecanum wheel, aligned on
a single axis. The total width from the controller to the wheels measures 150 mm. The mechanical interfaces are made
of ABS plastic, which is appropriately fabricated with FDM technology. In Fig. 3, the exploded view of the wheeled
foot is depicted. In this solution, the link encapsulates the gearbox. The motor is secured to a plate that is connected
directly to the bottom of the link. The controller is fastened to the plate below the motor, and uses a magnet to control
it. The motion from the gearbox to the wheel is transmitted through a plastic interface. This interface is attached to
the wheel using a hexagonal shaft and screws speciﬁcally for plastic. On the other side, the interface is attached to
the gearbox shaft using a key made of Aluminum. A bearing is also placed to support the relative motion between
the ﬁxed part, the link, and the rotating parts of the actuation system. Additionally, the bearing is used to prevent the
wheel from coming out of the system, using ﬂanges to prevent it from moving out. Following the assembly of the
mechanical and electronic components, a casing is installed to protect the power and data cables. Finally, the system
presents a total length and width of respectively 230 mm and 150 mm, and the total weight is 0.42 kg, as reported in
Tab. 1.

4.3. Hybrid System
The transition from legged locomotion to omni-wheeled movement presents signiﬁcant challenges in ensuring
stability and eﬃciency. Conversely, the reverse transition is straightforward, as the four wheels will generally remain
in contact with the ground, allowing the robot to start moving its legs from a stable condition. The most critical aspect
of the transition between legged and wheeled actuation is accurately maintaining proper contact between all four
wheels and the ground. Given that Mecanum wheels are composed of multiple rollers, which are convex cylinders
arranged around the wheel, the resultant ground-contact interface follows a non-uniform trajectory. This can make it
particularly diﬃcult to guarantee stable omnidirectional movement, as any misalignment of the wheels, such as failing
to be upright to the surface, can lead to a loss of required movement.
The integration of leg mechanisms substantially improves the ability to overcome these challenges. The legs play
a crucial role not only in positioning the wheels correctly but also in acting as a suspension system that helps absorb
impact forces [50]. This is advantageous in hybrid locomotion, where maintaining the proper ground-wheel contact
is essential. Having a 2 DOF leg is also beneﬁcial in ﬂat terrain scenarios as it constrains the foot to be in the upright
position and does not allow it to tilt, which could otherwise alter the wheels eﬀective radius of rotation. This structural
simplicity reduces the complexity of the control strategy needed to maintain the desired wheel orientation, which is
also a preferred pose of application for this robot.
As illustrated in Figure 7, when a wheel is placed partially on an obstacle, the presence of the free-rotating rollers
can cause instability due to a contact force that is not normal to the ground, causing the foot to slip on the edge of
the obstacle due to a potential loss of traction as the roller rotates. To prevent this, the implemented control strategy
moves the wheel forward beyond its radius before making contact, ensuring that the contact point aligns properly with
the wheel’s center and the gravitational force direction.

4.5.2. Obstacle Detection and Obstacle Climbing modules
The Obstacle Detection Module is responsible for identifying obstacles and estimating their height using torque
measurements from the wheel’s current sensor. Meanwhile, the Obstacle Climbing Module ensures the system main-
tains its centre of mass within the support polygon and plans the required motion to either climb over or avoid
obstacles. This module relies on IMU readings to adjust the base’s orientation appropriately.
When a wheel encounters an obstacle and gets stuck, the torque increases signiﬁcantly due to resistance, activating
the obstacle detection module. To overcome the obstruction, the Obstacle Climbing Module initiates a wheel-lifting
manoeuvre while keeping the wheel rotating to maintain elevated torque. This sustained torque helps the system
accurately detect when the wheel is still in contact with the obstacle. As the wheel clears the obstacle, the torque
drops to near zero, signalling that lifting is no longer needed. Once the robot is free from the obstacle, it can plan a
trajectory to avoid it and continue with the task.
When two wheels get stuck, the robot must employ a legged locomotion strategy to climb the obstacle. The
Obstacle Climbing Module ﬁrst decides which leg to lift and then computes the new centre of gravity based on the
remaining supported legs. The robot begins to unbalance its base by shifting its position and, if necessary, applying
pitch and/or roll compensation in height, as illustrated in Section 4.2. Once the two front wheels reach the top of
the obstacle, the robot stabilizes itself using IMU readings and continues rotating the wheels until it has traveled a
distance equivalent to the length of its body. When the front and hind wheels are at diﬀerent heights, the robot must
adjust its pitch angle to move forward to the centre of gravity. Before lifting the last leg, it performs a roll adjustment
to ensure proper stepping onto the obstacle.
4.5.3. Control Diagram
The control scheme of the entire system is shown in Fig. 8. The primary motion objective entails maintaining

The motion planning module generates the trajectory of the leg joints and the motion of the robot’s body. At ﬁrst,

from the current sensors present in the encoders of the wheels and IMU data to overcome possible obstacles that the

5. Experimental Validation
A series of experiments was conducted to assess the functional capabilities of the OmniQuad robot comprehen-
sively. Initially, we focused on tuning the PD controllers for the robots jointsa process that proved extremely helpful
for setting appropriate gains in all subsequent trials. Next, we carried out tests to evaluate the performance of the
wheel-foot design, speciﬁcally looking at slippage, friction, and the actuation systems behaviour on diﬀerent terrains.
Following this, the robot’s abilities in two distinct obstacle-overcoming scenarios were examined. The ﬁrst ex-
periment focused on passing under an overhead obstacle while maintaining the same orientation, demonstrating the
robot’s omnidirectional movements. The second experiment aimed at assessing the robot’s legged locomotion ca-
pabilities, achieved by climbing over an obstacle. Moreover, the joystick controller was also tested in an outdoor
environment.
Subsequently, stress tests were performed. Primarily, we observed the obstacle detection module in action, with
the robot successfully operating on only three wheels. Furthermore, a durability test is conducted, designed to evaluate
the robustness of the overall hardware. Finally, we assessed the maximum payload the robot can carry using its legs.
To view the experimental tests, please also refer to the attached video.
5.1. Compatibility hardware software test
During the compatibility testing phase of the OmniQuad robot, the PD controllers were carefully tuned. Proper
PD tuning is essential as it directly aﬀects the robots stability and responsiveness, ensuring smooth motion and precise
control over its actuators. A proportional gain of 150 Nm/rad and a derivative gain of 0.5 Nms/rad were established for
the leg joints. These values provide a balanced trade-oﬀkeeping joint error relatively low while allowing the actuators
to remain suﬃciently compliant, Figs. 9b, 9c, rather than overly rigid. This elasticity helps the robot adapt better to
dynamic tasks. Conversely, since the wheel joints rely primarily on velocity control, only the derivative term was set
at 0.95 Nms/rad, resulting in consistent and controlled wheel movement throughout all experiments, Fig. 9a.

6. Conclusion and Future Works
We have developed a novel type of robot, named OmniQuad, which integrates both mecanum wheels and legs
to enhance stability and maneuverability while mitigating critical steering issues. The robot features omnidirectional
wheels attached to its feet, enabling smooth and precise movement in all directions. Additionally, the incorporation
of legs provides OmniQuad with the capability to climb obstacles, traverse uneven terrain, and dynamically adjust its
height to pass beneath obstructions or navigate conﬁned spaces, signiﬁcantly expanding its operational versatility. To
govern its locomotion, we devised a control system grounded in the kinematics of both locomotion modes. In Tab.
4 are reported the speciﬁcations of the robot. Experiments conducted both indoors and in controlled scenarios show-
cased OmniQuad’s adeptness at reliably navigating challenging paths and overcoming obstacles, either by eﬀectively
passing beneath or scaling over them. Notably, the robot demonstrated the capability to reduce its height by up to
180 mm, allowing it to seamlessly traverse low-clearance obstacles, as well as to ascend steps as high as 100 mm,
even under varying payload conditions. Additionally, its agility in executing precise movements while consistently
maintaining its orientation and directionality underscores its versatility and suitability for demanding inspection tasks,
logistics operations, and search-and-rescue missions across structured and natural environments.
The robot has also been presented at the Maker Faire Rome 2023 and at the European Robotics Forum 2024 held
in Rimini, Italy.
In our future work, we intend to enhance the design of the wheeled foot. Speciﬁcally, we aim to reduce the total
length of the component for a more compact conﬁguration. Regarding the design part, we are planning to increase the
number of DOF of each leg, introducing the hip abduction joint, in order to improve the stability of the robot. In this
work, we achieve a fundamental level of autonomy for the Omniquad robot by leveraging encoder-based odometry and
orientation data, allowing it to reliably navigate structured environments with static obstacles. Although the current
autonomy is suﬃcient for simple scenarios, our future goals involve signiﬁcantly enhancing its navigation capabilities.
Speciﬁcally, we aim to incorporate perceptive sensors such as a depth camera and a LiDAR sensor, empowering the
robot to autonomously navigate through unknown and dynamic environments. Additionally, we will employ an end-
to-end approach [57, 58] to reﬁne the control loop to enable hybrid motion [59], seamlessly integrating both legged
