Abstract
A conventional embodied agent in an indoor environment
builds a map by accumulating noisy real-world measure-
ments and results from heavy inference to guide navigation.
While the detailed measurements may differ due to scene
changes or sensor settings, the unique topological structure
of navigable passages or the connectivity between rooms
may provide training-free guidance that complements local
observations. We propose a sparse topometric graph as an
efficient structural representation for robust indoor naviga-
tion, alleviating the need for fine-grained matching, which
is computationally expensive, memory-intensive, and prone
to real-world variations. The map consists of nodes that en-
code local 3D geometry and distance-weighted edges that
capture traversable connectivity. A learned graph-matching
network finds consistent subgraph structures across ses-
sions by considering both node-level geometric descrip-
tors and neighborhood connectivity. The holistic contextual
matching is robust to local ambiguities caused by weak dis-
tinctiveness or repetitive structure, and establishes stable
correspondences despite local scene changes, different sen-
sor modalities, or limited spatial overlap. We analyze the
practicality of the graph through cross-session point-goal
navigation, where a goal specified in a prior reference map
must be reached from an unknown initial pose in a later ses-
sion. Experiments show that our method achieves the high-
est success rates (77.1% on Gibson [34] and 40.0% under
3D-FRONT [9] scene changes), while reducing memory by
more than two orders of magnitude compared to point-level
representations. The proposed approach further supports
cross-session alignment and goal-directed navigation with

1. Introduction
Embodied agents in daily life need to understand spatial
context to interact in unstructured environments. Capturing
the spatial map of the deployed environment often requires
composing multiple measurements with minimal overlap,
as indoor scenes often contain complex concavities, fre-
quent occlusions, and confined navigable spaces due to fur-
niture, rooms, and narrow passages with corners. Reusing
the acquired map in a separate session poses additional dif-
ficulties. Transferring the high-quality geometry of the en-
tire deployed space may require extensive spatial memory,
and reusing the map with a different embodiment may en-
counter scene changes and different sensor configurations
with unknown relative poses.
Prior navigation studies [6, 7, 21, 25] have used differ-
ent spatial representations for mapping, localization, and
planning in 2D top-down maps of large-scale indoor envi-
ronments. Dense metric maps [6, 7, 12, 21] preserve de-
tailed geometry and support path planning, but their storage
and matching costs rapidly increase with environment scale.
Keyframe- [4, 5, 23] and appearance-based [2, 14, 15, 25]
maps are compact and visually informative, but their relia-
bility can degrade under viewpoint and scene changes. Se-
mantic and hierarchical scene graphs [11, 13, 32, 36] pro-
vide higher-level structure through layouts, objects, or lan-
guage descriptions, but often depend on reliable detections,
explicit labels, or predefined semantic categories.
In this paper, we propose a sparse topometric graph that
can reliably capture geometric context despite prevalent am-
biguities in indoor environments with repetitive structures,
moved furniture, and heterogeneous sensor modalities. In
the presence of such variations, local features are insuffi-
cient to extract reliable geometric context, and a broader
spatial arrangement must be considered.
Our proposed
graph representation summarizes the spatial organization
of traversable space. Each node is centered on traversable
structure rather than on a sampled point in the cloud, aggre-
gates the observed 3D points within a fixed-radius neigh-
borhood, and stores a compact geometry-only descriptor,
while each edge represents free-space connectivity with a
distance-based weight. This fixed-radius aggregation makes
local geometry reusable at the graph level instead of ty-
ing descriptors to raw point samples [20, 28]. Compress-
ing each local point set into a fixed-size node descriptor
keeps the map size proportional to the number of graph
nodes rather than the raw scene geometry, while preserving
enough local and structural information for cross-session
map reuse.
We demonstrate the usability of the representation in
cross-session point-goal navigation. The agent starts from
an unknown initial pose and must reach the goal pose, de-
fined on the reference map built in a previous session. The
agent first aligns its current observations relative to the ref-
erence map and selects waypoints toward the goal while
taking the navigable path topology into account. We train
a graph-matching network to predict cross-session corre-
spondences between graph nodes by jointly considering lo-
cal geometry and connectivity between neighboring nodes.
The predicted correspondences are used to estimate the rel-
ative transformation between the reference and current ses-
sions, after which the graph-based navigation policy guides
the agent toward the transferred goal. This setting lets us
study whether a single sparse representation can support
both cross-session alignment and downstream navigation.
Fig. 1 illustrates our pipeline with a real-world cross-
session example. The example highlights a key challenge.
Local geometric descriptors or appearance-based cues can
be ambiguous when similar structures repeat across par-
tially overlapping sessions.
The proposed representation
and graph matching reduce these ambiguities by consider-
ing both node-level geometry and neighboring graph con-
nectivity.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose a sparse topometric graph for cross-session
indoor map reuse. It represents traversable space with
compact geometry-only node descriptors and distance-
weighted graph connectivity, providing a scalable map
representation.
• We introduce a graph-node descriptor that aggregates
fixed-radius 3D observations around reusable node cen-
ters. This makes local geometry available at the graph
level instead of tying descriptors to individual point-cloud
samples.
• We introduce a learned graph-matching approach that
predicts cross-session node correspondences using both
local geometric similarity and graph connectivity, and we
use the same graph for cross-session alignment and way-
point selection.
• We evaluate the method on simulated cross-session nav-
igation, large-scale indoor scenes, scene-change settings,
and a real-world indoor study, demonstrating efficient and
reliable cross-session map use.

leading to degraded map quality [27]. Appearance-based
methods [15, 31] represent scenes via stored images or

sessions. They are more robust to spatial drift but remain

Object-centric scene graphs [10, 11, 13, 17] offer higher-
level reasoning by representing objects and their relations,
supporting semantically meaningful planning. In particular,
ObjReact [10] targets cross-session navigation by construct-

and semantic stability across time. To support a fair and
diverse evaluation, we adopt representative methods from
each of the above categories as baselines for cross-session
navigation: a metric-based grid map [6], appearance-based
maps [14, 15], and object-centric scene graphs [10, 11].
Beyond these, point-level geometric descriptors [20, 28]
enable fine-grained spatial alignment and have demon-
strated strong performance in localization and map registra-
tion tasks. Rather than using them densely, we adopt their
desirable geometric properties in a compact, node-anchored
form, combining their discriminative power with structural
abstraction for scalable cross-session navigation.
Our work bridges these extremes by introducing a sparse
topometric graph that generates a topological graph over
navigable areas, where each graph node is anchored with
sparse 3D geometric descriptors. This structure supports
both cross-session localization and goal-directed planning
in a single pipeline, offering a compact and robust map rep-
resentation for cross-session navigation.
2.2. Graph-based Correspondence
Graph-based representations encode spatial relationships
that support structural reasoning for localization and nav-
igation. Factor graphs have long provided the backbone
for multi-session SLAM [8], while learned approaches con-
struct topological maps where nodes represent visited loca-
tions and edges encode navigability [7, 25]. Recent work
studies cross-session localization and graph-based align-
ment over object- or scene-level abstractions, with node-
level features drawn from different modalities and seman-
tics [18, 24, 35]. These approaches demonstrate that rea-
soning about relational structure helps resolve ambiguities
that local features alone cannot resolve.
However, object-level graphs rely on stable semantic de-
tection, which degrades under scene changes or sensing
heterogeneity. Related work has also explored learned ge-
ometric descriptors and registration over point-cloud fea-
tures [20, 28]. Such methods typically operate at the point
or object level rather than over navigable spatial regions.
Our graph provides a sparse topological description
of traversable regions that compactly encodes reachabil-
ity from the agent’s current position. The graph structure
also enables holistic cross-session matching of connected
regions, which is robust to perturbations in local features
and to moderate topological deviations arising from scene
changes or measurement discrepancies.

3.1. Sparse Topometric Graph
The BUILDGRAPH stage in Alg. 1 (lines 2–6) constructs the
shared representation used by the rest of the pipeline. We
represent each session as a topometric graph G = (V, E).
Here, topometric denotes the joint encoding of topological
connectivity among navigable anchors and metric and ge-
ometric information. Each node vi ∈V corresponds to a
navigable anchor extracted from traversable free space and
is associated during graph construction with a 2D position
pi ∈R2 and a sparse 3D geometric descriptor fi ∈R32.
Edges E encode traversable connectivity between adjacent
nodes, where each edge weight stores the local distance
dij = ∥pi −pj∥2 between neighboring anchors.
The graph is derived from two complementary geomet-
ric representations. A top-down occupancy map is used to
identify reachable traversable free space and extract graph
nodes, while a depth-derived accumulated point cloud is
used to compute geometric descriptors within each node.
We first extract the portion of free space that is reachable
from the agent’s current position so that the graph remains
focused on reusable navigable structure rather than on every
nominally free cell in the dense map. Navigable anchors
are defined by the structure of the reachable free-space oc-
cupancy map, with graph nodes placed at uniform intervals
of d=5 cells (0.25 m) along each branch. The nodes anchor
local traversable areas from which features are extracted.
The key geometric design choice is how node descriptors
are defined. Unlike the original per-point formulation, de-
scriptors are anchored at navigable graph nodes rather than
at existing points in the cloud. For each node vi, we collect
points {q ∈P : ∥q −pi∥< r} within a fixed radius around
its construction anchor and compute a local geometric em-
bedding. We adopt a pre-trained rotation-equivariant point-
cloud encoder from BUFFER-X [28] with frozen weights,
providing robustness to partial observations and sensor het-
erogeneity. As a result, the representation remains com-
pact while each node still carries both local 3D context and
graph-level connectivity for cross-session correspondence
and downstream navigation. Implementation details such

3.2. Correspondence Estimation
The FINDCORR stage in Alg. 1 (lines 9–11) estimates cross-
session correspondences.
Given topometric graphs con-
structed from different navigation sessions, our goal is to
identify correspondences between nodes representing the
same physical locations in the environment. This problem
requires reasoning over a noisy topometric graph, in which
both the local geometry encoded in nodes and the graph
structure are perturbed by measurement noise, odometry
drift, and scene variation. We treat the topometric graph as
a shared representation, from which node correspondences
are inferred as a signal used jointly for localization and nav-
igation. These correspondences are reused for both pose
estimation and goal-directed navigation.
Rather than relying on dense frame- or point-level
matching, which can be disrupted by unobserved areas or
local scene variations, our approach operates on sparse
graphs. Each node aggregates local 3D geometry over a
traversable anchor using rotation-robust and view-invariant
descriptors (Sec. 3.1), yielding a compact representation
that naturally tolerates missing coverage under partial over-
lap and sensing heterogeneity. In addition, the graph struc-
ture encodes spatial relationships among nodes, providing
global context that helps disambiguate correspondences be-
yond local geometry.
Matching Objective. Our objective is to predict a soft par-
tial assignment between nodes in the reference and current
graphs by jointly reasoning over node-level geometric fea-
tures and graph connectivity. We employ IPCA-GM [33]
with Sinkhorn-normalized assignment to estimate corre-
spondence probabilities while allowing unmatched nodes
under partial overlap. Because the prediction is constrained
by both local geometry and graph structure, the resulting
correspondences are more robust to local ambiguity, re-
peated structure, and incomplete coverage than geometry-
only matching.
Training Pair Construction. As illustrated in Fig. 3, train-
ing pairs are constructed from two partial traversals col-
lected in the same scene with different spatial coverage. We
build graphs GA and GB from these sessions and define cor-
respondence supervision by spatial proximity between their
construction anchors across sessions. During training, we
perturb traversable edge distances to improve robustness to
odometry drift. Exact objective and implementation details
are deferred to the supplementary material.
3.3. Localization and Waypoint Selection
The NAVSCORE stage in Alg. 1 (lines 14–21) reuses the
predicted correspondences for both localization and way-
point selection. Using the RANSAC-filtered node matches
Min, each matched node pair provides a position corre-
spondence (pref
i , pcur
j ). We estimate the relative transforma-
tion Tref→cur ∈SE(2) from these inlier correspondences.
During deployment, odometry drift accumulates over time
as the agent explores. To reduce its impact on localization,
we construct the current-session graph from a spatially lo-
cal subset of recently observed traversable structure rather
than from the full accumulated map.
Our navigation policy directly reuses the topometric
graph and cross-session correspondences introduced earlier,
without additional representations or task-specific supervi-
sion. Given the correspondence, we perform cross-session
navigation by selecting nodes in the current graph as way-
points. The policy balances exploitation of available goal
information and exploration of unmapped areas using two
complementary signals, a goal-directed score Sgoal derived
from cross-session correspondences and a frontier-based
exploration score Sexplore that promotes explored area ex-
pansion. A goal g ∈R2 specified in the reference-session
frame is converted into scores on the reference graph, and
those scores are propagated to the current-session graph
through the predicted correspondence.
The exploration
term is computed on the current occupancy map and evalu-
ated at current-session graph nodes for waypoint selection.
Since the correspondence matches the holistic spatial con-
text rather than individual nodes, reliable cross-session evi-
dence becomes available only after sufficient exploration.
We therefore transition from using the exploration score
only to the combined goal-directed and exploration score
once cross-session evidence becomes reliable, as illustrated
in Fig. 2. The selected waypoint is then executed on the oc-
cupancy map using a Fast Marching Method planner [29].

4. Experiments
We evaluate our approach on cross-session point-goal nav-
igation experiments. We assess navigation under odometry
noise, varying scene scales, limited inter-session overlap,

4.1. Experimental Setup
We evaluate on three datasets: Gibson [34] for the main
evaluation under noiseless and noisy odometry, HM3D [22]
for large-scale evaluation, and 3D-FRONT [9] for scene-
change evaluation.
Experiments are conducted in Habitat [26] using the Lo-
CoBot action space [16]. The agent takes discrete actions
of moving forward by 0.25 m or turning left or right by
10◦. The success threshold is 2.0 m for Gibson and HM3D.
For 3D-FRONT, we use a 1.0 m threshold because the pri-
mary question is whether the representation remains locally
reusable under scene change, rather than long-range plan-
ning. Table 1 summarizes the step budgets and scene scales
used in each dataset. The graph matching network is trained
on Gibson training scenes and is applied without retraining
across all datasets. Unless otherwise noted, all methods are
executed under the same simulator and action setting. Ad-
ditional implementation details are deferred to the supple-
mentary material.
In every episode, the goal is specified on the reference-
session map, while the agent acts only in the current session.
The reference and current sessions are collected at different
times and from different start positions. The agent therefore
cannot rely on frame-to-frame continuation across sessions
and must recover a usable cross-session relation from par-
tial overlap. This shared protocol lets us compare how well
each representation supports map reuse rather than single-
session exploration.

4.5. Real-World Data Demonstration
We further evaluate the representation on real-world cross-
session data captured several weeks apart with heteroge-
neous sensors. One session uses a handheld LiDAR scanner
and the other uses a mobile phone with built-in LiDAR. The
two sessions are collected independently at different times
of day. The LiDAR session was recorded in daytime, and
the mobile-phone session was recorded at night. They cover
only partially overlapping portions of the environment. The
scene is an office building with repeated monitors, tables,
and cubicles of similar design, which creates strong local
ambiguity in both geometry and appearance across nearby
locations. We use this example as a focused correspondence
study rather than a closed-loop robot experiment. Acquisi-
tion and reconstruction details are deferred to the supple-
mentary material.

5. Limitations and Scope
This paper studies cross-session map reuse for single-floor
indoor navigation under a 2D top-down point-goal formu-
lation. Multi-floor environments and explicit vertical con-
nectivity are outside the current evaluation scope, and ex-
tending the representation to such settings is a natural di-
rection for future work. Within that scope, we focus on
cross-session reuse under partial overlap, sensing varia-
tion, and moderate scene change. More severe changes to
traversable connectivity, such as closed doors, blocked cor-
ridors, or large structural reconfiguration, were not system-
atically evaluated here and remain an important area for fu-
