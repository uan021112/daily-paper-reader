# Geometric Algebra for Neural Networks: A Survey 

Tao Zhong 1, Kaison Fong 2, Keqin Wang 1, Christine Allen-Blanchette 1

> 1

Princeton University · 2Stanford University 

Geometric algebra (GA) unifies geometric primitives and transformations in a single algebraic frame-work. Neural networks designed around this framework often represent features as multivectors and constrain layer operations to respect the algebraic structure, enabling principled treatment of geometric transformations and, in many cases, built-in equivariance. This survey reviews geometric algebra neural networks, including their building blocks, equivariant properties, application do-mains, empirical performance, and software support. We provide an algebra-selection guide, an empirical comparison of GA-based and other equivariant architectures, and a failure-mode analysis covering training pathologies, deployment constraints, and performance limitations. Across the sur-veyed literature, the strongest evidence for GA-based architectures appears in settings with explicit geometric symmetries and heterogeneous geometric entities; evidence for broader advantages in parameter efficiency, interpretability, and dense prediction is comparatively limited and uneven. We therefore conclude that geometric algebra neural networks are not universally superior to well-tuned non-GA equivariant baselines, and outline open problems in algebra selection, numerical stability, scalable implementation, and reproducible evaluation. 

Keywords Geometric algebra, Clifford algebra, geometric deep learning, equivariant neural networks, multivector representations, geometric transformations.

# I Introduction 

Neural networks (NNs) [1, 2] have achieved broad success across perception and representation learning [3, 4], but they often treat geometric data through scalar or tensor parameterizations that obscure its underlying structure. This limitation is especially acute in modern perception and reasoning systems, where models must jointly manipulate heterogeneous geometric entities, including points, pixels, poses, rays, lines, planes, rigid motions, and cross-sensor transformations. In conventional pipelines, these entities are typically encoded using disparate representations and connected through ad hoc conversions. When such information is flattened into unstructured feature vectors, relationships such as incidence, orientation, metric structure, and transformation equivariance are no longer explicit [5]. As a result, models may require extensive data augmentation to approximate basic symmetries [6, 7], generalize poorly to unseen geometric configurations [8, 9], and provide limited insight into the geometric computations they perform [10]. These difficulties point less to a lack of model capacity than to a mismatch between the structure of geometric data and the representations through which many neural architectures process it. Clifford algebra [11, 12, 13, 14], often called geometric algebra (GA) in applied settings, offers a compelling response to this representational fragmentation. It provides an algebraically coherent framework in which diverse geometric entities, including points, lines, planes, circles, and spheres, and transformations, including rotations, translations, reflections, and dilations, can be represented within a common language. This contrasts with conventional pipelines in which directions, rotations, rigid motions, and projective transformations are often handled by separate formalisms such as vector algebra, quaternions, homogeneous coordinates, or transformation matrices. By encoding geometric structure directly into representations and operations, GA-native models can impose geometric priors that are difficult to express in unstructured scalar feature spaces. In selected settings, these priors have been associated with improved data efficiency [15, 16] and more interpretable intermediate representations [17]. For the pattern analysis and machine intelligence community, GA is especially relevant when a task requires joint reasoning over multiple geometric entity types, as in 3D vision, embodied perception, multi-sensor fusion, pose estimation, and manipulation. In these contexts, a unified algebraic framework can simplify layer design and reduce representational fragmentation. Research on GA-based neural networks has grown rapidly alongside geometric deep learning (GDL), which seeks to encode geometric priors and symmetries directly into model architectures [18]. Recent GA-native models have appeared across biology [19, 20], physics [21], and computer science [16, 22], reflecting the appeal of GA as a common language for structured geometric computation. At the same time, the literature remains difficult to synthesize because methods differ in their choice of algebra, notation, equivariance target, architectural primitives, and evaluation protocol. This survey addresses that fragmentation by organizing GA-native neural networks according to shared mathematical foundations, design patterns, empirical evidence, and open limitations. Within GDL, many successful architectures are designed around particular data structures or symmetry groups. Graph neural networks (GNNs) [5] operate on relational and non-Euclidean data, while group-equivariant [6, 9] and steerable [23] convolutional networks encode prescribed transformation laws for images, volumetric fields, and related grid-structured signals. These ap-proaches provide strong inductive biases, but their mathematical formulations are often specialized to particular symmetry groups, domains, or representation types. GA-native networks offer a com-plementary route: they use multivector representations and Clifford-algebra operations to express geometric entities and transformations within a common algebraic language. II-B Key Algebras 

GA-native neural networks differ not only in architecture but also in the choice of underlying algebra. The most common choices in the current literature are Euclidean GA (EGA), projective GA (PGA), conformal GA (CGA), and spacetime algebra (STA). This choice determines the available basis blades, native geometric objects, transformation groups, equivariance constraints, and computational cost (see Table 1). Selecting the appropriate algebra therefore remains an active design question [39, 40, 41]: EGA is compact but lacks native translations, PGA represents rigid motions naturally, CGA adds conformal and distance-aware structure at higher dimension, and STA is tailored to Lorentzian geometry. 

Euclidean GA (EGA) 𝔾 3,0,0 [45] has basis vectors {𝑒 1, 𝑒 2, 𝑒 3} and is spanned by 2 3 = 8 basis blades {1, 𝑒 1, 𝑒 2, 𝑒 3, 𝑒 12 , 𝑒 13 , 𝑒 23 , 𝑒 123 }. Its multivectors contain scalars, vectors, bivectors representing oriented planes, and the pseudoscalar 𝑒 123 . EGA represents rotations and reflections naturally through the 𝑂 (3) action, but it does not encode translations or affine subspaces natively. Tasks involving rigid motions or incidence relations among affine objects therefore often require an augmented algebra such as PGA or CGA [12, 15, 46].

II-C Representing Geometry and Transformations 

The practical appeal of GA for neural-network design is that geometric objects and transformations can often be represented within the same algebraic system. The precise interpretation of a multivector depends on the chosen algebra: vectors represent directed quantities in EGA, planes in common PGA conventions, null points in CGA, and spacetime events in STA. Higher-grade blades, formed through the outer product ( ∧), represent oriented subspaces or composite geometric entities such as lines, planes, circles, and spheres, depending on the algebra. Thus, the choice of algebra fixes both the geometric vocabulary available to the model and the transformations that can be expressed naturally.

Transformations in GA are commonly represented by versors , products of invertible vectors that act by sandwiching. For a unit vector 𝑛 , the map 𝑥 ′ = −𝑛𝑥𝑛 reflects a vector 𝑥 in the hyperplane orthogonal to 𝑛 . More general orthogonal transformations are obtained by composing such reflections. In particular, the product of two unit vectors defines an even versor, or rotor , which represents a rotation in the plane spanned by the corresponding directions. A rotor can be written in exponential form as 𝑅 = 𝑒 −𝐵𝜃 /2, where 𝐵 is a unit bivector specifying the rotation plane and 𝜃 is the rotation angle. The rotated multivector is obtained by the sandwich action 𝑥 ′ = 𝑅𝑥 ˜𝑅, where ˜ 𝑅 denotes reversion. This formulation is dimension-independent: rotations are associated with oriented planes rather than with axes, making the same algebraic mechanism applicable beyond three dimensions. Rigid-body motions require both rotations and translations. In projective geometric algebra, these are represented by motors , even versors that implement elements of SE (3) through the same sandwich-action principle. Motors compactly encode rigid transformations and are therefore useful in GA-native models for poses, frames, articulated objects, and rigid-body tokens. However, motors represent orientation-preserving rigid motions; isometries involving an odd number of reflections require more general versors. For GA-native neural networks, these transformation mechanisms determine which quantities can be parameterized directly, which equivariances can be enforced by construction, and which operations require additional constraints or projections. 

II-D Algebra Selection Guide 

The choice of algebra is one of the most consequential design decisions in a GA-native network, yet the literature has not converged on a single selection procedure. Table 2 provides a compact decision aid distilled from the patterns observed across the surveyed works. The primary decision variables are: (i) the geometric objects required by the task, such as positions, directions, lines, planes, circles, or spheres; (ii) the target transformation group; (iii) whether metric quantities such as distances should be represented directly in the algebra; and (iv) the available engineering budget for numerical stability, memory, and implementation complexity. The table also makes explicit when GA may be unnecessary: if the task contains no structured geometric objects, if the relevant symmetry is already handled well by a mature non-GA architecture, or if the algebraic overhead exceeds the expected inductive-bias benefit, a non-GA equivariant or conventional neural architecture (see Section III-D) may be preferable. 

# III GDL Principles: Equivariance and Symmetries 

Geometric Deep Learning (GDL) studies neural architectures whose inductive biases are matched to the structure and symmetries of the data domain. Following the Erlangen-inspired perspective of Bronstein et al. [18], a learning problem is characterized by the transformations under which predictions should be invariant or equivariant. GA-native networks fit into this programme by representing geometric quantities as multivectors and using algebraic operations designed to respect the associated transformation groups.

III-B Foundational Group Theory for Equivariance 

We briefly recall the group-theoretic notation needed for the Clifford-group discussion below. A 

group (𝐺, ◦) is a set equipped with a binary operation satisfying closure, associativity, identity, and inverse axioms. Important geometric groups include the orthogonal group O (𝑛 ), consisting of origin-preserving isometries such as rotations and reflections; the special orthogonal group SO (𝑛 ) ⊂ O(𝑛 ),consisting of orientation-preserving rotations; and the special Euclidean group SE (𝑛 ), consisting of orientation-preserving rigid motions, i.e., rotations and translations. III-C Achieving Equivariance with Geometric Algebra 

GA provides a structured route to equivariant neural-network design because many geometric transformations can be represented as versor or Clifford-group actions on multivectors. When layers are built from operations that commute with these actions, such as geometric products, grade projections, invariant scalar functions, and appropriate sandwich actions, the resulting maps can be equivariant by construction [39, 19, 44, 15, 42]. Different GAs support different transformation groups and therefore induce different geometric biases. In Euclidean GA 𝔾 𝑛, 0,0, rotations and reflections are represented through the associated Spin (𝑛 )

and Pin (𝑛 ) actions, which double-cover SO (𝑛 ) and O (𝑛 ), respectively [62, 16]. Operations such as geometric products, grade projections, and rotor actions can preserve SO (𝑛 )- or O (𝑛 )-covariance when used in architectures that respect these actions [63]. Projective GA 𝔾 𝑛, 0,1 is well suited to Euclidean equivariance. Even versors, known as motors ,implement orientation-preserving rigid motions from the SE (𝑛 ) group, and odd versors, including reflections, implement motions from the larger E (𝑛 ) group. These transformations act on PGA representations of points, lines, and planes through sandwich products. The Geometric Algebra Transformer (GATr) [15] and related models [19, 39, 42, 64, 65, 66] are among the architectures that use PGA-style multivectors for E (3)- or SE (3)-structured learning. Conformal GA 𝔾 𝑛 +1,1,0 can also represent Euclidean transformations as a subgroup of conformal transformations [39]. Spacetime Algebra (STA) 𝔾 1,3,0 is the natural algebraic setting for Lorentz-covariant modeling. Neural networks built using STA or Lorentzian GA variants, such as L-GATr [44, 67], can be made equivariant to Lorentz transformations, which is central in relativistic physics. This hierarchy means that the choice of GA strongly influences the symmetry group that a network can represent naturally. Choosing an algebra that includes more structure than the task requires may introduce unnecessary dimensionality, constraints, or numerical complexity [15], whereas choosing an algebra with insufficient structure may leave important geometric relationships outside the model’s native representation. GA-native models should therefore be understood as one route to geometry-

aware learning, alongside non-GA equivariant approaches such as group-equivariant CNNs [6], steerable CNNs [23], Tensor Field Networks [68], and E (𝑛 )-equivariant GNNs [69]. The distinctive appeal of GA is not equivariance alone, which these alternatives also provide, but the possibility of expressing heterogeneous geometric entities and their transformations within a single algebraic framework, potentially simplifying layers that must reason jointly over positions, orientations, and higher-order objects.

For neural networks, the significance of the Clifford-group action is that it extends consistently from vectors to the full Clifford algebra. An orthogonal transformation of 𝑉 induces a grade-preserving algebra action on multivectors, so scalars, vectors, bivectors, and higher-grade components transform according to compatible rules [22]. This is essential for GA-native networks, which often operate on full multivectors rather than vector features alone. Without such an extended action, equivariance on the vector subspace would not determine how higher-grade components should transform, weakening the geometric interpretation of learned multivector features. CGENNs [22] exploit this structure by constructing layers from operations that commute with the Clifford-group action. In particular, geometric products, grade projections, scalar linear combinations, and invariant scalar nonlinearities can be composed to form equivariant multivector maps. This provides a principled parameterization strategy: rather than learning arbitrary coordinate-wise maps on multivectors, the architecture restricts learnable functions to combinations of Clifford-compatible operations. Subsequent work extends or adapts this idea in several directions. Ali et al. [73] learn the underlying quadratic form while preserving Clifford-equivariant structure; Liu et al. [56] use related polynomial constructions in simplicial architectures; Brandstetter et al. [37] apply Clifford layers to PDE modeling; and Liu et al. [60] implement grade-structured polynomial multivector functions for O(𝑛 )-equivariant architectures. Filimoshina and Shirokov [75] build parameter-light GLGENNs on generalized Lipschitz groups that also cover degenerate algebras such as PGA, sharing weights across grade subspaces. Across these examples, the common design principle is that equivariance follows from the closure of the chosen layer primitives under the Clifford-group action, not from arbitrary multivector-valued parameterization.

# IV GA NN Layers 

GA-native neural networks require layer primitives that operate directly on multivector-valued features while preserving the intended geometric transformation laws. This section reviews the main building blocks used in such architectures: multivector embeddings, GA-parametrized linear and bilinear maps, transformation-based layers, equivariant activations and normalizations, and GA-based attention mechanisms. 

IV-A Multivector Representations 

GA-native architectures begin by embedding data into a chosen geometric algebra. Scalars, vectors, and higher-order geometric objects are assigned to algebra-specific components, such as grade-0 scalars, grade-1 vectors, PGA points or motors, CGA null points, or bivectors. These multivectors then serve as the feature channels processed by the network. Depending on the architecture, grades may be preserved, mixed through geometric products, gated independently, or constrained to maintain equivariance [56, 60]. Thus, the algebra choice determines both the initial encoding and the layer operations, symmetry constraints, and computational overhead available to the model [39]. 

IV-A1 Embedding Primitive Data 

The simplest embeddings map primitive feature types to algebraic components with matching trans-formation behavior. Scalar quantities, such as mass, charge, time-independent material parameters, or categorical labels, are invariant under spatial transformations and are therefore represented as grade-0 elements in 𝐶𝑙 (0) (𝑉, 𝑞 ) [22, 15]. Directional or vector-valued quantities, such as velocity, force, displacement, or local orientation features, transform covariantly under rotations and reflec-tions and are commonly embedded as grade-1 elements in 𝐶𝑙 (1) (𝑉, 𝑞 ) [22, 15]. Position coordinates require more care: they may be represented as ordinary vectors in EGA, homogeneous objects in PGA, or null-point embeddings in CGA, depending on the target symmetry and algebra. 

IV-A2 Representing Complex Geometric Structures 

More structured inputs require embeddings that reflect both the task geometry and the chosen algebra. Point clouds and meshes illustrate the main trade-offs summarized in Table 4. In EGA ( 𝔾 𝑛, 0,0), a point is represented by an origin-relative position vector, giving natural 𝑂 (𝑛 ) or 𝑆𝑂 (𝑛 ) transformation behavior but requiring centering or additional mechanisms for translation equivariance [39]. PGA (𝔾 𝑛, 0,1) introduces a null basis vector 𝑒 0 and represents Euclidean incidence geometry more directly: in 3D PGA, points are typically represented as trivectors, lines as bivectors, and planes as vectors. This makes PGA a natural choice for architectures such as GATr [15], which process point clouds or meshes without manual centering. CGA ( 𝔾 𝑛 +1,1,0) embeds Euclidean space into a higher-dimensional algebra in which null-point inner products encode squared Euclidean distance up to convention-dependent sign and scale [76]. It represents circles and spheres as blades, at the cost of higher dimensionality, e.g., 2 5 = 32 basis blades in 3D, and possible numerical issues from the indefinite metric [39]. For graph-structured data, node, edge, or simplex features can be represented as multivectors [24]. Recent simplicial models [56] initialize higher-order relationships algebraically: an edge (𝑣 𝑖 , 𝑣 𝑗 ) may be represented through the geometric product ℎ𝑣 𝑖 ℎ𝑣 𝑗 , while a triangle may be initialized as ℎ𝑣 𝑖 ℎ𝑣 𝑗 ℎ𝑣 𝑘 .These constructions encode higher-order interactions through GA operations rather than treating edges and simplices as unrelated feature channels.

IV-E GA-based Attention Mechanisms

IV-F Building GA-NN Architectures 

Building on the layer primitives above, researchers have developed a variety of complete GA-NN architectures. The earliest attempts to integrate Clifford algebras into neural networks viewed them as generalizations of complex-valued or quaternion-valued networks [88, 89, 90, 91, 92, 93]. Pearson and Bisset [94] proposed Clifford MLPs for pattern recognition, and Buchholz and Sommer [95, 96, 97] further investigated Clifford neurons and learning rules. These pioneering works established the concept of multivector-valued neurons but focused on lower-dimensional algebras without fully exploiting equivariance. Modern architectures [22, 60, 98, 59] build on this foundation with principled equivariance guarantees, forming the backbone of more complex models. As discussed in earlier sections, Geometric Clifford Algebra Networks (GCANs) [16] represent a significant step towards leveraging the geometric power of GA in a principled way. A key design principle in GCANs is that layers ensure 𝑘 -vectors map to 𝑘 -vectors, preserving the geometric type of features. GCANs have been successfully applied to modeling dynamical systems, including 3D rigid body transformations and fluid dynamics simulations (e.g., shallow water equations, Navier-Stokes equations, often using 𝔾 3,0,0 for these examples). For data on regular grids, such as images or volumetric fields from PDE simulations, the convo-lution operation can be generalized. Clifford Convolutions [37] replace scalar-valued kernels with multivector-valued kernels that are convolved with multivector feature fields using the geometric product. GA-CNNs [99] further proposes representing different spectral bands of a hyperspectral image (HSI) as components of a GA multivector. Similarly, Clifford Fourier Layers [37, 100, 101] replace the standard Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) with a Clifford-Fourier Transform, enabling spectral methods on multivector fields. Clifford-Steerable CNNs [57] achieve equivariance to groups like Pseudo-Euclidean groups E ( 𝑝, 𝑞 ) by parameterizing the steerable convolution kernels implicitly with a CGENN-style MLP, which learns the kernel’s values as an equivariant function of the relative position vector. This study highlighted the trade-offs between these algebras in terms of expressivity, the size of the symmetry group effec-tively handled, sample efficiency, and training stability. ViNE-GATr [103] addresses the scalability of E (3)-equivariant transformers by projecting dense geometric data into a coarse-grained latent space of virtual nodes. L-GATr [67] extends the GATr framework to achieve Lorentz equivariance by employing Spacetime Algebra ( 𝔾 1,3,0). It introduces new equivariant layers (linear maps, attention, LayerNorm) specifically designed for STA. L-GATr has shown state-of-the-art results in high-energy physics applications, including amplitude regression, jet classification, and as the basis for Lorentz-equivariant generative models [44]. In core sequence modeling, Versor [104] replaces traditional linear vector sequence operations with CGA operations to inherently respect the symmetries of the physical universe, combating the "Euclidean Bottleneck". Beyond these general architectural classes, GA is also being incorporated into models tailored for specific, complex geometric tasks, such as protein design [19], implicit neural volumes [105], and grasp generation [42]. Table 5 summarizes the main family structure. These specialized architectures highlight a trend towards deeply integrating the specific geometric algebra and its elements that best describe the intrinsic geometry and symmetries of the target problem domain. This tailored approach often yields significant benefits in performance and data efficiency. We will discuss these tailored methods and applications in the following sections.

V-B Computational Biology and Chemistry 

GA’s 3D equivariance [65, 64, 87], ability to encode higher-order geometry [39, 19, 59, 20], and parameter efficiency [118, 60, 101] make it a natural fit for computational biology and chemistry. Multivector channels are also suited for large meshes, helping avoid oversquashing [64]. GA is well-suited for complex 3D biomedical meshes. The GATr architecture [15] has been applied to predict wall shear stress (WSS) on arterial walls. Following this, Suk et al. [64] propose LaB-GATr for anatomical 3D meshes. It embeds meshes in PGA 𝔾 3,0,1 and uses a GA tokenization module with message passing for efficient, global processing. This 𝐸 (3)-equivariant model addresses canonical alignment issues across patients and was also applied to volume-based velocity field estimation and newborn postmenstrual age (PMA) estimation [64]. LaB-GATr++ [65] is an extension using cross-attention instead of message-passing pooling, achieving stronger accuracy and runtime on the same tasks. Separately, de Haan et al. V-C Physics-Informed Machine Learning 

Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) [135, 136] seeks models consistent with known physical laws. GA-based PIML models embody a strong form of this prior: rather than treating coupled fields (e.g., scalar pressure 𝑝 (𝑥 ) and vector velocity 𝑢 (𝑥 ), or electric 𝐸 and magnetic 𝐵 fields) as separate channels, GA represents the entire physical state as a unified multivector field [37]. GA-based layers then process this object intrinsically, providing a deep architectural inductive bias beyond surface-level loss regularization. In the field of computational fluid dynamics (CFD), initial work focuses on developing GA-based layers as direct, geometrically-aware replacements for standard NN components. Brandstetter et al. [37] introduce Clifford Convolutions and Clifford Fourier Transforms to replace standard layers in neural PDE surrogates for 2D Navier-Stokes equations and weather modeling. Similarly, GCAN [16] demonstrates strong performance on modeling shallow water equations and incompressible Navier-Stokes. This field has rapidly evolved from GA-based layers to full-fledged GA-based neural operators. The Fengbo [17] model, for instance, is a Clifford Neural Operator pipeline designed for 3D CFD. It conceptualizes the problem as an interpretable mapping from the geometry of the domain (rep-resented as a multivector) to the physics of the solution (the resulting multivector-valued pressure and velocity field). It is built on Clifford-FNO layers and achieves competitive accuracy on 3D CFD benchmarks with significantly fewer parameters than competing graph-based methods. The domain of electromagnetism and relativistic physics also provides compelling evidence for the GA-NN thesis, as it demonstrates that the choice of algebra is a critical, non-trivial design decision. STAResNet [40] directly tackles the problem of solving Maxwell’s partial differential equations. Pepe et al. [40] compare a standard Clifford ResNet using Euclidean GA ( 𝔾 3,0,0) against their STAResNet, which operates natively in STA. The Euclidean model must learn from the "split" 3D vector and scalar time representation, whereas STAResNet operates directly on the unified STA multivector 𝐹 = 𝐸 + 𝐼 𝐵 

and achieves a lower error with significantly fewer parameters. This principle extends directly to high-energy physics, where Lorentz covariance is the fundamental symmetry. Spinner et al. [44] propose L-GATr that operates entirely in Spacetime Algebra ( 𝔾 1,3) to achieve full Lorentz equivariance. It has been successfully applied to diverse Large Hadron Collider (LHC) tasks, including amplitude regression and jet classification [67]. It was also used to construct the first Lorentz-equivariant generative model for particle physics.

V-E Other Notable Applications 

GA-based networks extend beyond geometry-heavy domains wherever data has multi-dimensional or relational structure. In graph representation learning, GGAN [24] generalizes message passing to a GA-defined feature space, encoding node attributes as multivectors and reporting improved expressiveness with reduced model complexity. GA embeddings have also been applied to cross-domain few-shot graph classification [25], where the geometric richness helps reduce feature distortion at depth. In temporal and signal processing, GA-LSTNet [143] represents each time-step as a multivector encapsulating all sensor channels, modeling joint dynamics more effectively than channel-wise models. Parametrized Hypercomplex NNs [144] provide a unified framework that automatically infers the optimal algebra (complex, quaternion, or higher-dimensional Clifford) for a given dataset, operating with fewer parameters. Wi-GATr [145] applies GATr to wireless signal propagation, predicting channel observations from scene meshes and antenna poses, with lower real-world error than hybrid methods and a calibrated ray tracer. Emerging interdisciplinary applications include CGA-based interfaces with language models for spa-tial instruction execution [98], GA multivectors as word embeddings [146], randomized GA methods for LLM fine-tuning [147], and GA networks for IoT anomaly detection on microcontrollers [148].

VI-A What transfers across the literature 

The strongest recurring evidence concerns symmetry handling. Architectures such as CGENNs [22], GATr [15], L-GATr [44], and CSMPNs [56] are equivariant by construction under the relevant Clifford-group, Euclidean, or Lorentz action. Empirically, these guarantees matter most under transformation shift. In GATr [15], performance is retained on translated 𝑛 -body systems, whereas non-equivariant baselines degrade markedly. On arterial wall-shear-stress estimation, GATr [15] is strongest on non-canonicalized meshes in the benchmark of Suk et al. [119], where the model must handle arbitrary orientation directly.

Data efficiency is the second consistent theme. GATr [15] is more sample-efficient than SEGNN and the SE (3)-Transformer on synthetic 𝑛 -body data, while iP-GATr and C-GATr [39] improve further on the same family of tasks. GCAN [16] reports similar low-data gains on dynamical systems. In robotics, GAGrasp [42] and hPGA-DP [66] report faster convergence or stronger low-data behavior, though only within their own benchmarks. A third pattern is geometric expressivity. Clifford neural layers [37] improve PDE surrogates by processing coupled scalar and vector fields as multivectors rather than as unrelated channels. On WSS estimation, the strongest GATr variants are precisely those with richer geometry-aware interactions, notably distance-aware attention in iP-GATr and C-GATr [39]. VI-C Computational cost and scalability 

A recurring cost of GA is dimensional overhead: dense multivectors scale with the number of basis blades, which grows exponentially with algebra dimension. In practice, this means wider hidden states, denser bilinear products, costlier normalization, and a larger implementation burden. The algebra-selection study of de Haan et al. [39] makes the trade-off explicit: E-GATr is cheapest but weaker, simple P-GATr is under-expressive, and C-GATr is strong but harder to stabilize. GA models are not uniformly unscalable. GATr [15] shows that a GA model can inherit Transformer-like scaling when its core interaction is efficient attention rather than dense message passing. LaB-GATr [64, 65] extends this to large biomedical meshes by compressing token sets while preserving Euclidean equivariance. By contrast, dense GA operations remain expensive in some PDE [16] and graph [56] settings. 

VI-D Failure modes and limitations 

We organize observed limitations into three categories: evidence quality, model and training failure modes, and practical deployment constraints.  

> Evidence-quality limitations.

Benchmark fragmentation remains severe: many papers evaluate on one or two specialized tasks with metrics and preprocessing that differ substantially across studies, making broad claims easy to overstate. Reproducibility is uneven, with several influential results existing only as preprints or workshop papers. Apparent gains can narrow or vanish under compute-, memory-, or latency-matched comparisons, since lower parameter counts do not guarantee faster wall-clock throughput.  

> Model and training failure modes.

Performance is highly sensitive to algebra choice: if the selected algebra or metric does not match the task geometry, GA introduces computational overhead without a commensurate inductive-bias benefit, and mature non-GA equivariant baselines may remain preferable; the benzene result in CSMPN [56] is a concrete example. Expressivity can also be structurally limited: vector–vector geometric products in 𝔾 3,0,0 lack the rank-2 ( 𝐿 = 2) component, which limits force-direction accuracy in tested Clifford interatomic potentials [151]. Optimization difficulties extend beyond isolated normalization issues. In practice, multivector activations can collapse toward a subset of grades (grade collapse), constrained geometric objects such as rotors or versors may drift from their intended manifolds unless explicitly re-normalized, and initialization sensitivity can be acute. These problems are exacerbated in indefinite algebras such as CGA and STA, where the C-GATr study [39] reports that non-positive norms and cancellation effects make standard deep-learning heuristics less reliable and often necessitate full 32-bit precision. Equivariance can

also leak in practice through preprocessing choices, discretization, neighborhood construction, or coordinate conventions that inadvertently break the intended algebraic structure. 

Practical deployment constraints. Geometric products and grade-coupled operations increase memory traffic and interact poorly with mixed-precision pipelines and current GPU kernel libraries, limiting throughput even when parameter counts are competitive. Nevertheless, slim and quantized L-GATr jet taggers are estimated to cut inference energy by an order of magnitude at a moderate performance loss [152]. For homogeneous point-feature tasks or dense 2D image/video pipelines, GA should currently be viewed as a specialized option rather than a default choice. Evidence on robustness under realistic perception perturbations (sensor noise, calibration error, partial observability, and distribution shift) remains limited. The strongest results arise when the task genuinely requires joint reasoning over heterogeneous geometric entities, frames, or coupled physical quantities. 

VI-E Summary of evidence status 

Across the settings surveyed above, the current evidence base can be summarized as follows: • Established: improved inductive alignment for tasks with explicit geometric symmetries, transfor-mations, or multi-entity geometric relations. • Plausible but not yet general: gains in data efficiency, robustness under distribution shift, and compactness through structured parameterization. • Currently unproven: broad superiority on dense perception, large-scale vision backbones, or standardized foundation-model-scale settings. 

VI-F Recommended benchmarking practice 

Future GA-NN papers should adopt the following protocol to make local comparisons cumulative without forcing the field into a misleading global leaderboard. 1. State the algebraic hypothesis. Specify the algebra, target symmetry group, and whether equivariance is exact or approximate. 2. Use matched baselines. Compare against both non-equivariant and strong equivariant baselines under matched parameter counts and training budgets. 3. Include symmetry stress tests. Report transformed-input evaluations and state whether canoni-calization was required. 4. Report low-data behavior. Include learning curves or sub-sampling experiments when sample efficiency is claimed. 5. Report cost alongside accuracy. At minimum: parameter count, training precision, wall-clock time, and peak memory. 6. Ablate the geometry. Remove multivector channels, replace geometric products with linear mixing, or swap to a mismatched algebra. 7. Release reproducibility materials. Code, splits, preprocessing, and evaluation scripts are essen-tial for custom pipelines.

# VII Software Libraries and Implementation 

GA neural networks rely on software libraries to represent multivectors, implement the geometric product and related Clifford operations, and integrate these objects into deep learning stacks. Recent libraries span Python, C++, Java, JavaScript, PyTorch, TensorFlow, and JAX. Table 8 summarizes the main ecosystem relevant to GA-native learning; the text below highlights representative tools and current bottlenecks. 

VII-A ML Libraries: PyTorch, TensorFlow, and JAX 

The primary ML-facing libraries are torch_ga [153] (PyTorch) and tfga [154] (TensorFlow), which both represent multivectors as framework tensors and provide Keras-style layers such as 

GeometricSandwichProductDense (analogous to Keras’s Dense ). Both libraries feature a GeometricAlgebra 

class supporting low-level tensor operations and higher-level Multivector abstractions with operator overrides. numga [155] targets JAX (with NumPy/PyTorch fallbacks) and symbolically fuses GA operations into optimized linear operators at compile time; for example, it can deduce that the product of two quaternions remains a quaternion. However, numga is limited to algebras of dimension 

≤ 6 due to computational overhead.

VII-B General Purpose Libraries 

Among scripting tools, ganja.js [159] is a lightweight JavaScript code generator with visualization and cross-language export; clifford [157] is a mature Python numerical toolkit; and Kingdon [158] adds symbolic computation accepting numpy, PyTorch, and sympy. Gaalop [163, 166] compiles GA expressions into optimized C++, OpenCL, or CUDA code. In C++, Versor [160] and GATL [161] use template meta-programming for compile-time optimiza-tion; Klein [162] provides SIMD-optimized 3D PGA; Garamon [164] generates code for user-defined algebras; and TbGAL [165] uses tensor-based blade representations for higher-dimensional perfor-mance. Benchmarking studies [167, 165] show GATL and Versor generally perform best, depending on operation mix and algebra dimension. 

VII-C Challenges and Future Needs for Software Support 

The 2 𝑛 scaling of basis blades limits most libraries to moderate dimensions: Gaalop [163, 166], Garamon [164], GluCat [168], and Versor [160] support at most 16-, 20-, 16-, and 7-dimensional spaces, respectively. Highly optimized libraries like Klein [162] sacrifice generality. Alternatives such as TbGAL’s tensor-based approach [165] and custom GPU kernels [22] are promising for higher-dimensional algebras. ML-facing libraries such as tfga [154] (unmaintained since 2023) and torch_ga [153] are im-mature: APIs remain rough, and higher-dimensional convolutions are not yet supported. More broadly, GA’s steep learning curve, inconsistent cross-library APIs, sparse documentation, and limited benchmarking [167, 165] remain barriers to wider adoption. 

# VIII Future Directions and Conclusion 

GA-native neural networks have progressed from conceptual proposals to practical architectures with promising results in several geometry-centric domains, including high-energy physics, robotics, and computational biology. Empirical gains are most consistent when the task requires joint reasoning over heterogeneous geometric entities and explicit symmetries; for homogeneous point-feature or dense 2D settings, mature non-GA alternatives remain competitive. Several frontiers remain. 

Algebra matching and numerical stability. Systematically mapping problem symmetries to the op-timal algebra remains open. Indefinite algebras (CGA, STA) can break standard components like LayerNorm [39]; numerically robust layers for these spaces are critical. 

Scalability and architectural depth. Dense multivector representations scale as 2 𝑛 , creating a tension between expressivity and feasibility that calls for sparse representations and custom GPU kernels [22]. Current non-linearities are typically scalar-gated; fully algebra-aware activations and richer GA attention mechanisms [39] remain underexplored. 

Interpretability and foundation-model integration. GA representations are argued to be more in-spectable, but controlled benchmarks are lacking. GA could serve as a geometric interface for embodied AI and language models [98], mapping symbolic instructions into continuous transforma-tions.

Standardized evaluation. Benchmarking fragmentation (Section VI) is arguably the largest barrier to cumulative progress. A shared evaluation suite spanning mesh regression, molecular dynamics, PDE surrogates, pose estimation, and robotic control, with standardized metrics and cost reporting, would significantly accelerate the field. Multiple GA-native architecture families now deliver reproducible gains on real-world tasks, supported by a growing software ecosystem, but larger-scale and more reproducible evidence is still needed before GA can be considered a standard tool. The central remaining challenges (algebra selection, numerical stability in indefinite metrics, scalable implementations, and reproducible benchmarking) are increasingly concrete; addressing them will determine whether GA becomes a routine component of the geometric deep learning toolkit. For the pattern analysis community, the most promising near-term opportunities lie in 3D vision, embodied perception, multi-sensor fusion, and manipulation, where heterogeneous geometric entities and explicit symmetries are central. We hope this survey provides both a practical reference for researchers entering the field and a concrete roadmap for the community.
