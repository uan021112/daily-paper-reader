# CoGaussian: Open-Vocabulary Gaussian-based Collaborative 3D Scene Understanding 

Om Shivam Verma 1∗, Shaya Farahmand 1∗, Lingjun Zhao 1, Lu Gan 1

Abstract — Collaborative perception enables agents to retrieve the scene semantics and geometry beyond the field of view of any individual agent. This broader scene understanding enhances transportation safety and improves efficiency in multi-robot systems. However, existing collaborative semantic occupancy methods rely on dense 3D voxel annotations and are limited to recognizing objects within a predefined set of semantic classes. To overcome these limitations, we present CoGaussian, an open-vocabulary Gaussian-based collaborative semantic occupancy prediction framework. CoGaussian rep-resents a scene as a set of foundation model-aligned sparse 3D Gaussians, which serve as a compact representation for communicating and fusing geometric and semantic information across multiple agents. On the Semantic-OPV2V benchmark, our method outperforms existing open-vocabulary single-agent approaches, highlighting the effectiveness of open-vocabulary collaborative scene understanding. 

I. INTRODUCTION Collaborative perception enables local agents to exchange information that may be occluded or beyond their field-of-view, improving geometric and semantic understanding of their environment, and enabling safer and more reliable decision making in autonomous driving and robot navigation. Semantic occupancy prediction (SOP) is a representative and challenging task in 3D spatial understanding, which aims to predict voxel-wise occupancy status and semantic class. Recently, 3D Gaussian-based methods [1] have been shown to be effective for SOP. The GaussianFormer family [2]– [5] represents scenes as 3D Gaussians, supervised by dense occupancy labels. VOGS-CP [6] extends GaussianFormer to collaborative SOP which communicates Gaussian primitives between neighboring agents. However, these methods are limited to predicting semantics within a closed vocabulary and heavily rely on densely annotated 3D occupancy labels that are expensive to obtain. Vision foundation models (VFMs), such as DINO [7] and CLIP [8] have demonstrated strong open-vocabulary capa-bilities in 2D perception. In addition, Talk2DINO [9] and DINO.txt [10] further align 2D VFM features with natural language. Building on the above VFMs, GaussTR [11] repre-sents scenes as a set of sparse open-vocabulary 3D Gaussians that are predicted in a feed-forward manner and can be queried via natural language. The Gaussians are encoded with geometry and semantic information by splatting into 2D views and aligning rendered features with foundation model-derived depth [12] and semantics [7], [8]. II. M ETHOD 

We represent a scene as a sparse set of 3D Gaussians with associated DINOv2 [7] features. Predicted Gaussians are splatted back into the source camera view and supervised by DINOv2 [7] features and Metric3Dv2 [12] monocular depth. During inference, semantic class logits are computed as similarity between the predicted Gaussian features and Talk2DINO [9] text embeddings for each class. 

A. Preliminaries 

Each 3D Gaussian G is parametrized as: 

G = {μ, s, r, α, f }, (1) 

where μ ∈ R3 is the mean (center), s ∈ R3 is the per-axis scale, r ∈ R4 is the rotation quaternion of the axes, 

α ∈ [0 , 1] is the opacity, and f ∈ RD is the high-dimensional DINOv2 feature vector. The model inputs an image and initializes a set of Gaussian embeddings {qi ∈ RE }Ni=1 ,where E denotes the dimension of the embedding. Each embedding vector is associated with a randomly sampled location (ui, v i) ∈ [0 , 1] 2 on the image and its correspond-ing interpolated Metric3D depth value. The coordinates are concatenated with the depth value, and then projected into 3D space via camera intrinsics and LiDAR-camera extrinsics to obtain the Gaussian’s mean position μ in the LiDAR frame. Once the Gaussian embeddings are initialized, they are refined through a set of transformer [15] layers F

that consists of a deformable attention module [16], a self-attention module, and an MLP layer, as shown in Fig. 2. The point (ui, v i) serves as the reference point for deformable attention over the image features:

The model is trained by differentiably rendering the DINOv2 features and depth from the predicted Gaussians using alpha-blending, implemented with optimized CUDA kernels [17]. The training objective penalizes the difference between the rendered outputs and corresponding predicted depth and features [11]. 

B. Joint Training 

GaussTR [11] is trained per camera view , with Gaus-sians predicted from each camera splatted back into that view for supervision. While this approach is effective for datasets with limited camera overlap, such as in the nuScenes dataset [18], it is suboptimal for multi-agent perception, where independent supervision fails to exploit overlapping observations across agents. Therefore, the model is trained 

per scene as shown in Fig. 2. All Gaussians predicted from cameras in a scene are aggregated and splatted into every camera in which they are visible, not only the cameras that produced them. Thus, predictions consistent with a single view but inconsistent with other views are penalized. During inference, Gaussians are predicted from each camera, aggregated, and then used by agents for downstream tasks. CoGaussian can use weights from either per-image or per-scene training. In Sec. III, we refer to the former as Zero-shot Concatenation , which combines per-image training with our proposed inference method, and the later as Joint Training ,which uses per-scene training to enable collaboration. 

C. Radius-Gated Attention Fusion 

Joint Training improves performance as reported in Ta-ble I. However, aggregating Gaussians from different cam-eras with overlapping fields of view causes the respective Gaussians to overlap. While this adds information for the ego, it also introduces noise. Additionally, the appearance of the same object often varies across different viewpoints. Thus, Gaussians for the same object may differ widely. Joint training helps mitigate such differences, but it does so im-plicitly during Gaussian prediction. Therefore, we make this 

explicit by introducing a fusion module. The fusion module aggregates useful information from Gaussians predicted by neighboring agents. We use single-head scaled dot product attention [15] between ego Gaussian embeddings and neigh-boring Gaussian embeddings. Afterwards, a Fusion Head 

with the same architecture as the Gaussian Head predicts Gaussians from the updated embeddings. The updated ego Gaussians and the neighboring Gaussians are aggregated to form the final set used for downstream tasks. The fusion pipeline is shown in Fig. 2.

Training. We follow a two-stage training paradigm. Stage 1: We train the Transformer Layer and Gaussian Head as described in Sec. II-B. Stage 2: The Transformer Layer and Gaussian Head are frozen, and the model learns the Radius-Gated Attention Layer and the Fusion Head. III. EXPERIMENTS 

A. Dataset and Metrics 

We evaluate on the Semantic-OPV2V benchmark [14], an extension of OPV2V dataset [13] with 3D SOP ground truth. OPV2V is a simulated dataset for vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) collaboration with two to seven collaborating agents in a scene. Each vehicle is equipped with 4 cameras. The ground truth consists of 12 semantic labels over a 40 × 40 × 3.2m

area with a grid size of 100 × 100 × 8, giving a voxel size of 

0.4m. Following previous work [6], [14], we report the per-class Intersection-over-Union (IoU), and its mean (mIoU), along with the overall occupancy IoU. Even though fully-supervised methods are included for reference, they are not comparable to our approach as they use dense 3D voxel supervision. 

B. Implementation Details 

The model is trained per-view for 6 epochs for single-agent and zero-shot concatenation. Joint per-scene training is then run for 3 epochs. For Radius-Gated Attention Fusion, the transformer is frozen, and the attention module and fusion head are trained for 3 epochs. All training runs use a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and take 8-14 hours. The losses converge within 2 epochs for all runs. Metric3Dv2 [12] provides depth supervision, and DINOv2 [7] provides open-vocabulary feature supervision. A. Quantitative Results 

Table I highlights quantitative results from previous works, as well as our experiments. Relative to the single-agent GaussTR baseline, zero-shot concatenation reduces performance by −4.45 /−1.45 (IoU/mIoU), showing that naive Gaussian aggregation introduces spatially inconsis-tent structure. Joint Training improves over the base-line by +1.96 /+0.48 (IoU/mIoU), indicating that cross-view rendering supervision reconciles these inconsisten-cies. Using Radius-Gated Attention gives a modest gain of 

+1.99 /+0.69 (IoU/mIoU) over the baseline. Class-wise behavior is mixed, with frequently observed classes like “road”, “sidewalk”, and “vehicles” benefiting the most from collaboration, as they have major overlap among agents and thus a stronger supervision signal. Prominent but less visible classes like ”vegetation” and ”terrain” struggle with collaboration and other small and uncommon classes have mixed results. 

B. Qualitative Results 

Fig. 1 shows that the collaborative methods recover struc-ture outside the ego view. Our method tends to produce volumetric predictions rather than one voxel thick surfaces in the ground truth. This reflects the nature of sparse 3D Gaus-sian representations, unlike methods like [2]–[4] that rely on 3D semantic voxel supervision and many surface-fitting small Gaussians. 2D image-based supervision also makes objects occupying large image regions more prominent in 3D space. The elongated prediction of Vehicle 1 and volumetric prediction of Vehicle 2 demonstrate this behavior. Joint Training reduces these inconsistencies, producing smaller and more conservative Gaussians, evident in Ve-hicle 2 and smaller elliptical predictions throughout the scene. The fusion pipeline preserves this coherence while recovering more dominant regions of the scene, including the Road and some Vehicles. However, thin and infrequent structures remain difficult to reconstruct reliably under purely 2D self-supervision. V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK We introduce CoGaussian which is, to the best of our knowledge, the first framework for open-vocabulary col-laborative perception that communicates foundation model-aligned 3D Gaussians among agents to represent a scene. Evaluation shows that collaboration is effective when inter-action is learned. Zero-shot collaboration hurts performance, whereas joint training and radius-gated attention improve over the single-agent baseline. CoGaussian also brings the flexibility of open-vocabulary to collaborative perception. We plan to explore different fusion architectures and evaluate additional downstream tasks such as BEV Semantic Segmentation and 3D Object Detection.
