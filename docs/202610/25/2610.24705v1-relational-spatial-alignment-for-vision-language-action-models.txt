Abstract: Spatial understanding is essential for robots to perceive their surround-ing environments and execute actions. Existing approaches typically encode 3D awareness into vision-language-action (VLA) models by directly aligning their representations with pre-trained 3D foundation models. However, we observe that such direct geometric feature alignment essentially exploits 3D foundation priors as a form of low-rank regularization in the feature space, resulting in se-vere rank collapse of the pre-trained VLA representations. In contrast, we shift the focus from enforcing point-wise equality to aligning pair-wise relationships. We propose a representation-alignment method that enables the injection of 3D spatial awareness without compromising the model’s original expressivity. Ex-tensive experiments on public benchmarks, our new spatial reasoning benchmark, and real-world manipulation scenarios demonstrate that our approach effectively incorporates 3D spatial representations into VLA reasoning, achieving robust per-formance and enhanced spatial awareness across diverse manipulation tasks. 

Keywords: vision-language-action models, spatial understanding 

## 1 Introduction 

Spatial understanding is fundamental to robotic manipulation, enabling robots to interpret their envi-ronment from multi-view RGB observations, estimate distances, localize objects, and infer 3D struc-ture either explicitly or implicitly. Traditional approaches [1, 2] typically follow a reconstruction-before-action paradigm, in which an explicit 3D scene is first reconstructed and then used for down-stream tasks such as path planning and control [3, 4]. Inspired by the success of end-to-end learning in natural language processing [5] and autonomous driving [6, 7], recent attention has shifted toward Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, a unified end-to-end learning framework for embodied AI. To incorporate spatial awareness, some prior meth-ods [8, 9, 10, 11] follow the reconstruction-before-action paradigm by leveraging explicit spatial representations, such as depth maps or point clouds, as additional inputs to VLA models. However, such approaches often depend on pre-scanned environments or high-quality sensor observations, both of which can be unreliable in real-world scenarios due to sensor noise, reflective surfaces, and occlusions. Consequently, such methods remain limited in constrained environments and struggle to generalize to open-world problems. To avoid reliance on explicit 3D representations, recent works [12, 13] shift the focus to encode im-plicit 3D geometric structure within the latent space. Building on recent advances in 3D foundation models [14, 15, 16], this approach transfers geometric knowledge into VLAs through feature-level distillation. While these approaches bypass the need for explicit 3D representations, the underlying mechanism of this latent geometric transfer remains underexplored. More importantly, it raises a fundamental question: Does injecting spatial awareness into the VLA with direct feature alignment compromise its original semantic-level representational capabilities?

VGGT [16] is used as the 3D teacher model. In this work, we systematically investigate the direct feature alignment between 3D foundation mod-els and 2D VLA models. Our analysis suggests that direct spatial alignment does not necessarily lead to genuine 3D understanding. Instead, it primarily behaves as a form of low-rank regulariza-tion. Specifically, we observe a substantial collapse in the numerical rank of the VLA feature space after alignment (Fig. 1(b); see Sec. 3.1), indicating a significant loss of representation capacity. Consequently, experiments across diverse benchmarks show that models trained with direct spatial alignment suffer from degraded generalization performance. To overcome these limitations, we propose a new paradigm of relational spatial alignment that aims to distill 3D awareness into 2D VLAs while preserving their expressivity. Motivated by the ob-servation that 3D foundation models encode spatial structures through cosine similarity (i.e., the attention maps) between corresponding features, we argue that effective spatial understanding can be achieved by aligning pair-wise relationships rather than enforcing point-wise equality . By su-pervising relative similarities instead of absolute feature directions and norms, our method allows the 2D VLA representations to maintain their native feature coordinate system, with 3D knowl-edge being encoded in its feature similarity. (Fig. 1(a)). Theoretically, we demonstrate that this relational alignment objective is inherently rotation-invariant : The optimization objective remains strictly unchanged under arbitrary orthogonal transformations within the latent feature space, which is crucial for preventing the feature collapse. To practically instantiate this paradigm, we intro-duce RIGID: Rotation-Invariant Geometry Distillation , which implements the relation align-ment paradigm and formulates the geometry knowledge transfer as an Optimal Transport problem over spatial attention distributions. Compared to direct feature supervision, RIGID improves spatial awareness while maintaining the numerical rank of the learned representations, effectively prevent-ing capacity collapse. Extensive experiments on both simulated and real-world robotic tasks demon-strate that RIGID achieves state-of-the-art performance, exhibiting better robustness and open-world generalization than direct feature alignment.

One main stream of methods embeds raw 3D observations, such as depth maps or point clouds, directly into the input space. Another paradigm utilizes auxiliary explicit 3D cues instead of raw sensory data. For instance, SpatialVLA [23] applies Egocentric 3D Position En-coding, and TraceVLA [24] leverages visual traces. Furthermore, approaches like ReconVLA [25], DreamVLA [26], and GuidedVLA [27] enforce 3D representation learning by employing explicit 3D feature reconstruction as an auxiliary supervision signal. Although these models provide preci-sion in handling height variations and object posing, they suffer from substantial data dependencies, as they often require high-fidelity 3D annotations or depth estimations that are frequently noisy in real-world environments. 

Implicit 3D representations in VLA models. Recent advances in 3D reconstruction have enabled the end-to-end learning of multi-view geometry [14, 28, 15, 16, 29, 30, 31]. These methods typically employ transformer-based architectures to infer geometric properties such as point maps, depth, and camera parameters from multi-view images. To circumvent the dependency on physical 3D sensors or explicit annotations, recent vision-language-action (VLA) models leverage these learned geomet-ric cues for implicit spatial grounding. Methods such as Evo-0 [32, 33] and GeoAware-VLA [34] directly integrate geometric features generated by 3D foundation models into the VLA backbone. Conversely, techniques including Spatial Forcing [12], ROCKET [13], and GLaD [35] leverage feature distillation. They align the intermediate latent states of the VLA with the geometric embed-dings of a frozen teacher model (e.g., VGGT) to acquire spatial awareness. However, this feature integration or direct alignment across different modalities can potentially compromise the 2D image-derived representations acquired during large-scale pre-training, such as semantic understanding and general reasoning abilities, thereby degrading the overall robustness of the model. 

Relational knowledge distillation. Relational Knowledge Distillation (RKD) [36] is built upon the knowledge distillation (KD) [37], which focuses on transferring knowledge from the teacher model by distilling the in-batch structure. While traditional RKD methods primarily focus on single-modality and inter-instance associations, our approach targets the latent feature alignment between VLA models and 3D foundation models in spatial through multi-view inputs.

Geometry-aware distribution alignment. Existing distillation objectives, such as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, enforce strict token-wise correspondence. Under a KL objective, a minor spatial shift is penalized equally to a completely incorrect prediction, offering poor gradient sig-nals when attention distributions do not perfectly overlap. ## 4 Simulation 

4.1 Setup Datasets and benchmarks. We train our model on the LIBERO [39] training set. We evaluate our proposed method across three simulated benchmarks: LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and our newly introduced LIBERO-Probing suite. We did not utilize the LIBEO-Plus training set. 

LIBERO-Probing. We introduce LIBERO-Probing, a new benchmark to systematically evaluate the representation capacity. Specifically, we re-render the LIBERO training environments to obtain ground-truth depth, semantic masks, and spatial correspondences. By freezing the pre-trained VLA and training a lightweight MLP, we can perform an evaluation of the learned representations using the same demonstration states as in the main task. 

Implementation details. Our training procedure on LIBERO follows OpenVLA and Spatial Forc-ing [40, 41, 12]. The base model utilizes a VLM backbone pre-trained on the Open-X-Embodiment dataset [42], incorporating a fused vision encoder that combines features from SigLIP [43] and DINOv2 [44]. To ensure a fair comparison with existing baselines, we train our method using LoRA [45]. More details can be found in the supplementary material.

## 5 Real-world experiments 

5.1 Experimental setting Setup. We perform the real robot experiments in a complex toy kitchen environment that contains various objects. In this setting, we define six tasks as shown in Fig. 7: (a) placing the pot in a sink. ,(b) opening the microwave , (c) taking down the fork , (d) stacking the bowls , (e) putting the block in pan , and (f) pouring water into the sink . As shown in Fig. 7, these scenarios require the model to handle complex environments and demonstrate spatial reasoning ability. We employ a Franka Emika Panda robotic arm with wrist and side cameras for all tasks. An addi-tional agent-view camera is reserved for visualization only. Both teleoperated demonstrations and robot manipulations operate at a control frequency of 15 Hz. For each task, we collect 10 demon-strations, yielding 60 task-specific trajectories. These trajectories are included in the full multitask dataset of 176 demonstrations, with the remaining demonstrations collected from other auxiliary tasks. For model training, we adopt a two-stage fine-tuning strategy: the model is first fine-tuned on the entire 176-demonstration dataset for 10k steps, followed by a task-specific fine-tuning for 1k steps using the corresponding 10 demonstrations. Finally, we evaluate the learned policies using 10 trials per task. 

Baselines. We select two representative baseline models: π0 [51] as our 2D VLA baseline, and Spatial Forcing [12] as its 3D counterpart. Following Spatial Forcing [12], we use the π0 as the backbone and integrate our proposed relation alignment. Our method and all baselines load the π0

checkpoints pre-trained on the DROID dataset [56] for initialization.

## 6 Discussion 

Limitations. Despite its effectiveness, RIGID relies on a pre-trained 3D expert during training. Therefore, the quality of spatial distillation inherently depends on both the teacher model’s capa-bilities and the scale of the training data. Specifically, we use VGGT [16] as the 3D teacher; while effective and generalizable, it still possesses known biases and limitations [30, 55]. Furthermore, the limited scale of the robot fine-tuning dataset presents another constraint. Although VGGT is pre-trained on large-scale 3D data, the comparatively much smaller fine-tuning dataset restricts the diversity and generalizability of the spatial knowledge transferred to the student model. 

Conclusion. We present RIGID, a framework designed to enhance spatial understanding in VLA models while remaining effective on VLA’s original capabilities. The key idea is that, unlike di-rect feature alignment with a 3D teacher model, RIGID distills 3D awareness into 2D VLAs while preserving their original representation space. To effectively incorporate spatial cues, we formulate geometry distillation as a Wasserstein-based optimization problem. Experiments in simulation, real-world robotic settings, and probing analyses, together with representation rank analysis, demonstrate that our method improves spatial understanding while preserving semantic understanding. 9Acknowledgments

## B Additional analysis on rank collapse 

Implementing details. In this section we present the implementation details for our rank analy-sis. We compare the visual features of our model against three baselines: OpenVLA [41], Spatial Forcing [12], and the 3D foundation model (VGGT) [16]. For each model, we process 50 batches (50 × 8 = 400 samples) of LIBERO-Spatial trajectories from the training split. We extract the hid-den states from layer 24, where both our method and Spatial Forcing incorporate depth information, and retain only visual patch tokens. For VGGT, we directly use the representations from its final layer, which also serve as the distillation target in Spatial Forcing. We then mean-pool the spatial patch tokens to obtain a single global feature vector per frame. Af-ter applying per-sample ℓ2 normalization, we construct a feature matrix and mean-center it across samples by subtracting the feature mean from each vector before performing singular value decom-position (SVD). From the resulting singular-value spectrum, we compute three metrics to assess feature diversity: the stable rank, which reflects the number of effectively utilized dimensions; the effective rank, which reflects the uniformity of variance across dimensions; and the PCA cumulative explained variance, which indicates the degree of feature collapse into dominant dimensions. We also visualize these properties using a Scree Plot (Fig. S1(a)) and a Cumulative Variance Plot (Fig. S1(b)). While the absolute rank values may be affected by the limited sample size, our analysis focuses on the relative differences across models. 

Additional analysis. In the Scree Plot (Fig. S1(a)), the log-scaled singular values across component indices reflect the representational capacity of the model, where a slowly decaying curve indicates a rich, high-dimensional feature space. As shown, the curve for our method closely aligns with that of the OpenVLA baseline. This similarity suggests that our method preserves feature diversity relative to the OpenVLA baseline. Conversely, the curve for the Spatial Forcing baseline shows a noticeable downward shift, indicating a loss of expressive power across higher-order components. This dimen-sional collapse is further validated by the Cumulative Explained Variance plot (Fig. S1(b)), where a steeper ascent implies that information is heavily compressed into fewer principal components. Loss function. We evaluated several loss functions as optimization objectives, including L1, Mean Squared Error (MSE), Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) [58, 59], and Wasserstein loss. Empirically, the Wasserstein loss achieves the best bal-ance across all perturbation categories. While KL divergence performs well generally, it yields suboptimal results on camera view change tasks. This aligns with our previous analysis 3.2: under a KL objective, a minor spatial shift is penalized equally to a completely incorrect prediction, which provides poor gradient signals when attention distributions do not perfectly overlap. Consequently, its generalization to viewpoint variations is less stable than that of the Wasserstein loss. For other losses, MMD utilizes a kernel-based metric measuring the distance between probabil-ity distributions and is position-agnostic. As a result, it shows suboptimal performance on spatial understanding tasks that require precise location information. By comparison, L1 and MSE are de-signed to match absolute values rather than measure distribution distances. They performed poorly on spatial understanding tasks but demonstrated excellent robustness against position-invariant per-turbations, such as lighting and background changes. Considering all these factors, we select the Wasserstein loss as the primary optimization objective for our method. However, we also observe its limitations in handling language and robot state perturbations. Effectively combining the strengths of different loss functions remains a valuable direction for future research.

## D Probing experiments 

Probing dataset construction. We generate a dedicated probing dataset using the LIBERO bench-mark [39], which provides 130 tabletop manipulation tasks across five task suites: LIBERO-Spatial (10 tasks), LIBERO-Object (10 tasks), LIBERO-Goal (10 tasks), LIBERO-10 (10 tasks), and LIBERO-90 (90 tasks). All demonstrations are rendered in the MuJoCo physics simulator via Robosuite [60]. For each task demonstration, we render two camera views simultaneously: an agent view (fixed third-person camera) and a wrist camera. For each view we record: • RGB images from two views. • Depth map in meters, from the simulation depth buffer. • Instance segmentation map of per-pixel instance IDs from the simulation scene graph. Robot body components and backgrounds are excluded and remapped to class 0. The resulting taxonomy contains 52 classes: background and 51 interactive object categories. • Cross-View Correspondence. For each demonstration frame, we compute up to 32 valid point correspondences between agent-view and wrist-view using geometric reprojection 17 through camera intrinsics and extrinsics provided by the simulation. Candidate query pixels are sampled uniformly from foreground pixels in the wrist-view image. Each LIBERO task provides 50 demonstrations that often exhibit near-identical initial object con-figurations, introducing significant redundancy. Therefore, for each task, we select five maximally diverse demonstrations using farthest-point sampling (FPS) on the MuJoCo state vector. FPS ini-tializes from demo 0 and iteratively selects the demo whose state vector is farthest (in ℓ2 distance) from all already-selected demos. We split at the task level rather than the demo level to test generalisation to held-out tasks and object configurations. For the four main suites, we assign the 8 tasks to training and the 2 tasks to testing. The LIBERO-90 suite (90 tasks) is assigned entirely to training following the setup in the original LIBERO dataset. As a result, it contains 122 training tasks of 610 demos and 8 testing tasks with 40 demos. 

Feature extraction. We extract the hidden state features from the 24th layer of OpenVLA [40], Spatial Forcing [12], and our proposed method. This specific layer is selected because the distilla-tion loss is applied to it during training, thus directly reflecting how distillation shapes the learned representations. All probes operate on these frozen features using lightweight output heads, detailed as follows. 

Depth probe. The output head architecture utilizes a 1 × 1 convolution to map the features to spatial scalars, followed by a Softplus activation to ensure positivity and then bilinear upsampling to input resolution. The L1 loss is applied. The evaluation metrics are δ1.25 , δ1.03 , and Relative Error (REL). 

Segmentation probe. A 1 × 1 convolution projects the features into 52 class logits per spatial location, which are subsequently bilinearly upsampled. This probe is supervised via the cross-entropy loss. The evaluation metric is Pixel Accuracy. 

Correspondence probe. A shared linear projection embeds both agent and wrist view features into a 256-dimensional space, generating a spatial correspondence heatmap via ℓ2-normalized dot-product attention. The objective is formulated as a 256-way cross-entropy classification loss applied over the flattened 16 × 16 token grid. The evaluation metrics are Mean Pixel Error (MPE) and PCK10.
