Abstract
Neural implicit representations provide memory-efficient
signed distance field (SDF) parameterizations for large-
scale LiDAR scenes, but the geometry recovered from
sparse and often noisy inputs carries jitter and artifacts.
Classical geometric optimization techniques resolve such
artifacts with surface energy functionals, whose minimiz-
ers correspond to smooth surfaces. Motivated by this prin-
ciple, we formulate neural LiDAR mapping as the opti-
mization of energy functionals over the parameter space
of neural implicit representations. Specifically, we regu-
larize the signed distance field with an energy functional
defined on the shape operator of its level set, which repre-
sents the curvature of the surface, thus encouraging smooth-
ness along the surface. This adapts classical geometric pri-
ors to the scale of million-point LiDAR maps. Experiments
on large-scale datasets show reconstructions with improved
smoothness at preserved geometric fidelity, and no addi-
tional inference-time cost. The code is publicly available
at https://github.com/HrishikeshVish/nieo
1. Introduction
Constructing accurate geometric representations of large-
scale environments is a fundamental task in 3D computer
vision. These spatial models serve as the foundation for
downstream visual reasoning tasks, simulation frameworks,
and autonomous systems [2, 3, 33, 34, 43].
To capture
these environments, cameras and LiDAR (Light Detection
and Ranging) are the two most common sensors utilized in
practice. While cameras capture dense photometric infor-
mation, LiDAR provides the depth perception essential for
spatial modeling and geometric mapping. However, rely-
ing directly on raw LiDAR scans presents challenges. Cap-
tured point clouds can be noisy and often sparse, particu-
larly in areas with limited sensor coverage. This sparsity
makes generating high-fidelity surfaces complex, and the
resulting mapped surfaces often exhibit jitter. Ensuring that
the reconstructed surface accurately represents the continu-
ous underlying geometry while preserving localized details
remains a challenge in large-scale 3D mapping.
Neural implicit 3D reconstruction frameworks typically
map scenes using learnable continuous feature representa-
tions [23, 62]. When handling large-scale geometry, these
frameworks represent the environment using sparse hierar-
chical spatial features. This serves as a differentiable latent
state, using which an implicit neural representation learns
a continuous signed distance field (SDF) [50, 67], often
using physics-based priors derived from partial differen-
tial equations (PDE). Leveraging the connections between
the SDF and equations such as Eikonal PDE and the Pois-
son PDE [54, 67], recent works have regularized neural re-
construction through PDE based objectives. While Eikonal
and Poisson constraints provide metric consistency to the
learned field, noisy and sparsely sampled LiDAR observa-
tions can still introduce spatial jitter in the reconstructed
surfaces.

A fundamentally different perspective on surface char-
acterization arises from smoothness priors derived from en-
ergy functionals.
Neural reconstruction approaches have
used Hessians and their energies as penalties [9, 66] but of-
ten lose geometric detail due to excessive smoothing [58].
This is addressed by instead imposing constraints on the
singular Hessian [60] or by aligning gradients and the Hes-
sians [58]. However, these approaches struggle in sparse
and large-scale scenes. A key challenge in LiDAR sweeps
is that we often have large open regions spanning meters,
sampled sparsely and noisily. In these settings, Eikonal con-
straints can capture larger geometric structures, but noise
manifests as surface-level jitter. Meanwhile, classical ge-
ometry processing has long leveraged optimization strate-
gies over surface curvature energies defined by shape oper-
ators [13, 14, 17, 20, 48], which enable surface smoothing,
fairing, bending, and the tuning of surface-level irregular-
ities [35, 45], but their use in large-scale LiDAR mapping
remains unexplored.
Our Contribution. Motivated by these classical tech-
niques, in this work, we pose neural LiDAR mapping as a
surface energy optimization problem defined over the pa-
rameter space of neural representations. We consider the
energy functional defined over the shape operator, which
measures the surface variations. We find that this energy
can characterize the sharp, jitter seen on the reconstructed
surface and optimizing over it seeks a minimum energy
configuration for reconstruction. By applying this energy
as the regularizer for a sparse hierarchical neural implicit
representation, we show that the resulting maps exhibit im-
proved geometric fidelity across large-scale datasets. We
evaluate our approach on a diverse set of object-, building-
, and street-scale scenes using established LiDAR map-
ping benchmarks, including KITTI [19], MaiCity [53], and
Newer College [40]. To evaluate reconstruction of more
complex surfaces, we additionally construct a synthetic Li-
DAR benchmark from the Tanks-and-Temples [28] dataset.
We show that our method consistently achieves the high-
est overall F-scores across these benchmarks, outperform-
ing both LiDAR mapping methods and neural surface re-
construction approaches.
2. Background
2.1. Standard and Neural LiDAR mapping
Typical LiDAR mapping approaches relies on a variety of
geometric and probabilistic techniques. These involve cor-
respondence point optimization [56, 65], incremental trian-
gulation [31], Gaussian Process-based meshing [26, 42],
surfel-based meshing [7], and coverage maps [46].
Ad-
ditionally, volumetric Truncated Signed Distance Function
(TSDF) integration frameworks, such as VDBFusion [55],
provide efficient spatial data structures for merging range
sensor data. While effective, these explicit methods often
struggle to produce globally smooth manifolds from highly
sparse data.
To address the limitations of discrete meshing, im-
plicit neural representations have emerged as powerful
continuous surrogates for SDF estimation.
Neural Ra-
diance Fields (NeRFs) and Neural LiDAR Fields (NFL)
[24] model scenes through coordinate-based networks [4].
For memory-efficient large-scale offline mapping, methods
such as SHINE [67], 3QFP [50], PIN-SLAM [38], and
LONER [25] introduce sparse, tree-based data structures to
accelerate neural implicit reconstruction.
2.2. Physics Based Approaches
The emerging physics-based mapping techniques integrate
mathematical priors into neural networks to constrain the
underlying geometry of the learned manifold.
Among
these, data-driven approaches [22] use gradient-based data-
dependent kernels within the deep learning framework
[23, 61, 62], while physics-informed approaches embed the
governing differential equations like the Eikonal [59, 67] or
Poisson constraints [39] as loss functions to guide learning.
PUMA [54] leverages Poisson constraints for LiDAR map-
ping. Fully data-driven extensions utilize neural operators
[5, 29, 52] to implicitly model the underlying PDEs across
various graphics tasks [1, 6, 10, 51].
2.3. Neural Signed Distance Functions
Neural SDFs for surface reconstruction often leverage
higher-order gradient terms for improving geometric fi-
delity. The volumetric Hessian energy, introduced for sur-
face reconstruction by [9], is applied to neural fields by
RegSDF [66], which penalizes the Hessian of the implicit
function directly, minimizing the Hessian energy together
with a minimal-surface term. [58] penalizes the product of
the Hessian and the local gradient, which vanishes when the
gradient is an eigenvector of the Hessian. Neural-Singular-
Hessian [60] minimizes the determinant of the Hessian near
the surface. But these approaches can fail in large-scale and
highly sparse settings. Other methods include penalizing
the divergence of the gradient field [8], leveraging periodic
activations [44], symmetric Chamfer data terms [21], per-
turbed query coordinates [32], and neural Galerkin solvers
[63].
However, it has been shown that directly optimizing Hes-
sian energies can often lead to oversmoothing and eventu-
ally mesh degeneracy [58]. In sparse LiDAR settings, en-
suring smooth reconstructions while avoiding mesh degen-
eracy remains a key challenge. We propose to leverage the
shape operators [13, 16, 30, 36, 64], wherein, we define the
variational energy over the curvature of the level set. We
detail this in Sec. 3.
3. Problem Setup
Let χ = {xi ∈R3, i = 1, ..., N} be the point cloud
sampled from the spatial domain Ω, where Ω⊂R3 de-
notes a bounded spatial domain equipped with the stan-
dard Euclidean metric. We seek to extract a surface mesh
M = (V, F), with vertices V and faces F, such that M ac-
curately reconstructs the underlying geometry of the scene.

We define this surface as the zero-level set of a continuous
SDF, u : R3 →R, denoted by S = {x ∈Ω| u(x) = 0}.
The discrete mesh M is extracted by applying marching
cubes to the signed distance field u. Our objective is to
learn the implicit mapping Gθ : χ →u, where G is a neural
network parameterized by learnable weights θ.

4. Training and Implementation Details
Dataset Details
We evaluate our approach on standard
scene-level LiDAR sweeps. The MaiCity dataset [53] is
a synthetic dataset generated from a 3D CAD model of an
urban environment. It contains 100 frames, which were cre-
ated by simulating LiDAR scans through ray-casting tech-
niques. The Newer College dataset [40] consists of data
captured at New College, Oxford, using a handheld sensor
setup, which includes a stereo camera and a 3D LiDAR.
This dataset includes 1,500 frames and is accompanied by
precise ground truth measurements. Finally, we also bench-
mark against the KITTI dataset [19], a widely used dataset
for outdoor odometry tasks. KITTI includes sequences cap-
tured with a 64-beam LiDAR sensor, along with camera
data, and provides near ground truth estimates for incremen-
tal mapping in both urban and rural environments.
Existing LiDAR sets are often captured at street-level or
at building-level. We aim to understand the impact of sur-
face energy optimization on more complex structures. We
therefore consider Tanks-and-Temples dataset [28], whose
training scenes are captured with outside-looking-in trajec-
tories and span sculptures, large vehicles and house-scale
buildings, and whose per-scene evaluation thresholds run
from 3 mm to 25 mm, a range. This set contains a survey-
grade laser scan per scene together with an evaluation vol-
ume and an alignment matrix. We use five of its scenes:
Ignatius, Truck, Caterpillar, Barn and Courthouse, repre-
sentng the natural progression from object level to scene
level captures. We additionally consider the indoor Red-
wood scene [11].
Constructing a synthetic LiDAR set from Tanks-and-
Temples
For each scene, Tanks-and-Temples provides an
image set, a camera trajectory, and a laser scan with an eval-
uation volume. The image set is the input a method is ex-
pected to reconstruct from, and the laser scan is held out
as the evaluation reference. To construct a synthetic Li-
DAR set, we convert the image set into a per-frame point
cloud. We use COLMAP [18] to reconstruct each scene
from the published camera poses, running feature extrac-
tion, sequential matching and point triangulation, followed
by dense multi-view stereo. This produces a depth map
per image, which we back-project into a per-frame point
cloud paired with that frame’s camera centre. Each frame is
cropped to the evaluation volume published with the scene.
The transform published with each scene includes a scale
factor, which we apply to the points, so that distances in
the converted scene are in metres. Camera intrinsics are es-
timated per scene: we project the ground-truth points into

Feature Encoding and Sampling:
Across these datasets,
we adopt the hierarchical parameterization strategy estab-
lished by recent offline implicit models [50, 67]. We param-
eterize the SDF using a differentiable hierarchical octree-
based feature grid. Multi-resolution features are stored in
hash tables indexed by Morton codes, enabling O(1) query
access without dense memory allocation. For a given 3D
point xi, the octree features are passed into a shared 2-
layer MLP with smooth activations in between the layers.
For ground-truth supervision, we compute the ray-aligned
signed distance di from the sensor origin to the measured
LiDAR point cloud. Following the sampling strategy es-
tablished in SHINE [67], we recognize that precise SDF
values far from the surface have very little impact on the
final geometry, whereas values close to zero define the sur-
faces and should exert a higher impact. Thus, the distance
is mapped to an occupancy probability li ∈[0, 1] using a
sigmoid function with a truncation hyperparameter σ, al-
lowing us to densely sample training points within a ±3σ
4


band around the surface. For the octree and the architecture
parameterizations we use the hyperparameters and training
setups specified in SHINE [67], which we provide in Ap-
pendix section C.
Our framework is designed for unbounded, incremental
large-scale mapping. Consequently, the computational do-
main Ωdynamically expands as the sensor traverses the en-
vironment. Because the map is unbounded, we do not im-
pose classical Dirichlet or Neumann constraints on a global
boundary ∂Ω. Instead, the energy prior is evaluated point-
wise over the aggregated coordinate pool (managed via our
sliding-window sampler). This ensures the smoothness con-
straint is enforced locally.

distance values, (oi = σ(Gθ(xi))). This formulation re-
flects the observation that surface reconstruction is gov-

Evaluation protocol:
After training, the mesh is ex-
tracted by querying the field on a regular grid spanning
the occupied octree nodes and running marching cubes, so
the vertices span points beyond the training set. Accuracy
is measured from the mesh vertices to the laser scan, and
completeness from the scan to the mesh surface using exact
point-to-triangle distance, both restricted to the published
evaluation volume.
Evaluation Metrics:
To quantitatively assess the fidelity
of our neural implicit reconstructions, we leverage the stan-
dard reconstruction metrics from the prior works [67]. Ac-
curacy (Acc.) measures the median distance from the re-
constructed surface to the ground-truth point cloud, whereas
Completeness (Comp.) quantifies the median distance from
the ground-truth points to the reconstructed surface, both
evaluated against the surface itself using a bounding vol-
ume hierarchy. The Chamfer-L1 (CD-L1) distance provides
a comprehensive bidirectional nearest-neighbor error. We
compute Precision (Acc. Ratio) and Recall (Comp. Ratio)
at a per-scene threshold τ to derive the overall F1 score. Fol-
lowing standard evaluation protocols, we employ an error
threshold of 10 cm for the MaiCity and KITTI datasets, and
20 cm for the NewerCollege dataset. For KITTI, we utilize
an aggregated projection of the point-cloud frames as the
ground truth, whereas NewerCollege and MaiCity provide
precomputed ground-truth point clouds.

of sparse point cloud mapping by identifying the geometri-
cally smoothest and most structurally stable manifold from
the family of valid distance fields satisfying the Eikonal
constraint. Lastly, simpler first-order or Laplacian-based
regularizers provide weaker geometric constraints, which is
consistent with prior works [47, 48].

variations in the normal field, which mathematically cor-

high-curvature topologies.

we observe that this shift toward structurally constrained,
noise-reduced mapping directly translates to improved per-
formance and stability in downstream tasks.
5.2. Cost-Performance Tradeoff
Embedding higher-order geometric priors inevitably in-
creases the cost of training, since the curvature energy re-
quires evaluating second-order spatial derivatives through
automatic differentiation. Compared to first-order objec-
tives such as the Eikonal loss, this incurs additional mem-
ory consumption and computation during optimization. We
investigate this overhead in Fig. 5, which computes the
wall-clock time by simulating autograd operations over
40K steps. The second-order formulation requires approx-
imately 45 minutes of training time compared to 25 min-
utes for a first-order baseline, reflecting the cost of enforc-
ing second-order geometric regularity.
Importantly, this overhead is incurred only during train-
ing.
The Hessian energy shapes the parameters of the
neural implicit representation, embedding the smoothness
prior directly into the learned field. Once optimization is
complete, inference consists only of evaluating the neural
implicit function itself, requiring no Hessian evaluations,
higher-order derivatives, or auxiliary optimization proce-
dures. Consequently, mesh extraction and downstream de-
ployment incur no additional computational overhead be-
yond that of a conventional neural implicit representation.
As an academic point of comparison, one may also con-
sider optimizing the fourth-order biharmonic residual as-
sociated with the Euler–Lagrange equation of the surface
Hessian energy (also shown in Fig. 5). Such a formula-
tion requires nested higher-order automatic differentiation
and rapidly becomes computationally prohibitive. Our ex-
periments indicate that a direct fourth-order implementation
requires nearly 300 minutes of training, yielding approxi-
mately a 6.6× increase in runtime relative to our Hessian
energy formulation.
What that additional training time buys is shown in the
middle panel of Fig. 5, which evaluates accuracy at interme-
diate checkpoints, not only at convergence. The two meth-
ods separate within the first few thousand iterations: the en-
ergy leads by 0.85 points at 15k, 0.9 at 25k, and 1.5 at 40k.
The unregularized baseline peaks near 25k and then drifts
back, while the regularized field continues to improve. The
prior therefore shapes the optimization trajectory from the
outset and does not act as a late correction to an already-
converged solution.

is the sampler batch size, fixed by the optimizer, so it is in-
dependent of scene extent and of the number of captured
points. Figure 5 (right) compares the peak GPU memory
with the number of input points. The optimized SHINE
framework does not incur significant GPU overheads for the

5.3. Downstream Tasks
To investigate whether the geometric regularity induced by
our energy formulation extends beyond surface reconstruc-
tion, we evaluate its impact on Simultaneous Localization
and Mapping (SLAM). In neural implicit SLAM systems,
pose estimation is performed by directly registering incom-
ing scans against the learned signed distance field (SDF),
with the optimization relying on both the predicted distance
values and their spatial gradients. Consequently, the quality
of the implicit representation can directly influence down-
stream localization performance.
While the Eikonal ob-
jective constrains the magnitude of the SDF gradient, our
Hessian energy additionally regularizes its spatial variation,
promoting a smoother and more coherent implicit geometry
throughout the mapped environment.
To quantitatively evaluate this effect, we measure the
root-mean-square error (RMSE) of the absolute trajec-
tory error (ATE) using Umeyama alignment on the Newer
College benchmark.
As shown in Tab. 4, incorporating
our energy-optimized implicit representation into the PIN-
SLAM mapping backend yields consistent improvements in
localization accuracy without modifying the odometry or
optimization pipeline.
Averaged across all evaluated se-
quences, our method achieves an ATE RMSE of 0.15 m
compared to 0.17 m for the original PIN-SLAM formula-
tion, corresponding to an 11.8% reduction in average trajec-
tory error. Improvements are also observed on several indi-
vidual sequences, including sequence 01 (0.43 m →0.39 m,
9.3%), quad e (0.09 m →0.08 m, 11.1%), and stairs (0.06 m
→0.04 m, 33.3%). Although the proposed energy formula-
tion is designed as a reconstruction prior and not a localiza-
tion objective, these results indicate that improving the reg-
ularity of the learned implicit field can positively influence
downstream registration accuracy, highlighting the utility of
energy-optimized neural representations for robotics appli-
cations, although a detailed analysis of this interaction re-
mains an interesting direction for future work.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we introduced an energy-based framework
for neural implicit surface mapping that embeds classical
smoothness priors directly into continuous signed distance
fields. By treating the neural implicit representation as a
parameterization of the minimum-energy configuration of a
continuous energy functional, our formulation unifies vari-
ational geometry processing with modern neural field rep-
resentations while remaining computationally practical for
large-scale LiDAR mapping. Experiments demonstrate that
this energy-driven perspective consistently improves recon-
struction fidelity and geometric regularity while generaliz-
ing naturally to downstream robotics tasks such as localiza-
tion and mapping. Broadly, we believe this work establishes
energy optimization as a principled foundation for integrat-
ing classical geometric priors into neural implicit represen-
tations, opening new directions for physically grounded 3D
scene understanding.

B.1. Smoothing Behaviour of the Energy
The smoothing behaviour induced by the continuous for-
mulation is illustrated in Fig. 6. For a signed distance field
corrupted by additive Gaussian perturbations, direct opti-
mization of the energy removes high-frequency oscillations
from the volumetric field, producing smooth zero-level sets
and well-conditioned iso-contours.

Connection to Classical Mesh Smoothing
The proposed formulation is a continuous, mesh-
free counterpart to geometric smoothing in classical
mesh processing. Penalizing curvature on a discrete
mesh requires finite-difference operators, lumped
mass matrices and conic relaxations [41]. Impos-
ing the prior directly on the continuous signed dis-
tance field evaluates the same quantity through auto-
matic differentiation, so the smoothing emerges dur-
ing standard neural optimization without auxiliary
variables or specialized solvers (Fig. 6).

E. Implementation and Experimental Details
To manage memory effectively and ensure scalable opti-
mization, we employ a sliding-window data sampler and
a multi-resolution feature octree. The structural parameters
of the decoder remain constant across all scenes: a 2-layer
MLP with a hidden dimension of 32, querying a feature oc-
tree with an 8-dimensional feature vector per node.
E.1. Dataset-Specific Hyperparameters
To accommodate the varying spatial scale and sensor den-
sities of different environments, specific hyperparameters
were tuned per dataset (summarized in Table 8).
• KITTI (Sequence 00): Due to the massive scale of the
outdoor trajectory, we utilized a slightly larger octree leaf
voxel size of 0.3 m.
The network was optimized for
40, 000 iterations with an initial learning rate of 0.05.
