H-2DGS: Height-Field Gaussian Splatting for Sharp and Deformable
Neurosurgical Surface Reconstruction
Zhang Zichuan1, Bart Brouwers2, Rembrandt Bakker1,2, Maud Landers2, Geert-Jan Rutten1,2, and Maxime Chamberland1
1Department of Computer Science & Mathematics, Eindhoven University of Technology, Eindhoven, The Netherlands
2Department of Neurosurgery, Elisabeth-Tweesteden Hospital Tilburg, The Netherlands
Abstract
Neurosurgical navigation commonly presents magnetic res-
onance imaging (MRI) as 2D slices, making it difficult to
relate the anatomy in those slices to the cortical surface
observed through the surgical microscope. We seek a com-
mon 3D visualization that combines a photorealistic recon-
struction of the exposed cortex during tumor surgery with
whole-brain MRI context and diffusion tractography path-
ways. This requires the reconstructed cortex to form a clear
boundary around the brain anatomy rather than a diffuse
volume that obscures internal structures. Conventional 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) can reproduce observed views
but, under limited surgical view coverage, may distribute
opacity through a thick and geometrically ambiguous band.
We introduce height-field 2D Gaussian Splatting (H-2DGS),
a three-stage pipeline that concentrates a robust 3DGS re-
construction, fuses its depth into a shared truncated signed
distance field (TSDF) surface, and represents that surface
with curved, surface-oriented Gaussian primitives. Sepa-
rate height, color, and opacity bases provide local geometry
and appearance, while a neighbor-transition objective co-
ordinates overlapping patches. The explicit surface further
supports postoperative alignment: MRI-derived depth de-
forms the reconstruction, and tracked-color supervision with
alternating optimization preserves its appearance. Across
five real neurosurgical cases, H-2DGS gives low multi-view
depth inconsistency, a compact 7.97 MB representation, and
interactive rendering at 29–42 ms per frame. The resulting
sharp, deformable surface integrates clearly with tractogra-
phy and MRI anatomy, including a postoperative resection
cavity.

Introduction
Medical visualization plays a central role in the interpreta-
tion, communication and education of neuroanatomy and
tractography. Interactive visualization is important in, e.g.,
neurosurgical planning, as it enables users to explore anatom-
ical structures and fiber pathways. Modern neurosurgery
uses dedicated navigation systems for surgical planning,
intraoperative localization, and postoperative assessment.
These systems commonly present registered magnetic reso-
nance imaging (MRI) as orthogonal 2D slices [6, 13]. The
slices contain rich tissue information, but they do not resem-
ble the exposed cortical surface seen through the surgical
microscope. Consequently, understanding where a visible
vessel, sulcus, or resection boundary lies within the MRI
volume requires a mental correspondence between two very
different views. This separation increases cognitive load,
and the missing 3D spatial context makes the relationship
less intuitive [3].
We instead aim to place the complementary information
in one 3D scene. Microscope video supplies the current
color and fine surface landmarks of the exposed cortex. MRI
supplies the surrounding whole-brain tissue context, while
diffusion MRI tractography exposes subcortical pathways
that are not visible at the surface. A photorealistic 3D recon-
struction can connect what the surgeon sees to these internal
structures under continuous viewpoint changes: the cortex
provides the visible outer layer, the MRI-derived model lo-
cates that layer within the brain, and tractography shows the
structures beneath it. The fused views in Fig. 1 illustrate this
intended relationship.
Creating this visualization is not only a registration prob-
lem; it first requires an appropriate representation of the visi-
ble layer. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [11] is attractive
because it reconstructs realistic appearance, renders rapidly,
and exposes primitives that can be manipulated. It has con-
sequently been adopted for static and deforming surgical
scenes [7, 9, 10, 16, 17, 22]. However, each conventional
3D Gaussian distributes opacity through a volume. With
the narrow camera trajectories typical of microscope video,
Gaussians at several depths can explain the same training pix-
els. The model may therefore look realistic from observed
views while forming a thick, blurry, or fragmented density
band elsewhere. When tractography is rendered beneath this
band, fibers can be obscured by or visually embedded in
the reconstructed cortex, so the boundary between visible
surface and subcortical anatomy becomes ambiguous.

We address this representation gap with H-2DGS, which
progressively converts a robust volumetric reconstruction
into a geometrically sharp yet photorealistic surface. Stage I
first uses conventional 3DGS to establish appearance with-
out prematurely imposing a fragile surface. Stage II then
measures the spread of density along camera rays and con-
tracts it toward the visible boundary. Multi-view depth from
this concentrated model is fused into a truncated signed dis-
tance field (TSDF), providing a shared surface rather than
unrelated per-view estimates. Stage III discards the volu-
metric Gaussians and places curved height-field primitives
on the TSDF zero level set. The height field lets each patch
follow local cortical curvature, separate color and opacity
bases retain spatially varying texture, and neighbor-transition
optimization coordinates overlapping patches.
The common 3D view must also remain meaningful when
anatomy changes. Brain motion and tissue removal alter the
relationship between the reconstructed surface and MRI [6];
after resection, the cavity can also invalidate vessel-based
correspondence. For the postoperative case, we therefore
render depth from the MRI-derived mesh and deform the
explicit height-field surface toward that geometry. Depth
determines the target shape, while tracked color supervision,
overlap regularization, and alternating geometry–appearance
updates prevent the alignment from erasing the photoreal-
istic cortical texture. Preoperative vessel-guided alignment
is retained only as a downstream use of the reconstructed
surface.
Our contributions are1:
• a three-stage conversion from volumetric 3DGS to a thin
TSDF-initialized cortical surface, including a differen-
tiable ray-wise thickness objective;
• a curved height-field Gaussian primitive with spatially
varying geometry, color, and opacity, together with overlap
constraints for locally continuous geometry;
• a depth-supervised deformation procedure that aligns the
photorealistic surface to postoperative MRI geometry

alization with whole-brain anatomy and tractography.

Related Work
Gaussian surface representations.
Standard 3DGS rep-
resents a scene with anisotropic volumetric Gaussians opti-
mized through differentiable alpha compositing [11]. Sev-
eral alternatives strengthen its geometric prior. 2D Gaussian
Splatting (2DGS) replaces ellipsoids by oriented disks and
uses ray–splat intersection to obtain a more surface-like rep-
resentation [8]. 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting (3D-HGS) splits
primitives with learned planes to sharpen discontinuities [14],
while Quadratic Gaussian Splatting (QGS) models local ge-
ometry with second-order surfaces [15]. These approaches
initialize their designed primitives directly from an input
point cloud. Under limited view coverage, the source geom-
etry can itself be weakly constrained. H-2DGS instead first
concentrates a robust volumetric reconstruction, fuses its
multi-view depth into a shared TSDF, and only then initial-
izes curved surface charts. Per-primitive appearance bases
are related to textured Gaussian representations [19–21, 23],
but our height basis additionally makes each primitive a
deformable local surface.
Surgical reconstruction and anatomical fusion.
Neural
and Gaussian representations have been applied to static
and deforming surgical scenes [17, 18, 22]. Most of these
methods prioritize view synthesis or temporal tissue motion.
Cross-modal methods connect intraoperative imagery to pre-
operative anatomy through inverse rendering or coupled
meshes [4, 5]. Our focus is complementary: we construct a
sharp, compact cortical boundary from limited microscope
views, use it as a substrate for tractography, and directly de-
form this renderable surface toward postoperative geometry.

3.1
Problem formulation and design rationale
The input is a set of calibrated microscope images and cam-
era projections

Our output is a renderable set of surface patches that must
satisfy three requirements. First, it should reproduce cortical
color and vessel landmarks from the input views. Second,
opacity should be confined to a well-localized surface so
that MRI anatomy and tractography remain visible on the
correct side of the cortical boundary. Third, the representa-
tion should expose depth and local geometric parameters for
subsequent alignment.
Directly fitting surface-oriented splats from the sparse in-
put point cloud is brittle in this setting: the same limited
camera coverage that creates 3DGS thickness also leaves the
initial geometry weakly constrained. We therefore separate
appearance acquisition from surface construction. Stage I
obtains a robust photometric reconstruction with flexible
volumetric Gaussians. Stage II makes this reconstruction
a reliable geometric proxy by contracting its density along
observed rays without changing the primitive type. Stage III
fuses the resulting multi-view depths into one surface and re-
places the volumetric model with curved height-field patches.
Thus each stage has a distinct purpose: bootstrap appearance,
localize geometry, and encode an explicit surface.

3.5
Initialization, continuity, and refinement
Surface positions alone are not enough to initialize the richer
primitive. For each patch, nearby TSDF zero-level samples
are expressed in its tangent frame, and the height coefficients
are fitted to their signed offsets from the tangent plane. This

converts the locally planar initialization into a curved chart
before image refinement. Nearby Stage II Gaussians transfer
color and opacity to the new patch; the volumetric Gaussians
are then discarded completely.
Because each chart is fitted locally, two individually accu-
rate patches can still disagree where they overlap, producing
cracks or abrupt normal changes. We therefore sample cor-
responding locations in neighboring charts and coordinate
them with
Ltrans = λcontLcont + λpresLpres + λcurvLcurv.
(11)
where λcont = 0.5, λpres = 2.0 and λcurv = 0.02. Lcont pe-
nalizes world-space position and tangent mismatch at paired
overlap samples; Lpres retains the TSDF-fitted heights and
gradients; and Lcurv suppresses oscillation. The three terms
have complementary roles: continuity closes patch inter-
faces, preservation prevents this pairwise adjustment from
drifting away from the multi-view TSDF, and curvature reg-
ularization avoids fitting the correspondences with ripples.
After this geometric transition, final photometric refinement
uses Eq. (2) to recover cortical appearance on the already
localized surface. Shared basis layouts, precomputed in-
verse squared bandwidths, and CUDA-side indexing reduce
rasterization overhead.

Postoperative Surface Alignment
4.1
Problem setup and optimization strategy
We use postoperative alignment as a representative scenario
to test the geometric and rendering flexibilty of the proposed
H-2DGS. In the postoperative scenario, the microscope re-
construction contains the post-resection photorealistic cor-
tex, and the postoperative MRI supply information after
the surgery. These two data modalities typically disagree
geometrically, due to the resection cavity that significantly
changes the surface morphology. We use the depth map
as the proxy of the surface geometry to supervise the de-
formation. The inputs are an initial height-field model G0
reconstructed from microscope video and a depth dataset

rendered from the postoperative MRI-derived mesh. For
the same cameras, the current height-field model produces
differentiable depths ˆDv. The desired output is a deformed
version of G0 whose surface follows the MRI depth while
retaining the initial cortical texture. This gives a direct image-
space comparison without establishing sparse cross-modal
point correspondences.
Naive depth fitting, however, admits undesirable solutions:
neighboring patches can move independently and tear, opac-
ity can change which surface is observed, and texture can
smear while geometry moves. Our optimization therefore
combines three mechanisms with distinct roles. Depth loss
supplies the target shape, the transition objective couples
adjacent charts, and tracked self-supervision transports the
original appearance. Alternating geometry and appearance
windows keep the latter two goals from competing through
the same updates.

5
Experiments
5.1
Setup and metrics
We evaluate five MRI cases using real neurosurgical videos:
three preoperative cases and two postoperative cases. For
each case, we select the continuous segment of the surgical
video with the most comprehensive viewpoint coverage and
uniformly sample frames from the original segment, result-
ing in a continuous sequence of 60–80 images for training.
The data were shared under the approved data-sharing agree-
ment between the participating institutions. All patients
provided written informed consent for the use of their data
for research purposes. All experiments run on an NVIDIA
RTX 3050 GPU with 4 GB memory, 32 GB system memory,
and an Intel i7-12700H CPU. H-2DGS uses 2,400 primitives
per case, with 50 color and 20 opacity bases per primitive.
We compare against 2DGS [8], 3D-HGS [14], and QGS [15],
trained with their released default settings.
PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS evaluate appearance. PSNR and
SSIM use the intersection of valid rendered pixels across
methods. Model size and per-frame time measure efficiency.
For geometry, multi-view depth consistency (MVDC) back-
projects valid depth from one evaluation view, reprojects it
into a neighboring view, and reports the median absolute
depth discrepancy normalized by scene extent.

5.2
Sharpness, consistency, and fusion
Table 1 exposes the intended trade-off. H-2DGS has lower
training-view PSNR and SSIM than the baselines, mainly
losing high-frequency highlights. Its geometric behavior
is substantially more stable: MVDC lies between 1×10−5
and 4×10−5. The representation is consistently 8 MB and
renders in 30–42 ms. QGS attains the strongest photometric
scores but uses 79–142 MB and 120–188 ms.
Differences that are muted in covered views become pro-
nounced away from the training trajectory. Figure 6 shows
that volumetric or weakly constrained representations can
extend into streaks and fragments. H-2DGS remains a rec-
ognizable cortical layer. This matters for anatomical fusion:
in Fig. 5, fibers appear attached to a defined surface instead
of intersecting a diffuse cloud. The tractography therefore
serves both as a target application and as a visual stress test
of geometric localization [1].
5.3
Postoperative deformation
On the resection case, depth fitting moves the post-resection
reconstruction toward postoperative MRI-rendered depth.
The optimized map follows the target, while the surface
stretches laterally and the cavity becomes shallower without
losing major tissue or vessel patterns (Figs. 7 and 8).
Depth supervision alone destroys high-frequency appear-
ance. Appearance-window updates restore cortical texture
with geometry frozen, while stronger transition regulariza-
tion improves local geometric and texture coherence (Fig. 9).

Discussion and Conclusion
Together with the proposed three-stage pipeline, H-2DGS
produces a photorealistic reconstruction of the cortical sur-
face with a sharp geometry and well-defined boundary. This
explicit surface enables direct integration with subcortical
tractography and whole-brain MRI context without the inter-
nal anatomy being obscured by diffuse volumetric opacity.
Compared with the baseline approaches, H-2DGS achieves
substantially better geometric generalization across view-
points: it consistently maintains a localized cortical surface
in uncovered views and obtains lower multi-view depth in-
consistency across the five cases (Tab. 1 and Fig. 6).
The clear geometry of H-2DGS also provides a suitable
foundation for deformation. Its surface primitives retain
sufficient geometric flexibility to represent substantial mor-
phological changes, allowing deformation objectives to be
defined directly on the primitive parameters and fitted to
a target geometry. The geometric and rendering flexibility
of H-2DGS is verified in the postoperative case, where the
fitting process is separated into depth-fitting and appearance-
preservation stages. The postoperative experiment demon-
strates that this strategy can accommodate a pronounced
geometric discrepancy while preserving the photorealistic
appearance of the original reconstruction.
The compact size of H-2DGS follows from its fixed 2,400-
primitive budget with per-primitive texture on an explicit
surface. We do not match primitive counts to the baselines,
since volumetric primitives lacking local texture generally
need more of them to fit the same images. Overall, the
smaller footprint reflects an advantage of the height-field
primitive, especially given its stronger geometric localiza-
tion.
A remaining limitation is rendering quality. As indicated
by PSNR and SSIM, H-2DGS does not reproduce the ob-
served images as accurately as the baseline methods. Re-
placing volumetric support with a thin explicit surface re-
moves geometric ambiguity, but also reduces the rendering
expressiveness available for reproducing high-frequency ap-
pearance variations. We introduce spatially varying texture
through the color bases to compensate for this loss, but a
clear photometric gap remains. Future work should therefore
investigate richer geometry-preserving appearance models
that improve rendering fidelity without reintroducing diffuse
volumetric opacity.
The current pipeline also depends on camera poses recov-
ered by structure from motion (SfM), whose static-scene
assumption can be violated in surgical videos. One addi-
tional sequence failed because physiological cortical pulsa-
tion introduced non-rigid inter-frame motion, causing SfM
to confound tissue motion with camera motion and produce
inaccurate poses. This error subsequently degraded all recon-
struction stages. Potential solutions include obtaining cali-
brated or externally tracked camera poses from specialized
surgical equipment, representing each primitive’s geometric
attributes as continuous functions of time, and estimating
the dominant pulsation frequency for phase-aware frame se-
lection or motion correction. Addressing both appearance
expressiveness and dynamic tissue motion will be important
8


for extending H-2DGS to more diverse surgical videos. In
the future, combining this approach with cinematic render-
ing [2, 12] should offer a unique opportunity to improve
surgical navigation.
