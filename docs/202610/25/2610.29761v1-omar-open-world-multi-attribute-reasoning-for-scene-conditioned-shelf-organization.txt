OMAR: Open-World Multi-Attribute Reasoning
for Scene-Conditioned Shelf Organization
Benedikt Kreis*,1,2,3 Rohit Menon*,1,2,3 Benno Wingender1,2,3 M. E. Ullah1 Nils Dengler4 Maren Bennewitz1,2,3
Abstract— With the advent of foundation models, robots are
increasingly capable of understanding and executing explicitly
specified human tasks. Extending these capabilities to open-
ended tasks, however, requires robots to infer appropriate
actions from the observed scene. Household organization exem-
plifies this challenge, as robots must often act without detailed
instructions, predefined object vocabularies, or known object-
receptacle associations. While robots may follow instructions or
learn organization preferences from demonstrations, inferring
the latent organization of an occasionally changing scene and
placing a novel object consistently with it remains challenging.
We propose an open-world multi-attribute reasoning pipeline
that grounds unknown objects across views and constructs
an instance-level 3D scene graph for placement reasoning.
First, a vision-language model proposes labels together with
attributes such as content and packaging type, which are
grounded through segmentation, depth, and multi-view associ-
ation. Then, an LLM reasons over this representation to infer
the latent shelf organization and rank scene-consistent object
placements without explicitly user-provided preferences. The
resulting placement rankings guide subsequent geometric and
kinematic feasibility checks for execution. Our experimental
evaluation shows that our approach leads to improved instance
mapping and placement coherence over single-view language-
grounding and geometry-only baselines, enabling autonomous
placement of previously unseen objects without predefined
object-receptacle mappings or class-specific retraining.
I. INTRODUCTION
Households are constantly changing environments, and
their organization reflects implicit and evolving human con-
ventions. To effectively organize objects in such environ-
ments, service robots must act beyond explicitly specified
tasks. When storing groceries, a robot must determine where
a previously unseen object belongs, even without detailed
instructions or user-provided preferences. As object locations
change, predefined object vocabularies and object-receptacle
mappings cannot fully specify appropriate placements.
We study scene-conditioned shelf organization, in which
the robot must infer the latent organization from the observed
arrangement and place a novel object consistently into it.
This task requires reasoning over multiple object attributes,
including content, packaging type, edibility, and orientation.

II. RELATED WORK
Picking and placing (P&P) previously unseen household
objects is a challenge at the intersection of perception and
manipulation. Recent advances in transformer-based founda-
tion models [7] enable broader generalization to unseen tasks
and objects compared to classical approaches.
A. Classical and Learning-based P&P Approaches
Pick2Place [4] uses object-centric perspective affordances
to produce task-aware 6-DoF grasps that align grasp selection
with downstream placements. Alternatively, cost-based joint
pick-and-place formulations produce place-aware grasps, as
demonstrated by Shanthi and Hermans [5] and, more re-
cently, UniP [6], which introduced a generalized placeability
metric that scores object configurations based on geomet-
ric and physical stability criteria. With a similar emphasis
on physical stability, Paxton et al. [8] learn stable object
placements. Moving from individual placements to multi-
step rearrangement, REAP [9] uses a heuristic learned from
motion-planner feedback, reducing motion-planning queries
while guiding search toward geometrically feasible actions.
Beyond physical feasibility, semantic placement planning
is considered by Wingender et al. [10], although the approach
uses predefined semantic labels. Similarly, Adeleye et al. [11]
combine object recognition with semantic reasoning to select
grocery storage locations in shelf environments, but operate
on a closed object set without checking geometric feasibility.
These approaches thus either omit placement semantics [4]–
[6], [8] or rely on predefined object sets without con-
sidering multiple object attributes [10], [11]. Furthermore,
some approaches [5], [8] do not address placements into
geometrically challenging confined spaces.
B. Foundation Model-based P&P Approaches
The generalization promise and semantic reasoning ca-
pabilities drives the increased use of foundation models
in robotic manipulation. For example, TidyBot [12] com-
bines open-vocabulary detection using ViLD [13] with
object-centric manipulation, using sparse user preferences

ConceptGraphs [16] and BBQ [17] use 3D scene graphs

language-model reasoning in observed objects and their
spatial context through scene-graph representations. Rather
than grounding a user-specified object query, we reason over
scene-graph attributes to infer shelf organization and rank
placements of unseen objects.
Foundation models have also been applied specifically
to task-oriented grasping. GraspGPT [18] and Foundation-
Grasp [19] leverage semantic knowledge to bias grasp
selection towards task-relevant affordances, while Grasp-
Molmo [20] combines a multimodal foundation model with
large-scale synthetic data to improve generalization. These
methods primarily reason about how an object should be
grasped, rather than how it should be placed.
Conversely, several recent approaches focus on semantic
placement. AnyPlace [21] learns generalizable object-to-
receptacle placements from multimodal supervision, Pla-
ceIt3D [2] reasons about language-guided placements in real
3D scenes but without robotic embodiment, and Set It Up [3]
targets functional object arrangements. These methods pro-
duce semantically meaningful placements but only partially
address geometric and kinematic constraints.
Integrated foundation-model-based systems that focus on
confined spaces are LLM-Pack [22] which uses VLMs to
identify grocery items and an LLM to generate human-like
packing sequences. EMPOWER [1] employs LLM agents
for perception, planning, and execution, enabling multi-step
object rearrangements through a set of predefined manipu-
lation skills. However, these systems do not explicitly cou-
ple semantic placement reasoning with the grasp-dependent
geometric feasibility of executing individual placements in
space-limited environments.
Our approach addresses the existing gaps by cou-
pling open-world, foundation-model-based semantic reason-
ing with grasp-placement compatibility. A persistent multi-
view instance map provides a holistic and instance-consistent
representation of complex scenes across viewpoints and
enriches objects with detailed semantic attributes beyond cat-
egory labels, enabling more informed placement decisions.
This allows to jointly consider semantic and geometric fit,
reachability, and grasp feasibility across multiple shelf boards
with varying clearance.

containing an open-set of packaged grocery and household
objects on multiple shelf boards, a robotic arm must pick
an unknown target object from a table and place it at the
semantically most coherent location on the shelving unit

IV. OUR APPROACH
We propose an open-world pipeline for placing a previ-
ously unseen object in shelf environments leveraging multi-
view semantic instance mapping. Converting the map into a
3D semantic scene graph representation allows LLM-based
multi-attribute reasoning of placements, which we combine
with target-oriented antipodal grasp generation to select
grasp-placement pairs to maintain the latent semantic shelf
organization. Fig. 2 provides an overview of the pipeline,
whose main components are detailed below.
A. Autonomous Open-World Scene Perception
To reason about placements without a predefined object
vocabulary, the robot must obtain more than category labels:
it requires attributes that both distinguish instances and
expose the organization of the observed shelf. We therefore
acquire RGB-D observations of the unknown target object
and the shelving unit and query a VLM at each of the n
viewpoints using our structured object descriptor.
For each object, the VLM predicts a textual label and
multiple attributes such as content (e.g., crackers, mustard),
packaging type (e.g., box, bottle), edibility (food or drink
versus non-food items), and orientation (e.g., upright, lying).
Applying the same schema to the target and shelf objects
creates a shared semantic representation for cross-view as-
sociation and subsequent placement prediction. Unlike label-
only perception [1], [10], [11], this attribute-rich, open-world
description provides complementary semantic and physical
cues without class-specific retraining or predefined object-
receptacle mappings.
We use the predicted labels as prompts for the SAM 3 seg-
mentation model [23] and thereby ground each description in
an image mask and its corresponding depth measurements.
The resulting RGB-D observations, masks, and semantic pre-
dictions form an attribute-annotated view set that is updated
as the robot moves across viewpoints.
In parallel, we generate a 3D mesh of the target object
from a single RGB-D observation using the generative flow-
matching model SAM 3D [24]. This mesh is used later for
grasp generation and geometric placement validation. The
view set, however, remains an aggregation of partial and
potentially inconsistent observations. To obtain the persistent
object-level context required for placement reasoning, we
next consolidate these observations across views into one
unified 3D representation.
B. Multi-View 3D Scene Representation
Integrating multiple viewpoints leverages close-up views
to reveal finer object details, enabling, e.g., more reliable
inference of object content. Furthermore, occlusions make
individual views incomplete, and viewpoint-dependent VLM
descriptions may assign different labels or attributes to the
same object. We therefore retain labels proposed for the same
object across views as alternative hypotheses, accumulating
evidence before selecting a canonical label. Naively fusing
these observations can duplicate instances, merge distinct
objects, or attach inconsistent semantics, distorting the shelf
organization presented to the placement predictor.
Our scene representation address these ambiguities in
two stages. First, a multi-view semantic instance mapper
combines geometric association with VLM-derived extent
cues and accumulates alternative label hypotheses instead
of committing to a single per-view prediction. Second, the
resulting persistent instances are converted into an instance-
level 3D semantic scene graph that jointly encodes object
attributes, geometry, and spatial relations. Unlike individual
images, the scene graph consolidates partial observations
into a view-independent representation with explicit object
identities and spatial relations, avoiding the need for repeated
inference of occluded, viewpoint-dependent observations.
The two-stage association and its conversion into a multi-
attribute scene graph provides the structured, scene-wide
context used to infer the latent shelf organization.
Multi-View Semantic Instance Mapping: We construct
a 3D semantic instance map Minst in which each object
instance i has a dedicated fine-grained TSDF volume, seman-


tic attributes, and a set of label hypotheses Hi. Maintaining
these hypotheses across views separates object identity from
viewpoint-dependent descriptions, allowing instance labels to
be revised without fragmenting the instance map.
For each detection d, candidate instances are filtered by
bounding-box center distance and 3D overlap. We measure
3D overlap by dividing the intersection volume of the
detection and instance bounding boxes by the volume of
the smaller box. Thus, a partial detection fully contained
within the accumulated instance box receives an overlap
score of 1, even when the boxes differ greatly in size. We
also compare the labels and attributes of the detection and
candidate instance, but a semantic disagreement rules out the
match only when both semantic estimates are supported by
consistent observations and sufficiently confident. When se-
mantic evidence is uncertain, we retain candidates that satisfy
the geometric matching criteria for further association.
Let cd and ci denote the bounding-box centers of detection
d and candidate instance i, respectively. We define the
association cost as C(d, i) = ∥cd −ci∥2 + Cext(d, i), where
the extent penalty Cext(d, i) measures disagreement between
observed dimensions and the VLM-derived typical extent
associated with the candidate instance. This penalty provides
a physical-size cue for distinguishing candidate instances
whose centers lie at similar distances from the detection.
Greedy one-to-one assignment repeatedly selects the lowest-
cost pair among unassigned detections and instances.

3D Semantic Scene Graph Generation: We construct a
3D semantic scene graph from the multi-view semantic in-
stance map, as exemplified in Fig. 3. Each object node stores
semantic attributes, including its label, content, packaging
type, edibility, and orientation, together with geometric at-
tributes, including its bounding-box dimensions and centroid
estimated from the instance TSDF volume.
Directed edges encode the shelf hierarchy: each shelf
board belongs to the shelving unit, and each object is linked
to its assigned board by an on top of edge, restricting
comparisons to a common board. We assume known shelf-
board heights and assign each object to the board whose
height is closest to its centroid height. Also, each neighboring
object pair on the same board is connected via directed edges.
Ordering centroids along the lateral axis of the board’s coor-
dinate frame determines left of/right of relations, while or-
dering by depth measured from the board’s front determines
in front of/behind of relations. Each relation is represented
together with its inverse directed edge. For example, a left
of edge from a bottle to a box is paired with a right of edge
from the box to the bottle. Centroid ordering provides a shelf-
referenced spatial description without relying on surface-
contact estimates from partially reconstructed geometry.
Coarse Mapping: In addition, we fuse all RGB-D obser-
vations into a coarse TSDF map Mcoarse [25] for collision-
aware planning.
C. Model-Aware Grasp Prediction
We sample antipodal grasp candidates on the target object
mesh using grids over its bounding-box faces, up to six
approach directions, and rotations about each approach axis.


The grid sampling provides systematic geometric coverage,
while perturbations of up to ±3 mm along the gripper
approach, closing, and binormal axes add grasp candidates
between grid samples.
To evaluate these candidates in the scene, Foundation-
Pose [26] estimates the object’s 6-DoF pose to transform
candidates into the world frame, where collision and inverse-
kinematics checks reject obstructed or unreachable grasps.
Then we rank the remaining candidates using a geomet-
ric quality score Gqual to favor antipodal finger-to-object
contact-normal alignment, finger-pad centering, and an ob-
ject enclosing aperture. A vertical approach preference biases
selection towards tabletop grasps.
D. Multi-Attribute-Aware Placement Prediction

observed scene across multiple attributes, we couple LLM-
based reasoning with mesh-based placement sampling. As
illustrated in Fig. 3, the LLM receives a prediction query,
the 3D semantic scene graph with observed object attributes,
such as content, packaging type, edibility, and orientation,
and the corresponding target-object attributes (see Sec. IV-
A). The query instructs the LLM to infer the shelf scene’s
organization by jointly considering these attributes and to
rank placement candidates anchored to existing shelf objects.

relevance without relying on a single attribute. For the top-

V. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
To demonstrate the performance of our approach in in-
stance mapping accuracy and semantic placement coherence
over single-view language grounding and geometry-only
planning, we conducted experiments with a diverse set of
packaged grocery and household objects in simulation and
on a real robot. We compare against a single-view LLM-
based baseline [1] using the same segmenter, assessing label
accuracy, geometric accuracy, and instance coverage. We
evaluate placement coherence through semantic fit against
both a LLM baseline and a geometry-only pick-and-place
baseline [6], measuring execution success to assess whether
greater placement coherence with the observed shelf organi-
zation is achieved while maintaining manipulation feasibility.
Across three scene sets, our approach improves all mapping
metrics over the single-view baseline and achieves higher
semantic fit than both baselines, with success rates com-
parable to geometry-only planning. An additional attribute
evaluation of the placement predictor highlights the benefits
of LLM-based prediction over score-based prediction for
finding semantically coherent placements.
A. Baselines
We use two different baseline approaches and one modi-
fied version of our approach for comparison. The first base-
line (BL1) predicts placements based on EMPOWER [1].
For a fair comparison, we replace its original segmentation
component with the same SAM 3 [23] object segmenter used
in our approach. In contrast to our method, BL1 operates on a
single RGB-D observation of the shelf, as EMPOWER does
not maintain a multi-view scene representation. Moreover,
EMPOWER derives object labels using part-of-speech (POS)
tagging, extracting nouns while discarding descriptive adjec-
tives. This can remove semantically relevant object informa-
tion required for informed placement decisions.
The second baseline (BL2), is a general pick-and-place
pipeline based on UniP [6], excluding its stability predic-
tion component, as we manipulate packaged grocery and
household items limiting reorientation to objects with planar
side faces ensuring a stable support (see Sec. III). BL2 uses
the coarse map to sample geometrically feasible placement
candidates but does not incorporate semantic information
into the placement decision.
We further evaluate a variant of our approach (Basic),
using an object descriptor that predicts labels from a basic
prompt without detailed attributes as the full approach.

B. Experimental Setup
For all experiments, we use the 6-DoF robot arm UR5e
equipped with the Robotiq 2F-85 two-finger gripper as end-
effector, which we both control with the Robot Operating
System (ROS 2) [28]. To capture RGB-D observations, we
use the Orbbec Gemini 336 camera (the Realsense L515
camera in simulation) mounted on the robot’s wrist. Fur-
thermore, we rely on LangChain as LLM/VLM API with
Pydantic for validation, NetworkX [29] to model the scene
graph, and Isaac Sim [30] for simulation experiments provid-
ing photo-realistic rendering. As LLM/VLM models for our
approach, we used OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol model and as LLM
experts we used Mistral’s Medium 3.5 model and Deepseek’s
V4.1-Flash model. We executed all experiments on a desktop
computer equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 7900X3D CPU,
128 GB RAM, and an Nvidia RTX 4080 GPU.
Scene Dataset: For the simulation experiments, we cre-
ated a diverse dataset containing 60 packaged grocery and
household objects divided into eight non-exclusive categories
(baking and pantry, canned goods, condiments, cleaning,
dairy, dry goods, personal care, and shelf stable drinks) and
five different packaging types (box, bottle, can, carton, and
tube) containing edible and non-edible products.
We procedurally generate three scene sets of 100 scenes
each, referred to as single-row, multi-row, and varying-
clearance scenes (see Fig. 4). In single-row scenes object
are standing next to each other with a minimal random
yaw perturbation of 10◦, representing scenes comparable to
other recent semantic placement works [1], [15]. In multi-
row scenes, we sample objects over the entire depth of
each shelf board, resulting in objects standing behind each
other, which leads to challenging object occlusions. In the
varying-clearance scenes, we also sample objects in multiple
rows, but additionally vary the shelf board heights, limiting
the available clearance. We randomly draw objects from
the complete dataset with sampling biased toward local
groupings of objects from the same category to obtain
realistic scenes. Candidate poses are accepted only if they
satisfy shelf bounds, height constraints, and collision-free
placement. In addition, we reserve free space for the target
object to ensure its placability at multiple shelf locations.
C. Metrics

using the following metrics measuring execution success,
semantic placement quality, and instance mapping accuracy.
For all metrics, we compared the paired per-run values using
the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test [31] to check for
statistical significance between our and all other approaches.

cessful runs. A run is successful if all components (see
Fig. 2) can be executed and the robot places the object within
6 cm of the planned placement pose.

Quantitative Placement Results: Tab. II shows that OURS
achieves higher success, SF-EQ, and SF-LLM than BL1
across all scenes. For multi-row scenes, SR increases from
20 % to 95 %, SF-EQ from 34 % to 63 %, and SF-LLM from
29 % to 58 %. This agrees with the instance-mapping results,
where the lower scene coverage of BL1 yields fewer object
anchors and less context to infer the shelf organization, which
limits the available placement relations.
Compared with the geometric-only baseline BL2, OURS
achieves similar success but consistently higher SF-EQ and
SF-LLM. For multi-row scenes, both methods obtain an SR
of 95 %, while SF-EQ increases from 53 % to 63 % and
SF-LLM from 46 % to 58 %. BL2 samples geometrically
feasible placements without semantic information, whereas
OURS additionally reasons about the latent observed shelf
organization. The results therefore show that semantic multi-
attribute reasoning improves placement consistency without
reducing execution success. The decrease in SR for both
methods in varying-clearance scenes is consistent with the
increased spatial limitations and object occlusions in the
scenes, while OURS maintains similar SF-EQ and SF-LLM
across all three scene sets.
Tab. III compares our LLM-based placement predictor
with an equally weighted score-based predictor (Score)
across scene sets with different dominant attributes. Score
ranks sampled geometric placement poses using Eq. (4)
with equal attribute weights, whereas OURS uses an LLM-
based predictor. This design provides a reference for scene-
dependent attribute relevance while separating weight deriva-
tion on four sets of ten scenes from evaluation on 20 unseen
sets. OURS obtains mean SF-LLM scores of 72 %, 83 %,
70 %, and 51 % for content, packaging, edibility, and orienta-
tion, respectively, exceeding Score by 4, 5, 4, and 10 percent-
age points. Equal weighting can favor agreement on several
incidental attributes over the attribute that characterizes the
shelf organization. The observed gains are consistent with
our predictor accounting for this scene-dependent priority
without manual weight tuning.

VI. CONCLUSION
We presented an open-world multi-attribute reasoning
pipeline for placing previously unseen objects consistently
with an existing shelf organization, without user-provided
preferences or predefined object-receptacle mappings. Our
pipeline grounds object labels and attributes across views
in a 3D semantic scene graph, which enables LLM-based
semantic placement reasoning followed by geometric and
kinematic feasibility checks. Quantitative results show im-
proved instance mapping and higher semantic fit over single-
view language grounding and geometry-only planning.
