In-Context Learning World Models for Robot Dual-Arm
Manipulation of Soft Garment with Partial Observations
Hengyi Sim, Quentin Rouxel, Dennis Hong, Member, IEEE,
Darwin Caldwell, Fellow, IEEE and Fei Chen, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Robust manipulation of soft objects, such as gar-
ments, is a critical skill for service and household robots to
adopt, but remains challenging due to its high-dimensional state
space, and material-dependent deformations. Humans address
this challenge by using mental world models that ”imagine” the
shape and material properties from partial observations, which
enables them to predict how actions affect future states. As
such, embedding such world models in robots allows for better
generalization in manipulating a wide variety of garments in
the real world. However, learned world models typically require
large amounts of real world data that is strenuous to collect, and
a mechanism to infer latent properties that govern dynamics.
We present a Soft-Object World Model that performs generative
full-state estimation and action-conditioned dynamics prediction
using a transformer backbone trained on large-scale simulated
soft object interactions, with measures to mitigate the sim-to-real
gap. By learning to infer latent shape and material cues through
in-context learning over a variable-length partial point-cloud
history, the model generalizes across robot embodiments, ma-
terial parameters, garment types and shapes, and manipulation
coordination modes without weight updates. The world model
is then integrated with a Goal-Conditioned action planner for
motion synthesis. We validate the approach in simulation and real
world for bimanual garment folding, reporting improvements in
reconstruction fidelity, prediction accuracy, and task success.

expensive, labor-intensive, and difficult to scale, particularly

The first major challenge in building soft object world
models is the high DOFs of soft object state space, where
there exists many valid state configurations that occupy a
complex manifold. In this space, small changes in contact
point, geometry, or material parameters can cause different
valid states with similar partial observations, further compli-
cating state estimation. Partial observation from self-occlusion,
folds, and camera poses routinely occludes task-critical regions
of the soft object, forcing the world model to infer plausible
unseen geometry. Selecting an effective state representation is
therefore essential, as it must retain sufficient geometric detail
for accurate prediction while remaining robust to sim-to-real
mismatch. High-dimensional point-cloud representations are
attractive because they preserve fine-grained geometry, map
naturally to depth sensors, and empirically transfer well from
physics simulators to real sensors when paired with appropri-
ate domain randomization and generative conditioning [12].
However, the data requirements for learning such expressive
models are large and collecting extensive real-world demon-
strations is expensive, thus motivating large-scale synthetic
corpus generation in simulators like Isaac Lab [13].
The second major challenge is dynamics prediction with
rapid adaptation to material properties during inference. Pre-
dicting the dynamics of soft objects is challenging because
deformations are strongly coupled to unknown material prop-
erties, grasp points, and the current unseen shape, where
the same action can produce different outcomes [14]. In-
context learning (ICL) provides a mechanism to reduce this
ambiguity at test time. By conditioning the model on a short
set of unrelated prior demonstrations, ICL infers material and
shape priors without gradient-based fine-tuning [15]. These
demonstrations can also include diverse exaggerated dynam-
ics, thus improving the model’s ability to estimate dynamic
properties, enabling rapid adaptation and more reliable forward
rollouts. Accurate, context-conditioned world models can then
be plugged directly into planning and control loops, serving
as forward models, as action proposal evaluators or model-
predictive controllers, therefore enabling robots to reason
about long-horizon outcomes and select manipulation strate-
gies that are robust across diverse garments.
In this study, we aim to introduce and experimentally
evaluate a Transformer-based soft object world model with
test-time in-context adaptation for robotic manipulation. The
framework reconstructs full object states from partial observa-
tions and predicts future deformations conditioned on planned
actions and past estimated demonstration states. An in-context
conditioning mechanism uses short sequences of estimated
states to infer object material properties at inference without
fine-tuning. To address data limitations and improve sim-to-
real transfer, we train on a large-scale and diverse corpus of
point-cloud-based soft object interactions, generated with Isaac
Lab with randomized material parameters. We evaluate full-
state reconstruction, multi-step action-conditioned dynamics
prediction, and adaptation to variations in object geometry
and material properties in simulation and real-world bimanual
garment-manipulation experiments. Finally, we demonstrate
sim-to-real transfer across multiple garment categories and
material variants without test-time parameter updates.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Soft Object Manipulation
Manipulating soft objects such as garments remains a long-
standing challenge in robotics, due to their high-dimensional
state space and complex, nonlinear dynamics. Some works
reduce the control problem by manually planning the ma-
nipulation trajectory and restricting learning to grasp-point
selection [16], an approach exemplified by methods that also
predict manipulation primitive types alongside grasp selection
[17], [18]. Unfortunately, these methods and some others that
rely on visuomotor servoing [19], [20], are constrained to
the pre-defined skill trajectory. Data-driven learning methods
offer an alternative by enabling end-to-end mapping from
unstructured perceptual inputs to control actions.
Such methods like model-free reinforcement learning (RL)
[21], [22] and imitation learning (IL) [23]–[28], has enabled
robots to learn action policies directly from high-dimensional
perceptual observations, such as visual inputs, without re-
quiring an explicit analytical dynamics model. This prop-
erty is particularly advantageous for robotic tasks involving
unstructured environments and complex sensory inputs. Al-
though model-free RL has been applied successfully to soft-
object manipulation tasks, including cloth folding [21], purely
model-free approaches do not explicitly represent soft-object
dynamics or account for latent factors such as material prop-
erties and object geometry. This limitation can hinder long-
horizon prediction and precise control of object deformation.
Conversely, model-based methods can enable shape control
during manipulation through explicit dynamics models, but
they require accurate state estimation. Such a requirement is
difficult to satisfy under partial observability, and learning such
models demands extensive data to cover the large state and
action spaces characteristic of soft object manipulation [29].
B. Soft Object World Models
Modeling soft objects such as garments are especially
challenging due to their high-dimensional state space and
complex, nonlinear and material-driven dynamics. Recent and
prominent research proposes to address the challenges posed
by material-driven dynamics by learning soft-object dynamics
models directly from empirical data [30]–[32]. The empirical
data are often sourced from simulators [13], as collecting
and curating real world data tends to be labour intensive.
However, certain works have instead opted for differentiable
simulators, where a small amount of real world data can be
used to train for simulating realistic soft object dynamics
via gradient optimization [33]. Such simulators can then be
used to generate an even larger corpus of simulated data that
more closely match real world dynamics. Another approach
is Graph-based neural networks (GNNs), which provide a
powerful alternative for modeling the dynamics, demonstrating
strong empirical performance across a variety of soft objects
such as plasticine [34], dough [35], cloth [36], [37], and fluids

[30], [38]. While these approaches demonstrate impressive

In robotics and vision, world models and predictive state
estimators have become central for planning and closed-loop
control, where compact latent representations enable state
estimation from partial observations and allow model-based
rollouts for trajectory scoring or MPC [11]. However, learning-
based dynamics models are required to predict state tran-
sitions from interaction data, whereby the choice of state
representation is crucial to capture the transitions. Point clouds
are selected as the state representation because it balances
fine-grained geometric detail and point-wise temporal corre-
spondence with robust sim-to-real transfer from simulators to
RGB-D sensors. However, point clouds are inherently high-
dimensional, hence they demand a backbone that scales well
while preserving fine-grained spatial and temporal consis-
tency, in which Transformers are well suited. Even so, partial
observations remain ambiguous as self-occlusion, folds, and
viewpoint changes can yield multiple plausible full states, mo-
tivating a generative state estimator. Diffusion models [39], a
class of generative models with expressive ability of capturing
complex, high-dimensional data distributions, have emerged
as a powerful paradigm and has been applied across diverse
domains, including generation of images [40], [41], videos
[42], [43], 3D shapes [44], as well as robot policy learning
[23], [25] and finally world modeling [45]–[47]. Motivated
by these developments, we investigate the effectiveness of
Transformer and generative-based models for predicting soft-
object dynamics and supporting manipulation planning.
C. In-Context Learning
In-context learning (ICL), along with few-shot test-time
conditioning, has recently emerged as a practical mechanism
for adapting models to new instances without weight updates
by ingesting short histories or exemplar interactions as context.
First introduced in natural language processing with models
like GPT-3 [6], ICL has since seen uses in robotics to enable
rapid task adaptation with foundation models [48], identify
invariant regions of state space for robust control [49], discover
consistent object alignments from few examples [50], and
train policies that generalize across tasks or embodiments
[51], [52]. Additionally, ICL have recently been used to per-
form real-time implicit identification for real world dynamics,
building upon its potential for dynamics identification [53].
Building on these ideas, ICL is extended to the soft-object
domain by conditioning a transformer dynamics model on
a variable-length of unrelated estimated states from partial
point-cloud demonstration. This test-time conditioning lets
the world model infer latent material and shape priors from
a handful of contextual interactions, and produce materially
aware, temporally coherent rollouts that can be used directly
for planning and control in soft object manipulation.
III. PROBLEM FORMULATION
We consider a discrete-time robotic manipulation problem
in which a bimanual manipulator interacts with a soft ob-
ject over time steps t = {0, 1, ..., T}; the model receives
camera depth partial observations history point clouds ot−H:t
of length H and plans manipulation actions at:t+T at time
t, that manipulates the soft object for time length T. The
learning objective is to train a predictive world model that (i)
infers a dense 3-D soft object state st ∈RN×3 from partial
observations and (ii) generates multi-step, action and context
conditional predictions bst+1:t+T +1 suitable for planning. To
enable the world model to infer the shape and material
properties for accurate soft object dynamics prediction, context
demonstration oc of length L, will be provided during test time
to help the model infer manipulation-relevant properties.

IV. METHODOLOGY
The world model as shown in Fig. 2, has state estimation
and dynamics prediction components. Given the camera data
of observations history ot−H:t and a context sequence oc, the
state estimator infers the full state in the form of a point cloud,
producing bst−H:t and bsc for history and context respectively.
Conditioned on these estimates and a candidate action plan
at:t+T , the dynamics model predicts the ensuing future states
bst+1:t+T +1. Thus, the world model will need to be trained and
conditioned with a large corpus of synthetic demonstrations,
and would require a model architecture that can handle high
dimensional representations like point clouds. Transformer
based models have shown success in predicting full soft object
configurations from limited observations. As such, this work
will leverage a Transformer for its ability to accept varying-
length conditioning and more expressive outputs.

be applied on fully observable states which should first be
estimated from partial observation. The state estimation model
is first formulated as a conditional flow matching (CFM)

point clouds constitute a mid-level visual representation that
preserves geometric invariances across domains. Within the
CFM framework, for an arbitrary latent sample z, the predic-
tion task is cast as an ordinary differential equation (ODE) of

The model architecture backbone used is a vanilla Vision
Transformer (ViT) architecture [54] as shown in Fig. 3 (i),
which demonstrated high scalability in high dimensional state
spaces such as image and video generation. For state estima-
tion specifically, the model takes the noisy gaussian source that
is transported through the velocity vector field vθ1 conditioned
on the partial observable point cloud. Farthest point sampling
(FPS) is then used to sample a fixed number of points, denoted
by Pv, as patch centers C ∈RPv×3. To patchify the point
cloud, the patch centers C is used to construct a Voronoi
diagram in the cartesian space, thus dividing the point cloud
into Pv separate regions. Each Voronoi cell is treated as a
distinct patch, which will then go through a PointNet [55] layer
for feature extraction and generating the tokens. Following the
tokenization process, the input token is directly subjected to a
sequence of transformer blocks for processing. For condition-
ing the point cloud embedding, an adaptive layer normalization
(AdaLN) [56] is added to incorporate conditional information,
and a cross-attention layer positioned after the multi-head
self-attention (MHSA) to incorporate conditional information.
In this cross-attention operation, the hidden states serve as
the query vector, while the conditional partial point cloud
information acts as both the key and value vectors. Lastly,

B. Dynamics Prediction with In-Context Learning
The goal of dynamics prediction is to predict the future
states of the soft object given robot actions. The state estima-
tion architecture is extended to model dynamics by modifying
the conditioning input to incorporate robot actions, as shown
in Fig. 3 iii). Additional temporal attention layers were added
to enhance the temporal modeling [12]. To learn the condi-
tional posterior distribution p(bst+1:t+T +1|at:t+T , bst−H:t, bsc),
the transformer model was trained via supervised learning.
Here, at:t+T represents the robot actions at time t and pre-
diction horizon of length T, bsc denotes the estimated context
of length L, bst−H:t denotes the estimated state history, and
bst+1:t+T +1 is the future soft object states to be predicted at
timestep t, where H, L and T can be set at any arbitrary
length. The model constructs the future full states by predict-
ing per step deformation field to be added to a last history
step, thereby strongly adhereing to the markovian process.
A Fourier feature-based embedding following NeRF [57] to
represent continuous spatial information is used to effectively
encode the action space of at:t+T ∈RT ×6.
However, the dynamics of soft objects such as garments are
highly dependent on their geometry, configuration, material
properties, and interaction history, all of which can affect how
the object deforms under robot actions. In-context learning
(ICL) is therefore leveraged to provide test-time interaction
data that informs the model of the object and task-specific
dynamics required for future-state prediction. The key to
implementing ICL is to train the world model on a large
corpus of simulated synthetic demonstrations, whereby each
demonstration follows a consistent interaction pattern. In this
case, the pattern consists of a pick-and-place task for the soft
object, where a random pair of pick and place points are
sampled from the object and grasped and placed, respectively.
Since the model is trained on synthetic data where material
parameters are randomized while the interaction pattern is
fixed, it learns to associate material-dependent deformations
with latent material properties. At inference, the transformer
attends to this context and modulates the dynamics rollout
without weight updates, enabling effective adaptation to dif-
ferent materials, while supported by improved prediction when
the context material matches and degraded performance under
mismatched contexts. The world model is trained with a
variable length of test time interactions states to emulate the
variability in test-time context length. Masking is first used
to randomly dropout different lengths of the partial point-
cloud conditioning, thereby simulating contexts with varying
amounts of available history. The action and partial point-cloud
observations are then projected into their respective latent
embedding space, and provided to a multi-head self-attention
(MHSA) module via cross-attention, along with the Adaptive
LayerNorm (AdaLN) layers. This fused representation is then
attended by the policy query to produce context-aware predic-
tions of the future soft-object states.
C. Goal-Conditioned Flow-Matching Action Generation
At inference time, the partial observations ot−H:t are
mapped to an action trajectory that drives the garment toward
the target configuration sg. However, these partial observation
point clouds do not contain sufficient information for the
policy to infer an optimal trajectory. Instead, they only support
the prediction of a statistically meaningful pattern of one,
which must be further evaluated by a model to yield accu-
rate action trajectories. Moreover, world models that rely on
optimizations that randomly samples actions are inefficient for
garment manipulation, as the high-dimensional state space and
sensitive deformation dynamics cause small action variations
to produce markedly different configurations. Consequently,
many feasible candidates are poorly aligned with the current
state and goal, making their world-model evaluation costly
and uninformative. A goal-conditioned generative policy is
therefore used and trained on the same demonstrations, to
generate a diverse set of smooth, geometrically meaningful,
and task-relevant trajectories. The world model then evaluates
these informed proposals and selects the trajectory with the
most desirable predicted outcome.

B. State Reconstruction
Training the state estimation model required approximately
eight hours per epoch on an GTX 3090 GPU. Ground-truth
and partial point clouds were sampled randomly from the
generated dataset. Fig. 4 presents state estimation results,
including the RGB input, segmented partial point clouds, and
predictions from the ViT-based state estimation model. The
baselines considered in the comparative study include a Point
Completion Network (PCN) [58], and Transformer models
trained via UniCloth’s Diffusion [12], Flow Matching (FM)
and MSE loss. Qualitatively, the estimated shapes from partial
observations closely resemble the corresponding real-world
soft objects, suggesting that the transformer can recover global
object geometry from occluded visual observations.
Comparison studies are conducted in simulation and re-
ported in Table I, with each method evaluated over 100
trials. PCN achieve the lowest CD and EMD loss across the
evaluated settings. However, PCN produces much higher mean
squared error (MSE), where its MSE for the shirt category is
approximately 20 times greater than that of the FM model.
MSE is particularly important for the subsequent dynamics
prediction stage because point-wise temporal correspondence
must be preserved. Therefore, FM provides the best over-
all performance, achieving the lowest MSE while remaining
highly competitive with PCN in terms of CD and EMD.

Training the dynamics prediction model required approxi-
mately one day per epoch. The dataset was identical to that
used for state estimation, except that each batch contained
temporally consecutive point clouds sampled from the same
demonstration sequence. Additionally, the material parameters
of every demonstration are recorded during data generation. At
each iteration, a target demonstration is sampled together with

the underlying dynamics than generative objectives as the
simulated dynamics are deterministic, fully observable, and
Markovian, and therefore do not exhibit multimodality.

models degrades as the prediction horizon increases, with
the GNN showing the fastest degradation. On average, the
MSE trained Transformer and GNN model achieved stronger
performance, likely due to the deterministic nature of their
supervised learning formulations. This is consistent with dy-
namics prediction, in which a fixed initial condition and fully
observable state should yield a single unique future trajectory.

To ensure the method’s ability to perform long-horizon

VI. DISCUSSION
The results show that proposed method reconstructs partially
observed soft object point clouds, predicts action-conditioned
deformations, and enables real-world folding across diverse
garments. The MSE-Trained Transformer achieved the lowest
MSE and slower autoregressive error growth than the gen-
erative and GNN baselines. This suggests that deterministic
supervised prediction is well suited to modeling deterministic,
fully observable, and Markovian dynamics, where a fixed es-
timated state, observation context, and action sequence gener-
ally correspond to a single unique future prediction. Generative
models may instead be more suitable for multimodal tasks,
including state estimation under occlusion and action proposal
generation. Although PCN achieves favorable CD and EMD,
flow matching attains lower MSE, which is important for

VII. CONCLUSION
This paper presents an In-Context Learning Soft Object
World Model framework for bimanual garment manipulation
under partial observations. This work combines generative
point-cloud state reconstruction, in-context action-conditioned
dynamics prediction, and goal-conditioned motion proposal
generation. Together, these components estimate garment ge-
ometry, model the dynamics of soft objects, to evaluate and se-
lect among candidate action proposals for real world bimanual
garment folding. Experiments in simulation showed that the
supervised Transformer provides accurate and stable dynam-
ics predictions while accurately inferring material behaviour
from context conditioning, and that the reconstruction mod-
ule produces correspondence-preserving full-state estimates
suitable for downstream prediction and planning. Real-world
experiments across cloths, shirts, trousers, material variants,
and two robot embodiments demonstrated successful sim-
to-real transfer and effective generalization, while achieving
high task success without additional real-world training. These
results suggest that In Context Learning World Models are
a practical approach for generalizable garment manipulation.
The current study focuses primarily on quasi-static folding,
where material-dependent effects may be less pronounced
than geometric and configuration-dependent cues. Future work
will investigate more dynamic garment interactions, evaluate
the role of material-history correspondence under stronger
dynamic excitation, and examine deterministic and generative
dynamics models under increasingly ambiguous future states.
Incorporating closed-loop state estimation and replanning will
also be important for reducing accumulated errors during
longer-horizon manipulation tasks.
