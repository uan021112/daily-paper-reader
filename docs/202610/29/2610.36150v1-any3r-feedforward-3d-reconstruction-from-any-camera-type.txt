Abstract
Geometry foundation models recover camera poses and
dense geometry from a set of images with a feed-forward
network, but they expect pinhole images. That assumption
is inside the backbone too: the positional encoding and the
training loss both bind an image position to a direction in
space, and both depend on the lens. We condition both on
the dense ray field, the unit direction through every pixel,
which describes any central camera without a lens type,
distortion coefficients or a bound on field of view. Angu-
lar coordinates give the rotary encoding the measured ray
angle of each patch, evaluated per view, and solid-angle
weighting scales the point loss by the area each pixel cov-
ers on the sphere, so neither the encoding nor the supervi-
sion depends on the lens. Any3R is therefore a feed-forward
model that takes any central camera, and is robust to mixing
many camera types per scene, with no retraining per lens.
On a benchmark of thirteen central cameras across three
datasets, where every method reconstructs with its own pre-
dicted camera poses, it is the most consistently accurate
method. Any3R enables scalable 3D reconstruction from
heterogeneous crowdsourced and archival imagery collec-
tions sourced across diverse camera types. Project page:
https://jingong-chen.github.io/Any3R/
1. Introduction
Recovering the 3D structure of a scene from a set of images
is a fundamental problem in computer vision. Geometry
foundation models such as VGGT [57], π3 [61], MapAny-
thing [25], VGGT-Ω[58] and Depth Anything 3 [34] now
solve it with a feed-forward network [4, 13, 29, 56, 59, 60,
65, 74]. A regular camera sees only a narrow part of a scene
in each view, so covering a whole room or street takes many
views captured densely and carefully.
The direct way to cover more of a scene per view is a
wider lens, and wide-angle and panoramic cameras are al-
ready common in autonomous driving [33, 72] and indoor
scanning [1, 5]. Feed-forward models cannot use such im-
ages as they are, because they read every frame as a pinhole
image. Recent work adapts them to particular lenses, with
a discrete token for the camera model, an adapter around a
frozen backbone, a pairwise alignment stage, a conversion
of the input to a cubemap, or retraining on equirectangular
images [3, 12, 18, 19, 22, 31], but each supports only a fixed
list of camera models, so an unseen camera needs retrain-
ing or a new conversion step. Mixing cameras of different
types in one reconstruction, such as a pinhole view, a fisheye
frame and a panorama of one scene as in Fig. 1, is harder
still. Wid3R [22] and Fisheye3R [12] mix only the camera

Both limits trace back to the backbone’s positional en-
coding, which none of these methods changes: it locates
each patch by its pixel index [49]. On a pinhole camera,
the index tells the backbone roughly where the patch looks;
on a fisheye, each pixel covers a wider angle, so the same
index points somewhere else (Fig. 2(ii)). The training loss
has a similar problem: it weights every pixel equally, but
a fisheye pixel near the edge sees a different amount of the
scene than one at the center (Fig. 2(i)).
We present Any3R, which removes the pinhole assump-
tion by giving the ray direction of every pixel to both the
encoding and the loss (Fig. 2(iii)). In pinhole, fisheye and
panoramic cameras, all rays pass through one point. Such
central cameras are fully described by the ray direction of
each pixel, whatever the lens. Angular coordinates posi-
tion a patch by the angle of its ray instead of its index, so
one unit of position means the same angle on every cam-
era. Solid-angle weighting scales the loss by how much of
the scene each pixel sees, so every part of the scene counts
equally whatever the lens. Because each view carries its
own ray field, Any3R reconstructs from any central cam-
era without rectification, and views from different cameras
can be processed together, in any number. On three pub-
lic datasets imaged through thirteen camera models, where
every method reconstructs with its own predicted camera
poses, Any3R gives the most accurate reconstruction and
camera poses on wide-angle and panoramic lenses and is
on par with the strongest pinhole baseline at 90◦. It also
outperforms the methods built for wide-angle or panoramic
lenses on every lens they take, and its pose estimates on
real pinhole photographs are competitive with the strongest
pinhole baselines. Its margin over the pinhole models is
smallest at 90◦and much larger on the wider lenses.
Our contributions are threefold:
• A feed-forward model that reconstructs from views of any
mix of central cameras, with no calibration search, no
undistortion and no retraining per lens.
• Angular coordinates and solid-angle weighting, two mod-
ules that condition the positional encoding and the train-
ing loss on the dense ray field, so that one set of weights
serves every central camera.
• State-of-the-art reconstruction and camera pose on three
public datasets imaged through thirteen central camera
models, where every method reconstructs with its own
predicted camera poses, with the largest margins on wide-
angle and panoramic lenses.
2. Related Work
2.1. 3D Geometry Foundation Models
Classical structure-from-motion recovers geometry by op-
timising correspondences and poses jointly [43], and per-
scene neural representations [26, 37] keep that optimisa-
tion while changing what is optimised. DUSt3R [60] in-
stead trained one network to regress a pointmap in a com-
mon frame, removing the optimisation loop and the need
for known intrinsics, and MASt3R [29] added a matching
head to the same backbone [11, 38, 62, 63].
Extending two views to many has taken several forms: a
global stage on top of the pairwise backbone [4, 13], a la-
tent state that lets views be streamed [56, 59], and set-level

2.2. 3D Reconstruction for Different Camera Types
A central camera is described by its ray field, parame-
terised compactly for rectilinear, fisheye and catadioptric
optics [16, 23, 36, 42, 48, 54, 78]. Learning pipelines in-
stead rectify the image to a rectilinear grid, which is lossy
at wide field of view and impossible past 180◦, so work
on panoramic and fisheye input changes the operator in-
stead: spherical convolutions [9, 10, 15, 50, 52], resampled
charts [8, 14], monocular panoramic depth [21, 32, 41, 44,
51, 55, 73, 80] and multi-view fisheye depth [27, 28, 33,
64, 72]. Each fixes one projection, so a change of lens is a
change of model.
Recent feed-forward methods attach the camera at the
backbone’s periphery, by retraining on one projection [18,
31], a token [22], an adapter [12], pairwise alignment [19]
or a cubemap [3], or train on renderings through many
lenses [30], but keep the pinhole meaning of a rotary co-
ordinate [49] inside the attention, which is what a change of
lens alters. RayTun3R [45] reaches the same diagnosis but
adapts per sequence at test time. G-ray [77] parameterises
the rotary phase by camera-local ray angles and evaluates
it on pinhole and fisheye crops, while we mix eight central
camera models from 90◦to 360◦. OmniPoint [69] encodes
the ray field of the same three projection families from a
single image, without relative pose or cross-view attention.
Ray and Pl¨ucker coordinates have also served as geometric
position [46, 75]. Reusing a pretrained rotary encoding adds
a requirement that a freshly trained one lacks: wherever the
pretrained coordinate already agrees with the ray angle, it
must be reproduced exactly, or a valid prior is lost.

Any3R is trained to read the ray field of each view

4. Experiments
4.1. Setup
We train Any3R for 40 epochs on 32 GH200 GPUs over
about 27 hours, on Matterport3D [5], Stanford2D3DS [1],
Structured3D [79], PanoCity and DL3DV [35] videos,
holding out all test scenes.
We evaluate on two indoor
datasets, Matterport3D and Stanford2D3DS, and one out-
door dataset, PanoCity, with 24 held-out scenes per dataset
and 3 views per scene. Every scene is rendered through 13
central camera models, the eight used in training and five
held out, from the same panoramas and view directions, so
two results differ by the lens alone, and Tabs. 1 and 3 report
the eight.
Every method fuses its views into one point cloud with
its own predicted camera poses, and we measure both the
cloud and the poses. Accuracy (Acc) is the mean distance
from a reconstructed point to its nearest ground-truth point
after the similarity alignment and ICP refinement of the
PanoVGGT evaluation [18], the point-cloud accuracy used
across feed-forward reconstruction [59, 61]. Pose accuracy
is the area under the curve (AUC) of the pairwise pose error,
the larger of the relative rotation error and the translation-
direction error, up to 15◦and 30◦, as in [57, 61]. Any3R
receives the ray map, MapAnything a pinhole intrinsic ma-
trix on the pinhole cameras, Wid3R the true ray field and
the other methods images alone, and MapA-ray is MapA-
nything given the true ray field on every camera.

4.3. Zero-Shot Real Lenses
The cameras above are rendered from panoramas, so Tab. 2
also tests real lenses on two datasets that are not in our train-
ing data, under the same protocol. ScanNet++ [70] records
each scene with a DSLR fisheye of about 146◦, and we take
one view triplet from each scene of its validation split, with
ground truth from the laser-scanned mesh.
FIORD [17]
records with a 200◦fisheye whose image reaches beyond
90◦from its axis, and we take 24 view triplets from two of
its scenes, with ground truth from a terrestrial laser scan.
Any3R, MapA-ray and Wid3R receive the ray field of the
calibrated lens. On FIORD, Any3R is the most accurate
and the best posed of the methods compared, with an AUC
at 30◦about half again that of the next one, and Wid3R
cannot represent the lens. On ScanNet++, Any3R is second
only to Wid3R and ahead of every other method in all three
measures. Without its ray map, Any3R∗falls far behind on
both datasets, so on real lenses as well the gain comes from
the ray map. In Fig. 5, the other zero-shot methods bend the
FIORD rooms into curved shells, while Any3R stays close
to the ground truth.

is their joint ablation, and Tab. 4 isolates the two remaining
choices on full panoramic sequences under the PanoVGGT
protocol. Per-view evaluation concerns what the angular
coordinates read in a sample whose views come from dif-
ferent cameras. With it, the coordinates of each view are
computed from that view’s own ray field, and without it,
from the ray field of the first view, which all views of the
sample then share. Every test view of Tab. 4 is a panorama,
so the two inputs give identical coordinates there, and the
difference between the rows was learned in training, where
the shared field assigns every view but the first the angles
of a lens it was not taken with and the network learns to
relate views through wrong angles. Reading each view’s
own field gives the largest pose gain on the indoor datasets,
and on PanoCity it lowers the median rotation error but also
the AUC. On its own, solid-angle weighting gives a smaller
gain by counting each direction of the scene once instead
of each pixel. The pose gains cost some depth accuracy,
most of it with per-view evaluation, which raises the mean
log10 depth error on Matterport3D from 0.033 to 0.045. We
accept this trade for the larger gain in pose.
5. Conclusion
Geometry foundation models typically assume pinhole
cameras, an assumption embedded in their positional en-
coding and training objectives.
Any3R conditions on
dense ray fields through angular coordinates and solid-angle
weighting, adapting each camera to the backbone to enable
a unified model across central camera types.
This feed-
forward approach reconstructs scenes from arbitrary combi-
nations of central cameras without calibration search, image
undistortion, or lens-specific retraining. Evaluated on thir-
teen central camera models across three datasets, Any3R
achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction accuracy using its
own predicted camera poses. Any3R enables scalable 3D
reconstruction from heterogeneous image collections, in-
cluding crowdsourced and archival imagery captured by
smartphones, fisheye surveillance cameras, and panoramic
imaging systems.

Any3R: Feedforward 3D Reconstruction from Any Camera Type
Supplementary Material
A. Evaluation Details
Each of the 24 scenes of a dataset is a set of three views
drawn without replacement, whose view directions are fixed
by a pre-registered rule on the 90◦pinhole and reused for
every camera. The thirteen camera models are the eight
used in training and five held out from it, FOV/ATAN at
150◦, stereographic at 180◦, equisolid and EUCM at 200◦
and cylindrical at 360◦. Structured3D is evaluated in the
same way on all 16 of its test scenes and reported only in
this supplement, since the views of a scene share no content
and relative pose cannot be recovered there.
Each method’s per-view points are placed with its own
predicted poses and fused, and 40,000 pixels are sampled
per scene. The evaluation function of PanoVGGT then fits
one similarity transform on the pixel correspondences be-
tween the predicted and the ground-truth points, refines it
by point-to-point ICP with a maximum correspondence dis-
tance of 0.1 m indoors and 1.0 m on PanoCity, and reports
Acc, the mean distance from a reconstructed point to its
nearest ground-truth point, and a cell’s value is the mean
over its scenes. The AUC follows the authors’ pose evalu-
ation, taking for every pair of views the larger of the rela-
tive rotation error and the translation-direction error, in 1◦
bins, and dropping pairs whose ground-truth translation is
below 0.01. Baselines with two input options, π3, VGGT
and VGGT-Ωat their native or their official resolution and
MapAnything with or without the pinhole intrinsics, are
shown with the option of lower median Acc in each cell,
and Tab. 17 lists both. MapAnything accepts a dense ray
map, but its released preprocessing fits a pinhole intrinsic
matrix to any ray map before cropping, so MapA-ray takes
the images through that preprocessing and then receives, for
every processed pixel, the true ray of the camera, which en-
ters its ray encoder unchanged.
The zero-shot samples of Tab. 2 use the calibrated
Kannala–Brandt model of each lens, inverted per pixel, with
the images resized to the size of the rendered cameras of
similar field of view.
On ScanNet++ the views are the
DSLR images of every scene of the validation split, the
full set with ground truth since the test splits publish no
laser scan, posed by its COLMAP reconstruction, which
lies in the frame of the laser scan, and the ground truth is
the range along each ray to the scanned mesh. On FIORD
the views are those of the second lens of the rig, posed by
its COLMAP reconstruction brought into the frame of the
laser scan by a similarity transform, and the ground truth
is the laser cloud z-buffered into each view; the scenes are
meetingroom and parakennus, and upstairs is left out be-
cause its reconstruction does not register to its scan. In each
scene the second view is the first one after an anchor that
differs from it by at least 15◦or 0.3 m and sees at least 30%
of its ground truth, and the third the first such view before
the anchor that also overlaps the second, a rule fixed before
any method was run. Wid3R receives the lens through its
Fisheye624 class, which contains this model, and the rays it
consumes are checked per pixel against the calibrated ones.
Where this supplement counts a method as best in a cell, a
pre-registered one-sided per-scene sign test also separates it
from the runner-up at p < 0.05.

F. The Ray Map Withdrawn
The last row is the part worth dwelling on. On equirectan-
gular input the fallback and the true ray field coincide up to
floating-point rounding, so the two columns are not merely
close, they are the same computation, and their Acc agrees
to three decimals on all four datasets. Everything the mod-
ule does elsewhere therefore costs nothing on the input the
backbone was pretrained for. Read together with the rest of
the figure, the pair says that the gain is not a general im-
provement to the backbone but a correction that activates
exactly where the pretrained assumption stops holding, and
is inert where it still holds.
Tab. 19 gives the Acc of one checkpoint under three ray
maps, and without the ray map its error grows on every
dataset. A small CNN trained leave-one-dataset-out to pre-
dict each view’s camera type and field of view gives a far
more accurate map than the fallback, yet recovers part of
the gap only on Matterport3D and is worse than the fall-
back elsewhere, so the backbone needs an accurate map
rather than an approximate one. The decoder places each
point along its ray, so a direction error moves a point in pro-
portion to its distance, which is largest on PanoCity, whose
outdoor scenes the predictor never saw, and the predictor
also replaces the exact map of the equirectangular frames,
where the fallback needed no correction. Fig. 10 shows two
of them on the same frames. With no calibration the back-
bone falls back to assuming an equirectangular grid, so a
90◦pinhole image is treated as a full sphere, and the geom-
etry is wrong over most of the frame. The bottom row is
the internal control: on equirectangular input the fallback is
the correct ray map, and the two columns are indistinguish-
able. That row is the exactness constraint of Sec. 3.2 made
visible.

G. The Camera Models and How Their Images
Are Made
Fig. 11 images one panorama through each of the eight cam-
era models, with a graticule drawn on the sphere before pro-
jection so the distortion each model applies is visible as a
deformation of the grid. The fraction of the sphere each one
observes is given under its panel, from 14.6% for a 90◦pin-
hole to the full sphere for equirectangular. The parameters
behind those panels are the ones the benchmark releases:
pinhole r = f tan θ, Brown–Conrady with k1 = −0.18
and k2 = 0.02, equidistant r = fθ, Kannala–Brandt with
a fourth-order polynomial truncated at θmax = 100◦, MEI
with ξ = 1.6, double sphere with ξ = 0.25 and α = 0.55,
and the two latitude–longitude grids at ±90◦× ±45◦and
over the full sphere. Fig. 12 shows how one such frame is
produced. The camera model enters in exactly one place,
when the ray map is built from the calibration; downstream
every lens is the same tensor of the same shape. Pixels out-
side a model’s invertible domain are marked invalid and are
never filled with a fabricated ray, which is what the validity
mask in the second row carries.

H. Accuracy Against Field of View
Normalising each method by its own 90◦value is what
makes the comparison about the lens rather than about the
method’s overall quality. A method that is uniformly weak
but equally weak everywhere traces a flat line; a method that
depends on the projection traces a rising one. Fig. 13 plots
the five field-of-view bands of Tab. 15 with each method
divided by its own Acc at 90◦and averaged over the three
datasets of the paired pool, so the curves start together and
only the slope is compared. A value below one means the
method is more accurate on the wider lens than on the nar-
row one it was measured against. Any3R stays below one
at every wider field of view, because the encoding and the
objective both read the measured ray field, so a wider lens is
simply more of the scene per view. PanoVGGT stays close
to one and dips below it everywhere except at 180◦, because
its fixed equirectangular assumption is satisfied at the wide
end and violated at the narrow end, which is the same fail-
ure as the perspective models with the axis reversed. Every
perspective-native method rises above one in every band.

Every camera in the benchmark is rendered from a
panorama, so a reader may reasonably ask whether the
model has learnt something about resampled imagery rather
than about cameras.
Tab. 20 therefore evaluates camera
pose on DL3DV [35], a capture set of real phone and cam-

over 60 scenes at three views each. On a pinhole the upper
half of the rotary frequencies still spans the image as the
stock encoding does, so the mechanism is meant to leave
this regime close to the pretrained one.

scenes of the per-scene median rotation error is the lowest
of the seven methods and our AUC at 30◦the second high-
est, while DA3 leads the AUC at 5◦. Any3R is thus at the

J. Matched Coverage
A wide lens shows more of a room than a narrow one, so
a benchmark spanning 90◦to 360◦invites the reading that
a method is credited for coverage rather than for geometry.
This section removes the difference in coverage and asks
the question again. Each input is centre-cropped and resized
back to its original pixel count, so the patch count and the
network are unchanged and the field of view is the only vari-
able. Each variant is scored against the ground-truth points
it can itself see, so a narrower view is not charged for the
part of the room it no longer contains. Cropping to half the
angular radius leaves 53◦for the two rectilinear cameras,
90◦for the equidistant fisheye, 100◦for KB4 and 180◦for
the equirectangular frame.
Tab. 21 answers the question by the field of view left
after cropping. Any3R is best in two of the ten cells, the
cropped equirectangular frame on both datasets, and ties π3
in four, the 90◦equidistant and 100◦KB4 crops. On the
four 53◦pinhole and Brown–Conrady crops it is behind,
and VGGT-Ωor π3 has the lowest median there. A nar-
row pinhole frame is what a perspective-native backbone

K. Training Convergence
The released model is trained for 40 epochs from the orig-
inal initialisation rather than from a warm start and is eval-
uated only at its selected checkpoint, so Fig. 17 shows the
per-epoch pose accuracy and depth error of an earlier full
run from the same initialisation, trained on eight A100s,
against the released panoramic baseline on the same pro-
tocol. That protocol is equirectangular input only, so it is
a narrower question than the tables ask, and the outcome
marks the boundary of the claim: final depth is below the
baseline on three of the four datasets and level with it on the
fourth, while final pose is slightly under it on three. Any3R
is the better model across camera models, which is what
Tab. 5 and Tab. 16 measure; it is not a better equirectangu-
lar model than one trained for equirectangular input alone.
L. The Frequency Split
The three arms differ in one switch and are otherwise the
same training run, so the columns can be compared directly.
On Matterport3D the constant-angle arm has the lowest me-
dian over scenes at 180◦and 200◦, and the released split
lands nearer the field-of-view arm. At 360◦the three medi-
ans are within a thousandth of each other on Matterport3D
and Stanford2D3DS, which is what the split is designed to
give, since the frequencies that carry the constant angle are
the lower half and those are the ones an equirectangular grid
barely exercises. At 90◦the constant-angle arm also has the
lowest median on both indoor datasets. The released con-
figuration is the split, which matches the field-of-view arm
on pose while recovering part of the constant-angle arm’s
advantage on geometry.
