Abstract
We present GaussianSpline, a dynamic 4D Gaussian Splatting method that models per-Gaussian motion using adaptive, non-
uniform piecewise cubic Bézier splines. Unlike approaches based on global deformations, fixed-order polynomials, or uniformly
parameterized splines, GaussianSpline jointly learns the Bézier control points and the temporal partition of each Gaussian’s
trajectory. This adaptive representation allocates greater temporal capacity to highly dynamic regions while maintaining com-
pact trajectories for near-static regions, enabling high-fidelity reconstruction of complex and rapidly changing motion. To
jointly achieve globally smooth motion and local adaptivity, we enforce G1 continuity between consecutive Bézier segments us-
ing a reflection-based constraint on their control points. This produces smooth, fully differentiable trajectories while preserving
the flexibility of non-uniform temporal parameterization. Experiments on the Neural 3D Video benchmark, the Google Immer-
sive dataset [BFO∗20], as well as on synthetic scenes demonstrate that GaussianSpline substantially improves reconstruction
quality in scenes with highly non-uniform and challenging dynamics, while maintaining competitive performance on general
dynamic scenes compared with state-of-the-art 4D Gaussian Splatting methods.

1. Introduction
Reconstructing dynamic 3D scenes from multi-view video is an
emerging problem in computer graphics and vision, with appli-
cations in virtual reality, telepresence, and film production. The
recent success of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [KKLD23] has
demonstrated that explicit, differentiable representations based on
anisotropic Gaussians can achieve high-quality reconstruction and
real-time rendering of static 3D scenes, motivating their extension
to dynamic 4D content. However, moving from static to dynamic
Gaussian Splatting introduces a fundamental challenge: the repre-
sentation must simultaneously capture the spatial structure of the
scene and the complex temporal evolution of potentially millions
of Gaussians. In particular, accurately modeling highly dynamic
regions requires flexible motion trajectories, while avoiding unnec-
essary temporal degrees of freedom in near-static regions.
Existing dynamic Gaussian Splatting methods typically rely on
neural deformation fields or fixed spatial-temporal representations.
Neural deformation approaches augment canonical Gaussians with
MLP-based motion predictors [HSY∗23, YGZ∗24, LKL∗25], in-
troducing additional per-Gaussian computation during rendering.
Structured 4D approaches instead factorize spacetime using voxel
grids or plane-based representations [XPL∗24, WYF∗24], but rely
on predefined spatial-temporal resolutions. These representations
therefore face a trade-off between computational or memory cost
and reconstruction fidelity, particularly for scenes with highly non-
uniform dynamics, where different regions require different levels
of temporal modeling.
To avoid the overhead of neural deformation networks, ex-
plicit trajectory-based methods [LCLX24, LDZY24] model Gaus-
sian motion using global parametric functions, such as polynomials
or Fourier bases. More recently, piecewise spline representations
have been explored for trajectory modeling, offering local con-
trol and compact parameterization [PBB∗25, YHOL25]. However,
these approaches typically employ a uniform temporal parameter-
ization across Gaussian trajectories, allocating the same temporal
capacity regardless of the underlying motion. This limits their abil-
ity to efficiently capture the highly heterogeneous dynamics of real-
world scenes, where different regions may exhibit slow, smooth
motion or rapid and complex changes.
To address these limitations, we introduce GaussianSpline, a per-
Gaussian trajectory-based 4D Gaussian Splatting framework that
models motion using adaptive piecewise cubic Bézier splines with
learnable, non-uniform temporal parameterization. Each Gaussian
is assigned an independent trajectory initialized with a minimal
number of spline segments. During optimization, the Bézier con-
trol points and segment intervals are jointly learned, allowing each
trajectory to allocate temporal resolution according to its local mo-
tion. Rapidly changing motion is captured with finer temporal in-
tervals, while slowly varying motion is represented with broader
intervals, avoiding unnecessary temporal degrees of freedom and
yielding a compact representation.
To ensure smooth motion across spline segments, we enforce G1
continuity using a reflection-based control-point formulation with
direction-locked tangents and learnable magnitude scaling. This
yields smooth, fully differentiable trajectories while preserving lo-
cal flexibility in motion speed and curvature. GaussianSpline in-
troduces no auxiliary neural networks and integrates directly into
the standard 3DGS optimization and densification pipeline, provid-
ing a compact and efficient representation for highly non-uniform
dynamics.
Experiments on the Neural 3D Video benchmark, the Google Im-
mersive dataset [BFO∗20], and synthetic scenes demonstrate that
GaussianSpline significantly improves reconstruction quality for
scenes with complex, highly non-uniform motion, while maintain-
ing competitive performance on general dynamic scenes compared
with state-of-the-art 4D Gaussian Splatting methods. Our contribu-
tions are summarized as follows:
• Learnable adaptive temporal parameterization. We jointly
learn Bézier control points and non-uniform segment intervals
to adapt temporal resolution to each Gaussian’s local motion.
• Smooth motion transitions. We introduce a reflection-based
G1 continuity constraint with learnable tangent magnitudes for
smooth and flexible trajectory modeling.
• Efficient 4D representation. Our adaptive trajectories integrate
seamlessly into the standard 3DGS framework, providing a com-
pact and efficient representation of dynamic scenes.
2. Related Work
In the following we discuss recent related works on reconstruction
of 3D dynamic scenes from multi-view video.
Dynamic neural radiance fields. Dynamic extensions of
NeRF [MST∗20] represent a scene as a time-varying radiance field,
either conditioning the field directly on time [LSZ∗22] or warping
each observation into a canonical frame through a learned deforma-
tion [PCPMMN21,PSH∗21], with later work improving robustness
on casually captured monocular video [LGM∗23].
These methods produce high-quality free-viewpoint video, but
inherit NeRF’s volumetric ray marching: rendering a single pixel
requires hundreds of network evaluations along a ray, so train-
ing takes hours to days and playback remains far from real
time [LSZ∗22]. Grid- and plane-based factorizations [FKMW∗23]
reduce this cost substantially, yet the scene stays an implicit field
sampled at a fixed spatial-temporal resolution, so motion is never
an explicit quantity that can be allocated where the scene actually
moves.
Dynamic 3DGS The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [KKLD23] has significantly advanced neural rendering
by replacing implicit volumetric sampling with explicit, differen-
tiable rasterization of anisotropic Gaussians. Building on this suc-
cess, recent work has focused on extending 3DGS to dynamic
scenes by modeling temporal deformation of Gaussian attributes
[LKLR24,LCLX24,WYF∗24].
Luiten et al. [LKLR24] directly optimize per-timestep Gaussian
positions and rotations under local rigidity constraints, enabling
dense 6-DOF tracking without an explicit deformation model.
In contrast, MLP-based approaches learn continuous deformation
fields in a canonical space. Deformable 3DGS [YGZ∗24] maps
Gaussians from canonical to observation space via a neural defor-
mation network, while GauFRe [LKL∗25] introduces a forward-
warping formulation with an explicit static component to decou-
ple moving and stationary regions. Although expressive, these


3 of 8
methods require per-Gaussian MLP evaluations at render time,
leading to significant computational overhead [LCLX24]. SC-GS
[HSY∗23] mitigates this issue by combining deformation networks
with sparse spatial constraints to reduce inference cost.
Another line of work improves efficiency through structured
spacetime factorization. Originating from NeRF-based representa-
tions such as K-Planes [FKMW∗23], these methods decompose the
4D domain into low-dimensional grids or plane representations.
This idea has been adopted in 3DGS extensions such as 4DGS
[WYF∗24] and 4K4D [XPL∗24], which model dynamic Gaussian
attributes using structured grid-based embeddings. While computa-
tionally efficient, these methods are inherently limited by discrete
resolution, leading to a trade-off between memory usage and recon-
struction fidelity [WYF∗24,LCLX24].
Explicit trajectory modeling. More recent methods abandon
neural deformation networks in favor of explicit motion trajecto-
ries.
Spacetime Gaussians (STG) [LCLX24] represent Gaussian mo-
tion using polynomial trajectories, improving temporal smoothness
and efficiency. Gaussian-Flow [LDZY24] further extends this idea
by combining polynomial and Fourier-based representations in a
dual-domain formulation, eliminating the need for neural networks
while improving expressiveness.
However, fixed-degree polynomial models assign uniform tem-
poral capacity to all Gaussians and may struggle to represent com-
plex, non-linear motion patterns [LCLX24]. This limitation has
motivated spline-based representations as a more flexible alterna-
tive.
Spline-based formulations provide smooth and locally control-
lable representations of motion trajectories. SplineGS [PBB∗25]
introduces piecewise cubic Hermite splines with motion-adaptive
control-point pruning, while also reducing reliance on pre-
computed camera parameters. However, its formulation targets
monocular video sequences rather than multi-view dynamic 4D re-
construction, as considered in our work.
More closely related to our setting, Yoon et al. [YHOL25] pro-
pose a method, also termed SplineGS, that employs NURBS to
represent Gaussian trajectories as combinations of learned repre-
sentative motion bases, improving temporal smoothness and spatial
coherence in multi-view 4D dynamic scenes.
However, both approaches rely on uniform spline parameteri-
zations with fixed control-point distributions. In contrast, our ap-
proach jointly learns adaptive, locally refined control-point distri-
butions based on motion complexity. This allows temporal capacity
to be allocated where it is most needed, providing a more efficient
representation of highly non-uniform motion and improving recon-
struction quality with fewer Gaussian splats.
3. Method
We first review the standard 3D Gaussian Splatting representation
and then extend it to dynamic scenes by assigning each Gaussian
a learnable piecewise cubic Bézier trajectory. Unlike fixed tempo-
ral parameterizations, our formulation jointly optimizes the spline
parameters and the temporal placement of control points, allowing
each Gaussian to adapt its temporal resolution to the underlying
motion (see Figure 2).

Learnable interval widths. Rather than using a uniform parti-

improving reconstruction fidelity over an equivalent uniformly pa-

Training. In each training iteration, we pick a random frame
index from the training set, calculate its timestamp and select a
subset of camera views captured at this timestamp. Then, every
Gaussian is evaluated at this timestamp through its active segment
(Eq. 6), and the photometric loss w.r.t. the selected views is back-
propagated to the spline and Gaussian parameters.
Together with the static initialization (all joints and control
points set to x0), this generates the Gaussian motion trajectories.
Note that in near-static regions the gradients average or are near
zero, leaving the trajectory approximately flat and consuming no
temporal capacity.
Furthermore, we apply Gaussians adaptive densification steps,
i.e., split/clone/prune operations in early stage training iterations
(as in [KKLD23]). Afterwards, the set of Gaussians remains fixed,
and only the spline parameters (joints, control points, widths, G1
scales) and the Gaussian attributes (opacity, color, covariance) are
optimized.
4. Experiments and Results
Implementation Details. We train for 30k iterations using Adam
on a single RTX 4090, optimizing an L1 loss with a D-SSIM
term (λ = 0.2), SH degree 3, and batch size 2. Following 3DGS,


6 of 8
the position learning rate decays exponentially from 1.6×10−4 to
1.6×10−6, while the learning rates for features, opacity, scaling,
and rotation are 2.5×10−3, 5×10−2, 5×10−3, and 10−3, respec-
tively. The spline joint-width learning rate is set to 0.3× the posi-
tion learning rate. Densification is performed every 100 iterations
from iteration 500 to 9k, using a gradient threshold of 2×10−4 and
a maximum of six densification events. Opacity is reset every 3k
iterations, and Gaussians with opacity below 0.005 or scale above
0.1× the scene extent are pruned. Unless otherwise stated, we use
K = 3 spline segments with non-uniform joint spacing and G1 con-
tinuity, initialized from approximately 8k COLMAP SfM points
from a single frame on N3DV.
Baselines We compare our method with three other SOTA
methods: Gaussian-Flow [LDZY24], STG (Spacetime Gaussian)
[LCLX24] and SplineGS [YHOL25].
We select these methods because they target the same multi-view
dynamic 4D reconstruction setting and, like our approach, explic-
itly model Gaussian motion trajectories. They therefore provide
relevant baselines for evaluating different approaches to tempo-
ral motion representation. STG represents Gaussian motion using
polynomial trajectories, Gaussian-Flow combines polynomial and
Fourier-based representations, and SplineGS employs piecewise
cubic Hermite splines with motion-adaptive control-point pruning.
These methods constitute the current set of SOTA approaches that
explicitly model Gaussian motion trajectories in the multi-view
dynamic 4D reconstruction setting, providing direct baselines for
evaluating our adaptive temporal parameterization on highly non-
uniform motion.
In our experiments, we use the default training schedules of 30K,
30K, and 20K iterations for Gaussian-Flow, STG, and SplineGS,
respectively. Initialization varies by scene type: for real-world se-
quences, we initialize from SfM, as ground-truth geometry is un-
available, while for synthetic scenes, we initialize from the ground-
truth point clouds. All methods use the same views, frames, and
image resolution, and are evaluated at their respective final check-
points.
For STG, we use the without temporal opacity configuration de-
fined in [LCLX24], in which each Gaussian’s opacity remains con-
stant over time. This setting enables a direct comparison of the ex-
plicit Gaussian trajectories and motion. Allowing temporal opacity
could otherwise represent motion implicitly by fading Gaussians
in and out rather than explicitly moving them through space. Since
trajectory-based methods model motion through explicit Gaussian
trajectories, we retain constant opacity for a consistent comparison.
Datasets. The Neural 3D Video (N3DV) dataset [LSZ∗22] com-
prises multi-view recordings of dynamic real-world scenes cap-
tured by 21 synchronized cameras at a resolution of 2704 × 2028
and 30 FPS. The six publicly available scenes contain approxi-
mately 10 seconds of video and feature complex non-rigid motion.
Following prior work [WYF∗24, LDZY24], we downsample the
images to 1352×1014.
We evaluate our method also on the Google Immersive light field
video dataset [BFO∗20] (flame scene), captured by an outward-
facing rig of 46 synchronized fisheye cameras mounted on a hemi-
spherical dome. We use the 45 calibrated cameras, holding out
camera_0001 for evaluation and training on the remaining 44,
over 50 frames. Following STG [LCLX24], the fisheye images are
undistorted to a pinhole model and downsampled to 1280 × 960.
The scene features a flamethrower whose rapidly evolving, semi-
transparent flame contrasts with a largely static background.
We additionally evaluate on two ray-traced synthetic dynamic
scenes, checker sphere and soft contact, with known ground-truth
geometry. Both scenes use the same camera rig of 20 static cameras
spanning 90◦in azimuth across two rings, with 60 frames captured
at 30 FPS. Images are rendered at 2704 × 2028 and downsampled
to 1352×1014, with the central view held out for evaluation.
checker sphere features a checker-patterned sphere moving lat-
erally within a five-plane room, following two sinusoidal motion
segments with different frequencies.
soft contact contains 16 balls with different masses (m ∝r3) un-
dergoing collisions with an invisible box. Contacts are modeled
using a penalty spring-damper over a finite 0.2 s interval, produc-
ing continuous velocities with large, localized accelerations. Both
scenes are procedurally generated, providing per-frame ground-
truth geometry for quantitative evaluation.

5. Conclusions and limitations.
We presented GaussianSpline, a dynamic 4D Gaussian Splatting
method in which each Gaussian follows a piecewise cubic Bézier
trajectory with a learnable, non-uniform temporal partition. With
a fixed segment budget, trajectories allocate temporal resolution
where motion is fast and use larger intervals where it is slow. A
differentiable reflection-based G1 constraint ensures smooth tra-
jectories without restricting local motion speed, and the method
integrates directly into the standard 3DGS optimization and densi-
fication pipeline without auxiliary networks.
Our ablation study shows that non-uniform spacing consistently
outperforms or matches uniform spacing across spline orders,
while increasing the order provides little benefit (Table 2). Gaus-
sianSpline achieves the best overall results on checker sphere and
leads PSNR on real N3DV and Google Immersive scenes.
Our approach has several limitations. First, while our spline rep-
resentation is effective for a wide range of motion, it can strug-
gle when the dynamics become highly transient. In Soft Contact,


8 of 8
for example, rapid collisions produce large accelerations over very
short intervals that are difficult to capture accurately with a fixed,
small number of spline segments. A promising direction is there-
fore adaptive segment insertion, guided by local curvature or re-
construction residuals, to dynamically increase temporal resolution
where needed.
Second, our trajectories are purely temporal and optimized inde-
pendently for each Gaussian, without explicit spatial motion priors
or coupling between neighboring primitives. As a result, rigid and
articulated structures must be learned through individual Gaussian
trajectories, while topological changes, such as the appearance and
disappearance of geometry, are handled implicitly through opacity
and densification.
Finally, our evaluation is limited to the publicly available N3DV
scenes, one Google Immersive scene, and two synthetic sequences,
with each baseline trained using its default optimization schedule.
Broader evaluation is desirable, but diverse multi-view dynamic
datasets with dense temporal sampling and accurate ground truth
remain scarce. As more public datasets become available, evalua-
tion across a wider range of motions and scene dynamics will pro-
vide a more comprehensive assessment.
