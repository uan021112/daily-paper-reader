Abstract 

Grasping is a foundational capability in robotic manipulation. Scene-level 6-DoF grasp synthesis relies on point clouds for metric geometry, yet real-world point ob-servations are often noisy, incomplete, and over-smoothed. RGB images provide object-region, boundary, and appearance cues, yet two bottlenecks limit their e ff ec-tive use in scene representation and local grasp reasoning. We present MMGNet, a Multi-Modal Grasp Synthesis Network that addresses these bottlenecks through hi-erarchical image-to-3D feature injection and rotational graspness modeling. Hierar-chical injection integrates multi-scale image features throughout 3D representation formation, while the rotational graspness jointly predicts the grasp approach direc-tion and in-plane rotation before organizing local image-informed features through grasp-shaded grouping. Ablation studies show that these two modules provide com-plementary gains. MMGNet achieves a mean AP of 60.34 on GraspNet-1Billion with RealSense, surpassing all compared methods. In real-world experiments with adver-sarial grasp objects, MMGNet improves the grasp success rate over AnyGrasp by 8.46 percentage points. Code, models, and data will be released upon publication. Keywords: Robotic perception, 6-DoF grasp synthesis, Grasp representation, Deep learning and computer vision.

Preprint submitted to Pattern Recognition October 27, 2026 arXiv:2610.34893v1 [cs.RO] 26 Oct 2026 1. Introduction 

Robotic grasping supports downstream tasks such as bin picking and packing [1, 2], object rearrangement [3], and tool manipulation [4]. Scene-level 6-DoF grasp syn-thesis predicts executable gripper poses for objects with diverse shapes, orientations, and occlusions. Most existing scene-level methods rely primarily on point clouds to represent the metric geometry required for 6-DoF grasp prediction [5–8]. However, real-world point clouds often su ff er from measurement noise, local incompleteness, and over-smoothing, while RGB images provide complementary cues about object regions, boundaries, and appearance. Accordingly, several grasp synthesis methods have incorporated image information to improve grasp prediction [9–11]. Nevertheless, two bottlenecks limit the e ff ective use of image information in scene representation and local grasp reasoning. First, some existing methods incorporate image cues through late feature fusion [10] or during local-region processing [11]. Consequently, multi-scale image context plays a limited role during 3D scene rep-resentation formation. We therefore argue that such image information should be integrated throughout this process. Second, even when image cues are embedded in the scene representation, the local representation used for detailed grasp prediction remains too coarse to capture grasp-quality di ff erences across gripper rotations. Ex-isting pipelines [6–8] first use grasp-quality scores to select promising grasp centers and approach directions, aggregate nearby 3D features, and only then predict the grip-per rotation around the selected direction. Consequently, grasps that share the same center and approach direction but di ff er in gripper rotation are evaluated from the same local representation. Thus, the embedded image cues are not organized according to the candidate rotation, limiting their ability to capture the corresponding grasp-quality di ff erences. We present the Multi-Modal Grasp Synthesis Network (MMGNet), a single-view scene-level framework designed to address these two bottlenecks. To address the first, MMGNet introduces hierarchical image-to-3D feature injection, which aligns multi-scale image features with scene points and injects them into successive stages of a 2sparse 3D backbone, allowing image context to shape the 3D representation through-out the backbone. To address the second, MMGNet introduces rotational graspness modeling to jointly predict the grasp approach direction and in-plane rotation before local aggregation. The selected rotation then guides grasp-shaded grouping, which gathers local image-informed 3D features in the corresponding gripper frame. To-gether, these designs make grasp-quality di ff erences across rotations explicit before local analysis and organize the resulting local features according to the selected grip-per orientation. Given an RGB-D observation, MMGNet first extracts a multi-scale image feature pyramid and establishes pixel–point correspondence with the full-scene point cloud. The aligned image features are injected into multiple sparse-encoder stages to form an RGB-informed 3D scene representation while retaining e ffi cient metric neighborhood aggregation. A foreground score then guides the sampling of spatially distributed grasp seeds. For each seed, rotational graspness selects a joint view–in-plane rota-tion, after which grasp-shaded grouping aggregates local features in the correspond-ing gripper frame. Finally, the operation head predicts approach depth and width of the gripper to produce the 6-DoF grasps. In GraspNet-1Billion [5] dataset, MMGNet achieves a state-of-the-art mean Aver-age Precision (AP) of 60.34 in RealSense scenes and a competitive mean AP of 50.72 in Kinect scenes. Ablation studies show that hierarchical image-to-3D feature injec-tion improves the point-only Rotational model by 5.39 AP points, while the rotational graspness model outperforms the View model by 8.39 points under the same hierarchi-cal injection. The two modules exhibit a positive interaction of 2.19 AP points, con-firming their complementarity. Cross-modal representation analysis further reveals stronger image–3D correspondence across backbone stages than early and late fusion. Point- and RGB-corruption experiments show that image-informed 3D features com-plement noisy or incomplete point observations and characterize how di ff erent fusion strategies respond to unreliable RGB. In real-robot experiments, MMGNet improves the grasp success rate over AnyGrasp [6] by 8.46 percentage points on adversarial grasp objects [12]. Our main contributions are: 3• We develop MMGNet, a scene-level multi-modal framework for single-view 6-DoF grasp synthesis. 

• We introduce hierarchical image-to-3D feature injection, which integrates multi-scale image cues throughout 3D representation formation. 

• We introduce a rotational graspness model that scores joint view–in-plane ro-tations and uses the selected rotation to organize image-informed local features through grasp-shaded grouping. 

• MMGNet achieves state-of-the-art performance on GraspNet-1Billion and demon-strates its applicability in real-world robotic grasping. 

• Extensive ablations and analyses validate the proposed modules across fusion strategies, grasp models, representation properties, and point- and RGB-corrupted observations. 

2. Related Work 

2.1. 6-DoF Grasp Synthesis for Cluttered Scenes 

Grasp synthesis predicts executable gripper poses from visual observations. Early image-based methods mainly considered 4-DoF top-down grasps, represented as pla-nar rectangles in RGB or RGB-D images [13–15]. Although e ffi cient, this formulation restricts the gripper approach direction. Six-DoF grasp synthesis removes this restric-tion and enables more flexible grasping in cluttered scenes. 

Point-cloud-based methods. Most scene-level 6-DoF methods operate on depth-derived point clouds. Zeng et al. [16] predict dense a ff ordances for robotic manipula-tion, while Fang et al. [5] lift dense grasp prediction into 3D scene points. GSNet [8] introduces graspness to identify promising regions before detailed grasp prediction, reducing the scene-level search space. Subsequent methods improve candidate se-lection through adaptive instance sampling [7], graspable-region prediction [17], or interest-point selection [18]. AnyGrasp [6] further incorporates object center-of-mass 4information to improve grasp stability. These methods progressively improve candi-date quality and computational e ffi ciency, but remain primarily dependent on partial and potentially noisy point observations. 

RGB-D and richer observations. Several methods incorporate image information or additional geometric context into 6-DoF grasp synthesis. RGB Matters [9] processes RGB and depth separately for gripper orientation prediction and analytic grasp search. Liu et al. [10] combine independently encoded image and point features through late fusion, while Qiu et al. [19] concatenate range-image and point-cloud features to re-cover boundary information missing from depth observations. CenterGrasp [20] com-bines object-aware shape reconstruction with grasp estimation, and ZeroGrasp [21] jointly reconstructs complete 3D geometry and predicts grasps from a single RGB-D observation using occlusion and inter-object reasoning. FlexLoG [11] combines flex-ible guidance with a local grasp model to support both scene-level and target-oriented grasping. Zhu et al. [22] improve robustness to occlusion by post-fusing an auxiliary RGB-D view and aggregating cross-view geometry around grasp candidates. These works exploit richer visual information through late feature fusion, shape reconstruction, local-region modeling, or cross-view aggregation. MMGNet follows the scene-level point-cloud paradigm and integrates multi-scale image information into the 3D scene representation. The resulting image-informed representation is then coupled with rotational graspness and grasp-shaded grouping for local grasp predic-tion across di ff erent gripper rotations. 

2.2. Image-to-3D Fusion for 3D Perception 

Image-to-3D fusion has been explored at input, intermediate, and output levels in 3D object detection [23, 24], segmentation [25], and scene understanding [26, 27]. Multi-level 2D–3D interaction has also been studied for scene understanding and 6D object pose estimation [28, 29]. These studies demonstrate the benefit of integrat-ing image information at multiple levels of 3D feature learning. Within this line of work, MMGNet introduces hierarchical image-to-3D injection into a sparse scene-level grasp backbone. We evaluate MMGNet from five complementary perspectives: benchmark accu-racy, the coupled e ff ects of hierarchical image-to-3D feature injection and the rota-tional graspness model, cross-modal representation formation, robustness to point and RGB corruptions, and real-robot grasping. We first report comparisons with state-of-the-art methods, then conduct Ablation studies and transfer analysis, and finally examine robustness, deployment cost, and real-world performance. 

4.1. Implementation Details 

Benchmark. We evaluate our method using the GraspNet-1Billion dataset [5], which includes 100 training and 90 testing scenes, each captured from 256 views with two commercial-grade cameras, RealSense and Kinect. The testing scenes are split into three categories: seen, similar, and novel, depending on whether the objects are in-cluded in the training data. 

Implementation. Our method is implemented using PyTorch. For scene-level grasp synthesis, we randomly sample N f = 20 ,000 points from the full scene point cloud as input and select N = 1,024 seed points for grasp prediction. Our image backbone is implemented as a ResNet34 encoder [30], followed by a PSPNet [31] with four pooling layers of kernel sizes (1 ×1, 2×2, 3×3, 6×6). The image backbone produces a multi-scale image feature pyramid at strides {1, 2, 4, 8, 16 }, and the channel dimension of the image features is Cimg = 64. MinkUNet14D [34] is used as our 3D backbone, producing image-informed 3D scene features with channel dimension C = 256. We implement hierarchical image-to-3D feature injection at encoder stages with strides 

{1, 2, 4, 8, 16 }, matching the multi-scale image pyramid to the corresponding sparse 3D hierarchy. The rotational graspness prediction stage employs an MLP with channel sizes (256 , 256 , M), where M = 300 × 12 denotes the number of joint view–in-plane ro-tation anchors, consisting of 300 views and 12 in-plane rotations. We set the quality threshold to c = 0.5. The grasp-shaded grouping stage samples K = 32 neighboring points from each gripper-aligned cuboid region. The cuboid dimensions are set to 12 Wmax = 0.10m, T f = 0.02m, and zmax = 0.05m, corresponding to the maximum gripper width, fingertip thickness, and maximum grouping extent along the depth direction, respectively. For the operation head, we use an MLP with channel sizes (256 , 256 , 2 × D) to predict grasp scores and gripper widths for depth anchors, where 

D = 4 is the number of depth anchors. In loss design, we set λo = 1, λr = 5, λs = 5, and λω = 1. 

Training. The network is trained on a single NVIDIA RTX3090 GPU using the AdamW optimizer [35] with a batch size of 6. A cosine annealing learning rate is used, starting at 1 .5 × 10 −3 and gradually decreasing to 10 −5. We train separate MMGNet models for the two cameras for 20 epochs. 

Evaluation. We evaluate grasp accuracy using Average Precision ( AP ) following the GraspNet-1Billion protocol [5, 8]. We compute AP th with the friction threshold 

th varying from 0.2 to 1.2. Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) is applied before retrieving the Top-50 highest-scoring grasps for evaluation, following [5]. For further details on the evaluation process, please refer to [5]. 

4.2. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods 

We strictly follow the evaluation protocol of GraspNet-1Billion [5] to ensure fair comparisons. Tab. 1 summarizes the results on real-world scenes from the RealSense and Kinect cameras. MMGNet achieves the highest mean AP on both camera set-tings, reaching 60.34 on RealSense and 50.72 on Kinect. On RealSense, it surpasses the strongest prior method, FlexLoG [11], by 4.32 mean AP and achieves the best performance on the Seen, Similar, and Novel splits. On Kinect, MMGNet is com-parable to the strongest prior result in mean AP (50.72 vs. 50.67), while attaining the highest Novel-split AP of 25.49. These results establish the overall competitiveness of MMGNet across the two camera settings, with particularly strong performance on Re-alSense and the Novel split. The contributions of hierarchical injection and rotational grasp modeling are isolated in the ablation studies below. Qualitative comparisons are provided in the supplementary material.

To examine what graspness information is retained before local aggregation, we freeze the View and Rotational models and fit linear probes to their pre-grouping image-informed 3D features at identical query 16 points. Comparison of Image-to-3D Fusion Scheme. Under the same rotational graspness model, we compare direct RGB input, early fusion, three late-fusion variants, and our hierarchical image-to-3D injection. As reported in Tab. 2, directly concatenat-ing RGB values with point coordinates slightly reduces the mean AP from 54.95 to 54.18, indicating that isolated color measurements provide insu ffi cient visual context. Early fusion improves the mean AP to 59.05 by introducing contextualized image fea-tures before sparse 3D encoding, but exposes the 3D hierarchy to this evidence only 17 once. The late-fusion variants provide smaller gains because image and geometric representations are formed largely independently; among them, Late (Add) reaches 58.58 mean AP. Hierarchical injection introduces aligned multi-scale image features at successive sparse-encoder stages and achieves the highest AP on all three test splits, improving the mean AP by 5.39 points. These results indicate that image features are more e ff ective when introduced before or throughout hierarchical 3D representation formation than when fused after the 3D representation has largely formed. Further analyses in the supplementary material show that, among the learned RGB fusion variants, the advantage of hierarchical injection is not attributable to larger model capacity, or a greater optimization budget, while its computational cost remains com-parable to the alternative fusion strategies.

5. Conclusion 

This work investigates how image information can be more e ff ectively exploited for scene-level 6-DoF grasp synthesis. MMGNet combines hierarchical image-to-3D feature injection with a rotational graspness model, enabling multi-scale image con-text to participate throughout 3D representation formation and allowing the selected grasp rotation to guide local feature aggregation. Ablation and representation analyses show that these designs improve image–3D correspondence and preserve more grasp-quality information among candidate rotations. MMGNet achieves 60.34 mean AP on the RealSense setting of GraspNet-1Billion and improves real-robot grasp success over AnyGrasp by 8.46 percentage points on challenging objects. The consistent gains obtained when applying hierarchical injection to other grasp models further suggest that this image-to-3D interface can benefit broader scene-level grasp pipelines. Several limitations remain. The current model relies on accurate image–point cor-respondence and lacks an explicit fallback when RGB information becomes unreli-25 able. The additional image backbone also introduces extra computation. Future work will explore reliability-aware and more e ffi cient image-to-3D fusion, execution-aware grasp scoring, and online feedback for avoiding repeated failures.
