# A Three-Dimensional Reverse-Projection Method for Sparse Point Cloud Completion and Its Application to High-Speed Train Nose Reconstruction 

Xiaozhen Ma, Zhao Tang ∗

, Hanbin Lai, Ruiqi Chen, Jin Jin 

Abstract 

Holes in LiDAR scans of environments with glass windows remain an unresolved problem in three-dimensional reconstruction. This study presents a pipeline for point cloud acquisition, filtering, completion, and surface reconstruction to address sparse sampling and missing window regions in scans of a high-speed train nose. FAST-LIVO2 provides the initial point cloud through multisensor odometry and mapping, and moving least squares (MLS) smooths the observations. We then introduce three-axis projection-based subdivision and interpolation with reverse hole boundary identification, referred to as three-axis reverse completion. The method interpolates missing regions from observations around each hole. Greedy projection triangulation, Poisson surface reconstruction, and a Marching Cubes-based pipeline generate meshes from the completed point cloud. Experiments on a proportionally scaled display model of a high-speed train nose show that the proposed method fills missing point cloud regions around the glass windows. Under the evaluation setting used in this study, greedy projection triangulation yields lower geometric distance errors than the other two reconstruction pipelines. The pipeline supports non-contact digital modeling of train nose geometry and provides a practical approach to reconstructing objects with glass windows. 

Keywords: high-speed train nose; point cloud completion; three-axis projection; hole boundary identifi-cation; moving least squares; greedy projection triangulation 

# 1 Introduction 

Mobile scans of a high-speed train nose often capture dense points on the streamlined shell but leave gaps around the cab windows. The point cloud acquired in this study contains approximately 9.7 million points, yet local holes remain. The total point count therefore does not indicate whether the surface is fully observed. Adding samples near existing surfaces alone does not cover the interiors of these holes and may incorrectly connect separate surfaces. Completion requires identifying the missing regions and using the surrounding observations to constrain the positions of new points, providing a geometric basis for digital modeling and subsequent design analysis [1, 2, 3]. Multisensor simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) methods fuse sensor measurements for pose estimation and point cloud registration [4, 5]. Local surface fitting and point cloud upsampling improve the smoothness and sampling density of existing observations [6, 7, 8]. However, missing regions present two specific difficulties. The absence of observations inside a hole makes its extent difficult to determine directly, while nearby points may belong to different sides of the object, causing interpolation to mix geometry from separate surfaces. Learning-based completion recovers missing structures by learning the relationship between partial observations and complete shapes [9]. Examples include PointAttN, which uses attention [10], and SuperPC, which uses a unified diffusion model [11]. This

To address these difficulties, we propose three-axis projection-based subdivision and interpolation with reverse hole boundary identification, hereafter referred to as three-axis reverse completion. Projections along three orthogonal directions reduce the dependence of hole identification on a single viewing direction. Unoccupied locations in each projection domain become candidate hole points, allowing regions without observations to participate in clustering. The method then separates overlapping surface layers, retrieves observations from the same layer in multiple directions around each hole, and applies inverse distance weighting to reduce cross-layer mixing and one-sided neighborhood support. Combined with FAST-LIVO2 acquisition, MLS preprocessing, and surface reconstruction, the method forms the train nose reconstruction pipeline shown in Figure 1. The main contributions of this study are as follows: 1. We propose three-axis reverse completion. The method converts unoccupied grid locations within valid projection domains into candidate hole points, identifies missing regions through clustering, and establishes their correspondence with surrounding observed boundaries to support the completion of regional holes. 2. We develop a multidirectional interpolation strategy using observations from the same surface layer. To address overlapping projections and uneven sampling along hole boundaries, the strategy combines surface layer separation with multidirectional boundary searches to constrain new points to the appropriate layer. The completed points from each projection direction are then recovered in three-dimensional space. 3. We validate the method on a point cloud of a proportionally scaled train nose display model acquired by our research team. We integrate the method into a multisensor point cloud reconstruction pipeline, compare it with nearest-neighbor interpolation, and compare the results of greedy projection triangulation, Poisson surface reconstruction, and Marching Cubes to examine window completion and differences in surface reconstruction.

22 Related Work 

Reconstructing a train nose from point clouds involves three connected stages: processing the observations, completing missing regions, and reconstructing the surface. Mobile scans require registration in a common coordinate system and filtering to reduce measurement noise. Densification or completion then addresses the sampling density of observed surfaces and the distribution of missing regions before surface reconstruction converts the discrete points into a continuous representation. The following discussion reviews work relevant to these stages. 

2.1 Geometry-Based Point Cloud Completion 

Sparse point cloud SLAM has advanced significantly in recent years, but its applications primarily involve robot localization and mapping in settings that are insensitive to holes in the point cloud. Geometric hole filling either operates directly on the point cloud or first constructs a mesh [12]. Mesh-based methods use boundary connectivity to generate filling patches. For example, Gou et al. introduce LIMOFilling, which detects boundary holes through local overlap analysis and uses neighboring structures to recover missing sharp features [13]. Hole detection also requires distinguishing the outer boundary of a model from internal hole boundaries. Yip et al. address this problem in edge-manifold triangular meshes with singular vertices through boundary traversal, complex boundary decomposition, and hole classification [14]. For gaps in terrestrial LiDAR data, Kang et al. compare nearest-neighbor, inverse distance, triangulated irregular network, spline, and Kriging interpolation [15]. Unlike deep learning-based completion, these geometric approaches generate new points from the boundaries and local surface constraints of the target data. Lan et al. combine boundary extraction, iterative slicing, and MLS with additional increment conditions for hole filling [16]. Zhang et al. identify holes using density and boundary information in two-dimensional projections and repair them using geometry and curvature [17]. These studies show that constructing candidate hole regions and selecting neighborhood constraints are central to methods that do not require training. For multiple holes, Hou et al. identify boundaries using a fitted global plane and fill the holes through local surface fitting near those boundaries [18]. Xie et al. use signal decomposition to formulate terrain hole filling with ambiguous boundaries as B-spline surface fitting and two-dimensional image inpainting [19]. For reflective regions, Sun et al. combine outlier identification, hole filling, and Poisson surface reconstruction to address missing observations [20]. Together, these studies motivate the joint treatment of missing-region identification and local geometric constraints. Our work focuses on hole localization and support point selection in sparse point clouds. 

2.2 Deep Learning-Based Point Cloud Completion 

Machine learning methods typically map sparse point sets to denser ones using point features and neighborhood relationships. PU-Net expands point features, while PU-GCN uses graph convolutions [7, 8]. PU-GAN adds adversarial learning to constrain the distribution of generated points [21]. The underlying representation also affects point generation. PU-VoxelNet combines voxel representations with density-guided resampling for arbitrary-scale upsampling [22], while PU-Gaussian samples and refines points using local anisotropic Gaussian representations [23]. These methods primarily improve sampling density and point distribution on observed surfaces. Recovering extended missing regions still requires additional geometric information. For extended missing regions, learning-based completion typically learns the relationship between partial observations and complete structures from training data [9]. PointAttN uses attention to model relationships between points [10], while SuperPC unifies completion, upsampling, denoising, and colorization within a diffusion model [11]. For the window regions of a train nose, our method constructs candidate missing regions from 3unoccupied locations in three orthogonal projections. It clusters the candidate hole points to localize individual holes and applies inverse distance weighted interpolation with multidirectional support from the same surface layer. The distinction lies in how hole localization and support point selection work together, rather than in the use of projection or interpolation alone. Completion relies on observations around each hole and does not require a pretrained shape model. 

2.3 From Discrete Points to Continuous Surfaces 

Geometric surface reconstruction establishes connections between discrete points through local meshing or global surface fitting. Greedy projection triangulation creates triangular faces from neighborhood projections [24]. Poisson surface reconstruction fits an implicit function to recover a global surface, while screened Poisson reconstruction adds point position constraints to keep the surface closer to the observations [25, 26]. For pipelines that use voxel-based scalar fields, Marching Cubes generates a mesh by extracting an isosurface [27]. Although these methods address connectivity and smoothness in different ways, reconstruction across extended gaps still depends on the geometry available around them. Neural implicit representations and three-dimensional Gaussian representations also support scene reconstruction. Huang et al. introduce neural kernel surface reconstruction to estimate implicit surfaces from sparse and noisy point clouds [28]. Shi et al. combine local signed distance functions into a lightweight neural representation for large-scale three-dimensional mapping [29]. With a different emphasis from direct geometric surface recovery, GS-LIVM fuses LiDAR, camera, and IMU observations and represents scenes with three-dimensional Gaussians for real-time photorealistic mapping [30]. These approaches address continuous geometry and scene appearance, respectively. Our study focuses on completing missing point cloud regions for train nose surface reconstruction and evaluates the resulting meshes through their shape and geometric distance errors. These studies offer several representations for recovering continuous surfaces from discrete observations. For large missing regions such as train windows, however, surface reconstruction first requires sufficient geometric samples. We address this requirement at the point cloud level through three-axis reverse completion, then compare different reconstruction pipelines in terms of the shape and geometric distance errors of the resulting surfaces. 

# 3 Method 

The proposed pipeline reconstructs the surface of a high-speed train nose from sparse point clouds. It combines multisensor point cloud acquisition with FAST-LIVO2, MLS filtering with normal estimation, three-axis reverse completion, and a comparison of surface reconstruction algorithms. 

3.1 Multisensor Point Cloud Acquisition 

3.1.1 Acquisition System 

We develop a portable three-dimensional reconstruction device that combines a camera, a light detection and ranging (LiDAR) sensor, and an inertial measurement unit (IMU). LiDAR provides global geometric observations, the camera supplies visual features and texture, and the IMU records motion. These complementary measurements support handheld mobile scanning of a proportionally scaled display model of a high-speed train nose and provide the initial point cloud for subsequent reconstruction. 43.1.2 Point Cloud Acquisition with FAST-LIVO2 

The acquisition system uses the open-source FAST-LIVO2 framework from the MaRS Laboratory at the University of Hong Kong [5], with Robot Operating System 2 (ROS 2) software adaptation and time synchronization for the device [31]. FAST-LIVO2 uses an error-state iterated Kalman filter (ESIKF) to estimate the system state from LiDAR, camera, and IMU measurements through sequential LiDAR-inertial and visual-inertial updates. Scan reprojection handles sensor synchronization, while forward and backward propagation correct motion distortion. The LiDAR module registers raw points directly to a voxel map and incorporates beam divergence into its noise model. The visual module uses LiDAR plane priors for affine warping, updates the state by minimizing photometric error, and estimates exposure time online to account for illumination changes. A unified voxel map supports visible-voxel queries, on-demand ray casting, and outlier rejection to select valid visual points, followed by normal refinement and reference patch updates. A ring buffer supports efficient local map maintenance [5].

3.3 Three-Axis Reverse Completion 

To address holes in acquired point clouds, three-axis reverse completion follows a sequence of dimension-ality reduction, interpolation within surface layers, reverse subdivision and clustering, multidirectional interpolation, and iterative refinement. Dimensionality reduction projects the three-dimensional point cloud onto the planes normal to the X, Y, and Z axes, converting the problem into two-dimensional domains to reveal hole boundaries. Interpolation within each layer first improves point distribution before the remaining holes are processed. Reverse subdivision and clustering generate candidate hole points and cluster them to identify hole boundaries. Distance-weighted interpolation uses support from at least six directions to connect the surfaces interpolated across large holes with the surrounding point cloud boundaries and the overall surface. Iterative refinement then addresses persistent holes to complete the point cloud. 

3.3.1 Orthogonal Projection 

For the original point cloud C = {p(x, y, z )}, projection onto the X = 0, Y = 0, and Z = 0 planes preserves the two coordinates within each plane and sets the perpendicular coordinate to zero. The projected points and their corresponding point sets are On Z = 0, p2DZ = ( x, y, 0), with point set C2DZ .On X = 0, p2DX = (0 , y, z ), with point set C2DX .On Y = 0, p2DY = ( x, 0, z ), with point set C2DY .

3.3.2 Iterative Subdivision 

A two-dimensional concave hull defines the valid boundary of each projected point cloud. The hull vertices form the set B, and the extreme coordinates within the projection plane determine the subdivision domain. A coarse-to-fine subdivision strategy balances efficiency and resolution, while octree-based radius searches retrieve the sample points required for interpolation. 

3.3.3 Separation of Two Surface Layers 

Points from opposite surfaces may overlap when projected onto the same plane. For example, projecting the left and right walls of a room onto a plane normal to the left-to-right direction removes their separation in the projection, although their original perpendicular coordinates remain distinct. To distinguish the layers, we calculate the extrema qmax and qmin of the perpendicular coordinate q, determine whether the 6samples form one or two layers, and use gradient information to locate the layers. 3.4 Comparison of Surface Reconstruction Algorithms 

We compare greedy projection triangulation [24], Marching Cubes [27], and Poisson surface reconstruction [25] using the completed train nose point cloud. 

3.4.1 Greedy Projection Triangulation 

Greedy projection triangulation incrementally adds triangular faces according to local geometry to approximate the surface represented by the point cloud. The process defines local neighborhoods, constructs triangles using a greedy rule, and checks topological consistency. For the input point cloud P = {p1, p 2, . . . , p n}, we estimate normals and align their orientations with the local surface. A radius search or a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) search defines the neighborhood of pi:

N (pi) = {pj | ∥ pj − pi∥ < r } (radius neighborhood), or 

N (pi) = {pj | rank( ∥pj − pi∥) ≤ k} (k-NN neighborhood). (14) Here, r is the search radius, and k is the number of neighbors. Starting from a seed point, the algorithm follows a shortest-edge-first greedy rule when connecting neighboring points to form a triangular face ∆pipj pk. The angle between the face normal and the local point normals must remain within the threshold θ, and each new face undergoes a topological consistency check against the existing mesh. Repeating this process produces a connected triangular surface.

# 5 Conclusion 

This study presents a pipeline that combines FAST-LIVO2 multisensor mapping, MLS smoothing, three-axis reverse completion, and surface reconstruction to address sparse sampling and missing glass window regions in train nose scans. Experiments use a proportionally scaled display model of a high-speed 13 train nose. The main conclusions are as follows: 1. MLS smoothing reduces the point count while preserving the geometric features of the train nose. The point count decreases by 88.47%, the Hausdorff and Chamfer distances are lower than those of random downsampling, and the point distribution becomes more uniform. 2. The proposed three-axis projection-based subdivision and interpolation with reverse hole boundary identification fills missing window regions through multidirectional projection, hole localization, and interpolation. Under the evaluation setting used in this study, the completed point cloud has an RMSE of 0.118 cm, lower than that of nearest-neighbor interpolation. 3. Among the three surface reconstruction algorithms, greedy projection triangulation preserves the train nose contour and curvature features well. Its Hausdorff distance of 0.0086 cm is lower than those of Poisson surface reconstruction and the Marching Cubes-based pipeline, making it suitable for reconstructing surfaces from the completed point cloud in this study. By linking sparse point cloud completion with surface reconstruction, this study establishes a pipeline for non-contact digital modeling of a train nose display model. Three-axis reverse completion uses the geometry around holes to identify and fill missing regions, improving data completeness near the glass windows and providing more continuous point cloud input for subsequent surface reconstruction. This workflow offers a practical basis for representing and analyzing train nose geometry. This study primarily validates the pipeline on a proportionally scaled display model and does not yet cover the range of acquisition conditions found in complex industrial environments. Three-axis reverse completion depends on valid boundary information around each hole. Insufficient boundary support leaves some regions near the rear of the model incomplete.
