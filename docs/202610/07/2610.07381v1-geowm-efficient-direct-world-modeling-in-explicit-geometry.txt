# GeoWM: Efficient Direct World Modeling in Explicit Geometry 

Mehrdad Noori, Guile Wu, Sam Hosseini, and Dongfeng Bai Huawei Noah’s Ark Lab, Canada 

Abstract — Modeling 3D scene geometry and its evolution over time is essential for autonomous driving and robotics. A common paradigm is to use world models to predict future images or latent representations of the environment and subse-quently recover geometry from these predictions. However, this paradigm does not explicitly model geometric structure and typically relies on recursive rollouts to reach longer prediction horizons, leading to error accumulation and increasing compu-tational cost. To address these limitations, we present G EO WM, a geometry world model that directly forecasts future scene geometry at specified future horizons without recursive rollout. The key idea is to leverage a geometry foundation model to transform observed RGB frames into a geometric history, which conditions a flow-matching transformer to predict the scene geometry at a specified future horizon. We further show that a lightweight camera-motion predictor can accurately estimate the future viewpoint, and that projecting the observed geometry into the predicted viewpoint provides an effective geometric prior for future geometry forecasting. Extensive experiments on four datasets spanning urban driving, aerial flight, and dynamic manipulation demonstrate that G EO WM outperforms the evaluated world models in forecasting depth, camera pose, and 3D scene geometry, while substantially reducing inference time at longer horizons. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Understanding the 3D structure of the environment and how it evolves over time is crucial for tasks such as au-tonomous driving and robotic navigation. Recent advances in geometry foundation models [1]–[4] have shown impressive capabilities in modeling 3D scene geometry and camera pa-rameters from images. However, these models are inherently observational as they focus on reconstructing the present scene within the given images rather than predicting its future evolution. As a result, they lack the ability to forecast how the scene will evolve as the camera and objects move, which is crucial for planning and control. On the other hand, world models have shown promise in predicting future states of the environment as images or latent representations, from which scene geometry can subsequently be inferred. DINO-Foresight [5] forecasts the semantic features of a frozen vision foundation model and attaches task heads to read depth from them. Video gen-erators such as Cosmos [6] synthesise future RGB frames, from which geometry is reconstructed afterwards. VGGT-World [7] moves closer to geometry, forecasting the feature states of a frozen geometry foundation model and decoding them with its pretrained heads. Although these approaches demonstrate the potential of world models for geometry forecasting, they struggle with representing complex 3D geometry accurately (Fig. 1). In this work, we propose G EO WM, a geometry world model that combines explicit geometry modeling with direct future prediction at specified future horizons. Specifically, we employ a geometry foundation model to extract geometric information from observed frames, forming a geometric history that serves as the condition for future geometry prediction. We then use a simple yet effective flow-matching diffusion transformer to predict the future geometry based on the observed geometric history, the predicted camera motion, and the requested future horizon. Each requested future horizon is predicted directly, without the need for recursive rollouts, which mitigates error accumulation and reduces computational cost. Furthermore, we employ a lightweight camera-motion predictor to estimate the future viewpoint, which is then used to project the observed geometry into the predicted viewpoint, providing an effective geometric prior for future geometry forecasting. In this way, G EO WM inte-grates explicit geometry modeling, direct future prediction, arXiv:2610.07381v1 [cs.CV] 5 Oct 2026 and camera-motion estimation into a cohesive framework for accurate and efficient 3D scene forecasting. We evaluate GEO WM on four datasets, ranging from urban driving and aerial flight to robotic manipulation, at different horizons. We compare against three recent state-of-the-art world models: the video-based Cosmos-3 [6], the latent-geometry VGGT-World [7], and the feature-based DINO-Foresight [5]. Across three families of metrics, future depth, future camera pose, and future 3D point clouds, GEO WM outperforms other methods across horizons. It lowers depth error by 4–58% at short horizons and 7–48% at long horizons against the strongest comparable baseline, with about 98% less inference time. Its predicted 3D point clouds are also the most accurate on all four datasets. Together with controlled ablations of each design choice, these results show that forecasting directly in explicit geometry is both more accurate and more efficient than forecasting in pixel or feature space, and they offer a practical route to world models for the many robotic tasks that depend on 3D understanding. In summary, our contributions are:  

> •

We introduce a geometry world model that forecasts future 3D scene geometry in an explicit geometry space extracted from observed RGB frames, providing a structured and interpretable representation for future prediction.  

> •

We develop a horizon-conditioned diffusion transformer that predicts the requested future geometry directly without recursive rollouts, mitigating error accumula-tion, so the cost of a forecast does not grow with the horizon.  

> •

Our method is efficient and outperforms previous state-of-the-art world models in future geometry forecasting at different horizons on driving, aerial, and robotic manipulation datasets, demonstrating the potential of explicit geometry as the state of a world model for tasks that depend on 3D understanding. II. R ELATED WORK 

Geometry Foundation Models. Feed-forward recon-struction offers an efficient alternative to optimisation-based pipelines for obtaining 3D structure from images. DUSt3R [1] showed that a single network can regress point maps, and hence depth and relative camera pose, from unposed image pairs, and VGGT [2] extended this to many views with a transformer that predicts camera parameters, depth maps, and point maps in one forward pass. Subsequent models improve the formulation, for example by removing the dependence on a reference view [3] or by accepting metric priors and calibration as optional inputs [4]. These models describe the frames they are given with high accu-racy, and we use one of them, frozen, to turn observed RGB frames into geometry. They are not designed to predict how that geometry will change; providing this is the goal of the present work. 

Video World Models. Large video generators such as Cos-mos [6], [11] and Wan [12], and driving-specific models such as GAIA-1 [13] and Vista [14], are used as world models by rolling out future frames, from which geometry can then be reconstructed. Geometry-aware variants generate depth or point maps jointly with the frames [15]–[17] or align the generator’s features with those of a geometry model [18]. In both cases the forecaster remains a video model whose capacity is spent on appearance, and geometry is obtained either after generation or as one of its outputs. Many video models generate bounded clips and extend them through au-toregressive feedback, and the mismatch this creates between training on real frames and generating from predicted ones is treated as a training problem: scheduled sampling [8] and Self Forcing [10] expose the model to its own generated context during training, and Diffusion Forcing [9] trains on noise-corrupted context so that imperfect history is seen at training time. These remedies reduce drift but keep the sequential mechanism and its cost, which grows with the horizon. 

Latent World Models. A second family forecasts in learned visual feature spaces. DINO-Foresight [5] and DINO-World [19] predict the future features of a frozen DINOv2 encoder and read out depth or segmentation with separately trained task heads, and V-JEPA 2 [20] trains encoder and predictor jointly for action anticipation and planning. DINO-World conditions on absolute timestamps, so a future time can be queried directly as well as reached autoregressively. Most recently, VGGT-World [7] moved the latent space to a geometry foundation model: it predicts the tokens of a frozen VGGT and decodes them with the backbone’s own pretrained heads, so the forecast is tied directly to camera parameters, depth maps, and point maps. This is cheaper and closer to geometry than pixel space, but the geometry is still decoded from the forecast rather than being the forecast. Latent models also roll out in the same way as video models: DINO-Foresight predicts features one step at a time, and VGGT-World predicts chunks of tokens, feeds them back, and relies on a flow-forcing curriculum to limit the resulting drift. Compared with VGGT-World, our method forecasts explicit geometric quantities and predicts each requested horizon directly, without recursive feedback, using predicted camera motion to condition the geometry forecast. GEO WM forecasts future scene geometry directly in the output space of a geometry foundation model (Fig. 2). Given the K most recent RGB frames, a frozen reconstruction backbone provides a depth map and a camera pose for each frame. A flow-matching diffusion transformer, conditioned on the depth history, this warped depth map (the anchor ), and h, then predicts the depth map at time t+h directly, without recursive feedback. A. Problem Formulation 

Let It−K+1: t denote the K most recent frames. Afrozen reconstruction backbone maps them to depth maps 

Dt−K+1: t, camera intrinsics, and camera poses, from which we take the K − 1 relative motions between consecutive frames. Given a positive horizon h from a dataset-defined set H, the task is to predict the depth map Dt+h as seen from the camera at time t + h, and the pose Tt→t+h of that camera relative to the last observed one, the transform that takes points from the camera frame at t into the one at t + h. We predict depth rather than point maps because, for a given camera, the pixel already fixes the viewing ray, so depth is the only free quantity per pixel; the point map follows from the predicted depth and pose. Every horizon is predicted from the same observed history, so no prediction at a shorter horizon is used as input for a longer one. 

B. Camera-Motion Prediction and Geometric Conditioning

A lightweight predictor maps this history directly to the relative camera poses ˆTt→t+h of every future step in H at once. We use ridge regression for rotation and a small regressor for translation; the predictor has at most a few tens of thousands of parameters. Its accuracy is evaluated in Section IV against the camera forecasts of the baselines.

C. Horizon-Conditioned Flow-Matching Transformer 

Architecture. The network is a plain diffusion trans-former [21] on depth maps. Its conditioning stack is ch =(Dt−K+1: t, Ah), the observed depth maps and the anchor. These K + 1 channels and the noisy target are stacked at 

256 × 256 , split into 16 × 16 patches, and linearly projected to 256 tokens with a learned positional embedding. Fourteen pre-norm transformer blocks of width 896 follow, and a linear head with unpatchification gives the output. Spatial conditioning is by channel concatenation only; the horizon and the flow time enter through the normalisation layers, as described next. The flow time τ and log h are each sinu-soidally embedded, concatenated, mapped by a small MLP, and injected into every block by adaptive layer normalisation with zero-initialised modulation (adaLN-zero [21]), so that the horizon modulates every layer. The transformer has 206M parameters and is trained from scratch; it operates directly on depth maps, with no learned autoencoder or tokenizer and no pretrained weight other than the frozen backbone.

The same network is therefore supervised at every horizon it will later be asked for, always from observed inputs. The transformer is trained from scratch on a single GPU in a few hours.

A. Datasets and Evaluation Protocol 

Datasets. To show that G EO WM is robust across domains, we evaluate on four challenging datasets that span driving, aerial flight, and robotic manipulation. In every dataset, a window consists of K = 5 observed frames, and the horizon set H is fixed per dataset. KITTI [27] provides 10 Hz driving sequences with LiDAR ground truth. We train on the 138 training drives of the KITTI depth benchmark and test on its 13 validation drives (3,257 windows), with stride 2 and 20 horizons from 0.2 s to 4.1 s. Cityscapes [28] provides 17 Hz stereo sequences. We train on the training split and evaluate on 500 validation windows, with stride 3 and five horizons from 0.18 s to 0.88 s. TartanAir [29] is a challenging aerial dataset of simulated flights through 18 photorealistic environ-ments with fast 6-DoF camera motion. We hold out 37 of its 369 trajectories (7,314 windows) and predict 20 horizons of 1 to 20 frames. DOMINO [30] is one of the most challenging manipulation datasets. Episodes are observed from a static head camera, and objects move independently of the robot. It covers 35 tasks and five robot embodiments. Since the public release has RGB only, we re-render the stored trajectories in SAPIEN [31] to obtain ground-truth depth. To measure generalisation beyond the training distribution, we train on 23 tasks with one embodiment held out and test on four disjoint sets: seen tasks with seen robots (in-domain), novel tasks, a novel robot, and both novel (37,360 windows, 157,325 window–horizon pairs), with seven horizons from 0.24 s to 7.7 s. 

Metrics. We evaluate three aspects of the forecast: the depth map, the camera motion, and the resulting 3D geometry. Depth is evaluated with the absolute relative error (AbsRel) at the target frame. Following VGGT-World [7], predictions are median-scaled per image on KITTI, Cityscapes, and TartanAir. Since manipulation requires depth at its true scale, on DOMINO we evaluate the raw output of our method. On KITTI, we average within each drive and then over the 13 drives. Camera motion is evaluated with the absolute trajectory error (ATE), relative translation error (RTE), and relative rotation error (RRE) after Umeyama alignment, as in VGGT-World. For 3D evaluation we follow the protocol of Gen3R [16]: predicted and ground-truth clouds are aligned with a similarity transform, voxel-downsampled at 5 mm and subsampled to 20k points each, and we report the mean of accuracy and completeness as the Chamfer distance (CD). 

B. Implementation Details 

While any feed-forward reconstruction model can serve as the backbone, we use a frozen VGGT-1B [2], which returns depth, intrinsics, and poses for the observed frames in one pass. Its depth is defined up to a scale per window, so during training the ground truth is brought to that scale with a factor computed from the last observed frame; on DOMINO, a single constant fitted on the training set converts the backbone’s depth to metres, so the network predicts depth at its true scale. Pixels without ground truth are masked, and ground-truth depth is capped at 80 m on the driving datasets and 100 m on TartanAir. The pure-noise fraction of training samples is 0.4 and the depth floor dmin of the inverse-depth weight is 0.05; on TartanAir, where scenes are mostly near-range, we omit this weight so as not to suppress the sparse far structure. C. Main Results 

Table I compares G EO WM with a recent, strong represen-tative of each paradigm in Section II: Cosmos-3 [6], a state-of-the-art video world model trained on large-scale video data, a 16B model that we run zero-shot as released and read out with the same frozen backbone, and VGGT-World [7], a state-of-the-art latent world model, which forecasts in the latent space of that backbone and reaches long horizons by autoregressive rollout. For VGGT-World we use the released checkpoints on KITTI and Cityscapes, and train it ourselves with the authors’ model and recipe on TartanAir and DOMINO, for which no weights were released. DINO-Foresight [5], which forecasts self-supervised image features, is listed for reference on the two driving datasets, and copy-last marks what no forecasting at all achieves. G EO WM achieves the lowest AbsRel at every horizon group and the lowest all-horizon CD on all four datasets among the ranked methods. On all three moving-camera datasets, it also achieves the lowest ATE and RTE. 

Depth forecasting. GEO WM has the lowest AbsRel at every horizon group on every dataset and the highest δ1 in all but one cell, and its margin grows with the horizon. On KITTI it matches Cosmos-3 at the first frame (6.6 vs. 6.8, within the seed-to-seed variation of this split) and leads by four points at the long horizons (17.4 vs. 21.2); on TartanAir the lead grows from 1.6 points at the first frame to 17 points at the long horizons (40.1 vs. 57.5). The rollout baseline shows the opposite trend: VGGT-World falls below copy-last from the mid horizons on KITTI and up to the mid horizons on TartanAir, which is the error accumulation that direct prediction avoids. On DOMINO, G EO WM cuts the error of every other method by about half at every horizon (9.6 vs. 20.5 for Cosmos-3 over all horizons). 

3D geometry forecasting. The Chamfer distance follows the depth results: G EO WM has the lowest CD at every horizon group on all four datasets, with one tie against Cosmos-3 at the mid horizons of KITTI. Over all horizons, the margin over Cosmos-3 ranges from 3% on KITTI to 39% on DOMINO.

Geometric conditioning. Removing the depth conditioning altogether leaves an error worse than copy-last and almost unchanged by how far ahead the model is asked to predict. The anchor and the observed depth history matter at different ranges. Replacing the camera-compensated anchor by a plain copy of the last observed depth map costs most at the short horizons, whereas keeping the anchor and dropping the history costs most at the long ones. Using only the last observed depth map removes both, and is worse than removing either one alone at the short horizons and over all horizons. Fig. 3(b) asks whether the forecast uses the particu-lar viewpoint the anchor encodes, or merely the presence of a warped depth map. Rebuilding the anchor with the predicted translation scaled by a factor α, rotation unchanged, traces a V whose minimum sits at α=1 , the predicted motion itself; removing the translation entirely costs 10.7% on KITTI and 18.0% on TartanAir. A model that ignored the viewpoint would trace a flat curve instead. 

Direct prediction. The second pillar is tested by feeding the model its own predictions back into the context, with the same weights and the same windows. The first step is identical by construction, and the two strategies separate immediately afterwards: at the long horizons a one-step recursive rollout reaches 38.9 AbsRel against 17.4 for direct prediction, and falls behind copy-last. Predicting two steps from each context halves the number of feedback stages and recovers part of the gap without closing it. G. Computational Cost 

Table IV compares inference cost. A horizon is an input to GEO WM rather than a number of steps to take, so the same four-draw read-out answers any horizon and the latency is flat at 0.11 s across the evaluated range; pro-ducing all twenty horizons costs 12 TFLOPs. VGGT-World and DINO-Foresight instead extend their forecasts through sequential rollout, so VGGT-World grows from 1.9 s to 36.9 s and spends 1116 TFLOPs over the same twenty horizons. Cosmos-3 does not roll out: it denoises a whole clip at once with a 17 B generator, which requires 4.9 s even at the nearest evaluated horizon. G EO WM therefore reaches the longest horizon over 300 × faster than VGGT-World and over 80 ×

faster than Cosmos-3, with the smallest forecaster of the four and the same frozen backbone as VGGT-World. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented G EO WM, a world model that forecasts directly in explicit geometry. G EO WM outperforms the eval-uated video- and feature-based world models, while represen-tation ablations further support predicting geometry directly. Direct horizon conditioning further avoids recursive feedback and its associated error accumulation, enabling efficient prediction at longer horizons. A lightweight camera-motion predictor improves the forecast by relating observed geom-etry to the future viewpoint. Across driving, aerial flight, and robotic manipulation, G EO WM produces accurate future geometry and camera-motion forecasts, while generalising to unseen manipulation tasks and robot embodiments. Together, these results establish G EO WM as an accurate and efficient approach to future geometry forecasting and demonstrate the value of combining explicit geometric prediction with direct horizon conditioning.
