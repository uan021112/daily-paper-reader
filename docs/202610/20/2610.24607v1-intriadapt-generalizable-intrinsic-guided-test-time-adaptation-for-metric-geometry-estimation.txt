Abstract—Recovering metric 3D geometry from images is
fundamentally ill-posed due to perspective projection ambiguity.
Current geometry models incorporate supplied or predicted
intrinsics during inference, but do not use them to supervise
model adaptation. To reﬁne these predictions, test-time adap-
tation (TTA) typically relies on external depth measurements.
In this paper, we instead use camera intrinsics as an output-
side geometric veriﬁer. Comparing the point cloud’s viewing
rays with those deﬁned by the known intrinsics yields a direct
measure of geometric inconsistency. We propose IntriAdapt,
a TTA framework that minimizes this closed-form geometric
residual. By optimizing a lightweight LoRA adapter on a frozen
geometry foundation model, our method aligns the prediction
with the speciﬁc camera model without depth sensors or external
priors. We further extend this approach to video sequences,
demonstrating that adapting a short sequence generalizes to
other sequences captured by the same camera. The adapted
model processes held-out images in a feed-forward pass, without
repeating optimization for each frame. Experiments on ten
single-image and seven sequence benchmarks across multiple
base models show that IntriAdapt consistently outperforms both
unadapted and oracle-intrinsics baselines in metric accuracy and
relative geometry.
Index Terms—Camera intrinsics, low-rank adaptation, met-
ric geometry estimation, monocular depth estimation, test-time
adaptation.
I. INTRODUCTION
Recovering metric 3D geometry from images alone with
accurate real-world scale is important for various applications,
from autonomous navigation to augmented reality [1]–[4]. The
core difﬁculty is fundamental: perspective projection is am-
biguous [1], [5]. A scene at twice the distance, viewed through
a camera with twice the focal length, produces a very similar
image. Resolving this from visual features alone is ill-posed.
This matters when deploying a model across cameras: an
image can support a visually plausible reconstruction while its
predicted distances and viewing directions remain inconsistent
with the acquisition setup. A useful metric estimator must
therefore combine learned scene priors with the projection
geometry of the target camera.
Existing feed-forward methods incorporate camera infor-
mation in several ways. Fig. 2 contrasts these uses with our
output-side supervision. Canonical normalization methods [3],
[5], [6] attribute metric ambiguity to focal length. They renor-
malize training data to a reference focal length f c by resizing
images and rescaling depths, enabling generalization to unseen
cameras. Conditioning-based methods [4], [7], [8] go a step
further by encoding intrinsics as explicit side information
fed directly into the forward pass. MapAnything [4], for

Supplying accurate intrinsics and correcting the learned

retaining errors in the underlying point map; similarly, intrinsic
conditioning leaves the response to the supplied camera depen-

Test-time adaptation can substantially correct pretrained
geometric priors at inference. Marigold-DC [10] guides a
pretrained diffusion model by jointly optimizing its depth
latent and global scale and shift against sparse depth observa-
tions. CAPA [11] freezes a 3D foundation model and updates
LoRA or visual-prompt parameters using sparse geometric
measurements. Both show that inference-time optimization can
improve metric predictions, but their supervision requires ex-
ternal depth, obtained from sources such as LiDAR or image-
based reconstruction. Such measurements are not always
available when only RGB images and camera calibration are
provided. A structural gap remains: camera intrinsics encode
the geometry of image formation, but these methods still rely
on external depth measurements for adaptation. The known
camera geometry itself remains underused as supervision for
correcting metric predictions at test time.
Closing this gap requires reframing camera intrinsics as
supervision. They act as a veriﬁer. Intrinsics deﬁne a geo-
metric constraint that any predicted point cloud must satisfy:
its induced per-pixel ray map should match the ray map
of the known camera. Their disagreement gives a closed-
form residual computable from image and camera alone, and
minimizing this residual adapts the network end-to-end. The
target ray map is deﬁned at every valid pixel, so the constraint
does not require sparse depth samples, pixel correspondences,
or a second view. It constrains viewing directions, while the
pretrained model supplies the scene and metric priors. Because
modern metric geometry models couple ray direction and
depth in learned geometric representations, correcting ray-map
inconsistency reshapes the point cloud and improves metric
geometry. Thus, intrinsics reused at the output become a self-
supervisory signal for metric prediction.
To this end, we propose IntriAdapt, which performs test-
time adaptation guided by camera intrinsics. Given an input
image from a known camera, a geometry estimation model
predicts an afﬁne-invariant point cloud and we can solve an
implied ray map. Comparing it against the ray map derived
from known intrinsics yields a residual that serves as self-
supervised guidance at test time. We apply this signal at test
time rather than during training, turning intrinsics from a
training-time distribution into a stronger output-time veriﬁer
for the speciﬁc image. Rather than optimizing the output point
cloud directly, we update model parameters so that the model’s
feature space serves as a geometric constraint and the adapted
model can directly generalize at inference without repeated op-
timization. To avoid corrupting the pretrained priors, we freeze
the backbone and train only a LoRA adapter to minimize this
intrinsic consistency residual on the target-camera images.
Furthermore, we show that IntriAdapt can be extended to
video sequences without test-time training on every frame.

Our contributions are as follows:
• We identify a new role for camera intrinsics in met-
ric geometry estimation: an output-side geometric veri-

paradigms, where the use of intrinsics remains limited.
• We propose IntriAdapt, which leverages ray-map consis-
tency as self-supervision for metric geometry estimation
at test time, enabling depth-sensor-free TTA. We further
extend IntriAdapt to video sequences and demonstrate
that training on only a small subset of frames is sufﬁcient
to generalize across the entire sequence.
• On zero-shot metric benchmarks, our method consistently

generalizes across frames captured by the same camera.

thing also demonstrate that the adaptation principle ap-
plies across different geometry representations.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Monocular Metric Depth Estimation
Recovering absolute depth from an image is a long-standing
challenge. Early supervised methods struggled to transfer
across environments [12]–[16]. Scaling to open-domain metric
prediction has since become central. A persistent obstacle is
that different datasets carry incompatible camera intrinsics, so
naive multi-source training can learn inconsistent metric scale.
Relative-depth models address part of this problem by
learning geometry without requiring a common absolute scale
across training datasets. MiDaS [17] develops mixed-dataset
training for cross-dataset transfer, while Depth Anything and
its successor [18], [19] strengthen relative-depth priors through
large-scale training. These priors support broad generalization,
but their outputs require additional modeling or supervision to
recover metric distances.
Metric estimators introduce different mechanisms to bridge
this gap. ZoeDepth [20] combines relative-depth pretraining
with metric ﬁne-tuning through lightweight metric-bin heads,
allowing different domains to share a common representation.
Canonical normalization methods, including Metric3D and
Metric3Dv2 [5], [6], normalize training samples into a canon-
ical camera space to reduce focal-length-induced ambiguity.
Depth Pro [9] combines a multi-scale vision transformer with
focal-length estimation, producing sharp metric depth without
requiring supplied camera metadata. These methods improve
how image features and camera information determine metric
depth in a forward pass.
Another approach supplies metric observations directly.
Prompt Depth Anything [21] fuses low-cost LiDAR measure-
ments into a depth decoder at multiple scales, while Prior
Depth Anything [22] aligns and reﬁnes incomplete metric
priors using a dense monocular prediction. These methods ex-
ploit complementary depth measurements to anchor scale and
reﬁne structure. IntriAdapt instead targets the setting where
calibrated images are available without depth observations:
it uses intrinsic consistency to adapt the learned geometric
representation, and obtains metric depth from the resulting
adapted camera-space point map.
B. Point-Map and Camera Estimation
Estimating a full 3D point map requires recovering both
scene depth and the camera geometry that lifts image
pixels into space. LeReS [23], [24] combines depth esti-
mation with camera-related recovery to reconstruct scene
shape. Conditioning-based methods, such as UniDepth and
UniDepthV2 [7], [8], couple camera and depth estimation
more closely: a dense camera representation conditions depth
prediction, making the relation between viewing direction
and scene distance part of the model. This formulation also
provides a way to incorporate known intrinsics when available.
Point-map regression offers another route to reconstruction.
DUSt3R [25] predicts dense 3D correspondences through
point maps, and subsequent models such as VGGT, Fast3R,
and π3 [26]–[28] extend learned reconstruction to multi-
view inputs and camera estimation. For monocular images,
MoGe [1] predicts afﬁne-invariant point maps and uses their
projective structure to recover camera parameters and axial
shift. MoGe2 [2] adds metric-scale prediction while retaining
this geometric representation. MapAnything [4] further com-
bines images with optional geometric inputs, including camera
rays, to recover metric multi-view geometry.
These models differ in how they represent camera infor-
mation, but each exposes geometric quantities from which
predicted viewing directions can be obtained. The common
requirement is a predicted ray map that remains differen-
tiably connected to the model parameters. IntriAdapt uses this
connection to turn camera inconsistency into a weight-update
signal, complementing the original models’ feed-forward esti-
mates and camera solvers.
C. Intrinsic-Guided Geometry Estimation
Camera intrinsics are fundamental in classical multiple-view
geometry, where reprojection consistency jointly optimizes
cameras, structure, and poses [29], [30]. These works estab-
lish projection geometry as a powerful constraint, but their
optimized variables are geometric quantities rather than the
parameters of a point-map prediction network. The resulting
correction is represented in a particular reconstruction; it does
not by itself produce an adapted predictor for new images.
Learning-based self-supervised depth also uses intrinsics, but
mainly as projection operators for multi-view photometric re-
construction; supervision comes from RGB consistency rather
than intrinsics themselves [31], [32]. Unlike these methods,
our formulation uses known camera intrinsics as the super-
vision target. A calibrated pixel grid deﬁnes a dense ray
map independently of scene appearance, camera motion, and
predicted depth. Comparing it with the model’s rays therefore

D. Test-time Adaptation
Test-time adaptation uses observations available at deploy-
ment to reﬁne a pretrained predictor. In depth completion, Park
et al. [33] use sparse-depth features as proxies for source-
domain representations. ETA [34] instead learns an energy
function on source data and uses it to guide updates of
a completion network on shifted test inputs. In the related
continual-learning setting, ProtoDepth [35] learns domain-
speciﬁc prototypes that adapt the latent features of a frozen
model while reducing forgetting. These approaches address
domain shift in systems that receive sparse depth alongside
the input RGB images at inference time.
Optimization can also act on a generative model’s latent
variables or on the alignment of a predicted depth map.
Marigold-DC [10] steers diffusion using sparse depth residu-
als, jointly adjusting the depth latent and global scale and shift
during denoising. SteeredMarigold [36] conditions diffusion
on available depth points to complete largely incomplete maps,
while zero-shot test-time alignment [37] adapts an afﬁne-
invariant depth prior to metric observations. These methods
demonstrate the value of pretrained priors, with measured
depths supplying the missing metric constraints.
CAPA [11] is close to our parameter-efﬁcient setting. It
freezes a 3D foundation model and optimizes LoRA or visual
prompts using sparse geometric cues; for videos, it shares
adapted parameters across frames. IntriAdapt changes the
source of supervision. Its residual compares predicted rays
with those determined by known intrinsics, without requiring
measured depth. Furthermore, the learned adapter can transfer
to held-out frames or sequences from the same sensor.
III. METHOD
We now formalize IntriAdapt, a test-time adaptation frame-
work in which intrinsics play the role of an output-side ge-
ometric veriﬁer. We present a model-agnostic afﬁne-invariant
point map view: a dense point cloud produced by any geom-
etry estimator that is not strictly calibrated can be treated as
a generalized afﬁne-invariant prediction. Thus, any geometry
estimation model can be corrected by our framework. An
overview of IntriAdapt is shown in Fig. 3.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate IntriAdapt under single-image adaptation,
sequence-level adaptation, and targeted ablations.
A. Setup
Benchmarks.
For single-image evaluation, we follow the
MoGe2 [2] protocol and report averages over the same 10
zero-shot benchmarks. For sequence evaluation, we use seven
camera-indexed benchmarks: three video datasets from the
CAPA [11] split (7-Scenes, ScanNet, Metropolis) and four
high-diversity image benchmarks (ETH3D, KITTI, DIODE,
HAMMER) re-organized by physical sensor. Since ScanNet
overlaps with the training sets of some compared baselines,
the main paper reports the leakage-free 6-dataset average,
with the full 7-dataset result deferred to the supplementary
material. Table I summarizes the constructed sequence bench-
mark: 191 sequences and 13,193 frames in total, or 135
sequences and 7,593 frames when ScanNet is excluded. These
are benchmark-composition counts before selecting adaptation
and held-out sequences. The processed horizontal FoV spans
42.3◦–90.0◦, and the average sequence length ranges from
23.2 to 100 frames. This combines dense video coverage
with shorter, more diverse image collections. Images are
grouped using a tolerance of 0.005 on normalized intrinsic
entries; KITTI denotes the KITTI_09_26 camera group. The
supplementary material retains the full table, including scene
type and resolution, together with the grouping, cleaning, and
sampling details for every sequence benchmark.
Implementation. The main experiments use MoGe2 [2] as
the frozen base model. We insert rank-16 LoRA adapters into
every attention layer of its DINOv2 encoder, giving 1.57M
trainable parameters, approximately 0.4% of the backbone
weights. We optimize the pixelwise ray-map residual with
AdamW at a learning rate of 3×10−5. Single-image adaptation
resets the adapter for every image and runs 10 optimization
steps before producing the ﬁnal adapted prediction.
For Seq-k adaptation, k denotes the number of sequences
captured by the target camera used to train one shared adapter.
We then evaluate it on held-out sequences from the same
sensor without further updates. The default is Seq-1 with 20
AdamW updates. At each update, a mini-batch covers 50%
of the available adaptation frames; gradients are accumulated
over four sub-batches before one optimizer step. Ray maps
are evaluated on a 64×64 grid. Metrics are computed per
image and then averaged, without temporal aggregation. All
sequence methods use the same held-out sequences within
each comparison to keep the evaluation split ﬁxed.
We also adapt UniDepthV2 [8] and MapAnything [4], using
the same LoRA rank, optimizer, learning rate, adaptation
steps, ray-map resolution, and held-out protocol. UniDepthV2
follows the MoGe2 batching strategy. For the multi-view
MapAnything model, each optimizer step instead uses one
batch of four views, without accumulating four sub-batches.
Other main results use MoGe2 unless stated otherwise.
Metrics.
For every prediction we report three levels of
geometric accuracy: scale-invariant (align s), afﬁne-invariant
(align s, t), and metric (no alignment). Each level is reported
both on the recovered point cloud (Point) and on the depth
channel (Depth). Within each level we report AbsRel (denoted
Rel) and δ1.03 (the fraction of pixels whose error ratio is
below 1.03), following the MoGe family [1], [2]. We also
report the per-column rank (Rank) across datasets, and a global
Avg. Rank obtained by averaging the ranks over all dataset-
metric pairs, where Rel and δ are ranked independently for
each dataset. When a method does not produce a particular

Generality across base models. Fig. 6 reports metric Point
and Depth Rel before and after applying the same intrinsic-

Direct point optimization baseline. Table VIII compares

baselines use 10 point-map optimization steps, matching the

optimization. After direct optimization, we apply the same
output-time reprojection used by MoGe2∗and Ours∗.
At the lower learning rate, direct optimization changes
metric Point Rel from 9.10 to 9.15 and metric Depth Rel
from 13.72 to 14.01. Although some aligned scores improve
slightly, absolute geometry does not. Raising the learning rate
worsens the two metric Rel scores to 24.29 and 57.81. Adding

that constraining changes to the initial depth does not supply
the missing depth correction. LoRA adaptation instead reaches

this favorable setting, directly optimizing the output point
map does not match LoRA-based adaptation: aggressive point-
level ﬁtting can reduce ray-angle residuals, but it distorts
metric geometry and cannot correct the biased depth values.

V. CONCLUSION
This paper shows that camera intrinsics can do more than
condition a feed-forward metric geometry model: they can
verify its output. By turning the mismatch between predicted
and camera-deﬁned ray maps into a test-time signal, IntriAdapt
uses information already available in calibrated cameras to
reﬁne metric geometry without depth labels. The consistent
gains over both unadapted and oracle-intrinsics baselines
suggest that simply providing intrinsics to a model is not
enough. Using them to close the output loop is crucial. Beyond
per-image adaptation, our results indicate that the learned
correction is largely camera-level. An adapter trained on a
small representative sequence transfers to held-out frames
from the same sensor, making intrinsic-guided adaptation a
reusable calibration mechanism for that camera.

summarize the average GT FoV of each benchmark in Table S1. We report the FoV after the benchmark preprocessing

1.2.
Sequence Benchmark Construction
Our 7 sequence benchmarks combine three densely-captured video datasets adopted by CAPA [11] (7-Scenes, ScanNet,
Metropolis) with four high-diversity single-image benchmarks (ETH3D, KITTI, DIODE, HAMMER) re-organized into
sensor-indexed sequences. The motivation for re-purposing single-image datasets is precisely that dense videos exhibit very
small inter-frame variability and therefore probe sensor-level generalization weakly; sequences mined from a static-camera
image collection have much larger inter-frame baseline and content diversity while still sharing one physical sensor.
Construction protocol. A valid sequence dataset, in our setup, must satisfy two intrinsic-consistency requirements: (i) all
images inside one sequence share a single 3×3 intrinsic matrix, and (ii) all sequences inside one dataset that we average
over also share that intrinsic matrix. For every candidate dataset we therefore group images by their per-image intrinsic
matrix and accept a group as one sequence only if all members agree within an absolute tolerance of atol=0.005 on
the normalized intrinsic entries. We chose 0.005 because it keeps the reported within-group FoV deviation at or below
0.57◦on all datasets (Table S2). The mean FoV columns use the processed target view of the evaluator. Looser tolerances
(atol=0.05) merge cameras that differ by several degrees of FoV and therefore violate requirement (ii); tighter tolerances
(atol=0.0005) over-fragment the data and shrink the largest usable camera group to a few sequences only.
Benchmark composition and statistics. Under this ﬁlter, the four re-organized single-image datasets (KITTI_09_26,
ETH3D, DIODE, and HAMMER) and Metropolis form intrinsic-coherent groups in the current evaluation index. For
KITTI, we use the KITTI_09_26 camera group rather than the full benchmark, which contains multiple intrinsic groups.
ETH3D requires light cleaning: we discard the office sequence because its photometric calibration yields a different
intrinsic matrix, as well as a small number of frames in pipes whose intrinsics deviate beyond 0.005. Since 7-Scenes

Adaptation data granularity
Table S5 gives the complete metric suite for the main-paper data ablation, averaged over seven sequence benchmarks.
Img-k uses k random images from the target camera, whereas Seq-k uses k full sequences and evaluates on held-out
sequences from the same camera. Seq-5 obtains the best average rank (2.27), but Seq-1 already captures most of the
metric Rel reduction and has the highest metric Depth δ1.03 (24.60). Seq-3 slightly worsens metric Depth Rel relative
to Seq-1, illustrating that added data does not improve every score monotonically. The default Seq-1 setting limits the
amount of camera-speciﬁc adaptation data while retaining substantial gains.
