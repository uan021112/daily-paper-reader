Jointly reconstructing RGB and X-ray provides new insights by connecting
external appearance with internal structure, enabling a more complete un-
derstanding of real-world objects than single modality. Yet existing methods
train separate models per modality and suffer from redundant geometry,
alignment artifacts, and loose coupling that limits cross-modal insight dis-
covery. We propose a unified representation in which all Gaussian primitives
share a single set of geometric parameters, with only surface-proximal prim-
itives additionally carrying appearance attributes. We perform cross-modal
registration through clean multi-view stereo and X-ray geometric proxies
instead of aligning two sets of optimized Gaussians, improving alignment
stability. We then jointly optimize the unified representation via supervision
from both modalities, complemented by a surface-depth consistency loss
derived from X-ray geometry to guide RGB reconstruction. Experiments on
the InsideOut dataset and our Flower & Insect dataset show that our frame-
work achieves more robust cross-modal alignment, higher RGB rendering

Introduction
Real-world objects are both surface and interior: we readily see a
flower’s outer petals, but not the structure within. Yet existing 3D
reconstruction typically captures only one of the two at a time. RGB
cameras record the external appearance through surface reflection,
while X-ray imaging penetrates objects to reveal their internal volu-
metric structure through attenuation, leaving the two as separate
digital records of the same physical object. We pursue a unified
3D representation that natively captures both, enabling consistent
rendering of an object’s exterior and interior from any viewpoint
(Fig. 1). Such a unified representation supports applications such

tion [Bergmann et al. 2019], and physically-grounded visualiza-
tion [Ni et al. 2024], providing a more complete understanding of
real objects than either modality alone.
Existing 3D reconstruction work has developed each modality
along a separate track. RGB-based approaches built on neural ra-
diance fields (NeRF) [Mildenhall et al. 2021] and Gaussian splat-
ting [Kerbl et al. 2023] reconstruct external surfaces but treat the
object interior as unobserved space. X-ray-based methods such
as NAF [Zha et al. 2022], X-Gaussian [Cai et al. 2024a], and R2-
Gaussian [Zha et al. 2024] recover internal density distributions
but provide no surface appearance. Training separate models per
modality is a natural starting point, yet such a decoupled approach
keeps two independent representations of the same object with no
cross-modal alignment, increasing parameter count and preventing
consistent rendering of external appearance and internal structure.
A more principled direction is to design a single framework tar-
geting RGB and X-ray jointly, and the recent InsideOut [Lee et al.
2025] is the first such attempt. It trains a separate Gaussian set
per modality and bridges them through hierarchical alignment be-
tween the two optimized representations. While this design takes
a first step toward joint reconstruction, it leaves several aspects
underexplored. First, the two Gaussian sets independently model
the same underlying geometry, doubling the parameter footprint.
Second, the alignment operates on optimized Gaussian primitives
that are the outcome of geometric and appearance fitting rather than
a faithful description of the object’s surface, and contain floaters and
optimization artifacts, leading to surface-to-interior misalignment
that compromises applications requiring aligned internal-external
display, such as digital cultural heritage [Bossema et al. 2024]. Fi-
nally, although the scene geometry is accurately recovered from
X-ray, it is still underutilized in RGB optimization, leaving RGB
weakly supervised and limiting surface fidelity in applications such
as industrial inspection [Bergmann et al. 2019].
To address these limitations, we present X-Splat, a unified Gauss-
ian framework for joint RGB-X-ray 3D reconstruction. Our design
is based on a simple observation: although RGB images arise from
surface reflection and X-ray images from volumetric attenuation,
both are ultimately conditioned on the geometry of the same physi-
cal object. This shared geometric origin suggests that the cleanest
design is not to model each modality with its own independent
geometry, but to let the two modalities share a single geometric
representation while specializing only the attributes that genuinely
differ between them. Concretely, we propose a unified Gaussian
representation in which all primitives share a single set of geo-
metric parameters, and only primitives close to the surface carry
appearance attributes, reflecting the physical fact that RGB obser-
vations arise primarily from surface reflection. This eliminates the
redundancy of maintaining two independent geometries and lets
gradients from both modalities flow into the same shared geometry.
To bring the two modalities into a common coordinate frame, we
register them through clean geometric proxies derived directly from
the input data, namely a multi-view stereo (MVS) [Cao et al. 2022;
Gu et al. 2020; Peng et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2020; Yao et al. 2018]
point cloud from the RGB views and a mesh extracted from the
X-ray reconstruction, rather than aligning the already-optimized
Gaussians. Finally, we exploit the shared geometry to enable joint
optimization across both modalities, complemented by a surface-
depth consistency loss using X-ray-derived surface geometry to
supervise RGB rendering. In summary, our contributions are:
• A unified Gaussian representation with shared geometric pa-
rameters and surface-restricted appearance attributes, en-
abling compact joint modeling of external appearance and
internal volumetric structure.
• A geometry-based alignment pipeline using clean MVS and
X-ray proxies to register two modalities, avoiding the surface-
to-interior misalignment that arises when registering two
sets of already-optimized Gaussians.
• A joint optimization scheme where both modalities update
shared parameters via their respective objectives, comple-
mented by an X-ray-derived surface-depth consistency loss.
We evaluate X-Splat on the InsideOut dataset [Lee et al. 2025]
and a new Flower & Insect dataset combining existing flower
scans [Ijiri et al. 2014] with newly captured insect specimens, broad-
ening evaluation to natural objects. Our method achieves more
robust cross-modal alignment, better RGB rendering quality with-
out compromising X-ray reconstruction, and is more compact than
two independent models.

Related Work
External Appearance Reconstruction. Neural radiance fields (NeRF)
[Mildenhall et al. 2021] have substantially advanced the appearance
reconstruction of 3D objects from RGB images, which established a
differentiable volume-rendering paradigm that fits a continuous radi-
ance field to multi-view images. Subsequent work improved its ren-
dering quality and efficiency through multi-scale anti-aliasing [Bar-
ron et al. 2021] and hash-grid encodings [Müller et al. 2022]. A
parallel line replaces the volumetric formulation with neural im-
plicit surfaces [Wang et al. 2021; Yariv et al. 2021] to recover sharper
geometry. More recently, 3D Gaussian Splatting [Kerbl et al. 2023]
represents the scene with explicit Gaussian primitives and a tile-
based rasterizer, achieving real-time rendering at comparable visual
quality, with Mip-Splatting [Yu et al. 2024] addressing aliasing un-
der varying sampling rates. Subsequent work has improved surface
fidelity by reformulating the primitives as 2D disks [Huang et al.
2024] or regularizing them toward the underlying surface [Guédon
and Lepetit 2024]. Other lines extend to sparse-view [Deng et al.
2022; Li et al. 2024b; Peng et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2024;
Zhu et al. 2024], pose-free [Fan et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2024; Shen et al.
2024; Wang et al. 2025; Ye et al. 2025], and dynamic [Fridovich-Keil
et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2024; Qian et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024; Xu et al.
2025; Yang et al. 2024; Zhan et al. 2025] settings. While these meth-
ods achieve high-quality external appearance reconstruction, the
internal structure of objects remains beyond their scope.
Internal Structure Reconstruction. X-ray and computed tomog-
raphy (CT) reconstruction [Gordon et al. 1970; Li et al. 2023; Yu
et al. 2025] recovers the internal density distribution of an object
from projection measurements. Classical analytical methods such as
FDK [Feldkamp et al. 1984] remain the standard for cone-beam CT,
producing volumetric reconstructions in closed form when projec-
tions are dense. To handle sparse-view settings, NAF [Zha et al. 2022]

for realistic radiographs [Gao et al. 2024]. While these methods
achieve high-quality internal density reconstruction, external sur-
face appearance lies beyond the X-ray modality.
Cross-modal 3D Reconstruction. 3D reconstruction has long ben-
efited from auxiliary modalities captured by physical sensors that
complement RGB cameras. Depth sensors such as LiDAR and com-
modity time-of-flight devices provide direct geometric measure-
ments that supplement photometric supervision in driving [Rematas
et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2024] and indoor scenes [Keetha et al. 2024].
Thermal and infrared imaging extend RGB reconstruction to scenar-
ios invisible to RGB cameras, with recent work introducing implicit
neural fusion of far-infrared and RGB imagery [Hassan et al. 2024;
Li et al. 2024a]. Multi-spectral and hyperspectral cameras likewise
enrich RGB reconstruction with material- and wavelength-specific
signals [Meyer et al. 2025; Thirgood et al. 2025]. Recent Gaussian-
splatting frameworks further model RGB jointly with thermal im-
ages [Lu et al. 2025], or with multiple modalities through shared
and modality-specific Gaussian components [Gu and Wang 2025].
These auxiliary modalities all carry surface-domain signals, leav-
ing the reconstructed target on the externally observable surface.
Few works have attempted to combine external appearance with
internal structure. Joining RGB and X-ray for 3D reconstruction
differs fundamentally, since they arise from different physical mech-
anisms. Prior combinations of the two modalities operate at the
image level in medical image fusion [Nair and Singh 2018] or serve
geometric calibration between the two sensing systems [Albiol et al.
2016], and learned priors have been used to predict unseen object
thickness from RGB-D observations [Nicastro et al. 2019]. None
of these produces a unified 3D representation of appearance and
internal structure. The most relevant prior work is InsideOut [Lee
et al. 2025], which hierarchically fits two independently optimized
Gaussian sets and bridges them by a post-hoc visual alignment. In
contrast, we align via purely geometric registration and adopt a
unified representation with shared geometric parameters, enabling
truly joint optimization.

parameter count than two separate single-modality reconstructions.
Optimization-wise, gradients from both modalities update the shared
geometry, anchoring it under X-ray supervision and compensating
for the indirect geometric signal of RGB photometry alone.

4.3
Geometry-Constrained Joint Optimization
The two modalities provide complementary supervision, but X-ray
imposes stronger constraints on the shared geometry than RGB. We
exploit this asymmetry by establishing the shared geometry from
X-ray first, then refining appearance attributes against this anchor.
Staged schedule. A coarse X-ray-only optimization suffices to re-
cover a reliable shared geometry that benefits both the alignment in
Sec. 4.2 and the subsequent RGB learning. We therefore split the op-
timization into two stages. In Stage 1, only the X-ray-related param-
eters {𝜇𝑖, 𝑅𝑖,𝑆𝑖, 𝜌𝑖}𝑁
𝑖=1 are optimized under the X-ray photometric
loss alone, starting from a standard FDK reconstruction [Feldkamp
et al. 1984; Zha et al. 2024]. This produces the coarse X-ray Gauss-
ian set Gcoarse used in Sec. 4.2 for CT mesh extraction. In Stage 2,
the appearance attributes {𝛼𝑖,𝑐𝑖}𝑖∈S are allocated and initialized
as described below, the X-ray-related parameters {𝜇𝑖, 𝑅𝑖,𝑆𝑖, 𝜌𝑖} are
inherited from their Stage 1 values, and all parameters are optimized
jointly. We retain the X-ray photometric loss throughout Stage 2
rather than freezing the X-ray side, since RGB gradients could oth-
erwise drift the shared geometry to improve appearance at the cost
of X-ray fidelity.

the representation compact by preventing interior primitives from

Experiments
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate our method on two datasets covering nine
scenes. The first is the InsideOut dataset [Lee et al. 2025], which pro-
vides four scenes (Matryoshka, Pharaoh, Skull, and Toy Gun) with
paired RGB photographs and ground-truth CT volumes. The sec-
ond, which we refer to as the Flower & Insect dataset, contains two
flower scenes (Tulip, Eustoma) from [Ijiri et al. 2014] and three insect
scenes (E02, E15, E32) acquired from prepared specimens, all with
paired CT volumes. To evaluate all baselines under identical X-ray
acquisition geometry, we follow the protocol of R2-Gaussian [Zha
et al. 2024] and re-synthesize cone-beam projections from ground-
truth CT volume using TIGRE [Biguri et al. 2016], sampling 50
training and 120 testing projections uniformly over [0◦, 360◦) at
512 × 512 resolution. For RGB, we use the real photographs released
by InsideOut [Lee et al. 2025], the Blender-rendered views of the
real-specimen Flower assets [Ijiri et al. 2014], and our captured
photographs of the prepared insect specimens, all downsampled to
width 800. We hold out every fourth view for testing and train on
the remainder. More details are in the supplementary material.
Metrics. For RGB, we report PSNR, SSIM [Wang et al. 2004], and
LPIPS [Zhang et al. 2018] on held-out test views inside per-view
foreground masks. For X-ray, we follow R2-Gaussian [Zha et al.
2024] and report 2D-PSNR/SSIM on rendered projections and 3D-
PSNR between the reconstructed and ground-truth CT volumes.
For the alignment, we report Chamfer Distance (CD) between the
aligned MVS and X-ray surfaces as a direct measure of cross-modal
alignment, in normalized scene units where each object’s longest
extent equals 2. For compactness, we report the number of Gaussians
(#G (k) in thousands) and storage (MB).
Implementation Details. We implement our framework on top of
the R2-Gaussian [Zha et al. 2024] codebase and reuse its X-ray train-
ing schedule for Stage 1. Stage 1 runs for 3,000 iterations from an
FDK initialization [Feldkamp et al. 1984], optimizing Lxray
photo together
with the 3D total-variation regularizer at 𝜆tv = 0.05. After Stage 1
we extract the CT mesh, recover the geometry-based alignment
(Sec. 4.2), and construct the surface set with surface distance thresh-
old 𝜏𝑑= 0.02 and reference opacity 𝛼surf = 0.5. Stage 2 then jointly
optimizes the unified representation for 12,000 iterations under
𝜆rgb = 𝜆xray = 1.0, 𝜆tv = 0.05, and 𝜆geo = 0.05. Each photometric loss
combines L1 with D-SSIM at 𝜆xray
dssim = 0.25 and 𝜆rgb
dssim = 0.2. The CT
mesh is re-extracted every 500 iterations during Stage 2, and Lgeo
is computed on 180 virtual cameras sampled by Fibonacci sphere.
All other hyperparameters follow 3DGS defaults [Kerbl et al. 2023];
complete settings, learning rates, and densification thresholds are
listed in the supplementary material. Training takes approximately
14–21 minutes per InsideOut scene on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000
GPU. As the official InsideOut [Lee et al. 2025] implementation is
not publicly available, we reproduce their method by following the
descriptions and hyperparameters reported in their paper. Specifi-
cally, InsideOut’s final X-ray Gaussians come from its X-ray-only
stage 2 (matching R2-Gaussian); we omit its subsequent X-ray re-
finement because the opacity-based rendering there is incompatible
with our density-based renderer. We denote this reproduction by
InsideOut† in all tables and figures. The full reproduction setup and
a discussion of discrepancies with its originally reported results are
provided in the supplementary material.
5.2
Per-Modality Reconstruction Quality
Tables 1 and 2 report per-modality novel-view metrics on both
datasets, with sub-tables (a) for RGB rendering and (b) for X-ray
reconstruction. On the RGB side, Ours achieves consistently com-
petitive metrics across all scenes on both datasets, improving over
either 3DGS variant by around 1 dB PSNR on InsideOut and over
2 dB on Flower & Insect. Even FDK initialization, which already
encodes the full volumetric structure, fails to outperform the MVS-
init baseline, confirming that better initial geometry alone cannot
break the single-modality ceiling without persistent geometric su-
pervision. InsideOut† [Lee et al. 2025] yields substantially lower
RGB quality in our experiments (reproduction setup in Sec. 5.1). On
the X-ray side, our representation produces reconstruction quality
consistent with R2-Gaussian [Zha et al. 2024] across both datasets,
with only minor fluctuations across individual metrics, while the
classical FDK reconstruction degrades substantially compared to
the Gaussian-based methods. The qualitative results shown in Fig. 4
and Fig. 5 suggest that our method recovers sharper surface texture
and fewer floater artifacts than all RGB baselines on scenes such as
Matryoshka and Tulip, and our reconstructed CT slices and X-ray
projections produce details closely matching R2-Gaussian on scenes
from both datasets. Joint training over a shared geometry there-
fore substantially improves RGB reconstruction quality without
compromising X-ray reconstruction fidelity, validating the staged
optimization of Sec. 4.3.
5.3
Joint RGB-X-ray Reconstruction
Cross-Modal Alignment. Table 3 reports Chamfer Distance to the
X-ray-reconstructed surface for our alignment pipeline and three
representative alternatives, all applied to the same source point
cloud and the same target geometry so that only the estimated sim-
ilarity transformation 𝑇varies. As shown by both Table 3 and Fig. 7
(e.g., the residual misalignment on Skull), the InsideOut hierarchical
alignment is less accurate than the other three methods. It operates
on optimized Gaussian primitives whose floaters and overfit details
propagate directly into the alignment. In contrast, aligning the clean
MVS surface and CT mesh proxies with PCA alone already outper-
forms the InsideOut hierarchical alignment, and our full PCA+ICP
pipeline improves Chamfer Distance by roughly 8×. The geometric
cleanliness of the alignment source is therefore more decisive than
the alignment algorithm itself. PCA provides global orientation and
scale alignment, while ICP refines the result locally. PCA alone is
insufficient for fine alignment, and identity-initialized ICP without
a good PCA seed can fall into local minima and even collapse to
near-degenerate scales on symmetric objects (e.g., E15 in Fig. 7). The
two stages are therefore complementary.

6
Conclusion
We presented a unified Gaussian framework for joint reconstruction
of external surface appearance and internal volumetric structure.
A single set of Gaussian primitives shares geometric parameters
across both modalities, with appearance attributes restricted to
surface-proximal primitives; cross-modal registration uses clean
geometric proxies; and joint optimization is complemented by an
X-ray-derived surface-depth consistency loss. Our design improves
RGB rendering quality, yields more robust cross-modal alignment
than post-hoc strategies, preserves X-ray fidelity, and is substantially
more compact than two independent models.
Limitations and future work. First, our appearance model assumes
that surface reflection dominates the RGB signal and therefore re-
stricts appearance attributes to surface-proximal primitives. This
assumption does not hold for transparent objects such as glass, or
for materials with strong subsurface scattering or transmission (e.g.,
biological tissue or wax), whose appearance arises from light inter-
acting with the object interior. Supporting such materials would
require a volumetric appearance model, and paired RGB–X-ray
datasets containing them are not yet available; we leave this to
future work. Second, our framework depends on a reasonably accu-
rate X-ray reconstruction to anchor the shared geometry, and may
degrade when the X-ray inputs are extremely noisy or blurry. Third,
current joint training improves RGB but matches the single-modality
baseline on X-ray; extending RGB supervision to also benefit X-ray
reconstruction is left as future work.

A.3
RGB Capture and Rendering
InsideOut. We use the original RGB photographs released by [Lee
et al. 2025]. Native resolutions vary by scene (from 1830 × 1830 to
2597 × 2597); for both training and evaluation, we downsample all
views to 800 × 800 pixels.
Flower. We render RGB views with Blender (800×800, 512 samples
per pixel) from the .blend assets distributed with [Ijiri et al. 2014].
Camera positions follow a smooth top-to-bottom spiral covering
5 revolutions across an elevation band of [−30◦, 85◦], yielding 80
views per scene.
Insect. We capture 36 RGB photographs of each prepared insect
specimen at native resolution 2048 × 1536 and downsample to 800 ×
600 for training and evaluation, preserving the 4:3 aspect ratio.
Foreground masks. Foreground masks are required by our RGB
rendering loss and metric computation. For the Flower scenes, we
directly use the alpha channel produced by Blender. For InsideOut
and Insect, we annotate masks with X-AnyLabeling [CVHub520 and
contributors 2024], an open-source labeling tool that integrates the
Segment Anything Model [Kirillov et al. 2023; Ravi et al. 2024]; the
SAM-predicted masks are then manually refined to remove spurious
holes and over-segmented backgrounds. PSNR / SSIM / LPIPS for
RGB are computed only inside the foreground mask.
Initial point cloud. As initialization for our unified Gaussian model,
we run an MVS reconstruction (MVSFormer [Cao et al. 2022]) on
the training RGB views of each scene rather than the COLMAP
sparse cloud used by 3DGS / InsideOut. The resulting dense point
cloud is filtered against the per-view foreground masks (keeping
only points seen inside the mask in at least one view), uniformly
downsampled by Furthest Point Sampling (FPS), and used both as
the alignment source (paired with the X-ray-derived surface) and
as the color initialization for the RGB Gaussian splats.

B.1

For ease of reproduction, Algorithm 1 consolidates the procedural

mization loop with periodic CT-mesh refresh (Stage 2, Secs. 4.3–4.4).
We reuse the symbols defined in Sec. 4 and refer to the loss equa-
tions there rather than restating them. Concrete iteration budgets
are 𝑁1=3,000 and 𝑁2=12,000, with mesh-refresh interval 𝑇mesh=500
and virtual-camera count |Cgeo|=180; the remaining hyperparame-
ters (𝜏𝑑, 𝜎, the loss weights, learning rates, and densification/pruning
thresholds) are reported in the subsections below.
A few aspects of Algorithm 1 merit emphasis. (i) The X-ray pho-
tometric loss is retained throughout Stage 2 rather than frozen, so
that RGB gradients cannot drift the shared geometry at the cost of
X-ray fidelity. (ii) The geometric loss Lgeo is evaluated on a virtual
viewpoint 𝑐𝑔sampled from a Fibonacci sphere Cgeo, decoupled from
the training RGB view 𝑐𝑟used for the photometric loss; this exposes
the surface to a denser angular cover than the training cameras
alone. (iii) The CT mesh Mct is re-extracted every 𝑇mesh iterations
from the current G, so 𝐷ct tracks the evolving shared geometry
rather than freezing at the Stage 1 estimate. (iv) Densification is
mode-aware: X-ray gradients can spawn primitives anywhere in the
volume, while RGB gradients can only do so within the surface set
S, preserving the surface/interior separation introduced in Eq. (5).

B.7
Hardware and Training Time
All experiments are run on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU (48 GB
memory). For the InsideOut dataset, training time (Stage 1 X-ray pre-
training + Stage 2 joint optimization, excluding evaluation) ranges
from 14 to 21 minutes per scene, averaging 16 minutes (Table 9). The
runtime correlates with the final number of Gaussian primitives,
with Matryoshka taking the longest because of its layered nested-
doll structure (∼215k primitives at convergence) compared with
∼50–150k for the other three scenes. Flower and Insect scenes use
larger native voxel grids than InsideOut’s uniform 2563 volumes and
therefore incur a proportionally higher cost in X-ray rasterization;
total per-scene runtimes are within the same order of magnitude.

B.8
InsideOut Reproduction
The official InsideOut implementation and its data preprocessing
are not publicly available, so we reproduce the method from the
descriptions and hyperparameters reported in its paper (Sec. 5.1). In
addition, only part of its capture data is publicly released (e.g., 251
of 555 views for Matryoshka). The released captures span multiple
physical placements of each object but carry no metadata relating
the placements, so only the views from a single object placement
can be registered into a consistent coordinate frame; the resulting
per-scene view counts are listed in Table 6. Note that InsideOut
reports RGB metrics only in its supplementary material, based on
its full non-public captures. The discrepancy between those results
and our reproduction in Table 1 therefore likely stems from the
unreleased pipeline and the substantially smaller registrable view
subset, and our reproduction may underestimate the method’s orig-
inal performance.
