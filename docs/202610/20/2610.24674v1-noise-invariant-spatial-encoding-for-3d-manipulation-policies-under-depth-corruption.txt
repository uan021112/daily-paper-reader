Abstract: 3D manipulation policies derive actions from explicit scene geometry rather than color images alone, enabling them to reason over metric distances, object relations, and robot-scene reachability. This geometric interface is also a vulnerability: commodity RGB-D sensors return missing measurements, bound-ary artifacts, and plausible but wrong surfaces around transparent, reflective, thin, or textureless objects. We study this failure under realistic depth corruption and observe a sharp depth-noise cliff across modern 3D policies, even when they are trained with matched noisy depth. This residual gap motivates a spatial-encoding approach: corrupted depth becomes entangled with the token coordinates and ge-ometry features consumed by the action expert. We propose Noise-Invariant Spa-tial Encoding (NISE), a 3D policy architecture that learns trustworthy geometry features even facing imperfect depth. NISE uses Geometric Cross-View Self-Supervision to shape shared policy features with calibrated multi-view depth, and Gaussian Marginalized 3D Attention to carry position uncertainty into deployed attention rather than treating every lifted token as exact. Across simulated bench-marks with sensor-realistic corruption and real-robot tasks involving transparent and reflective materials, NISE improves depth-noise robustness while preserving clean-depth performance, supporting 3D policies designed for commodity RGB-D deployment rather than clean-depth benchmarks alone. 

Keywords: 3D Manipulation Policy, Depth Noise Robustness 

1 Introduction 

3D manipulation policies derive actions from explicit scene geometry rather than color images alone, whether by processing point clouds [4, 5], encoding voxel grids [2], reasoning over virtual views [6, 7], or pairing visual tokens with 3D coordinates [8]. The appeal comes from the metric nature of manipulation, where contact, clearance, and reachability depend on detecting distances between the robot and the scene. Depth gives policies direct access to these relationships, turn-ing image observations into spatial representations that can guide action. This reliance creates a fragile premise: the depth measurements must be reliable enough to support trustworthy geometry. Commodity RGB-D sensors routinely violate this premise through missing depth measurements, boundary artifacts, and plausible but incorrect surfaces, especially around transparent, reflective, thin, or textureless objects. This motivates our central question: how severely does realistic depth corruption degrade current 3D policies, and how can 3D policies learn spatial representations that remain reliable under such noise?

We study this question by introducing realistic depth corruption into modern 3D policies. The failure is sharper than ordinary robustness degradation. Clean-trained policies deteriorate rapidly as depth corruption increases. Even after training on matched noisy depth, substantial degradation remains (Fig. 1). This suggests that the problem is not simply a missing training augmentation. These policies construct spatial representations from corrupted depth measurements, so residual depth errors become entangled with the features passed to the action generator. The key step is therefore to prevent depth corruption from being inherited by the spatial encoding. Prior work on depth-noise robustness has mostly intervened upstream of the policy, repairing the depth signal before it is consumed by the policy [9, 10]. These directions are valuable, but they remain observation-side repairs. Even with improved geometric representations, residual errors in depth-derived coordinates can continue to perturb positional attention and the action features it produces. The missing piece is therefore the spatial encoding itself: after depth is sensed, or repaired, the policy still needs a learned ability to form trustworthy geometry features for action prediction. To address this gap, we propose Noise-Invariant Spatial Encoding (NISE) for 3D manipulation poli-cies. Rather than treating depth corruption as an input-repair problem, NISE shifts the robustness target to the spatial representation consumed by the action policy. It introduces two complementary mechanisms. Geometric Cross-View Self-Supervision (GCVSS) trains the policy encoder with a cross-view geometry head that uses multi-view depth to encourage view-consistent spatial features. Gaussian Marginalized 3D Attention (GM3A) modifies 3D positional attention with a learned damp-ening surface, so unreliable coordinate bands have less influence on action-relevant spatial features. Our contributions are three-fold: 2• We identify and analyze a depth-noise cliff in 3D manipulation policies, showing that re-alistic depth corruption can cause sharp performance collapse across visual backbones and action-generation mechanisms, while matched noisy-depth training only partially recovers the loss. • We propose NISE, a 3D policy architecture that learns noise-invariant spatial encodings through Geometric Cross-View Self-Supervision and Gaussian Marginalized 3D Attention, rather than repairing the depth observation. • We evaluate NISE on simulated benchmarks with realistic depth corruption and real-world manipulation tasks involving challenging depth-sensing materials, demonstrating that 3D policies can be designed for sensor-realistic deployment. 

2 Related Work 

3D Manipulation Policies. Explicit 3D representations support both point-cloud recognition and robotic manipulation [2, 4, 6, 11]. Point-cloud policies use explicit 3D geometry to condition ac-tion generation [4, 5, 12]; virtual-view policies such as RVT-2 [6] and SAM2Act [7] render RGB-D observations into canonical views; and depth-lifted-token policies such as 3D Diffuser Actor (3DDA) [8] and 3D FlowMatch Actor [13] pair visual features with 3D coordinates before action prediction. These methods differ in action heads and backbones, but they share a useful interface: depth is converted into spatial tokens that the policy can attend to, sample, or aggregate. Auxiliary reconstruction and distillation objectives provide additional supervision for learning geometric and physical representations [14, 15, 16]. A parallel trend strengthens this interface with larger geometric backbones. R3DP [17], VGGT-DP [18], and GP3 [19] draw 3D features from frozen geometry transformers, while Spatial Forc-ing [20] distills VGGT features into a separate action stream. PromptDA-style depth fusion [21] and 3D rotary position embeddings [8, 13] further make metric scene structure available to downstream attention. Robo3R [22] reconstructs metric scene geometry from RGB images and robot states for downstream manipulation. The gap is that these policies usually treat the lifted coordinates as re-liable once they enter the model. Our work asks how the spatial encoder should behave when the depth that constructs those coordinates is uncertain. 

Depth Noise and Robustness in Robotic Manipulation. Depth robustness is often approached before the policy reasons over the scene. Classical filters and learned depth completion or estimation methods aim to recover a cleaner depth image [23, 24, 25]. Manipulation-specific systems such as CDM [9] and D3RoMa [10] follow the same observation-side logic: improve the depth signal before it is consumed by the policy. This direction is valuable, but its objective is still depth repair rather than learning how the policy should encode imperfect geometry. Recent work also addresses noisy geometry through the policy’s internal representations. SpatialAc-tor [26] separates semantic and geometric representations and fuses measured-depth features with pretrained depth-expert features. EquiForm [27] combines geometric denoising with contrastive equivariant representation learning, while PEAfowl [28] uses depth-distribution lifting, cross-view aggregation, and training-only depth distillation. These approaches advance robustness through ge-ometric priors, denoising, and depth-aware representation learning. Our focus is how residual depth errors affect both learned geometric features and the positional interactions between them. Improv-ing the features alone does not ensure that depth-derived coordinates are reliable for 3D attention. NISE addresses these coupled effects by learning geometric consistency directly from noisy camera views and adapting the frequency components of 3D rotary attention to coordinate uncertainty. 

3 NISE: Noise-Invariant Spatial Encoding 

NISE is an architecture for learning Noise-Invariant Spatial Encoding : spatial features that remain useful when the depth measurements used to construct them are imperfect. The design follows two 3questions. First, if clean depth is unavailable on real robots, what supervision can teach the policy representation to prefer consistent geometry? Second, if 3D attention receives coordinates from corrupted depth, how should it reason over positions that are uncertain rather than exact? Geometric Cross-View Self-Supervision answers the first question by using calibrated multi-view depth as a training signal for the shared policy features. Gaussian Marginalized 3D Attention answers the second by carrying position uncertainty into the 3D attention. Together, they move robustness inside the spatial encoding when facing imperfect depth. Figure 2 summarizes the architecture.

3.3 Gaussian Marginalized 3D Attention 

Cross-view supervision shapes the feature map, but it does not change the second place where depth noise enters the policy: the 3D positional attention itself. Depth-lifted 3D attention usually treats each token coordinate as a point estimate. This is brittle under corrupted depth because the coordi-nate controls the phase of 3D rotary position embedding; a small spatial error can become a large high-frequency phase error before the action expert ever sees the features. The method therefore needs attention to know when geometry should be used precisely and when it should be softened. The learned residual allows the coefficients to depart from the Gaussian frequency profile on a per-token, per-axis, and per-band basis, while regularization anchors them to the analytic prior. This gives the attention layer the two properties the failure analysis asks for: local variation, because geometry reliability changes across rays, boundaries, and materials; and task-adaptive selectivity, because different manipulation skills need different spatial frequencies. Coarse tasks such as but-ton pushing can rely mostly on stable low-frequency geometry, while precision tasks such as peg insertion or assembly may benefit from stronger dampening of unreliable high-frequency positional cues. GM3A is therefore complementary to Geometric Cross-View Self-Supervision: cross-view supervision shapes what the shared features learn, while GM3A changes how uncertain coordinates are allowed to influence deployed attention. 

3.4 Training and Deployment 

Training combines the base policy objective with the two NISE terms: 

Ltotal = Lpolicy + λgeo Lgeo + λreg Lreg 

where Lpolicy is the action-generation loss of the underlying policy class. The cross-view geometry head contributes Lgeo only during training. GM3A contributes Lreg during training and remains part of the attention computation at inference. At deployment, the cross-view geometry head is removed. The deployed graph keeps the backbone, shared feature extractor, 3D attention, and action expert. It does not require clean depth or a depth-completion preprocessor, and it adds no inference-time branch for cross-view reconstruction. 

4 Experiments 

We evaluate NISE in both simulation and real-robot manipulation. The experiments ask whether NISE can preserve clean-depth performance, improve robustness under realistic depth corruption beyond matched noisy training, transfer to real-world commodity RGB-D deployment, and attribute the gain to the proposed representation and attention components. 

4.1 Setup Simulation. All comparisons use the same depth-corruption interface: the calibrated noise wrapper is applied at the dataloader before each policy consumes RGB-D. This keeps the intervention at the sensor-observation level while preserving each baseline’s original architecture and action decoder. We corrupt simulator depth with a calibrated procedural pipeline targeting three sensor-realistic failure modes: missing measurements, boundary-localized depth discontinuity errors, and plausi-ble wrong-surface returns [34, 35]. Parameter ranges are calibrated against a pre-characterization protocol on RealSense D405 and Azure Kinect capture of tabletop scenes. The main experiments use three corruption levels — clean, mild, and saturated — bracketing the calibrated RGB-D sensor operating ranges. Saturated denotes the stress-test endpoint. GCVSS helps when view-specific artifacts disagree, but can reinforce systematic wrong surfaces when all cameras observe the same background through a transparent object. GM3A partially mitigates this by dampening high-frequency position cues, but does not fully solve systematic multi-view depth hallucination. Depth discontinuities and wrong-surface returns can produce biased or multimodal coordinate errors that a local Gaussian prior does not fully capture. Mixture models, ray-aligned covariance, and non-parametric uncertainty representations are promising extensions for modeling this richer error structure. These are scope boundaries rather than deployment blockers: NISE does not require clean depth at inference, and its supervision can be built from robot-collected multi-view data. 

Conclusion. Realistic depth corruption exposes a missing capability in current 3D manipulation policies: they lift depth into geometry, but their spatial encoders often treat corrupted measurements as reliable. We characterize the depth-noise cliff, show that matched noisy training only partially recovers performance, and introduce NISE to learn noise-invariant spatial encodings through cross-view geometry supervision and uncertainty-aware 3D attention. Across simulation, ablations, and real-robot tasks, the result supports a simple design principle: 3D policies for commodity RGB-D sensors should learn geometry features that remain trustworthy when depth is imperfect.

Visibility filtering removes inconsistent or non-overlapping supervision, but agreement does not guarantee correct object geometry. Multiple cameras can see through a transparent object and return the same background surface. G Training, Implementation, and Runtime 

G.1 Training Configuration Optimization and sampling. For the controlled PerAct-18 experiments, we train NISE with 32-bit LAMB and cosine learning-rate decay. The learning rates are 5 × 10 −5 for the DPT-style head and 

10 −4 for the policy. Training runs for 300 K updates with a global batch size of 64 . Clean, mild, and saturated depth inputs are sampled uniformly with a 1:1:1 ratio, using the corruption presets in Section A. 

Module configuration. The VGGT-Ω visual backbone remains frozen, while the DPT-style fea-ture aggregation head is trainable. The head fuses ViT layers 4, 11 , 17 , and 23 , projects 2048 -dimensional tokens to 120 channels, and contains 2.283 M trainable parameters. The shared cross-view geometry decoder follows the channel sequence 120 → 64 → 32 → 16 → 1. GCVSS pairs the four camera views round-robin with π = [1 , 2, 3, 0] and constructs the warped supervision targets described in Section D. GM3A uses two dampening MLPs, each with dimensions 4 → 64 → 60 ,taking the token’s XYZ coordinates and range as input and using GELU activation. They predict residuals around the analytic attenuation prior described in Section 3.3. 

Training controls. The baselines in Table 2 use the same clean/mild/saturated sampling distribution as NISE, retaining their respective policy architectures. This comparison reports mean success and standard deviation across three independent training runs. For real-robot experiments, all methods are trained on the same demonstration pool and raw RGB-D sensor streams, with each method using its own tuned training protocol; clean depth is unavailable. We will release the training drivers and evaluation harness. For the single-view MetaWorld experiments reported in Table 2 and Appendix H, clean simulator depth provides the auxiliary geometry target during training. RLBench uses the four-view cross-camera supervision described above.
