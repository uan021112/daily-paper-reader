# FLoTS-SLAM: Optical Flow-Derived Uncertainty for Monocular Dynamic SLAM with Two-Stage Optimization 

Haorui Du 1, Lipu Zhou ∗1

Abstract — Monocular simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) remains challenging in dynamic environments. Thus, real-time dynamic object identification in such environments is a critical problem. Recent approaches estimate per-pixel uncertainty from rendering residuals or multiview feature inconsistency to identify dynamic regions. These methods lack direct motion evidence, and their accuracy may degrade when reconstruction is inaccurate or features are ambiguous. In addition, such uncertainty merely downweights pixels in bundle adjustment (BA), so inaccurate uncertainty estimates can still impair camera pose estimation and 3D reconstruction. Accord-ingly, we present a monocular RGB SLAM system for dynamic scenes. Specifically, a motion uncertainty estimation method is proposed to derive uncertainty from the residual between optical flow and camera motion induced flow, directly reflecting object motion. The estimation varies with frame alignment accuracy, reducing false detections and yielding accurate pixel-level masks of dynamic regions. To make BA more robust, we estimate camera poses from high confidence pixels. These poses are then used to reproject dynamic pixels during joint refinement of poses and depth, reducing the influence of object motion on both pose and depth updates. Moreover, we provide a quantitative evaluation of dynamic masks and analyze how these masks affect the downstream tracking and reconstruction of dynamic objects. Our approach improves the mean dynamic mask IoU by 35% over a state-of-the-art baseline on DAVIS, and reduces the average ATE by 59% on Bonn and 19% on TUM relative to the SLAM backbone used in our system. The source code will be made publicly available. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Monocular simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) is a fundamental problem in robotics, with broad applications in autonomous navigation [1], augmented reality [2], and virtual reality [3]. However, dynamic objects disrupt the geometric consistency across views and may introduce erroneous correspondences. Such correspondences degrade camera pose estimation and 3D reconstruction, reducing SLAM accuracy and robustness in real-world scenes. Dynamic environments therefore remain a challenge for reliable SLAM. This paper focuses on monocular SLAM in such environments. Identifying dynamic objects underpins subsequent camera tracking and 3D scene reconstruction. Moving object seg-mentation (MOS) models [4], [5], [6] can identify dynamic objects in video, but these models typically have prohibitive inference costs for real-time tasks. Earlier dynamic SLAM methods instead use lightweight semantic segmentation or object detection models [7], [8], [9], which infer motion from

Fig. 1 illustrates the potential errors of the detection-based method NGD-SLAM [11]. Recent works [13], [14] estimate per-pixel uncertainty for dynamic regions, independent of object categories. This uncertainty is predicted from DINO [15] features with a shallow multilayer perceptron (MLP), supervised online by the discrepancy between input images and current renderings. The estimates degrade when the map is inaccurate. DROID-W [12] avoids this dependence on reconstruction accuracy by using multiview feature inconsistency. However, the feature inconsistency provides indirect evidence of motion and may be unreliable in semantically complex or weakly textured regions, as shown in the second row of Fig. 1. Optical flow captures interframe motion directly, and sev-eral SLAM systems use it to detect dynamic regions [8], [16], [17]. In these systems, however, the flow comes from a dedicated estimator, adding computational overhead and increasing per-frame processing time [18]. In addition, the identification scheme for marking a pixel as dynamic is fixed in advance [16], [19], [20]. Consequently, pose and depth errors may cause static pixels to be misclassified as dynamic. Apart from dynamic object identification, how the esti-mated uncertainty is used in optimization also affects system arXiv:2610.23496v1 [cs.RO] 16 Oct 2026 robustness. Existing uncertainty-aware systems [12], [13] apply uncertainty estimates in bundle adjustment (BA) as per-pixel weights, and refine poses and depths jointly under that weighting. However, motion residuals from dynamic pixels that are not fully suppressed still impact both depth estimates and keyframe poses. All pixels are used in BA, so inaccurate uncertainty at any pixel can affect the estimation. Motivated by the above observations, we present a monoc-ular RGB SLAM system for dynamic scenes built on FL ow-derived uncertainty and Two-Stage optimization (FLoTS-SLAM). Optical flow measures motion directly and is ob-tained from the existing correspondence predictions of the system tracker without additional computation. To identify dynamic objects accurately, we design a motion uncertainty estimation method based on optical flow residuals. In addi-tion, uncertainty weighting alone may not fully suppress the influence of dynamic pixels on pose and depth estimation, and removing these pixels would reduce the number of con-straints for optimization. We therefore perform optimization in two stages to improve robustness. In the first stage, depth is fixed and poses are estimated from high confidence pixels. In the second stage, poses and depth are jointly refined, using the first-stage poses to predict where dynamic pixels would appear if the scene were static. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:  

> •

We propose a motion uncertainty estimation method to improve the accuracy of dynamic object identification. It derives uncertainty from the residual between optical flow and camera motion induced flow, and adapts to frame alignment accuracy.  

> •

We introduce a two-stage optimization method to reduce the influence of object motion on pose and depth updates, and improve tracking robustness.  

> •

We conduct extensive experiments. FLoTS-SLAM at-tains state-of-the-art camera localization on Bonn and TUM, reducing the average ATE of its DROID-SLAM backbone by 59% and 19%. We also introduce Dynamic Mask IoU (DM-IoU) to quantitatively evaluate dynamic identification across methods. On DAVIS, our method improves the mean DM-IoU by 35% over a state-of-the-art baseline.

A. Moving Object Segmentation 

Moving object segmentation (MOS) aims to identify dy-namic objects in a video and produce masks for each frame. Early learning-based methods group pixels whose motion is coherent across frames [21], [22], and later work adds complementary cues such as layered representations, residual-flow modeling, and appearance features [23], [24], [25]. Recent methods instead inherit the generic priors of foundation models [4] or ground segmentation in pretrained 4D geometry [5], [6]. All of them, however, run a dedicated network over a multi-frame window, and their inference costs are prohibitive for real-time systems. 

B. Traditional Dynamic SLAM 

Dynamic objects produce correspondences that rigid cam-era motion cannot explain, degrading camera pose estimation and 3D reconstruction in conventional visual SLAM [26], [27]. Many dynamic SLAM methods therefore detect dy-namic regions and exclude them from optimization, recon-structing the static scene alone. Such regions are identified from geometric consistency across frames [28], [29], from semantic priors on movable categories [8], [9], or from a combination of the two. DynaSLAM [7], for instance, segments movable instances with Mask R-CNN [30] and verifies them by multiview reprojection consistency. 

C. Dynamic NeRF- and 3DGS-based SLAM 

Recent SLAM methods based on neural radiance fields (NeRFs) [31] and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [32] in-corporate dynamic region identification to handle moving objects. Dynamic extensions such as PG-SLAM [33], NID-SLAM [34], and SDD-SLAM [35] obtain motion masks from segmentation networks. The masks, however, follow object category rather than observed motion, and cover only the classes the network has seen. Another group of methods replaces category priors with a per-pixel uncertainty map [36]. WildGS-SLAM [13] and UP-SLAM [14] extend it to SLAM by regressing uncertainty from DINOv2 [15] features, supervised by the discrepancy between the input image and the current rendering. The esti-mate may degrade when the rendering is inaccurate. DROID-SLAM in the Wild [12] instead derives uncertainty from multiview feature inconsistency, requiring neither a rendered map nor a learned prior. However, all of these methods rely on appearance cues, which provide only indirect evidence of object motion. So our method derives uncertainty from the residual between optical flow and camera motion induced flow, which measures motion directly. 

D. Optimization in Dynamic SLAM 

Optimization in dynamic scenes should account for mov-ing objects. Early methods such as DynaSLAM [7] ex-clude detected dynamic regions from tracking and mapping. DynoSAM [37] jointly estimates camera poses, scene struc-ture, and rigid-object motion within a factor graph. BA-Track [38] learns to isolate camera-induced motion from ob-served point trajectories, providing correspondences for BA. Recent methods [12], [13] use per-pixel uncertainty to weight residuals during joint pose and depth refinement. How-ever, inaccurate weights may insufficiently suppress motion-induced residuals, compromising estimation accuracy. We therefore introduce a two-stage optimization method for more robust and accurate pose and depth estimation. III. M ETHOD

D. Two-Stage Optimization with Dynamic-Pixel Re-Anchor 

Uncertainty maps are not always exact, so the uncer-tainty attenuation may not fully suppress the residuals of dynamic pixels. Because object motion induces coherent pixel displacements, these residuals are systematic offsets rather than zero-mean noise. Least squares does not suppress such offsets. Under the reprojection geometry, these residuals decompose into an epipolar component, absorbed by the inverse depth of the pixel, and a perpendicular component, which biases the keyframe pose shared by all pixels. The depth error persists and can impact pose estimates on edges with a different baseline, through the coupling in E. So a high uncertainty pixel should not update the shared pose and the per-pixel depths simultaneously. We thus optimize in two stages: pose estimation and joint refinement.

B. Impact of Dynamic-Mask Accuracy on Downstream Tasks 

We evaluate how dynamic-mask accuracy affects dynamic-object tracking and reconstruction. To isolate mask quality, all methods use the same downstream pipeline. For track-ing, Mask R-CNN [30] identifies instances from the binary dynamic masks, and Hungarian matching associates them across frames. In tracking, false positives create spurious tracks on static objects, while incomplete masks break tracks of moving objects. In Fig. 4, DROID-W tracks stationary tables and monitors but loses continuity on people, whereas our masks preserve stable identities for moving people. In reconstruc-tion, false positives assign static geometry to the dynamic layer, leaving gaps in the static scene, while missed dynamic regions yield incomplete object geometry. In Fig. 5, DROID-W reconstructs walls and desks as dynamic objects and recovers only partial human geometry, whereas our method produces compact dynamic-object reconstructions with min-imal background contamination. So dynamic-mask accuracy is critical for downstream scene understanding.

E. Runtime Analysis 

All runtime experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU and a 96-core CPU, with the results reported in Table IV. The reported tracking time includes monocular depth estimation and DINOv2 feature extraction. FLoTS-SLAM incurs a modest additional cost due to the pose-only solve in the first optimization stage and the two re-anchoring tests. Nevertheless, it maintains a processing rate above 10 FPS on both datasets, achieving a favorable balance between robustness and efficiency. V. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we propose FLoTS-SLAM, a monocular RGB SLAM system for accurate dynamic object identifi-cation and robust camera tracking in dynamic environments. Our method derives motion uncertainty from optical flow residuals and adapts this estimation to frame alignment accuracy to improve dynamic object identification. A two-stage optimization method with dynamic-pixel re-anchoring reduces the influence of object motion on pose and depth updates, thereby improving tracking robustness. On DAVIS, FLoTS-SLAM improves the mean DM-IoU by approxi-mately 35% over a state-of-the-art baseline.
