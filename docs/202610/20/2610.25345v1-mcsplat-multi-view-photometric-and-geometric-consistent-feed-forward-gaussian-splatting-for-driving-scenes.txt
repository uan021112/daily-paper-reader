Abstract
Driving-scene reconstruction via feed-forward Gaussian splatting from sparse
multi-view images remains challenging due to two factors. First, cross-camera
photometric inconsistency, caused by variations in exposure, ISP, and illumination,
can be incorrectly absorbed into Gaussian geometry. Second, unreliable local
geometric supervision arises from normal-based constraints near depth disconti-
nuities. To address these issues, we present MCSplat, a multi-view consistent
feed-forward Gaussian splatting framework for driving scenes. MCSplat constructs
a canonical appearance space from depth-guided multi-camera correspondences to

Introduction
Neural rendering methods such as 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [2] have enabled high-quality 3D
reconstruction and novel view synthesis, making urban driving scene reconstruction as a cornerstone
of closed-loop autonomous driving simulation [3] and long-horizon planning [4]. Since per-scene
optimization remains computationally expensive, recent generalizable splatting frameworks [5, 1, 6]
directly predict Gaussian primitives from multi-frame images. Leveraging Geometry Foundation
Models (GFMs) [7, 8, 9, 10], they use dense depth predictions as priors for Gaussian means, along
with a Gaussian head to predict rotation, scale, opacity, color, and motion. Despite improved efficiency
and generalization, they still inherit two key challenges from 3DGS optimization:
Cross-view geometry-appearance ambiguity: Ideally, multi-view supervision should attribute cross-
view image differences to the underlying 3D structure rather than camera-dependent photometric
effects. In real-world driving scenarios, however, photometric consistency is difficult to guarantee
across views and time due to camera-dependent exposure, color response, automatic image signal
processing (ISP), and changing illumination. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (a), inconsistent appearance
in cross-camera overlap regions can cause the same structure, such as the roof, to be explained as
separate surfaces, producing floating Gaussians and degraded geometry [11].
Local geometric supervision unreliability: Driving scenes provide dense observations along the
ego trajectory but sparse off-trajectory views. Even with GFM-predicted depths as Gaussian means,
rotation, scale, opacity, and color remain weakly constrained by RGB and depth losses, allowing
multiple Gaussian configurations to fit training views while differing in off-trajectory renderings.
As illustrated in Fig. 1 (b), this can lead to geometrically incorrect yet view-consistent Gaussian
representations, making cross-lane novel view synthesis challenging. Geometric self-supervision
can alleviate this ambiguity by aligning Gaussian orientations with local surface normals. However,
surface normals are typically derived from local finite differences of depth or Gaussian centers, which
become unreliable near depth discontinuities and may introduce inaccurate supervision [12].
Existing methods attempt to mitigate the geometry-appearance ambiguity using appearance embed-
dings, global color transforms, explicit ISP parameters, or bilateral-grid-based local color trans-
forms [11, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. However, most of these methods rely on per-scene optimization,
which incurs high computational cost and limits cross-scene generalization. Recent feed-forward
bilateral-grid methods reduce the need for scene-specific optimization by assuming sufficient overlap
between reference and target views [18], making them less suitable for surround-view driving systems
where inter-camera overlap is inherently limited.
Beyond photometric variation, the under-constrained nature of geometric reconstruction is typically
addressed by introducing additional supervisory signals. Existing methods employ semantic, flow, or
normal priors to constrain geometry [19, 20, 21]. However, these priors rely on strong teacher models
and are costly to scale to feed-forward reconstruction, as they require additional data preparation,
storage, and training overhead. Self-supervised geometric constraints reduce the reliance on extra
priors but remain unreliable near depth discontinuities or when depth estimates from foundation
models are inaccurate [12].
In this work, we propose to enhance the GFM-based feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction for
driving scenes by combining geometry–appearance decoupling with uncertainty-aware geometric
self-supervision. To mitigate the geometry–appearance ambiguity, we provide appearance-consistent
inputs to the feed-forward Gaussian model, thereby reducing the impact of cross-view and cross-time
photometric discrepancies on Gaussian prediction. A lightweight transformer predicts multi-scale 3D
2


bilateral grids to map rendered images back to the original GT appearance, enabling photometric
supervision while preserving view-specific imaging effects. To handle local geometric supervision
unreliability, we use Fisher information with respect to the photometric loss to quantify geometric
uncertainty and construct an uncertainty-aware self-supervised geometric loss.
Our contributions are summarized as follows. First, we propose MCSplat, a GFM-compatible
geometry–appearance decoupling framework that builds a canonical appearance space and recovers
camera-specific appearance with a multi-scale Bilateral Grid Transformer, reducing cross-view
geometry–appearance ambiguity. Second, we introduce Fisher information-guided geometric self-
supervision that estimates geometric uncertainty from photometric sensitivity and adaptively weights
surface regularization, alleviating unreliable local supervision. Third, our method achieves state-
of-the-art results on the Waymo benchmark, including an appearance-challenging subset, while
producing more geometrically consistent novel-view reconstructions.

Related Works
Neural Scene Reconstruction for Driving Scenes.
NeRF [22] enables high-quality novel view
synthesis but is inefficient for rendering, while 3DGS [2] uses explicit learnable Gaussians for
real-time rendering, making it suitable for dynamic driving scenes. Many driving-scene methods
adopt per-scene optimization with priors, including LiDAR [23], semantics [24], optical flow [20],
human models [25], scene flow [26], and multi-scale voxels [21]. However, scene-specific retraining
limits scalability and cross-scene generalization. Feed-forward methods instead directly regress
Gaussian representations from images. STORM [27] predict pixel-aligned Gaussian motion. Driving-
Forward [28] and DrivingScene [29] infer Gaussian primitives from surround-view inputs. Recent
GFM-based methods, including DGGT [5], StreetForward [6], UniSplat [1], and ReconDrive [30],
leverage GFMs [7, 8, 9, 10] to constrain Gaussian positions and decode Gaussian attributes. Despite
improved efficiency and generalization, these methods remain vulnerable to geometry–appearance am-
biguity under cross-camera photometric inconsistency. Additionally, they are prone to geometrically
under-constrained reconstruction due to unreliable geometric supervision.
Appearance Harmonization in Neural Scene Representations.
Cross-camera photometric in-
consistency is inevitable in real world and induces geometry–appearance ambiguity. NeRF in the
Wild [11] and WildGaussians [13] model imaging variation with appearance branches or observation-
dependent embeddings. STORM [31] learns coarse global affine transforms for feed-forward re-
construction. PPISP [15] explicitly parameterizes ISP factors such as exposure, vignetting, and
camera response functions. However, these methods mainly rely on global or per-scene optimiza-
tion, making them less suitable for handling local and cross-camera discrepancies. Thus, refined
photometric operation has been employed in recent methods. Bilateral-grid [32] provides spatially
varying photometric transforms. BilaRF [16] uses bilateral-space affine transforms for pixel-wise
color adjustment. CHROMA [18] explores feed-forward bilateral-grid prediction for multi-view
harmonization. However, these methods require substantial overlap between reference and target
views, limiting their applicability to surround-view driving scenes with small inter-camera overlap.
Uncertainty Quantification in Neural Scene Reconstruction.
Uncertainty estimation measures
reconstruction reliability in challenging regions such as occlusion boundaries, weak textures, and
dynamic objects [33, 34, 35]. Fisher information [36] provides a parameter-sensitivity-based un-
certainty measure and has been used for active view selection, pruning, and robust reconstruction.
FisherRF [33] uses expected information gain for active view selection. PUP-3DGS [37] adopts
sensitivity scores for Gaussian pruning. OUGS [38] propagates Gaussian-parameter uncertainty to
pixel space for object-aware view selection. However, uncertainty has primarily been explored in
the context of per-scene optimization, with its role in feed-forward reconstruction remaining limited.
In contrast, we compute geometry uncertainty from the photometric loss within a feed-forward
framework, and leverage it for adaptive geometric self-supervision.

In our framework, given photometrically inconsistent multi-view images, we first construct a canoni-
cal appearance space using depth-guided correspondences, and learn Gaussian primitives within this
normalized domain. To recover the original, camera-specific appearance, a lightweight transformer
predicts multi-scale 3D bilateral grids and applies them to rendered images in a coarse-to-fine manner.

to adaptively weight geometric self-supervision in weakly constrained regions.
3.1
Robust Canonical Appearance Construction
To reduce geometry–appearance ambiguity at the input stage, we construct a canonical appearance
space using depth-guided correspondences. Given a multi-view camera set V, reference cameras
Vref ⊂V are selected according to rig topology and inter-camera overlap. Reference views define
the canonical appearance space with identity transforms, while non-reference views are aligned to
adjacent reference anchors. For example, in the five-camera Waymo benchmark layout shown in
Fig. 2 (a), we use Vref = {Front, SideLeft, SideRight}. Consequently, the two remaining cameras,
FrontLeft and FrontRight are each anchored by two neighboring references.
For each pixel pi in a reference camera view r ∈Vref with a valid depth Dr(pi), we back-project it to
3D coordinates and then reproject it into a new pixel ˜pi in a target camera view t, where t is selected
from the non-reference camera set V \ Vref. For sky regions without reliable depth, we establish
correspondences by intersecting each reference-camera ray with a distant ego-centered sky cylinder.
The resulting 3D intersection point is then projected into the target camera, yielding a sky-region
matched pixel.

Experimental Settings
Datasets and Metrics.
We primarily evaluate on the Waymo Open Dataset [39], which contains
798 training and 202 validation scenes, each lasting about 20 seconds at 10 Hz with 5 vehicle-
mounted cameras. Following UniSplat [1], we evaluate input-view reconstruction using images at

and LPIPS [41]. We also construct an appearance-challenging subset of 57 scenes selected by
input-only inter-camera appearance discrepancy scores from adjacent camera pairs in matched or
overlapping regions. More details and results about the subset are provided in Sec. A.5. We also
assess generalization on nuScenes using the protocol described in Sec. 4.2.

Baseline.
UniSplat [1] is a GFM-based feed-forward Gaussian reconstruction method for dynamic
driving scenes. It constructs a 3D latent scaffold from multi-camera videos, performs spatio-temporal
fusion in the scaffold space, and decodes dynamic-aware Gaussians using both point and voxel-based
branches. We choose UniSplat as our baseline because, to the best of our knowledge, UniSplat is the
state-of-the-art feed-forward method trained and evaluated under the full 5-camera setting.
Implementation Details.
We follow the baseline architecture, including the GFM backbone,
semantic feature extraction, and Gaussian head design. The input and rendering resolution is
350 × 518. Training is performed in two stages as described in Sec. 3.4. The Bilateral Grid
Transformer uses a DINOv2 [42] ViT-Small encoder for appearance token extraction and predicts
multi-scale bilateral grids for appearance recovery. We train the model using AdamW [43] on 16
NVIDIA H20 GPUs with a total batch size of 32. Additional details are provided in Sec. A.4.
4.2
Experimental Results
Image quality on Waymo.
We compare MCSplat with MVSplat [47], DepthSplat [48], Evol-
Splat [44], DriveRecon [46], and UniSplat [1], using the Waymo results reported by UniSplat for
comparison. As shown in Tab. 1, under the front-camera setting, MCSplat improves all reconstruc-
tion metrics over UniSplat (PSNR +2.12 dB and LPIPS −33.3%) as well as all novel-view synthesis
metrics. Although the front-camera input is unchanged (Sec. 3.1), canonicalizing its neighboring
views also benefits reference-view reconstruction. Under the 5-camera setting, MCSplat improves
all reconstruction metrics. Qualitative comparisons in Figs. 4 and 5 illustrate fewer texture-induced
geometric artifacts and improved cross-camera consistency; additional visualizations, including
cross-lane NVS, are provided in Secs. A.6 to A.8.
Image quality on nuScenes.
We additionally evaluate on nuScenes following the protocol used by
UniSplat [1]: the middle frame of each temporal interval is used as input, and the first, middle, and
last frames are evaluation targets. Thus, the nuScenes metrics in Tab. 1 jointly measure input-view
reconstruction and temporal novel-view synthesis. MCSplat achieves 26.19 dB PSNR(+0.82 dB) and
0.783 SSIM(+0.018). This supports generalization beyond the Waymo 5-camera configuration.
Cross-View Geometry and Normal Consistency on Waymo.
Image-quality metrics alone cannot
directly assess Gaussian geometry. We therefore evaluate cross-view depth reprojection errors and

5
Conclusion
We presented MCSplat, a multi-view consistent feed-forward Gaussian splatting framework for
driving-scene reconstruction. MCSplat decouples geometry learning from camera-specific appearance
by constructing a canonical appearance space, recovering raw camera appearance with a multi-scale
Bilateral Grid Transformer, and applying Fisher-information guided geometric self-supervision to
uncertain Gaussians. Experiments on Waymo and nuScenes demonstrate improved reconstruction
quality and cross-dataset generalization, while geometry-oriented evaluations show enhanced cross-
view depth consistency and local surface-normal agreement. Future work will explore stronger
temporal appearance modeling and explicit modeling of ISP parameters.

A.1
Compatibility with GFM-Based Feed-Forward Driving Gaussian Frameworks
Existing GFM-based feed-forward driving Gaussian frameworks[5, 1, 6, 30] usually follow a common
pipeline: multi-view images are first encoded by a pretrained geometry foundation model, then
decoded by a dense prediction or DPT-style Gaussian head to produce image-aligned Gaussian
primitives, which are finally supervised through differentiable rendering and reconstruction losses.
Although different methods may vary in temporal fusion, Gaussian parameterization, or auxiliary
dynamic modules, their core Gaussian prediction process can be abstracted as an image-to-Gaussian
feed-forward mapping followed by rasterization, as shown in Fig. 6(a).
MCSplat is designed to be compatible with this general pipeline. As shown in Fig. 6(b), we keep
the GFM backbone and Gaussian prediction branch unchanged, and introduce three modular additions:
canonical appearance construction provides photometrically consistent inputs and supervision, the
multi-scale Bilateral Grid Transformer maps canonical renderings back to raw camera appearance, and
Fisher-information-based uncertainty reweights geometric self-supervision. Therefore, MCSplat can
enhance existing GFM-based feed-forward Gaussian frameworks by decoupling geometry learning
from camera-specific appearance variation while preserving their original feed-forward reconstruction
structure. The ablation results in Tab. 4 further validate the effectiveness of the proposed modules.

A.4
Implementation Details
We provide additional implementation details omitted from the main text. We follow the baseline
architecture in the use of the GFM backbone, semantic feature extraction, and Gaussian head design.
The input and rendering resolution is fixed to 350 × 518.
Training is performed in two stages. In Stage 1, the GFM backbone is frozen, and only the 3D sparse
convolution modules and the Gaussian head are trained. Different from the baseline, both input and
supervision use the canonicalized images from Sec. 3.1. In Stage 2, all components are frozen except
for the Gaussian MLPs and the Bilateral Grid Transformer. This stage jointly learns bilateral-grid
appearance recovery and applies geometric regularization to the trainable Gaussian outputs.
The Bilateral Grid Transformer adopts a DINOv2 [42] ViT-Small image encoder to extract 384-
dimensional patch tokens for appearance conditioning. Both the Transformer encoder and decoder
contain 3 layers. The multi-scale bilateral grids use three resolutions [2, 2, 1], [4, 4, 2], and [8, 8, 4],
with guidance downsampling factors [4, 4, 2], respectively.
The model is optimized using AdamW [43] on 16 NVIDIA H20 GPUs with a total batch size of
32. For the training objective parameters introduced in Sec. 3.4 and above, we set λ1 = λ5 = 5.0,
λ2 = λ6 = 0.05, λ3 = 0.05, λ4 = 0.1, λ7 = 1.0, λorient = 0.05, and λflat = 0.5.
A.5
Validation Subset for Cross-Camera Photometric Inconsistency
Appearance-Inconsistent Subset Selection.
To identify Waymo validation scenes with large
cross-camera appearance variations, we compute an overlap-based appearance discrepancy score
for each scene. We select 57 scenes using these input-derived, model-independent scores to form a
challenging validation subset. Instead of comparing full images directly, we only evaluate pixels that
have valid geometric correspondences across cameras. The score is computed separately for ground
and sky regions.
For ground regions, we follow the same LiDAR-affine correspondence construction pipeline in
Sec. 3.1. Given a valid LiDAR-depth pixel in a reference camera, we back-project it to 3D and then
project it into the target camera. A match is retained only if it lies inside the target image, passes
valid-mask filtering, and satisfies the LiDAR depth consistency check when target depth is available.
The target RGB value is obtained by bilinear sampling.
For sky regions, where LiDAR depth is unavailable, we use the same proxy sky-point construction as
in Sec. 3.1: each sky pixel is lifted along its camera ray to a fixed sky depth and then projected into
the target camera. A correspondence is kept only when both the reference pixel and the projected
target pixel are classified as sky.

B
Further Discussion
B.1
Social Impact
Social Impact.
MCSplat aims to improve feed-forward 3D reconstruction and novel-view synthesis
for autonomous driving scenes. Its potential positive impact lies in enabling more reliable closed-loop
simulation, safer evaluation of driving policies, and more scalable reconstruction of complex urban
environments. By reducing geometry–appearance ambiguity and improving novel-view geometric
consistency, the method may help build more faithful simulation assets for testing autonomous
systems before real-world deployment.
However, the method should not be viewed as a substitute for real-world safety validation. Re-
construction errors may still occur in rare weather, unusual lighting, long-tail objects, or sensor
configurations outside the training distribution. If such reconstructed scenes are used uncritically
for safety evaluation, they may lead to overconfidence in downstream autonomous driving systems.
In addition, high-fidelity scene reconstruction techniques may raise privacy concerns if applied
to data containing identifiable people, vehicles, or locations. Our experiments are conducted on
established autonomous driving datasets, and the method is intended for research on reconstruction
and simulation rather than surveillance or identification. Responsible deployment should include
dataset governance, privacy-preserving data handling, and careful validation across diverse driving
conditions.
B.2
Limitations
Although MCSplat demonstrates strong cross-camera appearance consistency and camera-specific
appearance recovery, its performance can still be affected by the limited appearance diversity of the
Waymo dataset. In a few scenes, the recovered raw-domain appearance may be less accurate than
expected and exhibit color shifts. A promising direction is to improve appearance coverage through
stronger data augmentation and larger, more diverse training datasets.
