# Drive2Gauss: FeedForward 4D Gaussian Reconstruction from Multimodal Generative Driving Videos 

Tong Zhao 1,2, Zhiyuan Han 2, Lin Chen 3, Bowen Xie 4

Yunxi Qiao 5, Bohan Li 6, Weiqing Xiao 7, Gen Li 8

Shu Han 9, Xuyang Dai 10 , Cheng Bi 10 , Hao Zhao 1, Chaojian Li 11 †

Abstract — Generating reusable driving environments re-quires both controllable future observations and an explicit representation that supports rendering from different view-points, yet existing methods still recover explicit scenes from generated RGB videos through optimization for each sequence. To overcome these limitations, we present Drive2Gauss, afeedforward framework that converts jointly generated RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow into dynamic 4D Gaussian scenes. A Multimodal Video Generator predicts the three modalities from reference multiview images and future control signals. Furthermore, we introduce a Gaussian Query Decoder that uses depth to initialize sparse 3D queries and flow to associate the queries across time, and integrates appearance information through spatiotemporal feature aggregation and iterative query refinement. The reconstruction stage operates in a single feedforward pass without optimization for each scene. Extensive results on nuScenes demonstrate that our approach attains an FID of 6.38 and an FVD of 12.43 for future RGB generation, a PSNR of 29.91 dB when reconstructing its generated observations, and supports both direct rendering under lateral camera shifts and downstream video fixing. Code and more visualizations are available at https://zttt04. github.io/Drive2Gauss_page/ .

I. INTRODUCTION Controllable driving video generation produces future multiview observations conditioned on road layouts, object configurations, ego trajectories, and camera geometry [1]– [10]. Such observations provide a source of synthetic driving data, complementary to the real-world logs in large-scale driving benchmarks [11]–[14], but a video alone does not define an explicit scene that can be rendered repeatedly from different viewpoints. Dynamic Gaussian representations offer this capability by encoding scene appearance, geometry, and motion effectively within a unified representation [15]–[23]. RGB-first pipelines, such as DriveDreamer4D, MagicDrive3D, DreamDrive, and Recon-Dreamer [24]–[27], recover explicit scenes from gener-ated observations through per-scene optimization. Multi-modal generators provide an alternative source of structural information: DiST-4D generates RGB and metric depth, while other models incorporate occupancy and further scene modalities [28]–[32]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, existing gen-erative reconstruction pipelines recover each sequence from generated RGB through per-scene optimization, whereas our approach directly reconstructs jointly generated RGB, depth, and flow with a single feedforward decoder. Feedforward methods have also advanced explicit scene construction. Generalizable models infer geometry or Gaus-sian primitives from sparse views [33]–[35], while DGGT and STORM recover dynamic scenes from driving images and PointForward aggregates multiview temporal features onto sparse 3D queries [36]–[38]. WorldSplat directly con-nects multimodal latent generation with a feedforward de-coder that predicts pixel-aligned Gaussians [39]. These devel-opments motivate a more specific question: how can jointly generated depth and motion guide sparse query construction and temporal feature aggregation for temporally coherent explicit driving scenes? We address this question with Drive2Gauss , a frame-work for feedforward generative driving scene reconstruc-tion. Specifically, its Multimodal Video Generator jointly predicts RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow. The Gaus-sian Query Decoder then assigns these modalities distinct roles: depth initializes 3D anchors, flow associates them across context frames, and RGB features supply appearance information. In this framework, cross-attention aggregates arXiv:2610.26585v1 [cs.CV] 19 Oct 2026 features from multiple views and context frames, and iter-ative query refinement updates the locations where features are sampled. The resulting queries are then decoded into dynamic Gaussian primitives without per-scene optimization. In contrast to WorldSplat [39], our decoder uses generated flow to establish cross-frame correspondences. By drawing on jointly generated RGB, depth, and flow, it also removes the need for the scene graph and external depth estimator used by PointForward [38]. In summary, our main contributions are:  

> •

We present Drive2Gauss, a framework that couples RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow generation with feedforward 4D Gaussian reconstruction.  

> •

We develop a Gaussian Query Decoder that combines depth-based initialization, flow-guided temporal associ-ation, spatiotemporal feature aggregation, and iterative query refinement in a feedforward manner.  

> •

Extensive experiments on nuScenes [12] show that Drive2Gauss achieves state-of-the-art results in future video generation, feedforward 4D Gaussian reconstruc-tion, and downstream view fixing, and the lowest FID under all evaluated lateral camera shifts. II. RELATED WORKS 

A. Driving Video Generation 

Controllable driving generation has progressed from layout-conditioned multiview synthesis to predictive world models with longer horizons, broader scenario coverage, and richer control signals [1]–[10], [40]–[50]. These methods generate future observations or intermediate features for simulation and planning, but their outputs remain view-dependent rather than a persistent scene representation. Beyond RGB, several driving generators add complemen-tary scene signals: DiST-4D predicts metric depth together with RGB [28], occupancy-centric generators synthesize 3D scene structure directly [29], [32], [51], [52], and occupancy can also be estimated from images with Gaussian splatting [53]. Others instead reason jointly over multimodal 4D observations [54], synthesize views along flexible camera poses [55], or unify scene understanding and multimodal generation [30], [31]; comparable modeling appears outside driving as well, in generic video generation [56] and em-bodied manipulation [57]. A further line forecasts future 3D occupancy, either toward planning [58], [59] or as a pretext task for 4D scene understanding [60]. Drive2Gauss uses jointly generated RGB, depth, and flow as complementary inputs for explicit 4D reconstruction instead of producing only view-dependent observations. 

B. Driving Scene Reconstruction 

Neural radiance fields and Gaussian splatting provide continuous or explicit representations for novel-view ren-dering [15], [61]–[63]. Their dynamic extensions model temporal deformation, while driving-specific methods de-compose static backgrounds, moving objects, semantics, and motion fields in complex urban scenes [16]–[23], [64]–[68]; several systems further extend such reconstructions to sim-ulation, extrapolation, and editable assets [69]–[72]. These approaches typically optimize a separate representation for each observed sequence. Generative reconstruction meth-ods similarly convert synthesized observations into explicit scenes through per-scene optimization [24]–[27]. Feedforward reconstruction methods infer geometry or Gaussian representations directly from input observations [33]–[37], [73]–[80]. Generative approaches instead synthe-size structured 3D or dynamic scene representations [81]– [83], and diffusion-based refinement further improves the rendered observations [84]. Temporal correspondence models further provide persistent point tracks or geometry across video frames [85], [86]. Among the closest methods, World-Splat [39] decodes multimodal latents into pixel-aligned Gaussians, while PointForward [38] initializes sparse 3D queries from external depth and associates dynamic queries through scene-graph instance transformations. Drive2Gauss instead obtains both cues from its Multimodal Video Gener-ator: generated depth initializes 3D queries, and generated flow associates corresponding observations across context frames for feature aggregation. III. M ETHOD 

Our goal is to construct a future dynamic 4D Gaussian scene from a given reference frame of multi-camera ob-servations. To this end, Drive2Gauss consists of two key components: a Multimodal Video Generator and a Gaussian Query Decoder. Given the reference observations and control signals, the generator jointly predicts future RGB, depth, and dynamic flow, providing complementary appearance, geometric, and motion cues for reconstruction. The decoder then uses these multimodal observations to initialize queries, aggregate spatiotemporal features, and decode them into dynamic Gaussian primitives in a single feedforward pass. The generator operates in a compressed spatiotemporal latent space to support joint multimodal generation across multiple camera views [87]. A pretrained 3D VAE [88] separately encodes RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow into 16-channel latent features for each individual modality. The resulting features are concatenated along the chan-nel dimension to form a unified 48-channel representation, which is processed by an STDiT denoising backbone with a ControlNet-style control pathway [89], [90]. Reference observations and map features are injected into the control pathway, while 3D bounding boxes, ego trajectories, and camera poses serve as conditioning inputs for both the main and control branches, following the same conditioning design of [7]. 

Geometric and Motion Supervision. For each training clip, we construct metric depth and dynamic flow as complemen-tary geometric and motion cues for 4D Gaussian reconstruc-tion. The constructed signals serve as supervision targets for a multimodal video generation model, which learns to predict the corresponding modalities for future video frames. 

Metric depth. We follow the standard metric depth curation pipeline used in [28], which combines stereo depth from multiple views [91] and temporally aggregated LiDAR data, with dynamic objects and sky regions removed beforehand using semantic segmentation [92]. We then apply generative depth completion to obtain dense metric depth maps for all training frames [93]. 

Dynamic flow. We first estimate dense optical flow be-tween adjacent RGB frames using SEA-RAFT [94]. Since the raw optical flow also contains motion caused by the ego vehicle and camera movement, we construct a dynamic mask from the annotated 3D bounding boxes. Objects whose annotated velocity exceeds a predefined threshold are treated as dynamic. Their 3D boxes are projected onto the image plane and spatially dilated, and SAM-based instance masks [95] are used to refine the object boundaries. The dynamic flow is then obtained by masking the optical flow:

Given the initialized and temporally associated queries, we aggregate visual ev-idence from multiple camera views and context frames to refine both query features and spatial locations (Fig. 3). The sampled features are then aggregated through cross attention [97], where the query representation serves as the Query and the sampled multiview temporal features serve as the corresponding Keys and Values. Gaussian Decoding and Rendering. After spatiotemporal feature aggregation, each refined query contains an updated feature representation and a corresponding 3D position. We decode each query into a dynamic Gaussian primitive by predicting its spatial offset ∆μi, scale si, rotation ρi, opacity 

oi, appearance feature ai, and temporal lifespan ℓi. The Gaussian center at timestamp t is defined as: 

μi(t) = xi(t) + ∆ μi, (10) where xi(t) is the temporal anchor of query i at timestamp

The lifespan parameter ℓi controls the temporal visibility of each Gaussian, allowing static queries to remain stable while dynamic queries follow their estimated motion. For rendering at timestamp t′ and camera view c, the Gaussian primitives are projected using the corresponding camera geometry and rasterized with a differentiable Gaus-sian renderer [15]. Following SplatAD and UniSim [21], [98], each Gaussian stores a high-dimensional appearance feature representation rather than directly predicting the RGB color. The rendered feature map is finally decoded by a lightweight appearance UNet to produce the final RGB image outputs. 

Training Loss. The Gaussian Query Decoder is supervised directly in image space: the predicted Gaussians are raster-ized, the appearance UNet converts the rendered features to RGB, and these images are compared with the target RGB observations. The decoder objective is defined as follows: 

L = Lrgb + λpLLPIPS + λoLopacity + λsLscale , (11) where Lrgb is the Charbonnier loss for pixel reconstruction and LLPIPS measures perceptual similarity [99]. The terms Lopacity and Lscale regularize Gaussian opacity and scale, with weights λo and λs; λp weights the perceptual term. IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

Dataset. We train and evaluate Drive2Gauss on the nuScenes benchmark [12], which contains 1,000 urban driv-ing scenes with six surround-view camera views. Following [100], we upsample the scene annotations, including 3D bounding boxes and road map information, from 2 Hz to 12 Hz to align them with the corresponding video generation setting. We follow the official split, using 700 scenes for training and 150 scenes for comprehensive validation. 

Metrics. We use PSNR, SSIM [101], LPIPS [99], FID [102], and FVD [103] to comprehensively evaluate both the generated and rendered results. Higher PSNR and SSIM values are better, whereas lower LPIPS, FID, and FVD are preferred. Unless otherwise noted, the best and second best results in the comparison tables are clearly marked with 

best and second , respectively. 

Implementation Details. Both components are trained on eight NVIDIA H20 GPUs in total. For multimodal genera-tion, we initialize the generator from the pretrained DiST-T checkpoint [28] and train progressively at 224 × 400 

and 424 × 800 resolutions. The training targets comprise RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow, with the latter two constructed as described above in Sec. III-A. For recon-struction, each decoder training sample contains four frames from three camera views at 424 × 800 . We apply k = 2 

refinement iterations and optimize the decoder for four days using AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −3 and the corresponding image reconstruction objective in Sec. III-B, using loss weights λp = 0 .2, λo = 0 .001 , and λs = 0 .02 .

B. Future Video Generation 

We evaluate future video generation along the original camera trajectories of nuScenes. Each sample consists of 6 camera views and 17 consecutive video frames at 424 × 800 

image resolution and a rate of 12 Hz. Given the six RGB images from the first frame and the corresponding future control signals, Drive2Gauss is able to generate future RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow jointly in a single pass. 

Quantitative Comparison. We evaluate RGB generation quality using the FID and FVD metrics, as reported in Table I. Drive2Gauss obtains the lowest FID and FVD among the listed methods and is the only one that jointly generates RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow in a unified model. Relative to DiST-T, which generates RGB and depth without dynamic flow, FID decreases from 6.83 to 6.38 and FVD more than halves from 22.67 to 12.43, indicating stronger temporal consistency. The complete generator therefore re-tains high RGB quality while supplying the geometric and motion cues that are required by 4D Gaussian reconstruction.

E. Application to View Fixing 

Quantitative Comparison. We further evaluate whether the reconstructed 4D Gaussian scenes can be reused by a downstream view-fixing method. We apply SPVC [104] to Drive2Gauss and introduce an RGB-D projection baseline that directly lifts the generated RGB and depth into Gaussian primitives before applying the same SPVC module. Drive2Gauss + SPVC obtains the lowest FID and FVD in Table IV. Relative to RGB-D + SPVC, it improves both metrics at every shift, supporting the use of learned Gaussian reconstruction as input to video fixing. These scores charac-terize the combined reconstruction and SPVC pipeline; direct Gaussian rendering is evaluated separately in Table III. 

Qualitative Comparison. The effect of applying SPVC after a lateral camera shift is illustrated in Fig. 7. The shifted rendering distorts the road markings and nearby vehicle, while view fixing restores local road structure and foreground alignment. This supports using the reconstructed scene as an intermediate representation for downstream correction.

All variants share the same generated observations, query budget, four-frame three-view context, and training schedule; only the component under study is modified. For w/o Init Flow , we set the flow-derived temporal displacement to zero during query initialization, retaining the geometric anchors provided by source-frame metric depth. For w/o Cross-Attention , we replace cross-attention with an MLP-based weighted fusion module that predicts observation-wise weights for feature aggregation. Quality improves with the query budget, but the PSNR gain shrinks as the budget grows while SSIM continues to im-prove, so additional queries mainly refine structural fidelity at the upper end of the studied query range considered here. Training memory grows roughly linearly with the query budget in our setting and the largest configuration exceeds the memory available on a single H20 GPU, whereas inference time grows only sublinearly. CONCLUSION In this paper, we present Drive2Gauss, a framework for feedforward generative driving scene reconstruction. Its Multimodal Video Generator jointly predicts RGB, metric depth, and dynamic flow. These complementary predictions guide the Gaussian Query Decoder to construct reusable 4D Gaussian scenes through depth-based initialization and flow-guided aggregation, without per-scene optimization. Experi-ments on nuScenes demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in video generation, 4D reconstruction, and view fixing, with the lowest FID across all evaluated lateral camera shifts.
