PnP-Transformer: A Neural Pose Solver for
Zero-Shot 6D Object Pose Estimation
Emirkan Burak Yilmaz and Yakup Genc
Computer Engineering Dept., Gebze Technical University, Kocaeli, Türkiye
{emirkan.yilmaz, yakup.genc}@gtu.edu.tr
Abstract. We present PnP-Transformer, a neural pose solver designed
as an iterative-free, differentiable alternative to classical PnP-RANSAC
algorithms. PnP-Transformer regresses the 6D object pose by processing
predicted semi-dense 2D–3D correspondence fields alongside confidence
scores, while mapping geometric inputs into a continuous latent space.
To evaluate its practical utility, we integrate PnP-Transformer into a
zero-shot 6D pose estimation pipeline, where the correspondence mod-
ule and pose solver are trained separately. PnP-Transformer achieves
a 41.2% mean AR score with low latency (0.46 s/obj) on seven core
BOP benchmark datasets, surpassing all prior baselines (such as Ray-
Pose with 39.1% AR) but one (Co-op with 58.4% AR). When the pose
solver is replaced with EPnP-RANSAC (similar to Co-op) while keep-
ing the correspondence module unchanged, the RANSAC alternative
outperforms PnP-Transformer on six of the seven datasets by reaching
52.2% mean AR at a cost of an additional 60 ms per object, revealing
an accuracy-latency trade-off. Code and pretrained models are available:
https://github.com/ebylmz/pnp-transformer

Introduction
Estimating the 6-Degree-of-Freedom (6DoF) pose of objects from monocular im-
ages is a fundamental problem in spatial AI and computer vision. In robotics,
accurate prediction of the 6D pose allows embodied agents to grasp and manipu-
late objects precisely, facilitating automated warehouse operations and complex
assembly tasks. In augmented reality, establishing precise spatial alignment be-
tween physical objects and virtual overlays is essential for seamless, immersive
user experiences.
Traditionally, pose estimation pipelines have relied on a two-stage decoupled
architecture: first, predicting intermediate geometric representations such as 3D
scene coordinates; second, solving for the rigid transformation using a variant
of PnP [11] algorithm wrapped inside the RANSAC [10] solver. Although those
solvers provide robust geometric model fitting, their non-differentiable nature
does not allow the gradients to flow backward from the final 3D pose loss to

the feature extraction. Consequently, neural networks in these frameworks are
forced to rely on surrogate coordinate-regression or reprojection losses during
training, rather than optimizing the primary rotation and translation metrics.
However, optimizing intermediate representations independently of the down-
stream 3D spatial alignment task leads to sub-optimal performance, as small
errors in predicted 2D keypoints can result in completely different poses dur-
ing geometric fitting. To bridge this gap, recent works have proposed differ-
entiable geometric solvers that integrate pose optimization directly into end-
to-end training computation graphs. Prominent paradigms include probabilistic
hypothesis selection [2], implicit differentiation via the Implicit Function The-
orem [5], continuous probability density modeling on SE(3) [6], and linearized
covariance supervision [19]. Although these methods restore gradient flow, they
introduce substantial computational and numerical challenges. Optimization-
based differentiable layers depend heavily on iterative solver loops (e.g., Gauss-
Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt), matrix inversions, or expensive Monte Carlo
sampling procedures. These operations introduce significant memory overhead,
slow down training convergence, and remain prone to gradient divergence under
ill-conditioned Hessians caused by noisy, degenerate, or ambiguous point dis-
tributions. Alternatively, direct regression architectures [31, 33] bypass explicit
perspective solvers entirely by mapping learned dense representations directly
to rotation and translation. Although these feed-forward networks achieve im-
pressive speed, they discard explicit perspective projection constraints. Con-
sequently, unconstrained regression layers often exhibit poor outlier resilience
under severe occlusions, spatial domain shifts, and complex object geometries.
To overcome this divide, we introduce PnP-Transformer, a transformer-based
geometric solver that maps 2D–3D correspondence fields directly to 6D object
poses. Rather than treating PnP as an unlearnable, external post-processing step
or discarding explicit geometric constraints entirely, PnP-Transformer embeds
spatial relationships into self-attention blocks while learning a structured latent
space. To demonstrate its practical utility, we integrate PnP-Transformer into a
zero-shot 6D pose estimation pipeline for unseen objects, operating on a single
RGB scene image and a target 3D CAD model. Given a scene image, our pipeline
extracts semi-dense 2D–3D correspondence fields and confidence scores between
the query image and a sparse set of rendered template views. The correspon-
dence field from the highest-confidence template is passed to PnP-Transformer
to regress the relative transformation between the query and template frames,
which is composed with the reference template pose to recover the final 6D object
pose. When evaluated across seven core BOP benchmark datasets, our pipeline
operating with PnP-Transformer demonstrates competitive performance at low
latency. Furthermore, substituting EPnP-RANSAC for our neural solver, while
keeping the correspondence module fixed, yields higher overall accuracy. In sum-
mary, our main contributions are as follows:
1. We introduce PnP-Transformer, a neural pose solver designed as a differen-
tiable, iterative-free alternative to classical PnP-RANSAC algorithms.

Related Work
Differentiable Geometric Solvers. Geometry-driven pose estimation meth-
ods establish 2D–3D correspondences from RGB images and recover the object
pose using a PnP algorithm [11] combined with RANSAC [10]. However, non-
differentiable operations in standard solvers (such as discrete inlier counting
and hard arg max selection) prevent backpropagation during end-to-end train-
ing. To enable gradient flow, recent works integrate geometric optimization into
neural computational graphs. Differentiable RANSAC (DSAC) [2] replaces hard
hypothesis selection with a continuous probabilistic expectation over sampled
subsets, enabling scene coordinate regression driven by pose loss. To avoid sam-
pling loops, BPnP [5] formulates PnP as a declarative layer, leveraging the
Implicit Function Theorem (IFT) on the stationary optimality conditions of
the non-linear least-squares objective. BlindPnP [3] incorporates Sinkhorn op-
timal transport layers to estimate camera pose without requiring explicit prior
matches. To address gradient instabilities caused by multimodal ambiguities on
symmetric objects, EPro-PnP [6] models PnP output as a continuous probabil-
ity density over SE(3), optimizing Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence via Monte
Carlo sampling. Meanwhile, the Linear-Covariance Loss [19] linearizes the PnP
solver around ground-truth poses to supervise pose distribution covariance di-
rectly, eliminating gradient confusion caused by PnP averaging.
Direct Pose Regressors. An alternative paradigm bypasses geometric solvers
entirely by using deep architectures to directly map visual features to 6D pose.
To avoid topological discontinuities during direct rotation regression (such as
gimbal lock or double-cover ambiguities in quaternions), modern networks adopt
continuous 6D rotation parameterizations [35], which stabilize gradient updates
during training. Direct regressors like GDR-Net [31] combine dense 2D–3D coor-
dinate maps with surface region maps through patch-based convolutional mod-
ules (Patch-PnP) to predict rotation matrices and translation vectors. Foun-
dationPose [33] proposed a transformer-based iterative refinement network and

contrastive pose scoring modules, enabling zero-shot generalization across novel
objects.
Unseen Object Pose Estimation. Model-based approaches for novel object
pose estimation [4, 15, 17, 22, 23, 25, 28] can be broadly categorized into feature
matching and template matching [20]. Feature-matching methods extract local
descriptors from the input image, match them to features of the 3D model to
establish 2D–3D correspondences, and recover the pose using the PnP [18] algo-
rithm. Notable examples include MatchU [15], which combines 2D texture and
3D geometric cues using rotation-invariant descriptors and an attention-based
fusion mechanism. Template-matching methods [17,22,23,25,28] instead render
synthetic views of the 3D model from multiple poses and estimate the pose by
retrieving the closest matching template and aligning it with the observed im-
age. MegaPose [17] exemplifies this strategy by generating millions of synthetic
images to train a deep network for large-scale pose retrieval. GigaPose [25] im-
proves efficiency by reducing the number of templates considered by focusing
only on the out-of-plane rotations and learning rotation-invariant features.
Vision Foundation Models. Recent advances in self-supervised vision foun-
dation models provide strong visual representations for unseen object pose esti-
mation. Models such as DINO [27,29] and CroCo [32] produce stable, transfer-
able patch descriptors that generalize across categories, viewpoints, and imaging
conditions. FoundPose [28] demonstrated that patch descriptors extracted from
different DINOv2 layers encode varying levels of positional and semantic cues,
enabling effective correspondence-based pose estimation. Similarly, Co-op [23]
leverages CroCov2 to extract patch-level features and compute semi-dense cor-
respondences between the input image and pre-rendered templates. Together,
these developments highlight the strength of foundation-model features as pow-
erful appearance priors.

3.3
Estimating 2D–3D Correspondences
Our online pipeline begins by establishing semi-dense patch-level 2D–3D corre-
spondences between the query image and the onboarded object templates. As
illustrated in Fig. 1, our matching module features a shared ViT encoder [7]
for feature extraction, paired with a transformer decoder [30] utilizing cross-
attention blocks to blend and interpret the query and template representations.
We use an off-the-shelf object detector CNOS [24] to predict the bounding box of
the target object within the scene image. The region of interest is then cropped
and resized to a standard resolution of H × W to form the query image IQ.
The query image is then processed by the ViT encoder to extract the patch-level
feature map FQ ∈RN×D, where N =

class (as index N) to model background/occlusion in the query image.
To establish 2D–3D correspondences, each query patch center is first assigned
to its most likely template patch counterpart via a discrete argmax selection over
the row-wise match probabilities in M. To achieve sub-patch precision, this dis-
crete assignment is continuously refined by adding the predicted coordinate offset
O to the selected template patch center. The refined 2D template coordinates
are subsequently backprojected into the template camera frame using the pre-
rendered template depth map, yielding the absolute 3D coordinate for each query
patch center. We define a confidence value ci ∈[0, 1] for each correspondence as:

3.4
Pose Recovery via PnP-Transformer
Adopting the formulation of the Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem [11], we
propose a joint-embedding architecture, termed PnP-Transformer, to regress
the relative rigid transformation ∆= (R, t) ∈SE(3) from 2D–3D correspon-
dence fields while learning a structured latent space. As illustrated in Fig. 1(b),
the architecture comprises three core modules: (1) PnP-Encoder that maps
predicted correspondences to a latent vector z ∈R256, (2) Pose-Decoder that

Pose-Decoder. The Pose-Decoder module regresses the relative template-to-
query transformation (R, t) from the latent vector z. It is parameterized as a
lightweight MLP comprising two shared layers followed by independent predic-
tion heads for rotation and translation. The rotation head predicts an uncon-
strained continuous 6D representation [a1 | a2] ∈R3×2 [35], which is converted
to a valid rotation matrix R ∈SO(3) via Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization. The
translation head directly predicts the 3D translation vector t ∈R3 in camera
space.
Pose-Encoder. The Pose-Encoder module acts as an auxiliary regulariza-
tion module during training to structure the latent embedding space. It takes
the ground-truth transformation ( ¯R,¯t) as input in 9D representation [35], and
projects it through an MLP to output the target latent embedding ¯z ∈R256.
By enforcing an alignment between the predicted embedding z by PnP-Encoder
and the target embedding ¯z during training, the model learns a topologically
consistent representation of the SE(3) manifold.

Training
MegaPose Dataset. We trained our models on the MegaPose-GSO dataset
[17], which provides photorealistic multi-object scene renderings of assets with
object pose annotations, depth maps, and object masks. To construct our train-
ing query-template pairs, we randomly sample up to 4 objects per scene, en-
forcing a visibility threshold where at least 10% of the object’s surface must be

visible, similar to the protocol of the BOP datasets [26]. Each selected object
instance is cropped based on its region-of-interest bounding box and resized to a
standard input resolution of 224 × 224 pixels to form the query image, which is
paired with its geometrically closest template image with the same image resolu-
tion based on out-of-plane rotation. Ground-truth 2D–3D patch correspondences
are constructed by unprojecting the query patch centers into the query camera
frame using the ground-truth depth. These 3D points are then mapped to the
template camera frame via ground-truth relative transformation, and projected
onto the template image plane to identify the nearest template patch index and
calculate the sub-patch offset. We apply a 5% relative depth threshold during
the projection step. Query points that are projected outside the template mask
or exceed the depth error tolerance are assigned to the no-match class, and their
corresponding ground-truth offset vectors are set to zero.
Training Schedules. We optimize the correspondence decoder and the PnP-
Transformer modules separately. Although PnP-Transformer itself is differen-
tiable, the discrete argmax operation used for patch selection during correspon-
dence estimation breaks the gradient flow back into the matching network, pre-
venting joint end-to-end training. Consequently, both modules are trained sepa-
rately using distinct objective functions: the correspondence decoder is optimized
via match loss, whereas PnP-Transformer is optimized using pose loss.
Match loss. To optimize the correspondence decoder and its heads, we mini-
mize a joint objective combining a classification term for patch matching and a
regression term for continuous sub-patch displacements [23]. The patch classi-
fication loss Lcls is formulated as a standard cross-entropy loss over the match
matrix M against the generated ground-truth patch indices. For the displace-
ment vectors, we minimize the ℓ1 distance between the predicted offsets O and
the ground-truth offsets, denoted as Loff. The total match loss is defined as:

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Implementation Details. Our correspondence estimation network builds upon
CroCo [32], a vision foundation model pretrained specifically for 3D vision tasks.
We initialize our shared ViT encoder and transformer decoder with the pre-
trained weights of CroCoV2 to leverage its large-scale pretraining. During train-
ing, the ViT encoder is kept frozen, and we optimize only the weights of the
transformer decoder and its prediction heads, which are initialized from scratch.
Specifically, we employ the ViT-Large variant (24 layers, feature dimension
D = 1024) for the encoder and a ViT-Base architecture (12 layers, feature dimen-
sion 768) for the decoder. Each decoder prediction head is implemented as a sin-
gle linear layer. For PnP-Transformer, PnP-Encoder incorporates 4 self-attention
layers with a feature dimension of 1024, compressing the correspondence field
into a 256-dimensional latent space. Both Pose-Encoder and Pose-Decoder are
parameterized as 2-layer MLPs with a hidden dimension of 512.
All modules are trained using the AdamW optimizer on a single NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, with a cosine learning rate schedule decaying from a
peak of 2 × 10−5 down to 1 × 10−6. The correspondence decoder and prediction
heads are trained with a batch size of 32 for 500k steps (∼3 days) using the
matching loss with offset weight λoff = 2.0. PnP-Transformer is optimized in a
two-stage regime: it is first pretrained on ground-truth 2D–3D correspondences
with a batch size of 128 for 50k steps (∼5 hours), followed by fine-tuning on noisy
predicted correspondences from the decoder for 250k steps (∼3 days) using pose
loss with weights λtrans = 10.0 and λlat = 1.0.
Evaluation Metrics. Following the BOP Challenge protocol [26], we report Av-
erage Recall (AR) as the primary evaluation metric. AR is computed from three
complementary pose-error metrics: Visible Surface Discrepancy (VSD), Maxi-
mum Symmetry-Aware Surface Distance (MSSD), and Maximum Symmetry-
Aware Projection Distance (MSPD). For each metric, recall is averaged over
multiple predefined error thresholds, and the final AR is obtained by averaging
the three resulting recall values.
Datasets. We evaluate the proposed method on the seven core BOP chal-
lenge datasets [26]: LM-O [1], T-LESS [12], TUD-L [13], IC-BIN [8], ITODD [9],
HB [16], and YCBV [34]. Together, these datasets cover a broad range of ob-
ject categories and challenging scenarios, including severe occlusion, cluttered
scenes, and texture-less or symmetric objects. We use default CNOS [24] object
detections provided by the BOP [26].

LM-O (45.6% vs. 53.3%) and YCB-V (54.4% vs. 61.4%), this performance gap
narrows considerably. A detailed architectural analysis comparing the two solver
paradigms is provided in Sec. 4.5.

4.5
PnP-Transformer vs. Classical PnP-RANSAC
As reported in Tab. 1, replacing our neural pose solver with EPnP-RANSAC [18]
yields a higher AR score across the BOP benchmark datasets [26]. This perfor-
mance gap stems from the fundamental algorithmic differences between itera-
tive consensus-based sampling and single-pass global attention aggregation, com-
bined with the decoupled nature of our training pipeline. The correspondence
module is trained independently using local matching losses without pose-level
supervision, generating correspondence fields without explicit geometric consis-
tency. Given a sufficient ratio of valid correspondences, RANSAC reliably isolates
consistent geometric subsets by explicitly partitioning the input field into inliers
and outliers based on reprojection error. In contrast, PnP-Transformer processes
the entire correspondence set simultaneously in a single forward pass, relying on
self-attention mechanisms to summarize geometry into a global latent represen-
tation via a class token. While self-attention captures global context effectively,
it can remain sensitive to high outlier ratios, which are expectedly higher in
textureless and symmetric scenarios, as demonstrated by the performance gaps
observed on T-LESS [12] and IC-BIN [8] datasets (Table 1). Because our current
correspondence module utilizes a non-differentiable hard assignment operation
(argmax), end-to-end joint optimization could not be applied in this work, leav-
ing the task of resolving geometrically inconsistent correspondences entirely to
PnP-Transformer.
Despite the raw accuracy advantage of iterative RANSAC, PnP-Transformer
introduces several structural benefits for deep learning pipelines. Unlike stochas-
tic consensus algorithms, PnP-Transformer does not contain discrete sampling
operations, making it differentiable. Although the correspondence module and

to feature backbones, opening the door for end-to-end setups where the model

dense, local point fields. Furthermore, while CPU-bound RANSAC implementa-
tions exhibit non-deterministic execution times that scale with noise and outlier
ratios, PnP-Transformer runs natively on the GPU with constant forward-pass
latency, ensuring predictable throughput for real-time applications. Finally, be-
yond direct pose regression, the model maps arbitrary correspondence fields into
a continuous, topologically structured latent space, enabling downstream repre-
sentation tasks, fast pose indexing, and manifold analysis that are impossible
with classical geometric fitting routines.
5
Conclusion
We introduced PnP-Transformer, a differentiable neural pose solver that re-
gresses 6D object pose directly from 2D–3D correspondence fields in a single
forward pass. By utilizing a joint-embedding architecture, our model structures
a continuous latent space that captures the underlying topological geometry
of rigid transformations. When integrated into a zero-shot 6D pose estimation
pipeline, our framework achieves a 41.2% mean AR score across seven core BOP
benchmark datasets at an inference latency of 0.46 s/obj, outperforming prior
baselines such as RayPose (39.1%), FoundPose (37.3%), and GigaPose (27.6%),
while placing behind only Co-op (58.4%). To evaluate the standalone perfor-
mance of our learned pose solver, we conducted a controlled comparison by
replacing PnP-Transformer with an EPnP-RANSAC solver while holding the
correspondence module fixed. The EPnP-RANSAC variant achieves a 52.2%
mean AR score at the cost of an additional 60 ms per object (0.52 s/obj), rank-
ing second overall among zero-shot methods and setting a new state-of-the-art on
IC-BIN (61.3%). This comparison reveals a direct accuracy-for-latency trade-off
between iterative stochastic sampling and constant-time attention aggregation.
Future work includes integrating our differentiable solver into an end-to-end
framework to enable joint optimization of correspondence estimation and pose
regression. Furthermore, we plan to leverage the learned latent pose representa-
tions within Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) systems, exploring
their utility in Extended Kalman Filtering (EKF) and fast topological pose in-
dexing.
