for improving visual grounding in VLA manipulation.
I. INTRODUCTION
VLA models have become a promising paradigm for
language-conditioned robotic manipulation, mapping visual
observations, language instructions, and proprioception to
continuous actions [1], [2], [3]. Despite large-scale pretrain-
ing, a basic difficulty remains: knowing where to look. Many
manipulation failures arise not from misunderstanding the
language instruction alone, but from failing to attend to
small, transient, and ambiguous visual regions such as the
object, grasp region, contact point, handle, button, or target
location. Standard VLA policies must infer these regions
indirectly from action supervision.
Human gaze provides a natural signal for this missing
visual grounding. During manipulation, demonstrators often
look at task-relevant objects and target regions before or
while acting. Gaze can therefore provide a compact trace
of the visual evidence used to support action decisions.
However, the primary obstacle is not the idea of using
gaze, but the lack of robot learning data in which gaze is
aligned with the observations consumed by modern policies.
Large robot manipulation datasets typically contain images,
proprioception, and actions, but no human gaze. Conversely,

existing gaze-equipped manipulation datasets usually record
gaze in a single egocentric, head-mounted, or displayed view,
which does not directly provide supervision over the multi-
view robot observations used by current VLA policies.
We introduce GazeAct, a gaze-annotated manipulation
dataset and acquisition pipeline designed to close this align-
ment gap. Our key idea is to record gaze directly on the
robot camera stream used by the teleoperator, so each gaze
point is obtained in robot-image coordinates and tied to the
visual evidence used for action selection. We implement
this with a screen-based ALOHA teleoperation system in
which a Tobii Pro Spectrum eye tracker records gaze on
the active robot camera view while the robot logs multi-
view images, proprioception, and actions. Hardware triggers
and synchronized clocks align gaze and robot streams in
time, while active-view logging records which camera the
operator observed at each moment. Because only one view
is displayed at a time but VLA policies consume multi-view
observations, we transfer gaze to the remaining synchronized
camera views, producing spatially consistent gaze targets for
all visual inputs used by the policy.
The resulting dataset contains 12,000 demonstrations
across 40 manipulation tasks in five domains, with syn-
chronized gaze, multi-view RGB observations, propriocep-
tion, and actions. To turn this gaze into a learning signal,
we convert synchronized and cross-view-aligned gaze into
patch-level distributions over the policy’s visual tokens.
We then use these gaze-derived distributions as targets for
attention regularization, encouraging the policy’s language-
conditioned visual attention to align with human-fixated
regions during training., without changing the policy archi-
tecture or inference-time inputs.
Real human gaze therefore acts as an auxiliary supervision
signal: it guides visual grounding during training, but is
removed at deployment. We evaluate this supervision signal
through expanded real-world rollouts, robustness trials, gaze-
source and regularization-strength ablations, transfer across
VLA backbones, and simulation transfer. Across these set-
tings, real robot-view gaze improves policy learning most
strongly when manipulation depends on grounding small
objects, contact points, or target regions.
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce Gaze-
Act, a gaze-annotated manipulation dataset in which human
gaze is grounded in the robot’s own observations and made
available across synchronized camera views. Second, we de-
velop a screen-based, hardware-synchronized data-collection
and cross-view alignment pipeline for pairing high-frequency
human gaze with multi-view robot demonstrations. Third, we

II. RELATED WORK
Manipulation Datasets with and without Gaze. Large-
scale teleoperation datasets have driven the recent generation
of VLA models. RT-1 [4], BridgeData V2 [5] and DROID [6]
provide hundreds of thousands of demonstrations across
diverse robots and scenes, but record only what the operator
did, not where they looked. A smaller body of work adds
human gaze. HARMONIC [7] captures gaze with head-
mounted glasses in a single assistive-eating task, but projects
it onto the operator’s egocentric view rather than the robot’s
cameras. DAA [8] and GazeBot [9] record real gaze on
the robot’s stereo feed through an HMD (Head-mounted
display) at large scale, but the HMD shows a single stereo
composite, so gaze covers only one view and trajectories last
a few seconds, capturing brief reaches rather than complete
manipulations. In contrast, GazeAct records screen-based
gaze directly on the robot camera feed used for teleoperation,
synchronizes it with robot observations and actions, and
transfers it across camera views so that gaze can supervise
the multi-view inputs used by VLA policies.
Gaze as a Learning Signal. Human gaze has been used
in robot learning and visuomotor learning in several ways.
One line of work uses gaze as an offline cue for segmenting
demonstrations; for example, Takizawa et al. [10] use fix-
ation transitions to decompose long-horizon demonstrations
into sub-tasks. A second line modifies the perceptual input
pipeline, using gaze or predicted gaze to crop, zoom, or
foveate observations [11], [12], [13]. These approaches can
improve perception, but often change the input interface or
require gaze-like predictions at deployment. A third line,
closer to our work, uses gaze only during training to guide
internal attention. Prior work has shown that aligning model
saliency or attention with human gaze can improve imitation
learning and visual recognition while keeping inference gaze-
free [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]. In
vision-language models, Voila-A [23] uses gaze heatmaps to
modulate attention, while gaze-regularized models [24] align
attention with ground-truth gaze during training. GazeAct
brings this training-time gaze supervision paradigm to real
teleoperated VLA manipulation, using synchronized robot-
view gaze, cross-view alignment, and gaze-free deployment.
Vision-Language-Action Models and Attention. Our
method builds on transformer-based VLA policies, includ-
ing π0.5 [2], RT-1 [4], OpenVLA [3], RoboMamba [25],
Octo [26], and SAM2Act [27]. These models map vi-
sual observations and language instructions to robot ac-
tions through internal vision-language representations, often
including attention over visual tokens. GazeAct uses this
attention interface as the point of supervision: real human
gaze is converted into patch-level gaze distributions and used
to regularize language-conditioned visual attention during
training. Because the regularizer acts on the policy’s internal
attention rather than changing the input stream or action
head, it can be applied to different VLA backbones while
preserving the standard inference interface.

GazeAct provides a framework for converting operator
gaze, recorded on the robot camera feed during teleoperation,
into training-time supervision for multi-view VLA policy
learning. The framework has three components. First, we
collect a gaze-equipped teleoperation dataset in which human
gaze is recorded directly on the robot camera and synchro-
nized with images, proprioception, and actions (Section III-

Synchronization. Gaze, camera, proprioception, and ac-
tion streams are recorded by separate devices and at different
rates, so GazeAct uses explicit synchronization to align
them within each demonstration. Hardware TTL (Transistor-
Transistor Logic) triggers define shared trial boundaries: a
start trigger begins both gaze recording and robot-side log-
ging, and a matching stop trigger closes the same trial across
systems. Within these boundaries, chrony/NTP-synchronized
clocks support post hoc alignment of each 300Hz gaze
sample to the nearest robot timestep and camera frame. We
retain the full gaze stream, allowing brief fixations and rapid
gaze shifts around grasping, contact, and view switching to
contribute to the gaze heatmaps used for supervision. Fig-
ure 2 shows the data collection equipment and synchronized
recording setup.
Cross-View Gaze Alignment. Gaze is recorded on the
camera view displayed to the operator, but the VLA policy
receives multi-view observations. To use gaze as supervision
for all visual inputs, GazeAct transfers each active-view gaze
point to the remaining synchronized camera views. This
extends real human gaze beyond a single displayed view
and makes it usable as multi-view privileged supervision.

B. Real-World Evaluation
We evaluate whether attention regularization with real tele-
operation gaze improves VLA policy learning in a physical
robot setting. All policies are trained for 40k steps under
the matched conditions, using the same task demonstrations
and architecture; the only difference is whether gaze-derived
targets are used for attention regularization during training.
At inference time, both policies receive the same inputs:
images, language, and proprioception.
For each task and method, we run 100 real-world rollouts,
split evenly between 50 standard trials and 50 robustness
trials. Standard trials use the common task configuration
from data collection. Robustness trials preserve the same
language instruction and success criterion, but introduce
controlled spatial or visual changes. For example, in the
cup-placement task, the cup and plate may be moved to
new locations, nearby objects may change appearance, or
unseen distractors may be added. This protocol tests whether
gaze supervision improves not only execution under familiar
conditions, but also visual grounding when the policy must
identify task-relevant objects under scene variation.
Table II reports standard, robustness, and overall success
across all 100 rollouts, with Wilson confidence intervals
for the overall success rate. Attention regularization with
gaze-derived targets improves average standard success from
55.8% to 66.8%, robustness success from 40.3% to 50.3%,
and overall success from 48.1% to 58.6%, corresponding to a
10.5 percentage-point overall gain. The attention-regularized
policy improves over the baseline on every task. These results
provide real-robot evidence that teleoperation gaze can serve
as an effective training-time supervision signal for improving
task-relevant visual grounding in VLA manipulation policies.

F. Quantitative Analysis of Gaze Transfer
We performed a manual ground-truth evaluation of cross-
view gaze transfer on 300 synchronized frames. An anno-
tator viewed the source-camera image with the recorded
gaze location and marked the corresponding location in the
synchronized target view. Since gaze supervision is applied
at the patch level, we evaluate the distance to the manually
annotated ground truth in visual patches. Geometry alone
produced a mean error of 2.57 patches, while our geometry-
guided hybrid reduced this to just 1.36 patches, showing that
the transferred gaze remains close to the ground-truth patch
used for supervision.
We also measure transfer reliability across five represen-
tative tasks. On average, the hybrid geometry and LightGlue
transfer succeeds for 71.0% of right-wrist targets and 79.4%
of left-wrist targets. When reliable correspondences cannot
be found because of occlusion or limited view overlap,
the method falls back to a center prior. Because GazeAct
retains the original source-view gaze and synchronized cam-
era streams, gaze transfer can be recomputed with future
registration methods without recollecting demonstrations.
V. CONCLUSION
We introduced GazeAct, a gaze-equipped manipulation
dataset and training framework for improving visual ground-
ing in transformer-based VLA policies. GazeAct records
human gaze on the robot camera view used during teleopera-
tion, aligns it with multi-view robot observations and actions,
and propagates it across views through cross-view alignment.
This provides gaze-derived targets for multi-view policy
inputs, addressing a key limitation of prior gaze-equipped
robot datasets in which gaze is typically tied to a single
view. Using these targets, GazeAct regularizes language-
conditioned visual attention during training while preserv-
ing gaze-free deployment. Across real-world ALOHA tasks,
ablations, simulation transfer, attention visualizations, and
cross-architecture evaluation, attention regularization with
real human gaze improves visual grounding and manipulation
success. These results show that human gaze, when converted
into attention targets, can serve as a practical training-time
supervision signal for multi-view VLA policy learning.
