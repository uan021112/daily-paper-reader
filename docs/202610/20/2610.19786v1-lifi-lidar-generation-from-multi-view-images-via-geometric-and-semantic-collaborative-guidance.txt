Abstract
Generating LiDAR point clouds from multi-view camera images is a valuable task
with applications in controllable data synthesis, cross-modal simulation and various
perception tasks. However, most existing methods are not specifically designed for
this task, and the quality of their generated LiDAR remains limited. Moreover, these
methods typically focus only on semantic cues in images or generate LiDAR from a
monocular camera image. In this work, we introduce LiFi (LiDAR From images),
a dedicated framework for generating visually and geometrically aligned LiDAR
scenes from multi-view camera images. LiFi guides the latent diffusion process
through two complementary branches: geometry and semantics. The geometry
branch constructs scene features from images through a reverse sampling algorithm
and an uncertainty-aware geometric encoder combined with DepthAnything3,
whereas the semantic branch provides high-level semantic representations via
a cross-view interaction module. We further introduce a dual-branch balanced
classifier-free guidance (CFG) strategy, which enhances the model’s conditional
generation capability while preserving the independent learning and collaborative
controllability of the two branches. Extensive experiments demonstrate that LiFi
outperforms state-of-the-art methods in generating high-fidelity LiDAR scenes.
The code will be publicly available at: https://github.com/szzhou88/LiFi.

Introduction
The rapid progress of autonomous driving (AD) has drawn widespread attention [18–20]. LiDAR
sensors, which provide precise 3D structural information, have become a cornerstone of modern AD
systems [30, 32]. Nevertheless, acquiring large-scale, high-quality LiDAR data could be expensive
and the long-tail data captured by consumer devices might not employ LiDAR due to cost control
of the product, limiting the robustness and scalability of data-driven methods. To mitigate this,
pioneering LiDAR generation approaches [14, 52, 57] have been introduced, but they mainly focus
on unconditional generation and suffer from limited realism because they fail to exploit the diverse
contextual cues present in real-world scenes. Recently, conditional LiDAR point cloud generation [24,
28, 49, 51] has emerged, leveraging inputs such as text, maps, and semantic layouts to achieve notable
gains in both the photorealism and controllability of synthesized LiDAR scenes.
In this work, we study the conditional generation of LiDAR scenes from multi-view camera images.

therefore holds broad application potential, where it can complement high-quality 3D perception
under cost-sensitive sensor setups, and open up new possibilities for cross-modal simulation [11],
data augmentation [5], and sensor completion [6, 56]. However, this formulation remains relatively
under-explored. A major challenge is the cross-modal gap between images and LiDAR. RGB images

different viewpoints also introduce variations in field of view, occlusion patterns, and semantic

modalities, and are often limited to monocular inputs that lack comprehensive spatial awareness.
To address these challenges, we propose LiFi, a dedicated framework for generating high-fidelity
LiDAR scenes from multi-view camera images. LiFi guides the latent diffusion process of LiDAR
scenes, represented as range images, through two decoupled yet complementary branches: geometry
and semantics. In the geometry branch, we first leverage DepthAnything3 [31] to estimate per-
pixel depth and uncertainty maps from the input images. A range-view-centered reverse sampling
algorithm then is designed to align these dense predictions, together with the original RGB images,
to the LiDAR scanning pattern, producing depth, confidence, and appearance conditions. These
conditions are subsequently encoded into structured geometric features via an uncertainty-modulated
encoder that dynamically adjusts feature expressiveness based on local depth confidence. In the
semantic branch, we extract high-level visual tokens and fuse them with patch, view, and camera
extrinsic encodings. A cross-view attention module then aggregates global semantic context to
capture long-range dependencies and cross-view consistency. Furthermore, we introduce a dual-
branch balanced classifier-free guidance (CFG) strategy. By jointly training with full conditional,
single-branch-conditional, and unconditional settings, this strategy not only enhances the model’s
conditional guidance capability, but also improves the stability and effectiveness of collaborative
learning between the two branches.
We primarily compare LiFi with the state-of-the-art LiDAR generation models on the nuScenes dataset.
We also compare it with existing frameworks capable of performing camera-to-LiDAR generation.
To further evaluate the applicability of LiFi, we also compare it with several representative baseline
methods on the KITTI-360 dataset [7]. Experimental results demonstrate the superior performance
of our method in generating high-fidelity LiDAR scenes with geometric consistency and semantic
alignment. Furthermore, we apply LiFi to a downstream object detection task. Although this does not
directly demonstrate that LiFi-generated scenes can independently support perception performance

comparable to that of real LiDAR data, it nevertheless provides evidence of the effectiveness of the

In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We propose LiFi, which, to the best of our knowledge, is the first dedicated framework capable of
generating high-fidelity LiDAR scenes with geometric–visual alignment from multi-view images;
• We carefully design a range-view-centered reverse sampling algorithm, an uncertainty-aware
geometric encoder, and a cross-view semantic encoder to achieve precise cross-modal alignment at
the geometric level and effective global semantic guidance;
• We introduce a dual-branch balanced classifier-free guidance strategy, which effectively enhances
the model’s conditional guidance capability and collaborative learning ability;
• We conduct extensive evaluations of LiFi on the nuScenes dataset for both high-fidelity LiDAR
generation and downstream detection tasks, demonstrating the effectiveness of our method.

Related Work
Generative Models. Generative models synthesize realistic and diverse new samples by learning the
underlying data distribution. Early generative frameworks include GANs [8] and VAEs [23]. GANs
can generate realistic images but suffer from issues such as training instability and mode collapse.
VAEs provide an explicit probabilistic modeling formulation, but their generated results tend to be
overly smooth. Autoregressive models [48] achieve accurate generation by progressively modeling
conditional distributions, yet their sequential sampling process incurs high computational overhead.
Diffusion models (DMs) [12, 22, 36, 42, 43, 45–47] have demonstrated outstanding performance in
image synthesis. Subsequent research has introduced latent diffusion models (LDMs) [41], which
perform the diffusion process in the latent space, thereby significantly improving generation efficiency.
Meanwhile, they have also facilitated the advancement of conditional generation [35, 39, 53].
LiDAR Scene Generation. LiDAR scene generation aims to synthesize 3D point cloud represen-
tations with realistic geometric structures and scanning patterns. Caccia et al. [1] were the first to
adopt the range image representation and perform generation using VAEs and GANs. Subsequently,
LiDARGen [57] proposed a score-based model for LiDAR generation. UltraLiDAR [52] voxelizes
LiDAR point clouds and combines this representation with VQ-VAE for generation. R2DM [33], built
upon DDPMs, improves the fidelity of generated point clouds. RangeLDM [14] enables fast LiDAR
data generation by performing diffusion in a compact latent space. DriveLiDAR4D [3] improves
LiDAR generation quality by conditioning on three modalities: road sketches, scene descriptions,
and object priors. UniScene [24] introduces occupancy as an intermediate representation, thereby
reducing the difficulty of generating complex LiDAR scenes. OpenDWM [34] is built upon the DiT
architecture for video generation and further extends it to LiDAR scenes. LiDARCrafter [28] parses
natural language inputs into scene graphs to guide generation. LaGen [55] proposes an autoregressive
framework for generating long-horizon LiDAR scenes.
Camera-to-LiDAR Generation. Camera-to-LiDAR generation refers to generating 3D LiDAR
scenes from camera images. Unlike other conditional LiDAR generation models, research on
directly synthesizing LiDAR data from camera images remains relatively limited. LiDM [40]
proposes a multimodal conditional framework for LiDAR scene generation and also provides a
preliminary exploration of the camera-to-LiDAR task by extracting semantic information from
camera images. However, it does not investigate how to align the image and LiDAR modalities,
which differ substantially from each other, and thus its generation quality remains limited. X-
Drive [51] proposes a framework for the joint generation of LiDAR point clouds and multi-view
camera images, taking both LiDAR point clouds and camera images as inputs and generating the
two modalities simultaneously. It can be adapted to the camera-to-LiDAR task. However, its core
framework is not specifically designed for the image-only input setting, and therefore does not exhibit
clear performance advantages in this scenario. Moreover, it incorporates additional text prompts
and 3D bounding box information to assist the generation process. Other frameworks for the joint
generation of LiDAR point clouds and camera images include HoloDrive [50] and Genesis [10], but
they do not provide a camera-to-LiDAR mode. Our proposed method addresses the above challenges.

3.1
Preliminaries
Range Image Representation. We adopt the range image as the basic representation of LiDAR
data. Unlike directly processing sparse and unordered 3D point sets, it exploits the sensor’s scanning
pattern to encode point clouds into a regular 2D array. For a point p = (x, y, z) ∈R3, we first
transform it from the Cartesian coordinate system to a corrected spherical coordinate system [14]:

denote the sensor height and elevation angle estimated by Hough voting. We then map this spherical
representation to the range-view space and apply normalization, thereby obtaining the corresponding
range image. This representation transformation is reversible.
Adopting the range image representation not only transforms the data into a 2D representation but
also ensures that each pixel corresponds to a specific azimuth and elevation direction of the target
LiDAR sensor, thereby preserving the sensor’s structured angular sampling topology and inherent
sparsity. Furthermore, given a calibrated sensor model, the generated 2D representation can be
deterministically converted back into a 3D point cloud, improving generation efficiency.
Latent Diffusion Model for LiDAR Generation. We adopt a latent diffusion model (LDM) [41]
for conditional LiDAR data generation. The framework consists of a VAE and a UNet: the former
compresses the range image into a latent space, while the latter performs diffusion modeling in the
latent space. Specifically, given an input range image I ∈RHr×Wr×2, the encoder first maps it to
a latent representation ˆz ∈Rhr×wr×cr, and the decoder then reconstructs the range image from
this latent variable as ˆI = G(ˆz). During the diffusion process, the model starts from a clean latent
variable z0 and progressively injects Gaussian noise into it to obtain a noisy latent variable zt. During
training, the UNet takes zt, the timestep t, and the encoded conditional information c as inputs, and

3.2
Overall Framework
Given the multi-view camera images of a driving scene, V ∈RNv×Hv×Wv×3, along with the
corresponding camera extrinsics M = [R|t] ∈R3×4 and camera intrinsics K ∈R3×3, our goal is
to generate a 3D LiDAR scene that is geometrically and perceptually aligned with the input. To
achieve this goal, we first extract the following three types of visual priors from multi-view camera
inputs using a pre-trained vision foundation model: (i) pixel-wise relative depth estimation in the
camera image space: D ∈RNv×Hv×Wv; (ii) the corresponding uncertainty maps of depth estimation
U ∈RNv×Hv×Wv; and (iii) camera semantic tokens S ∈RNv×ls×cs. Taking DepthAnything3 [31]
as an example, it can jointly process multiple images captured from different viewpoints of the same
scene and natively output corresponding confidence maps.
Based on the above conditional information, we construct an image-to-LiDAR point cloud generation
framework guided by dual conditional branches with decoupled geometry and semantics. In the first
branch, we construct a geometry-conditioned encoder integrated with an uncertainty modulation
mechanism to fuse raw RGB images, depth maps, and uncertainty features. In the second branch,
we build a pose-aware semantic encoder to provide global semantic context and capture cross-view
relationships for the model. The overall pipeline of LiFi is illustrated in Figure 1.
3.3
Reverse Sampling Based Geometry-Aware Conditioning
In this section, we describe the construction of the geometry-aware conditional branch in detail. This
branch is designed to extract geometric features of the scene from multi-view camera observations,
thereby providing explicit spatial structural constraints for the subsequent diffusion process. To
effectively encode the geometric details contained in multi-view camera images, we first design a
range-view-centered reverse depth sampling algorithm.
3.3.1

proach is to project these depth maps pixel by pixel into LiDAR point clouds and then convert
them into range images. However, this strategy fails to ensure accurate alignment between pro-
jected depth maps and LiDAR scenes, while also introducing substantial computational overhead.

3.3.2
Geometry-Aware Condition Encoding with Uncertainty Modulation
To effectively exploit these LiDAR-aligned visual priors, we design a geometry-aware conditional
encoder. Specifically, we first introduce a 2D coordinate encoding for the range-view space, and
concatenate it with the depth map and RGB image as the input to the encoder. Next, we use an
encoder EG to jointly model the input features. This encoder consists of an initial convolutional layer,
several residual blocks, and downsampling layers, which progressively extract local geometric and
appearance information and map them to appropriate feature scales. Notably, rather than simply
treating the uncertainty map IU as an additional input channel, we inject it into the encoding process
as an explicit modulation signal through FiLM:
˜Hl = γl(IU) ⊙Hl + βl(IU),
(5)
where Hl denotes the output feature of the l-th residual block, and γl and βl are the scaling and
shifting terms generated by the FiLM module, respectively. This mechanism enables the model to
dynamically adjust the expressive strength of the conditional features according to the confidence
of local depth predictions. As a result, the encoder can preserve explicit geometric structure while
suppressing the propagation of unreliable conditional information in low-confidence regions.
This branch provides local, structured, and reliable geometric constraints for LiDAR scene generation.
Compared with directly using raw projection results, this design more effectively integrates depth,
appearance, and spatial positional information, while enhancing the consistency between the cross-
modal conditions and the target LiDAR scene.

a cross-view semantic encoder ES, which takes the flattened tokens from all views as input. We
further introduce three types of positional encodings to enhance its awareness of patch coordinates,
camera identity, and camera extrinsic parameters, thereby obtaining enriched feature representations:

3.5
Dual-Branch Balanced Classifier-Free Guidance
In the previous two sections, we introduced the geometric conditional branch and the cross-view
semantic branch, respectively. However, in practice, the model may become biased toward one
conditional branch during training, resulting in under-utilization of the other branch.
To mitigate this issue, we construct a dual-branch balanced classifier-free guidance (CFG) module.
Standard CFG typically enhances conditional guidance at inference time by randomly dropping all
conditions, thereby allowing the model to learn both conditional and unconditional distributions.
In contrast, we extend the condition dropout mechanism beyond the conventional “all-or-nothing”
scheme by additionally introducing cases where only geometric or only semantic conditions are
retained. This enables the model not only to possess the unconditional modeling capability, but also to
explicitly learn the individual effects of the two types of conditions as well as their joint contributions.
During inference, we adopt the same conditional guidance formulation as in standard CFG:
ϵ = ϵuncond + w · (ϵcond −ϵuncond),
(8)
where w is the guidance scale, ϵuncond and ϵcond denote the noise predictions under unconditional
and conditional guidance, respectively.
This strategy preserves the advantages of standard CFG while further balancing the learning process
between the geometric and semantic branches, enabling the model to leverage global semantic
information to generate more plausible details when the depth information from the geometric branch
is unreliable. With all the above designs, our model is able to jointly leverage local geometric
conditions and global semantic conditions, thereby establishing reliable cross-modal correspondences
between multi-view images and LiDAR scenes and generating high-fidelity LiDAR scenes.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setups
Dataset. We conduct experiments on the challenging nuScenes dataset [2]. It contains over a thousand
autonomous driving sequences collected in two geographically distinct urban environments with
complex traffic conditions. The nuScenes training set includes 297,737 LiDAR scans, while the test
set contains 52,423 LiDAR scans, each captured by a 32-beam LiDAR sensor. This dataset is widely
used in various autonomous driving tasks, such as 3D object detection [17, 25–27], multi-object
tracking [13, 38, 54], trajectory prediction [9, 29], and end-to-end autonomous driving [15, 16, 21].
We also conduct experiments on the KITTI-360 dataset [7], as detailed in the supplementary material.
Baselines. We compare LiFi with multiple categories of baseline methods. The first category
includes conventional LiDAR data generation methods, covering both unconditional and conditional
generation models. The second category consists of baselines capable of generating LiDAR from
camera images, including LiDM [40] and X-Drive [51]. The third category includes models for
generating continuous LiDAR scenes. In addition, we apply LiFi to a downstream 3D object detection
task and compare the performance of models with and without LiDAR input.
Implementation Details. We conduct distributed training on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Our model
is trained for 200 epochs, with a total of 176,000 optimization steps, and the learning rate is set
to 1 × 10−4. We use the DDIM sampler [44] for denoising, and set the total number of diffusion
timesteps T to 1024. The UNet consists of three downsampling blocks and three upsampling blocks,
each containing two Transformer blocks. We employ DepthAnything3 [31] as the pretrained visual
encoder, which can predict spatially consistent geometry from an arbitrary number of input views. It
is an instantiation choice that matches the requirements, rather than an irreplaceable component of
the framework. During inference, we perform 50 denoising steps to generate the target range image.
More experimental settings and implementation details are provided in the supplementary material.

4.4
Temporal Consistency of Continuous Scene Generation
We further evaluate the temporal consistency of LiDAR scenes generated by LiFi under consecutive
camera inputs. While this approach does not explicitly incorporate mechanisms for temporal gener-

Conclusion
We propose LiFi, a dedicated framework for generating realistic LiDAR scenes from multi-view
camera images. We introduce a dual-branch conditioning strategy to guide the latent diffusion process,
effectively addressing the challenges of cross-modal geometric alignment and cross-view semantic
aggregation. Experimental results show that LiFi is capable of generating high-fidelity LiDAR scenes.
Moreover, the LiDAR point cloud generated by it has certain value for some downstream tasks of
autonomous driving. In the future, we expect LiFi to play an important role in applications such as
simulation, planning, and control in various complex environments and long-tail scenarios.

Overview of the Appendix
The appendix is structured into the following sections to supplement the main paper. The first part

B
Limitations and Future Work
Our work presents several limitations. Primarily, relying on the range image representation is not
entirely lossless, resulting in the omission of certain camera image details within the synthesized
LiDAR point clouds. Refining this representation in future research holds the potential to further
boost model performance. Furthermore, the overall architecture of our method is not specifically
designed for explicit temporal modeling. Although this work does not focus on generating temporal
LiDAR sequences, incorporating temporal consistency mechanisms in future work could extend the
applicability of our method to a broader range of scenarios.
C
More Implementation Details
During training, our model is trained for 200 epochs with a total of 176,000 optimization steps. The
total batch size is set to 32, and the learning rate is set to 1 × 10−4, with a cosine annealing scheduler
applied. We perform distributed training on 4 NVIDIA H200 GPUs, with a total training time of
approximately 20 hours. For denoising, we employ the DDIM sampler [44], with the total number of
diffusion timesteps T set to 1024. During inference, we perform 50 denoising steps to generate point
clouds. The inference is conducted on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU.
We adopt DepthAnything3 [31] as the pre-trained visual encoder. For the semantic branch, we follow
DepthAnything3 and employ DINOv2 [37] to extract visual features. Based on the nuScenes dataset,
we use six camera images from different viewpoints as input to generate each scene.
In the geometry-aware branch, the encoder primarily consists of multiple convolutional layers,
normalization layers, downsampling layers, zero-convolution layers, and FiLM modules. In the cross-
view semantic branch, the encoder mainly includes a multi-head self-attention layer, a feed-forward
network (composed of two fully connected layers), and two Layer Normalization layers. Both the
attention layer and the FFN employ residual connections.
The VAE in the LDM is initialized with pre-trained weights from RangeLDM [14]. The range image
resolution is set to [1024, 32], and the VAE uses a downsampling factor of 4. The UNet consists of
three downsampling blocks (the latter two incorporating Transformer layers) and three upsampling
blocks (the first two incorporating Transformer layers). We employ circular convolution instead of
standard convolution in both the VAE and the LDM.

E
Standard Range-View Projection and Corrected Version
Range images offer a compact representation of LiDAR data by parametrizing unstructured point

Theoretical Analysis of the Geometry-Aware Mechanism
F.1
Computational Complexity
Let the resolution of the high-resolution camera depth map be Himg × Wimg, and the target LiDAR
range image resolution be Hr×Wr. Conventional projection methods require iterating over each pixel
of the depth map, calculating the 3D coordinates, and projecting them onto a cylindrical coordinate
system, followed by rasterization. The computational complexity of this process is O(Himg · Wimg).
Since this process involves mapping from a high-resolution image to a low-resolution LiDAR grid,
it typically results in many-to-one write conflicts. To address the occlusion problem, additional
aggregation operations are required, which introduce extra computational overhead.
In contrast, the reverse sampling method starts directly from the target LiDAR grid. For each query
point qk, the projection via the camera is:
[˜un, ˜vn, ˜wn]T = Kn(Rn · qk + tn),
(25)
where Kn, Rn, and tn are the camera’s intrinsic matrix, rotation matrix, and translation vector. The
overall time complexity is strictly constrained to O(Hr · Wr). Since in physical scenes, Hr · Wr ≪
Himg · Wimg, the reverse sampling significantly reduces the computational overhead.

Theoretical Analysis of the Cross-View Semantic Branch
In multi-view systems, the 2D images from different viewpoints are projections of the same scene
under different camera poses, and the imaging process can be represented as a projection mapping
π : R3 →R2. Due to the lack of depth information, the 3D correspondence of any pixel (u, v) is a
set of points along a ray:
π−1(u, v) = {X | π(X) = (u, v)},

parameter encoding Eext(T), to construct pose-conditioned features and explicitly introduce the

projection uncertainty.

Theoretical Analysis of the Dual-Branch Balanced CFG
In conditional diffusion models, the introduction of a conditional variable c = (cg, cs) aims to
learn the conditional distribution p(x | c), ensuring that the generated results maintain a statistical
dependency with the condition variable. Although the training objective of the diffusion model does
not directly maximize mutual information, the maximum likelihood objective can be interpreted as
implicitly encouraging the increase of mutual information between x and the condition variables.
According to the chain rule for mutual information, we have:
I(x; cg, cs) = I(x; cg) + I(x; cs | cg),
(42)
In standard joint training, if the geometric condition cg provides strong structural constraints, the
model may preferentially utilize cg to minimize the loss during the early stages of optimization,
thereby weakening its reliance on the semantic condition cs. This phenomenon can be interpreted as
an information shortcut, where the model tends to rely on the conditioning variable that is easier to
optimize. To mitigate this issue, we sample the condition c from the following set during training:
c ∼{∅, cg, cs, (cg, cs)},
(43)
and the corresponding optimization objective is defined as:

(44)
This objective is equivalent to simultaneously fitting multiple conditional distributions:

(45)
From the information-theoretic perspective, this training strategy explicitly encourages the model to
maintain robust predictive capabilities across different conditional subspaces, thereby preventing the
model from relying solely on a single conditioning variable and mitigating the issue of imbalanced
information utilization.

Discussion on Pre-trained Vision Models and Metric Scale
Replaceability of the Pretrained Vision Model.
From the perspective of the framework interface, LiFi does not depend on the specific internal
architecture of DepthAnything3. The geometric branch only requires the depth foundation model
to provide two types of per-pixel information for each input view: depth predictions and their
corresponding reliability estimates. LiFi’s reverse sampling module then aligns these predictions
with the target LiDAR scanning grid, while the uncertainty-aware encoder uses the reliability signals
to modulate the geometric features. Therefore, as long as an alternative model can provide outputs in
the same form, the subsequent inverse sampling, FiLM modulation, and diffusion generation pipeline
can remain unchanged.
19


Therefore, from an architectural perspective, any large monocular depth model capable of estimating
per-pixel uncertainty can technically be directly integrated into LiFi. However, if the model processes
each camera view independently, the predicted depths may have independent scales across different
views. In this case, we recommend introducing cross-view scale alignment before reverse sampling.
Specifically, one feasible approach is to use the known camera calibration and overlapping fields of
view to perform scale-and-shift alignment across the depth maps from different views. For example,
the prediction for each view can be expressed as: ˆ
Dn = anDn + bn, where an and bn are estimated
by enforcing consistency within the overlapping regions. After cross-view alignment, a unified
normalization scheme can be applied to all views before feeding them into LiFi.
Therefore, multi-view consistency is not a strict architectural requirement for LiFi, but it can
positively contribute to maintaining the quality of the geometric conditions. In addition, methods
with multi-view depth estimation capabilities, such as VGGT and MapAnything, can serve as
direct replacements for DepthAnything3. Therefore, DepthAnything3 is an instantiation choice that
matches the task requirements, rather than an irreplaceable component of the LiFi framework.
Metric Scale of the Simulator.
We first emphasize that the final LiDAR generated by LiFi is already represented in the metric LiDAR
coordinate system of the target dataset, rather than having only relative scale.
LiFi trains the diffusion model with range images constructed from real LiDAR scans as the target,
and therefore learns a mapping from relative visual geometry and image semantics to real metric
LiDAR distance distributions. After de-normalization, each valid cell in the generated range image
represents an actual distance in the target sensor coordinate system. It can then be back-projected
using the corresponding laser-beam elevation angles, azimuth sampling, and sensor calibration
parameters to obtain a 3D point cloud in meters. The range image transformation adopted in the
paper is itself reversible, and the generation target is constructed from the metric distances of real
LiDAR scans.
Therefore, for datasets such as nuScenes and KITTI-360, there is no need to fit an additional global
scale factor after generation. Metric LiDAR point clouds from datasets such as KITTI and Argoverse
can likewise be directly converted into range images according to the scanning pattern of the target
sensor and used as training supervision for LiFi.
If LiFi is to be adapted to a specific simulator, we recommend the following procedure:
1. If the simulator is intended to use a target LiDAR configuration consistent with those of current
mainstream datasets, the LiFi architecture trained on these datasets can be directly used.
2. If the goal is to simulate a completely new virtual target LiDAR configuration, the first step is to
specify the target LiDAR settings, including the beam elevation angles, horizontal angular resolution,
and extrinsic parameters. Based on these settings, the target range image representation can then
be constructed, and the VAE and diffusion model can be trained or fine-tuned using camera–metric
LiDAR data corresponding to that sensor. At inference time, the generated results can be converted
back into a metric point cloud according to the same normalization parameters and sensor model.
