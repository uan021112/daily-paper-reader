Abstract: Dexterous grasping in cluttered scenes remains a central challenge
in robotic manipulation, as it requires high-dimensional hand coordination un-
der complex object-environment constraints. To address this problem, we intro-
duce one of the largest simulation benchmarks to date, comprising around 0.5B
grasp annotations across eight dexterous hands and approximately 50K objects.
Furthermore, we propose T (R, O) Grasp++, a scalable diffusion-based frame-
work for cross-embodiment dexterous grasp synthesis in cluttered environments.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that our model significantly outperforms ex-
isting baselines, achieving an average success rate of 88% across eight dexterous
embodiments. Meanwhile, our pipeline achieves only 0.3s inference latency while
improving inference sample efficiency by over 10 times.

Introduction
Dexterous grasping in cluttered environments is essential for practical robotic manipulation. How-
ever, it remains challenging due to the high-dimensional action space and complex interactions
among object, hand and surrounding environments. Existing methods typically generate multiple
grasp proposals directly in the scene and rely on collision checking or grasp scoring to select exe-
cutable grasps [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. However, previous works have three key limitations. First, the
proposal-and-selection paradigm suffers from low inference sample efficiency. Since the relations
among the hand, object, and surrounding environment are not sufficiently encoded, many generated
proposals are either in collision or dynamically unstable, requiring a large number of candidates to

diversity. Existing datasets are limited in object and embodiment diversity, with redundant annota-
tions concentrated on a small set of objects [1, 2]. Third, existing grasp representations are insuffi-
cient. Prior methods either rely on hand-specific representations, such as hand joint values [1, 3] or
predefined grasp templates [4, 7], which limit generalization across hands, or adopt object-centric
representations for grasp optimization [2, 8], which is computationally expensive.
To address these limitations, we first construct a large-scale dataset across multiple embodiments
for dexterous grasping in cluttered environments. Our benchmark contains around 0.5B grasp anno-
tations in common manipulation scenes, with hierarchical difficulty scores that enable fine-grained
evaluation across varying grasp difficulties. In addition, we propose T (R, O) Graph++, a uni-
fied grasp representation that models spatial transformations among environment, robot, and object.
Built upon T (R, O) Graph++, our T (R, O) Grasp++ model performs unified diffusion-based cross-
embodiment grasping synthesis. In extensive experiments, our model achieves an average success
rate of 87.96% across eight dexterous hands, an inference speed of 0.34 seconds on an NVIDIA
4090 GPU, and over 10× higher inference sample efficiency than existing baselines.
In conclusion, our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We construct a large-scale cluttered-
scene dexterous grasping benchmark with 53,675 objects, around 0.5B grasp annotations, and dif-
ficulty scores for evaluation under varying grasp difficulties; (2) We propose T (R, O) Grasp++, a
scalable diffusion-based framework that integrates object-environment geometry with hand kine-
matics for cross-embodiment grasp synthesis in cluttered environments; (3) We conduct extensive
simulation and real-world experiments, where T (R, O) Grasp++ significantly outperforms all base-
lines by over 30%, while achieving fast and sample-efficient inference; (4) We present the first
scaling study for cross-embodiment dexterous grasping, leveraging our large-scale benchmark to
reveal how performance scales with training object diversity and model capacity.
2
Related Works
2.1
Dexterous Grasping Dataset
Creating large-scale datasets for dexterous grasping is challenging due to the high-dimensional hand
action space and complex hand-object contacts. One line of work constructs such datasets through
optimization-based synthesis, where grasp poses are directly optimized with force-closure, contact,
penetration, and stability objectives [1, 2, 9, 10]. Another line of work trains grasping policies
in simulation and rolls out on large object sets, collecting successful executions as grasp annota-
tions [11, 12, 13]. In our work, we train reinforcement learning policies for each dexterous hand and
use successful rollouts to annotate 444 million grasps across large-scale object datasets. As shown
in Tab. 1, our dataset covers 53,675 objects and 8 dexterous hands, while additionally providing
per-grasp difficulty scores and a mount link to approximate real-world hand-arm deployment.
2.2
Learning-based Dexterous Grasping Synthesis
Learning-based frameworks for dexterous grasping synthesis can be categorized into single-object
and scene-aware approaches. Single-object methods typically predict or optimize grasp poses for an
isolated target object without modeling environment constraints [2, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22,
23]. While these methods achieve promising results in single-object settings, their inference sample
efficiency drops substantially when applied to real-world cluttered environments, as many predicted
grasps suffer from severe collisions with surrounding objects or environment.
2


In comparison, scene-aware methods further incorporate environment constraints into grasping syn-
thesis [1, 2, 3, 4, 13, 24, 25, 26]. Despite their progress, existing methods still face limitations in
both task setting and grasp representation. In terms of task setting, existing methods generate grasp
proposals over the entire scene and then rely on collision checking, grasp scoring, or feasibility fil-
tering to select executable grasps [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. While suitable for open-world scene grasping, this
setting suffers from poor sample efficiency in target-object grasping, as many generated proposals
correspond to irrelevant objects or collide with the surrounding environment. As for grasp repre-
sentation, prior methods either directly predict hand-specific joint values [1, 4, 24], which limits
cross-embodiment generalization, or rely on object-centric predictions that require additional slow
optimization to recover executable joint configurations [2, 7]. To overcome these limitations, we
propose T (R, O) Graph++ as a unified grasp representation across embodiments, enabling direct
grasp prediction on the target object in cluttered environments within a unified framework.
3
Benchmark
We present a large-scale benchmark for cross-embodiment dexterous grasping in diverse cluttered
scenes, with grasp difficulty assessed by object-level graspability and scene-level clutter complexity
to support fine-grained evaluation across varying grasp difficulties. Our dataset comprises 53,675
objects from UniDexGrasp [27] and ObjaverseXL [28], including diverse cluttered scenes in table-
top, shelf, and box settings (as shown in Fig. 1). Across eight dexterous hands, we annotate around
0.5B valid grasp samples in simulation.
3.1
Object Collection and Scene Synthesis
We collect 13,049 objects from the UnidexGrasp dataset [27] and 40,626 objects from the Obja-
verseXL dataset [28]. For training, we use dexterous grasp samples in cluttered scenes across all
dexterous hands on 47,737 objects. For testing, we use the remaining 5,938 unseen objects to con-
struct cluttered environments for each dexterous hand. In each scene, 15 to 25 objects are randomly
placed on a supporting plane. We construct three common manipulation environments: tabletop,
shelf and box, with environment scale randomly sampled within predefined ranges.
3.2
Dexterous Grasp Annotation
To efficiently annotate dexterous grasps across robotics hands, we adopt a three-stage annotation
pipeline. First, we train embodiment-specific reinforcement learning policies to roll out power and
precision grasps on floating objects. Second, we generate stable object poses by randomly sampling
initial object poses and dropping the objects onto a tabletop in simulation. Each floating-object grasp
is then transformed according to the resulting object pose, and candidates with tabletop penetration
are filtered out. Finally, we construct diverse cluttered manipulation scenes across tabletop, shelf,
and box settings by placing additional obstacle objects outside the 2D convex hull of the hand-object
grasp configuration to avoid collisions. In this way, we annotate approximately 1K grasps in clutter
per target object for each dexterous hand.
To better reflect real-world deployment constraints, we attach a cylinder mount link to each palm
to approximate the connecting arm segment and include it in collision checking, ensuring sufficient
clearance for both the hand and its mounting structure. Our RL-based annotation pipeline supports
scalable data generation through massively parallel simulation. On an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU,
a trained policy generates 216 grasp annotations per second while also achieving a higher filtering
success rate compared with optimization-based methods [21, 29].
3.3

grained evaluation across varying grasp difficulties. Specifically, object-level difficulty Dobj captures
the intrinsic graspability of the target object, estimated by the number of RL optimization steps
required for reliable grasping. Environment-level difficulty Denv measures local clutter around the

3.4
Dexterous Grasp Evaluation in Simulation
We evaluate dexterous grasps in Isaac Gym simulation. A grasp is considered successful if it is
collision-free with the surrounding environment and lifts the target object above a predefined height
threshold. The hand is initialized at the outer grasp pose, driven to the inner grasp pose with position
control, and then lifted upward. For consistency, we use the same simulation protocol for both
training data filtering and model evaluation across our method and all baselines.
4
T (R, O) Grasp++ for Dexterous Grasping in Clutter
Given point-cloud observations of the target object and the surrounding environment, denoted as
Pobj and Penv, together with the URDF description of an arbitrary dexterous hand, our goal is to learn
a unified model that predicts link-wise SE(3) transformations representing a valid and collision-free
grasp in a cluttered environment. Finally, the predicted link poses are converted into executable joint
values by solving a real-time inverse-kinematics retargeting problem using Pyroki [30].
Built on T (R, O) Grasp [16], we propose a diffusion-based framework for cross-embodiment dex-
terous grasp synthesis in cluttered environments as shown in Fig. 2. Our method consists of two key
components: (1) a unified T (R, O) Graph++ representation that models hand-object-environment
interactions by encoding the kinematics of arbitrary dexterous hands, target-object geometry, and
cluttered-scene context as structured graph elements; and (2) a scalable diffusion model that per-
forms grasp synthesis based on T (R, O) Graph++ representation, enabling efficient training and
fast inference across diverse dexterous embodiments. We first introduce the unified graph repre-
sentation across arbitrary embodiments in Sec. 4.1. Then, we present the diffusion framework in
Sec. 4.2. Finally, we describe the training process and data sampling strategy in Sec. 4.3.
4.1
T (R, O) Graph++
Learning cross-embodiment dexterous grasping in cluttered environments requires a unified repre-
sentation that captures interactions among arbitrary dexterous hands, target objects, and surround-
ing environments. Compared with joint values or object-centric contact maps, interaction-based
representations [15, 16] have demonstrated better generalization and efficiency. Therefore, we ex-
tend T (R, O) Graph to T (R, O) Graph++, which jointly encodes object-environment geometry and
hand structure. Importantly, each hand link is encoded with both geometry and kinematics as a
unique footprint. This design enables the model to distinguish visually similar links across hands
for scalable multi-hand training, as validated by our ablation study in Section 5.5.

ϕenv. Specifically, we normalize both point clouds with the environment centroid and scale, aligning

5.1
Experimental Setting
We compare our method with several baselines on the test scenes described in Sec. 3.1. Following
the evaluation protocol in Sec. 3.4, we evaluate all generated grasps in Isaac Gym simulation and
report both success rate and grasp diversity across multiple dexterous hands. Due to limited avail-
able data and hand-specific hard-coded patterns in existing baselines, we conduct comparisons only
on embodiments shared by all methods. Since AnyDexGrasp [4] produces grasp proposals for all
objects in a scene, we first filter the proposals associated with the target object and then select the
highest-scoring one as its prediction. For DexGraspNet2 [1], we similarly select the highest-scoring
grasp from all generated candidates. Detailed implementation details are presented in Appendix.
5.2
Cross-Embodiment Grasp Synthesis in Cluttered Environment
Based on the 5,938 unseen objects as described in Sec. 3.1, we generate 20,000 test scenes for
each dexterous hand for evaluation. As shown in Tab. 2, our method significantly outperforms both
baselines, achieving an average success rate of 88.0% across multiple dexterous embodiments. Fig. 3
illustrates that AnyDexGrasp often over-emphasizes fitting grasp templates to the target object, while
neglecting environmental collision constraints. DexGraspNet2 leverages local geometric features
for cluttered-scene grasp generation, but its selected candidates still fail to achieve accurate target-
object contacts while avoiding scene collisions. In addition, AnyDexGrasp relies on hard-coded,
hand-specific grasp templates, while DexGraspNet2 directly predicts hand joint values. As a result,
neither method supports cross-embodiment dexterous grasp synthesis in cluttered environments.
To further explore the performance boundary in highly cluttered environments, we generate 20,000
challenging test scenes on unseen objects with denser clutter and a larger number of obstacle ob-
jects. Combining them with the original test set, we obtain 40,000 test scenes for each hand and
split them into four difficulty levels according to Denv. The score range of each level is shown in
Tab. 3. For comparison, we report the average success rate and diversity across all hands for our
method, and over the supported shared hands for each baseline. As shown in Tab. 3, our method
consistently outperforms both baselines across all difficulty levels. Its performance gradually de-
creases as Denv increases, but remains above 75% success rate even in Level-4 clutter, indicating a
robust performance boundary in highly constrained scenes.

Scaling Law and Multi-Trial Analysis
In this section, we first investigate the scaling laws of our method along model size and training
object scale. As shown in Tab. 5, scaling the model up to 200M parameters consistently improves

marginal gains, suggesting performance saturation beyond 200M under the current training setting
and data scale. Along another axis, increasing the number of training objects consistently improves
the success rate. This suggests that training on more objects exposes the model to a wider range of
successful grasp patterns, improving its generalization to unseen object geometries.
We further evaluate our method under a multi-trial evaluation setting, where each scene is tested
with multiple independent trials and considered successful if any trial successfully lifts up the target
object. As shown in Tab. 5, the success rate improves from 87.96% with one trial to 98.46% with four
trials, suggesting that multiple samples can recover feasible grasps missed by single-trial sampling.

training data are synthesized by projecting full point clouds from randomized camera viewpoints and

6
Limitations and Conclusions
In this paper, we introduce T (R, O) Grasp++, a scalable framework for cross-embodiment grasping
synthesis in clutter, supported by a large-scale benchmark containing around 0.5B grasp annotations.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that T (R, O) Grasp++ outperforms existing baselines in simu-
lation and remains robust in real-world cluttered scenes. We also validate its inference and sample
efficiency, and analyze scaling trends for future research directions.
However, our work has several limitations. First, T (R, O) Grasp++ does not support multi-modal
inputs, such as RGB channels, force feedback, or tactile signals. As a result, our model cannot
leverage appearance cues or contact feedback to refine grasp synthesis. Second, our data generation
pipeline has limited capability for functional grasp annotation. Therefore, T (R, O) Grasp++ does
not yet support functional grasp synthesis. We hope future work can address these limitations.

8.1
Data Annotation Pipeline
As introduced in the main paper, we use a three-stage pipeline to generate dexterous grasp anno-
tations in cluttered scenes. We first train embodiment-specific RL policies to roll out power and
precision grasps on floating objects. We then sample stable tabletop object poses and transform
each floating-object grasp accordingly, filtering out candidates with tabletop penetration. Finally,
we construct tabletop, shelf, and box clutter by placing obstacle objects outside the 2D convex hull
of the hand–object grasp configuration. In this section, we illustrate the details of each stage.
Stage 1: Floating Grasp Annotation.
We first partition the complete object set into small subsets,
each containing at most 128 objects. For each dexterous hand and each object subset, we train two
PPO [38] policies in Isaac Gym to roll out power and precision grasps on floating target objects. The
hand is initialized from sampled approaching poses around the object convex hull, and the policy
controls both the 6D wrist motion and the actuated hand joints. Each episode consists of a grasping
phase followed by a lifting and perturbation phase, where successful grasps are saved as floating-
object grasp annotations. The two policies share the same PPO framework but use different reward
designs. For the power-grasp policy, the reward encourages the hand to approach the object while
regularizing the hand posture, and gives a terminal reward for successful lifting:

Stage 3: Cluttered Scene Construction.
Finally, we construct cluttered manipulation scenes by
adding obstacle objects around each transformed hand-object configuration. For each transformed
grasp, we project the target-object point cloud and hand point cloud onto the tabletop plane, and
compute their 2D convex hull as a forbidden placement region that approximates the occupied grasp
area. Additional obstacle objects are sampled outside this region to introduce surrounding clutter
without collision. We consider three scene types: tabletop, shelf, and box. The tabletop scene uses
a square support plane with side length in [0.70, 1.00] m; the shelf scene has length [0.80, 1.30]
m, depth [0.20, 0.40] m, and height [0.35, 0.50] m; and the box scene has side length [0.70, 1.00]
m and wall height [0.25, 0.35] m. For each scene, we randomly place 15 to 25 obstacle objects,
simulate them until they settle, and discard invalid obstacles that collide with the forbidden region
or fall outside the environment boundary. The final annotation stores the environment point cloud,
target-object pose, obstacle poses, and hand joint values.

Training Configurations.
T (R, O) Grasp++ takes three inputs: the target-object point cloud
Pobj ∈R512×3, the surrounding-environment point cloud Penv ∈R2048×3, and the URDF descrip-
tion of an arbitrary dexterous hand. For the default 0.2B configuration, we train T (R, O) Grasp++
with a batch size of 48 per GPU and set the number of optimization steps to ensure full coverage
of the training dataset. We use the AdamW [39] optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, 1,000
warmup steps, gradient clipping of 1.0, and bfloat16 mixed precision. The diffusion process follows
a cosine noise schedule with T = 1, 000 timesteps and offset s = 0.008. For each training batch,
we sample Nt = 5 diffusion timesteps for supervision. For the loss function, we set loss weights
γpos = γrot = 1.0. We train the model on 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.
Inference Configurations.
During inference, we use DDIM sampling [34] with 20 denoising steps
and set η = 1.0. Given the point clouds of the target object and surrounding environment, together
with the URDF description of the specified dexterous hand, T (R, O) Grasp++ iteratively denoises
noisy robot-link poses and outputs link-wise SE(3) grasp poses. The predicted link poses are then
retargeted to executable joint values based on Pyroki [30] toolkit. To improve the accuracy of the
retargeting step, we solve IK from 8 different initializations in parallel and select the solution with
the lowest average target-link position error after forward-kinematics evaluation.
