# Look Before You Act: Bridging Active Perception and Vision-Language-Action Models via Observation-Aligned Data Adaptation 

Jiawei Geng 1, Zhenyang Liu 1,2, Yuzheng Wu 1, Yongchong Gu 1, Yanwei Fu 1,2

> 1

Fudan University 2Shanghai Innovation Institute 

Abstract — Robots manipulating objects in clutter must de-cide not only how to act, but also where to look. Although active camera motion can expose an occluded target, it may move the robot into an initial observation–state distribution that differs from the manipulation policy’s training distribution. We identify this problem as first-frame distribution sensitivity :under perturbed starts in LIBERO, naively prepending camera repositioning to π0.5 reduces success from 46.3% to 11.5%. We introduce ActiveAlign, a data-adaptation framework that trains a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy from the states at which active perception terminates. A learned viewpoint module first generates a camera trajectory. Offline, ActiveAlign uses closed-loop alignment to connect the trajectory endpoint to an existing demonstration, replays the remaining manipu-lation trajectory, and retains successful continuations for fine-tuning. At deployment, the same viewpoint module precedes the adapted VLA without modifying its architecture. Across 40 LIBERO tasks, ActiveAlign achieves 70.7% success with π0

and 72.7% with π0.5, outperforming their standard-training baselines by 40.0% and 26.4% respectively. Matched-state and data-matched controls support gains beyond prefix shortening and generic viewpoint augmentation; OpenVLA and GR00T N1 also show the same trend. Success rate under physical occlusion in real world increases from 35/90 to 83/90. These results show that active perception benefits manipulation when policy training is aligned with the observation and robot-state distribution induced by viewpoint selection. 

I. INTRODUCTION Effective manipulation begins with an observation that supports the intended action [1], [2]. A robot asked to retrieve fruit from a container or grasp a cup behind clutter may need to move its camera before it can see a useful approach direction. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [3]–[7] provide a powerful interface from visual observations and language to robot actions, but their input is still determined by where the robot looks. Repositioning an existing wrist camera is an appealing way to expose the target before manipulation. The resulting system must therefore solve two connected problems: selecting an informative view and continuing the task from the state produced by that selection. The most direct composition is to let an active-perception (AP) module improve the view, then hand control to a VLA trained on conventional demonstrations. Our experiments reveal a counterintuitive outcome: this composition can sub-stantially reduce task success. Under a shared perturbed-start LIBERO protocol, π0.5 falls from 46.3% to 11.5% average success after camera repositioning, and π0 falls from 30.7% to 7.0%. The qualitative examples in Fig. 1 illustrate the tension: a policy can act from a familiar but occluded view, yet struggle to exploit a view selected to reveal the target. Improving the observation and making it usable by the action policy are distinct requirements. We study this interface through first-frame distribution sensitivity . Here, the first frame is the observation at the handoff from active perception to manipulation. Wrist-camera motion changes image location, scale, and occlusion together with arm configuration and the relationship to the remaining task. A policy must consequently infer a suitable continuation from a different combination of visual and proprioceptive cues. Action chunking [8], [9] can propagate an early error through the actions executed before the next observation, even when later decisions use fresh images. This motivates examining the joint observation–action interface; a difference between first-frame image distributions alone does not establish the cause of every failure. Our experiments therefore pair the failure observation with controls that separate viewpoint information from robot-state progress and data volume. The central design insight is that the deployment viewpoint policy is available during data construction. Rather than requiring a manipulation learner to accommodate arbitrary sensing motions, we can expose it to successful continuations from the endpoints of the very module it will encounter at inference. This turns the composition problem into a question about supervision: how can existing demonstrations be made to begin where active perception stops? Demonstration-generation methods such as MimicGen and DemoGen [10], [11] motivate reusing recorded behavior, while the present setting adds a specific constraint: the starting observation and state are chosen by a task-conditioned camera policy. Visual augmentation alone does not supply the actions needed to continue from that state. 

ActiveAlign constructs these continuations offline. Given scene RGBD and a language instruction, a learned module predicts a wrist-camera trajectory. In a restored environment, we execute the trajectory and match its endpoint to anearby end-effector state in an early demonstration prefix. Closed-loop alignment bridges the residual gap, after which the recorded manipulation suffix is replayed. Successful alignment-and-replay trajectories become training data for the VLA. The active approach replaces part of the original demonstration, while the recorded continuation teaches the policy how to proceed after that approach. The procedure changes training data and policy weights while preserving the VLA architecture. Replay feasibility is essential to this construction. End-effector proximity does not ensure that object and contact states agree, so nearest-state matching alone cannot validate a continuation. Successful replay filters the candidate end-points and limits the training distribution to states for which usable supervision can be reconstructed. ActiveAlign conse-quently applies to resettable simulators and reconstructable physical workcells. This requirement belongs to offline data collection: deployment requires neither scene reset nor expert replay. The resulting scope is pre-manipulation view selec-tion, with longer-horizon tasks testing the lasting value of that initial view rather than continuous response to moving occluders. Our evaluation follows this reasoning from composition failure to controlled explanation and physical validation. On LIBERO Spatial, a matched-state camera-extrinsics control holds the demonstration step and manipulation suffix fixed and reaches 54.6%, compared with 78.2% for ActiveAlign. Data-matched random views and broad viewpoint jitter reach 58.0% and 61.4%, respectively. These comparisons support a benefit from the informative, deployment-matched observa-tions beyond shorter demonstrations and generic coverage. Across four suites, ActiveAlign improves π0 and π0.5 by 40.0 and 26.4 percentage points; OpenVLA and GR00T N1 extend the evidence to different action formulations. Initial-occlusion simulation tasks, diverse physical tasks, and endpoint perturbations further characterize when the construction is useful and where it breaks down. Our contributions are threefold:  

> •

We characterize the failure of naive composition of ac-tive perception and VLAs, and test competing explana-tions through matched-state and data-matched controls.  

> •

We introduce observation-aligned demonstration adap-tation, connecting active-view endpoints to success-ful manipulation through prefix matching, closed-loop alignment, and replay filtering.  

> •

We evaluate the construction across four VLA back-bones, 40 LIBERO tasks, initial occlusion, and physical manipulation, with sensitivity studies that quantify the feasibility–diversity trade-off and endpoint error toler-ance. II. RELATED WORK 

Vision-language-action policies. Large-scale language-conditioned robot policies such as RT-1 and RT-2 established a general interface from visual observations and language instructions to robot actions [12], [13]. Recent VLA models instantiate this interface with different action representations: 

π0 and π0.5 use flow matching [3], [5], OpenVLA predicts discrete action tokens [4], and GR00T N1 uses a diffusion-transformer action module [14]. Diffusion policies and alter-native action tokenizations provide additional formulations for visuomotor control [9], [15]–[17]. Despite these archi-tectural differences, each policy depends on the observation– state distribution represented in its training demonstrations. ActiveAlign is orthogonal to these action parameterizations: it preserves the policy architecture while adapting the fine-tuning distribution to the states induced by a deployment-time viewpoint module which offers a more informative view. 2Active and multi-view perception. Active perception [18], [19] and next-best-view methods [20], [21] select sensing actions that improve downstream decisions. Multi-view ma-nipulation methods aggregate or render observations from multiple viewpoints to improve spatial reasoning [22]–[25], while SAM2Act, VISO-Grasp, and ViewActive explore re-lated mechanisms for visual representation and viewpoint selection [26]–[28]. Our approach, instead, physically repo-sitions a wrist camera before invoking a separately adapted VLA, thus studying physical wrist-camera motion, which changes not only the visual input but also the robot configu-ration from which manipulation begins. Its focus is therefore the compatibility between a selected better viewpoint and the downstream policy’s manipulation distribution. Recent robotic systems learn physical sensing motion together with manipulation. Vision in Action couples sensing and bimanual behavior using a 6-DoF neck and VR teleoper-ation [29]; HoMMI combines egocentric human demonstra-tions, hand–eye policies, and whole-body control [30]; and ActiveGlasses uses a dedicated perception arm to coordinate camera and manipulation motion [31]. These systems collect or learn paired sensing and manipulation behavior for spe-cialized active-vision embodiments. In contrast, ActiveAlign uses an existing wrist camera for a single pre-manipulation viewpoint trajectory and connects it to an independently trained VLA through offline demonstration adaptation, with-out altering the VLA architecture or requiring continuous gaze control. GenDoP [32] studies related autoregressive RGBD-and-language camera trajectory generation [33]; our contribution concerns the robot-data adaptation interface. 

Demonstration adaptation and distribution shift. DAg-ger [34] addresses policy-induced state mismatch through iterative collection. Domain randomization and augmentation broaden visual coverage [35]–[37], including through gener-ated images [38]–[40]. ActiveAlign targets a different source of shift: the handoff states produced by a separate view-point policy used at deployment. Demonstration-generation methods reuse recorded behavior to expand the training distribution. MimicGen transforms demonstrations across ob-ject configurations [10], while DemoGen adapts trajectories and point-cloud observations for spatial generalization [11]. ActiveAlign instead adapts demonstrations to the physical endpoints induced by a task-conditioned viewpoint policy. Because camera motion changes both arm configuration and visual observations, geometric endpoint matching alone does not guarantee a valid manipulation continuation. ActiveAlign therefore uses closed-loop alignment and successful replay to validate supervision. Our matched-state and data-matched controls further compare this targeted, deployment-matched adaptation with shorter manipulation suffixes and broader random-view or viewpoint-jitter coverage. III. B. Active Perception Module 

View selection is conditioned on both the task and the scene: the instruction identifies what needs to be observed, while the surrounding geometry determines which camera motion may expose it. The module combines RGB features, depth features, and language features to predict a sequence of camera poses. RGB and depth use separate CLIP branches, and a conditional autoregressive decoder generates quantized pose tokens. The decoded extrinsics are passed to the robot controller, which turns the visual prediction into a candidate sensing motion. C. Observation-Aligned Data Adaptation 

In Algorithm 1, the environment is restored to each demonstration’s starting state. After executing A, we iden-tify the nearest end-effector state within a prefix window 

[αT, βT ]. Closed-loop Cartesian alignment closes the resid-ual gap, after which the recorded manipulation suffix is re-played. Only the alignment segment and suffix of successful attempts are retained; the active approach is not a VLA training target. 

Prefix matching. The default window is [0 .05 T, 0.30 T ].Earlier matching retains more of the original approach; later matching shortens manipulation and may require unrepro-duced scene changes. End-effector proximity does not ensure matching object/contact states, making successful replay a necessary filter. Our window study measures retention, diversity, and success. 

Closed-loop alignment. A recorded adjustment connects the active endpoint to the matched suffix using operational space control (OSC) in simulation and Cartesian control on the robot. The default simulation adjustment budget is 20 steps. Directly replaying the suffix from the unmatched endpoint can accumulate control error, so this transition is included in the adapted trajectory. The VLA therefore receives supervision for leaving the active endpoint as well as following the retained manipulation. Unlike IK repositioning, the alignment segment consists of executable VLA training actions.

IV. EXPERIMENTS 

A. Experimental Setup 

Environments and data. LIBERO [41] provides four suites of 10 tasks, with 50 original demonstrations per task (2,000 total). LIBERO-Occ [42] uses the same suites but occludes the target in the default initial view. The physical evaluation runs on two arm settings (an ARX X5 arm and an ARCHER-Y6 arm), both with scene-level and wrist-mounted Intel RealSense D435 cameras, and 100 original demonstrations. RLBench [43] is used only to visualize zero-shot transfer of the perception module. 

Backbones and training. We evaluate π0, π0.5, OpenVLA, and GR00T N1. We follow the model-specific recommended training recipes, with hyperparameters selected using the validation set. All models share the LIBERO Franka end-effector delta-pose and gripper interface. 

Perturbed-start protocol. Each LIBERO result uses 50 rollouts per task, with horizons of 220, 280, 300, and 520 steps for Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long. Initial end-effector pose coordinates receive zero-mean Gaussian perturbations with standard deviation 0.1 (translation in meters and rotation in radians). This perturbed-start protocol differs from the benchmark’s prescribed initial states and is held fixed across all method conditions. 

Conditions and statistical reporting. Standard trains on original demonstrations and uses no AP; Naive Ac-tive invokes AP only at inference; ActiveAlign trains on observation-aligned adaptations and invokes the same AP module. Data-matched controls fix retained episode count, filtering, suffix length, optimization, and mixing. LIBERO uses the environment completion checker. Physical results report binary counts and Wilson 95% confidence intervals.

C. Physical Evaluation 

Collection and reset. Marked fixtures restore object and container states within 1 cm and 3◦. Predicted camera paths are executed continuously; the recorded end-effector prefix is matched, closed-loop Cartesian control reduces the residual, and the manipulation suffix is replayed. An operator provides safety supervision only: near collision or force limits, the operator can abort but cannot correct a motion, teleoperate, or finish the task. The procedure retains 86 of 100 attempts. Reset and replay are used only for offline data construction.

V. LIMITATIONS ActiveAlign is currently limited by its offline data-construction assumptions. Constructing aligned continuations requires a restorable simulator or a reconstructable physical workcell, as well as demonstration prefixes that remain replayable from the endpoints generated by the viewpoint module. Because training trajectories are retained only after successful alignment and replay, the adapted dataset rep-resents a feasibility-conditioned subset of the deployment endpoint distribution. Difficult endpoints may therefore be underrepresented even though they can still occur at de-ployment. RLBench evidence is perception-only. The current system performs a single viewpoint-selection phase before manipulation. It does not reselect viewpoints in response to moving occluders. Extending observation-aligned adaptation to non-resettable environments, continuous viewpoint selec-tion, and broader physical deployments remains an important direction. 7VI. CONCLUSION ActiveAlign connects the decision of where to observe with the learned behavior needed to act from that observa-tion. By constructing successful continuations from active-view endpoints, it enables existing VLA architectures to benefit from a pre-manipulation sensing phase. LIBERO and physical results show consistent gains, while matched-state and data-matched controls support benefits beyond prefix shortening and generic viewpoint coverage. The resulting lesson is that viewpoint selection and manipulation supervi-sion should be designed together, even when the two policies are trained separately.
