# Foundation Model Driven Optical Flow 

Praroop Chanda 1,3 Suryansh Kumar 1,2,3,4,∗

Visual and Spatial AI Lab 1, VCCM Section College of PVFA 2, Department of ECEN 3, Department of CSCE 4,Texas A&M University, College Station, Texas, USA 

## Abstract 

We investigate how frozen pretrained visual and depth representations support dense optical flow when coupled with a supervised matching network. Building on TTSFlow, this paper studies Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) feature de-coding and one coarse-to-fine refinement stage. Our method fuses features from frozen DINOv2 and Depth Anything V2 models and estimates optical flow through a trainable transformer-based matching pipeline. After optical-flow training on FlyingChairs and FlyingThings3D, it achieves 2.37 endpoint error (EPE) on the Sintel training split, Final pass, compared with 2.52 for the FlowSeek (T) config-uration reported here. Our RoCo-Spring 2026 entry, RoCo-29 · VSAI , obtains a robustness-balanced score of 1.1038, Spring EPE of 1.411, and RobustSpring proxy EPE of 4.739, with a lower balanced score than seven of the twelve listed reference baselines. These results characterize the complete pipeline, which reuses frozen foundation backbones and performs one refinement stage. Code is available at: https://github.com/Visual-and-Spatial-AI-Lab/FFlow 

## 1 Introduction 

Dense optical flow lies at the core of visual perception [43]. It estimates a motion vector for every pixel between consecutive frames. It enables a wide range of applications, including video understanding [8], saliency detection [21], action recognition [62, 33, 45], dense 3D reconstruction [25, 19, 36], and motion modeling [11]. Over the past decade, deep learning-based approaches have dramatically improved the accuracy of optical flow [4, 5, 9, 12, 20, 24, 39, 40, 42, 44, 46, 47, 52, 53, 55, 63, 61]. Notable methods such as RAFT [47] and its variants [51, 12, 34] combine learned representations with repeated flow updates. This motivates studying how pretrained representations can be used within an alternative matching pipeline. In parallel, vision foundation models have reshaped the field of computer vision [31, 2, 17, 35, 7]. Models trained once on internet-scale imaging data now transfer effectively across computer vision tasks such as object detection [64], segmentation [60], depth estimation [57, 58], and geometric reasoning [22]. Rather than retraining specialized architectures for each problem, we increasingly reuse pretrained representations as general-purpose visual priors. These developments motivate reusing pretrained representations while learning the modules needed for optical-flow estimation. Pretrained representations have also been investigated for optical flow, including cross-view pretrain-ing in CroCo v2 [53], depth-informed estimation in FlowSeek [34], and pretrained input encoders in WAFT [50]. Our question is how to combine frozen visual and depth representations within a dense optical-flow pipeline. We evaluate that pipeline on cross-dataset optical-flow benchmarks and the RoCo-Spring corruption benchmark. Appendix C discusses these approaches and the related video-based reconstruction setting of D4RT [59]. This paper extends our TTSFlow framework [3], retaining its combination of frozen DINOv2 and Depth Anything V2 features before matching. The changes studied here are DPT-based decoding of

5. Refinement and Final Upsampling. Following GMFlow [55], we bilinearly upsample the propagated 1/8 flow by two and multiply its displacements by two. The detached flow warps the second-frame fused 1/4 features. The shared transformer processes the first-frame and warped second-frame features. Local matching in a 9 × 9 neighborhood predicts a residual added to the up-sampled flow. Propagation then uses a 3 × 3 neighborhood. Final learned convex upsampling uses 3 × 3 flow neighborhoods and a factor of four, also multiplying displacements by four. One refinement stage means one higher-resolution residual update after global matching, and thus not refinement-free inference.

3.1 Model Generalization 

Table 1 summarizes cross-dataset generalization results after training exclusively on the FlyingChairs and FlyingThings3D datasets, with no dataset-specific fine-tuning applied at test time. On FlyingThings3D validation, our EPE of 3.28 is below RAFT (4.25) and GMFlow without refinement (3.48), but above GMFlow with one refinement (2.80). On the Sintel training split, our Final-pass EPE is 2.37, compared with 2.48 for GMFlow with one refinement and 2.52 for the listed FlowSeek (T) configuration. On Clean, our EPE is 1.26, compared with 1.08 and 1.12, respectively. These results identify a Final-pass advantage within the listed configurations, while the ordering differs on Clean and Things. 

3.2 Result on Sintel and KITTI I. Results on Sintel. Table 2 reports Sintel training-set results after training on Chairs, Things, and TSKH. TTSFlow [3] achieves 0.847, 1.140 EPE on Clean and Final sequence, respectively. On Final, TTSFlow [3] outperforms RAFT, GMFlow without refinement, and FlowSeek, while our model obtains 0.816, 1.13 EPE superseding TTSFlow [3] results. SEA-RAFT achieves the lowest errors on both passes, although training configurations differ: it uses eight L40 GPUs, batch size 32, and 

432 × 960 crops, compared with two RTX 6000 GPUs, batch size 4, and 320 × 896 crops for ours. Both SEA-RAFT and FlowSeek also use additional TartanAir pretraining, whereas our approach reuses frozen foundation backbones without this flow-pretraining stage. Thus, these comparisons establish relative accuracy under the reported protocols, rather than isolate the effects of architecture or training scale. 

II. Results on KITTI. On the KITTI training set (Table 2), TTSFlow [3] achieves 1.99 EPE and 7.40% F1-all, slightly improving on the corresponding GMFlow configuration (2.06 EPE and 7.57%). In comparison, our model achieves 1.248 EPE and 4.01% F1-all, also outperforming GMFlow with one refinement (1.36 EPE and 5.17%). RAFT and SEA-RAFT have lower errors on both metrics. FlowSeek has slightly higher EPE (1.26 versus 1.248), but lower F1-all (3.90% versus 4.01%). Thus, our approach improves on GMFlow at both reported refinement counts, but the accuracy gap to the strongest KITTI baselines remains.

## 4 Conclusion, Limitations, and Broader Impact 

We presented a foundation-driven optical flow framework that reuses frozen visual and depth founda-tion representations for dense correspondence estimation. By combining semantic and geometric priors through lightweight fusion and global matching, our method estimates optical flow in a single forward pass without expensive multi-step test-time refinement, achieving strong cross-dataset gen-eralization and competitive accuracy. The main limitation here is that foundation features are not optimized specifically for optical flow. Consequently, the model may provide inferior results under extreme motion, severe appearance changes, occlusions, or fine motion boundaries. Lightweight multiple refinement, adaptive correction, or multi-frame temporal reasoning could improve robustness while preserving the efficiency of the proposed design. Broadly, this work points toward reducing task-specific training and test-time scaling in dense visual perception. Such approaches may benefit robotics, video understanding, and 3D scene analysis, but optical flow failures in safety-critical settings still require careful validation and uncertainty-aware deployment. 6References

(i) Learning-based Optical Flow. Early works such as FlowNet [6] introduced supervised regression, while PWC-Net [44] incorporated pyramidal cost volumes and warping to improve efficiency and accuracy. Subsequent approaches refined these ideas through multi-scale processing and architectural optimization. RAFT [47] established a dominant paradigm by formulating optical flow as iterative refinement over all-pairs correlations. Its recurrent update operator progressively corrects flow estimates, improving accuracy at the cost of increased inference time. SEA-RAFT [51] further optimized it, introducing probabilistic supervision and rigid-motion pretraining to improve efficiency and generalization, while [16] focused on speed and reduced memory needs. Despite these advances, RAFT and SEA-RAFT rely on multi-step test-time refinement to gain performance. Transformer-based models such as GMFlow [55] and FlowFormer [12] shifted attention toward global reasoning. GMFlow presented optical flow as a global matching problem using attention and softmax normalization, yet requires supervised training and, in practice, benefits greatly from refinement. FlowFormer [12] extended this idea via cost-volume transformers to model long-range dependencies. Our pipeline inherits GMFlow’s transformer, correlation-to-flow formulation, propagation, refinement, and convex upsampling. It replaces the CNN feature extraction path with fused foundation-model features while retaining supervised training of the downstream flow-estimation modules. 

(ii) Geometric Priors in Optical Flow. Incorporating geometric cues into optical flow has long been an attractive direction [43, 54]. Earlier methods relied on piecewise rigid motion assumptions [49, 56], whereas recent learning-based approaches inject depth priors directly into the network. Recently proposed FlowSeek [34] represents an interesting step in this direction. It integrates monocular depth foundation models [58] into a RAFT-style architecture and demonstrates improved cross-dataset generalization. FlowSeek uses depth features and depth-derived motion bases in a recurrent flow-estimation architecture. We instead fuse an intermediate Depth Anything V2 decoder feature with a separate DINOv2 feature stream before correlation-based matching. The foundation models remain fixed, while the downstream transformer and flow-estimation modules learn from optical-flow supervision. This distinction concerns where pretrained features enter the matching pipeline; it does not remove flow-specific learning. 

(iii) Vision Foundation Models for Dense Geometry. Vision foundation models have recently demonstrated remarkable transferability across tasks. Self-supervised models such as DINO [2] and DINO-v2 [31] learn dense, semantically meaningful representations that generalize across domains without explicit supervision. In parallel, depth foundation models such as Depth Anything [57] and its successors [58] achieve strong cross-dataset generalization and produce boundary-aware geometric predictions. Dense prediction architectures such as the Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) [37] further bridge the gap between transformer token representations and spatially structured outputs. DPT-style decoders reassemble intermediate transformer features into multi-scale dense maps, enabling tasks that require fine-grained spatial reasoning. Our visual DPT head decodes intermediate DINOv2 features before they are resampled to the global-matching and refinement grids. It is distinct from the pretrained DPT decoder inside Depth Anything V2. Figure 1 and Appendix G.1 specify both paths, including the exact depth-decoder feature used. 

Direct predecessor and other pretrained architectures. TTSFlow [3] is the direct predecessor of this submission: it already combines frozen DINOv2 and depth-foundation features, fusion, and global matching. Here we study DPT-based visual decoding, one higher-resolution refinement stage, and a Spring-adapted RoCo-Spring entry. The TTSFlow rows in Tables 1 and 2, and the fusion ablation in Table 4, reproduce the predecessor’s reported results rather than new evaluations of the submitted configuration. WAFT [50] uses frozen pretrained input encoders, including Depth Anything V2 and DINOv3 variants, with high-resolution warping and recurrent updates instead of a cost volume. Thus, pretrained encoders are not unique to our method. Our configuration combines two distinct frozen feature streams and uses explicit global correlation followed by one local residual update. CroCo v2 [53] pretrains a transformer through cross-view completion and adapts it to stereo matching and optical flow. Its cross-view pretraining differs from reusing the separately pretrained visual and monocular-depth models studied here. D4RT [59] uses a feed-forward video representation with spatiotemporal queries for dynamic 3D reconstruction and tracking. It provides a related example of unified geometric prediction, whereas our evaluated task is two-frame dense 2D optical flow. These are architectural 13 comparisons; no training-matched accuracy comparison with WAFT, CroCo v2, or D4RT is reported here. 

Baseline evaluation scope. The FlowSeek KITTI row uses the mixed-dataset configuration described in the submitted experiment. It is not evidence from a training-matched comparison with our additional KITTI adaptation. The table therefore compares the reported configurations rather than isolating the effect of the architecture.

G.1 Exact Feature Paths Visual branch. The RGB inputs are normalized and bilinearly resized to 448 × 448 for DINOv2-S. With patch size 14, each of blocks 3, 6, 9, and 12 supplies 32 × 32 patch tokens with 384 channels, excluding the class token. The visual DPT head applies 1 × 1 projections to 48, 96, 192, and 384 channels. Its reassembly operations have spatial factors 8, 4, 2, 1, yielding grids of 

256 2, 128 2, 64 2, 32 2, respectively. Each map is projected to 128 channels by a 3 × 3 convolution, then processed by the DPT fusion blocks. The finest output, 256 × 256 × 128 , is bilinearly resized independently to H/ 8 × W/ 8 and H/ 4 × W/ 4. In code these operations are in DinoV2SFeature 

and the visual DPTHead ; the 1/4 feature is not an interpolation of raw native 1/8 DINO tokens. 

Depth branch. Depth Anything V2-B uses its own DINOv2-B encoder and pretrained depth DPT decoder. The input is resized to 518 × 518 , giving a 37 × 37 patch grid with 768-dimensional encoder features. The feature used for flow is path_1 , the output of depth_head.scratch.refinenet1 ,before output_conv1 . In the released implementation its native size is 296 × 296 × 128 : the final fusion block upsamples the 148 × 148 level by two. This is distinct from the scalar depth output, which is computed but not consumed by the flow estimator. The selected feature is resized to H × W

and passed through three 3×3, stride-2 convolutions, with channel widths 128 → 192 → 256 → 128 .The second and third outputs supply the 1/4 and 1/8 grids. A separate 1 × 1 projection reduces the 

1/4 output from 256 to 128 channels. Image/feature resizing in these paths uses bilinear interpolation with align_corners=False .

Fusion and learned matching. Each scale concatenates two 128-channel features. Fusion consists of a bias-free 3×3 convolution at 256 channels, instance normalization and a leaky ReLU, followed by two residual blocks, each containing two biased 3×3 convolutions, instance normalization, and ReLU activations. A biased 1 × 1 projection gives 128 output channels. The fusion and output projection share weights across frames and scales. The six-block matching transformer uses single-head self- and cross-attention, width 128, and feed-forward expansion factor four. The cross-attention feed-forward network consumes the concatenated source and message, so its widths are 256 → 1024 → 128 . The spatial window partitions are 2 × 2 at the coarse scale and 8 × 8 at the fine scale, with alternating shifted blocks. Positional encoding is added before the transformer. For global propagation, the released code uses Q = ψq (F1) and K = ψk(Q), including the learned projections in the attention affinity. Fine-scale propagation uses independently projected queries and keys from the first-frame feature, within a 3 × 3 neighborhood. The convex-upsampling mask predictor takes the 128-channel first-frame feature concatenated with two flow channels and applies convolutions 130 → 256 (kernel 3) and 256 → 144 (kernel 1). For each of the 4 × 4 subpixels, a softmax over nine neighboring flow vectors supplies the convex weights. Flow-vector values are multiplied by four before aggregation. 

G.2 Frozen Parameters, Gradient Paths, and Counts 

Both pretrained foundation models are frozen. The public constructor loads DINOv2-S from the Hugging Face model and Depth Anything V2-B from the supplied depth checkpoint. The downstream transformer, depth projections, fusion, propagation projections, and upsampling predictor participate in flow-loss backpropagation. The matching transformer, propagation, and convex upsampling follow GMFlow; the depth projections and feature fusion follow the TTSFlow design. Architectural provenance does not, by itself, imply initialization from a pretrained GMFlow optical-flow checkpoint. The released constructor does not load such a checkpoint; subsequent –resume loads the specified FFlow state. 17 Visual-DPT gradient status. The public FFlow.forward wraps the entire dino_extract_ feature call in torch.no_grad() . That call includes the added visual DPT head as well as the frozen DINOv2 encoder. Thus, although the visual DPT parameters have requires_grad=True ,the released forward path does not produce flow-loss gradients for them. Their initial values come from the layer constructors unless overwritten by a resumed FFlow state; no separate pretrained visual-DPT checkpoint is loaded there. This distinction must be resolved against the training snapshot before attributing the reported DPT comparison to a flow-trained visual decoder. Merely count-ing parameters with requires_grad=True would overstate the gradient-connected portion of this release. Optimizer and sampling. The released defaults use AdamW with weight decay 10 −4, gradient-norm clipping at 1, and seed 326. Paired random crops. The training augmentors use random paired crops, not center crops. The same sampled crop is applied to the two images, the flow, and applicable validity information. Spatial resizing also rescales horizontal and vertical flow components. Dense-data crops sample a valid origin after spatial augmentation; the sparse KITTI augmentor samples with vertical/horizontal margins of 20/50 pixels and clips to valid origins, so its distribution is not the same uniform crop distribution. The Spring augmentor has its own padding, resizing, and random-crop procedure. In the released dataset definitions, the log 2 resize ranges are [−0.1, 1.0] for Chairs, [−0.4, 0.8] for Things,

for HD1K, [−0.2, 0.4] for KITTI-only adaptation, and [−1.0, −0.8] for Spring. The augmentors also enforce crop-size feasibility; these ranges alone do not specify the realized scale distribution. KITTI-only adaptation disables flips. 

Evaluation record. The main tables distinguish cross-dataset evaluation from adaptation to datasets subsequently evaluated on their training splits. Predictions, not model weights, are submitted to the challenge server. The public entry identifies the reported benchmark outputs, but the supplied release does not by itself establish the challenge checkpoint hash, inference resizing/padding command, or final unpadding procedure used for that entry. We do not infer those settings from training crop sizes or from the feature-branch input sizes.
