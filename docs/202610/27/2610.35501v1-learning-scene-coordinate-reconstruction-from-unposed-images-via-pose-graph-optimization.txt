Abstract
Learning-based structure-from-motion methods such as
ACE-Zero have demonstrated strong performance in esti-
mating camera poses and scene coordinates from unordered
image collections without requiring ground truth supervi-
sion.
However, the lack of global and multi-view con-
sistency constraints in ACE-Zero can lead to pose drift
and misalignment, particularly in complex or ambiguous
scenes. In this work, we propose a hybrid framework that
integrates pose graph optimization (PGO) into ACE-Zero to
refine camera poses and suppress incorrect refinements. We
construct pose graphs directly from ACE-Zero outputs by
extracting relative pose constraints from predicted scene co-
ordinates. Furthermore, we introduce an uncertainty-aware
optimization strategy by estimating confidence scores us-
ing geometric priors, including epipolar and optical flow
consistencies across views. Our approach improves the ro-
bustness and accuracy of pose estimation, demonstrating
that global geometric reasoning can effectively complement
learning-based inference in structure-from-motion.
1. Introduction
Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and visual localization are
fundamental problems in computer vision and robotics, en-
abling applications such as 3D reconstruction, autonomous
navigation, and augmented reality [16, 32, 41, 45, 53].
Recent advances [8, 49, 50] have introduced learning-
based approaches that bypass traditional feature matching
and bundle adjustment. Among these, ACE-Zero [8] has
emerged as a promising visual localization framework that
estimates camera poses and scene coordinates directly from
unordered image collections, without requiring ground truth
or explicit geometric supervision.
As shown in Fig. 1, ACE-Zero’s reliance on local neural
refinement can lead to locally optimal but globally inconsis-
tent poses, particularly in large-scale or ambiguous scenes.

To address these limitations, we propose a hybrid frame-

ACE-Zero does not produce explicit pairwise pose con-

Furthermore, we extend our framework with uncertainty-
aware optimization, which incorporates confidence esti-
mates into the graph refinement process. This allows the op-
timizer to weigh constraints based on their reliability, sup-
pressing the influence of uncertain predictions and improv-
ing robustness. While this requires additional computation
to estimate and propagate uncertainty, it significantly en-
hances the quality of the final pose estimates, especially in
scenes with occlusions, repetitive structures, or low texture.
These conditions often lead to ambiguous or unreliable 2D-
3D correspondences, which can severely degrade pose ac-
curacy. By integrating uncertainty modeling, our approach
reduces the impact of such ambiguities and improves ro-
bustness in these challenging environments.
This work bridges the gap between learning-based and
optimization-based SfM, showing that global geometric
reasoning can complement neural inference to produce
more reliable and consistent camera pose estimates.
We summarize our main contributions as follows:
• A hybrid framework that integrates pose graph optimiza-
tion into ACE-Zero to enforce multi-view and global con-
sistency.
• A method for constructing pose graphs from ACE-Zero
outputs without explicit geometric supervision.
• An uncertainty-aware optimization strategy that improves
robustness by accounting for prediction confidence.
• Our framework achieves more reliable camera poses esti-
mates while requiring on average half the number of iter-
ations compared to ACE-Zero alone.
2. Related Work
Traditional SfM pipelines [19, 30, 31, 39] rely on feature
matching, triangulation, and bundle adjustment to estimate
camera poses and reconstruct 3D scenes. While effective,
these methods are sensitive to textureless regions, occlu-
sions, and large viewpoint changes.
Learning-based SfM approaches [8, 49, 50] aim to over-
come these limitations by directly regressing scene coor-
dinates or camera poses from the images. Notably, ACE-
Zero [8] estimates camera parameters from unordered im-
age collections using a neural scene coordinate regression
model.
By predicting 3D coordinates directly from im-
ages and using reprojection-based losses instead of ground-
truth supervision, ACE-Zero enables self-supervised learn-
ing without relying on explicit feature matching or pose pri-
ors. However, we observe that ACE-Zero can suffer from
pose drift and misalignment in complex scenes. We hy-
pothesize that this is due to the absence of global consis-
tency constraints, as its refinement is purely local and does
not leverage sequential or pairwise structure. To address
this, we propose a hybrid framework that integrates pose
graph optimization to enforce multi-view consistency and
improve robustness.

DeepSFM [51] and GraphSfM [10] incorporate neural
architectures to replace traditional submodules like trian-
gulation and pose averaging. These methods often assume
structured inputs (i.e. image sequences or reliable initial-
ization) which inherently provide stronger geometric priors
and facilitate global consistency.
In contrast, ACE-Zero
operates on unordered image sets without pose supervision,
making it more susceptible to global misalignment.
Pose Graph Optimization (PGO) is a core technique in
SLAM and global SfM. It refines camera poses by mini-
mizing the inconsistency between relative transformations.
Each node in the graph represents a camera pose, and
each edge encodes a relative constraint derived from im-
age pairs. Optimization is typically performed over the Lie
group SE(3) or its variants, such as Sim(3) when scale am-
biguity is present [2, 15]. PGO has been widely adopted
in SLAM systems like ORB-SLAM [29] and GTSAM-
based pipelines [14], where it helps enforce loop closure
and correct accumulated drift. Recent works have explored
efficient initialization [2], robust estimators, and graph-
theoretic principles to improve scalability and convergence.
However, integrating PGO into learning-based SfM frame-
works remains underexplored, especially in settings without
ground truth or sequential data.
Uncertainty-aware optimization incorporates uncertainty
into the pose estimation for both object-centric scenar-
ios (e.g., single-instance localization) and scene-level re-
construction (e.g., multi-view SfM). Methods like UA-
Pose [25] and UnPose [21] leverage uncertainty estimates
to guide optimization and improve robustness to noisy in-
puts. These approaches typically derive uncertainty from
neural networks or diffusion models.
Uncertainty-aware
formulations of PGO have been explored in domains such as
robotics and object pose estimation, where edge weights are
adjusted based on measurement confidence [26, 28]. How-
ever, their application to learning-based SfM remains under-
explored, primarily due to the lack of explicit uncertainty
estimates and structured pairwise constraints in neural mod-
els like ACE-Zero.
Visual localization aims to estimate the camera pose of a
query image within a previously reconstructed scene. Clas-
sical approaches [34–36] rely on matching 2D features from
the query to 3D points in the scene, followed by pose es-
timation via PnP. For large-scale environments, retrieval-
based methods [33, 37] improve efficiency by narrowing
down relevant database images. Learning-based methods
offer alternatives by encoding scene geometry directly into
neural networks. PoseNet [22] regresses absolute poses di-
rectly from images, but struggles with generalization be-


yond training poses [38]. Relative pose regression [1, 46]
and scene coordinate regression [5, 6, 8, 9, 13] improve
flexibility by estimating geometric relationships or 3D co-
ordinates, enabling pose estimation via PnP and RANSAC.
These methods are typically trained in a supervised man-
ner, relying on ground-truth poses or 3D annotations. In
contrast, ACE-Zero [8] trains a visual localizer from un-
posed images. Our work builds on ACE-Zero by extending
it with global consistency via pose graph optimization and
uncertainty-aware refinement.
Hybrid approaches that combine learning-based inference
with optimization-based refinement are emerging as a pow-
erful paradigm in visual localization. HERO-SLAM [52]
and DeepSFM [51] demonstrate that neural representations
can be enhanced by geometric reasoning, leading to im-
proved accuracy and robustness. These methods highlight
the complementary strengths of data-driven and model-
based techniques. In this work, we build on this insight by
integrating pose graph optimization into ACE-Zero, using
learned scene coordinates to construct relative constraints
and geometric priors to estimate confidence. This hybrid
framework bridges the gap between implicit neural repre-
sentations and explicit geometric consistency.
3. Methodology
3.1. Preliminary
Pose graph optimization (PGO). PGO is a widely used
technique in structure-from-motion (SfM) and simultane-
ous localization and mapping (SLAM) to refine camera
poses by minimizing inconsistencies across pairwise rela-
tive transformations. A pose graph is defined as a set of
nodes and edges:
• Nodes: Each node i represents a camera pose Ti =
[Ri|ti] ∈SE(3), where Ri ∈SO(3) is the rotation and
ti ∈R3 is the translation.
• Edges: Each edge (i, j) ∈E represents a relative pose
constraint between nodes i and j, where E is the set of all
such connected pairs. Each edge is associated with a rela-
tive transformation measurement Zij ∈SE(3), typically
obtained from pairwise pose estimation.
The goal of PGO is to find the set of absolute camera
poses {Ti} that best explain the observed relative transfor-
mations, while ensuring global consistency among all poses
throughout the scene. This is formulated as the following
optimization problem:

Learning-based SfM frameworks typically rely on super-
vised training, where ground truth poses or depth maps are
used to guide the learning of scene geometry and camera
motion.
ACE-Zero [8] stands out in that it is unsuper-
vised. It recovers camera poses from unordered image col-
lections by iteratively refining pseudo-labels derived from
initial pose estimates. The method begins by selecting five
random seed images and uses ZoeDepth [4] to predict depth
maps for each. The seed with the highest relocalization rate
across 1000 mapping images is chosen to initialize the re-
construction. These depth maps are used to generate initial
pseudo-labels, which supervise the training of a scene co-
ordinate regression network. This network is then used to
register new images and refine their focal lengths and poses
using MLPs. What distinguishes ACE-Zero is its ability to
bootstrap accurate scene geometry and camera poses with-
out any external supervision, relying solely on internally
generated signals. The system jointly learns scene coordi-
nates and camera poses in a closed loop, where each compo-
nent improves the other over time. As more images are reg-
istered, the scene coordinate regressor is updated and pose
estimates are refined, gradually improving the reconstruc-
tion without requiring ground truth poses, depth maps, or
known 3D geometry. The reconstruction process stops ei-
ther when 99% of the images have been successfully regis-


tered, or when fewer than 1% of new images are registered
compared to the previous iteration.
ACE-Zero refines camera poses using a learned opti-
mizer that operates locally.
Specifically, it updates each
pose based only on image-specific information, without en-
forcing consistency across the entire scene. As a result,
the method lacks global constraints and is prone to accu-
mulating drift, leading to inconsistent camera trajectories.
To address this limitation, we propose integrating global
optimization techniques, such as PGO, which enforce con-
sistency across all estimated poses and improve the overall
accuracy and coherence of the reconstructed scene.

Once a sufficient number of reliable relative pose estimates

the graph corresponds to a camera pose Ti, and each edge
(i, j) represents a relative transformation Zij between two
images. The resulting graph captures the spatial relation-
ships between camera views and serves as the foundation
for global pose optimization.
While pose graph construction enables global refine-
ment, it introduces several challenges.
The accuracy of
the relative pose estimates is sensitive to the quality of the
scene coordinate predictions and the geometric consistency
threshold τ. A low threshold may reject valid correspon-
dences, while a high threshold risks incorporating noisy
matches. Additionally, although RANSAC with PnP is used
to robustly estimate relative transformations from matched
points, its success depends heavily on the presence of a suf-
ficient number of inliers.
In cases where the correspon-
dences are sparse or noisy, it may fail to produce a reli-
able estimate, even when using only geometrically verified
matches. These factors can lead to erroneous or missing
edges in the pose graph, which in turn affect the quality of
global optimization. To address these limitations, we in-
corporate uncertainty-aware mechanisms in the subsequent
optimization stage to improve robustness and reliability.
3.3. Uncertainty-aware pose graph optimization
Incorporating uncertainty into pose graph optimization has
been shown to improve robustness and accuracy, particu-
larly in scenarios where input data may be noisy or am-
biguous. By weighting constraints according to their confi-
dence, uncertainty-aware methods can down-weight unreli-
able measurements and prevent them from disproportion-
ately influencing the global solution.
In traditional SfM
or SLAM pipelines, uncertainty estimates are often derived
from sensor noise models, reprojection errors, or covariance
propagation during bundle adjustment.
However, ACE-
Zero does not provide explicit uncertainty estimates for its
predicted scene coordinates or camera poses. This presents
a challenge for integrating uncertainty into the optimiza-
tion process. To address this, we propose two unsupervised
strategies for estimating confidence in the predicted scene
coordinates based on geometric priors. These confidence
scores are then used to weight the relative pose constraints
in the pose graph, allowing the optimizer to account for the
varying reliability of different correspondences.

Optical flow-based uncertainty metric.

the essential matrix, resulting in misleading epipolar lines–

3.4. Global optimization
Once the pose graph has been constructed and the edge
weights have been adjusted based on uncertainty estimates,
we perform global optimization to refine the camera poses.
Specifically, we apply pose graph optimization at every iter-
ation of the ACE-Zero framework to continuously improve
the global consistency of the estimated trajectory. This iter-
ative refinement ensures that local updates to scene coordi-
nates and camera poses are propagated throughout the entire
graph, mitigating drift and reinforcing spatial coherence.
By integrating pose graph optimization into each itera-
tion of ACE-Zero, we enable consistent global refinement
that complements local updates, resulting in improved ac-
curacy and stability of the reconstructed camera trajectory.
4. Experiments
Implementation details. Our pipeline is based on the pub-
lic implementation of ACE-Zero [8] and all hyperparam-
eters are kept in their default settings.
All experiments
are initialized using ZoeDepth [4] and executed on a sin-
gle NVIDIA 3090 GPU. We initialize the geometric con-
sistency threshold at τ = 0.2m and progressively reduce
it to 0.05m over training iterations to enforce stricter cor-
respondence checks as predictions become more accurate.
We set α1 = 0.4 and α2 = 0.6, and scale the information
matrix using the median confidence value for numerical sta-
bility during optimization. During pose graph optimization,
a tight prior is applied to the first pose using a noise model
with a standard deviation of 10−6 for all components. Be-
tween factors are modeled with standard deviation of 0.1
for rotational and translational components. To improve ro-
bustness against outliers, we employ a Huber loss function
with a scale parameter of 1.0, which reduces the influence
of large residuals while preserving sensitivity to small er-
rors. For nodes that remain unconnected after edge filter-
ing, we add loose priors using a standard deviation of 10 for
all components to prevent optimization failure without in-
troducing strong constraints. The final optimization is per-
formed using Levenberg–Marquardt optimizer.
Baselines. We treat COLMAP-generated poses as pseudo
ground truth. Besides default settings, we also report re-
sults using the recommended parameter settings for large
image collections, which we denote as COLMAP (fast).
We compare against our most relevant method, ACE-Zero
(ACE0) [8] as well as two neural SLAM approaches, i.e.
DRIOD-SLAM [44] and VGGT-SLAM [27].
Datasets. We follow the evaluation protocol from [8] and
experiment on three benchmarks: 7-Scenes [40] for relocal-
ization, Mip-NeRF 360 [3] for view synthesis, and Tanks
and Temples [24] for 3D reconstruction.
Evaluation metrics.
As obtaining ground truth camera
poses in real-world settings is challenging, pseudo ground
truth camera poses are estimated rather than measured.
Consequently, some methods may outperform COLMAP in
certain scenes which aligns with prior findings that pose er-
ror computed against COLMAP can be misleading. Follow-
ing established methods [8, 23], we assess pose quality in
a self-supervised manner using novel view synthesis [47].
Specifically, each method estimates poses for all images in
a scene. We then split the images into training and test sets,
train a Nerfacto [42] model on the training set, and syn-
thesize views for the test poses. The synthesized images
are compared to the test images using peak signal-to-noise
ratio (PSNR). Pose quality influences PSNR in two ways:
accurate training poses enable the NeRF model to learn a
consistent scene representation, while accurate test poses
ensure alignment between synthesized and original views.

5. Conclusion
We presented a hybrid framework that integrates pose graph
optimization into ACE-Zero for learning scene coordinate
reconstruction from unposed images. By progressively en-
forcing global consistency and incorporating uncertainty-
aware weighting, our approach significantly improves pose
accuracy and robustness across diverse datasets. This work
demonstrates that combining neural inference with geo-
metric reasoning yields scalable and reliable reconstruc-
tions. Despite strong results, our method has several limita-
tions. First, uncertainty estimation relies on heuristic met-
rics, which may not generalize well to highly dynamic or
textureless scenes. Second, the hyperparameters requires
manual tunning. Third, while our approach improves global
consistency, it does not jointly optimize scene structure,
leaving room for future work on full bundle adjustment.

A. Extended implementation details and dis-
cussion
Overall algorithm.
We present the complete pseudocode
algorithm in Algorithm 1. Our key contribution is to sup-
press incorrectly refined camera poses, enable a more ac-
curate and globally consistent set of poses. This improved
alignment allows the scene coordinate regression network
to be trained on a more reliable pose set in subsequent it-
erations. Consequently, the overall reconstruction process
converges faster by reaching accurate camera poses earlier,
supported by pose graph optimization.
Impact of graph sparsity or connectivity on optimization
quality.
Pose graph connectivity plays a critical role in the
success of global optimization. A sparse graph, where only
a few reliable edges exist, limits the propagation of global
constraints and can lead to fragmented or weakly consis-
tent pose estimates. Conversely, an overly dense graph may
include noisy or incorrect edges, which can degrade opti-
mization quality by introducing conflicting constraints. We
observed that:
• Sparse Graphs: When the geometric consistency thresh-
old τ is set too low, many valid correspondences are re-
jected, resulting in fewer edges. This reduces the opti-
mizer’s ability to enforce global consistency, especially
in large-scale scenes with repetitive structures.
• Dense Graphs:
A high τ admits more matches, in-
creasing connectivity but also introducing outliers. These
noisy edges can bias the optimization, even when robust
loss functions are applied.
• Balanced Connectivity: Our experiments (see Fig. 5(b)
in the main paper) show that τ = 0.1 m achieves the best
trade-off between accuracy and connectivity. This set-
ting provides sufficient edges for global consistency while
minimizing the inclusion of unreliable matches.
Can the uncertainty-aware metrics provide supervision
signal to model training?
Our current framework uses
uncertainty-aware metrics exclusively for weighting con-
straints during pose graph optimization. These metrics—
epipolar error and optical flow consistency—quantify ge-
ometric reliability between image pairs without requiring
ground truth poses. This raises an interesting question: can
these metrics also serve as supervision signals for training
the ACE model? We summarize the potential benefits:

One major challenge is non-differentiability: epipolar
distance and optical flow errors involve discrete matching
and RANSAC steps, making direct backpropagation diffi-
cult. Additionally, these metrics depend on estimated poses,
which may be inaccurate during early iterations, potentially
introducing bias into the training process. Finally, comput-
ing optical flow and epipolar constraints for all image pairs
during training would significantly increase computational
overhead, making the approach less practical for large-scale
datasets.
Future work could explore differentiable approximations
of epipolar and flow consistency, enabling their integration
into end-to-end training.
Another promising direction is
to incorporate confidence-based weighting into ACE’s re-
projection loss, allowing uncertainty-aware metrics to in-
fluence optimization directly.

Full runtime breakdown.
For a 200 images frames
dataset, our additional pose graph optimization step re-
quires approximately 30 seconds per reconstruction itera-
tion, compared to 150 seconds for vanilla ACE-Zero. Of
this 30-second overhead, the actual optimization accounts
for only 0.016 seconds, while the remaining time is spent
on graph construction and uncertainty metric computation.
The scalability of this step depends primarily on graph
construction rather than optimization. The optimization it-
self is highly efficient, as it operates on a sparse graph and
optimizes only camera poses, resulting in near-linear com-
plexity with respect to the number of nodes and edges. In
contrast, graph construction involves pairwise consistency
checks and uncertainty metric computation, which can scale
quadratically with the number of images if all pairs are
considered. This explains why graph construction domi-
nates the runtime: it requires dense correspondence veri-
fication and optical flow estimation across multiple image
pairs, whereas the optimization step leverages sparse fac-
tor graphs and converges rapidly. For larger datasets, prun-
ing strategies or retrieval-based filtering will be essential to
maintain scalability.

C. Additional qualitative examples
Epipolar-based uncertainty metric (Fig. 10).
To further
analyze the behavior of the epipolar-based uncertainty met-
ric, we apply synthetic perturbations to estimated camera
poses. Specifically, we introduced rotational perturbations
around the z-axis and translational perturbations along the
z-axis to simulate varying degrees of pose deviation. For
each pose, we randomly sampled 100 predicted 3D scene
coordinates and reprojected them into the paired image.
We then drew the corresponding epipolar lines and dis-
carded any second-view projections that fell outside the im-
age boundaries to ensure valid comparisons.
Fig. 10 il-
lustrates qualitative results on two datasets (7-Scenes and
Tanks and Temples), where reprojected points and their
epipolar lines are color-coded by error magnitude (green =
low, red = high). Interestingly, in some cases, the highest
epipolar error occurs when no perturbation is applied, sug-
gesting that the original pose estimate is inaccurate. Intro-
ducing small rotational perturbations can reduce this error,
while translation perturbations occasionally lead to similar
effects. These observations indicate that epipolar error is
sensitive to interactions between pose components and does
not consistently correlate with absolute pose accuracy. Con-
sequently, we interpret this metric as a heuristic confidence
indicator rather than a strict geometric validator.
