Segmentation of Moving Objects During Camera
Motion Using the OWL Function
Daniel Raviv
Juan D. Yepes
Electrical Engineering and Computer Science Department
Florida Atlantic University, Boca Raton, Florida, USA
{ravivd, jyepes}@fau.edu
Abstract—We present a principled, analytical framework for
segmenting multiple independently moving rigid objects during
single camera motion, built directly upon the OWL perception
function [1]. OWL encodes two fundamental visual motion
cues, perceived looming L and perceived rotation ω, into a
single closed-form representation of relative 3D structure. A
key consequence of this structure is that the unit direction of
relative translation ˆt is analytically recoverable per image point
from L and ω alone. All points belonging to a non-rotating
rigid body share the same ˆt, whereas points on different rigid
bodies generally yield distinct values. This furnishes a natural
segmentation criterion: clustering per-point ˆt estimates partitions
the visible scene into independently moving rigid bodies. Building
on the 2D complex form of OWL, we derive the framework in
3D using quaternions, for a single moving camera. The approach
is parallel, camera rotation-invariant, and requires no training
data, no depth information, and no semantic priors.
Index Terms—motion segmentation, rigid body motion, vi-
sual looming, OWL function, Segment Anything, quaternions,
autonomous navigation, robotics.
I. INTRODUCTION
A. Conceptual Explanation
A central challenge in autonomous navigation and robotics
is the real-time identification and separation of multiple inde-
pendently moving objects in a dynamic scene, without prior
knowledge of the environment, without depth information, and
without trained models.
The OWL function [1] establishes that two instantaneous
per-point visual motion cues, perceived looming L and per-
ceived rotation ω, together encode the complete ratio between
the relative translational velocity and range from a single
camera to any visible 3D point. This ratio can be computed
from the image sequence, together with the camera’s rotation
rate, without any prior knowledge of the scene.
The present paper develops a further consequence of this
mathematical structure: the unit direction of relative transla-
tion ˆt is analytically recoverable per point from L and ω alone.
Points on the same rigid body share the same ˆt; points on
different rigid bodies produce generally different values. This
is the segmentation foundation developed here.
For each trackable image point, L and the magnitude of ω
fix the angle α between the relative translation direction ˆt and
the line of sight to that point, so ˆt lies on a cone around that
line of sight. Points on the same rigid body produce cones
that share a common direction, ˆt, which gives an intuitive
geometric picture. When the full perceived rotation vector is
available, as it is from de-rotated optical flow, a closed-form
quaternion expression recovers ˆt from each point indepen-
dently, and segmentation becomes a clustering problem on the
unit sphere.
OWL stands in contrast to learning-based and foundation-
model segmentation approaches, including the Segment Any-
thing Model (SAM) [2] and its video extension SAM 2 [3],
which require large-scale training data and provide no analyt-
ical relationship between visual motion and scene geometry.
Once objects are segmented by ˆt, the OWL representation or
its 3D extension RoT can be applied per segment to recover
a scaled 3D point cloud for each independently moving body.
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Motion Segmentation Methods
Motion segmentation has a long history in computer vision.
Classical factorization methods [4] decompose image mea-
surement matrices under affine camera assumptions. Costeira
and Kanade [5] extended factorization to multiple bodies.
The Generalized Principal Component Analysis (GPCA) of
Vidal et al. [6] provides a polynomial algebraic framework
for segmenting subspace-structured data. Sparse Subspace
Clustering (SSC) [7] exploits the self-expressive property of
unions of subspaces. Spectral clustering [8] and graph-cut
formulations group pixels by motion similarity. These methods
fit global models, such as subspaces or affine factorizations,
jointly across many trajectories, and are sensitive to noise and
outliers. OWL-based segmentation instead recovers ˆt analyti-
cally and independently at each point from the cues L and ω,
with no global model fitting.

C. Semantic, Instance, and Panoptic Segmentation
Semantic segmentation assigns a class label to every
pixel. Instance segmentation distinguishes individual object
instances. Mask R-CNN [14] established the dominant two-
stage paradigm. Mask2Former [15] unifies semantic and in-
stance segmentation using masked cross-attention. Panoptic
segmentation [16] combines both tasks. These methods are
fundamentally category-driven: they distinguish objects by
what they are rather than by how they move, and cannot
generalize to categories unseen during training. OWL-based
segmentation is category-independent and motion-driven: any
independently moving rigid body is generally naturally seg-
mented by ˆt, with no concept of object class and no training
required.
D. Foundation Models for Segmentation
SAM [2] is a foundation model for promptable segmen-
tation, trained on over one billion masks, generating high-
quality segmentation masks in zero-shot fashion given a point,
bounding box, or mask prompt. SAM 2 [3] extends this
paradigm to video via a streaming transformer with a memory
bank. The contrast with OWL-based segmentation is fourfold.
First, SAM operates on appearance, not motion, so it cannot
distinguish two visually similar objects moving in different
directions. Second, SAM must be prompted, either by a user or
by a dense grid of points in its automatic mode, and its masks
do not indicate which regions move together, whereas OWL-
based segmentation groups points by their motion without
prompts. Third, SAM requires massive training infrastructure,
whereas OWL-based segmentation requires none. Fourth, SAM
produces 2D masks; OWL-based segmentation simultaneously
produces a scaled 3D point cloud per segment. The methods
are complementary: ˆt clusters can serve as motion-grounded
prompts for SAM, and SAM masks can provide spatial regu-
larization for OWL clustering.
E. Biological and Ecological Perception
The OWL framework draws direct inspiration from bio-
logical visual systems. Gibson [17] introduced the ecological
approach to visual perception, emphasizing optic flow as the
primary cue for self-motion and object detection. Neurons
in the fly lobula plate are tuned to specific optic flow pat-
terns [18]. Looming-sensitive neurons in insects detect objects
on collision courses from expanding flow fields [19], a direct
biological analog of the looming cue L in OWL.
The perceived rotation cue ω has direct biological correlates
across species. In the fly, FD (figure-detection) cells in the
lobula plate are specialized for relative motion between an
object and the background, with their selectivity shaped by
inhibitory input from HS (horizontal system) cells via CH
interneurons, a circuit that effectively subtracts the global
rotational component from local motion signals, isolating the
translational residual [20]. Only the translational components
of optic flow contain spatial information, a principle the
fly visual system enforces neurally by separating rotation-
encoding (HS/VS) cells from de-rotated translational flow (FD
cells), mirroring OWL’s distinction between camera rotation
Ωand perceived rotation ω. In primates, area MSTd encodes
optic flow across a continuous spiral space combining radial
and rotational components [21], with the full spiral-space
population providing more accurate self-motion estimates than
expansion-tuned neurons alone [22], consistent with the brain
treating ToR = (L, ω) as a primitive joint representation
rather than decomposing L and ω into separate channels.
III. METHOD
A. Assumptions
The following assumptions define the geometric scope of
the framework.
1) The scene consists of relatively moving objects, one of
which can be the stationary environment.
2) Each rigid body translates without rotating. The camera
may both rotate and translate; its rotation is removed by
de-rotation (Section III-D).
3) Each rigid body (including the stationary environment)
has a distinct unit translation direction ˆtk relative to the
camera.
4) The relative translation is nonzero (t̸ = 0), so that L2 +
ω2 > 0.

E. Segmentation by ˆt Clustering
Given per-point ˆt estimates recovered via (9), segmentation
proceeds by clustering: all points sharing the same ˆt generally
belong to the same rigid body. In practice, since the theoretical
assumption of purely translational motion per object holds
only approximately, ˆt estimates from the same body form a
tight cluster on the unit sphere, while estimates from different
bodies form well-separated clusters. The number of clusters
generally corresponds to the number of independently moving
bodies in the scene, including the stationary background.
Any clustering method on the unit sphere can be used. In
the results of Section IV, a pixel is labeled as moving (white in
the binary masks) when ˆt·ˆtbg < 0.999, i.e., when its direction
deviates from the background direction ˆtbg by more than about
2.6◦.
Since ˆt is computed independently per point (requiring only
local L and ω values), the entire pipeline is fully parallel
and real-time compatible. Camera rotation is compensated by
construction through the de-rotation of the optical flow, so
the segmentation result is independent of camera ego-rotation.
Camera translation, by contrast, enters every ˆt, since ˆt is the
direction of the camera’s translation relative to each body. It
should be noted that rigid bodies whose relative velocities are
parallel and point the same way share the same ˆt and cannot
be separated by this criterion alone; Assumption 3 excludes
this case.

V. CONCLUSIONS
This paper has extended the OWL perception framework [1]
to the problem of segmenting multiple independently moving
rigid objects from visual motion cues observed by a single
camera. The central analytical result is that the unit translation
direction ˆt, recoverable per image point from the perceived
looming L and perceived rotation ω, is a signature of rigid
body membership: all points on the same non-rotating rigid
body share a common ˆt, while points on distinct bodies
yield different values, except in the degenerate configuration
excluded by Assumption 3.
The resulting framework is geometrically principled, depth-
free, training-free, and fully parallel. It exploits the quaternion
structure of OWL to recover ˆt from a single point when the
angular velocity vector ω is available, as it is from de-rotated
optical flow; when only the magnitude ω is known, three
points are needed, which gives an intuitive picture of ˆt as
the common direction of three cones. Camera rotation is com-
pensated by construction through the de-rotation identity for
ω. Once segmented, each cluster can be passed independently
to the OWL/RoT reconstruction of [1], which would yield
a scaled 3D point cloud per body, each with its own scale
factor 1/|tk|. Implementing this per-segment reconstruction is
ongoing work.
The present work focuses on the instantaneous derivation
of ˆt from a single time instant. An important direction
for future work is to observe ˆt over time: tracking how ˆt
evolves across frames will enable more robust segmentation,
noise rejection, predictions and the detection of objects that
change their direction of motion. This temporal extension,
together with integration with foundation-model pipelines such
as SAM 2 [3], can be the focus of subsequent work.
Together with [1], this paper forms the second element of
a unified, perception-grounded pipeline: the OWL paper [1]
establishes scaled 3D reconstruction and heading recovery, and
the present paper establishes multi-body segmentation as a
companion step. Applying OWL/RoT independently to each
segment, the subject of ongoing work, completes the pipeline.
Both steps require no training data and no depth sensors; they
need only the camera intrinsics and the camera’s rotation rate.
