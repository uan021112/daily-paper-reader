Abstract
The large diffeomorphic deformation metric mapping (LDDMM) framework offers
numerous techniques for analyzing shapes and images. Surface matching com-
putes a non-rigid, smooth, and invertible mapping that registers one surface to
another. The computed deformation may contain rigid body motion, which fre-
quently does not represent meaningful shape differences but is due to external
factors like imaging modalities, such as the object’s positioning during image
acquisition. We present a simple extension to the LDDMM framework’s surface
matching that uniquely decomposes the computed deformation into rigid body
modes (RBM) and RBM-free motion by introducing a suitable projection. This
enables a focused analysis of each component individually and additionally helps
to preserve the surface discretization quality. Three sets of examples illustrate
our approach. A small-scale example demonstrates the proposed method’s effects,
followed by an example using a single pair of heart geometries, and finally an
example involving a cohort of 98 heart shapes.

1 Introduction
Establishing spatial correspondence between medical images and the embedded
anatomical geometries is critical in many medical applications, offering significant
benefits for diagnosis, treatment planning, and monitoring. The geometries are typ-
ically derived through manual segmentation and are represented by their surface.
Several methods have been proposed to perform the registration task. Their catego-
rization is extensive, and a complete account is not provided here but can be found in
[31]. However, we outline the key ideas of two commonly used registration categories:
landmark-based and landmark-free registration.
In the landmark-based approach, usually, a trained expert places corresponding
landmarks in all images to track their position across a dimension, e.g., time. The
geometry change of interest is subsequently interpolated. However, the precise place-
ment of landmarks in anatomical images is challenging. It requires expert knowledge
and suffers from operator bias. Additionally, pathological conditions can severely alter
the anatomy, making the identification of consistent reference points difficult. A pure
landmark description of the geometry change cannot represent intricate local changes.
Landmark-free approaches describe the evolution of geometry in an integral way.
This captures the motion of the whole geometry by utilizing sophisticated surface rep-
resentations. A well-established method can be contained in the LDDMM framework
and is called surface matching [8, 28]. It defines a smooth and invertible mapping
between segmented surfaces where no point-to-point correspondence is assumed or
established. This mapping is expressed with a spatial motion field. The term LDDMM
was first proposed in [6] and has since evolved into a widely adopted and extended
framework. A comprehensive mathematical treatment of shapes and diffeomorphic
matching within this framework is given in the monograph by Younes [32]. A his-
torically motivated literature overview is given in [24]. LDDMM has a wide field of
applications which ranges from palaeoanthropology [9], to brain [11, 14, 21, 27, 30]
and heart [18, 19] structures to name a few examples. Partial surface registration
has been studied in [1, 7, 20]. [3] simultaneously incorporates multiple objects and
[2] enforces inequality constraints within a deformation model, including affine align-
ment. [27] reconstructed dense 3D anatomical coordinates from 2D measurements
using projective LDDMM.
In the examples above and in general, RBMs may be of interest for certain appli-
cations, but often need to be separated from shape-changing deformations. This is
because RBMs may arise from factors like patient positioning (e.g., lying down,
standing), breathing, or other external influences rather than intrinsic shape changes.
Consequently, isolating RBM motion from the total motion can significantly enhance
the information value of subsequent statistical and computational analyses. Other
approaches have addressed rigid motions within diffeomorphic registration, either by

Instead of adding new terms to the deformation model, in this work, we propose an
extension of the LDDMM framework that explicitly separates RBM motion from the
shape-changing motion within the matching framework. We start with an overview of
the essential aspects of LDDMM for surface matching to introduce necessary notation.
Subsequently, we briefly repeat subspace projections to eliminate rigid body compo-
nents from a given motion field and integrate them into the LDDMM framework.
Finally, we present a small-scale example and an application using heart geometries.
2 Surface matching with separation of rigid body

This section briefly discusses the basics of LDDMM surface matching, following the
works in [13, 14, 28].
2.1 Surface matching framework
The objective is to identify a diffeomorphic transformation through pseudo-time
t ∈[0, 1] deforming a source surface S(t) to optimally represent a target surface T .
Notably, this transformation is not limited to points located on the surfaces but instead
distorts the entire ambient space and, thereby, the surfaces embedded in it. In this
work, we limit ourselves to directly summarizing the spatially discretized framework
and skip going through the established underlying continuum theory for the sake of
brevity. Thus, a surface is represented by a mesh composed of nodes and triangular
elements, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

3 Examples
First, we present a small-scale example featuring simple ellipsoid surfaces to demon-
strate the impact of the decomposition. Next, we show an application using a single
pair of heart geometries. Finally, we present results on a cohort of 98 heart geometries.
We solve all examples with the projection-based method (28) and the standard method
(11) using an L-BFGS [23] optimizer. We discretize the pseudo-time-dependent dif-
ferential equations defining { ˙x, ˙c, ˙α} with ten pseudo-time steps (∆t = 0.1 s) and
solve them with an explicit second-order Runge-Kutta [10, 25] scheme. For the initial
guess we set {α0, ˆτ 0, ω0} to zero. To clarify the solution process, a pseudo-code for
the projection-based method can be found in Alg. 1, see App. F.

4 Discussion and conclusion
This work presents an extension to LDDMM surface matching that separates rigid
body motion (RBMs) and shape-changing motion. RBMs often arise in image and
shape registration due to variations in imaging conditions and can hinder the estab-
lishment of physically meaningful spatial correspondences when intermixed with
shape-changing motion. By isolating shape-changing motion from the rigid transforma-
tions, the proposed method enables a focused analysis of each component individually.
Overall, the proposed method achieved similarly high registration quality. We showed,
for one example, that the additive decomposition of motion components preserves the
surface mesh quality better.
To uniquely decompose motion into RBM and shape-changing components, a pro-
jector is introduced. The projector is constructed from a basis representing translations
and rotations. In this study, all translations and rotations were removed. However,
the projector can be adapted to exclude only selected translation directions or rota-
tion axes by modifying the projector basis (13). Notably, when only translational
components are considered, the projector remains constant over pseudo-time, elimi-
nating the need for recomputation at each pseudo-time step. The rotation basis vectors
are derived from linearized rotations, which necessitates careful adjustment of the
pseudo-time step to ensure sufficiently small rotation increments.
19


For simplicity and to provide an easily interpretable form, the RBM component
was parameterized using pseudo-time-constant translation and a rotational compo-
nent defined in terms of Euler’s angles. We note that Euler’s angles can suffer from
non-uniqueness, but, in our examples, this did not impede the solution of the surface
matching problem. Also, in our applications, we want to remove the RBM compo-
nent to exclusively focus on the analysis of the shape-changing motion, which, of
course, might be different in other settings. Alternatively, a rotation representation
based on the Lie algebra so(3) or quaternions can be chosen at the cost of increased
implementation complexity and reduced interpretability.
To assess the similarity between the registered surfaces, a surface matching metric
identical to the standard LDDMM approach was employed in the objective function.
However, the kinetic energy term was modified to be able to penalize RBM and shape-
changing motion individually. We chose to hardly penalize the RBM component to
focus on the shape-changing motion, which is responsible for surface mesh distortion.
In the single pair of heart geometries example in Sec 3.2, we showed that the pro-
posed method can effectively reduce surface distortion while promoting a more uniform
transformation.
Surface matching is a technique frequently used in various applications, particu-
larly in conjunction with biomechanical models. Accurately mapping organ geometries
between different states is crucial for calibrating physical parameters. To ensure ade-
quate coverage of the region of interest, control points were initially distributed on a
coarse grid encompassing the source surface. More sophisticated control point place-
ment and hyperparameter optimization strategies exist, but have not been discussed
here.
Our method resulted in a slightly higher node-to-node distance (Table 2) compared
to the standard method, which may be attributed to neglecting the cross-terms in
the kernel split (Section 2.3).
As the difference is significantly below the imaging
resolution and segmentation accuracy, we consider it negligible for our purposes.
Due to the very high registration quality, the decomposed motion’s flexibility, and
the improved mesh preservation, the proposed approach is expected to assist shape
analysis, especially for biomechanical problems.
