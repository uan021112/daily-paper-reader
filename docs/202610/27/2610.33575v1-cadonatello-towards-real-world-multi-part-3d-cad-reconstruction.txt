# CADonatello : Towards Real-World Multi-Part 3D CAD Reconstruction 

## Ahmet Serdar Karadeniz 1, Dimitrios Mallis 1, Parsa Akbari Chianeh 1, Kseniya Cherenkova 2,Anis Kacem 1, Djamila Aouada 1

Abstract 

Existing methods for CAD reconstruction from 3D scans achieve strong performance on standard benchmarks, yet their effectiveness often degrades in real-world scenarios where ob-jects consist of multiple parts and scan artifacts such as noise. A key reason is that current CAD reconstruction datasets pre-dominantly contain single-part models and clean geometric inputs, limiting the ability of learned models to generalize to realistic settings. In this work, we introduce CADonatello ,a part-aware framework for reconstructing parametric CAD models from real-world 3D scans. Our approach first decom-poses the input geometry into structurally meaningful compo-nents using a foundation-scale 3D part segmentation model, and then reconstructs each component independently through a cross-section–driven pipeline that performs sketch parame-terization followed by an extrusion. To improve the robustness of sketch parameterization to scan-derived cross-sections, we further introduce CC3D Cross-Sections , a dataset of paired rasterized cross-section images from realistic scans and their corresponding parametric sketches derived from aligned CAD models. This dataset enables effective domain adapta-tion from clean CAD sketch images to scan-derived cross-section images. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed approach improves generalization to noisy scans and enables reliable reconstruction of complex multi-part objects across multiple real-world CAD reverse engineering bench-marks. 

## Introduction 

Computer-Aided Design (CAD) is a fundamental step in manufacturing everyday objects, where components are typ-ically designed in CAD software before production. In prac-tice, however, the original CAD models are not always avail-able once an object has been manufactured due to legacy pro-cesses, data loss, proprietary restrictions, or undocumented modifications (Bénière et al. 2013). With the widespread availability of 3D scanning technologies, physical objects can be digitized as point clouds or meshes (Cherenkova et al. 2023). However, these geometric representations do not di-rectly provide editable, parametric CAD models. Recovering CAD models from scan data, i.e. , CAD reverse engineering, is therefore essential for redesign, modification, and remanu-facturing within standard CAD workflows (Khan et al. 2024). Designers manually reconstruct the model using parametric sketching and feature-based opera-tions such as extrusion and revolution (Mallis et al. 2023). While effective, this process is inherently time-consuming and labor-intensive, particularly for geometrically complex or multi-part assemblies. Automating CAD reconstruction from 3D scans has thus emerged as a key research chal-lenge (Wu, Xiao, and Zheng 2021; Uy et al. 2022; Ren et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2024; Rukhovich et al. 2025; Karadeniz et al. 2025b; Kolodiazhnyi et al. 2025). Formally, the goal is: given a 3D scan represented as a point cloud or mesh, predict the parametric CAD construction sequence that reproduces the observed geometry. Such a sequence typ-ically comprises 2D sketches defined by closed profiles of parametric primitives (e.g., lines, arcs), followed by 3D solid modeling operations such as extrusion and revolution. Recent CAD datasets, such as DeepCAD and Fusion 360 reconstruction (Wu, Xiao, and Zheng 2021; Willis et al. 2020), have accelerated research on this problem by provid-ing access to parametric construction sequences, typically structured as sketch-and-extrude programs. These resources enabled learning-based approaches that map 3D scans to CAD operation sequences either through geometry-grounded extrusion reasoning (Uy et al. 2022; Karadeniz et al. 2025b) or via end-to-end program inference (Dupont et al. 2024; Khan et al. 2024; Rukhovich et al. 2025; Kolodiazhnyi et al. 2025). Although these approaches achieved strong perfor-arXiv:2610.33575v1 [cs.CV] 24 Oct 2026 mance on benchmark datasets, their generalization capabil-ity remains inherently constrained by the distributional scope of their training data. First, existing datasets predominantly consist of single-part CAD models with relatively simple feature hierarchies. Consequently, learned models are biased toward such simplified design patterns and struggle to scale to complex, multi-part assemblies or industrial-grade geome-tries that deviate from the training distribution. Second, these datasets typically rely on synthetic, clean geometric inputs and do not provide realistic 3D scans containing acquisition artifacts such as noise, occlusions, and incomplete surfaces. In this work, we argue that enabling real-world CAD re-verse engineering of complex, multi-part assemblies from noisy 3D scans requires moving beyond learning a direct mapping from input geometry to CAD sequences using the aforementioned limited datasets. As shown in Figure 1, we adopt a structural decomposition paradigm that mirrors con-ventional design practice: we first segment the input geom-etry into semantically meaningful parts, reconstruct each part parametrically, and subsequently assemble the recov-ered components. To improve generalization beyond current CAD sequence datasets, we rely on models trained on large-scale data for both geometric decomposition and parametric reconstruction. Specifically, we leverage a foundation-scale 3D segmentation model (Ma et al. 2025) to identify part-level structure in the input scan. For parametric reconstruc-tion, we build on the cross-section paradigm of (Karadeniz et al. 2025b) and adapt it to part-level reconstruction through orientation-aware cross-section plane detection, image-level cross-section parameterization, and adaptive extrusion opti-mization. The cross-section parameterizer is pretrained on SketchGraphs (Seff et al. 2020) and further fine-tuned on paired scan-derived cross-sections and BRep sketches from CC3D (Mallis et al. 2023; Cherenkova, Aouada, and Gusev 2020), improving robustness to realistic scanning artifacts. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 1. CADonatello , a novel CAD reverse engineering framework that formulates real-world reverse engineer-ing as a structured, part-aware reconstruction problem, combining foundation-scale 3D segmentation with cross-section–driven parametric part modeling within a unified modular pipeline. 2. CC3D Cross-Sections , a newly curated dataset of 

44 k cross-sections comprising paired rasterized sketches extracted from realistic CC3D scans and their corre-sponding parametric representations derived from aligned BReps. The dataset explicitly captures scan-induced ar-tifacts and geometric distortions at the sketch level, en-abling the learning and evaluation of robust cross-section parameterization under realistic noise. The dataset will be publicly released. 3. Extensive experimental validation in realistic CAD re-verse engineering settings, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on noisy CC3D scans and, for the first time, on complex multi-part assemblies using the Fusion 360 Assembly dataset. 

## Related Work 

3D CAD Reverse Engineering. Early approaches to CAD reconstruction relied on Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) representations (Du et al. 2018; Friedrich et al. 2019; Kania, Zieba, and Kajdanowicz 2020; Sharma et al. 2022; Yu et al. 2022, 2023; Ren et al. 2021) based on Boolean operations over primitives, while later works focused on Boundary Rep-resentation (BRep) modeling (Lambourne et al. 2021; Willis et al. 2020; Jayaraman et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2021) to re-cover precise surfaces and topology. More recent methods learn sketch–extrude sequences, either through geometry-grounded approaches (Uy et al. 2022; Ren et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023; Karadeniz et al. 2025b) or language-based in-ference (Khan et al. 2024; Rukhovich et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024). Although effective on existing CAD benchmarks (Wu, Xiao, and Zheng 2021; Willis et al. 2020), these approaches typically reconstruct the entire object directly, which makes them fragile when applied to real-world scans containing multiple components. In contrast, CADonatello operates at the level of structural parts, enabling parametric recon-struction of multi-part objects. 

3D Part Segmentation. Segmenting 3D objects into mean-ingful components is fundamental for geometric understand-ing. Early approaches (Qi et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2024b; Hanocka et al. 2019) focused on supervised learning with part-level annotations (Mo et al. 2019; Li et al. 2022a; Slim et al. 2025; Li et al. 2022b) on point clouds or meshes to predict predefined semantic parts, but generalize poorly to unseen categories. Later work leverages large-scale 2D vi-sion models such as SAM (Kirillov et al. 2023) to bootstrap 3D segmentation (Tang et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025). These approaches in-herit the limitations of 2D priors, often leading to viewpoint ambiguities and inconsistent 3D boundaries (Ma et al. 2025). Recent foundation-scale native 3D models (Ma et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2025) learn part segmentation directly in 3D, en-abling category-agnostic decompositions and generalization to unseen objects. Despite strong results in part segmenta-tion, their potential for CAD reverse engineering remains largely unexplored. CADonatello leverages such models to obtain part-level decompositions of scans and enable part-aware reconstruction of multi-component CAD models. 

Parametric CAD Sketches. CAD sketches play an impor-tant role in 3D CAD reverse engineering, as they enable ed-itability and support controlled modifications of CAD mod-els. The emergence of large-scale sketch datasets (Seff et al. 2020; Ganin et al. 2021) has significantly advanced CAD sketch research, enabling progress in generative sketch mod-eling (Ganin et al. 2021; Seff et al. 2021; Para et al. 2021; Willis et al. 2021b; Chereddy and Femiani 2025), inferring design intent (Casey et al. 2025; Yang and Pan 2022), and image-based sketch parameterization (Ganin et al. 2021; Seff et al. 2021; Para et al. 2021; Karadeniz et al. 2025a, 2024; Wu et al. 2024a; Wang et al. 2024). Image-conditioned ap-proaches (Karadeniz et al. 2024, 2025a) recover paramet-ric sketches from 2D image inputs by predicting geometric primitives and constraints. More closely related to CAD re-verse engineering, (Karadeniz et al. 2025b) infers paramet-ric sketches from scan-derived cross-section images. How-ever, this method relies on synthetic noise augmentations of clean sketch images for training, which may not fully reflect the geometric irregularities present in real cross-sections. In contrast, our sketch parameterization model is fine-tuned on the introduced CC3D Cross-Section dataset with real cross-sections paired with parametric sketches, improving robustness to real-world scans.

## Proposed Method 

CADonatello tackles the problem with a focus on real-world scenarios where input meshes M exhibit complex multi-part assemblies and scanning artifacts. Rather than di-rectly learning the mapping between the input mesh M and the corresponding CAD sequence C = ( Kj , ej )ns 

> j=1

from existing limited datasets, CADonatello first decomposes the input mesh M into a set of K structurally meaningful parts {Mk}Kk=1 . To ensure generalization, CADonatello 

leverages a foundational model to perform this decomposi-tion. Each part Mk = ( Vk, Fk) is then reconstructed in-dependently by estimating a part-level CAD sequence Ck

that reconstructs Mk. For this stage, part reconstruction fol-lows a cross-section-based approach similar to (Karadeniz et al. 2025b), where constrained sketch parameterization is learned at the raster sketch level using a large-scale dataset, and extrusions are inferred directly from the input geome-try. To improve robustness to noisy scanning artifacts, the sketch parameterization module is further fine-tuned on a curated dataset of cross-sections derived from realistic 3D scans. The final CAD model C is obtained by assembling the reconstructed part-level sequences {Ck}Kk=1 according to their original spatial configuration. An overview of the proposed method is illustrated in Figure 2, and the details of the different steps are provided in the following sections. 

Part Decomposition 

To obtain the set of structurally meaningful part meshes 

{Mk}Kk=1 from the input mesh M, we leverage the foundation-scale 3D part segmentation model P3-SAM (Ma et al. 2025), which operates directly on 3D geometry and automatically produces candidate part masks. Unlike prior CAD reconstruction approaches that operate on complete ob-jects or decompose them according to the underlying CAD operations, we use category-agnostic part masks to define structurally meaningful units of parametric reconstruction. P3SAM follows the paradigm of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) (Kirillov et al. 2023) for 2D images, but is designed for 3D data. It is trained to segment 3D models into parts using a large-scale dataset containing more than

## CC3D Cross-Section Dataset 

To bridge the domain gap between clean CAD sketch im-ages and scan-derived cross-section images, we construct a paired cross-section dataset from the original CC3D dataset (Cherenkova, Aouada, and Gusev 2020; Mallis et al. 2023). Given aligned CAD BRep (STEP) models and their corresponding scanned meshes, both representations are sliced using uniformly sampled planes along the canoni-cal x-, y-, and z-axes. For each slicing plane, the BRep model yields parametric curves that are projected to 2D and converted into structured constrained sketches following the SketchGraphs format (Seff et al. 2020). The corresponding mesh produces closed-loop curves, which are projected to 2D and rasterized into binary images. Both representations are centered and normalized within a unit bounding box, re-sulting in paired samples (X, K), where X ∈ { 0, 1}H×W

denotes a scan-derived cross-section image and K the cor-responding BRep-derived parametric sketch. The resulting dataset contains 44k training and 2k testing image–sketch pairs. The introduced CC3D Cross-Section dataset is used to fine-tune the sketch parameterization model and im-prove its robustness to scanning artifacts. Additional details about the dataset are provided in the supplementary material. 

## Experiments 

In this section, we describe the experimental setup and the experiments conducted to assess the performance of CADonatello .

Evaluation Datasets. We evaluate our method on the real-world CC3D (Mallis et al. 2023) testing set containing 

2978 samples and a subset of the Fusion 360 Assembly dataset (Willis et al. 2021a) with 200 assemblies containing 

3780 parts. The constrained sketch parameterization mod-ule is evaluated on the testing set of the introduced CC3D Cross-Sections dataset containing 2k samples. Details of the evaluation datasets are provided in the supplementary. 

Metrics. Median Chamfer Distance, median Edge Chamfer Distance (ECD), and Invalidity Ratio (IR) are used to evalu-ate the quality of 3D CAD reconstruction. Accuracy (Acc), Primitive F1 (PF1), Sketch Chamfer Distance (SCD), and number of connected components (NCC) are used for sketch-level evaluations. Segmentation IoU (Seg. IoU) is used for segmentation performance. Further details on the metrics are provided in the supplementary material. 

Implementation Details. Input meshes are normalized to a unit bounding box and segmented using P3-SAM. Cross-sections are processed by a sketch parameterization network pretrained on SketchGraphs and fine-tuned on CC3D Cross-Sections. Reconstructed parts are instantiated and assembled in FreeCAD. Further implementation details are provided in the supplementary material.

## Conclusion 

In this work, we presented CADonatello , a part-aware CAD reverse engineering method that reconstructs para-metric CAD models from 3D scans by decomposing com-plex objects into structurally meaningful components. By combining foundation-scale 3D part segmentation with a cross-section-driven reconstruction pipeline, our method en-ables robust sketch parameterization and extrusion at the part level. We further introduced the CC3D Cross-Sections 

dataset, which bridges the domain gap between clean CAD sketches and scan-derived cross-sections, improving general-ization to realistic scanning artifacts. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on the CC3D-Complex and Fusion360-Assembly benchmarks while remaining scalable to complex multi-part objects. Similar to prior extrusion-based meth-ods (Khan et al. 2024; Karadeniz et al. 2025b; Rukhovich et al. 2025; Kolodiazhnyi et al. 2025), the current framework supports only extrusion operations; revolutions and sweeps are not modeled. In addition, parts are assembled via direct fusion rather than explicit joint relationships (Willis et al. 2021a). Future work will extend the framework to support additional operations and assembly joint prediction. Acknowledgements

Datasets 

This section describes the evaluation datasets and provides details of the introduced CC3D Cross-Section dataset. 

CC3D Test Set. We evaluate our method on the CC3D (Mallis et al. 2023) dataset, which consists of paired real-world scans represented as meshes and their correspond-ing CAD models. The CC3D test set contains 2978 samples. We further evaluate our method on a more complex subset (CC3D-Complex), where the number of construction steps exceeds 4, containing 1528 samples. 

Fusion 360 Assembly Test Set. To evaluate our approach on assemblies, we use a subset of the Fusion 360 Assembly dataset (Willis et al. 2021a). This subset contains 200 as-semblies comprising a total of 3780 parts. We also evaluate on a more complex subset of 126 assemblies, each contain-ing more than 4 parts. These assemblies represent realistic mechanical designs composed of multiple components. 

Non-Axis-Aligned Extrusions Test Set. To evaluate ro-bustness to extrusions that are not aligned with canonical axes, we construct a synthetic benchmark of procedurally generated CAD models. Each sample is created by stacking a sequence of primitive extrusions with randomly oriented directions in 3D. For each extrusion step, a sketch profile is randomly sampled from primitive shapes including circles, regular polygons ( 3–9 sides), and rectangles with random-ized sizes. The sketch is instantiated on a plane whose nor-mal corresponds to the sampled extrusion direction and is extruded by a length sampled from a uniform distribution in the range [0 .2, 1.2] . Successive sketches are placed on the top face of the previous extrusion, forming stacks of primitives with varying orientations and geometric complexity. The ex-trusion directions are sampled to allow tilts of up to 35 ◦

from the previous direction, producing non-axis-aligned ge-ometries that differ from the canonical orientations common in many CAD datasets. Using this procedure, we generate 

200 solids, each consisting of 2–7 sequential extrusion op-erations with varying profiles, scales, and extrusion lengths, which are exported as STEP and mesh files and used as an out-of-distribution benchmark. 

CC3D Cross-Section Dataset. We introduce the CC3D Cross-Section dataset, constructed from aligned CAD BRep models and real scanned meshes provided in CC3D (Cherenkova, Aouada, and Gusev 2020; Mallis et al. 2023). For each aligned BRep–scan pair, we sample 3 uni-formly spaced slicing planes along each of the canonical x-, 

y-, and z-axes and intersect both representations with these planes. From the BRep representation, each valid connected cross-sectional contour is converted into a parametric sketch of supported primitives in the SketchGraphs format (Seff et al. 2020), while the corresponding mesh cross-section is projected onto the slicing plane and rasterized into a bi-nary image. We filter samples by restricting the number of sketch entities to at most 16 . To ensure geometric consis-tency between the scan-derived cross-section images and the parametric sketches, we compute the sketch Chamfer distance (SCD) between their rasterized representations and discard pairs with SCD > 1. Duplicate sketches are removed. The resulting dataset consists of paired samples comprising a scan-derived cross-section image and its corresponding parametric sketch, which are used to fine-tune the sketch parameterization model and improve robustness to scanning artifacts. Example sketches from the dataset are shown in Figure 5. The final dataset contains 44k training pairs and 2k 

test pairs, with an average of 8.3 primitives per sketch.

The Num-ber of Connected Components measures the structural co-herence of predicted sketches, where a component is defined as a set of primitives whose start and end points coincide. Endpoints that lie within a small tolerance ( 0.01 ) are con-sidered identical to account for numerical noise. Primitive types without explicit endpoints (e.g., circles) are treated as individual components. Ideally, a predicted sketch forms a single connected structure, yielding an NCC value of 1.

Additional Implementation Details 

Input meshes are centered and normalized to a unit bound-ing box. Part segmentation is performed using P3-SAM (Ma et al. 2025) on 30k sampled surface points with a mask con-fidence threshold of 0.95 , and part submeshes are extracted from the predicted face labels. Part meshes with volume be-low 0.1 are discarded. For plane detection, 40 evenly spaced cross-sectional slices are taken by the learned plane detec-tion network after rotating each part into a canonical frame by computing its oriented bounding box. The resulting cross-sections corresponding to the key slice planes are rasterized at a resolution of 128 × 128 and processed by the sketch pa-rameterization network of (Karadeniz et al. 2024), which is pretrained on SketchGraphs (Seff et al. 2020) and fine-tuned for 20 epochs with a learning rate of 3 × 10 −5. The predicted sketches are postprocessed through a sequence of cleanup and refinement steps. Specifically, duplicate and degenerate primitives are removed, and collinear overlapping line seg-ments are merged. The maximum number of primitives per parametric sketch is limited to 16 . Extrusion parameters are optimized using differentiable extrusion over 8 candidate ex-trusion directions for 200 iterations with a learning rate of 

2 × 10 −3. Finally, reconstructed parts are instantiated and combined using Boolean operations in FreeCAD through its Python API (Community 2025).

Additional Dataset and Runtime Clarifications 

Input Mesh Provenance. CC3D contains real-world scanned meshes with acquisition artifacts, whereas the Fu-sion 360 Assembly inputs are meshes triangulated from BRep models. The same reconstruction pipeline is applied to both datasets without modification. Fusion 360 Assembly is not used for fine-tuning and therefore serves as a cross-dataset evaluation setting. 

Constraint Annotations. The parametric sketches ex-tracted for CC3D Cross-Sections contain coincident constraints. The sketch parameterization model retains its broader constraint prediction capability through pretraining on SketchGraphs and continued sampling of SketchGraphs during fine-tuning. 

Part Filtering Statistics. Part meshes with volume below 

0.1 are removed before reconstruction. Across the evalua-tion data, this filtering discards approximately 0.17% of the predicted parts. 

Runtime. Runtime is measured on a system equipped with an NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU and an Intel Xeon Gold 6226R CPU. Averaged over the evaluation set, part segmentation requires approximately 12 seconds per input, while the sub-sequent reconstruction stages require approximately 15 sec-onds, resulting in a total runtime of approximately 27 seconds per assembly. 

Failure Case Analysis 

We analyze common failure cases of CADonatello . Fig-ure 7 shows representative examples where the reconstruc-tion pipeline produces suboptimal results. Some failures arise from unsupported CAD operations, such as revolutions, lofts, or sweeps, which cannot be represented by our extrusion-based formulation. Additional failures occur when the recon-structed assembly contains disconnected components, result-ing in incomplete geometry. Finally, very thin structures such as chamfers or small fillets are often difficult to recover from scan data and may therefore be missed during reconstruction.
