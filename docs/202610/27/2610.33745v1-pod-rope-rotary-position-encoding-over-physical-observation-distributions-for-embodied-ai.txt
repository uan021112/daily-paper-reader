## ABSTRACT 

Embodied perception requires positional representations that capture both physical ge-ometry and the spatial and temporal extent of observations. We introduce POD-RoPE 

(Physical Observation Distribution RoPE), which represents token observation footprints as distributions over camera centers, viewing directions, and sampling times. Under inde-pendent sampling, expected rotations encode relative relationships between distributions; when each distribution collapses to a single address, the construction recovers pointwise RoPE. The same rotation statistics support Q/K matching and coherence-dependent V/O transformations, allowing observation geometry to guide both information selection and content aggregation. Per-token pre-aggregation avoids increasing sequence length or processing pairs of observation nodes. We evaluate POD-RoPE across four robot policy architectures, simulation and real-world manipulation, and complementary re-construction, depth-estimation, and novel-view-synthesis tasks. Integrating POD-RoPE into Fast-WAM improves mean success from 91.83% to 92.88% across all 50 RoboTwin 2.0 tasks, and from 32.5% to 52.5% on two real-robot tasks on Sharpa North. Con-trolled reconstruction ablations support the contributions of distribution encoding and V/O transformations. In H20 model-block benchmarks, POD-RoPE achieves median forward-plus-backward speedups of 1.91 × over P-RoPE and 5.34 × over RayRoPE, with a median latency overhead of 17.4% over Standard RoPE. These results support physical observation distributions as an effective and computationally efficient basis for geometry-aware attention. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Perception and interaction in the physical world require embodied systems to establish physically meaningful spatial and temporal relationships between observations. Standard RoPE (Su et al., 2024), however, typically defines relative positions using sequence indices or spatiotemporal grid coordinates: identical pixel offsets can correspond to different viewing angles, and identical frame-index offsets can represent different elapsed times. Positional encoding therefore needs to extend beyond discrete index differences to represent observation geometry and actual spatial and temporal scales. Geometric positional encodings such as P-RoPE and RayRoPE incorporate camera and ray informa-tion into relative relationship modeling (Li et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026), but incur substantially higher latency than Standard RoPE in our model-block benchmarks. Meanwhile, an image token aggregates information over a pixel region, and a temporally compressed video token combines observations from multiple times. A single center address cannot fully describe these observation footprints. Our central view is that positional encoding should represent not only where and when a token is observed, but also the spatial and temporal extent of its observations . We investigate how to encode these physical observation distributions and their relative relationships at low computational overhead. We propose POD-RoPE, which maps camera centers, viewing directions, and sampling times into a shared physical address space and represents each token’s observation footprint as an address distribution. Averaging address-dependent rotations yields a token-level positional operator; under independent sampling, the pairwise Q/K operator equals the expected rotation over the relative-address distribution (Eq. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Rotary positional encoding and geometry-aware attention. RoPE applies position-dependent rotations to queries and keys so that positional interactions in attention depend on their relative displacement (Su et al., 2024). For multi-view vision, GTA applies group transformations associated with camera geometry to attention matching and value-feature aggregation (Miyato et al., 2024). P-RoPE (denoted PRoPE in the original paper) further unifies intrinsic and extrinsic camera parameters 2Preprint through projective transformations, incorporating complete view-frustum relationships into relative positional representations (Li et al., 2025). RayRoPE constructs projective ray coordinates in the query-camera frame and predicts depth and its uncertainty. It uses expected RoPE to encode position distributions along rays and applies the transformations to Q/K/V/O (Wu et al., 2026). POD-RoPE addresses the observation footprints induced by tokenization. It jointly encodes camera centers, unit viewing directions, and actual time in a shared reference frame, averaging over the address distributions covered by pixels and source frames. Rotation statistics can be pre-aggregated per token and reused across attention interactions. The full method further uses rotation coherence to modulate V/O content transformations with an identity branch. 

Observation footprints and spatiotemporal positional representations. Extending positional encoding from points to regions allows representations to retain observation scale. Mip-NeRF approximates conical frustums with Gaussian distributions and averages sinusoidal positional features to obtain scale-aware integrated positional encoding (Barron et al., 2021). RayRoPE applies expected rotations to uncertainty intervals of predicted depth (Wu et al., 2026). Meanwhile, DiT performs diffusion modeling on image-latent patches (Peebles & Xie, 2023), and Wan compresses spatial and temporal dimensions through a causal video autoencoder (Team Wan et al., 2025), so one visual token corresponds to multiple pixels and potentially multiple source frames. We map these nominal footprints into physical observation distributions, using camera calibration and timestamps to determine their addresses and the phase and magnitude of average rotations to retain the observation composition. This applies regional integration to relative matching and content aggregation between compressed visual tokens, with one construction shared by static multi-view and video observations. 

Vision-action policies and world-action models. ACT predicts action chunks (Zhao et al., 2023), RDT uses a multimodal diffusion Transformer (Liu et al., 2025), and π0 combines a pretrained vision-language model with flow-matching action generation (Black et al., 2025). Fast-WAM jointly models video and actions during training but omits explicit future-video generation at inference (Yuan et al., 2026). We study observation geometry and footprints as positional inputs across these policy architectures; Section 4.1 specifies their integrations. 

Embodied data and visual geometry evaluation. We evaluate manipulation on RoboTwin 2.0 (Mu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) and Sharpa North, and reconstruction using RoboTwin, AgiBot World (Bu et al., 2025), and collected Sharpa North observations. Complementary geometry tasks use UniMatch (Xu et al., 2023) with the DeMoN depth protocol (Ummenhofer et al., 2017), and LVSM (Jin et al., 2025) for novel-view synthesis on RealEstate10K (Zhou et al., 2018).

## 5 CONCLUSION 

POD-RoPE represents token footprints as distributions of physical observation addresses. Expected rotations preserve relative Q/K interactions, while coherence-dependent V/O transformations reuse these statistics to guide content aggregation. The same construction handles static and temporal obser-vations without increasing sequence length. Complete policy integrations improve aggregate success rates in closed-loop experiments, including Sharpa North manipulation; controlled reconstruction ablations support the contributions of distribution encoding and V/O. Visual geometry evaluations reveal task-dependent gains and quality–cost trade-offs, with low additional model-block latency relative to Standard RoPE.

E.1 METHOD INTEGRATION AND TRAINING BUDGETS

For π0 and ACT, positional and content transformations operate within the existing visual attention, requiring no additional adapters or trainable parameters. E.7 REAL -R OBOT EVALUATION ON SHARPA NORTH 

Data and observations. Both methods train jointly on bottle opening and straw insertion, with task text distinguishing the operations. The two tasks have 343/1,351 training trajecto-ries and 244,097/197,660 training windows, respectively, and 35/185 validation trajectories with 25,141/32,787 validation windows. Splits are made by collection batch, and training mixes valid win-dows. Preprocessing excludes 304 straw-insertion trajectories with abnormal mobile-base behavior (37,803 windows). Means and standard deviations are estimated only from deduplicated data rows in the retained training windows, with a standard-deviation floor of 0.001. Images from the head’s left eye and both wrists are first remapped to 256 × 320 and then assembled into a 384 × 320 input: the head image is on top, with two 128 × 160 wrist images side by side below. The head uses batch-specific intrinsics and distortion parameters; the wrists use an approximation based on a historical fisheye model. Both methods share this preprocessing. Camera links are mapped to robot root through URDF forward kinematics; this geometry is not an independently and precisely calibrated world coordinate system. Head timestamps define 33-frame windows. Video uses nine frames at offsets 0, 4, . . . , 32 , while actions cover 32 steps. The 67-dimensional state/action comprises 7 dimensions per arm, 22 per hand, 7 for other joints, and 2 for mobile-base velocity. The policy reads only current observation geometry. 

Models and training. Both methods share the pretrained Fast-WAM backbone, data, and identical width-192, six-head modules in the video and action branches. The baseline uses standard RoPE; POD-RoPE uses Gaussian-9 ( σ = 0 .25 ) for Q/K and V/O with g = 1 . Visual tokens use robot-root addresses, actions encode sequence positions, and the original positional mechanism is retained. Both groups use 8 nodes with 8 H20 GPUs per node, batch 16 per GPU, global batch 1024, no gradient accumulation, BF16, and ZeRO-1, with gradient checkpointing disabled. AdamW uses a learning rate of 10 −4, β = (0 .9, 0.95) , weight decay 0.01, and gradient clipping at 1. A 100k-step cosine schedule includes 1500 warm-up steps; both real-robot evaluations use the 20k checkpoint. 

Evaluation and examples. For bottle opening, the robot grasps the bottle and unscrews the cap; success requires the cap to be fully unscrewed and separated from the bottle opening. For straw insertion, the robot grasps the straw, aligns it with the target container opening, and inserts it; success requires the straw to remain inside the container after release. Each method is evaluated for 20 trials per task. Only trials that complete all task steps count as successes; incomplete trials count as failures.
