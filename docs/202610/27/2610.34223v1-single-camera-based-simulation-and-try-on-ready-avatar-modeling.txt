Honghu Chen*, Bo Peng*, Jun Xiang, Jingtao Zhou, Xujing Chen, and Juyong Zhang†, Member, IEEE,
Abstract—Reconstructing and rendering high-fidelity, animat-
able, and try-on ready virtual avatars using only a single camera
is crucial for many applications. However, existing try-on-ready
human avatar reconstruction methods struggle to achieve this
from monocular videos or single images, primarily due to two
issues: (1) Existing approaches typically use a single layer to
represent both garments and the underlying body simultaneously,
ignoring their distinct motion characteristics; (2) Input captured
from a single camera often fails to fully cover both garments and
the underlying body. To address these challenges, we propose a
physically integrated double-layer GS-Mesh 3D clothed human
representation, reconstructing decoupled clothed avatars from
monocular videos or even single images by incorporating 2D
generated data. While 2D generation methods can produce
video sequences from given pose sequences, they struggle to
maintain inter-frame consistency. We further decompose the
inter-frame inconsistencies in generated data into geometric and
appearance inconsistencies. Based on the 3D representation, we
ensure the final reconstruction and rendering results are both
3D-consistent and temporally coherent. Compared to existing 3D
garment transfer methods relying on multi-view reconstruction,
or most 2D approaches that use diffusion models for image-based
garment editing without novel view/pose synthesis, our method
only needs monocular video or even a single image as input while
achieving visually plausible cloth dynamics effects during virtual
try-on and novel pose animation. This represents a significant
advancement in achieving virtual try-on with visually plausible
cloth dynamics with just a single camera.
Index Terms—physical simulation, reconstruction, virtual try-
on.

IGH-FIDELITY digital humans are widely applied in
fields such as VR/AR and the metaverse. Compared to
cinematic-quality digital human reconstructions that require a
large number of cameras, high-fidelity digital humans that can
be reconstructed at low cost are more suitable for widespread
promotion and mass applications. Existing monocular recon-

humans and represent the clothed human as a single layer. This
results in significant challenges for the human avatar when
changing clothing or encountering unseen poses.

We aim to develop a method to reconstruct decoupled
human avatars from monocular videos or single images, and
enable virtual try-on through physical simulation with visually
plausible cloth dynamics. However, this is a highly challenging
task due to the following reasons: 1) Monocular inputs only
provide single-view 2D image information, with unobservable
regions existing making it difficult to reconstruct the geometry
and appearance of decoupled clothing and body; 2) When
utilizing 2D generated data to supplement missing information
in monocular inputs, temporal inconsistency issues arise; 3) In
virtual try-on or animation production, ensuring the reliability
of rendering results in terms of 3D consistency, appearance,
and visually plausible cloth dynamics is crucial.
To address these challenges, we propose a novel method
for reconstructing a double-layered clothed human avatar from
monocular inputs, which integrates a physical simulator to
generate novel-pose animations with visually plausible cloth
dynamics. It also combines generative priors to ensure high
rendering quality when presenting novel clothing and novel
poses. When generalizing to challenging poses or clothing,
unseen regions may produce artifacts. This problem is over-
looked by most existing monocular human avatar reconstruc-
tion methods [1]–[5]. To solve this problem, we propose a
strategy that combines generation and reconstruction, using
the generated image information to guide the reconstruction
of unseen regions in the input images. However, generated
image sequences often suffer from inconsistency with real
data as well as inter-frame inconsistency within the generated
data. We address this issue by modeling inconsistencies as
geometric and appearance inconsistencies. Geometric incon-
sistencies include inconsistencies in human pose and geo-
metric shape. We design isolated deformation representations
for real and generated data and perform pose re-tracking on
them. Appearance inconsistencies include lighting and subtle
style inconsistencies, which we align using SH coefficients
and perceptual loss. Using this strategy, we can significantly
reduce the impact of inconsistency on the quality of generated
sequence reconstructions.
With the monocular inputs and generated image sequences,
our reconstructed clothed human avatar demonstrates strong
generalization capabilities for novel poses and novel cloth-
ing. Compared to existing GS-based monocular reconstruc-
tion methods with single-layer representation, our method
shows strong generalization ability to novel poses. Without
manual intervention, we directly apply it to virtual try-on
and demonstrate its effectiveness. Unlike existing 2D try-on
methods that typically fail to generalize to novel poses or
views, and 3D try-on methods that require multi-view inputs,

II. RELATED WORK
A. Reconstruction from Monocular Input.
Reconstruction from videos. Recently, several methods
based on mesh representations, implicit representations, and
Gaussian splatting have focused on reconstructing the geome-
try and appearance of clothed human bodies from monocular
videos.
Mesh-based methods use parametric human [6]–[9] and
clothing models to obtain reconstruction results through re-
gression [10]–[12] or optimization [13], but these methods
generally lack details. Other methods [14]–[16] rely on para-
metric models or specially designed templates and optimize
vertex offsets to achieve detailed reconstruction, but the tem-
plates are often not directly applicable to diverse categories of
clothing. Implicit function-based reconstruction methods use
neural networks to model occupancy fields [17], [18], signed
distance fields [19], [20], and density fields [1], [2] to represent
3D human or render fine details. These methods can repre-
sent various types of clothing without topological constraints.
However, these methods typically have long rendering times
and limited editability. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)-based
reconstruction methods [21] greatly improve rendering speed
and editability compared to NeRF-based approaches [22].
Some methods extend 3DGS to dynamic human bodies [3]–[5]
using SMPL-guided deformations, enabling faster generation
of editable animated human models.
Reconstruction from few-shot images. Reconstructing
avatars from few-shot images remains a highly challenging
task due to the sparsity of available input. PIFu [17] and fol-
lowing works [18], [23]–[26] introduce pixel-aligned features
to facilitate novel view synthesis. SHERF [27] learns a gener-
alizable human NeRF but suffers from slow rendering speeds,
while GAS [28] further incorporates a video diffusion model
to enhance output sharpness. A prominent direction in recent
work is to utilize diffusion priors to compensate for missing
information. This includes generating novel views to enrich
the input [29]–[32], and applying Score Distillation Sampling
(SDS) [33] to generate 3D avatars from 2D priors [34]–[38].
Although AniGS [32] also leverages generated observations
for single-image avatar reconstruction, it formulates cross-
view inconsistencies as temporal variations and handles them
using 4DGS. In contrast, our method targets a disentangled,
try-on-ready body-garment representation. We separately gen-
erate key-pose clothed-human observations and underlying-
body observations, and explicitly decompose generation errors
into geometric and appearance inconsistencies. In addition,
our representation supports garment transfer and subsequent
cloth simulation rather than skinning-based avatar anima-
tion alone. IDOL [39], Vid2Avatar-Pro [40] and LHM [41]
perform single-image human reconstruction efficiently in a
feed-forward manner, but often produce coarse results when
handling custom inputs. However, most of these methods treat
the clothed avatar as a whole. In contrast, our method employs
a double-layer representation and leverages 2D diffusion priors
for both the clothed avatar and the underlying body, which is
necessary for clothing transfer.
B. Virtual Try-on
2D Virtual Try-on. Image-based virtual try-on aims to
generate composite images of a person wearing a specified

3
garment. Deformation-based methods like VITON [42] and
CP-VTON [43] use Thin Plate Spline (TPS) [44] transfor-
mation and a staged approach, while PF-AFN [45] com-
bines knowledge distillation, and GP-VTON [46] improves
detail consistency through local flow, but alignment issues
remain. Diffusion-based methods overcome these limitations
by leveraging pre-trained models. WarpDiffusion [47] bridges
deformation and diffusion with local feature attention, TryOn-
Diffusion [48] uses UNet for feature extraction, LaDIVTON
[49] maps features to CLIP [50], StableVITON [51] introduces
a zero-cross attention block, and OOTDiffusion [52] fine-tunes
the garment UNet, further improving performance.
Image-based methods can produce high-quality images, but
they struggle to achieve good results across diverse poses and
to generate continuous and consistent results for sequences.
3D Virtual Try-on. The research on 3D virtual try-on
mainly focuses on modeling layered clothing and bodies,
studying how clothing deforms under the influence of the
underlying body’s shape and motion, or how it behaves accord-
ing to physical laws. These methods [53]–[55] can model the
dynamic deformation of clothing in great detail, but typically
at the geometric level, they are unable to achieve high-
fidelity rendering quality. Diffusion-based methods [56]–[58]
use differentiable 3D representations to guide 3D optimization
through diffusion models. These methods can guide outfit
changes via images or text but are unable to model physics-
related deformations.
Monocular reconstruction of layered NeRF and mesh rep-
resentations of human avatars [59], [60] can experiment with
clothing transfer, but cannot integrate physical simulation.
Some methods based on multi-layer 3D Gaussian Splat-
ting representations [21]. [61], [62] reconstruct multi-layered
clothed human bodies, achieving high-fidelity clothing transfer
effects. D3GA [63] and LayGA [64] model the non-rigid
deformation of clothing using pose- or time-dependent neural
networks, while PhysAvatar [65] and PICA [66] embed physi-
cal simulations [67], [68] to generate garment animations with
visually plausible cloth dynamics. However, these methods all
require multi-view sequences as input, which are not easily
accessible to the general public.
C. Consistent multi-view and video generation
Consistent multi-view generation. Zero-1-to-3 [69] pio-
neered camera-conditioned diffusion for zero-shot novel view
synthesis. SyncDreamer [70] and MVDream [71] introduced
3D-aware synchronization and cross-view attention to enforce
multi-view consistency. Wonder3D [72] further improved ge-
ometric detail by jointly generating aligned normal and color
maps. Current trends include discrete sequence modeling via
ViewMask-1-to-3 [73] and autoregressive frameworks like
MV-AR [74] that utilize preceding viewpoints as references
to maintain consistency across widely separated views.
Human multi-view generation utilizes parametric mod-
els as geometric priors to handle non-rigid motion and
complex clothing. Frameworks like PersonaCraft [75] and
Champ [76] employ 3D-aware control for occlusion-robust
and shape-preserving animation. Advanced models such as
D-Garment [77] and Disco4D [78] address dynamic cloth-
ing through physics-conditioned latent diffusion or disentan-
gled Gaussian representations. Scaling to high resolutions,
MEAT [79] introduces efficient mesh attention for megapixel
synthesis , while SyncHuman [80] synchronizes 2D multi-view
and 3D native branches to ensure structural integrity under
challenging poses.
For multi-view generation tasks, generators trained on 2D
data still suffer from inconsistencies across views, while those
trained on 3D data are typically limited by the scarcity of
high-quality 3D datasets. Therefore, we chose to leverage 2D
generation methods to utilize vast amounts of video priors for
3D reconstruction.
Video generation. Diffusion-based video generation has
advanced rapidly in recent years. Stable Video Diffusion [81]
leverages latent diffusion to significantly improve temporal
consistency and resolution. Models such as OpenAI Sora [82],
Google Veo 3 [83] and ByteDance Seedance [84] generate
long, high-resolution videos with high visual fidelity using
large-scale training and spatiotemporal Transformers [85],
while Wan [86] and HunyuanVideo [87] promote open-source
progress. These models serve as general-purpose video gen-
eration foundations and also possess certain capabilities for
human video generation.
Human video generation is evolving from 2D motion trans-
fer toward physically-aware simulations with environment af-
fordance and 3D geometric constraints. Animate Anyone [88]
established the technical foundation for identity consistency
via ReferenceNet; MagicAnimate [89] improved detail fi-
delity through dense features and joint image-video training.
Champ [76] introduced 3D parametric guidance (SMPL) to
solve shape alignment and complex pose control. Mimic-
Motion [90] optimized motion stability and hand precision
using confidence-aware guidance. Animate Anyone 2 [91]
pioneered environment-coherent interactions. While human
video generation is mostly concerned with how the person
moves, it lacks the ability to swap or change outfits.

appearance consistency constraints.

C. Key Pose Extraction
We aim to use the generated human image sequences as sup-
plementary data to enhance the performance of reconstructed
clothed humans in novel poses and garments. However, human
poses are highly diverse, and due to the computational cost of
generation and training, it is infeasible to directly use large-
scale pose data to generate sequences and train the model.
To ensure that garments are fully observed and optimized
under various typical body configurations, we design a set of
key poses aimed at maximizing the coverage of the human
motion space (e.g., turning, arm-raising, and walking). The
specific variations introduced by different garments—such as
unique wrinkles, folding patterns, and physical behaviors—are
explicitly modeled by our double-layer disentangled represen-
tation, physics simulation module, and deformation represen-
tation. This approach ensures the universality and robustness
of the key pose set across diverse garment categories.
We selected the key pose set by considering the following
three metrics: Pose Representativeness, Pose Similarity, and
Rendering Visibility. By combining these three indicators, we
aim to ensure that the selected poses not only capture the
essential variation of human motion but also exhibit sufficient
diversity and possess informative visual appearance.
Pose Representativeness. We employ Kernel Density Esti-
mation (KDE) on the dimension-reduced pose data to estimate
the probability density p(θ), for each pose θ. A higher p(θ)
indicates that the pose is more representative in the dataset.

and thus increase the overall diversity, we use the cosine

D. Data Generation
Key Pose Data Generation. When driving the clothed
human body to new motions, there may be large invisible
regions (for single image input) or occluded regions (such as
underarms, for monocular video input). To address this, we
construct more diverse generated data for the key pose data.
Using the extracted key poses, we generate clothed human im-
age sequences for specified pose sequences through a human
animation generation method [99]. Since the generated data
includes diverse poses, it effectively provides information for
the unseen regions, helping to eliminate artifacts.

Body Data Generation. Since we reconstruct decoupled
clothing and the human body, rendering artifacts may appear
in unobserved body regions when applying novel clothing or
novel poses. For example, when changing a long-sleeved outfit
to a short-sleeved one, artifacts may occur on the exposed
arms, as well as on body parts revealed by clothing movement
during motion.
To plausibly complete underlying-body regions that are oc-
cluded by clothing or otherwise unobserved in the monocular
input, we first employ a diffusion-based image inpainting
method to redraw the clothed person as a minimally clothed
person. Then we use a 3D human generation method [99] to
create 360-degree generated data for the minimally clothed
body and estimate SMPL-X parameters [100] for the redrawn
body generated data.
E. Generated Data Alignment.
Due to the inevitable inter-frame inconsistency in generated
image sequences, as well as the discrepancies between gener-
ated and real data, directly using such data for human avatar
reconstruction introduces significant artifacts. To address this,
we decompose the inter-frame inconsistency in generated se-
quences into geometric inconsistency and appearance inconsis-
tency. Specifically, geometric inconsistency comprises shape
discrepancy and human pose misalignment, while appearance
inconsistency manifests as subtle style variations and shading
incoherence.
Shape discrepancy. When generating human animation
sequences, since 2D generation methods cannot guarantee
accurate consistency—including both consistency with real
data and inter-frame consistency of the generated data—two
main issues arise: the body shape in the generated data cannot
perfectly match the real data, and the corresponding 3D human
geometry may vary between frames, manifesting as incon-
sistent garment lengths. To address the discrepancy between
generated and real data, we re-optimize the shape parameters
for the generated data. For the inter-frame inconsistency, we
employ an additional non-rigid deformation MLP defined in
Eq. 4 to adaptively learn the correspondence between the
model and the generated data.
Human pose misalignment. Since the generated image
sequence does not fully align with the input poses, we perform
re-tracking [100] on the generated image sequence to obtain
accurate body poses.
Shading incoherence. The appearance of the generated
data lacks temporal consistency, especially when the generated
background changes or shakes. This leads to unstable lighting
and shading effects. To solve this problem, we optimized
the SH coefficients for each frame of the generated data to
model the unstable lighting. We only optimize the global SH
coefficients, thus avoiding over-fitting to individual frames.
Subtle style variations. Since generated image sequences
may exhibit subtle stylistic variations (e.g., color drifting)
and lack local feature coherence, leading to inaccurate 3D
alignment and potential failure of per-pixel loss functions
during reconstruction, we employ perceptual loss terms [101]
as supervision for the generated data to mitigate reconstruction
errors caused by style discrepancies.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
We conduct qualitative experiments for novel pose and
quantitative experiments for reconstruction to demonstrate
the effectiveness of our method. For the quantitative exper-
iments, we use subjects from public dataset wearing different
clothes to evaluate the quality of reconstruction. For qualitative
experiments, we demonstrated the performance of driving
reconstructed human avatars to novel motions. Additionally,
we conducted ablation studies to showcase the effectiveness
of keyframe extraction, generated data and learnable local
properties. For applications, we presented the results of virtual
try-on and relighting. More dynamic results can be found in
the video.
A. Implementation Details
Our framework is implemented in PyTorch based on the
3DGS infrastructure, where we follow PICA [66] and anchor
13 Gaussians on each triangular face of the reconstructed
meshes. For data preprocessing of monocular video or single-
image inputs, we predict the initial SMPL-X parameters via
ExAvatar [100] and extract semantic segmentation masks
using SAM2 [104] and Sapiens [102], while employing 2D
generative priors (e.g., Viggle AI) to generate synthesized data
sequences. The optimization of our joint Mesh-GS represen-
tation consists of two sequential stages; first, the deformation
and appearance compensation networks are trained alongside
the Gaussian attributes to eliminate generative inconsistencies,
which takes 100K iterations to converge and requires about

10 hours on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU for a standard
sequence of 200 real frames and 200 MMR-extracted frames.
Second, for video inputs, the physical property fitting stage

GPU.

C. Quantitative Evaluation
We perform a quantitative experiment to evaluate our recon-
struction quality. Following PICA [66], we use three metrics to
quantify the reconstruction results: structural similarity index
(SSIM), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and the learned
perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) [101].
Reconstructing from monocular videos. Since there is no
ground truth mesh or images from another view, we extract
a subset of frames for each sequence for training and use
the remaining frames for testing. iHuman [5] uses monocular
sequences to reconstruct non-disentangled clothed humans.
PICA uses multi-view sequences to reconstruct clothing and
garments, and we adapt it to monocular sequences. For the
monocular PICA baseline, we provide only one view for each
frame instead of the original multi-view observations, while
keeping the original PICA representation and optimization
scheme unchanged. We use the same preprocessing and body-
pose initialization as our method. Our method achieves the
best performance on all metrics, as shown in Table I.
Reconstructing from single images. For single image
inputs, we select a frame from the provided real sequence data,
construct generated data for reconstruction, and evaluate on the
real sequence. MimicMotion [90] uses a single image as input
to generate image sequences of specified poses. However, the
generated results exhibit poor consistency in body shape and
style. ExAvatar [100] is based on the SMPL-X representation
and reconstructs from a single image in this experiment, while
it fails to accurately restore textures in regions unseen in the
input image, which highlights the importance of generating
information for unseen regions. Our method, with the assis-
tance of the inconsistency elimination strategy, can reconstruct
high-fidelity human avatars from single images and achieves
superior performance across all metrics, as shown in Table II.

to the final reconstruction. In particular, our alignment strategy
can alleviate inconsistencies across generated observations,

Although our method optimizes the physical parameters
from monocular videos, its fidelity cannot always be guar-

enhance its generalization, generative models may not always
produce high-quality results for all clothed human inputs. (4)
While our model demonstrates remarkable efficacy in both
try-on and novel pose synthesis tasks, its extension to multi-
layered garment animation introduces extra complexities. As
illustrated in Figure 16, evaluating the framework on sophis-
ticated multi-layered apparel can occasionally introduce unre-
solved cross-layer interactions during dynamic driving, which
may subsequently yield subtle geometric inter-penetration and
rendering artifacts. Addressing these complex layered config-
urations without relying on explicit physics engines remains
an open challenge that warrants further investigation.
V. CONCLUSION
We proposed a novel method capable of reconstructing
decoupled clothed human avatars from monocular inputs and
enabling virtual try-on through physical simulation with vi-


IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON VISUALIZATION AND COMPUTER GRAPHICS
14
sually plausible cloth dynamics. By combining input real
video data with generated data, our method can reconstruct
a double-layer Mesh-GS representation of the human body
with strong generalization capabilities for novel poses and
clothing, integrating physical simulation. For the inter-frame
inconsistency in generated data, we decompose it into geomet-
ric inconsistency and appearance inconsistency and address
them separately.
Thanks to this innovative approach, we can achieve human
avatar reconstruction for virtual try-on with only monocular
input, delivering high-fidelity results with visually plausible
cloth dynamics for novel garments and poses. Our method
requires only a single camera to achieve 3D physics-integrated
reconstruction, providing a technical foundation for animation
and virtual try-on applications.
