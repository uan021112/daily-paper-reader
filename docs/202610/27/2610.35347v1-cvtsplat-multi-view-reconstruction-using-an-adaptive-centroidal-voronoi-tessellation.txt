Abstract
Voronoi tessellations have recently emerged as an adaptive, space-
partitioning discretization for shape and appearance reconstruc-
tion from multi-view images. However, the irregular cells they
produce make the repeated reconstruction of the tessellation dur-
ing optimization costly. We therefore explore adaptive centroidal
Voronoi tessellations (CVTs), which extend standard CVTs with
non-uniform cell sizes while preserving the regular, well-shaped
cells that make them efficient to compute. In CVTSplat, cell size
is modulated by the evolving signed distance field, concentrating
sites near the zero level set while keeping cells nearly centroidal.
This regularity lets us specialize the whole pipeline, with GPU al-
gorithms for nearest-neighbor queries and approximate Voronoi
construction, and a rasterizer tailored to small, nearly isotropic cells.
Our Voronoi construction is 5–10× faster than general-purpose al-
ternatives in this regime. On DTU and BlendedMVS, CVTSplat
reaches geometric accuracy competitive with the best Gaussian-
splatting surface methods while training in about 2.5 minutes on a
single GPU.

Introduction
Multi-view reconstruction aims at recovering the geometry and
appearance of a 3D scene from a collection of images acquired
from different viewpoints. A central challenge is to design a rep-
resentation that provides sufficient spatial resolution to capture
fine surface details while remaining computationally efficient to
optimize and render. Implicit surface methods coupled with differ-
entiable rendering have demonstrated high reconstruction quality
[54], but their practical efficiency and compactness strongly depend
on how the scene volume is discretized. Uniform discretizations,
such as dense voxel grids, allocate the same capacity throughout
space even though accurate geometry and appearance are primar-
ily required near the reconstructed surface. Conversely, adaptive

Voronoi-based representations provide an interesting direction

and related cellular discretizations for neural rendering and recon-
struction [14, 15, 36, 47, 50, 56]. However, these representations
do not explicitly exploit one of the fundamental properties of Cen-
troidal Voronoi Tessellations (CVTs): their tendency to produce
regular, well-shaped cells. Such regularity is particularly attractive
computationally, since irregular Voronoi cells may require visiting
many neighboring sites when constructing the diagram [46]. At the
same time, a standard CVT tends toward a uniform distribution of
sites, thereby losing the main benefit of an adaptive representation.
We therefore investigate adaptive centroidal Voronoi tessella-
tions as a spatial representation for multi-view reconstruction.
We modulate cell size using the implicit surface, yielding a near-
centroidal tessellation with increased site density around the zero
level set (fig. 1). This combines the representational efficiency of an
adaptive discretization with the computational benefits of regular
Voronoi cells, while relying on a standard Voronoi diagram rather
than a power diagram. Specifically, power diagrams offer another
way to achieve adaptivity, but require an additional weight per site
and incur a higher construction cost [14, 49].
Based on this representation, we introduce CVTSplat, a differen-
tiable multi-view reconstruction method in which each Voronoi site
carries a signed-distance value and compact appearance attributes
decoded into view-dependent radiance. The near-centroidal struc-
ture of the tessellation lets us specialize every stage of the pipeline
to this regime. Since well-shaped cells have few and nearby neigh-
bors, the Voronoi diagram can be constructed from a small set of
candidate sites. We deliberately accept an approximate diagram in
the rare cases where this set is incomplete, as the resulting local
overlaps are compatible with differentiable primitive-based render-
ing [26]. Similarly, instead of ray tracing an exact diagram [15],
we directly rasterize the cells [14] and exploit their small, nearly
isotropic shape to tailor the rasterizer. Together, these choices make
CVTSplat a regular yet surface-adaptive discretization for efficient
geometry and appearance reconstruction.
The complete optimization pipeline is described in figure 2. It
consists of two components: the adaptive tessellation controls the
position of the sites, while the differentiable rendering branch op-
timizes the signed-distance and appearance attributes. The tes-
sellation is guided by the evolving implicit surface to produce a
surface-adaptive discretization. Our main contributions are as fol-
lows:
(1) We introduce an adaptive centroidal Voronoi discretiza-
tion for multi-view reconstruction, combining surface adap-
tivity with the regularity of centroidal tessellations. Our
complete reconstruction pipeline achieves geometric accu-
racy competitive with state-of-the-art 3DGS-based methods
[7, 13, 63] on DTU, while training in approximately 2.5 min-
utes on a single RTX 5090 and using 4 GB of VRAM.
(2) We exploit the regularity of the tessellation to make Voronoi-
based reconstruction computationally efficient. We intro-
duce specialized GPU algorithms for approximate Voronoi
construction, repeated 𝐾-nearest-neighbor queries, and De-
launay triangulation, substantially accelerating these oper-
ations compared to general-purpose approaches [4, 5, 15,
46, 49].
(3) We propose a rasterizer optimized for rendering small,
isotropic primitives, further consolidating our framework.

Related Work
We review the literature most closely related to our approach along
three directions: adaptive spatial discretizations for implicit 3D
reconstruction (Sec. 2.1), explicit primitive-based representations
(Sec. 2.2), and efficient GPU construction of 3D Voronoi and Delau-
nay diagrams (Sec. 2.3).
2.1
Adaptive Spatial Discretizations
Multi-view 3D reconstruction based on differentiable rendering
initially relied on continuous neural representations [37, 54], be-
fore increasingly adopting grid-based or hybrid spatial encodings
to accelerate optimization and rendering [6, 11, 34, 40, 53]. The
main objective of these representations is to allocate computation
sparsely, by concentrating capacity around the reconstructed sur-
face. However, grid-aligned discretizations typically follow a prede-
fined spatial structure that either stays fixed during optimization
or changes discretely across levels of detail.
Unstructured discretizations offer greater flexibility in adapting
both the number and spatial extent of their elements. Recent ap-
proaches have explored Voronoi or Power cells [14, 15, 47, 50], as
well as tetrahedral representations [29, 36, 52, 56]. These works
motivate spatial representations that retain the flexibility of unstruc-
tured discretizations while achieving the accuracy and efficiency
of highly optimized grid-based methods.
2.2
Explicit Splatting Representations
3D Gaussian Splatting [26] has become a widely used representation
for high-quality novel-view synthesis due to its adaptive distribu-
tion of primitives and efficient rasterization. However, recovering
coherent geometry from an unstructured set of Gaussians is highly
challenging [7, 13, 18, 19, 21, 45, 61, 63]. Existing methods intro-
duce additional constraints or regularization to suppress floaters
and improve depth and normal consistency [7, 63], or dedicated
procedures for extracting closed surfaces [13, 18].
Space-partitioning primitives such as tetrahedra and Voronoi
cells provide an alternative splat support [15, 36] in which neighbor-
ing elements form a coherent spatial decomposition rather than an
overlapping collection of primitives. This structure provides consis-
tent spatial ordering, but maintaining it while the underlying sites
move during optimization can introduce considerable computa-
tional overhead. Efficiently updating such adaptive representations
therefore remains an important challenge.

GPU methods for direct Voronoi construction were initially de-
veloped for relatively uniform point distributions [4, 46], provid-
ing large speedups over CPU implementations [31, 51]. More re-
cently, Taveira et al. [49] extended this family of approaches to
non-uniform point distributions. Following the clipping formula-
tion of Ray et al. [46], Voronoi cells are constructed independently
by successively clipping them against bisector planes induced by
neighboring sites. Although cells can be processed in parallel, the
clipping operations for each cell are sequential, and irregular cells
may require significantly more operations. Leaving cells unregular-

Fast and Adaptive Spatial Data Structure
Our spatial representation consists of a set of sites {x𝑖}0≤𝑖<𝑁(i.e.
3D points) that induce a Voronoi tessellation of the scene volume.
Each site stores the geometric and appearance attributes used by
the reconstruction, including a signed-distance value and view-
dependent radiance features. The Voronoi cells provide the volu-
metric primitives used for rendering, while their dual Delaunay
connectivity provides the local neighborhood structure required
for mesh extraction. Because the site positions evolve throughout
optimization, these structures must be updated repeatedly. A key
property of our representation is that the sites remain close to a cen-
troidal configuration, producing compact cells with a small number
of nearby neighbors and enabling efficient GPU construction.
In the following, we first describe how we obtain this adaptive
centroidal distribution (Sec. 3.1) and compute its centroids (Sec.
3.2). We then present our fast Voronoi construction (Sec. 3.3), its
conversion to Delaunay tetrahedra (Sec. 3.4), and the KNN kernel
supporting these operations (Sec. 3.5).

Feasibility test. We test feasibility with Seidel’s incremental algo-
rithm [2, 48], which maintains a feasible point and updates it only
when a new constraint is violated, by solving a 1D problem along
that constraint’s boundary line. Instead of shuffling the constraints
randomly, we process them in increasing distance order, as returned
by the KNN kernel (Sec. 3.5). The nearest planes are the most likely
to bound the cell, so infeasibility is usually detected early.
GPU implementation. We consider that only the 32 nearest neigh-
bors are likely to define active bisector planes, so there are 32
threads dedicated for building each cell. Each one tests a candidate
plane P𝑟against the 37 remaining constraints (31 other neighbors
and the 6 planes of the scene bounding box). The 2D constraints
are built on the fly from the site positions so no per-thread storage
is needed, which is a significant bottleneck in previous approaches
[4, 46] due to reduced GPU occupancy. At the end, each thread
outputs a single bit classifying whether the neighbor is Voronoi-
adjacent.
Approximation. The assumption that 32 neighbors are enough
generally holds in the case of our biased CVT. To further reduce
the number of missing adjacencies, we center the KNN queries
at the previously computed cell centroids rather than at the sites
themselves. This substantially improves the completeness of the
resulting Voronoi diagrams, as demonstrated in the supplementary
material. More neighbors could be included to handle more irregular
cells, though with a performance loss. If a true neighbor beyond the
𝐾nearest sites is missed, the cell will be enlarged and will overlap
its neighbor. A missing constraint can also enlarge the feasible
region of Eq. (15), so that a non-neighbor is wrongly marked as
adjacent. Both errors are local and only affect cells near the few
sites concerned.

We convert the approximate Voronoi diagram into the dual De-
launay triangulation for mesh extraction, illustrated in figure 6. For
conciseness, "Voronoi neighbor" will be shortened to "neighbor" in
the following discussion.
The Delaunay tetrahedra are groups of 4 sites (𝑖, 𝑗,𝑘,𝑙) that are
pairwise neighbors, and whose circumsphere does not contain any
other site. Thus, we enumerate all possible 4-cliques, and then
test whether their circumsphere is empty. Only ordered tuples
𝑖< 𝑗< 𝑘< 𝑙are emitted to avoid duplicates.
We build a binary adjacency matrix M for each site𝑖, that encodes
the adjacency relation of its neighbors: 𝑀𝑗,𝑘is 1 if sites 𝑗and 𝑘are
neighbors. To form a quadruplet, we look for a neighbor 𝑙of 𝑖such
that 𝑀𝑗,𝑙and 𝑀𝑘,𝑙are set to 1 in M.
We efficiently enumerate the quadruplets (𝑖, 𝑗,𝑘,𝑙) by leveraging
the binary nature of M: because each site has 32 neighbors at most,
the rows of M can be encoded as 32-bit integers. Thus, 𝑗indices
correspond to the direct neighbors of 𝑖, 𝑘indices correspond to the
bits set in row 𝑀𝑗, and 𝑙indices correspond to the bits set in the
binary AND of rows 𝑀𝑗and 𝑀𝑘. The __ffs CUDA intrinsic lets us
iterate over the toggled bits of an integer.
Not all quadruplets form Delaunay tetrahedra so we remove the
ones containing other sites in their circumsphere. In practice, we
only need to test the neighbors of 𝑖against the circumsphere. A
proof is available in the supplementary document.
Because our Voronoi diagram may be incomplete, our triangula-
tion is not guaranteed to be exact for cells with missing clipping
planes or in case of numerical ambiguities in the circumsphere
check. This can result in a few holes or non-manifold edges in
the extracted mesh out of millions of triangles. We quantify these
defects in the supplementary material.

Fast GPU KNN Search
We propose a fast K-nearest neighbor kernel, tailored for 𝐾= 32
and for repeated queries, to take advantage of our specific setting:
5


Toussaint et al.
the sites move by small amounts between rebuilds of the nearest-
neighbors lists, which implies a high degree of temporal consistency
that can be leveraged. Like Jakob et al. [24], we start by building
an acceleration structure to avoid a naive 𝑂(𝑁2) brute force check.
We use a classic LBVH [30] with up to 128 points per node. This
gives a shallow hierarchy well-suited for querying a relatively large
number of neighbors.
The nearest neighbors are gathered using ball-queries, i.e. gath-
ering all points within some distance of the query point, instead
of the more common heap-based approach [24, 46]. The ball-query
is restarted with a smaller or larger radius until the number of
gathered points is between 32 and 64, using a binary search. They
are subsequently sorted in increasing distance to the query point
[42] and the first 32 are returned. Each query point has its own
search radius which we save for later runs. The point cloud evolves
slowly so the search radius is likely to be valid the next time the
KNN routine is called, sparing repeated BVH traversals.
Heap-based approaches generally assign one thread per query
point, and therefore need a per-thread traversal stack or priority
queue, in addition to the heap already required to keep track of the
K closest points. While the heap can be stored in registers [24], this
reduces occupancy considerably for large values of K or spills the
registers to global memory.
Our strategy addresses these issues: we assign one warp per
query point, with all lanes visiting the BVH together. A single BVH
traversal stack is needed for the whole warp, and the points can
be loaded with coalesced memory accesses. The points within the
search radius are compacted to a shared memory buffer sorted in
parallel at the end, which is faster than iterative heap insertions.
4
Differentiable Volume Rendering
After gathering the nearest neighbors of all sites and computing the
Voronoi diagram, we can proceed with rendering of the scene. The
volume rendering integral is typically evaluated with ray-marching
[37, 54], by querying the radiance and density fields at arbitrary
locations along the rays. Directly adopting this formulation for our
discretization is ill-suited: interpolating point neighborhoods at
each ray sample is costly [58], while interpolating over the Voronoi
cells generally requires a tetrahedral mesh [36, 52]. Instead, we
follow RadiantFoam [15] and use piecewise integration where each
cell only contributes to the portion of the ray it intersects. Radiant-
Foam [15] and SDFoam [47] use a constant density inside the cells
which does not account for local surface orientation. In compari-
son, we define the SDF as a linear function in each cell to obtain
nonlinearities once composed with NeuS [54]’s SDF-to-density con-
version, and which crucially still gives a closed-form solution to
the volume integral over the ray segments.
Three elements are necessary: (i) the SDF gradient at each site
to define a piecewise SDF, (ii) per-primitive radiance and (iii) reg-
ularization terms to stabilize optimization. We reuse the nearest

5.3
Initialization and Densification
Initially, we spread the sites in the scene bounding box based on a
uniform distribution and apply 10 iterations of Lloyd’s relaxation to
uniformize the cells (𝑎= 0 in equation 1). The SDF is initialized from
a sphere, while the rest of the learnable features are randomized
based on a normal distribution.
We start with 200K sites, and increase their number every 10
iterations until we reach 4M sites by following a geometric progres-
sion. To place new sites, we pick a random viewpoint and render a
depth map and a feature map. The feature map is the 𝛼-blending
of the sites’ spatial features F𝑠. A new site is placed at the depth
sampled from a random valid pixel in the depth map, and its feature
vector F𝑠is sampled from the same pixel in the feature map.
The nearest-neighbors list is rebuilt each time new points are
added, while the Voronoi diagram is recomputed more frequently:
at each iteration in the beginning, and every five iterations towards
the end. We train for 10K iterations, with four training images per
mini-batch. New points are added starting from the 400th iteration,
until the last 400 iterations. For the last 400 iterations, we replace the
planar-based ZNCC with a nonplanar version to avoid suppressing
fine-scale details. More details regarding the multi-view loss and
the weight scheduling are given in the supplementary material.

Experimental validation
All experiments in this section used a workstation equipped with
an RTX 5090 GPU and an Intel Core Ultra 7 265K with 20 cores to
measure timings, with the exception of table 2 where we report
each method’s timing and corresponding hardware configuration.
Best, second and third best results are highlighted in the tables.
6.1
Multi-view Reconstruction
Evaluation on DTU. We evaluate the multi-view reconstruction
accuracy of our approach on 15 scenes from the DTU dataset [1].
Quantitative results are reported in table 2 and qualitative results
are shown in figure 10. We achieve state-of-the-art geometric accu-
racy, tied with GGGS [63] and BlobsToSpokes [13], while requir-
ing a training time of approximately 2.5 minutes. Our approach
reproduces high-frequency geometric details that GGGS and Blob-
sToSpokes miss, this is particularly noticeable in the close-ups on
scans 24 and 106 shown at the top of fig. 10. We hypothesize the
qualitative improvement does not lead to quantitative improvement
due to noise in the ground truth point clouds and down-sampling
of the input reconstructions by the official evaluation script.
For consistency with the 3DGS-like approaches [21], we discard
vertices that project outside of the training masks as well as hidden
vertices before Chamfer distance evaluation, since closed surfaces
tend to be penalized under the DTU evaluation protocol.
Evaluation on BlendedMVS. We also compare our approach on 8
scenes from the BlendedMVS [59] dataset in table 1, with qualitative
results presented in figure 11. We measure Chamfer distances as
point-to-mesh distances in the normalized reference frame used
for training, with the same evaluation protocol on this dataset as
ProbeSDF [53]. The images were cropped before running PGSR
and GGGS according to the 2DGS protocol [21], since they require
a projection center in the middle of the images. We achieve high
geometric accuracy using the same hyperparameters as on DTU,
with GGGS slightly ahead quantitatively but with noticeable noise
(see figure 11). PGSR fails to produce a mesh in two instances due
to ambiguous white backgrounds which caused the GPU to run out
of memory during mesh extraction.
Additional evaluations of photometric accuracy are provided in
the supplementary material.

6.3
Ablations on DTU
We ablate our losses and regularization terms in table 4, ordered by
decreasing importance. Figure 13 compares against the base model
qualitatively. Training using a linear radiance tone-mapped after
rasterization, compared to a typical sigmoid-bounded nonlinear
radiance, avoids a failure case on scan 65 due to large patches of
saturated pixels, but makes little difference otherwise. Removing
the multi-view loss (No ZNCC) or the depth smoothing loss has a
strong impact overall. Setting 𝑎= 0.05 in the biased CVT loss (eq.
1), instead of 𝑎= 0.10 prevents the cells from shrinking sufficiently
which reduces accuracy but does not create hard failure cases. The
reflection probes are important for recovering smooth geometry in
specular regions (scan 110, 69). Adding an environment map helps
compensating for slight inaccuracies in the supervision masks (scan
83). The rest of the components have lower importance: the SSIM,
color correction curves and view probes are added for robustness
against inconsistent illumination conditions. Removing one of them
does not affect geometry meaningfully. Finishing training with
the non planar ZNCC term instead of the planar ZNCC gives a
qualitative improvement in fine details (scan 106). Finally, removing
the normal smoothing term (eq. 24) slightly improves the Chamfer
distance compared to our base model but also introduces artifacts
(scan 110). The gradient modulation (eq. 44) does not change the
end result but makes training more stable.

MeshlessVoronoi [46] and RestrictedVoronoi [4] use the same
strategy so we only discuss [4] which was slightly faster. The input
points are partitioned on a dense voxel grid as a pre-process. Then,
each query point visits the voxels in concentric rings and keeps
track of the KNN using a heap in shared memory. Importantly, we
doubled their hard-coded maximum search radius to accommodate
our adaptive point distributions, otherwise these baselines fail to
gather all requested neighbors.
CuPy-KNN [23] is a re-implementation of [24], based on a BVH
and using a heap stored in the registers. Torch-kdtree [16] works
similarly to Cupy-KNN but builds its acceleration structure on the
CPU. Overall, the heap-based approaches are much less efficient
than our gather-then-sort approach, even more so when temporal
consistency is leveraged. TrueKNN [41] relies on the RT cores
of the GPU to benefit from hardware-accelerated BVH traversal.
This strategy is effective for uniform point distributions but under
performs on our point clouds since several retries are needed for
low-density regions. We also include scikit-learn [43] as a CPU
reference.

7
Limitations and Future Work
A limitation of our Voronoi diagram construction algorithm is its
approximate nature, which makes it unsuitable for applications
requiring exact adjacencies. Iterative retries with a larger number
of neighbors in the spirit of Basselin et al. [4] could recover the miss-
ing adjacencies. A fallback to double precision could also improve
robustness in degenerate configurations.
As evaluated in the supplementary material, we find the pro-
portion of incomplete cells to be low in practice for our choice of
hyperparameters. More aggressive adaptivity could be balanced
using a larger number of neighbors but at an increased cost. Deriv-
ing theoretical upper bounds on the required number of neighbors
based on cell regularity [22] could be useful but may be inapplicable
in our case since we make no guarantee on sites spacing.
Extending our adaptive discretization to open scenes is an in-
teresting direction for future research. The main obstacles are our
naive sphere initialization and the potential lack of scalability for
representing geometric and photometric details over large spatial
extents. The former could be addressed using monocular priors [32]
and the latter could benefit from adaptive densification based on
image content [15, 26], or primitives with greater expressivity [50].
8
Conclusion
We have presented CVTSplat, an approach for multi-view recon-
struction combining adaptive Voronoi tessellations with differen-
tiable implicit surface modeling. Our centroidal regularization plays
a central role: beyond producing well-shaped cells, it enables us
to specialize several components of the pipeline for efficiency. In
particular, we introduced a Voronoi diagram construction algorithm
that exploits the regularity of the tessellation. We further tailored
neighbor queries, centroid computation, rasterization and mesh ex-
traction to our representation. Together, these developments yield
a highly efficient pipeline that achieves geometric accuracy com-
petitive with the state-of-the-art on two object-level reconstruction
benchmarks.

Mesh Defects Analysis
Table 9 reports the average number of topological errors in our
extracted meshes. We count the number of non-manifold edges, i.e.
edges having more than two incident triangles, and boundary edges
that have a single incident triangle. The implicit surface touches
the bounding box walls in some scenes, which creates legitimate
boundary edges. Therefore, we only count the ones within 95% of
the bounding box that actually correspond to small holes in the
extracted meshes. Missing Voronoi adjacencies can prevent the
formation of tetrahedra and produce small holes, where a single
triangle is missing in general. Non-manifold edges may result from
inconsistent tetrahedron selection near degenerate configurations
or from incomplete Voronoi neighbor lists. Overall, these defects
are infrequent relative to the number of mesh triangles and could
potentially be addressed by topological post-processing.

Details on the Multiview Loss
We use a multi-view loss inspired by PGSR [7]. Pairs of views that
are close to each other are chosen at random during training (a
mini-batch contains 4 views so that pairs can be formed). During
the forward pass of volume rendering, the depth of the first zero-
crossing of the SDF is recorded for each ray to form a depth map.

We evaluate mutual visibility over each pixel in view A in a
similar manner to the planar case. First, by computing the normal

and second, by applying the same cycle consistency check. Invalid
pixels or pixels for which the mutual visibility tests fail are skipped
in the computation of the moments in equations 56-61.
I.4
Loss
Our multi-view loss is LMV = (1 −ZNCC), summed over all valid
pixels in view A. We use the planar ZNCC computation for most
of the training since it is more stable, but switch to the nonplanar
ZNCC near the end to recover finer details. Note that the grayscale
ground truth images and depth maps follow the same coarse-to-fine
training regime as the color losses, which means that the small 5×5
receptive field will actually cover a larger proportion of the field of
view in the beginning.
