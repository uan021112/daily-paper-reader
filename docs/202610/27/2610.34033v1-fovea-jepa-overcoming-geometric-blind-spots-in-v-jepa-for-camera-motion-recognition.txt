Abstract 

Camera motion recognition requires distinguishing viewpoint changes induced by camera translation, rotation, or zoom from visual variations caused by object motion and scene dynam-ics. Current approaches often rely on large vision-language or video-language models, but their overreliance on high-level semantic correspondences makes them unreliable in un-derstanding spatio-temporal relationships. Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs), which learn by predicting changes in spatio-temporal representations (e.g., V-JEPA), of-fer a promising way to address this issue. Nevertheless, our experiments reveal an inherent limitation: although JEPAs provide a strong baseline, their emphasis on spatio-temporal modeling may underrepresent the geometric cues of some crit-ical regions, causing even broadly applicable augmentation strategies such as temporal reversal to degrade their perfor-mance on several motion families. In contrast, weaker stan-dalone geometry-oriented predictors (e.g., VGGT) with the same augmentations improve across all motion families. We term these geometrically underrepresented regions in JEPAs 

geometric blind spots and propose a Fovea-JEPA framework to identify and address them. Specifically, given V-JEPA as the base model and VGGT as a geometry expert, (i) we first analyze their prediction errors to construct pseudo-labels for weakly supervising a blind-spot predictor. (ii) The blind-spot predictor then generates token-wise intervention maps, which localize geometrically underrepresented regions. (iii) Finally, guided by these intervention maps, we adaptively fuse V-JEPA and VGGT features to perform camera-motion recognition. In this way, Fovea-JEPA addresses the geometric blind spot issue, and experimental results on three public datasets vali-date its effectiveness: Fovea-JEPA improves the state-of-the-art performance by 9.67% on CameraMotionDataset, 8.67% 

on CameraBench, and 24 .38% on ShotBench, respectively. Code and models will be released upon acceptance. 

## 1 Introduction 

Camera motion recognition is challenging, as it requires iden-tifying how a camera translates, rotates, or zooms while sep-arating viewpoint changes from object motion and scene dy-namics. Recent approaches increasingly rely on large vision-language models (VLMs) and VideoLLMs, benefiting from their broad visual-semantic knowledge. However, their re-liance on high-level semantic correspondences makes them unreliable in capturing the fine-grained spatio-temporal and geometric relationships required for camera-motion recog-

For example, zooming and moving forward can appear similar, while independently moving objects can resemble camera-induced motion. This task therefore calls for representations that model tempo-ral dynamics while preserving discriminative geometric ev-idence beyond high-level appearance and semantic cues. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn la-tent representations via spatio-temporal constraints, thereby encouraging robust context modeling (Bardes et al. 2024). For example, compared with contrastive and reconstruction-based video models, V-JEPA (Mur-Labadia et al. 2026) gen-erates dense features that are robust to visual corruptions and better preserve fine-grained physical distinctions (Alrasheed et al. 2026). These properties make V-JEPA a strong candi-date for camera motion recognition. However, it remains unclear whether the representational advantages of JEPAs extend consistently across different camera-motion families, particularly when fine-grained geo-metric evidence is required. To investigate this, we use tem-poral reversal (Pickup et al. 2014) as a controlled probe because it reverses direction-dependent motion cues while largely preserving scene appearance and spatial content, thereby revealing whether a representation captures the tem-poral and geometric evidence needed to distinguish oppos-ing camera motions. We compare the responses of V-JEPA and a VGGT-based predictor specialized in geometric mod-eling (Wang et al. 2025a). As reported in Section 4.3, V-JEPA remains the stronger standalone predictor and improves overall under temporal-reversal supervision, yet its accuracy decreases for several motion types. In contrast, the weaker VGGT-based predictor improves across every evaluated mo-tion class. This contrasting phenomenon suggests that VGGT retains complementary geometric cues that V-JEPA under-represents in some critical regions. We refer to the above geometrically underrepresented re-gions in V-JEPA as geometric blind spots . Nevertheless, indiscriminately combining V-JEPA and VGGT to handle these blind spots is prone to interfering with reliable V-JEPA predictions. This leads to a critical design requirement of our method, i.e., geometric evidence should complement the primary V-JEPA representation only in regions where are beneficial. ## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Camera Motion Understanding 

Current approaches to camera-motion understanding are pre-dominantly built on VLMs and VideoLLMs. CameraBench evaluates motion primitives in diverse Internet videos and reveals complementary limitations of structure-from-motion and VLMs (Lin et al. 2025); ShotBench evaluates expert-level shot language (Liu et al. 2025); and CineTechBench extends evaluation to broader cinematographic understand-ing and generation (Wang et al. 2025b). CamReasoner trains VideoLLMs to perform structured ego-motion inference (Wu et al. 2026). Taking a different route from semantic model-ing, Geometry-Guided Camera Motion Understanding in-jects 3D-derived motion cues into VideoLLMs to strengthen their sensitivity to camera geometry beyond appearance and semantic context (Feng, Musunuri, and Su 2026). This geometry-guided direction builds on advances in 3D reconstruction. Classical structure-from-motion recov-ers camera poses and scene structure through optimization (Schönberger and Frahm 2016), whereas learned models di-rectly predict 3D structure: DUSt3R regresses point maps (Wang et al. 2024b), VGGSfM scales learned multi-view reconstruction (Wang et al. 2024a), and MonST3R extends such reasoning to dynamic scenes (Zhang et al. 2025). VGGT unifies camera, depth, point-map, and tracking prediction in a feed-forward model (Wang et al. 2025a), making it a suitable geometry expert for camera-motion recognition. 

2.2 Video Representation Learning 

Self-supervised video representation learning commonly uses contrastive objectives or masked prediction. Contrastive methods learn invariance across clips from the same video (Qian et al. 2021), while masked video models reconstruct pixels or predict teacher features (Feichtenhofer et al. 2022; Tong et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023). Temporal order pro-vides an additional signal for motion understanding. Prior work uses forward–backward classification, structured label reversal, or time-equivariant learning to learn temporal struc-ture and motion-sensitive representations (Pickup et al. 2014; Price and Damen 2019; Jenni and Jin 2021; Wei et al. 2018; Jenni, Meishvili, and Favaro 2020). Recent studies also find that VideoLLMs remain weakly sensitive to temporal rever-sal (Xue, Luo, and Grauman 2025; Han et al. 2026). In our work, temporal reversal serves as a shared supervision sig-nal for comparing how V-JEPA and VGGT respond across different camera-motion families. 

2.3 Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures 

I-JEPA introduced joint-embedding prediction of masked tar-get representations from visible context without pixel recon-struction (Assran et al. 2023). V-JEPA extends this principle to video through latent spatio-temporal prediction (Bardes et al. 2024). V-JEPA 2 scales it to large-scale video pre-training (Assran et al. 2025), while V-JEPA 2.1 improves the spatial organization and temporal consistency of dense video features (Mur-Labadia et al. 2026). A recent system-atic study further reports that V-JEPA variants outperform the evaluated alternative video models in corruption robustness, fine-grained physical discrimination, and temporal-direction sensitivity (Alrasheed et al. 2026). These findings establish V-JEPA as a strong video representation, but its potential limitations in representing fine-grained geometric evidence remain underexplored. We study this issue within the scheme of camera motion recognition. 

## 3 Methodology 

3.1 Overview 

Task Formulation. Let V = {It}Tt=1 denote an input video with T frames, and let K denote the number of camera-motion classes. Our goal is to learn a mapping F : V → 

RK that produces K classification logits. We implement 

F with the proposed Fovea-JEPA framework, whose core component is an adaptive geometric intervention paradigm, as illustrated in Figure 2. Specifically, Fovea-JEPA adopts a dual-branch architec-ture composed of a JEPA-based visual branch and a geo-metric branch. Both branches take tokens extracted from the frozen backbones of V-JEPA (Mur-Labadia et al. 2026) and VGGT (Wang et al. 2025a) as inputs, and produce two fea-ture tokens encoding the visual and geometric context of the video, respectively. The two resulting tokens are fused to form a global context token. Together with dense spatial tokens from VGGT, this global token is fed into a blind-spot predictor to yield a blind-spot map, which highlights regions that are geometrically informative yet neglected by V-JEPA. We subsequently aggregate this blind-spot map into a scalar score to modulate the geometric intervention from VGGT on the global context. Finally, the modulated token is leveraged to predict the target class label. Despite the intuitive design of the above Fovea-JEPA framework, its practical realization faces substantial chal-lenges, primarily due to the absence of ground-truth annota-tions for geometric blind spots. A naive fusion of V-JEPA and VGGT features via learnable weights lacks interpretability and may degrade overall model performance, as VGGT ex-hibits weaker visual reasoning capability compared with V-JEPA. To address these issues, we introduce an error-guided pseudo-label generation strategy for blind-spot estimation in the subsequent section.

3.3 Blind-Spot Map Estimation 

Given the video-level intervention labels and fine-grained token-level geometric saliency signals, we construct a blind-spot predictor to jointly infer where spatial geometric inter-vention is needed and whether global modulation should be activated for each video clip. We first construct a cross-branch context token that in-tegrates both feature-level and prediction-level statistics to bridge the visual and geometric branches and capture their complementary discrepancies. 3.4 Blind-Spot-Guided Geometric Intervention 

For video clips with reliable visual semantics, forced geomet-ric fusion inevitably introduces irrelevant geometric noise and offsets the discriminative visual priors. To address this issue, we propose a blind-spot-guided adaptive geometric in-tervention strategy, which leverages the predicted blind-spot response map for dual-level adaptive modulation. Namely, the predicted blind-spot map Q serves two core functions in our framework. First, Q guides fine-grained token-level feature fusion to activate geometric compensation on salient regions. Second, Q is aggregated into a global clip-level intervention scalar to determine whether global geometric modulation is required. 

Clip-Level Intervention Score Aggregation. To derive a global intervention trigger from the dense blind-spot map 

Q, we aggregate it into a clip-level scalar intervention score 

q via a two-stage TopMean pooling strategy. Concretely, we first compute the average response of the top ξ1 salient spatial tokens per frame, and then aggregate the top ξ2 frame-level scores to obtain the final clip-level score q. This design inher-ently filters out noisy and trivial geometric responses, retain-ing only highly discriminative geometric blind-spot cues.

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Datasets and metrics. We evaluate Fovea-JEPA on three camera-motion benchmarks, including CameraMotion-Dataset (Feng, Musunuri, and Su 2026), CameraBench (Lin et al. 2025), and ShotBench (Liu et al. 2025). CameraMo-tionDataset contains 12,274 clips annotated with 15 camera-motion primitives. We follow its official split of 9,819 training, 1,227 validation, and 1,228 test clips, and report exact-match instance accuracy ( Inst. Acc. ), Macro-F1, and Weighted-F1. On CameraBench, we report mAP following the benchmark protocol, and also employ the average bi-nary accuracy (AB-Acc.), which is defined as the unweighted mean of ordinary binary accuracy across the 15 motion tasks following CamReasoner (Wu et al. 2026). For ShotBench, we use ShotQA for task adaptation and evaluate on the official 464-example camera-movement subset, reporting camera-movement accuracy ( CM-Acc. ). 

Baselines. We compare against general-purpose VLMs and task-specific camera-motion models. We consider Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al. 2025) and InternVL2.5-8B (Chen et al. 2025) as the general-purpose baselines. The task-specific baselines include a VGGT camera-cue classi-fier (Feng, Musunuri, and Su 2026), Cam-Motion-72B (Lin et al. 2025), CamReasoner-7B (Wu et al. 2026), and ShotVL-7B (Liu et al. 2025). We directly adopt the officially reported results of all baseline methods for fair comparison.

and 518 × 518 , respectively. Five-fold cross-validation is conducted, and we use out-of-fold predictions to obtain in-tervention labels. Our model is trained for 12 epochs with AdamW using a batch size of 8, a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4,and weight decay of 1 × 10 −4. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose Fovea-JEPA, a dual-branch adaptive geometric intervention framework for video camera motion recognition. Our framework compensates for the geomet-ric blind spots of JEPA models. Different from static fu-sion strategies that may introduce noisy geometric cues, our method leverages hierarchical pseudo-supervision with clip-level intervention labels and token-level geometric saliency targets to train a blind-spot predictor. This allows us to locate critical geometric regions and identify unreliable visual pre-dictions, and eventually perform adaptive token-level feature modulation and clip-level threshold-based prediction correc-tion. Extensive experiments validate our design, which ef-fectively combines robust visual generalization and precise geometric structural priors. References
