# ScheNav: Schematic-Guided Object Goal Navigation 

## Guichen Huang 1,2 Chenchen Jing 3 Che Sun 2,* Yuwei Wu 1,2,* Yunde Jia 2,1 1Beijing Institute of Technology 2Shenzhen MSU-BIT University 3Zhejiang University of Technology 

## Abstract 

Object goal navigation requires an embodied agent to locate and reach target objects in complex environments. Humans often rely on low-cost yet informative schematic maps, such as rough sketches or directional annotations. We develop a workflow for schematic-guided embodied navigation with two components: a training-data genera-tion pipeline and ScheNav, a closed-loop navigation frame-work. Our lightweight data-generation pipeline turns 2D floorplans into navigation training environments, yielding Furnished-RPLAN, a dataset of over 33k furnished lay-outs with paired navigation episodes and noise-controllable schematic maps. ScheNav turns rough schematic guid-ance into exploration and stopping decisions by using SchemaField, a learned spatial module that fuses the guid-ance with accumulated observations and predicts spa-tial value fields. The framework updates these fields as exploration proceeds, with trajectory-based training and a cross-frame consistency objective supporting coherent guidance across changing viewpoints. ScheNav learns to use schematic guidance entirely from simulated floor-plans and transfers this ability to unseen embodied envi-ronments. Experiments show improved navigation success and path efficiency across schematic maps, goal ellipses, and pointed directions, together with tolerance to the tested schematic perturbations and omissions. Together, these re-sults demonstrate that rough schematic guidance offers a practical way to improve embodied navigation.

Map-free agents pay no cost for map acquisition, while map-based agents enjoy strong global guidance about where to go. We ex-plore whether rough schematic guidance can help embodied agents navigate without requiring an accurate global map. Humans routinely provide and use exactly this kind of guidance [35]. When searching for a room in an unfamiliar building, we rarely demand a precise map. A rough sketch of the layout or a pointed direction is usually enough to fo-cus the search. For example, marking a destination on a rough floorplan can help someone find a room without specifying an exact route. Figure 1 illustrates this setting, where the agent, given a rough sketch and lo-cal observations, is steered toward the goal. Can the agent reliably convert such imperfect schematic guidance into ef-fective closed-loop navigation decisions? We develop a workflow for schematic-guided embodied navigation with two components: a training-data generation pipeline and ScheNav, a closed-loop navigation framework. The workflow starts with 2D floorplans, from which we generate environments for learning how to use schematic 1arXiv:2610.34286v1 [cs.RO] 25 Oct 2026 guidance. A vision-language model furnishes RPLAN floorplans [37] and extracts machine-readable furniture lay-outs, which are processed against the original floorplan geometry. From these layouts, we generate navigation episodes, simulated local observations, ground-truth spatial fields, and paired schematic maps with controllable noise and incompleteness. Environment generation and naviga-tion training take place entirely in 2D, without constructing 3D scenes or collecting embodied training trajectories. This lightweight pipeline produces Furnished-RPLAN, a dataset of over 33k furnished layouts, and supports both training and controlled evaluation of schematic-guided navigation. ScheNav turns the resulting training resources into a framework for using schematic guidance during navigation. In each episode, the agent receives a goal category and guidance in the form of a schematic map, goal ellipses, or a pointed direction. As the agent moves, its observations accumulate into a map of the area explored so far, which serves as its map memory . SchemaField, the only learned model in the framework, fuses the guidance with this ob-served map and predicts two spatial value fields. The dis-tance field scores locations by their navigable proximity to the goal, while the corridor field highlights efficient routes toward it. The schematic is treated as a coarse prior to be interpreted alongside observations, rather than an accurate map on which to plan directly. Fixed rules read the fields to guide exploration and verify goal candidates. During exploration, the agent selects the frontier with the highest corridor score [39]. The Goal Verification module checks whether a detected instance of the goal category maintains a high distance-field value over consecutive steps. When this condition is met, the agent commits to a final approach and stops on arrival. A single SchemaField supports the dif-ferent guidance forms without retraining. ScheNav attaches to an existing navigation system as a guidance layer, leaving perception, mapping, and low-level motion to its host. Effective schematic guidance must remain coherent as the agent moves and new observations become available. In our initial implementation, adding a field predictor trained on individual observation snapshots did not improve closed-loop navigation, despite producing plausible per-frame pre-dictions. Snapshot supervision, commonly used in learned spatial prediction for navigation [13, 23], does not by it-self enforce agreement across viewpoints. We observed that predictions for the same place could disagree after transfor-mation into the world frame, causing successive frontier de-cisions to undo earlier progress; we call this failure cross-frame inconsistency . SchemaField is therefore trained on simulated navigation trajectories, with a cross-frame con-sistency objective that aligns predictions across viewpoints. The corridor field further concentrates value along near-shortest paths, turning small differences in goal proximity into clearer frontier choices. These designs allow the frame-work to update its guidance as exploration proceeds while maintaining coherent decisions across changing viewpoints. We evaluate this workflow by training ScheNav entirely on simulated floorplans and deploying it in unseen embod-ied environments without embodied training data. Inte-grated into UniGoal [41], ScheNav uses schematic guid-ance to improve navigation success and path efficiency on the instance-level HM3D benchmark. Experiments cover schematic maps, goal ellipses, and pointed directions, ex-amining how different forms of guidance support naviga-tion. Controlled perturbations on Furnished-RPLAN fur-ther test tolerance to schematic noise and missing informa-tion. Component ablations examine how trajectory-based training, cross-frame consistency, corridor prediction, and Goal Verification contribute to closed-loop performance. A supplementary demonstration uses a genuinely hand-drawn map as input to the navigation pipeline. Together, these experiments examine both the practical value of schematic guidance and the framework designs needed to use it effec-tively. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We develop a workflow for schematic-guided embod-ied navigation that connects lightweight 2D training-data generation with a closed-loop navigation framework, pro-viding a practical route for incorporating rough schematic guidance into embodied navigation. • We construct Furnished-RPLAN, a dataset of over 33k furnished layouts with paired navigation episodes, ground-truth spatial fields, and noise-controllable schematic maps, through a pipeline that operates en-tirely in 2D without requiring 3D scene construction or embodied trajectory collection. • We introduce ScheNav, a framework that converts schematic guidance and accumulated observations into exploration and goal-verification decisions. Trajectory-based training and cross-frame consistency support co-herent guidance, and experiments evaluate transfer to em-bodied navigation across multiple guidance forms and controlled perturbations.

Navigation with Human Spatial Guidance. Using human-provided spatial guidance, such as sketches, route maps, and verbal cues, is a long-standing goal in robot nav-igation [28, 32]. Early work localizes and navigates directly in hand-drawn sketches with unknown local metric distor-tions [4, 5]. Language-based guidance has also proven ef-fective, ranging from dialogue-based clarification [33] to Vision-and-Language Navigation with sequential instruc-tions [1, 11, 15]. More recently, HAM-Nav [31] interprets hand-drawn maps with a vision-language model under scale distortions and missing landmarks, and FloNa [16] shows that accurate architectural floorplans provide strong naviga-tion priors. This line of work, from sketch localization to floorplan priors, generally treats the provided map as the geometric space in which the agent localizes, plans, or rea-sons. We instead use the schematic as a weak spatial prior that is fused with the agent’s observations. ScheNav en-codes diverse schematic forms into a shared representation and grounds them in the partial map the agent has actually built, allowing the same framework to support schematic maps, ellipses, pointed directions, and no schematic input. A hand-drawn sketch can enter this interface after conver-sion, while its effect is continuously modulated by the qual-ity of the guidance and the evidence accumulated during navigation. 

Spatial Priors and Value Prediction for Navigation. Se-mantic priors learned from large-scale 3D environments, such as room layouts and object co-occurrences, improve navigation in unseen scenes [18, 20, 40], supported by sim-ulators and datasets including Gibson [38], Matterport3D [7], HM3D [22], and Habitat [25, 30]. Closest to our for-mulation, active exploration methods predict potential func-tions or semantic maps from the partial map and score fron-tiers accordingly [10, 13, 23, 24]. However, when these pre-dictors are supervised on individual snapshots, their outputs are not constrained to remain consistent across viewpoints, which can undermine closed-loop decisions. Our trajectory-based training and cross-frame consistency objective align predicted fields in the world frame under exact relative pose 3transforms, directly constraining the quantity used for nav-igation decisions. Complementary to existing 3D embod-ied priors, RPLAN provides large-scale structured floor-plan data [2, 21, 37]. Our training-data generation pipeline furnishes these floorplans with machine-readable layouts, paired navigation episodes, and schematic guidance, al-lowing us to train spatial prediction entirely on simulated environments and transfer the resulting framework to em-bodied navigation. ## 3. Method 

3.1. Framework Overview 

At each time step t, the agent receives a semantic obser-vation ot, its pose pt, and a goal category g, together with optional schematic guidance s: a schematic map optionally annotated with start and goal markers, goal ellipses alone, or merely a pointed direction. The guidance is imperfect. It may be noisy, incomplete, spatially distorted, partially an-notated, or entirely absent, and it is therefore treated as a weak human spatial prior rather than as metrically aligned supervision. ScheNav organizes navigation as a closed loop (Fig-ure 2). The map memory accumulates the observation history into the partial map mt and, through a standard frontier-extraction routine, supplies the current frontier can-didates and the visible instances of the goal category. 

SchemaField , the only learned component our framework introduces, predicts two value fields over the explored area, 

(Vt, C t) = fθ (s, m t, p t, g ), (1) where the distance field Vt scores every location by its nav-igable proximity to the goal and the corridor field Ct con-centrates value along efficient routes toward it. The decision transformation and the Goal Verification are two rules with no learned parameters: the former selects the next fron-tier by its corridor score, and the latter commits to stop-ping once the distance-field value at a detected goal instance stays persistently high. The execution component turns each decision into motion and feeds new observations back into the map memory, closing the loop. The loop dictates the central requirement on the learned model. Because SchemaField is re-queried at every step from a different pose, its outputs must agree in the world frame across queries. This consistency requirement is part of the model’s contract rather than an optional refinement, and it shapes both the training setup and the decision tar-get described below. The challenge throughout is not to reconstruct an accurate global map, but to convert weak schematic guidance into predictions that remain reliable over an entire episode while degrading gracefully as the guidance weakens. 

3.2. Host Navigation Interface 

Both components in this section are supplied by the host navigation system and are specified by interface rather than by a particular implementation; a lightweight floorplan sim-ulator instantiates them during training, and an existing nav-igation system instantiates them at deployment. 

Map memory. The map memory accumulates the obser-vation history into the partial map mt and answers two queries used by the rest of the framework: the current fron-tier candidates, obtained by a standard frontier-extraction routine, and the visible instances of the goal category, ob-tained by a non-learned connected-component detection on the egocentric semantic observation, which the Goal Verifi-cation evaluates to issue the stop signal. 

Execution. The execution component takes the selected frontier or homing target, invokes the underlying navigation stack to move toward it, and updates the map memory with the new observations. 

3.3. SchemaField 

At each step SchemaField receives an egocentric seman-tic crop of the memory, the schematic guidance, the agent pose, and the goal category. Whenever schematic input is present, it is coarsely registered into the egocentric obser-vation frame by the annotated start position and the agent heading, and the remaining distortion is intentionally left for the model to absorb. Structural schematic content and optional annotations are encoded separately, so that the same model can consume a schematic map with or with-out a goal marker, goal ellipses, a noisy pointed direction, or no schematic input at all. All inputs are mapped into a unified token space and pro-cessed by a single joint Transformer,

From the fused representation SchemaField decodes the two fields over the explored area. 3.4. Decision Transformation 

The decision transformation contains no learned parame-ters. At each decision point it scores the frontier candi-dates Ut queried from the map memory with the corridor field and steers the agent toward the highest-scoring one, 

u∗ = arg max u∈U t Ct(u), while stopping is governed by a dedicated gate. 

Goal Verification. The gate queries the map memory for the visible instances of the goal category and samples the distance field at their locations. When the sampled value ex-ceeds a threshold τstop for two consecutive steps, the agent commits, switches from exploration to homing on the best-valued instance, and stops on arrival. The commitment is never reverted, while the homing target keeps updating to the currently best instance. Because the absolute scale of the predicted field differs across input conditions, τstop is calibrated per condition on held-out simulated floorplans and then frozen before embodied evaluation. 

3.5. Trajectory-Consistent Training 

Failure mode. A distance field trained on independent observation snapshots can be accurate in every frame yet still fail to drive navigation. Because the field is predicted in the egocentric frame, two predictions made before and after a large rotation must agree once re-expressed in the world frame, but with single-snapshot training nothing en-forces this. Empirically, after the agent turns by 90 ◦ or more, the world-frame correlation between consecutive pre-dictions drops to −0.15 , and the movement direction im-plied by consecutive frontier decisions flips in 45 .6% of cases. In a closed navigation loop these flips cancel each other’s progress, which explains why a field that looks good per frame does not convert into navigation success. 

Sequence training. Instead of sampling independent snapshots, we generate training data along simulated navi-gation trajectories. The observed area grows monotonically as the agent explores, and the heading varies naturally along the path. Each trajectory contributes a sequence of (obser-5vation, schematic, target-field) tuples whose statistics match what the model encounters at deployment, including par-tially explored maps and arbitrary orientations. 

Cross-frame consistency objective. On top of sequence sampling, we add an explicit consistency loss. For pairs of frames of the same underlying state under different head-ings, and for adjacent snapshots along a trajectory, we align the two predicted fields with the exact relative rigid trans-form between the two poses and penalize disagreement on the jointly visible region. This directly optimizes the property that closed-loop navigation needs: predictions ex-pressed in a common frame must agree regardless of the agent’s viewpoint. Together, sequence training, the consis-tency objective, and the corridor target reduce the direction-flip rate from 45 .6% to 10 .1% and lift the post-turn world-frame correlation from −0.15 to +0 .80 . We ablate each design choice in the experiments. 

3.6. Furnished-RPLAN Benchmark 

We build Furnished-RPLAN , a large-scale benchmark of furnished indoor environments derived from the RPLAN floorplan dataset [37]. Each vector floorplan provides ex-act occupancy, semantics, and geodesic distance fields; on top of it, a two-stage vision-language pipeline produces a machine-readable furniture layout normalized to roughly forty Habitat furniture categories. We then generate nav-igation episodes and paired schematic maps with control-lable noise and incompleteness. The final model is trained on 33 ,992 furnished layouts, with layouts held out by floor-plan ID for validation and threshold calibration. Full con-struction details and pipeline visualizations are deferred to the supplementary material. 

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

Environments and protocols. We evaluate ScheNav on embodied navigation in Habitat [25, 30] with HM3D scenes [22], under the text-goal benchmark introduced by UniGoal [41]: 1000 episodes with the official success criterion, each targeting a specific object instance that baseline meth-ods identify through a free-form textual description; we adopt the protocol unchanged. ScheNav is integrated into UniGoal, replacing its frontier selection and stopping de-cisions; the replaced decisions are driven only by the goal category and the schematic guidance, while the rest of the UniGoal pipeline, including its text interface, is left un-touched, so all arms and baselines run the same episodes un-der the same criterion. We run the official UniGoal imple-mentation; its LLM serving stack is detailed in the supple-mentary material. This benchmark is distinct from, and sub-stantially harder than, category-level ObjectNav, on which the same baselines report much higher numbers. Controlled component analyses use a frozen 122-episode, 36-scene subset of the official episodes. 

Metrics. For navigation we report Success Rate (SR), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), soft-SPL, and distance to success (DTS). For frontier quality we report how often the selected frontier matches the ground-truth-optimal choice and the ground-truth geodesic distance of the selected frontiers. For cross-frame consistency we re-port the direction-flip rate, how often the movement di-rection implied by consecutive decisions reverses, and the world-frame correlation between consecutive predictions after large rotations. Field-quality metrics used for model selection and training hyperparameters are provided in the supplementary material.

4.5. Qualitative Analysis 

Figure 4 compares closed-loop trajectories on a representa-tive episode, with our four guidance levels and the unmod-ified host running the same episode. The input-condition gradient is directly visible: under the schematic-map, el-lipse, or direction conditions, the agent proceeds to the goal along a nearly direct route, while the no-guidance arm takes a longer but still purposeful path. The host, in contrast, wanders through repeated revisits and ends with an incor-rect stop. A demonstration in which a user hand-draws the schematic map and the agent navigates from it end to end is provided in the supplementary material. 

4.6. Failure Analysis and Limitations 

We classify all failed episodes of each guidance arm on the 1000-episode benchmark into three exclusive categories: 

wrong commits , where the agent commits to an incorrect instance; late stops , where the goal category is detected but the episode ends without a correct stop; and exploration misses , where the goal is never observed. With a schematic map, failures concentrate in the stopping chain: 67 .6% are late stops, and a quarter of failures end within one meter of the success radius, while wrong commits account for 

16 .3% . As guidance weakens, wrong commits take over, reaching 62 .1% under direction-only guidance and 74 .1% 

with no guidance, driven by host-perception false positives compounded by same-class ambiguity. Excluding episodes ended by wrong commits raises the schematic-map success rate from 61 .4% to 65 .5% , bounding the cost of host per-ception errors at four points. Two directions follow. First, under strong guidance the residual bottleneck is stopping precision rather than spatial reasoning; a finer-grained ter-minal policy on top of the Goal Verification is a natural ex-tension. Second, under weak guidance same-class instance ambiguity dominates, calling for instance-level reasoning beyond category cues. 

## 5. Conclusion 

We presented an end-to-end workflow for schematic-guided embodied navigation, combining a training-data generation pipeline with ScheNav, a closed-loop navigation frame-work. ScheNav grounds imperfect schematic guidance in the agent’s map memory and converts it into exploration and stopping decisions through a learned spatial module and fixed decision rules. We also identified cross-frame incon-sistency as a key failure mode when per-frame spatial pre-diction drives closed-loop navigation, and addressed it with trajectory-based training, a cross-frame consistency objec-tive, and a geodesic-corridor decision target. Trained purely on Furnished-RPLAN and integrated into UniGoal, the re-sulting framework raises the success rate of the underlying map-free host from 20.2% to 61.4% when given a schematic map, while even a single noisy pointed direction retains a substantial portion of the gain.
