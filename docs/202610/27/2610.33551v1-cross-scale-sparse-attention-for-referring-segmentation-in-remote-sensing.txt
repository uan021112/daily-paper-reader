Abstract
High-resolution remote sensing (RS) imagery provides fine spatial detail over large ar-
eas, making it essential for fine-grained scene understanding. Referring Remote Sens-
ing Image Segmentation (RRSIS) aims to segment regions described by natural lan-
guage, yet many existing methods rely on sequential alignment–fusion pipelines with
limited joint modeling of cross-scale visual hierarchies and linguistic dependencies.
Moreover, dense attention mechanisms struggle with the sparsity and wide spatial
dispersion of RS targets, while current datasets offer limited structured evaluation of
multi-target references, multi-hop relational reasoning, and multi-granularity descrip-
tions, limiting both model development and evaluation. To address these challenges,
we propose a Cross-Scale Sparse Attention Model (CSSAM), which aligns multi-
scale visual features with complex referring expressions through two key modules.
The Cross-scale Modality-aligned Fusion Module (CMFM) performs unified cross-
scale cross-modal fusion, while the Dynamic Multimodal Sparse Hybrid Attention
(DMSHA) selectively attends to informative visual–textual tokens, reducing dense
visual interactions. Furthermore, we introduce M3RRSIS-D, a RRSIS dataset explic-

1. Introduction
Large-scale remote sensing (RS) imagery, empowered by recent advances in imag-
ing technology such as array cameras [1–3] and high-resolution electro-optical sen-
sors [4], can now provide high-resolution detail information while covering vast ar-
eas up to kilometer-scale [5]. These characteristics make RS imagery well-suited
for fine-grained scene interpretation. In particular, Referring Remote Sensing Image
Segmentation (RRSIS)—which segments regions described by natural language—has
emerged as a core task for aligning visual content with linguistic expressions at the
pixel level. It allows a user to select particular objects, such as buildings beside a road,
rather than segmenting every instance of a predefined class.
Recent progress in RRSIS has been made through increasingly sophisticated text-
visual fusion strategies. LAVT [6] employed pixel-word attention to directly link
words with spatial locations; LGCE [7] used language to guide cross-scale visual
enhancement; RMSIN [8] introduced modules for intra- and inter-scale feature re-
finement; while FIANet [9] parsed referring expressions into structured semantic
parts. RSRefSeg [10] leveraged pre-trained models like CLIP and SAM to combine
global semantics with segmentation priors. Despite these advances, jointly modeling
cross-scale visual context and linguistic dependencies remains important. Moreover,
the sparsity and spatial dispersion of relevant targets in high-resolution RS imagery
motivate reducing unnecessary dense attention interactions. Fig. 1 illustrates these

In addition to architectural challenges, existing RRSIS datasets underrepresent key
semantic complexities inherent to RS scenes. Referring expressions often involve (1)
multi-target references, where multiple regions satisfy a description, sometimes also
requiring the model to possess counting ability (e.g., “three large baseball fields”);
(2) multi-hop expressions in the reference-dependent grounding sense [11], where
localization uses another object (e.g., “tennis courts below the parking lot”); and (3)
multi-granularity descriptions for the same target, from simple nouns (“vehicle”) to
complex attribute- and relation-based expressions (“red car between two black cars”).
These aspects introduce ambiguity and contextual grounding demands, yet are rarely
3


annotated explicitly in current benchmarks.
To bridge this gap, we introduce the Cross-Scale Sparse Attention Model
(CSSAM), a unified architecture integrating cross-scale multimodal fusion and
query-dependent sparse attention for remote sensing referring segmentation.
The
Cross-scale Modality-aligned Fusion Module (CMFM) combines bidirectional
cross-modal attention with text self-attention and multi-scale deformable visual
self-attention. The Dynamic Multimodal Sparse Hybrid Attention (DMSHA) then
predicts sampling offsets from each visual query and the pooled text representation,
and attends to the selected visual keys and values together with text tokens. Unlike
dense attention, DMSHA limits visual interactions to sampled locations; compared
with conventional hierarchical fusion, it explicitly predicts sampling locations
across scales based on each visual query and the referring expression. Our main
methodological novelty lies in DMSHA’s text-conditioned cross-scale sampling.
To support the training and evaluation of models like CSSAM, we construct the
M3RRSIS-D dataset, which explicitly covers three core complexities of RS referring
segmentation: Multi-target descriptions (M1), Multi-hop relational reasoning (M2),
and Multi-granularity expressions for the same target (M3). Grounded in diverse RS
scenes, M3RRSIS-D enables explicit assessment of model performance across mul-
tiple reasoning types, providing a benchmark closely aligned with real-world remote
sensing applications.
In summary, the main contributions of our work are as follows:
(1) A dedicated dataset: We introduce M3RRSIS-D, a benchmark for referring
segmentation in remote sensing that systematically covers three key complexities,
namely multi-target expressions, reference-dependent multi-hop reasoning, and multi-
granularity descriptions of the same target or group of targets.
(2) An integrated model: We propose CSSAM, a Transformer-based architecture
designed for cross-scale multimodal alignment in RS scenes. It includes CMFM,
which bridges text and multi-scale visual features through cross- and self-attention,
enabling consistent reasoning across spatial levels.
(3) A sparse attention module: We design DMSHA, which reduces dense visual
interactions by sampling cross-scale visual key-value pairs and attending jointly to
4


these samples and the text tokens. Sampling locations depend on both the visual
query and the referring expression.
2. Related work
Referring Segmentation in Remote Sensing Imagery. RRSIS aims to segment image
regions based on natural language expressions, requiring fine-grained alignment be-
tween high-resolution visual data and linguistic semantics. Early methods for refer-
ring segmentation typically use CNNs for visual encoding and RNNs for language
processing, with cross-modal embeddings for feature alignment, such as the end-to-
end CNN-RNN model by Hu et al. [12] and the Recurrent Refinement Network (RRN)
by Li et al. [13], which progressively refines multiscale features. Attention-based
cross-modal methods have also been widely studied. Representative works include
CMSA [14] for cross-modal self-attention, LSCM [15] for linguistically structured
context modeling, CMPC [16] for progressive entity-relation reasoning, BRINet [17]
for bi-directional cross-modal dependency modeling, CMPC+ [18] which is an im-
proved CMPC-based progressive comprehension framework with text-guided multi-
level feature refinement, and CrossVLT [19] for mutual early fusion between vision
and language encoders across all stages to enhance cross-modal context modeling.
LAVT [6] achieves early cross-modal fusion within a vision Transformer.
More recently, transformer-based methods have also advanced referring segmen-
tation in RS imagery. LGCE [7] enhances visual features from last two scales through
language guidance, RMSIN [8] addresses scale and rotation challenges via multi-scale
interaction and adaptive convolutions, FIANet [9] perform fine-grained text-image
alignment through expression decoupling, and RSRefSeg [10] leverages CLIP and
SAM for robust multimodal feature activation. Zhang et al. [20] further introduce a
hierarchical textual–visual guidance framework that parses complex referring expres-
sions and uses hierarchical textual cues to modulate visual representations. Mean-
while, large vision–language models such as LISA [21] and GeoChat [22] demonstrate
strong general referring capabilities, but their reliance on large-scale foundation archi-
tectures typically entails substantial computational overhead, which can limit practi-
5


cality in resource-constrained deployment scenarios.
SBANet [23] uses learnable query tokens for bidirectional vision–language align-
ment and a text-conditioned channel and spatial aggregator for cross-scale interaction.
DiffRIS [24] adapts pre-trained text-to-image diffusion features through a context per-
ception adapter and a progressive cross-modal reasoning decoder. CSSAM instead
predicts language-conditioned sampling offsets for each visual query and jointly at-
tends to sampled cross-scale visual features and text tokens.
Sparse Attention Strategies for Long-Sequence Transformers. Transformers excel
across vision and language tasks but suffer from quadratic complexity with input
length, limiting scalability for high-resolution or long-sequence data. To address this,
sparse attention mechanisms have been proposed, broadly categorized into KV cache
compression, blockwise KV management, sampling-based selection, and dynamic
hierarchical sparsity.
KV cache compression reduces memory by retaining key tokens or compressing
stored states, as in H2O [25] for heavy-hitter retention and SnapKV [26] for cluster-
ing critical positions. LLM×MapReduce [27] adopts divide-and-conquer long-context
processing. Blockwise KV management selectively retains full KV caches based on
attention roles, with DuoAttention [28] identifying retrieval-critical heads, Quest [29]
introducing query-aware KV loading, and SeerAttention [30] learning block-level
sparsity through gated attention. Sampling and hash-based selection reduces attention
overhead by estimating token importance, as seen in ClusterKV [31] with semantic
clustering, HashAttention [32] using Hamming-space mappings, and MagicPIG [33]
applying LSH for efficient sparse attention. Integrated dynamic sparsity methods like
NSA [34] combine coarse- and fine-grained selection into a hierarchical framework
for efficient long-context training and inference.
These sparse attention designs primarily target unimodal long-text modeling, with
limited adaptation to high-resolution RS imagery, where sparse and dispersed targets
naturally align with sparse modeling strategies.
In visual segmentation, EntroFormer [35] selects high-entropy regions to capture
sparse global context and combines it with dense local features. CSSAM instead
6


learns sampling locations conditioned on the visual query and referring expression.
CTVIS [36] addresses a different dependency: it aligns training with inference through
instance-embedding learning and a memory bank for temporal association in online
video segmentation. Our focus is spatial, language-guided sampling within a single
image, not entropy-based region selection or temporal consistency learning.
Datasets for Large-scale RS Scenes. Existing remote sensing (RS) datasets support
tasks like object detection and segmentation.
For object detection, the VisDrone
dataset [37] comprises 8,599 drone-captured images with over 540,000 bounding
boxes. The DIOR-R dataset [38] offers 23,463 images and over 190,000 instances
across 20 categories, and the DOTA v2.0 dataset [39] includes 11,268 images across
18 categories, with over 1.7 million object instances. For segmentation, the LoveDA
dataset [40] features 5,987 high-resolution images with 166,768 annotated objects.
The Vaihingen and Potsdam datasets [41] contain 33 and 38 image patches at ground
sampling distances of 9 cm and 5 cm, respectively, with true orthophotos and digital
surface models, while the Inria Aerial Image dataset [42] contains 360 high-resolution
images (5000 × 5000 pixels) over 810 km2, designed for urban semantic segmenta-
tion. Recent efforts have introduced two datasets for RRSIS: RefSegRS [7] provides
annotated expressions for object segmentation in RS imagery; and RRSIS-D [8] offers
a larger-scale dataset with diverse spatial scales and orientations.

3.1. Dataset Sources and Annotation Pipeline
The source datasets were selected to cover diverse RS imaging conditions, spa-
tial resolutions, environments, and object categories. Together, they provide a broad
range of aerial scenes with varied object distributions and land-cover characteristics,
supporting the construction of diverse referring segmentation samples. The referring
expressions and corresponding target polygons were manually annotated and indepen-
dently verified following the protocol described below.
LoveDA, DIOR-R, DOTA v2.0, and VisDrone images were used without addi-
tional processing. Potsdam and Vaihingen images were divided into 1024 × 1024
patches with a fixed stride of 512 pixels in both directions; Inria images were divided
into 1000 × 1000 patches with a stride of 500 pixels. No resizing was applied during
dataset construction. Annotators selected images supporting at least three referring
expressions. For LoveDA, DIOR-R, DOTA v2.0, and VisDrone, the original training
and validation assignments of the selected images were preserved. For Potsdam, Vai-
hingen, and Inria Aerial Image, the resulting image patches were randomly assigned
8


to the training and validation subsets.

3.2. Annotation Protocol
To ensure semantic validity and precise spatial grounding, M3RRSIS-D adopts a
dual-annotator protocol inspired by ReferItGame [43] and GigaGrounding [11]. The
annotation process involves 36 annotators in Group-A and 7 annotators in Group-B
and lasted approximately 100 days. The larger Group-A is responsible for the more
labor-intensive authoring stage, including image inspection, referable target selection,
expression writing, and polygon delineation. Group-B serves as an independent verifi-
cation group and performs text-only localization: it receives only the referring expres-
sion and independently annotates the target polygon without access to the Group-A
mask.
Dual-Annotator Workflow. Group-A generates natural language expressions and
delineates corresponding polygon masks.
Expressions are categorized as: (i) M1
multi-target, describing multiple target instances; (ii) M2 multi-hop, specifying
9


targets through relations to other reference objects; and (iii) M3 multi-granularity,
where up to five hierarchical expressions (L1–L5) incrementally add attributes such as
color, position, object type, shape, or relational context. The labels describe different
properties and may overlap.
Group-B independently annotates the target via polygons using only the textual
descriptions. An expression is retained only if each referred target satisfies an A/B
polygon IoU of at least 0.5. For multi-target cases, both groups must identify the same
number of instances; any missing or extra instance is treated as a failed annotation,
and every matched instance must satisfy the IoU threshold. Failed samples are revised
and re-evaluated; persistently ambiguous or inconsistent cases are discarded.
For M3 multi-granularity annotations, each L1–L5 expression is treated as an inde-
pendent referring expression and must pass the text-only localization check separately.
Therefore, the retained L1–L5 descriptions are not merely progressively extended at-
tributes; each level is independently valid, unambiguous, and able to localize the cor-
responding mask.
During quality control, approximately half of the candidate samples were dis-
carded, mainly due to ambiguous expressions, inconsistent A/B localization, missing
or extra instances in multi-target annotations, or masks that failed the IoU agreement
criterion. No LLM was used to generate the final referring expressions or polygon
annotations.
Semantic Structure of Annotations. M1 (multi-target) expressions refer to more than
one target instance to be segmented, e.g., “three large baseball fields”. Reference
objects used only for localization do not count as segmentation targets. Group-A an-
notates each target instance separately, and Group-B must correctly identify all targets
solely from language. This setting evaluates instance grouping and, where specified,
numerical cues.
M2 (multi-hop) adopts the definition of multi-hop expressions in GigaGrounding
[11], where a target is identified through its relationship with other reference objects.
In our dataset, these relationships are spatial, as illustrated by “the school to the right
of the river.” This category includes both single target–reference relations and more
10


complex relational chains involving multiple objects.
M3 (multi-granularity) labels expressions within annotated groups that share the
same target mask but differ in descriptive detail. Up to five levels are provided: L1
includes a single salient attribute; L2 combines two attributes; L3 adds object-type or
shape specificity; L4 incorporates relational context; and L5 integrates multi-object
spatial reasoning. Each level is validated independently. The mask may contain one
or multiple targets. For example, “three red houses to the right of the river” satisfies
both the multi-target and relational rules, and also belongs to M3 if it is part of such
an annotated description group.
3.3. Dataset Statistics
The M3RRSIS-D dataset contains 5,603 high-resolution RS images (4,842 for
training and 761 for validation) and 66,305 annotated referring expressions (57,720
for training and 8,585 for validation), covering multi-instance targets, relational rea-
soning, and multi-level semantic granularity.
Across the three semantic dimensions, M1 includes 7,723 expressions, M2 in-
cludes 23,357 expressions, and M3 includes 51,976 expressions. Within M3, five hi-
erarchical levels (L1–L5) contain 19,554, 19,554, 9,732, 2,538, and 598 expressions,
respectively, reflecting a progressive shift from coarse global semantics to fine-grained
local detail. These dimensions are complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and
a single expression may belong to multiple dimensions simultaneously.
The pairwise intersections of M1 and M2, M1 and M3, and M2 and M3 contain
2,260, 4,463, and 17,987 expressions in the training set, respectively, compared with
291, 750, and 2,783 in the validation set. The intersection of all three dimensions
contains 1,683 training expressions and 254 validation expressions.
M3RRSIS-D associates each image with multiple referring expressions, averag-
ing 11.83 expressions per image. As shown by the curves on the left of Fig. 2, mask
areas exhibit a long-tailed distribution—most targets are small (≤1%), while a few
cover large regions such as industrial complexes or airports—supporting comprehen-
sive evaluation of cross-scale semantic representation and fusion.

4. Cross-Scale Sparse Attention Model
4.1. Model Architecture
The overall architecture of CSSAM is depicted in Fig. 4. Given an image I and a
query text t, CSSAM predicts a binary mask M indicating the region referred to by
the query. The model consists of five components: visual and textual encoders that
transform the inputs into embeddings; a Cross-scale Modality-aligned Fusion Module
(CMFM) that aligns textual and visual features across spatial levels via cross- and self-
attention; a Dynamic Multimodal Sparse Hybrid Attention (DMSHA) module that
enhances visual features using dynamically sampled multimodal features; a fusion
gate that adaptively merges the outputs of CMFM and DMSHA at each scale; and a
segmentation head that decodes the final fused features into pixel-level predictions.

4.7. Loss Function
We adopt a hybrid objective that combines cross-entropy loss and Dice loss to
jointly optimize pixel-wise classification accuracy and region-level overlap. Specif-
ically, cross-entropy focuses on hard pixel discrimination, while Dice loss directly
maximizes mask similarity by measuring the overlap between prediction and ground
truth. The final loss is formulated as
L = (1 −λ)LCE + λLDice,
(17)
where λ = 0.1 is the default. In the ablation study, we also evaluate λ = 0 and 0.5.
5. Experiments and Evaluations
5.1. Implementation Details
Experiment Settings. CSSAM employs Swin-B [45] (window size 12) and BERT-
base-uncased [44] as visual and textual encoders, respectively, initialized from the
pretrained Grounding DINO-B checkpoint [46] provided by MMDetection.
Both
encoders are frozen, while the remaining modules are trained for 20 epochs using
AdamW (learning rate 10−3, weight decay 10−4) without warm-up. Training uses 10
NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with a total batch size of 20.
Images and masks are resized to 800 × 800 using bilinear and nearest-neighbor
interpolation, respectively. Images are normalized using ImageNet statistics, without
18


random cropping, flipping, or color augmentation. Text is tokenized using uncased
WordPiece with a maximum length of 256 tokens. CMFM consists of 12 layers with
256-dimensional features, four cross-modal attention heads, and eight deformable vi-
sual attention heads over four scales with four sampling points per scale. DMSHA
uses 256-dimensional features, eight heads, and the same four-scale sampling config-
uration.
Evaluation Metrics. Following prior RRSIS studies [6–10], we report mean
Intersection-over-Union (mIoU), overall Intersection-over-Union (oIoU), and Preci-
sion (P@τ), where τ ∈{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}. mIoU averages the per-query IoUs;
oIoU divides the summed intersections by the summed unions. P@τ is the percentage
of queries with IoU ≥τ. Accuracy values are percentages. In comparison tables, bold
and underlining indicate the best and second-best values.
5.2. Comparison with State-of-the-Art Methods on M3RRSIS-D
Quantitative Results. To evaluate the effectiveness of CSSAM, we compare it
against a comprehensive set of SOTA referring segmentation methods, including
LSTM+ResNet101 baselines [16, 18] and recent Transformer-based architectures
such as LAVT [6], RMSIN [8], FIANet [9], and RSRefSeg [10]. All methods are
retrained and evaluated on M3RRSIS-D using their original training settings.
As shown in Table 2, CSSAM consistently outperforms all competing methods
across all metrics. It achieves the highest oIoU and mIoU, outperforming the strongest
baselines by 1.60 and 2.79 percentage points, respectively. CSSAM also achieves con-
sistent improvements across all IoU thresholds, reflecting balanced gains in both seg-
mentation accuracy and localization precision. These results demonstrate the overall
robustness of the proposed cross-scale fusion and sparse attention design.
Qualitative Results. Representative visual results of CSSAM, FIANet, and RSRef-
Seg under multi-target (M1), multi-hop (M2), and multi-granularity (M3) settings are
depicted in Fig. 7. These examples illustrate more complete masks for multiple targets
and better localization under relational and fine-grained descriptions.

Figure 8b–e illustrates three failure cases. In M1, the model misses three small
white buildings and selects larger white structures near the sports field (IoU 0%);
similar color and shape may obscure the requested group. In M2, it selects a building

green” at L2: most of the field is missed and a nearby basketball court is selected.
More descriptive detail therefore does not always yield consistent grounding among
adjacent, visually similar facilities.

The strong performance on both public benchmarks demonstrates that CSSAM
generalizes well beyond the newly introduced M3RRSIS-D benchmark. On RRSIS-
D, CSSAM outperforms SBANet by 5.45 points in mIoU and obtains the highest score
on every reported metric except oIoU, where it ranks second. On RefSegRS, FIANet
and HTVG are marginally better at P@0.5 and oIoU, respectively, whereas CSSAM
improves mIoU by 4.43 points over HTVG and achieves substantially higher P@0.8
and P@0.9. These results indicate that the proposed cross-scale sparse attention de-
sign transfers well to existing RRSIS datasets and provides more accurate localization
under strict overlap criteria.
6. Conclusion
This paper presented CSSAM, a cross-scale sparse attention framework for
referring segmentation in remote sensing imagery, together with M3RRSIS-D,
a benchmark covering multi-target, multi-hop relational, and multi-granularity
expressions. CSSAM combines cross-scale multimodal alignment with dynamically
sampled sparse attention, enabling effective interaction between linguistic semantics
and visual features at different spatial resolutions. Experiments on M3RRSIS-D and
two public RRSIS benchmarks demonstrate competitive segmentation performance,
particularly under strict IoU thresholds. The proposed benchmark also provides a
structured basis for evaluating fine-grained reasoning in future RRSIS studies.
The current study is limited by the geographic and sensor diversity of the source
imagery. Failure cases also show missed small target groups, incorrect spatial rela-
tions, and inconsistent grounding across expression granularities. Future work will
extend the benchmark to broader multi-sensor scenarios, and explore lighter imple-
mentations for resource-constrained deployment.
