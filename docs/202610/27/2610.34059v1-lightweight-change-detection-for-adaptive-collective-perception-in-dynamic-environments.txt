# Lightweight Change Detection for Adaptive Collective Perception in Dynamic Environments 

Thiemen Siemensma, Tiago Travassos, Jiacheng Zhang, and Bahar Haghighat 

Abstract Swarm robotics enables scalable inspection through collective percep-tion, where robots infer environmental properties from distributed observations. A common benchmark considers robots exploring black-white tiled environments and determining the majority tile type. While most work studies static environments, dynamic environments remain underexplored. We study collective perception in dy-namic environments where the tile proportion and the spatial correlation of surface patterns may change over time. To enable implementation on resource-constrained robots, we integrate a lightweight change detection mechanism into a Bayesian collective decision-making framework that resets robot beliefs when distribution shifts are detected. The proposed detector requires constant memory and constant per-sample computation, unlike two recent competitive approaches that store the full sample stream, making it suitable for memory-constrained robots. We further introduce a method that detects spatially correlated sampling and adapts sampling intervals online to mitigate correlation effects. Our simulation results in Webots show competitive change detection delays, more consistent precision across communica-tion settings, and improved decision accuracy under changing spatial correlations. 

## 1 Introduction 

Robotic inspection has gained significant interest over recent decades [11]. A rising trend is the application of robot swarms to distributed inspection tasks [11, 8, 23, 10]. Robot swarms enable scalable sensing for applications ranging from infrastructure inspection to disaster response [29, 4]. During such operations, a crucial challenge is collective decision-making based on distributed observations made by individuals

in the swarm. In infrastructure inspection tasks, this may correspond to determining whether a structure is safe to operate or requires maintenance [30]. Numerous studies formalize collective decision-making tasks in best-of-𝑛 prob-lems, commonly framed as site-selection tasks (analogous to ant nest choice) or as a tile classification task in environments with distinct tile colors [35, 12]. The swarm is tasked to select the best option (e.g. site/tile type) among 𝑛 options available. In particular, the collective perception case for 𝑛 = 2 is well studied and is gener-ally formulated in black–white tile environments, where a swarm must identify the majority tile type [19, 34]. Prominent solutions include consensus-based strategies [34, 38, 36, 37] and Bayesian frameworks [9, 27, 26]. Across these approaches, a fundamental speed–accuracy trade-off arises: faster decisions typically reduce ac-curacy, and vice versa [24, 36, 40, 38]. The implications of this trade-off are well studied in static environments, however, few studies consider dynamic environments, where surface patterns may change over time [32, 12, 13]. Moreover, the few exist-ing studies primarily focus on changes in the fill ratio, while changes in the surface pattern correlations remain unexplored. Correlation in the surface patterns is known to considerably influence decision-making difficulty [3, 2]. In dynamic environments, a key limitation of existing decision-making algorithms is decision lock-in, representing an absorbing decision state that cannot be exited re-gardless of new observations, thereby preventing decision reversal [12, 39, 20]. Prior work mitigates lock-in using stubborn agents [22], discarding outdated information [31], and limiting communication [32, 1]. Lower sampling rates can further improve reactivity by reducing sample correlation [1]. In static environments, sampling rate should correspond to feature size [9, 2], yet robots have not been shown to estimate and adapt to changing spatial correlations. Several approaches propose to mitigate decision lock-in by extending the Bayesian decision-making framework Bayes Bot [9]. In Bayes Bots, robots model the fill ratio using a Beta distribution and use the corresponding confidence, called belief, to make final decisions. Within the framework, a hysteresis mechanism that allows flexible decisions by requiring robots to exceed an evidence threshold before deciding was introduced in [5], however, its performance in dynamic environments remains unstudied. The Dynamic Bayes Bots (DBB) algorithm is specifically designed for dynamic fill ratio decision-making by restraining the belief at 𝑝 lim and resetting decisions when the belief drops under the threshold 𝑝 res [20]. Moreover, robots enhance adaptivity through three types of feedback: (i) a model reset by a more experienced neighbor with an opposite decision, (ii) a model overtake by a more experienced neighbor while indecisive, and (iii) posterior updates from neighbors that share the same final decision. While effective, DBB’s accuracy is constrained by 𝑝 lim , and resets are slow when the Beta posterior becomes inertia-heavy [20]. To address these limitations, Dynamic Bayes Bot Change Point Detection (DB-BCPD) was proposed [21], incorporating the change detection algorithm Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) [14]. Robots reset either via PELT detections or through received feedback within a given time period when: (i) encountering 𝑛 1 resetting neighbors, (ii) if undecided, adopt the decision that is held by a 𝑛 2 majority of neighbors, or (iii) a decision inversion when 𝑛 3 neighbors hold an opposite decision. DBBCPD improves adaptivity over DBB but must store the full sample stream, re-quiring O( 𝑛 ) memory (with O( 𝑛 2) cumulative compute from re-running detection), which renders it infeasible on the memory-constrained (32 kB) Kilobots used in their agent-based simulation study. Moreover, the effects of collision avoidance, correlated sampling, and long-range communication (3 body lengths) remain unclear. As an alternative, computational expenses of change detection can be reduced using control charts, such as CUmulative SUM charts (CUSUM) [18, 6], which are lightweight statistical tools that integrate deviations from an online-estimated in-control mean with O( 1) computational complexity [17, 15]. However, such ap-proaches have not yet been applied to collective perception. This work improves the adaptivity and computational efficiency of Bayesian dynamic collective perception through three contributions: (i) an O( 1)-memory CUSUM fill ratio change detector; (ii) an online estimator of spatial sampling corre-lation; and (iii) an adaptive sampling strategy that mitigates correlation by adjusting sampling intervals. We evaluate the methods against DBB and DBBCPD in our physics-based Webots framework [30]. 

## 2 Problem Statement 

We consider a swarm of 𝑁 𝑟 robots operating in a 1m × 1m dynamic environment composed of black and white tiles of size ℓ m × ℓ m, where the fill ratio 𝑓 represents the proportion of white tiles. As illustrated in Figure 1, we consider three environ-mental changes: (i) changes in fill ratio ( 𝑓 0 → 𝑓 1), (ii) changes in spatial correlation due to clustering, and (iii) changes in spatial correlation due to feature size variations (ℓ0 → ℓ1), where (·) 0 and (·) 1 denote pre- and post-change values, respectively. The robots are tasked with determining the majority tile type over time while actively estimating and adapting to spatial correlations to enhance decision-making accuracy.

## 4 Experimental Framework and Scenarios 

We validate our approaches using our previously developed simulation framework, which utilizes a walled environment in the physics-based simulator Webots [30]. 

Robot Model : Our simulated robot shown in Figure 2a is based on [7], which we previously revised to improve traversability on metal surfaces and update the elec-tronics [28]. The model includes sensing and actuation components representative of the physical platform, including differential drive, collision avoidance sensors, and RF24-based communication. In earlier work, sampling was performed at fixed time intervals [30]. Here, we replace this with distance-based sampling using onboard wheel-encoder odometry. Under typical move–sense–move behavior at a speed of 5 cm/s, the battery lifetime of the platform is approximately 1200 s. 

Parameter Selection : Based on our previously optimized random walk times at 5 cm/s, we rescale them to 𝑅 ∼ Cauchy (0.393 , 0.536 ) m [30]. Moreover, robots use a uniform prior Beta (𝛼 0, 𝛽 0) = Beta (1, 1), and make final decisions when their belief crosses 𝑝 𝑐 = 0.95. We use Python-based Monte Carlo simulations to find the relations for the CUSUM threshold in Equations (3) and (4). We perform 1,000 simulations for each 𝜃 𝑠 ∈ 5, 6, . . . , 20 at 𝛿 = 0.05, and record the corresponding ARL 0 values under a no-change scenario with worst-case variability ( 𝑓 0 = 0.5). This yields the parameters (𝑐, ℎ ) = (21 .10 , 0.42 ) in Equations (3) and (4). Moreover, we set Δ𝑁 = 0.25 and 𝜂 = 0.2, so a robot enlarges its step size when 𝑁 𝑡 < 1−Δ𝑁 = 0.75 and otherwise shrinks it by 𝜂 Δ𝑁 = 5%. 

Baseline Comparison : Each robot concurrently runs DBB, DBBCPD, and CUSUM to ensure fair comparison by using the same samples. For DBB, the change detection parameters are set to ( 𝑝 lim , 𝑝 res ) = (0.99 , 0.55 ), consistent with the orig-inal formulation [20]. DBBCPD’s parameters 𝑛 1, 𝑛 2, 𝑛 3 depend on swarm density and are therefore rescaled with 𝑁 𝑟 , and capped at 𝑁 𝑟 − 1, to preserve equal spatial coverage over time. Additionally, we use the feedback time period of 50 s as in [21]. 

Experimental Scenarios : We study three experimental scenarios, averaging results over 100 simulation runs per configuration. All experiments run for 1200 s to match the robot battery life, with environmental changes introduced halfway. 

Scenario 1: fill ratio change detection We aim to evaluate change-detection performance in scenarios with inverted fill ratios, considering Δ 𝑓 ∈ { 0.1, 0.2, 0.4}.8 Siemensma et al. 

In this scenario, robots use fixed step sizes of 𝑑 = 𝜅ℓ at ℓ = 0.1 m. We study two settings: (i) 5 robots with all-to-all communication, representing a realistic scenario for our robot platform [30], and (ii) 8 robots with communication ranges of three body lengths, providing coverage over time similar to that in the original studies [20, 21]. For DBBCPD, setting (i) yields (𝑛 1, 𝑛 2, 𝑛 3) = (2, 3, 4) and setting (ii) yields (𝑛 1, 𝑛 2, 𝑛 3) = (3, 4, 4). For CUSUM, we set 𝜃 𝑝 = 0.6 in both settings. 

Scenario 2: Estimation of correlation We deploy a swarm of 5 robots with all-to-all communication and aim to estimate sampling correlation in environments with varying tile sizes ℓ ∈ { 0.05 , 0.1, 0.2} m, considering both cases where the step size satisfies 𝑑 ≤ ℓ and 𝑑 > ℓ . According to our algorithm in Section 3, estimation is only feasible when 𝑑 ≤ ℓ. Additionally, we evaluate how changes in spatial correlation, arising from clustering and tile size changes, affect the rate of false-positive fill ratio change detections under the assumption that the fill ratio remains constant. 

Scenario 3: Adaptation to correlation We use the same swarm size and com-munication settings as in Scenario 2, but now aim to enhance CUSUM’s accuracy by studying scenarios where the step size 𝑑 is either fixed or adaptive. We consider spa-tial correlations induced by both tile clustering and tile size variations. The threshold 

𝜃 𝑝 is set to 0.005, allowing a 0.5% false-alarm probability assuming ideal sampling. 

𝜃 𝑝 is an experimental operating point, not scenario knowledge the robot needs: Scenario 1 uses a permissive 𝜃 𝑝 = 0.6 for fast detection, while Scenarios 2–3 hold the fill ratio constant, so a strict 𝜃 𝑝 = 0.005 suppresses correlation-induced false positives. In deployment, 𝜃 𝑝 is fixed once from the tolerable false-alarm rate and is independent of connectivity, so the robot need not identify the scenario.

Its inferior detection delay in Scenario 1 appears to improve accuracy in this scenario through the detection delay–accuracy trade-off. In contrast, CUSUM’s accuracy degrades after the change and does not recover over time, although its performance is ex-pected to improve by lowering 𝜃 𝑝 , i.e., fewer false-positive detections. DBBCPD performs even worse as all-to-all communication causes excessive false-positive re-sets, thereby lowering accuracy even before the correlation change. Overall, changes in correlation highlight the speed-accuracy trade-off, where faster fill ratio detections cause lower accuracy in correlated environments, which is tunable for CUSUM. ## 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

We addressed collective perception in dynamic environments with changing fill ratios and spatial correlations. We introduced (i) an O( 1)-memory CUSUM fill ratio change detector, (ii) an online estimator of sampling correlation, and (iii) adaptive distance-based sampling to mitigate sampling correlation. CUSUM achieves competitive detection delays and more consistent precision across communication settings while remaining suitable for resource-constrained robots. Adaptive sampling substantially improves decision accuracy under strong spatial correlations. The correlation estimator cannot identify excessive de-correlation and may con-found environmental correlation with persistent-walk and boundary effects, while CUSUM can degrade as its reference absorbs post-change samples. Future work will investigate improved CUSUM references, simultaneous fill ratio and correlation changes, and real-world validation including wheel-odometry slip and drift.
