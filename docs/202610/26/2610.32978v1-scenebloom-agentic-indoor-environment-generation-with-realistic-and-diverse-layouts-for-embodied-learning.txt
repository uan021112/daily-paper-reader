# ABSTRACT 

Training generalizable embodied agents requires realistic, varied, and simulation-ready indoor environments. Language-guided methods support semantic planning and object-level reasoning, but precise spatial grounding remains difficult. Visual generative models offer rich priors over plausible arrangements, yet their outputs require geometric reasoning to become physically valid 3D scenes. We introduce SceneBloom, an agentic framework that combines visual layout sampling with contract-guided 3D compilation. A hierarchical sampler uses diffusion priors to generate furniture arrangements within functional zones. A compiler then uses re-lations extracted from each proposal to guide geometric fitting and local pose cor-rection. Across five room types, SceneBloom attains the highest visual quality and physical stability among the compared methods and is the only method with no de-tected mesh collisions and out-of-bounds objects. Its layouts are also closer to the distribution of real rooms than those of all compared baselines. Object navigation policies trained in these realistic scenes achieve a higher success rate in held-out real scans than those trained on an equal number of real training scans. # 1 INTRODUCTION 

Learning-based embodied agents benefit from training across varied environments. Prior work shows that procedurally generated environments support generalization across embodied tasks, and that language-guided scene generation can improve navigation in new scene types (Deitke et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024b). These findings motivate generators that provide diverse indoor layouts while retaining the spatial organization of real environments. Existing generators obtain layout structure from hand-crafted placement rules or learned distribu-tions of 3D configurations (Deitke et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024a), while language-guided and agentic systems combine flexible scene specification with spatial reasoning and iterative refinement (Yang et al., 2024b; 2026; Xia et al., 2026; Pfaff et al., 2026). Image diffusion models offer a complementary prior over how objects are arranged together. Recent methods exploit gen-erated images and visual guidance to construct realistic 3D scenes (Wang et al., 2024a; Gu et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2026). This suggests a division of responsibilities in which semantic planning establishes functional organization and visual sampling proposes detailed arrangements. The chal-lenge is to carry those arrangements into geometrically valid 3D scenes. Visual plausibility alone is insufficient for this realization. Depth, scale, and pose errors from 3D lifting produce overlapping or misaligned furniture when the reconstructed objects are assembled, so geometric correction is necessary. Moving or rotating objects to resolve these conflicts, however, can break the relationships that made the sampled arrangement plausible, for example by turning a chair away from its desk. Our guiding insight is to separate the qualitative relationships to preserve from the numerical poses that must be repaired .We introduce SceneBloom , an agentic framework that implements this separation through two stages, hierarchical visual sampling and contract-guided 3D compilation . In the first stage, a vision– language planner divides the room into functional zones, using retrieved exemplar rooms as refer-ences, and specifies each zone’s furniture inventory and approximate floor region. A diffusion model then samples a visual proposal of each zone’s furniture arrangement, so that zoning supplies room-level structure while each proposal models one furniture group jointly. The diffusion model uses asset-specific adapters, each trained on renders of one catalog asset, so that the proposals depict the objects used for 3D construction. In the second stage, the proposals are lifted into 3D. Before any object is moved, a vision–language model extracts relation contracts from the proposal images, recording relationships such as facing, alignment, and proximity among the lifted objects. A hierar-chical compiler then fits furniture within zones, assembles the zones into a room, and refines object poses to restore the contract relations. The contracts thus fix the relationships to preserve, while the compiler repairs only the numerical poses. Across five room types, SceneBloom scores highest on every visual-quality axis and on physical stability. It is also the only method with no detected mesh collisions and out-of-bounds objects. Its layouts more closely match real indoor layout distributions than all compared baselines on both Matterport3D (Chang et al., 2017) and Scan2CAD (Avetisyan et al., 2019). Ablations show that visual proposals outperform textual proposals on every metric, and that contract guidance further improves layout coherence and realism. For object navigation, a policy trained from scratch in our houses achieves higher success on held-out real scans than one trained on an equal number of real scans from the same dataset. Randomizing object placement within the same rooms removes most of this advantage, indicating that it arises from layout organization. Fine-tuning on our scenes also improves a pretrained policy, and controllable generation yields further gains by matching the difficulty of real homes. Our key contributions are: • We introduce an agentic framework for generating diverse, realistic, simulation-ready indoor environments for embodied learning. It combines diffusion-based layout sampling with contract-guided 3D compilation to preserve functional organization while resolving geometric conflicts. • We show that our scenes are both visually realistic and physically valid, and that their layouts align more closely with real rooms than those of all compared generators. • We demonstrate that object navigation policies trained on our scenes transfer to real scanned en-vironments better than those trained on real scans, and that controllable scene difficulty strength-ens this transfer. 2Preprint 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

3D Indoor Scene Synthesis Procedural methods assemble indoor scenes under hand-crafted spa-tial rules (Deitke et al., 2022; Raistrick et al., 2024), while autoregressive and diffusion models learn object configurations from annotated 3D scenes (Paschalidou et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2024; Lin & Mu, 2024). Physically guided variants further incorporate geometric constraints into layout sam-pling (Yang et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2026). The arrangements of all these approaches remain bounded by the rules they encode or the 3D scenes they learn from. To draw on broader visual knowledge, image-guided methods generate scene proposals through whole-scene synthesis or progressive in-painting, then recover and refine 3D object placements (Gu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024a; Ling et al., 2026). Although these methods bring rich visual priors into scene generation, image-level plausibility does not ensure compatibility with actual asset geometry, and correcting the resulting conflicts can disrupt the relationships that make the proposed layout functional. Our approach ad-dresses both problems, since its proposals depict the actual assets to be placed and its compiler repairs their geometry under relation contracts extracted from the proposals. 

Language-Guided and Agentic Scene Generation Language-guided methods prompt an LLM to write an object inventory and spatial constraints, which a solver satisfies (Yang et al., 2024b). Agentic systems turn generation into a feedback loop, in which a planner selects generation and editing tools step by step (Yang et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026) or specialized agents divide scene design, critique, and workflow control (Pfaff et al., 2026; Ran et al., 2026). Rendered views, geo-metric checks, and physics simulation guide each revision. Other work structures layout decisions through functional-zone graphs, semantic and physical decomposition, or compilable layout pro-grams (Zhang et al., 2026a; Luo et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026). Iterative refinement, however, re-peats tool calls, rendering, and review, which slows generation (He et al., 2026), and errors in object orientation or in coordination across stages can leave arrangements functionally inconsistent (Yang et al., 2026; Pfaff et al., 2026). Our framework uses agentic planning for functional zoning and diffusion sampling for furniture arrangement. Each zone’s arrangement is thus sampled in one pass rather than revised object by object, and relation contracts keep it functional during 3D compilation. 

Synthetic Environments for Embodied Learning Synthetic environments provide controllable variation in the scenes and interactions for policy learning. Procedural and language-guided genera-tors expand training coverage through alternative layouts, scene types, and object categories (Deitke et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2024b). Studies of synthetic navigation datasets further show that scene realism matters alongside dataset size, motivating attention to object occurrence and spatial arrangement patterns (Khanna et al., 2024). For manipulation, simulation frameworks pair varied environments and tasks with demonstrations (Wang et al., 2023; Nasiriany et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2026; Nasiriany et al., 2026). Real-to-sim approaches construct training scenes from ob-servations, including families of alternative assets and configurations that retain relevant geometric and semantic affordances (Dai et al., 2024; Ranawaka et al., 2026). Together, these directions mo-tivate training environments that combine variation with realistic spatial organization. SceneBloom provides both, and its generation settings can also adjust scene difficulty for policy learning. 

# 3 METHOD 

Given a room description, SceneBloom generates a simulation-ready 3D scene in two stages (Fig-ure 2). Hierarchical visual sampling (Section 3.1) divides the room into functional zones and sam-ples a visual proposal of each zone’s furniture arrangement. Contract-guided 3D compilation (Sec-tion 3.2) lifts these proposals into 3D, fits them into the room, and restores their functional relations before adding wall and surface objects. This division lets the visual prior supply plausible arrange-ments while compilation makes them physically valid. 3.1 HIERARCHICAL VISUAL SAMPLING

Functional zoning. Deciding which furniture a room needs and how the room divides into func-tional zones requires commonsense knowledge that a VLM has. The VLM therefore determines the room dimensions and divides the room into functional zones {zj }Jj=1 , using retrieved exemplar rooms as references (Appendix A.2). Each zone zj specifies a plan region, a furniture inventory Fj

with categories and counts, an anchor category for its central piece, the wall it adjoins, and a zone prompt qj . Placement within each zone is left to visual proposals and geometric compilation. 

Visual layout proposals. Image diffusion models learn from large image collections how furniture is typically arranged. We use this prior to propose the arrangement of each zone. Because the model generates an entire furniture group jointly, a proposal determines all relative placements at once rather than through sequential per-object decisions. For zone zj , a text-to-image diffusion model G

is conditioned on the zone prompt qj and on asset LoRA adapters Λj selected according to Fj :

Ij = G(qj , Λj ; ϵj ), (1) where Ij is the proposal image and ϵj is the diffusion seed. Each adapter is trained on renders of one catalog asset (Ruiz et al., 2023), so that the proposal depicts the assets used for 3D construction. Adapter fusion (Liu et al., 2025) allows several of these assets to appear in one image without interfering. Varying the seed ϵj then yields diverse arrangements of the same assets, each grounded in the objects to be built. Lifting a proposal into 3D introduces two kinds of error: geometric conflicts between objects and disrupted functional relations. Hierarchical layout fitting resolves the former, and relation contracts guide the recovery of the latter.

Hierarchical layout fitting. We resolve geometry within zones and then assemble the zones ac-cording to the zone plan, reconciling the planned zone regions with the footprint that the proposed furniture actually needs. Within each zone , the anchor of zj is aligned with the zone’s wall and faces into the room. The remaining objects of Lj are then separated from the fixed anchor and from one another to remove footprint overlaps, with Cj guiding how related objects are separated. The fitted objects define the zone envelope Ej , which is then frozen so that room-level assembly moves the zone only as a rigid whole. Among zones , the zone plan fixes for each pair of zones (zi, z j ) a separat-ing axis and their order along it. Along that axis, an envelope bound to a wall stays flush against it, any other envelope keeps its planned position where possible, and otherwise assembly shifts the later envelope along the axis until it starts at least a clearance gap after the earlier one ends. The room is enlarged only when the envelopes cannot fit within it. Fitting thus resolves geometric conflicts while preserving the arrangement of each proposal within its zone. 

Relational refinement. After assembly, geometric predicates check each high-confidence relation of Cj . Relation-specific operators propose local rotations or translations to restore violated relations, subject to room-boundary and footprint feasibility checks. Because these adjustments are local, they restore relations without disturbing the arrangement sampled in the proposal. Relations such as chairs facing their table decide whether a zone is functional and coherent, so restoring them directly supports visual quality. Figure 3 contrasts geometry-only compilation, which omits the contract, with contract-guided compilation. 

Scene completion. Finally, the VLM adds wall-mounted objects and small objects on furniture surfaces. For each wall or furniture top, it chooses object categories and suggests positions in that 5Preprint surface’s coordinates, and geometric placement maps these positions into the room while checking available space, boundaries, and intersections (Appendix A.4). 

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 SCENE GENERATION 

Implementation Details. We use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) for functional zoning and scene compilation, and FLUX.1-dev (Labs, 2024) with FreeFuse (Liu et al., 2025) and asset-specific LoRA adapters for visual layout proposals. OVMono3D-LIFT (Yao et al., 2026) lifts 2D boxes from Grounding DINO (Liu et al., 2023) into 3D, with depth and camera intrinsics from Depth Pro (Bochkovskiy et al., 2025). Reference exemplars are drawn from M3DLayout (Zhang et al., 2026b). Appendix A details generation configurations, asset sources, and reference-data separation. 

Baselines. We compare against three representative methods using their official codebases. Holodeck (Yang et al., 2024b) uses an LLM to specify spatial constraints for a layout solver. Sce-neWeaver (Yang et al., 2026) uses a self-reflective agent to coordinate generation and editing tools. SAGE (Xia et al., 2026) uses visual and physics critics to refine scenes and validate placements in simulation. We retain method-specific asset sources, use our Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct backbone for Holodeck and SceneWeaver for a controlled comparison, and keep the model pair specified in SAGE’s paper. Appendix A details these adaptations. 

Metrics. We evaluate physical plausibility, visual quality, and real-layout alignment. Following SceneEval (Tam et al., 2026), we report mesh-level collision rates at the object (Coll. obj) and scene (Coll. scene) levels and mean out-of-bounds objects per scene (OOB). We also report the stable-object rate (Stab.) under REST3D (Ma et al., 2026). Following SceneWeaver and SAGE (Yang et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026), GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) scores realism (Real.), functionality (Func.), layout coherence (Coh.), and completeness (Compl.). For real-layout alignment, Layout Distribution Discrepancy (LDD) applies unbiased RBF maximum mean discrepancy (MMD 2) (Gretton et al., 2012) to features encoding object composition and spatial relations, against two references of real rooms: Matterport3D (Chang et al., 2017) and Scan2CAD (Avetisyan et al., 2019). Appendices B and C provide metric definitions, scoring protocols, hardware, and resource accounting. 4.1.1 MAIN RESULTS 

We generate and evaluate 10 scenes for each of five room types: bedrooms, living rooms, dining rooms, bathrooms, and offices, using the template a <room type >.

Physical Plausibility. Our scenes are the only ones free of mesh collisions and out-of-bounds objects, and they are the most stable under REST3D (Table 1). The baselines add objects one at a time without resolving the scene as a whole, so conflicts accumulate as a scene grows: SAGE’s agent uses 407 of its 504 tool calls across its 50 scenes for placement but moves a placed object in only 44, and Holodeck spawns repeated copies of an asset at a single point. The instability also reaches the furniture itself: 31% of Holodeck’s and 26% of SAGE’s and SceneWeaver’s large objects, those at least 0.6 m across, move beyond the stability threshold, against 3% of ours. Our compiler instead resolves all furniture of a room jointly before completion and verifies each surface object against its support, so no conflict is left to accumulate. Additional stability evaluations appear in Appendix C. 

Visual Quality. Our scenes score highest on all four visual-quality axes. SAGE is a strong baseline and places the most objects, but its placement loop keeps adding them until floors and surfaces are crowded (Figure 4), while our scenes score higher on functionality and completeness with fewer and reasonable objects. Holodeck and SceneWeaver place furniture through textual constraints and edits, so functional relations are often lost, for example, dining chairs that stand apart from the table and face away from it. Our proposals depict each functional group as a whole, and the contract preserves its relations through compilation. Training Sources and Evaluation. We import scenes into Habitat (Savva et al., 2019) and construct episodes from visible goal viewpoints and reachable starts. We evaluate in held-out real scanned environments and report success rate (SR) and success weighted by path length (SPL) (Anderson et al., 2018). Setup. SemExp (Chaplot et al., 2020) builds a top-down semantic map and uses a learned goal policy to choose where to search. We train this goal policy from scratch with PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) for 2M steps in the framework of Wang et al. (2026). Each source provides 40, 80, or 120 houses, and each of our houses connects three to five generated rooms along a corridor. We evaluate on 1,000 episodes in 36 real HM3D vali-dation homes, where success follows the SemExp setup and requires calling STOP within 1 m of a goal object (Appendix E.3). At evaluation, the policy reads semantics predicted from RGB by a COCO-trained Mask R-CNN (He et al., 2017). 

Real-Environment Transfer. Policies trained on our houses transfer best to real scanned en-vironments, outperforming those trained on real scans, Randomized houses, and Agent-planned 8Preprint houses at every house count (Figure 6). Our houses raise SR from 29.7% for the untrained pol-icy to 35.3% on average, 1.7× the gain from training on real scans, even though the real scans come from the same dataset as the evaluation homes. Our houses also give the policy more complete searches to learn from. Their training episodes end at the policy’s own STOP in 91.9% of cases, against 75.0% in real scans, and the gap holds at every start distance (Appendix E.3). Our generator therefore supplies more effective training experience than real scans and scales more easily. Randomized houses place the same furniture in the same rooms at random, which often splits a room’s walkable floor and confines training searches to part of the house, whereas our arrangements rarely split it (Appendix E.2). Agent-planned houses share our functional zones and assets, but a text agent places objects less realistically than our visual proposals (Section 4.1.2), so the policy learns to search among arrangements that are less common in real homes. 4.2.2 FINE -T UNING A PRETRAINED POLICY 

Setup. We fine-tune OVSegDT (Zemskova et al., 2025), an open-vocabulary policy conditioned on RGB observations, language goals, target masks, and history. Every policy is fine-tuned from the released OVSegDT checkpoint (the pretrained policy) with PPO for 128,000 environment steps under an identical recipe. Each generated source provides 120 scenes, 24 for each of the five room types in Section 4.1, and real-scan continuation applies the same recipe to 142 HM3D training scans. We evaluate on held-out scenes from our generator and an 864-episode subset of HM3D-OVON Val Unseen (Yokoyama et al., 2024) covering all 36 of its scenes, where success requires calling STOP action within 1.0 m of the goal.

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We presented SceneBloom, an agentic framework that separates the relationships to preserve in a layout from the poses to repair. Visual proposals supply realistic arrangements, and contract-guided compilation repairs their geometry while keeping the proposed relations, so our scenes are physically valid, visually coherent, and closest to real room layouts. Sampling each zone once and resolving each room jointly also makes generation far faster than iterative agentic methods. These scenes in turn improve real-environment transfer for navigation policies trained from scratch and unseen-goal generalization after fine-tuning. Further gains from matching scene difficulty to real homes demonstrate the value of combining realistic layouts with adjustable task difficulty for embodied learning.

A.1 BASELINE CONFIGURATIONS 

We evaluate Holodeck (Yang et al., 2024b), SceneWeaver (Yang et al., 2026), and SAGE (Xia et al., 2026) from their released codebases, retaining their native asset sources and scene-generation pro-cedures. Asset library. Our asset library holds 192 assets: 75 floor-furniture meshes across 22 categories, 109 surface objects, and 8 wall objects. We generate most of them using the TRELLIS (Xiang et al., 2025) text-to-3D model. The 27 articulated floor meshes, which cover eight storage and sanitary categories, come from the ArtVIP dataset (Jin et al., 2026) instead. Our library is also extensible: once a category-compatible mesh has a trained LoRA adapter, the visual sampler can draw it, and the VLM, the compiler, and the prompts stay unchanged. Each baseline keeps its own asset source. Holodeck retrieves from Objaverse (Deitke et al., 2023), SAGE generates its assets with TRELLIS text-to-3D, and SceneWeaver generates most objects procedurally with Infinigen (Raistrick et al., 2024) and retrieves the rest from 3D-FUTURE (Fu et al., 2021b) and Objaverse. Each of these systems treats its asset pipeline as part of its contribution, so substituting our meshes would evaluate a different system. A.2 ZONE -W ISE VISUAL PROPOSALS 

For each room, retrieval restricts M3DLayout (Zhang et al., 2026b) exemplars to the requested room type and ranks them by cosine similarity between Qwen3-Embedding (Zhang et al., 2025) encodings of the room description and of each exemplar’s structured text. The top-ranked exemplar is kept as an anchor, and two more are drawn from the remaining candidates by similarity-weighted sampling without replacement. This varies the structural references while keeping the nearest neighbor. The retrieval corpus is built from two disjoint indices. The first holds 5,173 3D-FRONT (Fu et al., 2021a) rooms and covers bedrooms, living rooms, and dining rooms. The second holds 627 Mat-terport3D regions and covers bathrooms, offices, kitchens, lounges, recreation rooms, and meeting rooms; it contains no bedroom, living-room, or dining-room region. The real-layout references of Section B.1 are Matterport3D bedrooms, living rooms, and dining rooms, which neither index con-tains, and Scan2CAD rooms, which come from ScanNet and are absent from both. No room that enters either reference distribution can therefore be retrieved as an exemplar, and the conditioning set and the evaluation references are disjoint by construction. Every floor-furniture mesh the sampler can draw has its own LoRA adapter, trained on multi-view renders of that mesh with DreamBooth and prior preservation (Ruiz et al., 2023) and orthogonal adaptation (Po et al., 2024); wall and surface objects are placed geometrically after compilation and need no adapter. The pipeline inserts each adapter’s trigger token before the category word it stands for, so the word sofa in a zone prompt yields the particular sofa selected from our library. A zone uses at most five adapters, one per furniture category, and FreeFuse (Liu et al., 2025) runs them together in a single generation, binding each adapter to the tokens that name its object and to the image region those tokens attend to. Every furniture object compiled from the image is therefore the library mesh whose adapter drew it. This grounding motivates the adapters: it reduces the appearance mismatch between a proposal and the objects used for construction, so the compiler rebuilds the sampled arrangement from the objects the image shows rather than from meshes that merely share their categories. We follow SceneEval (Tam et al., 2026) for method-independent geometric evaluation. Object colli-sion is the percentage of objects participating in at least one mesh intersection, computed per scene and averaged over scenes, and scene collision is the percentage of scenes containing any such object; contact tolerance is zero. OOB is the mean number of objects per scene whose footprint falls outside the room under SceneEval’s ray-cast test with a 1% tolerance. Dynamic stability follows REST3D (Ma et al., 2026). We replay every nonempty exported scene in Isaac Gym (Makoviychuk et al., 2021) at 1/60 seconds, using six position iterations, one velocity iteration, and a maximum depenetration velocity of 5 m/s. Scenes settle for 120 steps. An object is stable when its final translation is at most 0.1 m and its rotation is at most 0.1 rad. Stab. pools all movable objects; position drift and peak linear and angular velocities provide severity measures. Every method uses the same simulator, timestep, thresholds, coordinate conversion, and evaluation code. B.3 VISUAL QUALITY 

We use GPT-4o-2024-08-06 to score realism, functionality, layout coherence, and completeness on a 0–10 scale, following the four axes used by SceneWeaver (Yang et al., 2026) and SAGE (Xia et al., 2026). The judge receives a top-down render and a text list of object positions, orientations, and sizes. We use temperature zero and average five judgments per scene, and all four methods are scored in one run. The evaluation interface and score bands are given in Appendix G.
