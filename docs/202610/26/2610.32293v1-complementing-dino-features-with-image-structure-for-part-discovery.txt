## Abstract 

Self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs) like DINO encode rich semantics, enabling training-free segmentation of objects from the background and discovery of their parts via feature clustering. This paper focuses on part discovery: par-titioning a foreground object into semantic parts. Existing approaches produce imprecise part boundaries. We trace this to the rigid ViT patch grid used in training DINO, where patches straddling boundaries contain pixels from multiple parts. While DINO’s invariance-based training could absorb this pixel-level mixing into clean features, experiments with over 26K foreground patches show it does not: boundary-overlapping patches produce features with higher entropy and lower assignment confidence, blurring predicted part boundaries. Rather than make the massive effort of retraining DINO over geometrically coherent regions, an easier approach is to take boundaries directly from a boundary detector, which supplies the geometric information DINO’s features lack. Motivated by this, we introduce Part-DINO, a training-free framework that combines boundaries from low-level image segmentation with DINO’s semantic features. We use the boundaries to partition the image into regions, pool DINO features within each region, and group them to discover parts. Without any training, Part-DINO produces structurally coherent part assignments, outperforming training-free baselines across four stan-dard benchmarks (CUB, CelebA, PartImageNet-OOD, PartImageNet-Seg) and remaining competitive with weakly-supervised and unsupervised methods. 

## 1 Introduction 

Self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs) encode rich object-level structure. Models like DINO (Caron et al., 2021), trained via self-distillation without any human labels, produce feature spaces that group semantic concepts. For example, on a bird, the eye patches cluster with eyes and wing patches with wings. This emergent property has enabled training-free object localization, segmentation, and coarse semantic part discovery. However, despite their strong semantic awareness, existing patch-based methods remain spatially imprecise, part assignments bleed across part contours, producing blurry boundaries between neighboring parts. Why does a model with such semantic awareness fail to localize where one part ends and another begins? We trace this to the rigid patch grid over which features are computed. ViTs divide images into a fixed-size square patches that are agnostic to object geometry. When a patch straddles a part

To investigate this, we analyze how patch features behave as a function of a patch’s distance to the nearest ground-truth part boundary. Analyzing 26K foreground patches, we find that as patch–boundary overlap increases, feature entropy rises and assignment confidence falls (Fig 1c), confirming pixel-level mixing propagates into feature space. This finding has a direct consequence: DINO’s semantic information is useful for grouping parts, but unreliable for identifying their boundaries. As shown in Fig 1d, the edges derived from DINO feature similarity are blurred at part contours, whereas edges derived from image contrast are aligned with physical boundaries. Rather than retrain DINO over geometrically coherent regions at massive cost, an easier approach is to fuse it with an independent boundary detector. Motivated by this, we propose Part-DINO, a training-free framework that combines the accurate boundaries from low-level image segmentation with the semantic information of DINO features. The boundaries partition the image into regions whose contours align with object geometry. We then pool DINO features within each region and use them for semantic grouping, assigning each region to its nearest part prototype. We evaluate Part-DINO across four standard part-discovery benchmarks (CUB (Wah et al., 2011), CelebA (Liu et al., 2015), and the OOD and Seg splits of PartImageNet(He et al., 2022a)), using standard metrics (NME, NMI, and ARI). Part-DINO is training-free: it relies only on the frozen DINO features and avoids the optimization required by unsupervised methods (which train without labels) and weakly-supervised methods (which use image-level labels). Despite this constraint, Part-DINO outperforms the patch-based training-free baseline on NME and ARI across all datasets, and on NMI for three of four. The slight NMI drop on PartImageNet-OOD reflects a known metric trade-off, where ARI rewards spatial coherence over fine fragmentation (Sec.4). Furthermore, Part-DINO remains competitive with trained models, demonstrating that integrating image geometry with frozen semantic features recovers part structure without learned objectives. Our contributions in this paper are: 1. We show that DINO’s spatial imprecision stems from the ViT’s patch grid. By analyzing features of patches that span boundaries, we find that pixel-level mixing propagates into the feature space, thereby blurring predicted contours. 2. We introduce Part-DINO, a training-free framework that integrates the boundary of low-level image segments to extract sharp regions and DINO features to semantically group them, achieving precise part discovery without dataset-specific training. 3. Part Discovery. This task aims to identify semantically consistent region (parts) across varying instances of an object category. Earlier approaches rely on weak supervision, discovering parts by using image-level category labels to optimize a classification objective that highlights discriminative regions (Huang and Li, 2020; Van Der Klis et al., 2023; Aniraj et al., 2024). Another line of work explores unsupervised methods that do not rely on any part annotations. Some approaches train a masked autoencoder that masks image patches and learns to reconstruct them (Xia et al., 2025), while others use an equivariance-based objective (Xia et al., 2024). However, both weakly and unsupervised methods need a computationally expensive and dataset-specific training phase. As an alternative, training-free approaches (Amir et al., 2021) bypass optimization entirely by clustering patch features from a frozen DINO backbone. Part-DINO follows this training-free paradigm, but use geometric regions as units instead of patches. 

Self-Supervised Vision Models. DINO (Caron et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2023) trains a Vision Trans-former via self-distillation, producing patch-level embeddings that are consistent across augmented views and naturally group semantic concepts. These features encode strong spatial layouts and have been leveraged for foreground localization (Siméoni et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Rawlekar et al., 2026) and coarse part discovery (Amir et al., 2021). Prior work has also investigated semantic entanglement in pretrained vision-language representations, demonstrating its impact on multi-object localization (Rawlekar et al., 2025). However, the unit used by these methods (the fixed-size square patch) is agnostic to object boundaries. Because this rigid grid ignores true object contours, boundary patches include multiple parts, producing semantically mixed features that limit spatial precision. Part-DINO avoids this by replacing the patch with geometric regions as the atomic unit, ensuring each feature vector corresponds to a single coherent part. 

Region Generation. To avoid feature mixing, recent works group pixels into atomic units (Shlapentokh-Rothman et al., 2024), often relying on superpixel algorithms like SLIC (Achanta et al., 2012) to define these regions. However, while SLIC clusters pixels in a joint color-spatial space and partially respects image gradients, it imposes an explicit compactness constraint that trades boundary adherence for spatial uniformity. When part contours are weak relative to this compactness penalty, SLIC superpixels bleed across the irregular edges of natural object parts. Instead, we use the Ramp Transform and Watershed (Akbas and Ahuja, 2020, 2014). By defining the units via contrast gradients (ramp edge discontinuities), we generate regions that adhere to low-level structural boundaries. 

## 3 Part-DINO 

The Boundary Patches: We now detail the analysis behind the finding that pixel-space mixing propagates into feature space Sec.1. For each foreground patch p, we measure four quantities: (1) Euclidean distance d(p) of its center to the nearest ground-truth part boundary, (2) its spatial purity 

π(p) = |majority-part pixels |/|pfg |, (3) its feature entropy H(p), computed as the entropy of the patch’s softmax similarity to per-part mean features extracted from clean interior patches, and (4) its top-2 assignment margin m(p), the similarity gap between p’s closest and second-closest part vector. Across 26 , 459 foreground patches, smaller d corresponds to larger patch–boundary overlap. As overlap increases, it causes spatial purity ( π(p)) to drop (Spearman correlation ρ(d, π ) = +0 .71 , Supp. Fig.7a), confirming pixel-space mixing. The key finding is that this pixel space mixing propagates into the feature space: patches closer to a boundary show higher feature entropy ( ρ(d, H ) = −0.38 )and a lower top-2 assignment margin ( ρ(d, m ) = +0 .36 ). Furthermore, this boundary mixing is widespread: 21 .9% of all foreground patches have a purity of π < 1. As shown in Figure 1c, H

and m vary smoothly with distance d, therefore the feature quality degrades continuously as overlap increases. This analysis has two direct implications for part discovery. First, the spatial unit must respect object geometry, since a patch that spans multiple parts cannot preserve the contour separating them. Second, the geometric signal must be independent of DINO: as Fig.1d shows, edges derived from DINO feature similarity blur at part contours, whereas edges from image contrast remain sharp and aligned with physical boundaries. 3.1 Region Generation 

Given an input image I ∈ RH×W ×3, our goal is to parse the object into a set of geometrically coherent regions R that respect part boundaries. Since we are interested in parts of the object rather than the background, we first isolate the foreground by extracting a binary mask Mf g ∈ { 0, 1}H×W

using foreground extraction methods. Within the foreground M , we compute a structural edge map 

Eedge from the image contrast. We apply the RAMP transform Akbas and Ahuja (2020), which estimates a per-pixel contrast magnitude based on local intensity transitions across four orientations (0◦, 45 ◦, 90 ◦, 135 ◦). By construction, Eedge depends only on pixel intensities, making it independent of DINO’s feature pipeline that runs in parallel to extract semantic descriptors. To generate the atomic regions from this edge map, we apply the Watershed transform Vincent and Soille (1991) to Eedge , which treats the edge map as a topographic surface and floods upward from local minima, placing region boundaries along the ridges where adjacent basins meet. Seeds are placed at the local minima of Eedge with a minimum inter-seed distance of dmin pixels. ## 4 Experiments 

Dataset & Implementation Details. We evaluate Part-DINO on four widely-used benchmarks: CUB (Wah et al., 2011), CelebA (Liu et al., 2015), PartImageNet Out-of-Distribution (OOD) and PartImageNet Segmentation (He et al., 2022a). Following prior works, we use DINOv2 ViT-B/14 with registers (Darcet et al., 2023) as our frozen feature encoder, and set the input resolution to 448 × 448 

for CUB and 224 × 224 for the remaining datasets. Foreground masks M are obtained using SAM3 (Carion et al., 2025). The RAMP transform is computed along four orientations ( 0◦, 45 ◦, 90 ◦, 135 ◦)with a maximum ramp width of 5 pixels. Watershed seeds are placed at local minima of the structural edge map with a minimum inter-seed distance of dmin = 2 pixels, and micro-regions smaller than 

smin = 10 pixels are absorbed into their most similar DINO neighbor. 

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate Part-DINO with three standard metrics. Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) measures the mutual information between predicted cluster assignments and ground-truth labels. A high NMI indicates that predicted clusters are strongly aligned with ground-truth parts. Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) measures whether pixel pairs from the same ground-truth part are assigned the same cluster, and pairs from different parts are assigned different clusters. ARI is adjusted for chance, so a random assignment scores near zero. Normalized Mean Error (NME) is calculated as the distance between centroid of each predicted region and measure its Euclidean distance to the nearest ground-truth keypoint (landmark such as eye or beak), and report the mean of these distances normalized by image size. Lower NME implies better centroid alignment. 

Results. Tables 1 and 2 show our quantitative results on the fine-grained CUB and CelebA datasets across part granularities ( K ∈ { 4, 8, 16 }). On CUB, Part-DINO outperforms the patch-based training-free baseline across all K, improving NMI (56.92 →65.45) and ARI (35.10 →46.00) at K=16. Despite requiring no training, Part-DINO matches or exceeds several weakly-supervised and unsupervised methods (Huang and Li, 2020; Van Der Klis et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2024) at K = 16 . On CelebA, where the patch-based baseline collapses (ARI < 0.1 across all K), Part-DINO outperforms the unsupervised GANSeg at k = 4 (ARI: 32.20 vs. 28.06), though trained methods remain stronger. Table 5 shows results on the PartImageNet OOD (PartImageNet O ) and Segmentation (PartImageNet S )splits. On PartImageNet O, Part-DINO achieves consistently higher ARI but slightly lower NMI compared to patch-based baselines. This reflects a known trade-off: NMI favors over-segmentation into many small, pure clusters, while ARI penalizes such fragmentation. ## 5 Analysis 

Unit (Patches vs Regions): To isolate the impact of the atomic unit, we test all combinations of patches and regions for prototype construction and label assignment. As shown in Table 3, improving only the prototype quality while keeping patches as the assignment unit (row 2) yields marginal gains over the baseline (+6.50 NMI, +2.14 ARI). This is expected: while the target prototypes are cleaner, the patch-based assignment still blurs features across part boundaries. Improving only the assignment unit while keeping patch-based prototypes (row 3) yields a larger gain (+9.97 NMI, +4.17 ARI), as geometric region produces clean query features even when the targets are noisy. Combining both changes (row 4), we match region features with region prototypes, reducing mixing on both sides of the assignment and improving NMI (32.83 → 53.93) and ARI (13.96 → 23.01). 

Region Generation: We compare RAMP-based Watershed Akbas and Ahuja (2020, 2014) against SLIC Achanta et al. (2012), keeping all other components fixed. As shown in Table 4, both methods improve over the patch baseline. At the same granularity of 500 regions, RAMP + Watershed improves ARI by +7.36 over SLIC, and even at a coarser granularity (142 regions) outperforms fine-grained SLIC (500 regions). The difference is visible in Figure 5b: SLIC impose an explicit compactness constraint that trades boundary adherence for spatial uniformity. RAMP + Watershed places boundaries along intensity gradients, resulting in regions that respect object geometry.

## 6 Discussion 

We have presented Part-DINO, a training-free framework motivated by an empirical finding: pixel-space mixing at boundary patches propagates into feature space. Across 26k foreground patches, feature entropy rises and the top-2 assignment margin falls as patch–boundary overlap increases, making the ViT patch the bottleneck for spatially precise part discovery. Part-DINO addresses this by replacing the patch with regions whose boundaries come from low-level image segmentation alone, independent of DINO. We pool DINO features within each region and use them only to group regions into parts, not to place boundaries. Part-DINO outperforms the patch-based training-free 9baseline on NME and ARI across all four benchmarks and granularities, and matches or exceeds several training-based methods. We present two limitations . (1) Fixed part granularity. Part-DINO requires the number of parts (K) to be specified in advance. While this is a common assumption across all part discovery methods, it limits applicability to settings where the correct granularity is unknown or varies across object categories. (2) Dependence on contrast. Because our region boundaries are derived from the RAMP transform, which relies on local intensity contrast, the edge response weakens in low-contrast scenes, uniform lighting or minimal texture variation between parts, leading to under-segmentation. Conversely, strong illumination gradients or cast shadows can introduce spurious edges that do not correspond to part boundaries. 

Future work. Part-DINO discovers parts as unlabeled clusters. A natural extension is to assign seman-tic labels to these discovered regions by leveraging vision-language models such as CLIP, bridging part discovery and open-vocabulary part segmentation. Broader Impact. Positively, our training-free approach reduces the computational energy footprint typically required for dataset-specific part discovery. Negatively, enhanced unsupervised localization capabilities could be intentionally misused for unauthorized biometric surveillance.

## B Boundary Analysis Details 

In the main text, we hypothesize that the fixed-size square grid ( 14 × 14 pixels) used by Vision Transformers acts as a fundamental bottleneck for spatial precision. Figure 7 visualizes the empirical validation of this hypothesis through an analysis of N = 26 , 459 foreground patches from the PartImageNet dataset, examining how semantic representation degrades as a function of distance to physical object contours. To rigorously evaluate this geometric relationship, we first constructed ground-truth part centroids using high-purity patches ( π > 0.95 ) from the training set, anchoring them to isolated, unambiguous semantic parts. For each patch p, we calculated its Euclidean distance d to the nearest ground-truth part boundary. We then mapped this distance against three key patch-level metrics: 1. Spatial Purity ( π): The fraction of the patch’s foreground pixels belonging to its majority part label. 2. Feature Entropy ( H): The Shannon entropy of the patch’s feature distances relative to the part-anchored centroids, measuring representational ambiguity. 3. Top-2 Margin ( m): The difference in similarity between the nearest and second-nearest part centroids, serving as a measure of assignment confidence. As illustrated by the distance curves in Figure 7, a patch’s proximity to a boundary heavily dictates its semantic reliability. We observe a strong positive correlation ( ρ(d, π ) = +0 .71 ) between boundary distance and spatial purity; patches directly on or adjacent to a contour unavoidably pool pixels from distinct parts. Crucially, this geometric mixing corrupts the underlying feature space. As patches move closer to the boundary (decreasing d), feature entropy rises ( ρ(d, H ) = −0.38 ) and the assignment margin steadily narrows ( ρ(d, m ) = +0 .36 ). While the dataset-wide mean patch purity is high ( 0.952 ), the sharp degradation of these metrics at d ≈ 0 quantitatively demonstrates that square ViT patches become inherently ambiguous exactly where precise boundary localization is required. 

## C Extended Qualitative Analysis: Semantic vs. Structural Boundaries 

In the main text, we established that extracting boundaries via semantic feature similarity leads to spatial imprecision, whereas relying on structural image contrast preserves object contours. Figures 19 6, 8, and 9 provide an extended qualitative comparison of these edge map modalities across various datasets. As demonstrated in the figures, edges derived from DINO similarity inherently suffer from feature degradation near part transitions, causing the predicted regions to bleed across actual object bound-aries. By contrast, our structural boundary formulation leverages the RAMP transform to isolate edge discontinuities independent of the DINO feature space. Across CelebA (Figure 6), and other complex object categories (Figures 8 and 9), the structural boundaries consistently reduce semantic bleeding artifacts and produce sharp parts. 

## D Extended Qualitative Analysis on Region Generation: SLIC vs. Watershed 

To construct atomic regions that do not arbitrarily cross object parts, the choice of the grouping algorithm is critical.Figures 10 and 11 provide an extended ablation comparing our chosen Watershed transform against SLIC superpixels across a variety of complex categories. While SLIC clusters pixels based on a joint color-spatial space, it imposes a strict spatial compactness penalty. As seen in the SLIC rows of Figures 10 and 11, when structural contours are weak or highly irregular, this compactness constraint dominates, forcing arbitrary, grid-like boundaries that cut directly across anatomical shapes. Conversely, our Watershed approach operates on the topographic valleys of the structural gradient. As shown in the Watershed rows, this allows the generated atomic regions to break free from spatial rigidity and seamlessly wrap around the true, natural contours of the physical objects. 

## E DINO-Free Region Generation 

DINO enters region generation only through the micro-region merge (Sec. 3.1), which absorbs each watershed region smaller than smin pixels into its most similar neighbor. Merging only removes boundaries and never places one. DINO therefore decides which watershed regions are grouped, the same role it plays in region assignment (Sec. 3.3), while boundary placement depends on Eedge 

alone. To confirm that the gains do not depend on this step, we disable it ( smin = 0 ), making region generation fully independent of DINO. Table 7 reports the result on CUB at K = 16 . Disabling the merge adds 18.5 regions per image and changes NMI and NME by only 0.03, while ARI drops by 1.91. Even without the merge, Part-DINO outperforms the patch-based baseline by +8.09 NMI and +8.82 ARI, with 0.51 lower NME.
