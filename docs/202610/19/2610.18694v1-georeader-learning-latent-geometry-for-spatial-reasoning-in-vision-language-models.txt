Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) struggle with metric and multi-view relationships that remain im-
plicit in RGB observations. Geometry foundation models recover this structure from RGB, but
existing methods either fuse the estimated features with visual tokens and learn to use them from
question answering alone, or align VLM latents with geometry features. Capturing geometry, how-
ever, does not ensure its use: zeroing the aligned latents of a released feature-alignment model
leaves its accuracy unchanged. We introduce GeoReader, a framework that learns geometric repre-
sentations for the VLM that reads them. First, we train lightweight Geometric Readers to produce
continuous input tokens, supervising them with geometry descriptions decoded by a frozen VLM,
so that the Readers express geometry in a form the target model can already read. We then freeze the
Readers to preserve this interface while fine-tuning the VLM on spatial QA, with geometry injection
conditioned on the question. Geometry descriptions serve only as training targets: at inference, the
VLM answers directly from the continuous geometric tokens without generating a geometry chain of
thought. With geometry predicted from RGB images, GeoReader reaches 94.3 on MindCube-Tiny
with Qwen3-VL-4B and, with a multi-source QA mixture, an average of 72.5 across six additional
spatial benchmarks with Qwen3.5-9B. These results suggest that a useful geometric representation
is one the receiving model can use, not merely one from which geometry can be reconstructed.

Introduction
Vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated increasingly strong capabilities in general mul-
timodal understanding, yet continue to struggle with spatial reasoning, a capability essential to em-
bodied intelligence in the 3D world [25, 32]. A central challenge is inferring and reasoning about
3D structure from 2D observations [33], as required by spatial questions involving camera motion,
viewpoint changes, and object distances. Recent advances in geometry foundation models [37, 57]
offer a promising opportunity to address this limitation by providing geometry information. How to
harness this information for VLM spatial reasoning has therefore become an important question.
Existing approaches to integrating geometry foundation models with VLMs have improved spatial
reasoning and can be broadly categorized into two streams. The first introduces geometry as an addi-
tional input modality [18, 63, 74], fusing features from geometry foundation models with the VLM’s
original visual features (Fig. 1a). This provides a direct way to incorporate geometry into existing
VLMs, and the VLM learns to use these features from downstream supervision. However, this su-
pervision alone may not teach the VLM to interpret geometry features. The second uses geometry as
supervision, typically by aligning VLM latent representations with features from geometry founda-
tion models [12] (Fig. 1b). This encourages the latent space to capture geometry information while

its use: zeroing the latents of the released first-stage 3DThinker [12] checkpoint leaves MindCube-
Tiny [59] accuracy essentially unchanged, and answer tokens barely attend to them (Tab. 8; Fig. 3).
Related analyses of latent visual reasoning methods such as LVR [29] and Monet [60] also highlight
a disconnect between intermediate representations and answer generation [21, 34, 46, 56, 79]. De-
spite their differences, neither approach explicitly bridges the gap between geometry representations
and language-grounded spatial relations for VLMs. Consequently, the role of geometry information
in the model’s reasoning remains uncertain. We bridge this gap by converting geometry into a form
that VLMs can both interpret and ground in language.
We introduce GeoReader, based on the core idea that geometry should first be converted into a
form that VLMs can interpret as spatial relationships in language, before VLMs learn to use it for
downstream tasks. To this end, a two-stage pipeline separates adapting geometry to the VLM from
applying it to spatial QA (Fig. 1c). In Stage 1, we train lightweight Geometric Readers to convert
per-view geometric signals into continuous geometric tokens. These tokens and the corresponding
images are given to a frozen VLM that predicts geometry descriptions. With the VLM frozen, this
supervision trains the Readers to express geometry information in a form that the model can interpret
through its existing language capabilities. In Stage 2, we transfer the pretrained Readers to spatial
QA, freezing the Readers to preserve their learned mapping while fine-tuning the VLM to use their
outputs. Since questions differ in how much they rely on each geometric signal, a geometry injector
conditions on the images and question to set each signal’s strength. Geometry descriptions are used
only for Stage 1 supervision; at inference, the VLM answers directly using the geometric tokens.
We evaluate GeoReader across seven spatial reasoning benchmarks and three VLM families. With
geometry predicted from RGB images, GeoReader achieves 94.3% accuracy on MindCube-Tiny
with Qwen3-VL-4B and, with multi-source QA training, an average accuracy of 72.5% across six
additional benchmarks with Qwen3.5-9B (Sec. 4.2). Comparisons with vanilla supervised fine-
tuning (SFT) under matched QA data and training settings show gains concentrated on questions
requiring metric 3D understanding. Ablations further show the benefits of readability pretraining,
freezing the Readers during downstream adaptation, and the geometry injector (Secs. 4.3 and 4.4).
Geometry interventions show that the model uses the supplied geometry: in a controlled MindCube-

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We propose explicitly connecting geometry information to spatial relationships expressed in lan-
guage, separating adapting geometry to the VLM from applying it to spatial reasoning.
• We introduce GeoReader, a two-stage framework that trains lightweight Geometric Readers
through language supervision from a frozen VLM, then freezes the Readers and adapts the VLM
for spatial QA with question-conditioned geometry injection (Sec. 3).
• We demonstrate the effectiveness of GeoReader across seven spatial reasoning benchmarks and
three VLM families, with ablations and geometry interventions supporting both the framework’s
design and the model’s use of geometry information (Secs. 4.2–4.5).

Related Work
Spatial reasoning in vision-language models.
SpatialVLM [10], SpaRE [41] and SPAR-
7M [70] build synthetic or 3D-grounded spatial QA data. SpatialLadder [31] and HiSpatial [36]
organize training from perception toward reasoning, while SenseNova-SI [8] studies scaling spatial
data. SpatialQA [7] and ViewSpatial [30] target depth and viewpoint relations; N3D-VLM [62]
links 3D object localization to reasoning. These works establish the value of spatial supervision; we
study how to present estimated geometry to the answering VLM.
Geometry-augmented VLMs.
DUSt3R [61] and MASt3R [28] estimate point maps and corre-
spondences; CUT3R [58] maintains a recurrent scene state. VGGT [57] predicts camera and dense
geometry, and Depth Anything V2 [67] and Depth Anything 3 [37] predict depth and any-view ge-
ometry. Geometry-augmented VLMs take such 3D structure as input: 3D-LLM [23] and LEO [27]
ground language in 3D scenes. LLaVA-3D [78], Video-3D LLM [76] and SR-3D [14] add spatial
positions or regions to visual inputs. SpatialRGPT [13], SD-VLM [11] and HiSpatial [36] use rel-
ative depth, depth positional encoding and metric point maps, respectively. VLM-3R [18], Spatial-
MLLM [63] and VG-LLM [75] fuse visual and 3D features; SpaceMind [74] uses camera-guided
fusion, and SpatialStack [71] spreads geometry across LLM layers. These studies vary the geometric
source and fusion; we train the interface for the receiving VLM.
Learning geometry representations for reasoning.
G2VLM [26] combines reconstruction with
spatial understanding, and GAP-MLLM [72] pretrains with sparse point-map and semantic pre-
diction. SpatioLM [64] uses pseudo depth and camera supervision but only RGB at inference;
GeoSR [73] finds that fused VGGT tokens are often underused and masks visual tokens during
training to force their use, with QA as the only supervision. 3DThinker [12] aligns generated latents
with VGGT features, and CoVT [48] learns continuous visual thoughts; LVR [29] and Monet [60]
reason with visual latents, and Coconut [22] reasons in a continuous latent space. Unlike these meth-
ods, GeoReader supervises its geometric input through description prediction by the frozen VLM,
and takes estimated pose and depth, not descriptions, at inference.
3
Method
We aim to supply a pretrained VLM with geometric input that meets two separate requirements: the
geometric tokens must be readable by the receiving model, and the model must use them for the
question at hand. GeoReader addresses them with two training stages (Fig. 2). After the prelim-
inaries (Sec. 3.1), we describe how a Geometric Reader encodes each view as a geometric token
(Sec. 3.2), how Stage 1 trains it for readability through the frozen VLM (Sec. 3.3), and how Stage 2
injects these tokens according to the question (Sec. 3.4).

3.3
Frozen-VLM Readability Training
QA supervision alone does not specify what the geometry encodes, and feature alignment measures
similarity to an external target rather than what the receiving VLM can read (Sec. 1). We instead
supervise each Reader through the frozen target VLM: given the images, the geometric tokens and
a fixed instruction, the VLM predicts a geometry description ym of signal m with teacher forcing.

in image 2 moves 0.4 meters right and 0.1 meters backward, and turns left by about 23 degrees,”
and the depth description lists objects per view, e.g., “guitar (left) at 1.7 meters.” The supervised
positions T m cover geometric content such as distances, directions and angles; template words,

Pair-supervised readability.
Descriptions of a signal such as camera pose relate each view to ref-
erence view 1. Group supervision supervises the descriptions of all V views jointly in one example;
the description of view i can then be predicted from any view’s tokens, so the loss does not tell the
Reader which token should carry that relation. Pair supervision removes this ambiguity by applying
Eq. 2 to each reference–target pair: for target view i ∈{2, . . . , V }, the Reader encodes only the
geometry of views 1 and i, and the frozen VLM predicts view i’s description relative to view 1 from
these two views, so only their tokens can explain it. Stage 2 inputs keep all views (Sec. A.1.1).
3.4
Question-Conditioned Geometry Injection
Freezing the Readers.
Stage 2 supervises answers, which do not explicitly require the Readers to
retain the geometric relationships specified by the Stage 1 descriptions. Updating the Readers under
this supervision may weaken their ability to express geometry beyond what the training questions
need. We therefore freeze the Readers and adapt the VLM to this fixed interface.
Question-conditioned readout.
Questions differ in how much they rely on each geometric sig-
nal, so we let the question set each signal’s strength. We compute it from the final-layer hidden
state of the last valid question token, which attends to all preceding visual and question tokens and
thus summarizes the task context. Geometric embeddings, however, enter before layer 1, and one
forward pass cannot use its own final-layer state to set earlier inputs. We therefore first run a read
pass over only the images and question to obtain this hidden state, hq, before adding geometry.
Geometry injector.
A geometry injector turns the frozen Reader outputs into the geometric to-
kens of the answer pass. For each signal m, it maps Zm through an adapter Pm, scales the result by
a gate computed from hq with a routing head Rm, and adds it to the slot embeddings Bm ∈RV ×d,
the learned input embeddings of the placeholder tokens at the geometric-token positions:

Experiments
We first compare GeoReader with existing models and vanilla SFT (the same QA fine-tuning with-
out geometry; Sec. 4.2). Next, we test Reader training and freezing (Sec. 4.3), then injection designs
and whether answers use geometry (Sec. 4.4). Finally, we compare geometric tokens with text
descriptions and present qualitative examples (Sec. 4.5).

Main Results
Comparison with existing models.

Interface Trade-oﬀs and Qualitative Analysis
Text versus continuous geometry.
As extra SFT targets, descriptions score 67.0%, below
vanilla SFT (68.6%; Tab. 5). Generating them at inference yields 72.9% and supplying them in the
prompt 81.0%, whereas GeoReader, using them only as Reader targets, achieves 89.2%. Descrip-
tions thus help more as Reader supervision than as text. With precomputed geometry, GeoReader
generates 16.2 tokens in 0.5 s, versus 119.3 and 2.5 s when generating them (Sec. A.4.7).

5
Conclusion
We presented GeoReader, which converts geometry into a form that a frozen VLM can read before
the VLM learns to use it. Geometric Readers learn through geometry-description prediction by the
frozen VLM, then stay frozen while the VLM and a question-conditioned geometry injector learn
spatial QA; descriptions serve only as training targets. Zeroing the aligned latents of a released
feature-alignment model leaves its accuracy unchanged, whereas removing GeoReader’s geometry
lowers it. With geometry predicted from RGB, GeoReader reaches 94.3 on MindCube-Tiny with
Qwen3-VL-4B and a six-benchmark average of 72.5 with Qwen3.5-9B. Capturing geometry does
not ensure its use; the representation that helps most is the one the receiving model can read.

A.1.1
Stage 1: Geometry-Description Data
Window corpus.
The window-based Stage 1 corpus is built from training scenes in ScanNet [16]
and ScanNet++ [69]. It contains approximately 29.5K Camera and 35.4K Depth description records,
drawn from a shared pool of 1,550 scenes. The two signals share the same windows. Camera
windows contain 2, 4, 8, 16 or 32 views; Depth windows additionally include single images.
Camera targets are constructed from annotated camera poses and describe planar translation in me-
ters and heading changes relative to the first view. For each window, we adopt the first camera’s
coordinate frame as the reference world frame and transform all camera poses into it. The first
camera therefore has identity pose and lies at the origin; the x and z axes point right and forward
relative to that view. Depth targets describe object positions using a 3 × 3 image grid (upper-left,
upper, upper-right, left, center, right, lower-left, lower, and lower-right), together with distances in
meters. We retain up to five object entries per view from projected instance geometry and annota-
tions. Single-view windows are omitted from Camera training; windows without any retained object
entries are omitted from Depth training. Scenes in the evaluation benchmarks are excluded before

MindCube pairs.
The description construction for MindCube [59] is separate from the window
corpus. It uses 2,072 training image groups, yielding 3,375 reference–target pairs for pair-supervised
Camera Reader pretraining (Eq. 2); Sec. A.4.7 gives the per-epoch example counts. A four-view
group yields three independent pairs (1, 2), (1, 3) and (1, 4), not all six possible pairs. Each pair
retains only its two images, the corresponding camera geometry and one relative-pose description,
with the reference coordinate frame and source camera geometry preserved; pairs repeated across
source groups are retained. The excluded views and their geometry are absent from a pair’s for-
ward pass, rather than merely excluded from the loss, and different pairs share model weights but
no attention context. Both group and pair supervision compute the loss only at geometric-content
positions (Sec. 3.3). Pairs use training groups only; QA inputs keep all views.

Stage 2: QA Data and Test-Set Exclusion
The multi-source QA collection has approximately 135K examples and is separate from the 10K
MindCube QA set used in the MindCube ablations.
These files combine training questions
from VSI-style tasks [66], SpatialLadder [31], SPAR [70], ViewSpatial [30], Spatial-MM [52],

We exclude official benchmark test questions and answers from Stage 2 training. Sources with
published training partitions are drawn from those partitions; separately generated questions use the

A.2.2
Injection Design and Computation
Question-conditioned injector.
GeoReader follows Sec. 3.4: its adapter Pm is a tokenwise
2048×2048 affine map with zero-initialized weights and bias, and its routing head Rm applies
LayerNorm, a 2048 →256 linear map, GELU, and a 256 →1 linear map before the sigmoid. The
final map is zero-initialized, giving an initial gate of 0.5 for available geometry.
Alternative designs (Tab. 7).
Tab. 7 replaces the whole injector with two simpler designs, using
annotated geometry and the same frozen pair-supervised Reader. For the geometric signal evaluated
here, let Zm ∈RV ×d denote the Reader’s raw output, with one token per view and d = 2048.
Direct injection inserts these outputs unchanged. Fixed scaling multiplies them by a single trainable
scalar, shared across examples and views, initialized to 1 and with no sigmoid or normalization. All
three designs bypass the Reader’s Stage 1 output gate (Sec. A.2.1). On all 1,050 MindCube-Tiny
questions, direct injection, fixed scaling and the injector answer 825, 719 and 937 correctly.

A.4.1
Geometry versus Vanilla SFT on Six Benchmarks
We compare GeoReader-3B from Tab. 2 with vanilla SFT, the same QA fine-tuning without geo-
metric inputs, to separate the effect of geometry from that of QA fine-tuning. Both use Qwen2.5-
VL-3B [49] with the same QA data, training settings and evaluation protocol; GeoReader also reads
DA3-predicted geometry through its frozen Readers.
Each benchmark mixes questions that depend on 3D geometry with questions that do not, so we
split the 59 native subtasks of the six benchmarks into those requiring metric geometry (object and
room size, camera motion, and distance and depth) and the others (direction, relation and other;
2D, counting and order), grouped by the quantity asked about. Camera motion covers questions
about the camera’s own motion or viewpoint change (MMSI camera motion and camera–camera
position, SPAR camera-motion and view-change inference); the size and distance-and-depth types
cover subtasks named by those quantities; 2D, counting and order covers 2D relations, counting and
appearance order; the remaining subtasks form direction, relation and other. Question-type scores
pool subtasks across benchmarks and weight each subtask by its number of questions, whereas
benchmark totals follow each benchmark’s own averaging (e.g., SPBench averages its SI and MV
splits equally), so question-type scores are not directly comparable with the totals in Tab. 2.
On the 27 subtasks requiring metric geometry, geometric inputs raise accuracy from 56.7 to 59.6, and
every question type improves: object and room size (+4.8), camera motion (+4.4) and distance and
depth (+2.2) (Tabs. 3 and 12). Individual subtasks gain much more, including +14.3 on SPBench-
SI relative distance, +13.5 on MMSI camera motion, +7.8 on SPAR camera-motion inference and
+7.4 on VSI-Bench object size; the subtasks in Panel C of Tab. 12 span 1, 2, 3, 8 and 32 input images
or sampled frames per question. On the other 32 subtasks, accuracy is unchanged (67.3 versus
67.5), and neither question type decreases. Geometric inputs thus improve accuracy on geometry-
dependent questions without lowering it on the others.

supervision through the frozen target VLM, then freezes the Reader for QA. The figure contrasts
these learning targets and input pathways side by side.
A.4.5
Alternative Interfaces
Shared setting.
The Panel B alternatives in Tab. 4 follow the MindCube ablation setting of
Sec. A.2.3 with answer-only supervision. The two adapted fusion rows use pre-extracted final-
layer VGGT [57] features from the input RGB images: one camera token and 1,369 patch tokens
per view, each 2,048-dimensional; the four register tokens are excluded. The VGGT and Qwen
visual encoders are frozen, while the full language model and fusion module are trained with QA
answer loss. Both fusion modules use per-frame cross-attention with a 512-dimensional attention
space and eight heads. They modify the Qwen image embeddings before one LLM pass, without
adding geometric tokens to the input or using a Geometric Reader.
VLM-3R-style adaptation.
Following the spatial–visual fusion idea of VLM-3R [18], each
frame’s Qwen image embeddings query that frame’s VGGT camera and patch features. A learned
output projection maps the attended features back to the Qwen hidden dimension, and its zero-
initialized residual is added to the corresponding image embeddings. We adopt only the fusion
mechanism; the original VLM-3R system uses CUT3R [58] features.
SpaceMind-style adaptation.
Inspired by the camera-guided fusion of SpaceMind [74], we re-
tain the same per-frame visual queries and VGGT features but use the camera feature to adjust
patch keys and values, weight patch values by learned importance, and apply a camera-conditioned
channel-wise gate to the fused residual before adding it to the Qwen image embeddings. Its output
projection is zero-initialized, so the residual starts at zero.
Per-token additive fusion.
This row uses the same annotated pose input as GeoReader: the 20-
dimensional descriptor of each image token passes through a zero-initialized linear map to the Qwen
hidden dimension and is added to its embedding. It adds no geometric tokens and uses one LLM
pass, whereas the Reader of GeoReader encodes the same descriptors into one token per view.
