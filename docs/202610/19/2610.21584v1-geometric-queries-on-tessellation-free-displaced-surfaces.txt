Displacement maps encode fine geometric detail in compact textures and
simplify surface authoring, but using them typically requires pre-tessellating
surfaces into dense triangle meshes, incurring a heavy memory cost. Re-
cent tessellation-free methods enable direct ray tracing. However, broader
geometric queries such as surface sampling, specular manifold search, and
closest-point queries remain less explored. Supporting these operations re-
quires surface enclosures, area estimates, and normal bounds, which scalar
height intervals alone do not fully provide. We present a first-order texture-
space hierarchy that unlocks these queries directly without pre-tessellation.
Each node stores a local affine height plane and a conservative residual
bound, retaining spatial height variation that scalar minimum–maximum
bounds discard. The plane and residual provide surface enclosures for inter-
section and distance searches, while the plane’s height and slopes supply area
estimates for sampling. Additional slope bounds support specular light-path
search. Building on this representation, we develop query-specific traversal,
pruning, and sampling algorithms, and demonstrate them in caustics render-
ing, sampling displaced emitters, and surface partial differential equation
(PDE) solvers. We demonstrate memory savings over pre-tessellation and
performance improvements over query-specific baselines, while retaining
efficient ray tracing.

Introduction
Displacement mapping is a widely used computer graphics tech-
nique that allows artists to create rich visual detail without modeling
and storing fine features as explicit geometry. However, tessella-
tion can negate this memory advantage by expanding the surface
into a dense triangle mesh. Recent tessellation-free methods avoid
this cost for ray tracing [Ogaki 2023; Thonat et al. 2021, 2023], but
using displaced surfaces across computer graphics applications re-
quires much more than ray intersection queries. Our goal is to make
tessellation-free displaced surfaces a general-purpose graphics prim-
itive by equipping them with the operations needed to support a
wide range of downstream applications.
These operations determine how detailed geometry participates
in rendering and broader geometric computation. Realistic render-
ing usually requires challenging light transport effects caused by
difficult light paths or complex light source distribution. Beyond
rendering, surface sampling supports point-cloud generation and
numerical integration [Ling et al. 2025], while solving partial differ-
ential equations (PDEs) on a surface requires querying the distance
to the surface. Although a displacement map makes it easy to eval-
uate the height at any single texture coordinate, answering these
queries efficiently requires bounds or estimates over entire surface
regions. The challenge is to obtain this regional information without
instantiating the detailed geometry.

The scalar height bounds typically used for direct ray tracing pro-

records the range of heights in a texture region but not how height

mation about slopes. Therefore, we propose to approximate height

We build a texture-space hierarchy of height planes and residual
bounds that is shared by all triangles using the same displacement
map. Together with the base geometry, each node’s plane and resid-
ual define a surface enclosure for intersection and distance queries,
while the plane’s height and slopes provide estimates for surface
area sampling. Additional slope bounds enable pruning tests during
specular path search. The hierarchy therefore provides both geomet-
ric bounds and sampling estimates, with query-specific data added
only where needed.
Building on this representation, we develop traversal, pruning,
and sampling algorithms for four geometric queries. For ray inter-
section, a piecewise-linear approximation of the nonlinear texture-
space ray enables inexpensive grid traversal and tests against the
stored height bands. For specular light path search, a first-order
bound over height and slope prunes regions and bootstraps local
constraint solvers, allowing them to find more admissible paths
faster. For surface sampling, our hierarchy estimates regional areas
to guide hierarchical sampling, with weighting strategies tailored
to specific applications such as emitter sampling. For closest-point
queries, we derive lower bounds on world-space distances that guide
the search and refinement to a requested distance tolerance. To-
gether, these algorithms greatly expand the application landscape of
compact displaced surfaces beyond ray intersection while retaining
efficient ray tracing.

Related work
Displacement mapping and tessellation-free ray tracing. Displace-
ment maps allow content authors to drive geometric detail using tex-
tures instead of manually sculpting every surface vertex [Cook 1984].
Tessellation makes these displaced surfaces accessible to standard
mesh processing or rendering, but storing the tessellated geometry
and its acceleration structure can be costly. Early work addresses
this problem by generating and caching displaced geometry dur-
ing rendering [Pharr and Hanrahan 1996]. Smits et al. [2000] avoid
storing the full tessellation by traversing an implicit barycentric
grid and generating displaced micro-triangles on demand. Maxi-
mum mipmaps [Tevs et al. 2008] accelerate height-field intersec-
tion through hierarchical height bounds. Modern GPUs support
hardware tessellation that splits coarse geometry into dense, high-
resolution geometry on the fly using dedicated hardware stages in
the rasterization pipeline [Nießner et al. 2016].
More recent work develops bounds and traversal methods
for direct ray tracing of displaced triangle meshes. Thonat et
al. [2021](TFDM) builds a min–max texture hierarchy and con-
structs world-space bounds during traversal and ray intersection
tests. The subsequent work [Thonat et al. 2023] (RMIP) alternates
between tightening texture-space regions and world-space bounds,
using elongated rectangular regions to follow the ray more closely.
Ogaki [2023] instead parameterizes the nonlinear texture-space ray
by rational functions of height, allowing direct intersection with
texture-space boxes. Projective displacement mapping [Hoetzlein
2025] uses fixed-step world-space ray marching with projective tex-
ture lookup, supporting interactive editing without a texture-space
hierarchy. In parallel, micro-meshes reduce storage by compressing
finely subdivided surfaces [Gruen et al. 2026; Maggiordomo et al.
2023] and supports hardware acceleration. Our focus is a texture-
space hierarchy for queries including and beyond ray intersection.
It retains local height variation through height planes and residual
bounds and, with query-specific enhancements where needed,
supports specular-path search, surface sampling, and closest-point
queries.
Bounding methods for geometric queries. Bounding methods are
widely used in graphics to accelerate ray intersection, collision de-
tection, and distance queries on various surface representations.
On explicit meshes, these operations use bounding volume hierar-
chies [Gottschalk et al. 1996; Larsen et al. 1999]. For continuous
surfaces, bounds can be derived from the functions that define the
geometry. Affine arithmetic guides subdivision for intersecting para-
metric surfaces [De Figueiredo 1996], while Taylor models combine
polynomial approximations with bounded remainders for culling be-
fore tessellation [Hasselgren et al. 2009]. Sharp and Jacobson [2022]
use range analysis for various geometric queries on neural implicit
surfaces. In displacement rendering, Heidrich and Seidel [1998]
bound procedural displacement shaders using affine arithmetic,
and Munkberg et al. [2010] combine normal bounds and min–max
mipmaps to enclose displaced Bézier patches. Recent work and ours
use bounds to query displaced surfaces without pre-tessellation.
TFDM and RMIP construct bounding volumes from the base ge-
ometry and scalar height intervals over texture regions. Queries
involving surface area and normals also require derivative informa-
tion. We store an affine height model and a bounded residual in each
texture region, with additional slope bounds where needed. From
this representation, we develop query-specific bounds, traversal
strategies, and sampling algorithms to support queries beyond ray
intersection.

Specular light transport. In Monte Carlo rendering, sampling spec-
ular paths that connect a receiver to a light through specular re-
flection or refraction is challenging for standard path tracing be-
cause the intermediate surface points must satisfy the geometric
constraints imposed by optical laws. This is traditionally handled
by photo mapping [Hachisuka and Jensen 2009; Jensen 1996] or
path regularization [Kaplanyan and Dachsbacher 2013] that intro-
duce bias (blurring). Manifold-based approaches [Hanika et al. 2015;
Jakob and Marschner 2012; Zeltner et al. 2020] explore or solve for
admissible paths on a low-dimensional path space manifold. These
approaches tend to fail on highly complex displaced surfaces due
to poor initialization of equation-solving. Path Cuts [Wang et al.
2020] use a hierarchical search to prune surface regions that cannot
form valid specular paths. Fan et al. [2024] propose a polynomial
formulation of specular constraints that can be solved efficiently.
Subsequently, Fan et al. [2025] derive position and irradiance bounds
for caustics cast by triangle tuples based on Bernstein bounds for
rational functions, which reduces the search domain for specular
light transport. These methods all expect input geometry as ordi-
nary triangle meshes (some need precomputation on them). They
are incompatible with tessellation-free displaced surfaces or at least
require significant extensions. With a conservative test derived from
height and slope bounds, our hierarchy supports efficient specular
next-event estimation queries without pre-tessellation.
Surface and light sampling. Surface sampling is a fundamental
operation in computer graphics [Alliez et al. 2003; Keller 1997; Li et al.
2010; Shirley et al. 1996], geometric machine learning [Hermosilla
et al. 2018; Qi et al. 2017], and point-based neural rendering [Xu et al.
2026]. Sampling a triangle mesh by area can be done by selecting
triangles proportional to areas and sampling uniformly within each
triangle [Pharr et al. 2023]. This first step requires enumerating over
all triangles. Ling et al. [2025] show hot to generate uniform surface
samples for arbitrary types of surface with a all-hit ray-intersection
routine. For rendering, sampling should also account for the lighting
integrand. Hierarchical product sampling combines multiple factors
during sample generation [Clarberg and Akenine-Möllery 2008;
Clarberg et al. 2005], and light trees use spatial, directional, and
emission information to select lights according to their estimated
contribution [Conty Estevez and Kulla 2018]. Our hierarchy instead
guides sampling within a displaced surface, using the stored height
and slopes to estimate regional area. Combining these estimates
with emission and receiver-dependent weights supports importance
sampling of displaced emitters without pre-tessellation.
Closest-point queries and surface PDEs. Bounding volume hi-
erarchies accelerate closest-point queries on triangle meshes,
as implemented in FCPW [Sawhney 2021]. When the mesh ap-
proximates a displaced surface, query accuracy depends on the
tessellation resolution. Methods for continuous representations
query the surface without first converting it to a triangle mesh:
Marschner et al. [2021] formulate closest-point queries on polyno-
mial patches through sum-of-squares optimization, while Sharp and
Jacobson [2022] use range analysis on neural implicit surfaces. We
derive distance lower bounds from the height planes and residuals
in our hierarchy, enabling closest-point queries directly on displaced
surfaces. These queries provide surface projections for Projected
Walk on Spheres [Sugimoto et al. 2024], which solves surface PDEs
through Monte Carlo walks with repeated closest-point projections.

We first define the displaced surface and its shell coordinates,
then describe the regional information needed for ray intersection,
specular-path search, surface sampling, and closest-point queries.

3.2
Geometric queries and their requirements
The surface definition provides positions and derivatives at indi-
vidual texture coordinates. Efficient queries also need information
over entire regions. A texture-space hierarchy groups neighboring
cells into progressively larger regions, allowing queries to reject
a region or assign it a sampling weight without examining every
texel. Tessellation-free ray tracers use regional height bounds to
guide traversal [Ogaki 2023; Thonat et al. 2021]. Here we describe
the requirements of the four queries illustrated in Figure 4.
Ray intersection. Ray intersection determines visibility and locate
surface interactions along light paths. Given a ray I(𝑡) = o +𝑡𝝎, the
closest-hit query seeks the smallest admissible 𝑡for which I(𝑡) =
S(𝑢, 𝑣). Regional height bounds allow traversal to reject portions of
the surface that cannot intersect the ray before performing local
intersection tests. Both scalar height intervals and our first-order
height bounds support this rejection; efficient evaluation also greatly
depends on the traversal strategy (Section 5).
Specular light path search. To render caustics, we seek points on
a displaced surface that connect a receiver to a light source via
specular reflection or refraction. Admissible light paths must obey
optical laws such as the law of reflection or Snell’s law, and arbitrar-
ily sampled surface points generally do not satisfy the geometric
constraints these laws impose. Finding valid specular connections
can therefore be viewed as a higher-order generalization of ray
intersection: rather than a single line segment, each solution is a
path made up of multiple segments.
As in accelerated ray intersection, our general strategy is to use a
hierarchical data structure to speed up the search. Unlike a standard
acceleration structure, however, ours must bound not only surface
positions but also normals, so that invalid regions can be pruned
before the constraint is solved locally. These bounds must account
for both the base geometry and the slope field that defines the
normals. Section 6 develops the pruning test based on a first-order
bound and the corresponding query algorithm.

4
First-order hierarchy
We represent height over each texture region by an affine function
and a conservative residual bound. The affine function retains local
height variation and provides slope estimates, while the residual
encloses the height function throughout the region. We construct
these records hierarchically so that queries can use them at different
spatial scales.

4.1
Node representation
We organize the height map into a regular texture-space quad-tree
hierarchy. We use 𝑛to index its nodes and denote the rectangular
texture region covered by node 𝑛as Ω𝑛. At the finest level, a leaf
covers one texture cell where the height should be bilinearly inter-
polated from four neighboring samples Then each parent covers
four children, as in a min–max mipmap used in [Ogaki 2023; Thonat
et al. 2021]. Notice that the hierarchy is shared by triangles using the
same height map. For a node covering Ω𝑛and a triangle with texture
footprint Ω𝑇, the query uses the clipped texture region Ω𝑛∩Ω𝑇
and its corresponding surface patch.
Previous methods [Thonat et al. 2021, 2023] use scalar interval
[ℎmin,ℎmax] to bound the heights within a region but does not de-
scribe how they vary with texture position. We instead approximate
the height function by a local plane. This first-order (affine) model
stores a height offset and two slopes. Higher-order models could
also capture curvature, but would require more coefficients and
more involved bound calculations. As shown in Figure 2, over each

In summary, each node’s height representation contains four
values (ℎ𝑛,𝑔𝑢,𝑛,𝑔𝑣,𝑛,𝑟𝑛) and we compute the texture region and

ray intersection, subtracting the height plane from a linear ray
segment reduces rejection to a test against the residual band. For
closest-point queries, the plane and residual combine with the base
geometry to give a world-space distance lower bound. The plane’s
height and slopes also provide a regional area estimate for sampling.
Specular-path search additionally requires bounds on the slope field
used to define its normals. The following query sections derive these
operations and the additional data they require.

5
Ray intersection
We begin with ray intersection, using the height planes and residuals
from Section 4 to reject surface regions during traversal. In texture
space, each node encloses the height function between bℎ𝑛−𝑟𝑛and
bℎ𝑛+ 𝑟𝑛. Testing whether a ray remains outside this band therefore
requires evaluating its height relative to the stored plane. Following
Ogaki [2023], we perform these tests in texture space, where the
bands are defined directly, rather than constructing world-space
enclosures that may overlap between neighboring texture regions.
The difficulty is that a straight world-space ray generally becomes
a curve in texture space (Figure 5(a, b)). Ogaki represents this curve
by rational functions of height and intersects it with texture-space
boxes through quadratic root solves. As traversal moves between
nodes, their box boundaries change, requiring new solves to de-
termine where the curve enters and leaves each box. We instead
approximate the curve by linear segments before traversing the
hierarchy. For each segment, successive grid crossings can be com-
puted incrementally, avoiding repeated nonlinear intersection tests.
This also simplifies evaluation of our first-order bounds: subtract-
ing a node’s affine height plane from the segment’s height gives
an affine function whose extrema occur at the endpoints. We can
therefore test the sloped height band directly without expanding
it into a scalar height interval. Padding accounts for the segment
approximation, with its limitations described in Section 5.2.
Our choice of linear segments is motivated by the observation
that many mapped rays deviate little from straight segments over
the traversed intervals. A small number of segments can therefore
represent these curves closely while enabling inexpensive traversal
and height-band tests. Across 228 mesh–displacement map pairs,
98.94% of 396.9 million ray–prism builds that produce segments use
at most four segments, with an approximation threshold of 0.06
texel. Section 5.3 evaluates rendering speed and agreement with

Uniform subdivision. Within each retained interval, we estimate
how many segments are needed by measuring the curve’s deviation
at the midpoint in height. We compare the texture position of this
point with the midpoint of the straight segment joining the interval’s
endpoints (Figure 5c). A small deviation allows fewer segments; a
larger deviation produces more. We then sample the curve at equal
height increments and connect consecutive samples. Thus, spacing
is uniform within each event interval, while the segment count
adapts to its estimated deviation.
We use an approximation threshold of 0.06 texel. The midpoint
estimate determines the subdivision count but does not bound the
maximum deviation between samples. Traversal therefore uses ad-
ditional padding to account for the difference between the segments
and the curve, as described in Section 5.2.

Specular next-event estimation
So far, we have used our hierarchy for direct ray tracing of displaced
surfaces, an application shared by previous techniques. Although
direct ray intersection is important, it is by no means the only appli-
cation. Many rendering and graphics tasks require more challenging
types of geometric queries on displaced surfaces. These queries have
largely been overlooked and still lack efficient, tessellation-free so-
lutions.
One important example arises in rendering caustics cast by (dis-
placed) specular surfaces. Caustics are light transport effects fre-
quently encountered in realistic scenes that form intricate, visually
striking patterns. They are challenging to render because they are
contributed by specular paths (Figure 4), a subset of light paths
that are hard or even impossible to sample by standard path trac-
ing [Veach 1997]. In particular, these specular light paths lie on a
low-dimensional manifold of the path space [Jakob and Marschner
2012] because their construction is governed by geometric con-
straints imposed by optical laws. This geometric nature of the prob-
lem motivates us to utilize and enhance our hierarchy to improve
the sampling efficiency.

With the conservative node-level pruning in Section 6.1, we are

At each level, we pop the parent node and examine each of its child

close, and the constraint C on a small smooth patch is nearly linear.

Surface sampling
7.1
Area-guided hierarchical sampling
The most basic form of surface sampling is to draw samples (approx-
imately) in proportion to surface area. For an explicit triangle mesh,
this is straightforward: one computes the cumulative distribution
function (CDF) of the triangle areas and draws samples by inverting
the CDF. However, this approach is not possible for a displaced
surface that is implicitly described by its displacement map, unless
the surface is tessellated. Inspired by Clarberg et al. [2008; 2005], we
propose a hierarchical sampling scheme in which we stochastically
descend the texture-space hierarchy. At each level, the decision
is guided by the estimated displaced area covered by each node’s
footprint, which is made possible by the first-order statistics stored
in the hierarchy. Our sampling method remains unbiased despite
relying on estimated areas, and we show that it is efficient.
The overall sampling process consists of two steps: (1) sampling
a base triangle and (2) sampling a point within that triangle which
is subject to displacement. For ease of exposition, we first describe
the second step, assuming that a base triangle 𝑇has already been
sampled. To initiate the texture-space descent, 𝑇is transformed to
UV space and locate the smallest hierarchy node that covers it. The

evaluates surface candidates during initialization and leaf refine-

at internal hierarchy nodes.
8.3
Refinement and termination
Following the branch-and-bound descending, we may finally reach
a texel leaf node, but this does not mean we have finished the search:
a texel may still be too large to meet the requested distance tolerance
and the closest point may lie anywhere within its displaced surface
region. We therefore subdivide its texture domain as needed, recom-
pute distance lower bounds, and evaluate new surface candidates.
This refinement returns the closest point it found (if any), and a
lower bound on distance to the entire leaf region (LeafSearch).
The supplementary material gives the leaf-refinement details.

becase another face may contain a closer point. Let 𝑈obj be the

8.4
Results
Our method enables closest-point queries directly on displaced sur-
faces without pre-tessellation. Here we evaluate the resulting mem-
ory savings at a common distance-error target by comparing against
FCPW [Sawhney 2021] on tessellated surfaces. We then ablate the
contribution of first-order bounds by replacing them with scalar
min–max bounds in the same search. Finally, we demonstrate the
use of these queries in Projected Walk on Spheres. Comparisons and
measurements were performed on CPU (see Section 6.3 for machine
specification).
Comparison with pre-tessellation. We compare direct queries with
FCPW [Sawhney 2021] on uniformly tessellated surfaces. We create
a test set containing 56 displaced surfaces using seven base meshes
and eight displacement maps. Our method reduces query memory
by at least 55.4× in geometric mean (Figure 12), counting all geom-
etry and acceleration data retained after preprocessing. We time
quries on 96 points at three surface-offset distances. Using 2,048
separate queries, we select tessellations with a maximum sampled
distance error of 10−4, testing up to 64 subdivisions per base edge
under a 50-million-triangle cap. Only 36 of the 56 displaced surfaces
meet the distance-error target within the tessellation budget. The
remaining 20 still fail at the finest feasible tessellation. We report
relative memory usage for all 56 surfaces. For the 36 surfaces whose
tessellations meet the target, FCPW provides 7.48× faster queries
in geometric mean. Our method instead prioritizes compact storage
and direct queries without pre-tessellation.

Projected Walk on Sphere (PWoS) application. Projected Walk on
Spheres [Sugimoto et al. 2024] is a Monte Carlo method for solving
PDEs on surfaces that repeatedly projects walk positions onto the
surface. Now we use our closest-point algorihtm for these point
projections, allowing the method to operate directly on displaced
surfaces without pre-tessellation. Our compact representation re-
duces query memory by factors of 12.52 and 91 at sampled distance-
error targets of 10−4 and 10−5, respectively. Since a tighter error
target requires finer tessellation, our method provides more mem-
ory advantage in high-precision scenarios. In a separate ablation of
complete walks, it is 3.19× faster than the min–max AABB variant.
We also apply PWoS to a more complex displaced coral surface
with locally prescribed boundary regions (Figure 14) and use the
resulting field to control the coral’s emission in Figure 1.
9
Conclusion and future work
In this work, we presented a first-order, texture-space hierarchy
for tessellation-free displaced surfaces that supports a diverse set
of geometric queries. Local height planes and residual bounds pro-
vide surface enclosures and area estimates, while additional slope
bounds support specular-path search. We demonstrated our method
across various applications including caustics rendering, sampling
displaced emitters, and closest-point queries for surface PDE solvers,
while retaining efficient direct ray tracing.
We believe our work opens up a fruitful avenue of research. Mod-
ern graphics, rendering, and geometry processing increasingly de-
mand high fidelity and fine detail, which translate into massive
amounts of geometry that often cannot fit into memory. Traditional
geometry and scene representations were generally not designed
for such memory-intensive workloads. Our work demonstrates that
tessellation-free displaced surfaces are a promising candidate for a
high-fidelity, general-purpose graphics primitive.
