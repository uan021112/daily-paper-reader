Abstract
A 3D asset admits multiple valid part decompositions,
while semantics-aware segmentation should reflect both the
desired granularity and the parts a user wants to extract.
Ideally, a practical segmentation model should strive for a
simple principle: what you ask for is what you get. However,
realizing this goal is hindered by two core obstacles: the
lack of multi-granularity training data and the absence of
a holistic control mechanism. We propose FLEXPART, a
unified framework for text-controlled 3D mesh segmentation.
To establish flexible control, we introduce a holistic con-

1. Introduction
3D part segmentation provides explicit boundaries on mesh
surfaces, facilitating downstream applications such as part-
level editing, rigging, animation, and 3D printing. Crucially,
a single 3D asset can be decomposed in multiple valid ways
depending on the user’s intent: coarse functional groups for
retargeting, individual accessories for texturing, or specific
named parts for localized edits (as shown in Fig. 1). How-
ever, two core obstacles remain in achieving this flexible,
intent-driven control. First, existing 3D datasets lack multi-
granularity semantic annotations. Second, the absence of a
holistic, view-independent control signal often leads to frag-
mented boundaries and unresolved occlusions. Therefore,
developing a unified framework that couples a global seman-
tic control signal with multi-granularity decomposition is
essential to realize the user-centric principle: what you ask
for is what you get.
Regarding the first obstacle, existing 3D datasets typically
provide only a single, fixed partition per geometry, mostly
sourced from open-source repositories or fixed artist cre-
ations, lacking consistent multi-granularity semantics-aware
annotations. Recognizing this data scarcity, recent methods
often lift 2D priors [11, 18, 21, 30, 33, 39, 48, 49] or directly
cluster 3D features [19] heuristically. Consequently, their
partitions are inevitably biased toward data-intrinsic bound-
aries and are largely governed by low-level geometric cues
rather than explicit user intent. For example, models trained
on fixed geometry segmented data rigidly segment an "arm"
into sub-parts, failing to align with diverse user intents such
as animating the whole arm or editing individual segments.
In essence, without multi-granularity data, achieving truly
flexible, intent-driven control remains fundamentally out of
reach.
Beyond the availability of suitable training data, the mech-
anism used to formulate and inject user intent is equally
critical. While some works attempt to control granularity
through global heuristics, such as a scale parameter [39], a
clustering threshold [30], or a target part count [19], these
mechanisms remain semantically blind, failing to specify
which boundaries actually matter. On the other hand, using
single-view 2D masks [14] introduces severe view depen-
dence, leaving occluded regions unspecified. Consequently,
the reliance on localized or view-dependent signals prevents
existing methods from producing coherent, holistic parti-
tions. Closest to our work, recent methods explore control
via explicit text schemas [51]. However, they still struggle to
offer flexible user-intent control, as these text schemas lack
spatial and geometric grounding.
We overcome these two obstacles and unlock flexible,
intent-driven control by introducing a control prompt as
our input signal, which comprises target part names paired
with their specific location and shape clauses. This prompt
dynamically defines the output label space: varying the
prompt directly specifies a coarse, medium, or fine parti-
tion, while leaving unspecified regions as background natu-
rally enables selective segmentation. To overcome the lack
of multi-granularity training data, we design an automated
annotation pipeline powered by a Vision-Language Model
(VLM, specifically Gemini). This pipeline analyzes artist-
authored components from HY3D-Bench [9] and hierarchi-
cally merges them based on semantic and functional relation-
ships to form three granularities, subsequently generating the
corresponding control prompts for each level. By applying
this pipeline, we annotate 57K objects, yielding a dataset of
150K prompt-target pairs.
Building upon this large-scale dataset, we introduce
FLEXPART, a unified framework for text-controlled 3D
mesh segmentation. Our architecture builds upon gener-
ative surface attribute models [13, 14] by integrating a part-
aware VAE and a text-conditioned Diffusion Transformer
(DiT) [23]. Given the input mesh and the control prompt,
the DiT generates a latent representation that decodes into a
native 3D part-identity field. Querying this continuous field
at the mesh’s face centres yields discrete, binary-encoded
part IDs, thereby directly labeling the input without alter-
ing its original geometry. Furthermore, to support selective
segmentation, we introduce a drop-to-background training
augmentation that randomly omits parts from the prompt,
implicitly assigning their corresponding regions to the back-
ground. This diverse training strategy compels the model
to follow the semantic prompt rather than being constrained
by low-level geometric priors. During inference, a VLM-
based planner automatically deduces the location and shape
clauses from 2D renderings, unlocking a truly effortless ex-
perience where providing simple part names yields precise,
intent-driven segmentation.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce a flexible, intent-driven control mechanism
via holistic control prompts, and propose an automated
VLM-based annotation pipeline to construct a large-scale,
multi-granularity dataset with structured part descriptions.
• We propose FLEXPART, formulating text-controlled 3D
segmentation as a native 3D part-identity field. Equipped
with a randomized background dropping strategy, a single
model seamlessly supports multi-granularity partitions and
selective segmentation.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate that FLEXPART out-

2. Related Work
Overcoming 3D data scarcity for part segmentation. Ex-
isting 3D datasets rarely provide multi-granularity semantic
annotations for the same geometry, so most methods rely
on external priors to infer parts. One line of work distills
features from 2D image foundation models [11, 22, 27] into
3D, either by optimizing a per-shape feature field [39] or
by predicting masks on multi-view renderings and lifting
them onto the surface [4, 8, 30, 37]. Per-object optimiza-
tion is costly, and mask fusion is sensitive to view coverage.
Open-vocabulary approaches [18, 21, 33, 48, 49] leverage
vision-language models by querying rendered views with
part names and aggregating predictions back to 3D, but oper-
ating in 2D projected space introduces multi-view inconsis-
tencies and fragmented boundaries on the mesh. A second
line learns part features natively in 3D from curated supervi-
sion. PartField [19] clusters a learned hierarchy at a chosen
level, and P3-SAM [20] predicts point-prompted masks and
automatically selects and merges them into a part segmenta-
tion. These methods produce high-quality decompositions,
yet because their training data contain only fixed geometric
splits, the resulting partitions are governed by low-level geo-
metric cues rather than user-intended semantic groupings.
Control signals for part segmentation.
Given the
geometry-biased representations above, various control
mechanisms have been proposed, yet all exhibit criti-
cal limitations.
Global heuristics like scales or target
counts [19, 30, 39] remain semantically blind, failing to
specify which boundaries actually matter. Localized prompts
such as clicks or boxes [20, 50, 52] leave semantic group-
ing implicit, while single-view 2D guidance [14] introduces
severe view dependence and leaves occluded regions un-
specified. Consequently, these localized or view-dependent
signals cannot produce coherent, holistic partitions. Closest
to our work, schema-driven approaches [24, 51] use text
schemas but lack spatial and geometric grounding; further-
more, they synthesize separate geometries rather than seg-
menting the input surface, and lack multi-granularity training.
Unlike these methods, FLEXPART conditions on a holistic
text prompt to directly generate a native 3D part-identity
field, explicitly trained on multi-granularity annotations to
achieve flexible, intent-driven segmentation.
Part-level 3D generation. While modern generators ex-
cel at producing high-quality holistic shapes [7, 10, 12, 36,
42, 43, 47], recent research has expanded into part-level
generation under two main paradigms. Single-stage meth-
ods synthesize all parts directly from an image or latent
representation, automatically discovering the decomposi-
tion [2, 3, 16, 31, 44]. Consequently, users lack explicit
control over the resulting part structure. Conversely, two-
stage methods take an existing shape alongside its decompo-
sition to generate the complete geometry of each individual
part [5, 15, 29, 38, 40, 41, 45], meaning their performance
is strictly bounded by the input segmentation. Our method
provides this segmentation from text, at a user-specified
granularity.
3. Method
As illustrated in Fig. 2, we propose FLEXPART, a unified gen-
erative framework for semantics-aware 3D part segmentation
driven by a holistic, view-independent text control signal.
By simply adjusting the text prompt, users can flexibly con-
trol the segmentation granularity and select specific target
parts. Building upon native 3D surface fields (Sec. 3.1), we
formulate segmentation as the generation of a continuous
3D part-identity field. Unlike prior methods that predict
noisy continuous colors, our field predicts discrete, binary-
encoded part IDs that explicitly align with the prompt’s label
space (Sec. 3.2). Our architecture features a part-aware VAE
to encode the mesh and its part IDs into a unified latent,
alongside a text-conditioned dual-stream DiT that generates
these latents from the holistic prompt. Finally, this robust
text-to-3D grounding is enabled by training the framework
on comprehensive multi-granularity data (Sec. 3.3).

output is a continuous part-ID field; querying it at the face
centres of the input mesh with Eq. 2 yields a per-face label
for each of the F faces {f1, . . . , fF }.

Binary-encoded part IDs. Prior generative segmentation
methods represent parts using distinct RGB colors [14].
However, this continuous RGB space 2563 often leads to
noisy, fragmented predictions within a single part, requiring
ad-hoc post-processing such as color clustering to recover
part identity. To resolve this, we represent surface attributes
using discrete, binary-encoded part IDs id ∈{0, 1}6 to rep-
resent the surface attribute that labels the continuous space.
Reserving the all-ones label for the background, this 6-bit
format supports up to K = 64 −1 distinct parts, which is
sufficient to accommodate the fine-grained structural com-
plexity of most objects. To effectively ground these IDs to
user text, we map the i-th prompt item to the i-th binary ID.
Unlike permutation-invariant approaches that lack structural
constraints, this locally aligned prompting provides explicit
ordering priors, yielding significantly more accurate part
assignments.
Part-aware VAE. We keep the voxel anchors of Eq. 1 as
queries, and the point cloud they attend to carries a part
ID alongside each point’s position and normal. An 8-layer
self-attention encoder ψ encodes the shape into a 2048 × 64
latent. The decoder φ decodes the latent into fields. As
the part field cannot be isolated from geometry information,
the latent encodes both geometry and part information. We
query the SDF using grid locations that make the latent aware
of the location, while querying the six-bit part ID using mesh
face centres as queries. We train the VAE jointly using
Lvae = λklLkl + λsdfLsdf + λpartLpart,

supervise the part IDs. For VAE training, part indices are
randomly shuffled before encoding to prevent order memo-

Dual-stream DiT. We employ a dual-stream architecture,
inspired by MM-DiT [1, 12], to jointly process the text con-
dition and the 3D latent. First, a frozen Qwen3-VL [25]
encoder extracts text tokens T, which are injected with a 1D
RoPE [35]. Simultaneously, to condition on the input geom-
etry, we run the VAE encoder with all part IDs set to zero to
produce a part-agnostic shape latent zs. This geometry con-
dition is added element-wise to the input noisy latent. These
latent tokens are then provided with a 3D RoPE [12, 13]
over the voxel indices V of Eq. 1. To prevent positional col-
lisions, text indices are strictly offset past the voxel indices.
At inference time, the DiT iteratively processes and denoises
the latent from noisy zt to clean latent z0. The clean latent
z0 is passed to the VAE decoder φ to predict the 3D part
segmentation with face centres as queries.

3.3. Data construction
Partitions.
We take the artist-authored components of
HY3D-Bench [9] as atomic units. Inspired by CubePart [51],
which uses set-of-mark renderings to group parts, we mark
every component across views and utilize a vision-language
model’s multi-view reasoning ability for three-level parti-
tions. Duplicate levels are dropped, so an object used in
training yields up to three partitions.
Text annotation. For each partition we render every grouped
part with its boundary overlay, annotate that part alone with
the clauses of Sec. 3.2, then aggregate the results and have a
language model refine them into one prompt. During training
we augment it with drop-to-background: a random subset
of items is dropped and their faces reassigned to bg. This is
where bg acquires its meaning, so it is what enables selective
segmentation, and it also strengthens the correspondence
between a named item and its label. The detailed pipeline is
described in App. A.1.
Inference. The semantic prompts are either provided directly
by the user or automatically generated by a prompt planner.
We instantiate this planner using a frozen Qwen3.8 [26]
model, which processes multi-view renderings of the input
mesh. The planner plays a critical role in selective segmenta-
tion scenarios: when a user specifies only a subset of target
parts, the planner automatically infers and fills in the remain-
ing background categories, ensuring a complete and valid
partition of the entire 3D asset.
4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental setup
Training data. We run the annotation pipeline of Sec. 3.3
over 57K objects sampled from HY3D-Bench [9], holding
the category proportions of the source library fixed. This
yields 151,492 prompt-target pairs. App. A.1 provides the
annotation details.
Benchmarks.
We evaluate our method on three main
datasets. PartObjaverse-Tiny [39] contains 200 public
objects, enabling direct baseline comparisons. Following
P3-SAM [20], we use its watertight version. Its original part
decomposition is used as given: our pipeline only writes the
text description of each part and never edits a part bound-
ary. FLEXPARTBENCH is our held-out benchmark of 364
assets proportionally sampled from PartNeXt and PartVerse-
XL. Each is annotated at three granularities with strictly
no training overlap. Selective-190 comprises 190 manually
constructed selective cases (ranging from 2 to 18 named
parts) across 28 FLEXPARTBENCH objects. We manually
inspected and removed objects with defective annotations
or source geometries; App. A.4 details the construction and
category distributions of all sets. Finally, we demonstrate
that our method generalizes well to generated meshes.
Metrics. The baselines do not provide the ordered prompt-to-
part-ID correspondence used by FLEXPART. We therefore
compare all methods using class-agnostic mIoU [19, 20],
averaging each ground-truth part’s maximum IoU over pre-
dicted regions. For FLEXPART, we also evaluate the same
predictions using -direct mIoU, which compares output
region k with the ground-truth region for prompt item k,
without rematching. For selective segmentation, we report
named-part and background IoUs separately to expose fore-
ground leakage.
Baselines. We compare against five methods, evaluated in
their intended settings (inputs specified in the Condition-

ties. PartField [19] clusters a learned feature field using the
ground-truth part count. P3-SAM [20] automatically selects
and merges predicted masks into a segmentation; we evalu-

exclude it from Tab. 2. SegviGen [14] requires a color-coded
guidance map. We evaluate it using both ground-truth maps
and maps automatically predicted by prompting SAM3 on a
front view with the target part names. For FLEXPART, we

4.2. Main results
Evaluation on PartObjaverse-Tiny. As shown in Tab. 1
and Fig. 3, FLEXPART achieves the highest accuracy. Lack-
ing semantic control, PartField, P3-SAM, and SAMPart3D
rely strictly on geometric boundary priors. Find3D uses
text but lacks clean global assignments, while SegviGen’s
single-view guidance is vulnerable to occlusions. In contrast,
our global text-driven approach inherently resolves these
occlusions. Furthermore, we evaluate an automated setting
(FLEXPART-Auto) that requires minimal user effort. Users

5. Conclusion
We advance the user-centric principle for 3D part segmenta-
tion: what you ask for is what you get. We present FLEX-
PART, a unified text-controlled framework that overcomes
two core obstacles: the lack of multi-granularity data and
the absence of flexible, intent-driven control. We introduce a
holistic prompt to dynamically define the target label space.
A large-scale, VLM-annotated dataset supports this design,
enabling text-guided segmentation. Generating a native 3D
part-identity field conditioned on these prompts, combined
with randomized background dropping, ensures the model
effectively follows semantic instructions rather than rigid
geometric priors. Extensive experiments demonstrate FLEX-
PART’s significant superiority over strong baselines in both
multi-granularity and selective segmentation tasks.
Limitations and future work. Fig. 8 illustrates limitations
in resolving part boundaries and distinguishing similar parts.
Location clauses may not uniquely identify repeated or sym-
metric components, merging two of a ship’s five sails, and
overlapping descriptions could leave the boundary between
adjacent parts ambiguous, as between a knight’s legs and leg
armour. Even when the model recovers the intended regions,
it could assign their identifiers incorrectly, as reflected in the
-direct results in Tab. 1 and Tab. 2. A promising direction is
8


an agentic workflow in which a language model and the user
iteratively refine the prompt after inspecting the returned
partition.

A.1. Annotation pipeline
This appendix describes the pipeline introduced in Sec. 3.3, in the same three stages: the partitions, the text annotation of each
part, and the planner that replaces the annotation at inference.
Stage 1: partitions.
We take the artist-authored components of each asset as atomic units, since an artist has already split
the mesh where a part boundary belongs, and the stage decides only which components belong together. Every component is
marked across the sixteen views (Fig. 9) and one vision-language call returns all three levels, with the component IDs as its
vocabulary:
The 3D asset has {n} parts (numbered 0 to {n-1}).
Produce THREE levels of semantic clustering in one pass:
COARSE -- top-level functional assemblies: each group is a complete,
independently meaningful sub-system (e.g. "wheels", "left arm").
Reference range: ~3-7 groups; let the object’s structure decide.
MIDDLE -- natural subdivisions within each coarse group. Separate
left/right symmetric pairs and functionally distinct sub-parts.
If a coarse group is a single atomic part, its medium entry may be
identical. Reference range: ~7-15 groups.
FINE
-- every semantically distinct part, with precise names.
Reference range: ~12-20 groups.
Every part ID must appear in exactly one group at each level.
Return ONLY this JSON:
{
"object_type": "brief description of the asset",
"coarse": [{"name": "name", "part_ids": [0, 1]}, ...],
"medium": [{"name": "name", "part_ids": [0]},
...],
"fine":
[{"name": "name", "part_ids": [0]},
...]
}
The three levels are requested independently, so a coarse group is the functional grouping an artist would ask for and need
not be a union of the fine groups. A level identical to another level is dropped, so an object yields up to three partitions. Each
surviving level is one segmentation target: the part_ids of its k-th group become label k on the faces of those components.

Consistent location clauses.
A clause that says “left” is only meaningful if the object has a front to be left of, and if the
annotator agrees with the reader about where that front is. Turntable renderings make this easy to get wrong: the same part is
on the left in one view and on the right in another. We therefore estimate, once per object, whether it has a well-defined front,
using Orient-Anything [34] over the same multi-view rig the planner sees at inference. That decision fixes which vocabulary
the SpatialRelation field may use:
13


directional
: You may use global directions -- left, right, front, back --
which are fixed for this object across all views.
non-directional: Use only relative terms: above, below, between, or adjacent
to a named part.
A directional object thus keeps one handedness across all sixteen views, and a symmetric one, a vase for instance, is
described by relations among its own parts.
A.2. Additional ablation studies
Multi-granularity training.
To evaluate the importance of multi-granularity training for semantics-aware control, we

performs poorly on coarse and medium granularities. By contrast, multi-granularity training significantly improves performance
across all semantic levels—raising the average mIoU from 0.708 to 0.845. This clearly indicates that multi-granularity data is
essential for empowering the model with robust, intent-driven segmentation capabilities.

A.3. Implementation details
Architecture.
The denoiser is an MM-DiT with 12 double-stream and 24 single-stream blocks, hidden size 1536 and 12
heads. Text is encoded by a frozen Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct [25], whose last hidden state is projected from 2560 to 1536 by
a trainable RMSNorm and linear layer under a 1024-token budget. The part-ID field is encoded by the part-aware VAE of
Sec. 3.2 at a resolution of 128, and geometry and prompt tokens are indexed in one sequence by the rotary encoding of Sec. 3.2.
Text is dropped for 10% of training samples, which provides the unconditional branch for classifier-free guidance.
Training.
AdamW, base learning rate 10−4 with β = (0.9, 0.99), ϵ = 10−6 and weight decay 10−2, a cosine schedule with
1,000 warmup steps, gradient clipping at 0.5, in fp32. The text projection trains at 5 × 10−5 while the denoiser trains at 10−4.
We train for 125K steps. At inference we use the flow-matching Euler sampler with 30 steps and guidance scale 6.0.
Prompt perturbations.
Sec. 3.3 introduces drop-to-background; a second perturbation is applied alongside it, and both act
on the prompt. Drop-to-background is applied to every training sample, dropping a fraction of the items drawn uniformly up
to 0.65 and reassigning their faces to bg. Part-list shuffling permutes the items and relabels the target accordingly, so an index
denotes the item’s position in the prompt list.

What the planner sees.
The planner is Qwen3.8 [26], held frozen; it is a separate model from the Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct
that encodes the prompt inside FLEXPART. Its visual input is the geometry alone, sixteen turntable renderings at 5122 of the
bare mesh under a neutral matcap shading (Fig. 11).
It is used in two regimes, differing in what the user supplies. When the user states the part names, as in the FLEXPART-Auto
row of Tab. 1 and the planner row of Tab. 3, the planner completes each name into a full item by writing its location and shape
clauses, in the field order the annotation stage of App. A.1 would have produced, which FLEXPART consumes unchanged; the
number of parts is then whatever the user listed. For a selective request it also appends the background items covering the
remainder of the surface, which the user does not enumerate. When no part names are specified, as for the generated assets
of Fig. 7 where no ground-truth decomposition exists, the planner receives only the renderings and a target granularity and
decides the inventory itself, so it is not told how many parts to return.

A.4. Additional experiments
Benchmark construction.
Sec. 4.1 names the three evaluation sets; this paragraph says how each was built.
For
PartObjaverse-Tiny we follow the watertight protocol of P3-SAM [20], which introduces a watertight version of this
benchmark and reports baselines on it: every asset is converted once, each new face taking the label of its nearest original face,
and that mesh is the only geometry any method sees. It is the stricter setting, since the original connected components correlate
with the parts and can be exploited whereas a watertight surface has neither connectivity nor color. On this benchmark our
pipeline supplies text only: the part decomposition released with PartObjaverse-Tiny is used without modification, and every
ground-truth region in Tab. 1 and Tab. 8 is a set of its original parts, so no part boundary is of our making. What our pipeline
adds is the object caption and the name, location and shape clauses each region is referred to by, which the original release
does not provide and which text-conditioned segmentation requires. Selective-190 is built by hand from 28 FLEXPARTBENCH
objects: for each case we choose a subset of the annotated parts as the named items and assign the remainder to background,
so the ground truth is the benchmark’s own annotation restricted to that subset rather than a new labelling. The number of
named parts per case runs from two to eighteen, concentrated on the even values between two and ten. FLEXPARTBENCH
itself is described below.
Constructing FLEXPARTBENCH.
FLEXPARTBENCH is assembled in four stages. We first shortlist candidates from
PartNeXt and PartVerse-XL, scoring each asset on whether its artist-authored components support three distinct levels, and
sample 400 objects in the category proportions of the source libraries. Sampling targets the fine part count as well: most objects
are asked to land between 20 and 28 fine parts, with a capped tail above that, so the benchmark is not dominated by either
trivial or extreme decompositions. Second, every asset passes the grouping and annotation pipeline of App. A.1, and each of its
three levels is checked automatically against a granularity contract: the three counts must strictly increase, the three partitions
must be pairwise distinct, and levels with excessive singleton groups, a dominant catch-all group, or micro-fragmented fine
partitions are rejected. Because the contract requires all three levels to survive, every object kept in FLEXPARTBENCH carries
exactly three partitions, whereas a training object yields up to three once duplicate levels are dropped (Sec. 3.3). No asset
carried a hard error at this stage; the automatic checks instead flagged 58 of the 400 as borderline for a human to read.
Third, and this is what the benchmark rests on, the authors reviewed the annotations by hand — every borderline case, and
the accepted ones by sampling — against the rendered partitions rather than the text alone. We removed 23 objects outright: 21
whose source geometry or component labelling was itself defective, which no annotation could repair, and 2 more the reviewer

A.5. Does the model use semantics beyond part count?
A potential shortcut is that the model might infer the desired granularity solely from the number of prompt items, K, while
ignoring their actual semantic content. To investigate this, we evaluate on the PartObjaverse-Tiny benchmark (Sec. 4.1), strictly
controlling for input geometry, target partition, K, model checkpoint, sampler, and random seed. Using the FLEXPART-GT
results from Tab. 1 as our full-prompt baseline, we compare the original control prompt for each object against two alternatives:
(1) a list of K generic placeholders, and (2) a mismatched prompt sampled from a fixed derangement of genuine annotations
with the same K. For the mismatched prompts, we replace the entire text—including the object caption, part names, location
clauses, and shape clauses—while keeping K unchanged. This ensures the prompt retains a natural linguistic structure and
annotation format, confirming that any observed effects are not merely artifacts of an unusual placeholder format. For instance,
a three-item prompt for an orc figurine is replaced by a genuine three-item sailboat prompt. For pools containing only a
single prompt of length K, we borrow genuine same-K text from another benchmark partition where possible; for the single
global-maximum K, we apply a fixed cyclic permutation to its item descriptions. Excluding this specific edge case does not
alter our overall conclusions. Finally, we aggregate the evaluation metrics per object and report the average across all objects.
Removing semantic content causes a large drop even though K is unchanged. More importantly, a genuine but mismatched
same-K prompt also reduces mIoU substantially. The predicted partition therefore depends on the requested semantic content
rather than acting only as a geometrically plausible K-way decomposition. This test establishes sensitivity to semantic
conditioning; it does not evaluate a second, independently valid decomposition of the same object.

A.6. Comparison with CubePart
CubePart [51] conditions a generative model on an open-vocabulary part schema. Its second stage can decompose an existing
mesh, which makes it the closest schema-conditioned method to our setting, and its code is released. A quantitative comparison
under our metric is nonetheless not available: CubePart synthesizes a separate mesh for each requested part rather than
labelling the faces of the input, so there is no per-face prediction to score without first projecting its output back onto the input
surface, a step of our choosing rather than theirs. Fig. 12 therefore compares the two visually, on the same watertight mesh
and with semantically matched part names. Each method receives prompts in its required format, at both coarse and fine
granularities.
CubePart represents each requested item with its own latent and generates its geometry separately. The number of generated
latent groups therefore grows with the schema, and the resulting part meshes must jointly reconstruct the input object. In these
examples, that reconstruction becomes less stable as the requested decomposition becomes more detailed. On the second
object the coarse schema names both arms and both legs as separate parts, yet CubePart’s generated leg surface absorbs the
arms, and at the fine schema the right hand comes back as a fragment. The third object shows the same trend at the scale our
benchmark asks for: the coarse request returns a clean decomposition, while the detailed request produces heavily fragmented
part geometry despite passing CubePart’s own validity check.
We treat these three objects as case studies rather than an estimate of CubePart’s overall failure rate. The displayed outputs
show missing surface regions, geometry extending beyond the input shape, and parts breaking into disconnected fragments.
Our formulation avoids this geometry-generation failure mode by retaining the input surface and predicting only its face labels.
A.7. Broader impact
Positive impact.
An explicit part decomposition is what part-aware workflows need: localized editing, rigging and retopology,
and semantic reuse of an asset’s components. Because the control signal is text, a user obtains one by naming the parts, and a
frozen vision-language model can write the prompt from renderings, which puts the capability within reach for education, indie
development and rapid prototyping. We label the faces of the input mesh to obtain a part decomposition of the user’s asset.
Negative impact and mitigation.
Like any generative 3D model, FLEXPART can be misused, for instance to decompose
and repurpose a copyrighted asset without attribution. It also inherits the biases of its training data, which over-represents
common object categories and the design conventions of the artists who built them, so both its part vocabulary and its notion
of what counts as one part are skewed towards those categories. We train only on license-compliant data, we recommend that
downstream users attribute reused assets and check outputs for intellectual-property concerns before deployment, and we do
not intend the method for identity-related or surveillance applications.
