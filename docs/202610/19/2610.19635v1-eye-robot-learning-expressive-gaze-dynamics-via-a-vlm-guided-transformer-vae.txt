Eye, Robot: Learning Expressive Gaze Dynamics via
a VLM-Guided Transformer-VAE
Yuhang Hu1,*, Junlin Lyu1,*,†, Yuan Fang1,
Xiaoying Shen1, Lechen Zhang1, Jiong Lin1,
Judah Goldfeder1, Steven Tian1, Hod Lipson1,†
1Creative Machines Lab, Department of Mechanical Engineering, Columbia University
* Equal contribution.
Abstract:
Gaze coordinates attention in human interaction through coupled eye, head, and
eyelid motion. A humanoid robot must choose where to look while generating a
suitable movement toward that target; fixed tracking rules alone do not model the
temporal structure of human gaze. We separate these tasks with an asynchronous
vision-language model that selects a scene referent, a reactive perception module
that maintains fixation, and a Realistic Gaze Transformer (RGT) that generates
target-conditioned motion. RGT is a Transformer-VAE trained on markerless hu-
man recordings retargeted to the robot’s 11-DoF joint space: self-supervised pre-
training encodes recent full-head history, and autoregressive fine-tuning exposes
the model to its own predictions during rollout. At inference, a four-dimensional
binocular-eye trajectory conditions coordinated neck and eye motion; an auxiliary
blink predictor triggers eyelid templates during playback. On a two-second bench-
mark in which all controllers receive the same ground-truth target, RGT-Base ob-
tains the lowest reported overall seven-joint MSE, and physical demonstrations
show the integrated targeting and control pipeline. The evidence establishes robot-
space kinematic fidelity and real-time RGT inference; perceived naturalness and
open-vocabulary target-selection accuracy remain questions for controlled evalu-
ation.
Keywords: Robot gaze, motion generation, human-robot interaction

Introduction
Gaze is a fundamental channel of nonverbal communication, regulating attention, conversational
turn-taking, intimacy, and emotional signaling [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Appropriate eye contact has further
been shown to increase perceived trustworthiness [7]. In Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), embodi-
ment allows robots to emulate these social cues through physical motion [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13], which
makes faithfully reproducing human gaze both a substantial challenge and a promising path toward
more natural interaction [14].
Scripted eye and head controllers provide a useful starting point, but fixed rules have limited flexibil-
ity across targets and motion styles [15, 16, 17]. Gaze also influences first impressions in social inter-
action [18, 19], making the timing of robot movements worth studying alongside endpoint tracking.
We therefore ask whether a prior learned from human motion can improve joint-space reproduction
under target-conditioned, closed-loop robot control (Fig. 1). The framework separates a deliber-
ative decision about the attended referent from the frame-rate generation of motor commands. A
VLM selects a referent asynchronously, a reactive perception module maintains its fixation between

target-conditioned Transformer-VAE with a latent bottleneck and autoregressive fine-tuning for co-

every controller with the same ground-truth eye target and scores seven neck/eye channels; physical
demonstrations show the integrated targeting pipeline. Code and simulation materials are linked at

Related Work
Learning on Humanoid Facial Robots. Early facial robots relied on hand-tuned, FACS-based
presets [9, 20], which scale poorly and feel mechanical.
Recent work shifts to learning-based
control across facial affect: self-supervised mimicry from motor babbling [13], expression genera-
tion guided by recognition networks [21] or human preferences [22], anticipatory smile coexpres-
sion [12], and multilingual lip synchronization [23]. Gaze, a primary channel for attention and social
engagement, has received less attention within this learning paradigm. Our setting specifically mod-
els synchronized robot-coordinate neck, binocular-eye, and eyelid trajectories with a target supplied
after the latent bottleneck.
Robot Gaze Control. Early systems scripted eye and head movements by hand to mimic human
attention patterns [15, 16]. Recognizing the limits of this approach, later work turned to bio-inspired
and model-based strategies—such as smooth pursuit, saccades, and joint attention—to coordinate

eye and head motion for more lifelike behavior [24, 25, 26, 17], and Saran et al. [27] used a monoc-
ular deep-learning framework for real-time gaze following. Despite this progress, realistic gaze
remains an open challenge, which motivates control that learns directly from human motion.

Representation Learning for Motion.
Contrastive methods [28, 29] and Temporal Convolu-
tional Networks [30] extract robust spatiotemporal embeddings that generalize to downstream mo-
tion tasks. Generative approaches advance this further: VAE and Transformer-VAE frameworks
[31, 32, 33, 34], and more recently diffusion models [35, 36, 37], enable controllable and physically
plausible motion synthesis, ranging from gaze-conditioned prediction to text-conditioned human
motion generation.

Methods
We address the problem of generating realistic, expressive gaze behavior for a humanoid face robot.
The platform is the soft-skinned humanoid face introduced by Hu [23], whose actuated facial mech-
anism we repurpose for gaze: in our configuration, 11 degrees of freedom (DoF) govern neck,
eyeball, and eyelid motion, driven in real time (see Appendix A.2). Central to our formulation is
a decoupling of high-level cognitive reasoning from low-level motor control: an upstream Vision-
Language Model determines where to attend, producing a target gaze trajectory, while the learned
action head is responsible for how that target is realized as lifelike full-head motion. Given a dataset
of human gaze motion sequences, each training sample consists of a history of full-head motion
Xt ∈Rp×11 over p past frames (neck, eyeballs, eyelids) and a future eyeball trajectory Ct ∈Rf×4
over f future frames. We learn a Transformer-VAE in which an encoder fθ maps Xt to a latent
code z ∈R64 parameterizing the posterior qθ(z | Xt), and a decoder gϕ jointly predicts the fu-
ture full-head motion ˆYt ∈Rf×11 and per-frame blink probabilities ˆPt ∈[0, 1]f from z and Ct:
ˆYt, ˆPt
= gϕ(fθ(Xt), Ct). The objective is to track Ct while preserving the temporal structure

that drive the robot’s head, eyes, and eyelids (Appendix A.2). The remainder of this section formal-

used to train the action head (Sec. 3.2, Sec. 3.3), and the Realistic Gaze Transformer with its training
procedure (Sec. 3.4, Sec. 3.5).
3.1
VLM-Guided Gaze Targeting
To drive the action head, we need a target eyeball trajectory Ct ∈Rf×4 that specifies where the
robot should look. This trajectory is produced by a two-stage pipeline inspired by human attention.
The first stage is a deliberative module built on a Vision-Language Model (we adopt Gemma 3 [38],
though the design is agnostic to the specific VLM): given a scene image It and a natural-language
instruction ℓ(e.g., “Notice the man in the white shirt”), prompt engineering constrains its output to
a structured referent rt = VLM(It, ℓ) describing the attended target and a rough region of interest.
The second stage is a reactive perception module (we use FastSAM [39]) that refines rt into a
frame-rate fixation ut ∈R2, maintaining the target between successive VLM calls. We project ut
into the robot’s eyeball frame to obtain an instantaneous gaze direction, which the eyeball trajectory
generator unrolls into the conditioning signal Ct for the RGT (Sec. 3.3). Prompt templates and
model-selection details are provided in Appendix A.3.
3.2
Data Preprocessing
The markerless, webcam-based facial motion data (see Appendix A.4) comprises timestamped se-
quences with 11-dimensional features per frame, including head orientation (yaw, pitch, roll), binoc-
ular eye gaze directions (yaw and pitch for each eye), and upper and lower eyelid motions (pitch for
each eyelid). First, abrupt spikes in the eye gaze channels are identified via threshold-based peak
detection and corrected by interpolating across the affected intervals. Second, high-frequency noise
is reduced using signal-specific low-pass filtering techniques. Blink labels Pt are extracted from the
upper eyelid signal using inverted peak detection. We generate both binary and soft labels, where
the latter are obtained by convolving discrete blink events with a Gaussian kernel to provide smooth
temporal supervision during training. All channels are resampled to a 60-Hz robot-command grid
before denoising; eye and eyelid artifacts are handled separately (Appendix A.5 and A.6). The tra-
jectories are retargeted to robot coordinates, including approximately ±30◦eye yaw and ±20◦eye
pitch. They are a shared kinematic reference, not optical-motion-capture ground truth.
3.3
Eyeball-Trajectory Generator
At both training and test time, we require a 4-D eyeball-condition signal Ct = [ct; ct+1; . . . ]⊤∈
Rf×4 that specifies where the robot should look.
When the processed human trajectories
are available (pre-training and fine-tuning) we take the ground-truth binocular gaze directions
(yawR, pitchR, yawL, pitchL) as Ct. During the evaluation of the real robot, Ct is synthesized online
by the condition generator in Appendix A.8. This procedure provides task-directed eye commands
while the RGT predicts coordinated motion and blink timing; the auxiliary template path realizes
blink eyelid playback (Appendix A.6). Optional stochastic eye-command perturbations are applied
only to the procedural condition stream at deployment, not to the ground-truth conditions used in
Table 1.
3.4
Realistic Gaze Transformer (RGT)
Our objective is to synthesize human-like gaze that remains controllable by a task-level eyeball
trajectory while preserving the rich micro-dynamics of natural head–eye coordination. To this end,
we introduce a Transformer-VAE (Fig. 2) whose self-attention layers capture the delayed, multi-
scale coupling between eyes, head, and eyelids. The motion history Xt is compressed into a latent
vector z that parameterizes a low-dimensional manifold of plausible human gaze dynamics. Because
the decoder has no skip connection to Xt, all information about the recent past must pass through z,
4


forcing it to summarize both kinematic state—inertia, head overshoot, microsaccadic patterns—and
motion style. At inference, conditioning on z biases generation toward this manifold. Stochastic
sampling of z yields different realizations of the same target Ct. Since Ct enters after the latent
bottleneck, the model cleanly separates where the robot looks (Ct) from how the movement unfolds
(z). We measure the resulting rollout diversity in the bottleneck ablation rather than treating it as a
perceptual score.
Model Architecture
Figure 2 depicts the Realistic Gaze Transformer (RGT), a Transformer-
VAE comprising three functional blocks: (i) a motion-history encoder, (ii) a latent-conditioned de-
coder, and (iii) an auxiliary blink head. Each training sample pairs a fixed-length history window
Xt ∈Rp×11 (neck, eyeballs, eyelids) with a future eyeball trajectory Ct ∈Rf×4. A linear layer
projects the 11-D face vector to a dmodel = 128 embedding, followed by a sine–cosine positional
encoding; the same procedure, with a dedicated weight matrix, is applied to the 4-D eye condition.
A 3-layer Transformer encoder with dff = 512 processes the embedded history—we found this
depth sufficient to capture eye–head coupling on the limited markerless recording set. The resulting
sequence h ∈Rp×128 is flattened and passed through a small aggregation MLP. With p = 60, all
ordered history tokens contribute to its 7,680-dimensional input; the representation retains their tem-
poral positions instead of reading only the final frame. Two linear heads then produce the posterior
parameters µ, log σ2 ∈R64. We then sample z ∈R64 via the reparameterization trick, linearly ex-
pand it to z1:f ∈Rf×128, and add it element-wise to the eye-condition embeddings, injecting style
information at every decoder position. A 4-layer Transformer decoder with dff = 512 processes the
combined tokens, after which two task-specific heads predict future facial motion ˆYt ∈Rf×11 and
blink probabilities ˆPt ∈[0, 1]f (MLP with ReLU and Sigmoid activations).
3.5
Model Training Procedure
We train the Realistic Gaze Transformer (RGT) on S1 in two stages: (1) self-supervised pre-training,
which learns a compact manifold of natural head–eye dynamics, and (2) autoregressive fine-tuning,
which adds long-horizon roll-out stability while tracking task-level eyeball commands. To eliminate
temporal leakage, each raw sequence is first split chronologically (80% train, 20% validation), and
only then are stride-1 sliding windows extracted within each partition.
Stage 1 – Self-supervised pre-training.
We construct overlapping windows with a 60-frame (one-
second) history and a future block of f frames from the normalized markerless data. Each sample
comprises (i) a history segment Xt ∈Rp×11 with p = 60 and (ii) a future eyeball trajectory Ct ∈
Rf×4. In the reported benchmark, f = 3 per model call. The Transformer encoder embeds Xt
and produces a latent posterior; a latent code z ∼N(µ, σ2) is added to the decoder input tokens
together with Ct, yielding reconstructions ˆYt ∈Rf×11 and blink probabilities ˆPt ∈[0, 1]f. Training

on the eye commands Ct+k and the model’s own previous predictions. We accumulate the same
VAE+BCE loss per block and back-propagate their mean. We fine-tune with a 10× smaller learning
rate (lr = 10−4) to preserve the pre-trained latent prior, and again monitor a held-out validation set
with the same early-stopping policy.

Experimental Results
We evaluate RGT against two families of baselines using S1 alone. The first comprises three heuris-
tic, rule-based controllers: Eyes-Only rotates the eyeballs toward the target with the head fixed;
Head-Only turns the neck toward the target with the eyes centered; Eye–Head Coord. drives both
channels with the eyes leading, following fixed coordination and blink heuristics. All three run in
closed loop. Because Eyes-Only and Head-Only are penalized for the joints they leave pinned, we
additionally report each controller’s error over only its actively commanded joints (“w/o fixed”),
isolating tracking quality from the cost of unmoved joints. The second family comprises learned
baselines that isolate the contribution of our architecture: RNN [41] replaces self-attention with
recurrence; VAE-CNN replaces the Transformer with ResNet [42] encoders/decoders; Flow Match-
ing [43] replaces our latent-variable VAE with a continuous-flow generative objective. All learned
baselines are matched to RGT-Base in parameter count and trained under an identical protocol;
RGT-Small and RGT-Large are reported separately as scale ablations. Every controller—rule-based
or learned—receives the same four-dimensional ground-truth eye condition. The benchmark there-
fore isolates motion generation from errors in VLM referent selection and visual tracking. From
chronologically held-out S1 tails, we extract 1,848 clips with 60 history frames and a 120-frame
(2-s) future at stride 10. A learned model predicts three frames per call, feeds back the first frame as
history, and yields 117 scored updates over the seven neck and binocular-eye channels. The separate
eyelid and blink path is not included in this score. RGT and VAE-CNN draw one reparameterized
latent per call; neither uses a posterior mean or best-of-K selection. For N clips, T = 117 scored
updates, and J = 7 joints, the reported joint-angle MSE is

4.3
Ablation Studies
We conduct ablation studies to better understand the contributions of key components in our training
framework, focusing on three factors: (1) the latent bottleneck design, comparing the stochastic
VAE bottleneck against a deterministic autoencoder and a skip-connected variant that bypasses the
bottleneck; (2) the two-stage training schedule, isolating the effects of teacher-forced pre-training
and autoregressive fine-tuning; and (3) the encoder/decoder depth, scaling the transformer backbone
from the base configuration up to larger variants.
We first compare the full RGT against a deterministic autoencoder (z=µ, KL disabled) and a skip-
connected variant whose decoder cross-attends to the encoder outputs, bypassing the bottleneck.
Table 3 reports reconstruction MSE under closed-loop AR rollout alongside the average pairwise
distance (APD) between K=10 stochastic rollouts of the same clip. APD is reported in rad2 in
Table 3; it measures output diversity rather than fidelity or observer preference. The deterministic
AE yields one rollout (APD=0), and the skip variant’s APD is smaller than the no-skip VAE’s. The
no-skip VAE has APD 4.4 × 10−6 rad2, compared with 5.5 × 10−7 rad2 for the skip model, while
their reconstruction MSEs remain of the same order. This is consistent with the decoder relying less
on the latent code when it can bypass the bottleneck, but APD alone does not diagnose posterior
collapse or show that the different rollouts are perceptually plausible. The deterministic variant also
obtains a slightly lower reconstruction error, making the fidelity–diversity tradeoff visible rather than
treating diversity as a free gain. Next, comparing pretraining-only against models further fine-tuned
with AR training (Fig. 4), the fine-tuned models consistently achieve lower prediction errors across
all behavior groups, confirming that AR fine-tuning is critical for reducing compounding errors and
improving long-term motion stability. Finally, we compare three RGT depths under the same recipe:
Small (2 enc, 3 dec, dff=256), Base (3 enc, 4 dec, dff=512), and Large (4 enc, 6 dec, dff=512). The
last three rows of Table 1 show that Base has lower overall MSE than Small or Large in this run; the
table alone does not identify the cause of the larger model’s difference. We therefore adopt Base as
the default RGT.
5
Conclusion
We presented a gaze-control framework that separates semantic target selection from the generation
of eye, head, and eyelid motion on a soft-skinned humanoid face. An asynchronous VLM and a
reactive perception module provide the target; the Realistic Gaze Transformer uses a one-second
motion history and a planned binocular-eye trajectory to generate coordinated 11-DoF commands in
real time. Its Transformer-VAE learns from retargeted human recordings, and autoregressive fine-
tuning addresses the distribution shift caused by feeding predictions back into the controller. Blink
timing is predicted by an auxiliary head and realized through template playback. In the reported
8


ground-truth-target benchmark, RGT-Base has the lowest overall seven-joint MSE among the tested
controllers. Bottleneck and training ablations, together with adaptation on five further subjects,
provide complementary evidence about the motion model. The physical demonstration shows the
targeting and control components working together. These findings motivate a next HRI evaluation
that tests whether people can infer the robot’s attention during handover or collaborative search, as
well as whether they prefer its gaze to alternative controllers.
6
Limitations and Future Work
Several limitations bound the interpretation of these results. The primary benchmark conditions
every controller on the same ground-truth eye target, which isolates how the robot moves but does
not measure whether the VLM picks the intended referent or whether the reactive module maintains
a fixation under occlusion. A controlled target-perturbation sweep and labeled open-vocabulary
grounding cases are needed to assess those errors and their effect on the complete pipeline. The VLM
also operates below the control rate and supplies no explicit policy for when to shift gaze during
turn-taking or mutual-gaze maintenance. Audio and conversational cues, together with feedback
from the human partner’s gaze and expression, could support such decisions; the present system
does not adapt to the partner’s affect in closed loop.
The reported MSE table is one stochastic run without fixed evaluation seeds or confidence inter-
vals. Repeated runs with prespecified seeds would quantify uncertainty, especially for the smaller
gaps among learned models.
MSE measures robot-space kinematic agreement and APD mea-
sures stochastic diversity; neither is an observer rating. Controlled studies are needed to assess
perceived naturalness, attention legibility, preference, and trust. The six-participant, non-clinical
sample and markerless webcam retargeting constrain generalization, including to atypical ocular
kinematics, different gaze conventions, and other robot joint layouts. Per-eye recalibration and ad-
ditional participant-specific adaptation may help, but were not tested here. Blink templates simplify
eyelid playback and do not provide a directly interpretable control over blink style or its relation to
attention. Finally, the scored rollouts last two seconds, leaving longer-interaction drift unmeasured.
Broader deployment should also study possible unwarranted trust or anthropomorphic projection.

A.2
Hardware and Control
Hardware.
We evaluate our method on a humanoid robotic head [23] with 11 degrees of freedom
(DOF), comprising 3 DOFs for the neck (yaw, pitch, and roll), 4 DOFs for the left and right eyes
(yaw and pitch per eye), and 4 DOFs for the upper and lower eyelids of both eyes. For each joint,
we define motion limits that closely resemble the natural constraints of human anatomy to ensure
lifelike articulation. For further details on the robotic platform, we refer the reader to [23].
Control.
The character is actuated using a set of joint torques τ, computed from the control actions
via an actuator model. Given an action input at, we derive the motor torques using a proportional-
derivative (PD) controller:

= KP · (at −qt) −KD · ˙qt,

where · denotes element-wise multiplication, qt is the current joint position, and ˙qt is the joint
velocity. The proportional and derivative gain terms KP and KD are set to predefined values based
on the actuator dynamics. In practice, head and eye movements tend to be rapid, so we use relatively
high values for KP to ensure fast and accurate tracking.
A.3
VLM and Visual Perception Module
Vision Language Model (VLM).
The deliberative stage is a Vision-Language Model (Gemma 3)
that jointly decides where the robot should attend and what, if anything, it should say. Crucially, it
13


is used off-the-shelf: the model is never fine-tuned, and all task-specific behavior is induced purely
through a fixed prompt template, so the same framework drops in any capable VLM without retrain-
ing. This makes the backend interchangeable—we support both a hosted API deployment, which
minimizes on-board compute and latency, and a fully local deployment for network-independent,
on-device operation—behind an identical prompt and output contract. Rather than relying on free-
form generation, we cast target selection as a constrained structured-output task. The template
frames the model as a chatting face robot and presents it with two parallel input streams at every
call: a VISUAL stream—the egocentric image It from the eye-embedded cameras—and an AUDIO
stream—a possibly-empty transcript ℓproduced by the streaming speech-recognition front end. The
system instruction then specifies the task and the exact output contract:
You are a chatting face robot. You receive two parallel streams: (1) VISUAL, an image;
(2) AUDIO, a transcript (possibly empty). Choose the single object or person in the scene
that deserves the most attention given both streams and return its bounding box as [ymin,
xmin, ymax, xmax] normalized to [0, 1000]; if the target is a person, return the box
of the person’s face. If AUDIO is non-empty, additionally generate a spoken response
(≤10 s); if AUDIO is empty, omit the speech field. Output only JSON.
This template enforces three properties that the rest of the pipeline depends on. First, the output
is a single JSON object with a bounding-box field and an optional speech field, which we ob-
tain reliably by requesting a JSON response type from the model; the structured referent rt parsed
from this object is precisely the rough region of interest consumed by the downstream perception
module. Second, the normalized [0, 1000] box convention decouples the prompt from the camera
resolution, so the same template transfers across the two eye cameras; we rescale the returned box
to pixels before projecting it into the eyeball frame. Third, instructing the model to return the face
box for people—rather than the full body—anchors the eventual fixation on the eyes and preserves
the realism of mutual gaze. Because such reasoning is comparatively expensive, the VLM runs
asynchronously and far below the 60 Hz control rate; the reactive perception module sustains the
fixation between successive calls, and the controller always consumes only the most recent VLM
result, discarding stale frames to bound latency. Unifying target selection and the verbal response
within this single templated call lets one scene understanding drive both gaze and speech, keeping
the robot’s attention and dialogue mutually consistent.
Visual Perception Module.
The robot carries two eye-embedded cameras with different focal
lengths: the left-eye camera is optimized for close-range perception and the right-eye camera for
long-range focus, reliably resolving objects at roughly one meter. The reactive perception module
of Sec. 3.1 operates on these streams, refining the VLM’s structured referent rt into a frame-rate
fixation ut ∈R2 and maintaining it between successive VLM calls.
We use FastSAM [39], the real-time variant of the Segment Anything Model, as this module; the
choice follows directly from our two-stage design. Because the upstream VLM selects targets by
language and context, the set of admissible gaze targets is effectively open—any person, object, or
region the VLM can designate—so the downstream localizer must not re-impose a fixed taxonomy.
A class-agnostic, promptable segmenter preserves this open-endedness: the rough region of inter-
est in rt is passed directly as a spatial prompt, and FastSAM returns a pixel-accurate mask of the
designated target, whose centroid we take as ut and project into the eyeball frame.
In contrast, the detector paradigms commonly used for robot perception fit this role poorly. Closed-
set detectors such as YOLO [44] are confined to a fixed label set and would bottleneck the VLM’s
open-vocabulary targeting; their bounding-box centers are also only coarse aim points, falling on the
torso rather than the face for a standing person, or off the object entirely when it is elongated or par-
tially occluded—both of which undermine the realism of eye contact. Category-specific landmark
models such as MediaPipe [45] yield precise points, but only for faces, hands, and body, and cannot
localize arbitrary referents. FastSAM retains the open-set, prompt-conditioned behavior of SAM
while running far faster than its ViT-based predecessor, placing class-agnostic segmentation com-
fortably within the frame-rate budget required to sustain a fixation between VLM calls and thereby
preserve the perceived liveness of the robot’s gaze.

A.4
Dataset Collection, Description and Representation
Our collection pipeline is markerless and uses a commodity webcam. Participants were recruited
for a non-clinical feasibility study. We first record raw RGB video of a seated participant and then
process every frame offline with the MediaPipe Face Landmarker [45], run in video mode with face-
blendshape and facial-transformation-matrix outputs enabled. The 11-DoF target representation is
read out directly from these outputs. Head orientation (yaw, pitch, roll) is recovered as Euler an-
gles from the 4 × 4 facial transformation matrix. Per-eye gaze is obtained from the directional eye
blendshapes—horizontal gaze from the difference of the eyeLookIn and eyeLookOut scores and
vertical gaze from eyeLookUp minus eyeLookDown, computed independently for each eye—and
linearly scaled to anatomically plausible ranges (±30◦yaw, ±20◦pitch). Upper-eyelid aperture
is mapped from the eyeBlink blendshape and lower-eyelid motion from eyeSquint, each cali-
brated to the robot’s eyelid travel. Each frame is timestamped, so the subsequent resampling stage
(Sec. A.5) can interpolate onto a uniform 60 FPS grid regardless of capture rate. Because the entire
pipeline relies only on pretrained MediaPipe models, new subjects or behaviors can be added with-
out any specialized motion-capture hardware. Details of the motion extraction process are provided
in the later section A.5.

A.6
Blink Template Extraction and Synthesis
Blink events are sparse relative to non-blink frames, which motivates a separate timing and playback
path in addition to the reconstruction loss. We therefore adopt a two–step strategy: (1) offline
extraction of blink templates from the markerless recording set, and (2) online synthesis that injects
these templates at run-time according to the blink–probability head of our model.
Template extraction.
A blink is deemed strong when the signal crosses a fixed threshold of
−0.8 (normalised units) and forms a local minimum separated from neighbouring candidates by
at least 30 frames (≃0.5 s).Around every detected centre we crop a 20-frame window (± 166
ms) comprising all four eyelid channels, yielding a tensor T ∈RN×20×4 of blink templates
(“open →closed →open”). Across six subjects we obtain N = 1 ,073 valid blinks. This corpus
serves two purposes: (i) supervision for the auxiliary blink head, and (ii) a library for synthesis.

Probabilistic blink synthesis.
At inference time RGT outputs a per-frame blink probability ˆPt ∈
[0, 1]. A blink is triggered when ˆPt exceeds 0.72, subject to a refractory period of 100 frames
(∼1.7 s) that prevents unnaturally rapid blinking. Once triggered, we draw a random template Tk
from the library and play it back frame by frame over its 20-frame window, substituting the four
template eyelid channels directly for the network’s eyelid outputs,

Observation 1: Eye–Head Co-movement
Prior human motor-control studies report coupling be-

among processed head, eye, and eyelid trajectories using Pearson correlation ρ. Figure 5a shows an
eye–head correlation of ρ = 0.86 for the reported yaw/pitch comparison. These descriptive corre-
lations characterize the collected kinematics; temporal autocorrelation means a naive independent-
frame significance test would be inappropriate.

and joint-limit checks.
Outcome.
The generator outputs a continuous stream of 4-D ocular commands with < 1 ms la-
tency, leaving all head and eyelid coordination to the RGT decoder. This division of labour keeps
the control stack simple and allows RGT to focus entirely on synthesising lifelike full-head motion
given a task-directed, but otherwise non-parametric, eye trajectory.
