Abstract
World-action models jointly predict actions and future world states, but long-horizon manipulation further requires language rea-
soning to plan subtask objectives, assess execution progress, and guide action selection. We present LAW, a closed-loop VLM–
WAM framework that unifies language reasoning, action generation, and world modeling through semantic subtask progress.
Within the LAW agent, a hybrid-expert vision-language model plans the remaining subtasks, estimates the values of and fuses ac-
tions proposed by a world-action model, and asynchronously assesses execution status to support continued execution and subtask
transitions. Within the world-action model, Mixture-of-Transformer Experts integrates modality-specific and shared computa-
tion for joint video–action modeling. Dual-stream hierarchical visual memory maintains temporal context for both models, while
subtask-local value learning aligns action evaluation with semantic objectives and preserves supervision from successful prefixes
of failed trajectories. LAW achieves 94.21% average success across 50 RoboTwin tasks. Replacing the WAM backbone with
OpenWAM-α further raises the average success rate to 95.56%, establishing a new open-source state of the art on RoboTwin.
Despite having only 2B parameters, our VLM also outperforms variants using Embodied-R1.5 and GPT-5.5 High. On three long-
horizon real-robot tasks, LAW achieves 65% average success, compared with 28.33% for π0.5 and 25% for Fast-WAM.

1. Introduction
Long-horizon manipulation is a sequence of semantic sub-
tasks, not merely a longer sequence of motor commands.
After reaching for an object, a robot must decide whether
the grasp actually succeeded; after a successful grasp, it
must recognize that the task has entered a new stage; before
executing the next motion, it must choose among several
physically plausible actions according to the current objec-
tive [22, 23]. Errors in any of these decisions compound
over time. Consequently, local action quality alone does
not determine task success. A capable agent must maintain
a persistent account of task progress, connect that account
to action generation, and revise its decisions when execu-
tion departs from expectation [35, 44].
Vision-language-action policies connect semantic repre-
sentations to control [8, 25, 60], while world-action mod-
els learn action-conditioned scene evolution and executable
behavior [27, 51, 57, 59]. These models substantially im-
prove action generation, but long-horizon deployment ex-
poses a complementary systems problem: reasoning and
control must remain aligned as the task evolves. A lan-
guage plan generated once at the beginning cannot deter-
mine whether its intermediate steps were completed [22],
and an action generator conditioned only on the current ob-
servation may not know which visual changes matter to the
larger goal [41]. Placing a planner before a policy there-
fore leaves three interfaces underspecified: how execution
progress updates the plan, how candidate actions are judged
against the current semantic objective, and how relevant his-
tory is retained without overwhelming either model.
Our premise is that semantic subtask progress should
serve as the temporal interface between language reason-
ing and world-action modeling. Subtasks provide more than
a decomposition of the instruction: they define when the
agent should advance its plan, which outcomes a candidate
action should optimize, and which observations should re-
main salient in memory.
This view reveals that compu-
tations occur at different rates.
State assessment should
run continuously without blocking control, candidate eval-

We instantiate this principle in LAW, a closed-loop

on the current subtask, the WAM generates diverse action
chunks; the value expert ranks and fuses them; and exe-
cution status is assessed asynchronously to determine sub-
task completion. Once the current subtask is completed,
the planner predicts the next subtask and the agent switches
to it; otherwise, the agent continues executing the current
subtask. The LAW agent coordinates planning, action se-
lection, and execution monitoring at different frequencies,
using feedback to continue or switch subtasks.
To strengthen joint world and action modeling, we in-
troduce Mixture-of-Transformer Experts (MoTE) into the
WAM, yielding MoTE-WAM. MoT projects video and
action features into a common attention space to ex-
change cross-modal information, but their subsequent trans-

MoTE introduces

The

strengthening video–action joint modeling beyond informa-

tions. Separate hierarchical memory streams for the VLM
and WAM retain recent observations and full-resolution

2. Related Work
2.1. Vision-Language-Action Models for Robot
Control
Pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) provide se-
mantic representations that vision-language-action (VLA)
policies connect to executable control [8, 14, 25, 28,
60], supported by heterogeneous robot datasets and gen-
eralist pretraining [17, 39].
RT-2 and OpenVLA rep-
resent actions as tokens, whereas π0 couples a VLM
with a flow-matching expert for continuous action genera-
tion [8, 25, 60]. Beyond action parameterization, CoT-VLA
predicts intermediate visual subgoals [54], while VEGA
aligns visual-encoder outputs with geometry-aware fea-
tures before vision-language fusion [45]. These approaches
strengthen semantic transfer and spatial grounding, mo-
tivating complementary mechanisms for coordinating in-
structions, execution progress, and action selection over ex-
tended tasks.
Language-guided systems connect reasoning to execu-
tion through affordance-based skill selection, environmen-
tal feedback, action hierarchies, and spatial value maps [4,
21–23]. SayCan grounds language plans in learned affor-
dances, whereas VoxPoser translates language-specified ob-
jectives and constraints into spatial maps for trajectory op-
timization [21, 23]. In navigation, NavCoT structures de-
cisions through future imagination and observation match-
ing [33], while Memoir retrieves environmental and behav-
ioral experience using imagined states [47]. Although nav-
igation differs from continuous manipulation, these studies
illustrate how intermediate reasoning and selective mem-
ory access support sequential decisions. For long-horizon
robot policies, the corresponding challenge is to retain task-
relevant history and execution feedback when semantic
stages extend beyond individual action chunks [41, 44].
2.2. World Action Models and Video-Based Policy
World models support control through action-conditioned
visual prediction or latent dynamics [1, 3, 15, 19, 58], while
video generators also enable synthetic robot training tra-
jectories [24]. Visual Foresight optimizes actions through
predicted observations, whereas Dreamer learns behavior
through latent imagination, providing two complementary
uses of predictive dynamics [15, 19]. Recent world-action
models (WAMs) connect future visual modeling with exe-
cutable action generation through joint diffusion or future-
latent alignment [27, 30, 32, 51, 53, 57, 59].
Their de-
signs differ in how predictive information is used during de-
ployment: LingBot-VA combines causal video–action mod-
eling with observation feedback and asynchronous execu-
tion [27], whereas Fast-WAM retains video co-training but
omits test-time future prediction [51]. WAM4D further in-
troduces geometric supervision through spatial register to-
kens and modality-specific causal attention [29]. These de-
velopments distinguish predictive supervision benefits from
the cost of generating future observations online.
Reliable world-model-based control also requires faith-
ful action consequences and informative evaluation sig-
nals.
WorldEcho diagnoses failures to follow off-expert
actions, and WorldSync improves action-conditioned gen-
eration through broader coverage and explicit action-effect
alignment [11]. Cosmos Policy jointly generates actions,

Mixed-Frequency Coordination The three experts and
the WAM operate at different frequencies, as illustrated in
Fig. 4. The planner is invoked at episode initialization and
near subtask boundaries to produce or update the remaining

60 ms and runs concurrently with action execution, enabling
non-blocking completion assessment on the latest observa-

3.3. Joint Video–Action Modeling with MoTE
The WAM must jointly model the evolution of visual ob-
servations and the robot actions that cause these changes.

and action branches exchange information through mixed

3.4. Dual-Stream Hierarchical Memory
Long-horizon manipulation requires historical context to
assess task progress and maintain consistent action genera-
tion [41, 44]. Retaining every past observation is computa-
tionally expensive, while compressing the entire history into
a single representation can discard recent motion details and
critical subtask transitions. LAW maintains separate VLM
and WAM memory streams over shared observations.
At time t, we retain the previous five observations at
a stride of four, giving the recent window {ot−20, ot−16,
ot−12, ot−8, ot−4}; unavailable elements are omitted near
the beginning of an episode, and the current observation
ot is provided separately. We also retain the initial obser-
vation and observations recorded at control-loop subtask
transitions when they fall outside this recent window. Ear-
lier frames are sampled every four steps within the cur-
rent subtask and every 16 steps within completed subtasks.
All selected timestamps share the phase t mod 4, and a
subtask-boundary frame is stored only once.

4. Experiments
4.1. Training Details
Training datasets. We use task-specific training data from
RoboTwin [12], RoboDojo [13], and real-world manipula-
tion. RoboTwin covers 50 bimanual manipulation tasks;
its public Aloha-AgileX release provides 50 clean and 500
domain-randomized demonstrations per task.
The offi-
cial RoboDojo training release contains 3,500 trajectories
across 35 task directories, with 100 trajectories per direc-
tory collected through automated synthesis or VR-based
teleoperation. We augment the simulation trajectories with
semantic subtask annotations and temporal boundaries for
planning and completion assessment.
For real-world training, we collect 300 teleoperated tra-
jectories per task on the AgileX Cobot Magic platform,
totaling 900 trajectories, with one scene camera and two
wrist cameras. Puzzle comprises three subtasks, each in-
serting one colored piece into its matching recess. Domino
comprises five subtasks: standing four dominoes individ-
ually and then toppling the row. Organize comprises nine
subtasks: opening the corresponding drawer, placing a col-
ored block inside, and closing the drawer, repeated for three
blocks. Each trajectory includes multiview observations,
robot states, actions, language instructions, and semantic
subtask boundaries. Successful and failed trajectories pro-
vide complementary supervision for value learning. Addi-
tional annotation and collection details are given in Appen-
dices E and B.2.
Overall training pipeline. LAW is trained in two stages.
In the first stage, we train the planner and state experts se-
quentially on semantic subtask annotations, while training
the WAM independently on paired video and robot-action
sequences. In the second stage, we freeze these components
and train the value expert on successful demonstrations and
annotated failure trajectories using the subtask-local objec-
tive in Section 3.5. All parameters are frozen for evaluation.
This procedure prevents value learning from destabilizing
the generator, planner, or state expert.
Hybrid-Expert VLM. We use Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct [2]
as the shared VLM backbone and keep its language and vi-
sion parameters frozen. The planner and state adapters use
rank r = 64, while the value adapter uses rank r = 128,

valid, visually ambiguous, and low-quality samples.
World-action model.
The WAM is initialized from

from the pretrained ActionDiT weights [51]. We optimize
the video and action DiTs, including the MoTE layers,
while keeping the remaining pretrained components frozen.
Training uses three camera views at 384×384 resolution, an
action horizon of 32, and subtask-conditioned text embed-
dings. We use AdamW with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, weight
decay 10−2, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The learning rate
is linearly warmed up for the first 5% of training and then
follows a cosine schedule.
Testing.
The WAM samples 32 candidate chunks using
three denoising steps, without explicitly generating future
images. The value expert retains the top eight candidates
with temperature T = 1, and the robot executes their fused
prefix before resampling from updated observations.

5.4. Subtask Granularity
We compare task-level control, low-level subtasks, and se-
mantic subtasks to examine how subtask granularity af-
fects long-horizon manipulation. The model without sub-
tasks directly conditions control on the overall instruction.
Low-level subtasks introduce finer stage partitions, whereas
semantic-level subtasks align each stage with a semantic
objective shared by the planner, state expert, and memory
system. Table 9 compares ten tasks.
Semantic-level subtasks achieve the highest ten-task av-
erage, improving from 92.4% without subtasks and 89.1%
with low-level subtasks to 95.8%. The largest gains appear
on tasks such as Handover Block, Place Can Basket, and
Place Object Basket, where progress depends on recogniz-
ing distinct manipulation stages. Finer partitions can frag-
ment a coherent semantic objective into short transitions,
making completion decisions and historical context less sta-
ble. Semantic stages instead provide consistent units for
planning, asynchronous state assessment, and hierarchical
memory. The semantic decomposition does not improve
every saturated task, but its aggregate advantage indicates
that the abstraction level of the subtask, rather than decom-
position alone, is important for long-horizon control.
5.5. Subtask-Local Value Learning Ablation
We compare action selection without value guidance, value
experts trained with episode-level supervision, and value
experts trained with subtask-local supervision on five se-
lected multi-subtask RoboTwin tasks. Table 10 reports the
resulting success rates under clean and randomized settings.
On these five tasks, subtask-local supervision achieves
90.80% average success, compared with 88.20% without
value guidance and 87.90% with episode-level supervision,
improving by 2.60 and 2.90 percentage points, respectively.
Its aggregate gains hold in both clean and randomized set-
tings.
Compared with episode-level supervision, it im-
proves Blocks Ranking Size from 71.5% to 77.0%, Put Ob-
ject Cabinet from 87.5% to 90.0%, and Stack Bowls Three
from 83.0% to 88.0%.
These results support assigning
credit at semantic subtask boundaries: completed stages of
failed trajectories retain positive supervision, allowing the
value expert to evaluate candidate actions against the cur-
rent objective rather than relying solely on the final episode
outcome.

6. Conclusion
This paper presented LAW, a closed-loop VLM–WAM
framework that unifies language reasoning, action gen-
eration, and world modeling through semantic subtask
progress. The LAW agent coordinates specialized planning,
completion assessment, and value-guided action fusion at
complementary frequencies, connecting execution feed-
back to continued control and subtask transitions. MoTE
strengthens joint video–action modeling through shared and
modality-specific experts, while dual-stream hierarchical
memory preserves recent context and semantic boundary
observations. Subtask-local value learning further retains
useful supervision from completed stages of failed trajecto-
ries. Integrating OpenWAM-α raises RoboTwin success to
95.56%, establishing a new open-source state of the art and
demonstrating LAW’s extensibility to stronger pretrained
WAMs for further performance gains. These results high-
light semantic subtask progress as an effective interface for
agentic physical intelligence. Future work will investigate
transfer across robot embodiments and adaptive subtask de-
composition for longer and more diverse tasks.

B. Real-World Experimental Details
B.1. Robot Platform and Observations
Experiments are conducted on an AgileX (Songling) Cobot Magic platform equipped with two six-degree-of-freedom PiPER
arms and parallel-jaw grippers. Figure 10 illustrates the hardware platform. Manipulation takes place in a fixed tabletop
workspace. Visual observations comprise three RGB views: a scene camera covering the shared workspace and a wrist-
mounted camera on each arm. The scene view provides task-level context, while the wrist views resolve local interactions
between the grippers, objects, and target receptacles. Robot placement and camera mounting geometry are held fixed during
data collection to maintain a consistent observation configuration.
21


B.2. Demonstration Collection and Data Representation
Human demonstrations are collected through teleoperation. Across episodes, object positions and in-plane orientations are
varied within the reachable workspace, subject to the geometric constraints of each task. This variation changes the required
grasps and placements while preserving the task objective. Each trajectory associates multiview visual observations with
bimanual robot states, action sequences, a language instruction, and an episode outcome. Subtask descriptions and boundaries
supervise action learning, planning, and state estimation.
The collection protocol retains successful, partially completed, and failed executions. Failures are annotated by the stage
at which they occur and their principal cause, preserving information about completed task prefixes. Recording validity is
assessed separately from task success: episodes with missing visual or robot data, unusable temporal alignment, or severe
occlusion of the manipulation are excluded. Collection uses no fixed success-to-failure ratio.
Trajectories are organized in LeRobot format with episode-level metadata and aligned camera streams. Recorded videos
are stored at 30 frames per second. LAW uses the end-effector action representation described in the main paper: a three-
dimensional position, a unit quaternion, and a scalar gripper command for each arm, giving a 16-dimensional bimanual action
vector. Semantic boundaries align visual observations, actions, and subtask labels along the trajectory timeline.
B.3. Task Definitions
The evaluation comprises three tasks with complementary requirements for sequential manipulation, spatial alignment, and
state-dependent interaction.
Domino. The robot arranges four dominoes into an upright row and initiates their toppling. Successful execution requires sta-
ble placement and sufficient spatial alignment for the intended toppling sequence. The five stages comprise four placements
and a final toppling action.
Puzzle. The robot inserts three colored pieces into their corresponding recesses in a puzzle board. Each piece constitutes one
semantic stage. The task requires matching and aligning each piece before insertion; success requires seating all three pieces.
Organize. The robot sorts three colored blocks into the corresponding drawers of a three-level cabinet. For each block,
the robot opens the target drawer, places the block inside, and closes the drawer, resulting in nine semantic stages. Success
requires correct block–drawer assignments and closure of all drawers. The task combines object manipulation with repeated
changes to drawer states.

D. Observation and Action Conventions
In the implementation, RGB inputs comprise one scene view and two wrist views, with separate historical visual-memory
streams for the VLM and WAM. The WAM receives a 16-dimensional EEF state; the VLM state expert receives textual values
for six joint positions and one gripper state per arm (14 dimensions), while the planner uses visual and task context. Actions
are absolute 16-dimensional EEF targets ordered as left (x, y, z, qw, qx, qy, qz, g) followed by right. Absolute poses use
scene-world coordinates with the controller-specific end-effector frame convention; no relative-action conversion is applied.
Gripper targets use the normalized environment scale, with zero closed and one open. WAM states and actions use per-
dimension global z-score statistics from the configured training datasets, cached in dataset stats.json; normalization
uses (x −µ)/(σ + 10−8), clipped to [−5, 5], rather than separate statistics for each action-horizon step. The same cached
statistics are loaded for evaluation. Predicted actions are inverse-normalized before execution, and quaternion targets are
renormalized to unit length. VLM joint-state values are supplied in their recorded scale.
