Generative simulation can automate robot environment construction, but physically valid environments are not
necessarily useful for training. The challenge is to identify environments that address a policy’s failures and supervision
that helps it overcome them. We introduce CoDA, a framework in which a simulation data engine and an action
policy co-evolve through a shared heterogeneous memory of environment construction, policy failures, and learning
progress. The memory compiles validated diagnoses from a vision-language critic into generation programs that
recreate difficulties and training programs that help the policy overcome them. We further propose Text2potential to
convert these diagnoses into staged potentials for action-chunk value learning, providing dense supervision for policy
updates while preserving the task’s optimal policy under standard shaping assumptions. Measured learning progress
guides further generation and program refinement. Across four manipulation task families, CoDA improves out-of-
distribution success from 5.3% after supervised fine-tuning to 27.6%, exceeding the strongest closed-loop baseline by
9.5 percentage points with the same generation budget. On LIBERO-Pro, 15 rounds of generation and training raise
success from 25% to 84% under position perturbations and from 1% to 76% under instruction perturbations.

1. Introduction
Simulation has become a primary source of robot manip-
ulation data, but building simulated environments has
long been the bottleneck: each one requires a task de-
sign, semantically appropriate assets, a plausible layout,
and a training signal, often all specified by hand [38].
Generative simulation removes much of that cost. Cod-
ing agents now synthesize scenes, articulations, and task
specifications directly [25, 37–39, 43], and language
models author the reward functions that accompany
them [42, 48]. Environments are no longer scarce.
A central challenge remaining is deciding which en-
vironments to build next and what supervision will
help the policy to learn from them. Existing engines
sample variation from hand-specified ranges [29], refine
a scene until it is physically valid [39, 43], or allocate
budget by per-task success rate [10].
These signals
indicate whether an environment is valid and how well
the current policy performs, but do not by themselves
measure how much the policy improves through training.
Without such feedback, a generator risks spending its
budget on environments the policy has already mastered
or cannot yet learn to solve.

CoDA closes this loop with a heterogeneous memory
shared by the data generator and the action policy. It
records repairs needed to make scenes valid, together
with policy failure diagnoses grounded in rollout evi-
dence and localized to one generative factor and one
trajectory phase. The memory tracks learning progress
for recurring difficulty modes to guide subsequent gener-
ation. This evidence is compiled into separate programs
for scene generation and policy training, with the latter
specifying subgoals and learning tools [38, 42].
As tasks involve longer sequences of dependent subtasks,
sparse success signals can slow value learning, while de-
signing effective dense rewards can require manual effort.
We therefore introduce Text2potential, which compiles a
failure diagnosis into a potential over the simulator state
and uses it to densify value targets for action chunks [7,
15]. The resulting value estimates guide policy updates
through weighted regression on the policy’s own rollouts,
favoring action chunks with higher estimated value.
Across four manipulation tasks, CoDA achieves 27.6%
out-of-distribution success, compared with 5.3% for the
supervised fine-tuning baseline. With the same budget
for generating environments, a loop guided by per-task
success rates achieves 18.1%, while open-loop variants
yield 8.5%.
Extending our loop to more rounds in-
creases out-of-distribution success to 42.1%. Moreover,
on LIBERO-Pro, training on source-task variants gener-
ated by CoDA increases success from 25% to 84% on po-
sition generalization and from 1% to 76% on task gener-
alization. Finally, we observe that reusing memory across
policy backbones transfers accumulated experience, en-
abling a second backbone to increase out-of-distribution
success from 3.8% to 21.1%.
In summary, the contributions of our work are:
• Closed-loop co-evolution. We introduce CoDA, a

2. Related Work
Agentic asset and scene synthesis. Recent methods im-
prove visual asset quality [40, 50] and model part struc-
ture, articulation, or physical properties [2, 3, 13, 17, 36,
46, 47, 51]. For robot learning, RoboGen [38] generates
tasks, scenes, and training supervision, while RoboTwin
2.0 [4] scales bimanual manipulation data through ex-
pert data synthesis and domain randomization. Sim-
Foundry [29] generates variations across objects, scenes
and tasks, and agentic systems automate scene construc-
tion and refinement for simulation [25, 37, 39, 43, 44].
Our work focuses on adapting environment generation
to the policy’s learning progress, using feedback from
training to guide what is generated next.
Curriculum learning and policy improvement. Cur-
riculum methods prioritize environments using regret,
learning potential, or parameter changes [6, 9, 23, 27,
49]. POET [35] coevolves agents and parameterized
environments, while language models synthesize re-
wards [19, 41, 42] and generate adaptive environment
programs [10, 16]. For policy improvement, VLA poli-
cies learn from their rollouts using sparse success re-
wards [14, 32]. Q-Planning [7] improves action selection
while keeping the base policy frozen. We adopt a critic
over action chunks [15] with advantage-weighted regres-
sion to update the policy [24]. Lifelong methods retain
reusable prompts or skill libraries [18, 45]. CoDA links
scene generation and policy learning through failure di-
agnoses localized to scene factors and trajectory phases.
3. Method
CoDA couples scene generation and policy learning
through a shared memory that guides both.
A data
engine G, implemented as a tool-augmented coding
agent, writes executable scene programs against a simu-
lator SDK. Each program defines an environment and a
machine-checkable success predicate ν. An action policy
πθ collects rollouts in these environments, and a vision–
language critic C diagnoses failures by identifying the
responsible factor, a trajectory phase, and supporting
rollout evidence. Failures intrinsic to motor execution
are recorded separately from those attributed to scene
factors. The memory M consolidates this evidence into
reusable difficulty modes. Validated modes are compiled
into two complementary outputs: generation programs

We first construct seed environments (§3.1) and de-

the memory to guide scene generation (§3.3), com-

3.1. Seeding and Scene Representation
Seeding begins with an object pool [5, 33, 51]. We use a
task family’s assets when available and otherwise sample
from a predefined pool. Given the sampled objects, the
engine proposes a task [38] and specifies its instruction,
participating objects, initial and terminal object states,
and executable success predicate ν. Proposals without
a verifier are discarded.
Instead of committing to a specific data generation
scheme, the engine writes factored scene programs. Task,
layout, and object attributes can then be perturbed inde-
pendently, letting the loop test a diagnosis by changing
one factor while keeping the others fixed. Failure di-
agnosis and scene generation need to share a common
representation. The critic should identify factors that
the engine can directly modify. We parameterize each
environment as s = (τ, σ, α), generated by executing a
scene program s=Exec(g) with g ∼G(·). The task fac-
tor τ specifies the goal predicate, instruction, and initial
and terminal object states. The object-spatial factor σ
specifies poses, support and containment relations, clear-
ances, and occlusion. The object self-attribute factor α
specifies geometry, metric scale, mass, friction, articu-
lation, and appearance. The SDK exposes these factors
through set_task, set_layout, and set_attributes,
so the engine can resample any one factor while keep-
ing the other two fixed. This factorization provides a
common representation for the critic’s diagnoses, the
engine’s interventions, and the applicability conditions
of compiled generation programs.
Verification. Each program emits a sparse, machine-
checkable success predicate ν over its scene objects.
This predicate determines task success independently
of the critic’s judgments and provides the common
success criterion across the methods we compare. Dense
training feedback is introduced separately through
potential-based shaping, rather than replacing the task
predicate with a synthesized reward [42, 48]. Before
policy deployment, each scene must pass verification
checks for collision-free placement, static stability during
a short passive simulation, and plausible metric scale.
Programs that fail these checks are excluded from policy
rollouts but retained in memory to inform subsequent
scene generation.
3.2. Shared Heterogeneous Memory
Memory combines two sources of experience:
the
engine’s construction and repair traces, and the pol-
icy’s rollout outcomes and failure diagnoses. Follow-
ing the episodic–semantic–procedural organization of
language-agent memory [30], we retain individual
records (episodic tier), abstract recurring failure patterns
(semantic tier), and compile reusable programs for gen-
eration and training (procedural tier).

Episodic tier:
construction and rollout evidence.
The append-only trace store B records each scene pro-
gram g, its verifier ν, and summaries of the genera-
tion trace, including violated constraints and verification
outcomes. For deployed environments, it also records
the success rate bp(s) over N rollouts and the learn-
ing progress LV(s) obtained from training [49]. The
vision–language critic C uses scene and rollout evidence
to produce a failure diagnosis ψ = (δ, p, c, e), where
δ∈{TASK, SPATIAL, ATTRIBUTE, EXECUTION} identifies the
attributed factor, p the trajectory phase, c a natural-
language description of the cause, and e the supporting
evidence. These diagnoses are stored alongside the corre-
sponding traces. The additional label EXECUTION covers
motor-execution failures.
Semantic tier: reusable difficulty modes. Episodic
records are clustered online into difficulty modes Kj
using the factor–phase pair (δ, p) and similarity between
diagnosed causes.
Each mode captures a recurring
failure condition that can arise across tasks and policy
backbones, such as “an occluder adjacent to the target
on the approach side”. We characterize each mode by
two quantities, hardness and importance. Hardness is
measured by the policy’s success rate bpj, with lower rates
indicating greater difficulty. Importance wj is a running
score updated from mode-level learning progress, mea-
sured as the change in policy success rate on that mode
before and after training [49]. These two quantities
reflect different properties of a mode. A mode can re-
main hard even when training on it yields little learning
progress, or become easy after substantial improvement.
Each mode also carries a validation status determined
by control batches (§3.3), and only validated modes are
compiled into procedural memory.
Procedural tier: generation and training programs.
The two consumers of memory improve differently. The
data engine G adapts in context by retrieving and exe-
cuting programs between rounds, while the action policy
πθ learns through gradient updates and acts from obser-
vations. We therefore compile the same validated mode
into two complementary programs:
• Generation programs, stored in Ω, encode how to
reproduce and vary the diagnosed scene conditions.
They retain the parent scene program, validated vari-
ants along one factor, compilation-check outcomes,
and the required relational structure expressed over
symbolic object roles.
• Training programs, stored in Φ, specify how to address
the policy failure through subgoal decompositions, po-
tentials, and learning tools. They retain the diagnosed
phase and evidence of which training programs im-
proved policy performance.
The generation compiler Rgrind produces the former,
while Text2potential implements the training compiler
Rhone (§3.4). During training, these programs evaluate
subgoal completion and compute potentials that shape
value targets. At deployment, the trained policy pre-
dicts actions from observations and the task instruction
without executing these programs.
3.3. Memory-Guided Scene Generation
At each round, memory guides which difficulty modes to
revisit and how to instantiate them in new environments.
We allocate the generation budget across difficulty
modes using an upper-confidence rule based on their
hardness and observed learning progress. An ε-fraction
of the budget is reserved for novel environments, while a
separate replay fraction revisits previously encountered
modes to detect performance regressions. A validated
generation program is added to the pool Ωwhen the
policy’s success rate on its variants falls within the
designated success band and is removed when success
rate is outside that band. For each selected mode, the
engine retrieves a generation program specifying the
scene conditions to reproduce and the factor to vary.
Generation programs express scene conditions in terms
of symbolic object roles, allowing the engine to reuse
them across task families by assigning those roles to
objects in a new scene (Appendix A).
For each selected candidate difficulty mode linked to
a scene factor, the engine generates a targeted batch
that varies the identified factor and a control batch
that varies a different factor. After verification checks,
we evaluate the current policy on both batches. If the
targeted variants yield a lower policy success rate than
the control variants, the proposed difficulty mode is com-
piled into reusable generation and training programs.
The policy learns from its rollouts using the success
predicate ν and the potential-based feedback supplied by
Text2potential (§3.4). A scripted planner also attempts
each valid variant, and trajectories that satisfy ν are
retained as demonstrations Dt, providing additional
imitation supervision when available.
3.4. Text2potential
The success predicate ν determines whether a gener-
ated task is complete, but provides little feedback on
intermediate progress. This makes value learning diffi-
cult when success requires a long sequence of actions.
Text2potential converts a validated failure diagnosis into
subgoals and potentials over simulator states. These po-
tentials provide dense feedback for value learning (§3.5).
Prior language-based reward synthesis provides dense
training feedback, but its iterative refinement relies on
humans to describe policy failures observed in rollouts,
and can produce rewards that favor behavior other than
task completion [42]. Text2potential instead uses a critic
to diagnose failures from the policy’s own rollouts and
compiles these diagnoses into a potential function that
shapes the value critic’s training targets, providing dense
feedback without replacing the original task reward.

At deployment, the trained policy predicts action chunks

For a fixed, bounded potential with appropriate terminal-
state handling, potential-based shaping preserves the
policies that maximize the original task return [20].
For any fixed policy, exact action and state values shift
by the same term, −βΦ(s), leaving action advantages
unchanged: AΦ = (Q −βΦ) −(V −βΦ) = A. Under
these conditions, shaping preserves the policies that max-
imize the original task return. This property concerns ex-
act values; the effect of shaping on the learned critic and
policy is assessed through subsequent policy progress.
Refinement and routing.
We assess each training
program by measuring the policy’s improvement on
the corresponding difficulty mode, using ν to deter-
mine task success. We also compare how quickly the
value critic converges with and without the compiled
potential. If the potential provides no improvement in
convergence speed over sparse task rewards alone, the
vision–language critic C revisits the failure diagnosis,
and Rhone revises the subgoal decomposition. For each
subgoal, we select a learning procedure based on the
policy’s measured success rate [38]. When this rate is
near zero and a planner demonstration is available, we
use behavior cloning on Dt to teach the policy an initial
solution. When the success rate falls within the target
band, we use Text2potential to shape the value-learning
targets. For difficulty modes with success rates above
the band, we periodically re-evaluate the policy to detect
performance regressions. If none of the subgoals falls
within the target band, we return the difficulty mode to
the data engine for further environment generation and
reduce its importance score when allocating subsequent
generation budgets.
Scheduling.
Each round generates B targeted envi-
ronments and their controls.
After verification, we
collect N policy rollouts in each valid environment. The
vision–language critic examines a stratified sample of at
most C failed rollouts per mode. Between generation
rounds, the value critic receives KQ updates and the
policy receives Kπ updates using the accumulated buffer.
After validation, difficulty modes are compiled into
training programs for policy learning and generation
programs for subsequent rounds.
A new generation
round is triggered when fewer than eight difficulty
modes remain within the success band or when learning
progress plateaus for 2k policy-update steps. The loop
terminates when no mode remains in the band or the
generation budget is exhausted.
4. Experiments
Experimental Setup.
We evaluate CoDA on four
manipulation task families and LIBERO-Pro [52] using
π0.5 [8] as the primary policy backbone.
Our main
comparison includes the released π0.5 checkpoint, super-
vised fine-tuning on seed demonstrations, and policies
trained with domain randomization, uniform environ-
ment variants [29], or success-rate-guided generation
[10]. Generation baselines use the same SDK and bud-
gets. We ablate persistent memory and the separation of
generation and training stores, comparing against single-
store alternatives that include a skill library [34]. We
report per-suite success rates and average success across
held-out suites, including generalization to unseen object
instances, layouts, tasks, and categories. Additional met-
rics assess generation quality through verification pass

5. Conclusion
We introduce CoDA, coupling environment generation
and policy learning through shared heterogeneous
memory. Failure diagnoses become reusable programs
for generating environments and training the pol-
icy. Text2potential provides dense supervision, while
learning progress guides generation and refinement.
Experiments show improved out-of-distribution success,
program transfer across task families, and memory reuse
across policy backbones.

A. Transferring Generation Programs Across
Task Families
A generation program can be reused in a new task family
when its object requirements and relational constraints
are satisfied in the new scene.
We call this transfer
lifting. Each program refers to objects by symbolic role,
such as target, obstacle, or support, rather than by
a specific object instance. Each role specifies a required
object category, such as container, or an affordance,
such as graspable. The program also specifies the rela-
tions required between these roles, such as an obstacle
occupying the approach path to the target.
To apply a program to a new scene, Rgrind matches these
requirements against the scene program’s symbol table,
which records object identifiers, categories, affordances,
poses, and relations. This information is available di-
rectly because the data engine constructs the scene pro-
gram. A valid binding assigns each role to a distinct scene
object and satisfies all role requirements and relational
constraints. If no valid binding exists, the program is
not transferred to that scene. Matching is exhaustive:
our programs contain at most five roles and our scenes
contain a few dozen objects, with matching taking mil-
liseconds in this setting.
When several bindings are valid, they are ranked first by
how closely their object categories match those in the dif-
ficulty mode’s previously validated variants. Ties are bro-
ken using the margins by which the relational constraints
are satisfied. We retain at most three bindings and exe-
cute the generation program separately for each, produc-
ing a scene variant and its own matched control batch.
Transferred programs undergo the same validation as
programs learned within the new task family. Their gen-
erated variants must pass the scene-validity checks, and
policy rollouts evaluate both the targeted variants and
their controls. A transferred program enters Ωfor the
new family only when the control comparison supports
the attributed difficulty and the measured success rates
of its variants fall within the success band. Thus, match-
ing establishes whether a program is applicable to the
new scene; evaluation establishes whether it reproduces
a useful training difficulty.
B. Implementation Details
Environments and evaluation. We construct MuJoCo
environments for four task families:
pick-and-place,
articulated open/close, put-inside, and stack/insert. Each
family includes an in-distribution evaluation suite and
four held-out suites of 100 environments each, testing
generalization to unseen object instances, layouts, tasks,
and categories, respectively.
Held-out environments
never enter M. In a second setting, we initialize the
loop with the 30 LIBERO source tasks and evaluate on
LIBERO-Pro [52] (Table 2). All results are reported as
mean ± standard error over three random seeds.
Assets, observations, and actions. The asset collection
comprises 1,200 rigid objects from Objaverse across 60
categories, 300 articulated objects from ArtiCraft-10K,
and 500 seed layouts from TableVerse [5, 33, 51]. The
robot is a 7-DoF Franka Panda with front and wrist cam-
eras, each providing 224 × 224 RGB observations. Con-
trol runs at 10 Hz using seven-dimensional actions: six
dimensions for the change in end-effector pose and one
for the gripper command. Each action chunk contains
h = 8 control steps.
Policy backbones.
Our primary policy is π0.5, a 3B-
parameter model with a flow-matching action head [8].
We initialize it from the released openpi base checkpoint,
or from its LIBERO-fine-tuned checkpoint for the LIBERO
experiments [26]. We also evaluate the 7B-parameter
OpenVLA-OFT [12] in table 7. Both policies are fine-
tuned using LoRA with rank 32 and a learning rate of
10−5. At each rollout decision, the policy samples j = 8
candidate action chunks. These are combined using soft-
max weights computed from Qϕ, with temperature 0.5.
Scene generation, diagnosis, and potential compi-
lation. The data engine, rollout-diagnosis model, and
potential compiler all use GPT-5.5 [21]. Planner-certified
demonstrations are obtained using cuRobo [31]. The
diagnosis model receives rollout video, contact informa-
tion, joint states, and the task success verifier’s output
at each timestep.
It assigns each diagnosed failure
to one of four factors (TASK, SPATIAL, ATTRIBUTE, or
EXECUTION) and one of five trajectory phases (approach,
grasp, transport, place or insert, and release).
For
potential compilation, Text2potential retrieves three

C. Additional Experiments
Complementary generation and training programs.
Table 5 evaluates the contributions of the generation
and training programs retained in memory. Retaining
only scene generation programs raises OOD success from
19.2% to 22.8%, while retaining only policy training pro-
grams raises it to 22.1%. Retaining both reaches 27.6%:
the resulting 8.4-percentage-point improvement exceeds
the sum of the individual gains, 3.6 and 2.9 points. The
three baseline variants that reuse the same memory rep-
resentation for both scene generation and policy train-
ing perform worse than retaining either type of special-
ized program alone. With both generation and training
programs, the policy reaches 25% OOD success in five
rounds, compared with fourteen rounds without memory.
Transfer across task families. Table 6 evaluates gen-
eration programs transferred to a different task family
by rebinding their object roles, without relearning the
programs. The baseline learns programs from scratch
in the target family using the same LLM token budget
as transfer. Averaged across all cross-family transfers,
reused programs achieve 60.3% of the learning progress
obtained with programs learned within the target family,
compared with 35.8% for the from-scratch baseline.
This advantage holds in every cross-family comparison,
demonstrating that generation experience can be reused
to support policy learning in new task families.
Transfer across policy backbones. Table 7 evaluates
whether memory collected with one policy backbone can
support training another, with the memory held fixed
and no additional environment generation. Transferred
memory improves OOD success by an average of 18.9
percentage points over the SFT baseline without memory.
In both transfer directions, performance remains within
two percentage points of running the full framework with
the target backbone. These results show that the accumu-
lated difficulty modes and programs can support policy
learning beyond the backbone used to collect them.

Generation quality and diagnosis validation. Table 8
shows that 88.9% of scene proposals pass the physical
validity checks, compared with 71.3% under uniform
sampling. Coverage entropy remains close to that of
uniform sampling (3.18 versus 3.42 bits), while targeting
precision increases from 24.6% to 66.7%. The engine
therefore improves scene validity and targeting while
maintaining broad coverage. We reject 18.2% of the
critic’s failure diagnoses because variants that modify the
identified scene factor are no harder than control variants
that modify a different factor. In the evaluation with two
annotators and 100 sampled diagnoses, this validation
step improves agreement with human annotations from
73.6% to 82.4%.
Computational cost and adaptive scheduling. CoDA
improves OOD success from 18.1% to 27.6% over scalar
feedback, a 52% relative improvement (Table 9). Its
computational requirements are 7.4M LLM tokens and
66 GPU-hours per 1,000 accepted environments. Adap-
tive scheduling improves efficiency, reducing both token
usage and GPU time by approximately 11% relative to
fixed round intervals while increasing OOD success from
26.1% to 27.6%. Thus, adapting when to generate new
environments reduces computational cost while improv-
ing policy performance.

D. Limitations
CoDA relies on foundation models for scene generation,
failure diagnosis, and potential compilation. Errors in
these models’ understanding of objects, physical interac-
tions, or task requirements can affect the resulting scenes
and training signals. Physical validity checks and control
experiments help detect such errors, but their coverage
depends on the checks and interventions used. Results
may also vary with changes to the underlying models,
making model versions and configurations important for
reproducibility. The framework requires additional sim-
ulation and model inference to generate environments,
evaluate policy rollouts, and validate diagnoses. These
costs constrain the number of rounds and task families
that can be explored under a fixed budget. Our evalu-
ation also focuses on simulated manipulation. Transfer
to physical robots remains to be established, particularly
for tasks sensitive to differences in contact dynamics,
sensing, and actuation.

E. Broader Impact
The ability to generate environments and training sig-
nals automatically has far-reaching implications for robot
learning, simulation, and embodied AI. CoDA contributes
to this space by turning environment generation into a
closed loop that responds to what a policy has learned,
which lowers the cost of producing diverse, verifiable
training data. This can accelerate the development of
capable manipulation policies, improve the fidelity and
coverage of simulated benchmarks, and support scal-
able data generation for research groups without large
teleoperation budgets. While lowering the barrier to
training capable robot policies raises potential concerns
about misuse and about biases inherited from the un-
derlying foundation models, our framework is designed
for research use, judges success only through machine-
checkable verifiers, and audits every automated diagnosis
with control experiments. We encourage responsible de-
ployment practices that keep such audits in place. Com-
pared to collecting real-robot demonstrations at scale,
the approach is computationally efficient and requires
no physical data collection, and we view its benefits in
controllable, verifiable data generation as outweighing
its risks when applied responsibly.
