ABSTRACT 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models are rapidly expanding what individual robots can accomplish, yet many real-world manipulation settings require multi-ple arms to operate as a coordinated system. Existing approaches organize multi-arm coordination in different ways, including centralized joint policies, high-level task or role assignment, and locally executed policies that react to teammates. However, how complete action-producing VLAs should explicitly interact with one another during action generation remains largely unexplored. We introduce VLA-Fabric, which organizes one complete VLA per arm while preserving lo-cal perception and action ownership, and enables coordination through learned interaction across policy boundaries. We first systematically study where such interaction should occur, how peer information should be aggregated, and how the interaction should be learned, revealing complementary coordination func-tions spanning shared context, peer-specific latent information, and action-stage interaction. We then re-instantiate these functions in a structurally different VLA architecture, showing that the coordination principle is not tied to a particular ten-sor layout or backbone implementation. Finally, we compose teams of three and four arms and evaluate them in simulation and physical experiments. On all four physical tasks, Full interaction improves success over independently trained local policies by 16–40 percentage points. Together, these results suggest that explicit interaction among complete VLA policies offers a scalable path toward networked embodied intelligence. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models are advancing the capabilities of individual robots by con-necting visual and language understanding with learned action generation (Brohan et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025). Many important robotic applications, however, require several manipulators to contribute to a common physical objective. Cooperative manufac-turing, multi-part assembly, shared-load handling, and flexible robotic workcells motivate systems in which multiple arms work together (Dio et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2025). As pretrained VLAs become more capable, their progress creates an opportunity beyond improving a single robot: organizing multiple action-producing policies into a collaborative system. The ability of each constituent model to perceive and act does not, by itself, specify how those models should work together. Recent multi-arm VLAs pursue this goal through different organizations. RDT-1B, ET-VLA, Twin-VLA, and Dexora place cross-arm dependencies within a joint policy (Liu et al., 2025; Li & Peng, 2025; Im et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). Other approaches coordinate at different levels: MA-VLA assigns atomic behaviors to individual arms (Zhang et al., 2026a), while CHORUS demon-strates implicit decentralized coordination through locally executed policies that observe and react to teammates without runtime messages (Doshi et al., 2026). These approaches establish effective collaboration, with coordination inside a joint policy, above the action policy through behavior as-signment, or indirectly through observations of teammates. This raises an organizational challenge 1arXiv:2610.23018v1 [cs.RO] 16 Oct 2026 Preprint as the number and roles of manipulators grow. How can a multi-arm system remain modular at the level of individual manipulators while still supporting tightly coupled collaboration? A natural way to obtain such modularity is to organize the system around complete arm-local poli-cies rather than a growing joint policy. We therefore treat each manipulator and its complete VLA as a self-contained execution unit that perceives its local context and produces its own actions. Local information can remain with the policy that uses it, and larger teams can be formed by composing additional execution units. Crucially, local does not mean isolated . Policies participating in the same physical task still need to adapt their actions to one another. The key challenge is to connect these local policies through explicit interaction without giving up their local action ownership. To realize this local-but-connected organization, we introduce VLA-Fabric 1, a multi-VLA coordi-nation architecture connecting complete arm-local policies through learned internal representation interaction (Figure 1). Each VLA retains local perception and action generation while selected peer representations condition its computation through explicit policy interaction. VLA-Fabric thus makes coordination among complete policies an explicit learning and architecture problem instead of absorbing all cross-arm dependencies into a joint policy. 2 RELATED WORK 

Cooperative physical manipulation. Multi-arm robotics has studied coordinated transport, coop-erative assembly, and learned dual-arm manipulation under shared physical constraints (Dio et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2025). These settings require compatible arm motions under a shared load or coordinated holding, alignment, and placement during assembly. Planning and control address the resulting task and motion dependencies. Our work studies the complementary policy-level problem of organizing complete learned VLAs into a collaborative system. 

Multi-arm VLA coordination. Multi-arm VLAs organize coordination through several mecha-nisms. RDT-1B, ET-VLA, TwinVLA, and Dexora resolve cross-arm dependencies within joint bi-manual policies (Liu et al., 2025; Li & Peng, 2025; Im et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b). MA-VLA uses high-level behavior assignment (Zhang et al., 2026a), while CHORUS coordinates locally exe-cuted policies through teammate observations without inference-time communication (Doshi et al., 2026). VLA-Fabric studies explicit learned interaction between complete arm-local VLA policies during action generation. 

Learned interaction among embodied policies. LatentToM combines ego and consensus rep-resentations in decentralized diffusion policies and supports explicit consensus exchange for co-operative manipulation (He et al., 2025). Its no-message variant instead infers teammate behavior from local observations. CommNet, DIAL, and TarMAC establish broader foundations for learning communication jointly with agent policies (Sukhbaatar et al., 2016; Foerster et al., 2016; Das et al., 2019). Here, each agent is a complete pretrained VLA containing perception, multimodal reasoning, and action generation. We study which internal coordination functions should connect these poli-cies and whether the resulting interaction organization transfers across substantially different VLA architectures.

VLA-Fabric organizes multi-arm manipulation around complete local execution units, termed VLA agents . Each agent runs a complete VLA policy and owns one arm’s perception-action loop, includ-3Preprint ing its policy parameters, inference state, private observations, proprioception, and action output. We leave these choices open here and investigate them through the controlled architecture study in Section 3.2. 3.2 DISCOVERING THE INTERACTION ARCHITECTURE 

To study interaction between complete VLA agents, we expose the cross-arm dependencies of a centralized policy while preserving its single-arm priors. TwinVLA provides two identifiable pre-trained SingleVLA branches (Im et al., 2026), which we separate into local policies and reconnect through explicit representation interfaces. Figure 2(a) organizes the design space around three decisions: where policies interact, how peer in-formation is combined, and how the resulting interaction is adapted. Interaction entry. Candidates range from Independent policies and Private K/V exchange to additional Common and action-side interaction. Interaction operator. Common exchange uses one-way transfer, symmetric averaging (AVG), or operator-level synchronization (RAW); action-side interaction uses Linear or Transformer fusion. Interaction adaptation. Single-stage enables the candidate architecture from the start of fine-tuning; Common-first initializes it from a Common-only policy before introducing the remain-ing interactions. Nine Single-stage designs and seven matched Common-first variants per task yield 32 runs on Han-dover Box and Shoes Table. Checkpoints are selected on 200 development conditions and evaluated on 200 disjoint paired conditions, as summarized in Figure 2(b). 

Where to interact. Private K/V alone improves over Independent policies on both tasks, and richer configurations obtain further gains from Common and action-side interaction. Although ad-ditional inputs do not always help, these comparisons support complementary interaction across policy stages rather than one insertion point for every coordination requirement. 

How to interact. The tasks impose different coordination demands, against which the operator choices expose different advantages. Symmetric Common exchange improves over one-way trans-fer in the Common-first comparisons, while action-side fusion provides further gains in several configurations. Neither AVG versus RAW nor Linear versus Transformer yields a consistent winner across both tasks. These observations motivate retaining complementary interaction functions that policies can exploit as coordination demands change, rather than defining VLA-Fabric around one task’s peak configuration. 

How to adapt. Common-first consistently improves the matched configurations, showing that in-teraction structure and its adaptation should be considered jointly when learning richer cross-policy coordination. The interaction functions and their adaptation recipe thus play distinct roles. The for-mer specify which peer representations enter local computation, how they are combined, and where they condition action generation. The latter determines how policies learn to exploit this information during task adaptation.

6 VLA-F ABRIC IN REAL -W ORLD MULTI -A RM SYSTEMS 

Having established the interaction architecture, transferred it across VLA families, and extended it to larger simulated teams, we now evaluate VLA-Fabric in physical multi-arm systems. The study covers four three- and four-arm tasks involving parallel placement, object relay, and shared-object manipulation. We collect 50 demonstrations per task and evaluate Full and independently trained local π0.5 policies over 25 physical trials per policy setting. Basket3 has three arms place vegetables into a shared basket. In Relay3 , one arm positions the receiving target, a second hands over an object, and the third receives and places it in the target. 7 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

VLA-Fabric makes a complete local VLA an explicit organizational unit of multi-arm coordination. Local perception and action ownership are preserved, while collaboration is learned through rep-resentation exchange across policy boundaries. The same coordination roles can be re-instantiated across VLA architectures, and additional policies can form larger teams without redefining a global joint action space. This separates progress in constituent models from progress in team organization, allowing the two to advance together. Stronger VLAs can improve the execution units, while inter-action mechanisms determine which information is shared, when it enters policy computation, and with which peers. Across the evaluated architectures, tasks, and team sizes, this common organiza-tion supports reuse of the coordination principle while allowing the underlying policy computation to change. Our experiments use homogeneous teams with fixed roles and up to four arms, leaving changing team compositions and heterogeneous VLAs as directions for broader composability. Broader com-position will require interfaces that accommodate differences in representation and action genera-tion while maintaining clear local policy boundaries. Such interfaces must align the meaning and timing of exchanged information across policies with different internal computations. The current interaction structure is static; adapting content, topology, and frequency to task phase and agent participation could make coordination more selective. Long-horizon, high-precision manipulation remains difficult, as illustrated by Arch Building, motivating complementary improvements in plan-ning and control of the constituent VLAs. Larger teams also motivate more selective representation exchange and interaction mechanisms that accommodate changing role assignments and available communication resources. 

8 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduced VLA-Fabric to address how multiple complete VLA policies can collab-orate as a coordinated system for increasingly demanding multi-arm manipulation. Each arm-local VLA remains a complete execution unit, preserving its own perception and action generation while acquiring collaborative capability through learned representation interaction. We established this organization through systematic exploration of interaction entry, aggregation, and adaptation, then re-instantiated its coordination functions in a structurally different VLA. Extending the same prin-ciple to three- and four-agent teams in simulation and physical systems completes a framework for discovering, transferring, and scaling inter-VLA coordination. This organization allows indi-vidual VLA capability and multi-VLA coordination to advance together, connecting improvements 9Preprint in local models with the mechanisms through which they collaborate. Beyond the operators stud-ied here, it opens directions in coordination-aware model design, adaptive representation exchange, communication-efficient deployment, and heterogeneous VLA teams. VLA-Fabric thereby offers a concrete step from increasingly capable individual embodied models toward networked embodied intelligence.
