Feed-forward Gaussian models have made novel view synthesis almost instantaneous. Feed-forward methods pre-dict Gaussians directly, either in two stages or in a single merged pass. Using two separately trained models is at-tractive because each can specialize in its own objective: one for accurate pose estimation and the other for high-quality reconstruction, rather than forcing a single jointly trained network to compromise between the two. A natural instantiation pairs a frozen pose estimator, VGGT, with a frozen reconstruction backbone, TokenGS. Prior work, such as VGGT-X and 3R-GS, also adopts a two-stage design, but attributes the resulting quality degradation to noisy pose es-timates and introduces learned refinement. We show that this is not the primary source of the problem. Through per-camera analysis across 107 scenes from two datasets, DL3DV and MipNeRF360, using COLMAP poses as the reference, we find that the rotation error after alignment is small (median 1.6◦), whereas the translation scale is offset by a large, dataset-consistent factor. Controlled ablations over 140 DL3DV scenes show that the primary obstacles are two integration failures rather than pose error: a coordinate-frame inconsistency and a translation-scale mismatch, both of which can be corrected deterministically. With these fixes, a frozen VGGT driving a frozen TokenGS achieves 

22 .47 PSNR, within 0.51 dB of the COLMAP reference, with no additional training, when poses are estimated from all scene views. In the sparse setting, where reconstruction uses only four context images under the standard pose-free protocol, the same pipeline matches this all-view quality, yielding a fully COLMAP-free pipeline without any refine-ment step. 

# 1. Introduction 

With 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [9], recent advances aim to make explicit reconstruction faster and more reliable without degrading quality. Feed-forward Gaussian methods [5, 8, 11, 20, 29] use feed-forward networks, MLPs and transformers to predict Gaussians directly, with small or no refinement iterations, which makes reconstruction faster. Conventional 3DGS [9] instead does reconstruction in two stages: during the first stage, COLMAP [18] estimates the poses, during the sec-ond stage it initializes the Gaussians and refines them with iterative optimization. Recent feed-forward methods fall into two families by how the pose and reconstruction components are trained. One family trains a single network end-to-end : NoPoS-plat [29], AnySplat [7], TokenSplat [11], SelfSplat [8], PF3plat [5], and Uni3R [20] jointly learn geometry and Gaussian prediction, so that pose estimation and reconstruc-tion are optimized together within one model. The other family composes separately-trained models : VGGT-X [13] and 3R-GS [6] take poses from a pretrained geometry esti-mator and add a distinct learned refinement or optimization stage on top. Our work belongs to the latter family struc-turally, but connects two frozen pretrained models through deterministic operations alone, with no refinement or train-ing. Given images and poses, TokenGS [17] unlike other feed-forward GS methods, predicts a fixed number of Gaus-sians, without other models that change the number of Gaussians based on image content. Its transformer archi-tecture uses poses as Pl¨ ucker ray embeddings to fuse with the image patches. But it requires already estimated poses, which is a blocker for using the model as a direct feed-forward pipeline that predicts Gaussians from unposed im-ages. An easy integration is to use a direct pose estimator such as VGGT [23] , MASt3R [10], or DUSt3R [24] to pre-dict the pose. In this way we can use two separately trained large models, each for its specific objective: one for pose accuracy, one for good reconstruction. However, directly integrating the estimated context view poses degrades re-construction quality substantially [6, 13], which raises con-cerns about the pose quality of these pose estimators. Prior work largely addresses the degradation from imperfect geometric initialization through learned or optimization-based refinement. VGGT-X [13] keeps the VGGT [23] predictions fixed and applies adaptive global alignment using XFeat correspondences, jointly refining 1arXiv:2610.23039v1 [cs.CV] 16 Oct 2026 the camera parameters with an epipolar loss, followed by MCMC-based 3DGS training with joint pose optimization. 3R-GS [6] initializes from MASt3R-SfM [2] poses and ad-dresses their imperfect initialization through MCMC-based pose optimization, an MLP-based global pose refiner, and a rendering-free epipolar geometric constraint. VGGS [27], in contrast, leverages VGGT’s multi-view geometric pri-ors for sparse-view surface reconstruction, using an anchor-calibrated depth estimation scheme and a relative-depth consistency loss to align VGGT’s depth predictions to the underlying surface. While it also contends with misalign-ment in VGGT’s outputs, its focus is depth-level surface fi-delity rather than the coordinate-frame and scale mismatch we identify when composing VGGT poses with a recon-struction backbone. Instead, we investigate the actual source of this mis-match, rather than fixing it implicitly with a refinement net-work. Through controlled analysis, we find that the failure is not caused by pose inaccuracy. After alignment, VGGT’s rotation error is small, while its translations are offset from the backbone’s expected distribution by a large, system-atic scale factor. Our controlled ablation over 140 DL3DV scenes shows that the major obstacles to merging VGGT and TokenGS are not pose errors, but two integration fail-ures: coordinate-frame inconsistency and translation-scale mismatch, both of which admit deterministic fixes. With frame consistency and scale correction, we compose two frozen pretrained models (VGGT and TokenGS) with no re-training or fine-tuning. When VGGT estimates poses from all scene views, the composition reaches within 0.51 dB of the COLMAP-initialized baseline. The same fixes hold in the sparse setting, where VGGT estimates poses from only the context views, maintaining quality with no per-scene optimization. In summary, our contributions are: • We diagnose two integration failures in two-stage feed-forward 3DGS systems : coordinate frame inconsistency and translation scale mismatch, and show via controlled ablation that these, not pose error, cause the quality col-lapse when connecting frozen feed-forward pose and re-construction models. • We provide a per-camera error decomposition across 107 scenes (DL3DV [12], MipNeRF360 [1]): rotation er-ror is small (median 1.6◦), while translation scale is offset by a large, dataset-consistent factor (approximately 7×). • We introduce deterministic, training-free fixes , frame consistency and scale correction, reaching 22 .47 PSNR on 140 DL3DV [12] scenes, within 0.51 dB of the COLMAP [18] reference, with poses estimated from all scene views, while maintaining the same inference speed. • We show the fixes hold in the sparse setting : whereas the result above estimates poses from all scene views, here VGGT processes only the 4 context views together with one target at a time (the standard pose-free proto-col), yet reconstruction quality is maintained, giving a fully COLMAP-free pipeline. 

# 2. Related Work 

2.1. Radiance Field Representation 

Radiance field reconstruction has developed along two main lines: implicit representations such as NeRF [15], and explicit ones such as 3DGS [9]. Both typically rely on COLMAP [18] to initialize camera poses. Given the posed images, an implicit network is trained to represent the scene, or explicit primitives learn features that represent it, in both cases optimized independently for each scene. Because this per scene optimization is slow, reconstruct-ing a scene takes considerable time, which is prohibitive for real time applications. Subsequent work reduces this cost through faster optimization and compact representa-tions [3, 4, 14], but the dependence on COLMAP [18] poses and per scene optimization remains. With the advancement of transformers, several methods predict pose and recon-struction in a single forward pass. We review these methods in the following subsections. 

2.2. Feed-Forward Pose and Geometry Estimation 

As networks become more powerful, 3D pose estima-tion can be solved directly by a neural network, almost entirely removing the time-consuming post-processing. DUSt3R [24], MASt3R [10], and VGGT [23] have shown results comparable to SfM based methods [18, 19, 22]. However, DUSt3R [24] and MASt3R [10] process only two images at a time and rely on post-processing to re-construct larger sets, fusing pairwise reconstructions into a global one. VGGT [23] instead processes one, a few, or even hundreds of views jointly in a single forward pass, achieving higher pose accuracy and often outperforming optimization-based alternatives without further processing. We build on VGGT as a state-of-the-art feed-forward pose estimator; independent evaluations [26] report its re-liability in sparse-view settings. VGGT [23] expresses all cameras in the coordinate frame of the first input view and is trained with scale-normalized supervision: two properties central to the integration failures we analyze in Section 4. 

2.3. Feed-Forward Gaussian Reconstruction 

A growing body of work builds Gaussian reconstruction on top of feed-forward geometry models [10, 23, 24]. These methods fall into two groups by how they use poses. 

Unified feed-forward networks. One group trains a sin-gle network end-to-end to output Gaussians directly from unposed images. A second group takes geometry or poses from a feed-forward estimator and then applies classical iterative optimization or learned refinement. VGGT-X [13] aug-ments VGGT with adaptive global alignment (via XFeat correspondences and an epipolar objective) and robust MCMC-3DGS training for dense-view synthesis, and 3R-GS [6] adds an MLP-based global pose refiner and a rendering-free epipolar constraint to MASt3R-SfM initial-izations. Both attribute the difficulty to imperfect or noisy pose estimates: VGGT-X frames VGGT’s outputs as de-grading initialization-sensitive training and reports that a gap to COLMAP persists even after adaptive alignment. VGGS [27] instead uses VGGT’s geometric priors for a different task, sparse-view surface reconstruction, calibrat-ing depth against multi-view anchors with a relative-depth consistency loss; its concern is depth-level surface fidelity rather than the coordinate-frame and scale mismatch we identify. Common to these methods is the assumption that feed-forward geometry cannot be used as is: unified networks resolve this by retraining, while the second group relies on per scene optimization or learned refinement on top of a separate estimator. Whether two independently pretrained feed-forward models: a pose estimator and a reconstruc-tion backbone, can be connected directly and kept frozen has not been studied. We show that they can. The ob-stacles are coordinate frame inconsistency and translation scale mismatch, and once these are addressed deterministi-cally, frozen VGGT poses drive a frozen TokenGS to within 0.51 dB of the COLMAP reference with no training, and the same fixes hold in the sparse setting. 

# 3. Preliminaries 

3.1. Feed-Forward Pose Estimation with VGGT 

Given N input images {Ii}Ni=1 , VGGT [23] jointly regresses camera extrinsics {Ei}Ni=1 , camera intrinsics 

{Ki}Ni=1 , dense depth maps, and point maps. It tokenizes each image with a DINOv2 [16] encoder and appends a learnable camera token to each frame’s tokens. These tokens are processed through alternating frame-wise and global attention layers, after which the attended camera to-kens are decoded into camera poses by a separate head. In this work, we use only VGGT’s predicted camera parame-ters. Two properties of VGGT are central to our analysis. First, VGGT predicts all camera extrinsics, point maps, and depth maps in the coordinate frame of the first input camera: the first camera is assigned the identity pose (zero trans-lation, no rotation), and all other cameras are expressed relative to it. This internal reference frame is unrelated to the world frame of any external reconstruction such as COLMAP [18]. Second, VGGT is trained with scale nor-malized supervision: each training scene’s ground truth is normalized by the average distance of its 3D points to the origin, so that camera translations and geometry share a canonical unit scale. Rather than applying this normaliza-tion as a post-processing step, VGGT is trained to emit pre-dictions already in this canonical scale. As a consequence, the scale of VGGT’s predicted translations is tied to its in-ternal normalization rather than to any external reference: 3the root cause, as we show in Section 4.1, of the translation scale mismatch when VGGT poses are supplied to a recon-struction backbone trained on differently scaled poses. 

3.2. Feed-Forward Reconstruction with TokenGS 

Because its poses come from structure-from-motion, To-kenGS implicitly assumes camera translations follow the coordinate convention and scale distribution of SfM recon-struction. This assumption is not unique to TokenGS: any feed-forward reconstruction backbone trained on posed im-ages inherits the frame convention and scale of its training poses. When poses from a different source, with a differ-ent convention and scale—such as VGGT’s internally nor-malized output—are supplied at inference, they may fall outside this expected distribution even when geometrically correct. We use TokenGS as a representative, state-of-the-art instance of this class; the integration failures we analyze arise from the general mismatch between how feed-forward pose estimators and reconstruction backbones define coor-dinate frames and scale.

4.2. Deterministic Integration 

Our pipeline connects a frozen VGGT pose estimator to a frozen TokenGS reconstruction backbone through deter-ministic operations that require no learned components and no ground-truth poses. Section 4.1 identified two failure modes, which we address in turn. We resolve the coordinate frame inconsistency with two operations, a convention con-version (§4.3) and frame-consistent inference (§4.4), and the scale mismatch with a single scale correction (§4.5). Figure 1 gives an overview. 

4.3. Coordinate Convention Conversion 

VGGT outputs camera extrinsics in a world-to-camera (W2C) convention following the OpenCV camera model (x-right, y-down, z-forward) [23]. TokenGS, instead ex-pects camera-to-world (C2W) poses in the NeRF/OpenGL convention ( x-right, y-up, z-backward). We bridge these with two deterministic steps. First, we invert each VGGT extrinsic E = [ R | t] to obtain a C2W pose P = E−1, whose rotation is R⊤ and camera center is c = −R⊤t. Second, we convert the axis convention by negating the y and z axes of the ro-tation, i.e., right-multiplying the C2W rotation by M =diag(1 , −1, −1) , yielding the final pose P ′. This axis flip acts only on the rotation, leaving the camera center un-changed, so the convention conversion is orthogonal to the scale correction of Section 4.5. 

4.4. Frame Consistency 

The dominant failure arises when context and target poses come from different coordinate frames (Section 4). Reg-istering external target poses into the context frame via Sim(3) alignment is ill-conditioned on near-collinear cap-tures. We instead place each target in the context frame by construction: the four context views and that target are passed through VGGT in a single forward pass, so all poses share one frame. Only the four context views are used for reconstruction; the target is included solely to obtain its pose. This follows the standard pose-free protocol and keeps reconstruction sparse while guaranteeing frame con-sistency, with no external alignment. 

Validation . To measure the composition against COLMAP under matched information, we additionally run VGGT on all scene views in one pass. Context and tar-get poses are then mutually consistent, and reconstruction from the four context views reaches within 0.51 dB of the COLMAP reference (Section 5)—confirming that VGGT poses drive TokenGS correctly once frame and scale are fixed.

4.5. Scale Correction 

After the convention and frame fixes, VGGT translations re-main systematically smaller than TokenGS expects. VGGT normalizes each scene to its own canonical scale, based on the mean point-to-origin distance of its 3D points, whereas TokenGS was trained on SfM (COLMAP)-scale poses; the two differ by a systematic factor. 1 We calibrate a single correction factor of 7.0 on 100 DL3DV training scenes, dis-joint from the evaluation set, and apply it directly to ev-ery VGGT translation. On the 140 evaluation scenes, the per-scene ratio has median 6.86 and mean 7.22 , confirming the constant is well-centered. The factor is dataset-specific (approximately 6.0 on MipNeRF360) but requires no per-scene computation. It could equivalently be absorbed into TokenGS’s fixed scale constant; we apply it externally to leave the backbone unmodified.

# 6. Conclusion 

We revisited the problem of connecting feed-forward pose estimation to feed-forward 3D reconstruction and showed that the apparent difficulty is not due to pose inaccuracy but to two overlooked, deterministically fixable integra-tion failures: coordinate frame inconsistency and transla-tion scale mismatch. With a convention conversion, all-frame inference, and a simple scale correction—no training, no ground-truth poses, no per-scene optimization—frozen VGGT poses drive a frozen TokenGS backbone to within 0.51 dB of the COLMAP-initialized ceiling at identical in-ference speed. Our per-camera and cross-dataset analyses establish that rotation error is small and scale offset is sys-tematic, reframing a problem previously attributed to pose quality as one of integration design. 

Why not joint training? A natural alternative is to train VGGT and TokenGS jointly, as in AnySplat [7] and To-kenSplat [11]. We deliberately avoid this: joint training is costly and, most importantly, would obscure the diagnosis. By keeping both models frozen, we isolate the integration failures and show they are fixable without any learning—a finding that transfers to future pose estimators and recon-struction backbones. Notably, even VGGT-X [13], which applies learned adaptive alignment to VGGT outputs in the dense-view regime, reports that a gap to COLMAP persists after refinement. This is consistent with our diagnosis: if the difficulty were purely unrefined pose error, such refinement should close it. Our results instead point to coordinate-frame and scale mismatches as the source, which admit a deterministic correction. 

Limitations. First, establishing a shared coordinate frame requires VGGT to process context and target views together. In the standard benchmark protocol, target viewpoints come from the same capture and are available to VGGT. For de-ployment with arbitrary user-specified viewpoints, the tar-get must instead be placed in the context frame; because VGGT estimates poses jointly, the cleanest option is to in-clude the target viewpoint in the same forward pass, rather than post-hoc registration, which our analysis shows is ill-conditioned on near-collinear captures. This affects only the specification of the target viewpoint, not the reconstruc-tion, which is built entirely from the context views. A full interactive implementation is left to future work. Second, the fixed multiplier is dataset-specific ( ×7.0 on DL3DV, ×6.0 on MipNeRF360); a per-scene normaliza-tion from context-view geometry would remove this depen-dence, which we leave to future validation. Finally, our end-to-end evaluation uses a single re-construction backbone (TokenGS) on a single dataset (DL3DV), constrained by checkpoint availability. Our pose-level analysis on MipNeRF360, where the same scale offset appears, suggests the failure modes are not backbone-or dataset-specific, but validating the fixes across other backbones end-to-end is left to future work. We hope this diagnosis guides the practi-cal, training-free combination of the increas-ingly capable feed-forward models now emerg-ing.
