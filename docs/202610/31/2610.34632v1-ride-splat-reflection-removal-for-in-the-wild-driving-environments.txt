Abstract— Driving video captured through a windshield
violates the world-space consistency assumed by 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS): the road scene exhibits parallax while cabin
reflections remain attached to the camera, causing standard
3DGS to embed reflected content into the reconstructed scene.
We introduce RIDE-Splat, a pipeline for reflection-free 3D
reconstruction from posed driving video without clean images,
reflection masks, or pretrained networks. RIDE-Splat assigns
world-consistent transmission through the windshield to a 3DGS
scene and the dominant camera-attached reflection to one
shared, signed RGB image. It builds the clean reconstruction
in two passes. First, it learns the 3D geometry and the
signed reflection image, representing both added radiance and
transmission attenuation. Then, it uses the reflection-removed
training images to fit a clean 3DGS model. At inference, the
reflection image is discarded, preserving the standard 3DGS
rendering path. We also introduce the RIDE-Splat Dataset, the
first 3D reconstruction benchmark for reflection removal from
driving video – with real driving scenes, physical windshield
reflections in training views, and separately captured non-
reflective evaluation images. Across the RIDE-Splat Dataset,
XLD, and Wayve, RIDE-Splat outperforms 3DGS, DC-Gaussian,
and RobustSplat on all nine aggregate metrics, improving PSNR
by up to 3.59 dB and reducing LPIPS by up to 0.0876. We
achieve the highest metrics in 38 out of 42 per-scene comparisons
and removes windshield reflections that remain visible in the
baselines while preserving the transmitted scene.
I. INTRODUCTION
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) enables real-time, photore-
alistic novel-view synthesis [1], but its standard formulation
assumes that posed images depict a coherent world-space
scene. Driving video captured through a windshield violates
this assumption because each frame contains the transmitted
road scene and reflections of the vehicle interior. Camera
motion reveals the key separation cue: the road scene exhibits
parallax and remains consistent in world coordinates, while
the dominant windshield reflection moves with the camera and
remains approximately fixed in image coordinates. Standard
3DGS can therefore fit reflected content as scene geometry
or appearance, contaminating novel views. Single-image
reflection removal cannot exploit this multi-view cue, and
rejecting contaminated observations discards valid transmis-
sion supervision. Conversely, a flexible per-view reflection
field can mistakenly absorb road appearance that belongs
in 3D. These failures motivate a constrained decomposition
that assigns each signal to the coordinate system in which it
remains consistent.
Historical dashcam archives and consumer driving videos
document diverse roads, weather, lighting, and rare events
that would be costly or impossible to recapture. Yet they
remain underused for 3D reconstruction because windshield
reflections make them poorly suited to pipelines designed
for clean, purpose-captured imagery. Recovering reflection-
free 3D scenes from these recordings could support closed-
loop simulation, sensor-realistic augmentation, and rare-event
replay without dedicated recapture.
We therefore introduce RIDE-Splat, a non-generative
3DGS formulation for reflection-free novel-view synthesis
using only posed driving video with windshield reflections. It
assigns world-consistent transmission through the windshield
to a 3DGS scene and the dominant camera-attached reflection
to one shared, signed RGB image. Because the reflection
image is fixed in image coordinates and shared across views,
it can represent image-stationary reflection content but cannot
follow parallax-bearing road texture. Its signed values capture
both added reflected radiance and transmission attenuation.
The first pass uses a geometry warm-up to establish scene
structure and a sparsity penalty to bias ambiguous content
toward the 3D scene. Reflection self-localization then derives

a fixed spatial weight from the recovered reflection image and
refits both representations from scratch with the weight frozen.
This second pass suppresses scene leakage without adding
optimized parameters. At inference, we discard the reflection

Existing benchmarks force a tradeoff: datasets with exact
non-reflective targets use synthetic reflections, while those
with physical windshield reflections lack clean targets. The
RIDE-Splat Dataset closes this gap as the first 3D reconstruc-
tion benchmark designed specifically for reflection removal
from driving video. For each real driving scene, it pairs a
training traversal containing physical windshield reflections
with a separately captured non-reflective evaluation traversal
of the same route. The paired traversals are recorded within
minutes using the same rigid camera mount, with only the
reflection-inducing materials removed. All methods train only
on the reflective traversal, with the non-reflective images held
out as evaluation targets.
Across the RIDE-Splat Dataset, XLD [4], and Wayve [5],
RIDE-Splat outperforms 3DGS, DC-Gaussian [2], and Ro-
bustSplat [3] on every aggregate metric under both physical
and synthetically added reflections. It improves PSNR by
up to 3.59 dB and LPIPS by up to 0.0876. These gains are
consistent across scenes, with RIDE-Splat achieves higher
performance in 38 of 42 per-scene metric comparisons. As
shown in Fig. 1, RIDE-Splat removes windshield reflections
that remain visible in the baselines while better preserving
the transmitted scene.
In summary, we propose the following contributions:
1) RIDE-Splat. A coordinate-based 3DGS method that
separates world-consistent transmission from camera-
attached windshield reflections.
2) RIDE-Splat Dataset. The first 3D reconstruction
benchmark for reflection removal from driving video.
II. RELATED WORK
Reflection removal has primarily been studied in image
space. Single-image methods separate transmission and
reflection using handcrafted or learned priors [6]. Multi-frame
methods use layered motion, low-rank structure, or temporal
consistency to distinguish the two signals [7]–[9]. RIDE-
Splat instead brings this motion cue into 3D reconstruction,
assigning world-consistent transmission to the 3D scene and
the dominant windshield reflection to image coordinates.
Neural radiance fields [10] and 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [1] represent view-dependent radiance in world
coordinates. Reflection-aware variants of these representations
model glossy appearance, separate transmitted and reflected
radiance, or reconstruct specular and semi-transparent sur-
faces [11]–[15]. Flash-Splat [16] separates transmission and
reflection with Gaussian primitives but requires paired flash
and no-flash captures. RIDE-Splat instead targets driving
videos and learns a windshield-reflection layer fixed in image
coordinates from reflective monocular video alone.
In-the-wild 3D reconstruction methods suppress transient
or view-inconsistent distractors through sample rejection or
transient modeling [17]–[19]. DF3DV-1K [20] benchmarks
anti-distractor 3DGS methods [18], [21]–[26], but does not
include reflections of the vehicle interior on the windshield.
RobustSplat [3], the strongest single-model baseline, de-
lays Gaussian densification and bootstraps transient masks
across scales. Our reflective weight maps are inspired by
SplatSuRe [27] which derives per-pixel weight maps from
multi-view sampling fidelity, but targets multi-view-consistent
super-resolution rather than reflection removal. Driving-
scene methods reconstruct large environments and dynamic
actors [28]–[31], but do not model windshield reflections.
DC-Gaussian [2] is the only prior 3D reconstruction method
designed specifically to remove windshield reflections. It
predicts a per-view reflection field from camera pose using
a hash grid and MLP, while a latent code modulates the
field’s intensity over time. RIDE-Splat instead uses one
shared, signed image to impose a coordinate-based separation
prior; this representation is orthogonal to DC-Gaussian’s
geometry enhancement. In Sec. VI, RIDE-Splat outperforms
DC-Gaussian and RobustSplat across all metrics.

B. Coordinate-Based Joint Optimization
For a world point X, its pixel pi(X) = πi(X) changes with
camera motion, but the same 3D Gaussian can approximate its
appearance in every view. For a rigidly mounted camera, the
dominant cabin reflection exhibits little image-space motion.
Encoding this reflection in the 3DGS scene G would require
inconsistent world geometry. Conversely, encoding parallax-
bearing road texture in the fixed image R would incur error
across views. Joint optimization therefore favors scene content
in G and camera-static residuals in R. The separation is not
unique because R can also absorb camera-static scene content
such as the vehicle hood or a co-moving vehicle.

IV. RIDE-SPLAT DATASET
Existing driving-scene 3D reconstruction benchmarks either
retain exact clean targets by adding synthetic reflections or
capture physical reflections without non-reflective targets.
Neither setting directly measures transmission recovery under
physical windshield reflections. To fill this gap, we introduce
the RIDE-Splat Dataset, the first 3D reconstruction bench-
mark designed specifically for reflection removal from driving
video. It pairs reflective training traversals of real driving
scenes with separately captured non-reflective evaluation
traversals, enabling quantitative evaluation against actual non-
reflective images. Table Ia compares the evaluation datasets.
We recorded H.264 clips in clear daytime conditions using
a Garmin S35 dashcam at 1920×1080 and 29.97 FPS. To
diversify reflection appearance, we placed common cabin
objects made of paper, laminated paper, aluminum, plastic,
painted wood, and steel on the dashboard. The object set
differs by scene but remains fixed within each reflective
traversal, producing scene-specific, approximately camera-
static reflections. Figure 3 shows representative reflective
training views from all three scenes. To prepare the footage
for 3D reconstruction, we removed the 40-pixel-tall dashcam
metadata banner, undistorted and cropped the frames to
1900×1025, and downsampled them to reduce dataset size.
Table Ib reports the downsampled frame rate and number of
training and evaluation frames for each scene.
The evaluated benchmark contains three scenes, Leesburg,
Martin, and Reston, spanning a historic town center, a tree-
lined institutional district, and suburban residential streets.

These settings vary in traffic, building density, tree cover,
and lateral parallax. For each scene, we recorded a reflective
training traversal and a non-reflective reference traversal of
the same route within minutes of one another. The camera
remained in the same rigid mount, and we removed only the

cross-traversal confounds, we visually selected sequences with
few transient objects and minimal visible change. We jointly
registered both traversals with COLMAP [32], placing their
camera poses in a common coordinate frame. All geometry
and camera poses were recovered from RGB footage alone,
without LiDAR, odometry, depth, or other vehicle sensors.
Only reflective frames are used for training, without
reflection masks or annotations, so methods must infer con-
taminated regions directly. Evaluation frames are uniformly
sampled from each non-reflective traversal. Each method
renders at these held-out camera poses and is scored against
the corresponding non-reflective images.
The fixed camera mount, short capture interval, and
sequence selection reduce cross-traversal variation, but traffic,
illumination, and camera trajectories cannot be matched
exactly. The resulting references are therefore approximate.
Nevertheless, they contain minimal windshield reflections
and are never used during optimization, so they directly


evaluate transmission recovery under physical reflections. We
will publicly release the RIDE-Splat Dataset, including the
evaluated benchmark and additional captured scenes.
V. EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate RIDE-Splat on fully synthetic scenes, real
driving imagery with synthetic reflections, and real driving
imagery with physical windshield reflections. Together, these
settings test whether it consistently recovers the transmitted
scene as both scene content and reflection formation become
more realistic. All methods receive identical reflective training
images and camera poses, while non-reflective targets are used
only for evaluation. Beyond the main comparison, we isolate
the contributions of the Reflection Layer and self-localization,
evaluate hyperparameter sensitivity, and stress-test the camera-
static reflection prior under increasing drift.
A. Datasets
We evaluate on three datasets that vary both scene content
and reflection formation. The RIDE-Splat Dataset pairs real
driving scenes with physical windshield reflections. We
use the Leesburg, Martin, and Reston splits in Table I.
XLD [4] pairs synthetic scenes with synthetic reflections,
while Wayve [5] combines real driving imagery with synthetic
reflections.
For XLD, we evaluate all six scenes: Town01, Town02,
Town03, Town04, Town05, and Town10. We add a camera-
static synthetic windshield reflection to 150 training images
per town and evaluate all 28 clean views captured at a 1 m
left lateral offset. For Wayve, we use the first five 10-second
sequences, scene_001–scene_005, and add static synthetic
reflections to 176 training images per sequence. Every eighth
original frame is held out, yielding 24 clean evaluation images
per sequence. Appendix Sec. A details the synthetic-reflection
construction and parameters for both datasets.
B. Baselines and Metrics
We use the official implementations and published settings
for all baselines. Every method receives identical training
images and camera poses and is evaluated against identical
targets. We compare against 3DGS [1], DC-Gaussian [2],
and RobustSplat [3]. DC-Gaussian specifically targets driving
video with windshield reflections, while RobustSplat is the
strongest single-model anti-distractor method in DF3DV-
1K [20]. We report full-image PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.
C. DC-Gaussian Evaluation Protocol
DC-Gaussian [2] introduces DCVR, a self-captured real-
world dataset with strong reflections, and also evaluates
on BDD100K [33]. Both are real-world dashcam datasets
with windshield obstructions, but neither provides non-
reflective transmission targets. DC-Gaussian decomposes
each view into a transmission image and an image-space
obstruction image, then combines them using a learned
opacity map. Its published PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS compare
this recomposed output with held-out observed frames. These
metrics therefore measure whether the two predicted layers
reproduce the observed obstructed frame rather than whether
the transmission recovers the reflection-free scene. Our
metrics instead compare transmission renders directly with
non-reflective targets: exact clean images for XLD and Wayve,
and separately captured images for the RIDE-Splat Dataset.
On real-world data, DC-Gaussian transmission renders
exhibit substantial underexposure and color shifts. We report
the unmodified renders as DC-Gaussian. To prevent these
errors from dominating the comparison, the + Oracle GT
Color Correction variant uses the released routine to fit
one global quadratic color transform from each transmission
render to its test target over all unsaturated pixel correspon-
dences. This transform is not part of the published method
or default evaluation and could be applied to any renderer.
Because it uses test-target oracle information, the variant
deliberately favors DC-Gaussian and is excluded from best-
result highlighting.
D. Implementation Details
RIDE-Splat uses the original 3DGS optimization settings
for G. We optimize R with Adam at a learning rate ηR = 0.01
and set β = 0.01. Following RobustSplat [3], we delay
activation of the reflection image behind a geometry warm-
up, ending at t0 = 2000 iterations. This places activation
after densification begins at iteration 500, and well before
it concludes, thus scene geometry is established before R
can compete for it. The self-localization pass uses σ = 8
and repeats the same schedule with W fixed. We ablate ηR,
β, and σ in Sec. VI-B. No pretrained image generator or
reflection-removal network is used.

A. Component Ablation
Table III applies the method components cumulatively
across XLD. Our coordinate-based joint-optimization method,
described in Sec. III-B, provides the primary improvement
across all metrics. Reflection self-localization, described
in Sec. III-C, further improves all metrics without adding
optimized parameters. The ablation confirms that coordinate-
based separation drives the gain and that self-localization
further refines the reconstructed scene.
B. Hyperparameter Sensitivity
We vary one hyperparameter at a time around the defaults
ηR = 0.01, β = 0.01, and σ = 8. Table IV reports the
results on Town01. Performance remains stable across the
tested learning rates. Reducing β to 0.002 increases the mean
recovered |R| by 40% while leaving PSNR and SSIM nearly
unchanged, suggesting that sharing the reflection image across
views, rather than sparsity alone, prevents it from absorbing
scene content. Increasing β to 0.05 over-regularizes the
reflection image and degrades all metrics. Among the tested
smoothing scales, σ = 32 yields the best LPIPS, whereas the
narrower support at σ = 2 lowers PSNR.
C. Robustness to Reflection Motion
We stress-test the camera-static assumption on Town01 with
a single seed using the Reflection Layer from the Coordinate-
Based Joint Optimization row from Table III. Within a fixed
support, the reflection appearance Lr is translated horizontally
by the specified number of pixels per frame over 150 frames.
At the largest drift of 1.2 pixels per frame, the cumulative
displacement reaches 180 pixels, or 9.4% of the image width.
Across the sweep, Reflection Layer PSNR varies by only
0.65 dB, compared with 1.45 dB for DC-Gaussian. The shared
reflection model therefore tolerates moderate horizontal drift,
although reflections with independent parallax or deformation
remain outside its scope.
VII. LIMITATIONS
RIDE-Splat assumes that the dominant windshield reflec-
tion is semi-transparent and approximately fixed in image
coordinates. The motion stress test indicates tolerance to
moderate drift, but substantial independent motion or defor-
mation may leave residual contamination. As a 3DGS-based
method, it also inherits challenges under sparse forward-facing
views, although its reflection formulation is orthogonal to
driving-scene extensions such as Street Gaussians [28] and
can be combined with them for large, dynamic environments.

Evaluating real windshield reflections against non-reflective
targets requires separate traversals, making exact temporal and
environmental matching impossible and leaving the references
approximate. Our physical-reflection evaluation currently
covers a three-scene subset of the broader RIDE-Splat Dataset,
and additional captured scenes will be publicly released.
VIII. APPLICATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
RIDE-Splat can convert existing dashcam video into
reflection-free 3D assets for closed-loop simulation and rare-
event replay. Beyond driving, its camera-coordinate prior
may extend to robot cameras behind protective windows or
transparent housings, where enclosure reflections move with
the camera rather than the environment. Future work can
relax the shared-image assumption through constrained low-
dimensional reflection motion and adapt the self-localization
weight using multi-view consistency, without introducing
unrestricted per-view capacity that can absorb scene content.
Expanding the RIDE-Splat Dataset across weather, lighting,
camera systems, and reflection dynamics, together with more
synchronized reference capture, will broaden evaluation and
improve reference fidelity.
IX. CONCLUSION
RIDE-Splat reframes windshield reflection removal as a
coordinate-separation problem for 3D reconstruction from
posed driving video. It assigns world-consistent transmission
through the windshield to a 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1]
scene and the dominant camera-attached reflection to a shared,
signed image, using camera motion to separate them without
clean supervision. Reflection self-localization further restricts


where the reflection layer can act in a second fit without
adding optimized parameters, and discarding the layer at
inference preserves the standard 3DGS rendering path. The
RIDE-Splat Dataset provides the first 3D reconstruction
benchmark for reflection removal from driving video, pairing
training traversals containing physical windshield reflections
with held-out non-reflective captures. Across the RIDE-Splat
Dataset, XLD [4], and Wayve [5], RIDE-Splat outperforms
3DGS, DC-Gaussian [2], and RobustSplat [3] on every
aggregate metric; per-scene and qualitative comparisons
confirm that this advantage reflects cleaner transmission rather
than a global appearance correction. Component ablations
identify the shared reflection image as the primary source of
improvement and self-localization as a consistent refinement.
More broadly, modeling structured contamination in the
coordinate system where it remains stable provides a simple
principle for reconstructing scenes from driving archives and
other video captured through glass.
