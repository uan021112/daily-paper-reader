RAIWAY: A Large-Wheeled Bipedal Robot with Contact-Informed
Blind Locomotion
Kyungmin Nam, Jiseong Lee, Donghyuk Choi, Jeongsoo Park, Woojin Kim, and Jemin Hwangbo
Abstract—We present RAIWAY, an in-house wheeled bipedal
robot with 20-inch wheels, together with a contact-informed
control approach for rolling locomotion across irregular and
discontinuous terrain. Such locomotion is fundamentally contact-
dominated, governed by local wheel–terrain interaction that is
impractical to instrument directly and depends on more than
terrain geometry alone. The proposed framework exploits the
contact information available in simulation, distilling multi-
contact events into a latent representation. At deployment, the
controller infers this representation online from proprioception,
requiring no contact sensing. In real-world experiments, the
proposed system traverses diverse environments including steep
slopes, gravel beds, studded surfaces, and rocky terrain, and
demonstrates continuous ascent on the tested outdoor staircases
with riser heights up to 15 cm. On our platform, the proposed
controller further outperforms baselines without the contact-
informed mechanism across challenging indoor and outdoor set-
tings. The video can be found at https://youtu.be/Ch0divLg64w.

I. INTRODUCTION
L
EGGED robots have demonstrated remarkable robustness
by utilizing articulated legs to traverse uneven terrains
and overcome complex obstacles [1]–[3]. Wheeled systems,
in contrast, provide high locomotion efficiency and speed on
benign surfaces [4]. To combine the strengths of both systems,
wheel-legged robots have recently emerged as a promising
hybrid platform [5].
However, the advantage of wheels fades at terrain disconti-
nuities. Most wheel-legged platforms rely on stepping motions
that treat the wheels as footholds [6]–[8]. This approach
is well-suited to systems with relatively small wheels but
compromises the speed and efficiency of continuous rolling.
An alternative is to roll over discontinuities by exploiting
wheel traction and drive torque. With large enough wheels, a
larger radius engages obstacle edges at a shallower approach
angle while tire compliance absorbs impacts, so that moderate
discontinuities are overcome by the wheel itself. Although
early attempts have been made [9], [10], robust traversal of

Unlike many locomotion problems that can be addressed
primarily through visual perception [2], [7], rolling-based
locomotion is fundamentally a contact-dominated problem,
governed by local wheel–terrain interaction. The optimal be-
havior at each instant is highly sensitive to this interaction,
yet the decisive contact state is unobservable to the robot.
A rolling wheel cannot be densely instrumented along its
continuously rotating surface, and external sensing captures
the surrounding terrain rather than the contact interface itself.
The signal is moreover complex, as small variations can
produce repeated impact, restitution, transient loss of contact,
and slip [11], depending not only on rigid-body geometry but
also on tire compliance and internal materials [12].
Prior work has approached this challenge on the modeling
side, refining the simulator until contact dynamics transfer
faithfully to hardware. This succeeds for point-foot con-
tact [13], but the contact of a wheel is far harder to formulate,
and modeling the wheel deformation explicitly [10] has left
a substantial sim-to-real gap. Therefore, rather than chasing
a faithful contact model, we exploit the contact information
available in simulation to learn a contact-informed latent repre-
sentation, which is subsequently inferred from proprioceptive
history at deployment. A similar idea has proven effective
in contact-rich manipulation, where contact-related state that
cannot be observed at deployment is recovered implicitly
through learning [14].
On our platform, the large wheels do more than carry the
robot over obstacles. Because contact spreads across spatially
distinct locations on the wheel surface, they yield a rich signal
of the wheel–terrain interaction, which we treat as privileged
information during training. Accordingly, we formulate a task-
and platform-specific representation of wheel–terrain multi-
contact information and integrate it into Regularized Online
Adaptation (ROA) [15]. To prepare the noisy raw contact
data for distillation, we design a temporal filter and a sorting
scheme that produce a refined, fixed-size contact vector.
We deploy the proposed contact-informed controller on
our in-house wheeled bipedal robot across diverse outdoor
environments, as shown in Fig. 1. The test set spans con-
tinuous contact variation at high speed on slopes and gravel,
spatially unpredictable contact on studded and rocky terrain,
and periodic discontinuities on staircases with riser heights up
to 15 cm. Along the way, it encounters contact events such as
wheel slip, whole-body lateral drift, and hard impacts, while
continuing locomotion in these representative deployments.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Existing Wheeled Bipedal Robots and Blind Locomotion
Wheeled bipedal robots have recently gained attention as
compact platforms that combine the efficiency of wheels with
the obstacle-negotiation ability of legs. Ascento introduced a
compact wheeled bipedal design with four-bar-linkage legs,
each constrained to a single actuated DoF and optimized
primarily for hopping over obstacles [16]. Its successor, As-
cento Pro, uses larger wheels and serial 2-DoF legs, on
which Klemm et al. [11] planned discontinuity traversal with
a non-smooth trajectory optimization that handles contact
switches and impacts, while Chamorro et al. [9] demonstrated
reinforcement-learning-based blind ascent of a 15 cm step us-
ing an asymmetric actor–critic with a stair-climbing mode flag.
More recently, the TRON1 platform pursues agility by pairing
small wheels with 3-DoF legs, on which Li et al. [8] pro-
posed contact-triggered blind climbing, where wheel–obstacle
contact initiates a learned leg-lifting maneuver.
Across these platforms, discontinuities are mainly handled
by interrupting or supplementing rolling with dedicated leg-
mediated behavior. This separation between wheeled travel and
leg-based clearance suits specific obstacle classes but does
not extend to general terrain, leaving the wheel underused
as a terrain-adaptive contact body. Large compliant wheels
instead negotiate moderate discontinuities mechanically: a
larger radius reduces the effective approach angle, while tire
compliance absorbs impacts, maintains traction, and smooths
edge engagement. Our platform therefore adopts large off-road
tires, making rolling the primary traversal mode, with the legs
regulating posture and load transfer.
B. Explicit and Implicit Use of Privileged Information
In reinforcement learning, leveraging privileged information
during training has proven effective for enhancing performance
and narrowing the sim-to-real gap [17], [18]. Privileged infor-
mation has been used in two closely related ways: explicit
estimation of deployable states and implicit adaptation via
latent variables.

For instance, Ji et al. [19] concurrently trained a locomo-
tion policy with an estimator to predict privileged quantities.
Alternatively, Kumar et al. [20] proposed a two-stage teacher–
student pipeline that reconstructs a latent encoding of privi-
leged factors from proprioceptive histories. Fu et al. [15] later
proposed Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA), which jointly
trains the teacher and student modules while regularizing the
privileged latent to remain predictable from onboard history.
Jeon et al. [21] applied ROA to whole-body manipulation
under unknown object properties.
Contact-related quantities have also been used as privi-
leged inputs. Lee et al. [1] included foot contact states and
forces, while Miki et al. [2] further included contact normals.
More recently, Wang et al. [22] included foot contact forces
among the privileged inputs in a concurrent teacher–student
framework. In these works, contact enters as one of many
privileged quantities used to improve locomotion in general.
Our problem, however, is itself contact-dominated, so contact
is the signal of interest rather than one input among many.
It is also richer here than at a point foot, which is what the
proposed representation is designed to capture.
III. METHOD
A. RAIWAY: The Large-Wheeled Bipedal Robot
Fig. 2. Design of RAIWAY: The main view shows the overall geometry and
key dimensions, with insets detailing the leg mechanism, the wheel-actuator
mount, and a cross-section of the closed-cell foam tire insert.
The proposed system is a 6-DoF wheeled bipedal robot,
shown in Fig. 2. Each leg comprises two pitch joints and one
wheel joint, and the robot employs differential steering without
hip roll or yaw joints. We reuse validated subsystems from
our previous in-house quadruped platform RAIBO2 [23]—
including the actuators, link mechanism, motor drivers, and
onboard power system—while redesigning the body frame and
leg layout for a two-legged morphology. The robot weighs
approximately 28 kg including the battery and stands 1.14 m
tall in its nominal configuration. It is equipped with an onboard
Jetson AGX Orin computer, a MicroStrain 3DM-GX5-AR
IMU, and joint encoders on all actuated joints.
Each wheel is mounted directly to the actuator output,
using 20-inch off-road bicycle tires. To improve robustness,
we replace pneumatic inflation with closed-cell polymer foam
inserts, which provide more repeatable compliance during
impacts and avoid both the excessive rebound of high tire
pressure and the rim strikes of low pressure. This predictability
also benefits sim-to-real transfer, as a more consistent tire re-
sponse narrows the gap between the contact dynamics experi-
enced in simulation and those encountered during deployment.

D. Training Details
We train the policy with PPO [24] in Raisim [25] using
200 parallel environments for 60,000 iterations, on an AMD
Ryzen 5995WX CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti
GPU in approximately 42 hours.
We apply domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer,
with ranges in Table I. Extending Lee et al. [7], who ran-
domized the wheel radius, we randomize various collision-
body parameters to better represent actual tire dynamics, and
randomize terrain parameters to reflect the slip and rebound
observed in real experiments. Discontinuous features such as
steps and stairs are generated with heights up to 15 cm. The
reward design reflects our primary objective of continuous
rolling driven by wheel rotation rather than stepping: we omit
the gait-shaping and foot-clearance rewards typical of legged
locomotion, and instead apply a contact penalty when a wheel
loses ground contact and a slip term to discourage excessive
slip, as listed in Table II.

B. Ablation Study
We compare the following controllers, all sharing the same
observation, network architecture, reward, and domain ran-
domization:
• Plain AC: Plain asymmetric actor–critic.
• AC + estimator: Plain AC augmented with a concurrent
estimator, following [19].
• Ours w/o Estimator: Ours w/o concurrent estimator.
• Ours w/o Filtering: Ours w/o temporal filtering.
The ablation study consists of two representative deploy-
ment tasks. Experiment 1 targets a 16 cm step, correspond-
ing to roughly 63% of the wheel radius. Each controller is
evaluated over 20 trials: Fail if the robot topples; Unstable if
a completed ascent has a base yaw deviation above 0.5 rad
or a peak pitch angular velocity |ωy| above 1.5 rad/s; and
Success otherwise. Experiment 2 evaluates sustained traversal
over distributed irregular contacts, a 20 m round-trip on
the same rocky terrain shown in Fig. 1(F). The traversal
distance is measured at ten checkpoints along the round-trip
route, reported as the percentage of the route completed, and
averaged across 5 trials. Results are reported in Table IV.

mechanism, struggle across both experiments but in opposite
ways. AC + estimator improves step climbing but performs
worse on rocky terrain. A post hoc simulation analysis showed
larger errors on rocky terrain for eight of ten estimator out-
puts, including all six wheel-specific quantities. Such diverse
contacts are hard to capture with a few scalars, so the policy
that acts directly on these estimates inherits their inaccuracy.

C. Outdoor Deployment across Diverse Terrains
We deployed the learned policy across a range of out-
door environments shown in Fig. 1, with dimensions and
command speeds summarized in Table V. A single policy
was used across all environments without retuning, terrain-
specific switching, or external perception. The environments
fall into three groups, each probing a different aspect of
contact-dominated rolling.
The first group (Fig. 1 A–D) involves terrain without severe
discontinuities, allowing traversal at high speed while the
contact conditions vary throughout. The rapid stair descent
(A) subjects the wheel to repeated impacts, the steep slopes
(B,C) shift the contact normal and traction demand with the
slope angle, and the gravel bed (D) yields and shifts under the
wheel. Across all four environments, the controller maintained
continuous rolling at high commanded speeds without falls.
The second group (Fig. 1 E–G) targets diverse and spatially
unpredictable contacts. On the studded slope (E) and the
rocky terrain (F,G), the contact location on the wheel changes
abruptly depending on which protrusion or rock face the
wheel happens to engage, and cannot be anticipated from the
overall terrain profile. Fig. 7 shows these local interactions
in the estimator and latent traces. In the rock-clearing case,
the left-wheel slip estimate turns negative during wheel spin
before (ii) and positive between (ii) and (iii) during clearance,
while the latent components separate slightly earlier than this
explicit response. In the studded-slope case, the lateral body
motion appears as a sustained lateral-foot-velocity estimate.
The estimator and latent thus remain responsive during abrupt,
short-duration contact changes.

The final group (Fig. 1 H–J) involves periodic disconti-

robot succeeded in 3 of 10 trials, with a mean ascent speed

D. Failure Cases
Two failure modes were observed during deployment. First,
the robot succeeded in all 20 trials on the indoor 16 cm
wooden step, but on the ten-step 15 cm granite staircase
(Fig. 1 J) it reached the top in only 3 of 10 trials (85.4%
per step) despite the lower riser. The wooden step’s sharp

edge gives the tire tread blocks a corner to catch on; the
granite staircase, with rounded edges and a rough, uneven
surface, offers none, so both wheels intermittently slipped
and the robot pitched forward and toppled. Our rigid-contact
simulator models neither such edge and surface variation nor
the tread engagement and compliance of the real tire, leaving
this contact mismatch unresolved.
Second, strong wheel rebound occasionally caused high-
frequency oscillations in the actions and joint torques. We
attribute this to wheel-restitution mismatch: the measured tire
restitution is about 0.61, but training sampled only up to 0.3
because larger values made the policy overly conservative.
Consequently, rebound events on hardware can expose the
policy to observation sequences outside the training distri-
bution. The post-landing oscillation after (iv) in Fig. 7(a) is
one such response that decayed harmlessly. In trials where
the oscillation persisted, it propagated into unfavorable whole-
body motion that ended in a fall. Addressing these failure
modes will require training over more diverse edge and surface
geometries, along with better contact modeling or fine-tuning
on real-world data.
V. CONCLUSION
We presented RAIWAY, a large-wheeled bipedal robot and
a contact-informed control approach for blind rolling locomo-
tion over irregular and discontinuous terrain. The framework
distills simulator-derived multi-contact events into a latent
representation that the deployed controller infers from propri-
oceptive history, enabling contact-responsive locomotion with-
out external perception or contact sensing. Across the tested
conditions, the resulting system traversed steep slopes, gravel
beds, studded surfaces, and rocky terrain, and demonstrated
continuous ascent on the tested outdoor staircases with riser
heights up to 15 cm, spanning a range of outdoor terrain
and discontinuous contact conditions. The benefits of contact-
informed control were demonstrated on our large-wheeled
platform, where obstacle traversal is driven primarily by wheel
rolling.
Future work will focus on incorporating exteroceptive per-
ception so that anticipatory terrain awareness and the present
contact-responsive behavior reinforce each other, enabling
traversal at higher speeds over more complex discontinuities.
