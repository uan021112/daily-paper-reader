Abstract
Recently, LLM-based autonomous agents have demon-
strated strong capabilities in iteratively generating, eval-
uating, and refining executable solutions. In robotic ma-
nipulation, Code-as-Policy enables these agents to search
over modular and editable robot programs. However, ef-
fective program search requires physical understanding:
agents must understand how objects can be manipulated,
how scene geometry constrains actions, and why interac-
tions succeed or fail. Existing methods rely mainly on exe-
cution feedback for program refinement, often leaving these
physical questions unresolved. To address this limitation,
we introduce RoboAuto, a framework for robot program
search that explicitly organizes the physical understanding
at the object, scene, and interaction levels through three
complementary components. First, the hierarchical knowl-
edge base combines executable skills with object-centric
knowledge of affordances, spatial relations, and action ef-
fects, which can be learned from embodied data and on-
line task solving. Second, scene grounding connects this
knowledge to the current environment by identifying rele-
vant objects and resolving action-critical quantities. Third,

multi-stage planning with self-designed experiments checks
whether each stage produces its expected physical effects
and actively tests competing hypotheses to resolve physi-
cal uncertainties. RoboAuto achieves superior performance
across five benchmark families, attaining an 89.1% success
rate on short-horizon LIBERO-PRO tasks and more than
twice the mean success rate of ASPIRE on LIBERO-PRO
Long and LIBERO-Plus. It also outperforms ASPIRE and
π0.5 on three challenging long-horizon real-robot tasks re-
quiring precise manipulation. We further demonstrate that
the hierarchical knowledge base transfers across simulators
and embodiments, and that program search performance
scales with the size of the knowledge base. Project page:

RoboAuto (Fig. 2), a framework for autonomous robot
program search through multi-level physical understanding.
Its hierarchical knowledge base combines object-centric
physical knowledge about affordances, spatial relations,
and action effects with executable skills. Physical knowl-
edge is acquired from embodied data and refined through
online experience, while the skill library expands as
verified programs are discovered through task solving.
For each task, RoboAuto retrieves relevant knowledge
and skills and grounds them in the current scene, using
visual tools, including Molmo2 [10] and SAM 3 [4], to
identify task-relevant objects and measure action-critical
quantities.
Through multi-stage planning with grounded
verification, it plans over stage dependencies and verifies
each stage’s physical outcome before proceeding. When
available evidence leaves a physical uncertainty unre-
solved, RoboAuto formulates competing hypotheses and
conducts self-designed experiments to distinguish them.
The resulting evidence refines the physical knowledge,
while verified programs expand the skill library, improving
both the current solution and future program search.
We evaluate RoboAuto on tasks from five benchmark
families. On LIBERO-PRO Long [52], RoboAuto achieves
a mean success rate of 73.4%, compared with 30.8% for
ASPIRE [25], the strongest coding-agent baseline on av-
erage.
On LIBERO-Plus [12], it reaches 90.3%, com-
pared with ASPIRE’s 44.7%, while achieving 89.1% on
the short-horizon LIBERO-PRO [52] tasks. On contact-rich
Robosuite [54] tasks, RoboAuto obtains 90.6% mean suc-
cess, compared with ASPIRE’s 81.0%. To evaluate knowl-
edge transfer, we apply the hierarchical knowledge base ac-
quired in LIBERO [24] to RoboTwin 2.0 [6], which features
a different simulator and a bimanual robot embodiment.
RoboAuto achieves a 91.5% success rate, compared with
24.5% without a knowledge base. Knowledge-base scaling
experiments further show that larger knowledge bases con-
sistently improve program-search performance. RoboAuto
also outperforms ASPIRE and π0.5 on three challenging
long-horizon real-robot tasks requiring precise manipula-
tion.
Our contributions are:
• We identify key limitations of existing autonomous Code-

• We introduce RoboAuto, which addresses these limi-
tations through a hierarchical knowledge base, scene
grounding, multi-stage planning with grounded verifica-
tion, and autonomously designed experiments.
• Across five benchmark families, RoboAuto substantially
improves program-search performance and demonstrates

2. Related Work
Robotic foundation models. Learned visuomotor policies
provide increasingly general foundations for robot control.
Diffusion-based policies model multimodal action distribu-
tions and have been extended to improve spatial general-
ization, inference efficiency, and closed-loop responsive-
ness [8, 9, 14, 27, 45]. At a larger scale, vision-language-
action models learn generalist language-conditioned con-
trol from diverse tasks, environments, and robot embodi-
ments [2, 3, 19, 21, 30–32, 50, 55]. World-action mod-
els complement this paradigm by coupling action gen-
eration with predictions of future visual or latent world
states [16, 42, 44, 48, 49, 53]. Whereas these robotic foun-
dation models acquire behavior primarily by training pa-
rameterized action policies on large-scale data, RoboAuto
introduces a fundamentally different paradigm in which ex-
plicit robot programs are autonomously searched and re-
fined through grounded physical understanding, execution-
based verification, and active exploration.

More broadly, autonomous

3. Method
RoboAuto enables autonomous robot program search
through multi-level physical understanding. It consists of
three components (Fig. 2). The Hierarchical Knowledge
Base (Sec. 3.1) maintains object-centric physical knowl-
edge learned from embodied data and executable skills
acquired through online task solving.
Physical Scene
Grounding (Sec. 3.2) identifies task-relevant objects and
measures action-critical quantities to instantiate candidate
programs in the current scene. Search and Exploration
(Sec. 3.3) decomposes long-horizon tasks, verifies interme-
diate outcomes, and designs targeted experiments to resolve
physical uncertainties.
3.1. Hierarchical Knowledge Base
Robot program search benefits from reusable experience at
two different levels of abstraction. General physical knowl-
edge describes what interactions are appropriate and what
effects they should produce, while executable skills encode
how those interactions are implemented with perception and
control primitives.
RoboAuto separates these two forms
of experience in a hierarchical knowledge base. Its high-
level layer contains object-centric physical knowledge that
transfers across tasks and scenes, while its low-level layer
contains executable skills that can be directly reused and
composed during program search. This separation allows
physical knowledge to guide the selection, grounding, and
verification of robot programs without tying it to a particu-
lar implementation.
Object-centric physical knowledge. Although online task
solving can continually expand an agent’s experience, dis-
covering appropriate interaction strategies solely through
program search can be difficult.
Without object-centric
physical knowledge, the agent may continue refining an un-
suitable strategy instead of recognizing that a fundamentally
different interaction is required. RoboAuto therefore orga-
nizes reusable physical knowledge into entries indexed by
object class and constructs these entries from both embod-
ied demonstration data and online task-solving experience.
Each entry captures three complementary forms of knowl-
edge. Affordances describe suitable interaction strategies
for an object; spatial relations characterize task-relevant
configurations such as support, containment, and relative
position; and action effects and preconditions specify the
state changes produced by an action and the conditions re-
quired for those changes. RoboAuto constructs these entries
from embodied data by analyzing demonstrated interac-
tions, object configurations, and state transitions, and con-
tinually refines them using evidence collected during on-
line task solving. To make this knowledge applicable across
scenes, each entry also specifies the conditions under which
a strategy is applicable and the scene-dependent quanti-
ties required to instantiate it. For each quantity, the entry
records its purpose and how it can be measured, while leav-
ing its value to be established in the current scene. Phys-
ical Scene Grounding (Sec. 3.2) measures these quantities
for the current objects and robot, allowing the same object-
centric knowledge to guide program search across different
scenes.
Executable skills. The second layer stores verified robot
programs as executable skills.
Whereas object-centric
knowledge captures transferable physical regularities, exe-
cutable skills implement concrete behaviors using available
perception and control primitives. RoboAuto retrieves these
skills as candidate implementations or building blocks dur-
ing program search, reducing the need to rediscover previ-
ously successful behaviors. Programs verified through on-
line task solving are added to the skill library, allowing the
executable layer to expand continually. Appendix C.1 pro-
vides more details.
3.2. Physical Scene Grounding
Object-centric knowledge describes reusable interaction
strategies and their grounding requirements, but its scene-
dependent quantities must be established for each task.
Given an instruction and the current observation, RoboAuto
identifies the task-relevant objects, assigns their roles in the
intended interaction, and associates them with the retrieved
knowledge entries and executable skills. It then follows the
entries’ measurement procedures to check strategy applica-
bility and obtain the required quantities. Rather than con-
structing an exhaustive representation of the entire scene,
this process is scoped by the task and the intended interac-
tion. RoboAuto combines visual perception models, includ-
ing Molmo2 [10] and SAM 3 [4], with depth-camera ob-
servations to identify and segment relevant objects and ob-
tain action-critical information such as grasp locations, met-
ric dimensions, orientations, and spatial relations. Ground-
ing can also involve active viewpoint selection. We intro-
4


duce active viewpoint selection, which enables the agent
to move the robot arm to obtain a more informative cam-
era view when the initial observation is insufficient, thereby
improving the accuracy of grounding task-relevant quanti-
ties. The resulting grounded representation binds general
physical knowledge to concrete objects and measurements,
providing the coding agent with the information needed to
select a feasible strategy and instantiate it as an executable
program. Scene grounding also records which physical con-
ditions have been established and which remain uncertain.
Grounded values inform program generation and planning,
while unresolved conditions are passed to Search and Ex-
ploration (Sec. 3.3) for further investigation. Grounding re-
mains open to revision throughout program search. An exe-
cution outcome may invalidate an earlier measurement, re-
veal a previously unmodeled condition, or change the scene
itself. RoboAuto can then remeasure relevant quantities and
reassess knowledge applicability to guide the next candidate
program. In this way, scene grounding provides a continu-
ally updated connection between reusable physical knowl-
edge and the physical state in which the robot acts. Ap-
pendix C.2 provides more details.
3.3. Search and Exploration
Multi-stage planning with grounded verification. Long-
horizon tasks require prerequisite physical changes to be
verified before subsequent actions proceed. RoboAuto de-
composes each task into stages, each defined by an intended
change in object state. Physical dependencies determine
their order, while expected effects define the verification cri-
teria. RoboAuto then searches sequentially over the stages.
For the current stage, it generates and executes a candidate
program together with an executable verifier that evaluates
the resulting state from a fresh observation. Search pro-
ceeds to the next stage only after the current stage has been
verified; otherwise, subsequent execution is stopped and re-
vision remains focused on the incomplete stage. This pro-
cedure prevents failures from propagating to later stages, lo-
calizes search to a specific physical transition, and preserves
the verified program prefix instead of repeatedly regenerat-
ing the entire long-horizon solution.
Because the executable verifier may misinterpret an ob-
servation, RoboAuto uses an independent auditor to re-
view unsuccessful task executions. Given the stage plan,
relevant physical knowledge, execution logs, and synchro-
nized scene and wrist-camera videos, the auditor determines
whether each stage achieved its intended object state, and its
judgments replace the corresponding verifier results. The
coding agent uses this feedback to determine whether the
stage program, executable verifier, or overall plan requires
revision. When the evidence indicates that a stage is in-
correctly defined, ordered under the wrong dependency,
or physically infeasible as planned, RoboAuto revises the
stage decomposition and regenerates the plan before resum-
ing sequential search, reopening completed stages when
their outcomes must be re-established.
Self-designed experiments. Existing autonomous Code-
as-Policy methods primarily acquire evidence by executing
candidate robot programs and revising them afterward. Be-
cause these executions are intended to complete the task
rather than investigate a specific uncertainty, their evidence
may not isolate the physical assumption responsible for
a failure.
RoboAuto instead actively explores the scene
through autonomously designed experiments. Based on its
current physical understanding and scene observations, it
formulates physical hypotheses, predicts their observable
consequences, and writes dedicated experiment code, sep-
arate from the candidate robot program, that specifies the
physical intervention and the observations required to eval-
uate the hypotheses. For example, RoboAuto can test sev-
eral candidate grasp poses through controlled grasp-and-lift
trials and incorporate the successful pose into the robot pro-
gram without repeatedly executing the full task. The out-
come can support, reject, or refine a hypothesis, providing
targeted evidence for subsequent program search. It also
updates the corresponding object-centric knowledge entry,
so that exploration during the current task improves future
task solving. Section C.3 provides more details, and Ap-
pendix E reproduces the prompts.

substantially outperforming the Code-as-Policy and VLA
baselines under a wide range of manipulation settings. This
advantage also holds in every category when all evalu-

Generality and transferability of object-centric knowl-
edge. The initial object-centric knowledge is constructed
from LIBERO-90 data and is designed to transfer: its entries
describe qualitative strategies and the quantities to be mea-
sured in each scene, contain no control commands, physical
values, task instructions, or specific object instances, and
are organized by general affordance, spatial, and effect cat-
egories rather than by task or object (Sec. B.1). Grouping
evaluation settings by similarity to LIBERO-90, RoboAuto
achieves the highest mean success in every group, including
settings with both unseen scene layouts and unseen objects
(84.3% vs. 68.8%; Table 3). Adding the knowledge base to
ASPIRE improves it on several tasks but does not close the
gap to RoboAuto (Sec. B.1.6), indicating that RoboAuto’s
gains also rely on scene grounding and grounded multi-
stage planning.

We study how the availability of hierarchical knowledge
base entries affects program search by comparing 0%, ran-

full knowledge base, every task first reaches 80% success
within 19 rollouts, whereas over the full budget only three
tasks reach this level with half of the knowledge base entries
and one task without the knowledge base. Scaling knowl-
edge availability therefore improves both search efficiency
and the range of interactions solved within a fixed rollout
budget. Per-task results appear in Sec. B.5.

5. Conclusion
We introduced RoboAuto, a framework for autonomous
robot program search that combines a hierarchical knowl-
edge base, scene grounding, stage-wise verified search, and
self-designed experiments.
Across five benchmark fam-
ilies, RoboAuto consistently improves task success and
more than doubles ASPIRE’s performance on LIBERO-
PRO Long and LIBERO-Plus.
Knowledge acquired in
LIBERO also transfers to a new simulator and bimanual em-
bodiment in RoboTwin 2.0, reaching 91.5% success versus
24.5% without it. These results show that transferable phys-
ical knowledge and executable robot programs play com-
plementary roles.
Limitations.
RoboAuto searches autonomously only in
simulation. On real robots, a human operator still resets the
scene after each rollout and keeps the arm and its surround-
ings safe. Automating scene resets and safety monitoring
would bring the same autonomy to real-world deployment.

A. Detailed Experimental Setup
This section specifies the benchmark tasks, RoboAuto
search and evaluation protocol, system configuration, and
baseline implementations. Quantitative results are reported
separately in the main paper and subsequent appendix sec-
tions.
A.1. Simulation Benchmarks
A.1.1. LIBERO-PRO
LIBERO-PRO [52] evaluates adaptation to controlled task
and scene changes. We use its Object, Spatial, and Goal
suites. Object requires picking a specified object among
distractors and placing it in a basket. Spatial requires iden-
tifying the target object through its position relative to other
objects and placing it at a specified location. Goal spans di-
verse interactions such as opening drawers, turning on the
stove, and pushing objects within a shared scene. For each
suite, we separately evaluate position perturbations, which
relocate task-relevant objects, and task perturbations, which
modify the goal condition and instruction. Each suite con-
tains ten tasks under each perturbation, producing 60 set-
tings in total.
Table 5 reports the suite-level evaluation,
while Tables 6–8 provide the complete task-level matrix.
A.1.2. LIBERO-PRO Long
LIBERO-PRO Long evaluates programs that must produce
several dependent physical state changes in the correct or-
der. We randomly select ten task–perturbation settings from
the Long suite, covering both task and position perturba-
tions. These tasks require program search to maintain phys-
ical progress over an extended horizon and avoid execut-
ing later actions before their prerequisites have been estab-
lished. All methods are evaluated on the same ten settings
listed in Table 9.
A.1.3. LIBERO-Plus
LIBERO-Plus [12] evaluates robustness to visual and ge-
ometric changes that preserve the underlying manipula-
tion objective. We randomly select three base tasks from
LIBERO-Plus. Each task is evaluated under one camera
variation and one layout variation, yielding six settings. For
each task and variation type, we use the highest available
difficulty level: level 5 for all settings except the camera
variation of the stove task, for which the highest avail-
able level is 4. Camera variations change the workspace
viewpoint and framing, while layout variations change ob-
ject positions and spatial configurations. These changes re-
quire the agent to re-identify task-relevant objects and re-
estimate scene-dependent quantities rather than reuse pa-
rameters tuned to the original scene.
A.1.4. Robosuite
From Robosuite [54], we use seven single- and dual-arm
manipulation tasks. Lift evaluates grasping and transport;
Stack and Restack require precise relative placement; Wipe
requires sustained surface contact; and Nut Assembly re-
quires accurate geometric alignment. Two-Arm Lift and
Two-Arm Handover additionally test coordination between
manipulators. Although LIBERO and Robosuite both use
MuJoCo, these tasks introduce different interaction struc-
tures, contact requirements, and robot configurations. The
complete task set is listed in Table 11.
A.1.5. RoboTwin 2.0
RoboTwin 2.0 [6] evaluates transfer to a different simulator
and robot embodiment. It uses a bimanual robot in a SAPI-
EN/PhysX environment [37], changing the gripper geome-
try, control primitives, contact dynamics, layouts, and task
definitions. To evaluate the transfer of the knowledge base,
we compare search without retained knowledge and with
the hierarchical knowledge base learned from LIBERO un-
der the same search protocol. Table 14 lists the four evalu-
ated tasks and both conditions.
A.2. RoboAuto Evaluation Protocol
Search and test splits. Unless otherwise specified in Ta-
ble 2, program search uses 15 training scenes with seeds
110000–110014, and final-program evaluation uses 50 test
scenes with seeds 1–50. Robosuite uses 100 test scenes with
seeds 1–100. The two sets are disjoint. Program search
uses only training-scene evidence; test-scene outcomes are
never exposed to the agent or used to update either layer
of the knowledge base. After search terminates, the last
program is fixed and executed independently on every test
scene. The benchmark environment predicate determines
task-level success, and suite means weight all listed task
settings equally.
Rollout accounting and termination. One rollout is de-
fined as executing a single program in a single training
scene. Search runs over a growing subset of the training

A.4. Baselines and Comparison Protocol
We compare RoboAuto with three autonomous Code-as-
Policy methods and one generalist vision-language-action
model. These baselines cover iterative program revision,
accumulated executable experience, repository-level policy
search, and direct action generation. All autonomous Code-
as-Policy methods write programs over the same set of per-
ception and control primitives, including depth-camera ob-
servations, Molmo2 [10] pointing, SAM 3 [4] segmenta-
tion, and Contact-GraspNet [29] grasp proposals.
CaP-Agent0. CaP-Agent0 [13] generates robot programs
over perception and control primitives and revises them
across multiple turns using execution logs and visual feed-
back. It can retain automatically synthesized skills from
previous task-solving experience. We compare against its
full configuration, which adds a synthesized skill library
and parallel reasoning to the multi-turn visual-differencing
tier (M4) of CaP-Bench.
CaP-Agent0 has no separate
search phase and uses no training scenes. Instead, it adapts
at test time within each evaluation episode: it executes an
initial program on the test scene and may revise it up to ten
times based on execution feedback, without resetting the en-
vironment, so each evaluation episode allows up to eleven
program executions. In our reproduction on LIBERO-PRO
Long and LIBERO-Plus, we keep its released configuration
unchanged and use Claude Opus 4.6 with a 1M-token con-
text window for all of its model queries.
ASPIRE. ASPIRE [25] performs evolutionary search over
candidate robot programs using multimodal execution
traces and retains a skill library from repair experience.
RHO. RHO [11] searches over a repository of robot poli-
cies and improves the repository using reward and execu-
tion feedback.
π0.5. π0.5 [19] is a generalist vision-language-action model
that directly predicts robot actions from visual observations
and language instructions, providing a non-programmatic

B. Additional Experiment Results
This section provides the complete benchmark results un-
derlying the main comparison and organizes the remaining
analyses around six research questions: the contributions of
RoboAuto’s components, the transferability of its hierarchi-
cal knowledge base, the effect of scaling knowledge-base
entries, its performance with a stronger coding model, its
sensitivity to search randomness, and its token efficiency.
B.1. Generality and Transferability of Object-
Centric Knowledge
The initial object-centric knowledge is constructed from
LIBERO-90 demonstrations, and part of our evaluation uses
the LIBERO-PRO and LIBERO-Plus benchmarks.
The
knowledge base is therefore designed to store physical un-
derstanding at a level of abstraction that transfers across
tasks, objects, and scenes. Each entry describes how a class
of objects can be manipulated and what must be established
in the current scene, while the concrete program and its pa-
rameters are left to program search and scene grounding.
We characterize this abstraction through four properties and
verify that every entry satisfies them.
Verification procedure.
We read every entry sentence
by sentence and checked it against the four properties be-
low. To complement manual inspection, we applied pat-
tern checks to every sentence: (i) numbers, matching any
digit sequence ([0-9]+(\.[0-9]+)?) and spelled-out
numerals; (ii) units, matching length, angle, mass, and force
(e.g., mm, cm, m, deg, rad, °, kg, N) following a number
or standing alone as a token; (iii) task instructions, match-
ing each instruction from all evaluation suites (LIBERO-
PRO, LIBERO-PRO Long, LIBERO-Plus, Robosuite, and
RoboTwin 2.0) after lowercasing and punctuation removal,
both as a full phrase and as its verb–object sub-phrases (e.g.,
“put the bowl on the stove” and “bowl on the stove”); and
(iv) API names, matching the name of every perception
and control primitive exposed to the coder as well as any
function-call pattern ([A-Za-z ]+\(). Every match was
reviewed manually.
B.1.1. Control Action Check
Entries describe interaction strategies qualitatively, such as
“rim straddle away from the handle,” and leave their imple-
mentation to program search. Manual inspection checked
every sentence for commanded poses, joint angles, trajecto-
ries, gripper commands, and executable code, and the API-
name pattern searched for every primitive name and any
function-call syntax. No entry contains these elements.
B.1.2. Physical Quantity Check
Entries identify which physical quantities matter for an ob-
ject class through their open quantities field, such as
the rim radius of a bowl, without recording values for par-
ticular objects or scene layouts. This keeps each entry ap-
plicable across object sizes and layouts, and the values are
measured anew in every scene by Physical Scene Ground-
ing (Sec. 3.2). The number and unit patterns confirmed that
no entry stores a physical value.
B.1.3. Task Instruction Check
Knowledge is organized by object class rather than by task,
so entries do not encode task instructions or ordered ac-
tion sequences for a particular goal.
We matched every
sentence against all evaluation instructions and their verb–
object sub-phrases, and manual inspection checked for task-
level plans; no entry contains either.
B.1.4. Object Instance Check
Entries are indexed by general categories defined by af-
fordances, spatial relations, and action effects, rather than
by specific object instances such as “cream cheese,” “wine
bottle,” or “cookie box.”
Manual inspection of every
object class and every sentence confirmed this orga-
nization. The complete list of categories follows.

B.1.6. Hierarchical Knowledge Base with ASPIRE
To test whether the hierarchical knowledge base also im-
proves an existing program-search method and whether it
alone accounts for RoboAuto’s gains, we add it to ASPIRE
on ten short-horizon LIBERO-PRO tasks. As shown in Ta-
ble 13, the knowledge base raises ASPIRE’s success on
three of the four Spatial tasks, from 8% to 40%, from 16%
to 40%, and from 20% to 38%, while picking the bowl on
the cookie box changes from 24% to 18%. On the three
Object tasks, where ASPIRE already reaches 96% to 100%,
success remains near ceiling, and on the three Goal tasks
ASPIRE with the knowledge base scores 0%. The knowl-
edge base therefore transfers to a different search proce-
dure but does not close the gap to RoboAuto, which reaches
84.8% mean success on these tasks. On task-perturbed bowl
placement on the stove, ASPIRE remains at 0% with the hi-
erarchical knowledge base while RoboAuto reaches 98%,
consistent with Table 12, where the hierarchical knowledge
base without scene grounding also yields 0%. The knowl-
edge base alone therefore does not reproduce RoboAuto’s
15


performance, which also depends on scene grounding to in-
stantiate the entries in the current scene and on multi-stage
planning with grounded verification and self-designed ex-
periments to refine them.

B.3. RQ1: How Does Each Component Contribute?
Table 12 evaluates the cumulative contributions of Search
and Exploration, the Hierarchical Knowledge Base, and
Physical Scene Grounding on five tasks. The base coding
agent achieves only 5.2% mean success. Adding Search
and Exploration raises success to 19.2%, including an im-
provement from 8% to 82% on the stove task.
Sequen-
tial stage-wise search and independent verification localize
incomplete interactions, while self-designed experiments
collect targeted evidence about uncertain physical assump-
tions. Adding the Hierarchical Knowledge Base further in-
creases mean success to 38.4%. The mug-and-pudding task
improves from 4% to 78%, illustrating how object-centric
affordance knowledge can introduce an appropriate interac-
tion strategy that repeated parameter revision does not dis-
cover. Finally, Physical Scene Grounding raises mean suc-
cess to 91.2%; drawer retrieval and bowl placement on the
stove, which remain unsolved by the partial systems, reach
78% and 98%, respectively. These results show that the
three components play complementary roles. Search and
Exploration structures the improvement process, the knowl-
edge base supplies reusable interaction strategies, and scene
grounding instantiates those strategies for the current ob-
jects and geometry.

Knowledge Base?
We evaluate RoboTwin 2.0 under the transfer conditions de-

Success rises from 24.5% without retained knowledge to

B.6. RQ4: Does RoboAuto Remain Effective with a
Stronger Coder?
We repeat five settings from the LIBERO-PRO Goal, Spa-
tial, and Long suites with Opus 5 with a 1M-token context
window as the coder for both the base loop and RoboAuto
(Table 16). RoboAuto reaches 77.6% mean success against
29.6% for the base loop, showing that its advantage holds
with a stronger coder.
Stronger coding does not ensure physical understand-
ing. A stronger model such as Opus 5 has a stronger coding
capability, and in particular a stronger ability to control the
robot arm through the primitives. It writes its own kinemat-

its own tooling, and, while evolving the program, attends
to execution details that Opus 4.6 with a 1M-token context
window misses, reading the rollout logs closely enough to
catch primitive errors and offsets. These abilities let it reach
78% on picking the bowl from the drawer in the base loop,
since controlling the arm reliably and reading the logs care-
fully are enough to reach the bowl. However, on tasks that
cannot be solved without grounded physical understanding,
these abilities are not enough. On the long-horizon mug-

C. Method Details
This section provides the implementation details of
RoboAuto’s three components. We first describe how its hi-
erarchical knowledge base is constructed and updated, then
explain how physical knowledge is grounded in the current
scene, and finally detail the stage-wise search, independent
verification, and self-designed experimentation procedures.
C.1. Hierarchical Knowledge Base
RoboAuto maintains two complementary forms of reusable
experience:
object-centric physical knowledge and exe-
cutable skills. Object-centric entries describe transferable
properties of physical interactions, while executable skills
retain verified program implementations. Both layers con-
tinue to expand through online task solving.
24


Constructing object-centric knowledge from embodied
data.
Figure 12 illustrates how the object-centric layer
is constructed from embodied demonstration videos.
A
vision-language model converts each video into an evidence
record through four steps.
It first identifies the entities
involved, segments the video into interaction events, de-
scribes each event using its preceding state, interaction, re-
sulting state, and visual evidence, and finally evaluates the
outcome of the episode. Object-centric knowledge is then
distilled across these evidence records.
Each resulting entry is categorized as an affordance, spa-
tial relation, or action effect. It specifies the relevant object
class and applicability conditions, describes candidate inter-
action strategies, and declares the scene-dependent quanti-
ties required to instantiate them. The entry also links re-
lated knowledge and records the supporting demonstration
events. During online task solving, new execution evidence
can support, challenge, or refine these entries. Listing 1
shows one stored entry of each type. The knowledge field is
abridged. Each of its sentences carries a tag stating whether
it was demonstrated, inferred, or remains untested, and the
open quantities name what must be measured in each scene
before the entry is applied.

Executable skills and online updates.
The executable
layer stores program components that have been success-
fully verified across the current search pool. Unlike object-
centric entries, these skills directly implement behaviors
using the perception and control primitives of a particular
robot interface.
After a task is solved, verified program
components are consolidated into the skill library, while the
physical evidence collected during search updates the cor-
responding object-centric entries. This separation allows
RoboAuto to retain both transferable physical knowledge
and reusable executable implementations.
C.2. Physical Scene Grounding
Physical
Scene
Grounding
determines
how
retrieved
object-centric knowledge applies to the current task and
scene. Given the task instruction and initial observations,
RoboAuto identifies the task-relevant objects, associates
them with retrieved knowledge entries, and checks the en-
tries’ applicability conditions. It then establishes the action-
critical quantities declared by those entries, such as object
centers, dimensions, orientations, grasp locations, handle
directions, and spatial relations.
RoboAuto combines visual reasoning and segmentation
tools, including Molmo2 and SAM 3, with metric obser-
vations from scene and wrist depth cameras. The result-
ing task-specific grounding record stores the quantities that
have been established, their supporting observations, and
any conditions that remain unresolved. The coder uses this
record to select feasible strategies and instantiate their pa-
rameters. Grounding remains revisable during search: an
execution outcome may challenge an earlier estimate, re-
veal an overlooked condition, or require an additional mea-
surement or targeted experiment.
C.3. Search and Exploration
Search and Exploration coordinates three roles: the coder
generates and revises programs, the environment executes
them, and an independent auditor reviews failed executions.
As summarized in Fig. 13, RoboAuto searches sequentially
over task stages, verifies their physical outcomes, and de-
signs targeted experiments when execution evidence does
not explain a failure.
Multi-stage planning and sequential search. The coder
decomposes the task into stages, each defined by an in-
tended change in object state and an executable verifier for
that change. The stages are ordered according to their phys-
ical dependencies and executed sequentially. A failed veri-
fier stops the remaining task stages, preventing later actions
from obscuring or compounding an earlier failure.
Program revision focuses on the earliest stage whose in-
tended state has not been established. Stages that already
succeed can therefore be retained while search concentrates
on the unresolved interaction. The coder may revise the
stage implementation, its verifier, or the broader plan. If
execution evidence shows that the current decomposition or
dependency structure is invalid, RoboAuto can redesign the
stages and replan the task rather than continuing to revise
an unrealizable stage.
Independent grounded verification. Program-generated
verifiers may themselves be incorrect, so RoboAuto uses
a separate language-model session to audit failed execu-
tions. The auditor receives the task instruction, stage plan,
relevant physical knowledge, execution logs, and synchro-
nized scene and wrist videos. It independently determines
whether each stage produced its intended object state and
describes the observed interaction.
When the auditor returns a judgment for a stage, that
judgment replaces the corresponding program-verifier ver-
dict.
If the auditor provides no judgment for a stage,
RoboAuto retains the program verifier’s original result. The
earliest stage judged incomplete becomes the focus of the
next revision. Previously verified stages may also be re-
opened when later evidence reveals a regression. The envi-
ronment’s task predicate remains the final criterion for task-
level success: successful scenes bypass video review, while
a verifier failure reported on a successful scene is recorded
as a verification error.
Self-designed experiments.
Recognizing an incomplete
stage does not necessarily reveal which physical assumption
caused the failure. When the available evidence is insuffi-
cient, RoboAuto formulates competing physical hypotheses
and predicts the observations that would support them. It
then writes a separate experiment program and correspond-
ing control code to test these predictions. These files are
distinct from the candidate robot program because their pur-
pose is to acquire diagnostic evidence rather than complete
the task.
Each experiment specifies a hypothesis, a predicted ob-
servation, an optional starting-state prefix, and controlled
test actions. For example, RoboAuto may generate an ex-
periment that evaluates several candidate grasp poses un-
der the same scene conditions and compares whether each
pose establishes stable contact. Prefix stages prepare the re-
quired starting state before the test actions. A failed prefix
verdict remains visible to the agent but does not prevent the
diagnostic actions from running, whereas an execution ex-
ception terminates the experiment. The returned logs and
camera observations are interpreted relative to the observed
starting state and recorded in a persistent diagnosis note-
book. The evidence then informs program revision and may
update the corresponding object-centric entry.

D. Behavioral Analysis of RoboAuto
This section analyzes how RoboAuto behaves during pro-
gram search and how its components change that behav-
ior. We use two complementary forms of evidence. First,
baseline case studies examine runs in which program revi-
sions continue without resolving the physical conditions re-
quired by an interaction, and compare them with RoboAuto
runs on the same task. Second, controlled ablations add
Search and Exploration, the Hierarchical Knowledge Base,
and Physical Scene Grounding cumulatively while holding
the task, rollout budget, and scene seeds fixed.
The cases cover three recurring difficulties. They con-
28


cern selecting a physically appropriate interaction strategy,
grounding the quantities required by that strategy, and ver-
ifying that an executed action produced its intended phys-
ical effect. Frames are taken from the cited trials. Code
and knowledge excerpts are condensed from the generated
programs and stored entries while retaining their original
identifiers and values.
D.1. Behavior Across Component Ablations
The component ablation in Table 12 compares four cumu-
lative variants on five tasks. We illustrate the progression
on the stove task.
The base loop revises complete pro-
grams from task-level rollout outcomes. The second variant
adds Search and Exploration, including stage decomposi-
tion, grounded verification, and self-designed experiments.
The third adds the Hierarchical Knowledge Base, and the
final variant additionally grounds its object-centric entries
in the current scene.
Figure 14 compares the four variants on a single task.
Across nine turns, the base loop succeeds on only one train-
ing scene and receives no information about which interac-
tion caused the remaining failures. Search and Exploration
verifies the knob stage in its first turn and the pan grasp in
its second, allowing subsequent revisions to concentrate on
placement. Adding the knowledge base supplies workable
knob and handle contacts in the first turn, but later revisions
must tune unmeasured carry and release constants. The full
system measures the pan, knob, and stove before generat-
ing its first program. By turn 9, after 36 candidate-program
rollouts, it solves 14 of the 15 training scenes. Each compo-
nent changes the point at which search stops rediscovering
the interaction and begins refining its execution.
The remaining four ablation tasks in Table 12 follow the
same progression. Without stages, the base loop rewrites
the complete program from a per-scene reward and does
not establish which interaction failed. Search and Explo-
ration localizes the failed stage, but when that stage ad-
mits several explanations it may investigate an incorrect
hypothesis first.
The Hierarchical Knowledge Base re-
moves much of the search over interaction families, and
every task with a matching entry passes its grasp stage
within the first one or two turns. What remains unresolved
are the scene-dependent quantities required by the entry,
which the knowledge-base-only variant approximates from
segmentation-mask spread and fixed offsets.
These ap-
proximations can place the gripper outside a rim or over-
shoot a placement. Physical Scene Grounding obtains these
quantities from geometric estimators cross-checked across
cameras and evaluates whether a stored strategy applies.
When no stored strategy is feasible, RoboAuto uses a self-
designed experiment to establish a new interaction.
D.2. Hierarchical Knowledge Base:
Discovering
Suitable Interaction Strategies
The book case illustrates how object-centric knowledge
supplies a suitable contact strategy when repeated param-
eter revisions preserve an unsuccessful grasp.
D.2.1. An Unsuitable Book Grasp Persists Across Revi-
sions
The book begins standing on its edge, as shown in Fig. 15.
In CaP-Agent0’s trial 02, the first top-down approach
knocks it flat. The following code block observes that the
book is now lying on the table but concludes that another
top-down grasp should work. Blocks 2–10 continue adjust-
ing grasp height and orientation around the book center, and
only block 11 of 12 attempts an edge grasp.
Across the 44 trials without service errors, every gener-
ated program uses top-down grasps, 32 grasp at the book
center, 13 explicitly note that the book has fallen, and none
completes the task. RoboAuto instead retrieves an entry for
manipulating a thin slab standing on its edge. The entry
prescribes a top-edge cover pinch supported by 14 book-
grasp demonstrations and declares the book yaw and thick-
ness as quantities to measure. RoboAuto obtains a thick-
ness of 2.8 cm, verifies consistency across the two cameras,
and checks that the 5.2 cm caddy slot can receive it. The
program pinches the covers before the book is disturbed,
verifies the gripper width and the displacement of the book,
and inserts it along the slab plane, reaching 74% success on
held-out test scenes.
The difference lies in the interaction strategy rather than
its parameters. Once the top-down grasp has toppled the
book, further adjustment of height or orientation within that
contact family does not recover the task.
D.3. Physical Scene Grounding:
Instantiating
Knowledge in the Current Scene
The mug case examines the effect of replacing an inher-
ited grasp-height offset with measurements of the current
object’s rim.
D.3.1. A Grasp-Height Rule Is Copied from Another Ob-
ject
ASPIRE’s skill library contains a manually implemented
top-down grasp with a fixed offset below the object top:
grasp z = obj z top - 0.02.
The rule was distilled from a run involving a different
mug in another scene. ASPIRE reuses it for the red mug and
grasps at the body center using the stored offset. Because
the same mug model appears across scenes, the resulting
grasp height is fixed at z = 0.1345 m in all 15 training
scenes. The program’s own findings report that this grasp
29


produces a gripper width of approximately 0.015 m, indi-
cating an air grasp, in approximately half of the seeds, while
the same code succeeds in others.
The stored rule contains no applicability condition that
explains the variation. It does not state the rim radius, fin-
ger opening, or geometric relationship for which the offset
was valid. ASPIRE’s repair loop ends at 0/50 success; its
reported 20% on this setting is obtained through its subse-
quent evolutionary search.
RoboAuto’s entry for an open vessel wider than the fin-
ger opening declares the rim radius and top height as quan-
tities to measure. Across test trials, it obtains a radius be-
tween 0.0431 and 0.0433 m and a top height of approxi-
mately 0.153 m. The program offsets the pads from the mug
center by the measured radius and checks the predicted pad
height against the rim before closing, lowering the hand if
the pads remain above it. The quantities vary little across
these scenes, but they are explicitly tied to and verified
against the object being grasped rather than inherited from
a procedure that succeeded elsewhere.
D.4. Search and Exploration: Localizing and Inves-
tigating Failures
The stove case illustrates why program search must verify
the burner’s state before proceeding to pan placement.
D.4.1. An Executed Action Is Mistaken for an Achieved
Physical State
The instruction is to turn on the stove and place the pan on
it. In CaP-Agent0’s trial 18, blocks 1–5 repeat the same
routine: descend toward the detected knob point, close the
gripper, and rotate the wrist by 90 degrees. Block 5 records
that the gripper remains at z ≈0.129 m, far above the esti-
mated knob height of z ≈0.018 m. Nevertheless, no block
checks whether the burner changed state.
The burner remains off through the first four revisions.
Later blocks proceed to pan placement, and the pan ends
propped against the stove edge. Across the failed trials,
most programs infer the knob geometry from its expected
position; only 7 of 93 reason explicitly about the knob ridge
or its orientation.
RoboAuto’s knob entry prescribes a ridge pinch and
twist and specifies the effect to observe: rotate until the
burner appearance changes, rather than by a stored angle.
The program descends to the grounded knob height, per-
forms the rotation, and measures the burner’s red fraction.
If the burner remains off, it re-grasps and rotates in the op-
posite direction. It grasps the pan near the handle root only
after burner activation has been verified, reaching 92% suc-
cess on test scenes. Executing the rotation is not equivalent
to achieving the intended physical state. Verifying the state
localizes the failure to the knob stage rather than allowing it
to appear only as a final reward of zero.
D.5. Summary of Observed Behaviors
The cases illustrate distinct sources of progress during pro-
gram search: object-centric knowledge supplies a cover-
pinch strategy for the standing book, scene grounding re-
places the mug’s inherited grasp-height offset with mea-
sured rim geometry, and stage-wise verification checks
burner activation before pan placement. The stove ablation
further shows how these components change the revisions
made during search, from discovering workable contacts to
measuring and refining their execution.

F. Real-Robot Experimental Details
Hardware setup. We conduct real-robot experiments us-
ing a Trossen Robotics WidowX AI arm equipped with an
Intel RealSense D405 RGB-D camera and a Logitech C920
RGB camera. The cameras provide complementary geo-
metric and visual observations of the robot, objects, and
workspace. All methods use the same hardware, sensing
configuration, and control interface.
Knowledge base.
For the real-robot experiments,
RoboAuto directly reuses its hierarchical knowledge base
acquired from LIBERO tasks, which combines reusable
object-centric physical knowledge with executable skills.
All real-robot tasks are held out and distinct from those
used for knowledge-base construction. For a fair compar-
ison, ASPIRE also transfers its skill library acquired from
the LIBERO tasks. Despite the domain shift from simula-
tion to the physical world, RoboAuto effectively retrieves,
grounds, and composes its accumulated knowledge for pre-
viously unseen tasks and configurations. Its substantial ad-
vantage over ASPIRE shows that the hierarchical knowl-
edge base acquired in simulation transfers to unseen real-
robot tasks.
Experimental setup. We use Opus 5 with a 1M-token con-
text window for the search component of both program-
search methods, RoboAuto and ASPIRE, and allocate each
a budget of 10 search rollouts to ensure a controlled com-
parison. Because no simulator success predicate is avail-
able on the real robot, a human operator judges whether
each search rollout succeeds; this judgment replaces the en-
vironment reward used in simulation and is returned to the
search loop. The operator also resets the scene after each
rollout. For both search and evaluation, initial object posi-
tions are randomly sampled within a fixed reset region, and
the positions used for evaluation are sampled independently
of and differ from those used during search. We evaluate
RoboAuto, π0.5, and ASPIRE on three long-horizon ma-
nipulation tasks using a WidowX AI arm. In Sort Balls, the
robot must place a green ping-pong ball on a plate and a red
ping-pong ball in a bowl. In Stack Dishes, it must place a
low-rimmed bowl on a plate and then place a cup upright
in the bowl. In Erase Board, it must pick up a compliant
eraser, erase “RoboAuto” from a whiteboard, and return the
eraser to its original position. Each method is evaluated over
20 trials per task with initial object positions drawn from
the same reset region but not used during search. After each
trial, a human evaluator assigns a single stage-completion
score, the fraction of stages completed, and performance is
the mean score over trials.
Task evaluation. We evaluate performance based on stage
completion rather than binary success on the full long-
horizon task. Sort Balls consists of four stages: (1) picking
up the green ping-pong ball, (2) placing it on the plate, (3)
picking up the red ping-pong ball, and (4) placing it in the
bowl. Stack Dishes also consists of four stages: (1) picking
up the bowl, (2) placing the bowl on the plate, (3) picking
up the cup, and (4) placing the cup upright in the bowl. Each
completed stage in these two tasks contributes one quarter
of the total score. Erase Board consists of three stages:
(1) picking up the eraser, (2) erasing the eight letters in
“RoboAuto,” and (3) returning the eraser to its original posi-
tion. Each stage contributes one third of the total score. Be-
cause the erasing stage involves eight letters, we assign pro-
portional credit based on the number of letters successfully
erased: erasing n letters yields n/8 of the score allocated
to this stage. This stage-based metric captures meaningful
partial progress and provides a more informative evaluation
than measuring only whether the entire long-horizon task is
completed.
