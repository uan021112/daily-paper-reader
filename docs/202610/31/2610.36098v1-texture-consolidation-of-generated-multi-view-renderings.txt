Abstract
We present a system for consolidating a sparse set of multi-view renderings into a single coherent texture image, provid-
ing a framework for integrating generative models for image generation within the geometry-processing pipeline. Given
a texture-atlased triangle mesh and a set of renderings with associated camera parameters, we generate the texture im-
age by: (1) back-projecting the renderings onto the mesh to obtain texture images with normalized colors and confidences;
(2) solving for flow-fields on the mesh that align the colors to each other, taking into account the associated confidences;
and (3) fusing the registered colors into a single texture image using gradient-domain stitching, again incorporating con-
fidences. Our approach complements existing techniques for multi-view image generation, leveraging mesh-based optical
flow and texture-space stitching. In doing so it addresses the challenge that while modern image generation pipelines gen-
erate highly convincing renderings, they struggle to produce renderings that are consistent across views. Code is available at
https://github.com/hliuw/TextureConsolidation.

1. Introduction
The generation of 3D assets from generated multi-view renderings
has advanced considerably in recent years [PJBM23, RMA∗23,
GLAK25]. Leveraging powerful diffusion-based models, these
methods generate high-quality multi-view images for diverse 3D
content. However, the renderings often exhibit inconsistencies
when mapped onto 3D geometry, making it difficult to integrate
them into a single, view-independent, model.
Motivated by this challenge, we consider the following problem:
Given a set of multi-view renderings and a texture-atlased mesh,
how do we construct a globally consistent texture image?
This question has been explored for real-world RGB-D datasets,
where approaches typically address the problem through view se-
lection, pose optimization, registration, and blending. However,
generated renderings differ from real-world captures in that they
lack a coherent, view-independent model from which the render-
ings are generated, often resulting in geometric and photometric
inconsistencies between renderings. As a result, while generating

higher-quality textures from a small number of renderings.
While many multi-view texture registration methods exist, they
are predominantly designed for dense RGB-D captures, relying on
view selection to discard low-quality frames affected by motion
blur, and having lighting and shading artifacts. generated multi-
view renderings that sparsely sample the view sphere present a dif-
ferent set of challenges: each point is visible in only a few views;
and generated views are not geometrically consistent, so that back-
projection produces misaligned textures.
We address these challenges by designing a confidence-aware
pipeline for multi-view texture consolidation that back-projects the
renderings to texture images and then registration and stitching
in the surface domain. We extend existing optical flow [PKC∗16]
and gradient-domain stitching [PGB03, LZPW04, PKCH18] tech-
niques, adapting them to support per-texel confidences. This is
achieved via quadrature-based finite-elements integration support-
ing sub-triangle evaluation of functions, thereby bridging texel-
resolution and mesh-resolution discretizations.
Our method is agnostic to the rendering pipeline, serving as a
general post-processing module that complements prior multi-view
generation methods. Empirically, we show that existing registra-
tion methods for RGB-D captures degrade on generated inputs that
sparsely sample the view sphere, while our framework produces
high-quality texture images in this challenging setting.
2. Related Work
Textured 3D Shape Generation Diffusion models have achieved
remarkable progress in 2D image generation [RBL∗22, YZS∗23,
ZRA23]. However, generating textured 3D models continues to be
a challenge. Approaches differ in their choice of geometric repre-
sentations and in how they couple geometry and color generation.
One common approach is to generate textured 3D shapes from
user-provided text input. Approaches using Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRF) [MST∗21] or Gaussian-Splats (GS) [KKLD23] often start
by either directly optimizing the scene from text prompts, or
leveraging diffusion models to first generate multi-view render-
ings which are then used to reconstruct the NeRF/GS model
[PJBM23, WLW∗23, LGT∗23, TCC∗24, TRZ∗23, YFW∗24]. Hy-
brid scene representations have also been used to transition be-
tween different intermediate representations during optimization
before extracting the textured mesh [CCJJ23]. Additionally, some
NeRF/GS methods enable mesh extraction as a final step [LGT∗23,
TRZ∗23,WZB∗25].
Another approach takes an input 3D geometry and generates
color textures by back-projecting multi-view renderings [RMA∗23,
CSL∗23, ZCQ∗24, GLAK25, CMZ∗24, ZSG∗24]. These methods
first generate multi-view rendering from pretrained 2D diffusion
models, often using depth maps as guidance. The composite color
texture is then produced through aggregation of multi-view render-
ings and progressive inpainting of untextured regions. Our work
focuses on this setting and addresses the challenge of registering
and compositing such multi-view renderings.
Texture Registration and Blending A challenge in back-
projecting multi-view renderings is the lack of consistency across
different views – a long-standing challenge in 3D reconstruction
from photographs. Such inconsistencies typically arise from vari-
ations in lighting, occlusions, and geometric distortions. generated
textures can exacerbate the problem by independently introducing
different details for different renderings.
Addressing these issues typically involves some form of registra-
tion and blending. A common strategy is to align textures through
warping. Eisemann et al. [EDDM∗08] and Casas et al. [CVCH14]
use reprojected images to compute a correction field via optical
flow. Dellepiane et al. [DMC∗12] compute optical flow between
overlapping photographs to locally correct image-to-geometry mis-
alignment. Aganj et al. [AMK09] compute the displacement using
reprojected feature correspondences across interest points in differ-
ent views. Inconsistency can also be mitigated by optimizing cam-
era pose. Zhou and Koltun [ZK14] jointly optimize camera poses
and non-rigid deformation per photo to maximize photometric con-
sistency across projected color images. Huang et al. [HTD∗20]
introduce adversarial texture optimization that learns tolerance to
misalignments by training a discriminator on view projections, later
extended by Zhao et al. [ZZS22] using Markov Random Field view
initialization and FFT alignment. Fu et al. [FYL∗21] incorporate a
texture detail layer to select the best-fit texture for each triangle
face. Camera pose is then optimized to align these renderings, and
they are regenerated at the texture charts’ boundaries to remove
seams. Wang et al. [WGZF24] propose a similar method by detect-
ing and excluding misaligned regions, optimizing camera positions,
and filling in holes. Kim et al. [KYL∗26] refine an initial texture
by jointly optimizing uncertainty maps and deformation grids to
handle inconsistent AI-generated targets within non-differentiable
production engines.
Other methods focus on view blending. This is often done as
a combination of view selection and stitching. An early example
is Gal et al. [GWO∗10], which assigns each face both a source
image and a local image-space translation compensating for mis-
alignment. Waechter et al. [WMG14] texture meshes from photos
by filtering occluders via photo-consistency checks, selecting views
based on gradient magnitude, and performing color adjustment us-
ing gradient-domain stitching [PGB03]. Pagés et al. [PBMG15]
blend the contributions of all cameras across the whole mesh, using
smoothly interpolated per-vertex camera ratings to suppress seams
while limiting blur. Du et al. [DCC∗18] instead focus on texture
blending, identifying seams using rendered depth maps and mod-
ulating them using geodesic fields. These methods are also appli-
cable to generated multi-view renderings. Zhao et al. [ZSG∗24]
select a subset of generated renderings to minimize inconsistency
via combinatorial optimization and warp them using SIFT flow
[LYT11]. The resulting textures are stitched by assigning each face
an optimal projected texture, formulated as a joint labeling prob-
lem. Im2SurfTex [GLAK25] introduces a neural back-projection
module that integrates textures by encoding geodesic-aware fea-
tures and fusing neighboring pixels through cross-attention.
In our approach we use optical flow to register the textures and

Optical Flow Optical flow techniques were originally developed
to identify the motion of objects in consecutive image frames
by estimating a vector-field under the assumption of luminance
constancy [HS81, FBK15, AMPA24]. They have also been used
to register textures from multi-view renderings by computing an
aligning flow-field and advecting the pixels [EDDM∗08,DMC∗12,
CVCH14]. Traditionally, though, these methods work in the (flat)
2D image domain.
Extending optical flow to the (curved) surface domain requires
evaluating the smoothness of tangent vector-fields [DGDT16,
Lee18]. This is achieved by defining a Dirichlet energy for tan-
gent vector-fields [SSC19, SWJG20, BSEH17]. With this energy,
the optical flow-field can be computed using a variational formu-
lation [LB08, PKC∗16]. Signals are then advected along the flow-
field to align details and reduce the ghosting artifacts that occur
with naive blending.
Gradient-domain Stitching A popular method for seamless
stitching fuses together signals by compositing their gradients,
rather than colors. The approach was initially formulated for 2D
images [LZPW04,PGB03] but has since been applied to the stitch-
ing of signals over a triangulated surface [DSWH14,CRK16]. More
recently, the approach has been extended to operate within the tex-
ture domain, handling cross-chart discontinuities and non-uniform
distortion in the texture-mapping [PKCH18].

can be integrated over the mesh.

As the solution⃗v should itself be time-varying, the optimization
is iterated – at each time-step the previously estimated flow-fields
are used to advect the source and a correcting flow-field is com-
puted to register the advected source to the target.
Implementation We follow the implementation of Prada et al.
[PKC∗16] which performs the time-stepping hierarchically – ini-
tially solving for a smooth flow-field that aligns the low-frequency
components and progressively solving for higher-frequencies. This
is achieved by modifying the input signals and the regularization
in Eq. 2 as a function of the hierarchy level. The advected source
and target are replaced by their blurred versions, with the amount
of blurring reduced at later levels of the hierarchy. Similarly, the
smoothness regularization weight βreg is decreased at later levels.
In principle, the deformation should be expressed as the com-
position of advections along the iteratively estimated flow-fields.
However, both within the algorithm and as its output, Prada et al.
propose using a single flow-field that is their sum.

Discretizing the computation of optical flow requires resolving two
conflicting challenges. On the one hand, the flow-field is expected
to be relatively low-frequency, corresponding to the fact that the

which also supports mixing different signal representations. For

6.1. Preprocessing
Our pre-processing consists of two stages. Back-projecting to turn
the renderings into texture color and confidence images, and nor-
malization to correct for low-frequency variation of color in the
generated rendering.

Color Given a rendering/camera {Yi,Ci}, we define the color at
a texel by locating the corresponding point on the mesh, back-
projecting using the camera parameters, and sampling Yi at the
back-projected position. The surface point is computed in two
steps. First, we compute the barycentric coordinates of the texel
with respect to the closest texture triangle. Then, we compute the
barycentrically weighted average of the triangle’s corner positions
in 3D. When the texel falls inside a texture triangle, the barycentric
weights are positive and the average of the corners lies on the mesh.
Otherwise, we map the average to the closest point on the mesh.
Confidence Because the generated renderings may have silhou-
ettes that are not consistent with the input geometry, back-
projection can cause colors to fall far from their desired location on
the surface. We address this by defining a confidence texture that
combines normal and depth information. (Fig. 4 shows an example
for the Bear model, where part of the belt near the silhouette of the
arm is back-projected to the wrist. Combining normal and depth
information, we assign texels near silhouettes lower confidence.)
Normal Confidence. We assign normal confidences by measur-
ing the extent to which texels are front-facing to the camera. These
are computed in the standard way, using the clamped dot-product of
the surface normal and the camera’s viewing direction. Though this
identifies locations on the silhouette, it fails to identify front-facing
background pixels that are back-projected to the foreground.

Depth Confidence. To assign lower confidence to texels back-
projecting to the foreground near silhouettes, we first identify pix-
els where the depth-map is discontinuous. This is achieved by ren-
dering the scene from the perspective of the camera, computing the
depth-map, and thresholding its Laplacian. The confidence is set
to zero where the Laplacian exceeds the threshold and is reduced
nearby. The confidence of occluded texels is also set to zero.
Finally, we multiply the depth and normal confidences and quan-
tize low-confidences to zero. (See App. B for more details.)
While we instantiate these weights using normal/view-direction
alignment and depth continuity, our approach equivalently ac-

6.4. Process Summary
Our processing pipeline is summarized in Alg. 1. Given the texture-
atlased mesh, input renderings, and camera parameters, the render-
ings are back-projected onto the mesh and the colors are normal-
ized (lines 1-5). Having initialized the flow-fields (lines 6 and 7)
they are refined hierarchically (lines 8-27). The final flow-fields are
then used to register the back-projected textures (lines 28 and 29),
which are stitched together to generate the output (line 30).
The aim of each hierarchical iteration is to adjust the previously
estimated flow-fields. As such, the iterations begin by advecting
textures along the previously estimated flow-fields (lines 9 and 10).
Then, a target signal maximizing overlap is selected (lines 12-13)
and the remaining textures are successively integrated into the tar-
get, queued by overlap with the target (lines 16-26). To track the
sources, a queue of texture indices is initialized (line 11) and ele-
ments are removed as their associated textures are processed (lines
14 and 18), while the merged textures are collected in a second
set (lines 15 and 19). Finally, the weights affecting the smoothness
of the correcting flow, βblur and βreg, are reduced so that the next
iteration can register finer detail (line 27).
To register, the advected source and target colors are smoothed
(lines 20 and 21) and a correcting flow-field is computed (line 22).
The source’s flow-field is adjusted to incorporate the correcting
flow (line 23) and used to advect the source color and confidence
(line 24). These are merged into the target (lines 25 and 26).
Function Spaces In generating the final texture image, our aim is
to reproduce fine detail while keeping the dimensionality of lin-
ear systems small where possible. To this end we use two dis-
cretizations of scalar functions – as per-vertex values at the mesh-
resolution using the “hat” basis, and as per-texel values at the
texture-image resolution using the basis of Prada et al. [PKCH18].
For tangent vector-fields, we use the mesh-resolution basis of Liu et
al. [LSV∗26]. We perform one-to-four planar subdivision to get
meshes with ~20K vertices. Using 1024 × 1024 textures, there are
~700K active texels.

Data We evaluate our method on two generated datasets produced
by different generation pipelines, each comprising ten objects, as
well as on two RGB-D scans commonly used in prior work.
For the first dataset (Instant3D), we generate multi-view images
and reconstruct the surface from them. First, we generate nine ren-
derings from a text prompt following the sparse multi-view genera-
tor in Instant3D [LTZ∗24]. The nine views are picked to span three
elevation angles with uniformly sampled azimuths. Then we feed
the renderings into MeshLRM [WZB∗25] to obtain the triangulated
surface.
For the second dataset (SyncDreamer), we pair generated multi-
view images with an existing ground-truth surface. We take a
ground-truth textured mesh from GSO [DFK∗22] and render a sin-
gle image at 30◦elevation. Conditioned only on this rendering,
SyncDreamer [LLZ∗24] generates sixteen uniformly distributed
views at the same elevation. Because the views are generated with-
out knowledge of the geometry, the renderings are less consistent
with the surface than in the first dataset.
While we focus on the consolidation of generated renderings,
we also consider real-world captures using the RGB-D scans of the
Fountain [ZK14] and Armchair [HTD∗20]. We use standard tech-
niques to extract a textured mesh from the RGB-D data (see App. A
for details). We use all 33 keyframes from the Fountain dataset and
randomly subsample 55 views from the full set of 550 scans of the
Armchair. Fig. 7 shows the views for a generated dataset and the
camera trajectory around the Armchair.

7.1. Our Method: Generated Data
As discussed in Sec. 3 and visualized in Fig. 3, it is only with the
combination of optical flow and gradient-domain stitching that we
successfully produce seamless and ghost-free images. Additional
results, shown in Fig. 6, confirm that our approach successfully
aligns photometric features and removes discontinuities. (The fig-
ure also highlights an advantage of using gradient-domain stitch-
ing. In the zoom-in on the blended results for the Hippo there is a
black region above the eye corresponding to geometry not imaged
by any camera. With gradient-domain stitching, colors are filled in
using an as-smooth-as-possible interpolant.)
7.2. Comparisons: Sparse-view Generated Data
We compare to the baseline methods in Fig. 8 and Fig. 9. The
rows alternate between showing zoomed-out views, and showing
zoom-ins on regions where the input is misaligned. MVS-tex gen-
erates consistent texture within patches imaged from a single view,
but produces seams at patch boundaries. Though ColorMap and
AdvIA perform registration, both are designed for dense RGB-
D video streams and performance degrades on sparse, generated,
multi-view data. Im2SurfTex produces blurry textures and ghost-
ing artifacts as it only aggregates colors from small fixed pixel-
radius neighborhoods in image space, so that misalignments larger
than the neighborhood cannot be reconciled. Additionally, we ob-
serve that our method reproduces colors more faithfully – MVS-
tex’s output tends to have higher contrast, AdvIA introduces chro-
matic shifts (e.g., for the Mask example, AdvIA’s colors become
greener), and Im2SurfTex can produce darker renderings (see the
Pharaoh example).
We perform an empirical comparison using Learned Percep-
tual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [ZIE∗18], Structural Simi-
larity (SSIM) [WBSS04], and Extended Convolution Descriptor
(ECD) [MBK∗20, Kaz26]. For each method, we render the out-
put textured mesh from the perspective of the input cameras, and
compare it to the input renderings. For SyncDreamer we addition-
ally evaluate in a leave-four-out (L4O) setting. Of the 16 generated
views we withhold four at 90◦intervals around the orbit, and reg-
ister the texture from the remaining 12. Each method’s texture is
rendered at the four withheld views and compared against a ren-
der of the original textured mesh from the same view. We do not
apply this protocol to Instant3D, as its nine views do not provide
sufficient coverage when a view is withheld.
Tab. 2 gives the scores averaged over all objects and views, with
the best and second-best results highlighted. Our approach ranks
best on LPIPS on every dataset. SSIM has been observed to favor
averaging-based reconstructions [ZZS22], which favors both naive
blending and the learned aggregation of Im2SurfTex, and does not
fully reflect the perceptual quality differences visible in Fig. 8. (See
App. C for more results and discussion of the metrics.)
Though we focus on view-independent texture generation,
Fig. 8 also shows renderings from the NeRF model produced by
MeshLRM [WZB∗25]. Using a view-dependent representation of
the surface color, NeRF reproduces the input renderings. However,
for the frontal views shown in the figure, which are not in the set
of input views, the renderings exhibit undesired artifacts. (For ex-
ample, the stripes of the flag on the Penguin are misaligned and the

8. Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work
Repeating Patterns In the context of generated images, repeating
patterns are a particular challenge as different renderings are not
guaranteed to produce the same number of elements or have the
same layouts. We demonstrate two such difficult cases in Fig. 13.
For the back of the Penguin shown in Fig. 8, renderings #1 and
#2 have different numbers of stripes than renderings #3 and #4,
making perfect registration impossible. This results in both twist-
ing (due to optical flow) and blurring (due to stitching). Notably, on
the front of the Penguin, where the number of stripes is consistent
across the views, our approach aligns the stripes and is not trapped
in the local minima that tend to arise when registering textures con-
taining repeating patterns.
For the front of the Maneki-neko shown in Fig. 13b, the render-
ings have different layouts of stripes. Still, our method produces an
output in which the individual stripes are sharp, with only a little
localized blurring resulting from merging inconsistent layouts.
Compatibility Our surface-domain optical flow and gradient-
domain stitching are easy to integrate within existing approaches.
For example, Fig. 14 shows that combining our registration and
stitching with ColorMap’s camera pose optimization produces
more symmetric textures than using either method alone.
Failure Cases As with appearance-based registration methods in
general, our method struggles when there is little overlap between
the renderings or when there is insufficient photometric detail in
the regions of overlap to guide the optical flow. This can be seen
in Fig. 15a where the limited overlap combined with the lack of
features results in ghosting around the paws.
Additionally, the optical flow energy is (approximately) invari-
ant to the application of the same deformation to all textures, with
only the regularization weight introducing a bias towards smaller
deformation. As a result, texture-registration may exhibit drifting,
where colors are aligned but move jointly from their “natural” lo-

methods for quantifying blurriness within our confidence measure,
particularly as this can reduce the running time when using denser
multi-view datasets. We would also like to consolidate and simplify
parameters, to make it easier to apply our method to data acquired
with different modalities.
More generally, we would like to explore learning confidence
values from the input renderings and their depth-maps. We would
also like to explore augmenting the optical flow energy with a term
measuring the alignment of photometric features to geometric fea-

Appendix A: RGB-D Scan Processing
To obtain texture-atlased geometry for the Fountain and Armchair,
we perform the following steps:
1. We remove background geometry from the RGB-D scans.
2. We reconstruct the surface [KH13] and remove spurious surface
components reconstructed in under-sampled regions.
3. We perform isotropic remeshing [HDD∗93] followed by
quadratic edge collapse [GH97] to ensure that meshes are of
similar quality and have comparable resolution.
4. We texture-atlas the geometry [SC17].
Steps 1-3 were performed using MeshLab [CMRC25].
Appendix B: Implementation Details
We discuss details of our implementation and parameter selection
for results shown in Sec. 7.
Back-Projection
We compute the depth confidence for visible texels in the i-th view
by measuring texels’ distance from the nearest silhouette and hav-
ing the confidence fall off for texels within a prescribed radius:
1. We compute the depth-map for each camera Ci.
2. We compute the Laplacian of the depth-map and scale it to have
standard deviation equal to one.
3. We detect discontinuity boundaries as locations where the
Laplacian is greater than one.
4. For each texel, we compute its position on the surface, back-
project it into the depth-map, and compute the distance to
the nearest discontinuity, Ddisc. We then set the confidence to

Optical Flow Registration
There are four parameters for op-
tical flow registration: the num-
ber of hierarchy levels, L; the
weights affecting the smoothness
of the estimated flow-field, βblur
and βreg; and the extent of the
smoothing used to estimate local
mean and scale, ϵn. The number
of levels is a trade-off, with too
few resulting in an overly smooth flow-field that fails to regis-
ter fine features, and too many resulting in a flow-field that better
aligns the features at the cost of allowing high-frequency oscilla-
tion in the deformation. Empirically, we found that setting L = 4
and βblur = 0.002 worked for most experiments. We set βreg = 0.2
for generated data and βreg = 0.1 for the RGB-D scans, and we set
the normalization weight to ϵn = 0.001. (The inset shows registra-
tion results for the mouthpiece of the Helmet in Fig. 3, computed
using different numbers of hierarchy levels, L ∈{2,4,6,8}.)
Gradient-domain Stitching
Though we had to fine-tune the gradient fitting weight in a small
number of cases, we found that αgf = 0.01 works well in general.
We fix the smoothness regularization weight at αreg = 10−8 so as
to only take effect in areas of zero confidence.
Baselines
In our evaluations, We use the publicly available implementations
of the baselines [WMG14,ZPK18,ZZS22,GLAK25].
For ColorMap we found that the alternating optimization did not
produce optimal results on our synthesized data. Instead, we eval-
uated three variants (alternating optimization, rigid-only optimiza-
tion, and rigid followed by non-rigid optimization) and present the
best visual result.
For AdvIA we lower the texture resolution to 256×256 for each
patch when processing the synthesized data to reduce the speckling
artifacts resulting from the sparse sampling of camera views. For
the RGB-D scans we use a resolution of 1024×1024.
For Im2SurfTex, we use the released implementation and pre-
trained weights, running only the neural back-projection stage. We
modify the implementation to accept full extrinsic camera param-
eters and to reduce its memory cost to fit our hardware. We set
the texture resolution to 256 × 256 to reduce speckling artifacts
on the synthesized datasets, and to 1024 × 1024 for the RGB-
D scans. Geodesic distances are computed using the affine heat
method [YS25].
As some baselines require a finer mesh resolution for better re-
sults, we tested all methods with zero, one, two, and three passes
of subdivision and present the best results. For MVS-tex and Col-
orMap, meshes are subdivided using planar one-to-four subdivi-
sion. For AdvIA, we use the provided Loop subdivision.
Appendix C: Quantitative Comparisons

ent orientations and magnitudes, ECD is more sensitive to blurring
and ghosting. Figure 16 shows an example of this – despite the
ghosting, LPIPS and SSIM exhibit a preference for the blended re-
sult while ECD identifies the sharper texture produced using our
approach as more similar to the reference.
