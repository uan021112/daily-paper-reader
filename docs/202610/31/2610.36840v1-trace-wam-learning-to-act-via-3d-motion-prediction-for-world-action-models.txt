Abstract
World Action Models (WAMs) learn representations for
robot control by coupling future visual prediction with
action generation. Successful manipulation depends pri-
marily on how the robot and task-relevant objects evolve,
whereas future-video prediction must also reconstruct the
appearance and background of the entire scene. We there-
fore propose Trace-WAM, which augments video-action
learning with future 3D motion prediction as an auxiliary
objective.
This objective supervises temporal point cor-
respondence together with image-plane displacement and
depth change, directly describing the interaction dynam-
ics and spatial changes involved in manipulation. To this
end, we adopt a three-branch Mixture-of-Transformer (Tri-
MoT) architecture to jointly learn video prediction, motion
prediction, and action generation. The motion expert pre-

Experiments on RoboCasa-

distribution performance and out-of-distribution general-
ization, with particularly clear gains over Fast-WAM under
limited demonstrations and distribution shifts.
1. Introduction
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [14, 16, 33] adapt
pretrained vision-language representations to robot control,
learning to generate actions from visual observations and
language instructions. Despite their strong policy-learning
capabilities, most VLAs focus on action prediction without

explicitly modeling how the scene evolves during execu-
tion. World Action Models (WAMs) incorporate this future
evolution into policy learning by combining future visual
prediction with action generation [12, 48]. Built on pre-
trained video models, WAMs transfer rich spatiotemporal
priors to robot manipulation by learning from future ob-
servations together with action sequences. Yet successful
manipulation depends primarily on how the robot and task-
relevant objects change over time, whereas future RGB pre-
diction must also model texture, illumination, and back-
ground appearance across the entire scene.
This differ-
ence motivates a complementary target that emphasizes in-
teraction dynamics while retaining the rich visual context
learned from future RGB. Recent approaches enrich pol-
icy representations by augmenting visual prediction with
auxiliary supervision for geometry, semantics, and interac-
tion structure. These objectives include depth and geomet-
ric prediction, semantic feature learning, and supervision
of affordances and goal regions [12, 24, 30, 47, 49, 53], en-
couraging representations to capture spatial layout and task-
relevant interaction constraints. A per-frame or region-level
target, however, does not by itself identify how the same
physical locations evolve throughout an interaction, a dis-
tinction also emphasized by 4D-WAM [42]. This temporal
correspondence connects the current scene to the sequence
of changes required to complete a manipulation task.
We therefore focus on 3D motion as an auxiliary predic-
tion target for WAM representation learning, motivated by
two complementary properties. First, each trajectory con-
nects a point in the current observation to its positions at
successive future steps, revealing the coordinated motion
of the robot and task-relevant objects (Fig. 1(a)). Second,
depth change complements image-plane displacement by
describing motion toward or away from the camera. In our
annotation census, among point tracks moving by at least
2% of the image diagonal over 32 steps, 79.3% in LIBERO
and 32.9% in Piper exhibit depth changes of at least 5% of
their initial depth (Fig. 2(a)). Depth variation therefore ac-
companies a substantial fraction of visible motion in both
simulation and real-world annotations. Its value for pol-
icy learning is supported by our depth ablation: retaining
the depth component improves LIBERO-Plus success from
82.4% to 84.5% (Fig. 2(b)). These properties describe the
temporal and spatial changes involved in manipulation.
Based on this formulation, we propose Trace-WAM,
which jointly learns future video prediction, future 3D mo-
tion prediction, and action generation. We represent mo-
tion using trajectories anchored to a fixed grid in the current
image, combining image-plane displacement with depth
change relative to each point’s initial position. Whereas 4D-
WAM [42] transfers trajectory knowledge by aligning tem-
poral feature changes and source-to-destination relations
with those of a pretrained 4D tracking model, Trace-WAM

2. Related Work
2.1. World Action Models
Early approaches formulate manipulation as visual plan-
ning, using generative models to propose future observa-
tions and learned controllers to translate the resulting visual
plans into executable actions [4, 10]. More recent World
Action Models integrate video priors more directly into pol-
icy learning, either conditioning actions on predictive video
features [13, 25] or jointly modeling future visual observa-
tions and action sequences [1, 18, 22, 31, 34, 41, 46].
Beyond RGB prediction, recent methods extend predic-
tive supervision to semantic features, depth and 3D ge-
ometry, and motion, encouraging policy representations to
capture object identity, spatial layout, and scene dynam-
ics [21, 30, 38, 40, 41, 47]. Complementary supervision of
affordances, object-centric interaction configurations, and
goal regions emphasizes which scene elements are involved
in the task and where the robot should interact [49, 53].
Trace-WAM follows this direction by using auxiliary fu-
ture 3D motion prediction to supervise temporal point cor-
respondence and depth change jointly with future RGB pre-
diction and action generation.
2.2. Motion for Policy Learning
Motion representations connect action generation to the
spatial and temporal structure of manipulation by describ-
ing how the robot and objects move throughout an inter-
action. Incorporating depth further captures displacement
along the viewing direction, complementing image-plane
motion with spatial information needed for reaching, grasp-
ing, and object transport. Existing approaches couple ac-
tion learning with optical flow or 2D point-motion predic-
tion, using motion features or predicted motion to guide
control [8, 37, 43], while others incorporate depth and 3D
motion to model spatial scene dynamics for action genera-
tion [20, 39, 52]. A complementary line of work uses mo-
tion supervision to improve policy representations, through
distillation from pretrained tracking models [36, 42] or
auxiliary 3D motion prediction during human–robot co-
training [21]. Trace-WAM directly predicts future 3D point
trajectories alongside RGB prediction and action genera-
tion, using numerical motion targets rather than tracker-
feature alignment.
The motion objective shapes video-
action representations during training; deployed actions use
current visual context, language, and proprioception with-
out future-video or motion prediction.
3. Method
Trace-WAM jointly learns future video prediction, future
3D motion prediction, and action generation. Future 3D
motion provides auxiliary supervision for video-action rep-
resentation learning, while the deployed policy generates
actions from current observations. Figure 3 summarizes the
Tri-MoT architecture and its attention masks.

3.2. 3D Motion Representation
Motion Targets.
Following the grid-based motion repre-
sentation of LaMP [39], we place Q anchors on a uniform
grid covering the current main-camera image and associate
each anchor with its positions over HM future steps. Our
reference configuration uses a 20 × 20 grid (Q = 400) and
HM = 32, giving 33 positions per anchor when the current
frame is included. Anchors follow a fixed row-major order.
Although the policy can receive multiple camera views, the
motion grid is defined only in the main-camera image.

3.3. Model Architecture
Tri-MoT Architecture.
Trace-WAM uses three Trans-
former experts with separate parameters and input/output
projections. A frozen causal video VAE encodes current
and future RGB as latent tokens. The video expert processes
clean current tokens V0 and, during training, noisy future
tokens Vf. The action expert embeds each step of a noisy
action chunk as a token. A trainable projection embeds the
current state as proprioceptive tokens; a frozen text encoder
supplies instruction embeddings via cross-attention.
Motion Expert.
The motion expert operates on noisy B-
spline coefficients. We arrange the 3K coefficients of each
anchor as channels on the spatial grid, then embed them
with a convolution whose kernel and stride are both 2 ×
2. With the reference settings, this maps the 20 × 20 grid
to 100 motion tokens. Each token contains four complete
future trajectories, preserving their individual coefficients.
This packs the motion horizon into token channels instead
of creating a token for every future time step.
We encode each patch center with two-dimensional
Fourier features and add their learned projection to its token
embedding. Noise-time embeddings modulate the Trans-
former normalization and gated residual updates. At every
layer, motion tokens attend to one another and to current
video features, while the instruction and proprioceptive to-
kens provide task and state conditions. The output head pre-
dicts 4 × K × 3 velocity components per token, unpacked
into per-anchor coefficient velocities.
Structured Attention.
At each layer, the experts project
their queries, keys, and values to a common attention di-
mension. The attention masks in Fig. 3 allow current video
tokens to attend only to one another, while future-video,
motion, and action tokens attend to their own group and to
current video. Attention within future video and within the
action chunk is bidirectional across time.
The video, motion, and action objectives are jointly op-
timized, using future 3D motion as an additional learning
signal for the representations used in action generation. Be-
cause neither current video nor action tokens attend to mo-
tion or future video, the deployed policy does not depend
on either prediction branch.

4.2. Main Results
Performance on RoboCasa.
As shown in Table 1, Trace-
WAM achieves success rates of 63.6% and 80.3% in the
few-shot and full-data settings, outperforming Fast-WAM
and Joint-WAM in both.
The gains are especially pro-
nounced with limited demonstrations, reaching 18.8 and 8.8
percentage points over the two baselines, respectively. Fig-
ure 4 further shows consistent generalization across unseen
appearances, object combinations, and object types. Trace-
WAM leads all three comparison methods in every setting
and reaches 58.6% average success, exceeding DIAL, Fast-
WAM, and Joint-WAM by 12.4, 16.3, and 8.6 percentage
points, respectively. Together, these results show that future
3D motion supervision supports both in-distribution perfor-
mance and OOD generalization.
Performance on LIBERO.
Trace-WAM achieves an av-
erage success rate of 98.7% across the four LIBERO
suites (Table 2), compared with 97.6% for Fast-WAM and
98.5% for Joint-WAM. This further demonstrates strong in-
distribution performance across diverse manipulation tasks.
Zero-shot Robustness on LIBERO-Plus.
Without fur-
ther adaptation, Trace-WAM reaches 84.5% success on
LIBERO-Plus (Table 3), exceeding Fast-WAM and Joint-
WAM by 18.3 and 5.8 percentage points, respectively.
It improves over Fast-WAM across all seven perturbation
types. In particular, success rates under Robot and Back-
ground perturbations reach 87.4% and 76.1%, improving
over Fast-WAM by 18.9 and 9.2 points, respectively. These
gains support the value of motion supervision for learning
interaction-relevant representations: temporal correspon-
dence and depth change describe how the robot and ob-
jects move during manipulation, encouraging features that
remain useful under altered robot initial states and task-
irrelevant background changes.
4.3. Ablation Study and Analysis
Motion Supervision Ablation.
Table 4 shows the contri-
butions of the two motion-supervision components. The
w/o Motion FM-split variant removes the separate bal-
ancing of moving and static anchors in the motion flow-
matching loss, achieving 80.4% average success. The w/o
Static-Motion Penalty variant achieves 83.6%. Trace-WAM
reaches 84.5%, improving over these variants by 4.1 and
0.9 percentage points, respectively. These gains highlight

Motion Conditioning Ablation.
The Motion Inject vari-
ant injects future 3D motion latents from each motion-
expert layer into the corresponding action-expert layer,
analogous to the layerwise video–action connection in
Joint-WAM, and achieves 81.6% average success.
The
Video–Motion Bid-Attention variant instead allows the
video and motion streams to attend to each other at ev-
ery layer and achieves 80.3%. Trace-WAM keeps future
3D motion as a jointly optimized auxiliary objective and
reaches 84.5%, improving over these variants by 2.9 and 4.2
percentage points, respectively. These results favor auxil-
iary 3D motion supervision within video-action representa-
tion learning, without bidirectional video–motion coupling
or action conditioning on future motion latents.

4.4. Real-world Experiments
Setup.
The experiments are conducted on the Piper plat-
form with a gripper and two Intel RealSense D435i cameras
providing fixed and wrist views (Figure 5). We evaluate
three tasks: Place Bread requires placing bread in a bowl;
Place and Close Drawer requires placing an object in the
drawer and closing it; and Wipe Blackboard requires eras-
ing the chalk markings. Each task uses 50 demonstrations,
and each method is evaluated over 20 trials per task.
Results.
Trace-WAM achieves 85.0% average success
across the three physical tasks, compared with 63.3% for
Fast-WAM (Figure 5). The largest gain occurs on Place and
Close Drawer, where success rises from 55.0% to 95.0%.
Wipe Blackboard improves from 55.0% to 75.0%, and Place
Bread in Bowl from 80.0% to 85.0%. These results extend
the benefits of auxiliary 3D motion supervision to physical
manipulation, including sequential placement and closing
as well as sustained surface contact.
5. Conclusion
We presented Trace-WAM, which uses auxiliary future
3D motion prediction to focus video–action represen-
tation learning on the scene dynamics most relevant to
manipulation.
The video, motion, and action objectives
are jointly optimized during training, while the deployed
policy generates actions from current observations, lan-
guage, and proprioception without running future-video or
motion prediction. Across LIBERO, zero-shot LIBERO-
Plus, and RoboCasa-GR1, Trace-WAM achieves strong
in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance, with
clear gains in the few-shot and OOD settings. Real-world
experiments on a Piper robot further demonstrate improved
success across three manipulation tasks. These results es-
tablish future 3D motion prediction as an effective training
signal for learning robust action representations in WAMs.
